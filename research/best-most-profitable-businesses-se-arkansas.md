# Best and most profitable businesses in Southeast Arkansas

**Research brief — not legal, tax, investment, licensing, or career advice.**  
**Geography:** Monticello / Drew County as the hub, with nearby SEAEDD counties (Ashley, Bradley, Chicot, Cleveland, Desha, Lincoln, plus Jefferson as the Pine Bluff labor market) where the evidence applies.  
**Written:** 14 September 2026. Figures are as published on the cited pages on that date; several Census and BLS series are later revised.

This brief ranks opportunities by **local demand durability + owner-economics + rural fit**, not by national “hot business” lists. Local employment counts and location quotients (LQs) come from the Bureau of Labor Statistics Quarterly Census of Employment and Wages (QCEW). National margin and startup-cost ranges are **illustrative benchmarks**, not Drew County P&Ls. Where a local competitor census was not published, this brief does **not** invent one.

**Uncertainty flags** appear inline. The single strongest next step is on-the-ground validation (section 9).

---

## 1. Criteria used in this ranking

| Criterion | What “good” looks like here | Why it matters in SE AR |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Profitability (margins)** | Owner can take a living wage **plus** a residual after labor, parts, insurance, and truck/shop cost. Prefer industries where well-run operators sit above a thin national median. | Drew County private average annual pay was **$43,975** in 2024 QCEW; a business that only replaces a $40k wage is a job, not an enterprise. |
| **Cash timing** | Invoices collect in days–weeks, not 90+ days; limited inventory float; deposits or COD possible. | Thin household liquidity (city median income far below the county) and Medicaid/Medicare lag punish slow-pay models. |
| **Demand durability** | Need is non-discretionary (heat/AC, vehicles, aging, industrial uptime) or tied to a multi-year employer pulse. | The county is **losing population** while the state is growing. Fashion retail and “another restaurant” fight a shrinking customer base. |
| **Capital intensity** | Lower fixed plant is better unless the asset is a scarce local bottleneck (housing, industrial service bays). | SBA-style rural credit is available in principle; cash and net-worth tests for Arkansas contractor licenses are real. |
| **Skill / licensing barriers** | A license that is painful to get is a **moat** if you already have it, and a **wall** if you do not. | HVACR, plumbing, electrical, pesticide, childcare, and Arkansas Permit of Approval (POA) healthcare are gated. |
| **Competition** | Prefer QCEW LQs **below 1.0** (local employment thinner than the U.S.) or documented shortages (HPSA, housing). High LQ + permit cap = hard to enter, not “easy money.” | Example: plumbing employment LQ **0.27**; home-health employment LQ **2.46**. |
| **Rural market fit** | Mobile or regional-radius service; customers who cannot easily drive to Pine Bluff / Little Rock / Monroe; B2B that follows timber, ag, healthcare, and UAM. | SEAEDD notes the district has **no interstate east of a Texarkana–Little Rock line**, which cuts chain retail/distribution but raises the value of local service. |

**How “best” and “most profitable” are split**

- **Best for a new entrant:** can start with a truck and a license (or no license), collect cash quickly, and survive a 17k-person county.
- **Highest absolute profit, hard to enter:** can throw off six-figure owner earnings or enterprise value, but needs credentials, POA/CON-like permits, crews, or seven-figure plant.

National **net** margins in services are often mid-single digits on revenue; owner-operators who do the work themselves look richer because owner labor is not fully loaded. This brief treats that distinction as material.

That is a **modeled** need, not a building-permit reality (4 permits in 2025).

### 2.3 Major employers and anchors

**University of Arkansas at Monticello.** UAM Institutional Research (Fall 2025): **3,544** students on all campuses (1,224 high school / concurrent, 1,947 undergraduate, 373 graduate); **448** in on-campus housing. A Pine Bluff *Commercial* report the same season cited **3,525** headcount and **1,221** concurrent — treat the IR page as primary and the last dozen students as a reporting difference. Concurrent growth (UAM news: **+23%** total headcount, **+136%** concurrent vs Fall 2024) inflates “college town” demand less than the headline implies: many concurrent students are not living or spending like on-campus undergraduates.

UAM’s own economic-impact study (public presentation; date on UAM news page, study year not independently re-run here) claimed **1,296 jobs** and **$292.6 million** annual activity in a 14-county SE Arkansas region, and **3,519 jobs / $925.0 million** statewide. That is an **impact model**, not a cash register.

**Hospital.** Baptist Health Medical Center–Drew County (formerly Drew Memorial): Baptist Health says it is a **60-bed** acute-care hospital serving “approximately **200,000** residents in southeast Arkansas,” with ED (Level IV trauma), cancer center, sleep center, surgical services, and a specialty clinic. Baptist began managing operations October 2023 and closed the acquisition December 2023. Arkansas Advocate (13 Jun 2023) documented pre-merger **staffing shortages, high turnover, and ARPA relief (~$5 million)**. Becker’s reported **43 layoffs** during the transition. **Uncertainty:** “200,000 residents served” is a hospital service-area claim, not the Drew County population.

**City employer list (dated).** Monticello *Vision 2047* (planning document; figures appear to be early-2020s, **not 2025 QCEW**) listed: UAM 392; Drew Memorial 365; Monticello School District 325; Maxwell Hardwood 172; Hood Packaging 153; “Advantages of SE Arkansas” 150; Drew Central 142; SeaArk Boats 140; Walmart 127; Interfor 120; Foam Holdings 117; War Eagle Boats 86; City 85. Use as a **named-employer map**, not current headcount. QCEW 2024 state-college employment (376) is in the same ballpark as the plan’s UAM 392.

**Forest products.** UAM Arkansas Center for Forest Business, *Drew County Forests and Forest Industry* (2021 profile): forests on **420,390 acres (79%** of land), **94%** privately owned; forest industry **705 jobs**, **$67 million** value-added, **11%** of a **$623 million** county GDP in that vintage; **12 logging firms**; mills named Interfor, LTY, Hood Packaging, Maxwell Hardwood Flooring. USDA Forest Service (2022 Arkansas forest update) mapped **Drew among the heaviest cutting counties** in the state.

**Weyerhaeuser TimberStrand (live industrial pulse).** AEDC (18 Nov 2024 announcement; 18 Jun 2025 groundbreaking) and Weyerhaeuser (8 Jul 2025): about **$500 million**, **~200** plant jobs, laminated-strand lumber, fiber from company timberlands, operations targeted **2027**, site described as **near Monticello and Warren** (Drew/Bradley / SE Arkansas Regional Intermodal). UAEX / UAM (9 Dec 2024) quoted Matthew Pelkki: ~200 jobs, **~300** related jobs, about **$25 million** added regional payroll. **Uncertainty:** induced-job and payroll figures are center estimates, not measured 2026 payroll.

**Agriculture.** USDA NASS *2022 Census of Agriculture*, Drew County profile: **260** farms, **102,338** acres, average **394** acres; top acres soybeans 21,294, cotton 10,276, forage 6,980, corn 6,836, rice 5,945; cattle **11,308**; broilers **716,060**; market value of products sold is in the NASS PDF (crops + livestock split shown there). SEAEDD CEDS: fewer, larger farms; aquaculture and local poultry **attrition**; Bradley County **seasonal tomato** labor. Bradley County Pink Tomato Festival (chamber site): tomato shipping culture since the 1920s; festival since 1956; Arkansas Legislature named the Bradley County pink tomato the state fruit and vegetable (1987). Festival attendance figures in lifestyle write-ups (e.g. “roughly 35,000”) are **not treated as audited**.

### 2.4 Traffic, infrastructure, and the “why not a chain store” constraint

SEAEDD’s 2022/2023 CEDS is blunt: the district has **no continuous four-lane and no interstate** east of a Texarkana–Little Rock line; many corporations **screen the region out** for distribution and large retail for that reason. Highways **US 425** and **US 278** are the Monticello cross. *Vision 2047*: commercial gravity shifted to the **northwest 425 / Walmart** corridor in the 1990s; downtown remains unusually alive for a Delta county seat but is not the volume retail node. The plan also flags **water/wastewater and drainage** as binding constraints on growth.

I-69 remains a **long-horizon** corridor in CEDS language, not a present traffic count. This brief found **no citable AHTD average-annual-daily-traffic extract** in the research window; do not invent ADT.

**Broadband:** 80.5% of Drew households subscribe — usable for a home office, **not** equivalent to urban fiber. About one in five households has no broadband subscription.

### 2.5 Healthcare access (shortage designations)

A secondary compiler of HRSA data (Plain Health Access, county 05043) reports Drew County with **4 active HPSAs** (2 primary care, 1 dental, 1 mental health), scores up to **21 / 26 / 21**, and an **MUA** index of **58.6**. HRSA’s official lists are the 15 Oct 2024 Federal Register notice and the live data.hrsa.gov warehouse (updated daily). **Verify live HPSA IDs before any practice-siting decision** — this brief did not download the full HRSA CSV.

University of Arkansas (2025–26 news): about **45%** of Arkansans live in rural areas; a Rural Health Transformation grant is building a statewide care-gap map (THRIVE RADAR). That supports “access is a statewide rural problem,” not a Drew-specific clinic count.

Arkansas still runs a **Permit of Approval** program (Health Services Permit Agency) for nursing facilities, assisted living, **home health**, **hospice**, and related facilities. Home-health need methodology (20 CAR § 255-301): an area with population **up to 30,000** may be approved for a **maximum of two** agencies, with an exception if existing agencies are not meeting need. Application fee **$3,000**; four filing dates per year. CMS has also posted enrollment moratoria on the AHSPA page (home health and hospice) — **check current status**; moratoria change.

### 2.6 What this context implies for “best businesses”

1. **Sell to payrolls and insurers, not only to $27k city households.** UAM, schools, Baptist, mills, loggers, and (from 2027) Weyerhaeuser are the solvent demand nodes.
2. **Services that travel beat stores that wait.** Population and traffic do not support another commodity box.
3. **Aging housing + heat/humidity + old HVAC stock** is a structural repair market even while **new** construction permits are almost zero.
4. **Do not confuse a high-LQ industry with a vacancy.** Home health and nursing homes already employ a lot of people. Plumbing/HVAC employment does not.
5. **A 2025–2027 construction/start-up pulse** around TimberStrand is real; treating it as a permanent restaurant or hotel thesis is not.

## 4. Top opportunities in detail

### 4.1 Licensed HVAC, plumbing, and electrical — rank 1

**Why it fits SE AR.** Hot-humid climate; median housing vintage ~1984; *Vision 2047* and CommunityScale both describe a worn, tight housing stock; CEDS demand-occupation list for SE Arkansas includes **49-9021 HVAC mechanics**, **47-2111 electricians**, and **47-2031 carpenters**. QCEW 2024: construction LQ **0.52**; plumbing/HVAC contractors (23822) LQ **0.27** with only **3** establishments and **14** covered employees. That is the clearest “thin supply” signal in the file. Weyerhaeuser’s 2025–27 build adds a **temporary** commercial/industrial service load.

**Who pays.** Homeowners and landlords (cash or card, often same-week); light commercial (UAM, clinics, restaurants, mills); insurers on casualty jobs. Household incomes are a **price ceiling** on discretionary replacement; emergency repair and financed replacement are the realistic mix.

**Capital (illustrative, not local quotes).** Independent HVAC/plumbing launch commonly cited in 2026 trade/lender write-ups at roughly **$30,000–$80,000** for a used van, tools, EPA card, insurance, and ~90 days of cash; **$75,000–$150,000** for a branded truck + inventory + one employee. Arkansas HVACR license **fees** themselves are small (Class A **$200**/year, Class B **$150**/year per 17 CAR § 261-106) relative to the truck. ACLB commercial contractor license is a separate gate for jobs **$50,000+** (widely stated threshold; confirm on the Contractors Licensing Board). Mechanical classification requires a master plumber **and** Class A/B HVACR employee on staff (ACLB rules).

**Margin signals (national).** VantaInsights cites IRS SOI corporate returns for plumbing/heating/AC at about **5.7% net** as a sector average; COREView’s 2026 SBA-style write-up for NAICS 238220 describes service-heavy firms with healthier EBITDA than install-only shops, and notes **housing-unit age** as a replacement pipeline. House-call software vendors advertise higher “well-run” nets (8–15%); treat vendor blogs as **marketing-adjacent**. Owner-operator shops can look like 15–25% if owner labor is unpaid on the P&L.

**Competition.** QCEW counts **establishments**, not Google listings or unlicensed side work. Three covered plumbing/HVAC employers does **not** mean three trucks in the county. Informal competitors are uncounted. Still, LQ 0.27 is directionally “not saturated in the formal sector.”

**Risks.** License and EPA compliance; summer cash / winter hole; refrigerant and equipment inflation; one large warranty claim; worker’s-comp and commercial auto; ACLB net-worth tests if you bid commercial; Weyerhaeuser construction work is **project, not annuity**.

**New-entrant note.** If you **already** hold HVACR / master electrician / master plumber, this is the best local “business-shaped job” in the evidence set. If you do not, the two-year experience + exam wall makes this a **hard** path, not a weekend startup.

### 4.2 Industrial, diesel, millwright, and mill/logging support — rank 2

**Why it fits SE AR.** Forestry/logging LQ **85**; wood-product manufacturing LQ **22**; 25 logging establishments and 164 logging jobs (2024); CEDS demand list includes **diesel specialists, millwrights, sawing-machine operators, truck drivers, log graders**. UAM-CTC $2 million HIRED grant (Dec 2024) and the Sustainable Trades Alliance exist because **industry asked for forestry-automation and heavy-equipment skills**. Weyerhaeuser’s $500 million plant is the largest announced industrial event in a generation for the Monticello–Warren pair.

**Who pays.** Interfor, Maxwell, Hood, Price Companies / woodyards, loggers, future Weyerhaeuser, farm operations with irrigation and grain equipment, municipal fleets. B2B terms (Net 30) are the cash-timing weakness versus residential HVAC.

**Capital.** A mobile diesel/welding truck can start in the same **tens of thousands** as HVAC; a shop with alignment, overhead crane, or CNC is **mid-six to seven figures**. No local bid sheet was found.

**Margins.** No Drew-specific P&Ls. National heavy-repair shops sit in the same mid-single-digit to low-teens net band as auto repair (section 4.3). Absolute profit scales with **uptime contracts** and mill outages, not with consumer coupons.

**Competition.** Twelve logging firms (2021 forest-business profile) and a handful of mills is a **small customer set**. Incumbent vendors (Fastenal is named in *Vision 2047* as a boat-industry supplier) already sit on the industrial parks. Winning work is relationship- and response-time-driven, not Yellow-Pages-driven.

**Risks.** Customer concentration; mill downtime or housing-market slump hitting stumpage (UAM Center for Forest Business has published soft 2024 timber-price language in UA Division of Agriculture ag-GDP notes); safety/OSHA; capital equipment obsolescence; 2027 plant start is **not guaranteed on the original calendar**.

**New-entrant vs hard.** A **one-truck mobile welder/diesel tech** is enterable. A **full industrial contractor** or woodyard is among the highest absolute-profit local plays and is not a new-entrant business.

### 4.3 Auto, tire, and mobile fleet repair — rank 3

**Why it fits SE AR.** No interstate, 21-minute mean commute, farm and log trucks, 10 motor-vehicle/parts establishments (117 jobs), 12 automotive-repair establishments (46 jobs). CEDS lists **automotive service technicians** and **diesel specialists**. Rural auto-repair credit analyses (COREView 2026) describe independent shops as the **default** service network in counties under 50,000 people. Vehicles are not optional here.

**Who pays.** Households (cash/card), farm and logging fleets (accounts), school and city fleets (bid).

**Capital (illustrative).** National compilations using Census SUSB put general automotive repair (NAICS 811111) average receipts around **$563k** with an **~8%** implied net and startup **$30k–$150k** (RevenueRanked citing SUSB 2021 / QCEW 2023). A mobile truck is the low end; a two-bay shop with alignment is the high end.

**Margins.** COREView rural auto/tire: median **net ~5.8%**, top quartile **9–12%**, EBITDA often **12–14%**. Tires are volume with commodity risk.

**Competition.** 12 covered auto-repair establishments in a 17k county is **not** an empty market (LQ 1.09). The opening is **diesel, farm, after-hours, mobile, or European/EV diagnostics**, not a fourth generic oil-change if one already sits on 425. This brief did **not** scrape Google Maps; do not treat “12” as “12 storefronts on Main.”

**Risks.** Parts inflation; technician scarcity (CEDS already lists the occupation as in demand); EV long-run mix (slow in this income band); environmental compliance for waste oil.

### 4.4 Non-medical senior care, medical transport, home modification — rank 4

**Why it fits SE AR.** CEDS: healthcare, “particularly nursing and care for the elderly,” has grown and the population is aging. Drew **18.9% 65+**; **15.7%** disability under 65; hospital and nursing-home employment already large (623 LQ **2.15**). PHI (2019) projected rural 65+ growth far faster than rural working-age population through 2030 — a **national rural** trend, not a Drew forecast. HPSA dental and mental-health scores (if confirmed on HRSA) imply **unmet clinical** need; non-medical care is the lower-barrier cousin.

**Important split**

| Segment | Barrier | Local signal | New-entrant? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Medicare home health / hospice | POA + CMS enrollment; possible moratorium; need methodology caps small counties at **2** agencies | QCEW **8** establishments / **177** jobs in 621610, LQ **2.46**, pay only **$21,717** | **No** — already thick, legally gated |
| Non-medical companion / homemaker / respite | Labor + Medicaid waiver rules (verify); usually **not** the same POA as “home health agency” | Same aging + disability facts | **Yes**, if you can hire |
| Wheelchair / dialysis transport | Insurance, vehicle, contract with facilities | Hospital + 65+ + no transit | Yes, small fleet |
| Grab bars, ramps, bathroom rehab | Contractor / building permits | Housing age + disability | Yes if you can build |

**Who pays.** Families (private pay), Medicaid HCBS waivers, VA, hospitals discharging patients, existing home-health agencies that need **aide** subcontract capacity. Medicare-certified skilled nursing is **not** the new-entrant product.

**Capital.** Non-medical care: bonding, payroll, EMR-lite, vehicle — often **low five figures** plus **weeks of payroll float**. Transport: van + lift + insurance. Home-mod: overlapping with section 4.1 / 4.5.

**Margins.** COREView rural home-health/hospice 2026: industry net near **4.2%**, independent EBITDA often **6–10%**, labor inflation faster than Medicare updates. QCEW pay of **$21.7k** in local 621610 is a **labor-quality and turnover** warning, not a profit prize. Non-medical private-pay can be healthier **if** you collect weekly and do not chase unpaid family balances.

**Risks.** Aide shortage (the binding constraint in every national home-care review); Medicaid rate risk; POA if you accidentally operate as a home-health agency; hospital system (Baptist) may expand or contract outpatient and home-health footprints; wage-and-hour.

### 4.5 Housing rehab, weatherization, small rental — rank 5

**Why it fits SE AR.** Documented shortage of **rentals and small houses** (*Vision 2047*, UCA CCED notes inside that plan); **4** building permits in 2025 versus a modeled need of tens of units per year; median rent **$725** and median value **$140,800** — cheap assets by coastal standards, but **financing, water/sewer, and construction labor** are the local constraints. Incoming TimberStrand workers (if 200 jobs land) will hit the same tight rental market.

**Who pays.** Tenants; employers needing temporary housing in 2026–27; USDA Rural Development / weatherization programs; landlords hiring rehab.

**Capital.** One rental house at local values can be **low-to-mid six figures** all-in after rehab — high capital, slow cash. A **rehab contractor** (labor + pickup) is closer to trades capital. Arkansas ACLB applies above the commercial threshold.

**Margins.** Construction firms nationally are cyclical and bid-thin; *Vision 2047* already says qualified labor is scarce. Absolute profit on a successful small-apartment conversion can exceed a service truck; **failure modes** (drainage, city water, vacancy) are also larger. CommunityScale’s 882-unit figure should **not** be read as “build 882 units and they will fill at any rent.”

**Risks.** Population still falling; student housing is a **different** product from family rentals; downtown historic overlay (NRHP downtown noted in CEDS); city infrastructure lag.

---

### 4.6 Bookkeeping, tax, insurance, and IT — local plus remote — rank 6

**Why it fits SE AR.** Professional services LQ **0.28** (22 establishments, 125 jobs). The county has **260 farms**, 25 logging firms, 17 manufacturers, 98 health establishments, and a university — all of which buy bookkeeping, payroll, workers-comp, and cyber-basic IT. Broadband at **80.5%** is enough for a practitioner to **export** services to Little Rock, DFW, or national e-commerce clients while living on SE AR costs.

**Who pays.** Local owner-operators (monthly retainers); remote SMB clients; insurance carriers (commission). Farms: UA Division of Agriculture / Center for Arkansas Farms and Food already pushes QuickBooks-style recordkeeping.

**Capital.** Laptop, E&O insurance, CPA/EA/IT certs — typically **under $15k** plus the cost of the credential.

**Margins.** Professional firms can run **high gross** and **high owner compensation** with almost no inventory. This brief found **no** Arkansas-specific net-margin series worth citing beyond “professional services are labor businesses.” Virtual CPA firms advertising Arkansas remote work exist; that is **existence proof of the model**, not a market study.

**Risks.** You are competing with TurboTax, national payroll, and every remote bookkeeper in the Philippines **and** in Northwest Arkansas. Local trust still wins farm and mill work. Broadband gaps and power reliability are operational risks for a 100% remote shop.

---

### 4.7 Commercial cleaning and property services — rank 7

**Why it fits SE AR.** UAM (classrooms + 448 beds), hospital campus, mills (shift change, industrial janitorial), 34 food-service establishments, and 7 landscaping-coded establishments (employment suppressed). NAICS 5617 has **12** establishments and **93** jobs (LQ 1.00) — not empty, not a gold rush. *Vision 2047* and CEDS both describe industrial parks and a university that outsource facilities work.

**Who pays.** Institutions on bid; restaurants (thin, high churn); mills (stricter safety).

**Capital.** Low for janitorial; higher for floor care and industrial.

**Margins.** Typically **labor-thin**. Night-crew janitorial is a job factory unless you hold a few **fat institutional contracts**.

**Risks.** Incumbent contractors; wage pressure; bonding on public bids.

---

### 4.8 Licensed childcare — rank 8 (high social value, weak “most profitable”)

**Why it is here at all.** UA Office for Education Policy (2025 baseline): even at full capacity, Arkansas licensed/registered providers **cannot** serve ~**137,400** children under six with working parents; full-time infant care ~**$9,600**/year (**16%** of statewide median income). Monticello providers (100 Families / Save the Children Head Start write-up) describe an **employment–childcare paradox** (no job without care, no subsidy without a job). UAM and mill shifts create **non-standard-hour** demand.

**Who pays.** Parents (price-sensitive), ABC / Head Start / School Readiness Assistance, employers (rare).

**Capital and barriers.** Facility, licensing, ratio staffing, insurance. Not a truck business.

**Margins.** Labor share is crushing; OEP notes low educator pay and no consistent statewide turnover metric. This is a **workforce-infrastructure** business, often nonprofit or subsidy-dependent — include it because the **need** is documented, not because unit economics are attractive.

---

### 4.9 Specialty food, value-add ag, seasonal hospitality — rank 9

**Why it is not rank 1.** USDA shows **commodity** acres (soy, cotton, rice, corn) and contract poultry — those are **not** “start a farm” small-business advice. Bradley tomatoes and the Pink Tomato Festival are **real cultural and seasonal retail** assets. CEDS wants tourism and value-add. HortScience (2023) found value-added adoption rises with **market access**; remote growers are less likely to capture it. A 2023 *Renewable Agriculture and Food Systems* study of frozen regional produce found **spinach generally unprofitable** at regional scale and blueberries only under specific shared-kitchen conditions — a warning against “just add a commercial kitchen.”

**What evidence does support.** Direct-to-consumer or food-service of **differentiated** product (pink tomatoes, wild game processing, smokehouse, UAM game-day, mill-shift boxed meals) can work as an **owner-operated** shop. Festival and hunting-season peaks (Felsenthal, Bayou Meto, Cane Creek State Park in the CEDS tourism list) are **real but seasonal**.

**Who pays.** Festival visitors, hunters, UAM, Little Rock weekenders — **unquantified** here.

**Capital.** From a market stall to a USDA-inspected plant. Do not underwrite a plant on festival folklore.

**Risks.** Population decline; grocery and dollar-store competition for ordinary food retail (section 7); food-safety regulation; weather.

---

### 4.10 Niche retail — rank 10, and only with a wedge

**Why the class is weak.** Retail is already **78** establishments and **873** jobs (LQ 1.38). General merchandise LQ **2.42** (Walmart-class). *Vision 2047*: 425 captured the volume; downtown survives on banks, a restaurant, and the Ridgeway senior apartments. USDA ERS (2024): dollar-store entry raises independent-grocery **exit** risk, with **larger, longer** effects in rural tracts (about **2.3%** average exit-probability increase; rural sales hit harder, ~**9.2%** in the rural subsample as reported in secondary coverage of the same study).

**What can still work (evidence-shaped, not a store list).** Categories that Walmart and Dollar General **do not** merchandising-lead: workwear/PPE for mills, hunting/fishing specialty, medical compression/mastectomy (hospital adjacency), UAM-specific, or a **destination** food shop on the square. Community survey respondents in *Vision 2047* asked for **restaurant variety** and more industry — that is **wish** data, not a feasibility study.

## 5. Best for a new entrant vs highest absolute profit (hard)

### 5.1 Best fits for a new entrant (lower wall, faster cash)

These assume legal compliance and insurance. They are **not** “easy.”

1. **Lawn, exterior, and handyman** if you already own a truck — 7 landscaping establishments (employment suppressed); recurring routes; Arkansas pesticide license required the moment you spray. Lowest capital in the set.
2. **Non-medical companion care / respite / medical transport** — demand is demographic; POA usually not the first gate; labor is the gate.
3. **Bookkeeping / tax / insurance / remote IT** — if you already have the credential; LQ 0.28; exportable.
4. **Mobile auto / farm-tire / after-hours diesel** — if you are already a technician.
5. **HVAC/plumbing service truck** — **only if already licensed**; otherwise this belongs in 5.2.
6. **Shift-meal / boxed-lunch catering** to mills and UAM — not another 35th sit-down restaurant (section 7).
7. **Commercial cleaning** targeting one institutional bid rather than 40 houses.

### 5.2 Highest absolute profit, hard to enter

| Business | Why the ceiling is high | Why the door is narrow |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Multi-crew HVAC/plumbing with maintenance agreements | National credit work shows service contracts drive EBITDA; local LQ is thin | License, techs, trucks, ACLB, working capital |
| Medicare home health or hospice platform | Aging + hospital discharge; AR agencies sell at PE multiples (broker commentary, 2026) | POA cap (~2 agencies / <30k pop), CMS rules, **8** existing 621610 establishments, thin aide wages |
| Assisted living / nursing development | 623 already large; 65+ share | POA, bed-need book, construction, Medicaid |
| Dental or mental-health practice | HPSA scores (verify) | License, recruitment, payer mix |
| Wood-products plant, woodyard, or large logger | LQ and Weyerhaeuser prove the cluster pays | Capital, timber contracts, environmental, safety |
| Small apartment / workforce housing | Modeled unit gap + 200 incoming jobs | Land, water/sewer, $ per door, population risk |
| Regional trucking / dedicated mill haul | CEDS transport growth; 85 trucking jobs already | Insurance, CDL, freight cycles, customer concentration |

## 6. Online and remote businesses that can be run from SE AR

Evidence supports **exporting skilled services**, not a generic “Amazon FBA from the Delta” thesis.

| Model | Evidence it can be run from here | Caveats |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bookkeeping, EA/CPA, fractional controller | PST LQ 0.28; farms and mills need books; Arkansas virtual CPA firms already sell the model | Credential and pipeline of clients — not a local-population business |

| Software / IT / cybersecurity for SMBs | UAM + remote work generally; 80.5% broadband | 19.5% of households lack a subscription; fiber quality not in this brief |
| Specialty e-commerce of **local** product (wood, food, hunting) | Forest and ag identity is real | Shipping, USDA/FDA, marketplace fees; no local GMV data |

**What is not evidence-backed here:** “dropshipping,” generic Shopify, or day-trading as a SE AR development strategy.

## 9. Next validation steps (do these before spending)

These are the steps that turn this brief into a go/no-go. None of them were completed as field work for this document.

### 9.1 County and official data (desk, one day)

1. Re-pull **Census QuickFacts** and **ACS DP03/DP04** for Drew + Bradley + Ashley the week you decide (PEP and ACS revise).
2. Download **HRSA** HPSA/MUA CSVs filtered to Arkansas and confirm Drew (and Bradley, Lincoln, Desha) scores and facility vs geographic type ([data.hrsa.gov](https://data.hrsa.gov/topics/health-workforce/shortage-areas)).
3. Open **AHSPA** home-health and hospice **need / bed-need** books and the current decision list. Count **how many POAs already cover Drew** and whether the two-agency cap is binding.
4. Re-pull **QCEW** `https://data.bls.gov/cew/data/api/2024/a/area/05043.csv` (and 05011 Bradley, 05003 Ashley) for the NAICS in section 8. Note disclosure (`N`) cells.
5. **Discover Arkansas** / ADWS: current demand-occupation list (CEDS cited 2022–23) and any newer 2025–26 projections.
6. **AHTD** traffic counts on US 425 and US 278 at the Walmart corridor and the square.
7. **USDA NASS Quick Stats** for 2022 (and any 2025 county estimates) plus FSA office conversation hours.
8. City of Monticello **water/sewer capacity** and any impact-fee schedule — *Vision 2047* says this is binding.
9. **Building permits** series (Census Building Permits Survey) for 2020–2025 to see if “4 in 2025” is a one-off.
10. Weyerhaeuser **public construction updates** (2026–27) before hiring for a “plant worker housing / lunch” business.

### 9.2 Competitor counts (desk + windshield, not invented here)

For each top pick, build a **named** inventory:

- Arkansas HVACR / electrical / plumbing **license lookups**
- Google Business + Facebook + *Advance-Monticellonian* ads (last 12 months)
- QCEW establishment counts as a **floor** on formal employers
- Hospital discharge planners’ **referral list** (home health, transport)
- UAM procurement / facilities vendor list if public

Do **not** stop at QCEW. Side-work techs will not appear.

### 9.3 Customer interviews (the actual test)

Talk to **15–20** paying entities, not friends:

| Segment | Ask |
| --- | --- |
| 8 homeowners 55+ in Drew/Bradley | Last HVAC/plumbing/auto failure; who they called; wait time; what they paid |
| 3 landlords | Rehab backlog; vacancy; contractor no-shows |
| UAM facilities + 1 school district | Outsourced trades, cleaning, after-hours HVAC |
| 2 mills or loggers | Diesel/millwright response time; what they wish existed |
| Baptist case management | Transport and non-medical gaps after discharge |
| 2 childcare directors | Waitlist, infant slots, second-shift |
| 1 insurance agent + 1 banker | Which small businesses actually make deposits |

Kill-criteria examples: if three mills already have exclusive diesel contracts and are happy, rank 2 dies; if homeowners report same-day HVAC and cheap prices, rank 1 is a **wage**, not a gap.

### 9.4 Money and legal (professional, not this brief)

- Arkansas Secretary of State entity, sales tax, and city privilege license
- HVACR Board / Board of Electrical Examiners / ADH plumbing / Plant Board pesticide
- ACLB if any job may exceed the statutory commercial threshold
- AHSPA **before** using the words “home health”
- Insurer: GL, commercial auto, workers-comp, pollution (shops)
- Bank/SBA: 90-day cash, not just equipment

## 10. Bottom line

**Best overall “business-shaped” opportunity in SE Arkansas, on the evidence:** a **service-heavy licensed trades shop** (HVAC/plumbing/electrical), especially if the owner is already the license, selling emergency and replacement work on a 1980s housing stock and light commercial accounts, with a **side door** into mill and Weyerhaeuser construction work.

**Best B2B absolute-profit path:** **industrial/diesel/millwright** and other forestry-cluster services — high LQ customers, high skill wall.

**Best demographic path that is *not* “start a Medicare agency”:** **non-medical care, transport, and home modification** for an older, high-disability population next to a 60-bed regional hospital.

**Best laptop path:** **exportable professional services** (books, tax, insurance, IT) from a low-cost county with a professional-services LQ of 0.28.

**Worst default ideas:** another restaurant, another grocery, another 425 commodity store, or a POA-gated home-health startup justified only by “the county is aging.”

None of this is a prediction that any named firm will succeed. It is a map of **where the published evidence is thicker than the folklore**.
