The cleanest cuttings we take are the ones the tree was going to lose anyway.

A prune day in 8a is usually late winter, after I trust the worst night more than I trust a forecast, and before the buds move. That window is short some years. We thin a bush so light can get in, so we can net, so a wrap — if we even wrap — has a shape. The pieces on the tarp are already the right caliper if we have been fertilizing like we mean it.

I sort on the tarp. Pencil-thick lignified, three nodes, firm: bag, label, wash later. Green tips: stick now or compost. Freeze-burned ends: recut until cream. Crossing twigs that are thin and shaded: compost. Do not bag trash because you are in a rhythm.

This is not the same as pruning for fruit. If I want breba, I leave last year’s wood. If I want a stool that comes back after a 11°F night, I leave low wood near the crown. If I want a tray, I take the extras from the middle. One prune can do two jobs. It cannot do three if you get greedy.

Dad will graft over a variety he is not a fan of rather than cut the tree down. That is a different harvest — scion, not cutting. Ol’ Rusty stays in the grafting story. This story is pruners and a tarp.

I do not prune a first-year in-ground fig for a group buy. That plant is still a pot-shaped root ball learning the clay. I do not prune a potted name we only have one of down to a stick so I can root ten. Root two, maybe, from wood you were shortening. Keep the mother.

Timing versus the mat: if the prune is in January and the shop is already full, the wood can go in the crisper — dormant, parafilmed if it will sit, double-bagged. If the prune is in March and the buds are swelling, the wood goes in mix that week. The prune does not get to invent storage.

8a people prune on a warm Saturday because it feels like spring. That Saturday can be followed by a 22°F night. I still prune if the wood is ready. I do not strip a tree to bone in a warm spell and then leave the wounds to a late freeze as if I planned it. Leave some canopy. The harvest can be smaller.

Zone 7 prune-and-bury is a winter system. Zone 9 prune-whenever is a jungle system. We prune when the tree is quiet and we have a use for the wood the same month. A pile of unlabeled sticks behind the shop is not a harvest. It is a compost delay.

Write the name on the bag before you walk to the next tree. I have mixed two varieties on one tarp because they both looked like brown sticks. They still look like brown sticks. The tag is the only difference until July.

A tarp on wet clay is a mess I have already made. I prune when I can walk the row without the boots sucking. Otherwise I track South Arkansas mud into the shop and the bags are dirty before the wash. The hose can rinse the sticks after they are named, not while they are still a brown pile I swear I will sort later.

I count cups before I count sticks. If the mats are loaded, I cut less, not more, and I leave the extras on the tree. A January prune that fills five grocery bags I cannot wash that day is how unlabeled wood ends up behind the shop. I would rather leave wood on a stool in clay than invent a third crate I will not open until the buds have moved.

Netting and wrap need a shape. I take the crossing wood and the shade wood first because that is the prune the tree wanted. Cuttings are what is left that passes pencil and three nodes. If the bag count is the goal, I have already stopped pruning and started raiding. Zone 7 can bury a hard cut. Zone 9 can grow it back. We still have to fruit in July on the wood we left.

A prune that is also a harvest has a rule: the tree looks better when you leave, and the bags have names. If only one of those is true, you did the wrong job.
