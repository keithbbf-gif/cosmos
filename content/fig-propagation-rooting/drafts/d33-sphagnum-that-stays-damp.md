Long-fiber sphagnum is what we use on layers. It is not the fine dust that turns to mud, and it is not a chunk of potting mix in a bread bag.

Wring it. You want damp fibers that stay apart, not a brown rope of water. If you can pour a stream out of the handful, you are building a swamp on a branch. If it dusts, you are building a nest that will be a brick in four 8a afternoons.

Wrap enough moss to make a fist around the wound, not a mattress. A mattress stays wet in the middle and dry on the skin. A fist you can keep even.

I soak the bale, wring a handful hard, then fluff it so the fibers separate. Long-fiber is the point — it holds a tunnel of air the dust will not. If the handful shines, I wring again. If it dusts, I missed the soak. I do not use the fine milled stuff we would amend a pot with. That turns to mud against a girdle. I do not pack potting mix in foil and call it a layer. Shop mix on a branch is a brick by week two or a swamp by Friday, and either way the wound sits in the wrong weather.

Plastic over the moss holds the weather. Leave a way to add water — a loose end, a bottle with holes, a pot you split and taped around a trunk if you are doing a big one. I have seen people seal a layer like a coffin and then wonder why it cooked. 8a sun on black plastic on a west branch is a heater. Shade the sleeve. White plastic or a wrap of paper over the plastic is uglier and kinder.

Shade the sleeve like you mean it. White plastic, or clear plus a scrap of paper, or a bit of shade cloth clipped on the west face. Black plastic on a west cane in 8a June is a heater. I have touched a sleeve that was hotter than the mat we run at 76–80°F. Roots will not thank you for that. I site the wrap where afternoon sun is broken — east side, interior cane, a little shade from the canopy I did not strip. Pretty branches on the field edge are for photos. Layers are for places a hose can reach.

Check by lift and feel, not by opening every other day. A light sleeve gets a drink. A heavy sour sleeve gets opened, wrung, and probably a new wrap if the wood is still firm. If the wood under the moss is brown and slimy, you are done. Cut the failure off so the tree can close.

Do not fertilize the moss. The mother is the fertilizer. Extra salt in a sleeve is how you burn new roots.

Ants will farm a wet sleeve. Birds will pick at shiny plastic. Dogs will help. Site the layer where you can see it and a hose can reach it. A layer at the back of a thicket is a forgotten sponge.

8a thunderstorms can fill a loose wrap and then bake it. After a storm I lift once. If it is a balloon of water, I drain it. That is not fidgeting. That is the puddle rule on a tree.

Zone 7 sleeves dry slower and rot slower. Zone 9 sleeves dry faster and need a drink on a schedule you will actually keep. We are the thunderstorm version. Feel it after weather, not after breakfast.

A drink is a squeeze of wrung moss or a squirt from a bottle through a loose end, not a hose blast that fills the bag. After a thunderstorm I lift. If the wad is a balloon I drain the corner and retie. After three dry 8a days I lift. If the wad is a biscuit I rewet and I look at the ties. I do not open to “check roots” on either of those days. Roots hate the walk from damp to wind. Week eight is when I am allowed to want a picture. Until then the moss is a climate I am keeping, not a show I am running.

The moss should look like a living thing when you open it at week eight: roots in fiber, not a black pudding. If you open it earlier “to see,” you dry the first roots. Clear plastic is how you peek without a full unwrap. Use it. Then walk away.
