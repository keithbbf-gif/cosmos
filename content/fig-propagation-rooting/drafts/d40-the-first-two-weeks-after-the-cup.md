Pot-up is a wound. The plant does not know you meant well.

Week one I want shade, a mix that is damp, and no show. No fertilizer. No full sun “to toughen it.” No daily lifting to see if the roots explored. They will explore or they will not. Your lifting dries the top and breaks what started.

Wilt the first afternoon is common if the beard was thin or the day was 92°F. Shade and a drink that does not flood. If it is still a rag on day four, scratch the cane. Green means wait. Brown means you potted a liar or you cooked it.

Week two I give it morning sun if the top is firm. Still no wagon ride across a gravel lot at noon. We move hundreds of pots. The new ones do not get to join the shuffle until they hold a leaf through a bright morning.

Water: pick up the pot. Light means drink. Heavy means wait. A #3 of our mix should not stay swamp-heavy for days. If it does, the holes are clogged or the mix is a peat brick. Drill more holes. Do not invent a schedule of “every morning” because that is how you water the shop.

I label again the day I pot-up. Paint pen, two sides if I am not lazy. The cup tag dies in the trash and the name dies with it.

8a storms will beat a tender pot-up flat and then the sun will steam it. I group new pots where I can throw a cloth over a rack. Ten minutes of cloth is cheaper than a week of sulk.

Jack’s gnats will follow a wet pot-up. Let the surface dull. Bits in the water if you must. Do not keep the #3 as wet as the cup was. The cup was a climate. The pot is a lung.

Zone 7 week-two can be a chilly surprise. A cloth for cold is different from a cloth for sun. Zone 9 week-two is only sun. We keep both cloths in the same month some years. April frost after a March pot-up is rude and legal. [VERIFY] your last frost. Ours is usually done by mid-April, and I still watch a late dip.

If I can only do one kindness, it is shade. If I can do two, it is shade and not feeding. The third kindness is leaving it alone. I am not good at the third. I try.

New #3s do not join the hundreds on the gravel. They sit on a rack I can cloth, east side, off black plastic. The fertigation siphon does not visit that rack the first week. Paint pen the morning I pot: name, date, two sides. If I am moving a wagon of old pots the same day, the new ones stay home. A shuffle is wind and sun and a pot that tips. Week-one roots do not need a tour of South Arkansas. They need a mix that can breathe — perlite and castings, not a peat brick — and a human who will pick the pot up instead of watering the shop’s habit onto a wound.

South Arkansas May can rain for three days and then cook. I pick up the pot. A #3 of our mix should go light. If I water because the calendar says morning, I am overwatering. Clay splash from the yard does not belong in the pot. If the surface stays shiny, I wait. Gnats mean I kept it too wet. Drill holes if it never lightens. I keep a cloth on a clip for the cell that flattens a tender pot-up and then steams it. Ten minutes before, ten minutes after if the sun comes back hard. I do not leave the cloth on for a week and bleach the plant. I do not “toughen” it on the wagon at noon to save myself a walk. If a pot blows over, I stand it up and check the wad. I do not write that down as a variety fault.

Day fourteen is a plant that held a leaf, not a graduate. I do not call it established. I do not put it in a clay hole. I do not jump it to a 5-gallon. August is hold even if week two looked brave. Pot-up before June so these two weeks happen in May, when shade and a drink can still turn into growth before the skillet. A mound in the clay is a later decision, and it is a seat, not a grave. Common figs. Long trials. No percent on a scratch test. Green wait. Brown compost. The paint pen is the only claim I will make at day fourteen: this name, this date, this pot.

Two weeks is not established. Two weeks is survived. Established is a later word. Do not spend it yet.
