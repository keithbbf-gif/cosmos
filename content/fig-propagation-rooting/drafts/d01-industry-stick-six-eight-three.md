People mail us mystery twigs and call them cuttings. Some are good wood wearing a bad name. Some are a node and a prayer.

I already wrote the floor on FigRoots and I am not moving it for a marketplace listing. A fig cutting, the kind you can build a tray on, is **6–8 inches** and **three nodes**. Not two. Not a chip you saved because the name was expensive. Three nodes is how the stick has a job underground and a job in the air.

Two of those nodes can go in the mix. One, maybe two, stay up for the shoot. If you bury the whole thing you get a buried battery with no light switch. If you leave four inches of bare wood in dry shop air you get a stick that wrinkles before it drinks.

Pencil-thick is the other half of the sentence. A soda-straw whip from a June flush will root for some people. It will also dry out on a heat mat while you are still proud of the name on the bag. I would not build a collection on chips unless the wood is rare and I have nothing else. Rare is not a method.

Zone 8a does not change the stick. It changes the calendar. We can take dormant wood after the leaves drop and, most years, stick it before a northern grower has thawed the crisper. That is not permission to take shorter wood. The extra weeks of spring are for roots, not for making two hopes out of one six-inch cane.

If someone sends a 14-inch piece with nodes all the way down, you can make two cuttings. That is what we did in the 2023 coir versus DE test — long wood, split, one into each cup. Do not split a six-inch stick into two three-inch wishes. You did not double your plants. You halved your odds and called it efficiency.

A heel — a bit of older wood at the junction — is a gift. It tells you which end was down. It also gives the callus a little more cambium to work with. I will take a heel over a perfectly even rod that looks like it came out of a pencil sharpener.

Zone 7 people sometimes cut longer because they store longer. Zone 9 people sometimes cut greener because the tree never really sleeps. Both habits leak into 8a internet advice. Steal the longer-storage caution from 7 if your wood is sitting until March. Steal nothing from the person who sticks four-inch green tips in September and then asks why January killed them.

Count the nodes in your hand, not in the listing photo. A photo can hide a crush in the middle. Three honest nodes you can feel are worth more than a variety name printed twice.

The tape on the shop bench is there because I got tired of guessing. I lay the cane on it, feel three nodes, and cut 6–8 inches. Long internodes still get three, even if the stick runs closer to eight. Tight internodes do not get extra length so the bag looks generous. A hose rinse at the door knocks South Arkansas clay off so I can see what I am counting. Wet Saturday bark slips. I have bagged a two-node leftover that way and called it a cutting.

I already ran the short-stick experiment the ugly way. After a group-buy trim we had a pile of 4–5 inch leftovers with two honest nodes. I stuck a tray on a mat at 75–78°F because I hated the waste. They leafed. Then the tops wrinkled in shop air and there was no spare buried node to drink. I did not get a second plant. I got cups I had to empty.

When a Zone 7 bundle lands in February it is often 10–12 inches because they cut for a long fridge sit. I recut it to the industry stick before it goes in a 32-ounce cup. A long cane in a short cup puts the wound in a sump or leaves a wick in dry air. Zone 9 wood can measure eight inches and still be green in the middle of the cane. I do not count that as the floor. I count brown nodes I can bury and one I can leave up. Replaceable extras can go longer in an outdoor bed if the clay is not holding a puddle. Named wood stays the boring 6–8 in a cup I can pick up and pot-up before June.

I will throw a one-node stick in a cup if it is the last of a name we care about. I will not tell you that is the industry cutting. The industry cutting is boring on purpose. Boring is how a tray fills.
