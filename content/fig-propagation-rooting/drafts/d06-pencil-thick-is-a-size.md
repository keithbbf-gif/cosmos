“Pencil-thick” is on the Fig Pop page because people kept sticking soda straws and then blaming the mix.

A #2 pencil is about 7 millimeters. That is the floor I want in my hand for a pop. A little thicker is better. A lot thicker — thumb wood — belongs in a deeper pot or an outdoor bed, not jammed into a 4×16 bag so the wound sits in a sump.

Too thin dries. The bark wrinkles. The nodes sink. You scratch and get dust. Thin wood has less stored water and less cambium. A heat mat makes that worse if the bag has any air gap. I have watched a whip look fine on Monday and look like a raisin on Thursday.

Too fat in a small bag rots. The mix cannot stay against the wood. The center stays wet. Fat wood also takes longer to callus in a cup that was designed for a pencil. If I get a beautiful ¾-inch cane, I give it volume: a 32-ounce cup, or a bed. I do not punish it with a snack bag.

The Pop page says pencil-thick **or larger**, lignified, fresh, clean. Larger is not “as fat as you can ship.” Larger is “not a whip.” People hear “or larger” and mail us a club.

Caliper changes with variety. Some LSU figs make generous wood. Some berry figs make skinny internodes even when the tree is happy. I still want three nodes. I will accept a slightly thinner stick on a tight-internode name if the wood is firm and brown. I will not accept a six-inch green thread because the listing said “pencil.”

8a summer flush makes a lot of thin wood. That is fertilizer and rain, not a cutting harvest. If we fertigate and the pots put on a foot of growth, I wait for that wood to brown before I steal it. Green caliper is a costume. Brown caliper is the size.

How I check without a caliper: I roll the stick next to a pencil. If it disappears, it is a whip. If it matches, it is a pop. If it looks like a broom handle, it is a pot or a bed.

Mail-order hides caliper. Photos are taken next to a coin that is closer to the lens. Open the box and sort. Thin goes in a high-humidity cup you will watch, or in the trash if it is also green and crushed. Fat goes in something with a gap under the base.

Zone 7 thin wood in a long fridge stay is dead wood wearing bark. Zone 9 thin wood stuck the same afternoon can work because the tree was never empty. 8a gets both stories in the same February. Sort with your fingers.

On a prune day I keep a real #2 pencil in my back pocket and three buckets on the tarp: whip, pop, bed. I roll each stick against the pencil before it hits a bag. Clay on the fat ones does not make them pops. Those go in the bed bucket if the name is replaceable, or in a 32-ounce cup with a 1–2 inch gap if it is not. The whip bucket is same-day stick or compost. Mixing the three piles because I am cold is how a soda straw ends up on a mat next to thumb wood.

Shop winter under glass stretches internodes. A pot that looks busy in a January photo is often skinny wood with long gaps. I do not harvest that costume. I wait for the plant to go back outside, take sun, and put on caliper, or I take a thicker piece from an in-ground stool. Zone 7 garage wood can be short and tight from real cold. Zone 9 shade-house wood can be fat and still half-green. Ours is the stretched shop cane that fools a camera.

Incoming boxes get the same pencil before they get a cup. I open, roll, sort. A 4 millimeter “pencil” from a listing does not join a named tray of honest sticks. It gets its own cup I will watch, or it gets photographed and refunded. I have already let a handful of straws hide in a line of forty and then blamed the mix when those sticks raisined. The mix was fine. The caliper was a lie. A mat at 75–78°F on a thermostat only makes that lie faster.

I would rather have eight honest pencils than twenty names on threads. The tray looks less impressive. The June pot-up looks like a nursery.
