You can kill a good variety by sticking it upside down. You can also spend three weeks watching a callus form on the sky end and call it a mystery.

Figs remember which way they grew. The end that pointed at the sky wants to make a shoot. The end that pointed at the roots wants to make a callus. If you reverse that, you can still get a knob of scar tissue. You may also get a confused stick that never decides to be a plant.

Look at the buds. They point up the cane, the way they grew. A scar where a leaf sat has a little direction to it if you slow down. A heel of older wood is the loudest clue: that junction was down.

If the seller mailed a perfectly even rod with the buds rubbed off, do not guess. Recut both ends. Look again. If you still cannot read it, mark nothing and do not stick it until morning light. At 11 p.m. every brown stick looks the same. That is how trays get inverted.

I mark the top. Paint pen, a wrap of parafilm, a rubber band — something I can see when the cup is in a line of forty. Parafilm on the top also slows the stick drying out, which is storage logic applied to a pop. It is not a substitute for mix against the bark.

People say figs will “figure it out.” Some will. I have seen a reversed stick push a shoot from a buried node and look like a success until you notice the architecture is a knot. I do not have time to grow knots. Orient it.

When you recut, recut the bottom just below a node and the top just above one if you can. A long blank internode at the top is a wick. A long blank at the bottom is rot space. Nodes are where the work happens.

8a shop light is yellow and flat in January. I sort polarity by a window, not over a heat mat. The mat is for after the stick is right-side-up.

If you inherited a tray someone else stuck, do not rip everything out on a rumor. Scratch a bud. If the up end is already leafing, leave it. If both ends are silent and the “top” buds point into the mix, you have a decision. I would gently lift one, check, and reset. I would not dump the tray.

Zone 7 mail-order in March arrives when you are in a hurry. Zone 9 local wood you cut yourself is easier to read because you were standing on the tree. 8a does both in the same week — a box from a group buy and a prune from the yard. The box is where polarity dies. Open it on a table, not over the trash can.

I already inverted a January tray under the yellow shop bulb. Half the cups were wrong-side-up. Callus formed on the sky end and I watched it for three weeks like it was a mystery. The mat was honest — 75–78°F on a thermostat, probe in the mix. The sticks were not. I lifted, reset, and lost a month I could have spent on roots. Morning window light would have been cheaper.

Clay on a heel is a gift. Wipe it at the hose. Do not recut the junction off to make the stick look like a pencil. That older wood tells you which end was down when the buds are rubbed and the shop is a pile of brown. Parafilm goes on the top after I know which end is the top. Marking first, wrap second. The other way around is how you hide the only clue.

I mark tops on the tarp before the wood reaches the hose. Paint pen on the sky end while the heel still has clay on it. If I wait until the sticks are washed and laid out in a brown row, I am guessing. I have washed a pile clean, lost every heel clue, and then sat under the glass at dusk trying to read leaf scars that all looked the same. A rubber band on the top is ugly and it works when the paint pen dies in January. I do not mark the bottom. I have done that and then forgotten which mark meant up. One convention. Write it on the tape on the tray so the next Saturday-me does not invent a second one.

The first ten seconds with the wood in your hand are cheaper than the fourth week of a silent cup. Which end is up is not a philosophy. It is a direction.
