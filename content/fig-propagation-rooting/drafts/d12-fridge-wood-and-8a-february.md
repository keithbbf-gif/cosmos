Northern people store cuttings because their outdoor year starts in April. We do not get that excuse every winter.

In 8a, February is often already a rooting month. The shop is cool, the mats can run, and March will give you weeks a Zone 6 grower pays for with electricity. If the wood is dormant and you have cups, I would rather see it in mix than in a crisper next to the carrots.

Storage is for overflow. Wood that arrived when the table was full. Wood you cut on a prune day when the mats were already loaded. Wood that has to wait on a name you have not labeled yet — and that last one is a paperwork failure, not a horticulture plan.

How we store when we must: wrap long sits in **parafilm**, **double-bag**, crisper, not the freezer shelf. That is already on the Fig Jam interview and I am not inventing a new fridge gospel. Check once. If you see condensation pouring down the bag, open, dry, rebag. If you see mold, wash what is firm. If you see mush, you stored wet wood.

The fridge is not dormant-making machine. It holds quiet wood quiet. It will not turn a milky February stick into a January stick. Non-dormant wood in a crisper rots or wakes up confused. Green wood in a crisper is a dare.

I label the bag with the name and the date it went in. Unlabeled fridge wood becomes “unknown” by March, and we already have a rule about unknown: the plate names it, not the drawer.

8a power blips and shop-sharing with food are real. Do not store cuttings above something that drips. Do not store them in a freezer because someone said “cold.” Cold and frozen are different deaths.

When I pull wood out, I let it sit on the counter, recut, scratch, then stick. Shocking a 35°F stick into an 80°F wet cup is how you cook the base. The A/B mats in the basement were 80°F because the room was cool. Your mix still needs a thermostat. The wood needs a few minutes to become a room-temperature stick.

Zone 7 can justify a longer sit. Zone 9 barely needs a fridge. 8a people copy Zone 7 storage and then wonder why March wood is tired. Tired wood still roots sometimes. Fresh wood roots more often. I would spend the parafilm on a name I cannot stick this week, not on a lifestyle.

I keep a cheap fridge thermometer in the crisper with the bags. Shop-share with food means someone will turn the dial. I have pulled wood that sat nearer to freezing than I wanted and wood that sat warm enough to sweat. The number on the door is not the number in the drawer. If I cannot keep it quiet and above a freeze, I stick the overflow that week. A mat at 75–78°F is already legal in our February shop. The drawer is not a better plan.

Overflow from a clay prune day gets a date and a pull date, not an open-ended sit. I write both on the bag. Seven days if I can. Fourteen if the mats are truly full. I do not write “later.” Later is April, and April wood here is tired wood I could have stuck in February while the yard was still 40°F at night.

When I do pull, I wash, dry the bark, recut, and let the stick sit on the bench until it is not fridge-cold. Then the cup — squeeze, about 30% dry coir, probe in the mix. I have skipped the bench sit and cooked a base. That failure was speed, not storage. Zone 7 can afford a longer drawer. We can afford a Saturday. I still pot-up before June either way.

Dad’s patience line is for trees in the ground. It is not a reason to leave a bag in the crisper until you “feel ready.” Ready is a Saturday with clean cups. February has several of those. Use one.
