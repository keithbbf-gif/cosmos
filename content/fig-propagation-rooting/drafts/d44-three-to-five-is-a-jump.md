Most of our collection lives in #3s, 3-gallons, and 5-gallons. That is not because bigger is always better. Bigger is heavier, wetter in winter, and drier on the rim in July if you do not change how you water.

I step a plant when the roots have filled the #3 and the top is actually growing, not when I want the bench to look like a nursery ad. A jump into a 10-gallon of wet mix is how you rot a small root ball in a lake. The pot should be a next size, not a swimming pool.

Our mix needs perlite to stay honest in a larger pot. A 5-gallon of straight compost is a swamp in April and a brick in August. I would rather have a tight #3 of open mix than a sloppy 5 of mud.

When I jump, I do it in spring if I can — same runway as the cup-to-#3. A July jump is possible in shade. It is not my first choice. The plant will spend a month making roots in a volume that is also a radiator.

I do not wash the old root ball. I crack the bottom if it is circled. I do not shave a healthy ball because a bonsai video said so. Figs in nursery pots will circle. A little tease is enough. A massacre is a cutting with leaves.

Weight: I have to move these. A 5-gallon of wet mix is a reason I built wagons and why hand-watering got old. Sprinklers and a brass siphon are for the block that earned them. A single new 5-gallon does not get a special hose personality. It gets the same pick-up test.

8a winter in a 5-gallon is still a cliff compared to the ground. The pot can freeze through. The shop or a buried pot or a huddle against the wall is the winter story — not this essay, but it sits on the same fact. Bigger pots freeze slower than cups and faster than the county. Do not call a 5-gallon “in-ground.”

Zone 7 jumps earlier into bigger pots they can garage. Zone 9 jumps into the dirt. We jump when the plant has used the pot it has. Promotion is a human word. The fig does not get a plaque.

How I know it is time: roots at the holes, top actually growing, not a leafy liar. I can lift the #3 and it is a root ball, not a cup of mix. Then I jump one size. I do not jump because the bench looked sparse or because a listing said 7-gallon. We have hundreds of pots. Most should stay #3s. The twenty-five to thirty that earned a hole can leave the plastic. A 5-gallon I cannot drag is a trap I built myself. Common figs I have plated get the hole. A maybe stays in a #3 I can still wagon.

The mix in the bigger pot is the same rule as the #3. Perlite and castings, open, water should not stand. I crack circled roots at the bottom. I do not wash the ball. I do not shave it. I fill around it, water once, shade. Paint pen two sides on the new pot. The old #3’s writing is not a reason to skip. Sun and wagons eat ink. No fertigation the first week. The volume is a radiator in July and a lake in a wet April. I learn the wet weight of our mix in a 5-gallon so I am not guessing. Overwatering a new jump is how you rot a small ball in a lake.

I jump in spring if I can — same runway as the cup-to-#3. Pot-up before June is the cup rule. The #3-to-#5 rule is the same skillet: get the volume under the plant before 95°F, or shade like you mean it. August is hold. I do not jump in August because the wagon was crowded. Crowded is a winter shop problem. A clay mound is a later decision, and it is a seat, not a grave. Established is two summers in that hole, not a Saturday promotion to a pot I cannot lift. Long trials. I do not quote a jump-survival percent. The plant that still wilts like a #3 after a month in a 5 is a mix problem or a shy root ball, not a story about the name.

If the #3 is healthy and fruiting, I may leave it another year. Fruit in a pot we can move is a kind of wealth. A 5-gallon I cannot drag is a kind of trap. Match the pot to the back you have, not the collection you imagine.
