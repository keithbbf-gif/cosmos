# The shop-safe fence

Finishing is already a hazardous hobby when you do it the ordinary
way. You do not need to make it a second hobby called "amateur
chemical engineering."

This draft is the fence for the whole series. Later pieces will
point back here instead of repeating a sermon.

## What we will teach

**Commercial products, used as products.** If a can is sold for
furniture, we will talk about how that class behaves: open time,
recoat, cure, what ruins it, what it ruins.

**Shop air.** Solvent finishes want moving air that does not blow
dust onto a wet film. Waterborne finishes still want air — the
carrier is still leaving. A respirator that is actually rated for
the vapors you are using is not a personality. It is a tool. Read
the cartridge like you read grit.

**Rags.** Drying-oil rags can heat themselves until they catch.
That is not folklore. It is the same oxidation you wanted in the
film, happening in a crumpled wad that cannot shed heat. Hang
them to dry, separately, outdoors or in a can the fire people
will recognize as the right kind of can. Water in a sealed metal
can is the other ordinary method shops actually use. Do not leave
a pile of linseed rags in a plastic bin by the water heater and
call it a system.

**Old paint.** A flea-market dresser can carry lead. Dry-sanding
a mystery film into household dust is how a furniture project
becomes a house problem. Test first. If the test is positive,
the job changes: containment, the right stripper class sold for
that work, or a refinisher who already has the setup. This series
will not walk a "just sand it off, it's probably fine" route.

**Waste.** Leftover solvent, sludgy cans, and stripper go to the
household hazardous waste channel your town already runs. That
is the legal shop practice. Pouring it on the gravel "because it
evaporates" is not a finish technique.

## What we will not teach

We will not teach you to **distill, concentrate, or synthesize**
solvents, alcohols, or resins. If a can is thick, buy the reducer
named for that system.

We will not teach you to **mix metallic driers, isocyanates, or
formaldehyde catalysts** from raw materials. Catalyzed finishes
exist as commercial two-component products for shops with the
booth and the training. They are not a kitchen-table upgrade.

We will not teach **explosives, fuels, or anything that is not
furniture.** Drying oil plus a rag is already enough fire chemistry
for one life.

We will not teach you to **defeat a label, a waste rule, or a
building rule.** If your apartment lease and a gallon of oil
varnish cannot be in the same story, the finish choice changes.
That is a real constraint, not a lack of commitment.

We will mention **methylene chloride strippers** only to say the
class is a health problem and is restricted for a reason. We will
not give a how-to. There are slower strippers and there is heat
and there is paying someone. Those are the doors.

We will talk about **"food safe"** as a claim that needs a cured
film and an actual use (cutting, serving, licking a spoon). We
will not bless an oily salad-bowl job that is still tacky.

## Conversion varnish, spray lacquer, the serious cans

They exist. They make beautiful production furniture. They also
assume spray equipment, capture, and a person who is not
improvising a booth with a box fan in a window pointed at the
neighbor's garden.

If a draft names them, it is to tell you why a dining table in a
house is usually oil, wiping varnish, waterborne, or a consumer
poly — not to walk you into a catalyzed system because a forum
said it was "what the factories use."

Factories have rooms you do not have. That is not a moral
judgment. It is a map.

## The tone from here

Later drafts get specific. Specific is how you stay safe: you
know which family you are in, so you know which rag rule, which
cleanup, which recoat clock. Vague is how people mix a waterborne
over a wax and then invent a more dangerous fix.

If a paragraph ever starts to sound like a recipe for a chemical
you cannot buy as a finish, it does not belong in this folder.
The fence is the feature.
