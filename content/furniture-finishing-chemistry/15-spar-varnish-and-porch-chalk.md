# Spar varnish and why porch chairs go chalky

Spar varnish is a long-oil varnish with **UV absorbers**
and a flexibility brief. It was named for spars — masts,
booms — wood that bends and lives in weather. A porch
chair is not a mast. It is still closer to a mast than a
jewelry box is.

People put interior PU on a porch because it is what they
own. The film is hard. The wood moves. The sun eats the
binder from the top. The surface goes chalky, then
crackle, then peel. They buy a thicker can of the same
interior film.

## What "spar" is supposed to mean

More oil length: the film can stretch a little with the
season. UV package: the sun does not get a free meal on
day one. Often a bit more build, because weather wants
thickness more than a dining table does.

What it does not mean: maintenance-free. UV packages get
used up. They are a sacrificial army, not a force field.
A south-facing rail is a consumable.

## Chalk is the binder dying in public

Ultraviolet light breaks polymer chains. Pigment and
some UV additives slow that. Clear films have less to
hide behind. The surface becomes a dust of broken binder
— chalk — before the film lets go in sheets.

Horizontal surfaces fail first. End grain fails first
among the places water also lives. The underside of a
chair looks fine while the seat looks ninety.

If you see chalk, you are not looking for a cleaner. You
are looking at a refinish clock. Cut back to sound film
or to wood, then start a spar schedule you are willing
to repeat in a few years.

## Interior spar on a table is a different mistake

Spar stays a little softer. It yellows. It can print. On
an interior table you have bought porch chemistry for a
dining room. Sometimes a person wants that amber and
that build. Say it. Do not call it "the good varnish"
as if oil length were virtue in every room.

## Application that matches weather, not a weekend

Spar wants dry wood, a forecast that is not a storm, and
coats you do not trap under a tarp while they are still
soft. Dew on a morning coat is a blush and a bond break.
People finish at dusk because they work in the day and
then they varnish into the night's moisture.

Flip the schedule: finish when the piece will have hours
of open, mild air. Not when your calendar has a hole.

## Maintenance is the finish

A yearly scuff and a thin refresh on sound spar is how
boats lived. A five-year neglect and a heat gun is also
a tradition. Pick one with the client. The chemistry
does not care. The sun does not negotiate.

## Shop-safe

Same solvent family as other oil varnishes for most
consumer spars. Same rag rules if there is oil in the
binder — there is. Outdoor work is not an excuse to dump
thinner in the yard. The gravel is not a filter.

If a product is a two-pack marine epoxy or a
professional yacht system, that is not this draft. That
is a materials job with its own SDS and its own room.
Porch chairs do not require it. They require a long-oil
film, UV honesty, and a calendar.
