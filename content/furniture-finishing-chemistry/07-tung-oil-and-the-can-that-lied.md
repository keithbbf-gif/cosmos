# Tung oil, and the can that lied

Real tung oil is a drying oil from the nut of *Vernicia fordii*
(and a couple of relatives). It is thicker than linseed in the
hand. It polymerizes with a kind of water resistance linseed
does not match, because the fatty acids are more conjugated —
they are already set up to link. The film, when you actually
make a thin one and let it, is a little harder, a little less
yellow, a little more "I can take a damp cloth."

That paragraph describes **100% tung**, or a tung that has only
been thinned with a named solvent so you can wipe it. It does
not describe the aisle.

## The aisle

A can that says "Tung Oil Finish" is often a wiping varnish:
alkyd or polyurethane, mineral spirits, a rumor of tung, a
label that learned what people search for. It can be a good
finish. It is not tung. Calling it tung is like calling a
Danish oil "the tree from Denmark."

Read the cleanup. If it says mineral spirits and the dry time
is "recoat in 4 hours, use in 24," you are holding a varnish
blend. Real tung, wiped thin, is not in a hurry that way. It
can take a day to be sanely recoatable and several days to
stop smelling like itself.

Read the ingredients if they exist. "Oil-modified
polyurethane," "varnish," "resin." Those words are the binder.
Tung is then a flavor, or a lie, or a small fraction.

## What real tung feels like to use

It does not flash off. It sits. You wipe it on, you wait longer
than your patience, you wipe it off harder than you think.
Leave a wet edge and you get a glossy snail trail that never
looks like the rest of the panel.

Second coat goes on after the first is no longer tacky and
after a light scuff if the surface feels glassy. People pile
coats daily because a YouTube voice said seven coats is
traditional. Seven coats of puddles is a soft amber lens. Two
or three honest wipes, a week of air, and a willingness to
add more next month is closer to how the oil wants to live.

## Why people wanted tung in the first place

Water. Not "leave a vase ring for a weekend," but the damp-cloth
life of a table that is not a museum. Tung's conjugated
structure makes a tighter network than linseed when both are
allowed to cure. That is the chemistry people are reaching for
when they say tung is "better."

It is better *at that*. It is worse at being cheap, at being
fast, and at being the same as the can you already own. It
still yellows some. It still needs oxygen. It still makes a
rag that can heat itself. Same family of fire. Same hanging
rule.

## Polymerized tung

Commercial polymerized tung is partly linked already, like
stand oil's cousin. It builds a bit more sheen, wets a bit
differently, and still is not varnish. If you buy it, buy it
as a known product with a name, not as a jug from a story.

We are not walking a home-heating procedure. Factory heat-
bodying is a factory job. The shop job is thin coats and air.

## How to not get lied to

If you want tung, the words you are looking for are **100%
tung oil** or a brand that will say, in a tech sheet, that
the solids are tung and the thinner is X. If they will not
say that, enjoy the wiping varnish — many are excellent —
and stop asking it to be a nut.

If a client says they want tung because their grandmother's
table had it, ask what they liked: the look, the feel, the
repairability. You can often give them that with a wiping
varnish or a hardwax and an honest name. The lie is not the
finish. The lie is the noun on the lid.
