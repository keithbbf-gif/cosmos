# A curing schedule that respects oxygen

Oil schedules fail in one of two ways. They are too proud
("seven coats in seven days") or too mystical ("it will tell
you when it's ready"). Oxygen is not proud and not mystical.
It is a rate.

## The clocks you actually have

**Flash / soak.** Minutes. The carrier, if any, is leaving,
and the oil is finding fiber. This is when you wipe off.
Miss this clock and you have started a film you did not
mean.

**Tack-free.** Hours to a day, thin coat, warm shop, BLO or
a wiping varnish. Raw tung and raw linseed can sit longer.
Tack-free means a clean knuckle does not come away with a
story. It does not mean cured.

**Recoatable.** After tack-free, and after a scuff if the
surface has sealed itself against the next wipe. If the new
coat beads or rolls, you are too late without scuffing, or
the wood is oily, or you have contamination. If the new coat
lifts a gum, you are too early.

**Serviceable.** Days. A glass can stand. A careful client
can dine. The film will still smell a little. The smell is
chemistry still working, not "the wood being wood."

**Cured.** A week to a month depending on oil, thickness,
temperature, and whether anyone piled coats. This is when I
will talk about food contact, or a hard use table, or
shipping a piece under a blanket.

Cans collapse these clocks into two numbers because cans
are small. Your job is to uncollapse them.

## Temperature is not a personality setting

A garage at 50°F is not a shop. Oxidation slows. Viscosity
rises. People leave more oil because it "isn't soaking."
They have just made the oxygen problem worse: thicker film,
colder air, longer wet.

Move the work, or wait for a week that is actually a
finishing week, or switch families to something that can
coalesce or evaporate in the conditions you have. Do not
punish linseed for a climate you chose.

## Air is a material

A coat in a closed cabinet dries on the front and stays wet
in the back. A piece stuffed with rags "to keep dust off"
is a piece you have denied oxygen. Dust is a problem. A
soft film under a sheet is a worse problem.

I finish oil work where I can walk past it. I do not seal
the room like a vault. I do not point a fan so hard that
the wet edge tracks. Moving air, not a wind tunnel.

## A schedule I will actually run

Day 0: final sand, vacuum, tack. First wipe. Ten to twenty
minutes. Wipe off like you mean it. Hang rags.

Day 1: if tack-free, second wipe, same discipline. If not,
do not add oil to oil.

Day 3: third wipe only if the second is honest. Then **stop
building.**

Days 4–10: air. No tablecloth. No vase. Light use if the
client lives in the house and you have said the words
"gentle."

Day 14+: a judgment call. If a fingernail at an unseen edge
does not print, the piece can go to work. If it prints, you
do not need another coat. You need more time.

Wiping varnish can compress this. Raw oils stretch it. The
shape of the schedule stays: thin, wipe, wait, stop.

## What "more coats" is buying

More coats of wiped oil are not more protection in a
straight line. The first coat does most of the occupying.
The second evens sheen. The third is often vanity. The
fourth is how you leave a layer that oxygen cannot finish
in the middle.

If you want more protection, change families. Do not try to
turn linseed into conversion varnish by arithmetic.
