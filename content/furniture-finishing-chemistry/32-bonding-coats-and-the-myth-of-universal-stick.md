# Bonding coats and the myth of universal stick

Casein milk paint wants something to hold.
Raw wood, a scuffed porous paint, a mineral
surface — it can bite. A factory lacquer, a
poly table, a laminate, a slick oil — it
can sit like pastry flour on glass and then
leave with a fingernail.

The industry invented **bonding additives**
and bonding primers so the old paint could
live on new furniture. They work when used
as products. They are not a spell.

## What a bonding additive is doing

Typically an acrylic or similar emulsion
that stays in the casein mix and gives the
dried film more grab on non-porous stuff.
You have just made a hybrid: protein plus
plastic. The look can stay matte. The
chip behavior changes. If you wanted chip,
you may have just bought it off.

Read the manufacturer's ratio. More is not
more stick forever; it is a different
paint. We are not formulating a third
thing from leftover primer and powder.

## Bonding primer instead

A commercial bonding primer on the slick
thing, then milk paint as itself, is often
cleaner than doping every cup of paint.
You have separated the jobs: stick, then
color. The primer's rules (scuff, clean,
dry) still apply. Milk paint does not
forgive a greasy primer any better than
anyone else.

## The myth

"Milk paint sticks to anything because
it's what they used in 1820." They used
it on bare wood, plaster, and hope. They
did not use it on a polyester boat finish
in a beach house and then put wet
swimsuits on it.

1820 also did not have a dishwasher
steaming next to a milk-painted island.
The chemistry did not get tougher because
the kitchen got prettier.

## When I refuse the job

A laminate with a peel already started.
A flexible plastic. A floor. A client who
wants milk paint on a dining table field
and also mustard-proof without a topcoat
that will change the look they pointed at.

I will do a chest, a frame, a cabinet
door with the right bonding story and a
topcoat conversation. I will not do a
myth.

## Laminate is a special liar

A clean, scuffed laminate with a bonding
primer can take milk paint for a cabinet
that is looked at. A laminate that is
already delaminating at the edge will
take your beautiful casein for a ride
when the substrate lets go. Paint cannot
re-glue a paper film to particle board.

If the edge tape is proud or peeling, fix
the furniture first. Milk paint will
report every ridge and then chip there
first, bonding additive or not.

## Metal, glass, and the hardware you
forgot

Casein on clean, scuffed wood is one
job. The same mix on a hinge, a glass
insert, or a factory-coated pull is
another. Tape what is not wood, or
accept that you are now in craft-paint
territory with a topcoat that can flex
on metal. I tape. I do not trust a
bonding story on a working hinge.

## Test the slick

One coat on the actual substrate, not on
a pine board. Dry. Then tape. Then a
fingernail. If it lets go, you have not
failed the tradition. You have found the
border of the protein. Add the bonding
product, or change the finish family, or
strip to something the casein can stand
on.

Do the tape test after the *whole* stack
you plan to sell, not after the pretty
first coat. A bonding additive can pass
on Tuesday and fail after a shrinking
topcoat on Friday.

Universal stick is a sales sentence. The
tape test is a shop sentence.
