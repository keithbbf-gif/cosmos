# Bloom, wrinkle, and the rag that never dries

Oil defects are almost never mysterious. They are thickness,
air, contamination, or time, wearing different hats.

## Wrinkle

A skin forms on a coat that is still liquid underneath. The
skin grows as it takes oxygen. The liquid does not. The skin
has more area than the footprint it started with, so it
ridges. You see this on the rim of a BLO can and on a table
where someone "let it soak overnight."

The fix is not a fifth coat. The fix is remove the wrinkled
skin — scrape, sand, mineral spirits on a rag for the gummy
part if the system allows — back to a surface that is either
wood or a sound, thin, cured film. Then wipe thin.

Prevention is the whole of draft 10. Wipe off.

## Bloom / blush / the dull fog

On oil, a bloom is often one of three things.

**Wax bloom** — if a hardwax or a paste wax went on too
soon or too heavy, a hazy veil appears, worse in cold
rooms. A gentle wipe with the system's cleaner, or a
fresh very thin oil wipe after the wax comes off, usually
tells the truth. If the haze is in a thick oil film, you
may be looking at incomplete cure, not wax.

**Moisture bloom** — oiling in a wet shop, or a cold piece
brought into warm air so water condenses into the film.
The film looks sleepy. Time and dry air sometimes clear a
light one. A real one needs to be cut back.

**Amine / house-cleaner fog** — someone cleaned the "cured"
oil table with a strong kitchen cleaner. The surface
duller is chemical, not romantic. Neutral cleaner, then
decide if you are refinishing a spot or a top.

Shellac blush is a different animal (alcohol and water).
Do not treat them as interchangeable just because both
words sound Victorian.

## The rag that never dries

A rag soaked with oil and wadded up is a reactor. It can
go from warm to smoke without you. That is defect-as-
disaster, and it belongs in the safety draft as much as
here. The shop version of "the rag that never dries" is
also the rag you left on the bench that stays oily for
days because the coat you applied was the same: too much,
not enough air.

If your wiping cloth is still wet tomorrow, you did not
wipe the work. You relocated the puddle.

## Fish-eye and crawl on oil

Oil is supposed to wet. When it crawls, something on the
wood has a lower surface energy than the oil wants:
silicone polish, wax from a previous life, the tree's own
oil on teak, a hand lotion print. Sanding through a
silicone contamination is a folklore that sometimes works
and sometimes just drives silicone deeper.

Wash, wait, sand a fresh surface, seal if the species is
oily, and test a corner. If it still crawls, change the
first product — dewaxed shellac as a diplomat, or a
different family — instead of adding a "flow additive"
you mixed in the kitchen. We are not formulating.

## Tack that lasts a month

Usually thickness plus cold plus a can of raw oil someone
thought was BLO. Sometimes a finish that was never going
to cure because it was not a drying oil — salad oil,
"food grade mineral oil" on a table that was supposed to
become furniture. Mineral oil does not polymerize. It
stays oil. That is correct for a butcher block you will
re-oil forever. It is a defect if you thought you bought
linseed.

Smell it. Mineral oil is quiet. Linseed is not. The
label, if anyone kept it, is even better.

## A defect log that is shorter than a forum thread

Write down: species, grit, product, room temp, time to
wipe, time to next coat, what you see. The fourth wrinkled
job in a row is almost always the same row of the log.
Forums will offer you ten new cans. The log will offer you
a thinner rag.
