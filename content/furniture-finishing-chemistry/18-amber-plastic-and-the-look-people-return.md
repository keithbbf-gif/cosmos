# Amber, plastic, and the look people return

People do not return a table because the iodine value was
wrong. They return it because it looks like a gym floor,
or like a smoker lived in the grain, or like nobody
touched it with a hand after the last coat.

Those are appearance complaints with chemistry underneath.

## Amber

Oil varnishes and oil PU start a little yellow and go
more yellow in the dark and with time. The wood
underneath is also changing — cherry, maple, walnut each
have a plot. The combination is a color you cannot
un-pour.

If the sample board was a day old and the delivery is
three weeks later in a box, the client is seeing the
second chapter and calling it a bait-and-switch. Show
them a two-week sample, or say the word yellow out loud
when they pick oil PU on maple.

Waterborne and some pale hardwaxes are how you dodge
this. Shellac has its own amber (orange) or its own pale
(super blonde) and still warms. There is no clear that
is optically nothing and also an oil varnish.

## Plastic

Plastic is thickness plus high sheen plus a film that
does not follow pore. A heavy gloss PU on red oak can
look like a photograph of wood under glass. Some people
want that. The 1990s wanted that. A lot of 2020s clients
do not, and they do not have the vocabulary, so they say
"cheap" or "not like the picture of the raw slab."

Thin the build. Rub out. Open the pore on purpose (oil,
hardwax, a wiped varnish stopped early). Or keep the
film and dull the sheen so the hand does not skate. Sheen
is half of "plastic." The other half is the hard, slightly
 viscose feel of a thick urethane. You cannot massage
that into oil by changing bulbs.

## The hand

A finish that looks right in a photograph and feels like
a credit card is a returned finish. Let people touch the
sample. If they flinch, do not talk them into a harder
film because you are afraid of mustard. Talk them into
the mustard conversation and a finish that matches the
flinch.

## Dust nibs and the "not professional" verdict

A perfect film with three nibs in the main field will
lose to a slightly thinner film that was rubbed. Rubbing
out — even a modest pad and a consistent motion — is how
varnish stops looking like a first try. It is not only
for pianos.

Do it after the film can take it. Rubbing a green coat
is how you make a matte bruise that will not polish back.

## Orange peel, brush mark, sag

Those are application, and they read as amateur even
when the binder is expensive. Leveling takes a coat that
is wet enough and a shop that is not a desert. Waterborne
and oil fail this in different tempos. The look people
return is often "I can see how you did it." The fix is
slower coats, not a more expensive can.

## Show the failure mode when you show the beauty

I keep two boards of the same species: the look they
pointed at, and the same look after a week of a wet
glass and a backpack. If they still want the beauty, we
proceed. If they want the beauty and the un-failed board,
we change families or we change use.

A returned table is usually a sample that only told the
first sentence.
