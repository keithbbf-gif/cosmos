# What shellac cannot survive

A finish is also a list of enemies. Shellac's list
is short and non-negotiable.

## Alcohol

Obvious, and still how furniture dies at parties.
Wine is worse than water. Spirits are worse than
wine. A spilled old-fashioned is a chemistry
lesson. If the piece will live with drinkers, shellac
is a sealer or a French-polish lid on a box, not
the field of the table.

## Heat

A hot mug. A hair dryer used as a "quick dry." A
radiator. Shellac is thermoplastic. It will print,
wrinkle, or pick up a texture you did not choose.
The repair is possible — you can sometimes
re-amalgamate or rub — and the client will do it
again next winter.

## Alkali

A lot of household cleaners are alkaline. They
dull and etch shellac. "I only used a little
kitchen spray" is a refinish. Tell people: damp
cloth, mild soap if any, dry after. The maintenance
draft will say this again because nobody reads
the shellac chapter when they are holding a spray
bottle.

## Weather

UV and rain eat it. Exterior shellac is a
historical curiosity and a bad idea. Even a
covered porch is a long insult.

## Water, given time

A ring from a cold glass is the classic. It is
not instant death every time — a quick wipe
sometimes leaves only a blush you can rub. A
sweating glass left while someone finds the
coaster is the white oval in every antique
mall. You can repair it. You cannot make shellac
into polyurethane by wishing.

## Being the only film on a kitchen

Cutting boards, counters, around a sink: no.
People will ask because shellac is "natural" and
"food safe" in a rumor. Cured shellac has a
history on wooden toys and some food-adjacent
objects. A kitchen counter is not a toy. See
the food-contact draft. Do not let a label
argument win against a sink.

## Perfume, medicine, and the dressing table

Shellac on a jewelry box lid will meet perfume,
hand sanitizer, and the alcohol in a dozen
pretty bottles. That is the same enemy as the
old-fashioned, in a smaller glass. A French
polish that will be a dressing table wants a
tray, or a discreet modern film in the field,
or a client who likes repair.

Iodine, some essential-oil crafts, and a
leaking bottle of toner will write their names
in the resin. This is not a defect in the
batch. It is the thermoplastic doing what you
hired it to do with alcohol — dissolve.

## Heat from things that are not mugs

A laptop. A charging brick. A candle that was
"just decorative." Shellac's softening point
is not a mystery number you need a lab for;
it is "hotter than a hand, cooler than a
skillet." If the piece will wear electronics,
ask now. A thin waterborne over dewaxed
shellac sealer is a different contract than
shellac as the last word.

## A stack it was not invited into

Waxy shellac under waterborne. Shellac over wax.
Shellac over a fresh oil puddle. Shellac as a
"universal fixer" on a peeling conversion
varnish. The diplomat has a passport, not a
superpower.

If a factory film is alligatoring, shellac
will not sew it back together. You would be
putting a reversible resin on a failed skin
and calling it conservation. Sometimes a
conservator does exactly that, locally, on
purpose, with a reversible goal. A dining
table is not that job.

## What it survives beautifully

Dry living rooms. Hands that are not wet. Repair
cycles. Time, indoors, if the enemies above stay
rare. Color that warms without a plastic sheet.
A sealer job that nobody ever sees and everybody
benefits from.

If you choose shellac as the *finish*, choose the
room it will live in at the same time. The resin
is old. The enemies are not new. The only modern
trick is honesty before the first cut goes on.
