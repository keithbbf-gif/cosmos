# Dye, stain, and the finish that has to live on top

Color is not a finish. Color is a layer
the finish has to tolerate.

## Dye

Dye is solution color. It soaks, it does
not sit as a mud of pigment in the pore
the same way a wiping stain does. Water
dye raises grain. Alcohol dye is fast
and can lap. Both can be clearer in the
grain than a thick pigment stain.

The finish on top must not lift them.
Waterborne on a water dye without a lock
is how you make a streaky ghost. A
dewaxed shellac wash is the usual lock.
Alcohol on an alcohol dye will move it —
that is the same solvent. Work fast or
lock with something that is not more
alcohol.

## Pigment stain

A wiping stain is pigment plus binder
plus carrier. The pigment hangs in pores
and scratches. That is why bad sanding
looks worse after stain than after dye.
The binder in the stain is a first
finish. If it is oily, your next coat
is now "over oil." If it is watery, you
have grain raise and a binder that may
fight a later oil.

Read the stain like a can of finish:
what is the binder, what is the cleanup,
what is the recoat.

Gel stains are pigment in a thick
package so they sit on blotch-prone
woods. They are a film. They can look
like paint if you leave them heavy.
They can peel if you put a hostile
clear on a thick gel that never keyed.

## Combination

Dye for the heart of the color, then a
light pigment for evenness, then a lock,
then the clear — that is a common
professional stack. It is also three
chances to contaminate and lift. Samples
are cheaper than a table.

## Conditioners

A "wood conditioner" is usually a thin
binder that occupies blotch-prone pine
or cherry so the stain cannot. It works
when it is compatible with the stain
that follows. It is not a moral primer
for every board. On some woods it just
makes the stain weaker and gray.

## The finish changes the color

Oil warms. Shellac oranges or warms.
Waterborne can leave dye looking colder
than the wet sample. A satin flattener
can make a color look dusty. Always
sample **through the clear**, not to the
wet stain.

Clients approve a wet board. Then they
hate a dry, sealed board. Show the dry,
sealed board.

## Bleach and the fence

Wood bleaches exist as commercial two-
part products for furniture. They are
alkali and oxidizer families depending
on the product. Use them as labeled, in
air, with the gloves the sheet names.
We are not mixing household bleach
cocktails or inventing a third bottle.
Household bleach is a poor wood bleach
and a good way to make a chlorine story
in a shop that also has ammonia
cleaners. Do not combine those worlds.

If a piece needs to go paler than a
dye can do, that is a refinish decision
with a commercial bleach or a different
wood, not a kitchen experiment.
