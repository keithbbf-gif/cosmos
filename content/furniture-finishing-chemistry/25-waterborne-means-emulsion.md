# Waterborne means emulsion, not "water paint"

A waterborne finish is not varnish with the mineral
spirits swapped for tap water. It is a crowd of
**polymer particles** — acrylic, polyurethane, or a
hybrid — hanging in water with help from surfactants
and a little real solvent called a coalescent. When
the water leaves, the particles must **knit**. The
knit is the film. If they do not knit, you have a
powder that looks like a coating until a fingernail
proves otherwise.

That is why a waterborne can feel dry to the touch
in twenty minutes and still be a liar about being
a finish.

## The sequence the film actually runs

Water evaporates. Particles pack. Coalescent
plasticizes them just enough to deform into each
other. The minimum film-formation temperature
(MFFT) has to be met. Then the coalescent itself
slowly leaves, and the film gets harder for days.

Touch-dry is step one. Recoatable is step two.
Hard is a later chapter. People tape a waterborne
table on day two and pull the sheen off in the
shape of a label. They call the brand cheap. The
clock was the brand.

## Why shops switched anyway

Less amber. Faster recoat. Cleanup that does not
smell like 1978. A landlord who will not survive
a week of alkyd. Grain that stays pale on maple
and white oak if you want that look.

The costs: grain raise, a learning curve on
leveling, cold-shop failure, and a feel that can
read as plastic if you build it like oil PU.

## It is still a chemical product

Water cleanup is not a children's craft. There
are coalescents and biocides in the ordinary
cans. Two-component shop waterbornes exist as
commercial products with an SDS; this series
stays with one-component consumer and small-shop
waterbornes. We will not walk a part-B
crosslinker mix.

You still want air. You still want a respirator
if the label and your lungs vote that way. You
still do not pour the leftover in the storm
drain because "it's water." The polymer and the
biocides are why the can has a waste sentence.

## Hybrid cans

"Oil-modified waterborne" and "waterborne alkyd"
are real categories. They try to steal leveling
or amber from the solvent world while keeping
the bucket washable. Treat them as waterborne
for grain raise and MFFT, and test them for
yellowing. Do not assume they melt into an old
oil film. They usually do not.

## The first habit

Stir. Do not shake, or shake and then wait for
the foam to become someone else's problem. Foam
is how a waterborne advertises that you were in
a hurry.

The second habit: a sample board in the same
weather as the job. Waterborne is weather's
child more than oil PU is. A January garage and
a June kitchen are different products that
happen to share a label.
