# Linseed oil polymerizes. It does not "dry."

Water dries. Alcohol dries. Linseed oil does something ruder. It
**takes oxygen from the room and turns into a different substance.**
The liquid you wiped on is a mix of triglycerides — fats from flax
seed — with enough unsaturated joints that oxygen can start a
chain. Peroxides form. The chains link. What was a wet rag-smell
becomes a thin, amber, not-quite-plastic solid in the fibers.

If you wait for it to "dry" the way a floor dries after a mop, you
will wait in a way that teaches you nothing. The clock is oxygen,
surface area, temperature, and whether the can already had **driers**
in it.

## Raw, boiled, polymerized — three different liquids

**Raw linseed** is the patient one. Days to tack-free in a thin
wipe. Weeks to a cure you would let a client set a glass on. It
yellows. It is honest. Almost nobody finishes furniture with it
unless they like the wait or they are following a historical
spec and mean it.

**Boiled linseed oil** in the hardware aisle is not oil you boiled
on the stove. It is linseed with metallic driers already in the
can — the commercial kind, already a consumer product. Those
driers hurry the same oxygen reaction. Hours instead of days, if
the coat is thin and the rag came off. The name is a fossil. Do
not recreate it. Do not add your own drier chemistry. Buy the
can or do not.

**Polymerized / stand / "heat-bodied"** linseed (the commercial
art and finishing versions) has already been partly linked in
the factory. It is thicker, often glossier, sometimes less
yellow-prone depending on the grade. It still needs oxygen to
finish the job on the wood. It is not a varnish until someone
has cooked resin into it, and we are not cooking resin in this
series.

## Thin is the only thickness that cures

The reaction wants air. A thin wipe on sanded wood has a lot of
air per molecule. A puddle in a pore has a skin on top and a
wet argument underneath. The skin wrinkles. The middle stays
soft for a month and then you swear linseed is a scam.

Wipe on. Wait a few minutes. Wipe off like you are trying to
undo it. The oil that remains is the oil that can finish
becoming a solid. The oil you left "for build" is the oil that
will teach you about tack.

## Yellow, and the light that makes it worse

Linseed yellows because the same unsaturation that lets it cure
is happy to keep making chromophores. Dark corners yellow more,
which is a cruel joke: the table leaves a pale ghost when you
move the fruit bowl. Some of that is the oil. Some of that is
the wood (cherry, maple) doing its own light work.

If a client wants "the wood, no amber," linseed is the wrong
binder. Waterborne or a pale hardwax is the conversation. If
they want the old farm-table warmth, linseed is not a defect.
It is the color of the chemistry you chose.

## What linseed is good at

It makes cheap wood look paid-for, if you do not drown it. It
repairs by adding more, after a scuff. It smells like a shop
instead of a lab. It leaves a surface that feels like wood,
which is the whole reason people keep coming back after they
have been burned by a plastic film.

It is not a bar top. It is not a porch rail in the sun. It is
not "food safe" the afternoon you wipe it on. Cure first. Then
read the food-contact draft before you bless a salad bowl.

## The rag is the other half of the molecule

The heat of oxidation does not only happen in the film. It
happens in the wad. Linseed rags are how ordinary shops have
ordinary fires. Hang them. Separate them. Use the metal can
method your fire department would recognize. The shop-safe
fence is not a footnote here. It is the rest of the procedure.

If you remember one sentence from this draft, make it this:
**linseed does not dry, and the leftover rag is still working
after you have gone to bed.**
