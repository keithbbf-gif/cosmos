# Milk paint over an old film

The usual job is not a raw pine chest. It
is a brown dresser from a relative, already
finished, already waxed once in 1998, already
cleaned with something that said lemon.

Milk paint on that, without a conversation,
is how chip becomes a surprise.

## First, name the old film

Shellac melts to alcohol. Lacquer melts to
lacquer thinner — and we are not walking a
strip with serious solvents in a hallway;
if you need to identify, a discreet test in
a hidden spot with the commercial tester
or a tiny alcohol wipe tells you shellac.
A film that shrugs at alcohol and water
is often varnish or poly. A film that
powder-chalks may be old paint, and old
paint may be lead. **Test for lead before
you sand a mystery.** That is the fence.
Positive changes the job.

Wax beads water. If water beads hard after
you thought you cleaned, degrease with the
product meant for wax, not with hope.

## Then decide: chip, or stick

If the client wants chip, you may scuff
lightly, skip bonding, and let the protein
fail in the high spots. That can be
beautiful. It should be a written choice.

If they want a cabinet that still looks
like a decision in two years, you scuff
thoroughly, degrease, bond (additive or
primer), and topcoat. The old film must
be sound. Milk paint will not glue down a
peeling varnish. It will peel with it, in
a prettier color.

## Cleaning without making a worse chemistry

No oven cleaner as a personality. No
mystery stripper in a closet. A grease-
cutting cleaner that is sold for the job,
rinse, dry. Alkali leftovers can mark a
casein film later. Rinse is part of
cleaning.

## Grain and dents still show

Milk paint is thin compared to a
built-up enamel. The old film's orange
peel and the wood's dent will telegraph.
People think paint erases. This paint
reports.

Fill first if you wanted hide. Or admit
the dresser will still be the dresser,
in color.

## One coat is a stain-ish veil, two is
paint, three is a build that can craze

Casein likes thin. A heavy third coat on
a slick old film is a mudflat in July.
It cracks because the film is thick and
the substrate does not drink.

Wash, then coat, then coat. The old
timers were not being rustic. They were
avoiding a thick protein skin.

## Hardware ghosts

Pulls, shields, and the pale ovals where
a lamp sat will telegraph through thin
casein. Clean the ovals. If they are
oxidized metal stains, you may need a
commercial cleaner sold for that mark,
then a rinse, then the paint. Milk paint
will not hide a ghost you were too polite
to name.

## The sample that is the actual dresser

Do the inside of a leg, or the back of a
door, through the whole stack you plan
to sell — including topcoat. The color
of dry milk paint is not the color of
wet, and it is not the color under oil
or under waterborne. Show the end. The
relative's dresser has enough history
without a surprise ending. If the sample
chips on the dresser's actual varnish,
believe the dresser, not the pine board
in your shop.
