# Film versus penetrating

Most finish fights are costume fights. Oil versus poly. Water versus
"real" varnish. Milk paint versus "a proper enamel." Underneath them is
one fork: **does the protection mostly live in the wood, or on it?**

Call the first family **penetrating**. Call the second **film**. Then
admit that almost every product you can buy is a mutt.

## What penetrating actually means

Wood is a bundle of straws that died standing up. A penetrating finish
wets those straws, occupies the cut cells at the surface, and leaves
little or no measurable crust. The hand still feels wood. The pore still
looks like a pore.

Linseed and tung, used as oils and wiped dry, live here. So do a lot of
hardwax oils, if you do not pile them into a failed varnish. So does a
true wipe-and-off Danish oil *if* the varnish fraction is small and you
are not building a skin "just to be safe."

The chemistry that matters is wetting and cure *inside* a thin, oxygen-
reachable layer. If you leave a puddle, you did not make a better
penetrating finish. You made a slow, wrinkling film of oil that never
got enough air in the middle.

## What a film actually is

A film is a continuous skin. Thickness is measured in mils, not in
hope. Varnish, polyurethane, waterborne acrylic, shellac built in
coats, milk paint with a clear on top — these are films. They can be
thin films. Thin is not penetrating. A two-thou waterborne that sits
on sealed maple is still a film. It just does not have the mass to
hide your sanding.

Films win when you need abrasion, solvent wipe, or a sheen you can
aim at. They lose when the wood moves more than the film can stretch,
or when a repair has to blend into a skin that does not re-dissolve.

## The mutts

**Wiping varnish** is a film wearing work clothes. Thin coats, wiped
on, sometimes wiped off. People call it oil because the rag is the
same rag. The binder is still varnish.

**Hardwax oil** is a penetrating oil with a wax fraction that occupies
the surface and the first pores. Used as directed, it behaves like a
refreshable surface. Piled on, it becomes a soft, cloudy film that
fingerprints and then gets blamed on "the brand."

**Shellac** can be either. A one-pound cut wiped on and sanded back
is a sealer, almost a stain of resin. A French polish is a film so
thin and so burnished that people argue about the category while
the piano sits there being a piano.

**Milk paint** is a pigmented protein film. It is not a stain. It
does not "become the wood." It sits. If the substrate is slick, it
sits badly.

## How to choose without a personality quiz

Ask what the piece has to survive.

A walnut side table that will see a lamp and a book can live on oil
or hardwax and look like itself for years if someone will feed it.
A kitchen table that will see mustard, a wet glass, and a laptop
every night wants a film, or a hardwax with an honest maintenance
speech attached.

A chair arm is abrasion. A window sash is weather and UV. A jewelry
box lid is hand chemistry and perfume. The fork does not care about
your identity as an "oil person."

## The repair tell

Here is the practical test I trust more than brochures.

If you can scuff a spot, add more of the same, and the repair
disappears into use, you are in penetrating country. If a scratch
is a white line in a skin, and the fix is blend, rub out, or
strip back to a break, you are in film country.

People want the look of the first and the armor of the second.
That product exists in ads. In the shop it exists as a compromise
with a maintenance plan. Write the plan down before you sell the
look.
