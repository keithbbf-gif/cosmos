# Rags, air, and the fire you do not see coming

The most dangerous chemistry in a
finishing shop is not a secret formula.
It is the reaction you wanted in the
film, happening in a wad.

## Why oil rags heat

Drying oils take oxygen and give off
heat. On a table, the heat leaves. In a
crumpled rag, the heat stays, the
reaction speeds, and you can get smoke
and flame without a match. Linseed is
famous. Tung, wiping varnish, oil PU
rags, hardwax pads — if there is drying
oil, the family is invited.

This is ordinary shop fire. It has
burned ordinary buildings. Treat it as
part of the coat, not as an afterthought
when you are tired.

## What shops actually do

Hang rags individually, open, on a line
or a rack, away from the water heater
and the sun-through-glass amplifier,
until they are stiff and truly dry.
Then they are trash or a shop rag pile
that is no longer a reactor.

Or put them in a **listed oily-waste
can** — the kind with a lid made for
this — or in a metal can with water
covering the cloths and a lid, then to
the waste path your town allows.

Do not: a plastic grocery bag on the
steps. A pile in a corner "for
tomorrow." A dryer. A wood stove as a
disposal. Those are how the fire
arrives after you have gone to eat.

## Air for people

Solvent finishes want air for your
lungs and air for the film. Those two
airs can fight: a gale puts dust in
the coat; a sealed room puts solvent
in you.

Cross-ventilation that is not a wind
tunnel. A respirator with cartridges
that match the solvent family, changed
when they are used, not when they
smell picturesque. Waterborne is
kinder and is not nothing — coalescents
and biocides still leave.

Spray is a different air problem. If
you do not have capture, you are
sharing overspray with the house and
the neighbor. Brush is how small shops
stay kind.

## Air for the film

Oil wants oxygen. Do not wrap a wet
oil table in plastic to keep dust off.
A box fan on high across wet
waterborne will flash it into sand.
A closed cabinet around wet shellac
on a humid day is a blush box.

Air is a material. Aim it.

## Cans

Lids. A skin on an oil can is a
wrinkle lesson and a waste. A
waterborne can left open grows a
science fair. Solvent cans near a
pilot light are a sentence you already
know and still see broken.

Store finish the way the label said —
temperature, not a frozen garage that
will break an emulsion, not a hot
shed that will pressure a can.

## Kids, pets, and the low shelf

A pretty milk-paint powder is still
lime. A shellac alcohol is still
denatured. The shop-safe fence includes
the four-year-old who can reach the
"water" cup you rinse brushes in.

Work high. Close. Do not store food
and finish in the same refrigerator
as a fun hack.

## If something is already smoking

Get people out. Use the extinguisher
that is meant for the fire you have.
Call the people you call for fires.
This paragraph is not a hero manual.
It is the opposite of a rag pile.
