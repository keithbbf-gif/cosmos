I am going to talk about money in the least sexy way I can, because sexy money talk is how people get robbed.

List is a number on a piece of paper. Sometimes it is a number on a website. It is the published retail, the sticker, the thing a civilian is shown so that a discount can later appear and feel like a gift.

Net is the number a trade account pays the showroom, or the number a showroom pays a shop, depending on how the room is built. People use net the way they use handmade — sloppily. Ask: net to whom?

A common old pattern in decorative furnishings looks like this, and I am describing a pattern, not our invoice:

The shop has a wholesale number that lets the shop live.
The showroom sells to the designer at a net that lets the showroom live.
The designer sells to the client at a price that lets the designer live.

Three livings. One table. If that shocks you, you have been shopping in rooms that hide the livings inside a "sale."

The designer's living might be a multiplier on net. It might be net plus a fee. It might be hourly, with objects at net. The hourly version is often the cleanest, and it is the one a certain kind of client still resists because they can see the labor. The multiplier version hides the labor in the object, which makes the object look expensive and the advice look free. Free advice is how rooms get specified by accident.

The showroom's living pays for the floor, the salesperson, the memo program, the coffee, the market week, the sample that got ruined by a belt buckle, the hour they spent talking a client out of a finish that would have looked like mud. If you want that room to exist, somebody pays. If you do not want that room to exist, you will meet the labor later as a mistake.

The shop's living pays for the wood, the people, the building, the truck, the redo when a board moves, the year we trained a finisher. I will not apologize for that living. I will also not pretend it is the only living in the stack.

Where the system gets ugly:

When list is a fantasy number invented so that everyone can "take fifty off" and still make their living. Then the civilian who pays list is a mark, and the civilian who demands fifty off on a handmade commission is repeating a mall ritual in the wrong temple.

When a designer takes a multiplier and also does not do the labor — no drawings, no freight plan, no finish fight — and the shop ends up doing the designer's job on the phone. Then the multiplier is a toll.

When a showroom adds a number the shop never saw and the client thinks we got rich. We got the shop number. The rest went to rooms they did not meet.

I am not reciting this to make you angry at designers. The good ones earn their stack twice over. I am reciting it so you can ask a grown question: what am I paying for besides the object?

If you are a client, you are allowed to ask how your designer bills. You are allowed to prefer a fee. You are allowed to prefer a multiplier if you also want them hungry to find a better object. What you are not allowed, morally, is to want museum-level advice and warehouse-level pricing in the same breath. That breath is how platforms trained you.

If you are a designer, show the stack before the client reads a blog post about it. Secrets make simple work look like theft.

If you are a maker, publish a trade structure you can defend at a finishing bench. If your list is a theater, the theater will travel to the internet and you will not be able to call it back.

Our shop's exact numbers are not this episode. They are a living conversation and they change with the work. The education is the parsing.

Three livings, or two, or one, depending on the door.

Door one, gallery: maker living plus room living, often as a split.
Door two, wholesale: maker living plus store living, store takes risk.
Door three, trade: maker, showroom, designer.
Door four, dropship: maker living plus platform living plus ads plus returns. The platform living can be larger than it looks because they own the customer.

When someone says a handmade table is expensive, ask them which livings they are counting. Sometimes they are counting all four and comparing the total to a carton that counted none.
