People imagine a showroom door opening because the work is beautiful.

Sometimes. Beauty is the cover charge. The room is deciding whether you are a reliable adult.

Can you make the thing you photographed?
Can you make it again in a different length?
Can you tell the truth about a lead time on a Monday when the shop is already late?
Can you survive a designer who changes a finish after the lumber is pulled?
Can you ship without turning their client into a freight expert?
Will you still exist in three years?

A principal looks at that list faster than they look at your veneer. I know because I have been on both sides of the coffee. We have been taken on. We have been passed over. We have passed over rooms that wanted us as decoration.

The process, stripped of romance:

You have a body of work, not a hero piece. A hero piece is a gallery object. A line is a family of decisions that can be specified.

You have a finish story that can be sampled. If every piece is a one-time chemical adventure, a showroom cannot sell you. They can admire you.

You have a map of where you already sell, because rooms care about territory. We will do territory next. Do not walk into Dallas pretending Houston does not exist.

You have a number. How many pieces can this shop honestly add if the room gets busy? If the honest number is twelve a year, do not promise a national rollout. Promise a careful city.

You meet. You leave a sample if they ask, and you understand that sample is now a hostage of their floor and your reputation.

They say yes, or they say not now, or they say we already have your cousin.

The cousin problem is real. Showrooms edit. If they have a walnut dining table with a certain posture, they may not need yours, even if yours is better. Better is not a category they can always use. Different is. Different and reliable is.

Why most shops should not want every city:

Because a sample in a city is a promise to support that city. Support means visits, training, replacing a ruined sample, answering the phone during their market week, making the piece that sold because your table looked like hope at 4 p.m. If you cannot do that, the room will quietly stop walking people to your piece. Then you will say showrooms don't work. What does not work is a ghost line.

Because lead time is a shared resource. Every new door that succeeds steals weeks from the old doors. I would rather be late never than famous everywhere. We make several hundred pieces a year. That is a box. The box does not get larger because a website said we should scale. Scaling a handmade dining table without hiring and training is how you ship a lesser cousin and keep the name. I will not do that. A lot of shops did, under pressure from doors we have not reached yet.

Because some cities are vanity. A famous address on your site does not pay a finisher. A working room in a less famous city might.

Our early years were the opposite of a national fantasy. I cold-called. I got a yes in a city I could actually drive to. I did the work. The map grew because the work could travel, not because the map looked pretty.

If you are a young shop, I would rather you have two showrooms that know your first name than twelve that know your PDF. If you are a buyer, a long showroom list is not a quality mark. It is a distribution mark. Ask how many pieces they make. Ask who finishes. Ask whether the sample is typical or a show pony.

Getting in is also not staying in. Rooms reshuffle. Principals retire. A building changes its fashion. You can do everything right and still lose a floor. Have more than one door. Do not have so many doors that the shop becomes a shipping department with a memory of craft.

The educational point: representation is not a prize you win at the end of a pretty portfolio. It is a capacity contract. Sign it like you would sign a lease.
