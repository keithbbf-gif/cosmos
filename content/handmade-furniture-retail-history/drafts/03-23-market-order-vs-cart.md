Write this on the inside of your catalog: a cart is not an order in the old sense.

A market order, when door two was healthy, was a bet between adults.

A store said: we will take these pieces, in these finishes, for this season.
The shop said: we will make them, at this price, on these terms.
Money moved in a sequence — deposit, balance, sometimes dating that let a store sell before they paid in full.
If the pieces did not sell, the store owned the problem. That was the point of wholesale. They took title. They took the floor risk. They earned their markup by being wrong sometimes.

A cart says: a stranger paid a website.
The website says: shop, ship it.
If the stranger is wrong, the website has a policy. The policy is usually faster than a dining table can turn around. The shop becomes the shock absorber.

Those documents are not cousins. They are different species.

I have watched shops celebrate a spike of carts like they had a good market. Then the returns started. Then the chargebacks. Then the portal suspended them for a rating that was about a carrier. Then they said wholesale is dead. Wholesale was not dead. They had not been doing wholesale. They had been doing door four at door-two prices.

What a real order still contains, when it is grown:

A name you can call.
A quantity that is not one unless one is the point.
A finish that was sampled.
A date that was argued about.
A freight plan.
A sentence about what happens if the store cancels.

What a cart contains:

A SKU.
An address.
A payment token.
A timestamp.

You can add chat. Chat is not terms.

I am not against a single-piece order. We live on single-piece orders. Ours come with an acknowledgement and a person. The thinness I am attacking is the thinness of the platform cart when it is used to move an object that needs a thick document.

Markets also had a culture of the rewrite. You wrote an order on Tuesday. You changed it on Thursday when you saw a better finish two buildings over. That rewrite was a conversation. A cart has a cancel button. The cancel button does not walk the aisle with you and help you think. It just yanks a slot you may have already given to a board.

If you are a shop being offered a "wholesale portal," read the cancellation and return paragraphs as if they were the only paragraphs. If a civilian can return a custom size because they measured a wall wrong, you are not a wholesaler. You are a volunteer.

If you are a store still doing the old job: thank you. You are rarer. You are the reason some factories still know how to plan a season.

If you are a buyer: a checkout page that felt easy was easy because someone else is holding the thickness. Ask who.

High Point did not become obsolete when the cart arrived. It became a recruiting office for carts. That is a later draft. First I want you to respect the old paper. It was slow. It was also how a shop knew what to mill next month.
