A gallery hangs twelve things so you can see one.

A showroom puts a sample in a conversation with six neighbors.

A store floor, even a sloppy one, has a wall. The wall ends.

A platform catalog, at the scale we are talking about, does not end. The S-1 brag was the number. Millions of products. The brag is also the injury. Looking cannot happen in an infinite aisle. Hunting happens. Ranking happens. Ads happen. Fatigue happens.

I said galleries taught comparison. Comparison requires a finite set. If I put two tables in a room, you will learn something about yourself. If I put two thousand tables in a scroll, you will learn something about your thumb.

The SKU flood did three things to handmade work.

It hid us. A bench-made table is one row among cousins that were born in a binder. The photograph dialect is the same. The buyer does not have gallery school. They have a filter for price.

It trained us, if we entered the flood, to multiply. More sizes. More names. More slight variations that are really one idea wearing hats. A shop that multiplies to be visible will spend the year making hats.

It cheapened refusal. Refusal is how a shop stays a shop. I will not do that width. I will not do that finish. In an edited room, refusal looks like expertise. In a flood, refusal looks like a missing filter. The buyer goes next door, which is not a door, which is a click.

People say more choice is kind. Sometimes. If you live two hundred miles from a design center, a catalog is a kind of access America House would have recognized. Kindness without teaching is how you get a warehouse of regret. The return rates of this industry are not only about quality. They are about people hunting without a teacher and then living with the object in a light the photograph never mentioned.

I like an edited floor the way I like a finish library. Not because I want to control you. Because I want you to have a chance to know what you are doing. A designer is an editor. A gallerist is an editor. A good store buyer is an editor. A ranking is a popularity contest with a paid lane.

If you build catalogs, try a cruel exercise. Cap a category at the number of objects that would fit in a real room. Make yourself choose. The things you cut will tell you whether you are a merchant or a pipe.

If you shop catalogs, try a kinder exercise. Shortlist five. Walk away. Come back the next day. If you cannot remember which one you liked, you were hunting, not looking. Go find a room.

If you make, do not measure your worth by whether you can be found in the flood. Measure it by whether a person who has seen the work in air still wants it. That metric is slower. It pays finishers.

The educational point: completeness is a business model. It is not a looking model. Handmade furniture is a looking object. Those two models are in a fight, and the fight is not fair.
