A return policy is a feeling. A returned table is a project.

I want to separate those so we can talk like adults.

The feeling says: I can be brave because I can undo. That feeling sold a lot of sofas. It sold a lot of objects that should have required a sit and a tape measure. Bravery without looking is not bravery. It is a trial balloon paid for by someone else.

The project says: we must move a large object again, inspect it, maybe refinish it, maybe not be able to sell it as new, maybe not be able to sell it at all if it was sized for a room that does not exist twice. A shop that makes several hundred pieces a year does not have a hospital wing for the undone. A factory with duplicated SKUs might.

Platforms like uniformity. Uniformity says thirty days, free returns, no questions. Uniformity is kind in a paragraph. On a dining table it is a transfer of risk from the hunter to the bench. The hunter had a bad light. The bench has a ruined week.

Chargebacks are a meaner cousin. The buyer tells the card company the object was not as described, or not received, or not wanted, and a bank that has never seen a leaf mechanism decides whether we get paid. Sometimes the buyer is right. Sometimes the buyer measured a wall with hope. Sometimes the photograph was the website's and the disappointment is now our name in a portal. A chargeback on a custom size is a special insult. The object cannot go to the next hunter. It is a sentence about a house.

I have less anger at buyers than you might think. They were trained. The early web trained them. The platforms industrialized the training. Free shipping, free returns, the smile. Then they were given a freight appointment and a bill of lading and a ten-minute window to note damage while the driver waited. Of course they missed the note. Of course the claim died. Of course they used the card.

Anger is a bad teacher. Structure is a better one.

If you sell:

Say what is returnable before the money. Stock size, maybe. Custom, no, except for our error.
Say who pays freight both ways.
Photograph the outbound condition.
Require the damage note in language a tired person can hear.
Price the hospital wing if you are going to have one.

If you buy:

Measure twice. Then measure the stair.
Understand that "not as described" on wood often means "not as lit."
If there is damage, write it down before you are polite.
If you are using a platform because you want the undo, pay for the undo in the price and do not pretend the shop is greedy.

If you design:

Do not specify an object into a house that cannot undo, and then encourage the client to treat it as a parcel. You are the adult in that sentence.

Our own door tries to make undo rare by making the front thicker: sample, finish board, acknowledgement, truck. Thickness is not a guarantee. It is a respect for physics. I would rather lose a cart than gain a chargeback.

The platform years made undo into a brand promise. Handmade furniture cannot keep that promise at scale without becoming a different kind of company — a company with a warehouse of extras and a budget for landfill or liquidation. Some companies did that. They are not shops in the sense I mean shop.

This is the last ugly draft of the platform stage. I wanted the ugly on the table before the walk-away, because walking away sounds like pride if you do not show the hospital.
