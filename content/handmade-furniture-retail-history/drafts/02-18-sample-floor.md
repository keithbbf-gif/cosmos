There is a table in a city I am not standing in.

Somebody is putting a hand on it right now, or nobody is, and it is doing its job either way. It is a sample. We made it. We shipped it. We hope they have not parked a coffee on it. It may never go to a house. It exists so a different table can go to a house without being a rumor.

That is a strange economic object. A factory that sells from inventory hates it. A gallery sometimes is it — the thing on the floor is the thing you buy. A custom shop that sells through trade rooms lives on it.

Why?

Because custom without a sample is a leap. I can send photographs. I can send a finish board the size of a book. I cannot send the way a base meets a floor, or the way a length changes a room, or the particular quiet of a top under daylight that is not a studio light. The sample is the leap made physical.

It is also a hostage. If the showroom lights are cold, our wood will look colder than it is. If the salesperson prefers another line, our table will be the one in the corner. If a market week wrecks the finish, our name is the wreck. We do not get to stand there every day and narrate. We have to build a piece that can narrate for itself.

There is a temptation to build a show pony — the most expensive wood, the most flattering size, the finish that photographs like money. I understand the temptation. I try to resist the dishonest version. A sample that is not typical is a lie you tell with hours. The designer will specify the cousin. The cousin will arrive. The client will say it is not what they saw. Then everyone will blame the light.

So a good sample is typical plus a little hope. Typical enough to be fair. Hopeful enough to be worth the floor.

Cost: a full piece of furniture, freight, sometimes a second one when the first gets loved to death. That cost is why shops cannot be in every city. People who have never made a table think a sample is marketing. It is inventory we cannot sell without replacing the argument.

Some rooms buy the sample. That is the grown relationship. Some rooms expect you to consign it. Door one inside door three. Read that twice. You are paying to be represented. Sometimes that is correct, the way a booth fee is correct. Sometimes it is a room that wants a free museum.

I like delivering samples in our own trucks when we can. I want to see the floor. I want to see the neighbor lines. I want to know whether our table is in a conversation or in storage. A sample in storage is a donation.

If you are a designer, treat samples like borrowed tools. Do not let a client climb them. Do not let a contractor use them as a desk. When you specify from a sample, say out loud what will change: length, wood lot, the fact that trees do not repeat. A client who thinks they are buying that exact slab is a client you have not taught.

If you are a client, the sample is not a rejection of custom. It is how custom stays honest. You are not getting a lesser thing because it is not the floor piece. You are getting a thing that was allowed to be itself because you both saw a cousin.

If you are a maker, budget samples as a line, not as an afterthought. A shop that cannot afford samples cannot afford showrooms. Then do not call the showroom system broken. Call your capacity what it is.

The educational point is simple. In handmade furniture, belief is a physical object. Websites tried to replace it with photography. Sometimes photography is enough for a lamp. For a dining table, I still want the hand.
