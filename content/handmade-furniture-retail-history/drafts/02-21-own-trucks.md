A table is not finished when it leaves the finishing room.

It is finished when it is sitting in the house at the height we promised, with the leaves in the place we promised, and nobody's doorway has a new scar. Everything between those two sentences is a chance to undo the year.

We deliver in our own trucks and vans, coast to coast, more often than people expect from an Indiana shop. That is not a flex. It is a refusal. I have seen what happens when a serious piece is treated like a carton in a network that was built for toasters. Blankets help. They do not replace a person who knows which edge is the one you do not bump.

Common carriers are a tool. We have used tools. I am not going to pretend every object we ever made rode with our name on the door. I am going to say the standard we aim at is: the same company that argued about the finish argues about the stair.

Why this belongs in the showroom stage:

Because a designer is buying a result, not a romance. Their client does not care that the joint is honest if the delivery ripped a wall. The showroom's reputation dies in that hallway with ours. Owning the truck is how we keep the three livings from being destroyed by a fourth party who was paid to be in a hurry.

It is also how we learn. A driver sees the house. They see the floor that is not level. They see the client who is kind and the client who is scared. They come home with information a photograph never sent us. That information goes back into the next acknowledgement. A platform driver does not come home to Ferdinand. Their information goes into a rating.

Cost: trucks, insurance, nights, people who can carry and also behave in a living room. That cost is in the price. If you want a shop to pretend it is not, you want a fiction. The fiction is how furniture websites advertised free shipping on objects that are not free to move. Someone paid. It was either hidden in the object, or taken out of the driver's back, or taken out of the shop when the claim arrived.

I like a loading dock that is indoors. I like blanket-wrapped work. I like the last look before the door closes. These are small rites. They are how a shop stays ashamed of sloppiness. Shame is useful. A carton with a smile sticker does not feel shame.

If you are a buyer, ask who is coming to the house. Ask whether they have seen a piece like yours before. Ask what happens if it does not fit the stair. The answer "we'll figure it out" from a person who has never seen the stair is not a plan.

If you are a maker, do not treat freight as an afterthought you hand to the cheapest account. Price it. Schedule it. Train it. Or partner with a white-glove outfit you would let into your own living room. The platform years trained shops to throw a table at a label printer. You can see the result in the reviews. The reviews are not all about taste. A lot of them are about a corner.

If you are a designer, put delivery on the drawing. Access. Elevator. The window that will have to come out. I would rather have that fight on paper.

This is the last showroom-stage draft. Remember the badge, the building, the map, the stack of livings, the sample, the paper, the call, the truck. That is door three as a living system.
