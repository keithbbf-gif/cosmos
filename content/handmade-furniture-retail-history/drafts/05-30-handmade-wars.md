October 2013. Etsy told a million sellers, on a webcast, that the rules were changing.

  <figcaption>Etsy’s handmade policy fights were really fights over factory help, disclosure, and who gets to use the word artisan.</figcaption>

You could hire people who did not live in your house.
You could apply to have work made by a manufacturer if you were still the author of the design and you disclosed the partnership.
You could use a shipping service.

To a shop like ours, parts of that sound like Tuesday. Of course you can hire. A one-person dining-table company that never hires is a company that will hurt someone, usually the one person. I have a cadre. I have always needed other hands. Handmade, in a serious shop, was never "no other humans."

The fight was not really about employees. The fight was about whether handmade is labor or authorship.

Labor says: a person made this, in a way another person in the trade would recognize as making.

Authorship says: a person designed this, and the making can be elsewhere if the idea stayed home.

Both can be honest. They are not the same promise. Early Etsy sold the first promise. The 2013 policy, and the Wired headline that followed — that the company's future depended on leaving behind the simply handmade — sold the second. Chad Dickerson's job was to let successful sellers grow without handing the site to a corporation that had never been a seller. The fear in the community was that IKEA would arrive in a cardigan.

IKEA did not have to arrive. The word arrived without them.

Partial production had already existed in a hazier form. 2013 made the haze a door. A door is better than a haze if you are trying to run a company. A door is worse if you were using the haze as a moat.

I am not going to perform outrage I did not live as an Etsy seller. I am going to translate the fight into shop language.

If a designer in New York sends us a drawing and we make the table in Ferdinand, who is the maker? We are. They are the designer. The trade has words for that. We do not call the table handmade-by-the-designer. We call it a commission.

If a seller in Brooklyn sends a file to a factory and lists the result as handmade on a site that taught buyers to hear "apartment labor," that is a different sentence. The policy tried to make it a disclosed sentence. Disclosure is ethical. It does not restore the old ear. The buyer still hears handmade. The ear was trained in 2005.

Why did the company do it? Volume. A marketplace that takes a cut needs more stuff, more sellers who can fulfill, fewer one-person bottlenecks. The snake I mentioned. Also fairness, in their telling: some sellers were already bending the rules, working themselves to injury, while others hid help. A clean application is better than a secret.

Why were sellers angry? Because they had paid for a room that excluded the factory, and now the factory could apply for a key. The listing fee had been a booth fee for a juried idea. The jury moved.

Both descriptions can be true at once. That is why the war lasted.

For furniture, 2013 mattered in a sideways way. It made it easier for designed-and-made-elsewhere objects to stand next to bench-made objects under the same banner. The banner could no longer be trusted as a labor claim. It became a brand claim. We already lived in a world of brand claims. We did not need another one wearing a craft apron.

If you buy on a handmade marketplace now, ask the 2013 question out loud: who held the work? Not who held the idea. Who held the work. A good seller will answer without theater. A bad listing will answer with a story about passion.

If you sell, say which promise you are making. Labor or authorship. I will respect either if you do not swap them mid-paragraph.

If you run a shop that hires, do not use this history to sneer at one-person makers. They are not a lesser category. They are a different capacity. The war got ugly when people treated capacity as virtue.
