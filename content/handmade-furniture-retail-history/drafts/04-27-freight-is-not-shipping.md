A book ships. A table is moved.

If you take nothing else from the early-web stage, take that.

Parcel shipping is a miracle for objects that fit a box, survive a slide, and can be walked to a porch by one tired person. The entire emotional design of online buying — the tracking number, the smile, the easy return — was built on parcel. Furniture websites borrowed the emotion and then hired a different industry to do the work. That industry is freight: pallets, liftgates, appointments, two people, a dock or a driveway, a claim form when the corner dies.

Overstock's people have said in trade interviews that they were figuring out how to get a sofa to a house without the old two-men-and-a-truck assumption. I believe they worked at it. Working at it is not the same as making it feel like a book. The feeling was the product they sold to the buyer. The work was the product they handed to the shop and the carrier.

What civilians were trained to expect:

A price that includes "shipping."
A date that is a day, not a window.
A return that is a label.

What a table requires:

A price that admits fuel, men, blankets, stairs.
A window, because weather and docks.
A return that is another move, often more expensive than the first, sometimes impossible without destroying the object.

When those two lists collide, the review is written. The review is often fair to the feeling and unfair to the physics. The shop lives in the physics.

I own trucks because I got tired of arguing with physics through a third party's claim department. A claim department does not love a finish. They love a checkbox.

Early sites hid freight as a late line at checkout. The cart looked friendly. The number at the end looked like a slap. Later sites hid freight inside the object price and called it free. Free is a lullaby. Someone is still paying the driver.

There is a third hide: "curbside." The table is on the pavement. The rest is your Saturday and your back. That can be honest if it is said early. It is often said in a footnote.

Handmade work has a special freight problem. We do not have a stack of the same table. If yours is damaged, we do not pull another from a warehouse. We make. The lead time restarts. A platform policy written for a duplicated SKU will punish a one-off shop for being a one-off shop.

If you sell furniture online, say the word freight out loud on the page. Say appointment. Say what happens at a walk-up. Say who files the claim. You will lose some carts. You will keep your name.

If you buy, ask:

Is this parcel or freight?
Who is coming to the house?
What is the claim window, and do I have to note damage on the bill of lading while I am also trying to be polite?
If it is wrong, who pays to take it away?

If you make, do not let a website teach your customers that your table is a parcel. You will spend years unteaching them, and you will do it on the worst day of the job.

The early web gave us payments and pictures and a lie about movement. Etsy will inherit all three. The platforms will industrialize all three. We had to get this scar named first.
