I should tell you who is talking, because the internet has a habit of putting a face on a script and calling it a maker.

My name is on the door. Keith Fritz Fine Furniture. We started in 1999. The first shop was a storefront on Capitol Hill in Washington. I lived above it. I still live above the shop. The building is different. It is a nineteenth-century factory in Ferdinand, Indiana, about thirty-three thousand square feet, and the wood comes in the back and the finished work leaves the front on our own trucks. We make on the order of several hundred pieces a year. That number matters. It means we are not a factory that can eat a weekend of returns. It means we are not a one-man Instagram. It means every bad door we walk through costs a real bench.

I am the son of a carpenter. I grew up in Siberia, Indiana, which is a real place and not a joke I tell for cities. I made my first serious piece at sixteen, a Bombay Chippendale secretary that took something like twelve hundred hours. That is not a brand story. That is how long a complicated piece takes when you do not yet know what you are doing and you refuse to stop. I went to seminary and graduate school and then I opened a shop anyway, which tells you something about the quality of my career counseling.

I used to cold-call designers. That is not glamorous. You get a name, you get a phone, you get a no. Every so often you get a yes, and then you have to be as good as the yes. In 2001 we built a dining table for President Clinton. I mention that once in this series so we can put it down. It is a true job. It is not the thesis. The thesis is the pipe.

We sell through high-end designer showrooms. That sentence is doing a lot of work. It means an interior designer or an architect is usually the person who writes the order. It means a homeowner can love a piece and still not be able to buy it the way they buy a toaster. It means we keep samples on floors in other people's buildings, and those samples are not merchandise in the Target sense. They are arguments. They are the quiet I talked about, built in three dimensions and left in New York or Dallas or Atlanta so a stranger can put a hand on it when we are two states away.

I am not a commentator who learned furniture from a podcast. I am also not going to pretend this shop walked every door in this history. We did not become an Etsy jewelry brand. We did not become a seven-million-SKU catalog. We did not become a craft-fair company with a van and a tent, not as the main living. We did live the showroom system. We did live the custom-commission system. We did watch the wholesale websites recruit on market floors. We did hear the word handmade get thinner every year.

That is enough authority to teach the map. It is not enough authority to narrate another shop's private ledger. When I talk about Overstock's fulfillment partners or Wayfair's niche sites or Etsy's 2013 policy change, I am talking about public mechanics. When I talk about living above the loading dock, I am talking about us.

Here is what this shop is not, because the words will try to swallow us.

We are not a store. A store buys inventory, marks it up, and hopes you walk in on Saturday. We make to order. The piece that leaves here usually already has a name on it.

We are not a dropship supplier in the platform sense. A dropship supplier lets a website set the retail relationship and then asks the shop to be a quiet warehouse with a freight account. We keep the relationship. We keep the truck. We keep the argument with the finish.

We are not a gallery. A gallery edits a wall and takes a consignment cut. We have been grateful for rooms that treated furniture as an object worth looking at. That is a different contract than a trade showroom, and I will not mash them together for the sake of a pretty sentence.

I still like pen and paper. The company runs on computers. Both of those can be true. I still think design is a journey that happens in a room, not a scroll. That opinion will show up in this series. You can disagree with it after you understand the doors. You should not disagree with it before, because then you are just defending a website.

If you came here for a tour of pretty tables, there will be pretty tables. They will show up as evidence. The product is the history. I want a younger maker to stop sending a dining table into a pipe built for earrings. I want a buyer to stop calling a designer showroom elitist before they know what the showroom is protecting — and what it is charging for that protection. I want the channel to be a place where the words get their jobs back.

That is who is talking. A shop. A door. A person who has been paid to make the quiet, and who is tired of watching the quiet get replaced by a filter.
