Twice a year, a town in North Carolina becomes a city that only the trade can see.

High Point Market. It started as the Southern Furniture Market, first formal edition March 1 through 15, 1909. Two local exposition companies joined up because they wanted a southern answer to New York, Chicago, and Grand Rapids. Attendance was modest. They kept at it. The Southern Furniture Exposition Building opened in 1921. The names changed — International Home Furnishings Market in 1989, then High Point Market — and the calendar settled on April and October. The public does not get to treat it like a weekend mall. That is not a snub. It is a wholesale floor.

I want you to hold three facts.

It is old. Older than the D&D. Older than America House. Older than every website in this series. When a platform recruiter walks a High Point hall, they are walking through a century of door two.

It is large in a way a design center is not. Millions of square feet. Thousands of exhibitors. Tens of thousands of people. You can have a useful week and still not see the same building twice. That scale is the point and the danger. Scale is how a buyer finds a line. Scale is also how a line becomes a blur.

It is not our daily door. We live in the design-center system. I still need you to understand High Point, because the Overstock and Wayfair chapter will not make sense if you think furniture wholesale was invented in Boston in 2002. Those companies went to market to fill catalogs. They went where the catalogs already knew to stand.

What happens at a market, when it is working:

A retailer walks a line and writes a wholesale order. Title will move. Risk will move. A container or a truck will eventually move. The handshake is door two.

A designer walks a line and thinks about a project. Sometimes they will specify. Sometimes they will send a client to a local showroom that carries the cousin.

A manufacturer watches who stopped, who photographed, who asked about a price they cannot have.

A platform buyer, in the years we will get to, asked a different question: can this entire catalog be turned into SKUs and photographs and a ship-from-anywhere promise?

The educational difference is the last person. High Point was not built for them. It was built for stores. When the stores thinned out and the websites thickened, the market did not disappear. It changed its customer without always changing its language. You can still hear people say buyer when they mean a person with a portal.

There is a romance I will allow. You walk into a building and see the industry's unconscious — the finishes of the year, the metal of the year, the revival of a shape everyone swore was dead. That weather moves into showrooms six months later and onto websites in a week. If you want to know why your feed looks like itself, someone saw it in High Point first, or in Milan, or in a Shenzhen hall I am not pretending I live in.

There is a fatigue I will also allow. Market weeks can turn a shop into a temporary restaurant. You smile. You stand. You say the same sentence about a finish. You go home and try to make the things you just promised. A handmade shop that treats market like a factory treats market will come home hollow.

If you are a maker deciding whether to take a space: the rent is real, the fatigue is real, and the customer in the aisle may not be the customer you want. A design-center shop and a casegoods factory are both "furniture," and they are not the same animal in that aisle. Stand where your animal drinks.

If you are a civilian who wants in: there are some public or semi-public events around the edges in some years. Do not confuse that with a right to wholesale pricing. You are visiting a workplace that happens to look like a city.

If you are a student of the platform years, keep this picture: a person with a badge from a website, walking a century-old wholesale floor, asking family shops for their catalogs. That picture is the hinge into dropship.
