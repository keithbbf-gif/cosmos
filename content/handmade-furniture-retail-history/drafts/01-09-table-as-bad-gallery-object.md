I make dining tables for a living. I say that without romance, because a dining table is a problem in almost every room except a dining room.

A gallery that learned its manners on jewelry, fiber, and pots is not stupid for struggling with us. The object is rude.

It is large. It eats the sight lines. You cannot hang twelve of them. You cannot rotate the inventory on a Tuesday. One table is a show. Two tables is a warehouse.

It is heavy. The freight is not a padded envelope. It is a crew, a liftgate, a set of stairs, a moment when someone says the doorway is a quarter inch too small. A gallery's insurance rider that was written for ceramics will get religious.

It wants to be sat at. A serious buyer will not decide from a velvet rope. They will pull out a chair that may not even be yours, they will put their elbows where the plate goes, they will look down the length and decide whether conversation can live there. That is not browsing. That is a rehearsal of a life. A lot of galleries are not staffed for rehearsal. They are staffed for looking.

It takes time. A table can occupy a floor the way a tenant occupies an apartment. If it does not sell, the room has hosted a large, polite, expensive roommate. If it does sell, the room then has a hole the size of a car and a freight schedule.

It is specific. A bowl is a bowl in most rooms. A table is a length, a height, a leaf story, a base that either clears a knee or ruins a dinner. The buyer in a gallery is often not carrying a tape measure for their breakfast nook. The buyer in a designer's office is.

So the table went looking for other doors.

The commission door: come to the shop, or send a plan, and we will build the one that fits. That is our daily work.

The showroom door: leave a sample in a building where designers already go with floor plans in the bag. The sample is not meant to be the table they buy. It is meant to be the argument.

The fair door: sometimes, if you can get it in the booth and out again without hating yourself.

The website door: a photograph and a hope. We will get there. I will tell you now that a photograph of a table is a rumor. It can be a beautiful rumor. It is still a rumor.

Galleries that did furniture well — the ones that called themselves galleries of original furniture on purpose — designed the room around the rudeness. High ceilings. Space to walk the length. Staff who would let you sit. Relationships with makers who could take a commission when the sample was only a cousin of what you needed. Those rooms are rare because they are expensive to run. They are also the reason some people still think a handmade table belongs in a white cube. It does, if the cube was built for it. Most cubes were not.

There is a kindness in this mismatch. It kept a lot of dining tables from being treated like seasonal product. You cannot stack us by the door for the holidays without looking foolish. The rudeness protected the category.

There is a cruelty too. A lot of good furniture makers never found a gallery that could host them, and they took that as a judgment on the work. It was often a judgment on square footage. Do not confuse those.

If you run a gallery and you want a table, budget like a landlord, not like a picture-hanger. Ask who pays to get it in, who pays to get it out, who is allowed to sit, and what you will show in the hole when it leaves. If you cannot answer, take a smaller object. The maker will thank you later.

If you make tables, stop trying to win rooms that cannot hold you. It feels like rejection. It is physics. Put your sample where a person arrives with a room already in mind. That is why the trade showroom, for all its velvet-rope politics, became the grown-up door for work like ours.

If you buy tables, do not trust a gallery lighting scheme to tell you the truth about a finish, and do not trust a website thumbnail either. Sit. Eat a cracker. Stay long enough to get bored. Boredom is how you find out whether a table is a sculpture or a companion.
