A gallery is not a place that hangs things. A gallery is a place that decides what is worth hanging, and then stands next to it until a stranger agrees.

In American studio craft, the common contract was consignment. Door one. You keep title. The gallery keeps the key. When the piece sells, you split. People say fifty percent the way they say homemade. It is a habit you will meet often enough that you should budget for it, and it is not a statute. Some rooms take less. Some take more. Some buy outright, which is wholesale, door two, and those rooms are rarer because they have to believe in you with cash.

I want to talk about what the split is buying, because makers get bitter in a way that is understandable and incomplete.

The split buys rent in a zip code where buyers already have money. That is crude and true.

The split buys editing. A good gallery will tell you no. That no is worth more than a yes from a room that will hang anything and therefore sells nothing. Editing is how a buyer relaxes. They do not have to be the expert. The room already threw out the work that would waste their Saturday.

The split buys talk. Someone has to explain why this chair costs what it costs without sounding like they are apologizing. That is a profession. Most makers are bad at it when they are tired. Most makers are also the worst judges of which story the buyer needs. A good gallerist will not tell your childhood unless your childhood is in the joint.

The split buys patience. A dining table can sit. Months. A year. The gallery is eating square footage. You are eating hope. Neither of you is a bank.

What the split does not buy is loyalty in the modern sense. If your work is slow, the wall will move. If a trend turns, the wall will move. That is not betrayal. That is a room with a lease.

The bitterness comes when a maker prices the piece as if they were selling from the driveway and then is shocked by the cut. If your number only works at one hundred cents on the dollar, you are not ready for a gallery. You are ready for a website or a client in your shop. There is no shame in that. There is shame in signing a contract you have not reverse-engineered.

Here is a simple way to do the reverse-engineering, without pretending I know your overhead.

Decide the number that lets the shop live if this piece sells through a gallery.
Decide the number that lets the shop live if this piece sells to a designer at net.
Decide the number that lets the shop live if this piece sells from your own floor.
If those three numbers are the same, you do not have a pricing system. You have a wish. A wish will not pay a finisher.

Galleries also train a kind of buyer we later lost: the collector of useful objects. Not a museum donor. A person who wants to live with one serious thing and is willing to be taught. Pritam & Eames on Long Island built a life around original furniture and, later, a smaller room of functional craft. Moderne in Philadelphia helped the secondary market for Nakashima and Esherick remember that studio furniture was not "used furniture" from New Hope. Those rooms were not our rooms. They are the type. They prove that furniture can be sold as a decision, not as a fill.

Furniture is still an awkward guest in a lot of galleries. We will do a whole draft on that. The ticket is the same even when the object is a bowl: title stays with you, talk stays with them, money moves if the quiet happens.

If you are a buyer in a gallery, ask three questions that keep everyone honest.

Is this on consignment or did the room buy it? That tells you who is motivated and who can negotiate.
Who made the last two inches — the finish, the hardware, the glue-up you cannot see?
What happens if I need a second one in two years? A gallery that cannot answer is selling a souvenir. A gallery that can call the shop is selling a living trade.

If you are a maker, read the contract for the boring parts. Insurance while it sits. Photography rights. Who pays freight home if it does not sell. How fast they pay after the buyer's check clears. I have seen shops wait on a sold piece like it was a rumor. A sold piece that has not been paid is not sold. It is a story the room is telling you so you stay quiet.

The web promised to cut out this middle. Sometimes that is a gift. Sometimes it cuts out the teacher and leaves you alone with a stranger who thinks your price is a starting point because every other door they know is a discount door.

Keep the gallery in your head as the cleanest version of door one. Next we look at the moment studio furniture tried to climb out of craft and into art — and what that climb did to a table you are still supposed to eat on.
