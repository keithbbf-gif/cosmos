**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

The World Health Organization released ICD-11 in 2018. The classification came into effect in 2022. Among its mental, behavioural, and neurodevelopmental disorders sits complex post-traumatic stress disorder — code 6B41 in the version this draft last checked. The diagnosis requires the core of PTSD plus a triad of problems in self-organization: affect, self-concept, relationships. It is aimed at people whose injury was prolonged or repetitive, hard to escape. Judith Herman had asked for a name like this in 1992. DSM, through DSM-5-TR (2022), still had not given her one.

The counseling-heritage pack already tells Herman’s hinge. This essay tells the *chart event*. Official language is slow on purpose. Survivors are not. The lag is the history.

## Two manuals, two habits

DSM is an American Psychiatric Association object that the United States uses for more than science: insurance, research grants, teaching. ICD is a WHO object that the rest of the world, and American epidemiologists, use for a different kind of counting. They influence each other. They do not obey each other. A diagnosis can live in Geneva and wait in Arlington.

ICD-11’s PTSD was already a slimmer object than DSM-5’s. The complex sibling is the interesting one for this pack. It admits, in a billing language, that duration and captivity change the picture. It does not admit every hallway diagnosis. It is not Developmental Trauma Disorder. It is not “ACE score of 6.” It is not a personality disorder with better public relations — though some critics will say it is close, and those critics have a point about overlap that a staged essay should not hide.

Field trials and later papers argued about whether the self-organization symptoms are distinct enough. That argument is science doing its ordinary unkind work. It is not a reason to tell a reader she does or does not “have C-PTSD.” Internet quizzes will do that without our help.

## What a code changes

Researchers can now write grants that say 6B41. Clinics in countries that use ICD-11 can write it on a form. Manual authors will produce protocols aimed at the new object; some will be serious, some will be the same old hour with a new cover. This series will not review those protocols. The historical claim is enough: a name that spent thirty years in feminist clinics and ISTSS hallways became, in the late 2010s, something a health ministry could print.

DSM’s refusal is also a change, in the negative. American training programs will keep teaching PTSD and then saying, in the next lecture, “clinically we often see…” The “clinically we often see” is where Herman still lives in the United States. ICD-11 moved her out of the lecture’s shrug and into a code. That is not nothing. It is also not a monument. Codes get revised.

*Truth and Repair* (2023) returned Herman to justice as a clinical topic. The book is not ICD-11. Do not file it under 6B41. Point to the other pack’s Herman essay for the life and the three books.

## Systems, not only syndromes

Trauma-informed *care* does not require 6B41. Harris and Fallot were redesigning buildings before WHO split the diagnosis. A child-welfare office can injure a person with an ordinary PTSD label or with a complex one. The code does not make the desk kind.

The code does, however, give a systems argument a sharper sentence: if your caseload is people who could not leave — children, hostages, partners in a house with a lock — your intake, your groups, and your idea of “compliance” may be built for the wrong injury. That sentence is still not a worksheet.

## Limits

This page will not apply 6B41 to you. It will not compare ICD and DSM as if one were the gold stamp — and it will not use that banned marketing phrase either. It will not teach Herman’s stages. If you are in danger, call local emergency services.
