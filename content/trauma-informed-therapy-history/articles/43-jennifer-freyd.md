**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Jennifer J. Freyd is living as of last verification (2026-09-14). This essay uses the 1994 paper, the 1996 Harvard book, and the 2013 *Journal of Traumatic Stress* paper with Carly Parnitzke Smith. Public papers only. No family gossip. No health talk. The memory-wars decade attached itself to her name in public ways this pack will not recycle.

Betrayal trauma theory, as she developed it in early-1990s talks and then in print, says there can be a social use in remaining unaware of abuse when the perpetrator is the person you must still depend on. Not-knowing, in that frame, can be an adaptation, not a parlor trick. *Betrayal Trauma: The Logic of Forgetting Childhood Abuse* (1996) is the book. Smith and Freyd 2013 moved the dependence into the building: a church, a campus, a unit that advertises safety and then punishes the reporter.

## *Ethics & Behavior* 1994 is an unusual door

Betrayal trauma did not first appear in a trauma-society journal. The 1994 paper sat in *Ethics & Behavior* — a venue that already knew dependence, harm, and the problem of what a person can afford to know. That bibliographic oddity is part of the history. A theory about not-knowing as an adaptive response to a needed caregiver is an ethics argument as much as a memory argument.

The 1996 Harvard University Press book is the long form. ## Betrayal blindness is a 1996 companion term

The 1996 book uses *betrayal blindness* for not-knowing that protects a
needed relationship. It is a companion to betrayal trauma, not a second
