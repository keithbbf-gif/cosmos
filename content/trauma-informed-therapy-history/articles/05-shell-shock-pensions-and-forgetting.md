**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

On 13 February 1915, *The Lancet* printed Charles S. Myers, “A Contribution to the Study of Shell Shock.” Myers was writing from a war in which men shook, went mute, went blind, and could not satisfy a surgeon looking for a lesion. He offered three early cases and a name that escaped the journal. The British Army soon hated the name. It sounded like an injury you could award a pension. Other labels followed — war neurosis, combat fatigue, NYD(N), “lack of moral fibre” — each one a bargain between medicine and a government that had to decide whether a broken soldier was a coward, a patient, or a bill.

The counseling-heritage pack already uses Myers as the opening of a short trauma map. This essay stays with the *administrative* problem. Shell shock is not only a clinical picture. It is a word that made a treasury nervous.

## A name that worked too well

Myers published further *Lancet* notes in 1915 and 1916. He was not a novelist. He was a physician trying to describe concussion, terror, and functional loss without a bullet to show the board. The public heard “shell” and imagined a single blast. Clinicians already knew some of the men had been terrified in quiet sectors, or buried, or simply finished. The mismatch between the popular picture and the ward is the first lesson. Official names try to be precise. They become posters anyway.

W. H. R. Rivers, at Craiglockhart and in his 1918 paper “The Repression of War Experience,” gave the English-speaking world a different tone: a talking treatment that took the man’s dreams seriously and did not always reach for electricity. Siegfried Sassoon and Wilfred Owen made the hospital literature. Poetry is not a diagnosis. It is how a later public remembered that a hospital had once treated speechlessness as an injury. Ben Shephard’s *A War of Nerves* (2001) is the readable synthesis that keeps the poetry from eating the files.

Armies also used faraday and shame. The “treatment” history of the war includes rooms this series will not reconstruct as instructions. The historical fact is enough: some doctors thought fear should be punished, some thought it should be listened to, and the pension office thought both kinds of doctor were expensive.

## Forgetting as policy

After 1918 the word receded. That recession is not a mystery of science. It is what happens when a state no longer needs a mass category and would prefer not to pay for one. Interwar psychiatry had psychoanalysis, asylums, and, later, new drugs. The next war reused old arguments under new labels. Korea and the early Vietnam years said “combat fatigue” as if fatigue were a kinder bill.

What the later trauma field calls “forgetting between wars” is, in this essay’s terms, *decommissioning a category*. You can decommission a category without decommissioning the people who still wake at night. Veterans’ organizations noticed. So, eventually, did the researchers who pushed PTSD into DSM-III in 1980. The Vietnam essay in this pack takes that bureaucratic success. Here the point is the pattern: a name appears when an institution must count bodies that will not fight; the name fades when the institution would rather count something else.

Women, children, and colonized people were not waiting for the Army’s permission to be injured. Burgess and Holmstrom’s 1974 emergency-ward paper, Fanon’s hospital next to a war, and Brave Heart’s later Lakota work are reminders that official memory is selective. Shell shock moved faster than incest in the Anglo-American chart. That is not because incest was rarer.

## Pensions, malingering, and the old railway problem

Railway spine already taught medicine that an invisible injury is a compensation problem. 1915 taught it again at industrial scale. Medical boards asked whether a tremor was “organic,” whether a mute man was refusing, whether a pension would fix the symptom in place. Those questions are ugly. They are also honest about what a state is doing when it funds a diagnosis.

A trauma-informed *system* in the 2001 Harris–Fallot sense is, among other things, an attempt not to reproduce the board’s sneer in a civilian waiting room. It can fail. A school that uses “trauma” to explain every difficult child is running the opposite error: paying the category so much respect that no other explanation survives. Both errors are in the 1915 file — over-naming and under-naming — if you are willing to see them.

## Limits

This page will not teach you Rivers’s method. It will not romanticize Craiglockhart. It will not diagnose a reader who has been to war or who has not. If you are in danger, call local emergency services.

WOW Therapies is a speech-language practice. Mutism after terror has a long medical history; that history does not turn a rural Arkansas clinic into a war hospital. Adult neurogenic speech and pediatric selective mutism are service-page questions, evaluated by a licensed SLP, not by a *Lancet* reprint.

Myers later regretted how loosely the public used his phrase. Inventors of names often do. The regret is part of the artifact. Once a word is out, armies and newspapers do what they want with it. “Trauma-informed” is learning the same lesson in a quieter register.
