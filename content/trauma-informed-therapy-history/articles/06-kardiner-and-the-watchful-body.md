**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 1941, as another war was already eating the calendar, Abram Kardiner published *The Traumatic Neuroses of War*. He had treated World War I veterans at the Bronx VA in the 1920s, long enough after the Armistice that the fashionable acute stories were gone and the chronic ones remained. He described what he called a physioneurosis: not only a memory problem, a *watchfulness* written into startle, sleep, and the sense that the world is still aimed at you.

The book is the hinge between Myers’s war and the DSM-III committee’s war. It is also a hinge this series needs because later popular talk — “the body keeps the score” — often arrives as if 2014 invented a sensation. Kardiner already had the sensation. He had a psychoanalytic education and a VA caseload. He tried to make them speak to each other.

## A clinic after the parade

Kardiner was not writing from a dressing station. He was writing from the decade when the United States had tried to forget 1918 and had not quite managed it. The men in his files had jobs, or did not; marriages, or did not; a tremor that made a foreman nervous. The Army’s old names were of little use. “Shell shock” sounded like a blast. These men had been home for years.

He kept the psychoanalytic habit of looking for meaning. He also kept a physiologist’s stubbornness: some of what he saw would not reduce to a symbol. Startle, irritability, a dream that repeats the injury, a contraction of the personality around danger — he organized those as a syndrome with a bodily center. Later readers would hear PTSD. He did not have that acronym. He had a monograph and a war coming.

Allan Young’s *Harmony of Illusions* (1995) is unkind, in a useful way, to any story that treats Kardiner as a straight line to 1980. Young wants the reader to see diagnosis as a social achievement, assembled from available pieces. Kardiner is one of the pieces. So are the later Vietnam clinicians, the feminist emergency-ward papers, and the DSM’s need for a reliable-looking criterion set. This essay agrees with Young on the assembly. It disagrees with any reading that makes the veterans’ watchfulness *only* an assembly. The men were not a theory.

## Why the field did not stay built

After 1945 American psychiatry had other monuments to raise: psychoanalysis at high tide, then the first modern psychopharmacology, then community mental health as a promise. Korea reused “combat fatigue.” The early Vietnam years did not reopen Kardiner as a national project. A book can be right about a body and still lose the decade.

What changed after Vietnam was not that someone finally read 1941. What changed was a veterans’ movement, a body of research (Mardi Horowitz’s *Stress Response Syndromes*, 1976, is the civilian-academic cousin), and a committee that needed a name that could live in DSM-III. Kardiner became, after the fact, a grandfather. Grandfathers are easier to honor than to fund.

The forgetting between wars, which the shell-shock essay treats as decommissioning, is visible here as a *library* problem. The Bronx files did not vanish. The profession’s attention did. Trauma-informed care, when it later asked organizations not to forget, was — among other things — asking them not to repeat this trick: we knew, we had a book, we had something else to do.

## The body, without a trade title

Kardiner’s physioneurosis is easy to loot. A slide deck can steal “the body stays on watch” and skip the VA, the 1920s, and the psychoanalytic sentences that will not fit on the slide. This pack’s van der Kolk essay will deal with the 2014 trade book as a publishing event. Here the rule is simpler. If you want a dated claim about a watchful body in official American psychiatry, 1941 is a better year than a podcast.

That claim is still not a method. Kardiner described. He did not hand this page a protocol, and this page will not invent one. Prolonged exposure and CPT are later, different objects, with their own trials and their own critics. Somatic schools that cite Kardiner as ancestor should be read as *citing*, not as continuing his clinic by other means.

## Limits

This page will not diagnose a reader’s startle. It will not tell a veteran what his rating should be. If you are in danger, call local emergency services. If you want care, use a licensed clinician and the institutions that actually exist in your county — not a 1941 monograph.

WOW Therapies does not run a VA clinic. An adult in Southeast Arkansas whose attention, voice, or swallow changed after a neurological event is a speech-language question on a service page. Combat trauma is not that page, even when some of the words (“watchful,” “startle”) sound neighboring.

The figure essay on Kardiner stays with the man: the Columbia training, the later *The Psychological Frontiers of Society* work with Ralph Linton, the long life. This era piece stays with 1941 as a document the later field needed and, for a generation, mislaid.

## The Bronx files and the decade that looked away

Kardiner’s veterans were not acute dressing-station cases. They were men whose fashionable stories were over: jobs that could not stand a startle, marriages that could not stand a dream, a personality contracted around danger. He kept a psychoanalytic education and a physiologist’s stubbornness. The physioneurosis is that stubbornness on the page. Later popular talk that treats 2014 as the year the body entered trauma medicine has not read 1941.

Why did the field not stay built? American psychiatry after 1945 had psychoanalysis at high tide, then new drugs, then community mental health as a promise. A monograph can be right about a body and still lose the decade. Vietnam did not reopen Kardiner as a national project because someone finally found him on a shelf. It reopened the problem because a movement, a set of 1970s books, and a criterion-hungry manual needed a name. Grandfathering happened after the fact.

Young 1995 is unkind, usefully, to straight lines. Kardiner assembled what he had. Assembly is not fraud. The men were not a theory. A systems pack keeps 1941 because “forgetting between wars” is also a library problem: the files did not vanish; the profession’s attention did. Harris and Fallot later asked organizations not to forget the people in front of them. That ask does not need Kardiner. It rhymes with the cost of the forgotten decade.

WOW Therapies does not run a VA clinic. Adult cognition after stroke is a service-page matter. Combat watchfulness is not that page, even when some of the words sound neighboring. Keep the rooms distinct.
