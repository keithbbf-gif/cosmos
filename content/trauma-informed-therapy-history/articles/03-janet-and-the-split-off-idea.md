**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 1889 Pierre Janet published *L’Automatisme psychologique*, the thesis that made him, for a time, the person you cited if you wanted to talk about ideas that acted without the person’s consent. He was thirty. He had already been watching patients at Le Havre. Paris, and Charcot’s Salpêtrière, would make him famous and then, after Charcot’s death, professionally colder. Henri Ellenberger’s *The Discovery of the Unconscious* (1970) is still the English door that refuses to let Freud’s century erase him.

This essay is not the figure piece. The figure piece stays with the life. This one stays with a single clinical claim: after a shock, some ideas do not join the story a person tells about herself. They arrive as attacks, sleepwalking, a narrowed field of consciousness, a hand that writes. Janet called the split *désagrégation* and the autonomous fragments *idées fixes*. Later English said dissociation. Later charts said PTSD and, later still, a dissociative subtype. The words are not synonyms. The family resemblance is real.

## A clinic, not a battlefield

Janet’s patients were not, for the most part, soldiers. They were the people a late-nineteenth-century French hospital already knew how to display: women called hysterical, men who did not fit the ward’s other drawers, cases Charcot had used on Tuesday to teach. That matters. A trauma history that begins in 1915 will treat Janet as a prologue to Myers. He is also a reminder that the civilian clinic had a language for post-shock automatism before the British Army needed a pension word.

He did not have “trauma-informed care.” He had hypnosis, careful history-taking, and a theory of psychological energy that later readers either translate into information-processing talk or politely skip. This series will not translate him into CBT. It will not run his hypnotic method as a how-to. The historical claim is smaller: by 1889 a philosopher-physician had described memory that returns as a symptom rather than as a narrative, and he had insisted the split was a fact of consciousness, not a moral failure.

Freud, for a few years, also wrote of seduction and trauma, then moved the center of gravity toward fantasy and inner conflict. The counseling-heritage pack tells that story as part of the psychoanalytic century. Here the point is narrower. Janet’s patients kept producing the same inconvenience: a past event that would not stay in the past. Psychoanalysis, after the seduction quarrel, had reasons to look elsewhere. Trauma medicine, after Vietnam, had reasons to look back. Neither glance is innocent.

## What “dissociation” is doing in 1889

Popular talk now uses dissociation for everything from daydreaming to a courtroom defense. Janet was more specific and, in another way, more ambitious. Automatism was a general psychology. Hysteria was a severe case of a common human capacity: parts of mental life can run without the “I” that thinks it is in charge. Shock, exhaustion, and a narrowed field of consciousness made the split more likely. That is a theory, not a video.

Ellenberger warned English readers not to flatten Janet into a forgotten Freud. The warning still holds. Janet outlived the Salpêtrière’s Tuesday theater. He taught at the Collège de France. He kept writing after the word *hysteria* had become an embarrassment. American trauma psychiatry rediscovered him in the late twentieth century, sometimes as a mascot. A mascot is not a reading. If you cannot point to *L’Automatisme* or to *The Mental State of Hystericals* (English 1901), do not quote him.

Onno van der Hart and other late-century clinicians built a “structural dissociation” vocabulary that claims Janet as ancestor. That vocabulary has a research file and a set of critics. This essay will not adjudicate it. It will say that ancestor-claims are themselves historical events. They tell you what a 1990s clinic needed from 1889.

## Forgetting Janet

The forgetting is part of the trauma story this pack keeps telling. Between wars, official psychiatry had other business: psychoanalysis, then psychopharmacology, then the next war. Janet did not fit the American training sequence. When DSM-III (1980) wrote PTSD, it did not write *idées fixes*. It wrote an event “outside the range of usual human experience” and a cluster of reexperiencing, numbing, and arousal. The kinship is visible if you are looking for it. It is not a citation.

A trauma-informed *system*, in Harris and Fallot’s later sense, is even further from Janet. He was not redesigning intake. He was describing a mind. The later federal pamphlets that ask staff to *recognize* signs owe him a debt they rarely name: the idea that a person may not be able to tell you, in order, what the sign means. That is not permission to diagnose from a flinch. It is a reason a swallow exam or a school intake might slow down.

WOW Therapies does not practice Janetian psychotherapy. A speech-language clinician who meets a child who goes mute in the chair is not thereby a Salpêtrière intern. The useful inheritance, if there is one, is modest: speechlessness has been treated as an injury before. Myers and Rivers will say it again in English, under fire.

## Limits

This page is not a dissociation workbook. It will not teach you to “map parts.” If you are in danger, call local emergency services. If a reader recognizes herself in Janet’s descriptions, the next step is a licensed clinician, not a nineteenth-century thesis.

We will not invent a Janet who already believed in ACE scores, SAMHSA’s four R’s, or complex PTSD. He died in 1947. ICD-11’s split of PTSD from complex PTSD is a twenty-first-century chart event. The resemblance is a historian’s observation, not his claim.
