**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 2013 the *Journal of Traumatic Stress* printed Carly Parnitzke Smith and Jennifer J. Freyd, “Dangerous Safe Havens: Trauma, Institutional Betrayal, and Health.” The paper took Freyd’s 1990s betrayal-trauma theory — not-knowing as an adaptation when the perpetrator is the person you must depend on — and moved it into the building. A church, a school, a university, a military unit, a hospital can be the depended-on object. When that object fails the person who reports, or makes reporting costly, the failure is not only bad customer service. It is, in their terms, a second injury with health correlates.

This essay stays with that move. The memory-wars essay already refused a verdict on recovered memory. The Freyd figure essay stays with the 1996 book. Here the dated claim is 2013: trauma-informed *systems* talk acquired a sharper noun for what a building does wrong.

## Dependence, not branding

Betrayal, in this literature, requires a relationship that mattered. A stranger’s cruelty is cruelty. A university that advertises safety and then buries a report is a different object. Title IX offices, campus clergy, military commands, and hospital patient-relations desks are the usual American examples. The paper’s “safe haven” in the title is ironic on purpose.

SAMHSA’s *resist re-traumatization* is a cousin verb. Institutional betrayal is more specific. It names the dependence and the expectation. A pamphlet can ask a staff to be kind. Freyd and Smith ask whether the institution’s *incentives* punish the reporter. Kindness trainings that leave the incentives untouched are how a campus becomes a case study.

Shay’s moral injury, especially the leadership-betrayal half, rhymes from the war side. Do not merge the constructs. A platoon is not a residence hall. The rhyme is useful for a systems pack: both literatures refuse to leave the injury inside one skull.

## What the 2013 paper is not

It is not a proof that every delayed report is true. It is not a proof that every institution is guilty. It is a framework and a set of associations. Associations are not verdicts. The memory wars taught this field, or should have, that frameworks get looted by people who already know the ending.

It is not a reason to tell a child in a speech clinic that the school is betraying them. WOW Therapies may later meet a family in a fight with a district. That fight has IEPs, advocates, and special-education law. It is not automatically 2013 JTS. Do not borrow a trauma noun to win an IEP meeting on a history URL.

It is not a protocol for “institutional courage,” a later Freyd phrase that some campuses adopted as branding. Branding is the risk. A building that prints courage and keeps the same general counsel script has learned the noun and missed the paper.

## Afterlives

The 2010s campus-assault debates, the Catholic Church’s long crisis, and later military reporting scandals gave the noun plenty of weather. Weather is not a validation study. It is why grant language and student-affairs offices now say “institutional betrayal” without citing Smith and Freyd. Leakage, again. Railway spine leaked. Shell shock leaked. PTSD leaked. Useful names leave the journal.

A trauma-informed *history* can treat leakage as a fact and still prefer the 2013 object: a paper, a theory of dependence, a warning that safe-haven advertising is part of the injury when the haven fails.

If you need to report, use the actual office or the police — and, if you can, a lawyer or advocate who does not work for the building you are reporting.

## JTS 2013 is a society journal, not a campus verdict

Smith and Freyd placed “Dangerous Safe Havens” in the *Journal of Traumatic Stress* — the ISTSS journal, a shop that already knew PTSD criteria. Putting institutional betrayal there asked a trauma society to look at buildings, not only at events. `[VERIFY]` pagination before a print footnote. ## A student-sample paper is not a census of buildings

Smith and Freyd 2013 asked a trauma-society journal to look at institutions.
The associations they reported came from the samples they had — not from a
census of every campus, diocese, or hospital. That methods limit is part of
the object. A later slide that treats the paper as a verdict on “the
university” has skipped the methods section.

Dependence plus advertising remains the structure worth keeping. A building
that sells safety and then manages the reporter as the risk is the structure
in ordinary English. This URL still will not file your complaint. It will
not score your health from a betrayal item. It will keep 2013 dated so
“resist re-traumatization” cannot stay a bullet without a building.
