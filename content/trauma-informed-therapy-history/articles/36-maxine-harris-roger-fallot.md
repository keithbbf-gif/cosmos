**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Maxine Harris and Roger D. Fallot wrote from Community Connections, a Washington, D.C., community mental-health agency, not from a federal office and not from a locked ward. In 2001 they edited *Using Trauma Theory to Design Service Systems* and, in that volume, “Envisioning a Trauma-Informed Service System.” Five values: safety, trustworthiness, choice, collaboration, empowerment. A distinction that keeps dying: trauma-informed *systems* are not trauma-specific *treatments*.

They are living as of last verification (2026-09-14), to this draft’s knowledge. Public books and the public agency only. No health talk. No private life. If an editor finds a death date this draft missed, correct the living line; do not invent a eulogy.

## An agency pair, not a brand

Harris’s earlier work on women and trauma, and Fallot’s work on services and (in other publications) faith and recovery, sat inside a D.C. weather of poverty, racism, housing, and a public behavioral-health system that had to take all comers. The 2001 book is not a vibe. It is a design argument: intake, space, staff, the number of times a person retells the worst day.

SAMHSA’s 2014 pamphlet cites them among key thought leaders, adds a sixth principle, and packages four R’s. Federal echo is not completion. Bloom’s Sanctuary Model is the hospital cousin. Do not merge the cousins. Community support has different levers than a night shift with keys.

The slogan *What happened to you?* is later popular weather (and a 2021 trade title). Do not back-date the catchphrase into Jossey-Bass. The 2001 ask — understand the person in context; treat many symptoms as adaptations — does not need a talk-show sentence.

## Why two names share a slug

This pack gives them one figure slot because the 2001 object is a collaboration. Splitting them into two 1,000-word saints would be a different piety. The era essay already did the book. This figure does the *shop*: Community Connections, a city, a pair who made the building the patient without forgetting the people in it.

They also wrote, in that volume and around it, about the risk that programs recreate injury — restraints, searches, a dozen intakes. Institutional betrayal, as Freyd and Smith later named a cousin problem, is not their vocabulary. The rhyme is the building’s incentives.
