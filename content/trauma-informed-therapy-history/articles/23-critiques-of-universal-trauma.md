**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 1995 Princeton University Press issued Allan Young’s *The Harmony of Illusions: Inventing Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder*. Young had sat in a VA unit and watched a diagnosis being taught, practiced, and believed. He did not say the veterans were pretending. He said the category is a cultural and institutional achievement — assembled from available pieces, useful to a late-twentieth-century American clinic and a pension system. In 2001 the *BMJ* printed Derek Summerfield, “The Invention of Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder and the Social Usefulness of a Psychiatric Category.” Summerfield aimed at the humanitarian export: a Western module arriving with the aid flight, sometimes ahead of land, law, and work.

This pack would be a brochure without those two books. It would also be a sneer if it let them erase Burgess’s ward, Brave Heart’s papers, or a child who cannot sleep. The job is to keep the critique and the waiting room in the same frame.

## What Young is good for

Young makes it harder to tell a straight story from Myers 1915 to DSM-III 1980 as if nature had been waiting for a committee. Railway spine, shell shock, Kardiner, Horowitz, a veterans’ movement, a manual that wanted checkable criteria — those are pieces. Assembly is not the same as fraud. Astronomy is also assembled. The difference is that PTSD decides who is paid, who is believed, and who is recruited into a treatment trial.

A trauma-informed *system* that cannot hear Young will treat SMA 14-4884 as revealed text. A trauma-informed *system* that hears only Young will treat every flinch as a fashion. Harris and Fallot were redesigning buildings because the people in them had been hurt. That empirical irritation survives a monograph about invention.

## What Summerfield is good for

Summerfield is good for the airport. He is good for the moment a checklist is translated and a political catastrophe becomes a symptom score. He is good for the reminder that some people want a house more than they want an hour. The refugee-program essay in this pack already walked his trouble through Mollica’s clinic. The trouble remains even if you dislike his tone.

He is less good as a universal solvent. “Invention” in a headline can be heard, by a person who was raped last year, as an accusation. A staged history can refuse that hearing without refusing the paper. Social usefulness cuts both ways. A category can be useful to an NGO’s budget. It can also be useful to a woman whose family preferred “personality.”

Ben Shephard’s *A War of Nerves* (2001) is the military historian’s cousin: less theory, more files, still unwilling to let psychiatry own the twentieth century’s nerves.

## Universal precautions, specific injuries

Medicine already knows “universal precautions” as a phrase about blood. Trauma-informed care sometimes talks as if every building should take universal trauma precautions: assume injury, avoid the second injury, do not demand the story. As manners, that is cheap and often right. As analysis, it can become a way not to think. A stroke is not a rape. A late talker is not a veteran. A Lakota grief group is not a Kaiser ACE quiz. The opening essay of this pack asked the rooms not to collapse. The critique of *universal* trauma is the same ask from the other door.

Overdiagnosis, in the later popular sense, is a real clinic complaint: everything is trauma, therefore nothing is. Underdiagnosis is the older complaint this pack has been telling since 1915: the official room forgets, especially when the injured person is not a man in uniform. A history that only has one of those complaints is a faction.

## Limits

This page will not tell you PTSD is fake. It will not tell you it is a natural kind like a femur. It will not help you deny a claim or win one. If you are in danger, call local emergency services.

WOW Therapies will not use this essay to sound worldly about “the trauma industry.” A speech-language practice that sneers at trauma talk on a parent-facing site is doing a different injury. The useful inheritance is modest: keep the rooms distinct; do not sell a federal pamphlet as a method; do not use a *BMJ* essay as a reason to ignore a child.

The Summerfield figure essay stays with the 2001 paper and the man. This era piece stays with the pair — Young and Summerfield — as the dissent this series required itself to read before it printed forty-five drafts.

## Shephard 2001 is the readable military cousin

Ben Shephard’s *A War of Nerves* is a synthesis of soldiers and psychiatrists across a century. Young 1995 is an ethnography of a diagnosis being assembled and taught. Summerfield 2001 is a short export argument in a general medical journal. Three dissent doors. A faction that only has Young will call every flinch a fashion. A faction that only has SAMHSA will call every pamphlet revealed text.

This pack kept all three on the calendar so the federal card cannot finish the story. Assembly is not fraud. Export is not an empty ward. Burgess’s emergency room and Brave Heart’s 1998 papers do not vanish because a critic is good. WOW will not use dissent to sound worldly on a parent-facing page, and will not use a pamphlet to end a quarrel.
