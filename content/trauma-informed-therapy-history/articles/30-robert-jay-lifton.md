**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Robert Jay Lifton was born in 1926 and is living as of last verification (2026-09-14). This essay uses the public books. No health talk. No private life.

*Death in Life: Survivors of Hiroshima* (1968) and *Home from the War: Vietnam Veterans—Neither Victims nor Executioners* (1973) are the two objects this pack needs. The first gave English-language psychiatry a long look at people who had survived a city’s murder. The second gave the American public a picture of Vietnam veterans that refused the available cartoons. DSM-III’s 1980 PTSD did not “come from” these books. The books helped make a public that could hear the diagnosis.

## A psychiatrist who wrote for more than a chart

Lifton’s method — long interviews, a willingness to let people talk past the symptom list, a moral vocabulary that makes some later empiricists nervous — is easy to praise and easy to dismiss. This essay will do neither at length. It will say that 1968 and 1973 are publishing events. They put survivor guilt, psychic numbing, and the veteran’s double bind (hero or monster) into sentences a non-specialist could finish.

He also wrote about Chinese thought reform, about Nazi doctors, about cultic formations. Those books are part of his fame. They are not automatically trauma-informed care. Do not file every Lifton title under SMA 14-4884. The man is larger than this pack’s lane. The lane takes the two survivor books and the Vietnam public.

## Neither victims nor executioners

The 1973 subtitle is the ethical claim. A veterans’ movement that wanted a diagnosis also wanted not to be reduced to it. Lifton sat with “rap groups” and with men who had done and seen things the later criterion set would file under “event.” The file is thinner than the life. Young 1995, again, is the unkind useful cousin: categories assemble. Lifton’s pages are one of the pieces the assembly used.

Chaim Shatan and others were in the same 1970s weather with “post-Vietnam syndrome.” This figure essay will not flatten them into one biography. It will say that Lifton’s gift to the later field was literary-clinical permission: you may write the war as a moral injury to character *and* as a medical object. Shay would take the first half further in 1994. The DSM took the second half in 1980.
