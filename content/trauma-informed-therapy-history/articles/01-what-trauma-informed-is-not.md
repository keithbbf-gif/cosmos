**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 2001, Jossey-Bass issued number 89 of *New Directions for Mental Health Services*: *Using Trauma Theory to Design Service Systems*, edited by Maxine Harris and Roger D. Fallot. The book did not announce a new psychotherapy. It announced a quarrel with waiting rooms, intake forms, restraint policies, and the habit of asking only what is wrong with a person who has already been asked that question in a dozen other buildings.

That quarrel is the subject of this series. The counseling-heritage pack already has a short map from Myers’s 1915 “shell shock” paper to Herman’s complex trauma and ICD-11. This pack stays with a later, plainer problem: how a word that once named a wound on a soldier or a woman in an emergency ward became a sticker on a school, a hospital, a child-welfare office, and, sometimes, a clinic website that does not treat trauma at all.

## Two jobs that share a word

**Trauma-specific treatment** is a billed encounter aimed at a diagnosed injury. Prolonged exposure, cognitive processing therapy, EMDR, and a dozen other schools belong to that job. They have manuals, training institutes, and argumentative research files. This series will name them as *arrivals*. It will not teach them.

**Trauma-informed care** (Harris and Fallot also wrote “trauma-informed service system”) is a claim about an organization. SAMHSA’s 2014 pamphlet — HHS Publication SMA 14-4884, *SAMHSA’s Concept of Trauma and Guidance for a Trauma-Informed Approach* — boiled the claim into four assumptions the pamphlet called the four R’s. A program, organization, or system that is trauma-informed *realizes* the widespread impact of trauma and possible paths for recovery; *recognizes* signs in clients, families, staff, and others; *responds* by putting that knowledge into policies, procedures, and practices; and seeks to *resist* re-traumatization.

Those verbs are about a building. They are not a CPT worksheet. A speech-language office that changes how it explains a swallow exam, how it seats a child who cannot bear to be touched at the jaw, or how it trains staff not to surprise a person from behind is doing organizational work. That work can be serious. It is still not “PTSD treatment.” Confusing the two is how a federal phrase becomes advertising.

Harris and Fallot, writing from Community Connections in Washington, D.C., listed five values for a trauma-informed system: safety, trustworthiness, choice, collaboration, and empowerment. SAMHSA later expanded the list to six principles and renamed the last item **Empowerment, Voice and Choice**, adding cultural, historical, and gender issues as a principle of its own. The expansion is a historical fact. It is also a reminder that pamphlets accumulate. They do not replace the 2001 book, and they do not license a clinic to treat a diagnosis it does not hold.

## What the word is doing when it is lazy

By the mid-2010s the phrase had escaped behavioral health. Schools printed posters. Hospitals added slides to orientation. Child-welfare agencies wrote it into requests for proposals. Some of that migration was earned: the ACE study (Felitti, Anda, and colleagues, *American Journal of Preventive Medicine*, 1998) had given public health a graded vocabulary for childhood adversity, and NCTSN (authorized in 2000) had given child-serving systems a funded network. Some of the migration was fashion. A word that costs nothing to print will be printed.

This pack will not sneer at schools and hospitals for borrowing a federal phrase. It will ask, in each essay, *which room* is being described. A VA form in 1980 is not a kindergarten in 2016. A rape-crisis exam in 1974 is not an ACE quiz on a parenting blog. A Lakota grief intervention described by Maria Yellow Horse Brave Heart in 1998 is not a Kaiser questionnaire. If those rooms collapse into one slogan, the history has failed.

WOW Therapies is a speech-language practice in Southeast Arkansas. The live site’s body copy offers evaluation and treatment of speech and language, including pediatric work and adult cognition, voice, and swallow. It does not offer a trauma clinic. These essays exist so a parent or a referral source who meets the phrase “trauma-informed” on a poster can tell a systems idea from a treatment claim. They are not a reason to book an appointment for PTSD.

## A short refusal list, in ordinary English

This series will not tell you how to move your eyes. It will not give you a thought record. It will not score your childhood on a ten-item form and call the number a diagnosis. It will not say that SAMHSA proved a method. SMA 14-4884 is guidance for an *approach*. TIP 57 (2014) is a treatment-improvement protocol for behavioral health services — a different federal object, still not a consumer workbook.

It will not say Judith Herman invented trauma. The other pack’s Herman essay is the place for *Father–Daughter Incest* (1981) and *Trauma and Recovery* (1992). Here she appears when a systems argument needs her hinge: prolonged captivity is not the same injury as a single incident, and recovery is social. The posters that turned her stages into three boxes are not the book.

It will not say “the body keeps the score” and sit down. Bessel van der Kolk’s 2014 trade title made a phrase people now bring to intake. The phrase is older than the book; Kardiner already wrote a physioneurosis. The figure essay in this pack treats the popular book as a publishing event and the Developmental Trauma Disorder proposal as a rejected chart — not as scripture.

## How to read the rest

The next essays go backward before they go forward. Railway spine (Erichsen, 1866) is an industrial name for shock that insurance companies already disliked. Janet’s 1889 *automatisme* is a clinic name for ideas that arrive as attacks. Ferenczi’s 1932 lecture is a family name that psychoanalysis tried to bury. Myers, Rivers, Kardiner, and the DSM-III committee are military and official names. Burgess and Holmstrom (1974) are an emergency-ward name. Harris and Fallot are a community-mental-health name. SAMHSA is a federal name. Brave Heart is a Lakota name that the federal pamphlet later gestured at under “cultural, historical, and gender issues.”

Derek Summerfield’s 2001 *BMJ* essay and Allan Young’s 1995 monograph are names for dissent: that PTSD is also a social tool, useful to some institutions, costly to others. A history that cannot hear the dissent will sell the pamphlet.

If you are in danger, call local emergency services. This page will not write your safety plan. It will not diagnose you from a table of contents. It will try, in the essays that follow, to keep the rooms from collapsing.
