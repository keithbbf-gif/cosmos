**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Sandra L. Bloom’s Sanctuary Model did not begin as a federal pamphlet. It began as a problem on a ward. In the 1980s and 1990s, working in and around Philadelphia inpatient and residential settings, Bloom and colleagues kept meeting the same ugly loop: people admitted because they had been hurt were being hurt again by routines — restraint, humiliation, a staff group that had itself gone numb or frightened. *Creating Sanctuary: Toward the Evolution of Sane Societies* (1997) is the book a later training industry cites. The argument is older than the trademarked workshops: a milieu is a clinical object, and an injured organization will reproduce injury.

## A hospital idea, not a poster

Milieu therapy already had a mid-century history — Maxwell Jones and the therapeutic community, for one English line. Bloom’s contribution, for this pack’s purposes, is to put *trauma* at the center of the milieu and then to say the staff are in the picture. Secondary traumatic stress, burnout, and what later writers called organizational trauma are not decorations on her model. They are why a ward goes mean.

Sanctuary developed a shared vocabulary for teams (later packaged as commitments and a “SELF” framework in training materials). This essay will not reprint the training package. Training packages are commercial objects with fidelity checklists. The historical claim does not need the checklist. The claim is that by the late 1990s an American psychiatrist had written a book-length argument that you cannot treat trauma in a building that is running on fear.

Harris and Fallot, a few years later, wrote from community support rather than from the locked ward. The cousins rhyme. They are not the same. A residential unit has different levers than a walk-in clinic: keys, nights, a staff who sleep poorly for a living. Bloom’s work shows its hospital origin in its attention to those levers. SAMHSA’s 2014 domains (physical environment, workforce, governance) are, in one reading, a federal translation of insights the ward already paid for.

## Organizations as patients — a metaphor with a limit

Bloom is willing to talk about an organization as if it had a trauma history: mergers, violence on the unit, a suicide, a funding cut, a scandal. The metaphor helps a board notice that last year’s horror is still in the staff meeting. The metaphor also risks nonsense. A corporation does not have a hippocampus. A funding cut is not a rape. A careful history keeps the metaphor as a *management* language, not as a diagnosis of the building.

Figley’s work on compassion fatigue and secondary traumatic stress (see his figure essay) is the parallel research file from the veterans’ and disaster side. Bloom’s file is the hospital. Both files matter to later “trauma-informed supervision” talk. Neither file is a reason for this page to teach a supervision protocol.

## What Sanctuary is not

It is not EMDR. It is not a consumer self-help book. It is not SAMHSA’s six principles, though a later trainer can map them onto each other in a slide that makes everything look inevitable. Maps of that kind are pedagogical. They are not how the 1990s happened.

It is not a proof that any facility now calling itself Sanctuary-certified has become kind. Certification is a later industry. Industries drift. A staged history can name the 1997 book and the ward argument without endorsing every licensee.

It is not a service WOW Therapies offers. A speech-language practice does not run a milieu. The transferable caution, if a human editor later wants one sentence on a service page, is small: staff who are frightened or humiliated will frighten and humiliate. That sentence is ordinary. It does not require a model name.

## Limits

This page will not walk you through a community meeting. It will not give you the SELF questions as a worksheet. If you are in danger, call local emergency services. If you work on a unit that is going mean, you need a real supervisor, a union, a licensing board, or an exit — not a 1997 table of contents.

Bloom is living as of last verification (2026-09-14). Public books and public trainings only. No health talk. The figure essay stays with the career. This era piece stays with the ward as the place trauma-informed *care* had to mean something before it meant a grant phrase.

A residential unit has levers a walk-in clinic does not: keys, a night shift, a community meeting that can go cruel, a restraint that becomes a habit. ## Therapeutic community is a cousin with a different war

Maxwell Jones and the mid-century therapeutic-community literature treated the social life of a unit as part of the medicine. Bloom read that cousin and then wrote a 1997 book whose center of gravity is trauma and staff injury. Cousin is not a reason to file *Creating Sanctuary* under Jones. It is a reason to notice that “the ward is the treatment” is older than the word trauma-informed.

What a later two-day training could not buy is a night supervisor who will look at restraint numbers. Fidelity checklists that never meet the night report have purchased a vocabulary. Figley’s helper-side file and Harris and Fallot’s community file remain parallel shops. Three vocabularies, one ugly observation: frightened staff frighten. WOW does not run a milieu. Ordinary caution on a speech hallway does not require a trademark.
