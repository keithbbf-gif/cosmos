**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Abram Kardiner was born in 1891 and died in 1981. He trained in the psychoanalytic world that mid-century New York took seriously — including time around the Berlin and Vienna circuits that American obituaries like to name — and then spent the years that mattered for this pack at a Bronx VA, listening to World War I veterans whose fashionable acute stories were over. *The Traumatic Neuroses of War* (1941) is the book. A physioneurosis: the body stays on watch.

The era essay stays with 1941 as a document the later field needed and mislaid. This figure stays with the man who wrote it and then went on to other fame.

## The VA years, not the later seminar

Kardiner’s later collaboration with the anthropologist Ralph Linton (*The Psychological Frontiers of Society* and related culture-and-personality work) is how some textbooks still file him. That work is real. It is not why he is in a trauma-informed *history*. He is here because he treated men whose startle, sleep, and contracted world would not reduce to a symbol, and he was honest enough on the page to keep a bodily center next to a psychoanalytic education.

Honesty of that kind does not make a lasting field by itself. After 1945 American psychiatry had other monuments. Korea said combat fatigue. Vietnam, eventually, said PTSD. Kardiner became a grandfather after the fact. Grandfathers are cheaper than clinics.

Young 1995 will not let a biographer turn 1941 into a straight line. Good. The man assembled what he had: VA files, analytic theory, a coming war. Assembly is the job of a clinician who writes a monograph. It is not a smear.

## What he did not write

He did not write SAMHSA’s four R’s. He did not write a consumer book whose title became a proverb. He did not write a protocol this page can teach. Later somatic schools that cite him as ancestor are *citing*. Citation is not continuation.

He did not center civilian sexual violence. That centering is Burgess 1974 and Herman 1981/1992. A figure essay can say the limit without scolding a 1941 VA doctor for not being a 1974 emergency nurse.

## Afterlife

Horowitz 1976, the ISTSS generation, and the DSM-III committee retrieved the watchful body under a new acronym. Retrieval looks like justice in a slide deck. It looks like delay in a veteran’s life. Both readings are allowed.

WOW Therapies will not hang his portrait in a swallow room. No portrait in this wave. If a later editor finds a license-clear photograph, log it. Do not generate a face.

## Limits

This page will not diagnose your startle. It will not fill a VA form. If you are in danger, call local emergency services. If you are a veteran, use the VA and accredited services that exist now.

The man is a dated door into the watchful body. The door is enough. We do not need him to be proto-2014. We need the 1941 object and the forgotten decade after it.

## After the monograph: a classroom belt

Kardiner’s Columbia years after the war are the part most blogs skip. He taught psychosomatics to people who would later staff VA clinics and city hospitals. The 1941 book was the assigned ancestor; the classroom was the transmission belt. A history that only cites the monograph misses the students who carried “the body keeps a watch” into rooms that never heard the author’s name.

He also wrote as an anthropologist of American institutions, not only of soldiers. That double training is why the 1941 text feels less like a casebook and more like an ethnography of a nervous system under contract. The contract was military. The method was psychoanalytic. The result, awkwardly, was usable by people who rejected both the military and the couch.

If a 2026 clinician quotes Kardiner to sound learned, the quote should be short and dated. The longer gift is the refusal to treat startle as metaphor. That refusal is already in the complementary pack’s trauma-lineages essay. This figure page exists so the refusal has a name and a year attached.

## 1939 is an anthropology book, 1941 is the war book

*The Individual and His Society* (1939), with Ralph Linton’s ethnographic material, is Kardiner the Columbia comparativist. *The Traumatic Neuroses of War* (1941) is Kardiner the clinician of veterans. Students who only meet the watchful body in a 2014 trade title have skipped both dates. The physioneurosis claim — startle as physiology, not metaphor — is a 1941 sentence. Cite it that way.
