**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

John Eric Erichsen was born in Copenhagen in 1818, trained in London, and died there in 1896 as a knighted surgeon whose name still sits on a generation of surgical textbooks. Trauma-informed care does not descend from him. What descends is a problem he could not leave in the operating theatre: a passenger who walks away from a collision and then, days later, cannot, with no wound a coroner admires.

The 1866 lectures published as *On Railway and Other Injuries of the Nervous System* are the reason he is in this pack. The era essay on railway spine stays with the system — steel, insurance, a lesion you could argue about. This figure stays with the surgeon who made the argument from a hospital that taught University College students how to see.

## A surgeon in a new machine’s decade

Erichsen was already a famous operator when the railways became a mass fact. Fame matters. Plaintiffs like a name a jury has heard. Railway companies like a name they can hire to disagree. Herbert Page’s 1883 book is the disagreement this pack treats as the other half of the man: not a personal feud, a structural one. Erichsen believed something physical had happened even when the knife found little. Page believed fear, suggestion, and the lawsuit did more work. Both men were doing medicine in a courtroom.

Do not make Erichsen a humanitarian saint. He was a Victorian surgeon with a Victorian surgeon’s habits of mind. Do not make him a company villain’s opposite number. He was trying to name an injury his instruments could not hold. The later trauma field’s entire credibility problem is already in that try.

He wrote “railway and *other* injuries.” The title’s second half is easy to skip. It is the half that lets a later reader see he knew the train was an example, not a species. Industrial accidents, later motor crashes, later blast waves — the “other” would grow. He did not live to see DSM-III. He did not need to. He had already met the invisible-injury bill.

## What he did not found

He did not found psychotherapy. He recommended rest and the ordinary caution of his decade. He did not describe a talking cure. He did not anticipate Janet’s *idées fixes* except in the weak sense that both men took post-shock nervous life seriously. Weak senses make bad history.

He did not prove a “molecular” spinal lesion in the way a later pathologist would want. His physicalism was a bet. Page’s psychologizing was a bet. The twentieth century would run both bets again under other names — organic versus functional, malingering versus illness, Criterion A versus “personality.” A figure essay can say that without declaring a winner.

## Afterlife

American and British case law used “railway spine” until the phrase became a joke and then a footnote. Historians of PTSD (Young 1995 is the monograph door) retrieved Erichsen as a rehearsal. Retrieval is not ancestry in the family-tree sense. It is a historian pointing at a rhyme: compensation, time-lag, a body that will not display.

## Limits

This page will not diagnose your last car crash. It will not help you sue. If you are in danger, call local emergency services. If you need medical care after a collision, use an emergency department, not an 1866 lecture.

The man is a dated door into industrial shock. The door is enough. We do not need him to be kind, modern, or proto-SAMHSA. We need the 1866 object and the 1883 reply.
