**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 2001, *New Directions for Mental Health Services* number 89 appeared under the title *Using Trauma Theory to Design Service Systems*, edited by Maxine Harris and Roger D. Fallot. The pair worked at Community Connections, a Washington, D.C., agency that had already learned — the hard way, from the people who used it — that a large share of adults in community mental health and substance-use programs carried trauma histories the building was not designed to notice.

Their chapter “Envisioning a Trauma-Informed Service System: A Vital Paradigm Shift” is the essay this pack treats as the organizational birth certificate. Not the birth of trauma. Not the birth of PTSD. The birth of a claim that the *system* is a clinical object.

## From “what is wrong” to “what happened” — and the slogan problem

Harris and Fallot asked staff to understand a whole person in context, and to treat many “symptoms” as adaptations to intolerable circumstances. Later trainings boiled that ask into a slogan: *What happened to you?* instead of *What’s wrong with you?* The slogan is useful on a slide. It is also younger, as a catchphrase, than the 2001 book. Bruce Perry and Oprah Winfrey’s 2021 trade title made the question famous with a different public. Do not back-date the catchphrase into Jossey-Bass. Do not pretend the 2001 argument needed a talk-show sentence to be serious.

The 2001 values were five: safety, trustworthiness, choice, collaboration, and empowerment. Those words are easy to print and hard to fund. Choice, in a community mental-health agency that also manages housing or a court mandate, is not the choice of a private-pay hour. Harris and Fallot wrote from that constraint. A later consultant who sells “choice” to a jail has not read the constraint.

They also distinguished trauma-informed *systems* from trauma-specific *treatments*. The opening essay of this pack exists because that distinction keeps dying. A building can be trying to avoid a second injury and still not offer prolonged exposure. A clinician can be certified in a method and still work in a building that humiliates people at the desk. 2001 is the year the second sentence became, in print, a design problem.

## An agency, not a cloud

Community Connections is a place. Washington, D.C., in those years, had a public behavioral-health system with all the weather that phrase implies: poverty, racism, housing, cocaine and then other drugs, a federal city watching itself. Harris’s earlier work on women and trauma, and Fallot’s work on services and faith, sat inside that weather. A history that treats “Harris and Fallot” as two names on a SAMHSA bibliography has already lost the agency.

They wrote about intake, physical space, staff training, and the risk that a program’s own routines — restraints, sudden searches, a dozen retellings of the worst day — recreate the original injury. Sandra Bloom’s Sanctuary Model, running in parallel in the 1990s, is a cousin with a different vocabulary (and its own essay in this pack). The cousins should not be merged. Bloom came out of a Philadelphia hospital milieu. Harris and Fallot came out of community support. Both were answering the same ugly observation: the people who use public systems are not a random sample of “mental illness.” They are a sample of injury plus poverty plus the system’s own habits.

## The federal echo

SAMHSA’s 2014 SMA 14-4884 cites Harris and Fallot among its “key thought leaders.” The pamphlet adds a sixth principle (cultural, historical, and gender issues), renames the fifth as **Empowerment, Voice and Choice**, and packages four assumptions as the four R’s. That is how federal documents work. They stabilize a conversation so grant writers and state agencies can point to a number. Stabilization is not theft and not completion.

TIP 57 (2014) is the behavioral-health protocol cousin: longer, more clinical, still not a consumer workbook. This series will not collapse TIP 57 into the 2001 book. Different objects. Different readers.

## What 2001 does not license

It does not license a speech-language website to advertise PTSD care. WOW Therapies may, if a later human editor so decides, say that its *building* tries not to surprise, shame, or rush people who have reasons to flinch. That sentence would still need review. It would still not be a treatment claim.

It does not license a school to call itself trauma-informed because it hung a poster. Harris and Fallot were talking about redesign. Redesign costs money and changes who has power. Posters are cheaper.

It does not license this page to teach the five values as a script. If you are in danger, call local emergency services. If you want care, use a licensed clinician. If you run an agency, read the 2001 volume and then look at your actual desk, not at a webinar.
