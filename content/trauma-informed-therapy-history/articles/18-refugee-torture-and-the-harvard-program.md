**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Richard F. Mollica’s Harvard Program in Refugee Trauma grew from work that began in the late 1970s and 1980s with people who had fled Cambodia and other wars, and from a clinic habit of taking torture, starvation, and forced migration as medical events. *Healing Invisible Wounds: Paths to Hope and Recovery in a Violent World* (2006) is the book a general reader can hold. The program is the institution: training, screening instruments (including the Harvard Trauma Questionnaire), and a claim that refugee care is not a luxury psychotherapy for the documented few.

This essay is about that institutional claim. It is also about the quarrel that arrived with it. Derek Summerfield’s 2001 *BMJ* piece argued that PTSD had become a humanitarian export — a Western category that could medicalize political suffering and fund a certain kind of NGO. Allan Young had already made the category look assembled. Mollica’s clinic is one of the places those arguments have to walk through, because the people in the waiting room were not a thought experiment.

## A questionnaire and a kitchen

The Harvard Trauma Questionnaire (HTQ) and related instruments tried to make the unspeakable countable: a list of events, a list of symptoms, translations that had to be argued over. Counting is how programs get funded and how they notice they are missing someone. Counting is also how a life becomes a score. Mollica’s later writing is full of stories about food, work, and the ordinary dignities that a symptom list does not capture. This page will not retell those stories as if they were ours. It will say that the program’s more interesting historical tension is internal: the same shop that built a scale also kept saying the scale is not the person.

Torture treatment centers — the IRCT network and national programs — sit in the same decades. They produced a literature on medico-legal documentation (the Istanbul Protocol is the later public name) that is not psychotherapy and not trauma-informed care in the Harris–Fallot sense. It is evidence for a court or a commission. Do not file it under SMA 14-4884. Do not file SMA 14-4884 under a torture affidavit.

## Humanitarian psychiatry’s useful trouble

Summerfield was not writing against the existence of Cambodian suffering. He was writing against a style of aid that arrives with a PTSD module and leaves the land reform, the asylum interview, and the racist landlord to someone else. A staged history can grant him that trouble without granting him a monopoly on the waiting room. People who have been tortured sleep badly. Some of them want a clinician. Some of them want a job and a passport. A decent program notices the order of those wants. An indecent program bills the first and ignores the second.

Fanon and Martín-Baró, again, are the older shadows: hospitals next to wars, psychology that either faces the war or becomes decoration. Mollica’s Harvard address is not Blida and not the UCA. The rhyme is the refusal to let “symptom” eat “history.”

## What this means for a systems pack

Refugee care forced trauma-informed talk to include interpreters, asylum clocks, detention, and the risk that retelling for a lawyer is a second injury. Those are Harris–Fallot problems with worse furniture. SAMHSA’s pamphlet can host them. It cannot substitute for an immigration attorney.

WOW Therapies is a speech-language practice in Southeast Arkansas. Interpreters, accent, and swallow after neurological injury are real clinic matters. A refugee torture history is not a reason to invent a program the clinic does not run. If a later editor wants one honest sentence on a service page, it would be about how to refer, not about how to treat.

## Sequence of rooms, and who owns the story

The 1980s refugee clinic sat at an awkward junction of specialties. Infectious-disease doctors wanted parasite panels. Orthopedists wanted films of old fractures. Psychiatrists wanted to know if this was PTSD or depression or both. Interpreters wanted to know whether the session would be recorded. Lawyers wanted a letter that a judge would accept as evidence of past persecution. The same person sat in all of those rooms. A trauma-informed reading of that clinic does not collapse the specialties. It asks whether the *sequence* of rooms re-creates the interrogation schedule the person already survived: wait, strip, answer, wait again.

Mollica’s later writing on the “trauma story” is easy to misread as a request for confession. The more careful reading is archival. The story is a document the person owns. The clinician is a scribe with a duty not to lose the file, not a priest with a duty to extract it. That distinction matters when a WOW clinician writes a letter for a family whose child is in speech therapy and whose parent is in an asylum proceeding. The letter can say what the clinician observed. It cannot say what the parent has not asked to have said.

UNHCR and IOM trainings in the 2000s and 2010s borrowed the SAMHSA four R’s without always citing them. Borrowing is not theft when the principles are public. It is a problem when the slide deck claims a local clinic “is trauma-informed” because the deck was shown once. A history of HPRT is a history of *practice under constraint*: too many languages, too few hours, too much paperwork. That is closer to a rural Arkansas Monday than a Boston grand-rounds fantasy.
