**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 1994 Jonathan Shay published *Achilles in Vietnam: Combat Trauma and the Undoing of Character*. He was a VA psychiatrist who had spent years listening to Vietnam combat veterans and reading Homer with them — not as decoration, as a working comparison. He argued that some of what wrecked these men was not only fear and memory. It was the betrayal of “what’s right” by commanders, by the war’s own rules, by a homecoming that wanted either a hero or a monster. He called the wreckage moral injury.

The phrase is older than the research program. Shay made it usable in English-language psychiatry. Brett T. Litz and colleagues, in a 2009 *Clinical Psychology Review* paper, tried to make it usable in a different way: a definition, a model, a research agenda for “moral repair.” Between 1994 and 2009 the word moved from a book with hexameters in it to a construct psychologists could put on a poster. That move is the history.

## Not another name for PTSD

Shay did not offer moral injury as a synonym for the 1980 criterion set. A person can meet PTSD criteria and also be morally injured. A person can be morally injured — ashamed, disgusted, unable to trust a chain of command or a God — without the classic reexperiencing cluster. The point of a second name is the second injury: a violation of a moral expectation, often by an authority, sometimes by oneself.

*Odysseus in America* (2002) took the homecoming half: the veteran as a dangerous guest, the family as a shoreline, the culture as a set of tests. This page will not retell Odysseus. It will say that Shay’s second book is why later chaplains and social workers latched on. Homecoming is where the VA’s form is least like a life.

Litz et al. 2009 operationalized: potential morally injurious events, including perpetration, witnessing, and betrayal; outcomes that include shame and withdrawal. Operationalizing invites measures. Measures invite trials. Trials will, if the funding holds, invite manuals. This series will not get ahead of those manuals. It will not teach a “moral repair” hour.

## Leadership, not only the inner world

Shay is stubborn about command. Achilles breaks, in his reading, when Agamemnon steals honor in public. Vietnam, in the veterans’ accounts he trusted, broke men when officers wasted lives, when rules flipped, when the official story insulted the people who had been there. That is a political claim wearing a clinical coat. Trauma-informed *systems* talk that never mentions leadership betrayal has read SAMHSA and skipped Shay.

Institutional betrayal, in Freyd and Smith’s later civilian sense, rhymes. It is not the same construct. A university that mishandles an assault report is not a platoon. The rhyme is the building: the injury includes what the institution did after, or instead.

## Drift

By the 2010s “moral injury” had drifted into journalism, then into civilian workplaces, then into any situation where a person felt compromised. Drift is how useful phrases die. A nurse under a cruel staffing ratio may be morally injured in a serious sense. A customer-service worker who uses the phrase because a shift was annoying is doing something else. A history can name the drift without policing every nurse.

Summerfield’s skepticism about exported PTSD should make a reader cautious here too. A new name can organize care. It can also become a way to talk about war without talking about the war. Shay, to his credit, talks about the war.

## Limits

This page will not diagnose your conscience. It will not assign you Homer. It will not tell the VA what to rate. If you are in danger, call local emergency services. If you are a veteran in the United States, the VA and accredited veteran-service organizations are the actual doors — not a 1994 table of contents.

WOW Therapies does not treat moral injury. A speech-language practice may meet an adult whose communication changed after neurological injury, including veterans. That is still speech. Do not file it under Shay.

Shay is living as of last verification (2026-09-14). Public books only. The figure essay stays with the man. This era piece stays with 1994 and 2009 as the years the name became, first, literary-clinical, then researchable.

## From hexameters to a 2009 model

Shay 1994 made moral injury usable: a leadership betrayal of “what’s right,” a wreckage of character that the 1980 criterion set did not exhaust. Shay 2002 took homecoming — the veteran as a dangerous guest, the culture as a set of tests. Litz et al. 2009 operationalized: potential morally injurious events (perpetration, witnessing, betrayal) and outcomes that include shame and withdrawal. Operationalizing invites measures. Measures invite trials. This series will not get ahead of those manuals.

The construct is not PTSD with better public relations. A person can have both. A person can have the shame without the classic cluster. The point of a second name is the second injury. Shay’s stubbornness about command is why a systems pack keeps him. Trauma-informed talk that never mentions leadership betrayal has read a pamphlet and skipped the VA office.

Freyd and Smith’s institutional betrayal rhymes from the civilian side. A university that mishandles a report is not a platoon. The rhyme is the building. Drift in the 2010s took “moral injury” into any compromised shift. A nurse under a cruel staffing ratio may be using the word seriously. A bored office that uses it for a long Tuesday is doing something else. Summerfield’s caution about names that talk about war without talking about the war applies here too. Shay, to his credit, talks about the war.

## Consensus ages; chaplains were already in the room

The 2009 Litz paper is a consensus document, not a commandment. Consensus documents age. Later writers split moral injury into perpetration-based and betrayal-based forms, or into religious and secular forms, or into injury that looks like PTSD and injury that does not. A 2026 reader should treat those splits as *research programs*, not as settled anatomy. The clinical danger is using the newest split as a reason to stop listening to the oldest sentence: “I cannot live with what I did” or “I cannot live with what they did to us.”

Chaplains remain under-cited in the scientific literature and over-present in the actual room. A 2010s VA chapel hour might hold more moral-injury talk than a PTSD clinic that week. That is not an argument against clinics. It is an argument against histories that only count what PubMed counts. WOW is not a chapel. WOW is also not forbidden from noticing when a veteran’s voice work stalls on a word that is also a vow.

If a future DSM or ICD adds a moral-injury code, the code will be a filing tool. It will not retire Homer, and it will not retire the 1994 book that made Homer a VA document. Until then, the honest sentence is the one this essay already used: adjacent construct, growing literature, not a substitute for PTSD and not a license to treat from a blog.
