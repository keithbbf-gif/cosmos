**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Pierre Janet was born in Paris in 1859 and died there in 1947, having outlived Charcot’s Tuesday theater, Freud’s century of publicity, and two wars that renamed the injuries he had already described in civilian wards. *L’Automatisme psychologique* (1889) made him, for a time, the person you cited if you wanted to talk about ideas that acted without the person’s consent. Henri Ellenberger’s 1970 book is still the English door that refuses to let the psychoanalytic century erase him.

The era essay in this pack stays with the 1889 claim. This figure stays with the life that claim had to survive.

## Le Havre, then the Salpêtrière, then the Collège

Before Paris finished making him famous, Janet had already been watching patients at Le Havre. The provincial years matter. He was not only a student of Charcot’s displays. He was a philosopher who had decided that consciousness could be studied in pieces. The Salpêtrière gave him cases and a public. Charcot’s death in 1893 gave him a colder city. He taught at the Collège de France. He kept writing after *hysteria* had become an embarrassment.

Freud, a few years younger, took more of the English-language shelf. The counseling-heritage pack tells that shelf as part of a broader therapy history. Here the narrower justice is Ellenberger’s: Janet had a psychology of the split-off idea that did not require the later Freudian machinery. American training sequences forgot him anyway. Forgetting is not a refutation.

He used hypnosis. He took long histories. He believed in a kind of psychological energy later readers either translate or skip. This page will not translate him into information-processing CBT. It will not run a hypnotic method. The man is not a protocol.

## Rediscovery and the mascot problem

Late-twentieth-century trauma psychiatry found him useful. Onno van der Hart and others built ancestor-claims. Mascot-Janet appears on slides as if he had already believed in DSM-5’s dissociative subtype. He died in 1947. He believed in *désagrégation* and *idées fixes*. A staged history can welcome the rediscovery and still ask for a page number.

The figure’s usefulness to a *systems* pack is modest and real. Harris and Fallot asked staff to recognize that a person may not be able to tell, in order, what a sign means. That ask does not need Janet. It rhymes with him. Rhyme is not lineage. Lineage talk is how trainings get longer.

## 1907 Harvard lectures are a third door

*The Major Symptoms of Hysteria* (1907) is Janet in English, on a Harvard stage, explaining to an American medical audience what his clinic had been naming in French. It is not *L’Automatisme* (1889) and not the 1901 translation of *L’État mental des hystériques*. Three doors. Students who only meet him as a 1990s “parts” mascot have opened none of them.

He used hypnosis. That is a historical fact and not an invitation. Ellenberger 1970 remains the English refusal of the erasure. Charcot died in 1893; Janet kept the Collège de France years. American sequences forgot him anyway. Rediscovery without a page number is how slides get made. A mute child in a speech chair has many explanations. This figure will not choose the Salpêtrière’s.
