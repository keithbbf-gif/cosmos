**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

By the early 1990s American clinics, talk shows, and courtrooms were in a fight that later writers called the memory wars. One file said: adults were recovering, in therapy, memories of childhood sexual abuse they had not been able to tell, and the science of repression explained the gap. The other file said: some of those memories were being *made* in rooms that used hypnosis, leading questions, and a plot the therapist already believed, and the science of suggestion explained the gap. Families broke. Boards investigated. A few therapists lost licenses. A few perpetrators, almost certainly, used the chaos as weather.

This essay will not pick a president of the war. It will not teach a memory-recovery method. It will not teach you to dismiss a delayed report. The historical job is to keep both files on the table, because trauma-informed care, if it means anything in a real building, has to survive this decade without becoming either a blank-check belief machine or a machine that calls every late disclosure a contagion.

## Two research programs, one social disaster

Elizabeth Loftus had already spent years showing that memory is reconstructive — that people can be led, that details move, that confidence is not a photograph. *The Myth of Repressed Memory* (1994), with Katherine Ketcham, took that program into the recovered-memory clinic and the courtroom. Richard McNally’s *Remembering Trauma* (2003) later gave the skeptical side a denser academic book: trauma is often remembered all too well; the laboratory does not kindly support a special repressed-memory mechanism of the kind some 1980s therapists assumed.

Jennifer J. Freyd’s *Betrayal Trauma* (Harvard, 1996), developed from talks in the early 1990s and a 1994 *Ethics & Behavior* paper, offered a different mechanism. If the perpetrator is the person you must still depend on, not-knowing can be an adaptation, not a parlor trick. Freyd was not writing a TV script. She was writing a theory of information blockage in a necessary relationship. She is living as of last verification (2026-09-14). This essay uses the books and papers. It does not use family gossip, including gossip that attached itself to her name in public.

Those programs did not have to be mortal enemies in a careful science. They became enemies in a culture that wanted a verdict: either the clinics were rescuing a hidden nation of survivors, or they were manufacturing one. Both pictures flattered someone. Neither picture is the whole 1990s file.

## What a history can say without a protocol

Some methods used in some rooms in that decade harmed people. This series will not reprint them. “Guided” recovery, certain uses of sodium amobarbital, and the hunt for satanic liturgies belong in a refuse pile, not in a how-to. Official inquiries and civil suits documented enough of that harm that a responsible history cannot call it a myth invented by perpetrators.

Sexual violence against children is also not a myth. Ferenczi said so in 1932. Burgess and Holmstrom wrote a civilian emergency-ward paper in 1974. Herman’s 1981 incest book and 1992 *Trauma and Recovery* forced the crime into official American psychiatry. Pediatric clinicians and child-protection statutes exist because the crime exists. A delayed report can be true. A confident, detailed report can be false. Those two sentences are allowed to sit next to each other. A website that cannot bear both will injure someone.

The false-memory side sometimes talked as if children were not being abused in ordinary houses. The recovered-memory side sometimes talked as if a therapist’s hunch were evidence. Trauma-informed *systems* later tried to write policies that do not require a clinician to be a detective or a denier. They do not always succeed. Child-welfare agencies, in particular, live in this vise every week.

## Institutions, not just memories

Freyd and Carly Parnitzke Smith’s later work on *institutional betrayal* (the 2013 *Journal of Traumatic Stress* paper is the usual door) moved the argument out of the individual skull and into the building: a school, a church, a university, a military unit that fails people who report. That move is why this era essay sits in a systems pack. The memory wars were about what happened in a house. They were also about what a clinic, a campus, and a court were willing to do with a story.

SAMHSA’s 2014 pamphlet does not adjudicate repression. It asks organizations to realize, recognize, respond, and resist re-traumatization. A building can do those verbs badly in either direction: by forcing a narrative, or by making reporting so costly that silence is the only safe speech. The 1990s are the scar that should keep both failures visible.

## Limits

This page will not help you recover a memory. It will not help you disprove one. It will not diagnose you or your parent. If you are in danger, call local emergency services. If a child is in danger, use the reporting path your state requires.

WOW Therapies is a speech-language practice. An SLP who takes a history from a child is not thereby a memory therapist. The useful caution is ordinary clinical manners: do not suggest a plot, do not treat a speech error as a coded disclosure, and do not advertise expertise you do not hold. That caution is not a trauma protocol.

McNally 2003 and Freyd 1996 will still be cited against each other in 2026. A staged essay does not have to end the citation war. It has to refuse the cartoon.
