**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 2007 *The Counseling Psychologist* printed Robert T. Carter, “Racism and Psychological and Emotional Injury: Recognizing and Assessing Race-Based Traumatic Stress.” The paper is long, careful, and written for a journal that already knew multicultural counseling as a training requirement. Carter wanted something more specific than “racism is stressful.” He wanted an injury that could be assessed — a race-based traumatic stress reaction — without pretending that every slight is PTSD and without pretending that racism is only a political opinion.

This essay treats that paper as a research-object event. It will not turn racial trauma into a hashtag history. It will not apply Carter’s assessment logic to a reader. It will not use WOW Therapies’ Arkansas address as proof of anyone’s virtue.

## A 2007 argument with older shadows

Frantz Fanon had already written, from a colonial hospital, that the violence of a racial order lives in bodies and in wards. The counseling-heritage pack has a Fanon figure. Ignacio Martín-Baró wrote from El Salvador that psychology which ignores the social war is a luxury. Those books are not Carter 2007. They are why a later American counseling psychologist could insist the injury is not new, only newly *assessable* in his discipline’s terms.

The DSM’s PTSD, especially in its early “outside the usual” wording, was a bad fit for chronic racial violence. Usual, for whom? Carter’s move was to stay inside counseling psychology’s assessment tradition rather than wait for Arlington to rewrite Criterion A. That is a professional strategy. It has limits. Assessment instruments can make a phenomenon discussable. They can also turn a social fact into a score that a clinic files and a city ignores.

Mamie Phipps Clark’s doll work and the Clarks’ later Northside Center belong to the other pack’s figure essay. They are a mid-century reminder that American psychology has, at times, taken racial injury into the courtroom and the clinic. Carter is a later, different instrument.

## What the object is not

It is not a synonym for historical trauma. Brave Heart’s Lakota papers are about a people’s cumulative losses. Carter’s 2007 paper is about assessing the psychological injury of racism in individuals, including — not only — people whose family history is not a named genocide. Collapsing the two is how a training slide gets shorter and worse.

It is not a reason to tell a Black child in a speech clinic that his articulation error is racial trauma. WOW Therapies evaluates speech and language. Racism can shape who gets referred, who is believed, and whose dialect is treated as a defect. That is a history the SLPWOW pack and this pack can both notice. It is still not a diagnosis of the child.

It is not SAMHSA’s sixth principle wearing a new shirt. The 2014 pamphlet’s “cultural, historical, and gender issues” can host this literature or it can hide behind it. Hosting would mean reading Carter, reading the later reviews, and changing who has power in the building. Hiding would mean a bullet on a grant.

## After 2007

A literature grew: race-based traumatic stress, racial trauma, sometimes “racial battle fatigue” from a different scholarly door. The terms are not identical. This essay will not police them into one. It will say that by the 2010s American counseling and some clinical programs had a citeable object, and that 2020 made the object public in a way journals do not control. Public weather is not a validation study. It is weather.

Critics, including some from the Summerfield side of trauma skepticism, will ask whether expanding “trauma” to chronic social injury empties the word. That question is allowed. So is the reply: the word was never as narrow as a 1980 soldier, and the soldier’s chart was already a political achievement. Keep both sentences. Do not let either become a sneer.

## Assessment as a professional strategy, and its limit

Carter 2007 wanted an injury that counseling psychology could assess without pretending every slight is PTSD and without pretending racism is only an opinion. That is a professional strategy. Instruments make a phenomenon discussable. They can also turn a social fact into a score a clinic files and a city ignores.

## Ninety-three pages in *The Counseling Psychologist*

Carter’s 2007 article is long on purpose. *The Counseling Psychologist* gave race-based traumatic stress a monograph’s room inside a journal: assessment without pretending every slight is PTSD, and without pretending racism is only a feeling. A counseling program that adds the citation to a multicultural week and changes no intake power has hosted the paper and hidden it.

Southeast Arkansas has its own racial history. This national research-object essay will not write that history as décor. Hosting, if a later local piece is commissioned, would mean who gets believed at intake and who has to bring a witness. Until then, keep Carter as 2007, a journal, and a refused collapse into the ACE mural.
