**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 1980 the American Psychiatric Association’s *Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders*, third edition, included post-traumatic stress disorder. The criteria required an event “outside the range of usual human experience,” then a cluster of reexperiencing, numbing, and arousal. The sentence about “usual human experience” later collapsed, because usual human experience, for many people, includes violence. The acronym did not collapse. It became the way English-speaking institutions filed a certain kind of injury.

This is not a story about a committee that finally saw the truth. It is a story about a veterans’ movement that would not accept “personality” as the explanation, researchers who had been writing through the 1970s, and a manual that was already busy converting American psychiatry from psychoanalytic prose to checkable criteria. PTSD rode that conversion. It also needed the conversion. A pension office can do more with a criterion set than with a physioneurosis described in 1941.

## Politics plus numbers plus a book

Mardi Horowitz’s *Stress Response Syndromes* (1976) gave the academy a language of intrusion and denial that did not belong only to combat. Robert Jay Lifton’s *Home from the War* (1973) gave the public a literary-clinical picture of Vietnam veterans who were neither saints nor villains. Chaim Shatan and others had already been talking about “post-Vietnam syndrome” in rooms the manual did not yet enter. Charles Figley’s edited volumes, including *Trauma and Its Wake* (1985), and the Society for Traumatic Stress Studies he helped found on 2 March 1985, turned a scatter of papers into a field with a journal (*Journal of Traumatic Stress*, first issue January 1988).

Allan Young’s *The Harmony of Illusions* (1995) will not let this paragraph become a victory lap. Young sat in a VA unit and watched a diagnosis being *made* — taught, practiced, believed. He did not say the men were faking. He said the category is a cultural and institutional achievement. A staged history can hold that sentence without sneering at the achievement. Forms are how modern medicine remembers. Forms are also how it excludes.

DSM-IV (1994) and DSM-5 (2013) rewrote Criterion A and the clusters. Those rewrites are professional history. They are not a reason to diagnose yourself from a table of contents. ICD-11’s later split of PTSD from complex PTSD is a different official admission, treated in its own essay in this pack.

## What the form changed

Before 1980 a veteran might be depressed, alcoholic, “personality,” or unlucky. After 1980 he might be PTSD, with a code, a rating schedule, and a clinic that knew what to print on the chart. That is not a small change. It is also not a cure. A name can organize care and still leave a person watchful.

The form changed research. You cannot easily randomize a treatment for a syndrome that does not officially exist. Foa’s 1991 rape-survivor trial, Resick’s CPT manuals, Shapiro’s 1989 EMD papers — they inherit a billable, researchable object. This pack will name those arrivals without turning them into homework.

The form changed who counted. Combat was the injury a government had to count. Burgess and Holmstrom had already counted a civilian injury in 1974. Herman would force incest and domestic captivity onto the same table in 1992. The DSM’s first PTSD was still shaped like a soldier. A counselor in a small American city still meets people who are not soldiers and inherits a chart that remembers 1980 more clearly than 1974.

## Forgetting, again, in a new key

The shell-shock essay described decommissioning a category after 1918. 1980 is the reverse trick: *commissioning* a category so hard that later critics (Summerfield 2001; parts of Young 1995) would ask whether the West had begun to medicalize ordinary political suffering. Both tricks can be true in different rooms. A veteran denied a name for a decade is not the same person as a humanitarian catalog that prints PTSD on every refugee. The critiques essay in this pack stays with that tension. Here the dated claim is simpler. In 1980 the official American chart acquired a trauma diagnosis. Institutions that needed it used it. Institutions that needed it not to apply to women, children, or colonized people were slower.

## Limits

This page will not fill out a VA claim. It will not tell you your DSM number. It will not compare treatments. If you are in danger, call local emergency services.

WOW Therapies does not specialize in PTSD. A history series on a speech-language site exists so the phrase on a poster has a past, not so a parent books an evaluation for the wrong injury. Adult cognition after stroke remains a service-page matter. Combat trauma does not become speech therapy by sharing a word.

Figley’s 1985 breakfast meeting in Washington and the Atlanta conference later that year are the professional afterparty of the 1980 criterion set. Fields have conferences. They also have gatekeeping. Herman’s complex trauma sat at that gate for decades. The ISTSS essay-of-record in this pack is the Figley figure and the NCTSN institution piece, not a cheer.
