**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 1998 the *American Journal of Preventive Medicine* printed Vincent J. Felitti, Robert F. Anda, and colleagues, “Relationship of Childhood Abuse and Household Dysfunction to Many of the Leading Causes of Death in Adults: The Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACE) Study.” The data came from more than 17,000 adults in the Kaiser Permanente San Diego program who had completed a questionnaire about ten categories of childhood adversity and whose medical records could be joined to the answers. The paper reported a *graded* relationship: more categories endorsed, more later risk for a list of adult diseases and health behaviors.

That sentence is the artifact. The industry that followed — school quizzes, poster scores, “ACE-informed” everything — is a different artifact. This essay keeps them apart.

## What the study was

Felitti had noticed, in an obesity clinic, that a number of patients who dropped out of weight loss had childhood sexual abuse in their histories. That clinical irritation became, with Anda at the CDC, a large collaboration. The ten categories mixed abuse, neglect, and household dysfunction (a parent treated violently, substance use in the home, mental illness in the home, separation, incarceration). The sample was not “the United States.” It was insured adults in one HMO who were willing to mail a form. Middle-class enough. San Diego enough. Still large, still useful, still limited.

The authors did not say that an ACE score of 4 is a diagnosis. They said that childhood adversity, counted in this coarse way, tracks adult health in a dose-looking curve. Epidemiology loves a curve. Clinics love a number. The love affair is the later problem.

## What the study was not

It was not a census of trauma. A child can be terrorized by an event that is not on the list. A child can endorse several items and be, in adulthood, reasonably well. The categories collapse very different crimes and very different homes. “Household mental illness” is not the same object as rape. The score adds them anyway. Addition is a public-health convenience. It is a clinical crudity.

It was not a mechanism paper. Later writers reached for cortisol, epigenetics, and “toxic stress” posters. Some of that later work is careful. Some of it is a TED slide. This series will not launder a 1998 questionnaire into a brain lecture. If you want neurobiology, you need a different study and a different level of caution than a waiting-room handout.

It was not permission to quiz every child in a speech clinic and write the number on the chart as if it explained a lisp. WOW Therapies is a speech-language practice. An ACE form in that waiting room would be a claims problem and a manners problem. The 1998 paper is a reason public health started talking about childhood adversity as a *population* exposure. It is not a pediatric intake protocol, and this page will not write one.

## How a paper became a sticker

By the 2010s cities were “ACE-aware.” Trainings sold the ten items as a personality forecast. Journalists wrote as if the score predicted your future the way a cholesterol number predicts a bill from a cardiologist. Felitti himself, in later talks, sometimes sounded more missionary than the 1998 tables. Living people get to give talks. This essay uses the paper.

SAMHSA’s 2014 concept paper sits downstream. The federal pamphlet needed a way to say trauma is common. ACE gave it a citation that health departments already knew. Harris and Fallot (2001) did not need ACE to redesign a service system; they had a clinic full of people with trauma histories. The later merger — ACE numbers plus trauma-informed posters — made grant language easy. Easy grant language is not automatically dishonest. It is automatically thin.

Critics have said, fairly, that ACE talk can become another way to stare at poor families, or another way to ignore present housing, racism, and wages while counting a parent’s old arrest. The 1998 sample, ironically, was not a poor sample. The afterlife often is. A history that mentions ACE without mentioning that drift is doing public relations.

## Limits

This page will not score you. It will not ask you the ten items. It will not tell you your children are doomed. If you are in danger, call local emergency services. If you want medical care, use a clinician who treats the condition you actually have — diabetes, depression, a swallow problem — not a number from a magazine.

The figure essay on Felitti and Anda stays with the collaboration and the obesity-clinic origin story. This era piece stays with the 1998 object: a graded association, a Kaiser sample, a later sticker industry. Trauma-informed care that treats ACE as destiny has not read the paper. Trauma-informed care that pretends childhood adversity is rare has not read it either.

## A Kaiser envelope, not a destiny machine

More than 17,000 insured adults in San Diego mailed a questionnaire about ten coarse categories and allowed those answers to be joined to medical records. The paper reported a graded curve: more categories, more later risk for a list of adult diseases and health behaviors. Epidemiology loves a curve. Clinics love a number. The later love affair is the problem.

The sample is not “America.” It is not a foster-care census. It is not a Lakota grief theory. The categories collapse very different homes. Addition is a public-health convenience and a clinical crudity. The authors did not say that a score of 4 is a diagnosis. Later cities said it for them.

Felitti’s obesity-clinic irritation — patients leaving, childhood sexual abuse arriving in the history — is the origin story he has told in talks. Anda’s CDC address is why the paper could speak the language of leading causes of death. Later talks sometimes sounded more missionary than the 1998 tables. The 1998 sample was not a poor sample. The afterlife often is. A speech clinic that quizzes the ten items to explain a lisp is making a claims mistake and a manners mistake. ## After Kaiser: state modules, not a second 1998

Later Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System modules asked ACE-like questions in state samples that were not the original Kaiser HMO. Those modules are a public-health afterlife. They are not a second Felitti–Anda paper and they are not a reason to say “the ACE study proves our county.” A state prevalence estimate is a different object with a different sampling story. Keep 1998 as the mailed San Diego envelope and the graded tables in the *American Journal of Preventive Medicine*.

The later “ACE quiz” on a parenting blog is a third afterlife, coarser than the modules and far coarser than the paper. This pack will not score a reader. It will say that a ten-category screen was built for research association, not for a speech-language intake. If a school forwards a score with a referral, write the school’s words and the child’s speech goals. Do not write destiny in the chart.
