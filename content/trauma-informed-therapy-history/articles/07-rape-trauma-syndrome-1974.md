**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 1974 the *American Journal of Psychiatry* printed Ann Wolbert Burgess and Lynda Lytle Holmstrom, “Rape Trauma Syndrome.” The authors were not writing from a VA. They were writing from Boston, from an emergency-ward collaboration between a psychiatric nurse and a sociologist, after sitting with women who had just been raped and then sitting with them again. They described an acute phase and a longer reorganization. They gave civilian medicine a name that did not require a uniform.

The counseling-heritage trauma-lineages essay already flags this paper as the cousin textbooks under-cite. This piece stays with the *ward*. Trauma-informed care, when it later talked about not adding a second injury in a hospital, was talking — whether it knew the citation or not — about rooms like this one: bright lights, questions, a kit, a police officer, a body that has just been treated as an object.

## A syndrome from interviews, not from a trench

Burgess and Holmstrom interviewed ninety-two women and eight extra cases in a 1972–1973 Boston series, then followed them. The numbers matter because the paper is often remembered as an essay and was, in fact, a study. They organized what they heard into a syndrome: immediate shock or a controlled facade; nightmares; fear; later work of reorganizing life, sometimes with a move, a changed phone number, a changed route home. They were careful, more careful than some later popularizers, to say that women differed.

They were also writing into a legal weather. “Rape trauma syndrome” would be asked to do courtroom work: to explain why a victim delayed, why she seemed calm, why she knew the man. Expert testimony of that kind has a contested afterlife. This essay will not retry those cases. It will say that a clinical name, once useful to a prosecutor or a defense, inherits the old railway problem. Visibility, credibility, compensation — here the “compensation” is sometimes belief.

Herman’s 1992 book could point to 1974 when someone said trauma meant combat. Courtois’s *Healing the Incest Wound* (1988) belongs to the same civilian opening, aimed at a crime that still preferred the consulting room’s older words. The feminist clinic culture in Boston — including work later associated with the Women’s Mental Health Collective — is the neighborhood, not the footnote.

## What the ward taught the later pamphlets

SAMHSA’s 2014 four R’s include *resist re-traumatization*. The 1974 paper is an ancestor of that verb even if the pamphlet does not open with Burgess. A pelvic exam after a rape can be done as care or as a second assault. A swallow exam, a restraint, a seclusion room, a school search — later systems borrowed the moral from a ward they had not staffed. Borrowing can be honest. It can also be cheap: print the verb, change nothing about the kit.

Harris and Fallot’s 2001 values — safety, trustworthiness, choice, collaboration, empowerment — read differently if you imagine them in an emergency department at 3 a.m. Choice is limited. Collaboration with a police officer is not the same as collaboration with a therapist. The 1974 authors were already living those limits. They did not wait for a federal list.

## What this page will not do

It will not describe a rape. It will not give a survivor a checklist that doubles as a verdict on her calm or her delay. It will not teach a “grounding skill.” If you are in danger, call local emergency services. If you need a rape-crisis line, use the one your city actually funds. This history page is not that line.

It will not claim that Burgess and Holmstrom invented trauma. Janet had patients. Myers had soldiers. Kardiner had veterans. What they invented, with a sociologist’s ear and a nurse’s hours, was an *American journal name* for a civilian injury the combat literature had no reason to center.

It will not turn WOW Therapies into a forensic nursing program. A speech-language practice may, in the ordinary course of pediatric or adult work, meet a person whose communication changed after violence. That meeting is a reason for manners and for referral, not a reason to advertise a specialty the clinic does not hold.

## Afterlives and thinning

By the 1980s “rape trauma syndrome” had thinned into a phrase lawyers and journalists could use without reading the follow-up. Thinning is the usual fate of useful names. The original paper’s virtue is the follow-up: they went back. A trauma-informed *system* that never goes back — that trains once, prints a poster, and calls the building finished — has learned the phrase and missed the method of the phrase.

DSM-III’s 1980 PTSD criteria would absorb some of this civilian work without always citing the ward. That absorption is a success and a slight. Official language is slow and greedy. It takes what it needs for a criterion set. It does not owe a nurse a monument. This pack can owe her a dated essay.
