**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Mardi J. Horowitz was born in 1934 and is living as of last verification (2026-09-14). This essay uses the public book. No health talk.

*Stress Response Syndromes* (Jason Aronson, 1976) is the object. Horowitz described phases of response to serious life events — intrusion and denial, oscillating, a person flooded then numb — in a vocabulary the academy could cite without putting on a uniform. Four years later DSM-III printed PTSD. The manual did not photocopy the book. The book is one of the reasons a committee had a civilian-academic language ready when a veterans’ movement demanded a name.

## A clinic and a lab, not a trench

Horowitz worked at the University of California, San Francisco, in a tradition that wanted both psychotherapy observation and something that looked like a model. Information-processing talk, states of mind, a willingness to study bereavement next to disaster — that mix is why later CBT-adjacent trauma therapies could claim a cousin without claiming a trench. Foa and Resick are later, different objects. Do not make Horowitz their father. Do not use that banned kinship word at all. He is a 1976 monograph.

The era essay on DSM-III already named him as the civilian-academic cousin. This figure stays with the habit of mind: you can describe a syndrome after a fire, a death, a crash, a rape, and a war without pretending those events are the same. The later ACE industry sometimes lost that habit. The later “everything is trauma” slide lost it on purpose. Horowitz’s book, read cold, is more precise than the slide.

## What the 1976 book is not

It is not a consumer workbook. It is not SAMHSA. It is not a reason to diagnose yourself from a phase name. Phases are models. Models leak. Leakage is how “denial” became a kitchen insult. The insult is not in the monograph.

It is not Kardiner 1941. Kardiner had a VA physioneurosis. Horowitz had a university clinic and a broader event class. Both are pieces Young 1995 would call assembled. Both are still worth reading as dated objects.

## Afterlife

Figley’s 1985 volumes and the new society needed academic furniture. Horowitz was furniture of the better kind: a book you could put on a syllabus. ISTSS’s later journals would fill with trials he did not run. That is how fields work. The inventor of a language does not own the trials.

WOW Therapies will not advertise “stress-response therapy.” A speech-language practice that meets a recently bereaved adult whose voice has gone is doing speech and manners, not Horowitz.

## Limits

This page will not walk you through intrusion and denial as a homework chart. If you are in danger, call local emergency services. If you want care after a terrible event, use a licensed clinician.

## Grief arguments did not retire 1976

Horowitz’s later work on grief and relational configurations expanded the 1976 book without retiring it. Grief research in the 1990s and 2000s argued about whether “complicated grief” was a disorder or a severe normal. Horowitz had already given the field a way to talk about oscillation without waiting for that argument to end. The argument still has not ended. The oscillation description still works in a progress note that never uses the word grief.

UCSF as an institution matters. West-coast academic psychiatry in the 1970s was not the same animal as East-coast psychoanalytic institutes. Horowitz could publish a book that clinicians in community clinics actually bought. That market fact is part of the history. Ideas that stay in institutes do not become SAMHSA bullet points thirty years later. Ideas that travel in paperback might.

A 2026 student should read one chapter of *Stress Response Syndromes* before reading a tweet about triggers. The chapter is slower. It is also the reason the tweet has a vocabulary.

Horowitz’s person-schemas language was never meant to be a self-help grid. It was meant to give clinicians a way to write oscillation without pretending every terrible event shares one plot. That modesty is what later “trauma-informed” slides often remove when they need a single bullet for a grant. The 1976 book is still the antidote to the slide, if you read it as history rather than as a manual.

Horowitz’s states-of-mind language gave clinicians a way to write intrusion and denial, approach and avoidance, without waiting for a later grief-disorder vote. Complicated-grief arguments in the 1990s and 2000s did not retire the 1976 oscillation description. A progress note can still use the description without the word grief. Living author: public book only.

ISTSS later filled its journals with trials Horowitz did not run. That is ordinary field manners: a language book and a trial literature are related, not identical. Read the 1976 monograph when you want the language; read the trials when you want the methods fight — and remember this pack refuses to teach the methods here.
