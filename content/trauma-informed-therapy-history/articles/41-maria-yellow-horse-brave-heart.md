**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Maria Yellow Horse Brave Heart is living as of last verification (2026-09-14). This essay uses the 1998 papers and the public HTUG (Historical Trauma and Unresolved Grief) career. No health talk. No private life. This page will not speak for Lakota people.

The era essay stays with 1998 as the year the citation became checkable and with the warning that SAMHSA’s sixth principle is a gesture, not a people. This figure stays with the social worker who, with Lemyra M. DeBruyn and earlier collaborators, put *historical trauma* and *historical unresolved grief* into journals other scholars could find.

## Terms developed earlier, printed in 1998

Brave Heart has described work beginning in the 1970s and terms developed in the late 1980s. A 1998 publication date is not an origin myth. It is what a staged history can footnote. “The Return to the Sacred Path” reported a psychoeducational group intervention. “The American Indian Holocaust,” with DeBruyn, placed Lakota losses — wars, reservation, Wounded Knee, boarding schools, ceremony outlawed or managed — in a grief frame that ordinary PTSD criteria do not exhaust.

DeBruyn’s name belongs on the second paper. This slug is Brave Heart because the later public career and the HTUG trainings are often filed under her. Do not disappear the collaborator.

This page will not list ceremony as a technique for outsiders. It will not enroll you in a group. The 1998 intervention paper is about education, sharing in a traditional context, and the beginning of grief work. Those sentences are description. They are not a protocol.

## Not ACE, not Carter, not Danieli

A Kaiser questionnaire is not a Lakota grief theory. Carter’s 2007 race-based traumatic stress paper is a different research object. Danieli’s 1998 comparative handbook is a third door. Brave Heart can be cited next to them. She cannot be replaced by them. Training slides that print “historical / racial / intergenerational” as one blob have already lost her.

Southeast Arkansas is not a Lakota reservation. WOW Therapies does not become historical-trauma-informed by printing a land acknowledgement it has not earned. A speech-language history series names her because the 2014 federal pamphlet gestured at “historical” issues and this pack refused to let the gesture stand alone.

## *Smith College Studies in Social Work*, 1998, and a second byline

The venue matters. *Smith College Studies in Social Work* is a social-work journal, not a psychiatric manual and not a public-health questionnaire. Brave Heart’s 1998 article on returning to a sacred path among the Lakota sits in that shop on purpose. A later slide that prints her next to an ACE score and calls the blob “historical” has changed venues without saying so.

The companion 1998 paper with Lemyra M. DeBruyn in *American Indian and Alaska Native Mental Health Research* is the communal citation. Dropping DeBruyn makes a people-sized concept look like a solo invention. Historical unresolved grief is a companion term in that pair, not a synonym for a bad decade in any family. The losses she named did not stop because a journal issued a volume.

Yael Danieli’s 1998 *International Handbook of Multigenerational Legacies of Trauma* is a parallel, plural volume from the same year — many peoples, many authors. Parallel is not a reason to file Brave Heart under Danieli. It is a reason not to pretend 1998 had only one door. WOW’s nearest honest use remains the refusal to analogize a child’s late talk to a nation’s grief. If a family names boarding-school history, write the words they used. Living author: public papers only. No ventriloquy.
