# Edges go first

The table looked fine in the field and tired on every arris.
The stain had found a rolled edge that was still 80 under a
220 field. Finishes fail on edges first because the film is
thin there and because the machine wrote a different grit
than the one you named for the top.

Hand the edges. Same last grit, lighter pressure. A rolled
edge that is coarser than the field will take stain like a
marker. A rolled edge that is finer and burnished will refuse
stain and look like a waxed rim.

## What the machine does

A ROS hanging off an edge is a rounding tool and a deeper
cutter on a crescent of disc. A belt is worse. A block that
hangs off is a hand-shaped version of the same lie.

I tape arrises I want to keep crisp while I do the field. I
pull the tape and hand the edge at the last grit. Or I ease
the arris on purpose, the same amount all around, because
the design wanted a soft break. Purpose is a line you can
draw. Accident is a line the stain draws for you.

## Consistency

One edge soft, one edge sharp, is a piece that was sanded
in the order you walked around the bench. Walk a plan, not
a path of least fatigue. I do all edges at the same stage,
same grit, same backup.

Corner intersections are three edges. They get too much
time because they are where you start and stop. They go
round first. Watch them.

## Film build

Even if the scratch matches, the film is thinner on an
arris. Waterborne and varnish pull away. Oil looks hungry
sooner. That is not only a sanding problem, but sanding
that thins the wood at the edge makes the film problem
worse. Do not "blend the edge into the field" with a fine
disc until the field is thin too.

## Chamfers and breaks

A drawn chamfer is an edge you meant. Sand it as a face
with a width, not as an arris you are "easing." A block
that fits the chamfer keeps the two arrises of the chamfer
alive. A ROS that kisses a chamfer turns it into a round
and then you have a different drawing.

A "break the edge so it doesn't cut" is two or three
strokes of the last grit, by hand, all around, the same
count. Count. If you freehand until it feels safe, one
side of the table will be safer than the other.

## Shop consequence

Inspect edges as their own surface. Raking light along the
arris. If the highlight is a fat sausage, you rounded. If
the stain is a dark line, you left coarse tooth or you cut
through a sealer later (draft 35).

I have a table in a house whose breadboard looks soapy
because I looked at a phone. I tape now. Draft 17 said
lift. This draft says the edge was the first thing the
lift was protecting.
