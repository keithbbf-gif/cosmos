# What to tell a client who wants buttery

They run a hand across the sample and say buttery. Buttery
is a hand word. It can mean a fine last grit, a built
film, a rubbed sheen, a rounded arris, or "please do not
leave 120 under oil." If you do not translate it, you
will sand to 400 on oak and still fail the handshake.

The sentence you owe them is not a yes. It is a
translation plus a consequence.

## What I ask

Do you want the *wood* to feel closed, or the *finish*?
A closed wood under stain is blotch on maple (draft 40).
A closed film is a build and a rub (draft 37). Those are
different clocks and different repairs when a mustard
bottle arrives.

Do you want the edge soft? Soft is a drawing. I can ease
an arris the same amount all around. I cannot ease it
with a ROS while we talk and call it design.

Do you want quiet color? Quiet color is even tooth, maybe
a washcoat, maybe a different species. It is not a finer
sleeve.

I make them touch two offcuts: 180 oil, 220 film, or
whatever pair is honest for the job. Hands lie less when
they have a choice.

## What I will not promise

A pine table that feels like maple. Two woods (draft 32).
A veneer that I will "just take a little further." A
flea-market paint I will sand in the driveway. A Friday
show top if the 120 box is empty and the inspection light
is a fluorescent.

Buttery forever on a dining table is a maintenance speech.
Hands, grit in the air, a wet glass. The finish pack owns
that speech. This pack owns the part where they think
sanding can pre-load forever.

## What I write down

Last grit. Binder. Edge treatment. Whether ends will go
darker. Whether we are popping grain. Whether the sheen
is from the can or from a rub. If it is not written, it
will become "I thought you meant."

I do not invent a brochure paragraph. I invent a sample
they take home and a sentence on the work order.

## When they are right

Sometimes buttery means: please do the last grain pass,
please hand the edges, please do not skip. That is this
whole pack. I say yes. I do not say yes to 400 on bare
oak because the word sounded expensive.

## Kids and mustard

A family table that will see homework and mustard wants
a film that can take a rub later, not a 400 bare maple
under oil that feels buttery on delivery day and stained
on Sunday. Say that. The sample they fall in love with
in the shop is a handshake in a clean room. The room at
home is the critic.

## Shop consequence

If you cannot translate buttery into a grit and a binder,
you are guessing with a dining table. Guessing is how
you recut a film you already cured.

I have delivered a top that was technically finer and
felt colder, because I chased buttery with paper instead
of with a film. They wanted a handshake. I gave them a
polish. The next time I brought two boards and shut up
until they picked one.
