# Wet-sanding a film is not wood

The internet will show you a slurry and a happy bucket.
On a cured film, that slurry is a leveling move — silicon
carbide, water or a compatible lubricant, a hard-enough
backup, a film that can take it. On bare furniture wood,
a slurry is a swollen panel and grit in the pores.

Do not borrow a car-finish habit onto a bare oak top and
call it refinement.

## Film, cured enough

Wet-sanding a varnish or poly that has actually cured is
how some shops level before they rub out. The water (or
the mineral-oil-adjacent lubricant some systems want)
floats the waste so the paper does not cake as fast. You
are cutting finish, not wood. If you cut through, you
are in draft 35's pale line, only wetter and worse.

The fence: use the lubricant the finish system allows.
Do not invent a solvent bath. Do not wet-sand a
waterborne that still fears water. Do a test. If the
film blushes, turns white, or lifts, you are not in a
wet-sand job.

## Bare wood

Water pops grain (draft 34) and drives particles into
open pores. You will spend the next coat fishing grit
out of oak. You will also swell a breadboard. I will
not wet-sand bare furniture wood as a habit. If a
specific tradition calls for it on a specific species,
it is a specialist recipe, not this pack's default.

## The slurry is waste

Wipe it up. It is ground finish and broken mineral. If
it dries on the surface, you have cemented a gray film
that the next coat may not like. Clean as the system
wants — often water and then a thorough dry, or a
specified solvent wipe, not a shop rag that has known
oil.

Slurry in a carve or a corner is how you get a pale
deposit forever. Brush, vacuum after it dries, look.

## Hands and slip

Wet paper slips off edges. Edges cut through. Wet
floors are a shop-safety ordinary: you fall, the piece
falls. This is not heroic. It is a puddle.

## Water near hide glue and veneer

A wet rub on a film over hide glue, or over a veneer
that already wanted to lift, is how you teach a panel
to bubble. If the piece is old and the glue is a
rumor, stay dry. A dry micron film on a cured finish
is slower and safer.

If water gets in a crack, it will swell the crack and
then you are sanding a proud lip tomorrow. Tape cracks.
Work small.

## Shop consequence

If you want a leveled film, wet-sand a film that is
ready, then rub (draft 37). If you want refined wood,
use dry grits and light. If a video used a bucket on
a tabletop, ask whether that tabletop was already a
coating.

I have wet-sanded a poly that was ready and been glad.
I have wet-sanded a top that was still wood and been
educated. The bucket does not know the difference. You
have to.
