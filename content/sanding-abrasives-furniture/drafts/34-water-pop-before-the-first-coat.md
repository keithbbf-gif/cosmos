# Water pop before the first coat

The first waterborne coat felt like a beard. I had skipped
the pop because the top already felt smooth. A thumb after
lunch (draft 06). The grain that wanted to raise raised in
the film, and then I had to sand a coating I had just
applied.

A damp cloth, dry, a light re-sand, then stain or finish.
The grain that wanted to raise in the first coat raises
now, when you can still cut it.

## What I am doing

I wet the surface evenly — not a puddle, not a decorative
mist that misses the edges. Let it dry. The raised fuzz is
the future first coat. I knock it with the last grit or
one step finer, lightly, with the grain. I am not starting
the sequence over. I am cutting whiskers.

If I cut through to a coarser scratch while I knock, I
have a new photograph. Light hands. A hard block on a
flat. Not a ROS that dishes while I "just knock the
raise."

## When I do it

Oak that will get a waterborne. Any piece that will live
in a wet-wiped kitchen. Dye stains that will raise on
their own. Softwoods that fuzz.

When I skip: an oil I already planed and the client likes
a little tooth; a carving I cannot get back into (draft
28) unless the finish forces me; a day when the shop will
not dry the board before I stain, because staining a wet
pop is a different, worse story.

## Water is not innocent

Water swells. A panel that was flat can show a joint. End
grain drinks and looks wild until it dries. MDF is not a
candidate (draft 29). Veneer can lift if the glue is
already a rumor. If the piece cannot take water, do not
pop it, and think twice about a waterborne.

Clean water. A dirty cloth is a stain. Wipe with the
grain. Do not flood a breadboard and starve the field.

## After the pop

Dust. The fuzz you cut is dust. Vacuum. If the next can
is fussy about contaminants, tack as that can wants.
Stearate leftovers (draft 13) plus a pop is a chemistry
you should test.

## Shop consequence

If the first coat raises a beard, you owe yourself a pop
on the next piece, not a more aggressive between-coat
sand that cuts through edges. Between coats is draft 35.
This draft is the beard you can still cut on bare wood.

I do this on oak that will get waterborne, and I skip it
when I am oiling a surface I already planed and the
customer likes a little tooth. I do not skip it and then
complain about the first coat feeling like a beard.
