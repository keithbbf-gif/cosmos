# Heat is a burn, not a polish

The maple went glassy and I liked it for a minute. It felt
finished. It was cooked. Friction closes the surface. Stain
blotched. Oil looked like a coating that could not find a
way in. Heat is a burn, not a polish.

A dull disc plus pressure is how you cook a top and call
it smooth.

## Where heat comes from

Lingering. Pressing. A dead pad. A glazed belt. High speed
on a ROS that is jumping. Closed coat on pine. Ceramic
belts doing their job too long (draft 12). A scraper you
are forcing. A disc that has become a burnisher and still
says 180 (draft 10).

The machine sounds the same. The surface gets slick. Your
hand likes it (draft 06). The light, later, shows a shine
that is not finish.

## Species

Maple tells first. Cherry slicks and can darken in a way
that looks like a burn. Pine weeps resin and then resists
finish. Beech and birch have opinions. Oak will burnish
the latewood and still drink in the pores — blotch with
a rustic excuse.

If you smell toast, you are late.

## What to do

Lift. Change the disc. Slow down. Let the board cool. Cut
the burnish with a grit that actually cuts — often the
same number, fresh mineral, lighter hands — not a finer
grit that will only polish the cook.

If you have a real scorch, you are in removal. A belt
will make it worse if you hover. A plane or a scraper may
be the first tooth. Then the sequence. Then inspect. A
scorch under a clear film is a permanent tea stain.

## Edges and veneer

Edges heat faster (less mass, more hanging disc). Veneer
heats and then you have a bubble or a sand-through. If
the face gets hot, stop. Heat plus a thin face is how
you meet the core and a glue line in the same afternoon.

## The phone pause

I have held a running ROS on a top while I read a
message. The pad was still. The orbit was not. That
circle is a heat dish. Lift first (draft 17). Heat is
why that dish stains differently even after you think
you flattened it. You cooked the fiber.

A "quick" 220 to even a repair is how cherry gets a
toasted patch the color of tea. If the repair is local,
keep the machine moving off the patch as much as on it,
and let it cool. Touch the wood. If it is hot, you are
not sanding. You are ironing.

A belt that stops in the field is the same story with
a deeper brand. If you have to answer the door, the
machine is off the wood. Not idling. Off.

## Shop consequence

Smooth-and-hot is a stop, not a pass. If the disc is
making heat and not dust, it is not cutting. Cutting
makes dust. Burnishing makes shine and smell.

I still press when I am late. The maple sample on the
shelf (draft 40) has a cousin that is just heat. I keep
that one too. Two ways to ruin a quiet wood with "better"
sanding.
