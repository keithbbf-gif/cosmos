# Between coats, do not cut through

The edge went raw in a pale line the length of the top.
I had been "just knocking nibs" with 320 and a block that
loved arrises. Between coats: dull the sheen, take the
dust nibs, do not cut through.

Cut-through on an edge is a dark line if you stain
underneath, a raw line if you do not, a place water will
find. The field can look perfect while the edge tells on
you.

## What the pass is for

Dust nibs. A little dust in the shop air (you sanded near
the finish table; of course there are nibs). A raised
grain the first coat created because you skipped the pop.
A sheen you want to dull so the next coat has a mechanical
tooth.

It is not for flattening a table that you should have
flattened on bare wood. If you are cutting to flatten a
film, you are in rub-out or in a recut. Different job,
different grit language (draft 37).

## Grit and mineral

Often 320–400 on a film, SiC or stearated finish paper,
or a nonwoven pad (draft 15) when I want conformity and
I am afraid of the edge. Lighter than you think. The film
is thin on the arris even when it is generous in the
field.

If the coat is not ready, the paper balls. Wait. A
calendar is not a cure. A fingernail dent that prints is
not ready.

## Edges, again

Hand them with a light pad or skip them if the film is
thin and the next coat will cover a dull field. A hard
block on an arris is how the pale line is born. I have
made that line after knowing this sentence.

If you cut through a stained sealer, the repair is not
more stain on the line. The line is raw wood next to a
sealed field. You may be in a local re-color and a new
build, or an honest conversation.

## Dust

The dust you make now is finish dust plus whatever is
on the paper. It sits on the surface and becomes the
next nibs if you do not clean. Vacuum. Tack as the
system wants. Do not wipe with a filthy rag that is
just moving grit in circles.

A shop that sands near the finish table is a shop that
owns nibs. I am not going to pretend I have a hospital.
I am going to say the last hour before a final coat is
not the hour you scrape a lot of oak.

## Recoat window

Some films want sanding inside a window; some want you
to wait for a full cure and then scuff. Read the can.
Sanding a half-cured oil-modified poly makes worms. Waiting
forever on a film that wanted a chemical recoat, then
cutting hard, is how you get a pale edge and a bad bond.

This pack will not become a recoat chart. It will say:
the nib pass assumes the film is ready for a mechanical
tooth. If it balls, you are early. If it powders white
and you are through in ten strokes, you were late or
the film was thin.

## Shop consequence

If you need to flatten, do it on the board that is still
wood, or schedule a real rub-out. If you need to knock
nibs, knock nibs. The words "while I'm here" are how
edges die.

Feel the film with a palm, see the nibs in a raking
light, then stop when they are gone. The extra minute
is the minute that cuts through.
