# End grain is a different job

The breadboard went black. The field looked like the sample.
Same stain, same minute, same rag. End grain drinks and
scratches deeper. The same 180 that looks polite on the face
looks like 120 on the end. The pores are standing up and
saying hello.

Treat end grain as a second sequence, or accept a darker,
wilder frame and call it on purpose.

## Why it scratches deeper

The fibers are pipes. A tooth that skates on long grain dives
into open cells. A leftover coarse scratch on end grain is a
set of trenches that stain will fill like ink in a cut.

I often last-sand end grain a step finer than the field — 220
if the field is 180, sometimes a careful 320 if the finish is
a thin oil and the species is thirsty. I do not chase 400 on
oak end grain and then wonder why it looks burnished and still
drinks.

## Sealing is not this draft

People size end grain with glue or a washcoat. That is a
finish decision and it belongs with the can. This draft is
the scratch under that decision. A washcoat on 80-grit end
grain is a washcoat in trenches. Do the tooth first.

## Breadboards, legs, chamfers

Breadboards are the usual surprise. Legs are the usual
hand-touch. Chamfers and miters are end grain wearing a
pretty angle. Hand them. A ROS on the end of a top is how
you round the breadboard and deepen the scratch in one move.

When you sand from face onto end grain in one stroke, you
drag face grit lines onto the end. Those lines become stripes
around the corner. Stop at the corner. Change direction. Yes,
it takes longer.

## Inspection

End grain shines. Raking light can hide a deep scratch in
glare. Use the pencil. Use more than one angle. Look when it
is dry. Wet end grain always looks refined.

## Legs, feet, the hand-height ends

Table legs are mostly long grain until the foot, where
someone put a taper or a pad and you suddenly have a lot
of end. People sand a leg like a stick and then the foot
goes dark. Last-sand the foot as end grain. The client
will look at the foot when they sit down. They will not
look at your fluorescent.

A through-tenon that shows is end grain in a face. Treat
the tenon end as its own postage stamp: finer tooth, maybe
a washcoat, no ROS that also hits the rail.

## Shop consequence

If the sample was face grain, the sample was a liar about the
ends. Make an end-grain sample. If the client wants the ends
as light as the field, you need finer tooth, a washcoat, or
both — and you need to say so before the stain goes on the
real top.

Draft 39 is cross-grain at the joint. This draft is the end
that is the joint's neighbor. They meet at the breadboard and
ruin Sundays together if you treat them as "the rest of the
top."
