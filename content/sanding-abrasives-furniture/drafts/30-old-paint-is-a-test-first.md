# Old paint is a test first

The dresser was "just a sand-and-paint." The film was older
than the story that came with it. A flea-market piece can
carry lead. Dry-sanding a mystery film into household dust
is how a furniture project becomes a house problem.

Test first. If the test is positive, the job changes. This
pack will not walk a "just sand it off, it's probably fine"
route. Draft 08 is the fence. This draft is the surface that
makes people hop the fence because the piece was cheap.

## What "test first" means

A kit sold for lead on household paint, used as the
instructions say, on a place you actually intend to cut.
Not a scratch on the back of a leg you will not sand. If
you do not know how to read the kit, you do not start the
sander. You learn the kit on a day that is not Friday.

A negative test is not a personality. It is a result for
that spot. Weird overpaints exist. If the piece is old and
the plan is aggressive, more than one spot.

I am not going to write a medical or legal manual here.
Household lead practice is already documented by the people
who regulate it. Follow that, or hand the piece to someone
whose job is that. This series stays inside ordinary
furniture-shop practice: we name the stop, we do not invent
a home-brew containment.

## If you may not sand

The job becomes: a method sold for that work, a refinisher
with capture, or a paint-over strategy that does not require
you to grind the film into air. Paint-over has its own
adhesion rules. It is still not this draft's how-to for
lead.

## Heat guns, strippers, the other doors

They exist. They have their own harms. Methylene chloride
is a name this pack will say only to refuse (draft 08).
Slower strippers and heat are doors. They are not sanding.
If you choose them, choose them as those products' labels
and as the finishing-chemistry fence, not as a paragraph I
hide inside a grit essay.

## Modern paint you may sand

If the test and the story agree it is ordinary modern
film, treat it as a finish film. Open coat, extraction,
respirator, a grit that cuts coating (often SiC or a
stripping-adjacent paper), then a wood sequence once you
are on wood. Do not stay on 80 after you are on wood
because 80 was "working on the paint." You will dish a
carcass that only needed the film gone.

## Shop consequence

The cheapest dresser is expensive if you put it in the air
of a house. If the client wants "just sand it," the sentence
you owe them is: we test, then we choose a method. If they
want speed more than a test, they want a different shop.

I have skipped a test on a "probably latex" nightstand. I
was lucky. Luck is not a sequence.
