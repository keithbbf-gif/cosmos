# Start as coarse as the defect

The top came off the planer looking like a grown-up. I reached for
180 because 180 feels like finishing. An hour later I had a shiny
field and a chatter line that had only gotten more polite. I had
polished the rims of a trench. The trench was still a trench.

Start as coarse as the defect, not as coarse as your mood, and not
as fine as your hope.

## Three common first surfaces

**A planed or jointed show face** that already looks honest can
often start at 120, sometimes 150, then on. The knife left a
regular pattern. Your job is to take the mill marks without
inventing a deeper set. If you start at 80 on a clean plane pass
you are paying in thickness and in time you will spend erasing 80.

**A wide-belt or drum that left 80** wants 80, or at least the
grit that can erase 80. Jumping to 150 because the belt "already
did the coarse work" is how those 80-grit valleys become a
permanent photograph. The machine did the coarse work. You still
have to erase it.

**A repair, a scrape, a glue ridge, a dent you steamed** wants
whatever grit will cut the local problem without dishing the field.
That is often a block and a small piece of 80 or 100, then a
return to the field sequence. A random-orbit on 80 over the whole
top because one corner had a ridge is how you make a shallow bowl
with a nice edge.

## Anger is not a grit

People grab 60 when they are late. 60 on furniture is a removal
tool. It belongs on a paint-scraper successor job, a badly cupped
panel you have already decided to recut, or a paint film you have
already tested and been told is safe to cut. It does not belong on
a cherry dining top because Friday is coming.

If the defect is milling tear-out, the first answer is often a
scraper or a different cutting direction, not a coarser disc. Paper
will take the tear-out down. It will also take the surrounding
field down. You will see that in raking light as a valley the
finish loves.

## Thickness is a budget

Veneer has almost no budget. Solid tops have more, and still not
as much as you think if the top is already at finished thickness
and the breadboards are proud. Every coarse start spends wood.
Spend it on a defect that needs it. Do not spend it because coarse
is faster in the first three minutes. Coarse is slower in the last
hour if you have to climb back out.

I mark the first grit on the inside of a rail or on the shop door
for that job. If I cannot name the defect, I have not chosen a
grit. I have chosen a habit.

## The test

Look at the defect in raking light. Ask: will the next finer grit
erase this, or only shine its edges? If it will only shine the
edges, you have not started coarse enough — or you have started
on the wrong tool. A card scraper, a hand plane, or a local block
may be the real first grit, wearing a different name.

Then write the sequence. Then buy the missing sleeve before you
start, so you do not invent a skip because the 100 box is empty.
That skip is draft 04. This draft is the decision before the
sequence even exists.
