# Cross-grain at the joint is a stripe

The breadboard had a set of lines that did not belong to
it. They belonged to the field. I had run the ROS off the
top and onto the end like the joint was theoretical. Cross-
grain at the joint is a stripe. Stain makes it a headline.

Stop at the joint. Change direction. Yes, it takes longer.

## Where it happens

Breadboards. Apron-to-leg. Frame-and-panel where you sand
the frame across the panel's grain. A door rail you sand
with the stile's motion because you did not lift. Any
place two grains meet and a machine does not care.

Hand tools do it too. A block that crosses a joint writes
the same stripe, only narrower.

## The move

Sand each grain as itself at the end of the sequence. The
middle grits can be a little sloppier if you will erase
them. The last two grits cannot. Mask if you have to. A
piece of tape on the breadboard while you finish the
field, then the breadboard by itself, with the grain of
the breadboard — which may be the end-grain rules of
draft 26.

A card scraper can take a cross-grain scar if it is
shallow. If it is deep, you are in a local recut and a
thickness budget.

## Why we do it anyway

Because we are tired and the pad is already moving. Because
the joint is flush and the machine likes flush. Because
the stripe is invisible until color or oil. Those are
reasons. They are not excuses the dining room will accept.

## Inspection

Look along the joint in raking light, from both sides.
A cross-grain scratch shines differently than a with-grain
scratch. Once you have seen one, you will see them for a
year on other people's tables. That is useful and slightly
ruining.

## Frames, panels, the floating field

A frame-and-panel door is a nest of joints. If you sand
the panel after it is in the frame, you will cross the
rails. Sand the panel as a panel, then the frame as a
frame, then a light hand at the reveal if the proud
needs it. A floating panel that you sand in place is
how you write cross-grain on a field that will move
anyway and show the stripe twice — winter and summer.

Apron-to-leg is the same law in a smaller room. The
apron grain runs one way, the leg another. A ROS that
"blends the corner" blends nothing. It writes a fan.

## Shop consequence

If the piece has a breadboard, the sequence includes a
sentence: *stop*. If the piece is a face-frame, the
sequence includes a sentence: *lift*. Write it on the
door with the grit list.

I still cross a joint when I am late. I also still recut
when the stain tells on me. The recut is longer than the
lift. I know that and I still need this draft.
