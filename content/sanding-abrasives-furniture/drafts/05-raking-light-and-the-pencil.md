# Raking light and the pencil

I used to inspect under the shop fluorescent, standing up, the way
you look at a floor you just swept. The top looked fine. The client's
west window is not a fluorescent. Their window is a raking light with
opinions.

Look across, not down. Down is how managers inspect. Across is how
the dining room inspects at dinner.

## What raking light is for

A low LED, almost on the surface. A window if you have one and the
sun is cooperating. You are looking for leftover scratch pattern,
dishes, an arris you rounded, a glue line that went shiny, a swirl
the orbital left because you lingered.

You are not looking for "smooth." Smooth is a feeling. Pattern is a
fact. The light turns the fact into something you cannot negotiate.

Move the light. A single angle will hide a set that another angle
will shout. I walk the long side, then the short side. Breadboards
and end grain get their own pass, because they shine differently
and they hide a deeper scratch in the glare.

If you cannot get the shop dark enough to see, you will ship
scratches. That is not a moral failing. It is a lighting budget.
A cheap bar light on a stick is a tool. Buy it before you buy
another sander.

## What the pencil is for

Graphite on the field. A light scribble, not a children's drawing
that loads the paper with wax and dirt. Sand until it is gone. What
stays is low, or a scratch the current grit has not reached, or a
pore line that was never going to take graphite the same way.

On open-pore oak the pencil will sit in pores after the field is
clean. That is not a reason to keep sanding the field into a dish.
That is a reason to know pores from scratches. A scratch is a line
that crosses pores. A pore is a hole that was in the tree.

On maple the pencil is almost too honest. It will show a swirl you
were about to call done. Good.

Wipe the graphite off before finish. Leftover pencil under oil is
a gray joke. Vacuum, then a clean tack if the finish wants one.

## The two tools together

Pencil finds the low. Light finds the pattern. A surface can be
flat and still be a swirl photograph. A surface can be pattern-free
in one angle and dished like a shallow plate. You want both facts
before you change grits and before you open a can.

I do the pencil at the coarse-to-middle changes, when I am most
likely to skip. I do the raking light at every change and again
before the first sealer. The last raking pass is draft 46. This
draft is the habit that makes that last pass boring.

## When the light lies

Glossy burnished maple will flash and hide a scratch in the shine.
A wet wiped board will hide everything. Inspect dry. If you just
water-popped, wait until it is dry and dull again.

Dark walnut hides swirl until the finish goes on and the swirl
becomes a silk moiré. On dark woods I use more pencil and more
angles, not less, because my eyes want to quit.

A phone flashlight in a bright shop is a toy. It can work if you
get it low and you get your head low. It will not work if you wave
it from standing height and nod.

The inspection is the skill. The paper is the expense. People
reverse that.
