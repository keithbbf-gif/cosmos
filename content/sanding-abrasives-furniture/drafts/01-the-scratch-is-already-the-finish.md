# The scratch is already the finish

The four-o'clock sun found the table before I did. Little arcs, the
memory of 80 grit, filled with oil that only made them glossier. The
top had felt smooth at noon. Finish is a lens. The scratch was already
the finish. I had just not poured it yet.

Sanding is not a chore you do so the art can start. Sanding is the
shape of the surface the binder will have to live on. Oil will leave
that shape almost naked. A built film will bury some of it and then
telegraph the rest when the light is low and honest.

## What a scratch is

Paper does not polish wood. Paper cuts a set of grooves. The number
on the sleeve is a claim about how fine those grooves are, on a scale
that may or may not match the next sleeve in the drawer. The wood
keeps the grooves. The finish keeps the wood.

A coarse groove has a valley and two rims. Stain pools in the valley.
Oil looks wetter there. A film bridges it if you build enough, and
then the bridge is a different thickness than the field, and you have
invented a stripe. People call that "the wood being figured." Sometimes
it is. Sometimes it is 80 grit you were too proud to admit was still
there.

A fine groove is still a groove. Burnish far enough and you close the
surface. Maple will take that and look like plastic under oil. Oak
will take that and then drink stain in the earlywood anyway, because
the pores were never a grit problem.

## The finish you have not opened

Thin oil and wiping varnish want the last scratch to be one you can
live with forever. They do not have a hiding budget. A 120 last sand
under oil is a 120 photograph. I have delivered that photograph. I
do not pretend it was 220.

A filled, built polyurethane can start the film earlier. You pay in
plastic. You also pay later if you skip and leave coarse valleys under
a glossy skin. The skin is a magnifier on Sunday lunch.

Waterborne will raise whatever fuzz you left, and then it will sit on
the scratch pattern you thought was gone. Shellac will show it in the
first French-polish myth you believed. Milk paint will chalk over it
and then chip along it if the film is thin on the rims.

None of that is a reason to sand to 400 on every board. It is a reason
to know which scratch you are keeping.

## Why this pack starts here

Later drafts will argue minerals, pads, veneer, and the client who
wants "buttery." Those arguments are downstream of this one. If you
do not believe a scratch is already a finish decision, you will buy
a finer disc and call it progress.

The cheap tools are old. A raking light. A pencil scribble. A last
pass with the grain on a show top. A refusal to skip because the 120
box is empty.

The expensive tools are also old. A return. A recut. A table that
looks finished until the sun is low.

## The board is the critic

Do one offcut of the same species, same last grit, same finish. Look
at it across, not down. If you do not like the photograph, the grit
was wrong, or the skip was real, or the mineral had already died and
you were burnishing.

That is the whole series in one board. The rest is how not to lie to
yourself on the way there.
