# The shop-safe fence

Sanding is already a hazardous ordinary job. You do not need to
make it a second hobby called "amateur mineral processing" or
"careful dry-sanding of a mystery film."

This draft is the fence for the whole series. Later pieces will
point back here instead of repeating a sermon.

## What we will teach

**Commercial abrasives, used as abrasives.** Sheets, discs, belts,
mesh, pads, steel wool and its synthetic cousins, sold for wood
and finish. How they cut, load, burnish, and lie.

**Shop air.** Hardwood dust is not a rustic smell. It is a lung
problem. A respirator you actually wear on a machine pass — and
on the "quick" hand sand — is a tool. Extraction that is hooked
up is a tool. A broom as the only dust plan is a delay.

**Combustible dust as housekeeping.** Fine wood dust can burn. The
shop move is not a chemistry lesson. The shop move is: do not let
dust build in a layer on motors, boxes, and the water heater.
Vacuum with a system meant for wood dust. Empty bags. Do not blow
a shop into a cloud with a leaf blower and call it Friday.

**Old paint.** A flea-market dresser can carry lead. Dry-sanding
a mystery film into household dust is how a furniture project
becomes a house problem. Test first. If the test is positive, the
job changes: containment, a method sold for that work, or a
refinisher who already has the setup. This series will not walk a
"just sand it off, it's probably fine" route.

**Waste.** Used paper, loaded pads, and dust bags go in the
ordinary shop waste stream unless a lead or finish-strip job has
already changed the rules. Those changed rules are not this pack's
how-to. They are a reason to stop and change the job.

## What we will not teach

We will not teach you to **make, coat, or "improve" abrasive
minerals.** If a disc is dull, throw it away. Do not bake, wash in
solvent, or recut a mineral at the bench.

We will not teach you to **dry-sand lead or unknown old paint**
with a household sander and a dust bag you will empty in the yard.

We will not teach **blasting, grinding, or floor-edger tricks**
on furniture because a video made it look like a shortcut. Those
are different trades with different capture.

We will not teach you to **defeat a dust rule, a waste rule, or a
building rule.** If the apartment and a belt sander cannot be in
the same story, the tool choice changes. That is a real
constraint, not a lack of commitment.

We will mention **methylene chloride strippers** and other serious
stripper classes only to say: they are a health problem, they are
restricted for a reason, and they are not a sanding technique. We
will not give a how-to.

We will not teach **fire as a project.** Sanding dust plus a shop
vac full of fine powder plus a spark is enough. Empty the vac.
Do not write a second essay in the motor.

## Machines that assume a room you do not have

Wide-belts, stroke sanders, and production booths exist. They
make beautiful furniture. They also assume capture and a person
who is not improvising a booth with a box fan in a window pointed
at the neighbor's garden.

If a draft names them, it is to tell you what a glazed belt does
to a surface — not to walk you into a production machine because
a forum said it was "what the factories use."

Factories have rooms you do not have. That is not a moral
judgment. It is why this pack spends so many words on a cork
block and a raking light.

## The ordinary fire and the ordinary lung

Oil rags are a finishing problem (see the finishing-chemistry
pack). Sanding has its own ordinary harms: breathing hardwoods,
especially as a daily habit; cutting old paint; filling a room
with dust and then finishing in that room.

I wear a respirator when I am on a machine, and I still forget
on a quick hand sand, and I am wrong when I forget. Draft 41
says it in the shop voice. This draft says it as a fence: the
pack will remind you, and it will not joke you into skipping it.

If a later piece seems to invite a shortcut through this fence,
the fence wins. The board can wait. The lung does not get a
recut.
