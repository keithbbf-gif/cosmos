# P and CAMI are not the same number

I have a drawer that looks disciplined. 80, 100, 120, 150, 180, 220.
Some sleeves say P. Some do not. I used to treat the number as the
number. Then a "120" disc left scratches a "P120" sheet would have
already erased, and I spent twenty minutes hunting a skip that lived
in the scale, not in my hands.

P80 and 80 are neighbors. They are not twins. The letter is the
claim. Ignore the letter and you have invented a skip that will not
show up in the pencil scribble until the next real grit.

## Two common shop scales

American shop paper spent a long time on **CAMI** (the old U.S.
grading). A lot of big-box sheets still talk that way, or talk as if
they do. European and a lot of better discs use **FEPA**, the P-grade.
P120 is not a poetic way to write 120. It is a different sieve
history.

In the coarse and middle range the numbers sit close enough that a
tired person will mix them and get away with it. In the finer range
they drift. A P220 and a CAMI 220 are not the same cut. People who
"always finish at 220" and then switch brands mid-job are often
switching scales mid-job. The wood does not care about your drawer
labels. The wood keeps the deeper set.

I am not going to paste a conversion chart I have not re-measured
on this year's boxes `[VERIFY]`. The shop move is simpler: **do not
mix scales in one progression.** Pick a sleeve family for the piece.
If you have to change brands, treat the first sheet of the new brand
as a possible skip and inspect like you just jumped.

## Micron is a third language

Some finish films and some mesh discs talk microns. That is honest
when the number is a measured particle, and it is marketing when the
sleeve just wanted to look medical. A 30-micron film is not "finer
than 400" unless you know which 400. I use micron language on rub-out
and on finish sanding of a film. I do not use it to feel smarter than
the P-grade on bare oak.

If a catalog gives only microns and you are on bare wood, find the
P or CAMI equivalent from that maker, or stop and do a test board.
Do not invent a translation at the bench.

## The drawer lie

Organizing by number only is how a P150 and a 180 end up in the same
slot because the ink is small. I write the scale on the bin, not just
the number. I throw away the sleeve that tore the P off. A naked disc
is a rumor.

Production shops already know this because a wide-belt spec is a
scale plus a brand plus a wear state. A furniture bench forgets
because the drawer is mixed leftovers from three years of "whatever
was on sale." Leftovers are a scale problem wearing a thrift costume.

## Shop consequence

If the last grit looks "too scratchy" after a brand change, do not
automatically go finer. Check whether you went coarser by accident.
If the stain is striping in a pattern that matches the previous disc,
you may have used a finer number that was a coarser cut.

The pencil scribble still works. The scribble does not know scales.
It knows whether the current mineral has erased the last groove.
That is the only conversion that matters on the board.

Read the letter. Then cut.
