# The life of a shipment

An island does not “go to Massachusetts.” It goes through a chain of rooms,
some of them with forklifts, some of them with a porch light and a dog.

If you cannot narrate that chain, you will promise the wrong day and lose the
wrong argument. This draft is the walk-through BBF should be able to give
without looking at a slide.

## Origin, which is us

The piece is finished, inspected, and — if we are doing the job — photographed.
Someone writes a bill of lading: shipper (Bradley Brand Furniture, Warren),
consignee, piece count, weight, a description a stranger can understand
(“hardwood kitchen island, 1 pc”), who pays, and any accessorials we already
know (liftgate, residential, inside).

If the description says “furniture” and nothing else, we have already started
the claim we will lose. A clerk in a terminal will not love our island. They
will love a label they can match to a PRO.

Pickup is not delivery. Pickup is a truck backing to our dock, or a driver
walking a liftgate up to a shop door because Warren is not a cross-dock. The
driver counts pieces. The driver should look at the carton or the pad wrap.
If they sign the BOL clean, they are saying the piece looked sound enough to
take. That signature is not a compliment. It is the first page of the claim
file.

## The quiet middle

On general LTL, the origin truck rarely runs your island to the customer’s
street. It runs to a terminal. The piece sits. It may be unloaded, staged,
reloaded. It may wait for a trailer going the right direction. It may
interline — another company’s trailer, another company’s dock, another
company’s habit with a finished edge.

This is where “out for delivery” fantasies go to die. Tracking that says
“in transit” for four days can be honest. The freight is in a building you
will never see, waiting for cube on a outbound door.

Specialized furniture / blanket-wrap is a shorter story: fewer terminals,
often a van that loaded at origin and aims at destination with pads and
straps instead of a forklift ballet. That is why it costs more and why it
exists. It is still not teleportation. Drivers sleep. Appointments slip.
Weather sits on I-40.

## Destination city

A local driver gets a list. Your island is one stop among many if it is LTL.
The truck may be wrong for the street. The appointment may be a four-hour
window. The consignee may be at work. The driver may not have a helper. The
liftgate may be on a different truck than the one that has your freight.

If the order was sold as white glove and the freight is on a 28-foot city
unit with one driver and no pads, the sale and the truck are not the same
job. That mismatch is how porches become claim photos.

## The porch

Someone signs a delivery receipt (sometimes still paper, often a phone).
That signature, plus whatever they write or fail to write, is the loudest
document after the BOL. If they sign clean and open the carton tomorrow,
they have a concealed-damage fight. If they write “crushed corner, island
top” before they sign, they have a chance.

The driver is not a villain for wanting to go. The driver is paid to complete
stops. Our job is to teach the consignee — retailer, designer, or household —
that the walk-around happens while the truck is still there.

## After

Proof of delivery posts. Invoice posts. If the piece is fine, the life of the
shipment is over and nobody writes a novel. If it is not fine, the life of
the shipment becomes a file: photos from Warren, photos from the porch, the
BOL, the delivery receipt, the invoice, a dollar demand. See Stage 6.

Marketplace versions add a portal. The portal is not a carrier. It is a
scorekeeper that will deduct first and ask later. The physical life of the
shipment did not change. The money life did.

## A Warren-shaped example

A Caney-sized island, boxed or pad-wrapped, picked up on a Tuesday. Terminal
Wednesday. Linehaul. Another terminal. Appointment Friday that slips to
Monday because the consignee’s “someone will be home” was a hope. Delivery
Monday with a liftgate. If we sold “this week,” we sold a hope. If we sold
“freight, appointment, five to ten working days, we will call you when we
have a PRO,” we sold the life of the shipment.

Company truck shortens the story: our hands, our straps, our day. That is a
gift. It is also a capacity decision, not a default.
