# Carton, crate, and when wood earns the freight

Packaging is how you tell the network what kind of animal you are sending.

A commodity LTL network wants a handling unit: something a forklift can
hurt without immediately hurting the furniture. A blanket-wrap van wants
the furniture. If you send the wrong skin into the wrong network, the
network will not apologize.

## Carton

Double-wall, sized to the piece, with blocking so the island does not
become a clapper inside a box. Edge protectors. A top that can take a
little compression because someone will test it. Labels on two sides.
“This side up” is a prayer; still pray, and also design the carton so an
upside-down hour is not death.

Cartons are right when:

- the piece will see terminals
- the finish can live behind corrugated
- you can still lift or palletize the unit honestly
- the customer’s program expects a box (many marketplaces do)

Cartons are wrong when the box is a costume: one-wall, floating piece,
no corner board, a hope that tape is structure.

## Pallet

A carton on a pallet is a forklift citizen. A piece strapped to a pallet
with a prayer is a fork magnet. Overhang is how tines kiss rails. If the
footprint is bigger than the pallet, build a bigger base or crate.

Pallets add height and weight. They also add a chance to be stacked. If
you cannot be stacked, say so *and* build a top that still survives a
bad decision.

## Crate

Wood (or engineered panels) that makes a room around the piece. Slats or
sheathed. Internal blocking. A crate is right when:

- the piece is heavy, odd, or expensive
- the ride is interline-heavy
- export or a long project warehouse sit is in the story
- glass, stone, or a fragile gallery finish is along for the ride

Crates add cube. Cube adds freight. People skip crates to save the cube
and then spend the crate on a refinish. Do the ugly math once per SKU.

A crate is not automatically honest. A crate that rattles is a drum. A
crate with one loose 2×4 is a weapon.

## Loose / pad only

Right on a furniture van. Wrong on general LTL unless the carrier’s own
rules say they will take it and you believe their dock. Most of the time,
loose on LTL is how you donate an edge.

## The concealment problem

A perfect carton hides a cracked rail until the porch. That is concealed
damage, and it is a harder claim than a crushed corner the driver can
see. Packaging that is too good at looking fine is not a victory if the
inside is trash. This is why origin photos of the *piece*, not only the
closed carton, matter, and why a customer still has to open promptly.
See [35-obvious-vs-concealed-damage.md](35-obvious-vs-concealed-damage.md).

## The costume carton, in one picture

Single-wall, piece rattling, one strip of tape, a label on the only
face that will be against the wall. It photographs like a shipment.
It travels like a suggestion. If we are still making these, we are
not short on corrugated. We are short on a standard.

A second picture: a crate so overbuilt the freight class climbs and
the customer pays for our anxiety. Middle is a spec: enough
structure for the network we booked, not a monument.

## BBF-shaped defaults (starting points, not dogma)

- Boards and small goods: parcel carton that can take a chute.
- Stock islands into retailer docks: carton + pallet or a crate the
  portal will accept.
- Finish-sensitive one-offs: specialized pad wrap, or crate + LTL if the
  van is not available.
- Healthcare / hospitality sets: packaging that matches the installer’s
  receiving, not our nostalgia.

Write the default on the SKU. Do not reinvent it at 4:30 p.m. because the
truck is early.
