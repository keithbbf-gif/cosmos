# Corners, glass, stone, and the pieces that travel badly

Some furniture rides like a good dog. Some furniture rides like a tray of
water.

BBF’s islands are mostly wood, which is already a tray of water if you
count finish. Add glass, stone, a live edge, a long rail, a proud corner,
and you have a piece that will find every bump you did not budget.

## Corners are antennas

A corner is where a strap prints, a doorway clips, and a carton crushes.
It is also where the customer’s eye goes first. A dent in the middle of a
side can be a story. A mashed corner is a refund.

Protect corners twice: a hard guard (cardboard, foam, a purpose-made
edge) and then the pad or carton. A pad alone on a sharp corner becomes
a stamp pad for the ratchet.

Designers love thin, proud edges. Freight hates them. If BBF sells a
profile that cannot take a kiss, the quote must include the crate or the
van. Do not discover this on a claim.

## Long and skinny

Rails, tops shipped off the base, bed parts, tall cases. Length is a
lever. A little drop at one end is a big crack at the other. These pieces
want internal blocking, a crate, or a van with a place that matches their
length. They do not want to be “the thing we slide in last.”

If a top ships separate from a base, it is two personalities. Label them
as a set. Photograph both. The claim that “the top arrived, the base
didn’t” is an OS&D story you can only win with paper.

## Glass

Glass is not wood with shine. It wants its own crate or a factory rack,
edge protection, and a network that will not treat the crate as a bench.
Tape on glass is a debate; do what the glass supplier says, not what a
freight clerk says.

Never pad-wrap glass as if it were a dresser. A pad does not stop a
point load. A crate with blocking does.

If the glass is a customer’s own insert, write who supplies it and who
ships it. Mixed responsibility is how nobody insures it.

## Stone and composites

Weight plus brittleness. Forks under the wrong third of a slab will
finish the story. Crates, A-frames, or a specialized stone/furniture
hybrid — not a carton that looks brave.

Stone also hides concealed cracks. A hairline that opens after a week of
house heat will be called “settling.” Photograph origin. Tell the
receiver to inspect in daylight, not at 8 p.m. after a long truck wait.

## Hardware, doors, and things that swing

Doors that are hung for the photo should be pinned or removed for the
ride if they can become hammers. Adjustable feet should be up. Shelves
should not be loose missiles. This is shop work, not carrier magic.

A hospitality job with fifty doors and fifty bags of pulls is a
short-and-damage festival if the bags live in a random pad.

## The honesty test

Before you book, ask: if this piece falls two inches onto a corner, is
it furniture or firewood? If firewood, you need a crate, a van, or a
redesign. Released value will not rebuild a slab.
