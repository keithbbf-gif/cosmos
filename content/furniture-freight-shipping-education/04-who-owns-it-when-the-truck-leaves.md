# Who owns it when the truck leaves

The hardest sentence in furniture freight is also the most ordinary: once the
carrier takes the piece, the wood is on someone else’s risk.

That sentence has wrecked more customer conversations than a late truck.
People hear “we shipped it” as “it’s still yours until it looks like the
catalog in my dining room.” The law of the road, and most factory terms BBF
has lived with, do not work that way unless the order paper says they do.

## Possession is the fork

On a typical domestic furniture job:

- BBF owns the piece in the shop.
- The carrier signs for it.
- Risk of in-transit loss or damage often shifts to the buyer (or to the
  buyer’s retailer program) at that handoff, especially on FOB origin /
  “shipper’s dock” terms.
- The buyer (or the program) is the one who must file against the carrier,
  unless the contract says BBF will file and chase.

FOB destination, prepaid-and-add, “we’ll take care of freight” — those
phrases can move money without moving risk. You can pay the freight invoice
and still not own the claim. You can own the claim and still not have
written the check for the truck. Say the two jobs separately: who pays the
carrier, and who bears the loss if the top is cracked.

## Why this feels cruel

Because the customer paid BBF, not YRC or Estes or a furniture van they
never chose. Because the piece still has our name on the carton. Because we
are the ones who know what the finish looked like on Tuesday. None of that
rewrites a clean delivery receipt or a contract that said risk passed at
pickup.

Cruel and true can sit in the same chair. The educational move is to say
the fork *before* the truck is booked, not after the porch photos arrive.

## Customer-own freight

When the buyer sends their own carrier, the fork is sharper. Their agent
walks our dock. Their agent signs our release. After that, BBF is not the
freight company and not the insurer. We are the factory that handed over a
sound piece — if we photographed it and got a clean signature.

If their driver refuses to look, we write that. If their driver wants to
grab and go, we still walk the piece. A rushed signature is how a factory
eats a crack that happened on I-40.

## Marketplace freight

Retailer programs often take the booking out of Warren’s hands. The BOL may
already check residential, liftgate, inside, stairs. Ownership language
lives in the retailer agreement, not in our memory of 2013. Risk may sit
with the retailer, the customer, or a forced chargeback to the vendor
regardless of Carmack. Read [12-marketplace-vs-factory-direct.md](12-marketplace-vs-factory-direct.md)
and [41-retailer-chargebacks.md](41-retailer-chargebacks.md) before you
comfort anyone with “the carrier will pay.”

## What we still owe

Passing risk is not passing shame. We still owe:

- a piece that was sound at origin
- a BOL that describes it
- photos
- a straight story about how to inspect and notate
- help assembling the file, even when we are not the claimant

We do not owe a rewrite of physics or of a signature they signed clean
because they were cold on the porch.

## How to say it without sounding like a lawyer

Try: “Once the truck has it, the carrier is responsible for how it rides.
If something is wrong at delivery, it has to be written on their paper that
day. We will help you with photos from the shop. We cannot invent a note
that was not taken.”

If BBF’s actual terms on that order are kinder than FOB origin, say the
kinder thing — but say it because it is on the order, not because the
customer is loud.
