# Interline, terminals, and the extra set of hands

Every time the piece changes hands, it changes luck.

Interline is the polite word for “another company took it from here.”
Terminal is the polite word for “a building full of forklifts.” Both are
normal. Both are why a carton that left Warren square can arrive looking
tired.

## What interline actually is

Carrier A picked up in Arkansas. Carrier A does not deliver that ZIP, or
does not want to this week. They hand the freight to Carrier B under a
joint rule. Your PRO may stay with A. Your delivery truck says B. Your
claim may bounce between them like a chore neither kid wants.

This is not a conspiracy. Coverage maps have holes. Regional carriers live
on partnerships. Rural porches at the end of a long lane are where
partnerships go to work — and where furniture goes to get an extra dock.

If the quote was cheap and the destination is a county you cannot find
without a paper map, assume an extra hand until the scan events prove
otherwise.

## Terminals are factories for exceptions

A good terminal is a ballet. A bad night is a pile. Your island does not
get a vote.

What happens in a terminal that does not happen on a dedicated van:

- unload
- sort
- maybe a restack
- maybe a weekend on a concrete floor
- reload against a door that is trying to leave on time

OS&D gets written here more often than on a porch. If a terminal notes a
crushed carton and nobody tells BBF, the porch will discover a crushed
carton and think it was born that way.

Watch the scans. A “arrived terminal” followed by three quiet days is a
wait for outbound cube. A “transfer” or a new SCAC in the history is
interline wearing a work shirt.

## Why extra hands hate finish

Forks miss. Cartons snag. A strap gets yanked. Someone uses your crate as
a stair. A bag of something dusty leans on a finished side because the
side looked like a wall.

Specialized networks spend money to have fewer of these rooms. Commodity
networks spend money to have more of these rooms in more towns. That is
the trade.

## What BBF can do about a network it does not own

- Carton or crate for the roughest room, not the nicest brochure.
- Put “do not stack” where a forklift driver will see it, knowing some
  will not.
- Avoid a second carrier when the finish is a one-off — pay for coverage
  or use specialized / company truck.
- On customer-own pickup, ask who the delivering carrier is, not only who
  is backing to our dock.
- When tracing, ask “has this interlined?” if the destination city driver
  is a name you do not recognize.

You cannot forbid interline on a cheap LTL tender to a thin ZIP. You can
stop pretending the first logo on the quote is the last pair of hands.

## How to explain it

“Your piece may change trucks. Each change is normal. It is also why we
box it like it will be handled by strangers, and why we ask you to look
at it before you sign.”

That is enough. Do not narrate the entire terminal industry on a sales
call. Do narrate it inside BBF so nobody promises “one truck, our guys,
your driveway” on a general LTL PRO.
