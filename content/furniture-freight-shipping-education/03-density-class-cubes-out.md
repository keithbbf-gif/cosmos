# Density, class, and why furniture cubes out

Freight is a business of air. Carriers sell trailer space. A hardwood island
is heavy for a pillow and light for a steel plate. It occupies a rectangle
the size of a small appliance and it cannot be stacked like cans. That is
why the invoice looks insulting if you only believe in pounds.

## Class is a filing system for air

National Motor Freight Classification (NMFC) puts commodities into classes.
Lower class generally means denser, tougher, cheaper per hundredweight.
Higher class means the opposite: light, awkward, fragile, or all three.
Furniture spends a lot of its life in the high-class neighborhood because it
is mostly box and finish.

People quote class numbers like sports scores. Do not memorize a number from
a 2017 partner spreadsheet and tattoo it on the shop wall. Look up the
item, the packaging, and the density the carrier is actually using. Cartoned
furniture, crated furniture, and “loose” furniture are not the same filing.
A knockdown piece in a carton is not a fully assembled island.

If a portal once said an island should move UPS Small Parcel, that was a
system’s dream, not a density lecture. The piece was still a cube with a
finish.

## Density is homework you can do with a tape

Density is pounds per cubic foot.

1. Measure the handling unit as it ships — carton or crate, not the pretty
   catalog drawing.
2. Length × width × height, in inches, divided by 1728, is cubic feet.
3. Weight ÷ cubic feet is density.

An island that ships 34 × 28 × 40 and weighs about a hundred pounds is
roughly 10 × 6.5 × 3.3 feet of air? No. Do the inches: 34 × 28 × 40 =
38,080 cubic inches. ÷ 1728 ≈ 22 cubic feet. 100 ÷ 22 ≈ 4.5 pounds per
cubic foot. That is pillow territory. That is why freight class climbs and
why a “cheap” island can have an expensive ride.

A stack of cutting boards in a tight carton is a different citizen. Same
shop, different air.

## Cubing out vs weighing out

A trailer *weighs out* when it hits legal weight before it is full. Steel
does that. A trailer *cubes out* when it is full of air and still under
weight. Furniture does that. One island does not fill a trailer, but a
day of LTL is a jigsaw of other people’s air. Your piece is paying for the
rectangle it occupies and the fact that a finished top does not want a
pallet of dog food on it.

Blanket-wrap furniture vans sell space even more honestly: they often will
not stack on your finish. You are buying the air above the piece. That is
not a luxury tax. That is the product.

## What gets people hurt

- Declaring a fantasy weight so the quote looks kind. The scale at the
  terminal will not play along. Reweigh fees and class corrections show up
  later, when the customer already thinks shipping was “included.”
- Declaring a fantasy class because last year’s island was “class 125.”
  Packaging changed. The item number changed. The carrier’s rating engine
  changed.
- Quoting “it only weighs a hundred pounds” to a household. They hear
  parcel. They do not hear 22 cubes of air.

## What BBF should put on the sheet

On every freight quote worksheet, write the three numbers: weight, cube,
density. Then the class you actually used and the NMFC item if you have it.
Then the mode. If you cannot fill those, you are guessing, and guessing is
how a $185 island grows a $400 freight argument.

Do not hide cube from the customer on a factory-direct job. You do not need
to lecture them about NMFC. You do need to say: this piece is large for its
weight, so freight is a space charge.
