# Released value, declared value, and cargo insurance

If a $2,000 island is wrecked and the carrier tenders $50, someone
will say the system is broken. The system is doing what the released
value said.

This is the least loved lesson in the pack. It is also the one that
keeps BBF from promising a replacement we cannot fund.

## Released value is not a mystery

Most motor carriers limit cargo liability to a number per pound
unless you pay to raise it. The number lives in the tariff and on
the BOL you did not read. It can be small. Furniture people have
been surprised by this since there were trucks.

A hundred-pound island at a dime a pound is a ten-dollar tragedy.
Even a more generous released value is often not a remake.

This is why “the carrier will make it right” is a sentence that
needs a calculator.

## Declared value

You write a value on the BOL and pay for a higher limit (or you
think you do). Some carriers treat declared value as a liability
increase. Some treat it as a request that still has caps, exclusions,
and a packing standard you failed.

Declaring a value without reading the rule is how you pay a fee
and still get a dime.

If we declare, we declare the remake-and-freight number we would
actually need, not the wholesale we wish we still had.

## Insurance

A cargo policy — ours, the customer’s, a third-party shipper’s
interest policy — is a different product than carrier liability.
It can cover the gap. It has its own deductibles, exclusions
(improper packing, delay, inherent vice, moisture), and clocks.

Households sometimes think their homeowner’s policy will care. It
might, after a fight. Do not sell that hope as a plan.

BBF should know what *our* policy does when the piece is on our
truck vs on a common carrier. Those are not the same sentence.

## Inherent vice and wood

Carriers love the phrase. It means the thing broke because of what
it is: a check in a wide top, a finish that moved, a joint that
opened. Sometimes they are right. Sometimes they are hiding a drop.
Origin photos and a porch inspection that shows impact are how you
separate vice from violence.

## A napkin you should actually run

Island sells $1,100. Weighs 102 lb. Released value, if it were a
dime a pound, is about $10. Even at $0.50/lb it is about $51. A
refinish is hundreds. A remake is the island plus two freights.

Write those four numbers once per SKU family. Then decide, as a
shop, whether we buy declared value, buy a policy, or self-insure
with our mouth shut about “the carrier will replace it.”

## What to tell a customer

“The trucking company’s automatic responsibility is often a small
per-pound number, not the price of the piece. If you need the piece
insured for replacement, we have to buy that on purpose. If we
don’t, a bad ride can be a repair conversation, not a new island.”

If BBF’s practice is to self-insure factory-direct — we remake and
chase the carrier for scraps — say that, and price it into freight
or into margin. Silent self-insurance is how shops go quiet.
