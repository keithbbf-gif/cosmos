# Mixed freight — the sofa that scars your island

Your island rarely travels alone. Even on a furniture van, it has
neighbors. On LTL, it has strangers.

The sofa problem is any neighbor that can leave a mark: upholstery dye,
a metal foot, a pallet of something with a smell, a stone A-frame, a
leaking carton of who-knows. The neighbor does not hate you. The neighbor
is heavy and close.

## On commodity LTL

Mix is the business model. Your carton is in a wall. The wall is built
for cube, not for sheen. This is why the carton has to be the argument.
“Do not stack” stickers are a suggestion to a person who is trying to
close a door.

If the mix includes hazardous, food, or dirty industrial, you will not
always know. You will know when the carton smells like the neighbor.
Photograph the carton at delivery before anyone kisses it to a white
wall.

## On a furniture van

Mix is supposed to be a designed mix: pads, posts, lanes. The sofa
problem still happens when:

- a dye-transfer arm rubs a light maple
- a metal sofa foot finds a pad gap
- a mattress bag sweats on an oil finish
- the crew is late and “it will ride”

Ask specialized carriers how they separate case goods from upholstered
on the same truck. If they look at you like you asked about poetry, they
are running a mixed truck with hope.

Light finishes and oil finishes are dye magnets. If BBF is still in the
honest-wood business, say so at booking. A dark espresso might forgive a
neighbor. A natural oak island will not.

## Exclusive use is how you buy solitude

If the piece cannot have neighbors, you are buying exclusive use or a
company truck or a crate that makes neighbors irrelevant. That is a
price. It is also a clean story.

Do not whisper exclusive use and then accept a “we’ll tuck it in” to
save a day.

## Claims that are really mix claims

A linear rub at sofa-arm height. A pattern that matches a strap or a
foot. Dirt in a band where a pallet sat. These are mix. They are still
carrier problems if the carrier chose the mix and the wrap.

Your file needs the pattern, a ruler in the photo, and the origin photo
that shows the face clean. Without origin, mix looks like shop grit.

## Dye transfer, said so finishing does not get blamed first

A pale oak or a natural oil that comes back with a ghost of
burgundy on one flank is often a sofa arm or a red carton that
sweated in heat. Finishing will be asked to “buff it out.” Sometimes
they can. Sometimes they will sand into a story that was never our
stain.

Before anyone touches it, photograph the ghost in raking light and
look at the height. Sofa-arm height is a mix fingerprint. A shop
defect usually does not wear a horizontal band at 24 inches on one
side only.

## What we can do on our dock

- Clean piece, clean first-layer pad if we supply one.
- Corner guards that survive a neighbor.
- A carton when the van is actually LTL.
- A no on a pickup that is already a rolling garage sale.

We cannot inventory the other shipper’s sofa. We can refuse to pretend
mix is not the product.
