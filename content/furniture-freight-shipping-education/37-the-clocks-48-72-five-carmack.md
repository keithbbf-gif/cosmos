# The 48–72 hour talk, the five-day talk, and Carmack

There is not one clock. There are at least three, and furniture
people mash them into a folk song. The folk song loses claims.

This draft is the distinction BBF should be able to teach without
pretending we are the cargo clerk’s lawyer.

## Clock A — the retail / household folk clock (48–72 hours)

Marketplaces, designers, and a lot of factory packets say: look at
it within two or three days and tell someone. That is good manners
and good evidence. It is often *stricter* than the law and *looser*
than a specific tariff’s inspection request.

Use it as a customer habit: open in daylight, soon. Do not use it
as the only sentence we know.

## Clock B — concealed-damage notice (often five days in NMFC country)

For many motor carriers that live under NMFC claims rules, concealed
damage is supposed to be reported to the delivering carrier on
discovery, with a request for inspection, confirmed in writing.

A widely used NMFC item (the 300135 family, as carriers republish
it) has said, in substance: if more than **five days** pass between
delivery and the report / inspection request, the consignee has to
bring *reasonable evidence* that the wound did not happen after
delivery. A clean delivery receipt makes that climb steeper.

Details move. Some carriers still talk like older 15-day habits.
Some furniture programs still print 48 hours. **Read the guide you
booked.** Teach the habit that survives all of them: notice the
same day you see it, in writing, with photos, and ask for
inspection.

Notice is not the formal claim. Notice is the flare.

## Clock C — the formal claim (Carmack, nine months minimum)

Interstate motor carriers in the United States live under the
Carmack Amendment (49 U.S.C. § 14706). They may not give you *less
than nine months* from delivery to file a claim, or *less than two
years* from a written disallowance to sue. They can give you more.
They cannot shrink those floors in the fine print and have it
stick.

49 C.F.R. Part 370 is the federal claims-process neighborhood:
what a claim has to contain, how they acknowledge, how they
dispose. The cargo clerk’s checklist comes from this world plus
their tariff.

So: a customer who waits six months to *mention* a crushed corner
they saw on the porch is in trouble on Clock B even if Clock C is
still open. A customer who flared on day one and files the dollar
demand on day forty is usually still in the legal room.

## What a “claim” has to be

Not a vibe. Typically: the shipment identified (PRO, dates), a
statement that the carrier is liable, and a *specific amount*.
Plus the documents in
[39-building-a-claim-file.md](39-building-a-claim-file.md).

An email that says “we’re unhappy” is not a claim. An email that
says “PRO 123, delivered 3/2, demand $1,840 for refinish and
freight, see attached” is in the neighborhood.

## Why BBF must not collapse the clocks

If we tell a designer “you have nine months, relax,” they will
relax past the inspection request and then we will all be telling
a story about inherent vice and living-room kids.

If we tell a household “48 hours or you get nothing,” we may be
harsher than Carmack and harsher than the tariff — useful as a
nudge, ugly as a legal conclusion, and sometimes wrong for a
noted obvious exception that was already on the POD.

## A sentence we can stand on

“Write what you see before you sign. If you find something after
you open it, photograph it, keep the packaging, and email the
carrier and us the same day with the PRO and a request to
inspect. The formal claim with a dollar number can follow; the
flare cannot wait.”
