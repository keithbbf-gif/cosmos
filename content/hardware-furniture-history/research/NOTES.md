# Research notes

Stage 1. Working notes. Not the essays.

## The object class

Furniture hardware is not architectural ironmongery, though they share smiths and later factories. A butt hinge on a room door and a butt hinge on a wardrobe are cousins. A till lock, a campaign flush pull, a 35 mm cup hinge — those are furniture problems. This series stays with furniture unless a door lock (Bramah, Chubb, Yale) changed what a cabinet could do.

Three families, because that is how a body meets a piece:

- **Hinges** — let a plane rotate without falling off.
- **Pulls** — give the hand a place that is not the wood.
- **Locks** — decide who is allowed to rotate or pull.

Everything else (casters, stays, catches, escutcheons, corner guards, screws) is either a sibling or the dating evidence.

## Hinges — working timeline

- Ancient and medieval furniture often avoids a metal hinge: leather, wooden pintles, pegged lids. Metal shows up where weight or status demands it.
- Wrought strap hinges: the default visible hinge on chests and cupboard doors into the 18th century in vernacular work. Decorative ends (bean, spear, rat-tail) are smith's extra heat, not a catalog SKU.
- H and HL hinges: surface-mounted colonial American (and English) grammar, 17th–18th c. The L wraps a stile so the door cannot sag off a pair of butts that were never there. Often painted with the case. Reproductions are everywhere; look at the bevel and the nail/screw story.
- Butt hinges: mortised, knuckle in the reveal. They win when cabinetmakers want the door to read as wood, not iron. Common on finer 18th-c work and then on nearly everything.
- Butterfly / surface decorative hinges: more jewelry than structure on later pieces; earlier they can be honest surface hinges.
- Parliament hinges: offset so a door can swing past 180° and lie against a wall. More architecture than casework, but they show up on large cupboards.
- Knife hinges: thin leaves in a saw kerf, 18th-c fine work. Almost invisible closed. Easy to wreck with a fat screwdriver.
- Rule-joint / drop-leaf hinges: unequal leaves, countersinks on the *reverse* of a normal butt, pin aligned with the molded arc. The wood carries the dinner plates; the hinge only locates the rotation.
- Continuous / piano hinge: one long knuckle. 19th c onward. Piano lids, boxes, campaign falls, cheap modern boxes that want no layout.
- Soss invisible hinge: Joseph Soss, idea after a shipboard stumble (company story); U.S. patents in the 1920s. Mortised into the edge. A different concealment than a Euro cup.
- European cup hinge: Blum 1964 into production (after ANUBA 1958). Industry standard 35 mm cup. CLIP 1985 is the tool-free marriage of door to body. Soft-close integrated later (CLIP top BLUMOTION 2009; BLUMOTION as a family from 2001). Salice, Grass, Häfele are the other names in the shop, not just Blum.

## Pulls — working timeline

- Medieval / early modern chests: iron rings, hasps, drop handles. The pull and the lock often share a plate.
- William and Mary: cast brass drops, pear or round, often hollow-backed, thin chased backplates, cotter-pin clinch inside the drawer.
- Queen Anne into Chippendale: post-and-bail, thicker plates, pierced rococo / willow / batwing. Posts threaded, crude nuts inside. Chippendale draws brasses; Birmingham and London founders cast them. Do not write "Chippendale made the handles."
- Hepplewhite / Federal: stamped and rolled plates, ovals, urns, eagles. High-pressure rolling leaves wrinkles on the back of honest period plates (Parcher). Still often Birmingham brass on American mahogany.
- Empire / Rococo Revival / Renaissance Revival: hardware recedes; knobs and rings. The wood is doing the talking.
- Eastlake / Aesthetic: stamped incised geometry comes back. Catalogs make this a national style, not a shop style.
- Shaker: wooden mushroom knobs. Hardware as a refusal.
- Victorian glass, porcelain, mineral: the parlor wants a glint. Pressed glass, milk glass, mercury-glass lookalikes. Mineral knobs (colored clay) on cheaper work.
- Arts & Crafts / Mission: hammered copper, iron, simple bail. Stickley etc. The hammer mark is sometimes a factory texture.
- Art Nouveau: whiplash, organic. Art Deco: stepped, chrome, Bakelite.
- Mid-century: pulls as small sculptures — tapered brass, walnut plugs, ceramic. Then IKEA-era: the pull becomes optional (tip-on, leather tabs, routed finger pulls).
- Japanese tansu: *hikite* / *warabi-te* iron, big lock plates, cloud-dragon chasing. Chinese hardwood cabinets: round lock plates, hasp-and-pin, baitong/paktong on finer work. Do not collapse East Asia into one "oriental brass."

## Locks — working timeline

- Egyptian wooden pin-tumbler is real as a *principle* and over-cited as a bedtime story. Use it as ancestry, not as a furniture lock you will meet.
- Warded furniture locks dominate English casework into the late 18th c (Berry). Rim, flush, mortise. The key's bit snakes past wards. A bent wire laughs at many of them. They still keep honest children and casual servants out. That was often the job.
- Barron 1778: double-acting lever/tumbler. The key must lift to a height, not just miss a ward.
- Bramah 1784: sliders in a cylinder, odd little pipe key, expensive. Used on furniture as well as doors. Challenge lock is shop-window theater with a real mechanism behind it.
- Chubb 1818: detector lever lock. Wrong key or pick over-lifts; lock sulks until reset. Furniture and doors.
- Yale Jr. 1861: patent text is drawers, closets, cupboards. 1865: the flat key that emptied the old bit-key from the front door — and later from a lot of cabinets. Cam locks and cylinder cabinet locks are the grandchildren.
- Furniture-specific bodies: till lock (drawer, often the top drawer of a desk or chest, sometimes locking a rail that locks the others), box/chest lock (hasp or hook), wardrobe lock (longer bolt), rolltop/tambour locks, desk gallery locks.
- Escutcheons are not locks. They are the lock's face. They get replaced even more often than pulls.

## Dating traps (write these into the essays, do not bury them)

1. **Replaced pulls.** Victorian owners "updated" Georgian chests. Colonial Revival owners "restored" them back, often wrongly.
2. **One modern screw.** A Phillips head is a repair until five of them agree.
3. **Screw lore is mushy.** Sources split ~1812 partial machine thread, ~1846/1848 gimlet point, ~1856 machine slot. Treat as a band, not a birthday.
4. **Bright brass.** Fresh polish can make 1780 look like 1980. Patina is evidence; stripping it is vandalism dressed as love.
5. **Pot metal / zinc.** 20th-c die-cast that intercrystalline-corrodes ("zinc pest"). Crumbled pulls on otherwise sound veneer.
6. **Soss or Euro hinge on an "18th-century" door.** Someone wanted the door to work.
7. **Cut nails vs wire nails vs screws** must agree with dovetails and saw marks. Hardware alone is a witness, not a verdict.

## Open questions (do not fake answers)

- Exact year ANUBA left the farrier trade and entered furniture shops as a *common* fitting — 1958 is production, not saturation.
- Who first standardized the 35 mm cup across makers. Blum popularized; "the" inventor of the cup format is a fight I am not settling from web pages.
- Waterbury, CT brass vs Birmingham on American Federal hardware: both real; ratios want a catalog census I do not have.
- Chinese cabinet lock typology needs a better primary (classical hardwood scholarship) than dealer blogs. Draft 35 stays careful.
- Hobbs's 51 hours: widely repeated; treat as the received Exhibition story, not a stopwatch I held.
