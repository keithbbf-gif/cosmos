# Outlines

Stage 2. One argument each. Drafts in `../drafts/` keep these numbers.

## 01 — The thing you never look at
The photograph is always the carving. The evidence is the hinge, the pull, the lock. Hardware is how furniture admits it has to move. Open the series on that admission.

## 02 — A hinge is a joint that admits it
A wood joint pretends permanence. A hinge is a joint that scheduled its own failure modes: wear, sag, pin walk. That honesty is the whole craft.

## 03 — Wrought iron before the foundry
Before cast and stamp, a hinge is a smith's drawing-out. Hammer, fuller, the fat at the barrel. Machine evenness is a later dialect.

## 04 — Strap hinges and the blacksmith's signature
The strap is a visible sentence on a chest. Decorative ends are extra heats. Read the nail pattern before you trust the fleur-de-lis.

## 05 — H and HL: American colonial grammar
H and HL are not rustic decoration. They are a way to hang a door on a stile without a mortise the shop may not want to cut. Paint hid them. Reproductions shout.

## 06 — The butt hinge hides, and that's the point
Mortising the knuckle into the reveal is a taste decision: wood first. Once butts win, the hinge becomes a commodity and a dating problem.

## 07 — Butterfly, parliament, and other theatrical hinges
Some hinges want to be seen. Some want a door to lie flat against a wall. Theater is still engineering.

## 08 — Knife hinges and furniture that pretends it has none
Fine 18th-c doors that vanish into a carcase. Kerf leaves. One fat screwdriver ends the illusion.

## 09 — Rule joints and the drop-leaf problem
Dinner weight cannot hang on screws. The molded joint carries the leaf; the special hinge only locates the arc. Named for a carpenter's rule.

## 10 — Piano hinges: one long argument
A continuous knuckle is a refusal to choose two or three points. Useful. Also how cheap boxes hide bad layout.

## 11 — Joseph Soss and the disappearing door
Not Ralph. A shipboard stumble (company story) and 1920s patents. Mortise concealment is not a Euro cup. Different disappearing.

## 12 — Blum, the 35 mm cup, and the kitchen that forgot its face
1952 horseshoe stud, 1958 ANUBA, 1964 concealed hinge. Overlay kitchens stop showing knuckles. The door becomes a slab.

## 13 — ANUBA to CLIP: how a horseshoe stud remade the cabinet
Follow Blum's own dates through MODUL (1977), CLIP (1985), soft-close. Tool-free mounting is a labor history, not a gadget footnote.

## 14 — The pull as a handshake
You meet a chest with your hand, not your eye. A pull is a designed first contact. Bad pulls bruise. Missing pulls are a speech.

## 15 — Rings, drops, and the medieval chest
Iron ring and hasp as one thought. The chest is luggage and safe. Later brass drops remember this without admitting it.

## 16 — Brass from Birmingham
Anonymous catalogues, 33 founders by 1770, brass crossing the Atlantic onto American mahogany. The town, not the cabinetmaker, often made the shine.

## 17 — Chippendale's backplates (and who actually cast them)
*Director* plates and the Harewood invoice. Pattern, cast, chase. William Bent and the London/Birmingham split. Do not give Chippendale the foundry.

## 18 — Federal eagles and stamped ambition
Rolled plates, patriotic stamps, Birmingham still in the mix. Parcher's wrinkles and square nuts. Nationhood as a drawer handle.

## 19 — The Shaker knob
Wood, mushroom, refusal. A theology you can grab. Locks still exist; ostentation does not.

## 20 — Porcelain, mineral, and the Victorian parlor
Clay and glaze as hardware. Mineral knobs as the cheap cousin. The parlor wants a glint that wood will not give.

## 21 — Glass knobs: light as hardware
Pressed glass, milk glass, the mercury look. They catch lamp light and they shear in a moving van. Beauty with a known failure.

## 22 — Cup pulls and the apothecary drawer
A finger under a hood. Density of small drawers. Later "bin pull" revival is nostalgia with better screws.

## 23 — Eastlake's incised honesty
Stamped geometry after the Revival knobs. Catalog Eastlake vs Eastlake's book. Honesty as a factory texture.

## 24 — Hammered copper, Arts & Crafts
The hammer mark as moral. Factory hammers learned the dialect. Stickley and the problem of sincere texture.

## 25 — Nouveau curves, Deco steps
Whiplash then machine steps. Chrome and Bakelite enter. Two modernisms, two hands.

## 26 — Bakelite, catalin, and the plastic that still smells
Early plastic pulls that outlasted the veneer. Color as hardware. The smell in a shut drawer is not nostalgia; it is phenol.

## 27 — Mid-century: the pull that wanted to be sculpture
Tapered brass, walnut plugs, ceramic. Then the pull starts to vanish into the slab again.

## 28 — Japanese tansu iron
*Hikite*, *warabi-te*, lacquer-baked plates, Sendai clouds and dragons. Iron as the face of the chest. Not "oriental brass."

## 29 — The lock before the key was a key
Wooden pins, hasps, wards. A lock is a social device before it is a puzzle. Furniture locks were often about the household, not the burglar.

## 30 — Wards: security as theater
Berry's pre-1778 furniture locks. A bit that snakes. A wire that doesn't care. Theater still works on the people it was built for.

## 31 — Barron, Bramah, Chubb: the lock race
1778, 1784, 1818. Lever, slider, detector. Hobbs at 1851 as the public ending that wasn't an ending.

## 32 — Yale started on drawers
Patent 31,278 is cupboards and drawers. 1865 is the flat key. The front door stole a furniture problem and never gave it back.

## 33 — Till locks, box locks, the desk that keeps secrets
The top drawer that locks the rest. Gallery, tambour, wardrobe bolt. Secret compartments are usually just a lock you didn't see.

## 34 — Escutcheons: the keyhole as jewelry
Cover plates, drops, thread escutcheons. Replaced more often than the lock. The hole remembers the old shape.

## 35 — Chinese round plates and the pin-and-hasp cabinet
Round lock plates, hasp and pin, baitong on finer work. A different theory of "locked" than a mortise till. Stay humble on typology.

## 36 — Campaign brass: furniture that travels
Flush pulls, corner guards, skeletonized early handles. Packing, stacking, tropical glue. Brass as a second joinery.

## 37 — Keys you can still feel in your pocket
Bit, pipe, flat, barrel. Weight and bow as class marks. The key is the lock's public half.

## 38 — The empty hole tells you more than the plate
Ghost holes, filled mortises, extra posts. Read the wood the hardware left. This is the dating essay's twin.

## 39 — Dating by the screw (and why that's a trap)
Handmade, partial machine, gimlet, machine slot, Phillips, Robertson. Bands, not birthdays. One screw is a repair.

## 40 — Pot metal and the hardware that eats itself
Zinc die-cast, zinc pest, crumbled Deco and cheap 20th-c pulls. The furniture outlives the handle.

## 41 — Restoration: when not to polish
Patina as record. Matching the screw, not the Pinterest plate. Reversible work. Love that looks like theft.

## 42 — Catalog hardware and the death of the village smith
Sears, Birmingham pattern books, Grand Rapids. Style becomes a page number. The smith doesn't vanish; he gets a SKU.

## 43 — Ormolu and the poison gilt
Mercury gilding, French mounts, the line between hardware and sculpture. Beauty with a body count in the workshop.

## 44 — The caster, the stay, the catch
Hardware that isn't a handle. Quadrant stays, table forks, bullet catches, casters that wreck floors and save backs.

## 45 — What IKEA did to the hinge (and why that isn't only a loss)
Cam locks, dowels, tip-on, Euro cups on particleboard. Decoration leaves. Function becomes a system. The series ends on what we traded.
