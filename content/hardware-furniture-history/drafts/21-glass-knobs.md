A glass knob is a lamp that learned to be a handle.

Pressed glass, cut glass, milk glass, the ones that look like they have mercury in them and usually don't. Clear, amber, amethyst, a watery green. Under a table lamp they do a trick wood cannot do: they carry the room's light into the drawer front. That is why they exist. Not because glass is strong. Glass is a terrible idea for a handle and everyone who has packed a dresser knows it.

They shear. They chip at the brass collet. The screw loosens and the knob turns without the drawer, a little planet with no tide. In a moving van they become gravel. I have swept that gravel. I have also kept a single survivor in a cup because the color was a kind of blue I do not see in new production, and I am not above sentiment.

Depression-era glass knobs get a lot of talk. Some of them are that. Some of them are later, or earlier, or just cheap. Color is not a date. The mount is a better witness: the way the brass collar is spun, the thread, whether the glass is pressed in a mold that still has a seam you can feel with a nail. Pairing is a fantasy. Sets get broken and mixed. A dresser with seven matching knobs and one milk-glass orphan is a dresser that has been loved.

There is a cruelty in how pretty they are. They invite a child's mouth, a ring, a hard pull. They do not forgive. If you put them on a piece that gets daily use — kitchen, kid's room — you are choosing beauty over a future. I have done it. I will probably do it again. I will not call it wise.

Sandwich glass and the later pressed houses get invoked the way Chippendale gets invoked: as a name that makes a knob cost more. Some knobs are that good. Most are a mold and a collar. The collar is the weak point. Brass thin as foil, rolled over the glass, then a screw that loosens a quarter turn every year. Tighten it a half turn too far and the glass stars. I have starred one. I still have the star.

Light as hardware. That is the whole pitch. When the room is dark they go quiet and the dresser is just wood again. When the room is lit they are the first thing your hand wants, which is a problem if they are also the first thing that will break. Handle them like they know that. They do not. You do.
