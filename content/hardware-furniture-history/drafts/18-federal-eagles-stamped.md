The eagle on the drawer is not wildlife. It is a stamp.

Federal furniture — Hepplewhite and Sheraton in the American mouth — wants ovals, urns, husks, a little classical grammar on a mahogany front. High-pressure rolling and stamping can put that grammar on thin brass all day. The plate is no longer a pierced rococo island. It is a shallow relief, a medallion, a patriotic souvenir that happens to have a bail. Flags, drums, eagles. The nation is new. The hardware says so every time you open a sock drawer.

A lot of that brass is still Birmingham. The joke is so good I almost don't want to explain it: American identity, English metal. The catalogues do not care about your revolution. They care about a pattern that will sell. Waterbury and the Connecticut brass trades come on later and thicker; I will not pretend I have a census of which oval came from which river. I will say the Atlantic was not a wall for small cargo.

Parcher again, because she looked at the backs: period rolled plates can show wrinkles from the rolling. Reproductions often do not. Earlier work likes thick square iron nuts. If the back is clean as a new pan and the nut is hex and zinc, you are in a different century no matter what the eagle swears.

Then the taste turns. Empire, the revivals, the heavy wood — hardware recedes. Knobs. Rings. The drawer front wants to be a column, not a medal. Stamped brass will come back with Eastlake, louder and cheaper. The Federal oval is a specific ambition: the republic as a thing you can grab. It lasts in the catalogs long after the republic's furniture has moved on, which is how you get a 1930s bedroom set with an eagle that thinks it is 1795.

Urns and husks are the quieter Federal. They travel better than eagles if you are tired of being shouted at. An oval with a classical pot is still a stamp. It is still often English brass on American veneer. The patriotism was optional. The rolling mill was not.

I like the stamped ones that still have a little softness in the relief. The eagle is not a coin strike. It is a press that almost made it. Ambition, and the metal showing the effort. That is more American than the eagle, if I am allowed a cheap line. I will allow it once. Then I will turn the plate over and look for the wrinkle. The wrinkle is the part the nation did not intend to print.
