A cup pull is a hood for a finger.

You do not wrap it. You do not pinch it. You hook from below, the way you hook a ledger, and the drawer comes out in a straight line because that is how a small drawer wants to come. Apothecary chests, spice drawers, card files, the dense stacks of little mouths in a pharmacy or a seed shop — those fronts do not have room for a bail that swings and rattles. They have room for a cup.

Brass cups, stamped or cast. Iron cups on plainer work. Later, the "bin pull" in every revival kitchen, often too big, often too shiny, often on a drawer that is a bread bin and not a place for pills. I do not hate the revival. I hate the scale mistakes. A cup the size of a gravy boat on a three-inch drawer is a joke the kitchen did not ask for.

The old ones wear on the lip. That is where the finger goes. A polished crescent on an otherwise dull cup is a use record. If the whole cup is bright, someone had a rag and a political feeling about tarnish. If there is no wear and the chest claims 1880, look at the screws.

Density is the point of the form. An apothecary chest is a wall of labels and cups. The hardware has to stay out of the next drawer. A swinging bail would clatter like a wind chime. A knob would bruise the drawer above. The cup tucks in. It is polite in a way Chippendale piercing is not trying to be.

Card catalogs and library drawers learned the same hood. So did sewing-machine cabinets and some dental chests. Once you have a grid of small fronts, the cup is almost inevitable. A knob farm would look like a field of boils. I have seen that farm on a restored spice chest. It was sincere. It was also wrong in the hand: you hit the knob above with your knuckle every time.

I have built a run of small drawers and cheapened out with routed finger pulls. They work. They are not cups. A cup is a little roof. It catches dust and also catches your hand when you are not looking. That is the apothecary motion: not looking, reaching, knowing. The label does the seeing. The cup does the rest.
