Not every hinge wants to disappear.

A butterfly hinge is a pair of wings. Surface-mounted, decorative outline, the pin as a body. On a small box or a cupboard door it can be honest structure — two leaves, a knuckle, screws that you can see and therefore can also judge. On a lot of later work it is theater: a shape that says *old* the way a font says *old*. I do not mind theater. I mind theater that pretends it is load analysis.

The parliament hinge is theater of a different kind. The leaves are offset. The pin stands out from the face so the door can swing past a hundred and eighty degrees and lie against the wall like a book cover that decided to rest. You meet them on shutters, on large cupboard doors in tight rooms, on anything that had to get out of its own way. The name comes from the buildings, or from the trade's habit of naming anything official-sounding after Parliament; I have not chased the first catalog entry to a first door in Westminster, and I am not going to fake that chase. The geometry is the fact. The offset is the fact. A door that can kiss the wall is a different door than a door that stops at ninety and sulks in the room.

There are others in the troupe. A screen hinge that folds both ways. A counter-flap hinge that lets a bar top lift and lie flat. A card-table hinge that lives with a rule joint and a felt that cannot be snagged. A chest hinge with a stop so the lid does not go over back and punch the wall. Each one is a special case that a butt refused to handle.

I call them theatrical because they make a show of a problem. The butterfly shows the problem as a pretty silhouette. The parliament shows the problem as a door that refuses to occupy the room. A stop hinge shows the problem as a lid that will not be allowed to learn gravity the hard way. This is not an insult. Theater is how you teach a body what to do without a paragraph of instructions.

If you find a parliament hinge on a dainty bedside cupboard, someone catalog-shopped a word. If you find a butterfly on a heavy maple door with two screws per wing, someone catalog-shopped a shape. The old ones, when they are old, have wear in the right place: a polished arc on the offset of a parliament, a slight belly in a butterfly wing where the file finished what the casting started. Theater that worked for a century earns the costume.
