The smith does not vanish. He gets a SKU.

Birmingham's anonymous catalogues are already a death and a birth. You do not walk to the forge with a lid. You point at a page. The page has a price written in after the copperplate. That is the village becoming a mailbag. Sears and Montgomery Ward will finish the job for American kitchens and farmhouses: a hinge, a catch, a knob, a number. Grand Rapids will ship a bedroom set with the pulls already chosen so you cannot even point. The pointing was done in a factory office.

I do not mourn as hard as the story wants me to. A village smith was not available to every farm. A catalog hinge that works is a democratic object. The wound is particularity. Two chests from the same fire rhymed. Two chests from the same page photocopy. When everything photocopies, dating becomes a paper chase: which year the page existed, which finish the company used that season. The iron will not tell you a person's hammer anymore.

Colonial Revival is catalog memory. It sells you 1760 by number. The number is 1915, or 1938, or 1982. Each of those years has a look if you handle enough of them — the brass alloy, the plating, the screw. People still say "original colonial hardware" about a 1938 page. The page is original. The century is not.

The smith's grandson is on the press. I said that in the Eastlake draft and I mean it here as the actual ending. Skills move. A man who would have drawn out a strap now sets a die. That is not nothing. It is a different pride. The pride is repeatability. Repeatability is how a kitchen gets built in a week.

If you want a smith now, you can find one. They are a luxury, which is a joke the eighteenth century would have understood in reverse. Handmade iron is a choice you pay for because the catalog exists. Without the catalog, handmade was just how iron happened. Death is the wrong word. Displacement is the word. The village is a page. The page is in a PDF. Your hinge arrived in a bag. It will work. It will not remember you.
