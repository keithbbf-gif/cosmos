A drop-leaf table is a polite way to admit your room is too small.

The leaf hangs, then it rises, then it is supposed to take plates and elbows as if it had always been a table. If you hang that leaf on ordinary butts, the screws and the pin take the dinner. They will, for a while. Then the leaf droops a little, the joint opens, crumbs go in, and someone puts a matchbook under the sag and calls it character.

The old answer is a rule joint: a shouldered roundover on the top, a matching cove on the leaf. When the leaf is up, the cove sits on the roundover and the wood carries the weight. The hinge is only there to keep the rotation honest. When the leaf is down, the edge reads as an ovolo, a molded lip, not a butchered butt joint. The name comes from the brass joint on a folding carpenter's rule — the same interlocking idea, small enough to put in a pocket.

You cannot use a hardware-store butt. The leaves of a drop-leaf hinge are unequal. The short leaf goes to the fixed top. The long leaf spans the cove on the leaf. The countersinks are on the reverse of a normal hinge, because the knuckle is mortised into the underside and the pin has to sit on the center of that molded arc. Get the pin off the center and the joint binds or opens a mouth. I have done both. The mouth is more embarrassing. You can hear it when someone puts a glass down.

Supports still matter. A swing arm, a finger, a gate leg — the rule joint carries distributed weight; it does not replace a leg. People forget that and then blame the hinge. The hinge was never the table.

I like this hardware because it is humble and particular. It does not show. It has a job that a generic part will not do. It is the opposite of a butterfly on a jewelry box. Nobody bought a drop-leaf hinge because it looked like 1760. They bought it because gravy is heavy.

If the underside of an old table has three mismatched hinges and a scar where a fourth used to be, believe the scars. Leaves get replaced. Hinges get replaced. The molded edge, if it still mates, is the witness that the idea was understood. A router-cut rule joint with the wrong hinge is a common modern tell: someone had the bits and not the hardware. The bits are the easy part.
