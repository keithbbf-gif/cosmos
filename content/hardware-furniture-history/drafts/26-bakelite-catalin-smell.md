Open a shut drawer from the 1930s and sometimes the first fact is not visual. It is phenol. A sweet chemical, old radio, old billiard ball, old handle. People call it nostalgia. It is a polymer.

Bakelite and its prettier cousins — Catalin and the other cast phenolics — made pulls that could be color in a way brass is not color. Butterscotch, jade, cherry, marbled. They could be cheap and still look like a decision. They could also outlast the veneer. I have seen a dresser whose mahogany paper has lifted like bark and whose knobs are still glossy little planets. The hardware won.

They crack. They fade in a sunned window. They are brittle if you lean a hip. They are not pot metal; they do not crumble into grey cancer. They snap. A snapped Bakelite pull is a clean break, almost polite. You can glue it and see the line forever. I leave the line. The line is a year.

Collectors get weird about the names. Catalin this, Bakelite that, "real" phenolic. I care less about the trademark than about the fact that furniture learned plastic before the kitchen learned Formica, or at least alongside. The pull was a test. If the hand accepted plastic, the rest of the century could happen.

Do not polish them with anything heroic. Do not soak them. Do not put them in a dishwasher because a forum said so. Wipe. Dry. If you want them brighter, you are asking a 1938 color to compete with a 2020 LED. It will lose. Let it lose.

The hot-needle test is a collector trick I will mention and not recommend on a piece you love: phenolic smells formaldehyde-sharp when wounded; later plastics smell like a grocery bag. Wounding a knob to name it is a kind of scholarship I can live without. The color, the weight, the coldness, the way a chip looks — those are enough for a furniture draft.

The smell in a shut drawer is information. It says the plastic is still the plastic. When the smell is gone and the knobs are new acrylic, the dresser has been through a cleanup. Cleanup is a kind of forgetting. I am not in charge of your forgetting. I am in charge of saying that the smell was a witness.
