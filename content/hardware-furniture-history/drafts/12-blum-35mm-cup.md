Julius Blum was a farrier. 1 March 1952, Höchst, Austria, a horseshoe stud so a horse would not slip. That is the company's own first sentence. I like it because it is not a design-school sentence. It is a nail.

The first furniture fitting is the ANUBA hinge, 1958: a three-knuckle thing for doors, windows, furniture — still a hinge you can see. The sentence that changes kitchens is 1964: the first concealed hinge goes into production. Not "is imagined." Into production. After that, a cabinet door can be a slab. Overlay. No knuckle in the reveal. No face frame if you do not want one. The hinge lives in a cup bored into the back of the door — 35 mm has become the shop standard, about 13 mm deep in ordinary practice — and on a plate on the carcase. From the front, the kitchen is a set of planes. From the inside, it is a small machine.

I am not going to give Blum the entire invention of the European cup as if Grass and Salice and a dozen unnamed toolmakers were not in the same weather. I will give Blum the dates they published, and the fact that when a cabinetmaker says "Blum hinge" they often mean the whole class, the way people say Kleenex. That kind of theft is a kind of victory.

What the cup did to the face is the part I care about. For two hundred years a door told you it was a door: a rail, a stile, a panel, a knuckle, a pull. The cup hinge, plus particleboard, plus a 35 mm bit in a drill press, plus overlay, made a door that is a rectangle of laminate. You open it because you know where kitchens keep cups, not because the hardware invited your hand. Later they even took the pull (tip-on, BLUMOTION, SERVO-DRIVE — the company timeline is a list of ways to avoid a knob). The kitchen forgot its face on purpose. Clean was the moral.

I have bored a lot of 35 mm holes. The bit is greedy. It wants to skate. The cup wants to sit at a bore distance someone chose from a table — 3 mm, 4 mm, 5 mm — and if you pick the wrong table the door hits the next door or shows a reveal that looks drunk. This is not romance. This is a jig and a swear word. It is also why a 1960s-and-after kitchen is easy to date from the inside and hard to date from a magazine photograph. The photograph is all slab. The inside is cups and plates and a factory stamp.

A farrier's stud to a kitchen that has no knuckles. That is not a metaphor I am forcing. That is the company's own ladder. The horse stopped slipping. The door stopped showing its joints. I am not sure the second thing is as kind as the first, but I live with both. I cook in the second.
