A knife hinge is a vanishing act with a blade.

The leaves are thin. You saw a kerf in the edge of the door and a matching kerf in the carcase, you slide the leaves in, you pin the knuckle so it sits in the corner like a secret. Closed, the door is a plane. Open, you get a little metal glint if you look for it, and most people do not look. Eighteenth-century cabinetmakers who wanted a secretary or a cabinet door to read as architecture used them. So did later people who wanted that same silence and had a catalog.

They are easy to love and easy to wreck. The leaf has no extra meat. A screwdriver that walks off the slot will chew the only face the hinge has. A door that is used as a handle — yanked by the far stile because nobody put a pull where a pull should be — will try to lever the kerf open. Glue in the kerf is a debate I will not pretend to settle for your shop; I will say that a knife hinge that has to do the work of a butt is already in trouble.

I have opened doors that I would have sworn were sliding panels until the knuckle showed. That is the point of the thing: furniture that pretends it has no hardware. It is the opposite of an HL. It is also the ancestor, in spirit, of the Soss and the Euro cup. All three are answers to the same embarrassment. The knife hinge answers it in the thickness of a fine door. The Soss answers it by eating a mortise in the edge. The cup answers it by hiding on the back of a slab. Different centuries, same blush.

Dating them is ugly. Reproductions are good. The tell is often the steel, the machining of the knuckle, the screws, and whether the kerf was sawn by a hand that wandered. A perfect kerf on a door with machine dovetails and a plastic roller catch is a kitchen in costume. A slightly proud leaf on a mahogany door with a lock that still throws — that I will stay in the room with.

If you are building new and you want the silence, use them where the door is light and the pull is honest. Do not ask a knife to hang a pantry. Vanishing is a privilege of things that do not weigh much.
