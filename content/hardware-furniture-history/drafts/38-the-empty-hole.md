Take the plate off.

I mean it. If the piece is yours, if you will put the screws back in the same holes, take the plate off and look at the wood. Extra holes. A filled slot. A shadow of a bigger plate. A pair of posts that do not match the current bail. A mortise for a lock that is not there. A knife-hinge kerf under a pair of butts. This is the essay the photographs cannot write.

Victorian owners updated Georgian chests. Colonial Revival owners "restored" them, often into a fantasy. Kitchen people put cup pulls on everything. Someone always puts bigger screws in stripped holes and calls it fine. The wood keeps the minutes of the meeting.

A ghost of a backplate is a pale oval or a willow silhouette in the oxidized finish. You cannot fake that easily. You can fake a hole. You cannot easily fake twenty years of the sun not hitting the wood under a plate. If the current plate is smaller than the ghost, you are looking at a reduction. If there is no ghost and the finish under the plate is the same color as the rest, the plate and the finish may be the same age — which might be last year.

Filled holes are little graves. Wax, putty, a peg, a mix that has shrunk. Probe gently. I have found three generations of pulls in one drawer front: cotter-pin era, post-and-nut, then a single modern screw knob. That drawer had a social life.

Empty lock mortises tell you the bolt length, the selvedge, whether it was flush or rim. An empty escutcheon rebate tells you the hole was dressed. A hole with no rebate tells you the hole was enough.

I trust empty more than I trust bright. Bright is a story someone is telling me. Empty is a story someone forgot to finish. The unfinished ones are where dating actually happens.

Put the plate back with the same screws if the screws were honest. If the screws were not honest, this is the moment to put honest ones in and stop pretending. The empty hole already told on you. You might as well agree with it.
