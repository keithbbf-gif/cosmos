A lot of American mahogany is wearing English metal.

Birmingham in the later eighteenth century is not a footnote. It is a town that learned to found, stamp, chase, and catalog small brass until the world could order a drawer handle the way it ordered a button. A directory lists thirty-three brass founders there in 1770. The number grew. Trade catalogues — some of them stubbornly anonymous, no founder named on the plate — show bail handles and backplates in rococo fretwork, page after page, prices written in by hand after the copperplate was printed. Those pages crossed water. They sat on a cabinetmaker's bench in Philadelphia and Charleston and London. The chest was local. The shine often was not.

I like the anonymity more than I should. We are trained to want Chippendale's name on everything. The catalogues refuse. They glorify the object. A thousand patterns, no author. That is a factory culture before we had the word. It is also why matching a plate to a "maker" is a sport that humiliates people. Joan Parcher, who has handled more of this than I ever will, still talks about marks as a hunt: Thomas Hands, William Jenkins, a name under a nut if you are lucky. Most plates do not introduce themselves.

Bristol and Liverpool brass, canal boats after the 1760s, Quaker capital — the industrial story is thicker than a furniture essay can carry. Jessica Frankis wrote a thesis on the politics of that trade, the lobbying, the protectionism. Hardware sits in that weather. A drawer pull is a little piece of a town that was arguing about tariffs.

When you hold a period plate, the back can tell on it. Rolling wrinkles. Casting texture. A crude square iron nut, thick, not a hex from a blister pack. The pommel threads that only go halfway. Parcher uses those tells to separate Federal-era work from later reproductions that learned the front and not the back. I have started turning plates over like coins. The face is the costume. The back is the shop.

Do not write "the cabinetmaker made the brass" unless you have a furnace. A few London men did — Chippendale had knowledge, a furnace is mentioned, an invoice for carving patterns and casting and chasing exists for Harewood in 1774. Even then, the smart money is that a lot of the work was bought in from founders in the same lane. Birmingham did the rest, and the rest is most of it.

The town made the shine. The chest took the credit. That is not a scandal. That is how a trade works when it is working.
