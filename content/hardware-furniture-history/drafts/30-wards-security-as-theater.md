J. A. Berry says the English furniture lock before 1778 is, in the main, warded. Rim, flush, or mortise. The key's bit is cut to snake past internal fences. If the bit is the right shape, it turns and the bolt throws. If it is not, it hits a ward and stops.

A bent wire does not care about a lot of those fences. Locksmiths have been saying this for as long as they have wanted to sell you the next lock. They are not wrong. They are also not the audience. The audience was a household. A warded lock keeps an honest child out of the tea. It keeps a casual hand out of the till. It announces, by the fact of a keyhole, that this drawer is spoken for. Theater. Theater works.

I have opened warded furniture locks with the wrong key that was almost the right key. I have also been stopped by a ward I did not respect. The theater is not nothing. It is a maze for a bit, not for a mind. Once minds get professional, you need Barron.

The cases are small art. Some are engraved inside where nobody will see. Some have a locksmith's pride that never makes the catalog. The keys have bows that tell a class — simple loop, fancy bit, a family crest if someone had money and a need to show it on a drawer. The bow is public. The bit is the maze.

Banbury locks — wooden-stock rim locks with the metal sitting in wood — show up more on doors than on drawers, but the conservation literature keeps them in the family. I mention them so "furniture lock" does not shrink to a brass till. Some households locked a cupboard with the same mind that locked a room.

If you pick a warded furniture lock to prove a point, congratulations. You have proved you are not a child. The lock did not fail its job. You failed to be the person it was built against.

Berry's types still matter on the bench. A rim lock sits on the face. A flush lock sits in a cut-out so the plate is even with the wood. A mortise lock lives in a pocket. Each leaves a different scar when it is replaced. Read the scar. A mortise that now holds a cheap cam lock is a twentieth-century sentence in an eighteenth-century pocket. The wards are gone. The theater is a cylinder and a flat key. Different play.
