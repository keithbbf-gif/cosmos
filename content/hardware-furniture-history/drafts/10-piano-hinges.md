A piano hinge is a refusal to choose.

Instead of two butts or three, you run a continuous knuckle the length of the lid. Every inch shares the load. The pin is a long wire. The leaves are thin. You see them on piano lids, which is the name, and on blanket chests, toolboxes, campaign falls, modern cabinets that were designed in a hurry, and cheap boxes that would rather not lay out hinge centers.

I respect the argument. A long lid wants a long hinge. A piano lid is a sail; two butts would teach it to twist. A continuous hinge says: we will not pick favorite inches. That is grown-up engineering dressed as a strip of brass.

I also distrust the argument when it shows up on a box that had the time to do it the other way. A continuous hinge can hide bad layout. No centers to miss. No mortises to chop. Screw it on, clip the length, done. A lot of "clean modern" lids are just that: a piano hinge and a decision not to think. The hinge is not at fault. The hurry is.

Brass continuous hinges tarnish in a way I like. Steel ones paint or plate and then chip along the knuckle. The pin walks out if it was never staked. You find the pin on the floor years later and the lid has a new sag that no one can explain until they look. Stake the pin. Or buy the ones with a knurl. This is not romance. This is Tuesday.

On campaign furniture and on some desks, the continuous hinge is the right special case: a fall that has to be thin, strong, and flush. A secretaire fall with two butts will telegraph the knuckles through a thin board and then split it. A continuous leaf spreads that insult. On a six-dollar trunk from a mall, the same hinge is a way to say *chest* without building one. Both exist. The length of the knuckle will not tell you which one you have. The wood will. The screws will. Whether the leaf was let in or just slapped on the back of a plywood lid will.

Brass versus steel is a class mark and a maintenance mark. Brass files clean and takes a dulling. Steel wants paint or a zinc coat and then shows every chip as a rust freckle along the barrel. I have replaced a steel piano hinge on a tool chest because the pin rusted to the knuckles and the lid opened like a rusty gate. The replacement was brass. It will need the pin staked again in ten years. That is still cheaper than a lid that teaches you about twist.

One long argument. Sometimes it is the only argument that works. Sometimes it is a shrug with a pin through it.
