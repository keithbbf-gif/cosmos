The name on the patents is Joseph Soss. Not Ralph. I wrote Ralph in my head for years because that is how oral shop history works: a name that sounds like a brand gets a first name that sounds like a neighbor. The patents say Joseph. The company story says Joe, born in Romania, Montreal in 1894, contractor, a trip that included the Philippines, a stumble on deck over a cargo-hold hinge, and the idea of a hinge that would not trip anyone again.

I am not going to swear to the stumble. Company histories polish origin stories the way dealers polish brass. I will swear to the patents. Invisible hinge, Joseph Soss, United States, mid-1920s filings, 1928 issues, more through the 1930s. The idea is a linkwork hidden inside two mortised bodies. You cut into the edge of the door and the edge of the frame. Closed, there is no barrel in the reveal. Open, the links unfold like a small steel wrist. It is a different disappearing than a knife hinge and a different disappearing than a Euro cup.

A Soss eats thickness. That is the tax. You need a door with meat, a frame with meat, a mortise that is accurate or the door will wind. When it is right, the door is a slab that levitates. When it is wrong, you have a very expensive way to make a door that never sits down. I have seen both. The wrong ones get blamed on the hinge. The hinge was following the mortise it was given.

The later life of the company — Warren, Michigan, for the car years; Pioneer, Ohio, under Universal Industrial Products — is American industrial weather. The hinge shows up in buildings, elevator cabs, machines, anywhere a pin in the reveal would be an insult. Furniture uses it when the furniture wants to be architecture. A bar cabinet. A flush door in a paneled room. A lid that cannot have a barrel.

Do not confuse this with Blum. People say "concealed hinge" and mean the 35 mm cup on the back of a kitchen door. That is concealment from the front of a cabinet. A Soss is concealment from the edge. You can have a kitchen full of cups and still see a line of barrels if you open the door and look at the old house doors across the room. Different disappearing. Different century. Different tax.

If a piece claims 1810 and the door hangs on Soss links, the door has been to the twentieth century. That is allowed. Call it a repair, or a modernization, or a door that finally works. Do not call it original. Joseph did not invent 1810. He invented a way to stop tripping on the joint.
