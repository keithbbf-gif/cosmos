People treat H and HL hinges like barn decor. They are not. They are a grammar for hanging a door on a stile when the shop is not going to mortise a pair of butts, or when the door is boarded and the frame is a face, or when the whole cupboard is going to be painted and nobody needs the knuckle to vanish.

The H is two verticals and a bar — a surface hinge that looks like the letter because that is the cheapest way to get leaves on both sides of a pin without inventing a new shape. The HL adds a horizontal leg that wraps onto the door or the stile and fights sag. That L is not a flourish. It is a brace. A tall pine door on a cupboard, painted iron-red or grey-green, will try to drop. The L says no, or at least not this year.

You see them on New England cupboards and on English work too. "American colonial" is the dealer tag because that is the room we put them in. The iron does not have a passport. What is American, often, is the paint: hardware the same color as the case, not polished out as jewelry. A lot of reproduction HLs are sold already black and already shiny in the wrong places. Period ones were tools. They took paint. They rusted under the paint. You find them with a knife tip if you are rude, or with a raking light if you are not.

Reproductions shout. They are even. The bevels match left and right as if a CNC had manners. The holes are drilled, not punched, and they come with screws that have a black coating and a Phillips cross. I do not hate them. I hate the sentence they are used to write: this 2008 entertainment center is 1760. No. It is 2008 with a dialect. Fine. Say the dialect.

On an old piece the HL will disagree with itself. The L on the top hinge and the L on the bottom hinge were not twins. The pin may be a wire that was cut and peened. The door will have a wear crescent in the paint where the knuckle rubbed. That crescent is worth more than the hinge if the hinge has been swapped. Swapping is common. Sagging doors get "helped." The helper goes to the big-box aisle and comes home with a letter of the alphabet.

If you are dating, do not date from the letters alone. Date from the way the letters entered the wood: nail type, paint stratigraphy, whether the stile was ever cut for a butt and then patched. An HL on a mortise that wanted a butt is a second draft. The cupboard is allowed to have a second draft. You are not allowed to call it the first.
