A dovetail wants you to believe in forever. That is its job. Pins and tails, glue, a shoulder that closes like a mouth. The joint says: we are one piece now. A good carcase lets you believe it.

A hinge cannot say that. A hinge is a joint that scheduled its own wear. Two leaves, a pin, a set of knuckles that will polish themselves shiny and then sloppy. If you build a door into a cupboard with only wood — a wooden pintle, a leather flap, a lid that is just a board dropped into a rabbet — you are still making a hinge. You are just making one that pretends it is not metal yet.

I like the honesty. Woodworkers get mystical about joinery. Fair enough; I have too. But the mystical joint is the one that refuses motion. The useful joint, on anything you live with, is the one that allows motion and then has the manners to come back to the same plane. That is a hinge. Everything else is sculpture you can sit on.

The failure modes are known. Sag: the door drops a sixteenth and starts kissing the stile. Pin walk: the pintle creeps, the barrel ovalizes. Screw tear-out: you hung too much mahogany on too little thread in end grain. Rust in the knuckle: the door develops a personality. A strap hinge on a chest lid will rack the lid if the straps are not in agreement. A pair of butts that were never let into the same air will bind like a bad marriage. A knife hinge will look perfect until someone leans on the door as if it were a butt.

None of this is a defect in the idea. It is the idea. You asked wood to do a thing wood does not love — rotate — and you hired metal to take the blame. The metal will, for a century, sometimes three, if the pin is sized and the screws find long grain and nobody "tightens" a stripped hole with a fatter screw and a prayer.

When a hinge is visible, it is also a style. When it is hidden, it is still a style. The colonial HL on the face of a cupboard is a sentence in iron. The mortised butt on a Georgian wardrobe is a sentence that says: look at the mahogany, not at me. The Soss in an edge and the Blum cup in the back of a slab are later sentences, both meaning *disappear*, but they disappear in different directions. One eats the thickness of the stile. One eats the back of the door and a plate on the carcase. Same wish, different century, different shop.

I do not think of hinges as hardware first. I think of them as joints that told the truth. The dovetail lied kindly. The hinge said, this will move, and this will wear, and when it wears you will know where to put the oil.
