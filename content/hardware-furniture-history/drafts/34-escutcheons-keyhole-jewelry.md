An escutcheon is not a lock. It is the lock's face, and faces get replaced.

A thread escutcheon is a little oval or circle inlaid around the hole, often bone or ivory or boxwood on finer work, brass on the rest. A plate escutcheon is a brass shield. A drop escutcheon covers the hole with a tiny lid, a politeness, also a way to keep dust out of a ward lock. Keyhole covers on French work can be jewelry that happens to have a function. English work can be a plain oval that has been polished until it is thinner than a thought.

They come off. They get lost. They get upgraded. A Georgian desk will wear a Victorian cover, then a Colonial Revival cover, then nothing, just a chewed hole. The hole remembers. An old thread escutcheon leaves a shadow in the finish. A plate leaves screw holes or pin holes. A drop leaves a tiny hinge scar. Read those before you buy a "matching set" of covers from a catalog. The catalog is guessing.

I have a weakness for a drop escutcheon that still clicks shut. It is a small noise. It says the lock is a room you do not enter by accident. Most of them do not click anymore. The drop hangs open like a mouth.

Ivory thread escutcheons are a conservation problem and a moral one. I am not going to tell you to pry them out. I am not going to tell you to buy new ivory. Bone and boxwood exist. So does leaving the empty rebate as a scar. Scars are honest.

French thread escutcheons can be oval islands of holly or ebony in a veneer sea. English ones are often plainer because the lock plate is doing the talking. American Federal work sometimes puts a tiny urn on a cover and thinks that is enough nation for a keyhole. It is enough.

The keyhole is already a shape. The escutcheon is how the piece admits that shape is worth dressing. When the lock is gone and the escutcheon remains, you have jewelry without a body. When the lock remains and the escutcheon is gone, you have a body without manners. I prefer the second. I see more of the first. People love a pretty hole. They forget what the hole was for.
