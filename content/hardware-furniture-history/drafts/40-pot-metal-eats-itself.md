Some pulls do not wear out. They die of their own alloy.

Pot metal, zinc die-cast, the cheap twentieth-century soup — zinc and whatever was in the pot, a little aluminum, a little lead, a little regret. It will take a mold. It will take chrome. It will look like a Deco sculpture or a cheap Colonial swirl. Then, in some houses, in some decades, it intercrystalline-corrodes. Zinc pest. The metal expands, cracks, crumbles to a grey sugar. You pick up a drawer pull and it remains in your hand as a concept.

The furniture is often fine. Veneer still down. Joinery still tight. The handshake is powder. I have bagged those fragments and labeled the bag because I am the kind of person who labels bags, and because a restorer may want the shape. You can recast. You can print a pattern. You cannot un-eat the alloy.

Not every zinc piece goes. The pest is a reputation, not a guarantee. Storage, impurities, age. I will not give you a humidity sermon I cannot back. I will say: do not force a cracked Deco bar. It will finish dying in the screw holes and then you have zinc in the wood.

Chrome on pot metal is a particular sadness. The chrome stays in flakes, bright, and the grey body shrinks under it. It looks like a fish rotting in a sequin dress. Leave it on a shelf if the shape matters. Do not put it back in daily service and call that conservation.

Brass can fatigue. Iron can rust through a strap. Those are weathers I understand. Pot metal is a twentieth-century surprise: the hardware is newer than the oak and it will be gone first. The century that invented disposable objects invented disposable pulls and put them on furniture that was still trying to be an heirloom. The heirloom is the carcase. The pull was a product.

Die-cast is not a slur by itself. Good zinc alloys still make hardware that will outlive me. The slur is the cheap pot and the chrome that hid it, and the hope that a living-room pull could be as disposable as a toy car. Toy cars were allowed to die. A dresser was not supposed to.

If you are buying, squeeze. Gently. A sound bar is a bar. A bar that dusts your fingers is a warning. The warning is kind. Listen. Then look at the screw posts. If they have already grown and split the drawer face from inside, the alloy has been talking for years. You were late to the conversation.
