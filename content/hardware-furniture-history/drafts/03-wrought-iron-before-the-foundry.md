Before there is a catalog, there is a heat.

A strap hinge that started life in a village fire is not a product. It is a drawing-out. The smith takes a bar, gets a yellow that would scare a hobbyist, and pulls the iron toward a taper the way you pull taffy if taffy could take your hand off. The barrel is the fat that did not get used up. He wraps that fat around a mandrel, or around the pintle itself, and the knuckle is born ugly and then filed until it will turn. If he has pride or a customer who will pay for pride, he splits the end of the strap into a bean, a spear, a rat-tail, a cross. Those shapes are extra heats. They are not SKUs.

I have heard people call this "primitive." They mean it as a compliment and it still lands wrong. Primitive is the wrong word for a joint that has been opening a food chest since before your family owned a surname. The right word is *wrought*: worked. The opposite is not refined. The opposite is cast, then later stamped, then later injected. Each of those is a different intelligence. Cast iron can take a pattern a smith would not have the hours to file. Stamped brass can put an eagle on every drawer in a Federal bedroom. Neither of them has the drunk line of a handmade strap under raking light.

Look at the bevels. Handmade iron is not even. The file wandered. The hammer left a scatter that a factory texture tries to imitate and always spaces too politely. Look at the nail holes — nails, often, not screws — punched or drifted, not drilled on a jig. Two straps on the same lid will not be twins. If they are twins, someone in 1985 made them twins.

The foundry is not the enemy. I am not writing a romance of the village. Foundries let ordinary houses have locks and pulls that used to belong to the people who could keep a smith on a job. Birmingham in the late eighteenth century is one of the reasons an American chest can wear English brass. That is a real gain. What you lose is the particularity. A wrought hinge is a person. A cast hinge is a pattern. A stamped hinge is a pattern that learned to hurry.

If you are dating a piece, the wrought story is useful and dangerous. Useful: a handmade strap with rosehead nails and a pin that was peened, not capped from a bag, will not be sitting on a circular-sawn carcase unless someone mixed centuries on purpose. Dangerous: decorative "hand-forged" iron is a catalog aisle. They heat it just enough to put dimples on. The dimples are even. The evenness is the tell.

I do not need the smith's name. I need the heat to still be visible. After the foundry, the heat goes inside the factory and does not come out on the face. That is also a kind of honesty — industrial honesty — but it is a different sentence. This one is older: I drew this out, I wrapped this barrel, I will hang your lid, and when I am gone the lid will still remember the way my hammer walked.
