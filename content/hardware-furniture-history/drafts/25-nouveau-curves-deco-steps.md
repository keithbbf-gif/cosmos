Two modernisms, two hands.

Art Nouveau wants the pull to grow. Whiplash, a lily, a woman who is also a stem, bronze that looks like it could wilt. The hand is asked to hold a stem. Some of those stems are exquisite. Some are a nightmare to grab if you actually need the drawer. Jewelry that forgot it was a tool.

Art Deco wants the pull to be a machine that went to a party. Steps, ziggurats, a bar, a chrome speed line, Bakelite in butterscotch or black. The hand is asked to hold geometry. Geometry is easier to grab. It is also easier to mass-produce. Deco hardware floods the middle market. Nouveau, at its richest, stays closer to the goldsmith.

I do not stack them as better and worse. I stack them as a turn. The curve is a refusal of the Victorian catalog's stiff incising. The step is a refusal of the curve. Both refusals are sincere. Both get copied until they are a punchline. A "Nouveau" backplate from a 1970s kit is a limp vine. A "Deco" bar from a 2000s big-box aisle is a chrome tube with no steps and no nerve.

Chrome is a date. Not a precise one — plating is a long twentieth century — but a feeling. Cold. Clean. It chips and then the brass or the pot metal shows through like a bad lining. Bakelite is a date with a smell. I will get to the smell. Here I only want the hand: curve versus step. One asks you to follow. One asks you to click.

Wiener Werkstätte and Mackintosh sit on the curve side if you want names, though most of what you will meet is a distant cousin in stamped brass. Hoffmann's geometry is already walking toward Deco before Deco has the word. I will not force a museum tour. I will say the hand can feel the turn: a vine that wants to be held versus a step that wants to be clicked.

On a piece that has both — a sideboard with original Deco bars and a single Nouveau leftover in a junk drawer — I trust the bars. The leftover is a house's memory, not the object's. Hardware migrates inside a home. Drawers are where migrations end. If you are dating, date the holes that match the bars. Leave the vine in the junk drawer. It has already found its century.
