The parlor wanted a glint that wood would not give.

Porcelain knobs — white, painted, transfer-printed, sometimes a little flower that has been half rubbed off by a hundred years of hands — turn a drawer into a small piece of tableware. Mineral knobs, the cheaper cousins, are colored clay, mottled, brown and rust and black, sturdy, less china-shop. Both show up on American and English casework in the nineteenth century when the brass bail has stepped back and the wood has gotten heavier and the room wants a spark under gaslight.

I have always liked the mineral ones more than I was supposed to. They are not trying to be precious. They look like geology. They chip instead of shattering into a myth. Porcelain will break on a hardwood floor and then the drawer is a wound. You can find a pair in a box at a flea market that almost matches. Almost is the Victorian condition.

Painted porcelain is where taste goes to be embarrassed later. A pink rose on a commode. A child's face. Gold lines. I will not sneer. Someone loved that. Someone's hand went there every morning. The embarrassment is ours, not theirs. If you strip a painted knob to "clean white," you are doing the same violence as polishing the gilt off a Federal plate. The paint is the record.

These knobs are also a dating trap. They get swapped onto earlier chests because they are easy to find and they say *old* in a house that does not know 1810 from 1880. A Federal chest with mineral knobs and extra holes is a chest that went through a parlor. Believe the holes.

The screw is usually a machine screw into a brass insert, or a bolt through the drawer front with a nut inside. Look at the nut. Look at the washer. Look at whether the porcelain has been ground to sit flat. A wobbling knob on a Victorian commode is a small misery. Someone will glue it. The glue will fail. The misery is part of the object now.

White porcelain on a dark walnut dresser is a contrast the nineteenth century loved: clean against heavy. Mineral on oak is quieter, almost camouflage. If every knob on a set matches too perfectly, including the tiny flaws, you may be looking at a recent set from a reproduction house that sells "Victorian" by the dozen. Real sets have been through breakage. Perfect is a tell.

A parlor is a social room. The hardware is social. Clay and glaze are how a drawer learned to dress for company. When the company left, the glaze stayed, chipped, and that chip is the part of the party that is still honest.
