A butt hinge is a decision about vanity.

You cut a mortise in the door and a mortise in the carcase, you let the knuckle sit in the reveal, and when the door shuts the iron goes away. What remains is a thin line of barrel, if that. The wood is the face. The hinge is the servant. Once cabinetmakers could cut those mortises cleanly — and once butts were cheap enough that a wardrobe could have three pairs without a conversation — the visible strap started to look rustic even when it was not trying to.

I have laid out a lot of butts. The job is simple and easy to do badly. The mortises have to be the same depth or the door winds. The pin has to be in a straight air or the door binds. The screws have to find something other than the first inch of a soft stile. Brass butts on a mahogany door look like money. Steel butts painted over look like a kitchen. Neither is older by default. Brass can be last Tuesday.

The butt becomes a commodity in the nineteenth century and then a problem for anyone who wants a date. A five-knuckle loose-pin butt is a gift when you have to hang a door alone: pull the pin, set the leaves, drop the pin in. That convenience is late enough to be a tell on a piece that claims to be earlier. Fixed pins, rough cast leaves, filed knuckles — those can be older. Or they can be a foundry that never modernized the cheap line. Hardware catalogs are full of both.

There is a taste story hiding in the mortise. Georgian and Federal casework often wants you to forget the door is a door. The panel, the veneer, the cockbead — those are the show. The hinge is a necessary embarrassment, minimized. Arts and Crafts sometimes puts the hinge back on the face and calls the embarrassment honesty. Mid-century puts the hinge on the back side of a slab and calls it clean. Same metal family. Different shame.

If you take a door off and the butt mortises are chopped with a chisel that wandered, I trust the door more than I trust a door whose mortises are router-perfect and whose screws are Pozidriv. Perfection is a date. So is a slightly ugly rectangle that has been carrying that walnut since the year the walnut was fashionable.

The butt hinge hid because someone wanted wood to win. Wood did win, for a long time. Then the kitchen slab arrived and even the little barrel in the reveal was too much confession. That is another essay. This one is only the first vanishing: the hinge that agreed to live in a pocket and keep its mouth shut.
