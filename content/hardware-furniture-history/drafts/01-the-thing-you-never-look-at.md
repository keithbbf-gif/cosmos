The photograph is always the carving.

A dealer tilts a phone, a museum tilts a light, and the thing that gets the pixel is a shell, a claw-and-ball, a fielded panel with a shadow that looks expensive. I do the same thing. I have stood in front of a chest and loved the wood like it was the whole story. It is not the whole story. The story is the part that moves.

Furniture that never moves is architecture that lost its nerve. A chest has a lid. A cupboard has a door. A desk has a fall, a drawer, a well. Those planes have to rotate or slide and then come home again, year after year, without the carcase coming apart in their hands. The solution is almost never more wood. The solution is a small piece of metal that the photograph crops out.

Look at the hinge. Not as jewelry. As a confession. Someone admitted this door would have to leave and return. They picked a strap, or a pair of butts, or a knife hinge you can only find with a fingernail, or a 35 mm cup bored into the back of a slab so the kitchen can pretend it has no joints at all. Each of those choices is a date, a shop, a price, a theory of what a face should do.

Look at the pull. You meet a drawer with your hand. The woodworkers can talk all day about grain and the eye; the body goes for the bail. A missing pull is a sentence. A replaced pull is a different sentence, often a louder one. Victorian owners "improved" Georgian chests the way people improve kitchens: they put on what the year wanted. Later owners put the old shape back, or what a catalog said the old shape was. The drawer front keeps both stories if you let it. Extra holes. A shadow where a backplate used to be. A bright brass oval on a mahogany that has not been that color in two hundred years.

Look at the lock. A lot of furniture locks were not built for the burglar we invent in our heads. They were built for a household — a servant, a child, a second wife, a clerk, the version of yourself that should not open the till before Sunday. Warded locks, which is most English furniture locks before the late 1770s, are not puzzles. They are manners with a key. The serious locks come later, and they come with names (Barron, Bramah, Chubb, Yale) because security had become a public argument, not just a hasp.

I am not going to tell you hardware is more important than wood. That would be a cute inversion and it would be false. Wood is the body. Hardware is the joints the body is willing to admit. You can fake a period with a router and a stain. It is harder to fake the iron that was drawn out in two heats, or the Birmingham plate with rolling wrinkles on the back, or the till lock that still throws into a slot cut by a man who expected that bolt and no other.

This series is staged because I do not want to bluff. Research first, then an outline that has to say what the piece is *for*, then a draft in a voice I would use if we were both standing with the drawer open and the flashlight in your mouth. I have not handled Joan Parcher's ten thousand brasses. I have handled enough replacement knobs, Euro hinges, and "original" plates with Phillips screws to know what a lie feels like on a fingertip.

The thing you never look at is looking at you. It has been, every time you opened the door.
