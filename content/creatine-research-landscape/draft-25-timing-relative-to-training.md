# CRL-25 — Timing relative to training: what designs can isolate

> **Educational landscape only.** A design-limits chapter. Not a
> pre/post-workout schedule, not medical advice, and not a
> recommendation to use any product. Does not claim that creatine
> diagnoses, treats, cures, or prevents any disease.

## Learning aims

- Separate *days-to-raise-the-pool* from *minutes-around-a-session*.
- See why most "timing" studies are underpowered for a small contrast.
- Keep Harris's exercised-limb observation from turning into a
  stopwatch religion.

## Two clocks people mash together

**Clock A — accumulation.** Muscle TCr rises over days to weeks
(Hultman 1996). For a Saturday test, the question is whether the pool
is up, not whether Thursday's scoop was at 16:10 or 18:40.

**Clock B — peri-session timing.** Some papers ask whether swallowing
the day's creatine just before or just after a lifting session changes
training outcomes across weeks. That is a small contrast sitting on
top of Clock A. Both arms usually end up with a raised pool if the
daily total is the same.

If Clock A is doing most of the work, Clock B will look noisy. That
is the default prediction, not a scandal.

## What a timing study would need

Same daily total. Same training. Same vehicle. Blinding that survives
the locker room. A sample large enough to detect a *difference
between two active arms*, which is harder than detecting creatine vs
placebo. A pre-registered primary outcome. Ideally a pool assay, which
almost none of these papers have.

Many published timing studies fail several of those. They can still
be read as **exploratory**. They cannot carry a confident "must take
it after" rule. This series will not write that rule.

## The exercised-limb thread, kept small

Harris et al. (1992) discussed greater creatine increases in muscle
that had been exercised. That is a **local uptake** hint, possibly
blood flow, possibly transporter, possibly both. It supports "the
limb you work might take up a bit more during a loading week." It
does not support a minute-by-minute scoop ritual, and it does not
say the unworked limb stays at baseline.

## Habit vs kinetics

A scoop next to a training session is a way to remember the day's
inlet. Adherence is a real variable (CRL-23). Remembering is not the
same as a unique anabolic window. Papers that measure adherence
better than they measure Clock B are, in a sense, timing studies
about human memory.

## What this draft does not claim

- That peri-session timing cannot matter at all. It is hard to
  isolate, not metaphysically impossible.
- That readers should place a scoop at a named minute.
- That Harris 1992 was a timing-of-the-scoop study. It was not.
