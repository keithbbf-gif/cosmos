# CRL-41 — Labels, assays, and the powder that is not the paper

> **Educational landscape only.** How research material and shop
> material come apart. Not a brand ranking, not medical advice, and
> not a recommendation to use any product. Does not claim that
> creatine diagnoses, treats, cures, or prevents any disease.

## Learning aims

- Separate a lab-weighed monohydrate from a grocery tub.
- See contamination and mis-labeling as **methods threats**,
  especially in sport.
- Treat third-party testing as a reduction of one risk, not as a
  proof of effect.

## Two objects that share a name

**Object A.** A chemical from a catalog, assayed, weighed on a
balance, put into coded bags. Harris-era papers are closer to this.

**Object B.** A consumer tub that says "creatine" and may contain
monohydrate, a blend, fillers, leftover moisture, a dash of
another stimulant, or less creatine than the label.

Citing Object A's literature while swallowing Object B is a
category error. The literature can still inform a *question*. It
cannot certify a *tub*.

## What "contamination" means in this file

Sport anti-doping bodies repeatedly warn that supplements can
contain undeclared prohibited substances. Creatine as a named
ingredient is not listed on the WADA Prohibited List as of the
2026 published list (the word does not appear there). That fact
does **not** make a tub clean. A prohibited stimulant in the same
scoop is a different molecule. Strict liability in sport does not
care that the label was innocent.

This series does not tell athletes to take or skip powders. It
tells readers of papers to ask whether the authors assayed the
product, and it tells readers of labels that a society stand is
not a certificate of analysis.

## Third-party marks

Programs that assay for identity, quantity, and selected
contaminants reduce one class of risk. They are not a peer-review
of Harris 1992. They are not a disease-claim license. They are
not eternal; batches change.

## How a careful paper talks

Manufacturer, lot, independent assay of creatine content, and a
statement about other bioactive ingredients. "Commercially
available creatine" without an assay is a fog.

## What this draft does not claim

- That all consumer monohydrate is adulterated. Many assays find
  what the label promised.
- That a third-party mark makes a product appropriate for a
  person.
- That absence from a prohibited list is a purity badge.
