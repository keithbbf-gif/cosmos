# CRL-01 — The creatine research landscape in one map

> **Educational landscape only.** This draft maps how researchers study creatine.
> It is not medical advice, not a supplement label, and not a recommendation to
> use any product. It does not claim that creatine diagnoses, treats, cures, or
> prevents any disease.

## Learning aims

- Place creatine in three nested files: a small molecule, a human metabolite,
  and a heavily studied oral powder in exercise physiology.
- See why "the creatine literature" is not one literature.
- Know which stage of this series answers which kind of question.

## Three files, not one slogan

Creatine is easy to sloganize and hard to keep straight. The same word names:

1. **A guanidino compound** (N-methylguanidinoacetic acid) that cells make and
   that some foods contain.
2. **A phosphagen partner** that exists in equilibrium with phosphocreatine
   through creatine kinase.
3. **An oral research material**, almost always creatine monohydrate in the
   papers that matter, used to raise the muscle creatine pool and then to ask
   what happens to a task.

Those three files share a chemical, not a conclusion. A paper that measures
quadriceps total creatine after five days of feeding is not making the same
claim as a paper that measures a 30-second cycle sprint, and neither paper is
a license to talk about health conditions. This series stays inside the first
two files and the *methods* of the third.

## Why the landscape is thick

Oral creatine became a modern human-research object in the early 1990s, when
needle biopsy showed that swallowing the compound could raise the muscle store
(Harris, Söderlund, and Hultman, 1992). That single observation pulled several
older traditions into one room:

- Nineteenth-century meat chemistry (isolation and naming).
- Mid-century muscle biochemistry (phosphagens, creatine kinase isoforms).
- Late-century sport science (repeated high-intensity tasks, resistance
  training outcomes).
- Measurement science (biopsy, 31P magnetic resonance spectroscopy, urinary
  creatinine).

The result is a corpus that looks unified from a shop shelf and fragmented from
a methods table. Reviews often collapse "creatine works" across tasks that do
not share a metabolic bottleneck. Meta-analyses then average those tasks. A
landscape reading asks, first, **what was loaded**, **what was measured**, and
**in whom**.

## A working geography

Think of the published map as six provinces:

| Province | Typical question | Typical bottleneck |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Chemistry and synthesis | What is it; how is it made endogenously? | Pathway, not performance |
| Distribution and transport | Where does the pool sit; how does it enter cells? | Transporter kinetics, tissue fraction |
| Measurement | How do we know the pool moved? | Biopsy variance, MRS assumptions |
| Feeding protocols | What oral schedule changed the pool? | Dose, split, duration, co-ingestion |
| Exercise tasks | Did a named task change? | Task duration, rest, training status |
| Product and statute | What powder is in the bottle; under what law? | Hydrate vs salt; supplement vs drug |

This series walks those provinces in that order. Stages 5 and 7 are not
"special populations to treat." They are **modifiers of the measurement**:
habitual diet changes the baseline store; sex and age band change who was
enrolled; brain MRS is a different compartment from vastus lateralis biopsy.

## What "evidence" means here

A creatine paper can be strong on one axis and silent on another.

- **Pool evidence:** muscle total creatine or phosphocreatine rose. This is the
  load-bearing fact of the 1990s biopsy work.
- **Task evidence:** a performance or training outcome moved in a named
  protocol. This is a different claim and is more sensitive to design.
- **Mechanism story:** the phosphagen system is offered as the reason. Stories
  are allowed; they are not pool evidence.
- **Position-stand evidence:** a society summary. Useful as a map of what a
  community was willing to say; not a primary experiment.

CRL-03 walks a paper with those four axes marked. CRL-32 treats position
stands as secondary literature.

## How to use the rest of the folder

If you want the molecule, start at CRL-05. If you want to know why "20 g for
6 days" appears in so many methods sections, start at CRL-19 and read it as
**history of a protocol**, not as advice. If you want to know why some people
in trials are called non-responders, start at CRL-23. If you want the honest
edge of the map, start at CRL-47.

Read CRL-02 before you quote any draft out of the folder. The claim ban is the
spine of the series.

## What this draft does not claim

- That creatine is indicated for any health condition.
- That any oral schedule is recommended for you.
- That the size of the literature equals the size of a single effect.
