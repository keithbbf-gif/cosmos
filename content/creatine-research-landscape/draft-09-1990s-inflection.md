# CRL-09 — The 1990s inflection: Harris, Greenhaff, Hultman, Green

> **Educational landscape only.** A cluster of methods papers, not a
> hero story and not a dosing guide. Not medical advice. Does not claim
> that creatine diagnoses, treats, cures, or prevents any disease.

## Learning aims

- Name the five papers that still write most methods sections.
- Separate what each paper actually measured.
- See how a cluster becomes a cliché (and how to un-cliché it).

## Why a cluster, not a single shot

Oral creatine in humans did not become a research object because of one
press release. A small group, working with biopsy and with the
Bergström–Hultman muscle-metabolite tradition, published a tight
sequence. Later writers compressed that sequence into "load 20 g, then
5 g." Compression is how methods become folklore. This draft unpacks the
sequence.

## Harris, Söderlund, Hultman 1992 — the pool can move

*Clinical Science.* Oral creatine raised muscle creatine in healthy
people. A 5 g dose was compared, in the text, to the creatine in a large
amount of steak, which told readers the experiment was a dietary-scale
addition rather than a trace. Increases were larger when the starting
total creatine was lower. Exercise of the sampled muscle was discussed
as a possible local modifier of uptake. This paper licenses a **pool
claim**. It does not, by itself, license a sport.

## Greenhaff et al. 1993 — a task sits on the pool

*Clinical Science.* Repeated bouts of maximal voluntary isokinetic
exercise. Torque in later bouts was the kind of outcome that matches a
phosphagen story: not a long time trial, a repeated maximal effort with
limited recovery. Read it as **Layer B** in the CRL-03 sense, still close
to the biopsy tradition.

## Greenhaff, Bodin, Söderlund, Hultman 1994 — PCr after contraction

*American Journal of Physiology.* Oral creatine and the resynthesis of
phosphocreatine after exercise. This is the paper people mean when they
say "it helps you recover PCr between efforts." The claim is about a
measured PCr time course in a protocol, not about "recovery" as a
lifestyle word.

## Hultman et al. 1996 — schedules, hold, washout

*Journal of Applied Physiology.* Thirty-one men, several intake patterns.
Reported findings that later hardened into slogans:

- About **20 g/day for 6 days** raised muscle total creatine on the order
  of **20%** in that sample.
- **2 g/day** afterward held the elevation across a further 30 days in
  the arm that continued.
- Without that continued intake, concentration drifted back, and by
  about **30 days** it was no longer different from the
  pre-supplementation value.
- **3 g/day for 28 days** produced a similar elevation more slowly.
- Urinary creatinine rose as the pool and breakdown rose.

Those numbers are **this paper's results**, not universal human
constants. They are why washout, "load," and "slow path" entered every
later methods section (CRL-19, CRL-20, CRL-22).

## Green et al. 1996 — carbohydrate after creatine

*American Journal of Physiology.* 5 g creatine, four times a day, for
5 days, with or without a large simple-carbohydrate drink about 30
minutes later. The carbohydrate arm retained more muscle creatine; insulin
was the offered mediator. Steenge, Simpson, and Greenhaff (2000) later
asked whether protein plus carbohydrate could play in the same space.
These papers are about **retention under a feeding design**, not about a
required shake recipe (CRL-21).

## How the cluster became a cliché

Textbook chapters kept the 20 g / 6 day / 2–5 g hold and dropped the
slow 3 g path, the 30-day washout, the baseline-dependence, and the
steak comparison. Brand chapters then swapped monohydrate for other
salts without repeating the biopsy. A landscape reader puts the five
papers back on the table and asks which later study actually inherited
which measurement.

## What this draft does not claim

- That 1990s male biopsy samples are all humans.
- That you should follow any of these schedules.
- That later work cannot revise the percentages. It can; it must show
  its assay.
