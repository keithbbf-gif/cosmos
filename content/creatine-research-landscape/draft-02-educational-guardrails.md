# CRL-02 — Educational guardrails: what this series will not claim

> **Educational landscape only.** This draft states the claim boundary for every
> file in the folder. It is not medical advice, not a supplement label, and not
> a recommendation to use any product. It does not claim that creatine
> diagnoses, treats, cures, or prevents any disease.

## Learning aims

- Separate *describing a literature* from *making a health claim*.
- Recognize the sentence shapes that turn a methods fact into an illegal or
  unethical disease claim.
- Know what a responsible educational summary is allowed to say.

## Why a whole draft on what we will not say

Creatine sits in a crowded sentence market. Shop copy, social posts, and even
some reviews slide from "muscle creatine rose in a biopsy study" to "this
compound is for [a named condition]." Those are different speech acts.

In U.S. food-and-supplement language, a product sold as a dietary supplement
is not allowed to claim that it diagnoses, treats, cures, or prevents disease.
This series is not a label, but it uses the same bright line as a teaching
discipline: **we describe studies; we do not write indications.**

The line is also epistemic. Exercise-physiology trials in healthy volunteers
do not become clinical evidence by quotation. A review that lists many clinical
papers is still a review. This folder does not summarize those papers and does
not inherit their hoped-for uses.

## Allowed speech

A draft in this series may:

- Name the molecule, the pathway, the tissues that store it, and the assays.
- Report that a **named paper** measured a **named outcome** in a **named
  sample**.
- Say that a finding was mixed, small, unblinded, industry-adjacent, or
  confined to one sex or diet.
- Describe oral schedules **as they appear in methods sections**.
- Describe regulation (supplement vs drug) as a legal category, not as a
  quality badge.
- Say that a society published a position stand, and treat that stand as
  secondary literature.

## Banned speech

A draft in this series will not:

- Claim that creatine treats, cures, prevents, or manages any disease or
  named medical condition.
- Offer personal dosing, stacking, or "what you should take."
- Translate a healthy-volunteer safety paper into a promise for a reader with
  a medical history.
- Use brain, aging, or occupational studies as a back door to a clinical
  indication.
- Treat "the ISSN said" or "a meta-analysis found" as a substitute for the
  underlying design.
- Imply that absence from a banned-substance list means a product is
  uncontaminated or appropriate.

## Sentence shapes that fail the test

These fail even when they sound cautious:

- "Creatine may help with [condition]."
- "Researchers use creatine for [condition], so it is a therapy."
- "Safe and effective for patients with…"
- "Everyone should take 3 to 5 grams for health."

These pass, because they stay inside description:

- "Harris et al. (1992) reported a rise in muscle total creatine after oral
  creatine in healthy volunteers."
- "Hultman et al. (1996) compared a short high-intake schedule with a longer
  lower-intake schedule and reported similar pool changes by about four weeks."
- "Some reviews discuss papers from clinical settings; this series does not
  evaluate those papers and does not adopt their aims."

## What we do with neighboring literatures

Reviews of creatine often wander into injury, aging, and clinic-adjacent
outcomes. Those neighboring files exist. A landscape map can say **the
neighbor exists** without crossing the fence:

- We do **not** name conditions as uses.
- We do **not** quote clinical efficacy numbers.
- We do **not** tell a reader that a sport-nutrition position stand is a
  medical guideline.

CRL-43 discusses what healthy-volunteer safety papers actually measured
(symptoms, blood marks, dropouts). That is still not a clearance for any
person. CRL-44–46 discuss brain creatine and cognitive-task designs as
**measurement problems**. They are not treatment chapters.

## Who this series is for

Adults who want to read the exercise and metabolism literature without being
sold a story. Students. Coaches who want to stop repeating folklore as if it
were a methods table. It is not for self-diagnosis, not for product
formulation, and not for writing a supplement label.

If you need a personal decision about food or a powder, that decision sits
with you and a qualified clinician who knows your history. Nothing in this
folder is a substitute for that conversation.

## What this draft does not claim

- That the claim ban is a finding that creatine "does nothing" outside sport.
  Silence is not a negative proof.
- That every paper outside this scope is poorly done. Out of scope is not a
  quality verdict.
- That educational speech is a weaker form of advertising. It is a different
  act.
