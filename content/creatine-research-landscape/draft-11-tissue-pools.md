# CRL-11 — Where the creatine pool sits

> **Educational landscape only.** Distribution, not targeting advice.
> Not medical advice, not a supplement label, and not a recommendation
> to use any product. Does not claim that creatine diagnoses, treats,
> cures, or prevents any disease.

## Learning aims

- Remember that skeletal muscle holds most of the body's creatine.
- Treat "brain creatine" and "muscle creatine" as different compartments
  with different assays.
- Read any single-site biopsy as a local sample.

## A body-majority in muscle

Reviews commonly state that on the order of **95%** of the body's
creatine sits in skeletal muscle, with the remainder in other tissues
that still run CK (brain is the compartment this series will mention
again; other small fractions exist). The exact percentage is a
textbook rounding, not a census you can audit in a living person
without extraordinary methods.

That 95% figure is why sport-science papers can talk as if "creatine"
means "vastus lateralis TCr" and usually get away with it for
**exercise questions**. It is also why a brain-MRS paper is not a
muscle paper with a different title (CRL-44).

## Inside muscle: free Cr, PCr, and a ceiling story

In muscle, a substantial fraction of the total is phosphorylated at
rest — reviews often say on the order of two-thirds PCr, one-third
free Cr, with the ratio shifting as soon as contraction starts. Total
creatine in mixed-fiber human biopsy samples is often introduced as
about **120 mmol/kg dry mass**, with an oft-quoted upper neighborhood
near **160 mmol/kg dry mass** after feeding in people who had room to
rise (Hultman line; restated in later ISSN-style reviews). Fiber type,
prior diet, and assay method all nibble at those round numbers.

The "ceiling" is a **population observation**, not a law that every
fiber obeys. It is the reason "more powder forever" is a weak
prediction for muscle TCr, and the reason baseline matters (CRL-23).

## Other compartments, named only as compartments

Brain creatine is measurable with proton MRS. Oral schedules that
change muscle TCr in days do not automatically write the same
percentage onto a brain voxel; Dechent et al. (1999) reported a
smaller, regionally uneven rise over weeks (CRL-44). That is
distribution, not a use case.

This series does not tour other organs as treatment targets. If a
review lists many tissues, the educational move is: **CK-using tissues
can hold a pool; each pool needs its own assay.**

## Why a biopsy is not a body

Vastus lateralis is convenient. It is not gluteus, not soleus, not a
heart voxel, and not "the athlete." Local uptake may differ when the
limb was exercised during a loading week (Harris et al. discussed
exercise of the sampled muscle). A paper that biopsies one site and
then talks about whole-body creatine mass is inferring.

Whole-body estimates can be built from muscle mass assumptions plus
excretion. Those estimates are models (CRL-17).

## Water lives in the same sentence

Creatine is osmotically active. Muscle water can rise when the pool
rises. That fact links this draft to body-mass changes and to
two-compartment "lean mass" numbers (CRL-24). Distribution is not
only about creatine molecules. It is about the water that follows
them.

## What this draft does not claim

- That raising muscle TCr raises every other tissue's pool on the same
  timetable.
- That a 95/5 split is measured in each volunteer.
- That any tissue pool is a therapeutic target.
