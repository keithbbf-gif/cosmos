# CRL-07 — Dietary creatine: flesh, not plants

> **Educational landscape only.** Food chemistry and intake ranges from the
> literature. Not a meal plan, not medical advice, and not a recommendation
> to use any product. Does not claim that creatine diagnoses, treats, cures,
> or prevents any disease.

## Learning aims

- See why dietary creatine tracks animal muscle and some fish, not plants.
- Treat food tables as order-of-magnitude maps, not as a barcode for your
  plate.
- Connect habitual diet to the baseline muscle store that feeding trials
  later try to move.

## Where the food molecule lives

Creatine in the human diet is, for practical purposes, a **flesh
molecule**. Skeletal muscle and some seafood carry it because the animal
already used the phosphagen system. Plant foods, in ordinary tables, do
not contribute a meaningful amount. That is a compositional fact, not a
moral ranking of diets.

Reviews and food-chemistry papers repeatedly point to herring, beef,
pork, salmon, and similar muscle foods as the dense sources. A figure
that often gets repeated (and should be treated as a **teaching
approximation**, not as a lab ticket) is that on the order of a kilogram
of uncooked steak is in the same conversation as a 5 g research dose of
monohydrate — Harris et al. (1992) used a steak comparison of that kind
when they explained why a 5 g experimental dose was dietary-scale rather
than pharmacological-scale in their framing. Cooking, drip loss, and
cut all move the number. Do not meal-plan from that sentence.

Dairy and eggs are sometimes mentioned as minor contributors; they are
not the herring-and-beef part of the map.

## Habitual intake as a research variable

An omnivorous pattern is often summarized in reviews as supplying about
**1 g/day**, with a wide individual spread driven by how much muscle
food is actually eaten. A pattern that excludes those foods sits near
**zero dietary creatine**. The body still synthesizes creatine (CRL-06).
The muscle store, in several biopsy comparisons, sits lower on
low-animal-product diets (values in the neighborhood of 90–110 mmol/kg
dry muscle appear in reviews that cite vegetarian cohorts, versus a
mixed-diet average often quoted near 120 mmol/kg). Those ranges are
**group sketches**. They are why diet is a design variable (CRL-33),
not why a diet is "deficient" as a moral term.

Burke et al. (2003) and Watt, Garnham, and Snow (2004) are the papers to
open if you want the vegetarian comparison done with muscle measurements,
not with slogans.

## Food vs powder is a dose-and-matrix difference

Food creatine arrives with water, protein, fat, and whatever else the
cut contains. Research powders arrive as a defined solid, usually
monohydrate, in gram quantities that are awkward to eat as steak four
times a day. That is a **practical and kinetic** difference. It is not
proof that the food form is "inactive" or that the powder is a
different molecule. The molecule is the molecule. The schedule and the
matrix change the experiment.

Food matrices also make blinding impossible. That is one reason
exercise trials use powders and placebos (CRL-31).

## What food tables will not do for you

They will not tell you the creatine content of a restaurant burger. They
will not tell you how much survived a stew. They will not convert a
blood creatinine number into yesterday's meat intake. And they will not
turn a low-meat diet into a medical diagnosis. They tell you why two
arms of a trial can start with different muscle stores before anyone
opens a tub.

## What this draft does not claim

- That people who eat little animal muscle "need" a powder.
- That meat is required for health. This is a creatine-content map.
- That a gram in food is physiologically unlike a gram from monohydrate
  once absorbed. The open questions are about dose, timing, and matrix,
  not about a second compound.
