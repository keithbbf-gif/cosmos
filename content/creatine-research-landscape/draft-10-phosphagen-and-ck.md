# CRL-10 — The phosphagen system and creatine kinase

> **Educational landscape only.** Bioenergetics vocabulary. Not medical
> advice, not a supplement label, and not a recommendation to use any
> product. Does not claim that creatine diagnoses, treats, cures, or
> prevents any disease.

## Learning aims

- Write the creatine kinase reaction without turning it into a slogan.
- Keep cytosolic CK, mitochondrial CK, and serum CK as different
  appearances of one enzyme family.
- Know which exercise tasks sit near this bottleneck and which do not.

## The reaction, said slowly

Creatine kinase (CK) catalyzes:

**phosphocreatine + ADP + H+ ⇌ creatine + ATP**

In a cell that is suddenly using ATP, the forward direction (as written
from PCr toward ATP) can keep the ATP/ADP ratio from collapsing while
glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation catch the load. In recovery, the
reverse direction rebuilds PCr. The reaction is fast. Physiologists treat
it as near equilibrium in many resting conditions, which is why PCr is a
useful MRS readout of energetic state (CRL-15).

Two implications for readers of feeding papers:

1. Extra creatine in the muscle can mean extra PCr capacity **if** the
   phosphate and the kinase are there to use it. Harris-era papers
   measured TCr and often PCr; they did not assume the whole increment
   was PCr.
2. A task that never stresses ATP turnover on a short time scale has a
   weaker a priori claim on this reaction. That is why 30-minute steady
   rides and 6-second sprints should not share a forest plot without a
   fight (CRL-26, CRL-29).

## Isoforms, as a map, not as a blood test

CK exists in cytosolic dimers (MM in skeletal muscle, MB more associated
with cardiac muscle in textbook teaching, BB in some other tissues) and
as mitochondrial octamers (sarcomeric and ubiquitous mitochondrial CK in
the Wallimann vocabulary). The teaching point of Wallimann et al. (1992)
is **compartmentation**: CK is not a blob of activity in a bag of water.
It sits at sites of ATP use and ATP production. The "PCr circuit" or
"CK shuttle" is the name of that spatial hypothesis (CRL-13).

**Serum CK** on a clinical chemistry printout is mostly about enzyme
that has leaked into blood after muscle stress or damage. It is a
**different question** from "does the phosphagen system work." A rise in
serum CK after a hard session does not mean a person's creatine powder
"failed," and a normal serum CK does not mean their muscle TCr is full.
Do not import a blood-panel reflex into a sport paper.

## What the phosphagen system is not

It is not the only anaerobic pathway. Glycolysis is the other headline
on short hard efforts, especially as bouts lengthen past a handful of
seconds. It is not a calorie store on the scale of glycogen or fat. The
whole-body creatine pool is measured in tens of grams, not in thousands
of kilocalories. People who talk about creatine as "energy" in the diet-
label sense are using the wrong budget.

It is also not a switch that oral powder flips from off to on. Unsupplemented
muscle already has a large TCr pool. Oral studies move that pool **within
a range** (reviews often quote something like 120 mmol/kg dry toward an
upper neighborhood of 160). The physiology is increment, not invention.

## Connecting the cartoon to a methods section

When a paper says "we chose repeated sprints because of the phosphagen
system," the honest version is: **we chose a task whose metabolic
bottleneck *might* sit near PCr.** The cartoon cannot spare them from
measuring the task, and it cannot spare them from saying whether they
measured the pool. Greenhaff 1994 is the model of measuring PCr kinetics
rather than only narrating them.

## What this draft does not claim

- That raising muscle TCr always raises performance on any named task.
- That CK isoforms are discussed here as diagnostic targets.
- That the shuttle hypothesis is settled in every tissue. It is a
  productive, argued model.
