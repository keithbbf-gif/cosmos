# CRL-08 — From meat crystal to muscle metabolite: a short history

> **Educational landscape only.** History of a research object. Not medical
> advice, not a supplement label, and not a recommendation to use any
> product. Does not claim that creatine diagnoses, treats, cures, or
> prevents any disease.

## Learning aims

- Put dates on the isolation, the phosphagen, and the oral-biopsy turn.
- See that "creatine research" is older than the 1990s powder era.
- Avoid founding myths (that a single Olympiad invented the molecule).

## Nineteenth century: a crystal with a flesh name

Michel Eugène Chevreul reported creatine from meat extract in 1832 and
named it from *kreas*. Justus von Liebig and others in the mid-nineteenth
century treated it as a characteristic constituent of muscle. That is
food chemistry becoming animal chemistry. Nobody in that century was
running a randomized sprint trial.

The early work matters because it fixed the **identity** of the compound.
Later sport science did not discover creatine. It discovered that
**swallowing extra creatine could raise the muscle store in living
people** and then asked task questions.

## Early twentieth century: diet, excretion, creatinine

Otto Folin and others developed quantitative methods for creatinine and
debated dietary creatine. The intellectual move was to treat creatine and
creatinine as **measurable human metabolites** with a diet term. Some of
that debate was wrong in detail; the habit of measuring excretion is the
inheritance (CRL-16).

## Mid-twentieth century: phosphagens and the enzyme

The phosphagen concept — a reservoir that can rapidly reform ATP — and
the characterization of creatine kinase turned creatine from a meat
crystal into a **bioenergetic actor**. Lohmann's work on ATP and
phosphagens, and later isoform work on CK (Wallimann's circle is the
reviewable modern statement), gave the mechanism cartoon that 1990s
exercise papers still draw. CRL-10 and CRL-13.

Needle biopsy of human muscle, developed in the Bergström–Hultman line,
made metabolites in vastus lateralis a routine measurement. Without that
method, Harris 1992 does not happen.

## 1970s–1980s: the quiet before the oral papers

Animal feeding and some human metabolic work existed. Creatine was not
yet a mass-market powder. Reviews from this period read like biochemistry.
Keep them; they are less contaminated by brand.

## 1992–1996: the modern human file opens

Harris, Söderlund, and Hultman (1992) showed that oral creatine raised
muscle creatine in healthy people, more so when the starting store was
lower, and they compared a 5 g experimental dose to a large steak. That
paper is the hinge.

Greenhaff and colleagues (1993, 1994) added task and PCr-resynthesis
measurements. Hultman et al. (1996) mapped schedules: a short high daily
intake versus a longer moderate intake, plus a lower daily intake to hold
an elevated store, plus a washout measured in weeks. Green et al. (1996)
showed that a large carbohydrate drink after creatine could increase
retention, with insulin as the offered mechanism.

Those five years created almost every methods cliché still in use. CRL-09
reads them as a cluster, not as scripture.

## After 1996: volume, products, and reviews

Once the pool could be moved, the literature split:

- Replication and task expansion (what efforts care).
- Training studies (what happens across weeks with lifting).
- Form-and-brand papers (other salts; often thinner pool data).
- Measurement alternatives (MRS).
- Position stands (ISSN 2007 and 2017 as visible examples).
- A large neighboring clinical file that **this series does not enter**.

Founding myths about a particular Olympics "introducing creatine" are
secondary color. Some athletes were already using it by the early 1990s;
the biopsy papers are the reason the rest of us have a file.

## What this draft does not claim

- That earlier chemists were doing sport science.
- That the 1990s cluster closed the mechanistic book.
- That later volume equals later rigor.
