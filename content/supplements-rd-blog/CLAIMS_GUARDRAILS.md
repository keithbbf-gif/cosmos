# Claims guardrails — what this brand must never say on-site

Internal. Not a blog post. Counsel still owns the last read.

These are the sentences that turn a dietary supplement into an unapproved drug, a deceptive ad, or a product a 3PL will not ship. Drafts in this folder stay on the safe side of the line. Product pages, ads, Amazon copy, influencer scripts, and pack inserts must stay there too.

## The statute, not the vibe

Under DSHEA (Pub. L. 103-417) and 21 U.S.C. § 321(ff), a dietary supplement is a food category. It is not a drug. A product is a drug if it is intended to diagnose, cure, mitigate, treat, or prevent disease (21 U.S.C. § 321(g)(1)(B)). Intended use is read from labels, websites, social posts, and customer-service scripts — not from what the formulator meant.

Structure/function claims are allowed if they are truthful, not misleading, and substantiated, and if FDA is notified within 30 days and the DSHEA disclaimer appears (21 CFR 101.93). "Supports immune function" is a different legal object from "prevents COVID-19."

FTC's *Health Products Compliance Guidance* (December 2022) is the advertising rule. Competent and reliable scientific evidence, usually well-controlled human studies, for the claim as a reasonable consumer would read it. A rat paper and a podcast quote do not get you there.

## Never say (or imply)

### Disease and infection

- Prevents, treats, cures, mitigates, or diagnoses COVID-19, flu, colds, cancer, heart disease, stroke, diabetes, Alzheimer's, depression, PCOS, endometriosis, osteoporosis as a treated disease, or any other disease.
- "COVID protocol," "anti-viral stack," "natural vaccine," "replaces your medication."
- "Clinically proven to lower your risk of [disease]" on a supplement SKU.

Vitamin D, zinc, vitamin C, elderberry, quercetin, NAC, and silver all showed up in FDA/FTC COVID warning letters in 2020–2021. The ingredient was not the violation. The intended-use claim was.

### Longevity and aging-as-disease

- "Reverses aging," "adds years to your life," "cellular age of a 25-year-old."
- "NMN is an FDA-approved longevity drug" (false). NMN's supplement status changed in 2022 and again in FDA's 29 September 2025 citizen-petition responses. Status ≠ efficacy.
- NAD+ or NMN "treats metabolic disease / diabetes / fatty liver."

### Women's health

- "Hormone replacement," "bioidentical HRT in a capsule," "treats menopause," "prevents breast cancer," "cures hot flashes."
- NAMS 2023 did **not** recommend herbal supplements for vasomotor symptoms. Do not outrun that document.

### Brain, mood, sleep

- "Treats anxiety / depression / insomnia."
- Ashwagandha as "anxiolytic drug" or "safe for everyone." LiverTox and DILIN case series exist. Do not hide them.

### Sports

- "Steroid alternative," "increases testosterone into the clinical range" without a human trial that actually measured that endpoint on *this* formula.
- WADA/banned-list silence presented as "NSF Certified for Sport" when the lot is not in the NSF or Informed-Sport database.

### CBD / hemp

- CBD as a dietary supplement for interstate sale. FDA's 26 January 2023 conclusion: existing food and supplement frameworks are not appropriate for CBD; Epidiolex already triggered drug exclusion.
- "FDA-approved CBD supplement." Epidiolex is a drug. That is the opposite fact.
- Pediatric gummies, pregnancy use, or "treats epilepsy / pain / anxiety" on hemp SKUs.

### Quality theater

- "Pharmaceutical grade" as a consumer claim. That phrase is not a defined USP mark for supplements.
- "FDA approved" on a supplement. FDA does not pre-approve supplement SKUs.
- "USP / NSF / Informed-Sport certified" unless the *current lot* and *exact label* are in the program database. A factory visit in 2019 is not a mark.
- A supplier COA treated as a finished-product COA.
- "Heavy-metal free." The honest sentence is "tested; results below [limit] [method] [lab]."

### Personalized tests

- "This at-home DNA kit tells you which supplements to take."
- Using a lab-developed test as a diagnosis, or as a drug-claim engine.

## Allowed shapes (still need substantiation)

- "Helps fill a dietary gap for [nutrient] when intake is low."
- "Supports [normal structure/function]" with a notified, evidence-backed claim and the DSHEA disclaimer.
- "Contains [dose] of [ingredient], as [form], lot-tested for identity, potency, and [named contaminants]."
- "Third-party tested to [named standard]. Certificate [ID]."
- "Not evaluated by FDA. Not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease."

## Process

1. If a sentence would be illegal on a label, it is illegal in a blog caption that sits next to an add-to-cart button.
2. Influencer briefs get the same list. A Reel is labeling if you paid for it.
3. When evidence is mixed (vitamin D and COVID; omega-3 drug trials vs 1 g fish oil), say so. Do not average a prescription icosapent ethyl trial into a 300 mg softgel claim.
4. New ingredients (NMN, NAC, hemp-derived cannabinoids): check the *dated* FDA page before the PDP goes live. This folder is a 2020–2026 snapshot, not a standing legal opinion.
