**Disclaimer.** Educational only. Not medical advice. Dietary supplements are not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease (DSHEA; 21 U.S.C. § 321(ff); 21 CFR 101.93).

March 2020. Plants that had been running one shift added a second. Vitamin C, D, zinc, and elderberry were the SKUs. FDA's field force, already thin for dietary supplements, pulled back from routine inspections. New Hope Network reported it in plain language: the industry was on the honor system for GMP at the exact moment volume went up.

That is the year to study if you make or white-label anything botanical.

## Demand, with numbers that have a source

CRN funded an Ipsos COVID survey in late July–early August 2020 (2,004 US adults; 1,488 supplement users). Among users, the products they said they *increased* were led by multivitamins (+59%), vitamin C (+44%), and vitamin D (+37%). Zinc was +17%. Those are self-reports, not Nielsen units. They still tell you where the purchase intent sat.

Nutrition Business Journal's ~15% industry growth for 2020 was widely repeated (KUER's 2022 piece on Nevada plants cited it). I have not opened the paid NBJ table for this draft. `[VERIFY]` the exact NBJ percentage against the yearbook if you put it on a slide. Directionally: immune SKUs and the category as a whole had an abnormal year.

NOW Foods' CEO told KUER sales were up nearly 20% and still short of demand. One company. One interview. Useful as color, not as a market model.

## What FDA actually wrote

FDA did not issue a new "pandemic GMP waiver" that legalized dirty powder. It deprioritized inspections and spent its public energy on *claims*.

The consumer page — *Fraudulent Coronavirus Disease 2019 (COVID-19) Products* — is a running roster of warning letters. Firms landed there for selling products "to mitigate, prevent, treat, diagnose, or cure COVID-19." FRS International (letter 606701, 15 June 2020) is a clean example: quercetin chews and concentrates on frs.com, reviewed with FTC, treated as unapproved new drugs. The molecule was not banned. The intended use was.

Bautista et al., writing in late 2020 (PMC7528445), coded 98 COVID-focused letters from March–July. Most of those letters called the products drugs. Ingredients that recurred: vitamin C (14 letters), CBD (13), vitamin D/D3 (12), silver (11), elderberry (6), zinc (6). Four letters in that set mentioned adulteration; three of those involved dietary supplements. So the 2020 public record is *mostly misbranding and unapproved-drug theory*, not a pile of seized lead-tainted lots.

That split matters. A founder who only watches warning letters will over-learn claims and under-learn incoming inspection of botanicals. Both failure modes were live. Only one was loud.

## Adulteration: the memo that was not a surprise

Mark Blumenthal at the American Botanical Council wrote, in email quoted by New Hope, that more adulterated botanical raw materials and extracts were a "virtual certainty" because demand plus disruption plus price spikes is the classic incentive. ABC's Botanical Adulterants Prevention Program already had bulletins on elderberry, turmeric, ginkgo, grape seed, and saw palmetto from *before* COVID. The pandemic did not invent economic adulteration. It raised the bid.

What "adulteration" means in this trade, concretely:

- **Substitution.** A cheaper berry or leaf for the named species.
- **Dilution.** Extract standardized on a marker you can spike (or on a marker that is not unique).
- **Spiking.** Pure caffeine in "green coffee"; lead chromate in turmeric to fake color and weight.
- **Wrong plant part.** Leaf for root, stem for fruit.
- **Drug spillage.** PDE-5 analogues in sexual-enhancement capsules — older scandal, same incentive structure.

Elderberry is the 2020 case students should keep. Demand for *Sambucus* extracts jumped. Identity testing that only looks at a generic anthocyanin number will pass a lot of things that are not elderberry. If your COA says "4% anthocyanins" and does not say the method or the species confirmation (HPTLC, DNA where it is valid, anthocyanin *profile*), you bought a color.

I have not seen a peer-reviewed 2020–2021 paper that quantifies the *increase* in adulterated lots entering US supplement plants during the inspection pause. `[CITE NEEDED]` for any "X% of lots failed" claim you want to print. The qualitative risk call from ABC is on the record. A percentage would be a different object.

## Inspections paused. 21 CFR 111 did not.

Part 111 still required you to establish specifications for identity, purity, strength, and composition; to test incoming dietary ingredients or qualify a supplier with appropriate verification; to test finished batches or a scientifically valid skip-lot program; and to keep batch records. "FDA is not walking the floor this quarter" is not a specification.

What broke in practice, when it broke:

- Incoming ID skipped because the usual lab had a six-week queue.
- A second supplier onboarded in a week with a PDF COA and no method notes.
- Label claim for vitamin D3 still 125 mcg, assay on the new lot 60% of target because the premix was short — or the opposite, a hot lot, because someone doubled the trituration.
- Elderberry 10:1 from a broker who was not the extractor.

None of those require a cartoon villain. They require a purchasing manager under a retailer chargeback.

## Scrutiny after the spike

By 2021–2022 the public conversation had two tracks. Track one: more COVID letters (Mercola.com, 18 February 2021, letter 607133 — liposomal C, liposomal D3, quercetin/pterostilbene sold with COVID intended use). Track two: trade press and ABC continuing to treat supply as the silent problem.

Retailers and platforms tightened paperwork. Amazon's supplement and COVID policies are a moving target; do not cite a 2020 seller-central screenshot as current law. `[VERIFY]` any marketplace rule against the live policy page before you brief a 3PL.

Third-party programs (USP, NSF, Informed-Sport) did not get easier to join. They got more useful as a *signal* because the alternative was a PDF. That is the subject of piece 10.

## Operator notes that survived the year

1. **Identity is a method, not a vibe.** HPTLC plates, HPLC profiles, and a botanist on the supplier file beat a broker's "it's elderberry."
2. **Match lot to COA to label.** If the COA lot is not the drum in the cage, you do not have a COA.
3. **Price spikes are a test.** When elderberry or ashwagandha doubles in eight weeks, assume someone will offer you the old price. That offer is the sample you send to a second lab.
4. **Claims are the fast way to get a letter.** Adulteration is the slow way to get a recall, a civil case, and a retailer dump. Budget for both.

## What changed since 2020 (box)

Inspections came back. The memory of a year without them should not. Any brand that built its 2020 volume on a single broker and a supplier COA is still one failed assay away from the same week.

The honest 2026 sentence is not "the industry cleaned itself up." It is: the firms that kept Part 111 real during the quiet inspections are the firms you want to contract with now. Ask them for the 2020–2021 deviation log, not the pitch deck.
