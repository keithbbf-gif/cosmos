The word on the bottle is usually "probiotic." The word in the papers is a strain ID. Those are not interchangeable, and the 2020 AGA guideline made that hard to ignore — then tripped over it in its own tables.

## The definition that still governs the serious end of the trade

ISAPP (Hill et al., 2014; PMID 24912386): live microorganisms that, when administered in adequate amounts, confer a health benefit on the host. "Live," "adequate amounts," and "health benefit" are load-bearing. A dead powder with no CFU at end of shelf, or a blend that has never been in a trial, does not inherit the definition because the label is green.

Effects are **strain-specific**. *Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus* GG (ATCC 53103) is not "Lactobacillus." A 50-strain shotgun mix is not "the microbiome in a capsule." If the trial used one strain at 10^10 CFU for 8 weeks, your 2-billion-CFU untyped "lactobacillus blend" is a different product.

## AGA, June 2020: GRADE, not a vibe

Su et al., *Gastroenterology* 2020 (PMID 32531291). Technical review: Preidis et al., same issue. The panel used GRADE on named indications. They did **not** say "probiotics work for gut health."

The public-facing AGA page (published 9 June 2020) is the version a clinician will quote:

- **Prevention of *C. difficile* infection in people on antibiotics:** conditional suggestion of specific organisms / combinations (including *Saccharomyces boulardii* and a short list of defined multi-strain mixes) over "no or other probiotics."
- **Pouchitis:** a named eight-strain combination (the De Simone / VSL#3-type recipe, listed as subspecies).
- **Preterm, low-birth-weight infants / NEC prevention:** combinations the guideline names. That is a hospital neonatology question, not a DTC gummy.
- **Crohn's, ulcerative colitis, IBS, treatment of *C. difficile*, acute infectious gastroenteritis in the US context:** probiotics only in a clinical trial, or not recommended — read the exact row before you paraphrase. AGA was not kind to the IBS wall of SKUs.

ISAPP's contemporaneous note is worth keeping next to the guideline. They agreed strain-specificity is the science, then pointed out that several AGA-recommended options were written at species level, which "will likely lead to confusion for implementation." That is a fair complaint. A guideline that says strains differ, then lists "*L. acidophilus*" without a deposit number, hands marketing a loophole.

For a brand, the operational reading is stricter than AGA's weakest row: **if you cannot print genus, species, strain designation, and CFU through end of shelf, you are not selling the evidence.**

## What the 2020s did *not* deliver

A single "microbiome test → probiotic SKU" loop that performs in blinded outcomes. See piece 13. Consumer sequencing got cheaper. Evidence that *your* 16S report should pick *this* capsule did not keep pace.

Synbiotics and postbiotics entered the trade vocabulary. ISAPP has published consensus statements on those words (2019–2021). They are not magic prefixes. A postbiotic is not "probiotic, but we don't have to keep it alive," unless the preparation matches a definition and a trial.

*Saccharomyces boulardii* is a yeast, not a bacterium, and it shows up in AGA's antibiotic-associated *C. difficile* prevention row. That still does not make a DTC capsule a *C. diff* drug. CFU and viability for a yeast are a different QC problem than *Lactobacillus* (they tolerate different water activity and heat). If the CMO's probiotic SOP was written for bacteria only, ask what they changed.

## Label and COA problems that are specific to live organisms

1. **CFU at manufacture vs CFU at expiry.** If the label says 30 billion and the 24-month real-time data say 4 billion, you have a potency problem. Part 111 cares.
2. **Strain drift and supplier swaps.** The Chinese plant that sold you "HN019" last year may ship a different isolate this year. Ask for the deposit number and a genetic ID method.
3. **Survivability theater.** Enteric coatings and "stomach-proof" claims need data in the matrix you sell, not a generic acid-bath graph from 2011.
4. **Moisture and 3PL.** Live bacteria in a Houston warehouse in August is a stability study, not a vibe. Desiccant, water activity, and a ship-test belong in the file.
5. **Drug claims.** AGA wrote about *C. difficile* and NEC. Those are diseases. A supplement PDP that repeats the AGA *C. diff* sentence next to an add-to-cart button has a drug problem even if the guideline is real. Hospital use and DTC copy are different rooms.

## The evidence bar, written as a buying rule

| You have | You can say, maybe | You cannot say |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Strain ID + CFU-through-shelf + a human trial on *that* strain for a structure/function endpoint | The endpoint, hedged, with disclaimer | "Clinically proven gut health" as a category claim |
| Species only, CFU at manufacture | Almost nothing about trials | Any AGA indication |
| "50 billion + 15 strains" and a stock photo | That you spent money on CFU printing | That more strains are better |
| A 16S stool kit upsell | That you sold a kit | That the kit prescribed the bottle |

## What changed since 2020 (box)

AGA 2020 is still the US GI-society document people argue with. It raised the cost of vague copy for anyone who sells to clinicians. It did not empty the Amazon probiotic aisle. The split between those two markets is the story. If you want the clinician market, print strain IDs and stop quoting "the microbiome" as if it were one organ with one pill.

ISAPP's 2014 definition did not expire. Brands that treat it as optional will keep paying for it in returns, Reddit threads, and, if they copy AGA disease language, in letters.
