Ashwagandha (*Withania somnifera*) had a very good 2020s as a retail word. It also picked up a LiverTox chapter that got longer. Both things can be true. A brand that only quotes the stress RCTs is not doing R&D. It is doing selection.

## What "adaptogen" is doing in the sentence

The word is marketing anthropology. It is not a USP class. It groups ashwagandha with rhodiola, eleuthero, holy basil, sometimes ginseng, under "helps you handle stress." The FDA does not recognize "adaptogen" as a get-out-of-substantiation card. Each plant, extract, and withanolide profile is its own file.

If you cannot name the species, plant part (root vs leaf is not a detail), extract ratio, and marker method, you do not have a formula. You have a story.

## The human trials that get cited

Lopresti, Smith, Malvi, Kodgule, *Medicine* 2019 (PMID 31517876): a randomized, double-blind trial of a specific root extract vs placebo on stress/anxiety scales in adults. It is one of the papers every ashwagandha sales sheet includes. It is not a 2,000-person VITAL. It is not a license to say "treats generalized anxiety disorder."

Other industry-linked RCTs exist (sleep latency, "sports recovery," testosterone in men). They are typically small, short, and extract-specific (KSM-66, Sensoril, Shoden — different withanolide and withaferin profiles). **Extract-specific** means you do not transfer a KSM-66 endpoint onto a 5% withanolides leaf powder from a broker.

Vaidya et al., 2024 (PMID 38154316): a clinical safety/tolerability evaluation of a root extract in healthy volunteers. Useful as a *this extract, this dose, this duration* safety note. Not a population tox waiver.

I am not going to invent a pooled SMD for "ashwagandha vs stress." If you need one, commission a systematic review or cite one that exists and survive its risk-of-bias table. `[CITE NEEDED]` for any numeric "X% reduction in cortisol" on a PDP.

## The liver file

LiverTox (NCBI Bookshelf NBK548536, updated as cases accrued): clinically apparent liver injury has been reported with commercial ashwagandha products. Typical pattern in the chapter: onset 2–12 weeks, cholestatic or mixed, jaundice and itch, not usually flashy immunoallergic features. Course often prolonged, then resolution. Rare failure or transplant, especially with preexisting liver disease. Early trials did not advertise enzyme spikes; case reports started stacking after 2017 and kept coming.

**Björnsson et al., *Liver International* 2020** (PMID 31919589): five cases, Iceland plus DILIN. Latency 2–12 weeks. Cholestatic/mixed. Chemical analysis on available products confirmed ashwagandha; no other toxicants found in those tests. Four patients were also on other supplements; rhodiola was a possible co-suspect in one.

**Philips et al., Indian multi-center series** (PMC10531359, data 2019–2022): 23 ashwagandha-associated injuries; 8 single-ingredient after exclusions. Male-skewed. Cholestatic hepatitis common. Five had underlying chronic liver disease; three presented with ACLF and died on follow-up. Chemistry on implicated products showed phytochemicals, not a spiked drug. That last sentence is the one brands hate: *it was not the contaminant this time.*

Withanone DNA-adduct work (2021, PMC8320610) is mechanistic, in vitro / chemical. It is a hypothesis for *how*, not a human incidence rate. Do not turn it into "ashwagandha causes cancer." Do not ignore it either.

**What this is not.** It is not "ashwagandha is thalidomide." Case series plus LiverTox means a **rare, real, signal**. People with cirrhosis should not be the target market. Anyone jaundiced, dark urine, or right-upper-quadrant pain on the product needs to stop and see a clinician. USP-style liver warnings already exist on *black cohosh*; ashwagandha is now in the same conversation even if USP has not copied the sticker word-for-word. `[VERIFY]` current USP advisory language before you print a warning.

## Quality problems that are not the liver

- **Leaf vs root.** Leaf is cheaper and can be high in withaferin A. If the trial was root, and the drum is leaf, you have a different product and a different tox guess.
- **Withanolide assay theater.** A single HPLC number without a chromatogram or a method ID is how you buy spiked marker.
- **Multi-herb "cortisol" blends.** When DILI happens, causality gets ugly. Philips excluded multi-herb on purpose. Your SKU may not have that luxury in a courtroom.
- **Adulteration / substitution.** ABC BAPP logic from piece 02 applies. Price collapse or a too-good root extract is a retest.

## Other "adaptogens," shorter

**Rhodiola rosea.** Some fatigue/stress trials, species adulteration with other *Rhodiola*, rosavin/salidroside as markers. Not a free pass.

**Panax ginseng.** Older literature, blood-pressure and sleep interactions, quality wildly variable. Do not call it HRT.

**Holy basil (*Ocimum*).** Thinner clinical file than ashwagandha. Do not borrow ashwagandha's RCTs.

If the blend has six adaptogens at fairy-dust doses, you have a label, not a trial.

## How a 2026 SKU stays adult

1. One plant, named part, named extract, dose that matches a human study you will show counsel.
2. Finished-product identity (HPTLC) + withanolide profile + contaminants + residual solvents + heavy metals.
3. On-label: not for pregnancy (the traditional and the cautious modern position); stop and get care if signs of liver trouble; not for people with liver disease unless their clinician says so.
4. No "replaces your SSRI," no "cures insomnia," no "adrenal reset."

## What changed since 2020 (box)

In January 2020, Björnsson's DILIN/Iceland series had just published. By 2023, India had a single-ingredient death-containing series, and LiverTox had stopped treating the injury as a rumor. Retail did not slow down. That lag — science of harm moving faster than Amazon titles — is the R&D story. A brand that puts the warning on the label before a retailer forces it is the one I would contract-manufacture for.
