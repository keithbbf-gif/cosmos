The console is a table that gave up one long side.

French rooms pinned tables to walls with brackets. English and American rooms made free-standing versions that still behaved as if the wall were a partner. The depth shrank. The back could be unfinished. The front became a face. That is the form we now park opposite a sofa or beside a door and argue about.

In a hall, depth is the honesty test. Twelve to sixteen inches lets people pass. Eighteen starts to feel like furniture. Twenty-four is a sofa table that got lost on the way to the living room. I have installed 16-inch consoles that did more work than 24-inch ones because the 24-inch ones became places to pile laundry. Surface is a demand. People will fill it.

A drawer in a console is a gift if it takes the mail. Two drawers are a gift if they do not turn into a junk museum. Open shelves below are a gift only if you like dusting or you put baskets you will actually empty. I prefer a single drawer and a finished underside. The hall is not a library.

History's console was often a display for a clock, a pair of lamps, a card receiver. Our console is a landing for Amazon and a school form. Same height, different liturgy. Design for the liturgy you have. A clock can still live there. So can a dish that is ugly and right for keys.

If you work at a console, you are using a hall table as a desk. I allowed that in draft 16 as a two-minute note. Beyond that, your back and the passersby will file the same complaint. The console gave up a side. It also gave up a job. Do not hire it for both.

Height on a console is usually 30–34 inches because it wants to meet a hand with keys, not a forearm with a pen. That is why it fails as a writing desk even when the depth is honest. You will shrug up. The shrug is data. Listen to it and keep the console for the keys.
