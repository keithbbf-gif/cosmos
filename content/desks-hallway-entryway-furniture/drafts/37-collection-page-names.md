A collection page that calls every bench a hall tree is teaching the wrong century.

Words are how buyers learn. If we say hall tree and mean a shoe bench with three hooks, the next time that buyer sees a Victorian stand they will think it is the same object with extra carving. Then they will buy the stand for a 36-inch hall and hate us. If we say secretary and mean a writing table with a hutch, they will buy a hutch and wonder why the work cannot disappear. The damage is quiet and it compounds.

I keep a short glossary for adjacent pages. I would tattoo it on a catalog if I were allowed.

- Desk: overnight writing surface with a place for the legs.
- Writing table: desk that admits it is a table.
- Secretary: flap or doors that hide the work.
- Pedestal / kneehole: stacks and a void. One face.
- Partner: two faces.
- Roll-top: a curtain that moves.
- Console: table that gave up a side.
- Pier table: parlor form between windows. Rarely a hall.
- Hall stand: Victorian vertical machine — hooks, often glass, often a drip.
- Hall tree: combined hang-and-sit, if the sit is real.
- Coat rack / rail: hang only.
- Settle / bench: sit, maybe store. Not automatically a tree.
- Mudroom: dirt zoning. Not a foyer costume.

You can sell a shoe bench without the glossary. You cannot educate with the wrong noun. The BBF channel said, in another folder, that stolen words are how rooms hide. This is that fight in oak.

If a collection already shipped with the wrong name, the adjacent draft should say the quiet part: we used a prettier noun. Here is the job. Here is the older object. You can still buy the bench. You should know what you did not buy.

I would rather a page say "entry bench with hooks" in plain type than "Victorian-inspired hall tree" under a piece that has no drip, no glass, and no hall. Inspired is the softest verb in the catalog. It means we liked a silhouette. Silhouettes are not history. History is a room and a weather and a ritual. Use the plain type. The bench will still sell if the seat is real.
