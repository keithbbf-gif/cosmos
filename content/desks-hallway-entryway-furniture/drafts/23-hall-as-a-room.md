If the hallway is a room, it deserves furniture that admits it.

American houses before the bungalow often had a hall you could stand in with your hat on and not feel like you were in a closet. That hall took a stand, a chair, a table, a stair. It had a job: receive, sort, and release people. Furniture historians get excited about the parlor. I get excited about the strip of floor that decided whether the parlor would stay clean.

When halls shrank, we stopped calling them rooms. We called them circulation. Architects love circulation. Circulation does not get a bench. Then the family arrived with shoes and the circulation became a pile. The pile is a design failure we blame on children.

I walk a hall and ask four questions. Where does the wet stop? Where does the bag land? Where does a person sit? Where does the next person walk? If I cannot answer all four, I do not specify a "set." I specify one object that answers the worst question, then I stop. A house can survive without a console. It cannot survive without a path.

The Victorian hall was etiquette. The ranch hall is weather. The apartment entry is a mat and a prayer. Same room, different budget. History is not a demand that you buy a 90-inch Renaissance Revival hall stand. History is a demand that you admit the room.

BBF collections will sell sets because sets photograph. Adjacent writing should say when a set is three objects too many. One bench with two hooks can be a hall. A matching console, bench, coat rack, and cabinet can be a furniture store that moved in.

If you cannot stand in the hall with your coat half off and not feel like a problem, the furniture is too much or the plan is too little. Either way, do not buy the fourth object. Stand there on a Thursday with groceries. Thursday is the critic the catalog does not hire.
