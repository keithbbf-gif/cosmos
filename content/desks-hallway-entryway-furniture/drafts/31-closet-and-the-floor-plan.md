The closet did not kill the hall tree. The floor plan did.

By the time built-in coat closets became standard in American houses, the foyer had already shrunk or vanished. Bungalows opened into living rooms. Ranches offered a mat and a hope. There was no volume of air for a 90-inch carved stand, and there was a door in the wall that could hide the coats. The freestanding piece looked like a leftover relative. Of course it went to the barn.

Closets are good. They also lie. A packed closet is a black hole. You cannot see the rain jacket. You cannot sit to put on a shoe. You cannot drip. So the overflow returns to a chair, then to a store-bought tree, then to a "entryway collection." We are not inventing a form. We are correcting a floor plan that hid the job.

I will take a shallow closet plus a bench over a deep closet and no bench. I will take hooks on a wall that you can see over a closet you will not open. Visibility is a design tool. The Victorian stand understood visibility as status. We can use it as inventory.

If you have a closet, leave two hooks outside it for the daily coats. If you have no closet, do not apologize with a tiny rack that holds two hangers. Hold the household or admit you need a wardrobe in the next room. Furniture cannot invent square footage. It can stop pretending.

Adjacent collections should ask, in the first photograph, whether a closet is already in the room. If it is, the hall tree is overflow equipment. Price and scale it that way. If it is not, the hall tree is the closet. Build it like one.

I have taken doors off hall closets that nobody opened and put the same hangers on a rail you can see. The closet became a pantry or a vacuum house. The hall became honest. That is not always the right trade. It is a trade worth walking through with the tape before you buy a matching set to put in front of a door you already own.
