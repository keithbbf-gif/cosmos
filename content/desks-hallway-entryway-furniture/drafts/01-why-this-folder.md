I am not going to start with a product shot.

If I start with a product shot, you will look at the finish, and that is a good way to waste a Saturday and a bad way to understand a desk or a hall. The finish is the easy part. The hard part is the job. A desk has to hold work overnight without making you feel like a guest in your own room. A hall piece has to take a wet coat, a bag, and a body in a hurry, and still leave a path for the next person.

BBF collections can show you a desk, a hall tree, a console, a bench. That is a real door. This folder sits beside that door. It is the century that produced the forms, and the measurements that keep the forms from failing. If a collection page uses a historical name, the name has to survive the essay next to it. If it cannot, the page should use a plainer word.

I have built desks that were too deep for the room and too shallow for the work. I have hung hooks that looked right in a photograph and hit a child in the temple. I have watched a console eat a hallway because nobody measured the door swing. Those are not style problems. They are furniture problems. Style is what you argue about after the tape has spoken.

You will find dates. William S. Wooton patented a cabinet secretary in Indianapolis in 1874. Hall stands show up around 1840 with almost no ancestors except wall hooks. Pier tables lived in parlors, not halls, no matter what a catalog says. You will also find shop numbers — twenty-nine inches of writing height, twelve to sixteen inches of console depth, a bench you can actually sit on. Shop numbers are not statutes. Local code and the body in the chair win.

You will find refusals. Some rooms should not get a desk. Some halls should not get a hall tree. Saying that early is cheaper than saying it after the carton is on the porch.

Read this like a person talking, because that is what it is. If a sentence sounds like a brochure, it is wrong. Mark it. We will cut it on the next pass.

The fights this folder is built to win:

1. Desk history, so you stop treating a writing surface as a 2012 laptop table.
2. Desk design, so height and depth beat the photograph.
3. Hall history, so you know a hall stand from a pier table from a mudroom bench.
4. Hall design, so wet, grit, and passage win over the styled entry.
5. Collection adjacency, so BBF pages can sit beside this history without lying about the century.

If you only have twenty minutes, skip to the collections draft and the sequence draft. If you have an afternoon, start here and keep walking. The desk and the hall will still be in the house in ten years. They should earn the floor they occupy.
