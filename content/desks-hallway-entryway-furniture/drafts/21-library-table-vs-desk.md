A library table is a desk that invited company.

It sits in the middle of a room or under a window, drawers in the apron, legs you can walk around, a top you can spread a newspaper or a map on. Nineteenth-century libraries used them because a pedestal desk is a one-body machine and a library is a two- or three-body room. You can sit opposite someone. You can stand along the side. You can leave the books out overnight and the table will forgive you, which is my desk test, but it will also forgive a guest, which is the extra test.

I use the distinction when a client wants a "desk" in a living room. If they mean a cockpit, we are about to ruin the seating. If they mean a library table, we can keep the room. The drawers will be shallower. The cables will be uglier unless you plan a grommet and a floor box. The look will be furniture, not workstation.

Mission and Craftsman shops in America made honest library tables — oak, through-tenons, a drawer that smelled like wax. Those pieces still teach. The top is thick. The stretchers keep the rack out. You can pull a chair to any side. A laptop looks temporary on them, which is correct. Work in a shared room should look temporary enough that someone else can sit down.

Do not put a hutch on a library table and call it a secretary. Do not put a keyboard tray on one and call it a computer desk. If the machine needs a tray, the machine needs a different piece or a different room.

Adjacent collections sometimes photograph a console as a library table. A console has a wall. A library table has air. If you cannot walk around it, you bought the wrong name.

Stretchers are the quiet part. A library table without a stretcher will rack when someone leans to read the far page. The Arts and Crafts shops knew that and showed the join on purpose. You can hide a steel rod. You cannot hide a wobble. If the table is going to be a desk three days a week, build the stretcher as if those three days are the real job.
