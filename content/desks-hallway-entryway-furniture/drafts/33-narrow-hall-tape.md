A narrow hall will tell you the truth if you let a tape do the talking.

I measure the clear width at the pinch points: the open door, the radiator, the stair newel, the thermostat someone put at hip height. Then I subtract the furniture. A 36-inch hall with a 16-inch console leaves 20 inches if the console sits tight. Twenty inches is a shoulder turn. Two people cannot pass without a conversation they did not want. I like 36 inches of remaining walkway when I can get it. I will take 30 if the household is one body at a time. Below that I start refusing objects.

Doors are furniture's enemy in a hall. A storm door, an interior swing, a closet door — each one draws an arc. I tape the arc on the floor. If the bench lives in the arc, the bench will be hated. If the tree lives in the arc, the hooks will take a beating. People buy sets from a photograph taken in a 10-foot-wide studio foyer. Your hall is not that foyer.

Length matters too. A console that is 60 inches long in an 8-foot hall is a train. Leave wall for a person to stand and take a coat off without knocking a lamp. Leave wall for a future picture you will actually hang. Empty plaster is not a failure. It is clearance.

I will put furniture on one wall only in a hall under 42 inches wide. Two-sided furnishing is a luxury of width. If you must have hooks opposite a bench, use a shallow rail, not a matching cabinet.

This is the same math as kitchen aisles, and the kitchen drafts in this repo already said the aisle wins. The hall aisle wins too. Collections can sell you the object. The tape sells you the refusal. Use both.

Radiators and floor registers steal width you thought you had. I have specified a 12-inch ledge over a register and called it a console because the air had to live too. Do not block heat for a matching cabinet. Heat is furniture in winter. The tape should include the grate.
