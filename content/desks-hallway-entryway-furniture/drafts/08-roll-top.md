The roll-top is a curtain, not a personality.

Late nineteenth-century American offices had a paper problem and a pride problem. The work was ugly when you left it. The S-curtain — slats glued to a canvas, running in a track — let a clerk hide the ugliness without stacking it. That is a tambour. Furniture people will say tambour if they want to sound like they have been in a shop. Roll-top is the name that stuck because you can see it from the door.

The curtain is the invention. The pigeonholes behind it are older. The lock is older. What is new is the theater: one motion and the office becomes furniture again. Families loved that. So did bosses who walked the floor.

I will not tell you a roll-top is "back." Tracks swell. Slats shrink. A cheap tambour is a future repair bill. A good one is a joy and a maintenance plan. If you buy an antique, budget for the curtain. If you buy new, ask who made the track and what happens when the house is dry in January.

The form also lies about depth. People see the cylinder and think the writing surface is generous. Sometimes the cylinder ate the depth. You get a pretty hood and a skinny board. Measure with the curtain up. That is the desk. The hood is a hat.

Indiana and the Midwest made a lot of these when oak was a religion. They sit in farmhouses and law offices and they still work if the track is true. They do not work as a laptop cave unless you like typing in a wooden mouth. I have done that. I do not recommend it for eight hours.

If a collection uses a roll-top silhouette in a photograph, ask whether the curtain moves. A fixed hood is a hutch in costume. Adjacent history only helps if the moving part moves.

I have freed stuck curtains with wax and patience and I have given up and left the hood open like a mouth. An open roll-top that cannot close is still a desk. It is just a desk that lost its trick. Buy the trick only if you will maintain it. Otherwise buy a lid you can lift with one hinge and one honest motion.
