Twenty-nine inches is not a suggestion I invented.

American case shops have landed seated writing tops around 29–30 inches for a long time. European antiques sometimes sit a little higher or lower. Drafting tables tilt and jump. Kitchen counters are 36 and will ruin a writing session if you treat them as a desk. None of these numbers are law. They are habits that fit a lot of bodies in a wooden chair with an 18-inch seat.

I start there and then I look at the person. Long femurs want a higher chair and then a higher top, or they want a footrest and a pillow they will lose. Short femurs want a lower chair and a top that does not make the shoulders climb. A thick upholstered chair steals an inch of effective height. A keyboard tray steals more and adds rattle. Measure from the finished floor to the underside of the top for knee clearance, and from the floor to the writing surface for the elbow.

Laptop users cheat. They raise the screen and float the keyboard. That is two heights. A desk that only has one height will lose. I would rather give you a 29-inch top and a separate keyboard plane than a 27-inch "computer desk" that makes handwriting feel like a child's table.

Standing is a different number and a different draft. Do not split the difference at 33 inches and call it versatile. That height is where both jobs fail. I have seen collection pages do it. I will not.

Shop practice: mock the height with plywood on sawhorses for a day. If your neck complains, move the board. The desk you buy should match the board you could live with, not the rendering. Twenty-nine inches is a starting camp. It is not a shrine.

Children's "youth desks" that sit at 26 inches are fine for a year and then they are a posture tax. I would rather give a kid a 29-inch top and a footrest they will outgrow than a cute silhouette they will hate in seventh grade. Height is not a theme. It is a joint. Treat it like one.
