The modesty panel is a Victorian leftover that still earns its keep in an open room.

It began as etiquette. A woman's ankles, a clerk's knees, a desk in a parlor that was not supposed to show the underside of work. We can laugh. Then we put a desk in a living room, facing the sofa, and discover that cables, knees, and the ugly back of a drawer box are now part of the seating arrangement. The panel is a wall for a piece of furniture that has to be seen from two sides.

I do not require it in a study against a wall. I do require a decision. Either the back is finished like furniture — real panels, not stapled cardboard — or the desk lives where nobody walks behind it. Collection desks with a pretty face and a warehouse back are for cubicles. If your house is not a cubicle, pay for the back or hide it.

The panel also stiffens a long top. A 72-inch writing board without a rail or a panel will rack if the pedestals are shy. Structure first, etiquette second. The Victorians lucked into both.

Open-plan houses made this urgent. The desk became a room divider. A partner desk does this with two faces. A modest pedestal desk does it with a panel and a finished back. An island-style desk on legs does it with nothing, which is why the cables show and the dog sleeps in the kneehole.

If you hate the word modesty, call it a privacy rail. I do not care. I care that the living room does not have to look at your power strip. Adjacent collections should photograph the back. If they will not, assume cardboard.

A panel can also take a shallow shelf facing the room — a place for a book, not a second junk drawer. Keep it shallow. The moment the back of the desk becomes storage for the household, the living room has hired a sideboard. Hire a sideboard if you want one. Do not make the desk wear two coats.
