A pier table is not a hall table, and I am tired of catalogs that mix them.

The pier is the wall between two windows. In early nineteenth-century American parlors — Philadelphia inventories are blunt about this — the fashionable table sat there, often with a mirrored back, often under a pier glass, bouncing light and showing you off to yourself. Marble tops. Gilt. A form that wanted to be architecture. Chipstone's furniture journal has walked the inventories: these things lived in front parlors. They almost never lived in halls.

Why do catalogs call every skinny table a pier table? Because the word sounds expensive and old. A console is the honest modern word for a table that gave up one long side to a wall. A slab table in an eighteenth-century hall is a different ancestor. A pier table is a parlor light machine. If your table has no pier, no glass story, and no parlor, you bought a console. Say console.

I will not die on marble. Marble in a hall is a cold place for keys and a bad place for a wet bag. Marble in a parlor is a serving and lighting surface. Different weather. If you want stone by the door, I want a drip tray and a finish that can take grit. I do not want a name from 1830.

Design steal that is fair: a mirrored back can deepen a narrow hall if you accept that you will see your own mess. The Federal parlor used that trick for candles. We use it for shoes. Same physics. Different dignity.

Adjacent collections should retire "pier" unless the photograph shows two windows and a glass. History is not a spice blend. It is a room.

I will allow a skinny marble-top table in a wide foyer if the marble can take a wet bag or you put a tray on it. I will not allow the word pier to do the selling. Sell the depth. Sell the stone. Sell the fact that you have the width. The 1830 parlor will not mind being left out of the SKU.
