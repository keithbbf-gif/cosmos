Ready-to-assemble is a door, not a sin.

A crate that becomes a desk on a Saturday is how a lot of American work found a surface after 1980. Cam locks, dowels, paper laminate, a hex key, a missing barrel nut. I have cursed those boxes. I have also watched a student write a thesis on one. Usefulness is not a joinery contest. It is a surface that holds the night.

What I ask of RTA, because I will be adjacent to collections that live in cartons:

- The top should not oil-can. If it flexes under a laptop, the desk is a tray.
- The kneehole should exist after assembly, not only in the exploded drawing.
- Hooks should hit a rail that can take a yank.
- A bench should take the weight the copy promised, on a floor that is not perfectly flat.
- Hardware should be replaceable. A proprietary slide that dies in three years is a landfill plan.
- Instructions should admit which wall needs a stud.

Handmade work is another door. Antique is another. Steel surplus is another. The handmade-retail folder in this repo already walked the selling rooms. This folder will not rerun that map. It will say: pick the door that matches the job and the money, then apply the history. A $200 hall tree that has a drip pan and a real seat beats a $2,000 stand that cannot take weather. A $4,000 fall-front that you will use beats a $400 hutch you will photograph.

Collections are allowed to be the $200 door. They are not allowed to wear the $4,000 story as a costume. Adjacent writing is the costume check. After the check, I hope you assemble the thing and live with it. A door you will not walk through is just a wall with a SKU.

Assembly is part of the design. If two people cannot square the carcass on a carpet, the piece will rack for its whole life and you will blame the brand. Square it on a hard floor. Tighten again in a week. That is not a confession of cheapness. That is how a carton becomes furniture. Handmade work gets a clamp. RTA gets a second pass with the hex key. Both are shops.
