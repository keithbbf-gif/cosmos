# Sources — desks and hallway / entryway furniture

Paraphrased. Not copied. Date-stamp a reprint before you hand a number to a client or say a date on camera.

## Desks

- William S. Wooton, U.S. patent 155,604 (6 October 1874), “Wooton’s Patent Cabinet Office Secretary.” Indianapolis production roughly 1874–1890s. Awarded at the 1876 Centennial Exhibition. See Encyclopedia of Indianapolis; Smithsonian booklet *The King of Desks: Wooton’s Patent Secretary*.
- Fall-front / secrétaire à abattant lineage: 18th-century European writing furniture with a hinged flap that becomes the work surface and locks the pigeonholes. American slant-front desks carry the same job in walnut, cherry, and mahogany.
- Partner desk: 19th-century banking and counting-house form. Two working faces, one carcass. Not a synonym for “large pedestal desk.”
- Roll-top / S-curtain: late 19th-century American office furniture. The tambour is a privacy lid, not a style name.
- Campaign furniture: British military traveling forms, 18th–19th century. Knock-down, brass corners, short legs. A desk that had to survive a hold.
- Standing to write: older than the computer. Counting-house high desks, clerks’ ledgers, and 18th–19th century writers who stood. Sit-to-stand hardware is new; the posture is not.
- Typewriter: late 19th / early 20th century, dropped the writing surface conversation into machine height and a return for the carriage.
- Typical seated writing height in American casework: about 29–30 inches to the top. Shop number, not a statute. Chair seat near 18 inches. Confirm the body in the chair.

## Hallway / entryway

- Hall stand / hall tree: a 19th-century form, appearing around 1840, with almost no earlier ancestor except wall hooks. Coat hooks, often a mirror, often an umbrella well. Calling-card shelf on better examples. See *Antique Trader* “Furniture Detective: Hall Stands”; common encyclopedia entries under hatstand / hall tree.
- Victorian front hall as neutral territory: coats, hats, gloves, overshoes, cards. Not the parlor. Not the kitchen.
- Renaissance Revival hall stands of the 1870s as the large, status version. Golden Oak later. Form fades as halls shrink; by the 1920s often a chair, a table, a closet.
- Pier table: early 19th-century parlor form, typically between windows (the pier), often with a mirrored back and a pier glass above. Chipstone / *American Furniture* 2008: Philadelphia inventories put them in front parlors, almost never in halls. A catalog that calls every console a pier table is teaching the wrong room.
- Console: a table that gave up one long side to the wall. Depth is the honesty test.
- Card receiver / calling-card tray: mid-to-late 19th-century social hardware. The furniture exists because the ritual existed.
- Built-in coat closet and bungalow / ranch floor plans: mid-20th-century reason the freestanding hall tree went quiet.
- Modern “drop zone” / mudroom: late 20th–21st century American answer. Shoes, backpacks, wet. The Victorian hall stand’s job without the calling card.

## Design practice (shop, not code)

- Hall passage: keep enough clear width that two people can pass or a door can swing. Thirty-six inches of walkway is a common planning target, not a law. Local codes and accessibility rules win.
- Console depth in a working hall: often 12–16 inches so knuckles and hips still fit. Deeper is a sofa table pretending to live at the door.
- Hook height: adults often 60–66 inches; a second row near 36–42 inches if children live there. Measure the people.
- Bench height: near 18 inches if you mean to sit and tie a shoe. Shallower seats are for photographs.
- Wet and grit are the first materials of an entry. Finish and hardware have to admit that.
