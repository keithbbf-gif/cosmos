The gauge pin — or the little knife in a cutting gauge — follows the fence down the oak, and a bright line appears that is not graphite. Graphite is a suggestion. This line is a wall for a chisel. If I saw past it, I will see the step for the life of the piece. If I stop in it, the joint will look like I meant it.

I mark the face of every rail with a loop and a tail, the old cabinetmaker’s scribble, so I know which face the fence of every tool should kiss. When I skip the scribble I cut a tenon from two different faces and the shoulders do not match and I pretend, for a minute, that the wood moved. The wood did not move. I did.

## Knife, not pencil, where a cut will live

A pencil is for shop notes, for a cut that will be hidden, for a story stick. A marking knife is for dovetail shoulders, hinge gains, the baseline of a tailboard. The knife severs fibers so the saw has a track and the chisel has a registration. A pencil line has width. Your saw will choose a side of that width, and it will not always choose the one you meant.

A striking knife, a chip-carving knife, a dedicated layout knife — I care less about the handle than about a bevel I can keep against the square. One bevel, flat to the stock, so the line sits tight to the rule.

Squares: I have one I trust and I do not drop it. I check it against itself — draw a line, flip, draw again. Two lines are a square that has become a story. I have a cheap square I use for rough. It does not touch a carcase shoulder.

## Story sticks beat tape measures in a room

A tape measure is how you get a kitchen full of 1/16-inch disagreements. A stick with the hinge locations, the drawer heights, the reveal — you carry the room to the saw. I have built a wall of drawers from a stick and I have built a wall of drawers from a cut list I typed. The stick was faster and the reveals matched.

On a commission, the story stick is part of the drawing. It goes in the job folder. The next person can rebuild the reveal without calling the house.

## Gauges: fence to the face

A marking gauge with a pin is old and it works and it can tear. A cutting gauge with a knife is cleaner across grain. A wheel gauge is what a lot of benches have now. All of them are only as good as the face you register to.

Mortise gauges with two pins: set to the chisel, not to a ruler, if you can. The chisel is the mortise. A ruler is a rumor of the chisel.

I have watched people set a gauge from a ruler, then pick a chisel that was “close.” The mortise was close. The tenon was close. The glue was asked to be wood.

## Machines do not excuse sloppy layout

A tablesaw fence with a tape that is off by the thickness of a hook will give you a project that is consistently wrong. I cut a test on a scrap and I measure the scrap with a rule I like. I do not trust the Chinese decimal on the rail after someone bumped it.

A mortiser with a fence: the same face, every time. A router table: the same face. If the stock is cupped and you flip it because it sits better, you have just moved the joint.

CNC and point-to-point machines are layout in a file. The file can be wrong in a more expensive way. I still want a face mark on the part if a person will hand-cut a hinge later. Mixed shops fail at the handshake between the file and the knife.

## The line you do not cut

A centerline on a tabletop for the breadboard glue zone. A mark on the underside that says which way the grain runs when the slab is flipped. A “this edge to the wall” on a counter that has a bad knot you hid. Those lines are kindness.

I also mark the waste with a scribble of X so I do not cut the good side of a line after lunch. I have cut the good side. The X is cheaper than the board.

## Teaching the first line

If I have someone new, I do not start them on a dovetail. I start them on a gauge line and a chisel wall. Pare to the line, stop. If they cannot stop, they are not ready to saw a pin. This sounds stern. It is shorter than unteaching a year of “close enough.”

Close enough is how a door binds and a drawer racks and a pair of legs are two different lengths that both match the cut list because the cut list was written from two faces.

## A carcase that was two faces

I cut four legs from a story stick and I still ended up with a table that rocked, because two of the legs had been marked from the face and two from the other face after a cup made them sit “better” on the saw. The stick was innocent. The face marks were missing on the last pair. I recut two legs. I write the scribble first now, before I am sure I will remember which face I liked.

A hinge gain laid out with a pencil on a painted door can be a sixteenth wider than you meant. The knife wall would have stopped the chisel. The pencil invited the chisel to choose. The door still closes. The gap is a little drunk. I look at it when I visit that house.

## Sticks in a kitchen

A wall of drawers from a stick: I held the stick against the opening, I marked the reveals, I carried the stick to the saw. The tape measure stayed in the pouch. The drawers landed with gaps that matched. The next kitchen I typed a cut list from numbers I had written on a dusty wall. Those drawers were cousins, not siblings. I went back to the stick.

The stick goes in the job folder with the elevation. If they want a matching run in two years, the stick still knows the fridge that was proud.

## Light

Layout wants raking light too. A knife line disappears under a fluorescent that sits directly above. I tilt the work. I use a headlamp like a miner if the shop is a cave. I am not proud. I can see the line.

In late afternoon in a tin building in south Arkansas the light goes gold and every line looks warmer and less precise. I do not cut tenon shoulders at that hour if I can help it. I cut parts. I save the knife for morning. That is not poetry. That is eyes.

The first line on a job is usually the face mark, not the heroic one. A loop and a tail. Then the gauge. Then the saw is invited, and only then. If you invite the saw first, you are negotiating with a mistake. I have been in those negotiations. They are expensive and they always end with the gauge, later, on a new piece of wood.
