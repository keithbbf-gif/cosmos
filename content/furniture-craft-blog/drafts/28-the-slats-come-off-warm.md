The slat comes out of the box in a cloud and it is heavier than it was, limp in a way dry oak never is, and you have seconds that feel like they should be minutes. The strap goes on the outside of the bend. The slat finds the form. The clamps close from the middle out, or from the end if that is the form you built, and you do not stop to admire the steam. The steam is already leaving. What stays is a bend that will try to remember it was straight.

Springback is not a failure. It is the wood saying it is still wood. You overbend by an amount you learned on a scrap of the same lot, or you leave it on the form until it is dry and it still moves a little when you take it off. If you fight springback with a dry clamp in the assembled chair, you are storing a split.

## What steam does

Heat and moisture plasticize the lignin enough that the fibers can take a new set. Too little steam and you break the slat — a sharp crack, usually on the outside of the curve, a handful of expensive kindling. Too much time in a dirty box and you have cooked the surface, a dark case-hardening, a slat that looks old before it is born.

Green wood bends more kindly than kiln-dried. Chairmakers who rive oak and bend rungs live in that kindness. A furniture shop that buys 8-percent slats and wants a tight curve lives in a harder church: thinner stock, better grain selection, maybe a lamination instead of a solid bend.

I pick boards with grain that follows the slat. A run-out on the outside of a bend is a break you can predict. Straight grain, no knots, the growth rings oriented so you are not peeling a veneer off the curve. Quartersawn can be nice for a slat. Flatsawn can be a peel.

## The box, the form, the strap

A steam box is a tube that gets hot and wet, pitched so condensate does not soak one end, a kettle or a wallpaper steamer that is not a toy. I want steam, not a sauna of boiling water on the blank. The blank should be supported so it does not sit in a puddle.

The form is the shape plus the springback you measured. It is plywood or MDF or a hardwood rib, strong enough that the clamp does not become the form. I draw the chair’s curve on it from the drawing, then I add what the last three slats taught me.

The strap — metal, on the tension side — takes the stretch so the outer fibers do not blow. This is not optional on a tight solid bend. I have blown slats without a strap. They open like a book. The sound is distinctive. You will not forget it.

## Time, thickness, the clock

Shop talk says a minute per eighth, or an hour per inch, or other rhymes. [VERIFY] against FPL notes and a scrap. A 1/4-inch slat and a 3/4-inch slat are not the same day. I would rather undercook a scrap and break it than overcook a job lot into a brittle tan.

Out of the box, onto the form. I do not walk to the office. I have had a slat stiffen in the air while I looked for a clamp that was on the other bench. The clamp lives at the form now.

Leave it until it is dry enough to hold. Overnight is common. A week is better if the schedule has a week. A slat that feels dry on the outside and wet in the core will move after you mortise it.

## Laminations when steam is the wrong tool

Thin strips, glue, a form, no steam. You get a curve that is more stable, a grain you can pick, a repair story that is different (you can see the lines). A bent-lamination arm is a lot of modern chairs. It is not a lesser chair. It is a chair that did not want to argue with a solid 6/4 bend.

I do not hide lamination lines and call it solid steam-bent if the customer cares. Most customers care about the curve, not the theology. A designer who specified steam can get steam if the wood will take it. If the wood will not, I send a sample of the break and a sample of the lamination. The break is persuasive.

## In the chair

A bent slat in a back is a spring. It should be mortised without flattening the curve you paid for. A shoulder that fights the curve will split the slat at the joint — the other distinctive sound. Dry-fit. Pare. Do not machine a straight tenon on a curved rail without a jig that knows the curve.

Upholstery will hide a lot. It will not hide a back that is fighting the sitter because the slats sprang to a shape that is not the drawing.

## Safety, briefly

Steam burns. The box is a scald. Gloves, a path, no audience leaning in for a photo of the cloud. The photo can wait until the clamps are on. I have seen a person take a facial of steam for a picture. The picture was not good enough.

## A break I still hear

No strap, a tight curve, kiln-dried oak, a crack on the outside of the bend like a book opening. The sound is specific. The slat was kindling. The strap is not optional on a solid bend that is asking a lot. I overbend on the form by what the last scrap taught me. I do not fight springback with a dry clamp in the assembled chair.

A slat that felt dry on the outside and wet in the core moved after I mortised it. I meter now, after the rack, not after the romantic cloud. The cloud is a minute. The rack is a night or a week.

## Lamination as the adult answer

Thin strips, glue, a form, no steam: a curve that stays closer to the drawing. I show the lines if the customer cares about theology. Most care about the arm. A designer who specified steam gets a sample of a break and a sample of a lamination when the wood will not take the solid bend. The break is persuasive.

Short grain on a sawn curve is a weak spot you can point to. A bent slat keeps the fibers running around the corner. That is why we bother, when we bother.

## Why a shop in this climate still bothers

Because a bent arm is a different object than a sawn curve. Sawn curves cut across grain and make short-grain weak spots. A bent slat keeps the fibers running around the corner. That is strength, and it is a look — the grain follows the arm the way it follows a branch.

We have oak. We have humidity that will help you and then, in a house with winter heat, undo you if you rushed the dry on the form. I meter a slat after it comes off, not because I love meters, because I have assembled a chair that later relaxed into a shrug.

The slats come off warm. I still like that moment. Then I do not trust the moment. I trust the form, the strap, the scrap that already sprang, and the time on the rack. The chair will be cold in the dining room. It should have already forgotten the box.
