I set the unfinished side chair on the concrete and put a hand on the crest and a foot, lightly, on the front rail like a customer who will never admit they do this. The chair tried to walk. Not a step — a rack. The rectangle of the front elevation became a whisper of a diamond, then came back. The tenons in that front rail are the whole day. If they are dowels, the day is shorter than you think.

A table can hide a timid joint under a top. A chair cannot. A chair is a stick diagram that people trust with their whole weight, then they lean back, then they scoot, then a teenager rocks onto the back legs because no one has ever successfully forbidden it. Joinery that survives that is not “better craftsmanship” as a slogan. It is geometry plus long-grain glue plus a mechanical lock when you can get one.

## The loads are not polite

Dead load: the sitter. Live load: the sitter moving. Side load: the scoot. Torsional load: one leg on a rug, one on the wood. The back legs take a bending moment when someone slumps. The crest rail wants to tear the posts apart. The seat rails want to pull out of the legs in a way that looks, in a failed antique, like a clean circle of end grain where a dowel used to live.

Post-and-rung chairs — the ones with turned legs and round stretchers — solve this with dry, slightly oversized rungs in bored holes, often with a taper, often in wood at a moisture content that will swell in the post. That is a whole church (Windsor, ladderback, the Welsh stick). The glue is secondary. The swelling is the lock. A rung glued wet into a wet hole is a chair that will loosen in the first winter.

Framed chairs — mortise-and-tenon rails into square or shaped legs — solve it with shoulders. The front rail is the one I treat like a small table apron that happens to live under a human. Twin tenons if the rail is wide. A decent haunch. Pins if the design allows a show of them, or hidden dowels *through the tenon* as pins, which is not the same as replacing the tenon with dowels.

## Stretchers are not decoration

I have seen chairs sold with a stretcher that does not quite reach, glued to a flat, hoping. A stretcher is a beam in compression and tension depending on the rack. H-stretchers, box stretchers, a single medial stretcher with careful joinery — they are the lower half of the diagram. Leave them off for a “floating” look and you had better have heavier rails, deeper tenons, and a customer who does not have tile floors.

The rear stretcher on a dining chair is doing quiet work when someone leans. The side stretchers keep the front and back from doing the parallelogram. If you must slim them for a drawing, slim the *section* after you have kept the tenon cheeks. A skinny stretcher with a proud turning and a 1/4-inch tenon is a toothpick with a finish.

Angles: chairs are not boxes. The back legs rake. The seat trapezoid means the side rails meet the front and back at angles that punish a careless mortise. You cut those mortises to the rake, or you build a jig that holds the leg the way the floor will hold it. A square mortise in a raked leg is how you get a rail that is tight on one shoulder and open on the other.

## Seats that help, seats that hide

A woven seat, a slip seat, a solid wood saddle — each changes how the rails are loaded. A solid seat screwed down can act like a structural panel if the screws are in slots or if the seat is framed to move. A slip seat in a rabbet is mostly upholstery and a rectangle. It will not save a loose front rail. Upholsterers have been covering sins since there were tacks. The sin is still in the rail.

Corner blocks: I like them. Big, long-grain, screwed and glued to the rails, not to the legs alone. They turn the corner into a small braced frame. They are ugly from underneath and I do not care. The people who complain about corner blocks are not the ones who pay for the warranty visit.

A drop-in seat that rattles is a fit problem. A chair that racks with the seat in is a joint problem. Diagnose with the seat out.

## Glue, pins, and the repair thirty years out

Hide glue on chair joints is not cosplay. Chairs get loose. The right repair is knock apart, scrape, re-fit, glue. PVA in a chair joint is a future fight with a heat gun and bad language. If the shop standard is PVA because the schedule says so, say so to yourself, and pin the tenons.

A through-pin, drawbored, pulls the shoulder home and keeps it there when the glue fogs. You offset the hole in the tenon a hair toward the shoulder — a hair, not a rumor — so the pin cams the joint tight. Too much offset and you split the tenon. Too little and you have only a decorative peg.

I have repaired factory chairs whose “solid oak” rails were dowels in end grain, three in a row, glue starved. The fix is to cut the rail back, put in a new tenon or a floating tenon of real size, and stop pretending the original was a philosophy. Customers hear that if you say it without a sneer. They can see the old holes.

## The Arkansas chair, without a speech

Dining rooms in this climate swing. A chair built tight in August and sold into a winter house will check if the mortise walls were thin. A chair built of wet rungs will loosen when the heat comes on. Kiln-dried hardwood, meter-checked, is not romance; it is the minimum. [VERIFY] the shop’s target moisture for chair stock before anyone writes a number on a hangtag.

Warren’s mill years shipped furniture stock as well as boards. [VERIFY] the mid-century mix before a catalog sentence. What I trust without a citation is the physics: oak and hickory have been chair woods here because they take a beating. Walnut is beautiful and a little more forgiving to the tool, a little less stupid-proof at the rail. Cherry will move and will darken and will show every loose joint as a shadow line.

## How I test before the seat goes on

On the floor. No carpet. Hand on the back, side load, twist. Listen. A tick is a shoulder that is not home. A creak can be a clamp still in the room; take the clamps off and try again. Sight the front legs. If one has walked in, the side rail lengths are wrong or a tenon bottomed out in a shallow mortise.

Then I sit, carefully, on the rails like a fool, because the customer will. If I am not willing to do that, the chair is not done.

Designers sometimes send a drawing with a 1-1/2-inch rail and a 3/4-inch section at the tenon because the CAD fillet looked light. The light look is a loose chair. I send it back. The conversation is shorter if the first drawing already showed the tenon.

A chair that tries to walk on the shop floor will run in the house. Glue it anyway and you have sold a noise. Fit the front rail until the shoulders shut and the rack is a dull, solid nothing. That nothing is the product.
