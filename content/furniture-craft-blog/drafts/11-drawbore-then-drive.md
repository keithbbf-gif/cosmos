I bore the mortise walls first, assembled, dry, a bit that matches the pin. Then I take the joint apart and mark the tenon through those holes. Then I move the mark toward the shoulder — a little. The little is the whole craft. Too little and the pin is a passenger. Too much and the pin splits the tenon or refuses to start, and you stand there with a pretty piece of riven oak and a joint that will not go.

Drawboring is older than the F-clamp. Joiners used it because a frame on a jobsite does not have a dozen clamps and a Saturday. The pin pulls the shoulder closed and keeps it closed when the hide glue has gone to powder. I use it because I have built tables whose clamps could not reach, and because I like a joint that still means something if the glue line becomes a historical artifact.

## The offset is not a personality

People write romantic numbers. I have used something in the neighborhood of a strong 1/32 on small work and a little more on a fat table tenon, and I have also split a tenon by believing a video. [VERIFY] against a scrap of the same species and the same tenon thickness. Oak is not walnut. A dry tenon is not a damp one.

The pin wants to be harder than the surrounding wood, or at least as stubborn. Riven oak pegs, split along the grain so they do not wander, slightly tapered or headed with a simple chamfer so they start. Round dowel from a bag will work if you have to. It is more likely to be cut across some useful grain. I split when I can.

A square peg in a round hole is a different, also old, trick: the corners bite, the pin does not spin, the look is a little medieval. I use it on a bench. I use round on a dining frame if the drawing is quiet.

## Driving without theater

Wax the pin. Line it up. A few taps to start, then a driving rhythm that does not bounce the frame off the bench. Support the far side. A pin that exits should meet a block, not the air, or you will blow out the mortise wall and swear at a defect you made.

If the shoulder was open and the pin closed it, good. If the shoulder was open and the pin bent, the offset was greedy or the hole in the tenon was not clean. Bore again in a scrap. Do not “go up a size” on the pin and call it fate.

I cut pins long, drive, then flush with a saw and a block plane. Proud pins on purpose are a look. They catch sleeves. I like them on a workbench. I flush them on a table a child will crawl under.

## Glue and the pin together

Hide or PVA in the mortise, then the pin while the glue is still a film, not a skin. The pin is not a substitute for a fit. A sloppy tenon drawbored is a sloppy tenon with a bent pin in it. Dry-fit the shoulders first, always.

Some period work is drawbored with no glue. It can hold. I glue. I like both votes. A shop that is PVA-only still benefits from the pin when the piece is a chair or a rail that will see rack. The pin is the part that makes the warranty less interesting.

## Where not to

A tiny rail. A tenon that is already a sliver. A show surface where a blown-out exit would be the only thing anyone sees. A plywood edge pretending to be a mortise wall.

I do not drawbore a biscuit. That sentence should not have to exist.

## Pegs as costume

A dowel glued into a fake hole on a plywood “farm table” is a costume. Customers have learned to spot it: the peg that does not go through, the peg that sits in a hole with a gap, the row of pegs that do not line up with any tenon that could exist inside. If you are going to fake it, I would rather you did not. If you are going to pin, pin a tenon.

There is a middle: a peg that is structural but stops in the tenon, not through. Fine. It still has to meet wood that is doing work.

## This county has seen pins

Not every mill-era crate was drawbored. A lot of it was nails. The joinery that was meant to last — wagon, mill bench, the better furniture stock — knew the pin. I do not need a fake 1901 story to use a 17th-century joiner’s trick in a 2026 dining rail. The physics did not expire. The offset still pulls.

A piece that leaves a Wilmar floor with pins in the rails is a piece I can defend in a kitchen that gets leaned on. The pin is visible if you look. I want it to look like it belongs to a hole that goes somewhere, not like a sequin.

## A table I could not clamp

The dining base was already too wide for the pipe clamps I had on that wall, and the customer was coming at four. I dry-fit, I drawbored four rails, I glued, I drove, I watched the shoulders shut without a single pipe. The pins did the work the clamps would have done if I had owned more pipes. I bought more pipes the next week anyway. The pins stay in the method. The pipes are for panels.

That day taught me to bore the walls while the joint was dry-assembled and sitting on a flat bench, not on my knee. A hole bored on a knee is a hole that exits where a show face did not ask for it.

## Pins in a humid week

Oak pins driven in August into a tight hole will swell and can split a thin mortise wall. I wax, I do not pound as if I were closing a crate, I stop when the shoulder is home. Winter will ease the pin. If the pin was the only thing holding a sloppy tenon, winter will also ease the joint. The pin is not a pardon.

I have pulled a pin that had bent into a banana inside a tenon. The shoulder was closed. The banana was a record of too much offset. I keep that pin in a drawer. It is a better teacher than a diagram.

## The scrap practice I still do

Every time the species changes, I cut a mortise-and-tenon scrap and I drawbore it. I look at the shoulder. I cut it open once in a while to see if the pin bent. A bent pin that still closed the joint is information. A bent pin that opened a split is a smaller offset next time.

The drive is a short act. The layout is the long one. Bore, mark, offset, bore the tenon, chamfer, wax, drive. If you skip the mark and “eyeball toward the shoulder,” you will get a pin that is a passenger or a split, and both of those look like confidence in a photo taken from far away. Close, they look like what they are.
