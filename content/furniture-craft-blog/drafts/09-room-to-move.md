Pick up a cabinet door in January and shake it, gently, as if it were a gift you suspected. The panel should talk. A little wooden flutter in the groove. If it is silent and solid as a cutting board, someone glued the panel, or the panel has swollen to a press fit that will be a split in the stile by late summer, or you are holding plywood and the rules changed.

Frame-and-panel is not a style. It is a treaty. The frame is the structure, grain running around the rectangle. The panel is the field, grain running one way, free to grow and shrink across its width in a groove. The groove is the demilitarized zone. Glue is not invited.

## Why the old doors last

A wide board used as a slab door will move across the width and take the hinges with it, or cup, or split along a glue line that was asked to be a frame. A frame of stiles and rails, mortised, keeps its rectangle. The panel can be wide, even wild, because it is not in charge of the hinges.

Raised panels, flat panels, bead-and-coped, tongue-and-groove backs — the family is large. The treaty is the same. I have opened 1920s cupboards in farmhouses around this county where the panels had shrunk enough to show a raw stripe of never-finished wood at the edge. The stripe is ugly if you are selling new. It is also a record that the door worked. We finish the panel edges now so the stripe, when it comes, is not raw. We still do not glue them.

## Grooves, beads, the space

A 3/8-inch groove is common; so is 1/4 on lighter work. The panel’s tongue or its full edge sits in that groove with space at the bottom — space, not a hope. In a wet season the panel grows toward the groove floor. If it already sat on the floor in the shop, it will bow or split the frame.

How much space: enough for the species and the width. A 12-inch flatsawn oak panel wants more thought than a 5-inch quartersawn walnut. I have used rubber balls, space balls, a dab of silicone in the groove as bumpers so the panel does not rattle in a shipping truck and then still has room to grow. The bumper is not glue. If it acts like glue, you have failed the treaty.

Sticking — the molded inside edge of the frame — is millwork language. A good sticking hides the seasonal gap a little and catches light. A heavy, cheap profile tries to look expensive and collects finish. I like a small ovolo or a bead I can still dust.

Cope-and-stick on a shaper is how doors get made in a hurry without losing the treaty. Mortise-and-tenon frame with a groove run after is the slower cousin and the one I trust on a heavy door. The cope is only as good as the glue surface in the cope. A hairline cope on a wide rail is a joint that will open at the shoulder and still call itself a door.

## Panels that fight

Pith in the middle of a panel. Wild cathedral that wants to cup. A bookmatch that is pretty and mechanically stupid because both cups go the same way. I flip boards in a glue-up so the cups argue with each other. I do not put a board with a hidden check into a panel and hope the frame will keep it.

Plywood and MDF panels in a frame are stable. They are the right answer in a painted kitchen if the frame is still a frame. A “raised panel” routed into MDF is a shape, not a treaty, and that is fine if no one is selling it as quartered oak.

Solid panels get finished before they go in, at least on the edges and the back. A raw back and a varnished front is a cup in slow motion. Both faces. The back can be a simpler coat. It cannot be nothing.

## When the rattle is a defect

A rattle in a dining-room glass door is a different problem — you want glass stops, not a flutter. A rattle in a cheap slab that is not a panel is a hinge. A rattle because the panel is undersized by a quarter inch and walks from side to side is sloppy layout. I want a small winter talk, not a panel that can migrate and show a sliver of groove on one stile.

Pins or a spot of glue at the *top center* of a panel, sometimes, to keep a raised field centered so the reveal stays even as it shrinks toward that pin. One spot. Not a campaign. I have also seen a pair of brads in the groove, slots in the panel edge. Same idea. The panel still moves. It moves around a decision.

## Cabinet work in this climate

South Arkansas will test a glued panel before the invoice is paid. Air conditioning helps the house and lies to the shop if the shop is a metal building that swings twenty grains of moisture in a week. We stack doors on sticks. We do not crate them from the press to the truck in the same afternoon if the panels were just glued up as frames. The glue in the *frame* needs time. The panel needs to have already been a panel for a while.

A mill that sells raised-panel doors in quantity already knows this or they have a returns aisle. Architectural millwork standards exist for a reason; they are tedious and they keep lawyers out of the finish room. [VERIFY] any quoted tolerance against the AWI edition on the shelf.

## A silent January oak door

A solid oak panel that did not talk in January had been glued or swollen to a press fit. I put it down carefully and I did not praise it. August would have been the split. A bumper in the groove is not glue. If it acts like glue, the treaty is broken.

I finish panel edges before they go in so the winter stripe is not raw wood. Both faces of the panel, or a cup in slow motion. A plywood panel in a “solid wood” tag is a conversation I will have.

## What I look for on a door I did not make

I look at the back. Glue squeeze on a panel edge is a failing grade. A plywood panel in a “solid wood” sales tag is a conversation. A mortise that does not quite meet a tenon at the corner, filled, is a door that will open at that corner. Hinge mortises that were cut after finish, raw, will take on moisture and stain around the metal.

Then I shake it. January talk. August silence. Both can be correct if the calendar matches. A silent January panel in solid oak is the one I put down carefully and do not praise.

The treaty is older than any shop now working in Warren or Wilmar. You can see it in church pew ends and in kitchen cabinets that outlasted the families that ordered them. The panel was never the boss. The frame kept the rectangle. The groove kept the peace. When I hear that faint rattle, I set the door in the opening and I am satisfied in a way a perfectly silent slab never satisfies me. The slab is pretending. The rattle is the truth, quietly, where a customer might never put their ear.
