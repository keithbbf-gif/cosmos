I unroll a leaf of quartered oak and the flake repeats, a bookmatch that would have taken two perfect solid boards and a prayer about movement. The core is stable — plywood, or a lumber core that I trust, or an MDF I will only use where the piece can stand MDF. The face is the oak. The joint at the edge is a band of solid so a hip does not meet a paper-thin arris.

Veneer has a bad twentieth century in the public mouth because cheap print and cheap peel taught people to hate the word. Furniture shops used veneer for four hundred years because a crotch is a gift and a slab of crotch is a split. I use it when the face wants to be one thought and the structure wants to be another. I do not use it to lie about a particleboard box unless the box is an honest painted box and the veneer is not invited.

## What it is good for

Flake, burl, a matched sunburst, a door that must stay flat, a tabletop that is a picture and not a climate argument, a repair on an old piece that was veneer to begin with. Architectural panels. A reception desk that would be a ton in solid and a movement circus.

What it is not good for: a cutting surface, a place that will be filed and sanded by a stranger for twenty years, a child’s desk edge without a solid band, a wet bar top without a film that can actually live there.

## The core is the furniture

A good plywood core, even, dry, the face veneer oriented so the pull is balanced — you veneer both sides or you buy balanced panels. One-sided veneer on a wet day is a cup. I have cupped a door and blamed the glue. The glue was fine. The other face was empty.

Lumber core is old and good and heavy. MDF is flat and heavy and a sink for screws if you do not plan. Particleboard is a price. I will paint particleboard. I will not put a $200 leaf on it and call the result a craft object.

## Glue, press, the hammer

Hide glue and a veneer hammer: still a living method, still reversible, still how a blister gets dealt with. A vacuum press and a modern glue: how a shop does a run. A caul and a pile of weights: how I did it before I had a bag.

The leaf must be dry enough and not so dry it shatters. Tape the seams. The match is layout. I spend more time on the match than on the press. A bad match in a good press is a bad door.

## Edges

A solid band, mitered or butted, thicker than a thumbnail. Iron-on tape is for a closet. A shop that sells a dining top with iron-on tape is a shop that will meet that hip again.

I plane the band flush with a tool that will not dive into the leaf. A block plane, a scraper, a flush-trim that is set and not hungry. A hungry flush-trim is a crescent of core showing. The crescent will not stain like the face. It will look like what it is.

## Sanding through

The fear is correct. Veneer is thin. A wide-belt and a heavy hand and you are in the core, a pale wound. I mark the thickness in my head. I sand less. I scrape more. I do not “blend a scratch” with a ROS on a leaf.

If I sand through, I patch a leaf or I remake the panel. I do not paint a lightning bolt of dye and hope the room is dark.

## Honesty in the room

I say veneer when it is veneer. I say solid when it is solid. A mixed piece — solid legs, veneered panel — is a lot of good furniture. The customer can hear that in one sentence. If the sales channel cannot say the sentence, the sales channel is the problem.

A “solid oak” tag on a veneered top is a problem I will not help with. A “quartered oak face, stable core” is a sentence I will stand next to.

## Repair

Old hide-glue veneer: heat, moisture, a knife, patience, new hide. Old PVA veneer: a fight. A missing chip at an edge: a patch from a saved leaf of the same lot if you were kind to your future self. I keep offcuts of the leaf in the job folder. The folder is part of the joint.

On a repair I write the glue on the back of the panel: hide, date, which face is show. The next person with a heat gun should not have to guess whether the original shop was kind.

## Sanded through, once

A wide-belt and a heavy hand on a quartered oak leaf: I was in the core, a pale wound. I remade the panel. I keep offcuts of the leaf in the job folder now. A hungry flush-trim on an edge band left a crescent of core. The crescent would not stain like the face. I band thicker than a thumbnail. Iron-on tape is for a closet.

One-sided veneer on a wet week cupped a door. The glue was fine. The other face was empty.

## This shop, this county

We are a hardwood shop. Veneer does not make us less of one. A mill that sliced oak for faces is in the same family as a mill that sawed oak for solids. [VERIFY] any historic slicing claim before a brochure; I do not have a plant record of veneer slicers in my hand.

What I have in my hand is a leaf that lets me put rift stiles and a flake panel in one door without building a propeller. The door stays a rectangle. The flake stays a flake. The hip meets a band. That is enough craft for the word to stop being an apology.

When the press releases, I listen. A crackle can be a bond that is not a bond. A quiet panel is a quiet panel. I knock it. I look at the seams. I tape a note to the back: both faces, date, glue. The next person, sanding, might read it before they reach for 80. I hope they read it. I still sand as if they will not.

A reception desk that would be a ton in solid and a movement circus is where a leaf earns its keep. A cutting surface, a child’s desk edge without a band, a wet bar without a film that can live there: I do not put a leaf there. The match is layout. I spend more time on the match than on the press. A bad match in a good bag is still a bad door.

Hide and a hammer still fix a blister. PVA veneer is a fight later. I write the glue on the note on the back so the next repair does not start with a guess. The hip meets a band. That is the sentence I want in the room when someone asks what they are buying.
