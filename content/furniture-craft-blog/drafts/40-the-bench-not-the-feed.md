The feed wants a flat-lay of tools that have not cut anything this week, a sentence about passion, a before-and-after that hides the filler. The bench wants a sharper iron and a decision about a tenon. I know which one pays the photographer. I know which one pays the next thirty years of the table.

I am not against pictures. A good shop photograph of a dry-fit, a flake, a winter breadboard line — those are records. I am against a culture that has more to say about identity than about a shoulder that will not close. The word *maker* got tired from being asked to mean a personality. In this county it used to mean a shift.

## What I will keep from the current noise

People teaching joinery in public, for free, with the camera on the work instead of on their jaw. That is a net good. A kid in a town without a mill can see a mortise. I would have liked that.

People buying decent tools instead of the cheapest, and then learning to sharpen them. Also good.

People admitting a mistake on a video. Rare, useful.

## What I will leave

The contest of tiny pins. The distress kit. The “I built this in a weekend” on a piece that will be in a landfill in a decade, not because weekend work is bad, but because the boast is the product. The brand collaboration that puts a logo on a dovetail as if the dovetail needed a sponsor.

The sneer at production. Production is how kitchens happen. A well-jigged drawer in maple, square, that still runs in August, is a higher object than a sloppy hand-cut drawer that was filmed. I have made both. I know which one I would put in a house I loved.

The sneer at handwork from the other side, the mill that thinks a chisel is cosplay. A chisel is how you square a router mortise and how you save a hinge gain. The mill already knows this in the knife room. The furniture floor should not forget it.

## A mill town’s version

Warren and Wilmar do not need to import a maker story. They have a hardwood story, a railroad story, a furniture-stock story, a 2008 shop story, a list of rooms that already have the work in them. The heritage page keeps the corporate lines clean. The culture should keep the same cleanliness: we build here, we do not wear 1901 as a costume, we do not pretend a CNC is a moral fall.

CNC is a tool. A hand plane is a tool. The feed treats them as teams. The bench treats them as a sequence. I will nest a part and I will still shoot a shoulder. The customer is buying the shoulder, not the team.

## Teaching without a brand

If someone new comes in, I do not start them on a social post. I start them on a face mark, a gauge line, a mortise wall, a meter. If they want a following, they can follow the work home. If they want a job, the wall has to be flat.

I do not mind if they take a picture of a finished joint. I mind if they take a picture instead of pairing the joint.

## The customer is not an audience

A commission is not content. I ask before I photograph a house. I do not invent a testimonial. I do not put a child’s room on a network because the light was good. This should be obvious and it is not, in a culture that eats rooms.

A shop that needs the feed to eat will start designing for the feed: louder grain, louder joints, louder stories. Some of those pieces are fine. Many of them are loud in a dining room. I design for the dining room.

## Improvement without bloat

A better shop is not a bigger shop necessarily. It is a sharper knife, a clearer drawing, a finish that matches the use, a stack that is metered, a person who can say stop at the saw. It is also the joint you stopped doing because it was theater.

I have dropped details that photographed well and did not live well. A proud pin that caught a sleeve. A live edge that held bark and a future pest. A glaze that looked “historic” and looked dirty by week two. The feed liked them. The room did not.

## A detail I dropped

Proud pins that caught a sleeve. A live edge that held bark and a future pest. A glaze that looked historic and looked dirty by week two. The feed liked them. The room did not. I dropped them.

I will nest a part and I will still shoot a shoulder. The customer is buying the shoulder, not which team cut it. A good photograph of a dry-fit or a winter breadboard line is a record. A flat-lay of unused tools is a costume. I know which one I want on the wall of a shop that still has to ship on Friday.

## Where the brand sits, softly

Bradley Brand Furniture, Saline River Workshop, the mill footnote — they can use this pack later as a voice, not as a keyword net. The voice is: serious about the bench, light on the speech, unwilling to fake a quote or a corporate bloodline. If a piece needs a link, it can go to /heritage or /craft as a footnote. If it does not need a link, it should not have one.

I would rather a reader finish this essay and go sharpen something than follow anyone. The stone is closer than the feed. The stone does not care if you posted. It cares if the iron raises a wire edge.

The bench is not a metaphor for a life. It is a height, a set of holes, a place where a clamp can hang. I keep it clear enough to work and dirty enough to prove I did. That balance is the only culture I am interested in exporting. The rest can stay on the phone, face-down, in the office, until the clamps are on and the quiet has started.

Warren and Wilmar do not need to import a maker story. They have a hardwood story already in the rooms. We do not wear 1901 as a costume. We also do not sneer at a chisel from the knife room. The chisel squares the router mortise the machine left proud.
