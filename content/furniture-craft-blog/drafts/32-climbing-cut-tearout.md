The jointer takes a bite and the oak comes off in chunks, not shavings, a crater in the cathedral’s valley. I know before I turn the board over. I turned it the wrong way, or there was no right way, because this board is two grain directions in one face, which is what a cathedral is. I can keep going and make a deeper crater, or I can flip, take a lighter cut, close the cap iron, scrape the valley, and stop pretending the machine is a moral authority.

Tearout is fibers lifted ahead of the cutter and broken below the line you wanted. It happens on a plane, a jointer, a planer, a router, a shaper, a tablesaw that is dull. The fix is direction, sharpness, a smaller bite, a different cutter geometry, or a different tool. The fix is not “sand until the crater is a valley.” That sanding is how a flat becomes a dish.

## Reading the edge

On the edge of the board the fibers show as lines that run into the face or away. You want the cutter to slide off the fibers, like petting a dog the way the fur lies. When the fur dives into the face, you are petting backward. Flip the board. When the fur dives both ways, you are in the middle of a cathedral or a curly mess, and you work from both ends toward the middle, or you scrape, or you take a climbing cut with a router if you know what that costs you in control.

I mark arrows. I trust the last cut more than the last theory.

## Machines that punish

A planer with dull knives pounds. The pounding is tearout with a drumbeat. Change knives. A shallow cut. A slow feed on a wild board. A helical head is kinder; it is not a pardon for a board you should have scraped.

A jointer fence and a board that you refuse to flip because the cup sits nicer this way: you will get a nice cup and a torn face. I joint the face that lets me cut downhill, then I live with the cup in the planer, or I rip the board into narrower, less angry pieces.

Router: conventional cut vs climb cut. A climb cut can give a cleaner edge on nasty grain and it can also take the router for a walk across the shop, or across a hand. I climb only with a small bite, a wide stance, a bit that is taking a skin, never a hog. I would rather make two conventional passes than one heroic climb. The safety essay will not be kinder about this than I am.

Shaper: the mill’s version. Backing boards, sharp knives, the right rotation, a power feeder so a person is not the feeder. Tearout on a door lip is a door that needs a filler and a lie.

## Figured wood is not a surprise

Curl, birdseye, interlock, a crotch — you knew when you bought it. You do not get to be shocked at the bench. Sharp, light, scraper, a plane with a tight mouth and a cap iron close. A card scraper will save a crotch that a planer will eat.

If the figure is the sale, the extra hour is in the quote. If the figure is not in the quote, the figure should not have been in the glue-up.

## Glue lines

A glue line is harder than the wood. It nicks irons and it can chip out. I scrape glue before I plane a panel. I do not run a glued panel through a planer as a first move unless I like nick stories. A wide-belt with a good belt can take a glue-up. A planer can too, if the glue is fully cured and flush-ish and you accept a knife meeting a harder line.

Squeeze-out that you plane immediately is a smear in the pores. Let it gel, scrape, then plane. I have smeared. The smear stains darker. It looks like a mineral streak that follows the joint, which is a tell.

## When tearout is a design cue

A rustic piece can keep a little pick. A formal dining top cannot. I decide at the drawing, not at the finish. “We will just sand it” is not a drawing note.

Painted work can tolerate more. The crater is still a crater under paint if it is deep; it is a shadow. Fill, or recut.

On a profile run I will stop and scrape a torn quirk before the next stick copies the pick. Tearout is contagious in a molder the same way it is on a jointer: one bad pass teaches the machine a habit.

## A smear, a walk, a crotch

I planed squeeze-out while it was still a smear and drove it into the pores. The stain found it: a mineral streak along the joint, a tell I still remember. Let it gel, scrape, then plane.

A climb that was hogging, not skinning, walked the router. I was lucky. Two conventional passes are slower; I still have a shop.

Figured wood not in the quote should not be in the glue-up. I have put it in the glue-up anyway. The planer ate a crotch the scraper would have saved if I had started there.

The jointer cratered a cathedral valley because I fed it like a board with one direction. I worked from both ends, scraped the middle, and I did not sand the crater into a dish and call it blended.

## Teaching it

I hand a new person a scrap with an obvious grain dive and I let them joint it the wrong way once. The crater teaches better than a speech. Then the right way. Then the scraper. Then the raking light. Then I take the board away if they reach for 80 grit to “level” the hole. We fill, we recut, or we live with a shallow pick the finish will not turn into a cave. The customer’s top is not the classroom. The scrap is cheap. The speech is cheap. The top is not.

Tearout is the wood’s punctuation. You can read it and change direction, or you can talk over it with a sander. I have done both. The rooms that still look like I meant them are the ones where I changed direction.

On a glued panel I scrape squeeze-out before the plane sees the joint. A knife that meets cured glue is a nick that will print on the next ten boards. I have paid for that nick in time at the stone. The stone is cheaper than pretending the nick is not there.

Dull planer knives pound. The pounding is tearout with a drumbeat. Change knives. A helical head is kinder; it is not a pardon for a board you should have scraped. I mark arrows on the edge and I trust the last cut more than the last theory. A jointer fence and a board you refuse to flip because the cup sits nicer will give you a nice cup and a torn face. I joint the face that lets me cut downhill first.
