The flakes sit in alcohol like amber gravel and then they disappear, and the jar becomes a liquid the color of weak tea or of something stronger, depending on the grade. I write the date on the lid. Old shellac in a bottle from a store can be a lottery: it may still dry, it may stay tacky forever, a film that never becomes a film. Fresh cut from flakes is one of the last shop finishes that still feels like a shop.

Shellac is a resin from a bug, filtered, sometimes bleached, sometimes left orange. That sentence is enough zoology. What I care about is: it dries fast, it sticks to a lot of things, it seals in a way that helps blotchy woods, it dissolves again in alcohol so you can repair it, and it is not a good bar top for a wet glass left overnight unless you built it like a French polish and you still should not.

## Cuts, wax, the jar

A “cut” is pounds of shellac per gallon of alcohol. A 1-lb or 2-lb cut is a sealer and a washcoat. A heavier cut builds. I mix what I need for a week or two. [VERIFY] the house sealer cut before anyone prints a recipe on a hangtag.

Dewaxed if I am going over it with something that hates wax — many polyurethanes, some waterbornes. Waxy shellac is an old furniture finish and a pad polish friend. It can be a problem under a modern film. I keep dewaxed in the sealer jar and I do not mix the stories.

Alcohol: ethanol or the denatured the hardware store sells. The smell is the shop. The ventilation is not optional. I do not make a French-polish cloud in a closed room and call it atmosphere.

## As a sealer

A thin coat on pine, cherry, soft maple before stain: it can tame blotch. A thin coat on oily exotics before a film: it can help adhesion, sometimes. A thin coat on a repair next to old finish: it can be a bridge.

I sand it lightly. It powders. If it balls, it was not dry or it was old. If it powders into a gum, something is wrong with the mix or the wood was oily in a way I did not see.

Knots: shellac on a pine knot is old medicine. It still helps pitch bleed. It does not make a knot into clear wood.

## As a finish

French polish is a pad, a circle, a dull shine that becomes a depth, hours of labor, a table that looks like a violin. I have done it for a piece that asked. I do not do it for a kitchen island. There is a padded-on shellac that is quicker and still beautiful on a jewelry box, a clock case, a small top that will be cherished and not wet-cycled.

A brushed or sprayed shellac is a legitimate film. It chips if you knock it. It water-spots. It is easy to repair. A bedroom chest in shellac and wax is a civil object. A hotel bar in shellac is a dare.

## Under other finishes

Shellac under lacquer is a classic. Shellac under oil: you can, with care; the oil will not penetrate the same. Shellac under waterborne: often good if dewaxed, [VERIFY] the waterborne sheet. Shellac under oil-based poly: dewaxed, a test board, always a test board.

I have had a waterborne crawl on waxy shellac. The crawl looks like a finish that is ashamed of the wood. It is ashamed of the wax.

## Color without stain

Orange shellac warms a cold board. Blonde stays paler. A wash of orange on maple can look like a lie or like a sunrise; the sample decides. I would rather warm with shellac than with a brown stain that muddies the grain.

Garnet is a darker story. Pretty on walnut, easy to overdo on cherry that will darken anyway.

## Repair

A ring in shellac: alcohol, a pad, patience, sometimes a little more shellac. A ring in a conversion varnish: a different day, a different person, sometimes a full panel. This is why I still put shellac on pieces that will live with grandchildren and coasters that will not be used.

A crack in an old finish that is shellac: you can melt in. A crack in a poly: you are filling or recoating.

## The pad

A pad of wool or cotton in a linen cover, barely wet, a drop of oil for French polish if you are in that church. The pad is not a brush. It should not drip. Circles, then long strokes, then stop before you lift the half-dry film and leave a skip.

I have lifted. The skip is a valley. You let it harden and you level and you go again. You do not chase it wet.

## A bottle that never dried

I used a store-bought can that had sat on a shelf through a summer. The coat stayed tacky. I wiped it with alcohol, I cut fresh flakes, I started again. I write dates on jars now. I do not trust a mystery can on a customer’s chest.

Dewaxed under a waterborne that I had not tested: the film crawled, ashamed of wax I thought I had avoided. The can said dewaxed. The coat said otherwise, or I had contaminated the brush. I threw the brush, I recut the sealer, I tested. The test board is not optional when the topcoat is a stranger.

## French polish, once

A small mahogany box, a pad, circles, a depth that looked like a violin. Four hours. I would not do it on an island. I would do it again on a box that will be opened by a person who notices. The pad must not drip. I lifted early once and I left a skip. I let it harden, I leveled, I went again. Chasing a skip wet is how you make a valley.

## Why a mill-country shop still keeps a jar

Because a sealer coat on a blotchy door is cheaper than a stripped door. Because a small run of walnut boxes can be finished without firing the big gun. Because a repair on a piece that left the shop five years ago can be a half hour instead of a refinish. Because the smell, to some of us, is the smell of taking the work seriously in a way a spray can does not.

I am not anti-spray. I am anti-only-spray, the shop that cannot finish a sample without a booth. The jar is a booth for a board.

The flakes settle if I mix too much. I write the date. I shake. I strain if I see specks. I put a thin coat on a scrap of the job wood and I scratch it with a fingernail tomorrow. If it scratches white and powders, I have a finish. If it scratches gummy, I throw the jar out and I do not blame the tree.

A wash of orange shellac on a cold maple sample can look like a sunrise or like a lie. The sample decides, in their room, not in the spray bay. I would rather warm with a thin cut than with a brown stain that muddies the grain. Garnet on cherry that will darken anyway is easy to overdo. I stop early. Cherry will finish the sentence.
