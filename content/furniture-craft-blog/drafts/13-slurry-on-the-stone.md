The stone is already dirty when I start, a gray slurry from yesterday’s iron. I add a little water. The iron’s bevel finds the grit the way a shoe finds a step you have used for years. I am not meditating. I am raising a wire edge and taking it off. If I skip this, the next hour is me leaning on a plane that is scraping, and then I will sand, and then I will say the wood was difficult.

Sharpening is the most skipped “advanced technique” in the trade. It is also the least advanced. A millwright who would never run a dull knife in the molder will still push a cabinet scraper that has no hook and call the tearout character. I have done it. The stone is cheaper than the character.

## What sharp is

A cutting edge is two faces meeting. When they meet in a line you cannot see, and that line is smooth, the tool parts fibers instead of ripping them. You feel it as less force and a shaving that has weight. You see it as a surface that does not need 80 grit to erase a crime.

The wire edge is a little curl of steel that tells you the two faces have met. If you never feel it, you have been rounding the bevel or you have not reached the edge. People polish the bevel prettily and leave the last twenty-thousandths dull. Pretty bevels do not plane.

I use waterstones because I already own them and because the slurry does work. Diamond plates are faster to flatten and they stay flat. Oil stones still cut and they make less mess in a place that already has enough water. Pick a system and stop shopping. The shopping is a way of not sharpening.

A grinder, hollow grind, then the stone — that is a production rhythm. I hollow-grind when the bevel is nicked or the shape is gone. I do not grind every time I want a fresh edge. Grinding eats steel and heat-colors an iron if you are greedy. Straw color on a carbon iron is a warning. Blue is a different iron.

## The hook on a scraper

A cabinet scraper is not a worn-out card you found. You file the edge square, you polish the faces, you turn a hook with a burnisher at an angle you learned by ruining two scrapers. The hook is a tiny plane. It takes a shaving. If it takes dust, the hook is dead or you never had one.

I would rather scrape a tricky patch of grain than sand it into a dip. Sanding a dip is how tabletops get waves under raking light. The scraper keeps the surface in one plane if your hands are honest.

## Chisels are not pries

A mortise chisel can take a beating. A paring chisel cannot. Both need an edge. The mortise chisel’s edge is a little more blunt in angle because it is a wedge as well as a cutter. The paring chisel is acute and you do not lever it in a socket. I have a chisel in the drawer whose corner is missing because I did. It still works as a paint can opener. It is no longer a chisel I take to a dovetail.

Microbevels: a slight increase at the last edge, faster to refresh. I use them. I do not preach them. A single bevel that you can actually keep is better than a theory of primary and secondary and tertiary that you abandon on a Thursday.

## How often

When the plane starts to shine the wood instead of cut it. When the chisel crushes the end grain in a pin socket. When the knife does not leave a clean layout line. That is often, on a real bench day. A dedicated sharpening hour once a month is how you spend a month dull.

I keep a coarse diamond in the apron for a nick, and the stones on a board that lives. The board does not get buried. If I have to excavate the stones, I will not excavate the stones.

## Shop culture, not guru culture

There is a way of talking about sharpening that is a hobby inside the hobby: grit progressions, lapping films, Japanese names as a personality. Fine. I have been that person on a Sunday. On a Monday in a shop that has to ship, I want an edge that cuts, a stone that is flat enough, and a habit that the newest hire can copy without a seminar.

Flatten the stones. A dished stone rounds the iron. A rounded iron is a dull iron that looks mysteriously bad at the corners. I scribble pencil on the stone and rub it on glass with paper or on a diamond plate until the pencil goes. This is boring. It is also the whole game.

## Arkansas and the oilstone rumor

The state has a stone in its name, in the older catalogs — Washita, Arkansas stone, the white and the black. [VERIFY] any shop claim that “we still use true Arkansas” against what is actually on the bench. A lot of those stones are in drawers as inheritance. They still cut if you oil them and you are patient. They are not magic. The magic was always the habit.

A mill’s knife room is the grown-up version of this essay. They do not run a molder with a dull head because the profile goes to hell and the motor works too hard and the finish comes out burnished and burned. Furniture benches forget they are the same industry. The iron in a No. 4 is a tiny knife.

## A dished stone

I polished a bevel prettily on a hollowed stone and I left the last of the edge dull at the corners. The plane tracked. I scribble pencil on the stone and I flatten until the pencil goes. Boring. The whole game.

A dull smoother is a scraper with a tote. I keep a coarse diamond in the apron for a nick and the stones on a board that is not buried. If I have to excavate them, I will not excavate them. The mill’s knife room already knows this. The No. 4 is a tiny knife.

## The test I trust

I do not shave my arm for an audience. I cut end grain on a scrap of pine or poplar. If the cut is shiny and I did not have to lean, I go back to the job. If the cut is mashed, I stay at the stone. A plane shaving off the first board in the stack is the other test. A long, thin curl. Not dust. Not a scrape that sounds like a chair dragged on concrete.

Then I wipe the iron, set the cap iron close if I am in tearout country, and I put the stone back wet so the next person is not starting from a lie of dryness.

Dull tools are dangerous in a way people do not like to say. You push harder. The tool slips. The chisel finds the hand that was not a clamp. Sharp tools cut wood. They also stop where you meant to stop, because you were not leaning your weight into a failure.

The slurry dries on the stone in the shape of the last iron. I leave it. Tomorrow it will take water again. That is the whole culture I want: not a shrine, a stone that is obviously in use, sitting where a person would trip over it if they tried to pretend they did not need it.
