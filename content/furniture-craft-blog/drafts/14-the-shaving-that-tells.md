The first pass after lunch is always a little worse. The iron has picked up a nick from a glue line I should have scraped, or my hands are thinking about the invoice. I stop. I look at the shaving. If it is torn, if it is dust, if it is a thick ribbon from the middle and nothing at the edges, the plane is telling me the same things it always tells: mouth, camber, depth, grain direction, a cap iron that is too far from the edge for this board.

I can run that top through a wide-belt and have a thickness in a minute. I often do. The wide-belt does not read the grain. It leaves a surface that is technically flat and optically busy, scratches in a direction that finish will light up. The last pass with a plane, or a scraper, is how I decide what the room will see at 4 p.m. when the light comes in low.

## What the plane is for in a shop that has machines

Jointer and thicknesser get the board into the family. The bench plane gets the glue-up flush without a dip at the joints, takes the last thousandth, and leaves a surface that oil will love. A smoother (No. 3, No. 4) is a finishing tool. A jointer plane (No. 7, No. 8) is a straightedge that cuts. A jack is the worker that takes the ugly off. If you own one, own a jack and a smoother and learn them. The rest is appetite.

Machines have snipe, they have dull knives that pound, they have feed rollers that crush a soft earlywood. A plane has your stupidity, which is easier to correct mid-board.

I do not plane a kitchen run of twenty doors by hand to prove a point. I plane the doors that will be touched, the tops that will be photographed in raking light, the repair where a sander would dish the old surface.

## Set-up is the job

The sole should be true enough. It does not need a granite ritual every Monday if it was true in the spring and you have not dropped it. The iron should be sharp — see the stone essay; I will not repeat it except to say a dull smoother is a scraper with a tote.

Camber: a jack can have a curve so it takes a deep cut without digging tracks. A smoother has a whisper of camber or a very slight easing of the corners so you do not leave two railroad lines. A jointer is closer to straight. People fight about thousandths. I look at the surface. Tracks: more camber or lighter cut. A hollow in the middle: the iron is too far out at center or I am pressing like I am kneading.

Cap iron (chipbreaker) close to the edge on interlocked grain. This is the move that turned mysterious tearout into a solved problem for a lot of us who grew up sanding it. Close — a hair. Not covering the edge. [VERIFY] a measurement if you publish one; the wood will still get a vote.

Mouth tight on a smoother when the grain is ugly. Open on a jack when you are taking a lot. A plane with one mouth setting for every job is a plane you will fight.

## Grain is a map, not a mood

Read the edge. The fibers are a deck of cards. You want to slide off the deck, not into it. When the grain dives, you turn around, or you scrape, or you take a lighter cut with the cap iron in close. When the board is a cathedral face, the grain reverses in the middle. That is not a surprise. That is the drawing on the board.

Cherry loves a plane and then a streak of gum will tear. Walnut is mostly kind and then a curly patch will not be. Oak, especially red, will flake if you are dull. Quartersawn oak planes like a gift and then a ray fleck will pick up if you are greedy.

I mark arrows on the edge during the glue-up. I do not trust memory after a phone call.

## The last pass

Light. Skewed a little if it helps the iron enter. The whole sole on the work, especially as you start and as you finish, or you will round the ends and the table will rock on a glass. Listen. A good cut is a hiss with a little song. A scrape is a scrape.

Then raking light. A raking light is not optional on a top. Overhead fluorescent lies. I use a low window or a raking LED and I look for tracks, glue smears, a ridge at a joint. The sander can take the ridge. The sander can also take the flat.

I have handed off tops that looked perfect under shop light and showed every track in a dining room with a west window. That is on me. The shaving had already said I was rushing.

## Planes in a mill culture

A planing mill is a different scale: knives in a cylinder, feed, the smell of cooked resin if the knives are dull. The bench plane is the same idea, slowed down so a person can choose. I like both. I do not like a furniture shop that owns $40,000 of sanding and no iron that can take a shaving. That shop will sand through veneer and they will sand a glue line into a valley and they will call it a finish problem.

Wilmar can be both shops in one building if the habit exists. The habit is a plane that is not a decoration on a peg.

## A top that taught me raking light

I handed off a cherry dining top that looked like glass under the shop fluorescents. In the house, at four-thirty, every jack track from a pass I had taken too heavy showed as a shallow valley. I went back with a smoother and a scraper and I did in an hour what I should have done before the truck. Now I keep a cheap LED I can set on the floor. If I cannot see the window, I make a window.

The shaving that day had been thick in the middle and nothing at the edges. The plane had already written the report. I had filed it under “close enough.” The dining room filed an appeal.

## Cap iron, the close setting

On interlocked walnut I set the cap iron close enough that I can barely see a line of bright iron. The shaving crumbles instead of lifting a wedge of fiber ahead of the edge. This is not mystery. It is the chip breaking before the tear starts. When I get lazy and leave the cap iron a sixteenth back, the same board tears. I do not blame the board.

A tight mouth helps the same problem. I have a smoother with a mouth I can adjust. I close it for the wild stuff. I open it when I am taking a heavier cut on a kind board. One setting for every board is how a plane becomes a decoration.

## When I put the plane down

Painted work that will be filled. A plywood show face (you are cutting glue and veneer; a scraper or a fine sanding is often kinder). A profile that is already shaped and would be ruined by a flat sole. A customer who has paid for a perfectly uniform factory sheen and will see hand-plane tracks as defects. That last one is a sales conversation, not a moral failure. Some rooms want the wide-belt. Some rooms want the shaving.

I keep a few shavings on the bench until the job leaves, a stupid superstition. They say the iron was alive that day. When the job is gone I sweep them. The next board will need its own report.
