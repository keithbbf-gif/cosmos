The bridle is the joint you cut when you want the strength of a mortise-and-tenon and you want to see, from the edge, that the tenon goes all the way through the open slot. It looks like a fork and a tongue. It is a corner that does not hide. I use it on trestle uprights, on frame corners that can stand a little architecture, on a stand for a slab that already has enough mystery in the grain.

The open mortise is three-sided. You can cut it on the table saw with a tenoning jig, or with a router, or with a saw and chisel if the day is that kind of day. The tenon is the full width of the rail, usually, cheeks pared to a tap. Shoulders — there they are again — have to close on both faces or you have a fork that is only a decoration.

## What it is good at

In the plane of the frame, a bridle has a lot of glue surface and a mechanical lock against rack that is easy to pin. A table trestle where the upright meets the beam: the bridle lets you assemble like a person, not like a puzzle box. Knockdown versions get a bolt through the cheeks. Permanent versions get glue and a pair of pins.

It is faster than a stopped mortise if you are set up. It is also easier to get wrong in public, because every gap is a show gap. I like that. It keeps the work honest.

Compared with a half-lap, the bridle keeps more of the upright’s section. A half-lap on a skinny trestle is a skinny trestle with a bite out of it. A bridle leaves the cheeks.

## How it goes cheap

Uneven shoulders. One face closed, one face with a hairline that collects stain and looks like a crack. You cut both shoulders from the same reference, or you sneak the proud one with a shoulder plane until the light goes out.

A tenon that is thin because the sawyer (you) split the difference wrong. One-third / one-third / one-third is the usual starting split of the stock thickness. On 8/4 it is generous. On 5/4 you have to decide whether the cheeks have enough meat to take a pin.

Burned cheeks from a dull blade. They will not glue as well and they look like you were in a hurry, because you were.

A bridle used on a chair rail that wanted a stopped mortise for the look of the room. The open slot on the top of a dining chair leg is a dirt collector and a visual shout. I save the shout for the pieces that asked for it.

## Pins, bolts, the knockdown

A tusk or a bolt through a bridle is old shop practice and good traveling furniture. You bush the hole if you will assemble it more than twice. You do not overtighten a bolt into end grain and wonder why the cheek crushed.

Pins: two, staggered, not in a line that splits the tenon down the middle like a coupon. Drawbore if the shoulders need a pull and you do not want clamps on a tall trestle.

I have built a writing desk with bridles at the stretcher and stopped mortises at the seat rails — mixed joinery, same philosophy. The customer does not need the names. They need the desk not to rack when they lean to the ink drawer. People still lean to drawers.

## Cutting without a sermon

Table saw: tenoning jig, the open mortise in two passes with a little left in the middle for a chisel, or a dado set if you trust your dado set, which I do after I have cut a scrap. Bandsaw: the cheeks, then a chisel to the line. Router: a straight bit and a jig that keeps the fork from becoming a smile.

The inside corners of the open mortise want to be square. A router leaves a radius. You square it or you round the tenon shoulders to match. Matching a radius is fine. Leaving a radius and a square tenon is a gap that will not close.

Dry-fit until the frame is coplanar. A bridle can twist if one cheek is thicker. You will see it as a winding in the frame. You will feel it when the slab does not sit.

## Finish at the fork

Oil gets into the end grain of the tenon and can swell a tight joint if you finish before glue — so do not, unless you are finishing the parts and taping the glue surfaces, which is adult behavior. After assembly, the end grain of the tenon at the edge of the bridle will drink. I seal it so it does not photograph as a dry stripe.

A film finish will bridge a small gap and make it look like a glue line. Better to have no gap.

## A day on one trestle

I cut the beam first, because its width sets the fork. The uprights get the open mortises from a tenoning jig I have already tested on a scrap of the same thickness. Two passes, a wafer in the middle, a chisel to the line. The tenons are the full width of the beam’s meeting face, cheeks sneaked with a shoulder plane until the dry-fit is a tap and the shoulders shut on both faces of both uprights.

Then I stand the assembly on the floor without the top. I rack it. I look down the beam for wind. I mark the pin holes, I take it apart, I offset the tenon marks a hair, I bore, I glue, I drive. The whole dance is an hour if the stock was already milled. The hour is cheaper than a base that chatters under a two-hundred-pound slab.

I have used a bolt instead of pins when the table had to come apart for a stair. The bolt gets a washer that is large enough not to crush the cheek, and a hole that is not so sloppy the bridle can rack around the shaft. A sloppy bolt hole is a dowel’s problem in a nicer joint.

## Species at the fork

Oak will take a pin and a wedge and a little abuse. Walnut will show a crushed cheek forever if you overtighten. Cherry will darken around a glue smear you left in the fork. Soft maple will dent under a clamp pad you forgot. I pad. I wipe. I do not treat the bridle as a place the finish will hide.

A painted trestle can still be a bridle. The paint will show a gap as a dark line. I would rather the gap not exist than the paint be asked to caulk it.

## The trestle in the room

A dining trestle with bridles is a piece that admits it is a structure. In a mill-country room that is not an affectation. In a room that wanted a floating slab on hairpin legs, it may be the wrong joint because it is the wrong base. I do not put a bridle on a design that is ashamed of thickness.

Wilmar has cut enough oak that a trestle feels native. The joint should look like it grew up with the boards, not like it was applied from a magazine corner. That means the proportions of the fork follow the stock you actually have, not a sketch that assumed 10/4 when the yard sent 8/4.

I knock the assembled frame with a knuckle. It should sound like one piece. If it chatters, a shoulder is open or a pin is a passenger. Fix it before the top goes on. The top will hide the sound until the first dinner, when someone leans, and then the sound is a conversation you did not want to have over food.
