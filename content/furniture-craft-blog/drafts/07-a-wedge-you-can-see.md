The wedge makes a sound when it goes — not a crack, a rising tightness, like a jar lid that has found its thread. I stop before the sound gets bright. The through-tenon has come through the mortise, the kerfs have opened, and the tenon cheeks have pressed into the mortise walls as if the joint had grown a pair of shoulders inside the hole. Glue is in there. The wedge would still do something if the glue were a rumor.

I leave the tenon proud a little, then plane it flush with the wedge after the glue has set. You can read the joint. You can also read whether I rushed the kerf.

## Why through, why wedged

A stopped mortise is cleaner on the show face. A through mortise is stronger to inspect and, if you wedge it, mechanically locked against pull-out. Trestles, benches, worktables, the occasional dining base that can stand a little medieval in the right room — this is their joint.

The wedge does two jobs. It expands the tenon in the mortise so the fit gets tighter after assembly. And it makes a withdrawal without destroying the rail a harder project, which is either a virtue or a problem depending on whether you are building or repairing.

Fox-wedging (the wedge buried in a stopped mortise, expanding a tenon you cannot see) is a joint I use rarely and only when I trust the dry-fit with a stand-in. If the wedge is too fat you split the rail or the post and you do not know until you hear it. Through-wedging is the honest cousin: you see what you did.

## Kerfs and the end of the kerf

I saw one or two kerfs in the tenon from the end, stopping short of the shoulder. A kerf that runs to the shoulder is a split waiting for the first humid week. A hole at the end of the kerf — a tiny stop-bore — is old practice and still good. The wedge is hardwood, grain running so it does not fold, tapered thin enough to start and fat enough to do work before it bottoms on a shoulder you did not cut.

Two wedges on a wide tenon, outside thirds, so you are not blowing the middle into a barrel. One wedge on a small tenon. Contrast wood if the design wants a reading from the chair across the table. Same wood if you want the joint to be felt, not waved at.

The mortise can be slightly flared toward the show face so the expanded tenon keys in like a dovetail in section. That flare is a chisel trick. Too much and you have a sloppy start and a tight finish and a tenon that is not doing even work. I flare a little. I do not sculpt a trumpet.

## Fit before theater

The tenon should already be a tap-fit without the wedge. The wedge is not how you hide a thin tenon in a wide hole. I have seen that. The glue slush, the wedge slamming, the rail sitting drunk. It photographs if you crop. It racks in a year.

Through-tenons on a painted piece can be the same joint with less show. On a clear oak trestle they are part of the elevation. I square the protruding end, I break the arris, I do not leave a mushroom of crushed fiber. Crushed fiber is a tenon that was too fat or a mallet that was too romantic.

## Drawbore and wedge, not always together

Drawboring pulls the shoulder in. Wedging expands the tenon. You can do both. You can overdo both. A drawbore pin through a wedged tenon is a lot of machinery in a small piece of oak. I pick. On a bench I often wedge and pin from the side if the design wants iron or hardwood pins as a third vote.

Knockdown versions exist: a tusk tenon, a wedge *outside* the post, removable. That is a different and older honesty — the trestle table you can take apart for the move. The wedge you can see, and you can also pull. I like those for a table that will live in more than one house. I do not like them if the wedge is a sliver that children will find.

## Where the joint is wrong

A delicate chair crest. A jewelry-box lid. A rail so thin the kerf is the rail. Through-tenons on a piece that must present a calm face to a formal room — unless the room asked for the joinery. I will not force a craftsman look onto a cabinet that wanted to be quiet.

I also will not through-tenon a post that has no meat beyond the mortise. The wall beyond the hole needs to exist. A skinny modern leg with a through-tenon is a hoop. The wedge will split it because there is nothing to be a hoop with.

## Mill memory, shop present

Workbenches and mill fixtures have used this joint longer than furniture showrooms have put it on Instagram. A sawmill that built its own benches left through-tenons because the next shift could see if they were tight. That is a good test for a dining trestle too. If you cannot see it, you had better have been more careful, not less.

In humid south Arkansas the tenon will swell in the mortise. A joint that was already at crush will split the post in August. I leave the tenon a fit, not a fight, and I let the wedge do a measured expansion. Winter will ease it. The glue and the geometry should still hold. If your only hold was August swell, you built a seasonal chair.

## A bench that taught me the stop-hole

The first through-tenon I wedged without a stop at the end of the kerf split on a Thursday in a wet week. The kerf ran, the wedge followed, the apron opened a smile at the shoulder. I patched it with a slip and I still walk around that bench in my head. Now I bore a small hole at the kerf’s end, or I stop the saw a full half-inch short and I accept a wedge that does not go as deep as my ambition.

On a workbench stretcher I use two wedges and a through-tenon that is a little proud of the leg until the glue sets. Then I plane. On a dining trestle I may flush the same day if I am sure, and I still prefer the next morning. A wedge that is planed while the glue is rubber will smear into the pores of the post and become a lightning bolt under oil.

I keep wedges in a tin, already tapered, same species and a contrast species. I pick at the assembly, not at the saw when the glue is open. The tin is a jig of a kind. The open time is not a sawing time.

## When the wedge is a tusk

A tusk tenon — the wedge outside the post, removable — is the traveling table. I cut the mortise through the tenon’s protruding end, I taper the wedge, I leave a little extra length so the next house can tap it tighter after a dry winter. I do not glue that wedge. I do not lose that wedge. I have made a spare and wired it under the top. The spare is not cute. It is how the table stays a table after a move.

## The last pass on the show face

Plane the proud tenon after a full cure. A plane, not a sander that rounds the post around the joint. The end grain of the tenon will want to tear. A sharp iron, a tiny bit of moisture on the end grain if you must, a direction that does not pick up the post’s grain and take a chunk out of the arris. Then the finish. Oil will show every fiber you crushed. A film will hide a little and I still do not crush.

I like to leave one bench in the shop with a tenon still proud, unplaned, as a reminder of the step people skip when they are tired. The reminder works until someone planes it. Then I make another.

The wedge is a small triangle of leftover oak or walnut. It has a job. When it has done the job, it disappears into the flush face and becomes a reading. That is enough theater. The rest is the sound when it went home, and the refusal to hit it again.
