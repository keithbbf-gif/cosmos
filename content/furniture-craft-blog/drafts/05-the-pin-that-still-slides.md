In February the breadboard shoulder shows a dark line on the left, maybe a thirty-second, maybe more if the top is wide and the house is dry. Someone who bought the table in August will call it a crack. It is not a crack. The panel got narrower. The breadboard did not, not across that width, because its grain runs the other way. The outer pins have slid in their slots. If they have not — if someone glued the whole tongue, or drove square pegs through round holes like a punishment — the top has split, and the call is a different call.

I run a finger along that winter line and I am glad to see it. It means the joint was built by a person who believed the handbook.

## What the breadboard is for

A tabletop is a wide board, or a glued-up panel, grain running the length of the table. Across the width it moves. Hoadley is not optional reading if you sell tops. Oak moves more than walnut; flatsawn moves more than quartersawn; a 40-inch dining top in a house that goes from humid August to forced-air January can shed a dimension you can measure with a tape if you marked it. [VERIFY] the swing for the species and the room; do not hang a single number on a showroom wall and call it law.

The breadboard is a piece whose grain runs across the end of that panel. It keeps the end grain from cupping and gives you a finished end that can take a little abuse. It is not a clamp that holds the panel at summer width forever. It is a rail that stays straight while the panel breathes underneath it.

The tongue-and-groove (or a long tenon, or a series of tenons) is the register. Glue lives in the *center*. The outer thirds are drawbored or pinned through *elongated* holes in the tongue so the panel can shrink toward the middle, or swell, without detonating the breadboard.

## How it fails, in order of popularity

Gluing the entire tongue. The joint wins for a season. Then the panel loses.

Pins through tight holes at the ends, no slots. Same failure, prettier hardware.

A breadboard thicker than the top, proud as a lip, collecting crumbs and looking like a mistake even when the movement is correct. Flush is harder. Flush is the job.

A tongue that bottoms out in the groove so the breadboard cannot pull up tight at the shoulder. You get a gap on day one. Day-one gaps do not get called “seasonal.”

A breadboard in a different species with a different movement rate, chosen because a leftover board was pretty. Pretty is not a coefficient.

Screws from underneath in holes that are not slotted. The screw becomes a pin that does not know it is a pin.

## Cutting it so the winter line is the only line

I mill the panel flat and I let it sit. If it stills moves after a week in the shop, it will move in the house. The tongue is centered if I can help it, or biased to the underside if the design wants a crisp top shoulder and a hidden underside rabbet. Shoulders must be even. A high shoulder on one face is a breadboard that rocks.

Dry-fit the breadboard. Mark the center. Glue only what you marked. I like a tight tongue — tap, not pound — because slop here becomes a breadboard that is not in the same plane as the top.

Elongate the outer pin holes in the *tongue*, not in the breadboard, if you want the show pegs to stay centered on the breadboard face. Some shops reverse that for a look. Pick one and draw it. Drawbore if you want the shoulders pulled up without a clamp across a six-foot top you cannot reach. The offset is small. The pin is often a square peg in a round hole, corners taking the wood, or a round peg that you do not glue at the ends.

When I see a winter gap, I want it even, both ends, both sides if the top is finished the same. A gap on one end only means the panel is bound on the other — a hidden glue smear, a pin that did not get a slot, a finish that glued the shoulder by accident.

## Finish is a movement decision

A film finish on the top and a raw underside is how you cup a top. Coat both faces, not with the same number of romantic coats if you do not want to, but with the same idea: slow the exchange. Oil finishes “breathe” in the catalog sense; they still need to be on both faces. A breadboard finished as an assembly can glue itself at the shoulder with varnish. I tape, or I finish the panel first, or I am very careful at the line.

People will oil a top forever and never touch the underside. Then they ask about the crack. The crack is often a cup that split. The breadboard did not cause it. The climate and the one-sided finish did.

## Why shops skip it

Time. A breadboard is a day if you include the thinking. A plain end with a slight chamfer is an hour. Many good tables have no breadboard. A well-made breadboard is a choice to keep the end straight and to show that you understood the panel. A bad breadboard is worse than none.

I skip it on a narrow console, on a plank that is stable and quartersawn and not wide enough to pick a fight. I do not skip it on a wide flatsawn oak dining top if the customer wants that look and that end treatment. I explain the winter line *before* February. The sales conversation is part of the joint.

## Arkansas rooms, Arkansas oak

A house on the Saline side of the county, window units in August, heat in January, will move wood harder than a sealed museum. Furniture built here and shipped north into dry winter apartments will move harder still. The breadboard is one of the places you can *see* that the shop did not lie. The other places are back panels, drawer bottoms, and the underside of a table that is finished at all.

Warren cut a lot of oak. Quartersawn stock shrinks less across the width of a top than flatsawn of the same species. That is a reason to pay for rift and quarter on a wide glue-up, breadboard or not. The breadboard does not excuse a wild flatsawn panel with a pith-adjacent board in the middle. That panel will try to become a propeller. The pins will slide and the top will still embarrass you.

## The check I do before it leaves

I mark a faint pencil witness on the underside at the outer pins. If the shop is honest, those marks will not stay aligned with the breadboard edges through a season. I want the owner to see, if they ever look, that the movement went into the slot.

I also sit at the end and put a glass down. If the breadboard is high, the glass talks. Flush, then a light break on the arris so a sleeve does not catch. The winter line can be there. A ridge cannot.

The pin that still slides is a small wood part in a small slot. It is the least photogenic hero on the table. I like it more than the figure in the panel. The figure is luck. The slot is a decision.
