The spokeshave is a short plane with handles that teach you, immediately, whether you are cutting or tearing. I start at the high spot of a cabriole knee, or at the long fair of a back post, and I listen for the hiss. When the hiss becomes a chatter, I have gone uphill, or the iron is proud at a corner, or I am taking too much, or the wood has a knot that I should have seen in the blank.

Chairs are full of curves because bodies are. Machines can rough them: a bandsaw, a spindle sander, a lathe if the leg is a turning. The shave, a rasp, a spokeshave’s cousin the drawknife on green work — those are how the curve becomes a line you meant. A spindle sander left alone will leave a series of flats that finish will read as a cheap faceting. The shave blends the flats into a fair.

## Flat, round, concave

A flat-bottomed shave is a plane for gentle convex work and for chamfers that change. A round-bottomed shave gets into a hollow, the inside of a seat scoop, a concave stretch. I keep both. I do not try to make one sole do the other’s job and then blame the tool.

Wooden shaves can be lovely and they can be warped. Metal shaves with a good adjustment are what I reach for on a Tuesday. The iron is sharpened like a plane iron, maybe a little more camber, and it must be sharp. A dull shave is a scraper that chatters. People say they cannot get a spokeshave to work. They are usually holding a dull one at the wrong angle, taking a cut that would make a No. 4 sulk.

## With the grain around a post

A back post is a changing map. You work downhill on each facet of the curve, flipping the tool, sometimes pulling, sometimes pushing. The shave is one of the few bench tools that is truly two-handed and bidirectional. I mark the high spots with pencil and I cut them off until the pencil is a ghost and the fair is a fair.

A travisher is the seat-maker’s version, a curved iron for saddles. I will not pretend I saddle a Windsor every week. When I do, the travisher is the difference between a seat that looks carved and a seat that looks sanded into a depression.

Drawknife on green oak: fast, a little dangerous, the shavings like apples. Then the shave for the dry, final fair. Chairmakers who work from the tree live in that sequence. Furniture shops that buy kiln-dried blanks live in the shave and the rasp. Both are chair work. The green path is not holier. It is wetter.

## Rasps, files, the lie of 80 grit on a curve

A good rasp (and they are not all good) takes the bandsaw marks without digging a trench. Then the shave. Then a scraper if the grain picks up. Then a sanding that is *not* a reshaping. If you reshape with a sander, you will flatten the curve you just paid for.

I have ruined a knee by sanding the highlight off it. The highlight was the design. The sander made it safe and dull, like a bar of soap.

## Fair is a word you have to earn

I sight the leg as I would sight the sheer of a small boat. I look down the line. I do not trust the bench view only. I hold the leg in the light I will sell it in. A bump that feels like character in the vise looks like a mistake at the table.

Pairs of legs must match without being twins from a CNC if the design is handmade. I work them as a pair, a stroke on one, a stroke on the other. I have a story stick for the thickness at three stations. The tape measure is how they become cousins who do not speak.

## Where the shave does not belong

A profile already cut by a shaper knife — you will wreck the knife’s work. A flat panel. A joint surface. A painted MDF radius that wants a sander and primer. I do not shave plywood edges for fun; the glue lines are a nick factory.

## The nick

One nick in the iron is a raised stripe on every stroke after. I feel it as a ridge. I go back to the stone. If I keep going, I will sand that ridge and I will create a flat next to it and I will chase the curve around the leg like a fool. The stone is five minutes. The chase is an hour.

## A pair that did not match

I shaped the left back post until it was fair, then I shaped the right from memory. From the vise they were twins. From the door, the right knee was fat. I had not used the story stick at the three stations. I took the right one back to the shave and I cut to the stick, a stroke on the left for reference, a stroke on the right. Twenty minutes. The door agreed.

Now I work them as a pair from the first bandsaw cut. I do not fall in love with one leg. Love is how pairs become cousins.

## Green and dry

A drawknife on green oak is fast and the shavings are heavy and wet. The fair is easy. Then the piece dries and the fair changes a little. I leave meat for the dry shave. A shop that buys kiln-dried blanks does not get the wet speed. It gets a harder shave and fewer surprises after the fact. I do not lecture people into a green-wood religion. I lecture them into leaving meat.

A travisher on a seat: I work downhill from the high spots, I mark with pencil, I stop when the pencil is a ghost and the saddle is a saddle, not a bowl. A bowl dumps a person forward. A saddle holds them.

## Mill-country chairs

We sell chairs that have to take a person. The curve is not the product. The curve is how the product meets the person. A back post that is fair is less likely to have a short-grain weak spot you sanded into existence. A foot that is crisp at the floor and gentle above does not chip the first time it is dragged.

I do not need a brand story to say that. I need a shave that is sharp and a pair of legs that match at the three stations. When they do, I set them on the floor and I look from the door. If the pair reads as a pair, I can go to the mortises. If one leg is a little proud at the knee, I pick up the shave again. The door is farther than the vise. The door is closer to the truth.
