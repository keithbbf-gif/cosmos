There is a patch of curl in the walnut, right where a hand will land on the table edge, and the plane has already said no — a little tear, a roughness you can feel with a thumb. I could sand it. The sander would take the tear and it would also take a dish, because I would stay too long where the tear was, because that is what hands do. The dish would show under finish as a shadow that moves when you walk past.

I take a cabinet scraper, a rectangle of steel with a hook I turned this morning, and I lean it a little and I push. A shaving comes, thin, the color of the board. The tear is gone. The plane of the top is still a plane. That is the whole argument for this tool. It is not a rustic accessory. It is how you stay flat.

## Turning a hook

I file the long edge square on a mill file, polish the faces on a fine stone so the hook has a clean corner to start from, then burnisher, a little oil, a pass or two flat to draw the steel, then a tilt to turn the hook. The angle is learned. If I skip the polish, the hook is ragged and the scraper chatters. Too much tilt and the hook is a claw and the cut is a grab. Too little and you have a dull square edge that makes dust. I test on a scrap of the job wood. The scrap tells me faster than a theory of degrees.

I have a card scraper and I have a No. 80, the one with a sole and a thumbscrew that bows the iron. The No. 80 is easier on the hands and easier to keep at a consistent cut. The card is what fits in a pocket and gets into a cove if you bend it. Both need a hook. A scraper without a hook is a piece of steel you heat with your thumbs for no reason.

Thumbs: they burn. People wrap tape. I sharpen more often so I am not leaning. A sharp hook cuts with less heat because you are not grinding the wood with a dead edge.

## Where it earns the bench

Curl, interlock, a grain reversal next to a knot, a glue line that would nick a plane iron, a veneer that would blow if you planed it. The last coat of a film finish, rubbed, sometimes, with a dull scraper as a leveler — careful, that is a different religion and you can cut through.

End grain of a breadboard: a sharp scraper can take the fuzz the plane left. End grain of a through-tenon: same.

I do not scrape a whole flatsawn oak top as a personality. Oak can be scraped; it can also be a lot of time. I scrape the trouble and I plane the rest, or I sand the whole if the job is paint.

## Chatter

Chatter is a hook that is too big, a lean that is too steep, a thin scraper that is vibrating, a species that is hard and you are going too fast. I flatten the hook and turn a smaller one. I change my angle. I put a thumb in the middle of a card to bow it. I slow down.

If the scraper skips at a glue line, the glue line is proud. I take the glue off with a chisel first, not with a hundred skips.

## Scrapers and the mill

A mill’s scraper is sometimes a machine, a row of knives that live after the planer. The bench scraper is the handmade version of “we are not done just because it is thicknessed.” I like seeing both in the same culture. A furniture top that only knows a wide-belt has a surface. A furniture top that has been scraped in the wild places has a surface that will take oil without a map of scratches fighting the grain.

## The sander truce

I still sand. 180 or 220 after a scraped and planed top, lightly, to even the scratch if the finish wants it. Some oil finishes look better off the plane. Some films need a scratch to hold. The scraper is not a vow of no abrasives. It is a refusal to let abrasives do the shaping.

A random-orbit on a curl patch is how you get a dip that you then “blend” into a larger dip. I have blended. I have regretted.

A No. 80 with a bow in the iron is kinder to my thumbs on a big top. I still keep a card for coves and for a patch the sole will not enter. The card lives on the bench, hook up, not in a drawer under chisels.

## A curl patch on a dining edge

The plane tore a little crater in walnut curl, right where a hand lands. I scraped, a shaving, the crater gone, the plane of the top still a plane. Then I oiled. If I had sanded, I would have stayed in the crater until it was a dish, and the dish would have been a shadow at dinner. I have that dish on an older piece I do not show.

Glue lines: I scrape the glue first. A scraper that skips a hundred times on a proud line is a scraper I am using as a dull chisel.

## Hands

The scraper will teach you to keep your wrists in a way a plane’s tote does not. If your hands hurt every time, the hook is dull or your bench is too low or you are doing a job that wanted a No. 80. I am not interested in suffering as proof of craft. I am interested in a flat top.

When the shaving turns to dust, I stop and I turn the hook again. That might be every fifteen minutes on hard maple. Maple is honest that way. Walnut will let you go longer and then surprise you with a gum streak.

I put the scraper on the bench, not in a drawer under a pile, because a rolled hook dies in a pile. A living hook sits where I can see it, next to the stone, which is the other half of this sentence. On a maple top the hook dies every quarter hour if I am leaning. I stop, I turn it again, I do not keep going into dust and heat. The thumbs burn when the hook is dead. Tape on the thumbs is a confession that I stayed too long. A No. 80 and a living hook are kinder than a story about calluses. The surface stays a plane. That is the only proof I want.
