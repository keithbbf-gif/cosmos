The panel comes off the raise head and the field is a tabletop in miniature, a bevel that catches the light, a shoulder that will sit under the sticking of the frame. I run a thumb from field to bevel. If I catch a ridge, the knives were not in the same world, or I fed a cupped panel, or I took a second pass that did not quite meet the first.

Raised panels are how a wide board becomes a door without becoming a slab. The raise is not decoration first. It is a thinning of the edge so the panel can live in a groove, and a leaving of thickness in the field so the door has a face. The light on the bevel is the bonus that sold a hundred kitchen catalogs.

## Flat, bevel, the shoulder

A small raise for a kitchen, a deeper raise for a period room, a bevel that dies to a thin edge that will not survive a hungry sander. I leave meat at the edge. A paper edge in a groove will crumble and rattle in a new way — the way of a broken panel.

The field must be flat. A wide-belt after the raise can dish the field if you stay too long. I raise last, or I raise and then only kiss the field. I do not sand the bevel with a flat pad. I have flattened a raise into a cone. The door looked surprised.

Back side: a shallow raise or a rabbet, depending on the tradition, and always the space in the groove. The frame-and-panel essay is the treaty. This essay is the field.

## Machines, hands

A panel-raise head on a shaper, a router bit in a table for small work, a hand plane for a single panel if the day is that day. The hand-raised panel is a beautiful thing and a slow thing. I will do it for one door that must match a 19th-century raise that no modern bit quite gets. I will not do it for a kitchen of twenty.

The bit leaves a few telltales: a radius at the shoulder if it is that cutter, a slight burn if you dwell. I do not dwell. I feed. I take a second pass if the first was light, the same fence, the same face down.

## Grain on the bevel

The bevel cuts across the end grain of the panel at the top and bottom. Those ends will be pale if you stain, thirsty, a different color unless you size them. I size them. I have forgotten and I have made a door with two dark ends, a little frame of thirst.

On the long grain of the bevel, tearout is a pick in a highlight. Sharp knives, a downhill feed if the panel has a direction, a climb only if I am adult about it.

## Fitting the frame

The panel is smaller than the groove-to-groove in the wet month. I cut it to the winter-and-summer math, not to a snug pride. I finish the panel, especially the edges, before it goes in. A raw edge that shows in February is a blond stripe. A finished edge that shows is a quieter stripe.

A pin at the top center if I want the field to stay centered as it shrinks. One pin. I have said this. I still do it.

## Painted raises

A painted raised panel is a light-catcher. The bevel must be even or the paint will show a wobble as a shadow. Primer on the end grain. A little filling of pores if the species is oak and the paint is a high enamel. I often use poplar or MDF for painted raises. I do not apologize. I apologize for an oak raise under a thin paint that looks like a topographic map of pores.

## Matching a house

Old doors have raises that are not 15 degrees and a 3/8 shoulder. I take a section. I have a knife ground. I do not put a kitchen-catalog raise in a 1910 hall and call it close. The hall will not call it close.

A furniture piece — a cupboard, a chest — can use a raise as a quote of millwork. It should still float. I have glued a raised panel in a chest because I was thinking like a box maker. The chest split at the stile. The field was pretty. The pretty is in a photo in a drawer I do not show.

A second pass that did not meet the first left a ridge my thumb found before the sticking went on. Same fence, same face down, or recut. A wide-belt after the raise will dish the field if you stay too long. I raise last, or I only kiss the field afterward.

## A kitchen raise in a 1910 hall

The catalog angle was not the house angle. I took a section, I had a knife ground. The hall would not have called it close. A glued raised panel in a chest, thinking like a box maker, split the stile. The field was pretty. The pretty is in a drawer I do not show.

## The thumb test

Field, shoulder, bevel, sticking, after assembly. No step that catches. No daylight at the cope. A little winter talk in the groove. If the thumb is happy and the rattle is honest, the raise did its job. The light will do the rest, late in the day, when the bevel goes gold and the field stays quiet. That is the hour these doors were for. I try to be in the shop at that hour when I can. The fluorescent does not know what a raise is for.

A hand-raised panel for one door that must match a nineteenth-century section is a day I will spend. A kitchen of twenty is a head I will keep sharp. I finish the panel before it goes in, especially the edges. A raw winter stripe is a blond I caused. A finished stripe is quieter. Space in the groove is still the treaty from the frame essay. This essay is only the field, the bevel, and the thumb that finds the ridge two passes did not meet.

Painted raises need an even bevel or the paint shows a wobble as a shadow. Poplar or MDF without apology. Oak under a thin enamel is a map of pores. A bit that dwells burns the shoulder. I feed. I do not dwell.

A paper-thin edge in a groove will crumble and rattle in a new way — the way of a broken panel. I leave meat at the edge. One pin at the top center if I want the field to stay centered as it shrinks. I still do it.

The light on the bevel sold a hundred kitchen catalogs. The groove keeps the door a door. A small raise for a kitchen, a deeper raise for a period room: the section on the house drawing decides, not the catalog angle on the box. I do not sand the bevel with a flat pad. I have flattened a raise into a cone.
