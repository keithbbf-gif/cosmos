The board has been in the shop for a week and it feels like furniture. The meter says it does not. I take three readings, I avoid a knot, I go past the end grain that always lies a little, and I write the numbers on the edge in pencil. If the core is wet and the shell is dry, I have a board that will move after I have been clever. I put it back on sticks. I do not argue with a number I asked for.

Moisture content is the quiet half of joinery. A perfect tenon in wet oak is a loose tenon in January. A drawer fitted in a wet week is a drawer that will not open in a wetter week, or a drawer that is sloppy when the heat comes on, depending on which way you guessed.

## What the number is

Water in the wood, as a percent of the dry weight. A tree is soaked. Air-drying gets it toward the air. A kiln gets it toward a target. A house in winter with heat on can be drier than a shop in June. The wood will try to meet the air it lives in. Equilibrium moisture content is the grown-up phrase. I think of it as: the room will vote, later.

Furniture for interior use is often targeted in a band that people quote as 6–8 percent in dry northern houses and a little higher in humid places. [VERIFY] the house target for this shop and this customer climate. A piece built at 11 percent for a winter apartment in Chicago is a different spec than a piece built for a lake house with windows open.

I do not ship a chair at a shop-wet number because the schedule is a shop-wet number.

## Meters, and the ways they lie

Pin meters: good, a hole, a species setting, a temperature. Pins that only kiss the surface are a surface story. Longer pins, or a pinless that you have learned, for a core story. Pinless meters are fast and they can be fooled by density, by a wet sticker stain, by your hand.

A meter that has not been checked against a calibration block is a rumor. I check. I still take more than one reading.

End grain reads differently. Fresh from the planer, a board can look drier because you are in a new face. I wait a beat. I meter again tomorrow if the number is on a knife edge.

## Acclimation is not a weekend unless it is

A delivery of oak in January into a heated shop will lose moisture and cup if it was wetter than the shop. A delivery in August may gain. I sticker it. I give it time that matches the thickness. 8/4 is not 4/4. A weekend is a story we tell ourselves about 4/4 that was already close.

I have stickered a lift in the machine bay while the finish room held the same species a point drier. On a wide top I would rather wait a day and assemble in the room that matches the house than win a fight with a clamp.

If the customer wants a slab next week and the slab is 12 percent, the answer is no, or the answer is a design that can move, or the answer is a different slab. I have said no. It is cheaper than a yes that becomes a crack.

## Case-hardening, the kiln scar

A kiln that dries the shell too fast and leaves the core in tension: you plane the board and it moves as if it were alive in a bad way. You rip it and the kerf closes on the blade or opens like a mouth. That board is a mill problem. I send it back if I can. I cut it into short parts if I cannot. I do not make a 40-inch top out of a case-hardened hero.

## Finish slows, it does not stop

A film on both faces slows the vote. It does not cancel it. Oil slows less. Paint on one face is a cupping machine. I have said this in other essays. I say it here because the meter is why those essays exist.

A piece that leaves a humid shop and sits in a truck in the sun, then in an air-conditioned showroom, then in a dry house, will move even if the meter was perfect on Tuesday. I design joints for that trip. I do not design joints for a museum.

## Shop practice

Write the number. Date it. If two people meter, they use the same species setting. I have argued about a board that was two species settings apart on the same meter. The board was innocent. We use the same setting now. A meter that has not seen a calibration block is a rumor. I check the block.

Keep a sample of the job wood in the finish room and in the machine room if those rooms live different lives. A metal building in Arkansas can swing. The finish room with a dehumidifier is a different climate than the assembly floor. Assemble where it will be, if you can, or accept a small fight.

## A slab that wanted Saturday

The slab was handsome at twelve percent and the party was Saturday. I said no. They took a different slab. The handsome one sat on sticks and became a better table a month later. A yes that weekend would have been a crack in their photos.

A pinless reading fooled by a sticker stain once sent me into a glue-up I had to recut. The stain lied; the joint did not forgive.

## Why a mill town should already know

Because the mill’s product was, in part, a moisture product. Flooring at the wrong MC is a lawsuit. Furniture stock at the wrong MC is a shop’s winter. The old plants had kilns and they had men who understood a stack. We have a handheld meter and no excuse.

I do not need 1901 to take a reading. I need the reading.

When the meter says yes, I still look at the board. A yes with a twist is a yes I cut around. A yes that is beautiful and too short is a yes for another job. The meter is not a designer. It is a gate. I have started to like the gate. It has saved me from being interesting on a board that only wanted another week on the sticks.

Finish on both faces slows the room’s vote. It does not cancel it. The winter house will still speak. The joints have to have been cut as if it would.

I have built a top that left the shop at a number I trusted and still opened a hair at a breadboard pin because the truck sat in the sun. The meter was not wrong on Tuesday. The trip was still real. I design for the trip now the way I design for January.

A board that reads dry on the shell and wet in the core is a board that will move after I have been clever. That is why I write three numbers on the edge, not one hero reading from the pretty face.
