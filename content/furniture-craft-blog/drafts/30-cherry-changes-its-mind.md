I set a new cherry apron next to a rail we made two summers ago and the customer thinks I am showing two species. I am not. The old rail has gone to that deep, even russet people mean when they say cherry. The new apron is a blond with a blush, gum streaks, a little green if the light is ugly. I tell them the apron is late. The apron will catch up if we let the sun do some of it and if we do not bury the piece under a tablecloth for a year and then remove the tablecloth and call the pale shadow a defect.

Cherry changes its mind in public. That is the sale, if you tell it. If you do not tell it, the sale is a refinish.

## Light, oxidation, the tablecloth

UV and air both work. A window will advance a top faster than the underside. A runner on a dresser will leave a stripe. I finish both faces, I warn about runners, I suggest they let the piece live without a full cover for the first months if they want even color. I cannot make them. I can refuse to call the stripe a shop error.

Some finishes include UV inhibitors and will slow the show. Some people want that pale Danish look. Fine. Say so. Do not use a UV-heavy film and then complain that the cherry is not “cherry enough” in October.

## Gum, pitch, the streaks

Cherry gum pockets are brown-black seams. They can bleed into a finish. I pick them, I shellac them, I accept a small one as the wood. A big one in the middle of a dining top is a board I should have turned into an apron.

Pitch streaks: I do not stain over them to hide them. I rearrange the glue-up. If the only boards in the lift are streaky, we are in a different conversation: a calmer finish, a panel in a frame, a painted piece.

## Blotch

Cherry takes pigment stain like a newspaper takes water. I do not pigment-stain cherry if I can help it. A dye, a washcoat, a toner — maybe, for a match. The better match is time. The better color is the one the wood is already walking toward.

If a house needs a match to an old, darkened cherry door, I take a sample and I do not promise the new work will be there on delivery day. I can get close with color. I cannot get two years in a week without looking like I stained it, because that is what I would have done.

## Working it

Cherry burns with a dull tool: a brown burnish on a saw cut, a shiny scrape on a plane track. Sharp, light, a scraper on the curl. It machines well and it dents less than walnut and it will still take a ring.

It moves. Not oak-moves, not nothing. Breadboard rules still apply. A wide flatsawn cherry top will cup if you finish one face.

It sands into a surface that looks finished before it is. That is a trap. I still build the finish for the use.

## Sapwood

Cherry sap is pale. A little at the edge of a rustic piece can be fine. A sap streak through a formal top is a lightning bolt. I cut it out or I put it on the underside or I sell the piece as a piece that includes it. I do not stain the sap and the heart as if they were the same thirst.

## Shop sequencing

A cherry job next to a finishing job that throws UV or next to a window will start to color before its twin on the cart in the dark. I keep a set together. I have had two doors of a cupboard come out cousins because one door sat on the sunny bench. They evened, mostly, after a few months in the same room. The customer still noticed on delivery day. Together is kinder.

## Arkansas cherry

Cherry grows here. The furniture cherry in the shop may be regional or not. Same rule as walnut: do not print local if you cannot name the lot. The species belongs. The climate will color it. Our sun is not shy.

## A runner stripe

They covered the dresser for six months, then took the runner off. A pale stripe. Not a shop defect. I had said so. They had not heard. I hear it now in the first minute: it will darken, it will not darken under a runner, keep the cloth off for a while if you want even color. I cannot make them. I can refuse to stain the stripe into a lie that will then age beside the rest.

Two doors of a cupboard, one on the sunny bench, one on the cart in the dark: cousins on delivery day. They evened in the room. The delivery day still hurt. I keep a set together.

## Gum in the field

A gum pocket in the middle of a dining top bled into the first oil coat. I should have turned that board into an apron. I pick, I shellac, I accept a small seam. A large one is a recut. Stain will not make gum into quiet cherry. Time will not either. Layout will.

## What I tell them in one minute

It will darken. It will not darken under a runner. Gum is not a crack. Do not stain it to yesterday. If you want yesterday, we can talk about color as color, not as time in a can. If you want cherry, wait, and keep the cloth off for a while, and do not panic in week three.

I show the old rail. I let them hold it. The hand believes the old rail. The eye will believe the new apron later. My job is to keep them from making me stain the apron so the later never comes.

When they call in a year, sometimes they are calling to say they get it. Those calls are short and good. Sometimes they are calling because a leaf they had in a closet is pale next to the table. That call is the tablecloth lesson again. I do not charge extra to repeat it. I do charge extra to stain a leaf into a lie that will then age beside a table that is still changing its mind.

A UV-heavy film will keep cherry paler. Say so if that is the look. Do not use it and then complain the piece is not “cherry enough” in October. Sap through a formal field is a lightning bolt I cut out, hide, or sell as itself. I do not stain sap and heart as if they had the same thirst. The old rail on the bench is still the best sample I have. I let them hold it. The hand believes the old rail. The eye will believe the new apron later, if we do not stain the later away.

Cherry burns with a dull tool: a brown shine on a saw cut, a track on a plane. Sharp, light, a scraper on the curl. It still takes a ring. It still moves. A wide flatsawn cherry top wants both faces finished and a breadboard that can slide. The species is not a force field. It is a color that arrives late, if we let it.
