The top looks done from the door and unfinished from a foot away: a faint orange peel, two nibs, a bit of dry spray at the back edge. This is a film that was sprayed honestly. The gun does not make a piano. The pad does.

Rubbing out is leveling and then polishing, or leveling and then stopping at satin. It is not “sanding the finish off.” It is taking the high film down to the low film until the light does not break. If you cut through, you have a raw island, and on a stained top that island is a different country.

## Let it cure

If you rub too soon, the film gums the paper and you make a mess that is worse than peel. The data sheet has a time. The shop has a climate. South Arkansas humidity will lie to a schedule that was written in a drier state. I push a fingernail in an unseen corner. If it prints, I wait. If I am late on a job, I still wait, or I ship a satin from the gun and I do not call it rubbed.

## Level first

Wet-sand with a flat block, a fine grit, a lubricant — water with a drop of soap, or mineral spirits, depending on the film. [VERIFY] compatibility; some waterbornes do not want your habits from solvent days. The block is the point. Fingers dish. A foam pad can follow a hollow and polish a wave.

I scribble a light wax pencil or I watch the peel. When the peel is gone and the surface is an even dull, I stop leveling. The dull is ugly. The dull is correct.

Edges last, and lightly. Edges cut through. I tape them or I use a light hand and I accept a little peel at the arris rather than a raw line.

## Then the sheen

Pumice and oil, rottenstone, or the modern analog: foam pads in a grit sequence, then a polish. Satin is often just the leveled dull with a very fine scuff and a wax, or a satin pad. Gloss is a longer walk. I do not sell gloss unless the room wants a piano and the customer can live with the piano’s honesty about dust.

A rubbing compound that is too aggressive after a soft film will haze and you will chase the haze. Change compounds. Do not lean.

## Dust nibs

I pick the big ones with a blade, carefully, before I level, or I let the level take them if they are small. A picked nib can be a hole. A hole needs a drop of finish and a wait, not more compound.

The finish room that sands oak in the same hour is donating nibs. I have donated.

## Why bother

Because a dining table is a mirror for windows whether you chose gloss or not. Because a film that is not level will telegraph every overlap of the gun, and those overlaps look like incompetence even when the film is thick enough to last. Because the hand reads orange peel as cheap, even on a $8,000 table. The hand is not wrong.

Production kitchens often skip this and use a satin conversion that hides peel. That is a system. It is valid. A show piece that was priced as a show piece should not hide behind the kitchen system.

## Wax after

A thin wax on a rubbed film can add a feel and a little protection. A thick wax is a smear that fingerprints. I apply thin, I buff, I do not make a museum fog.

Wax on an oily rag in a pile is the fire story again. Flat, or in water, or out.

## Cure, climate, a fingernail

I rubbed a conversion top that still printed a fingernail. The paper gummed, the sheen went to a smear, I recut a coat and I waited. The sheet had a time. The shop had a wet week. The sheet won. I wait now, even when the truck is booked. A satin from the gun is better than a gummed rub I have to explain.

Edges cut through first. I tape them. I have a raw line on a stained apron that became a dark thread. I touched it with dye and I can still find it. Tape is cheaper.

## The block, not the fingers

Fingers dish a field. A flat block, a lubricant the film will accept, a scribble of wax pencil to show the peel. When the peel is an even dull, I stop leveling. The extra pass is the cut-through. I have taken the extra pass.

A corner that was light from the gun will cut through first. I watch that corner. Guns are people. People get tired at the back edge.

## The repair problem

A rubbed table that gets a later scratch is harder to spot-fix invisibly than a satin-from-the-gun table. I say that before we rub. Some customers still want the depth. Some switch to satin and I am glad they did, because their house has a dog and a homework station.

Spot-rubbing a repair is how you get a shiny island in a satin sea. You rub the whole panel or you live with the scratch or you recut the coat.

## Hands and shoulders

This is physical in a dull way. I change sides of the table. I do not bear down on one corner because I am facing that way.

When the light stops breaking, I stop. The extra ten minutes is how you cut through. I have taken the extra ten minutes. I have used a drop of dye and a prayer. The prayer is not a finish system.

I turn off the overhead and I use the window. If the top holds the window as a shape, not as a broken glitter, I can call it. Then I walk away before I find a reason to take one more pass. The last pass is how good rub-outs die.
