I pick up a board by the end and I look at it the way a mill buyer looks at it, which is also the way a person looks at a cut tree if they are honest. The rings are a map of years and they are a map of how this board will move. If they run in tall arches across the end, I have a flatsawn face waiting, a cathedral, a cup toward the bark. If they stand like a fence, I have rift or quarter, a calmer width, a chance of fleck on oak.

The show face is a sales face. The end grain is the contract. I have been fooled by a pretty face. I have not been fooled by an end, except when I did not look.

## How to look

A clean end, not a ragged chainsaw smile. A little raking light. Count nothing. See the orientation. See the pith — a tiny broken star, a board that was too close to the middle, a candidate for a split. See the checks already started, a dark line that will run. See the density, the latewood, a hard ring that will telegraph in a floor.

On a glued panel I look at every end. A panel of mixed orientations is a panel that will argue with itself. Sometimes I want the argument (cups opposing). Sometimes I want a family of vertical rings and a quiet top.

## Pith, juvenile, the first years

Wood near the pith moves more and splits more and is often weaker in the way a chair rail cannot be weak. I cut it out of furniture parts that take a load. I can leave it in a rustic slab if the slab is supported and the customer heard me. I do not leave it in a tenon.

Juvenile wood is a phrase from the handbook. In the hand it is a board that twists after you have been kind to it. I have been kind. I still cut it into shorter parts.

## Bark edge, wane

Wane is the missing corner, the leftover curve of the log. A little wane on the back of a rail is yield. Wane on a show arris is a hole in the finish. I rip it off or I hide it. Live-edge work is wane as a design. Then the edge must be cleaned of rot and most bark, and the support must be real. An end-grain look at a live edge is a different story — the rings run out, the movement is a fan. I do not breadboard a fan the same way I breadboard a rectangle. I may not breadboard it at all.

## Reading species without a label

Oak: big pores, rays if quartered, a smell you know. Walnut: chocolate pores, a different smell, a sap that is obvious. Cherry: fine, a blush, gum. Maple: pale, tight, hard to stain. Pine: resin, knots, a softness a thumbnail can dent.

I still like a certainty when the lot is mixed. A wrong species in a glue-up is a finish surprise and a movement surprise. The end grain plus a knife test plus the smell is usually enough. If it is not, I do not guess a customer’s table into a mystery wood.

## Why furniture people stop looking

Because the face is pretty and the clock is on. Because the board is already paid for. Because the end is painted with a mill stamp or a sealer that hides the rings. I cut a fresh sliver if I have to. The sliver is cheaper than a top.

A sealer on the end is not a villain. Ends lose moisture faster; a sealer slows a check in the yard. In the shop I still want a read. I cut.

## Mill habit

A mill grades from the face and the cuttings it can yield. A furniture shop should grade from the end and the job. We are in the same pile of boards with different questions. The mill asks: what cuttings? I ask: what joint, what width, what room?

Warren’s hardwood years were, in part, an education in ends — flooring, furniture stock, the difference between a board that would stay and a board that would cup in a house. [VERIFY] the product mix; the habit is older than our LLC.

## Pith I left in a rail

A tiny broken star in the end, a chair rail, a split along the pith after a dry week. I cut pith out of load parts now.

## Two ends, one price conversation

Why one oak table costs more than another: I show two ends, not a brand story. Rings standing or arching. The face can be a pleasure. The end is a fact.

## Teaching it

I have a new person hold two oak ends, quarter and flat, and I have them guess the faces before they turn the boards over. They get it wrong once. Then they do not. It is the fastest lesson I have. Faster than a speech about coefficients. The coefficients are there when we need a number. The end is there when we need a decision in the aisle.

Then I have them meter the same two boards. The orientation and the number together are the whole material essay of this pack, in a minute. Everything else is a longer way to say: look at the end, then believe the meter, then cut the joint so the winter has a place to go.

I stack the keepers with the ends out, a little, so I can read them next week without a hunt. A stack that hides the ends is a stack that will surprise me. I am too old for that surprise. The face can be a pleasure. The end is a fact. I buy facts, and I let the pleasure follow if the fact allows it.

When a customer wants to know why one oak table costs more than another, I show them two ends, not a brand story. The rings stand or they arch. The heritage footnote can wait until they have seen the fence of rings and understood that someone at a mill, and then someone at a bench, chose that fence on purpose.

A mixed-orientation panel argues with itself. Sometimes I want opposing cups. Sometimes I want a family of vertical rings. I look at every end in the glue-up, not just the pretty faces. A sealer on a mill end hides the rings; I cut a sliver. The sliver is cheaper than a top that cups in the dining room.

I still like a species certainty when the lot is mixed. A wrong wood in a glue-up is a finish surprise and a movement surprise. The end, a knife, the smell: usually enough. If it is not, I do not guess a table into a mystery. The mill asks what cuttings. I ask what joint, what width, what room. Same pile. Different question. The end answers first.

Juvenile wood twists after you have been kind. I cut it into shorter parts. Wane on a show arris is a hole in the finish. A little wane on the back of a rail is yield I will take.
