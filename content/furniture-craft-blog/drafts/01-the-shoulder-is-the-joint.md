The left cheek of the tenon was a little proud. I felt it before I saw it — a tightness when the apron kissed the leg, a hair of light still showing on the right shoulder. Chalk on the high cheek, two strokes with a shoulder plane, dry-fit again. The shoulders closed. That is the whole argument, if you want it in one motion: the joint is not the tenon floating in a hole. The joint is the shoulder, bearing, and the cheeks keeping the tenon from walking.

A dowel does not have a shoulder worth talking about. It has a cylinder and a hole and a hope that glue will make them one piece. Sometimes it does. On a cabinet side, a shelf-hole row, a light frame that never sees a hip, dowels are honest enough. Furniture that gets sat in, leaned on, dragged an inch to sweep, will ask the joint a different question.

## What a mortise-and-tenon is actually doing

Take a table. Four legs, four aprons, a top that wants to stay flat and a person who will, at some point, sit on the corner. The apron is a beam. The leg is a post. The load at the corner is not a clean compression. It is rack. The rectangle wants to become a parallelogram. Every dining table in a house with children has already started that conversation.

A tenon answers rack by giving you long-grain glue surface on two cheeks and, more important, two shoulders that hit the leg and stop the rotation. The mortise walls take the shear. If you drawbore or pin, you add a mechanical lock that still works when the hide glue has gone to dust — and hide glue does, eventually, in a hot kitchen. The pin is not decoration. It is the joint admitting that glue is a guest.

Cut the tenon too thin and you have a popsicle stick in a slot. Cut it too thick and you have split the leg before the first holiday. The old rule — tenon about one-third the thickness of the rail — is a starting point, not a religion. On a 7/8-inch apron it lands near 5/16. On a heavier trestle it can go thicker if the mortise still leaves meat on both walls. [VERIFY] production thicknesses on a given line before anyone prints a spec.

Haunches matter on a table apron that meets the top of the leg. The haunch fills the open top of the mortise so the leg does not split when the tenon swells, and so you do not have a hole staring up under the tabletop. A bare-faced tenon on a thin rail can be the right call. A twin tenon on a wide rail keeps the cheeks from becoming one huge cross-grain argument. None of this is mystery. It is the wood telling you where it will split.

## What a dowel is actually doing

A dowel is a loose tenon that forgot its shoulders. You drill two or three holes, dip fluted birch, clamp, and the frame looks like a frame. The glue surface is the cylinder. The resistance to rack is the shear strength of those cylinders and whatever wall of long grain you left around the holes.

In a factory that already owns a multi-spindle boring machine, dowels are fast. They are also how a lot of “solid wood” chairs from the last sixty years came apart at the front rail — not because wood is weak, but because a 3/8-inch dowel in end grain is a short lever with a round glue line. End grain drinks glue and still does not hold like long grain. You can size the holes, you can use better glue, you can add a third dowel. You are still asking a pin to do a tenon’s work.

There is a cleaner cousin: the loose tenon, floating tenon, Festool-domino-shaped stick of yellow poplar or beech in matching mortises. That joint has cheeks. It has a little shoulder if you cut it so. It is not a dowel, and it is not a cop-out if the mortises are honest and the fit is a tap, not a pounding. Shops that pretend a biscuit is the same thing are selling a different product. A biscuit is an alignment aid that got a marketing department.

## The dry-fit is the inspection

Glue hides nothing from time. It hides plenty from a tired Friday. Dry-fit until the shoulders close on both faces, until the apron sits flush, until you can pick up the pair and the joint does not wink. If you need a dead-blow to persuade it, the tenon is fat or the mortise is tapered from a wandering chisel. Pare. Do not sand the cheeks “until it goes.” Sanding rounds the arrises and you lose the register.

Marking is not a personality trait. A cutting gauge from the face side, the same face side, every time. Mortise first or tenon first is a shop argument with two good answers. What is not optional is a single reference face and a square that you trust more than the last cup of coffee.

On production work the hollow-chisel mortiser or a slot mortiser does the holes; a tenoner or a well-set dado stack does the cheeks. The machine does not excuse a bad shoulder. A shoulder that was cut with the fence a degree off will telegraph as a gap you can feel with a fingernail. Fill that with glue and you have made a glue line into a hinge.

## When I still reach for dowels

I dowel a tabletop leaf alignment, a breadboard dry-run that will get pegs later, a jig, a battens on the back of a slab that is already held by screws in slots. I will dowel a light mirror frame if the customer is buying a frame, not an heirloom, and the shop rate says so. I will not dowel the front rail of a side chair and then talk about generations.

There is a class of knockdown work — bed rails with hooks, trestles with bolts — where the mechanical fastener is the joint and the wood is the bearing. That is a different honesty. A hidden dowel pretending to be joinery is the one that bothers me.

Southern shops have been boring dowel holes since the machines showed up. Warren’s mill century made furniture stock and millwork for people who already knew both methods. [VERIFY] mid-century plant practice before anyone writes “they always tenoned.” The point is not nostalgia. The point is that a county that cut oak for a hundred years still has to decide, on a Tuesday, whether this rail gets cheeks or cylinders.

## Glue is not a personality

PVA is what most shops reach for. It is strong, cheap, and creepy — it does not like a starved joint, and it does not like a joint you will want to take apart in twenty years. Hide glue still earns the pot: reversible, sharp cleanup, no glue-line creep in the same way. Epoxy fills the gaps you should not have cut. Polyurethane foams and stains and makes a mess that looks like competence in a photo.

None of them replace a shoulder. If the dry-fit already closes, almost any glue will do the ethical thing. If the dry-fit shows daylight, you are gluing a wish.

## The piece in the room

A table that stays in a family does not stay in a showroom climate. Summer in south Arkansas will swell the tenon. Winter heat will shrink it. The mortise walls need meat. The shoulders need to have been cut to the line, not to the hopeful side of the line. A little seasonal movement in a well-cut joint is a living thing. A gap that was there on day one is a shop that blinked.

I like to see a through-tenon on a trestle or a bench, wedged, proud a sixteenth, then leveled. You can read the joint. You can also see if it was rushed. Hidden work should be as clean as the show face — not because a magazine will cut it open, but because the next person who repairs it will curse you or thank you, and they will be right.

Dowel chairs can be repaired. You drill out, you plug, you bore again, you lose a little dignity and a little wood. A blown mortise can be patched with a slip and a new tenon. Both repairs are possible. Only one of them started with a joint that understood rack.

The chalk is still on the bench from that apron. The shoulders closed. The clamps will go on later, even, and not too hungry. The table does not know the names of the joints. It will know, in ten years, whether they were there.
