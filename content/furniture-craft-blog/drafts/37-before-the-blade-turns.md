I stand at the saw and I look at the floor before I look at the fence. A cutoff on the floor is a roll waiting for a foot. A push stick on a hook twenty feet away is a hand that will get invited. The riving knife is on, or I do not start. This is not bravery. This is the thirty seconds that keep the rest of the day ordinary.

I am not a safety officer and this is not a manual. Machines have manuals. Dust has rules. Finish rags have a chemistry that does not care about your experience. What I can write is the culture that makes those rules feel like the work instead of a laminated sheet that no one reads.

## The pause is the device

The most useful guard is the second thought. Why is this cut the one I am doing with a small offcut and a fence and my hand in the neighborhood? Is there a sled? Is there a stop? Is this a job for a bandsaw? Can someone stand at the outfeed so the board does not fall and pull back?

I have skipped the pause. I still have all my fingers. That sentence is luck, not a method. I do not teach luck.

A shop that mocks the pause — “we’ve always done it” — is a shop that will eventually pay. I have been in those shops. I left some of them. I try not to run one.

## Push sticks, hold-downs, the cheap ones that exist

A push stick that is on the saw, two of them, a hook that is at the saw, a featherboard that is not a rumor in a drawer. I make push sticks from scrap so I am not precious about them. When one gets cut, I make another. A push stick you are afraid to cut is a hand you will use instead.

A riving knife or a splitter that is actually there. A blade guard that is not in a box because “I can’t see.” If you cannot see, change the light, not the guard, or change the cut.

Miter gauge and fence together is a kickback recipe. I do not. A crosscut sled is the adult.

## Dust is not atmosphere

Hardwood dust is a lung problem and a slip problem and a finish problem. I wear a respirator on the wide-belt and the saw when I am making a cloud, and I still get lazy on a “quick” sand. I am wrong when I get lazy. Oak and beech and a long life of “I only did it a little” is a medical file.

Collection that actually collects: a hose on the tool, a filter that is not packed, doors on the collector. A shop that smells like a mill all afternoon is a shop that is sharing the mill’s worst habit.

## Finish, rags, the can

Oil rags heat. They do not need a villain. They need a pile and a little air. I put them in a metal can with water, or I spread them flat outside until they are crunchy and dead, or I follow the finish maker’s sheet. I do not leave a wad on the bench overnight because I will “get it in the morning.”

Solvents: lids, air, no pilot light conversation in the same room if the sheet says so. I am not going to write a booth design here. I will say: a fan in a window is not always a booth, and a headache is a message.

## Hearing, eyes, the boring ones

Glasses that are on your face. Hearing protection that is not in the truck. I have the ring in the ears to prove I was young and sure. I put the muffs on now. I look like I care about my own head. I do.

Gloves at a spinning blade are a different conversation — often a no. Gloves at a finish table are a yes. Know which table you are at.

## Saying stop

The culture part: a new person can walk up and say the fence looks wrong, the blade is high, the other person is tired. No joke that makes them smaller. No “relax.” If they are wrong, you thank them and you show them. If they are right, you fix it and you do not punish the pause.

I have been the tired person. I have been told to stop. The feeling is ugly and then it is gratitude, later, if you let it be.

A shop that only lets the owner say stop is a shop with one pair of eyes. Boards do not care who owns the building.

## Machines we love that do not love us

The tablesaw is famous. The router table will take a bite out of a habit. The shaper is a mill killer if the knife is loose. The jointer loves a hand that goes too far over the cutterhead. The bandsaw is “safer” until a round log rolls and a finger meets a blade that does not care about reputation.

I unplug to change a blade. I wait for the saw to stop. I do not brake a blade with a scrap. I have seen that trick. It is a trick.

Climb-cut bravado on a handheld router: a small bite or not at all. I wrote that in the tearout essay. I write it here because the walk-across-the-shop is a hospital story.

## Housekeeping is safety

A clear path to the exit and to the extinguisher. A stack that will not fall on a person. A cutoff bin that is not the aisle. Lighting that is not a cave at the saw and a glare at the finish table.

I sweep because I am about to walk there, not because Friday exists.

Oil rags in a wad on the bench overnight: I do not. Metal can, water, or flat outside until they are dead. I have been the tired person who was told to stop. The feeling is ugly and then it is gratitude, if you let it be.

## This county’s shops

Mill towns have scars. They also have men and women who went home every night because someone was hard about a guard. I would rather be boring on this subject than interesting. Interesting is a story people tell about a hand.

Before the blade turns, I look at the floor, the stick, the knife, the person at the outfeed if there is one, my own head. If any of those is a no, the switch stays down. The board can wait. The board is not a reason.

This pack will not keep anyone safe. A habit might. The habit is the pause, the stick, the can, and the permission to stop. I can write those four. The rest is the manual, the law, and the person you work next to, who should be allowed to be right about a fence.

A push stick on a hook twenty feet away is a hand that will get invited. I keep two at the saw, made from scrap, not precious. When one gets cut, I make another. A stick you are afraid to cut is a hand you will use instead.
