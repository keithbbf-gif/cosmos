The dark board is never as dark as the customer’s memory of a piano, and it is darker than the board next to it that came from the same lift. Walnut is a range: chocolate, purple, gray-brown, a streak of mineral, a sapwood that is the color of cream and bone. I pull the boards and I lay them on the floor in the light they will be sold in. I walk away and I come back. The one that looked heroic on the rack looks loud in a set. The quiet one is the top.

I do not stain walnut to make it “walnut.” I cut sapwood off or I keep it on purpose. A thin, even sap edge can be a drawing. A smear of sap in the middle of a table is a smear. Staining the sap to match the heart is a brown fog that wears off at the arris and returns as a stripe. I have done the fog. I do not advertise it.

## Color that changes its mind

Fresh walnut can be bright, almost loud. Oil and air and a year will quiet it. A UV finish will keep it paler longer; some people want that. Some people want the old brown. I say this before the sale because a customer who bought a purple-brown slab will call in November about a browner slab and think we swapped the table.

Steaming walnut in the mill — a common practice to even color and to marry sap and heart — is a thing you should know about when you buy. Steamed walnut is a little more uniform and a little more “walnut” in the catalog sense. Unsteamed can be more wild. Neither is fake. Both should be named if the customer is the kind who names things.

## Working it

Walnut is kind to planes and kind to edges and then a curly patch will tear and a gum pocket will smear a finish. It dulls tools less than maple and more than pine. It dents. A dining top in walnut will take a ring and a backpack buckle. I say that. A film helps. An oil will repair. The species is not a force field.

Glue: walnut can be oily enough that a wipe with a solvent before glue helps on some lots. I test. I do not have a superstition I apply to every board.

Dust is dark and it gets into every pore of a maple door across the shop. I sequence the jobs. I have stained a painted cupboard with walnut dust and called it a mystery until I was honest.

## Slabs

A walnut slab is a religion. I like a slab that is dried, flattened, and has a breadboard or a battens plan if it needs one. I do not like a slab that is a live-edge shrine with the pith in the middle and a river of epoxy because the check would not stop. Epoxy rivers are a product. They can be handsome. They are not “more honest.” They are a different honesty: we kept the crack and we filled it with a plastic.

Nakashima’s eye is cited too often by people who did not take his care with the board. The care is the selection and the support, not the leftover bark. Bark is a pest hotel and a finish problem. I remove most of it. I leave a live edge when the edge is a good line. I do not leave rot.

## Arkansas, without forcing it

Walnut grows in this state. A shop here can buy regional boards and also buy from the same national kilns everyone else buys from. I will not print “local walnut” on a piece whose lift came from a lot I cannot name. If we know the lot, we can say so. If we do not, the species is enough.

The mill memory of this county is more oak and pine than walnut in the old product lists. [VERIFY] before a brochure sentence. Walnut in the shop is still at home. It does not need a 1901 escort.

## Matching a set

Chairs and a table: the chairs can be a little darker, a little less figured, so the table can speak. A set where every piece is a hero is a showroom, not a room. I hold a chair leg next to the top and I squint. If the leg is a different wood pretending, I stop. If the leg is the same wood from a quieter board, I go.

Drawer sides in walnut on a walnut case are a luxury. Maple sides are a tradition and a wear surface. I like maple sides. I will make walnut sides if the open drawer is part of the elevation.

## Finish

Oil on walnut is almost too easy — it looks finished before it is protected. I still build a finish for the use. A film on walnut can look like a candy apple if it is too thick and too amber. I keep it thinner than the oak jobs. The wood is already a color.

A waterborne that goes gray-cool on walnut will disappoint a person who wanted warmth. A wash of shellac under it can help. A sample in their room is cheaper than a recut.

## Sapwood I stained once

A formal top with a sap lightning bolt: I stained it into a brown fog. The arris wore, the stripe came back. I recut the top later, on my dime, and I kept the sap off the field. Now I cut it off, or I design with it, or I say no to formal. A rustic edge of sap can be a drawing. A smear in the middle is a smear.

Steamed lots are more even. Unsteamed can be louder. I name which if the customer names things. Fresh walnut is brighter than their piano memory. A year will quiet it. I say that before the truck.

## Dust on a maple door

Walnut dust is a dark snow. It found a tacky maple door across the shop and wrote a constellation. I sequence jobs now. I finish maple when the walnut is not in the air. The constellation sanded off and still wasted an hour.

## The board I keep walking past

There is always one in the stack that is darker, wilder, a crotch, a swirl. It wants to be a small top, a box lid, a panel in a frame. It does not want to be one of six boards in a glue-up next to a quiet rift. I keep it. I wait. The waiting is part of buying walnut. If I force it into the dining top, the dining top becomes a poster of that board and the rest is an apology.

When I finally use it, I let it be the piece. That is the only time the dark board is the boss. The rest of the time, the room is the boss, and the room usually wants a quieter walnut than the stack’s loudest face. I have learned to like the quiet ones. They still darken. They still look like walnut in ten years, when the loud one looks like a decision you made when you were tired.
