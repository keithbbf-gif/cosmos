The elevation is taped to the shop door at eye height so I hit it with my shoulder and look at it. A table, a height, a note that the apron cannot be 4 inches because a knee lives there, a circle around a drawer that the client added after they said they were done adding. The paper is dirty. The dirt is the job.

A commission without a drawing is a disagreement scheduled for delivery day. A drawing that was never taken to the room is a pretty disagreement. I measure the room. I look at the base, the outlet, the window that will rake the top, the path the piece will have to walk to get in. I have built a bookcase that would not turn the stair. The drawing was correct. The stair was also correct. The two had not met.

## What the drawing must decide

Height, width, depth — the obvious. Then the joints that cannot be faked later: a breadboard or not, a solid panel or a frame, a slide or a wooden runner, a finish that can take a wet glass. Then the things people forget: overhang at a dining apron for a chair, the chair itself if we are not making it, the thickness of a stone top if someone else is supplying it, the reveal under a cabinet if the floor dips.

I draw the tenon. Not always as a shop drawing for the customer, but as a note for me. If the CAD pretty-picture thins the rail to a candy bar, I thicken it on the door and I send that. The pretty picture is not the load path.

A story stick for a wall of drawers. A section for a molding that must meet a house. A finish sample with a date.

## The room is a liar on the phone

“We have 36 inches.” They have 35 and a half and a hose bib. I go. If I cannot go, I do not promise a built-in. A table can be built from a tape a careful person sends, plus photos with a tape in the photo, plus a warning. A built-in cannot.

Outlets, vents, a thermostat, a window trim that is not square, a ceiling that drops. I photograph. I write. I do not trust memory after three other jobs.

## Changes

There will be changes. I date them on the drawing. I initial. If the change is a different species, a different finish, a different height, the money and the time change. I say that without a speech. A shop that eats changes is a shop that resents the piece, and the piece shows it.

A change after parts are cut is a new drawing. I have tried to keep it in my head. The head lost.

## What I show the client

Elevations they can read, not a nest of hidden lines. A sample of the wood, not a phone photo of a different lot. A finish sample in their light. A sentence about movement if there is a breadboard or a wide top. A sentence about lead time that includes finish cure, not just machining.

I do not show a photoreal render that implies a flake we do not have. I have been in that lie. The delivery is colder than the render.

## Shop drawings vs sales drawings

The door gets the shop version: grain direction, mortise depth, a note that the left door is narrower because the fridge is proud. The client gets a cleaner sheet. Both must agree on the outside dimensions and the finish. I have had them disagree. The assembler used the pretty one. The pretty one was short.

CNC files are drawings. They need the same discipline. A file that is “almost” the last revision is a part that is almost the job.

## Commission as a conversation, not a performance

I am not trying to win a design award on their dime. I am trying to put a piece in a room that will be used. If they want a sculpture, we can talk about that price. If they want a table, the chairs have to go under it.

Named series — a Saline Creek something, a Lumberjack something — can be a starting point, a known joinery and a known proportion. A commission is often a series that grew a drawer or lost a stretch. I keep the joint and I change the box. I do not keep the name and change the joint into a dowel because the drawing got thin.

## The stair that was also correct

The bookcase matched the drawing and would not turn the landing. The drawing was right. The stair was right. They had not met. I measure the path now, not just the room. A table can travel in pieces. A built-in that cannot turn is a built-in I recut in the hall, once, and will not again.

A CAD rail thinned to a candy bar: I thicken it on the door sheet and I send that. The pretty picture is not the load path. The shop drawing and the sales drawing must agree on the outside dimensions. An assembler used the pretty one. The pretty one was short.

## Changes after parts

A drawer they added after they were done adding: I date it, I initial, the money moves. A change in my head is a change I lose. CNC files are drawings. Almost the last revision is a part that is almost the job.

I ask before I photograph a house. A commission is not content. The finish sample sits in their light overnight. The species sample is from this lot, not a phone photo of a prettier lift.

## Delivery is still the drawing

How it comes apart, how it goes up the stair, whether the top is separate, whether the finish needs a week before a tablecloth. I write that on the same door, at the bottom, in a smaller hand. The small hand is how pieces get their corners broken in a stairwell.

I have stood in a dining room with a top and a base and a client and a drawing, and we have all agreed the drawing was right and the room felt different. Then we sneak an overhang, or we recut a leaf, or we live with it because the room was the inspector and the inspector had a point. I would rather sneak before the finish than after. That is why the first visit is not optional.

The elevation comes off the door when the job ships. I fold it into the folder with the offcuts of veneer and the finish formula and the meter numbers. The next time they want a companion piece, the door will get a new sheet, but the old one will still be allowed to argue. The old one knows the stair.

I draw the tenon on my sheet even if the client never sees it. If the series starting point — a known apron, a known joint — grows a drawer, I keep the joint and I change the box. I do not keep the name and thin the rail into a dowel. Delivery notes go on the same door, small hand: how it comes apart, whether the top is separate, whether the finish needs a week before a cloth. The small hand is how corners survive a landing.
