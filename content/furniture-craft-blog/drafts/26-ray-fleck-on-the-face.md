You tilt the board and the rays catch, a flash that is not figure in the maple sense and not a stripe of stain. It is the medullary ray, sawn on the quarter, laid out on the face like a quiet heraldry. People call it tiger, flake, fleck. I call it a reason to pay for the extra work at the mill, and a reason a wide top will stay closer to the width I cut it.

Quartersawn is not a species. It is a geometry. The growth rings stand more or less vertical on the end grain. The board shrinks less in width than a flatsawn board of the same oak. It cups less. It wears in a floor as a tighter story. It also wastes more of the log if you are trying to get every board cathedral-pretty for a pallet. Mills that did this for a living — and Warren was a hardwood town — knew the math. ## How the log is opened

True quarter: the log is quartered, then each quarter is sawn so the blade is radial. You get boards with rings at 60–90 degrees to the face, rays showing on white oak especially. Rift: rings more like 30–60, a straight grain, less flake, a look a lot of modern furniture wants because it is calm.

Flatsawn (plain sawn): the cathedral, the U-shaped grain, the board that moves more across its width and cups toward the bark face as it dries. Most oak in a big-box rack is this. It is not lesser wood. It is a different assignment. I use it where the width is modest, where a panel can float, where the cathedral is the point.

A “quartersawn” stamp on a board that shows one ray and a lot of cathedral is a hopeful stamp. I look at the end grain. The end grain does not hope.

## White oak, red oak, the fleck

White oak shows the flake more proudly. Red oak can flake and also shows the open pores that drink stain. I do not treat them as interchangeable on a tabletop that will get wet — white oak’s tyloses make it the wetter-wood of the two, the boat wood, the exterior-adjacent wood. Red oak is a lot of the furniture oak in this country because it is there and it machines. Both are oak. Both move. Quartering helps both.

## Why I specify it

A wide dining top. A door stile that cannot be a propeller. A table apron that should stay the height I cut. Flooring that will stay tighter at the seams. A chair seat that is a slab and not a saddle of green wood.

I do not specify it for a painted poplar cupboard. I do not specify it to impress a tag. The tag can say oak. The drawing should say QSWO or rift if the drawing needs it.

Mission and Greene-and-Greene and a hundred factory lines made quartered oak a period look. The look is the flake. The reason I still buy it is the movement. If you only want the flake, you can veneer it. Veneer is a different essay and a valid one. A solid quartered top is a heavier promise.

## Working it

Quartered oak planes well if the iron is sharp and you are not a hero at a ray. The rays can pick up, a little chip, a sparkle that becomes a void. A scraper helps. A light cut helps. A dull wide-belt will burnish the rays and then tear them.

Glue-ups: alternate the ring direction if you have flatsawn in the mix. If the whole top is quartered, you still pay attention to color and to a board that is actually rift sneaking in and reading as a stripe.

Fasteners: you still slot the breadboard. Quartered is not “no movement.” It is less. Less is not none. I have seen a quartered top split because someone glued a breadboard like a clamp.

## Arkansas oak, without a brochure

This county grew and cut oak. The Saline bottoms and the upland stands are not one oak. The furniture stock that left Warren in the century of mill work included hardwoods that other towns wanted for floors and chairs and millwork. ## A glue-up that mixed cuts

I put a rift stile next to a flatsawn rail in a door because the faces both said “oak” and the ends were in the stack. The door’s rail moved more than the stile. The haunch opened a hair in winter. Now I read the ends before I read the faces. A family of vertical rings for a frame. A cathedral for a floating panel. Not a cathedral in a 3-inch rail if I can help it.

Rays that pick: a light plane cut, a scraper. A dull wide-belt will burnish the flake and then tear it, a sparkle that becomes a void. I would rather scrape than polish a void.

## The look in a room

Fleck changes with the walk-by. That is a gift in a quiet room and a fight in a room full of competing grain. I do not put a wild-flake top under a busy chandelier and a busy rug and then wonder why the room is loud. I put it where a window can rake it at the hour someone sits down.

Rift is the diplomat. Straight, calm, a little silk. I use rift for stiles and for a modern case that wants oak without a 1910 speech.

If a customer points at a cathedral board and says “I want that quartersawn look,” we have a vocabulary problem. I take them to the end grain. I show them a flecked face. I let them pick the one they actually want. The word was never the board. The end grain is the board.

I keep a cutoff with a perfect flake on the windowsill of the shop. People pick it up and tilt it. That tilt is the sale, when the sale is this wood. When the sale is a painted cupboard, the cutoff stays on the sill and does not need to perform. It is still the best teacher I have for what a sawyer did on purpose.
