The first stroke on a dovetail is a nick on the far corner of the line, then the near, then the saw sits in a kerf you made on purpose. After that, if you steer, you make a banana. I have made bananas. They look like confidence from three feet and like a bent pin from the inside of the socket.

A saw is a row of tiny chisels. Rip teeth are filed to chisel along the grain. Crosscut teeth are filed to knife across it. A hybrid “toolbox” tooth does both badly enough that people buy a tablesaw and forget the handsaw, which is fine until the tablesaw is the wrong tool — a tail, a tenon shoulder, a cut in a house that is already finished.

## Set, plate, the kerf that binds

Set is the left-right bend of the teeth so the plate can follow. Too little set and the saw binds and heats and wanders. Too much and the kerf is a ditch and the line is a suggestion. New saws from a decent maker come with a set you can live with. Old saws from a flea market come with a mystery. I joint, file, and set if the plate is worth it. I do not romanticize a rusted plate that is kinked. A kinked saw is a kinked cut.

Wax on the plate. The cheap trick that still works. A bound saw is often a dry plate in resinous pine, not a moral failing.

## Which saw for which cut

Dovetail saw: thin plate, fine rip, for pins and tails. I do not crosscut tenon shoulders with it if I have a carcase saw, but I have.

Carcase saw: bigger, still back-sawed, shoulders and small panels.

Tenon saw: deeper plate, longer teeth, the cheeks of a tenon if you are not on a bandsaw.

Rip handsaw: long, no back, down a board. People think this is theater. It is also a board that did not fit the tablesaw, or a jobsite, or a power outage, or a preference.

Crosscut handsaw: the one that makes a cleaner end than a circular saw if you can keep it square. I often cannot, at length, without a guide. I use a guide. Pride is not a square.

Coping saw: waste between pins, curves, the cope on a molding (different essay, same saw). The blade is cheap. Change it. A dull coping blade is a fight in a small mouth.

## The tablesaw is not the enemy

I rip on the tablesaw. I crosscut on a sled. I still need a handsaw for the last bit of a tenon, for a cut that would put my hands near a blade in a stupid way, for a dovetail. A shop that is only machines is a shop that designs around machines. That can be a good shop. It will not cut a single half-blind pin in a drawer front without a jig, and the jig will not teach the next person what a line is.

The danger talk belongs in the safety essay. Here I will say only: a handsaw that slips because you were pushing sideways is a cut on a knuckle. Let the teeth cut. Your job is alignment and patience. The stroke is long. Short, chopping strokes are how a saw chatters and leaves a ragged kerf.

## Filing is not a lost art if you do it

I am not a saw doctor. I can joint a toothline, I can file rip teeth reasonably, I can set with a pistol. I send a good saw to a person who files for a living when the toothline is a disaster. That is not failure. That is knowing the difference between maintenance and a rebuild.

A cheap hard-point saw from a big box is sharp until it is not, and then it is a scraper. I use them on dirty work, nail-embedded lumber, the job you would not feed a plate you filed. Then I throw them away without a ceremony.

## Starting the cut

Thumb as a fence, or a knife wall, or a shallow chisel trench on a shoulder. The saw wants to skate. Give it a track. On a long rip, I watch the line ahead of the saw, not the teeth. The teeth are already in the past.

On a tenon cheek, I stay off the shoulder line and I finish with a chisel or a shoulder plane. The saw that kisses the shoulder from the wrong direction is a shoulder with a bite out of it.

## Southern pine, oak, the gum

Southern yellow pine, if you are in millwork, will gum a saw and show you every wander. Oak will burnish a dull tooth and then split if you force a bound rip. Walnut is kind. Maple is hard and will heat a thin plate. I slow down in maple. I do not lean.

Resawing on a bandsaw is a different tooth and a different essay in the mind: drift, fence, a blade that is not a handsaw. I still mark the same line. The line does not care which family of teeth you invited.

## A banana in a pin board

I steered a dovetail saw because the line looked like it was getting away. The kerf became a banana. The pin was fat at the top and thin at the baseline. I pared it into a pin that almost worked and I still see the almost when the drawer is open. Now I nick far, nick near, and I let the saw cut. If the line is getting away, I stop and I start a new kerf, or I accept a slightly fat pin I will pare. I do not steer.

Wax on the plate in resinous pine is the difference between a zip and a bind. I have forced a bound rip and watched the plate heat and wander. The wander is a board I recut. The wax is a stub of candle in the apron.

## Filing vs throwing away

A hard-point big-box saw that hit a nail is scrap. A plate I filed, that hit a nail, goes to a person who can put a tooth back if the plate is worth it. I am not that person every week. I am the person who stops at a crunch and looks for metal in the kerf. I have launched a tooth. I wear glasses.

On a jobsite I still carry a cheap saw for dirty work and a decent carcass saw for a shoulder I cannot take to a sled. The decent saw does not cut painted nailers.

## What I want to hear

A good saw in good wood is a zip, not a crunch. A crunch is a dull tooth, a nail, or a cut across a check. I stop at a crunch. I have hit a nail in reclaimed oak and I have seen a tooth leave the plate. The tooth can be replaced by a person who knows. My eye cannot.

I hang the saws I use. I do not pile them. A bent plate is a morning. A hung saw is a habit. In a shop that also has a sliding tablesaw, the hung saws are a statement only if they are dull. If they are sharp, they are just tools, which is the only statement I want.
