**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 1796 the York Retreat opened on the edge of a Yorkshire city that already knew how to sit in silence. The founders were Friends. They built a house, not a dungeon, and they asked the people who lived there to take meals, walks, and worship as a household. Later textbooks would call this "moral treatment" and then argue about whether it was kindness, labor discipline, or both. What it was, on a Tuesday, was a circle that did not yet have a CPT code.

This essay is not a hunt for the first group therapist. It is a walk through rooms that already knew how to put chairs in a ring — churches, revivals, lodges, asylums, mesmeric parlors — before Joseph Pratt called a tuberculosis meeting a class and before Jacob Moreno fought for a phrase.

## Silence, speech, and a weekly hour

A Quaker meeting is not psychotherapy. Saying so protects both objects. The meeting gathers people who share a discipline of waiting. Speech, when it comes, is unscripted and public. No one is the identified patient. The historian who collapses that hour into "group" is doing advertising, not archives.

The same warning applies to the Methodist class meeting of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, to the camp meeting that shook the American South and Midwest, and to the lodge night that bound men (and, in other orders, women) into testimony and dues. Those rooms trained a population to expect that trouble could be spoken in company. They also trained a population to expect a leader, a hymn, a collection plate, and a moral. When Pratt's patients later kept diaries and encouraged one another to sleep outdoors, they were not inventing sociability. They were borrowing a Protestant habit and hanging a thermometer on it.

Moral treatment's European addresses — Pinel and the Pussins at Bicêtre and the Salpêtrière, Tuke at York, later American asylums that copied the plan and then filled it past the plan — belong in the sister pack as asylum history. They belong here as a fact about furniture. People ate together. They worked in sight of one another. Superintendents wrote as if the *community* were the instrument. That sentence can be a reform. It can also be a way to make unpaid labor sound like a cure. The circle does not settle the ethics. The circle is the ethics' stage.

## Mesmer's tub and the parlor

Franz Anton Mesmer's Paris rooms in the 1780s gathered patients around a *baquet*, a tub of iron filings and "animal magnetism," hands linked, crises staged. A royal commission (Franklin among the names) doubted the fluid and noticed the theater. The parlor survived the fluid. Nineteenth-century hypnotists and "electro-biologists" kept putting bodies in a room and asking the room to watch. Charcot's Tuesday lessons at the Salpêtrière were a medical amphitheater, not a therapy group, but they taught a public that suffering could be demonstrated *in company*. The talking cure later fled that amphitheater for a private couch. Group therapy, when it arrived under medical letterhead, had to decide whether it was fleeing the parlor or returning to it.

Do not tidy this into "Mesmer invented group therapy." He invented a ticketed scene. The scene's descendants include circus, clinic, and séance. A contemporary clinician who fears being a performer is fearing a real ancestor.

## The mutual-aid reflex

Industrial cities produced sick clubs, burial societies, immigrant *landsmanshaftn*, and union halls. A member paid in; a member drew out; a member sat with another member's widow. The help was cash, food, and presence. It was not insight. Historians of social work later named "social group work" as a profession (see the Coyle and Konopka essays). Before the profession, there was the reflex: people who shared a risk sat in the same room so the risk would not be faced alone.

That reflex is why Alcoholics Anonymous, when it formed in 1935, did not feel like a spaceship to the people who walked in. It felt like a meeting. The Big Book (1939) is a dated object with its own theology. This series will not reprint the Steps as a clinic handout. It will say: the meeting is older than the clinic's interest in the meeting. When psychiatrists later sat in the back of church basements taking notes, they were visitors in a room that did not ask their permission to exist.

Women's sewing circles, Black church auxiliaries, and burial societies in the Jim Crow South did similar work without being invited into psychiatric journals. A history that starts in Boston in 1905 and never looks at those rooms is a history of who got to publish, not a history of who sat together.

## What a clinic later borrowed

When Pratt wanted poor consumptives to keep doing a tedious home regimen, he used a weekly class. When Worcester wanted nervous parishioners treated without scandal, he used a church. When Marsh wanted a ward to feel like a school, he used a revival's heat. When Slavson wanted children to do something other than sit still, he used play. None of those moves required a theory of "therapeutic factors." They required a population already trained to show up.

The borrowing has a cost. A church can become a clinic without saying so. A clinic can become a church without saying so. Consent forms exist because those two conversions kept happening in the dark. This pack will keep naming the conversion.

A small-city practice in 2026 that never runs a psychotherapy group still inherits the furniture. Waiting rooms are accidental groups. Parent education nights are accidental classes. Support groups at the library are cousins. The job of this series is to keep the cousins from being billed as identical twins.

## What not to do with a prehistory

Do not tell a reader that sitting with friends is "just as good as" a licensed hour. Do not tell a reader that a licensed hour is a betrayal of "real community." Do not invent an Indigenous or African "always-already group therapy" as a decorative opening. Those cultures have their own gathering practices, which deserve their own historians and their own names. A paragraph that treats them as raw material for a Boston origin story is extraction.

Do not use York Retreat as a brand mood board. The Retreat was a specific house with specific records. Digby's monograph is a door into those records. A stock engraving of "kindly attendants" is not.

The consulting room of the sister pack — Freud's hour, Rogers's hour, Beck's hour — needed a door that closed. The circle needed a door that opened onto other faces. Both doors are inventions. Both can injure. The rest of this calendar is the list of people who tried to write rules for the open door, and the list of people who got rich or famous or wrecked when the rules failed.
