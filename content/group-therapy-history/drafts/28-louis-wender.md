**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Louis Wender was born about 1890 in Lithuania and died in New York on 8 February 1966 (*New York Times*, 9 February). He trained in the orbit of William Alanson White and St. Elizabeths, directed Hillside Hospital at Hastings-on-Hudson from 1927 to 1943, later worked at Beth Israel, and from 1954 to 1963 directed Pinewood in Katonah. The obituary called him a pioneer of group therapy and also mentioned insulin and metrazol — a reminder that a 1930s hospital doctor held more than one instrument, some of which later ethics would not bless.

This essay will not reconstruct shock protocols. It will not rank him against Burrow. It will treat the 1936 paper as an American hospital's claim that the group had *dynamics*.

## Hillside as a room

AGPA's house history remembers Wender holding meetings with a sanitarium population: talk, then patients talking. The pattern is not Pratt's diary and not Marsh's hymn. It is closer to a discussion hour inside a hospital that still had a medical director's authority. Authority does not leave the room because the chairs are in a circle.

He tried, the secondary literature says, to distance himself from Burrow and Pratt. Distancing is how a second wave makes a first wave into "not yet psychotherapy." The distancing is itself historical. This pack keeps all three on the calendar so the wave metaphor cannot erase the class or the commune.

Paul Wender, his son, became a well-known psychiatrist in another area (attention and stimulant debates). Do not confuse the files. Do not use the family as color.

## Somatic treatments in the same career

Insulin coma and metrazol are part of the 1930s–40s hospital. A group pioneer who also used those methods is not a contradiction. He is a hospital man. Later readers who want a gentle ancestor will skip the obituary's second clause. Do not skip it. Do not sensationalize it. Name it as the century's toolkit.

Laura Bender, Paul Schilder, Alexander Wolf — AGPA's "second wave" list — share a city and a decade. Wender is the hospital-director version.

## What not to do with Wender

Do not cite 1936 "dynamics" as a license to interpret your staff. Do not use Hillside's later institutional history (it has had more than one life) as if it were his 1930s. Do not scrape portraits.

Type: "Louis Wender, c. 1890–1966."

A small-city psychiatrist who runs a unit group and also writes orders for medication is living Wender's structural problem: the same person holds the circle and the key. Ethics codes got longer because that double hold kept going wrong. He is an ancestor of the problem, not a villain of it. Villain stories are too easy for 1966.

## White, Hillside, and the two instruments

William Alanson White's St. Elizabeths was a federal hospital with a teaching culture. Wender as a student of that culture is a Washington-to-New York career. Hillside at Hastings-on-Hudson (1927–43) is a long directorship in the Depression and the war. Long directorships decide which groups happen.

Insulin and metrazol in the same obituary as group therapy is the 1930s toolkit in one sentence. Later ethics will not let a heritage page admire the second clause. Naming it is the duty. Describing how to do it is forbidden here for more than one reason.

Pinewood in Katonah (1954–63) and Beth Israel are the late addresses. New York's psychiatric geography is a map of buildings that change names. Do not caption a current building as his 1936 room.

The *Times* said 76. About 1890 is honest enough until a birth record appears.

A medical director who also runs the circle holds the key and the talk. Later codes tried to split holds that cannot be fully split on a small unit. He is an ancestor of the unsplit hold. Naming him is not accusing him of a 2026 boundary violation. It is dating the furniture: Hastings-on-Hudson, a sanitarium population, a 1936 journal willing to say "dynamics."

## How to hold a toolkit

An obituary that names group therapy and shock treatments in one column is a 1966 honesty. Later heritage pages that keep only the first clause are doing makeup. This pack does not describe the second clause. It will not disappear it.

Hillside's name has had more than one life. Do not paste a current photograph as his 1936 room. 59 East 79th Street, in the biographical notes, is an office, not a shrine. Offices close.

A *New York Times* obituary from 9 February 1966 is the public death notice. Heart attack, Montefiore, age 76. Pioneer, the headline said, and then the other instruments. Keep the headline's second clause in the file even when the slide drops it.

Hastings-on-Hudson as a sanitarium address is a river-town fact. River towns hosted private hospitals that were not state asylums and not New York offices. He ran one for sixteen years. Sixteen years is longer than Northfield and longer than a Bethel summer. Long running is how dynamics get noticed.

This file is not the interwar essay. That essay holds a wave. This file holds Hillside 1927–43, a 1936 dynamics paper, a 1966 obituary with two instruments. Sixteen years is the figure. The toolkit's second clause stays named.

A later editor should open the 9 February 1966 *Times* and the 1936 *JNMD* paper in one sitting. The sitting joins a hospital director's dynamics to an obituary's two instruments. Then do not caption a current Hudson-river building as 1936. Names of hospitals move. His sixteen years at Hillside do not.
