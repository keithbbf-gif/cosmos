**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Alexander Wolf died on 25 September 1997; a paid death notice in the *New York Times* of 27 September named him and the Postgraduate Center for Mental Health's group department. Birth year 1907 remains a working date until a fuller obituary or license record is in hand — `[VERIFY]` before a carved caption. AGPA's house history says that in 1938 he began a group of ten of his own analysands, kept psychoanalytic principles, and added "alternate sessions": the patients met without him. By 1942 he had several groups, including a couples group. He called the work psychoanalysis *of* groups, a phrase that nods at Burrow and then walks another way.

The 1962 book with Emanuel Schwartz, *Psychoanalysis in Groups*, is the public object.

## The empty chair that is not empty

An alternate session is a radical furniture decision. The conductor leaves. The group continues. Later writers would see danger (scapegoating, affairs, a group that becomes a second family without a witness). Wolf saw a way to let transference move among members, not only toward the analyst. Both the danger and the hope are in the later literature. This pack will not teach you how to schedule an alternate. It will say the 1938 decision is why some American analytic groups felt different from a Foulkesian circle with a conductor who stays.

"Going around" — each person speaking in turn — is another of his named habits in the house history. A go-round can be democratic. It can be a roll call that traps the silent. He is not the only person who used a circle's geometry. He is an early namer in the American analytic file.

## Against the class, toward the couch's cousins

He knew Wender, Schilder, Burrow. He wanted more Freud in the room than Pratt could offer and less commune than Lifwynn. The 1938 analysands were already in individual treatment. The group was an addition for people who could pay for analysis in New York. Class filter again. History should say it.

Couples in a group in 1942 is easy to cheer as innovation. It is also a privacy problem. Consent in a marital circle is a thicket this page will not untangle.

## What not to do with Wolf

Do not start alternate sessions because a history post mentioned them. Do not "go around" as a party trick. Do not scrape portraits.

A small-city clinician who has never heard his name still meets the problem he named: what happens among members when the leader is not the only object. Yalom would later call some of that interpersonal learning. Wolf called it psychoanalysis. The nouns differ. The furniture is related.

## Ten analysands and a couples circle

Ten of his own analysands in 1938 is a small, paying, already-selected room. It is not a CMHC. The alternate session — they meet without him — is the decision that still startles trainees. Startle is not a reason to copy it. Copying it without a theory of scapegoating is how a group becomes a party with transference.

Emanuel Schwartz as co-author in 1962 is a reminder that even "Wolf's method" had a second name. This pack has been putting second names back (Jennings, Lewis, Fuhriman, Stone, Shay).

The 1938–42 AGPA work dates stand whether or not 1907 survives a license check. Use the work dates if the birth year slips.

Going around the circle can be a kindness to the quiet and a prison for the same person. He named the habit. He cannot name your Tuesday.

A couples group in 1942 is easy to cheer until you ask who could leave. Leaving a marital circle that also contains your analyst's other patients is a thicket. He opened the thicket. Later ethics built fences. This page will not teach you to open it again. It will date the opening: New York, paying analysands, a war on.

## Postgraduate Center and a 1962 binding

The *Times* notices of 27 September 1997 place him at the Postgraduate Center for Mental Health as a founding member of its group department, a training analyst, and a senior supervisor. House language in a paid notice is not a curriculum. It is an address. The address matters because American analytic group work in mid-century New York ran through institutes as much as through AGPA hotels. Wolf and Schwartz 1962 is the book a library can still hold. Institute lore is the rest.

"Psychoanalysis of groups" as a phrase nods at Burrow's earlier wording and then walks toward paying analysands rather than a commune. Walking away from Lifwynn toward a private New York practice is a class move and a theory move. This pack keeps both moves visible so a student cannot call 1938 "the beginning of analytic group" without Pratt, Wender, and Schilder already in the room.

Emanuel Schwartz stays on the 1962 line. This pack has been putting second names back (Jennings, Lewis, Fuhriman, Stone, Shay). Dropping Schwartz to make a hero is the same habit as dropping Rickman from Northfield.

The empty chair is why he sits on this calendar. It is also why a later intern should not copy him on a Wednesday. Copying an alternate session without a theory of scapegoating is how a group becomes an unsupervised party with history. Date the furniture. Do not sell it.

AGPA's house chronology also places him among Americans who tried group methods while in uniform. War rooms are not 1938 paying analysands. Keep the two furniture arrangements on two lines. The paid notices' tally of books and articles is reception, not a reading list. This page assigns Wolf and Schwartz 1962. It does not assign a method.
