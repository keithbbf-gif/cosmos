**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 1970 Basic Books published Irvin D. Yalom's *The Theory and Practice of Group Psychotherapy*. Residencies put it on the shelf. The shelf was already crowded with Slavson, Foulkes, Moreno, and mimeographed encounter notes. What the new book did was American and pedagogical: it named mechanisms — instillation of hope, universality, imparting of information, altruism, corrective recapitulation of the family, development of socializing techniques, imitative behavior, interpersonal learning, group cohesiveness, catharsis, existential factors — and it wrote as if a resident could *watch* for them. Some of the names came out of a research program with Morton Lieberman and others. Some came out of rooms. The list leaked. People who never read the chapters still say "universality."

This essay is the era, not the life. The figure essay takes the living writer. The sister pack's Yalom essay takes the existential novels. Here the object is the outpatient process group as a 1970–1990 American institution.

## A craft against cheapness

Group as a hospital solution — one doctor, eight chairs — is older than 1970. Yalom's book refused to let cheapness be the whole theory. If a silent member mattered, a resident needed a sentence for why. The factors supplied sentences. Sentences can become checklists. Checklists can become a way not to listen. The book is better than the checklist. The checklist is what busy services copied.

He wrote about outpatient groups that lasted, and about inpatient groups that could not. Later editions kept the book current while short-stay units made the year-long process group a memory in many zip codes. The gap between the book and the census is now part of the history. Administrators who bought the book and cut the length were not reading the chapters on time.

Interpersonal learning sat at the center: the group as a social microcosm, the here-and-now as the laboratory. That center is a choice. A psychoeducation group has another center. A skills group has another. A twelve-step meeting has another. The 1970 book did not abolish the cousins. It occupied the medical-school slot.

## Who could stay in the room

Process groups, as taught, wanted people who could talk, come weekly, and not blow up the room. That wish is a clinical prudence. It is also a class filter. People with chaotic housing, people who work shifts, people whose English is not the room's English, people whose insurance allowed eight sessions — they got a different group or none. The textbook's ideal member looks, in retrospect, like a person who can use a university clinic.

Women, people of color, and queer patients wrote later about being the "only" and about a universality that was not universal. The factors did not include "the conductor shares the town's racism." Later editions and later teachers had to add the sentence. Adding it is not "woke retrofit." It is the record catching up.

Co-leaders, process notes, recording — the training apparatus around the book became a culture. Some of that culture protected patients. Some of it produced institutes that treated the group as a shrine. Shrines are bad at endings.

## The listicle problem

*The Gift of Therapy* (2002) is a later advice book. This series will not turn it, or the 1970 factor list, into a clinic-blog listicle. Copyright sits on the books. So does a boundary: a published case is not a license for a local clinician to write similarly without consent. Yalom's prefaces wrestle with disguise. Wrestling is the ethical remainder.

Existential factors on the 1970 list connect to the 1980 existential textbook. Death in a group is not a chapter title when it is a phone call. The era's honesty, when it had it, was that a process group could talk about death without a workbook. Workbooks arrived anyway, from other schools.

## What not to do with 1970

Do not memorize eleven factors and call yourself trained. Do not print the list on a waiting-room wall. Do not tell a reader that interpersonal learning is "what group really is." It is what one influential American book said group could be.

Do not invent Yalom's opinions about 2026. He is living as of last verification (2026-09-14). Public books only.

Molyn Leszcz's later co-authorship of editions is a dated fact about succession: the textbook became a two-person instrument. Succession is how a book stays assigned. It is also how a book becomes an institution that outlives a style.

A small-city clinician who ran a careful weekly group in 1988 and then watched managed care take the ending is a witness to this era. This pack will not invent her. It will say the witness is common.

## Co-leaders, notes, and the one-way glass

Training groups behind glass, once a glamour, became an ethics exam: who owns the tape, who is in the frame, what a silent member does when they know a class is watching. Palo Alto and Philadelphia had already filmed families. Group residencies copied the glass. Consent forms got longer because the glass outlived the charm.

Process notes — the conductor's private or supervisory record — are a legal object in a way Yalom's published cases are a literary one. Mixing the two is how blogs get clinics sued. This pack will not mix them.

Co-leadership, as taught, was supposed to catch what one conductor missed. It also doubled the fee problem and the rivalry. Rivalry in the conducting pair is a group event. He wrote about it. Later services assigned a student and a tired staffer and called it co-leadership. The assignment is a budget.

The 1970 book's American English traveled. Translations and pirated photocopies in later decades are a reception history. Reception is not the same as the Basic Books first edition. Cite the copy you hold.

## Translations and the stolen list

The factor list appeared on workshop slides without the chapters on selection or time. Stolen lists are how a 1970 book became a meme. Memes do not train a conductor. They train a person to say "universality" when someone cries. Crying is not a factor. It is a person.

Foreign-language editions carried American interpersonal theory into rooms that had Foulkes or Moreno already. The carrying is not always a courtesy. Sometimes it is a market.

Residency shelves in 1975 held the 1970 book next to a stolen encounter packet. The two objects trained opposite hungers: accumulation versus climax. Services that wanted climax used the packet. Services that wanted a note in the chart used the book. Patients got whichever hunger the budget preferred.

Basic Books in 1970 was a trade-and-academic hybrid press. Hybrid presses put a residency book in stores where non-residents could buy it. Non-residents then wanted fireworks. The book is not fireworks. The buyer's hunger is part of the era.
