**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

By the late 1960s an American who wanted a group did not have to find a clinic. Hotel notices, campus posters, and the Esalen catalog offered "encounter": a short, hot sitting in which strangers were invited to drop manners and speak. Carl Rogers, already famous for a softer hour, wrote *Carl Rogers on Encounter Groups* in 1970 as a man who had watched the form spread faster than anyone's ethics committee. He was curious. He was also uneasy. Both tones are in the book.

This is not a how-to and not a travel piece about Big Sur. It is a history of a fashion that forced psychotherapy to say what a group *was for*.

## What "encounter" meant

The word came through Moreno's cosmic vocabulary and through a culture that was tired of the gray flannel mouth. A T-group's here-and-now, stripped of NTL's research manners and dressed in California, could look like encounter. A Gestalt hot seat could look like encounter. A weekend of structured exercises from a mimeographed packet could look like encounter. The label sold. The contents varied from careful to reckless.

Rogers described a process that, in his telling, moved from milling to resistance to a kind of freedom. That sequence is his observation, not a law, and not a script you should try to produce. Other leaders used attack, nudity, or sleep deprivation as if intensity were a proof. The proof was circular: if people cried, it was working.

Universities hosted groups for students. Churches hosted groups for marriages. Corporations hosted groups for "openness." The same decade as the Community Mental Health Centers Act (1963) thus had two group booms: a public-clinic boom and a counterculture boom. They shared chairs and did not share contracts.

## Rogers on the road

The sister pack's Rogers essay is the person-centered life. This essay is the touring. He sat in large rooms and tried to be the same listener he was with one person. Sometimes the room allowed it. Sometimes the room wanted a prophet. He knew the difference. The 1970 book is a primary source because it is modest in a loud year.

Critics said encounter was narcissism with a permission slip. Supporters said it was democracy after McCarthy and after the silent suburban dinner. Both could point to a weekend and be right about that weekend. A culture argument cannot be settled by one group's tears.

Women's consciousness-raising groups of the same years were a different sovereignty: political, often leaderless on purpose, not a product of Esalen. Do not absorb CR into encounter. Do not absorb the Black freedom movement's mass meetings into encounter. Those rooms had enemies and goals. Encounter often had a fee.

## Research walks in

Stanford and other labs tried to measure what the catalogs would not. The 1973 Lieberman–Yalom–Miles volume is the next essay's center. Here it is enough to say: by the early 1970s the fashion had a casualty literature. Trainers who had said "trust the process" had to hear that process was not a person and could not be sued. People could be hurt.

AGPA and the analytic institutes used the casualty news to police a border: *we* are therapy, *they* are encounter. The border leaked. Many therapists had gone to a weekend and brought home a louder style. Many encounter leaders had read a little Yalom. Patients arrived in clinics expecting fireworks. The Tuesday group that worked by accumulation looked dull. Dull is sometimes the ethical setting.

## What not to do with a loud decade

Do not advertise an "encounter-style" evening. Do not use Esalen as a mood. Do not scrape workshop nudes. Do not tell a reader that manners are the enemy of health. Manners are sometimes how a room keeps the weakest person alive.

Do not sneer from a distance as if 1968 were a moral defect. People were lonely. The product sold because the loneliness was real. Products that sell on loneliness need a historian more than they need a sequel.

A small-city clinic in 2026 meets the afterlife as "authenticity" talk, as trauma-retreat ads, as the expectation that a group should climax. History's job is to date the climax culture. The climax culture is not a treatment plan.

## Films, campuses, and the fee

Campus groups in 1968 could be free and still coercive: a professor's "experiment," a dorm's fashion, a counseling center's overflow. Fee and force are different violences. Encounter sold both. A student who could not leave a required sensitivity section is not a customer. A paying weekend client could still be unable to leave in another sense — shame, the parked car, the leader's charisma.

Films of Rogers and of Perls circulated as training and as celebrity. Copyright sits on most of them. This site will not embed a hot-seat clip. Clips teach a hunger for climax. Tuesday groups starve that hunger on purpose.

Humanistic psychology's associations (AHP) and the growth-center catalogs are ephemera a librarian can still find. Ephemera are the primary sources of a fashion. Textbooks are the aftertaste.

A Southeast Arkansas reader in 1972 might have met encounter only as a magazine. Magazines are how a coastal product becomes a national mood. The mood leaked into churches that would not have said Esalen. Leakage is not the same as a local institute.

## Marriage weekends and the unlisted casualty

Church marriage-encounter weekends borrowed the word and sometimes the heat. They had their own theology. Do not merge them with Esalen. Do not merge them with a licensed couples group. Three rooms, one English noun.

Unlisted casualties — people who never entered a Stanford study — are the ordinary remainder. A fashion that lasts five years leaves a decade of people who will not sit in a circle again. Their refusal is data. It does not appear in a catalog.

Paperback racks put Rogers next to Perls next to a diet book. The rack is a historical source. It shows that encounter was a consumer good as well as a campus mood. Consumer goods leave casualties that look like buyer's remorse until someone counts them. The next essay counts.

Humanistic psychology's associations kept minutes. Minutes are dull and therefore useful. They show when a fashion became a dues-paying society and when the dues-paying society started to sound like a clinic. Sounding like a clinic is not a license. This pack will not upgrade a 1970 catalog into a treatment.

A later editor who finds a 1969 campus flyer should photograph the flyer, not the students. Flyers are ephemera with clearer licenses than faces. Faces in a circle are the one image this series has banned from the start.
