**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Hollymoor Hospital sits in Northfield, on the south side of Birmingham. From 1942 it was a military neurosis centre: soldiers who could not go back, or not yet, under a psychiatry that had to answer to an army. Out of that pressure came what later writers bundled as "the Northfield experiments." The bundle is too neat. There were at least two movements, different staff, different relations to rank, and a lot of later memoir.

John Rickman arrived late in 1942. Wilfred Bion worked with him. Their first experiment is usually dated to the turn of 1942–43 and lasted on the order of weeks, not years. They tried to make the study of the group's own tensions the task — "leaderless" in a building that was not leaderless, because the building was the British Army. *The Lancet* published "Intra-Group Tensions in Therapy" in November 1943. The army ended the first experiment. Legend says it was too chaotic. Harrison and Clarke (1992) and later reappraisals say: look at the higher-order system. A ward project that ignores the colonel's need for return-to-unit numbers will be treated as insubordination, however interesting the process.

## The second movement

S. H. Foulkes (born Siegmund Heinrich Fuchs) was at Northfield from 1943. Harold Bridger's hospital-wide work is associated with 1944. Tom Main arrived in 1945. Patrick de Maré is in the later lists. Foulkes's *Lancet* paper of March 1946, "Group Analysis in a Military Neurosis Centre," is the public marker of a longer, slower attempt: groups as a treatment, the hospital as a web of groups, process named without pretending the army had left the room.

Foulkes later wrote that therapy was *of* the group, *by* the group, including the conductor. That sentence is a theory. At Northfield it was also a staffing plan. There were more patients than couches. There were psychoanalytically literate doctors who had been refugees, soldiers, or both. The instrument they had was the circle and the hospital's day.

Do not merge Bion and Foulkes into one smiling photograph. They were not a duo. Bion's later *Experiences in Groups* (papers from 1948, book 1961) went toward basic assumptions and the pair, the fight-flight, the dependency — a severe poetry of what a group does when it cannot do the work. Foulkes went toward a conductor who trusts the group's conversation. British group culture split along that grain. Trainees still pick a grain, often without knowing they are picking a 1944 argument.

## What "experiment" hides

The word sounds like consent and a protocol. Northfield was a wartime hospital. Soldiers were patients and manpower. Recovery meant, for the institution, return. A humane doctor could still be an instrument of the war. Main's later Cassel work and his 1946 "hospital as a therapeutic institution" paper grew from this tension: the building is not neutral. Bridger treated the whole hospital as the object. That is the seed of "therapeutic community" as a British phrase, which Maxwell Jones would carry in another key.

Patients' names are not in this essay. Memoirs exist. This series will not mine them for color. The ethical remainder of Northfield is that a legend was built from people who did not get to edit the legend.

Women appear in the published Northfield story mostly as nurses, auxiliaries, and later historians. Eve Lewis co-authored with Foulkes in 1945. The conductor's chair in the famous papers is male. A history of the circle that only cites the *Lancet* will repeat that chair.

## After 1946

Foulkes left in the spring of 1946, in the usual dating. The Group-Analytic Society (1952) and the Institute of Group Analysis are peacetime institutions that claim a wartime parent. Bion went to the Tavistock and then a more private, ferocious writing life. Main went to the Cassel. Jones built therapeutic communities that American visitors later photographed. The United States had already had Pratt and Marsh. What it imported after 1945 was a *prestige*: group as psychoanalytic object, not only as class.

American AGPA, founded out of Slavson's New York world in 1943, was a parallel, not a child of Northfield. The two stories met in journals after the war and then spent decades misunderstanding each other's accents.

## What not to do with Northfield

Do not run a "Northfield-style" weekend. You do not have an army, a neurosis centre, or the right to experiment on employees. Do not use "leaderless group" as a party game. Bion and Rickman's paper is a 1943 object written under fire. It is not a facilitation tip.

Do not say the experiments "proved" group therapy. They proved that a military hospital could be reorganized, briefly, around talking, and that the reorganization would be remembered as a founding. Foundings are political. Birmingham is not a shrine. Hollymoor's later life as a civilian hospital and then a closed site is a separate civic story. If you use an exterior photograph, license it. Do not use patient stills.

A contemporary ward group that lasts one admission is not Northfield. Calling it Northfield is costume. The useful inheritance is smaller: notice the institution that holds the circle. Notice who can end the experiment. Notice whether "process" is a way to avoid the colonel's question or a way to answer it.

## Papers as wartime objects

Rickman's 1943 notes, Bridger's 1946 Menninger piece, Bion's "leaderless group project" (1946), de Maré's later large-group work — the bibliography is a dispersal. Dispersal is what happens when a hospital experiment ends and careers begin. A later conference at Hollymoor (2018, in one reappraisal's telling) treated the site as a memory. Memory sites are not methods. They are how a profession pilgrimages.

The soldiers' diagnoses — "neurosis," exhaustion, the older shell-shock word already half retired — were army words as much as medical ones. A man who could not fire, or could not sleep, or could not take an order was a manpower problem first. Group analysis that forgets the manpower problem becomes a seminar. Group analysis that only serves the manpower problem becomes a repair shop. Northfield sat on the seam. The seam is why the legend is morally uneasy and why it should stay uneasy.

American readers after 1945 met Northfield as a citation, not as a census. The citation made British group work look like a war birth. American group work already had Pratt and Slavson. The citation was prestige. Prestige is how a Birmingham suburb entered a New York syllabus.

## Nurses, rank, and the unfilmed hour

Hollymoor had nurses, orderlies, and a military chain that does not appear in the *Lancet* as characters. A group that ignores the night staff is a doctor's group. Main would later write the night into the file. At Northfield the night is mostly unwritten. Unwritten labor is how legends stay clean.

Rank in the circle — a sergeant next to a private, a medical officer who is also a major — is not a process detail. It is the British Army sitting down. "Leaderless" in that furniture is an aspiration or a joke. Bion and Rickman knew it was both. Later workshop leaders who borrow "leaderless" without rank in the room are borrowing a word, not a hospital.
