**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Anne W. Alonso died at Massachusetts General Hospital on 22 August 2007, aged 73; Bryan Marquard's *Boston Globe* obituary ran on 2 September. A 1933 birth year remains the working date this pack uses — aged 73 in August 2007 allows 1933 or 1934 until a fuller record is in hand. She spent her career at MGH from a 1968 internship onward, founded the hospital's Center for Psychoanalytic Studies (also called, in later memorials, the Center for Psychoanalytic Psychotherapy), and taught as a clinical professor of psychology at Harvard Medical School. She wrote about the difficult patient in the group and about the conductor's job when the room wants to expel someone. She taught supervision as if supervision were a group fact, not a secret. *The Quiet Profession: Supervisors of Psychotherapy* (Macmillan, 1985) is the public book.

This essay will not describe how to handle a difficult patient. It will treat a teaching life as the reason Boston became a late-century address on this calendar.

## Boston as a school

Rutan founded the Center for Group Psychotherapy at MGH (1985–1996 in later NSGP telling) and ran an observation group there with Alonso. Two centers, one hospital, two names. Oral memory of who sat in which room is not a citation. The published *IJGP* papers are. List the ones this site leans on by year. Do not invent page numbers.

The 1979 Rutan and Alonso paper on intimacy, individuation, and interdependence — as later textbooks cite it — is a dated claim that a group format forces a person to notice they affect others. Forcing notice is not always kind. It is a structural fact of a circle that lasts. They named the structure without turning it into a weekend.

Women teachers in AGPA's later decades are not Durkin's 1952 committee only. Alonso is a 1980s–2000s version: a supervisor whose name trainees carry. Names trainees carry are how professions actually work.

## The difficult patient as a group product

A patient is "difficult" sometimes because the group needs a difficulty. That sentence can be a revelation. It can be a way to blame the scapegoat. Alonso's reputation, in the literature we will cite when we have the issues in hand, is that she held the paradox. Holding is not a technique on this page.

## What not to do

Do not use "difficult patient" as a chart insult. Do not scrape MGH photos. Do not invent her opinions.

A small-city supervisor who still meets an intern after a bad group night is in her profession's ordinary. Ordinary is the point. Famous theory is cheaper than a Wednesday supervision. She spent a career on the Wednesday.

## Supervision as a group fact

An intern tells a story about a group. The supervisor hears a group in the telling. Alonso taught as if that recursion were the job. Recursion is not a trick. It is how a profession reproduces itself without only reading Yalom.

Boston's density of group teachers — MGH, the Institute, a short walk between buildings — is a local accident of hospitals and universities. Most cities do not have it. Most interns get a supervisor who also does something else at 4 p.m. The 4 p.m. supervisor is the American norm. She wrote as if the norm could still be serious.

The *IJGP* years stand even if the 1933 birth slips. Use the papers.

Do not use "difficult patient" as a slur in a chart. The phrase is a 1980s–90s literature, not a personality.

A Wednesday supervision after a bad group night is the unwritten textbook of this profession. She wrote toward that Wednesday. Famous 1970 books do not sit in the room at 5 p.m. A supervisor does. Boston had more of them than most cities. She is why this calendar includes a teacher who did not need a Yalom-sized trade paperback.

## Presidents, Fielding, and a 1985 book

She served as president of the Northeastern Society for Group Psychotherapy and later of AGPA. AGPA now gives an award in her name; MGH hosts an Anne W. Alonso Lecture. Honors are reception. The 1985 Macmillan book is the object a library can still hold: supervision as a public craft, not a secret corridor. Fielding Graduate University awarded the clinical-psychology doctorate in 1980; she later endowed the Alonso Center for Psychodynamic Studies there. Harvard's 2001 Clifford Barger Award for mentoring and AGPA's Woman of the Year the same year are public facts in the *Globe* notice. Do not turn them into a brand credential for WOW.

Harvard's later memorial minute (2008) places her among four founders of MGH's General Psychiatry Practice in 1975 and in the Acute Psychiatric Service from the internship years — rooms that held very disturbed patients, a love "not always shared by psychoanalytic practitioners." That sentence is why a group teacher belongs next to a hospital, not only next to a hotel.

Rutan's name is in the citations. Neither life is only a co-author. Interns left Boston and seeded Wednesdays elsewhere. She wrote as if those Wednesdays could still be serious. Seriousness without a famous 1970 spine is the reason she has a file.

Harvard's memorial minute also records an Endowment for Psychodynamic Psychotherapy she founded, with the public motto that there should always be someone to talk to. Mottos are reception. They are not a WOW tagline. A 2007 *IJGP* piece of hers on a writing group, published the year she died, is one more dated object: she kept treating groups of clinicians as groups. Do not invent her opinions. Do not scrape MGH photos. If 1933 slips, keep 1968, 1975, 1980, 1985, 2007.
