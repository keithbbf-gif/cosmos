**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Gisela Peiper Konopka was born in Berlin on 11 February 1910 and died in Minneapolis on 9 December 2003. Her parents had left Poland after pogroms. She survived imprisonment in the Nazi years, reached the United States in 1941, earned a Columbia doctorate in social welfare (1957), and taught at the University of Minnesota, where she directed the Center for Youth Development and Research. Germany later decorated her for return work on social services. "Justice with a heart" is the phrase the memorials like. Memorials flatten. The books do not have to.

*Social Group Work: A Helping Process* (1963) is the American textbook that took Coyle's professional object into a new decade: the Great Society at the door, youth as a national anxiety, group work as a named helping process rather than a club accident.

## Refugee as method

She knew what a group can do when the state is the enemy. She also knew what a group can do when the state is a social-work employer. That double knowledge is why she belongs next to Foulkes and Lewin on a calendar of people who crossed water and then taught Americans about sitting together. The sitting was not therapy in her profession's sense. It was help, citizenship, sometimes control. She wrote as if the helper could admit the control and still try for justice.

Youth — especially girls in trouble with institutions — was her long subject. Institutions for girls are a brutal American file. A group worker inside them can be a witness or a soft guard. She wanted the first. Wanting is not the same as the institution becoming kind.

Eleven books and hundreds of articles, the memorials say. This page will not list them. It will say: she made group work internationally teachable from Minneapolis, which is not New York and not London. Geography matters. The Midwest address kept the method in land-grant and public-university air.

## Not AGPA's child

She is not a footnote to Slavson. Social group work had its own schools, its own CSWE fights, its own later identity crisis when "clinical" social work pulled students toward the couch. The crisis is a 1970s–90s story. She is the 1963 marker: a helping process with a German-Jewish refugee's moral heat.

Do not absorb her into "group psychotherapy pioneers" as if the NASW and the APA were one guild. They were not.

## What not to do with Konopka

Do not use "justice with a heart" as a clinic slogan. Do not scrape University of Minnesota photos. Do not invent her views on a current juvenile-justice bill. Do not put a child's face on this post.

A small-city youth program that sits in a circle after school is in her profession's descendant line. A psychotherapy group for adults is not. This pack exists to keep those lines visible so SEO cannot braid them into "we do group."

## Berlin rooms and a Minnesota center

Two rooms behind a grocery in Berlin, a father dead in 1935, a sister killed in a bombing, a mother and another sister to Palestine: the JWA article is the public family file. This page will not novelize it. It will say she knew displacement before she taught American youth workers about groups.

The Center for Youth Development and Research at Minnesota is a land-grant fact. Land-grant universities took European refugees and made them public teachers. That is an American story this pack can tell without a flag.

"Justice with a heart" as a German decoration's gloss is a memorial phrase. Use it once. Do not make it a brand. She published eleven books. Assign one. *Social Group Work* (1963) is the door.

Do not put her on an AGPA founding slide. She is NASW's cousin, not Slavson's child.

A land-grant center in Minneapolis is a long way from a Berlin grocery. The distance is the American mid-century deal: refugees teach the public's children. She took the deal and then flew back to Germany to help rebuild services. Return is not forgiveness of a state. It is a worker's stubbornness. Stubbornness is in the 1963 textbook's moral heat.

## How to hold a refugee teacher

A Berlin grocery, a Minnesota center, a German medal, eleven books: the memorial list is true and flat. Flattening happens to people who lived a century. This pack's job is the 1963 textbook and the refusal to plate her as AGPA's diversity paragraph.

Youth in institutions is a brutal American file. A group worker inside it can be a witness. She wanted that. Institutions remain. This page will not staff them.

A 11 February 1910 Berlin birth and a 9 December 2003 Minneapolis death are LC-quality dates. Use them. Use the 1963 Prentice-Hall book. Do not use a child's face. Do not use a medal as a logo.

Prentice-Hall in 1963 is a textbook house, not a psychoanalytic press. Textbook houses decide what public-university students assign. Assignment is how social group work stayed a profession while psychiatry's hotel told another story. This file is the refusal to let the hotel eat the textbook.
