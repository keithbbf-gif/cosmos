**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Morton A. Lieberman died in February 2019, aged 87; the UCSF Department of Psychiatry posted an in-memoriam note on 25 February. Birth around 1931–32 follows from that age and still wants a vital-records check before a carved caption. His name on *Encounter Groups: First Facts* (1973) is why a fashion got a public harm-and-help literature. He also spent a long career on self-help groups, widowhood, and aging — rooms that are cousins of therapy and not therapy.

The harm-era essay holds the 1973 book. This essay holds the researcher who would not let the catalog have the last word.

## Measurement as a moral act

Encounter leaders said peak experience could not be measured. He measured what could be measured and named the rest. That is not a hostility to groups. It is a hostility to unaccountable heat. Casualties, leader style, conditions: the variables are in the book. This page will not turn them into a consumer checklist.

Yalom and Miles were collaborators. Collaboration is how a Stanford-adjacent study got done. Lieberman's later work moved toward the groups people join without a billing code — widows, illness, the mutual-help ecology that includes and exceeds AA. A man who measured encounter and then studied self-help is a man who knew the cousins.

The UCSF note is enough for a clean CV line: Ph.D. in social psychology from the University of Chicago in 1956; twenty-six years on that faculty in psychiatry and human development; UCSF Department of Psychiatry from 1982. Later titles there included the Aging and Mental Health Program (1985–1992), the UCSF Alzheimer's Center (1986–1998), and co-direction of the multi-campus UC Self-Help Research Center (1987–1993). Visiting time at Stanford, Wisconsin–Madison, and the Tavistock Institute is named in the same notice. Letterheads matter less than the papers. The letterheads are no longer a guess.

## Aging and the unfashionable circle

Old people in groups are not a 1960s poster. They are a 1980s–2000s research object: bereavement, peer support, the question of whether a circle helps when the culture wants youth. This pack will not summarize those findings as a promise. It will say the researcher stayed with unfashionable rooms after the encounter wave broke.

## What not to do with Lieberman

Do not use 1973 as a reason to defund every group. Do not use it as a reason to skip consent. Do not scrape university portraits.

## Widows, peers, and the unfashionable sample

After encounter, the fashionable subject disappeared. He stayed with people who join groups because a spouse died or a diagnosis arrived — samples that do not look like Stanford undergraduates in 1969. Unfashionable samples are how a researcher stays honest.

Self-help, in his later vocabulary, includes rooms this pack has been calling cousins. Measurement of cousins is a different design than a randomized skills group. He knew the difference. Bloggers who cite "Lieberman" for any group number often do not.

The 1973 title page still holds if a birth year slips. Use the book.

A career that runs from encounter casualties to widow groups is a map of American sitting: first the fashionable heat, then the ordinary loss. Ordinary loss does not get Esalen catalogs. It gets a church basement and a researcher willing to count. Counting is not cold. It is how unfashionable rooms get a literature.

## Before 1973 and after it

*Psychotherapy Through the Group Process* (1964), written with Dorothy Stock Whitaker, is the professional book that precedes the encounter study. Whitaker's name stays on the line. A 1969 case study on Arab and Jew in Israel, listed in the UCSF notice, is human-relations work in a different country — cousin to NTL, not a second *First Facts*. *Last Home for the Aged* (1976) and *The Experience of Old Age* (1983) moved the same habit of follow-up into institutions that do not look like Stanford undergraduates in 1969. *Doors Close, Doors Open: Widows, Grieving, and Growing* (G.P. Putnam's Sons, 1996) is the house this pack wanted on the calendar so encounter cannot keep the only research noun. Library catalogs also lock his birth year at 1931.

The 1976 *Annual Review of Psychology* chapter "Change Induction in Small Groups" is a dated survey, not a slogan. *Self-Help Groups for Coping with Crisis* (with Leonard Borman and others, 1979) is the cousin literature: rooms people join without a billing code. Measurement of cousins is a different design than a randomized skills group. He knew the difference. Bloggers who cite "Lieberman" for any group number often do not.

This file is not the harm-era essay. That essay holds 1973 as an event. This file holds a career that left the event for widows, Alzheimer's-center administration, and a self-help research center. Follow-up is why casualties and widows both got a literature. Encounter leaders wanted a peak. Widows wanted a Tuesday that still existed in month four. He measured both and did not pretend they were one sample.

*Last Home for the Aged* (1976) and *The Experience of Old Age* (1983) are the unfashionable objects: institutions, stress, survival. Old people in groups are not a 1960s poster. They are a research program medicine can ignore. He did not ignore it. That is a figure's claim to a chair on this calendar. Do not use 1973 as a reason to defund every group or to skip consent.
