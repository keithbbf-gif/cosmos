**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In the summer of 1946, in New Britain, Connecticut, Kurt Lewin and colleagues ran a workshop for community leaders at the request of the Connecticut Interracial Commission, with the American Jewish Congress Committee on Community Interrelations in the neighborhood of the invitation. The point was not therapy. The point was skills against discrimination and for fair employment. Staff met at night to discuss what they had seen in the small groups. Participants asked to listen. Then they argued with the staff's account of what had happened. The argument was more alive than the lesson. That aliveness became the T-group: a training group whose curriculum is its own behavior.

Lewin died in February 1947. He did not see the first summer of the National Training Laboratory for Group Development at Gould Academy in Bethel, Maine. Leland Bradford, Ronald Lippitt, and Kenneth Benne did. A history that says "Lewin founded NTL at Bethel in 1946" fails this pack's guardrails.

## A cultural island

The staff picked Bethel as a "cultural island": far enough that habit might loosen. Teachers, social workers, clergy, managers, a few clinicians rode north. The early laboratories were occupationally mixed. The method had names that shifted — Basic Skills Training Group, then T-group — and a faith that feedback in the here-and-now would make better democrats. Lewin's earlier experiments on autocratic, democratic, and laissez-faire climates (with Lippitt and White, 1930s) sat in the background like a proof.

What happened in the room is not ours to script. What happened to the method is public. It became a prestige vacation for people who could get three weeks off. It became a tool of organization development. It became, in some companies, a pressure cooker that did not say it was a pressure cooker. Employees who could not refuse a "lab" were not the 1946 community volunteers.

## From race workshop to corporate retreat

The New Britain brief was interracial and interreligious tension in Connecticut. The Bethel afterlife was often whiter and more managerial than that brief. The irony is ordinary and bitter. A method born in a fair-employment workshop became a way for executives to practice candor with people they outranked. Some trainers kept the justice thread. Some sold "authenticity."

Chris Argyris, Edgar Schein, Warren Bennis, Douglas McGregor — names from the management shelf — drank from this well. This pack will not retell the history of Theory Y. It will say: the T-group is one reason your workplace has "norms," "process checks," and a facilitator with a marker. It is also one reason some people flinch when a manager says "let's go around."

NTL's *Journal of Applied Behavioral Science* (from 1965) is a dated object: research plus a movement. Movements publish journals when they want to be sciences.

## Therapy or not

T-groups were not billed as psychotherapy. People still wept. People still used the week as if it were a clinic. Trainers still faced casualties. The later encounter-group literature (Lieberman, Yalom, Miles 1973) is a different decade and a louder culture, but the casualty problem did not start in Esalen. It started whenever an intense small group had weak screening and a trainer who believed more heat was more truth.

Clinicians attended Bethel and then imported "here-and-now" into therapy groups. Yalom's later textbook made here-and-now a craft. The import is a success story if you like process groups. It is a category error if you think a T-group *is* a therapy group. Different contract. Different exit. Different duty.

## What not to do with Bethel

Do not run a T-group on your staff because you read that Lewin believed in democracy. Do not require hourly employees to "share feedback" as a condition of keeping a job. Do not call a trust fall a T-group. Do not put a 1946 race-workshop halo on a 2026 offsite.

Do keep the sequence straight: New Britain 1946, Lewin's death, Bethel 1947, corporate travel, encounter's louder cousin, then a long professionalization into OD. Gould Academy's campus is not a shrine you photograph for a clinic blog without a license.

A small-city practice that never sends anyone to Maine still lives in a country whose managers learned to talk about "the group" at Bethel or at a knockoff. The talk leaked into schools, churches, and, yes, clinics. Leakage is not consent.

## Office of Naval Research and the NEA

Funding for the 1947 summer included, in the institutional tellings, the Office of Naval Research and the National Education Association, where Bradford ran adult education. A race-workshop child thus had a Navy parent and a teachers'-association parent. Those parents wanted skills, not catharsis. What they bought, over time, was a method that produced both. Buyers do not always read the whole product.

The Research Center for Group Dynamics moved from MIT toward Michigan after Lewin — a later institutional story. NTL stayed, for decades, entangled with Bethel's summers and then with a Maryland headquarters. Headquarters are how a movement becomes a vendor.

Japanese laboratory-method histories (the 1950s–60s translations) show the T-group traveling as a U.S. export. Export is not encounter's later celebrity. It is a quieter Cold War educational product. This pack notes the export so Bethel is not only a Maine postcard.

If a later editor quotes Kleiner's *Age of Heretics*, quote it as management history, not as a primary 1946 source.

## Feedback as a postwar manners

"I" statements and "feedback" as a civic duty leaked from labs into American schools and marriages. The leak is why a 2026 reader thinks a group should comment on their behavior. That thought has a 1947 parent. The parent wanted democracy after fascism. The child sometimes wanted a weapon. Weapons are how a manners becomes a trial.

Gould Academy's campus rented space. Town and gown in Bethel is a local history NTL's brochures soften. Softening is ordinary. A clinic blog should not pretend a Maine town was a laboratory without residents.

A three-week summer in Maine assumed a salary that continued. Teachers and clergy sometimes had that summer. Hourly workers did not. The T-group's democracy thus studied people who could buy time. Time is the real cultural island. Bethel was just the town that rented the island a campus.

Benne, Bradford, and Lippitt are three names this pack will not collapse into "Lewin's students." They ran a summer he did not see. Running a summer is authorship. Authorship is why NTL's later vendor life cannot be blamed on a man who died in February. Blame and credit both need dates. 1946 New Britain. 1947 Bethel. Those are the only two summers this essay needs.
