**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 1935, in Akron, Ohio, a New York stockbroker who could not stay sober and an Akron surgeon who could not stay sober sat down together. The fellowship that grew from that sitting called itself Alcoholics Anonymous. The first edition of the Big Book appeared in 1939. The meeting — church basement, clubhouse, later Zoom — became one of the most common circles in American life. It was not psychotherapy. It said so. Group therapists spent the rest of the century not quite believing it.

This essay will not reprint the Twelve Steps or the Twelve Traditions. Those texts are copyrighted, living, and not a clinic's to assign. It will not tell a reader to go, or not to go. It will describe a historical object that every later "support group" had to stand next to.

## A fellowship with rules about money and fame

AA's traditions, as they hardened, included a suspicion of professionalism, a refusal of outside controversy, and a ban on members using the fellowship to promote a clinic. That ban is part of why this series can write *about* AA and cannot pretend WOW Therapies "uses" AA. A speech-language practice has no business annexing a fellowship.

The meeting's form — a leader who is not a doctor, a story, a desire to stop drinking "one day at a time" (a phrase that became folklore), coffee — trained millions of people to speak in a circle without a billing code. Psychiatrists sat in the back. Some were helpful. Some recruited. Some wrote papers that treated the meeting as a primitive group therapy that needed an expert. The papers are dated. The arrogance in some of them is also dated and still around.

Kurtz's *Not-God* (1979) remains a standard history: AA as a religious (or "not-God") answer to modern isolation, born from the Oxford Group and then breaking with it. The Oxford Group lineage matters. So does the break. A clinic that collapses AA into "Frank Buchman's moral re-armament" is doing a take. A clinic that collapses AA into Yalom is doing another take. The meeting is its own take.

## What group therapy borrowed

Universality, hope, identification, the helper-therapy principle (the helper is helped) — later textbooks found these in AA and then found them in Pratt and then found them in Yalom's factor list. The borrowing is real. The citation is often missing. AA members also borrowed from medicine: hospitals, "allergy" language, the disease model as it was available. The traffic went both ways.

Treatment programs after the 1950s built "28 days" around meetings, sometimes with care, sometimes as a warehouse that marched people to a church at 7 p.m. and called it a continuum. Court-mandated meetings changed the room. A mandated circle is not the same object as a voluntary one, even if the readings are the same. Historians of criminal justice and historians of group work share that problem; this pack will not solve it.

Women's meetings, LGBTQ meetings, meetings in languages other than English, meetings that rejected the God language — these are internal AA (and sister-fellowship) histories. A 1939 book written by white American men is not the whole story. Al-Anon (1951) and later family fellowships are cousins of Marsh's relative groups and of multifamily psychoeducation, with a different sovereignty.

## Harm, limits, and the right to leave

AA has been a lifeline. AA has also been a room where people were shamed, where medical care was delayed, where women were unsafe, where atheists were told they were not working the program, where the only story allowed was one story. Both sentences can be true. A history that only inspirationalizes the basement is a tract. A history that only prosecutes the basement is a different tract.

This series will not adjudicate "whether AA works." The outcome literature is a field of its own, with selection problems and definition problems and a lot of heat. Name the field. Do not pretend a blog post settled it.

Narcotics Anonymous, Overeaters Anonymous, and the wider twelve-step ecology are later dated objects. Synanon (1958) claimed a harder method and became a warning (see the harm essay). Do not slide from AA to Synanon as if they were one "group." That slide has been used to smear fellowships and to excuse cults. Keep the names.

## What not to do with this cousin

Do not print the Steps on a clinic handout. Do not require attendance as if a history essay were an order. Do not use "higher power" as a clinical intervention you invented. Do not put a booking button under this article. Do not photograph a meeting. Do not scrape a Big Book jacket.

A person who wants help with drinking needs a person, a license, a fellowship, or a hospital — sometimes more than one — and not a heritage page. If you are in danger, call local emergency services.

The church basement remains, in American English, a joke and a sanctuary. Group psychotherapy had to decide whether it was the same room with a credential. It is not. The chairs look related. The sovereignty is different. That difference is the whole essay.

## Hospitals that rented the basement

By the 1950s and 1960s American treatment programs walked patients to meetings and wrote "AA" in the chart as if a fellowship were a discharge plan. Some counselors were sober members who knew the difference. Some were employees who needed a box checked. The Traditions' suspicion of professionalism exists because the chart kept arriving.

Hazelden and the Minnesota model are a neighboring industry history. This pack will not retell it. It will say: a fellowship and a treatment center can share coffee and still not share sovereignty. When they share a building, someone should say who can make you stay.

Research on AA is a thick, arguing literature — selection, spirituality as a variable, court referral as a confound. Kurtz is a historian, not a meta-analyst. Burlingame's world is numbers. Keep the shelves apart when you write a sentence about "what works."

Akron and New York are the origin cities in the official telling. The telling is a published story. Other cities' early meetings are local histories. A small-city meeting in 2026 is not Akron 1935. It is a descendant with a coffee pot and a fight about candles.

## Chips, smoke, and the clock

Early meetings smelled like cigarettes. Later meetings smelled like coffee and sanitizer. The smell is not trivial. It is how a room remembers its decade. The clock — an hour, then the Lord's Prayer or a secular close, depending on the meeting — is a technology of ending. Process groups envy a clean end. Fellowships built one and then argued about God. The argument is internal. This site is not a party to it.
