**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Joseph Hersey Pratt was born in 1872 and died in 1956. He was a Boston internist who wanted Osler's home-care advice to work for people who could not buy a sanatorium week. The era essay on the class method holds the argument about 1904 versus 1905, Emmanuel Church versus Massachusetts General, and whether a tuberculosis meeting is psychotherapy. This essay holds the man who would not give up the word *class*.

## An internist, not a psychiatrist

Pratt trained and worked in medicine, not in the new psychoanalysis. That fact is the most useful thing about him for this pack. Group treatment's American pedigree includes a chest physician with a notebook. Later psychiatrists who wanted a respectable ancestor found him in *JAMA*. Later psychiatrists who wanted a *psychological* ancestor found Moreno or Burrow. Pratt remains the reminder that the weekly room entered American medicine as hygiene.

He wrote as a doctor who believed rest, air, food, and a visitor could change a course of disease in an era before streptomycin. He published numbers. The numbers traveled. This series will not recycle a 76 percent "cure" as if the definition of cure, the selection of patients, and the century had not moved. Osler's praise of Pratt's personality — reported in biographical essays — is a period compliment. Personality is not a protocol.

The out-patient department at MGH saw Irish immigrant labor and people who looked "fairly healthy" and wanted a tonic. Pratt's 1907 paper is vivid on that scene. Vivid is not the same as a license to invent a composite patient for a blog.

## Two classes, one habit

The tuberculosis class (mid-1900s through the early 1920s in the Emmanuel-linked telling) taught a regimen in public. The Thought Control Class at the Boston Dispensary, from about 1930 until near the end of his life in one retrospective (`[VERIFY]` the 1930 opening against a dispensary report), applied the same habit to complaints that did not resolve on a film: a room, a teacher, encouragement, a record. Psychosomatic writers of 1990 returned to that second class as a cheap instrument. Cheap is a motive. It is also a risk. A circle that exists because it is cheap will be asked to hold whatever the budget will not.

He did not become a group analyst. He did not write about transference in the group. He wrote about compliance and morale. A contemporary skills-group leader who cites him is citing a cousin, not a supervisor.

## Boston as a machine

Pratt's career is a downtown career: hospital, parish, dispensary, medical society. Richard Cabot's social service, Worcester's Emmanuel clinic, and Pratt's class shared streets and volunteers. A history of "the Boston school of group" is tempting and slightly false. They shared a city more than a theory.

He wanted to be like Osler, the biographical title says. Wanting to be like Osler is a career in American medicine. It is not a group-therapy joke. It means: bedside, essay, authority, a moral tone. The class is what you do when the bedside cannot hold the city's poor.

## What not to do with Pratt

Do not call him the father of group therapy. He did not call himself that. Do not put his face on a "join our group" page. Do not scrape a Countway portrait without a license check (`portrait: confirm`). Prefer a 1907 or 1912 title page.

Do not tell a reader with a cough to start a class. Tuberculosis is a public-health disease with a modern treatment. This page is not that treatment.

A small-city internist in 2026 who runs a diabetes education night is closer to Pratt than a process-group conductor is. The closeness is historical, not a credential. Education nights need their own consent and their own facts.

## Letters, students, and the unmeasured variable

Osler's compliment, as biographical essays report it, named the variable Pratt's papers underplayed: the physician's own force in the room. A class method that requires a Pratt will not survive a locum. A class method that survives a locum will become a protocol. He published toward the protocol. Admirers quoted toward the man. This pack keeps the split visible.

Students who sat in the Thought Control Class later wrote reminiscences that a dispensary historian should gather before anyone calls the class "CBT." It was not CBT. It was a 1930s Boston room with a name that now sounds like a joke. Jokes are how later schools hide their parents.

He died in 1956, the year American group work already had AGPA, Beacon, and the memory of Northfield. He did not need those children. They needed a *JAMA* grandfather. Grandfathers get simplified. Read 1907 instead of the slide.

The porch-sleeping instruction belongs to a tuberculosis era and to a climate. Do not romanticize cold as character. Poor patients froze. That is in the method. This page will not ask anyone to freeze.

Countway and MGH archives, if a later editor is granted them, will hold more than *JAMA*. Letters, class lists, a visitor's report: those are the documents that would thicken a 76 percent figure into a human week. This pass does not have those folders. It has 1907, 1910, and 1912. Those years are enough to refuse a father-poster and enough to keep a porch from becoming a brand.

He taught medical students who then practiced in towns that had no Emmanuel Church. The method traveled as a sentence in a lecture: gather them weekly. Gathering is cheap. Visitors are not. Towns that copied the gathering and skipped the visitor got a lecture hall. He had already said the visitor mattered. Copiers skip the expensive sentence. That is how a class becomes a warehouse two generations early.
