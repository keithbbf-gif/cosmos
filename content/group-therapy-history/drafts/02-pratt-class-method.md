**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Myer Solis-Cohen, writing in *JAMA* in 1910, dated the inauguration of Joseph Pratt's tuberculosis class to 1 July 1904. Later retrospectives prefer 1905 and pin the room to the Emmanuel Church Tuberculosis Class in Boston. Pratt's own 1907 paper in *JAMA* describes the out-patient department of the Massachusetts General Hospital, the patients who could not afford Rutland, and a method that was stubbornly practical: rest, outdoor air, a diary, a visitor, a weekly meeting. The date fight is not pedantry. It is a warning against the sentence this series refuses: that someone "invented group therapy" on a calendar day.

## What the class actually did

Pratt was an internist who wanted Osler's home-care advice to work for people who still had to live in Boston tenements. Patients were asked to sleep outdoors when they could — porch, roof, yard tent — and to write down hours, pulse, temperature, food. A nurse or friendly visitor came to the house. Once a week the class met. Members compared records. Members who had stayed out in the cold told newer members that the cold was survivable. Pratt examined. He talked. He was not running a process group. He was running a regimen in public so the regimen would stick.

He published results that later readers should handle with tongs: high rates of "cure" defined by fever, weight, and return to work, in an era before antibiotics, with selected patients and a physician whose personality Osler later praised in a letter. A 76 percent figure travels through biographical essays. This pack will not convert that figure into a 2026 outcome claim. Tuberculosis treatment changed. The class method's afterlife is social, not bacteriological.

The name "class" mattered to Pratt. He kept it. When the same man, decades later, ran a "Thought Control Class" at the Boston Dispensary for patients with chronic complaints that did not resolve on a chest film, he still used the schoolroom word. A class has a teacher. A therapy group, in later American usage, pretends it does not — or pretends the teacher is only a "facilitator." Pratt did not pretend.

## Church, hospital, and who paid

The Emmanuel connection is easy to overplay and hard to ignore. Elwood Worcester's parish was already experimenting with a medical-religious clinic (see the next essay). Pratt's tuberculosis work sat near that parish and near MGH. Money, volunteers, and rooms moved along those streets. Poor Irish patients in the out-patient department were not a seminar in interpersonal learning. They were people who could not buy a train ticket to a sanatorium.

Home visits made the class a public-health instrument. The visitor saw the house. The visitor saw whether the diary was a fiction. That is surveillance as well as care. A history that only celebrates "mutual encouragement" is leaving out the notebook that could be used against a patient who would not comply. Pratt wrote as a doctor who believed compliance saved lungs. A later group therapist who cites Pratt as an ancestor is citing a hygiene project.

Other American cities copied the class for tuberculosis and then for diabetes, heart disease, obesity, pregnancy. The copies prove that medicine already knew how to lecture a room. They do not prove that every copied class became psychotherapy. Most stayed classes.

## The argument about ancestry

Mid-century group therapists needed a pedigree. Pratt was useful: American, medical, published in *JAMA*, safely dead (1956) by the time the textbooks thickened. Moreno was useful in a different way: he claimed he had coined "group psychotherapy" and he wanted the phrase back from people he thought were running lectures. Foulkes was useful to the British. Slavson was useful to AGPA. A clean origin story cannot hold all four.

The honest sentence is smaller. Pratt showed that a weekly room could carry a medical regimen for people who would otherwise be lost to follow-up, and that the room's encouragement was part of the carrying. Whether that is "group psychotherapy" depends on whether you require free interaction, a theory of transference, a trained conductor, or only a gathering with a clinical purpose. This series will not legislate the definition. It will keep Pratt in the class.

H. S. Rosenbaum and later psychosomatic writers returned to the Thought Control Class as a cheap instrument for complaints that filled general medical offices. That return is a 1990 paper's problem, not a protocol. The class looked efficient. Efficiency is a historical motive. It is also how a circle becomes a warehouse.

## What the diaries were

An hour-by-hour record is not a journal of feelings. It is a time sheet. Pratt's patients were being asked to become clerks of their own rest. The class then audited the clerk. There is a straight line from that audit to every later "homework" group, and another line to every later complaint that group is just school for the sick. Both lines are real. A skills-training group in the 1990s is not Pratt's class. It rhymes with the diary.

Do not assign diaries. This is a history of a form.

## What not to do with Pratt

Do not put him on a poster as the father of anything. Do not use his cure rates in a clinic blog as if streptomycin had not happened. Do not imply that WOW Therapies runs a class method. Do not tell a reader with a cough to start a porch-sleeping group.

Do notice the class's class politics. The method existed because sanatoria were for people with a little money or a state bed. The weekly room was what you did when the state bed was full. Later community-mental-health groups inherited that arithmetic: one clinician, eight chairs, a census that will not wait. Pratt's arithmetic was pulmonary. The later arithmetic was psychiatric. The furniture was similar.

Boston in those years was also the city of the Emmanuel Movement, of Richard Cabot's social service at MGH, of a medical elite that could walk from hospital to parish in a few minutes. The class method is a downtown invention. It traveled because *JAMA* traveled. It did not travel evenly into rural counties that had no visitor and no porch.

## Visitors, funds, and the copycats

The paid nurse and the volunteer "friendly visitor" were not decorations. Solis-Cohen's 1910 *JAMA* piece, written as the method spread, spends its useful pages on money: class funds, inspection of the house, the investigation of a family's means. A weekly room that does not see the house is a different object. Pratt's class saw the house. Seeing the house is public health. It is also a class relation — the visitor enters, the patient is entered.

Other Eastern cities copied the form for tuberculosis and then hung other diagnoses on it. The copies prove that *JAMA* could move a furniture arrangement. They do not prove that every copied class had Pratt's personality, which Osler, in the biographical telling, thought was the unmeasured variable. A method that depends on a personality will not scale. A method that scales without the personality will become a warehouse. Both futures are in the 1910s mail.

Rutland, the state sanatorium named in the 1907 paper, is the ghost in the room: the place you went if you could pay a little or if a bed opened. The class existed because the ghost was full. Later community groups exist because the inpatient bed is short. The arithmetic is the inheritance.

When antibiotics arrived, the tuberculosis class lost its medical reason. The Thought Control Class did not, because its reason had never been a bacillus. That second class is why psychosomatic writers of 1990 still wanted him. They wanted a cheap room for complaints that fill general medical offices. This pack will not help them staff it. It will say the want is old.
