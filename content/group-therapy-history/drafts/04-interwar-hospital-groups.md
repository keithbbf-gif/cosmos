**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Between the Armistice and the next war, American state hospitals were cities. Thousands of beds. Few physicians. A lecture hall was cheaper than a psychoanalysis. Into that arithmetic walked men (almost all men, in the published record) who thought a crowd might do what a private hour could not. Edward Lazell gave talks to patients with schizophrenia at St. Elizabeths in Washington. Louis Wender wrote about the "dynamics" of group psychotherapy in 1936 as if the phrase were already available. L. Cody Marsh, a minister-physician, ran revival-shaped classes at Kings Park and later Worcester State and coined the line everyone still quotes: "By the crowd they have been broken, by the crowd they shall be healed." Trigant Burrow, who had been analyzed and had analyzed, tried to make the group itself the instrument of a new science he called phyloanalysis.

None of this is Northfield. Northfield is the next essay. The interwar American ward is the essay the British story sometimes skips.

## Lectures at St. Elizabeths

Lazell's date is one of those numbers textbooks love and archives complicate. Many secondary accounts say 1921. `[VERIFY]` against Lazell's own papers and St. Elizabeths reports before a caption uses 1921 as granite. What is not in dispute is the form: a physician speaking to a room of patients about conflict, habit, and the contents of a mind, in a federal hospital that already knew how to assemble bodies. The form is Pratt's class without the sputum. It is also a classroom. Patients listened. Some spoke. The physician remained the physician.

Later group analysts would call this "group therapy of the group" versus "in the group" versus "by the group" — Foulkes's prepositions. Lazell was mostly *to* the group. That preposition still exists every time a unit runs "medication education" and calls it group on the census sheet.

## Marsh's revival

Marsh's 1931 paper in *Mental Hygiene* is the document. He listed lecture titles that sound like a Chautauqua stacked on a ward: adjustment to hospitalization, work and relaxation, family, sex, superstitions, "how to raise a baby," a final examination. Recovering patients gave testimony. There was singing. He wanted the hospital thought of as a school and the psychiatrist as an educator. He said individual treatment was still required. He said the class hour was, for most, "an hour of happiness."

Happiness is not a trivial claim in a 1931 state hospital. It is also not an outcome trial. Marsh's motto about the crowd is half hymn and half indictment. The crowd that broke people included families, streets, and the hospital itself. The crowd that would heal them was a class he conducted. The circularity is obvious. He did not hide that he was the moderator. He hid, perhaps, how much the institution needed the class to make "better hospital citizens" — his phrase — which is close to "quieter wards."

In 1935 he wrote about the psychiatric clinic; in 1936, about groups for relatives of psychotic patients. The relative group is an ancestor of later multifamily psychoeducation, which is a different essay and a different ethics. Marsh got there as a pastor who thought families needed a lecture too.

## Wender's dynamics

Wender's 1936 paper uses "group psychotherapy" as if the noun had settled. He was a hospital psychiatrist interested in what happens *among* patients, not only what is said *to* them. The paper is short by later textbook standards and large as a marker: an American journal was willing to treat the group as a dynamism, not only as a loudspeaker.

Do not upgrade Wender into Foulkes. Do not downgrade him into a footnote. He is a hospital doctor writing in the Depression, when private analysis was a luxury good and the state hospital was the mental-health system.

## Burrow's other science

Trigant Burrow is the strangest figure in this set, which is why he gets his own essay. Here he matters as an interwar alternative. He thought neurosis was a social disorder of the species and that a group of researchers living the method could see it. The Lifwynn Foundation was not a ward class. It was closer to a commune with a theory. Mainstream psychiatry found him unreadable. Group therapists later raided him for the word "group analysis" and then discovered Foulkes had meant something else.

Burrow's presence in this era essay is a brake. Not every interwar experiment wanted more patients per doctor. Some wanted a different human animal.

## Race, diagnosis, and who sat

St. Elizabeths, Kings Park, Worcester: these were segregated or differentially populated in ways the papers rarely center. Diagnosis in the 1920s–30s was a moving target — dementia praecox becoming schizophrenia, paresis still in the wards, "constitutional psychopaths" as a dumping language. A lecture series that includes "problems of sex" in 1931 is a lecture series inside a moral order. This pack will not reconstruct those lectures. It will say they happened, and that the happening was treatment *and* social control.

Women physicians and social workers ran groups that did not make *Mental Hygiene*. Attendants ran unofficial circles — smokes, cards, the day room. The published interwar story is a story of named men. The unnamed rooms were larger.

## What not to do with the interwar ward

Do not revive Marsh's revival as a weekend workshop. Do not use "better hospital citizens" as a compliment. Do not cite a 1931 happiness sentence as evidence that coercive campuses were gentle. Do not skip this decade because the British war story is prettier.

A contemporary inpatient "process group" that lasts twenty minutes because discharge is tomorrow is closer to Lazell's lecture, in structure, than to Yalom's outpatient year. The census always wins unless someone writes a different budget. The interwar writers wrote inside the budget. Their honesty, when they had it, was the honesty of scarcity.

## Loudspeakers and the other Americans

Austin Riggs, in the secondary timelines, lectured over loudspeakers to patients in a Stockbridge hospital in the same years Marsh was exhorting a ward. A loudspeaker is a group technology that does not require a circle. It is Pratt's class without the faces. This pack mentions it so "group" does not always mean chairs. Sometimes it means a wire.

Paul Schilder and Laura Bender in New York belong to the same interwar file: analytic talk in rooms that were not yet AGPA. Schilder's name appears in later house histories as a bridge toward Wolf. Bender's children's work is a different door (and a later controversy file this pack will not open as entertainment). Name them as neighbors. Do not stack them as a school.

State-hospital budgets in the Depression made the lecture look like mercy: more patients seen. Mercy that is also throughput is the American group problem in one sentence. Northfield will dress the sentence in khaki. Yalom will dress it in factors. The sentence does not change.

What patients made of the lectures is mostly unrecorded in the papers the doctors signed. Unrecorded is not the same as silent. Attendants' memories, when they exist in oral-history projects, are a later historian's job. This series will not invent a patient's voice to warm a paragraph.
