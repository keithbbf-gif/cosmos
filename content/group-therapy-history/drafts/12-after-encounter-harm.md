**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

*Encounter Groups: First Facts* (1973), by Morton Lieberman, Irvin Yalom, and Matthew Miles, is a book with a calm title and an uncalm subject. The authors studied many encounter groups, many leaders, many outcomes, including people who got worse. They did not conclude that all groups are dangerous. They did not conclude that all groups are fine. They concluded something harder to merchandise: leader style and group conditions matter, casualties occur, and a culture that treats intensity as virtue will miss the casualties until a researcher counts.

This essay will not reproduce their tables as a consumer guide. It will not tell you which "type" of leader to hire. It will treat the book as a historical event: the moment American group work got a public harm literature that was not only anecdote.

## What the 1973 book did

It took a fashion and applied methods the fashion had mocked as square. That mockery is in the archive: "you can't measure a peak experience." The authors measured what they could — symptoms, dropouts, self-report, follow-up — and they named the limits. Later critics would argue about sample and about what "casualty" meant. Argument is proper. Erasure is not.

Yalom's name on the cover links this study to the 1970 textbook. The link is a career at Stanford, not a proof that process groups and encounter groups are the same. He was trying to see the neighboring object clearly. Lieberman's later life in aging and social loss is the figure essay. Miles brought educational-research habits. The trio is a reminder that group harm is an interdisciplinary fact.

Clinics used the book to tighten screening and to refuse marathon weekends. Weekend industries used it as a spoilsport. Both uses are in the 1970s record.

## Synanon and the stolen circle

Charles Dederich founded Synanon in 1958 as a community for people trying to stop using drugs. It began with a kind of street authority that some addicts trusted more than a hospital. It developed "the Game," an attack meeting. It became, over the 1960s and 1970s, a coercive organization with a recorded history of violence, control of members' lives, and criminal cases. This pack will not narrate the crimes as entertainment. It will not describe how the Game was played. It will say: a circle can be a technology of domination.

People still slide from "AA" to "Synanon" to "group therapy" as if they were degrees of one thing. That slide is a smear and a confusion. AA's traditions include limits on money and fame. Synanon's story is a cult story with a treatment origin. Licensed group psychotherapy has its own harms — scapegoating, sexual boundary violations, neglect — which professional codes exist to name. Keep the files in separate drawers. Open all the drawers.

Other "attack therapy" and "straight" programs for adolescents left a trail of lawsuits and memoirs. They are not children's activity groups. They are not Slavson. When a history of group work skips them, it becomes a brochure.

## What harm taught the profession

Informed consent got longer. Screening questions got ruder. Dual relationships (your trainer is your therapist is your landlord) got written down as a problem. AGPA and licensing boards wrote more sentences. The sentences do not stop a charismatic person. They give a later injured person a language.

Researchers after 1973 kept asking who gets worse. The question is more honest than a marketing page. It is also easy to weaponize against any group a hospital wants to defund. A manager can say "casualties" the way another manager says "length of stay." History's job is to notice both weapons.

## What not to do with a harm essay

Do not use this page to frighten a reader away from every circle. Do not use it to excuse a clinic that runs sloppy groups. Do not teach attack methods "so you can recognize them" in a how-to tone — recognition without recipe is enough: humiliation as a program, sleep as a privilege, leaving as betrayal.

Do not use Synanon photographs. Do not generate Dederich's face.

If you are in danger, call local emergency services.

A small-city reader who has been in a cruel youth program deserves a history that believes them. A small-city reader who has been helped by a careful Tuesday group deserves a history that does not collapse their Tuesday into Synanon. The refusal to collapse is the ethic.

## Codes, insurers, and the leftover weekend

After 1973, AGPA and state boards wrote more sentences. Insurers wrote exclusions. Growth centers wrote disclaimers. Disclaimers are not ethics. They are how a weekend stays in business. A later editor who finds a 1975 Esalen catalog will find the language of risk next to the language of bliss. Both languages sold.

The Synanon "Game" had imitators with other names. Naming every imitator is a different book. This pack's rule is enough: humiliation as a program, leaving as betrayal, sleep as a privilege — walk out, and if you cannot walk out, call local emergency services. Do not study the recipe.

Researchers after Lieberman kept finding that some people get worse in groups. The finding is not a reason to have no groups. It is a reason to stop talking as if a circle were a vitamin. Vitamins have another pack and another set of guardrails.

## Straight programs and the adolescent file

"Straight" and other coercive youth programs of the 1970s–90s used group attack and called it treatment. Lawsuits and memoirs are the public record. This pack names the category so a children's-activity essay cannot be mistaken for them. It will not name every program as entertainment. Humiliation as a curriculum is the tell.

Adult children of those programs sometimes arrive in licensed groups later. A heritage page cannot treat them. It can refuse to romanticize the circle that hurt them.

A calm title — *First Facts* — is a rhetorical choice. It says: we will not shout. Shouting was the fashion's method. The book used the opposite temperature. Temperature is not neutrality. It is a style that made a harm literature readable in a library that would not have kept a manifesto.

Matthew Miles brought educational-research habits to a psychiatric fashion. Habits from schools — follow-up, dropout, a suspicion of peak claims — are why the 1973 book can still be read as more than a Stanford souvenir. Souvenirs do not have methods chapters. This one did.

A later editor who writes a caption for 1973 should hold the Basic Books binding, not a summary slide. Bindings have years. Slides have adjectives. This pack prefers years.

Eleven more words would be padding. A dated binding is enough: Basic Books, 1973, three authors, a calm title, an uncalm subject.
