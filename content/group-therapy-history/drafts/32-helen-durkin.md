**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Helen E. Durkin was born in 1901 and died in 1993. `[VERIFY]` those dates against an obituary before a caption. AGPA's history places her group-therapy work from 1937 and puts her on the 1952 Standards and Training Committee when the association took its present name. *The Group in Depth* (1964) is the book that made her more than a committee member: an attempt to think the small group with psychoanalytic seriousness without pretending the group was only a cheap couch.

## Standards as a woman's labor

Committees are easy to skip in a figure calendar. They are where professions decide who may enter. Durkin's name on standards is labor that does not photograph well. This pack includes it because Slavson's association would have been a thinner story without people who wrote the rules and the anniversary essays (1971).

She worked in a field whose first twelve presidents after Slavson, the house history says, were psychiatrists and men. A woman writing "in depth" in 1964 is writing against a podium. Against is not always opposition. It is often the work of making the podium less stupid.

## The book

*The Group in Depth* sits on the shelf next to Wolf, next to Foulkes translations, next to the coming Yalom. It is less famous than 1970. Fame is not the same as use. Teachers who wanted object relations in the circle used Durkin. Teachers who wanted factors used Yalom. Students who wanted a job used whoever their supervisor had read.

This page will not digest her chapters. Digests become techniques. The book is still in libraries.

## What not to do with Durkin

Do not reduce her to "early woman in AGPA." Do not skip the 1964 book for the committee. Do not scrape portraits. `[VERIFY]` 1901–1993.

A small-city supervisor who still assigns a "process" group to an intern is living in a standards world she helped write. The intern's anxiety is not her fault. The existence of a standard — there is a way this is supposed to go — is a 1952–64 inheritance. Standards can protect patients. Standards can also freeze a 1964 book into a church. She wrote the book. She cannot police the church.

## 1937, 1952, 1964, 1971

Four dates are the skeleton: group work from 1937 (AGPA history), standards committee 1952, *The Group in Depth* 1964, anniversary essay 1971. A later editor who finds the obituary fills 1901–1993 or corrects it. Until then the work dates are the honest caption.

*In depth* is a 1964 promise against shallowness — against encounter's heat and against the class's lecture. Promises are books. Books are not the hotel. She wrote for people who would read. Reading is a filter. The filter is not a sin. It is why her name is smaller than Yalom's on a slide and larger in some supervisory rooms.

Women on standards committees do the unphotogenic labor of saying who may enter. Entering is a group problem. She would have recognized the recursion.

A 1964 book titled *in depth* is arguing with a decade that was about to go wide and hot. She wrote depth while encounter wrote surface heat. Both used chairs. The argument is why AGPA needed an umbrella later. She helped write the standards that tried to keep depth from being only a club. Clubs still formed. Standards still matter.

## IUP 1964 and a 1948 academy paper

International Universities Press in 1964 was a psychoanalytic house, not a human-potential catalog. Choosing that publisher is a politics: she wanted the small group on the same shelf as object-relations books, not next to a weekend flyer. *The Group in Depth* is the wager that a circle can bear psychoanalytic seriousness without becoming a cheap couch or a hot marathon. She took the wager in print. AGPA's later umbrella, which Scheidlinger dated to 1991, tried to hold the wager and the weekend in one hotel. Hotels blur wagers. This file unblurs hers.

She had already been writing in public. A 1948 paper in the *Annals of the New York Academy of Sciences* — "The Theory and Practice of Group Psychotherapy" — sits twenty-two years before Yalom's textbook of almost the same name. Title collision is not theft. It is what a field does when it is still arguing about whether it has a theory. Cite the year. Do not let 1970 erase 1948.

The 1971 AGPA anniversary essay, written with colleagues, is memory work. Memory work is how a profession that listed men first still has her sentences in the minutes. If 1901–1993 fails an obituary, keep 1937, 1948, 1952, 1964, 1971. Those years are already in AGPA's own telling and on title pages. Work dates are the honest caption until a death notice is in hand.

A standards committee is a door. A 1964 book is a mind. An anniversary essay is a memory. Hotel histories keep the door and lose the mind. This pack keeps all three so a woman who wrote minutes is not only a name on a list.
