**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Samuel Richard Slavson did not start with a couch. He started with children who needed something to *do*. At the Jewish Board of Guardians in New York, in the 1930s, he and colleagues ran activity groups: wood, clay, games, a room that was not a classroom and not a ward. *An Introduction to Group Therapy* (1943) is the book that took that room into print. The same years he was chalking a luncheon invitation on a blackboard at the American Orthopsychiatric Association (February 1943) and helping to found what was first called the American Group Therapy Association.

This essay is about the children's room as a historical object. It is not a guide to play therapy. It will not describe how to set out materials. Children's faces do not belong on the featured image.

## Why activity, not talk

A child who has been sent to an agency is already a problem on someone's form. Asking that child to sit in a circle and "share" can be a second form. Slavson argued — in the language of his decade, with the Freudian furniture of his decade — that doing something side by side let conflict show itself without a command performance. The therapist was present, not lecturing. The group was small. Selection was a fuss. He wrote as if selection were half the method.

Later child clinicians would say: of course. Later critics would say: the agency decided which children were treatable, and "activity" could be a way to keep poor children busy while richer children got analysis. Both comments fit the archive. JBG was a Jewish social-service world in a city of immigrants and of American anti-Semitism. The groups were a minority institution's tool. When AGPA later courted the American Psychiatric Association, Slavson's wish for psychiatric prestige (so the later house histories say) sat next to that origin.

## From children to a profession of adults

AGPA's early constitution restricted membership to psychiatrists, psychologists, and psychiatric social workers with specified supervised hours. The children's method did not determine the adult profession. The profession determined who counted as a colleague. Helen Durkin, Saul Scheidlinger, and others would spend decades writing the child and adolescent group back into an association that had become a hotel-ballroom of adult therapists.

Scheidlinger's career is the figure essay for that insistence. This era essay only needs the fork: activity group as a 1930s–40s New York invention, and "group therapy" as a postwar adult product that sometimes forgot the woodshop.

Anna Freud's war nurseries and the Hampstead work are a different children's story, told in the sister pack's Anna Freud essay. Do not merge London nurseries and JBG activity rooms. They shared a war and a Freud, not a floor plan.

## Race, diagnosis, and the quiet child

Published cases from this world are not ours to re-narrate. What can be said: agencies sorted children by behavior, by family, by who would come after school. Black children in New York had other agencies and other exclusions. A history of "the" children's group that stays at JBG is a history of one door.

Diagnosis moved from "behavior problem" and "neurotic child" toward later school languages — ADHD, autism, trauma — that Slavson did not have. Retrofitting those words onto a 1943 book is a student error. The book is a 1943 book.

Coercive "holding" therapies and attachment-cult groups of later decades sometimes stole the phrase "children's group." They are not Slavson's activity room. Name the theft if you must mention them; do not teach them. This pack refuses conversion and coercion as content.

## Schools, speech rooms, and a boundary

WOW Therapies is a speech-language practice. School SLPs already know a small-group articulation session. That session is not Slavson's activity group and not a process group. It is instruction. This series will not raid SLP small-group work for a pedigree, and it will not tell parents that "group therapy" in a counseling sense is what happens at the speech table. Different licenses. Different records. Different consent.

The temptation to cross-link is SEO. Resist it.

## What not to do with the woodshop

Do not put tools in a room and call it Slavson. Do not photograph children. Do not use a 1943 selection rule on a 2026 school. Do not imply that quiet children need a group or that loud children need a group. Those are clinical decisions, not heritage conclusions.

Do notice that American group psychotherapy's professional society grew out of a children's agency lunch as much as out of a war hospital. The origin is less glamorous than Northfield and more accurate to how American jobs get associations: a blackboard, a hotel, a constitution, a fight over who may enter.

Slavson's later *Analytic Group Psychotherapy* (1950) moved the chair toward talk and toward adults. The move is a career. It is also a market. Adults pay. Children are brought. The activity room remains the more interesting object: a refusal, for a while, to make children into little analysands in a circle of chairs.

## Supervision, clay, and the after-school clock

Activity groups ran on an after-school clock. Working parents, agency hours, the child who missed the bus: those are not in the 1943 book's glamour. They are why selection was a fuss. A child who cannot come weekly is not "resistant." They are a transit problem. Later manuals that treat attendance as a personality trait are repeating a luxury.

Clay and wood produce objects. Objects go home. Objects can be a report to a parent. A process group's words dissolve. An activity group's boat sits on a refrigerator. The refrigerator is a public. Publicness is an ethics problem Slavson did not write as a 2026 IRB. He wrote as a 1940s agency man.

School-based groups after the Elementary and Secondary Education Act and after special-education law are a later American object. Counselors, social workers, and SLPs all say "group" on a schedule. This pack has already drawn the SLP line. Draw it again: an articulation table is instruction. A counseling group in a school is a different license and a different record. A Slavson activity group is a third, historical object. Three nouns. One white board in a tired school will still say "group." The white board is not a historian.

## Boys, girls, and the agency door

Published activity-group writing often assumed a boy with "behavior." Girls' groups existed and were written less. The less is a source problem. Later feminist child clinicians had to invent a literature the 1943 book did not finish.

The Jewish Board door was one door. Catholic Charities, public child-guidance clinics, and segregated agencies ran other rooms. A calendar that only cites JBG is a New York Jewish professional history — which this essay is — not a national children's history. Say the limit.
