**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Eric Berne was born in 1910 and died in 1970. He trained as a psychiatrist, broke with ordinary psychoanalytic manners, and gave the 1960s a vocabulary — Parent, Adult, Child, games — that escaped the clinic and sat on paperback racks. *Transactional Analysis in Psychotherapy* (1961) is the professional book. *Games People Play* (1964) is the public one. Groups were a natural home for a method that already thought in transactions between people.

This essay will not teach a game. It will not diagram a triangle. The diagrams are copyrighted and are not a website's to assign as homework.

## A seminar that was a group

TA groups often looked like classes: a blackboard, a term, a moment of recognition. Recognition is why the paperback sold. People felt seen by a cartoon of their marriage. Feeling seen is not the same as a treatment contract. Berne wrote as a man who liked clarity more than mystery. Clarity can be a mercy. Clarity can also be a way to win.

He died the year Yalom's textbook appeared. The two 1970 objects — a dead popularizer, a living professor's craft book — mark a fork. One fork went into ITAA institutes and a worldwide TA culture. The other went into residencies. They still glance at each other with suspicion.

Canada-born, California-working: the life is in the biographies. Do not invent the life. The books are enough.

## Popularization's cost

Once a method is a bestseller, untrained people run it. Berne is not guilty of every living-room diagnosis. He is the author of a book that made living-room diagnosis tempting. History can hold both.

AGPA's umbrella years had to decide whether TA was group psychotherapy. Umbrellas leak. TA had its own association. Parallel professions again.

## What not to do with Berne

Do not label your relatives. Do not print "games" as a clinic worksheet. Do not scrape ITAA photos. *Games People Play* is still in copyright.

## 1957–58, Grove, and a 1964 association

Before the paperback, there was a paper. "Transactional Analysis: A New and Effective Method of Group Therapy" was given at the 1957 Western Regional Meeting of the American Group Psychotherapy Association in Los Angeles and printed in the *American Journal of Psychotherapy* in October 1958. The title already places the method in a *group*. Two 1957 papers on ego states and intuition sit next to it. This page will not diagram those states. It will date the moment TA entered an AGPA room as a group method.

Tuesday-night San Francisco Social Psychiatry Seminars, in the ITAA's own institutional telling, began in 1957. Earlier Carmel evenings appear in the UCSF finding aid for his papers. The seminars incorporated and began a *Transactional Analysis Bulletin*. On 20 October 1964, at the seminars' annual business meeting, the International Transactional Analysis Association was founded. Parallel profession: AGPA did not absorb it. Umbrellas later glanced at it. Glancing is not merging.

Grove Press in the 1960s liked to shock a mainstream. Putting a psychiatry book there is a reception choice. *Transactional Analysis in Psychotherapy* (1961) is the professional book. *Games People Play* (1964) is the escape. Paperback racks put "games" into households that would never see a psychiatrist. Household use is cultural history. It is also how a clinical noun becomes an insult at a dinner table. Insults are not in the 1961 book. They are in the afterlife of 1964.

He died in 1970, the year Yalom's textbook appeared. Two 1970 objects, two professions. This site will not reprint Grove diagrams, will not list games, and will not treat a heritage page as TA training. If a staging page grows a triangle, delete the triangle. Copyright, dinner-table harm, and this series's ban on how-to are three reasons.

## Montreal, the army, a name change

He was born Eric Lennard Bernstein in Montreal on 10 May 1910, took an M.D. at McGill in 1935, and later worked under the shorter American name. The UCSF finding aid for his papers is the public trail for that change; this page will not invent a motive. Army psychiatry in the Second World War is in the same trail. After the war he studied at the San Francisco Psychoanalytic Institute and, around 1950, left that training. Leaving is why TA exists as a break, not as a footnote inside classical analysis.

Carmel evenings and San Francisco Tuesdays are two rooms. Local stories become world associations. World associations still fight about who owns a diagram. Diagrams are property. Grove's copyright is not a suggestion. A 1964 paperback that teaches a nation to diagnose its dinner table will be used as a weapon at that table. He wrote for clarity. Clarity at a dinner table is not a clinic. The 1961 book is for historians and for people in actual TA training. Training is not a blog.
