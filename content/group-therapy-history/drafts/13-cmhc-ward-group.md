**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 1963 the United States enacted the Community Mental Health Centers Act. The statute is a political object: Kennedy, the aftertaste of *Action for Mental Health*, a promise that care would move toward the community. What it produced, among other things, was a need for groups. A center could not give every arriving person a fifty-minute individual hour. It could put eight people in a room at 10 a.m. and 2 p.m. and 6 p.m. The room became the system.

This essay is not a policy brief and not a defense of any administration. It is a history of a furniture decision.

## Outpatient rooms with fluorescent lights

CMHCs hired people with various licenses and some with almost none, depending on the state and the year. They ran aftercare groups, medication groups, "life skills" groups, family nights. Some were careful. Some were warehouses with coffee. The range is the story. A single success clinic in a journal and a single horror story in a newspaper are both true somewhere.

The group here was often psychoeducation before the word was fashionable: how to live with a diagnosis, how to use a bus, how to talk to a landlord. That is not Yalom's interpersonal learning. It is Pratt's class in a new building. Patients who wanted process were told they were "too high functioning" or "not appropriate." Patients who wanted a bus route were put in a process group because that was the Tuesday slot. Mismatch is an institutional emotion.

Rural centers — including in states like Arkansas — inherited the Act's ambition and the Act's funding cliffs. A history written from Manhattan and Palo Alto will miss the center that is also the county's only sliding-scale door. This pack will not invent a Delta CMHC case. It will say the statute was national and the rooms were local.

## The ward after the census collapsed

Deinstitutionalization emptied some state hospitals and failed to build the promised community. The people who still needed a bed arrived at general-hospital psych units with shorter and shorter stays. A group on such a unit is not a year-long circle. It is a snapshot: whoever is here, today, including the person admitted at 3 a.m. Yalom's *Inpatient Group Psychotherapy* (1983) is a book-length attempt to take that snapshot seriously instead of pretending the unit is an outpatient clinic with locks.

Administrators liked groups because they are visible and cheap per head. Nurses liked them when they calmed a milieu. Patients liked them when they were the only hour anyone made eye contact. Patients hated them when they were a mandatory meeting after a bad night. All of that can be in one week.

Bion's basic assumptions show up on units without anyone reading Bion: the group that wants a savior, the group that wants a fight, the group that pairs two people and goes to sleep. Naming that is not a treatment. It is a way for staff not to take the whole weather personally. Taking it personally is how staff burn.

## Privacy, seclusion, and the public circle

A ward group happens in a place where seclusion and restraint also happen. The contrast is not a metaphor. People are asked to speak in a circle in a building that can lock them down. Consent is therefore a special problem. This series will not write a unit protocol. It will say the problem is old: Marsh wanted better hospital citizens; Northfield wanted soldiers back; the CMHC unit wants a discharge. The circle serves the discharge unless someone writes a different purpose on the schedule and means it.

Record-keeping turned the group into a note: "Pt attended. Participated." The note is a legal object. It is also a joke about what the hour was. Later electronic records made the joke faster.

## What not to do with the fluorescent room

Do not romanticize the CMHC as "community." Community is a word that can mean neighbors and can mean a parking lot. Do not romanticize the state hospital as a lost home. Homes do not seclude you. Do not use this history to argue for or against a current bond issue. Do not put a booking CTA under a ward story.

Do not photograph units. Do not use restraint images.

A speech-language pathologist who does a swallow group on a medical floor is in a different license and a different essay. Do not merge dysphagia groups into psychiatric ward groups because both say "group" on a white board.

The 1963 Act's afterlife includes Reagan-era block grants, managed care (next-but-one essay), and the assertive-community-treatment teams that sometimes used groups and sometimes used vans. The van is not a circle. It is another solution to the same scarcity.

## Aftercare, vans, and the last state bed

Aftercare groups — the Tuesday circle for people discharged last month — were the CMHC's promise in furniture. Some lasted years. Some collapsed when the grant ended. A group that ends because a grant ends is an abandonment with a fiscal name. Patients experience it as the same old leaving.

ACT teams (assertive community treatment) and clubhouses (Fountain House's descendants) are neighboring objects: not quite groups, not quite hospitals. Clubhouses have a work-ordered day. This pack will not annex them. It will say they exist because the 1963 promise needed more than a fluorescent hour.

The last state-hospital beds in many counties became forensic or long-stay islands. A group on those islands is closer to Marsh than to Yalom. The census is the theory. Anyone who writes otherwise is writing a brochure.

Rural Arkansas and counties like it met the Act as a traveling clinic, a borrowed church, a worker who was also the crisis phone. This series will not invent that worker. It will say the statute did not arrive as a Cambridge seminar.

## Day hospitals and the in-between

Partial hospital and day-treatment programs filled the gap between the 1963 outpatient dream and the locked unit. Their groups ran all afternoon: goals, crafts, a community meeting. Crafts are Coyle's cousin. Community meetings are Jones's cousin. The cousins sit under fluorescent lights and a discharge plan. This pack will not call that sitting a therapeutic community unless the building earned the word.

Kennedy's signature on P.L. 88-164 did not staff a rural county. Staffing arrived as a person who was also the evening crisis phone. That person ran groups because the schedule said group. The schedule is the theory. Anyone who writes a prettier theory without the phone is writing from a city with a backup.

A borrowed church in a rural county is a 1963 descendant with folding chairs. Folding chairs are not a failure of the Act. They are the Act arriving without a building. This pack will not mock the chairs. It will not call them a therapeutic community either.
