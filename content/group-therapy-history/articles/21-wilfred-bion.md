**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In November 1917, at Cambrai, a twenty-year-old officer in the British Tank Corps took a machine into battle and lived. Wilfred Ruprecht Bion was born on 8 September 1897 in Muttra (Mathura), then in British India, sent to England as a schoolboy, and given a war before he was given a profession. He received the Distinguished Service Order and the French Légion d'honneur. The later psychoanalytic fame can make the uniform look like a preface. It was a life. He wrote about it, decades afterward, in a memoir published after his death as *The Long Weekend*. Panic, in his telling, was not a metaphor. It was something that happened to a group of men in a field.

This essay is part of a WOW Therapies series on the history of group therapy. It is a life. The Northfield experiments have their own essay. The basic-assumptions theory has another. Neither is a parlor kit for labeling your committee "fight-flight" and calling the label treatment. Bion did not prove that groups have organs. He offered a language. Then he left groups for a more private psychoanalysis.

## From a tank to a ward

After the war he read history at Queen's College, Oxford, then medicine, qualifying from University College Hospital. He joined the Tavistock Clinic in the 1930s to train in psychotherapy. He was analyzed by John Rickman and, later, by Melanie Klein. The Kleinian years would pull him toward psychosis, thinking, and a prose that got more difficult as it got more famous. The group papers are, in that long career, an earlier book.

The Second World War put him in uniform again, this time as a psychiatrist. With the War Office Selection Boards he watched candidates in working groups and treated the tension as information about leadership. At Hollymoor Hospital in Northfield, with Rickman, he ran what historians call the first Northfield experiment: a ward treated as a group whose task was to study its own tensions. They published in *The Lancet* in 1943, "Intra-Group Tensions in Therapy." The army, which had wanted men repaired for duty, was not uniformly grateful. The experiment lasted on the order of six weeks. Accounts differ on whether it was closed for being too disturbing, too insubordinate, or simply too early. Tom Harrison's later book is the place to watch the disagreements rather than a sentence that says "the army shut it down."

S. H. Foulkes arrived as the second experiment got under way. Syllabi yoke them. The lives do not. Foulkes built a civilian profession: conductor, matrix, a society. Bion wrote as if a group would rather not do its job, and as if the excuses were the interesting part.

## *Experiences in Groups*, and the three names

The essays that became *Experiences in Groups and Other Papers* appeared in *Human Relations* from 1948 and as a Tavistock book in 1961. Training programs still assign the volume as if it were a personality. The hospital, the war, and the refusal to cheer are easier to skip.

The useful distinction, historically, is between a work group — people trying to do a job — and the states he named as basic assumptions: **dependency** (wait for a leader who will save), **fight-flight** (an enemy will organize us), **pairing** (two people, or a hope, will produce a future that excuses the present). He wrote as if these were shared, unconscious, and contagious. He did not write as if they were a diagnostic toy, a slide, or a way to win an argument in a staff meeting.

Later consultants carried the language into businesses, churches, and universities. A. K. Rice and the Tavistock-Leicester conferences treated organizations as systems that could be studied in public. That was serious work. A staff-meeting slide that pins "fight-flight" on a team is not. It is a quote with a laser pointer.

He did not prove the metaphysics. He offered names that some rooms recognized. Other rooms recognized something else: scapegoating, hope, a good hour. A heritage series can hold both recognitions. It cannot turn the language into a diagnosis of the reader.

## What he left, and what he left behind

An anecdotal tradition says that Melanie Klein, toward the end of his analysis, told him to stop bothering about groups and to concentrate on psychoanalysis proper. Whether or not the anecdote is neat, the career turned. From the 1950s he wrote on thinking, psychotic mechanisms, container and contained, the "bizarre object." *Learning from Experience* (1962) and later books belong to that interior. He served as president of the British Psychoanalytical Society in the mid-1960s. In 1968 he moved to Los Angeles. He died in Oxford on 8 November 1979.

*Experiences in Groups* remained, to his surprise, the book that sold. Students still prefer the younger man's volume. The later Bion did not write a second group textbook. He did not owe anyone a sequel. The early papers never stopped being read, which is a different fate from being a group therapist who stayed in the room.

He also wrote, or left, autobiographies: *The Long Weekend* (1982) and *All My Sins Remembered* (1985). They are sources for the tank and the tone. They are not manuals.

## What this life is not

It is not a method for running a leaderless group in a county clinic. Northfield was a military hospital. The official job was still the war. Tavistock study groups were not a billing code. A contemporary process group that borrows a sentence from 1961 without the hospital, the war, or the later caution is doing atmosphere.

It is not a personality test. Dependency, fight-flight, and pairing are historical terms from a set of papers. They are not a way to sort patients, coworkers, or relatives. If a line in this essay could be used that way, it has failed.

## Why a clinic still reads this

Because the tank officer never quite left the room. A man who had watched panic in a crew was unlikely to believe that a circle is automatically kind. The basic assumptions are names for moods that eat a task. They are not a way to sort the people in the waiting room.

Because he left groups. The profession sometimes talks as if walking away were a betrayal. Bion's later work is a reminder that group theory can be a chapter. The chapter can remain useful without pretending it was the whole life.
