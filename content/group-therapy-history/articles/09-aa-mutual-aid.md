**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In May 1935 a New York stockbroker named William Griffith Wilson sat in the lobby of the Mayflower Hotel in Akron, Ohio, after a business deal had gone sour. The bar was available. A church directory was available too. He telephoned a clergyman, the Rev. Walter Tunks, and was passed along to Henrietta Seiberling, who thought an Akron surgeon, Robert Holbrook Smith, might need to talk to another alcoholic. The two men met. On June 10, 1935 — the date the fellowship later treated as Dr. Bob's last drink — a story that would be told in church basements for the rest of the century acquired a calendar page.

This essay is part of a WOW Therapies series on the history of group therapy. It is not a speech-pathology history. It is not a manual for a meeting, and it does not reprint anyone's Steps as homework. Alcoholics Anonymous is complementary to the individual psychotherapy-history pack in the same way a lodge is complementary to a consulting room: related as a human form, stubbornly not the same job.

## Akron, New York, a book

Wilson had already been detoxed more than once at Towns Hospital in New York, where Dr. William D. Silkworth described alcoholism as a malady of body and mind — an "allergy" plus an obsession, in the language of that clinic. A friend, Ebby Thacher, had carried a religious-practical message from the Oxford Group, Frank Buchman's evangelical fellowship of confession and "life-changing." Wilson's own conversion experience in late 1934 is a period document, not a template this site will teach. What mattered for group history is simpler. He stayed sober, for a stretch, by talking to other drunks. In Akron he found one who was a physician. They worked with a patient at Akron City Hospital. Those three, AA's own later narrative says, were the nucleus of the first group, though the name Alcoholics Anonymous was not yet in use.

A second cluster formed in New York that fall. Cleveland appeared in 1939. Four years of work produced, by AA's count, about a hundred sober alcoholics. Early in 1939 the fellowship published *Alcoholics Anonymous*, the fat first edition later nicknamed the Big Book, through Works Publishing. Wilson drafted. Early members argued over the pages. The book mixed a recovery program, case histories, and a physician's preface. It is a historical publication. It is not a worksheet on this site.

Jack Alexander's *Saturday Evening Post* article of March 1, 1941, did what a medical journal could not: it made the rooms famous to people who did not know a surgeon in Akron. Membership jumped. The later Twelve Traditions — drafted in the mid-1940s in the *A.A. Grapevine* and adopted at Cleveland in 1950 — were a constitution for a movement that had already outrun its founders. One of those traditions insisted that the fellowship remain nonprofessional. Another kept it from endorsing outside enterprises. The point, historically, was survival without a clergy, a board of psychiatrists, or a product line.

## What Kurtz named

Ernest Kurtz's *Not-God: A History of Alcoholics Anonymous* (Hazelden, 1979) is the book a historian can put on the table without using a meeting as a source. Kurtz, who wrote as a scholar of the fellowship rather than as its publicist, argued that AA's theology of limitation — the alcoholic is not God, not self-sufficient, not the center — was the intellectual content under the stories. The title is a thesis. It is not a slogan to assign.

Kurtz also did what later websites often refuse to do: he kept AA inside American religious and temperance history instead of pretending it sprang from a vacuum in 1935. The Oxford Group is in the file. So is the older American habit of testimony. So is the medical claim, borrowed and simplified, that alcoholism is a disease rather than a failure of manners. A heritage series can say all of that and still refuse to collapse the rooms into "group therapy with coffee."

William L. White's recovery-history essays, dated pieces rather than a protocol, sit in the same neighborhood: mutual aid as a lineage with its own archives, failures, and splits (Narcotics Anonymous and other fellowships would later copy the form). White is a historian of recovery. He is not a reason to print a meeting list here.

## Form, without the collapse

A fair comparison of forms is allowed. A lazy identity is not.

Both a closed AA meeting and a therapy group sit people in a ring, or in rows that pretend to be a ring. Both use narrative: I drank; I was afraid; I did this next. Both have a chairperson or a leader who is not supposed to be the only person in the room who matters. Both can humiliate. Both can keep a person alive through a week that would have been lethal in a kitchen alone. Pratt's tuberculosis class, the settlement club, the ward meeting, and the later process group are cousins in that modest sense. The first essay in this series already said the circle is older than a CPT code.

The differences are not decorative. AA has no licensed clinician as the authority of the hour. It has no chart that another professional will read. It does not bill an insurer for a unit of care. It does not, in its own Traditions, take a professional fee for the talk. Membership is self-claimed: the person who says they have a drinking problem is, for the fellowship's purposes, qualified. A therapy group, by contrast, is a clinical service. Someone holds a license. Someone writes a note. Someone decides who may enter. Diagnosis language, however gently used, is in the building. The fellowship spent decades refusing that building's name.

Clinics later borrowed anyway. From the 1950s onward, hospital alcoholism wards and, later, Minnesota-model programs and "twelve-step facilitation" as a researched treatment (Project MATCH in the 1990s is the famous trial) used AA language inside professional walls. That borrowing is part of the history of treatment. It is not proof that AA consented to be therapy. The rooms often treated the compliment as a threat: professionals would capture the message, charge for it, and dilute the amateur stubbornness that had been the point.

A second confusion runs the other way. Group therapists, anxious for ancestors, have sometimes listed AA on a slide titled "early group therapy." The slide is flattery. Kurtz's book is the correction. Historians may compare. The brand must not collapse.

## What the rooms were not

They were not Pratt's class. Consumptives in Boston were poor and contagious and under a physician's regimen. Early AA members were often middle-class white men, and the first edition's stories show it. Women, people of color, and people whose drug was not alcohol had to fight their way into a fellowship that said it was for anyone with the desire to stop drinking and then, in practice, looked like Akron and Manhattan. Later editions and later fellowships are the record of that fight. A heritage series that prints only the Mayflower lobby has chosen a postcard.

They were not a T-group. Bethel wanted feedback as skill. AA wanted sobriety as a daily limit. They were not an encounter weekend. The heat, when it came, was testimony and interruption, not a paid stripping of the self. They were not Foulkes's matrix. There was no conductor. There was a format, a coffee pot, and a literature that members treated as scripture, handbook, or both.

They also were not harmless. Any room that lives on story can punish a story that does not fit. Apostasy, sponsorship as domination, and the shaming of people who use medication for depression or for opioid dependence are part of the later record. Saying so is not an attack on sobriety. It is what "no fabricated compliance" requires of a history post.

## Why a clinic still reads this

Because patients still arrive having been saved by a fellowship, harmed by one, or confused about whether the group on Thursday night is "the same as AA." A small-city practice that answers its own phone has to know the difference without sneering at either room. The consulting-hour pack in this brand's other series will keep Freud, Rogers, and Beck where they belong. This essay keeps the circle. AA is the American circle that most loudly said: we are not you.

Because mutual aid is a technology of the unlicensed. Burial clubs, immigrant lodges, and temperance meetings sat in this family before 1935. The clinic did not invent sitting with others. It professionalized a subset of the sitting and then spent a century arguing with the subset that refused the diploma.

Because the temptation to steal a method is perennial. A therapist who assigns the Steps as homework has turned a fellowship's literature into a worksheet. This series will not do that. History may describe that a book published in 1939 contained a numbered program. History may not teach the reader to run it.

The next essays leave the church basement and enter hospitals that wanted the whole institution to be the group — Maxwell Jones, Thomas Main, and the American community-mental-health law that tried to empty the warehouse.
