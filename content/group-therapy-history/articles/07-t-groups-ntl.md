**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In the summer of 1946, in New Britain, Connecticut, Kurt Lewin helped staff a workshop the Connecticut Interracial Commission had asked for: train local people to work on racial and religious discrimination and on fair employment. The staff met at night to talk about what they had seen in the small groups. Participants asked to sit in. They contradicted the observers. The argument about the day became, in the later legend, more educational than the day.

Lewin died in February 1947. He did not see the first summer of the National Training Laboratory for Group Development at Gould Academy in Bethel, Maine. Leland Bradford, Ronald Lippitt, and Kenneth Benne did. The T-group — training group — became an American export: a circle whose curriculum was its own behavior.

This essay is part of a WOW Therapies series on the history of group therapy. The T-group was often not therapy. It was training, research, and, later, a corporate fashion. The three leaked.

## Lewin's last laboratory

Lewin was born in 1890 in Mogilno, then Prussia, made his name in Berlin, and left Germany after 1933. Iowa and then MIT's Research Center for Group Dynamics were the American addresses. He had already published on groups, on "life space," on the way a social field could be changed. Action research was a phrase he used as if research and change were allowed to share a room. New Britain was action research with community leaders in it.

The here-and-now insight — that the group's own talk tonight is data — did not require a couch. It required an observer, a blackboard, and a permission structure in which a participant could disagree with a social scientist and not be sent home. That permission was the invention. Later trainers would ritualize it. Rituals get lazy.

Bethel was chosen, the institutional memory says, as a "cultural island": far enough from the office that a person might speak. The Office of Naval Research and the National Education Association helped pay. The first summers mixed educators, managers, and researchers. Paul Sheats, J. R. P. French, and others appear in the early staff lists. The method spread faster than anyone's ethics code.

## Therapy's neighbor

Clinicians noticed. A T-group looked like a therapy group that had dropped the symptom list and kept the heat. Some psychologists and psychiatrists trained at Bethel and then imported the heat into clinics. Some trainers insisted they were not doing therapy and then watched people cry as if the disclaimer were a rug.

Carl Rogers's later encounter work is a cousin, not a child. The humanistic California of the 1960s would claim spontaneity and regard. NTL had claimed feedback and skill. Both used a circle. Both would be blamed, fairly and unfairly, when a participant left worse than they came.

The research problem was obvious by the late 1960s. If a method is powerful enough to change a person in a week, it is powerful enough to harm. Lieberman, Yalom, and Miles's *Encounter Groups: First Facts* (1973) is the next essay's book. It is already visible from Bethel if you refuse the brochure.

## Industry, and a word that thinned

By the 1960s the T-group was a management tool. Companies sent people to learn "sensitivity." Some learned to listen. Some learned a new way to bully with feelings. The phrase *team building* is a later, softer descendant. A heritage series can say the descent without blessing the offsite.

NTL still exists, under a longer name. Institutional histories are house documents. Use them for dates. Use Bradford, Gibb, and Benne's 1964 collection for the mid-century theory. Use Lewin's own pages for the mind that did not live to see the brand.

## Why a clinic still reads this

Because "here-and-now" did not come from a therapy manual. It came from a race-relations workshop and a night argument about an observer's notes. A therapy group that uses the phrase as if it were timeless is borrowing a 1946 permission and forgetting the commission that paid for it.

Because the border between training and treatment is a historical border, not a natural one. Pratt's class was treatment that looked like school. The T-group was school that looked like treatment. Patients and trainees have been confused ever since. A clinic that names which room it is running is doing the minimum Lewin would have recognized as a task.

## What 1946 was for, besides the legend

The Connecticut Interracial Commission did not hire MIT to grow personalities. It hired help for a political job: fair employment, less street-level hatred, local people who could run a meeting without making it worse. Role-play was already on the staff's table. Observers were already taking notes. Lewin's contribution, if the later stories are even half right, was to treat the night argument as part of the data rather than as a leak.

That is a research ethic as much as a training trick. It says the subjects of a race-relations workshop are allowed to disagree with the scientists about what happened. In 1946, in an American city, that permission was not a small thing. Later T-groups that used "feedback" as a weapon against the least powerful person in the room had forgotten the commission. Forgetting is how a method travels.

Bethel's first summers, after Lewin's death, mixed the Office of Naval Research's interest in groups with the NEA's interest in adult education. The mix produced a laboratory that felt like a campus and billed like a seminar. Bradford, Benne, and Lippitt's 1964 collection is the mid-century theory book: laboratory method, change-agent language, a faith that a cultural island can teach what the office cannot. The faith is dated. The date is useful. Every later "offsite" that promises honesty in a hotel ballroom is in this family, usually without the research staff.

Kurt Back, Morton Deutsch, and other social psychologists passed through the early years. Their later careers went toward experiments that did not look like T-groups. The split between laboratory social psychology and laboratory training is part of why a psychology department and an HR department can use the word *group* and not mean the same object. This essay keeps the training object. The experimental object has its own histories.

A clinic in Arkansas does not need Bethel. It needs to know that "here-and-now" has a 1946 address, a race-relations budget, and a 1973 book that counted what happened when the address became a fashion.

Lewin's own pages, gathered after his death in *Resolving Social Conflicts* (1948), are a better door than a consultant's origin story. He wrote about minority problems, about Germany, about the field in which a person actually moves. The T-group was one experiment in that field. It was not the field. A heritage series that treats Bethel as the whole of "group dynamics" has confused a summer laboratory with a science.
