**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In February 1947 Kurt Lewin died of a heart attack in Newtonville, Massachusetts. He was fifty-six. He was director of the Research Center for Group Dynamics at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, and a consultant to the American Jewish Congress's Commission on Community Interrelations. He would not see the first summer of the National Training Laboratory for Group Development at Gould Academy in Bethel, Maine. Leland Bradford, Ronald Lippitt, and Kenneth Benne would. The T-group — training group — became an American export: a circle whose curriculum was its own behavior. Lewin's name went on the export. His body was already in Boston.

This essay is part of a WOW Therapies series on the history of group therapy. It is a life of a social psychologist. The T-group laboratory has its own essay. Lewin was not a group psychotherapist. He was a researcher of fields, of prejudice, of what a small group does when someone is allowed to disagree with the observer's notes. The three leaked into clinics anyway.

## Berlin, a war, a flight

He was born on 9 September 1890 in Mogilno, then Prussia, now in Poland, one of four children in a middle-class Jewish family. Berlin was the city of the Gymnasium and then of the university. He studied at Freiburg, Munich, and Berlin, took a doctorate from Berlin in 1914, served in the German army in the First World War, and went back to the Psychological Institute as a Gestalt-trained experimentalist who wanted theory to touch a social world. Field theory was his habit of mind: behavior as a function of person and environment, a life space that could be drawn. The formula is in the textbooks. The life is the flight.

He came to the United States as the German situation closed. A 1932 visit became, after 1933, exile. Cornell's School of Home Economics gave him two years. In 1935 he went to the Iowa Child Welfare Research Station, which remained his base into the mid-1940s. Iowa is where many American psychologists first met him: a man who ran experiments on groups of children with Lippitt and Ralph White, comparing climates they labeled authoritarian, democratic, and laissez-faire. The labels are period science. They are also a moral argument made in a decade when European authoritarianism was not a metaphor.

During the Second World War he took applied problems: morale, food habits when meat was scarce, psychological warfare. He wanted a center that would study group dynamics as a science and as a way of changing ugly social facts. In 1944–45 MIT took the bet. The Research Center for Group Dynamics opened in Cambridge. Dorwin Cartwright, Leon Festinger, J. R. P. French, Lippitt, Alvin Zander — the staff list is a later hall of fame. After Lewin died, funding trouble at MIT and the pull of Michigan's social-science resources moved the Center to Ann Arbor in 1948.

He was also building, with the American Jewish Congress, the Commission on Community Interrelations, an attempt to put action research onto prejudice: study a problem in order to change it, and let the change revise the study. He had written that the fight of the Jews was part of the fight of all minorities for equality of rights. The sentence is political. It is also the reason New Britain happened.

## New Britain, 1946

The Connecticut Interracial Commission asked for a workshop: train local people to work on racial and religious discrimination and on fair employment. In the summer of 1946, at a teachers' college in New Britain, Lewin, Lippitt, Bradford, and Benne staffed a two-week program. About forty community leaders. Small groups. Research woven in, as Lewin's method required.

The staff met at night to talk about what they had seen. Participants asked to sit in. They contradicted the observers. In the later legend, that night argument taught more than the daytime curriculum. Lewin had borrowed "feedback" from electrical engineering. What was new was the permission: a community leader could disagree with a social scientist and remain in the room. Later laboratories turned the permission into a ritual. Rituals thin out.

The T-group essay in this series follows the insight to Bethel. This life stops at the death. He and his associates obtained interest from the Office of Naval Research and the National Education Association. Bethel was chosen, the institutional memory says, as a "cultural island": far enough from the office that a person might speak. Lewin did not attend. Bradford, Lippitt, and Benne founded the laboratory that claimed his heritage.

A formula about unfreezing, moving, and refreezing traveled into management books. So did "action research." So did a hope that a circle could change a company. The later encounter boom, and Lieberman, Yalom, and Miles's *Encounter Groups: First Facts* (1973), are descendants with casualties. Lewin is not innocent of the hope, even when his own rooms were about race relations and not about a new self in a long weekend. He made the group's own talk tonight into data.

## What this life is not

It is not a team-building exercise. The 1946 workshop was paid for by a state interracial commission. Fair employment was the civic task. A corporate offsite that borrows "here-and-now" as a mood has forgotten the commission.

It is not group psychotherapy, though clinicians noticed. A T-group looked like a therapy group that had dropped the symptom list and kept the heat. Some psychologists trained at Bethel and imported the heat into clinics. Some trainers insisted they were not doing therapy and then watched people cry as if the disclaimer were a rug. Carl Rogers's later encounter work is a cousin, not a child. Lewin did not live to referee the cousins.

Honorary titles are a monument. He was a Berlin exile who ran an Iowa station, an MIT center, and one famous workshop, and then died.

## Why a clinic still reads this

Because he died in February 1947, before Bethel, the brand grew without him. NTL, sensitivity training, team building, and the encounter weekend all claimed a piece. A heritage series can name the New Britain workshop without blessing the offsite.

Because a Berlin exile spent Iowa and MIT years on prejudice, food habits, and leadership climates, not on a new self in a long weekend. The T-group's "here-and-now" was a 1946 civic permission, paid for by an interracial commission. A therapy group that uses the phrase as weather has borrowed the permission and forgotten the bill.

Because the border between training and treatment is not a law of nature. Pratt's class looked like school. The T-group was school that looked like treatment. Naming which room is running is still the task.
