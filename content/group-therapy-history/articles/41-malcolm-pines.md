**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Routledge published *The Evolution of Group Analysis* in 1983, Malcolm Pines editing. The volume is an introduction to S. H. Foulkes and to the arguments that grew on his sentences: the group as matrix, the conductor rather than the star, analysis *through* the group. Pines (25 April 1925 – 3 July 2021) had been close enough to Foulkes to make the book more than a festschrift and late enough that the founder was already becoming a statue. His public job, for decades, was to keep the statue from replacing the practice — and, at the same time, to make a British school travel: Scandinavia, the European symposia, the journal *Group Analysis*, an International Library that put other people's books into English.

This essay is part of a WOW Therapies series on the history of group therapy. It is not a group-analytic training. It does not teach a conductor how to sit.

## Maudsley, Foulkes, and a London practice

He was born in London, trained in medicine at University College, specialized in psychiatry, and met Foulkes while training at the Maudsley. Foulkes became his training analyst. That sentence is in the Royal College of Physicians memoir and in the Wellcome description of his papers. It is a lineage fact, not a scene. With Foulkes, Robin Skynner, de Maré, and others he belonged to the first civilian generation that had to turn a wartime hospital method into a profession with a door, a fee, and a qualifying course.

The Group Analytic Practice is usually dated 1964 in Pines's own later accounts; the Institute of Group Analysis, as a training institute, 1973. Those years sit next to de Maré's overlapping story; small mismatches in founding folklore should be marked `[VERIFY]` if a plaque is being written. What is not in mismatch is Pines's later institutional load: editor of *Group Analysis* for many years after Harold Behr; president of the Group Analytic Society; president of the International Association of Group Psychotherapy (1980–1984); consultant posts that public memoirs list at St George's, the Maudsley, and the Tavistock. He helped seed trainings in Denmark, Norway, and Sweden. He kept attending the Society's European symposia into 2015.

He was a psychoanalyst of the British Society as well as a group analyst. The dual membership matters. Group analysis in London never fully left the couch; it argued with it. Pines wrote papers that tried to keep child development, mirroring, narcissism, and the self in conversation with Foulkes's matrix without simply translating the group back into individual analysis done in public. *Circular Reflections* (Jessica Kingsley, 1998) is one later gathering of that effort. Posthumous collections, edited with John Schlapobersky, continue the stacking. They are books. They are not a license to treat this webpage as supervision.

## The 1983 book as an act of interpretation

*The Evolution of Group Analysis* (London: Routledge & Kegan Paul, 1983; later Jessica Kingsley reprint) includes the first Foulkes Lectures, papers on research, on Lacan, on Europe, and Pines's own exposition of Foulkes. An editor of a school is not a secretary. Selection is a theory. What got in — child development, outcome research, continental voices — tells you what 1980s group analysis wanted to be seen as: not a closed British club, not a wartime anecdote, not Bion.

Bion remains the other Northfield. Pines wrote about him too. The school that kept Foulkes as founder still had to decide what to do with *Experiences in Groups*: absorb it, fence it, or send students to Tavistock for a different drink. Pines's public manner, in obituaries, is the interpreter who would not let the quarrel become a silence. He also introduced large groups into trainings and into AGPA contexts. That is a cousin of de Maré's wager, not a copy. The American Group Psychotherapy Association's later uses of large groups at conferences are a separate institutional story; do not flatten them into a single "Pines method."

The International Library of Group Analysis, which he served as general editor, put dozens of titles into a recognizable spine. Yvonne Agazarian's systems-centered work is among the books that library carried in its Routledge phase. A series editor is a gate. Gates have politics. This page will not pretend otherwise, and will not inventory every title.

## What an interpreter is for

Founders die. Foulkes died in 1976. The annual Foulkes Lecture began soon after; Pines was among those who made it a repeating public ritual, and he gave one in 1982. Rituals keep a name warm. They also risk turning argument into incense. Pines's better work, on the printed page, is the opposite of incense: he kept explaining Foulkes to people who had not been in the room, including people whose first language was not English. That is why a county clinician might still meet "matrix" in a workshop without ever seeing Daleham Gardens.

He is not the only interpreter. Elizabeth Foulkes, later editors of the journal, and a now international IGA network all hold pieces. Pines was, at his death, described by colleagues as the last of the Institute's founder members who had worked with Foulkes in the flesh. That is an ending of a generation, not a proof of orthodoxy.

## Why a clinic still reads this

Because most of us meet Foulkes through someone else. Pines is that someone else for a large fraction of the English-language bookshelf. Reading him is a way of watching a school decide what will count as "group analysis" once the army hospital is a conference anecdote. A contemporary clinician can take the warning without joining the Institute: when a method travels, an editor is doing as much of the founding as the founder.

Because interpretation is not the same as loyalty. *The Evolution of Group Analysis* is a 1983 object. Later group analysis has had its own fights — race, the large group, research, the status of the individual hour. Pines is a hinge, not a ceiling. The next essay crosses the Atlantic again to Jerome D. Frank at Johns Hopkins, who measured groups as a researcher of persuasion and demoralization rather than as a conductor of a matrix.
