**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

*No licensed still. Type only: Irvin D. Yalom, b. 1931. Living as of 2026-09-14.*

In 1970, Basic Books published Irvin D. Yalom's *The Theory and Practice of Group Psychotherapy*. Residents who had been handed a process group and a shrug now had a textbook with chapters, a research habit, and a list of "therapeutic factors." The book did not invent the American outpatient group. It organized the craft so a profession could teach it without only imitating a charismatic supervisor. Later editions grew thicker. The first edition is the historical object this pack cares about.

This essay is part of a WOW Therapies series on the history of group therapy. It treats Yalom as the author of the group book. It does not reprint his cases. It does not teach a here-and-now technique. The individual-hour pack may keep the novelist and the existential clinician. This page will give *Existential Psychotherapy* (1980) one short paragraph and then return to the circle.

## Stanford, a textbook, a list

Yalom was born on 13 June 1931 in Washington, D.C., to parents who had come from Russia after the First World War. He trained in medicine and psychiatry and spent the teaching career that made the textbook at Stanford University School of Medicine, where he is now professor emeritus. Those are public facts. This series will not narrate his household or his health. He is living as of the last verification date on this file, 14 September 2026.

The 1970 book offered American psychiatry a usable theory of what, if anything, a talking group did. Yalom gathered mechanisms other writers had named in pieces — hope, universality, altruism, cohesion, catharsis, interpersonal learning, the replaying of family, and the rest of the inventory that later editions numbered as eleven — and treated them as researchable claims rather than as poetry. The list is a historical artifact of a textbook. It is not a menu. A clinic that recites "universality" at a new member is quoting a chapter heading, not proving an outcome.

He wrote as a clinician-researcher who had already been looking at groups that were not clinics. With Morton Lieberman and Matthew Miles he studied encounter groups; *Encounter Groups: First Facts* (1973) is the monograph that counted casualties as well as benefits. The encounter essay in this series holds the boom. Yalom's share of that work matters here because the 1970 textbook and the 1973 research book are arguments with each other. One teaches a craft with screening, time, and a licensed leader. The other shows what happens when heat is sold without those boring conditions. Readers who only know the textbook miss the warning. Readers who only know the warning miss the craft book that tried to keep the craft from becoming a weekend.

Later editions, some with Molyn Leszcz as coauthor, folded in new research, diagnostic manuals, and the long afterlife of cohesion studies. Mentioning those editions is bibliography. It is not an update of anyone's treatment plan. Burlingame and others would later argue about cohesion and outcome in a more statistical dialect (see the research-era essay). Yalom remains the name on the syllabus.

## Inpatient is not outpatient

In 1983 he published *Inpatient Group Psychotherapy*, a book that is easy to skip if you only meet him through the 1970 outpatient textbook. Acute wards in the 1980s already had short stays, heavy medication, and a turnover that made a six-month process group a fantasy. Yalom argued that hospital groups still mattered and that they could not copy the long outpatient hour. The ward group had a different clock, a different mix of illness, and a different job: often a single session with people who might not return tomorrow.

This series will not excerpt his ward models. Naming the 1983 book is enough. The historical point is the refusal of a single "group therapy." Pratt's tuberculosis class, Foulkes's matrix, Jones's community meeting, and a Stanford outpatient process group were never the same object. Yalom, more than most American writers, wrote the difference into two separate books rather than letting the outpatient fantasy colonize the ward.

He also wrote, in public interviews and prefaces, about interpersonal learning as the center of his outpatient theory: people get into trouble with people, and a group can become a social microcosm if the talk can be brought to what is happening among those present. Historians can report that claim. A website that then says "try this with your friends" has left history for a parlor.

## One paragraph for the other book

*Existential Psychotherapy* (1980) is a large textbook on death, freedom, isolation, and meaning, written for a course that, Yalom later said, did not yet exist. It shaped his novels and the popular essays that made him a public writer. Those books are not this pack's subject. A group essay mentions the 1980 volume only to keep the lanes honest: the man who listed therapeutic factors also wrote a philosophy of the individual hour. Do not copy that hour into this URL. Do not mine *Love's Executioner* or the fiction for case color. Public books may be named. Their patients are not ours to retell.

## Why a clinic still reads this

Because if a county practice owns one group book, it is still often this one. That is not a reason to treat 1970 as current procedure. It is a reason to know what American training actually inherited: an interpersonal, here-and-now, factor-listing tradition that sits beside — and sometimes refuses to hear — Foulkes, Bion, Pichon-Rivière, and the social-work canon.

Because inpatient and outpatient groups still get confused in staffing meetings. Yalom's 1983 title is a historical fence. A day program is not a Tuesday night process group. The fence was built for a reason.

Because he is alive. Heritage writing about a living person is a narrower job than writing about Pratt. No gossip. No health. No invented intimacy. The 1970 book, the 1973 research, and the 1983 ward book are the public record this page will use.

A small-city clinic that never assigns Yalom still lives in a billing world his textbook helped make thinkable: group as a professional service with a theory, not only a warehouse solution. That thinkability has a date. 1970. The date does not make the book eternal. It makes it a document.
