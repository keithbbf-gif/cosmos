**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

*No licensed still. Type only: Michael Balint, 1896–1970.*

In 1957, London publishers issued Michael Balint's *The Doctor, His Patient and the Illness*, a book grown from seminars with general practitioners at the Tavistock Clinic. The GPs brought cases. The talk was not about how to run a therapy group. It was about the relationship that appears in a ten-minute surgery when a person keeps returning with a body that will not explain itself. Later generations named those seminars Balint groups. This series will describe them as a historical form. It will not teach anyone to convene one.

This essay is part of a WOW Therapies series on the history of group therapy. Balint belongs here because physicians sat in a circle and used the circle to study their own work. He belongs in the consulting-room pack as a psychoanalyst of the pair. Here he is only the second fact: a group of doctors, a leader trained in analysis, a research-cum-training habit that leaked into family medicine worldwide.

## Budapest, Berlin, Manchester, London

He was born in Budapest in 1896, the son of a general practitioner, and trained in medicine while the First World War interrupted the calendar. He encountered psychoanalysis in the city of Sándor Ferenczi, whose influence he never dropped. He worked in Berlin and returned to Budapest. With his first wife, Alice Balint, herself an analyst, he moved in the Hungarian psychoanalytic world of the 1920s and 1930s. Later writers say he already tried teaching groups of physicians there. The first-group date is the kind of origin the profession likes and the archive muddies `[VERIFY]` before printing a Budapest year as if it were a plaque.

The family left Hungary in 1939. Alice died soon after the arrival in Britain. Balint worked in Manchester as a psychiatrist, then moved to London in 1945, took citizenship, and in 1948 joined the Tavistock Clinic. He stayed on staff until a 1961 retirement that did not end the work. He served the British Psychoanalytical Society as scientific secretary in the early 1950s and as president at the end of his life, 1968 until his death in 1970. The psychoanalytic Balint — *The Basic Fault*, regression, the pair — is another shelf. This essay leaves most of that shelf closed. This series is not a second Freud biography with a Hungarian accent.

Enid Flora Eichholz, a social worker and marital researcher at the Tavistock, became his collaborator and, in 1958, his wife. Institutional histories that print only "Balint groups" as a man's invention are doing the usual theft. Enid Balint co-led, wrote, and after 1970 continued the GP research. *Six Minutes for the Patient* (1973) is the later report of a 1966–1971 seminar; Michael had died before it appeared. A heritage series that treats her as a helpmeet with a filing cabinet is repeating a 1950s staffing chart.

## The Tavistock seminars as a group form

The seminars that fed the 1957 book are usually dated to the turn of the 1950s. The International Balint Federation's institutional narrative places a first recruitment at the Tavistock in 1949 `[VERIFY]`. Balint called them training-cum-research. The phrase is a refusal of two later mistakes: that the room was only therapy for doctors, and that it was only a lecture with extra chairs.

What happened, historically, is that a general practitioner presented a patient who would not fit the hospital's organ map. The group talked. The leader, an analyst, did not turn the hour into a case conference of specialists. Attention went to the doctor's feelings, hesitations, overwork, and the ways a practice can lose a person among consultants — Balint's "collusion of anonymity" — or treat the doctor's own presence as the thing that changes the illness. The 1957 book popularized the idea of the doctor as a drug: the person of the physician as an active substance, with effects and side effects. Naming that idea is history. Offering a reader a dose is not this site's job.

The form mattered to group history because it was a peer group with a task. Members were not patients. They were workers studying their work. T-groups at Bethel, a few years earlier, had another version of that sentence. Encounter weekends would later sell a hotter version. Balint's room stayed close to medicine: cases, surgeries, the National Health Service's clock. Thomas Main, who had been in analysis with him, took a related curiosity about staff back to the Cassel. The Ailment (1957) and *The Doctor, His Patient and the Illness* (1957) are the same year's two British warnings that helpers are not a sterile instrument. They are not the same paper.

Balint groups spread through family-medicine training in Europe, North America, and elsewhere. Societies formed. The International Balint Federation still exists as an institutional descendant. None of that is a license for a website to publish a leader's manual for those seminars. Other organizations train leaders. This series will not.

## What to keep, and what not to steal

The book is full of case material from the 1950s National Health Service. This essay will not reprint those cases. They are clinical stories with a date. A contemporary reader who wants technique has textbooks written for that purpose, under other duties.

What a historian can say without drifting into a manual: mid-century British psychoanalysis found a way to sit primary-care doctors in a group without calling the group psychotherapy, and the doctors' group changed how some of them heard their patients. The change was argued, not proven by a modern trial in 1957. Later medical education treated Balint as a humanizing ritual. Some of that ritual is serious. Some of it is a name on a lunch hour.

He also belongs in the quarrel about who owns "group." Foulkes's group analysis wanted a matrix and a conductor. Bion wanted a work group and its refusals. Jones wanted a hospital that learned in public. Balint wanted a professional seminar that would make the doctor-patient pair visible to other doctors. The chairs look similar in a photograph. The tasks do not.

## Why a clinic still reads this

Because many people who come to a therapy group have already sat across from a physician who did or did not have a place to think. Balint's 1957 book is a record of that ten-minute relationship becoming a public object in a staff room. A counseling practice in Southeast Arkansas is not the Tavistock. It still lives downstream of a century that taught doctors, slowly, that their own presence is part of the case. Group psychotherapy learned a parallel lesson in other buildings: the leader is in the room, not above it.

Because Enid Balint's name is easy to drop. Restoring it is not a diversity paragraph. It is accuracy about who ran the historical form.

Because the temptation, on a health website, is to offer Balint groups as a wellness product for burned-out clinicians. This series refuses the product. The form had rules, a tradition, and a research claim. It also had a limit: it was never a substitute for a doctor's own treatment if the doctor needed treatment, and it was never a patient's group.

Michael Balint died in 1970. The book stayed in print in revised editions. The seminars went on without him, which is what a method does when it is more than a personality. Personality still founded it. History can hold both sentences.
