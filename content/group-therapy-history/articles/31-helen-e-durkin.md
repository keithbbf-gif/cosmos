**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

*No licensed still. Type only: Helen E. Durkin, 1901–1991 `[VERIFY]`.*

In 1964, International Universities Press published Helen E. Durkin's *The Group in Depth*, a thick comparative book that tried to put psychoanalytic group therapy in the same volume as group dynamics and the existential fashions then crowding American rooms. Martin Grotjahn reviewed it the next year in the *International Journal of Group Psychotherapy*. Students who only know Slavson, Wolf, or Yalom as "the" American line miss the woman who wrote the survey that made those lines comparable.

This essay is part of a WOW Therapies series on the history of group therapy. It is not a lesson in analytic group technique. It is a portrait of a psychologist who treated theory as something you could set side by side without pretending the sides agreed.

## Cornell, Brooklyn, a profession trying to become one

Library catalogues file her as Helen Elise Durkin, born 1901 `[VERIFY]`. A Scarsdale newspaper notice of 16 April 1948, announcing her election as vice-president of the American Group Therapy Association, gives a checkable educational trail: B.A. from Cornell in 1922, an M.A. in social science in 1925, a Ph.D. in psychology in 1937. The 1937 dissertation appeared as *Trial-and-Error, Gradual Analysis and Sudden Reorganization* in *Archives of Psychology*. She was already a researcher before she was a guild officer.

AGPA's later institutional history places Durkin and Henriette F. Glatzer at the Brooklyn Juvenile Protective Association (later Brooklyn Psychiatric Centers) in the late 1930s. Their work with children is dated to 1937, with mothers in 1938, in a "relationship therapy" line associated with John Levy and then Lawson Lowrey. The Brooklyn clinic is credited, in that official narrative, as an early site that treated parents in groups in their own right, not only as adjuncts to a child's hour. The claim is AGPA's. A historian should keep the citation on the table. It is how the American profession remembers a women's clinic practice that did not wait for the 1970s to notice mothers.

Durkin spoke on play therapy and on children's misbehavior to parent audiences in Westchester — the 1948 notice lists Larchmont and Crestwood PTA talks. Those talks are period pieces. They are also a reminder that group work with children and parents was a public-facing craft, not only a closed analytic circle. The child-activity essay in this series holds Slavson. Durkin is the other New York psychologist in the early association, writing and chairing while the men argued about Moreno.

## Standards, 1952–53, and a book that compared schools

Though AGPA had a Committee on Standards and Training as early as 1947, the association's own history says the committee began to function in earnest when Durkin became chairman in 1952. In October 1953 her committee submitted a report that tried to say a difficult thing: AGPA was a membership association, not a certifying board, and yet it would publish standards for people and programs that wanted to train group therapists. Qualifications for candidates were adopted. Standards for training programs — facilities, curricula, supervision — were tabled after debate. That tabled half is as historical as the adopted half. A young profession wanted respectability without becoming a guild that could be sued for excluding. Durkin's committee put the tension on paper.

*The Group in Depth* (1964) is the book that earned her a permanent citation. It is not a how-to. It is a map of approaches: analytic work with adults, the different demands of other populations, the temptation to hybridize therapy groups with training groups. Durkin wrote that intensive analytic psychotherapy might be the method of choice for some adult neurotic and character problems, while other rooms — hospital, school, industry, community mental health — needed other levels. She cited Harris Peck on "viable hybrids." She did not settle the nomenclature fight about what to call those hybrids. She said the needs had to be assessed and the training named. A 2020s reader who wants a protocol will be disappointed. A historian who wants to see a woman theorist refusing a single American method will not be.

By 1972 she was publishing "General Systems Theory and Group Therapy: An Introduction" in *IJGP*, from an affiliation at the Postgraduate Center for Mental Health in New York. The systems turn in American group therapy has several parents. Durkin is one. Yvonne Agazarian, later in this wave, would build a named school (SCT) out of living-systems theory. Durkin's 1972 paper is an earlier, cooler introduction: von Bertalanffy in the group journal, a psychologist already known for depth work asking whether a general theory of systems could talk to a clinical circle. The paper is theory history. It is not a warrant to diagram a reader's workplace.

## Why a clinic still reads this

Because the American Group Psychotherapy Association did not professionalize itself only through Slavson's children's rooms and Wolf's alternate sessions. A psychologist who had been a vice-president in 1948 and a standards chair in 1952 wrote the 1964 book that still sits in bibliographies when someone needs a pre-Yalom American theory text. Women's work in this series is not a closing section. Durkin is the essay.

Because *The Group in Depth* is a comparative habit. Yalom's 1970 textbook would soon occupy the American syllabus with a more interpersonal, research-friendly voice. Durkin's book is what a psychoanalytic group psychologist wrote when the field was still a quarrel of schools. Reading her is a way not to let 1970 erase 1964.

Because standards committees are group history. The 1953 report's split — members yes, training-program rules tabled — is the ancestor of every later fight about who may lead a group, under what license, with how much supervision. A small-city clinic that hires a group leader is living inside that unfinished committee, whether it knows Durkin's name or not.

No session scripts. No play-therapy instructions. The Brooklyn mothers' groups are a dated clinic practice, not a homework circle.
