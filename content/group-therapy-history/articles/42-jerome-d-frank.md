**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Johns Hopkins Press published Jerome D. Frank's *Persuasion and Healing: A Comparative Study of Psychotherapy* in 1961. The book is not a group manual. It is an argument that healing practices — psychiatry, counseling, religious cure, the placebo of a ritual — share features that restore morale when a person has lost the sense that anything can be done. Frank (30 May 1909 – 14 March 2005) had already been running and studying outpatient groups at the Henry Phipps Psychiatric Clinic. The group papers and the 1961 book belong together historically: the circle was one of the rooms in which he watched persuasion work, fail, and drop people. This essay will not turn either object into a method you can follow at home.

This essay is part of a WOW Therapies series on the history of group therapy. Efficacy sentences stay dated and named. They never become "group will help your anxiety."

## Harvard, Phipps, and a war in the middle

He was born in New York City. Harvard gave him an A.B. (1930), an A.M. (1932), a Ph.D. in psychology (1934), and an M.D. (1939). He studied in Berlin in 1930–31, a year that later biographers treat as a social-psychological education under the shadow of a collapsing republic. In 1940 he went to Johns Hopkins as a junior psychiatrist under Adolf Meyer. World War II took him out as a neuropsychiatrist with the 118th General Hospital. He returned to Hopkins, became professor of psychiatry in 1959, and emeritus in 1974. The Chesney Archives hold the papers. Johns Hopkins portraits are copyright. Type only.

The Phipps outpatient department, in the 1950s, had the problem every crowded clinic had: more people asking for talk than hours in which to give it, and a leaky bucket — many patients left before anyone could call the work a treatment. Frank and colleagues (among them Morris Parloff, Anthony Stone, Earl Nash, Lester Gliedman, Stanley Imber) published on "diagnostic" groups, on who remained in group psychotherapy, and on cohesiveness. "The Use of 'Diagnostic' Groups in a Group Therapy Program" appeared in the *International Journal of Group Psychotherapy* in 1954. "Some Determinants, Manifestations, and Effects of Cohesiveness in Therapy Groups" followed in 1957. A 1957 companion paper asked what predicted remaining in group at all.

The clinic population in those papers is not a private-practice seminar. Frank's own outpatient descriptions from the period sketch urban adults, many of grammar or high-school education, lower and lower-middle class, a mix that would never have been mistaken for a Bethel T-group. A 1959 comparison, in the research literature that later common-factors writers still cite, looked at group therapy, weekly individual therapy, and minimal contact. Average measured change at six months did not crown a winner. That sentence is a historical report about a study. It is not a claim that all treatments are equal, and it is not permission to skip a clinician. A follow-up line of work at Hopkins looked at placebo and expectancy — the same curiosity that feeds *Persuasion and Healing*, not a trick to teach a reader.

## Demoralization, not a slogan

Frank's later short form, including "Psychotherapy: The Restoration of Morale" in the *American Journal of Psychiatry* (1974), named *demoralization* as the common distress that many therapies actually address: a loss of mastery, a sense that the future is closed. The 1961 book had already compared professional psychotherapy with other healing forms without sneering them off the table. Later editions, including work continued with his daughter Julia B. Frank, kept revising the argument. This pack cites the 1961 Johns Hopkins Press book as the dated event. Later editions are later events.

What this means for group history is easy to overstate. Frank did not "prove group works." He treated the group as a social situation in which belonging, example, emotion, and the chance to practice a new stance in front of others might combat isolation — and in which dropout was a first fact, not an embarrassment to hide. Cohesion, in his 1957 paper, is already a research object. Later reviews (Burlingame and colleagues, Yalom's factor lists, Lieberman–Yalom–Miles 1973 on encounter casualties) are other dated objects. They do not form a single ladder with Frank as the bottom rung.

He also wrote about nuclear weapons, race in Baltimore, and leadership. Those campaigns belong in a full life. This pack is the group-heritage lane. The consulting-room pack may keep other pieces of twentieth-century psychotherapy. Do not copy paragraphs across packs. The group claim here is specific: a Hopkins psychiatrist put outpatient groups into a research program and then wrote a book that made "common factors" thinkable for people who would never run a group.

## What 1961 does not license

*Persuasion and Healing* can be misread as cynicism: if ritual and expectation do the work, technique is a costume. Frank's own pages are more careful. A rationale and a ritual, in his account, are how a healer and a sufferer share a story that makes effort possible. That is not an instruction to fake a method. It is not an instruction to start a group because groups are efficient. The Phipps papers are full of inefficiency: people who did not come back.

Do not take cohesiveness research as a prompt to "build cohesion" with icebreakers. This series will not publish them. History may say a 1957 paper existed. History may not turn it into Monday's plan.

## Why a clinic still reads this

Because a patient who sits down in a group is often already demoralized in Frank's sense: tired of being the only one, tired of a symptom that has not moved, tired of experts. Naming that historically is not the same as diagnosing the reader. Frank's usefulness to a heritage series is the refusal of miracle talk. Groups, in his clinic, leaked. They also, for some who stayed, did work that an individual hour had not. The evidence was mixed, local, and mid-century. Anyone who upgrades that into "studies prove group is better" has left the file.

Because research is part of the American group story, not an insult to it. Pratt counted weight. Lieberman counted casualties. Frank counted who stayed.
