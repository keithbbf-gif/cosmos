**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Enrique Pichon-Rivière was born in Geneva on 25 June 1907 and died in Buenos Aires on 16 July 1977. The family crossed to Argentina when he was a small child and lived in the interior — the Chaco cotton country of the period, then other river towns — before he studied medicine in Buenos Aires. English textbooks that need a European origin for every method sometimes file him under "Klein in the Southern Cone" or "Bion with a Spanish accent." That filing is a failure of reading. He read European psychoanalysis. He also worked for years in Argentine asylums, helped found the Asociación Psicoanalítica Argentina, broke toward social psychology, and left a vocabulary — *grupo operativo*, *vínculo*, ECRO — that Argentine, Uruguayan, and later Spanish and Mexican schools still argue with as their own.

The book a historian can put on the table, in Spanish first, is *El proceso grupal* (Buenos Aires: Nueva Visión, collected editions; a 1975 collected edition is often catalogued). The Spanish title stays in Spanish on first mention. English paraphrase comes after, and thinner.

This essay is part of a WOW Therapies series on the history of group therapy. It does not teach an operative group. It does not offer an ECRO as a worksheet.

## Hospicio de las Mercedes, not Hampstead

After qualifying in medicine in the 1930s he worked at the Hospicio de las Mercedes in Buenos Aires, the large public asylum later known as Hospital Borda, and in related institutions. Public biographical reconstructions place him there for something on the order of fifteen years, including admission work and an attempt to make a ward for psychotic adolescents. He ran study groups and, in later tellings by students, groups with nurses as well as with patients. Exact first-group dates inside the hospicio are the kind of institutional memory that should stay marked `[VERIFY]` when a secondary web page is the only source. What is not in doubt is the building: a nineteenth-century public hospital in a city that was not London and not Boston.

In 1942 he was among the founders of the Asociación Psicoanalítica Argentina, with Ángel Garma, Celes Cárcamo, Marie Langer, Arnaldo Rascovsky, and others. He took a training analysis in that world. Argentine psychoanalysis in the 1940s was already a translation project — Freud, then Klein — under local political weather. Pichon-Rivière's later move "from psychoanalysis to social psychology," the subtitle that Nueva Visión hung on the collected papers, is a move inside Argentina, not a delayed Northfield.

He was not Foulkes. He was not Bion. Readers who know *Experiences in Groups* will hear rhymes: anxiety in a group, a task that the group would rather evade, a leader who is not quite a leader. Pichon-Rivière's published difference, as later Argentine commentators stress, is the *tarea* — the task — as the object the group relates to, and a social field that includes family, class, and the city. Kleinian positions appear in his pages. So do hospital corridors that Klein never walked.

## Operative groups, Rosario 1958, and the ECRO

The *grupo operativo* is the device his name carries. In the Argentine literature it is a small or restricted group organized around a task, whose learning includes the obstacles and anxieties the task wakes up. It can be used as therapy. Therapy is not its only destiny. There are operative groups in teaching, in community work, in institutional consulting. A heritage series that reduced the term to "how Argentines do process groups" would be doing the European-copy job again.

The 1958 Experiencia Rosario, planned with the Instituto Argentino de Estudios Sociales (IADES) and faculties in Rosario, is the public laboratory later writers treat as a founding demonstration: a city weekend of groups, heterogeneous and homogeneous, an attempt to think a whole urban field with a method. Numbers of participants get inflated in retellings. This page will not certify a headcount. The year and the city are the checkable core.

ECRO is an acronym he made unavoidable: *esquema conceptual, referencial y operativo* — a conceptual, referential, and operative scheme. It is, in his usage, an apparatus for thinking a practice: the ideas one actually works with, the references one admits, the operations one can perform. Students still draw boxes. This essay will not. The historical claim is that an Argentine psychiatrist tried to name how a worker's theory is already an instrument, and that the name stuck in Spanish-language training houses from Buenos Aires outward.

*El proceso grupal* is a collection, not a single 1950s monograph written in one sitting. Edition years wander in catalogues (1971, 1975, many reprints). If a citation in a classroom handout disagrees with the copy on the table, mark `[VERIFY]`. Do not silently "correct" a living school's canon.

## Against the import stamp

Latin American group work is not a delayed delivery of Tavistock. Pichon-Rivière's Switzerland is a birthplace, not a school. His France is a surname. His working language and his hospitals were Argentine. Juan Tubert-Oklander and Reyna Hernández de Tubert have written English-language introductions for readers who need a bridge; name their book if you quote them. Until then, the decent English sentence is modest: an Argentine school of operative groups and social psychology grew in the middle decades of the twentieth century, in dialogue with psychoanalysis and with a politics that North Atlantic syllabi still under-teach.

## Why a clinic still reads this

Because a method can be world-class and still be missing from an American intake form. A clinician in Arkansas who has never heard of Rosario can still use the historical warning: the group in front of you has a task, even when the brochure says "process," and the task is never only the inner child. Pichon-Rivière's pages, in Spanish, keep the social in the room without turning the room into a rally.

Because translation is a power relation. Leaving *El proceso grupal* in Spanish on first mention is a small refusal to pretend the real book was always already English. The next essay returns to Britain, to Patrick de Maré, who took Northfield's hospital and pushed the circle past the small analytic group into median and large rooms where dialogue, not family transference, was the wager.
