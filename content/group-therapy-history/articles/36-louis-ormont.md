**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

St. Martin's Press issued Louis R. Ormont's *The Group Therapy Experience* in 1992 with a subtitle that told the truth about the intended reader: *From Theory to Practice*. The book is a late-career summing-up by a New York psychologist and psychoanalyst (1918–2008) who had already spent decades running groups and teaching a method his students called modern analytic. American group psychotherapy already had Slavson, Wolf, Durkin, Yalom, and the Boston textbook line. Ormont's claim, in print, was that the group should not be an audience for serial individual work. Members were to talk to one another. He named one of his devices *bridging*. This essay uses that word as a historical term. It does not teach the device. It does not script a question to ask a neighbor in a circle.

This essay is part of a WOW Therapies series on the history of group therapy. It describes a school and a book. It is not a workshop.

## Philadelphia, New York, and a crowded lineage

Public archival notes at Adelphi University, where Ormont later taught at the Derner Institute, place his birth in Philadelphia in 1918, a bachelor's degree at Temple, a turn through playwriting at Yale, and clinical psychology at Columbia. The theater chapter is easy to over-read. Moreno also loved a stage. Ormont did not become a psychodramatist. He became a group analyst in the New York sense of the phrase: long groups, psychoanalytic talk, a leader who worked resistances in public. Obituaries in 2008 recorded his death on 15 November, after heart surgery, at ninety.

The intellectual neighborhood is modern psychoanalysis as Hyman Spotnitz and colleagues named it in New York: a mid-century American attempt to take psychoanalysis into rooms — including groups — that classical institutes had often declined. Ormont helped found the Center for Modern Psychoanalytic Studies in 1970. In 1989, colleagues founded the Center for Group Studies in his honor, a freestanding New York training house that still advertises his name. Institutional pages are not neutral. They are still dates. A heritage series can say the centers existed. It cannot reprint their curricula as care.

New York already had Alexander Wolf's psychoanalysis-in-groups, Eastern Group Psychotherapy Society rooms, and a market of institutes. Ormont's difference, as his 1992 book and the later collected papers (*The Technique of Group Treatment*, 2001) argued, was a relentless preference for member-to-member talk over leader-centered interpretation. Other schools wanted that too. Foulkes wanted the group to become the therapist. Ormont wanted the emotional traffic among members to do work that a single transference to the leader would not. Historians can hear the rhyme without collapsing London into Madison Avenue.

## "Bridging" as a word in a book, not a lesson

Chapter titles in *The Group Therapy Experience* include the phrase. Ormont defined bridging, in that book, as a way of evoking talk between members so the group would function as a working unit rather than a row of witnesses. Later journal articles carried the same word into the 1990s. Students still repeat it as if it were a trademarked tool. The historical fact is narrower: a New York clinician named a family of leader moves and wrote them down for other clinicians.

This pack's claims file is strict here. History may describe that a school used a named intervention. History may not teach a reader to do it. No sample questions. No "if the silent member…" drills. No contract language copied as homework. The 1992 book remains a professional object. So do the papers in *International Journal of Group Psychotherapy*, *Group*, and *Modern Psychoanalysis*. They belong on a syllabus with a live supervisor, not on a clinic blog as a kit.

What a general reader can honestly take is the quarrel inside the word. American groups in the decades after the war kept sliding toward the leader: the wise doctor, the charismatic encounterist, the intern who interviews eight people in a circle because that is what individual training taught. Ormont's published campaign was against that slide. He was not the only campaigner. He was one of the loudest in a particular New York accent.

## What the obituaries inflate, and what they do not

Training-house biographies like large numbers. They are welcome to them. This page will not certify a session count. What the public obituaries do settle is occupation: decades of group practice in New York, teaching at Adelphi and at modern-analytic institutes in other cities, a 1992 trade-press book that reached readers outside the institute hallway. He wrote as a clinician who expected to be understood in ordinary sentences. That prose is part of the history. It is also a temptation to treat the book as friendly instruction. The temptation is refused.

He is not Yalom. *The Theory and Practice of Group Psychotherapy* (1970) organized a research-friendly American mainstream. Ormont's book organized a school. Readers who collapse every New York group teacher into "modern analytic" will lose Wolf, the interpersonalists, and the hospital workers who never joined an institute. The map is crowded. That is the point of a figure essay: one name, a date, a limit.

## Why a clinic still reads this

Because every therapy group still has to decide whether the silent person is the leader's problem or the group's. Ormont's school answered, in print, that the leader's job was to put people in contact with one another rather than to become the only interesting object in the room. A contemporary clinician can hear that as a warning about narcissism of the chair. Hearing a warning is not the same as running an Ormont group.

Because "technique" books age into documents. *The Group Therapy Experience* is a 1992 artifact of a New York method that still has institutes. Those institutes have their own ethics, tuition, and fights. This series will not recruit for them. It will also not pretend the method never existed. The next essay leaves the analytic consulting building altogether and enters social work, where Gisela Konopka was already writing about youth and groups as a helping process, not as a psychoanalysis with extra chairs.
