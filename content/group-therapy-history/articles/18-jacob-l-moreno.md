**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 1934 the Nervous and Mental Disease Publishing Company in Washington issued a book with a question for a title and a diagram for an argument: J. L. Moreno's *Who Shall Survive?* It offered sociometry — a way of mapping who chooses whom — as if attraction and rejection could be drawn and then acted. The book is grandiose. It is also the object a historian can put on the table when the later legend starts talking about a man who invented the group. He did not invent the group. He named psychodrama and sociometry, built a small publishing empire at Beacon, New York, and spent decades insisting that S. R. Slavson's professional circle had stolen a word and missed a revolution.

This essay is part of a WOW Therapies series on the history of group therapy. It is a life. The psychodrama rooms have their own essay in this series. Neither piece is a lesson in role reversal, doubling, or the empty chair. Those techniques have teachers. This site is not one of them.

## Vienna, a stage, and a claim

Jacob Levy Moreno was born in 1889. Bucharest is the usual city; the family story moves. He studied medicine in Vienna, qualified as a physician in 1917, wrote pamphlets, provoked audiences, and preferred a hall that looked like cabaret. After the war he organized the Theatre of Spontaneity — the Stegreiftheater — where a scene was dared more than it was blocked. *Das Stegreiftheater* appeared in 1924. He later translated it himself as *The Theatre of Spontaneity*.

The later myth says he discovered that play could heal. The plainer file says he discovered that a public who had just survived a war would pay attention to unrehearsed life. Healing was one of the words he used. Theater was another. Religion — a private cosmology of encounter — was a third. Readers who want a single secular inventor will be disappointed. Moreno did not want to be secular or single.

He later dated the genesis of his group work to 1913–14 in Vienna, among other rooms, including work he described with prostitutes. Treat that dating as his claim. Mutual aid, hospital wards, and Pratt's Boston class were already sitting people together. What Moreno added was a director, a stage, and a refusal to let the audience remain only an audience.

He also refused the couch as the last word. In *Who Shall Survive?* he wrote that there was no controversy between his approach and psychoanalysis because he himself was the controversy. The line is his. It is not a proof.

## 1925, New York, Beacon

He came to the United States in 1925. New York was the city he wanted: a density of strangers, schools, prisons, and hospitals that might sit still for a diagram. He worked on interpersonal theory and on the cluster of names he treated as a single invention: psychodrama, sociometry, sociodrama, group psychotherapy, sociatry. *Who Shall Survive?* used the New York Training School for Girls at Hudson, and a pandemic of runaways there, as a sociometric case. The sociogram — a picture of choices — looked like science to some mid-century Americans and like a parlor diagram to others. A later research tradition would try to measure cohesion with questionnaires. Moreno wanted a drawing that could be played.

Beacon, on the Hudson, became the address. Beacon House published. A sanitarium took patients. Visitors came to watch a director, auxiliaries, a protagonist, and a group that was not permitted to stay in the dark. Later ethics codes would have a long afternoon with that file. People enacted family scenes in front of one another. Relief and ambush both appear in the later testimony. Lieberman, Yalom, and Miles (1973) were counting casualties in a cousin tradition, encounter, not psychodrama proper. The warning still attaches: heat is not automatically help.

Zerka Toeman entered that world in 1941 as a student and collaborator and, after 1949, as his wife. Her own essay in this series is not a footnote. She kept the method circulating after 1974 in a way his self-myth could not.

In 1942 he founded the American Society of Group Psychotherapy and Psychodrama. The name was a claim on a word. Within a year Slavson and colleagues were organizing what became the American Group Therapy Association, later AGPA. Two societies, two journals, two origin stories. The quarrel outlived both men.

## The quarrel with Slavson

The fight was jurisdictional and temperamental. Slavson, at the Jewish Board of Guardians, wanted children's activity groups, psychoanalytic respectability, and a professional association that psychiatrists would join. Moreno wanted the word *group psychotherapy* and a universe in which the stage was more honest than the couch. AGPA's early decades leaned Slavson: membership rules, a journal, a medical look. Psychodrama built institutes, trainers, and a parallel world that still exists.

Choosing a winner is fandom. Hospitals that never owned a stage still borrowed Moreno's words: role, encounter, here-and-now. Process groups that never named a protagonist still used an audience. Influence leaked both ways.

Sociometry as a research tool and sociometry as a charismatic practice are easy to confuse. The first can be a quiet questionnaire. The second can become a public ranking. Historians should keep the two apart. This series will not teach either.

He died at Beacon in 1974. The house style of psychodrama did not die with him. Zerka, the journals, the ASGPP, and a scatter of institutes carried the vocabulary into a century that still likes action more than it likes sitting still — and still argues about whether that appetite is treatment.

## What this life is not

It is not a license to run a scene. History may record that Moreno used a stage and asked people to reverse roles. History may not hand the reader a script. The distinction is the claims file of this series, not a mood.

It is also not a story in which one man owns the circle. Pratt's class, Burrow's social theory, Schilder's hospital seminar, Foulkes's matrix, Slavson's children's rooms, Lewin's T-group — they do not vanish because Beacon printed a letterhead. Moreno's usefulness is the opposite of monopoly. He named the theater that other traditions pretend is only conversation.

## Why a clinic still reads this

Because every therapy group has a stage, even when the furniture denies it. Someone is on. Someone is the chorus. Someone is waiting to be cast as the difficult one. Moreno named the theater. A clinician who has never watched a psychodrama can still use the naming: this comment was an aside; that silence was a refused role.

Because the profession's origin stories are a fight. Pratt, Slavson, Moreno, Foulkes — each has a constituency. A small-city clinic that tells only the Yalom story has chosen the textbook that residency programs already own. That is a fine choice if it is a choice. It is a poorer choice if it is ignorance of Beacon.

Because charisma is part of the record. Moreno wrote as if the method and the man were the same event. Students still have to separate them. The book of 1934 is one object. The sanitarium is another. The quarrel with Slavson is a third. None of them is a workshop you can take home from a website.
