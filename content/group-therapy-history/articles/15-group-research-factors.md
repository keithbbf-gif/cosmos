**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 1970 Basic Books published Irvin D. Yalom's *The Theory and Practice of Group Psychotherapy*. Residents who would never read Foulkes, Jones, or Slavson now had a thick American textbook that began, not with a founder myth, but with a list. Yalom called the list therapeutic factors — he had earlier used "curative factors" — and treated them as the elements of a complicated process, not as a recipe. The list traveled anyway. By the 1980s a student could recite hope, universality, cohesiveness, and catharsis as if they were organs. Reciting is not the same as measuring. The next decades were an argument about measurement.

This essay is part of a WOW Therapies series on the history of group therapy. It is a history of research programs, not a proof that "group will help your anxiety," not a ranking of brands, and not a how-to for producing a factor. Yalom as novelist and existential clinician belongs to the individual-hour pack. Here he is the 1970 group book and the 1973 co-author who counted casualties.

## A list that became a language

The 1970 book named eleven primary factors: instillation of hope; universality; imparting of information; altruism; corrective recapitulation of the primary family group; development of socializing techniques; imitative behavior; interpersonal learning; group cohesiveness; catharsis; existential factors. Later editions shuffled emphasis; Q-sort studies sometimes split interpersonal learning into input and output and produced a twelve-item folklore. The historical fact is the publication. The professional fact is that American training used the list as a common tongue when the field did not have one.

Yalom did not claim to have discovered that people feel less alone when they hear a similar story. Pratt had noticed encouragement among consumptives in 1907. AA had built a fellowship on identification. CR groups had treated shared testimony as political data. What Yalom did was give outpatient psychiatric group therapy a vocabulary that sounded like mechanisms, and he tied it to an interpersonal theory: the group as a social microcosm, the here-and-now as the working surface. Students who learned only the vocabulary missed the theory. Students who learned only the theory sometimes talked as if a list published in 1970 were a law of nature.

Other traditions already had other lists. Bion had basic assumptions. Foulkes had the matrix. Frank, a decade earlier, had something more unsettling: perhaps the lists were all local names for persuasion.

## Frank, and the common engine

Jerome D. Frank's *Persuasion and Healing* (Johns Hopkins Press, 1961; later editions followed) did not begin as a group-therapy book. It was an argument that psychotherapies, religious healing, and placebos share a structure: a suffering person, a socially granted healer, a rationale, a ritual that dramatizes the rationale. Expectancy, hope, a relationship — the "nonspecific" factors that specific-school loyalists hate to hear named. Group rooms are full of those ingredients. A circle can raise hope or crush it. A leader can be a socially granted healer even when the theory says the group is the therapist.

Frank's later group research at Johns Hopkins, including work with Florence Powdermaker in the 1950s (*Group Psychotherapy*, 1953), sits in the AGPA-era file: psychoanalytic eclecticism, process notes, a wish to study rather than only to proclaim. The 1961 book is the one that still rattles manuals. If persuasion is the engine, then Yalom's factors, CBT's homework alliance, and a day-hospital community meeting are dialects. That sentence is a research hypothesis with a date, not a reason to stop distinguishing rooms. Frank did not say all groups are equivalent. He said healing systems rhyme.

Living writers in this neighborhood — Yalom among them — get only their public books on this page. No gossip, no health claims, no workshop legend.

## First Facts, and the ugly word

Morton A. Lieberman, Irvin D. Yalom, and Matthew B. Miles published *Encounter Groups: First Facts* (Basic Books, 1973). The encounter essay in this series already named the book. It belongs again here because it is the moment American group research admitted casualties as an outcome. Some participants improved. Some did not change. Some were worse. Leader style mattered in their sample: aggressive, intrusive leadership correlated with more harm. Reviewers later argued about measures and generalizability. They did not make the injured people disappear.

The book punctured two brochures at once. The encounter brochure that sold heat as vitamin. The professional brochure that implied a circle, any circle, was automatically a treatment. Heat is a temperature. A group can be hot and useless. A group can be quiet and useful. Those sentences are empirical claims about particular studies, not folk wisdom, and they still get flattened into folk wisdom.

*First Facts* also trained a generation to ask a question manuals sometimes skip: not only "did the mean improve?" but "who was hurt?" Outcome research that reports only averages is, after 1973, incomplete as history even when it is publishable as a paper.

## Cohesion, and a dated review

If Yalom's list had a favorite child in the research literature, it was cohesiveness — the "we" of a group, later defined more carefully as a bundle of member–member, member–leader, and member–group bonds. The word is borrowed from cartels, platoons, and sports. Group therapists liked it because it sounded measurable. Then they discovered that every questionnaire measured a slightly different we.

Gary M. Burlingame, Addie Fuhriman, and Jennifer E. Johnson published "Cohesion in Group Psychotherapy" in *Psychotherapy*, volume 38, in 2001: a review that treated cohesion as the therapeutic relationship in group form and summarized a then-current empirical pile. A later, also dated, review by Burlingame, McClendon, and Alonso appeared in *Psychotherapy* in 2011 as part of an APA task-force series on relationship elements. Fuhriman and Burlingame's *Handbook of Group Psychotherapy* (1994) had already tried to synthesize process and outcome. These are public reviews by living scholars. Cite the year. Do not write "Burlingame says group works."

What the reviews, taken as historical events, did was modest and stubborn. They kept finding a positive association between cohesion and outcome that was real, not huge, and messy — moderated by theoretical orientation, length, and how cohesion was measured. They did not license a slogan that cohesion is a sufficient treatment. They did not end the argument about whether cohesion is cause, consequence, or both. A student who needs a cleaner story will be disappointed. Clean stories are how First Facts get forgotten.

Other outcome arguments piled up in the same decades: meta-analyses of group versus waitlist versus individual (the honest ones refuse the marketing sentence "studies prove group works better"); differential dropout; the problem of nesting people inside groups inside leaders inside clinics, which ordinary statistics used to ignore. Dorothy Stock Whitaker and Morton Lieberman's *Psychotherapy through the Group Process* (1964) is an earlier, more clinical-process book on the same mountain. The mountain did not produce a single summit. It produced a literature.

## Why a clinic still reads this

Because a county group still lives on hope, on the shock of "you too," on whether the room holds or expels. Yalom gave those events names a staff meeting can use. Frank warned that the names might be local. Lieberman, Yalom, and Miles warned that a room can wound. Burlingame's dated reviews warned that the we-feeling researchers can measure is not a mascot. A clinic that uses only the mascot will miss the wound.

Because research is part of the heritage, not a later audit from outside. Pratt published in *JAMA*. Bion and Foulkes published in *The Lancet*. The 1970s put the circle into the same outcome machinery as the individual hour. That machinery has biases — short treatments, diagnosable samples, manuals — which is why structured CBT groups and process groups did not get equal time in the journals. History of research is also history of what counted as a countable group.

Because this is history, not a treatment-selection tool, not a promise, and not a substitute for a licensed clinician. Efficacy sentences on this site stay attached to named books. A missing attachment is a missing claim.
