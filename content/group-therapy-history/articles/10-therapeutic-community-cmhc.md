**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

On October 31, 1963, in the Cabinet Room of the White House, President John F. Kennedy signed Public Law 88-164, the Mental Retardation Facilities and Community Mental Health Centers Construction Act. Title II is the piece group historians still nickname the CMHC Act. Kennedy said custodial mental institutions would be replaced by therapeutic centers, and that the census of the big hospitals might fall by half in a decade or two. Three weeks later he was dead. The statute remained. So did the temptation it tried to kill: the warehouse.

This essay is part of a WOW Therapies series on the history of group therapy. It describes hospitals and laws. It is not a ward protocol, not a staffing recipe, and not a claim that a county clinic in Southeast Arkansas is a therapeutic community. The individual psychotherapy-history pack keeps the consulting room. This pack keeps the institution that tried to become a group.

## Main coins a phrase; Jones writes a book

The English phrase *therapeutic community* did not wait for Kennedy. Lt. Col. Thomas Main used it in "The Hospital as a Therapeutic Institution," published in the *Bulletin of the Menninger Clinic* in May 1946, in a Northfield set of papers. Main meant the hospital itself — staff meetings, the culture of the place, the refusal to treat "treatment" as something that happened only in a doctor's office while the rest of the building ran on keys. After the war he became medical director of the Cassel Hospital in Richmond, Surrey (appointed in the mid-1940s; he stayed until 1976). Maxwell Jones had also applied for that post. Main got it. Jones went to the other side of London.

Jones, born in 1907 in Queenstown, South Africa, took over the Industrial Neurosis Unit at Belmont Hospital, Sutton, in April 1947: a hundred beds, ministries of Health, Labour, and Pensions in the background, a population the letters of referral called hopeless — chronically unemployed "neurotics" and people labeled psychopathic, a vocational problem as much as a symptom list. The unit later became Henderson Hospital. Jones published *The Therapeutic Community: A New Treatment Method in Psychiatry* with Basic Books in 1953. The American edition traveled farther than the Surrey wards. Visitors came. Films were made. "Max" was available to be challenged in the community meeting. Hierarchy was supposed to flatten. Patients and staff were supposed to look like participants in the same social experiment.

They were not the same man. Main's Cassel kept a thicker psychoanalytic hour inside the community: individual and family work, a "culture of enquiry" (his later gloss) rather than a perpetual town hall. Jones wrote more, moved more — leaving Henderson in 1959, working in the United States, then directing Dingleton Hospital in the Scottish Borders in the 1960s — and sold, in print, a method other hospitals could copy. Copying is where the warehouse returns. A community meeting in a hundred-bed unit is a method. A community meeting in a two-thousand-bed asylum that has not changed its staffing ratio is a ceremony over a lock.

## The Ailment

In 1957 Main published "The Ailment" in the *British Journal of Medical Psychology*. It is not a cheerful paper. It is about a certain kind of patient who splits a staff, about nurses who become secretly special, about the hospital's feelings as a system. Group therapists who know only Yalom's later interpersonal list sometimes meet Main here with surprise. He was writing about the institution as the patient, decades before "systems" became a workshop adjective. The paper belongs to the Cassel and to a British psychoanalytic hospital tradition. It does not belong to a how-to for managing a difficult member. This series will not extract one.

Northfield sits behind both men (see the group-analysis essay). Bion and Rickman, Foulkes, Bridger, Main: the military neurosis centre had already tried to treat a ward as a group and a hospital as a society. Peacetime psychiatry inherited the permission and lost the uniform. What it did not automatically inherit was money, trained staff, or a public willing to live next to the people the asylum had hidden.

## Day hospitals, and the group as a census tool

If the therapeutic community wanted the whole building, the day hospital wanted a building you could leave at night. Joshua Bierer and others in postwar Britain developed psychiatric day hospitals as a middle form: treatment without a bed, or without a bed every night. In the United States the same arithmetic appealed to anyone staring at a state-hospital budget. Partial hospitalization — one of the five "essential services" later expected of a federally aided community mental health center, alongside inpatient, outpatient, emergency, and consultation-education — is the American bureaucratic name for a cousin of the day hospital.

A day program is a group-heavy place by necessity. There are not enough individual hours. There is a lunch. There is a community meeting that may be inquiry or roll call. Pratt's old class method, Marsh's revival-equivalent, and Jones's town hall can all be heard in the same corridor if you refuse to tidy the ancestry. Efficiency is not an insult if you say it. It becomes an insult when a brochure calls a packed dayroom "community" and the staffing sheet says otherwise.

## The American law, and what it did not buy

Public Law 88-164 authorized construction money for community mental health centers. Later amendments added initial staffing. The Joint Commission's *Action for Mental Health* (1961) had already argued that the state hospital was a failure of policy, not only of architecture. Chlorpromazine and its cousins had changed what a ward could survive. Civil-rights litigation and exposés would soon make the old asylum harder to defend. Deinstitutionalization had many parents. The 1963 Act is the one with a signing photograph.

What the Act did not do is as historical as what it did. It did not permanently fund operations. It did not build a house, a job, and a clinic on every block the hospital had vacated. It did not settle who was qualified to run a group when the country suddenly wanted thousands of them. Scheidlinger and Schamess, in their 1992 AGPA history, noted the scramble: individual therapists assigned to groups, paraprofessionals extended into the circle, ambitious reconstruction of character replaced on paper by "social competence" because the census would not wait. By 1982 they counted hundreds of CMHCs. The number is less important than the pattern. When a law creates a demand for groups faster than a profession can train conductors, the warehouse reappears in community clothes.

Federal mental-health money later shifted toward block grants. States chose. Some centers thinned. Some became the only front door in a county. People who would once have been on a back ward were now in shelters, in jails, in family living rooms, or in a day program whose "group" was twenty chairs and a television. A heritage series that cheers 1963 without that sentence is doing public relations for a signing ceremony.

## Why a clinic still reads this

Because many people still meet a therapy group first as a program: partial hospital, intensive outpatient, a circle after lunch, a community meeting whose job is partly attendance. Those rooms descend from Jones, Main, Bierer, and a statute, not from a single textbook chapter on "cohesion." Naming the descent is not a claim that the rooms are equivalent.

Because the warehouse is a recurring design. Enlarge the group, thin the staff, call the result access. Main's "Ailment" is a warning that staff feelings will organize themselves around that design whether anyone studies them or not. Jones's book is a warning of a different kind: a method that lives on charisma and visitors will be photocopied without the visitors.

Because this is history, not a manual for turning a clinic into Henderson Hospital. Henderson, as a named institution, had its own later fate, including closure in the twenty-first century `[VERIFY]` against a current institutional history before printing a last-year date. The method's descendants are scattered: democratic therapeutic communities in Britain, milieu programs in American hospitals, day treatment that is careful, day treatment that is storage. A small-city practice does not need to be a TC. It may still need to know when a "community" is a word covering a census.
