**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 1943 the Commonwealth Fund in New York published S. R. Slavson's *An Introduction to Group Therapy*, a 352-page book about a method the Jewish Board of Guardians had been using with children since 1934. The volume is about rooms with materials in them: wood, paint, tools, a chance to make something or wreck something without a lesson plan. It is not a book of children's inner monologues. Later American group psychotherapy would talk as if the circle had always been a conversation among adults. Slavson's first public monument was a children's shop.

This essay is part of a WOW Therapies series on the history of group therapy. It is history, not a playroom manual. It will not offer child vignettes, composite patients, or a list of activities to try. The individual psychotherapy-history pack keeps the consulting-room lives of founders. This essay keeps the children's group as a profession's opening act — and as a reminder that talk is not the only old method.

## 1934, a child-guidance clinic, a recreational program

Samuel Richard Slavson was born in 1890 in Poltava, then in the Russian Empire, and reached New York in 1903. He came to the Jewish Board of Guardians from progressive education and social group work, not from a medical school. The agency, a child-guidance service for boys and girls up to eighteen, already had casework, psychiatry, and psychology. In 1934, at its Child Guidance Clinic, Slavson introduced what he first described as a creative recreational program for small groups of girls who had been called socially maladjusted. Arts, crafts, poetry, a room that was not a classroom. Staff reported that behavior eased. Slavson, reading session notes, decided the useful agent was not the object made. It was the group of children, the relative neutrality of the adult, and a climate that did not lecture.

With the backing of the agency — then led by his brother John Slawson, a different spelling and a different job — the work became a "Group Therapy" project and spread across the Board's borough clinics. Betty Gabriel's activity-interview groups, begun in 1937, added talk to activity for children who could use both. Adult groups would come. The children's room remained the shop window. Lawson G. Lowrey, editor of the *American Journal of Orthopsychiatry* and chair of the February 1943 American Orthopsychiatric Association special section on group therapy, cited a Jewish Board of Guardians study of more than a hundred children in activity group therapy and said the technique looked as effective as individual therapy. The sentence is a period claim, printed in 1943. It is not a current outcome study, and this page will not recycle it as one.

## Play, activity, and the talk that came later

American child psychotherapy already had play. Melanie Klein and Anna Freud had fought, in Europe, about what play meant in an individual analysis. Margaret Lowenfeld had a "world" technique. Those rooms were usually a child and an adult. Slavson's wager was that some children needed other children more than they needed an interpretation, and that the adult's job was to be present without becoming the teacher or the cop. Materials were a medium. Aggression could go into wood. Withdrawal could be a chair in the corner. The leader, in the theory, did not run a lesson.

That theory is easy to sentimentalize. A "permissive" adult in 1930s New York was still an agency employee in a child-saving institution, writing reports a court or a parent might feel. Consent, as a contemporary IRB would define it, is not the atmosphere of those files. Children were brought. They did not always choose the group. A historian who converts the shop into a soft-focus advertisement for "the healing power of play" has skipped the Board, the case record, and the class of families who used a Jewish child-guidance agency in the Depression and the war.

Activity group therapy was also not the only children's group. Settlement houses had clubs. Schools had classrooms. Moreno had stages. Konopka and social group work (their own essays later in this series) already believed a group could socialize, protect, and politicize an adolescent without calling the meeting psychotherapy. Slavson wanted the medical-psychoanalytic prestige anyway. He was not a physician. He spent the rest of his career making sure the association he founded looked like one.

The later split between "activity" and "talk" still structures children's services, even when nobody says Slavson's name. A play-based children's group in a community clinic, an occupational-therapy craft group on a unit, a process group for teenagers that is all chairs and no table — these are descendants that barely speak. Adult group therapy's talk bias made the children's shop look like a preface. It was, for American AGPA, closer to a founding room.

## 1943, a book and a luncheon

The same year as the Commonwealth Fund book, Slavson chalked a luncheon invitation at the Hotel New Yorker during the Orthopsychiatric meetings (the AGPA essay follows that lunch). Children's activity groups were not a side interest the profession later outgrew. They were the evidence Lowrey could quote, the practice the Jewish Board could show, and the content of the only book-length American group-therapy text some of those luncheon guests had read. Moreno had already published *Who Shall Survive?* (1934) and built Beacon. Schilder and Wender had published hospital papers. Slavson's book was the one that looked like child guidance, social work, and psychoanalysis in a single respectable binding.

After the war the method was filmed. *Activity Group Therapy*, a training film associated with the Jewish Board of Guardians, Columbia University Educational Films, and Slavson, was copyrighted in 1950. Hidden cameras, boys in a room, a claim that a club of children could work something out. The film is a primary source for historians of method and of surveillance. It is not a demonstration this educational series will describe shot by shot. Concealed recording of children is part of the file. So is the era's confidence that a camera in the wall was science.

Slavson directed group therapy at the Jewish Board until 1956. He remained a jealous guardian of psychoanalytic identity in AGPA long after children had ceased to be the association's only population. Adult outpatient groups, veterans, and then the encounter boom would crowd the syllabus. Pediatric work continued in agencies, hospitals, and schools, often under other names: activity therapy, play group, social skills (a later, more manualized cousin that belongs to the structured-groups essay). The 1943 book stayed in bibliographies as a founder’s object. Founder objects get cited more than they get read.

## Why a clinic still reads this

Because a children's group in a county clinic is still tempted by two errors. One is to run a little adult process group and call the silence "resistance." The other is to run a crafts hour and call it psychotherapy because a clinician is in the room. Slavson's file does not solve the temptation. It dates it. Materials, peers, a non-lecturing adult, an agency chart: that combination is 1934. Whether it is treatment, recreation, or custody depends on staff, consent, and purpose — administrative facts, not a vibe.

Because the profession of group therapy, in the United States, did not begin as an adult talking circle that later added kids. It began, in its organized AGPA form, with children's activity rooms and a social-work agency, then spent decades recruiting psychiatrists so the lunchroom would look like medicine. The children's essay is therefore also a profession essay. The next one but one will incorporate the association. This one keeps the table with paint on it.

Because this is not a manual for treating a child, not a set of play prescriptions, and not a substitute for a licensed clinician who actually works with minors. Composite "a boy once…" stories are banned here for the ordinary reason: they invent patients. The 1943 book has cases. A web essay does not need to reprint them.
