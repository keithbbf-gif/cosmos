**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 1933 a German-Jewish psychoanalyst named Siegmund Heinrich Fuchs left Frankfurt. The Psychoanalytic Institute there had shared a building with the Institute for Social Research. Colleagues included Karl Landauer, Erich Fromm, Frieda Fromm-Reichmann. He had been director of the outpatient clinic. The sociologists upstairs — Max Horkheimer's circle — were not his trade, but the building was an argument that a person is already social. Norbert Elias was a friend. Trigant Burrow's papers on group analysis were among the things a Frankfurt analyst could already read. Then the new German state made the reading list secondary to a passport.

He reached England after a brief stay in Geneva, at the invitation of Ernest Jones. In 1938 he became a British citizen and took the name S. H. Foulkes. Friends called him Michael. The initials are what the books carry.

This essay is part of a WOW Therapies series on the history of group therapy. It is a life. The Northfield hospital has its own essay. Foulkes is not Wilfred Bion with a milder prose style. They shared a war and a brick complex on the edge of Birmingham. They did not share a temperament, a theory, or a later school.

## Karlsruhe, Vienna, Frankfurt

He was born on 3 September 1898 in Karlsruhe. He studied medicine at Heidelberg, Munich, and Frankfurt, graduating in 1923. Neurology with Kurt Goldstein taught him Gestalt: figure against ground, the whole that is not a pile of parts. Psychiatry with Otto Pötzl in Vienna, a training analysis with Helene Deutsch, supervision with Hermann Nunberg, seminars that included Wilhelm Reich — the ordinary expensive education of an interwar analyst. In 1930 he went back to Frankfurt to run Landauer's clinic.

The Frankfurt years matter because group analysis is sometimes taught as if it were born in a British army hospital from nothing but khaki. Foulkes brought a Freudian ego psychology, a neurologist's habit of seeing wholes, and a sociologist's friendship. Karl Mannheim, another Frankfurt exile, used the phrase "group analysis" in a social-science key; Foulkes later acknowledged the priority of the term. What he would do with it was clinical.

Exile is not a seminar. He practiced in London, then in 1939 moved to Exeter, a provincial town where a refugee analyst could work and where, in relative isolation, he began to put patients into groups. The waiting-room experiment — asking people already gathered to speak — is part of the later lore. Treat the lore as lore. The dated fact is that group-analytic practice began in England, far from the institutes that had trained him, before the army posted him.

## Northfield, 1943, and a vocabulary

Hollymoor, the army's military neurosis centre, had already had Bion and Rickman's short first experiment. Foulkes arrived in 1943 as a specialist psychiatrist, a major. The hospital years are the Northfield essay's property. What belongs to the life is this: he walked into a second, longer experiment with Thomas Main, Harold Bridger, Patrick de Maré, and others, and he left it with a civilian vocabulary.

The 1946 *Lancet* paper, "Group Analysis in a Military Neurosis Centre," and the 1948 Heinemann book, *Introduction to Group-Analytic Psychotherapy*, are the documents. He preferred **conductor** to leader. He used **matrix** for the web of communications in which a remark has more than one addressee. He wanted analysis through the group, not a private hour with extra chairs. The words stuck.

He was not Bion. One man built a society and a training institute around a hopeful practice. The other wrote a bleak theory of how groups avoid work, then walked away. British group culture still argues in that split. An American residency that assigns only Yalom will not hear it.

## Society, institute, Maudsley

After the uniforms, Foulkes and colleagues founded the Group Analytic Society in London in 1952. E. James Anthony is among the names attached to that founding. The Institute of Group Analysis followed in 1971 as a training body. *Group Analysis*, the journal, became a house organ and a quarrel organ. Patrick de Maré would push toward larger rooms. Malcolm Pines would become an editor of the memory. Foulkes himself worked as a training analyst in the Anna Freud grouping of the British Psychoanalytical Society and as a consultant psychotherapist at the Maudsley Hospital, teaching psychiatrists who had not come to become group analysts and some who did.

*Therapeutic Group Analysis* (1964) restated the vocabulary: matrix, mirroring, resonance, the conductor as dynamic administrator of a situation that has to be protected as well as interpreted. The book is a theory of a practice. It is not a worksheet. Farhad Dalal and others later argued that there was a more social, less polite Foulkes buried under the clinical one. That argument is for the journal, not for a how-to.

He died on 8 July 1976 in London, during a seminar he was leading for senior colleagues. The death is of a piece with the life: he was still in a group when the work stopped.

## What this life is not

It is not a manual for sitting in the conductor's chair. History may describe that he preferred conductor to leader. History may not teach a reader to conduct. The claims file of this pack forbids the lesson.

It is also not a merger with Tavistock group relations. Bion, A. K. Rice, and the later Leicester conferences are a neighboring British industry. They share a war and a city. They do not share a clinic. Collapsing them into "Northfield" is how textbooks save a slide and lose a quarrel.

## Why a clinic still reads this

Because Fuchs-to-Foulkes is the biography inside the method. A Frankfurt clinic director became an Exeter provincial, then an army major, then the man who sat through a 1976 seminar until he died in it. The conductor was not a personality type. It was a refugee's professional name.

Because American textbooks often answer "what is the group?" with Yalom's interpersonal learning. Foulkes answered with a matrix. The disagreement is not a slide. It is two lives that shared a hospital and then refused to merge.

Because exile is part of the method's biography. Group analysis is a German-Jewish, Frankfurt-inflected, English-speaking practice. The conductor's calm was learned after a flight. That origin does not make the hour sacred. It does forbid a story in which the theory dropped from the sky over Birmingham.

Because war is an honest motive. These rooms were not founded only to grow the self. They were founded, in part, to return soldiers to a machine. Foulkes's civilian later life did not erase that. It translated it.
