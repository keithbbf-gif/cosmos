**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 1984, J. Scott Rutan and Walter N. Stone put a Boston-and-Cincinnati craft into a book with a plain title: *Psychodynamic Group Psychotherapy*. Later printings, at Guilford, grew thicker. Joseph J. Shay joined the later editions. The object on the table is still the same kind of object: a professional textbook for people already in, or entering, the American group-psychotherapy guild. It is not a consumer pamphlet. It is not a 1970 Yalom rewrite, though it lives in the same American library. This page uses that public book, and the public posts attached to it. It does not invent a birth year. A mid-century American psychologist and trainer is what the open record comfortably supports. Treat any exact year as `[VERIFY]` until a primary source is in hand.

This essay is part of a WOW Therapies series on the history of group therapy. Rutan was living at last verification (2026-09-14). Living writers are not public property. No workshop gossip. No health speculation. Public books, public lectures, and public publisher biographies only.

## A Boston address, not a myth of origins

The American group story that textbooks like to tell still begins, too often, in Pratt's tuberculosis class or in Slavson's children's rooms, then jumps to Yalom at Stanford. Boston had its own mid-to-late-century occupation of the circle: hospital teaching, institute training, a regional society that took supervision seriously. Guilford's public biography of Rutan, the one that sits beside the fifth edition (2014), names him as a psychologist in private practice in Chestnut Hill, Massachusetts; a Distinguished Fellow and past president of the American Group Psychotherapy Association; founder of the Center for Group Psychotherapy at Massachusetts General Hospital / Harvard Medical School; and a cofounder of the Boston Institute for Psychotherapy. Those are institutional claims a reader can check against the publisher, not a childhood.

The Massachusetts General address matters because it is a hospital, not a growth center. Residents who rotate through a general hospital inherit groups the way they inherit call: as work that will happen whether they have a theory or not. Rutan's public career, as the textbook and the AGPA record describe it, was to give that work a psychodynamic vocabulary that could survive an American clinic — insurance, mixed professions, a weekly hour — without pretending the room was either a British group-analytic seminar or an encounter weekend.

Anne Alonso, whose own essay belongs later in this calendar's Boston cluster, appears in the AGPA journal record as a collaborator in that same city. Bibliography for this series points to Alonso and Rutan papers on the Boston tradition in the *International Journal of Group Psychotherapy*. The tradition is a teaching culture: observation, long supervision, a preference for talking about what the group does to the therapist as well as to the patient. That is history of a school. It is not a screening checklist.

## The book that kept being rewritten

First editions are thin for a reason. The 1984 *Psychodynamic Group Psychotherapy* — catalogued from Collamore / related 1980s imprints; later the Guilford sequence — was already arguing that a therapy group is not a cheaper individual hour with extra chairs. Later editions added what American training programs asked for: more on time-limited groups, more on combined treatments, more on the fights that actually show up in a clinic (the member who never comes, the member who comes for everyone else). Shay's name on the later title pages is not a courtesy. It marks a Boston generation that inherited Rutan and Stone and had to teach in a managed-care decade.

A heritage series that treated the book as a current protocol would be doing the one thing this series refuses. The chapters on selection, composition, and leadership are craft for licensed readers in a classroom. They are dated. They argue. They do not become, on wowtherapies.com, a recipe for starting a process group. The historical fact is simpler: for several decades, if an American trainee wanted a psychodynamic group text that was not Yalom and not Foulkes, this was often the spine on the syllabus.

Stone's Cincinnati psychiatry and Rutan's Boston psychology are a useful pair on a title page. American group psychotherapy has always been a mixed shop — physicians, psychologists, social workers, later counselors — and AGPA's long argument was how to keep them in one association without dissolving the work into encounter. A past presidency of AGPA is, in that light, a political fact as much as an honor. Scheidlinger's association histories belong in the profession essay. Rutan's name belongs there as a late-century officer who also wrote the book residents actually carried.

## What the public record does not settle

Publisher pages do not replace an archive. They do not give a birth year this series will print as fact. They do not give a private life. They do not license a photograph. University and conference headshots remain copyright. `PORTRAIT_SOURCES.md` is honest: living, no-portrait, type only. Graphics will set a name, not a guessed face.

The record also does not settle a single "Boston school" the way group analysis can point to the Institute of Group Analysis. Boston had institutes, a hospital center, the Northeastern Society for Group Psychotherapy as a regional room, and a textbook that traveled farther than any one building. Readers who want a founder will be disappointed. Rutan's public usefulness is the opposite of founding: he is what a craft looks like when the founding fights are already old and the work still has to be taught on a Tuesday.

Translations of the textbook — Italian, German, Hebrew, and Chinese are among those listed on public professional pages — are a reminder that "Boston" here is an export label. Export is not proof of universal fit. It is proof that a Guilford object moved.

## Why a clinic still reads this

Because a small-city clinic that will never host an AGPA presidency still inherits the question the Rutan–Stone–Shay books kept asking in public: is the group a delivery system for individual psychodynamic work, or is the group itself the treatment? Yalom answered with interpersonal learning and a list of factors. Foulkes answered with the matrix. Rutan's American textbook answered, across editions, by staying inside a psychodynamic clinic and refusing to pretend the chairs were incidental.

Because living authors still revise. A fifth edition in 2014 is a dated event. It is not a commandment to buy the book, join a training, or treat this webpage as supervision. It is evidence that psychodynamic group work in the United States remained a written craft after the encounter boom, after the first CBT manuals, and after the profession's own research turn. The next essays in this wave leave Boston for New York modern analysis, then for social work, Argentina, and the large-group rooms that never fit a seven-to-nine-person hour.
