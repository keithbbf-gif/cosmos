A ring of chairs is older than a CPT code. People have sat in circles to pray, to drill, to mourn, to confess, to learn a trade, and to keep a sick neighbor from dying alone. The later profession of group psychotherapy needed those rooms the way a new language needs a throat. It did not invent the throat.

This essay opens a WOW Therapies series on the history of group therapy. It is not a speech-pathology history. It is not a manual for running a circle. It is also not a museum of "wise ancients" who secretly invented Yalom's factors. Most of what follows would not be recognizable as clinical work. That is the point.

## Congregations and the problem of the witness

A congregation is a group with a text and a specialist. The specialist may be a priest, an imam, a rabbi, an elder. The text may be read aloud. The bodies face the same way, or they face one another. Historians of psychotherapy sometimes flatten all of this into "early group therapy" because someone cried and someone else listened. That flattening is flattery. A liturgy is not a process group. The rules of speech are public, inherited, and often gendered. A person may be forbidden to speak. A person may be required to speak. Confidentiality, if it exists, is a seal or a shame, not a license law.

What a later clinician can honestly borrow from this long record is modest. Human beings already knew that a trouble said in front of others is a different trouble than a trouble said to one paid stranger. They also knew that a room can punish. Public penance, shunning, and revival benches are part of the same family as the circle. Anyone who tells the Boston origin story without the bench is selling a cleaner past.

Military formations are another ancestor that group textbooks mention and then drop. Drill is a group technology. So is the after-action talk, the mess, the hospital tent. Twentieth-century army psychiatry would later make those rooms explicit (Northfield, the leaderless group, the therapeutic community). The army did not wait for a journal to discover that men change each other.

## Wards, classes, and the counted body

Hospitals sat patients in rows before they sat them in rings. A teaching ward is already a group: one body, many eyes, a doctor who narrates. Charcot's Friday lessons at the Salpêtrière were spectacle. They were also a claim that a room of witnesses could stabilize a diagnosis. Group psychotherapy would later spend a century arguing about whether the witness heals or harms. The argument is older than Pratt.

Classrooms matter too. A tuberculosis class, a Sunday school, a settlement-house cooking lesson, a union hall — each is a pedagogy with attendance. Joseph Pratt's 1905–1907 Boston work, the next essay in this series, grew out of a medical clinic that already thought in classes. He did not walk into a void. He walked into a city that knew how to take roll.

Jane Addams's Hull-House, opened in 1889 on South Halsted Street in Chicago, is the American settlement most historians can still name. *Twenty Years at Hull-House* (1910) describes clubs, reading rooms, a labor museum, and the daily traffic of neighbors who were not patients. Social group work would later write its own canon (Konopka, Schwartz). The settlement is not a preface to AGPA. It is a different profession that already believed a group could teach, feed, and politicize. When psychotherapy later claimed the circle, social work had been in it for decades.

## Fellowships that refused the clinic

Mutual-aid societies — burial clubs, immigrant lodges, temperance meetings, the later Alcoholics Anonymous (1935) — organized help without a license. They had rules. They had stories. They often had a chairperson who was not a doctor. The clinic would spend the rest of the century deciding whether those rooms were "really" therapy. The rooms, in many cases, declined the compliment.

Indigenous talking circles, palavers, and council forms are likewise not a preface to a billing code. They are their own histories: land, kinship, specialists whose authority did not come from a university in Boston or Vienna. A heritage series that steals a ceremony as an "origin of group therapy" is doing colonial work with a smile. This pack names the theft as a temptation and leaves the ceremonies to the people who hold them.

## What is missing: the hour, the chart, the factor

Three things that define a contemporary therapy group are scarce in this older record.

The **hour** as a commodity — ninety minutes, a fee, a closed membership, a note — is a creature of clinics, insurance, and the professions that grew after 1900.

The **chart** — the sentence that will be read by another clinician, an auditor, or a patient with a right of access — did not exist in a lodge hall. Minutes of a society are not a mental-health record.

The **factor** — Yalom's later list, cohesion research, a claim that "universality" is a mechanism — is a mid-century research habit. Older rooms had hope and humiliation. They did not publish *Encounter Groups: First Facts*.

Without those three, you can have wisdom, cruelty, hospitality, and neglect. You do not yet have group psychotherapy as a job.

## Why a clinic still reads this

A person who walks into a group room in Southeast Arkansas did not invent sitting with others last year. They inherited churches, locker rooms, break rooms, family tables, and, if they have been in a hospital, a dayroom with a television that no one chose. Some of those rooms were kind. Some were a court.

The useful inheritance from this long preface is a warning dressed as a courtesy. The circle is powerful because it is ordinary. A clinic that forgets the ordinary — the roll call, the gossip, the person who never speaks, the person who speaks for everyone — will treat group as a cheaper hour and then wonder why the hour bites.

The next essay leaves the long preface and enters a Boston outpatient clinic in the first decade of the twentieth century, where a physician asked consumptives to keep hourly diaries and to meet as a class. He thought he was fighting tuberculosis. Later textbooks thought he was founding them.

## A few rooms with dates, so the preface does not float

If the long past is left unnamed, a reader will fill it with a slogan. Better to put a few dated objects on the table and then stop.

John Wesley's class meetings, organized among Methodists in the eighteenth century, sat a dozen people with a leader and a weekly inquiry into conduct. They were not a clinic. They were a discipline. Historians of mutual aid sometimes list them because the form — regular attendance, speech about the self, a group that remembers last week — is easy to recognize. Recognition is not descent. A later therapy group that quotes Wesley as an ancestor is doing Protestant interior decorating.

The York Retreat, opened by William Tuke's Quaker circle in 1796, is usually filed under moral treatment and asylums (the individual-history pack has that room). It belongs here only as a reminder that a household of the distressed was already a group with meals, work, and a watchful kindness that could also be a watchful control. Pinel's Bicêtre and Salpêtrière reforms, whatever one thinks of the later paintings, were likewise institutional group life before they were myths of unchaining.

Nineteenth-century American asylums ran amusements, chapel, and "occupations" in rooms that later activity therapists would claim. The claim is half true. The other half is census: a superintendent needed the day filled. When S. R. Slavson later built children's activity groups at the Jewish Board of Guardians, he was in conversation with play and with psychoanalysis, not with a sudden invention of sitting together. The sitting was already the hospital's cheapest instrument.

Labor unions and strike kitchens, Hull-House clubs, and the foreign-language lodges of Chicago and New York are the civic cousins. Addams wrote them as education and politics. A psychotherapy syllabus that steals them as "group process" has reversed the debt. The settlement taught the clinic that a neighborhood already knew how to meet. The clinic did not teach the neighborhood how to be a neighborhood.

## What this series will not do with the preface

It will not print a timeline that runs "shaman → church → Pratt → Yalom" as if those were promotions in one company. It will not use a talking circle, a sweat lodge, or a palaver as a decorative origin. It will not tell a reader that their Sunday school was secretly therapy. The useful sentence is narrower. Before there was a profession, there were rooms. The profession inherited their power and their capacity to shame. Everything after 1905 is an argument about who may charge for the inheritance.
