**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

The years between the Armistice and the next mobilization were crowded with hospital experiments that later syllabi compress into a single slide: "precursors." The slide is lazy. A 1931 paper that compares a ward meeting to a revival is not the same object as a 1938 chapter that tries to do psychoanalysis with several people in the room. Both are group history. They do not want the same thing.

This essay is part of a WOW Therapies series on the history of group therapy. It describes rooms. It does not offer a ward protocol.

## Why hospitals tried it

The arithmetic was ugly. State hospitals and urban psychiatric wards held more people than a physician could see. Insulin, malaria therapy, and later electroconvulsive treatment had their own decades. Talk, when it happened, was scarce. A group let a doctor address twenty people at once. Efficiency is not a dirty word if you say it plainly. It becomes dirty when a later brochure pretends the motive was always "the healing power of the group."

Louis Wender, working in New York hospital settings in the 1930s, wrote about groups as a way to reach patients who would not, or could not, use an individual hour. Paul Schilder, Viennese, then at Bellevue after he came to the United States, put group work inside a book titled simply *Psychotherapy* (1938). Schilder had already written on the body image and on the way a person inhabits a body other people can see. A group, for him, was not only cheaper. It was a place where the seen body and the spoken life could be argued with in public.

L. Cody Marsh published in *Mental Hygiene* in 1931 on "the group treatment of the psychoses by the psychological equivalent of the revival." The title is a period piece and a method. Marsh wanted the heat of a religious meeting without, or not only, the theology: testimony, hope, a crowd that answers. Later process-group teachers would wince. Historians should not. The American group tradition has a revival bone. It also has a lecture bone. Both were visible before the war.

## Lectures, classes, and the physician who talks

Some interwar "groups" were lectures. The doctor spoke. Patients listened. Questions, if allowed, came at the end. Pratt's class method is in this family even when the later guild files him under psychotherapy. So are the didactic groups that still exist on medical units: a nurse explains a medication, a social worker explains a discharge. To call every lecture a therapy group is to empty the word. To pretend therapy groups have no lecture in them is to lie about Mondays.

The interesting interwar work sits in the middle. A physician begins with a topic — fear, a symptom, a hospital rule — and then the room answers back. Schilder could sound like a seminar. Marsh could sound like a tent. Wender could sound like administration with a conscience. None of them had Yalom's later vocabulary. They had wards, attendants, and a hope that talk in public would loosen something talk in private had not.

European clinics were not waiting for New York. Trigant Burrow had already been arguing, from the 1920s, that neurosis was a social process and that the analytic pair was too small a sample (his own essay is later in this series). Moreno had already opened rooms in Vienna and then in Beacon that did not look like wards at all. The hospital tradition and the theater tradition would spend the 1940s and 1950s calling each other unserious. The quarrel is part of the history. It is not a problem this series needs to settle.

## What the notes do not show

We do not have, for most of these rooms, the kind of recording Carl Rogers later made of an individual hour. We have papers, a few hospital reports, and the memories of people who became founders. The patient who sat in Marsh's revival-equivalent and felt humiliated is harder to find in the file. A heritage series that only reprints the physician's optimism is doing public relations.

We also do not have a clean line from these wards to "evidence-based group CBT." The interwar hospital group was often mixed-diagnosis, involuntary, and short on consent as a contemporary IRB would define it. Later outpatient groups would inherit the form and quietly change the population: people who could arrive on time and leave.

## War as the next laboratory

The Second World War did not invent group treatment. It funded it, uniformed it, and then published it in *The Lancet*. Northfield, Bion and Rickman, Foulkes, Main, Bridger — those names belong to the next essays. What the interwar hospitals left them was a permission structure: it was already thinkable to put patients in a room and call the room treatment.

They also left a temptation. If a group can treat a ward, a manager can enlarge the ward. Every later generation of group therapists has had to fight the warehouse. The fight is not theoretical. It is a staffing ratio.

## Why a clinic still reads this

Because the hospital is still where many people first meet a therapy group: a unit, a partial hospital, a day program, a circle after lunch. The interwar file says that this meeting is old, that it has always been partly about census, and that census is not an insult if you refuse to let it be the only theory.

It also says that American group work has more than one accent. Revival, lecture, and analysis were already in the building before anyone printed "therapeutic factors." A student who learns only the factors will not hear the tent or the podium when they show up.

## Names the slide usually drops

Edward Lazell, working with psychotic patients in Washington in the 1920s, lectured to groups on psychoanalytic ideas when individual analysis was a fantasy of staffing. The lectures are easy to mock from a later process-group chair. They are also a document of what a hospital could do with one physician and many beds. Trigant Burrow's experimental communities, which have their own essay, were not wards and did not want to be. Putting him on the same slide as Lazell is how a syllabus lies.

In Britain, Joshua Bierer would later push the day hospital and social clubs as alternatives to the long stay; Maxwell Jones would name a therapeutic community. Those are postwar names. The interwar file is their permission structure: it was already thinkable that the meeting, not only the tablet or the coma, was a treatment. Thinkable is not the same as funded. Superintendents still counted sheets.

New York's hospitals, because they sat next to institutes and next to publishers, wrote more of what Americans later read. That is a bias in the record, not a proof that Cleveland or New Orleans lacked rooms. A heritage series based in Southeast Arkansas should say the bias out loud. Most group history is still a history of cities that printed journals.

Schilder's death in 1940, in an accident in New York, cut off one of the few European-trained voices who had tried to put group work inside a general psychotherapy book rather than a manifesto. The war then printed its own manifestos. The interwar hospital group became, in memory, a preface. Prefaces get skipped. This one should not be. It is where census and talk first signed a contract that later outpatient groups still live under, even when they pretend they chose each other.
