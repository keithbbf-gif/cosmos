In 1938 W. W. Norton published Paul Schilder's *Psychotherapy*. It is not a group-therapy textbook. It is a psychiatrist's book that happens to contain, among other rooms, the hospital group: a physician who begins with a topic — fear, a symptom, a hospital rule — and then lets the ward answer back. Schilder had already written the work he valued most among his later books, *The Image and Appearance of the Human Body* (1935). A group, for him, was not only cheaper than an hour. It was a place where the seen body and the spoken life could be argued with in public.

He had two years left. In December 1940, in New York, he was killed after being struck by a car. He was fifty-four. The American association that would later elect medical grandfathers was still a rumor. Schilder would be filed, when he was filed, among the interwar hospital names: Wender, Marsh, a Viennese at Bellevue.

This essay is part of a WOW Therapies series on the history of group therapy. It is a life. The interwar hospital essay in this pack is the wider ward. Schilder is the neurologist-analyst who did not live to see AGPA, Yalom, or the argument about therapeutic factors.

## Vienna: neurology, philosophy, a society

Paul Ferdinand Schilder was born in Vienna in 1886. Sources disagree on 2 February versus 15 February; the year is not in doubt. `[VERIFY]` the day against a library necrology if a caption needs it. He took his M.D. at the University of Vienna in 1909 and, after army service in the First World War, a Ph.D. in philosophy in 1917. From 1918 he worked under Julius Wagner-Jauregg at the Psychiatric-Neurological Clinic. He became *Privatdozent* in 1920 and professor extraordinarius in 1925. He was a member of the Vienna Psychoanalytic Society. He also published on his own hook, including doubts about a death drive. He was the sort of analyst who remained a neurologist.

Body image is the idea that made his name travel. He assembled Carl Wernicke's somatopsychic, Henry Head's postural model, and Freud's hint that the ego is first a body ego. The result was not a slogan. It was a claim that a person inhabits a body other people can see, and that the image of that body is built from perceptions, affects, fantasies, and the gaze of others. *The Image and Appearance of the Human Body* is the 1935 statement. Later clinicians in Britain and France took the notion into their own vocabularies. The group implication is simple to state and hard to live: other people are already inside the body a person thinks is private.

In 1928 and 1930 he taught as a visitor at Johns Hopkins, in Adolf Meyer's world. In 1929–30 he left Vienna for New York. From 1930 he was medical director of the psychiatric division of Bellevue Hospital and a research professor at New York University's College of Medicine. In April 1938, after the Anschluss, the University of Vienna stripped him — already gone, and Jewish — of his right to teach. The paper from Vienna arrived in a life that had already changed countries.

## Bellevue, a book, a ward

Bellevue in the 1930s was census and attendants. Wender, elsewhere in New York, wrote about groups for patients who would not or could not use an hour. Marsh, in 1931, wanted the heat of a revival. Schilder's accent was the seminar: a neurologist-analyst putting a topic on the table and letting the ward argue. The interwar hospital essay in this pack holds the three accents together. This life holds the Viennese who sounded like a lecture that could still be answered.

*Psychotherapy* (1938) is the book to put on the table. It gathers a clinician's method at the end of a Viennese career and the beginning of an American one. Group work sits inside it, not on the cover. That placement is honest. He was not building a guild. He was treating a hospital.

With Lauretta Bender, whom he married in 1937, he also worked with psychotic children. Their joint names belong to child psychiatry as much as to group history. This series will not reconstruct those rooms. Children's faces are not a lead image. The historical fact is enough: a Bellevue director thought a group was usable with patients other textbooks still called unreachable.

He resigned from the New York Psychoanalytic Society in 1937. The American psychoanalytic politics of that decade are a different pack's fight. He kept writing. Bibliographies count his papers in the hundreds. The group profession, when it later needed hospital ancestors, found him because he had written in English, in New York, in a book a library already owned.

Death in December 1940 was an accident after a hospital visit to his wife and a newborn daughter. Encyclopedias say 7 December; a University of Vienna memorial uses 8 December. `[VERIFY]` if a plaque needs a day. The year is the point. He did not see the war's Northfield papers. He did not see Slavson's association. He did not see the T-group. The interwar hospital permission structure — it was already thinkable to put patients in a room and call the room treatment — is part of what he left. So is the temptation that came with it: if a group can treat a ward, a manager can enlarge the ward.

## What this life is not

It is not a claim that he invented group therapy. Pratt's class was a generation earlier and was not psychotherapy. Burrow's social theory was already in print. Moreno wanted a stage. Schilder ran hospital groups and wrote them into a general book on psychotherapy. The later honorary triad (Pratt, Burrow, Schilder) is a cataloguing habit.

It is not a body-image workbook. The 1935 book is a theoretical and clinical monograph. This page will not excerpt exercises, postures, or anything a reader could do to a body in a mirror and call treatment.

It is not a full Vienna-to-Freud biography. The consulting-room pack may keep the psychoanalytic Schilder. Here he is the hospital group and the seen body.

## Why a clinic still reads this

Because many people still meet a therapy group first in a hospital: a unit, a partial program, a circle after lunch. Schilder is the interwar name who took that meeting seriously without reducing it to a revival or a lecture. A county unit that has never read him still sits in his problem: the body in the chair is being seen.

Because he died before the brand. Founders who survive get textbooks named after them. Schilder got a chapter in other people's histories. Reading the 1938 book is a way to hear a hospital voice that has not yet learned "therapeutic factors."

Because body image, in the later culture, became a slogan. Schilder's version was social, neurological, and psychoanalytic at once. A group is one of the places that version can be tested. This series will not run the test. It will say that the test is old.
