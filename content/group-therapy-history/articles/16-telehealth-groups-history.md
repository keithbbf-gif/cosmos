**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 1993 *Social Work with Groups* published a paper by Lori S. Wiener, E. D. Spencer, R. Davidson, and C. Fair on national telephone support groups for HIV-infected children and their families. The groups had no waiting room and no parking lot. They had a conference line, a scheduled hour, and the particular American geography of an epidemic that had already taught clinics to think about distance, stigma, and who could not walk into a building. The paper is a dated object. It is not a script for a call.

This essay is part of a WOW Therapies series on the history of group therapy. It is a short history of groups on a wire: telephone in the 1990s, forums in the 2000s, a pandemic shift in 2020 that turned a niche into a default. It will not teach anyone how to run an online group. It will not praise a brand of software. The individual psychotherapy-history pack may keep the tele-hour between two people. This essay keeps the circle when the circle is a roster of phone numbers.

## Before video, a line

Telephone was already a mental-health technology. Crisis lines from the 1960s onward had made a voice without a face ordinary. That is not group therapy. It is the infrastructure group therapy later borrowed. By the 1990s, conference calling was cheap enough for agencies to try scheduled groups: oncology, HIV, rural parents, people whose illness or whose town made a weekly drive a fantasy. Wiener's 1993 paper is one document in a small stack. Lazar and Erera's 1998 essay on the telephone helpline as social support is another, looking at the line as a form rather than as a gadget.

What those rooms had, historically, was sound without bodies. Cohesion researchers later worried about that (see the research essay): the we-feeling that lives in a glance, a shift in chairs, a silence you can see. Telephone groups had silences you could only hear. They also had access. A historian who treats 2020 as year zero of remote groups is erasing a literature that social workers, nurses, and AIDS-service organizations wrote when videoconferencing was still a corporate luxury.

AA and other fellowships used phones too — "twelfth-step" calls, later "meeting by phone" as a named format — without calling the result telehealth. The fellowship's refusal to be therapy (see the AA essay) continued on the line. Clinics that ran telephone therapy groups were doing something legally different: a professional service, a chart, a license in a jurisdiction. The wire did not settle the old identity quarrel. It carried it.

## Forums, listservs, and the 2000s public

Daniel Q. Huang's 2022 paper "Cyber Solace" reconstructed *alt.support.depression*, a Usenet forum of the 1990s whose readership, at a peak, was estimated in the tens of thousands, with hundreds of posts in a day. Lay expertise, consumer-survivor talk, the unfinished manners of early internet culture: it was a group in the anthropological sense and not a therapy group in the AGPA sense. No one was licensed to conduct it. No one could promise confidentiality in a medium built to copy.

The 2000s moved that public onto the web: bulletin boards, commercial "communities," email listservs, later blogs and social networks. Some spaces were moderated by patients. Some were marketing. Some mixed both until the mix was the point. Clinicians argued, in print, about whether lurking in a forum was care, harm, or a new kind of waiting room. The American Psychological Association's later telepsychology guidelines (2013 is a dated professional marker) were still mostly thinking about the pairwise hour. Group lagged, as it often does, because a group on a screen is a licensing puzzle raised to the power of the roster.

Privacy law was already on the books. The Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act of 1996 created a federal vocabulary for protected health information in the United States. It did not, in 1996, imagine a member joining a therapy group from a kitchen while a roommate filmed the laptop. Forums were often outside HIPAA's clinic-shaped fence. That gap is historical, not a how-to for encryption.

## 2020, as history

In March 2020, as SARS-CoV-2 shut clinics, American Medicare and many private insurers expanded payment for telehealth. State emergency orders loosened, temporarily, some rules about where a patient could sit and which technologies a provider could use. The federal Office for Civil Rights announced enforcement discretion for certain telehealth privacy violations — an administrative event, not a repeal of HIPAA. Overnight, groups that had been chairs in a room were rectangles on a screen. AGPA and other guilds issued guidance. Special issues and books followed (Haim Weinberg and Arnon Rolnick's *Theory and Practice of Online Therapy*, 2020, is a dated title in that rush). This series will not extract their methods.

The shift was not a miracle of a particular commercial platform. Several products existed. Clinics used whatever their hospital already had, or whatever a member could install. Brand worship is a press release. The historical facts are uglier and more useful: unequal broadband, members who could not find a private room, children and partners audible off-camera, people who disappeared when the video froze and were later found to have been in crisis, people who would never have entered a building and finally entered a grid of faces.

Casualties, after Lieberman, Yalom, and Miles, were not a surprise in theory. They were hard to count in a year when everything hurt at once. Some groups held. Some dissolved. Some members were safer at home; some were less safe. A heritage series that prints only "access expanded" has chosen a press conference.

When emergency orders lapsed, the argument remained: which groups should stay remote, who is licensed to see whom, whether a hybrid circle is a form or a mess. Those are live policy fights. This page is not a position paper. It is a marker that 2020 was a break in delivery, not the invention of the remote group.

## Licensing and privacy as historical problems

American psychotherapy is licensed by states. A group whose members sit in two states has always been, on paper, two possible unauthorized practices. Telephone groups in the 1990s already knew this and often restricted enrollment or pretended geography was a courtesy. 2020 made the pretense impossible to maintain. Compacts such as PSYPACT (psychology's interstate agreement, enacted by states over the 2010s and operational around the turn of the 2020s) `[VERIFY]` exact operational year against the compact commission before printing a first-state date — are a legal patch, not a clinical theory. Social work, counseling, and medicine had their own patchworks. A video group did not create the patchwork. It displayed it.

Privacy is the twin problem. A closed therapy group in a building can still gossip in the parking lot. A closed therapy group on a wire can be recorded, screenshotted, joined by the wrong household member, or hosted on a product whose vendor is the real archivist. HIPAA, state confidentiality statutes, and professional codes were written for offices and charts. They were stretched over conference calls and then over video. The stretch marks are the history. A page that offers "tips for a confidential online group" would be a manual. This series does not publish one.

If you are in danger, call local emergency services. That is the whole crisis sentence. No safety-planning protocol belongs here.

## Why a clinic still reads this

Because patients now arrive with a decade that includes both a fluorescent dayroom and a rectangle at the kitchen table, and they will talk as if those were the same group. Sometimes the membership was the same. The medium was not. Telephone, forum, and video each hide a different part of the animal Bion and Yalom tried to name — bodies, reputations, the glance that tells you who is about to leave.

Because the wire does not abolish the old fights. AA is still not therapy on a phone. CR is still not a protocol on a forum. A CBT manual is still a manual when the homework is a file. A process group is still a process group when the "here-and-now" is a lagging connection. Medium is not method. 2020 confused them because everything happened at once.

Because this is history, not a telehealth implementation guide, not software advice, and not a substitute for a licensed clinician who is actually allowed to treat the person in that chair — wherever the chair is. Complementary to the individual-hour pack, which can tell the story of the pairwise screen. Complementary to SLPWOW, which has its own telepractice history in speech-language pathology. Do not merge the calendars.
