**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

*No licensed still. Type only: Morton A. Lieberman, 1931–2019.*

In 1973, Basic Books published *Encounter Groups: First Facts*, by Morton A. Lieberman, Irvin D. Yalom, and Matthew B. Miles. The study had watched a set of groups with different leaders and styles, and it had looked for people who got worse. The authors used the word *casualty*. They meant it to be ugly. American encounter culture had been selling heat as a vitamin. The monograph is the document this pack will not let the brochure eat.

This essay is part of a WOW Therapies series on the history of group therapy. It is a portrait of the researcher who already knew, before the boom's loudest years, that a group is a process that can be described, and who then measured a fashion. It will not teach an encounter exercise. It will not tell anyone to join or avoid a weekend.

## Chicago, Whitaker, a 1964 book

Lieberman received a Ph.D. in social psychology from the University of Chicago in 1956 and stayed there for roughly a quarter century as a clinician and faculty member in psychiatry and human development. In 1964 he published, with Dorothy Stock Whitaker, *Psychotherapy through the Group Process* (Atherton). Whitaker has her own essay later in this pack. The 1964 book is an attempt to talk about therapy groups as groups — focal conflict, the group's own work — rather than as a cheap circle of individual hours. British readers sometimes hear an echo of Foulkes. The Chicago book is its own object: American, process-minded, written before encounter had become a market.

That sequence matters. Lieberman did not arrive at casualties as a scold from outside the field. He had already spent a decade thinking about how a group produces a shared problem and a shared attempt to solve it. When the 1960s offered marathons, T-group descendants, and leaders with uneven training, he had a researcher's appetite and a clinician's memory. Visiting appointments later included Stanford — the Yalom collaboration is not a random pairing — Wisconsin, and the Tavistock Institute. In 1982 he moved to the University of California, San Francisco, and turned much of his later career toward aging, widowhood, Alzheimer's services, and self-help groups as social phenomena. *Self-Help Groups for Coping with Crises* (1979) sits on that shelf. So do books on institutionalization of the old. The man who counted encounter casualties also counted what happens when a society warehouses people and then calls the warehouse care.

UCSF published an in-memoriam notice on 25 February 2019, noting a funeral on the 27th at Fernwood Cemetery in Mill Valley and an age of 87. This pack now prints 1931–2019 from that memorial, not from a later web echo that had drifted to 2022. He had retired from UCSF in 2006 as professor emeritus and was still publishing into the 2010s, including work on internet groups for people facing illness. That late research is a footnote here. The historical Lieberman of this series is the 1964 theorist and the 1973 counter.

## First Facts, without a protocol

The 1973 book did several things at once. It treated encounter groups as a family of practices, not as one sacrament. It compared leaders. It asked about benefit, no change, and harm. Aggressive, intrusive leadership, in their sample, went with more harm. That sentence has been repeated until it sounds like folklore. It was an empirical claim about particular groups, measures, and a moment. Later reviewers argued about generalizability. They did not make the injured participants disappear.

Lieberman liked the phrase *change induction groups* for a wider set: therapy groups, T-groups, encounter, some self-help rooms. The phrase is cold on purpose. It refuses the idea that a circle is automatically a clinic or automatically a church. A group induces change, including change nobody wanted. *First Facts* is the adult document of the encounter essay in this series. The figure essay exists so that Lieberman's name is not only the middle of a citation. He was first author. The design bears his Chicago habits: count, compare, do not believe the leader's press.

What the book is not: a guide to running a safer marathon. A heritage website that excerpted "leader style" as tips would be doing the thing the book was written against — turning other people's injury into a workshop handout. History may say that style mattered in their data. History may not offer a style to copy.

The encounter boom receded. Insurance and licensure and a more anxious university did some of the receding. Lieberman moved, in print, toward self-help and aging, where another American faith lives: that sitting with people who share a burden is obviously good. Sometimes it is. His career is a long argument that "obviously" is not a method.

## Why a clinic still reads this

Because patients still arrive with a story of a circle that helped and a story of a circle that used them. 1973 is not the only year those stories exist, but it is the year a major press printed a research book that used the word *casualty* on the same shelf as Yalom's 1970 textbook. The two books should be taught as a pair. Training programs that assign only the textbook are assigning hope without the invoice.

Because Whitaker and Lieberman 1964 is a reminder that American group theory did not begin when a Stanford psychiatrist wrote a bestseller. Chicago social psychology was already in the room. The later Dorothy Stock Whitaker essay will return to focal conflict. Here the point is partnership: a woman and a man wrote a process book nine years before *First Facts*. The boom did not invent process. It industrialized it.

Because research is a moral act when the subject is harm. Lieberman's later gerontology can look like a subject change. It is the same appetite: what does a setting do to a person who cannot easily leave. A therapy group in a small city is a setting. People can leave, usually. The leaving is part of the ethics. *First Facts* is what happens in the historical record when leaving was harder than the brochure admitted.
