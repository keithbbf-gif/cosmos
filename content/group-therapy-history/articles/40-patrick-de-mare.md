**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Patrick Baltzar de Maré was born in London in 1916 and died in 2008. In the last years of the Second World War he worked at the military neurosis centre at Hollymoor, Northfield, in the long second experiment that also holds S. H. Foulkes, Thomas Main, and Harold Bridger. After the uniforms came off he became a consultant psychotherapist at St George's and a builder of the civilian group-analytic institutions that still occupy London: the Group Analytic Practice, the Group Analytic Society, later the Institute of Group Analysis. His distinctive claim, written most fully in *Koinonia: From Hate, through Dialogue, to Culture in the Large Group* (Karnac, 1991), with Robin Piper and Sheila Thompson, was that the small analytic group was not the end of the method. Size changes the object. Dialogue in a larger room is not the same work as family transference in eight chairs.

This essay is part of a WOW Therapies series on the history of group therapy. It will describe median and large groups as historical forms. It will not teach anyone how to conduct one.

## Northfield, then the small-group shop

He qualified in medicine from St George's in 1941, entered the Royal Army Medical Corps, and ran an exhaustion centre before returning to Northfield. Tom Harrison's *Bion, Rickman, Foulkes and the Northfield Experiments* (2000) is the book that keeps the hospital arguments from collapsing into a single heroic week. de Maré's place in that cast is not Bion's six-week first experiment and not Foulkes's conductor. He is in the second, institutional experiment: the hospital as a group of groups. Main would carry "therapeutic community" into peacetime. de Maré would keep pushing the *size* of the talking room.

After the war he worked at St George's and at Halliwick. With Foulkes he belonged to the cluster that made a private group-analytic practice in London. Published dates for that practice's founding wander between 1960 and 1964 depending on the obituary and the later IGA memoir. `[VERIFY]` the year against an institutional minute if you need a cornerstone. The Institute of Group Analysis, as a training body, is usually dated 1971–1973 in the same literature. de Maré's 1972 book, *Perspectives in Group Psychotherapy: A Theoretical Background* (Allen & Unwin), is already reaching for sociology, communication, and systems, as if the small group could not hold the questions he wanted to ask. With Lionel Kreeger he published *Introduction to Group Treatment in Psychiatry* (Butterworths, 1974), a short hospital-facing text. Those dates are enough to show a man who did not wait until retirement to leave the family-sized circle.

## Median, large, and a Greek word

In 1975 he began a large group under Institute of Group Analysis auspices. In 1976 Piper joined him. The membership settled, in later tellings, around twenty, and the word *median* entered the shop: larger than the classic small group, smaller than the conference crowd of fifty or a hundred. A 1984 seminar on large groups became, in 1986, part of a Large Group Section of the Group Analytic Society. The arithmetic is less important than the argument. de Maré held that the small group socializes the individual — it is still haunted by the family — while the median and large group can humanize a society, or at least let citizenship, class, and hatred appear as themselves rather than as mother and father.

*Koinonia* (1991) is the book in which that argument is fully staged. The subtitle is a sequence, not a promise of comfort: from hate, through dialogue, to culture in the large group. *Koinonia* is borrowed from Greek for a fellowship that is impersonal — amity without the intimacy the small group sells. Lionel Kreeger, writing later in *Group Analysis*, glossed the word as a culture of togetherness that is not personal friendship. This page will not improve on that gloss with a how-to. Hate, in de Maré's sequence, is not a malfunction to be soothed away at the door. It is what a larger room first produces when people cannot hide in pair-talk. Dialogue is the work of transforming that heat into a speaking culture. Whether a given large group ever gets there is an empirical and ethical question later conductors still fight about. Lieberman, Yalom, and Miles (1973) had already counted casualties in a different, hotter American form. de Maré's rooms were not Esalen. They still had bodies, silence, and the possibility of harm.

Do not take the above as instructions. No seating plan. No "go-round." No advice to found a median group in a church hall.

## Dialogue against small-group analysis — as a quarrel, not a coup

Group analysis after Foulkes already believed the group was the therapist. de Maré did not need to invent that sentence. What he pressed, against his own school, was that the small group's unconscious is still too familial, and that psychoanalysis-in-a-circle cannot empirically handle the socio-cultural field the larger group makes visible. Inner objects, he argued in *Koinonia*, meet systems and structures in a room that is no longer a household. Critics inside group analysis heard grandiosity, politics dressed as method, or a simply unworkable size. The quarrel is the history. A heritage series that declared a winner would be doing fandom.

Malcolm Pines, the next figure in this wave, would become the school's editor and interpreter, keeping Foulkes readable for people who never sat at Northfield. de Maré is the sibling who kept enlarging the furniture. They shared a London. They did not share a preferred diameter.

## Why a clinic still reads this

Because many "groups" a contemporary patient actually meets are already larger than eight: a day program, a community meeting, a psychoeducation class, a hospital community. Calling every one of them a therapy group is sloppy. Ignoring that size changes speech is sloppy in the other direction. de Maré named the large room as a different culture. A clinic can take the naming without importing a 1975 IGA experiment into a county conference room.

Because hate in a crowd is not a metaphor. The 1991 sequence — hate, dialogue, culture — is a theory with a date, written while Europe was still counting its late-century nationalisms. It is not a civic workshop. The next essay stays in the same London house and watches Malcolm Pines edit the memory of the school so that Foulkes could travel.
