**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

The 31 August 1907 issue of *JAMA* carried a paper by Joseph Hersey Pratt whose title still refuses the later brochure: "The Class Method of Treating Consumption in the Homes of the Poor." He was an internist in the outpatient department of the Massachusetts General Hospital. The patients were poor, many of them Irish immigrants, and they had pulmonary tuberculosis. State sanatorium beds were scarce or priced out of reach. William Osler had already argued, in 1900, that most consumptives would have to be treated at home if they were to be treated at all. Pratt tried to build a home sanatorium in a tenement: rest, outdoor air, an hourly diary, a visitor, and a weekly class.

He was thirty-four. He was not inventing a process group. He was trying to keep people on a regimen copied from expensive air.

This essay is part of a WOW Therapies series on the history of group therapy. It is a life of a Boston physician. The tuberculosis class as an origin story has its own essay in this series. The two should not be collapsed. Pratt did not walk into a void in 1905 and found a profession. Mutual aid, church meetings, and hospital wards already knew how to sit people together. What he put in print was a physician's class.

## Hopkins, Osler, and a Boston internist

Pratt was born on 5 December 1872 in North Middleborough, Massachusetts, south of Boston. He began medical school at Harvard, then transferred to the new Johns Hopkins Medical School. He took his M.D. in 1898 with the second Hopkins class. The teacher who marked him was William Osler. Half a century later Pratt still had the student notes. He published them in 1949 as *A Year with Osler, 1896–1897*.

The discipleship was real and a little costly. Pratt measured Boston against Baltimore and did not always hide the comparison. He worked in pathology with William Councilman, counted autopsies in the hundreds, and, on Osler's advice, spent part of 1902 with Ludolph Krehl at Tübingen. He came home wanting the laboratory and the ward in the same week. He opened a consulting practice while drawing a thin outpatient salary at Massachusetts General and keeping animals for pancreatic and biliary work largely at his own expense. He published on platelets, typhoid, gout, heart disease. In 1908 he declined a chair at Michigan. Harvard did not keep him. His appointment there faded around 1917. Later he asked Councilman why he had not risen. The answer, as Charles Ambrose later reported it from Pratt's own telling, was blunt: they did not like him.

During the First World War he screened recruits for tuberculosis. He remained an internist. The later guild that wanted a medical ancestor liked that fact more than it liked the tents.

## Emmanuel Church, and what the class was

Around 1904 he began gathering consumptive outpatients into a home-treatment program. By 1905 the weekly meeting had an address: Emmanuel Church, Boston, where the rector, Elwood Worcester, helped pay for a "friendly visitor" — a prototype of the medical social worker — and for the tents and blankets a poor household needed if it was going to imitate Saranac on a roof. Pratt ran the tuberculosis class for years; church histories say eighteen. He judged success the way a chest physician of his decade could: fever gone, weight up, a return to work.

The 1907 *JAMA* paper is about wages, blankets, and the people who said they only needed a tonic for the cough. The weekly meeting is in the paper. Mutual encouragement is in it too, as something he noticed when the plan was rest and air. Patients who had already endured cold nights told newer ones to persist. Later textbooks reprint that notice as if it were a theory of cohesion. Pratt wrote it as a clinical observation in a public-health program.

Osler wrote to him in 1917 about the tuberculosis papers and about personality — Pratt's, which the internist had not advertised. The letter survives in the Osler correspondence. A later industry would turn that heat into a "factor." Pratt was trying to keep poor patients on rest and air.

The class was not unique. Churches, dispensaries, and charity wards already gathered the sick. What he added was a physician's insistence on a copied regimen plus a weekly public accounting. Other clinics later borrowed the form for diabetes, heart disease, obesity, pregnancy. That lineage runs toward shared medical appointments as much as toward Irvin Yalom. The two descendants barely speak.

## Dispensary, "thought control," and a hospital with his name

In 1927 Pratt became medical director of the Boston Dispensary, an old New England institution for the indigent. He helped assemble what became the New England Medical Center, with the Boston Floating Hospital and, after a gift from William Bingham II of Bethel, Maine, a diagnostic hospital that Bingham insisted carry Pratt's name rather than his own. The Joseph H. Pratt Diagnostic Hospital opened as a place concerned mainly with diagnosis. In the 1930s Pratt recruited refugee physicians from Nazi Germany onto the staff. Samuel Proger became his successor.

The Dispensary's ledgers showed a crowd of patients labeled psychoneurotic whose complaints were fatigue, headache, indigestion. They were not lining up for psychoanalysis. Pratt, who had already stretched one internist across a tuberculosis class, opened in May 1930 what a patient later called thought-control classes: weekly meetings, an exhortatory talk, a hope that morale and habit could be trained. He had been reading Jules Dejerine on re-education. He published little about this second class, and in journals that did not travel as far as *JAMA*. Hospital chaplains later inherited the program; it did not flourish under them.

A historian who turns those hours into "the invention of group psychotherapy" is doing the guild's work, not Pratt's. He remained an internist running a class. The later name was applied by people who needed ancestors.

He died in Boston on 3 March 1956. The *Boston Globe* eulogized him among the city's medical names. The eulogy did not have to mention a circle. The profession of group therapy, by then, already had.

## How an origin gets elected

When American group psychotherapy organized in the 1940s, it needed a medical ancestor who published in a journal hospitals already trusted. Pratt was available. He was not Moreno and he was not Burrow. Putting him first on a syllabus is a guild choice. It is not a photograph of 1905.

What he did show, in print, is narrower and still worth saying. A weekly meeting of people with the same illness could change whether they kept the regimen, and the change could be written down in weights and fevers. Melancholy, trauma, and character were other people's later claims.

He also did not show that poverty is a side issue. The 1907 title puts the poor in the headline. A later outpatient group that forgets who can come weekly, who can miss work, and who can be seen entering the building is not descending from Pratt. It is forgetting him.

## Why a clinic still reads this

Because the internist and the origin myth came apart. Pratt wanted rest, food, air, and a roll. The guild wanted a date on a slide. His patients were fighting a bacterium. The weekly meeting was how a poor household stayed on the regimen. Forget that, and "we descend from Pratt" is costume.

Because Osler's shadow is part of the American medical inheritance, including in rooms that never mention him. Pratt wanted to pattern a life on a teacher. Personality leaked into the method. He did not write a theory of that leak. Later writers did, and then pretended the theory had been the point.
