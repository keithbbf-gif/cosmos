**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In the outpatient department of the Massachusetts General Hospital, in the first years of the twentieth century, Joseph Hersey Pratt kept seeing new cases of pulmonary tuberculosis among people who could not buy a bed at a sanatorium. William Osler had already written, in 1900, about home care for consumptives who would never see Saranac or Rutland. Pratt, then a young internist who admired Osler the way a certain kind of American physician did, tried to build a home sanatorium in a tenement: rest, air, an hourly diary, a visitor, and a weekly class.

He published the method in *JAMA* in 1907 under a title that does not flatter later textbooks: "The Class Method of Treating Consumption in the Homes of the Poor." The paper is about tents on roofs, blankets, wages, and the people who said they only needed a tonic for the cough. The weekly meeting is in the paper. It is not yet "group psychotherapy." Later writers would promote him anyway. Origin stories need a date. 1905 is the date the profession likes. The archive is a little looser — around 1904 he was gathering patients; 1907 is when he told the country.

This essay is part of a WOW Therapies series on the history of group therapy. It does not teach a class method. It does not tell anyone to sleep outdoors.

## Emmanuel Church, a rector, and a friendly visitor

The class met at Emmanuel Church in Boston. The rector, Elwood Worcester, who held a doctorate in psychology and a talent for institutional friendship, helped pay for a "friendly visitor" — a prototype of the medical social worker — and for the tents and blankets a poor household needed if it was going to imitate a sanatorium on a roof. Worcester's own later project, the Emmanuel Movement, began as weekly health conferences in 1906 and grew into a crowded ministry of talk, prayer, and physicians. Historians of pastoral counseling claim that room. Historians of group therapy claim Pratt's. They shared a building. They did not share a theory.

Pratt ran the tuberculosis class for years — church histories say eighteen. He judged success the way a chest physician of his decade could: fever gone, weight up, a return to work. He wrote that patients who had already endured cold nights encouraged the newer ones. That sentence is why group textbooks still reprint him. Mutual encouragement was not his first plan. It was what he noticed when the plan was rest and air.

Osler wrote to him in 1917, praising the papers and adding that Pratt had not stressed his own personality. The letter is a period piece. It is also a warning that later "therapeutic factors" lists still have not finished digesting: the leader's heat is part of the room, and the leader is the last person who should advertise it as a technique.

## What the class was not

It was not a process group. Members were not there to examine their childhoods. They were there because they were poor and contagious and the state sanatorium had a waiting list or a fee. The diary was a surveillance tool as much as a teaching tool. The visitor walked into homes. A contemporary ethics board would have a long afternoon with that file. A historian who sanitizes the class into a warm circle of "support" is lying about class, in both senses.

It was also not unique. Churches, dispensaries, and charity wards already gathered the sick. What Pratt added was a physician's insistence on a regimen copied from expensive air, plus a weekly public accounting. Other clinics later borrowed the form for diabetes, heart disease, obesity, pregnancy — "class" as a way to stretch a doctor across many bodies. That lineage runs toward shared medical appointments as much as toward Yalom. The two descendants barely speak.

## How an origin gets elected

American group psychotherapy, when it organized in the 1940s around S. R. Slavson and the American Group Therapy Association (the name AGPA came in 1952), needed respectable medical ancestors. Pratt was a physician. He published in *JAMA*. He could be introduced without Moreno's theater or Burrow's phyloanalysis. The election of Pratt as "founder" is a professional act. It is not a fact about 1905.

European rooms were already sitting patients together. Revivalists had already sat sinners together. Hull-House had already sat neighbors together. Pratt's usefulness to a later guild was that he looked like medicine. The guild, anxious about psychiatrists, psychologists, and social workers in the same lunchroom, liked a medical grandfather.

A fairer sentence is available. Pratt showed, in print, that a weekly meeting of people with the same illness could change adherence and morale, and that the change was visible enough to write down. He did not show that a circle is a treatment for melancholy, trauma, or character. Those claims would be made by other people, in other wars, with other instruments.

## Why a clinic still reads this

Because the origin story is a temptation. A practice that tells itself "we descend from Pratt" can forget that Pratt's patients were fighting a bacterium with rest, food, and air, and that the group was a way to keep the poor on the regimen. A practice that tells itself "we have nothing to do with Pratt" can forget that every therapy group still has a hidden class: who can come weekly, who can miss work, who can be seen entering the building.

Boston in 1907 is also a reminder that group work is always entangled with a building someone else owns. Emmanuel Church was not a clinic. Massachusetts General was not a church. The class lived in the gap. Many later groups still do — a borrowed room, a donated hour, a hope that the rent will be paid by a story about healing.

The next essays leave the tuberculosis class and enter the interwar hospital, where psychiatrists tried to do with wards what Pratt had done with roofs.

## What the 1907 paper actually says

A reader who only meets Pratt in a textbook first paragraph should read the *JAMA* article. He describes the outpatient crowd at Massachusetts General: hundreds of consumptives a year, two or three new cases in a morning of clinic, a state sanatorium at Rutland that could take the lucky or the solvent, and a larger group who looked "fairly healthy" and asked for a cough medicine or a tonic. He is writing as a physician who has decided that advice without supervision is theatre. The class is the supervision. The diary is the hour-by-hour proof that rest happened. The visitor is the proof that the household could bear the proof.

He is also writing about money without euphemism. Tents, blankets, a roof that will hold a frame, a wage that disappears when a breadwinner lies down — these are the materials. Later group theory likes to lift "mutual encouragement" out of that file and leave the tents. The lift is how an origin becomes a logo. The tents are how a poor city actually fought a bacterium before streptomycin.

Church histories that mention a recovered family on a rooftop in 1907 are period publicity. They may be true and still not be a license to reprint faces. This series's photo rules are blunt: no unlicensed family photographs, no AI tents. Type and a citation are enough.

## After tuberculosis

Pratt did not freeze in 1907. He later applied class methods to other chronic complaints, and other clinics copied the form for diseases that were not tuberculosis. The copy is a second history: shared medical appointments, diabetes classes, cardiac groups that a health system can schedule. Those rooms are cousins of Pratt and only distant cousins of a psychodynamic process group. A brand that collapses them into one "group therapy" slide is doing marketing.

The Emmanuel Movement, which shared a building and a decade, went the other direction: talk, prayer, nervous illness, a crowd. Worcester and the neurologists who would work with him belong as much to pastoral counseling's memory as to this series. The shared address is the historical fact. The shared theory is a wish.

When American group psychotherapy needed a medical grandfather in the 1940s, Pratt was available, published, and safely dead enough to quote. He had not asked to be a grandfather of process. He had asked poor consumptives to sleep in the air and to come back next week. The profession's gratitude is real. So is the profession's projection.
