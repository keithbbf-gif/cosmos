**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

*No licensed still. Type only: Yvonne M. Agazarian, 1929–2017.*

In 1997, Guilford published Yvonne M. Agazarian's *Systems-Centered Therapy for Groups*. The same year the American Psychological Association named her Group Psychologist of the Year. The book offered a theory of living human systems and a named practice, SCT, that shifted the group's center of gravity from the individual self to the system. Institutes still train people in that school. This page will not. It is a historical account of a late-century American theory that tried to make group dynamics and psychoanalysis speak a common, operational language.

This essay is part of a WOW Therapies series on the history of group therapy. It describes a school. It does not teach functional subgrouping, "boundarying," or any other SCT move.

## London, Philadelphia, a theory that wanted operations

Yvonne Agazarian was born in London on 17 February 1929 and died in Philadelphia on 9 October 2017. She took a doctorate in education at Temple University, practiced in Philadelphia, and taught and supervised internationally. She was a Fellow of the American Group Psychotherapy Association and sat on the board of the International Association of Group Psychotherapy. In 1995 she founded the Systems-Centered Training and Research Institute in Philadelphia. Workshop stills from that world remain under copyright. Type only.

Before SCT had a three-letter name, she had already been trying to see two groups at once. *The Visible and Invisible Group* (1981), with Richard Peters, is the earlier book: the group you can watch, and the group as a system of roles and communications you have to theorize. Members talk. Roles repeat. A person who looks like "the angry one" this month may be filling a function the system needs. The 1981 book is a theory object, not a seating chart.

She had also worked, with Anita Simon, on SAVI — a system for analyzing verbal interaction — a research instrument, not a parlor game. Inside AGPA she sat with colleagues on a General Systems Committee that tried to write a systems language the association could share. *Autobiography of a Theory* (with Susan Porter Gantt) later told that path in the first person. Use it as a theorist's memoir, not as a license to reprint workshop dialogue. The committee did not become SCT. The school is what she built when committee English was not enough.

Susan Gantt's 2019 obituary in *American Psychologist* is the public professional notice: Agazarian as theoretician, clinician, and teacher who wanted constructs operationalized so that an intervention could test the theory. That appetite is unusual in psychoanalytic group culture, which often prefers a rich metaphor to a test. Agazarian wanted both Lewin and Bion in the same toolbox, plus Ludwig von Bertalanffy's general systems theory, without becoming a consultant who only diagnoses committees.

Helen Durkin's 1972 *IJGP* paper had already invited general systems theory into the American group journal. Agazarian is the later figure who built a school, a training institute, and a vocabulary that students could be examined on. Continuity is real. Identity is not. Durkin compared. Agazarian founded.

## What the 1997 book claimed, historically

The 1997 volume argued that living human systems survive and change by discriminating and integrating difference. Groups, in that picture, get stuck when they cannot take in a difference without exploding or pretending it is not there. SCT's published method names phases (authority, intimacy, work, in the book's map) and a hierarchy of defenses to be modified in sequence. This series will not walk a reader through those phases. Naming them as chapter furniture is already close to a syllabus. The historical fact is that a 1997 Guilford book tried to make group therapy look more like a sequenced theory than like a gifted leader's vibe.

She also wrote *A Systems-Centered Approach to Inpatient Group Psychotherapy* (2001). Like Yalom's 1983 ward book, it is a reminder that inpatient time is not outpatient time. Do not collapse the two titles. Do not excerpt the inpatient model.

Critics of SCT, inside AGPA and outside it, have said the method can sound managerial: too much leader activity, too much naming of defenses in the moment, too little of the Foulkesian faith that the group will become the therapist if the conductor can wait. Agazarian's own contrast, in the 1997 marketing copy and in the text, was that many therapies follow the group while SCT requires the therapist to structure the work. Whether that is a virtue depends on the school you already belong to. A heritage essay can report the contrast. It cannot settle it, and it cannot train you into her structure.

Living-system talk escaped, as Bion's talk escaped, into organizational consulting. Some of that carrying is serious. Some of it is a slide. Agazarian is not responsible for every slide. She is responsible for a theory that made the slide easier, because it already claimed to apply from a person to a nation. Grand theories do that. Historians should keep the clinic and the cosmos apart even when the founder did not.

## Why a clinic still reads this

Because late-century American group therapy did not only become CBT manuals and Yalom reprints. It also grew a systems school with its own institute, fellows, and a 1997 book that still appears on specialist lists. A small-city practice that will never send anyone to SCT training can still use the historical warning: a group is not only a collection of autobiographies. Roles recur. Differences get exiled. Durkin, Agazarian, and the older Lewin line all said some version of that. They said it in different decades with different machinery.

Because women theorists in this series are not an appendix. Agazarian's APA award in 1997 is a dated public honor. The theory is the work. The honor is a profession noticing, late, a psychologist who had been building in Philadelphia while New York and Boston argued other canons.

Because a website about groups is tempted to offer "systems thinking" as a modern upgrade. SCT is not a free upgrade. It is a school with trainers who take money and time. This series will not impersonate them.
