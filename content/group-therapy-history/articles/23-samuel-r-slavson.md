**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 1903 Samuel Richard Slavson, born in 1890 in Poltava, then in the Russian Empire, landed in New York with his parents and a younger brother. The family's name would split in English: Samuel Richard Slavson, and John Slawson, who later ran the Jewish Board of Guardians. The boy went to work. Evening school at Cooper Union gave him a B.S. in engineering. He was not a physician and never became one. By 1934 he was director of group therapy at the Board. By 1943 he was the first president of the American Group Therapy Association and the author of *An Introduction to Group Therapy*, the Commonwealth Fund book that made a children's shop look like a profession.

He wanted psychoanalytic respectability for a circle. He treated J. L. Moreno as a threat to that respectability. Both facts outlived him.

This essay is part of a WOW Therapies series on the history of group therapy. It is a life. Children's activity groups and AGPA as a guild have their own essays in this pack. Do not read this page as a playroom manual.

## Educator, not internist

After Cooper Union he passed through Columbia Teachers College, progressive schools, labor and socialist rooms, a spell as educational consultant to Pioneer Youth of America, and, in the late 1920s, research in child psychology at Malting House in Cambridge, England. He arrived at treatment sideways: an engineer who had become a teacher who had become an agency man.

The Jewish Board already had casework and psychiatry. Slavson, with his brother's backing, built a "Group Therapy" project from a creative program for girls staff had called socially maladjusted. He read session reports and decided the useful agent was not the object made but the relations in the room and the relative neutrality of the adult. Mothers' groups, borough clinics, a therapeutic nursery, and later federal interest from the early NIMH, widened the shop. He held the director's post until 1956.

He wrote more than twenty books. The 1943 volume is the one a historian can assign. The Menninger Foundation later listed it among psychotherapy "classics," which is a mid-century compliment, not a current protocol. The book is agency case material. It is not a worksheet. The rooms themselves belong to the children's-activity essay.

## A profession, and a quarrel

The logo with 1942 notwithstanding, the meetings that founded what became AGPA sit in 1943, around the Orthopsychiatric sessions and a Hotel New Yorker luncheon. The guild essay in this pack follows that lunch. What belongs here is the temperament Slavson brought to it: he wanted the association recognizable to psychiatrists. He wanted it classically Freudian. Neo-Freudians, existentialists, transactional analysts, and, above all, Moreno's psychodrama, were to be kept outside the fence or at least off the letterhead. He served as first president, then as the man who would not leave: administrative committees, a bulletin, and, after the *International Journal of Group Psychotherapy* began in 1951, a de facto editor into the early 1960s. The first dozen successors to the non-medical Slavson were psychiatrists. That is an institutional joke that is also a policy.

The quarrel with Moreno was decades long. Moreno had founded the American Society of Group Psychotherapy and Psychodrama in 1942 and wanted the word *group psychotherapy* as if it were a personal patent. Slavson wanted a guild that hospitals would trust. Two journals, two memberships, two origin stories. Scheidlinger and Schamess, in the 1992 AGPA anniversary paper, are a better source for the association's memory than either man's polemic. Picking a champion is fandom. Children's rooms and a stage both mattered. Their bibliographies still barely overlap.

Slavson retired from the Jewish Board in 1956 and kept writing on adolescents, delinquency, and hospital groups. He lectured. He guarded the association's psychoanalytic look long after children had ceased to be its only population. He died in 1981. The fence he built later admitted more schools than he liked. That, too, is a historical fact, not a moral.

## What this life is not

It is not a recipe for activity group therapy. History may record that children met with materials and a relatively quiet adult. History may not teach a reader to assemble the room, screen the children, or interpret the clay. Children's faces do not belong on this page. Neither does a protocol.

It is not a story in which Slavson invented the group. Pratt's class, Burrow's social theory, Schilder's hospital seminar, and a century of social group work (Addams, later Konopka and Schwartz) were already there. What Slavson built was a New York agency practice and a professional association with a medical hunger.

It is not a license to treat psychoanalytic orthodoxy as the only serious group. His fence kept some charlatanism out. It also kept psychodrama, social work's own canon, and later research traditions in separate buildings. The profession is still walking between those buildings.

## Why a clinic still reads this

Because the American group profession has a social-work and child-guidance root that residency programs, drunk on Yalom, can skip. Slavson is that root at its most organizational: an agency, a book, a president who was not an M.D. and who then made sure the next presidents were.

Because respectability is a historical appetite. A small-city clinic that wants the hospital to take groups seriously is in Slavson's debt whether it likes his Freudian fence or not. A clinic that wants the fence to be the whole truth has not read Beacon, or Foulkes, or the later research.

Because the quarrel is not over. ASGPP and AGPA still exist. Their journals still barely cite each other. A reader who knows only one origin story has chosen a side without noticing.
