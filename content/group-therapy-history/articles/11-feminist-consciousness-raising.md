**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In February 1969, in Gainesville, Florida, Carol Hanisch wrote a memo to the women's caucus of the Southern Conference Educational Fund. She was a New York organizer on subsistence pay, scouting whether a women's liberation project could live in the South. Another staff member, Dottie Zellner, had argued that consciousness-raising looked like therapy and that the new independent women's liberation movement might not be "political." Hanisch's reply was originally titled as an answer to Dottie. When Shulamith Firestone and Anne Koedt printed it in *Notes from the Second Year: Women's Liberation* (1970), they put a different title on the piece: "The Personal Is Political." The slogan outran the memo. Historians still have to put it back in 1969 if they want the argument and not the bumper sticker.

This essay is part of a WOW Therapies series on the history of group therapy. It is not a living-room how-to. It will not tell anyone how to run a consciousness-raising session, what to ask, or how to sit. CR was a political practice. Later clinics borrowed its talk. The two facts are related. They are not a protocol.

## A circle that refused the clinic's name

New York Radical Women, Redstockings, and other small formations of the late 1960s treated talking from one's own life — housework, sex, abortion, pay, fear — as a way to see a pattern that was not a private defect. Kathie Sarachild and others brought "consciousness-raising" as a named program into the movement's conferences (the Thanksgiving 1968 gathering near Chicago is the meeting most narratives still date). The method had ancestors: the Chinese Communist practice of speaking bitterness, the civil-rights testimony meeting, the left-group criticism session. It also had a neighbor the movement did not want to be: the therapy group.

Hanisch's memo is the document that makes the neighbor explicit. Therapy, as she heard the word, assumed a sick person and a personal cure. Women, she argued, were messed over by conditions, not messed up as patients. The sessions might feel "therapeutic" in the narrow sense that self-blame loosened. She preferred to call even that "political therapy," a period phrase, not a billing code. The point of the gathering, in her account, was not to solve a member's private problem in the room. It was to notice that the private problem was widely shared and therefore a political fact.

A historian should not baptize that argument as timeless truth or as a smear on psychotherapy. It was a 1969 fight inside a movement that was being told, by some of its own left, that talking about bedrooms and kitchens was navel-gazing. The slogan "the personal is political" is a wartime caption for that fight. Firestone and Koedt's title made the caption famous. Hanisch later wrote that the title was theirs. Cite the memo as a memo. Cite the slogan as a period slogan with editors.

## What the rooms were, historically

They were usually small. They were often women-only in a movement that was arguing with men on the left and with a profession that had spent decades explaining women's misery as anatomy or envy. They had no license on the wall. They had no chart. They had, sometimes, a suspicion of experts — including feminist experts — that could harden into dogma. They produced analysis that traveled: rape as a political issue, housework as labor, the beauty contest as a public text (Hanisch had helped conceive the 1968 Miss America protest). They also produced splits, expulsions, and later memoirs of cruelty. A heritage series that prints only the sunrise meeting is doing the same postcard work it refuses in the AA essay.

They were not Yalom groups. Interpersonal learning as a Stanford-era craft was not the assignment. They were not T-groups, though some women had met Bethel's here-and-now and recognized a cousin. They were not encounter marathons for sale at Big Sur, even when the emotional temperature could match a weekend's. The payment, when there was one, was political loyalty and time. The product, when there was one, was supposed to be a movement, not a better-adjusted wife.

Race and class tore the rooms in ways the early mimeographs did not always want to print. Black feminists, Chicana feminists, working-class women, and lesbians wrote their own documents when the default "we" of some CR groups turned out to be a particular white, educated "we." Combahee River Collective's 1977 statement is a later, dated marker in that argument; it is not a footnote to Hanisch. A group-history pack that files all women's circles under one slogan has done what the slogan was written to stop: it has made a particular personal stand in for a politics.

## After the mimeograph: feminist therapy, women's groups

The clinic did not stay out. By the early 1970s a literature of *feminist therapy* was in print. Phyllis Chesler's *Women and Madness* (1972) indicted psychiatric power over women as a public fact. Journals, storefront clinics, and training programs tried to take the CR insight — that a woman's "symptom" might be an accurate reading of her situation — into a licensed hour. In London, Luise Eichenbaum and Susie Orbach opened the Women's Therapy Centre in 1976. American cities had their own addresses, some lasting, some gone in a year.

Those rooms were therapy. They had fees, notes, and, increasingly, the same insurers other clinics had. They were also political in ways a 1950s psychoanalytic group had not claimed to be. The borrowing from CR was real: all-women membership, attention to housework and sex as speakable content, a skepticism toward "adjustment." The drop was real too. A therapy group's job, even a feminist one, is not to found a movement. It is to treat the people in the chairs. Hanisch's offense at the word *therapy* is part of why some organizers never followed their members into those clinics. Some members went anyway, because misery is heavy and a movement meeting is not a substitute for a clinician — a sentence this educational series has to say in the other direction as well.

Mainstream group psychotherapy noticed, slowly. AGPA grew a women's task force in the 1970s; Henriette Glatzer had already been the association's first woman president in 1976; Alonso and Rutan published on women in group therapy at the end of that decade (see the AGPA essay). The professional literature absorbed "women's groups" as a format: same-sex membership, certain themes, a hope that identification would be easier. That format can be careful work. It can also be CR without the politics — a circle that talks about the personal and never reaches the street. History can name the thinning without sneering at people who needed the thinner room.

## What this series will not do

It will not reconstruct a CR agenda or a list of questions for a living room. It will not tell a reader that her unhappiness is "really" political or "really" clinical. Those verdicts are how both a movement and a profession capture a life. A small-city clinic that treats women in groups is not running New York Radical Women. Saying so is a historical distinction. It is not a loyalty test.

The consulting-room pack in this brand's other series will keep the long fight over women and psychoanalysis, over Freud's cases, over later feminist clinicians. This pack keeps the circle that said the living room was a political cell. Complementary, not merged.

## Why a clinic still reads this

Because patients still use the slogan, often without the memo. "The personal is political" on a tote bag is not Hanisch in Gainesville. A clinician who hears the phrase can know the date, the argument about therapy, and the later split between movement rooms and treatment rooms. Knowledge is not agreement.

Because group therapy's women's rooms have a double parentage: the professional line that runs through Slavson, AGPA, and Yalom, and the movement line that runs through mimeographs and storefronts. Pretending there is only one parent produces a syllabus that cannot hear why some people will not enter a clinic and why some people fled a movement for one.

Because a history of groups that files "diversity" as a last paragraph has already decided who the main story was. CR is not a diversity paragraph. It is an era with documents, dates, and a refusal.
