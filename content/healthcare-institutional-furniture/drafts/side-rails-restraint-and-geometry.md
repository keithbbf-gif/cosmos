# Side rails, restraint, and geometry

A rail is a piece of steel that can be a handhold, a barrier, a hanger for a urine bag, a restraint, or a gap. The word you pick does not change the opening.

CMS cares whether a device restricts a resident’s freedom of movement. FDA cares whether a body can go into the system and not come out. Those are different questions. The Medline-era catalog often answered a third question: what photographs as “safe.”

I am not going to teach you how to document a restraint to survive a survey. I am going to teach you why a mill and a furniture specifier have to stay out of the steel unless they are ready to join the bed system.

## History: from ward wall to safety alert

Hospital side rails grew up as a ward tool. A row of beds, a night nurse who could not stand at every mattress, a fear of the floor — the rail was a wall you could lower for a bath and raise for a shift. Mid-century catalogs treated that wall as ordinary equipment. The object was not yet a public death file. It was metal that came with the bed.

By the time a southern hardwood mill was putting wooden headboards next to those beds, the file was public. FDA’s Safety Alert of August 23, 1995, named entrapment hazards with hospital bed side rails in language a purchaser could not pretend not to have seen. The Agency count this series is allowed to carry: from January 1, 1985, to January 1, 2006, about **691** entrapment reports, **413** deaths, **120** injuries, **158** near-misses. Nursing homes are in that population. A floral pad does not take you out of it.

The Hospital Bed Safety Workgroup sat between the alert and the March 10, 2006, guidance. The 2006 document is the drawing set: seven zones, recommended limits on four, a warning that accessories change openings, and a note that rails get left in an **intermediate** position. Intermediate is how a house tries to be kind and still keep a wall.

Restraint culture moved on a different clock. OBRA 1987 made freedom of movement into survey language. Culture-change houses later said rail-free the way they said homelike — as a direction, and sometimes as a slogan. The steel did not vanish because the word improved. It moved, or it stayed and got a wooden cap, or it came off and left the furniture to become the climb. The honest shop job, arriving after that file, was not to add a new opening while staining a headboard to match a nightstand.

## Two houses, one object

**Restraint** is a clinical and regulatory house. A rail used to keep a person from getting out of bed can be a restraint under the Requirements of Participation. A rail used as a mobility aid may not be. The difference is use, assessment, consent, and a care plan — not the powdercoat color. I will not quote a tag number I have not opened this week for the restraint definition. Open the current SOM. `[CITE NEEDED: current F-tag for restraints / bed rails in the SOM you are using.]`

**Geometry** is the 2006 FDA house. Seven zones. Recommended limits on four. A guidance, not a gift of immunity. The 1995 safety alert is the older public shout: side rails were already killing people before the Workgroup finished drawing.

A building can pass its restraint paperwork and still have a Zone 3 canyon. A building can take the rails off and still have a fall program that now includes the floor. Neither house is a furniture brochure.

The two houses meet in the same room and almost never in the same meeting. Nursing owns the care plan. Biomed or the bed vendor owns the assessment, if anyone does. Purchasing owns the punchout. The mill owns a panel that can become Zone 6 if someone bolts it on as if it were a footboard. If those people do not share a tape, the resident owns the gap.

## Shop geometry: openings, not nicknames

The catalog nicknames are a dialect.

A full-length rail is a wall with holes. A half rail is a wall that stops, which is how Zone 5 and Zone 6 get a life. A quarter rail or “assist bar” is a handle that can still make an end bay (Zone 4) if the mattress and the board do not agree. An integrated assist — a loop in the deck — is an attempt to give a handhold without a cage. It has its own way of catching a strap.

I do not care which nickname is on the punchout. I care whether anyone measured the openings at **flat and articulated**, **up and intermediate**. Intermediate is the position people leave a rail in when they want to believe they are being kind. The guidance names it on purpose.

Shop geometry I will own as shop geometry, not as a substitute for the HBSW methods:

Zones 1 through 3 carry a recommended limit smaller than **120 mm (4.75 inches)**. Zone 4 — the end bay under the rail — is tighter: smaller than **60 mm (2.375 inches)**, with an angle condition in the guidance’s dimensional table. Do not recite the angle from a finish board. Open the table. Zones 5, 6, and 7 are named without the same FDA dimensional limits in that 2006 document. Named is not safe. Named is **less numbered**.

A wooden cap or a decorative rail cover adds thickness. Thickness can close a hole you liked and open a hole you did not measure. A bumper meant to “fill” Zone 3 is an accessory. Accessories change openings. Headboard and footboard panels are furniture until they sit close enough to be the system. Zone 6 lives between the end of a rail and the side of a board. Zone 7 lives between the board and the mattress end. If a mill panel is “just matching the suite,” measure the daylight. Daylight is not a test. It is a reason to stop calling the panel decoration.

I will not publish a homemade cone. If the house needs an assessment, it uses the published method and a person whose job is that assessment. If the mill needs a decision, the decision is: **join the system in writing, or stay far enough away that you are furniture.** Far enough is a measurement at more than one deck angle. Articulation is the measurement people skip. Head up changes the rail’s relationship to the mattress. A tired foam changes every number you took on install day. Write the mattress on the same line as the rail or admit the number is a souvenir.

## Straps, loops, and the hanger problem

The 1990s bunk-bed death file has a cousin here: people hang things on rails. In youth furniture it is a belt. In a SNF it is a call cord, a restraint vest that was supposed to be gone, a purse, a feeding tube, a fall-alarm strap.

I am not writing a clinical guideline. I am writing a furniture observation: **a rail is a hanger**. If you specify a decorative rail cover or a wooden cap, you have added a thickness and maybe a new opening. If you specify a nightstand with a hook facing the rail, you have added a snag. Shop knowledge: I have seen purse hooks sold as dignity. Dignity that strangles is not a pull.

Cords love a loop. A loop in an assist bar is a handhold until a strap finds it. I have watched houses clip alarm straps to the “safe” quarter rail because the full rail was gone and the strap still needed a home. The opening is still an opening.

## When furniture pretends to be a rail

This is the mill’s sin.

A wooden side panel that “keeps her from rolling out” is a rail you did not assess. A bumper that closes a gap and opens a different one is a rail you did not assess. A mattress wedge from a punchout is a rail you did not assess.

If you want to close Zone 3, you are in the bed system. You talk to the bed-maker and to the people who use the HBSW methods. You do not talk to a hardwood shop about a pretty plank.

Bradley Brand’s job, when we were honest, was **not to become a rail**. Headboard panels either belonged to the bed-maker’s geometry or they stayed far enough away to be furniture. Far enough is a measurement, not a vibe.

The temptation is strongest on a “wood bed” package. The metal frame is the device. The wood is a costume that clips or stands. Specifiers who write “headboard to match nightstand” without naming the bed-maker have specified a Zone 6 kit in maple.

## Taking the rails off

The late era loved “rail-free” as a culture-change sentence. Sometimes it was right. A person who climbs a rail is not safer because the rail is floral. Sometimes it was a slogan over a low bed and a mat and a chair alarm that everyone hated.

Furniture consequences of rail-free:

- The chair next to the bed becomes the climb.
- The nightstand becomes the climb.
- The overbed becomes the climb.
- The gap at the headboard is still a gap.

If you take the steel down and leave a 28-inch-high bedside with a drawer pull like a rung, you have specified a wooden rail. Sit on the floor and look at the room the way a confused person looks at it at 3 a.m. The pulls will announce themselves.

Rail-free is not a furniture style. It is a fall program that relocated the hazard. A half rail left “just for transfers” is still a rail at 2 a.m. if no one put it down.

## Channel: nicknames, pads, and the photograph

Medline-era punchouts sold full, half, quarter, and assist as comfort grades. Pads — vinyl, foam, floral — changed openings and became thicker hangers. The 2006 guidance’s accessory warning is the sentence the JPEG never printed.

The channel liked wood next to metal because the photograph looked like a bedroom. Coordination in the room is whether the panel joins the system. I have cut a headboard return because it crowded a rail end.

Special-buy rail kits that arrived a year after the beds were a mixed-system problem wearing matching paint. If vendor A’s bed and a later rail share a statement, ask who measured the openings after the kit went on.

## What I want written on a furniture sheet

“No decorative element shall function as a bed rail or close a bed-system opening unless the bed manufacturer and the facility’s bed-safety program have accepted the geometry in writing.”

That sentence will lose you a pretty SKU. It will keep you out of Zone 6.

Also write: bed-side furniture heights and pulls shall be reviewed for climb and snag if the room is rail-free or half-railed. Write the rail type as the bed-maker names it, not as “assist,” and write that a wooden cap is an accessory.

## What to look for on a floor

From the door, without touching a stranger’s bed: is there daylight between mattress and rail, between split rails, between rail end and board? Is a rail sitting halfway? Is a strap hung on a loop? Is a nightstand hook facing the steel? Is a wooden panel pretending to be the footboard?

If you are staff or a vendor with permission: look at flat and head-up, rail up and intermediate. Look at the mattress name. If it is a later punchout than the bed, you are already in the other draft. Look at the working side. A pretty bedside next to a rail that staff must drop is a mill gift to a future complaint. If you are a visitor: tell nursing what you saw and stop. This series is educational. It is not a bedside procedure.

## What I will not do

I will not tell you which residents “need” rails. I will not give you a script for a family meeting. I will not tell you how to hide a restraint in an assist.

I will tell you that geometry is prior. If the opening is wrong, the paperwork is a story about a death that has not happened.
