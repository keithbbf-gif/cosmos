# CAL 117, TB 133, the Fire Card

*Educational history of healthcare and nursing-home furniture. Not a purchasing specification for a named building, not a survey opinion, and not clinical advice about beds, rails, or seating for a particular resident.*

The label is sewn into the underside of a dining chair, where a stapler already lives (chapter 36). Black text on a white or yellow ground: California, a bulletin number, a date, a sentence about smoldering ignition. The chair is in Michigan. No one on the wing has a cigarette as a care-plan tool, and the building’s smoking courtyard is a later story with a locked door (chapter 34). The label traveled anyway. A healthcare buyer files a fire card the way a healthcare buyer files a bleach card: as proof that a catalog object has been somewhere official. The official somewhere, for a generation of upholstered seating, was a California bulletin. This essay is about that travel. CAL TB 117-2013 is a smolder test. TB 133 was an open-flame test for public-occupancy seating. Around 2019 California pulled TB 133 back from many of the uses that had made it a national password `[VERIFY scope and which occupancies still named it]`. The hangtag did not explain the difference. The file still wants a PDF.

Foam and vinyl are a system. A welt is a capillary (chapter 36). A molded poly shell is a different system (chapter 37). Fire language that treats “the chair” as one material is already lying. The bulletins, at their best, knew that. The punchout line that read “CAL 117” did not.

## A cigarette test that became a password

California’s Bureau — the name on the door has moved through Household Goods, Home Furnishings, Thermal Insulation, and other letterheads — published Technical Bulletin 117 as a way to talk about whether upholstered furniture would start from a smoldering cigarette. The older TB 117 family, in trade memory from the mid-1970s forward, mixed component tests and, in some versions, more aggressive asks of filling materials `[VERIFY a given year’s method before quoting a flame]`. What a SNF purchasing office needed was not the genealogy. It needed a yes. Vendors learned to print “Meets TB 117” on a card. The card entered a Medilodge-era binder next to a vinyl bleach boast and a Wyzenbeek number. Three different tests, one plastic sleeve.

TB 117-2013, the revision that took effect in California at the start of 2014 `[VERIFY the precise enforcement date on the bureau page you are holding]`, is a smolder standard. A lighted cigarette, a specified cover-and-filling assembly, pass/fail language about when a specimen chars and when it does not. It is not an open-flame room test. It is not a spray-booth certificate. It is not NFPA 101. ASTM E1353 sits in the same family of cigarette-ignition methods; labs and specifiers treat them as cousins, not as interchangeable stamps `[VERIFY the method a given card actually cites]`. A Michigan wing that specified 117-2013 after 2014 was borrowing a California consumer-furniture rule and asking it to stand in for “the chair will not be the thing that starts.” That is a lot of work for a cigarette test. It is also more honest than an older component stamp that never saw the welt.

Healthcare vinyl over polyurethane foam is the common SNF assembly. The bulletin tests a system: cover, filling, sometimes a barrier if the factory builds one. Change the cover — a Crypton-class weave instead of a PVC skin — and the system is new. Change the foam grade to meet a different flame story and the sit is new. Reupholstery on the wing, the staple-and-hope kind, can void the logic of the label while leaving the label sewn in. The faded capacity sticker (chapter 43) and the fire label then agree on one thing only: they describe a chair that no longer exists.

Cigarettes in a SNF are a policy history, not a test method. Indoor smoking receded. Outdoor courtyards took the ashtrays, then rules tightened again. The smolder test remained because California had written it, because factories already ran it, and because a GPO template is easier to leave than to rewrite. A hangtag is conservative that way. It outlives the habit that justified it.

## TB 133: the open flame that purchasing meant

Technical Bulletin 133 was the other California password. It was an open-flame test for seating furniture used in certain public occupancies — a burner, a full piece or a full-scale mock-up, heat-release ideas that a cigarette test does not pretend to own `[VERIFY apparatus and pass criteria before quoting a number]`. Hotels, auditoriums, some healthcare waiting rooms, the kind of lobby that bought Nemschoff-class seating instead of the distributor vinyl set (chapter 29): those were the rooms where an architect or an authority having jurisdiction said “133.” The test is harder on a chair. It pushed factories toward barriers, toward different foams, toward the chemical flame-retardant story that later statutes would fight about.

A SNF dining chair was not always in the occupancy class that required 133. A SNF lobby sometimes was, depending on the year, the state, the inspector, and whether anyone had written the furniture into a life-safety review. Channel habit flattened that. Corporate purchasing, buying for fifteen Michigan buildings, asked for a 133 letter on a lounge because a hospital consultant had asked for one once, or because the punchout had a checkbox. House-brand vinyl stackers arrived with a 117 card and a shrug. Name-brand lounge groups arrived with a 133 report and a price that had already lost the visitor chair in the room package (chapter 23). Two fire stories, one corridor.

California, around 2019, pulled TB 133 back from many of the uses that had made it a national shorthand. The exact remaining scope — which occupancies, which state documents, which AHJs still name the bulletin — wants a `[VERIFY]` against the bureau and against the edition of NFPA 101 or the state fire code you are actually in. Do not write “TB 133 is dead.” Do not write “every healthcare chair still needs 133.” Write what the hangtag on the chair in front of you says, and write that a 2019-era California pullback changed what a factory could honestly print for a lot of public rooms. Files in Michigan did not all get the memo. Binders are older than bulletins.

Flame-retardant chemistry is adjacent and not this essay’s center. California’s later limits on certain additives in some furniture (trade histories point to the late 2010s) `[VERIFY statute number before naming one]` changed how a foam house met a smolder test. A reader who wants a chemical lecture is in the wrong magazine. A reader who wants to know why a 2008 lounge and a 2018 lounge can carry different fire sentences on the same silhouette is in the right room.

## NFPA 101 and the card in the drawer

NFPA 101, the *Life Safety Code*, is the document many healthcare occupancies actually live under, through CMS and through state adoption. It talks about interior finish, about new versus existing occupancies, about drapes and cubicle curtains (chapter 24), and, in places, about upholstered furniture. It is not a California bulletin. It may reference smolder resistance, or a local amendment, or nothing as specific as a hangtag. Surveyors walking F584 and F917 are not, on most days, holding a 133 report. Fire marshals might be. The furniture file that tries to satisfy both is how a SNF ends up with a stack of PDFs that do not name the chair on the floor.

Cubicle curtains have their own flame language — NFPA 701 is the textile cousin often named for draperies `[VERIFY the edition in a given spec]`. A reader should not collapse 701, 117, and 133 into “the fire rating.” Three tests, three objects. The curtain that does not close (chapter 24) can still hold a 701 label. The chair with a split welt can still hold a 117 label. The label is not the object.

Mattresses and bed assemblies have still other documents — federal mattress flammability rules, medical-bed electrical standards (IEC 60601-2-52), the 2006 FDA system guidance that does not mention a cigarette. Do not hang a dining-chair bulletin on a deck. Do not hang a mattress 16 CFR on a geri chair (chapter 25). The card in the drawer is useful only if someone matches it to the piece. House-brand punchout furniture often arrived with a generic letter: “our seating meets TB 117-2013.” Which seating. Which cover. Which foam lot. The letter does not say. A claim desk, later, will say the letter was the product (chapter 42).

A Medilodge-class multi-building file, around the 2011 *Provider* portrait of MediLodge Group and the 2013 Prestige sale of a block of homes, is a good mental picture of how cards accumulate. Fifteen buildings, renovations on different years, a corporate spec that named a bulletin, local fire visits that named another, a lounge replaced after 2020 disinfection killed the vinyl (chapter 10, chapter 36) with a chair whose label is 117-2013 and whose foam is a post-chemistry-limit recipe. The lawn sign can change (chapter 44). The binder keeps every letter.

Cubicle-curtain specifiers who already live in NFPA 701 language sometimes try to put that number on a chair. It will not go. Seating foam is not a drape. The inverse error is a lounge specified “117” in a room where an AHJ still wants an open-flame story — rare after the 2019-era pullback `[VERIFY]`, not imaginary in a file that has not been updated since 2012. Hardware, casters, and HPL do not carry these labels. They carry other failures (chapters 37, 38, 40). The fire card is an upholstery document that purchasing treats as a room document. That category error is how a metal overbed table gets asked for a 117 letter and a vinyl chair gets asked for nothing because it looks like equipment.

## What the label cannot do

The sewn-in label cannot make a chair homelike. It cannot settle F558 seat height. It cannot tell EVS which bottle to pour. It cannot survive a reupholstery that changes the system and leaves the old tag for luck. It can tell you which story a factory was prepared to sell in a given year: smolder, open flame, or a vagueness that hoped you would not ask.

Open flame and smolder are not degrees of the same test. They are different questions. A cover that self-extinguishes a cigarette can still feed a burner. A 133 chair can be a stiff, barrier-heavy sit that residents hate and that still shows up in a lobby because an AHJ once asked. After the 2019-era pullback `[VERIFY]`, some of those lobby chairs became surplus with excellent paperwork. Some of the replacement chairs are honest 117-2013 assemblies with a softer seat and a thinner story. The wing sits both.

The residual object is the label you have to flip the chair to read. California is on it. Michigan is under it. The cigarette test did the traveling. The open-flame test did the intimidating, then receded in the state that had written it, in a scope that still needs checking. Healthcare kept filing the card because healthcare files cards. Foam and vinyl remain a system; the welt remains a hole; the fire sentence remains only as good as the assembly that still matches the tag. The next essay leaves the hangtag and opens a wardrobe that has already lost its pull — hinges, bumper edges, the hardware that takes the cart at med pass.

## Notes

- CAL TB 117-2013: smolder / cigarette ignition for upholstered furniture assemblies. Not an open-flame room test. Enforcement dating `[VERIFY]` on the bureau’s 2013 bulletin and the 2014 start-of-year habit.
- Older TB 117 versions: do not quote an open-flame filling test as if it were 117-2013.
- TB 133: historical open-flame seating test for certain public occupancies. California pullback ~2019 — remaining scope `[VERIFY]` against bureau language and the AHJ/code edition in force.
- NFPA 101: Life Safety Code; CMS-related healthcare occupancy rules travel through adoption. Not a hangtag.
- NFPA 701: textile / drape cousin; do not collapse with 117 or 133.
- ASTM E1353: related cigarette-ignition family; match the method the card names.
- No current catalog claims; no “fireproof chair.” Labels describe tests, not destiny.
