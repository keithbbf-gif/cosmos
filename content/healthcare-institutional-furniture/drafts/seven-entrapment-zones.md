# Seven entrapment zones

A gap is a dimension that wants a body.

FDA’s March 10, 2006, guidance, built on the Hospital Bed Safety Workgroup, names **seven** places in a hospital bed system where a person can be caught. It puts recommended dimensional limits on **zones 1 through 4**, because those were the openings in most of the reports. Zones 5, 6, and 7 are still named. They are not thereby safe. They are thereby **less numbered**.

From January 1, 1985, to January 1, 2006, the Agency’s files held about **691** entrapment reports: **413** deaths, **120** injuries, **158** near-misses. The people named are the people in this series: elderly, frail, confused, restless, uncontrolled movement. The buildings include nursing homes.

If you specify furniture around a bed and you cannot point at the seven zones, you are decorating a hazard.

## The seven, in the Agency’s order

I am paraphrasing the public drawings, not replacing them. Open the PDF if you are doing a real assessment. The drawings are the document.

**Zone 1 — Within the rail.** The opening inside the rail’s own perimeter. A head, a neck, a chest can use it. The recommended limit for zones 1–3 is a space smaller than **120 mm (4 3/4 inches)**.

**Zone 2 — Under the rail, between supports or next to a single support.** The space under the rail’s lower edge, in the bays. Same 120 mm conversation for the dimensional recommendation.

**Zone 3 — Between the rail and the mattress.** The canyon. This is the furniture-adjacent zone even though the nightstand is not in the diagram. A mattress that is too narrow, too soft, too short, or from another vendor is how Zone 3 is manufactured after the fact.

**Zone 4 — Under the rail, at the ends of the rail.** The end bay. FDA’s recommended limit here is tighter: smaller than **60 mm (2 3/8 inches)**, with an angle condition (over 60 degrees) in the guidance’s dimensional table. Do not recite the angle from memory in a courtroom. Open the table.

**Zone 5 — Between split rails.** Two half-rails and a space between them. Chest and neck. The 2006 document does not give FDA dimensional limits here the way it does for 1–4.

**Zone 6 — Between the end of a rail and the side of a head or foot board.** The furniture temptation. A decorative headboard that does not belong to the bed-maker lives here.

**Zone 7 — Between the head or foot board and the mattress end.** The footboard canyon, the pillow canyon. Again, named, not given the same FDA limit in that guidance.

IEC 60601-2-38, as the 2006 text discusses it, addressed limits for zones comparable to 1, 2, 4, and 6, and was moving toward 1–6. That is history of a standard, not a permission to ignore 3 and 7.

## Why a mill is in a bed document

Because we build Zone 6 and Zone 7 when we are careless.

A wooden headboard panel that “matches the suite” and sits an inch off the frame is a Zone 6 kit. A footboard that is really a furniture panel with a mattress that has crept is a Zone 7 kit. I have refused, in my own head, the sentence “it’s just decorative.” Decorative is a gap with stain on it.

The 2006 guidance also warns that accessories and replacement components change openings. A mill that ships a new bedside into a room with a legacy bed has not created a zone. A mill that ships a headboard that becomes part of the system has.

If you are Bradley Brand in a package, the ethical drawing is a drawing that **does not join the system** unless the bed-maker says it does. A floating panel with a measured clearance is a decision. A panel that pretends to be the footboard is a different decision. Write which one you are making.

## 120 mm is not a vibe

Four and three-quarters inches is larger than people think until they put a fist in it, and smaller than a catalog’s “generous rail” photograph.

The number comes from anthropometry and from the death file, not from a furniture tradition. It is a recommended limit in a guidance document, not a regulation that makes a bed illegal by itself. The availability notice is explicit that the guidance is the Agency’s current thinking and does not confer rights. Facilities still use it because the deaths were real.

Do not “improve” the number. Do not convert it twice and ship a 5-inch opening because your tape is coarse. Do not assume a foam that compresses five inches still meets a 120 mm gap you measured on an unused mattress.

## What the reports were

The 2006 text and the 1995 safety alert sit on a pile of FDA reports. Ferlo Todd, Ruhl, and Gross went through 1985–1995 and put a paper in the *American Journal of Public Health* in 1997. The later guidance extends the pile to January 1, 2006.

I am not going to re-tally their tables from memory. I am going to say what a furniture person is supposed to take: **this is not rare in the way a mill wants defects to be rare.** Hundreds of deaths are not a rounding error in a finish lot.

Long-term care is named. If your customer is a SNF, you are in the population.

## What I will not do with a cone or a probe

The HBSW test methods and tools exist so a facility can assess openings. I am not publishing a how-to for defeating a tool. I am not publishing a homemade cone.

If you need an assessment, use the published method, the current tool, and a person whose job is that assessment. If you need a furniture decision, stay out of the opening with your decorative panel.

The bunk-bed series in this tree talks about probes for a different law. Do not mix the probes. A CPSC bunk probe is not an HBSW bed tool. A child’s bunk is not an 86-year-old’s rail. The shared idea is only this: **openings are designed, even when they are neglected.**

## How to look, if you are allowed to look

From the door, without touching: is there daylight between mattress and rail? Between mattress and headboard? Between split rails? Daylight is not a measurement. It is a reason to get the person whose job is measurement.

If you are staff or a vendor with permission: measure at flat and at head-up, rail up and intermediate, and write the numbers down. If you are a visitor: do not perform a test on a stranger’s bed. Tell nursing what you saw and then stop.

This series is educational. It is not a bedside procedure.
