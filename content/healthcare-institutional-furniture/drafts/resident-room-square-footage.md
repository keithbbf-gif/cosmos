# Resident room square footage

Furniture is a volume that pretends to be a surface.

CMS writes the resident room as an area: at least **80 square feet per resident** in a multiple bedroom, at least **100 square feet** in a single. Those numbers are in 42 CFR 483.90(e)(1)(ii). They are floors, not designs. They are also the quiet reason a Medline-era nightstand is narrower than a bedroom nightstand and why an overbed spends its life in the aisle.

If you specify furniture without standing in the square footage, you are specifying for a warehouse.

## What the number includes, and what people think it includes

The CFR says the room must measure at least those areas. It does not, in that clause, give you a furniture deduction. People still argue about bathrooms, vestibules, and whether the built-in closet counts. I am not going to pretend I am the SOM. I am going to say what a mill sees: **the usable rectangle is smaller than the number on the plan.**

A 100-square-foot single that is 10 by 10 with a toilet room punched out of it is not a 10-by-10 life. A double that is 16 by 12 “meets 80 each” on a good day and then loses a four-foot aisle, two bed envelopes, and a radiator. The furniture lives in the leftover.

States may be stricter. Some are. `[CITE NEEDED: a state-by-state table is a different project; do not invent one.]` If your job is in Michigan or Arkansas or Texas — the geography of the public project list — you read the state licensure book before you read the catalog. The federal floor is the floor that lets a chain run one package in three states. The state book is what gets a wardrobe rejected at the door.

## The bed envelope is the real room

A hospital-style bed is not a twin. It is a moving machine with a width that changes when the rail is up, a length that changes when the foot extends, and a need for staff on at least one long side.

Shop ranges, not a standard: a lot of the beds I saw wanted something like 36 to 42 inches of mattress width in the standard class, more in bariatric, and a working side that was not a theoretical 24 inches. If you put nightstands on both sides of a double because the rendering looked balanced, you have already stolen the working side.

The 80-square-foot floor assumes you will be ruthless about what gets a footprint. The overbed is supposed to nest. The visitor chair is supposed to live somewhere that is not the fire path. The wardrobe, if the closet is a rumor, is supposed to be a closet and not a second dresser.

When a package “doesn’t fit,” it is usually not because the mill built a 30-inch bedside. It is because someone drew two 30-inch bedsides, two chairs, and a wardrobe into a leftover that could take one of each.

## Aisles, oxygen, and the crash

Square footage is a plan metric. A live room has a crash path.

I have watched an overbed become a barricade because it was specified with a base that could not park under the bed. I have watched a visitor chair become the thing they threw into the toilet room when the lift arrived. I have watched a wardrobe door swing into the only place a CNA could stand to boost.

None of those are CMS square-footage violations. They are how a legal room becomes an illegal night. The accident tag and the dignity tag will get there before F917 does.

If you want a working rule from the mill side: **draw the lift, then draw the furniture.** If the lift does not fit, the furniture is wrong, even if the area table is right.

## Doubles punish width

A double’s cruel dimension is width, not area.

Two beds, a curtain track, two people who both own a chair, and a staff member who needs to see a face from the door. The leftover for case goods is a strip along the wall. That is why institutional nightstands grew up tall and shallow instead of low and deep. A 24-inch-deep residential cabinet is a luxury the aisle cannot pay.

Shop range: a lot of the bedsides we built or competed with were in the 18-to-22-inch depth class, 20-to-24-inch width, height matched to a seated reach from the bed, not to a lamp. I will not tattoo a model number on that range. If your catalog’s “homelike” bedside is 28 inches deep, ask where the aisle went.

Wardrobes in doubles are worse. A 24-inch-deep wardrobe with a 18-inch door swing can eat the roommate’s chair. Sliding doors were the honest answer and the maintenance headache. Bi-folds were the other honest answer and the other headache. A pair of handsome hinged doors was the catalog answer.

## Singles punish expectation

A 100-square-foot single feels huge on a brochure and small when the family arrives with a recliner.

The regulation wants personal belongings to the extent possible. The square footage wants you to say no. This is the fight F584 actually creates. A mill cannot solve it. A package that includes a “family chair” without a parking dimension is a package that will lose the chair to the hallway.

In new plants that actually built larger singles, the furniture problem inverted: too much wall, not enough object, a room that looked like a motel until someone spent money on a second sitting place and a table that was not the overbed. That is a better problem. It is still a problem.

## How the channel used the number

Medline-era bids loved “meets all federal requirements” as a furniture sentence. Square footage is not a furniture requirement. It is a **room** requirement that furniture can violate by greed.

I have seen a vendor win a package on price and then “value-engineer” the visitor chair out because the rooms were tight. The area table still passed. The interpretive guidance that names a visitor chair did not vanish.

I have seen an owner keep a legal double and buy a private-room package because the JPEG was a single. The extra chair lived in the shower.

The educational use of the number is as a **constraint**, not as a boast. When someone tells you the furniture is healthcare-grade, ask them to draw it in 80 square feet with a roommate, a curtain, and a lift. If they cannot, they are selling a warehouse object.

## What I write on a plan

If I am marking a teaching plan, I write three areas, not one:

1. CMS floor (80 / 100).
2. Bed-and-lift envelope.
3. Leftover for case goods and sitting.

Then I assign objects only to (3). If (3) is a sliver, the honest package is a shallow bedside, a wall closet or a sliding wardrobe, one chair, and an overbed that parks. Everything else is a brochure.

The number in the CFR is simple on purpose. The room is not. Furniture that ignores the leftover is how a legal area becomes an illegal life.

## What 80 and 100 are for

They are a density floor from a payment world that needed a number a surveyor could tape. They are not a dignity floor. They are not a bariatric floor. They are not a memory-care floor. They are the smallest rectangle the federal book will still call a bedroom.

A chain can run one furniture package across Michigan, Arkansas, and Texas because 80 and 100 are the shared floor. The state book may take more. The body in the room may need more. The lift almost always needs more than the leftover the package assumed.

When a brochure says “spacious private suites,” ask for the number. If the number is 100, it is the federal minimum wearing a marketing adjective. If the number is honestly larger, the furniture problem changes and I will be glad. I will still draw the lift first.

## Bariatric rooms and the same floor

The CFR’s area sentence does not grow when the resident does. A bariatric bed is wider. The working side wants more, not less. The chair is a different object. The toilet plan is a different object. If you drop a standard package into that room because the area table still says 80 or 100, you have met the floor and failed the body.

I will not invent a federal bariatric square-footage number that is not in 483.90(e)(1)(ii). I will say the leftover math is merciless, and the honest package for those rooms is a different kit, not a plus sign on the same nightstand.

## How I have seen people cheat the tape

Closet counted as room. Toilet counted as room. A vestibule counted as room. I am not your SOM. Some of those arguments win and some die on appeal. The mill’s version of the cheat is simpler: drawing furniture as if the area were empty. A plan that meets 80 per person with two beds already placed is a plan. A plan that meets 80 on a blank rectangle and then adds beds is a wish.

If you are marking a teaching file, tape the bed envelopes first. Write the leftover second. Then decide whether you still believe the catalog JPEG.
