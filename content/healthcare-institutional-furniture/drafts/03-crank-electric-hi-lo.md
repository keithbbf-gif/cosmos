# Crank, Electric, Hi-Lo

*Educational history of healthcare and nursing-home furniture. Not a purchasing specification for a named building, not a survey opinion, and not clinical advice about beds, rails, or seating for a particular resident.*

The crank lives under a painted footboard, a steel handle you have to stoop for. It has a fold or a pin so it will not catch a passing cart. On a good day it turns through a gearbox and the knee section of the deck rises a few inches. On a bad day it binds, the pin is missing, and someone has already learned to kick the footboard instead. That handle is the first honest sentence the hospital bed tells about itself. The deck learned to move. Everything after — motors, pendants, hi-lo travel, a low click bought for a fall program — is a later dialect of the same sentence.

Gatch and Fowler are medical words that became furniture geometry long before Hill-Rom put wood on a frame. A Fowler position is a way of sitting a patient up. A Gatch frame is a way of breaking the mattress into sections so the furniture can hold that sitting. The names traveled from surgeons and hospital shops into catalog copy and then into the muscle memory of aides who never heard them. What the aide knows is head up, knees up, bed down. What purchasing knows is travel, capacity, and whether the pendant has a lockout. What the sheet often forgets is that *deck height* is not the top of the mattress, and that a Medilodge-era mattress from a second book can erase an inch the frame was sold on.

## Making a deck that bends

A flat iron bedstead is a platform. A hospital bed that can sit a person up is a machine. The difference is joints, a frame in three or four sections, and a way to drive those sections without dumping the occupant on the floor. Early Gatch-type beds used cranks and rods. The geometry is ordinary and easy to romanticize. It is also easy to get wrong. If the thigh section rises without the knee break, the person slides. If the head section is too short, the pillow walks. If the foot section droops, heels press. Furniture shops learned those failures the way they learn any finish failure: by returns and by nurses who would not buy the next one.

The manual crank at the foot is a manufacturing choice as much as a clinical one. It keeps the mechanism out of the aisle. It gives one person, stooping, control of a several-hundred-pound assembly. Some frames had two cranks, or three: head, knee, height. Height on a manual hi-lo is slow work. The handle turns and the whole deck climbs or drops on screws or a scissors. Staff who worked those beds remember the time, not the inch marks. A Medilodge-era building that still had a manual in a storage room was not keeping a relic for a museum. It was keeping a spare that did not need an outlet.

Electric actuators replaced the stoop with a motor and a pendant. Company histories put a Hill-Rom electronic bed at 1950; treat any “first” as `[VERIFY]`. What the motor changed in the shop was wiring, limit switches, a duty cycle, and a new spare-parts list. What it changed on the floor was frequency. A deck that can move twenty times a shift will be asked to. Head up for a meal. Down for a transfer. Up so an aide does not bend as far. Down so a person who rolls will meet a shorter drop. The furniture became a program. Chapter 12 takes the low-low end of that program. Here the problem is the motion itself: crank, then current, then *hi-lo* as skilled-nursing speech.

Hi-lo is not a brand. It is a corridor word for a bed whose height travel is the point. Hospitals had high-high frames for procedures and for staff backs. Nursing homes wanted the other end of the travel — low enough to argue with a fall, high enough to argue with a transfer. Typical modern SNF electric travel is often cited in a 7-to-30-inch *deck* neighborhood. Treat that as a range, not a SKU. `[VERIFY]` any exact travel against a named model year. Deck height is the sleeping surface of the frame. Top-of-mattress is a different number once foam, air, or a replacement surface is on the deck (chapter 16). Catalogs blur the two when they want the low number to look lower.

## Specifying motion without specifying the room

A one-page bed spec in the Medline/Medilodge era could look complete. Electric hi-lo. Pendant. Side-rail sockets. 35- or 36-inch width. Capacity in the 350–450 lb class `[VERIFY model]`. Battery backup, or not. Floor lock, or total-lock casters. The sheet almost never said where the outlet was, how thick the mattress would be, or whether the raised headboard would clear the wall when the deck went to its highest click. Those omissions are chapter 11’s subject. They begin here because *travel* is the number that makes the omissions expensive.

IEC 60601-2-38 was the older particular standard for electrically operated hospital beds. IEC 60601-2-52 is the later medical-bed standard; a 2015 consolidated edition sits among FDA-recognized consensus standards. Those documents are manufacturer and test-lab language. They are not a Medilodge purchasing memo. They exist because a moving, powered deck is a device, and devices collect standards the way furniture collects finish chips. A house-brand punchout bed that claims compliance is claiming a lab path, not a Batesville pedigree. The channel can print the IEC number. It cannot print the room.

Survey language, for its part, wanted a bed of proper size and height — F917 / §483.90(e), older F461 — without saying 18 inches or 7 inches. Proper is a surveyor’s word. Travel is an engineer’s. The chair in chapter 4 is the same gap in another object: the statute changed rights; the furniture still had to be bought by someone looking at a range. Hi-lo travel entered SNF buying as a fall and transfer argument. It entered the building as a frame that needed clearance, a cord, and a mattress that matched the 2006 FDA system if anyone was still looking at zones (chapter 14). Most purchasing offices were looking at price and at whether the last actuator had failed under warranty.

Specifying “assist rail compatible” on the same page as hi-lo travel is how two problems share a SKU. The rail wants a deck and a mattress that keep Zone 3 from becoming a fist-sized gap. The hi-lo wants a low click. The low click plus a thicker mattress is a different geometry than the drawing the factory used. None of that is a protocol. It is why a furniture essay has to keep saying *deck* when the brochure says *bed height*.

Making the later electric is a supplier problem as much as a Batesville one. Actuator vendors, pendant vendors, and caster vendors are not always the name on the headboard. A Carroll-class or Invacare-class frame assembled in the late Medline era could wear a house-brand stencil next year if the distributor changed the badge. The travel range stayed in the same neighborhood. The spare pendant did not. Maintenance learned to keep a bin of look-alike controls that were not interchangeable, and to write the actuator part number on the frame with a marker because the spec sheet in the purchasing file had already been thrown away.

## Buying the motor, wearing the handle

Hospitals bought electric beds as capital and as status. Nursing homes bought them as replacements and as programs. In a Medline-era punchout, the same silhouette could be a capital project or a supply line depending on who signed and what the threshold was (chapter 41). A Medilodge-class operator buying for fifteen buildings wanted a class, not a showroom: Carroll- or Invacare-class electrics, Joerns-class frames in some rehab rooms, house-brand MDS-prefix beds when the number had to come down. The crank bed disappeared from the new-buy column and lingered in the building. Maintenance kept cranks in a bin because electric beds still have emergency cranks on some models, and because a dead motor at 3 a.m. is a furniture problem that feels like a clinical one.

Wearing is where the dialects show. A crank handle loses its grip tape and then its pin. An actuator starts to sound like a drawer that will not close. A pendant gets juice on it, or a pillow over it, or a broken rocker that sticks the head section on the way up. Casters pick up tape. The floor lock is not locked (chapter 38). Staff learn the bed’s personality the way they learn a door that sticks. Purchasing learns a failure rate when the warranty desk stops answering or when the house brand and the name brand have different claim windows (chapter 42).

The hi-lo habit changed bodies as well as objects. Aides who had cranked now waited for a motor. Residents who had sat on a high deck to stand now met a low click they had not ordered. Therapy, as a buyer seat, wanted the deck up for a transfer practice and down for a different argument. The bed became a piece of gym equipment that lived in the bedroom. Chapter 33 will take the gym. The point here is that once the deck could move, every department had a height it preferred, and the frame could only be in one of them.

What EVS poured on the pendant is a cousin of chapter 10. Bleach on a membrane switch is not a furniture standard. It is a nightly fact. Vinyl-covered pendants survived. Some did not. The crank, being steel, laughed at that particular war and lost a different one: rust, paint, a missing handle that turned the bed back into a platform until maintenance found a replacement in a coffee can.

## Residual travel

The painted footboard with a crank under it is still the clearest picture of the problem. Someone had to stoop. The deck had to move. The names — Gatch, Fowler, hi-lo — are catalogs trying to sound like medicine. The inches are ranges that collapse the moment a mattress is swapped. IEC 60601-2-52 will tell a lab how to treat a medical bed. It will not tell a Michigan wing why the overbed table now hits the raised knee section, or why the low click that purchasing liked is the click the resident cannot rise from.

A Medilodge-era double room could hold two electrics with different travels and one leftover crank in the hall “for a minute.” The book sold them as the same class. The floor knew they were not. Nightingale’s bed did not move. Batesville’s bed moved and wanted to look like a dresser. The SNF bed moves, looks like a dresser, and is asked to be a fall program, a transfer tool, and a place to sleep. The crank under the footboard is the ancestor that did not pretend to do all three at once. It only pretended to raise the knees. That was enough work for a handle.

The next essay leaves the gearbox and opens a statute that changed American nursing homes without naming a chair.
