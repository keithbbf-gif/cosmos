# The Visitor Chair That Became a Bed

*Educational history of healthcare and nursing-home furniture. Not a purchasing specification for a named building, not a survey opinion, and not clinical advice about beds, rails, or seating for a particular resident.*

The sleeper is folded at 6 a.m., and folded wrong. Vinyl creased across a mechanism nobody on the night shift was hired to understand, a blanket that belongs to the building, a pillow that belongs to nobody, a chair that has become a thin bed and then failed to become a chair again. Family has gone for coffee or has not. The aide needs the floor to move a person. The gait belt — named here only as a *path*, not as a lesson — wants a square the sleeper is still occupying. That is the hospitality object after it stayed.

Sleepover chairs and fold-outs entered American hospital rooms as a family amenity and entered SNF rooms as a culture-change and short-stay promise: you can stay. They stayed as an infection-control problem, a crowding problem, and a seating problem F558 does not know how to tag because the object is not the resident’s chair. Wooden visitor armchairs — the other species — never folded and still blocked the same square. This essay is about both, and about the difference between a rehab night and a long-stay decade.

## Two chairs, one footprint

The wooden or laminate-armed visitor armchair is the older object. A contract chair from the lobby line, vinyl or a fabric that EVS hated, a seat height that was a waiting-room height, arms that helped a visitor stand and did not help a resident (chapter 26). It sat at the foot or between the beds. It held a purse and a coat that would not go on the 66-inch rod (chapter 21). It was heavy enough that EVS moved it once a week. It was light enough that a family member dragged it onto the overbed table’s toes. When the capital number shrank, this was the piece the package cut (chapter 23). Rooms then had a bed and no second seat, which is how the bed became the visit.

The vinyl sleeper is the later object. A mechanism — click-clack, pull-out, a pad that unfolds toward the door — dressed in healthcare vinyl so that someone could write “bleach 1:10” on a card (chapter 10, chapter 36). The vinyl made it wipeable in theory. The mechanism made it unwipeable in practice. Crumbs, hair, a spilled drink in the hinge, a tear at the welt where the fold happens every night. After 2020 the sleeper was the chair EVS did not want to unfold because unfolding it was a project and because the inside of the fold had been in someone’s mouth. Plastic on the lounge had a cousin here: a sheet thrown over the vinyl, which then slid, which then became another textile in a fire-card story (chapter 39).

Bariatric visitors exist. A 300-pound sleeper next to a 750-pound bed (chapter 13, chapter 30) is the half-purchase again. Wide sleepers eat the roommate’s floor. Narrow sleepers refuse the person who came to stay. Dignity is a furniture problem for families too, and this series rarely says so. A visitor who cannot sit in the room sits in the lobby, which is how visits die.

Nemschoff-class and peer healthcare sleepers — the hospital’s specified ones, with a mechanism that survived a decade — appeared in a few SNF privates and in hospice rooms. The Medline-era book sold a cheaper fold-out that photographed as the same idea. Chapter 29 will take that split in the lobby. In the resident room the split is meaner, because the cheap mechanism is what a family member’s back meets at 3 a.m.

## Short-stay nights, long-stay years

Short-stay rehab after a hospital week is the use the sleeper was sold for. A spouse stays two nights. A daughter stays until the first therapy session. The object is a bed, then a chair, then gone. Infection control can stand a two-night fold if someone actually wipes it. The fire card can stand it if the foam and vinyl were a system when they left the factory. The crowding can stand it if the roommate is out for the weekend. None of those ifs are furniture specifications. They are occupancy luck. A Medilodge-class rehab wing in the 2000s, chasing the post-acute census, ordered sleepers by the corridor. The photographs looked like hospitality. The mornings looked like the opening of this essay.

Long-stay is a different user. Family comes in the afternoon with a bag and does not sleep. The sleeper becomes a recliner that does not recline, a storage shelf, a place to pile the laundry that the wardrobe will not take. Or it becomes the resident’s second bed — a nap that is not on the electric, a place to sit that is softer than the dining chair — which is how a visitor object turns into an unassessed resident surface. F558’s seat-height and arm language is about the resident’s seating. A sleeper used as a day bed is a geri-chair cousin (chapter 25) without the casters and without the tag anyone files. This essay will not turn that into a program. It will say the object changed jobs and nobody respecified it.

Hospitals learned, in the same decades, to put a designated family sleep surface in private rooms and to keep it out of the transfer square. The SNF double cannot designate what it does not have. One sleeper in a double is a roommate argument. Two sleepers is a fire-load and a mopping argument. Zero sleepers is a family complaint on the tour. Corporate spec, facing fifteen buildings, picked one and lived with the other two complaints.

Wooden armchairs in long-stay rooms earn a patina the sleeper never will. They become “Mom’s chair” without being Mom’s. They block the same square. They do not hide crumbs in a hinge. EVS will wipe a wood arm. EVS will not disassemble a sleeper. That is why some DONs, after 2020, asked purchasing to stop replacing sleepers and to send armchairs. That is why some families, on a Medilodge-era tour, asked where they would sleep. Both questions are furniture questions. Neither is a care plan.

Hospitals had already learned a version of this in the private-room boom: a designated family sleep surface, sometimes a better mechanism, sometimes a recliner that was honest about being a recliner. The SNF copied the wish and not the square footage. A Medilodge-era short-stay corridor chasing post-acute length-of-stay ordered sleepers the way it ordered overbed tables — one per bed in the photograph, one per two beds in the basement, zero in the room that had a bariatric frame (chapter 13) because the wide deck had already eaten the leftover floor. Hospitality is a leftover-floor product.

CAL 117 hangtags and the fold are a construction argument chapter 39 will generalize. Here the fold is specific: the foam must bend every night and still pass a smolder test that imagines a cigarette on a still seat. Folding foam delaminates. The vinyl at the crease whitened, then split. After 2020, bleach on that crease made the split earlier. The wooden armchair had no crease. That is why some buildings, as already said, went back to wood arms and called it infection control when it was also mechanics.

## The square the belt needs, and the vinyl the bottle meets

Crowding is the polite word. A double room with two electrics, two overbed tables, two cabinets, a wardrobe, a curtain that does not close (chapter 24), and a sleeper at 6 a.m. has no remaining square. Lifts need a square. Chairs that help a person stand need a square. The visitor object occupies the only leftover. Staff move it into the hall, which is how the corridor becomes a furniture warehouse and how the fire marshal and the surveyor both find a chair “for a minute.” The geri chair in the hall (chapter 25) and the sleeper in the hall are cousins. One is a resident. One is a guest. Both are in the egress.

Infection control did not invent the sleeper’s dirt. It named it. Healthcare vinyl on a sleeper is still a face and a welt and a zipper (chapter 36). The mechanism is a third material: grease, painted steel, a fabric sling inside some pull-outs that is not on the vinyl card. List N disinfectants (chapter 10) were a class of bottle, not a furniture standard. What EVS poured on a sleeper in 2020 was whatever they poured on the day-room sofa, and the mechanism rusted or the vinyl stiffened or the fold seized. The chair that survived 2020 was often the wooden armchair. The sleeper that survived was the one nobody unfolded, which is another way of saying it had already quit being a bed.

CAL TB 117-2013 hangtags sit in the sleeper’s zipper as they sit in every other padded object (chapter 39). Historical TB 133 talk wandered into some specs because a sleeper looked like a public-occupancy couch. California later pulled TB 133 from many uses; the scope of that pullback wants `[VERIFY]` before anyone treats a date as a ban. The SNF file still wants a card. The sleeper still wants a foam that can fold. Folding foam is a different construction from a lounge seat. Failures start at the fold.

Family as furniture users is the sentence purchasing forgets. Spec sheets imagine a resident and a staff body. They do not imagine a visitor who is 80, who will sleep at a bad angle, who will use the chair arms to stand, who will set a coffee on the overbed table and pull the sleeper into the cabinet. F584 likes family photographs and drapes. It does not specify a sleep surface for the person in the photograph. Culture change (chapter 6) wanted the family in the room. The punchout sold a fold-out. The gap between those two sentences is this chapter.

Short-stay rehab census made the sleeper a marketing object in the 2000s in a way long-stay never had. A hospital discharge planner walking a Medilodge-class wing was shown a fold-out and told the spouse could stay. The spouse stayed two nights and then drove home because the vinyl crease and the roommate’s television were not rest. The object remained. The next short-stay family inherited a mechanism already tired. Long-stay families, by then, had stopped asking where they would sleep. They asked where they would sit for an hour. The wooden armchair answers that question if it is still in the room. The package, next, is how it leaves.

## Folded wrong

The residual is the fold. A sleeper that will not become a chair again is a hospitality promise that has become a crowding device. A wooden armchair that never tried to be a bed is the older, smaller, more honest object — and it will still be the piece the package cuts when the capital number shrinks. The next essay is that number: a one-line room package, bed through visitor chair, color-matched laminate, six freight appointments, and the chair that disappears first.

## Notes

- F584 / §483.10(i): homelike environment, personalization, family presence as a survey mood — not a sleeper specification.
- F558: resident seating accommodation. A visitor sleeper used as a resident day bed is a job change, not a tagged seating program in this essay.
- CAL TB 117-2013: smolder hangtag class. Historical TB 133 and later California pullback: `[VERIFY]` scope; see chapter 39.
- Vinyl cards, bleach ratios, and 2020 stress: chapters 10 and 36. Mechanism dirt is a construction fact, not a disinfectant recipe.
- Short-stay vs long-stay use: composite Medline/Medilodge-era occupancy, not a named wing’s census.
- No transfer sequence, no gait-belt method. The belt appears only as a path the sleeper occupies.
