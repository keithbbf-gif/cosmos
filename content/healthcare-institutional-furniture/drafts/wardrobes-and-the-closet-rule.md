# Wardrobes and the closet rule

A wardrobe is a closet the architect forgot, or a closet the value-engineer deleted, or a closet the 1968 plant never had.

CMS does not say “wardrobe.” It says **individual closet space** in the bedroom with clothes racks and shelves accessible to the resident, and **private** closet space — your clothes not the roommate’s. Appendix PP is the long version: hanging you can reach, shelves you can reach, everyday clothes in the room, season stuff maybe elsewhere, help if you cannot use the closet.

I have built a lot of tall boxes that were trying to be that sentence. Some of them were. Some of them were furniture.

## History: the locker, the reform, the tall box

Wards stored clothes in lockers and in a room down the hall. A metal locker is a staff object. It photographs as storage and behaves as a warehouse. Hill-Burton hospitals and the nursing homes that inherited their density did not owe a bedroom closet in the domestic sense. They owed a count of beds.

OBRA 1987 made quality of life into federal language. The living descendants are 42 CFR 483.10(i)(4) and 483.90(e)(2)(iv): closet space, racks, shelves, privacy from the roommate. F584 points at that closet. F917, in the teaching-file map, is the furniture-and-closet tag. Older binders say F252 and F461. Translate the number. Do not discard the sentence.

Homelike wanted the locker to look like a bedroom. Mills answered with a tall wooden box. Some boxes had a rod a seated person could reach. Some had a cathedral arch and a rod at 68 inches. The punchout sold the arch. The tag wanted the inch.

Culture-change houses sometimes built real closets and then bought wardrobes anyway because the package was a package. I have seen both in the same corridor: a built-in with no rod on the construction set, and a freestanding box in front of the only outlet. The tag will take either a closet or a wardrobe. It will not take neither.

Medilodge-era special-buys froze a tall box so a warehouse could stock a pull. That is a good idea if the rod height was measured against the people in the chair. It is a bad idea if the rod height was the shop default for a standing coat. I will not invent a PO. I will say the public geography on our list sat in the same argument as every other chain of that window: everyday clothes in the room, or a pretty box that is a staff closet.

## Shop geometry: rod, depth, door, leftover

A residential wardrobe rod sits where a standing adult in a showroom can hang a coat. A SNF resident may be in a wheelchair, may not abduct a shoulder, may be 5-foot-1 and 89.

If the rod is at 68 inches and the only person who can use it is a CNA, you have specified a staff closet. The tag can live with staff help. The tag does not live with a story that the resident “doesn’t care about clothes.” Dignity is in the same book.

Shop practice I will own: I want a rod height **named on the drawing**, often with a second rod or a hook rail lower, and shelves that do not start at the moon. I will not tattoo a single number as law. Measure the people. Measure the chair. Measure the reach. Then write the inch.

A pull-down rod is a mechanism and a pinch and a thing that dies. A low rod is a dimension that does not die. Prefer the dimension.

Clothes want depth. A 12-inch box will hold folded things and a hook. It will not hold a hanging shirt without crushing. A 24-inch box will hold hanging and will eat the room. Shop range: **20–24 inches deep** if it is truly a hanging closet; shallower if it is a cubby and you have admitted that. Do not call a 12-inch wall cabinet a closet and then wonder why the tag mentioned racks.

Shelves: reachable, not a hotel extra at 72 inches. A high shelf for season stuff is fine if the everyday stuff is not up there. The guidance allows season elsewhere. Use that permission instead of stacking a life over someone’s head.

Doors are aisle. Hinged doors need swing. In a double, swing is the roommate’s shin. Sliding doors need a track that will collect lint and jump. Bi-folds need hinges that will fail and a habit of pinching fingers.

I like sliders in tight leftover and I hate their tracks. I like hinged doors in privates that have the swing. I like a curtain only when the fire standard is named and the resident can work it. A curtain is not a joke. It is a cubicle idea applied to clothes. It can be the right object in a memory-care room where a door is a puzzle.

CMS’s **80** / **100** square-foot floors are why this paragraph exists. The leftover after two beds and a curtain track will not forgive a 24-inch box with an 18-inch swing. Draw the lift, then the door swing, then the chair. If the chair lost, the wardrobe is wrong even if the area table passed.

A 72-inch box in a room that also has a low bed is a climb. Pulls become rungs. A drawer or a shoe shelf at shin height becomes a step toward a window sill. The low-bed draft asked you to look from the floor. Do it here with the door open.

## Privacy is a partition, not a lock

Private closet space means separation from the roommate, not a padlock. A shared open rod in a double is a tag. A wardrobe with a divider and two doors is a closet. Two small wardrobes are a closet. A locked staff cabinet with everyone’s Sunday best is a warehouse.

Locks on wardrobes have the same dignity fight as locks on nightstands. A memory-care lock that keeps a person from wearing three dresses at once may be a care-plan object. A lock that keeps a person from their sweater is a different object. Write who holds the key on the drawing so the punch list cannot invent it.

F917 wants clothing protected from casual access by others. That is the roommate and the hallway. It is not a permission to hide the resident from their own hanger. If the person cannot use the closet, the facility owes assistance or alternative storage they can use. A rod at the moon plus “we help” is a story. A hook rail they can reach is a closet.

## Tip, anchor, and the youth-furniture cousin

A tall box with a heavy door is a tip. The youth-furniture series in this tree spends a lot of pages on dressers and STURDY. A SNF wardrobe is not a clothing-storage unit in the CPSC sense just because it holds clothes — I will not drag 16 CFR 1261 onto a millwork closet without a lawyer — but **physics does not care about the CFR number**.

Anchor it if it can walk. Especially if a drawer or a pull-out becomes a step. Especially in memory care. Especially if the vinyl floor is slick and the buffer hits the plinth.

A mill that ships a 72-inch box with no anchoring story is shipping a wish. Write the fastener, the wall type, and who installs it. A wish on a slick floor in a **71 to 81 °F** room — warm vinyl, a buffer, a confused pull on a door — is how a tall box becomes a fall program.

## Built-in vs. freestanding

A built-in closet is architecture. A wardrobe is a product. The tag will take either. Maintenance will not.

Built-ins hide plumbing and then someone has to cut them. Freestanding boxes can move when a bed program changes. Freestanding boxes also migrate into hallways.

If the architect drew a closet, count the rod on the construction set, not the furniture set. I have seen packages that bought wardrobes for rooms that had closets, and rooms that had neither.

Sprinkler clearance is the two-inch cut I keep confessing. Crowns lose two inches in elevators and under heads. Specify a crown you can recut, or specify no crown. A laminate box with a swollen core cannot take that recut. Arkansas hardwood can. That is not a sales sentence. It is a Thursday on a live floor.

## Channel notes

A chain special-buy wardrobe is how you get 80 boxes that share a pull. Good. Freeze the rod height in that buy. Bad special-buys freeze the cathedral arch and leave the rod at the shop default.

The Medline punchout loved a pair of handsome hinged doors with a cathedral arch. The cathedral arch is how you sell homelike. The swing is how you fail the leftover.

Medline-era freight: a wardrobe is an LTL object that gets a crushed corner. Specify a corner that can take a crush or a carton that is real. Install: the elevator, the door, the sprinkler clearance.

Bradley Brand’s Arkansas hardwood story belongs here if anywhere. A solid box can be recut. A laminate box with a swollen core cannot. The bleach-and-hardwood draft is the chemistry. This draft is the tag.

GPO language that said “meets closet requirements” on a 12-inch box was a hope. Requirements are reachable racks and private space. Depth is how hanging happens. A hope does not hold a shirt.

## What I ask in a walkthrough

Open the door. Can I, sitting in a chair, reach a hanger? Is there a second person’s clothing on the same rod? Is there a lock, and who has the key? Does the door hit a bed? Is the box anchored? Is there a smell that says the interior was never finished?

Look at the leftover with the door in motion. Look at the outlet the box covered. Look at the crown under the head. Look from the floor if the bed is low. Look at whether everyday clothes are in the room or in a warehouse down the hall.

If you can answer those, you are closer to F917 than a finish board will ever get you.
