# HPL, TFL, Thermofoil, Poly Skin

*Educational history of healthcare and nursing-home furniture. Not a purchasing specification for a named building, not a survey opinion, and not clinical advice about beds, rails, or seating for a particular resident.*

The nightstand corner is swollen. The oak-look top still photographs like the 2005 binder. Under the edge band, the particleboard has taken a drink — bleach drip from the overbed table, a water pitcher, the mop that is lifted too late, urine that found the joint the sales sheet did not draw. The vinyl on the visitor chair can split at a welt (chapter 36). The casegood fails at an edge. Four skins have been asked to prevent that drink: high-pressure laminate, thermally fused laminate, a thermofoil wrap, and a molded polyurethane that tries to retire the edge altogether. This essay is about those skins. The Medline-era room package sold them as oak, then as cherry, then as gray woodgrain (chapter 9). The board underneath stayed hungry.

A Medilodge-class operator specifying a resident-room collection did not usually write “HPL.” It wrote a color number and a price. The factory chose the skin that fit the number. House-brand bedside cabinets and wardrobes in a punchout could be TFL boxes with a thicker top, or thermofoil throughout, or an HPL horizontal and a paper vertical. Kwalu-class molded polyurethane showed up more often on seating and on high-abuse tables than on a full nightstand run, and it showed up when someone had a different budget than the package. The swollen corner does not care which meeting approved it. It cares whether the edge was a band, a wrap, or a pour.

## High pressure, low pressure, the same oak photograph

High-pressure laminate — HPL — is a stack of kraft papers and a decorative sheet, pressed with resin until it is a hard skin you can buy in a sheet and glue to a panel. NEMA LD 3 is the factory-language cousin for how that sheet is tested: wear, stain, scuff, a boiling-water idea of abuse `[VERIFY the edition a given mill cited]`. It is not a CMS tag. Horizontal surfaces in healthcare — tops that take a pitcher, a chart, an elbow — are where HPL earned its keep. A nightstand top in HPL over particleboard can outlast the drawer box. The failure migrates to the edge band and to the back corner that meets the wall wipe.

Thermally fused laminate — TFL, sometimes still called melamine in hallway speech — is a thinner decorative paper fused to the board in the press. No separate sheet to glue. Cheaper. Fine for a vertical that is mostly looked at. Less fine for a top that is used as a bumper. A Medline-era wardrobe side in TFL with an HPL top is a common hybrid, not a fraud. It is a price. The photograph in the book does not label the hybrid. The mop does.

Both skins want an edge. PVC or ABS banding, ironed or hot-melted, is the usual answer on a rectangular box. The band is a different polymer from the face. It shrinks. It is hit by the med cart (chapter 35) and by the bed’s side rail in the raised click. Once the band lifts, the particleboard is a straw. Swell is not a mystery chemistry. It is water plus a wood-chip panel that was never meant to be a boat. Formaldehyde-era boards and later CARB-era boards both swell. The regulation changed the glue story. It did not change thirst.

Post-formed HPL — a sheet rolled over a bullnose so the top and the edge are one piece of laminate — shows up on nurses’ stations and on some dining tables (chapter 27). It is better than a skinny band on a tight radius. It is still a sheet with a joint somewhere, usually at the underside drip. Sales language likes to imply there is no joint to fail. On a post-form top that can be almost true along the front edge. On a four-sided nightstand it is a wish. The back corner, against the wall, is where the pitcher drips and the wipe does not quite dry.

## Thermofoil: the wrap that remembers a sink

Thermofoil is a vinyl film, heated and vacuum-formed over a shaped MDF or particleboard. Kitchen-cabinet shops know it. Healthcare casegoods used it because a shaped edge could look residential without a banding line, and because a membrane press is cheaper than a post-former for small runs. The film remembers heat. It remembers a wet cutout. On a vanity or a bedside cabinet with a fake drawer radius, the foil creases. At a sink — even a resident-room sink the casegood only neighbors — steam lifts the film. Once lifted, the MDF swells in a softer, more tragic way than a TFL box. It becomes fur.

Peel starts where a router left a sharp corner or where someone put a hot hair tool on the nightstand that was also a vanity (chapter 20). The Medline-era “homelike” story loved a profiled top. Thermofoil delivered the profile. A decade later the profile is a white line of substrate. Replacement is usually a new box, not a new film. Maintenance cannot iron a membrane. It can tape the lift, which is how a wing acquires silver tape as a finish.

Thermofoil also photographs well in a punchout. The gray woodgrain of the 2010s was often a film. The honey oak of the 2000s was often a film or a TFL. Families touching a drawer front are touching plastic either way. Culture-change language that asked for “real wood” (chapter 6) sometimes got a thicker veneer on a lobby piece and thermofoil in the room. The survey word *homelike* (F584) can accept both. The mop distinguishes them.

## Molded polyurethane, and the edge that is a pour

Kwalu and its peers sold a molded polyurethane skin — a hard elastomeric shell over a core — as the answer to the edge-band problem. Chairs, tables, some casegood-like pieces: the face and the corner are the same material. Gouges still happen. Fade still happens. A joint still exists where the shell meets a frame or a bottom cap. What does not happen, if the pour is honest, is a PVC band lifting off a thirsty core after the cart clips the corner. That is a construction fact, not a slogan. The banned sales word for this idea is the one that means “without a joint.” Use it only when the object actually lacks the joint you are naming. A molded arm cap can be one pour. A nightstand that is a poly box glued to a laminate back is two products.

These pieces entered SNF dining rooms and activity rooms more readily than they replaced every bedside cabinet. Price, weight, and the corporate color board all argued. A Medilodge-era package that had to match fifteen buildings’ oak TFL was not about to throw the nightstand specification to a molded house. What you see, on a composite Michigan wing (chapter 44), is a poly dining chair at a TFL table on HPL feet, next to a thermofoil dresser whose foil has already lifted at the profile. Four skins, one room, one mop.

Poly skins also change repair. You do not staple them. You do not iron them. You live with a gouge or you replace the shell. Maintenance, which is good at screws and bands (chapter 40), has less to do. That can be a virtue. It can also mean a damaged chair sits in the hall the way a freight-damaged chair sits in the hall (chapter 42): no one has a repair ritual that matches the material.

## The board, the bumper, the wall

Particleboard and MDF are the cheap cores. Plywood shows up in better tops and in older casegoods. A swollen corner is usually particleboard because that is what the package bought. Moisture from the floor — a flood, a mop, a bathroom that shares a wall with a wardrobe — enters at the toe. Moisture from the top enters at the band. Moisture from the side enters where the cart’s PVC bumper (chapter 35, chapter 40) has already knocked the band loose. The same bumper specification that was meant to save the box becomes, once cracked, a gutter.

F917 / §483.90(e) wants functional furniture. A drawer that will not close because the box has swollen is not functional. A wardrobe whose toe-kick has become compost is not a closet a resident can use (chapter 21). Survey language does not say HPL. It says the furniture works. The skin is how purchasing thought it would keep working. The swollen corner is how the wing reports otherwise.

Wall clearance is a hidden finish spec. A nightstand shoved to the headwall so the bed can hi-lo (chapter 11) puts the back edge in the splash of a handwash and a pitcher. HPL on the top does not protect a raw back that was never banded because it would face drywall. Installers skip the back band. Years later the back swell meets the outlet the spec also forgot. The furniture and the device share a stain.

House-brand and name-brand boxes can share a core and differ only in the thickness of the HPL and the quality of the hot-melt. A claim desk will argue about a dent (chapter 42) more readily than about a swell that appeared in month fourteen, after the warranty’s “moisture exclusion.” Thermofoil warranties, when they exist in the file, often exclude heat and standing water. Standing water is the nightstand’s job description. The exclusion is the real specification.

Dining tables show the same skins at a larger scale (chapter 27). An oak-look apron that a wheelchair cannot clear is often TFL on a particleboard rail; the flip-top that EVS loves is often HPL because someone knew the top would take a pour. When the flip hardware fails (chapter 40), the top still looks new and the rail has already swollen at the screw. Therapy mat tables (chapter 33) take a wooden box step and a bleach wipe on a skin that was specified as “hospital laminate” without naming HPL or TFL. The gym does not file NEMA numbers. It files a work order when the corner goes soft.

Color boards hide construction twice. First in the photograph: gray woodgrain can be a 0.040-inch HPL or a film. Second in the replacement: a damaged nightstand is swapped for “the same oak” from a later punchout, and the later oak is a thinner TFL with a paler core. The wing then has two honeys that do not meet at the drawer line. Homelike (F584) does not score that mismatch. Families do. Maintenance does, because the new box takes a different screw length and the old bumper does not clip on (chapter 40).

The residual object is the corner you can press with a thumb and feel give. Oak, cherry, gray — the print is intact. The board is not. Four skins were available in the same decades, in the same catalogs, to the same multi-building Michigan buyers. The package chose the skin that fit a number. The mop chose the failure. The next essay drops to the floor, where casters — total-lock, directional, a stem full of hair — decide whether the box you just specified can leave the room without waking the wing.

## Notes

- HPL / TFL / thermofoil / molded polyurethane are construction classes, not current SKUs. Kwalu appears as a class of molded healthcare skin, not as a storefront.
- NEMA LD 3: laminate performance language used by mills. Not a survey tag. Edition and test list `[VERIFY]` if quoting a number.
- CMS F917 / §483.90(e): functional furniture; the regulation does not name a laminate grade.
- F584 homelike: finish color is not the test; a swollen box can still be oak-look.
- Moisture exclusions in warranties are channel habit; do not invent a named mill’s clause. See chapter 42 for freight and claims.
- “No joint” / one-pour language: a construction fact only when the skin is actually a single molded face. Do not borrow the sales adjective the validator bans.
