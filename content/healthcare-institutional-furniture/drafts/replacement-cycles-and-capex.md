Furniture does not age as a set.

The vinyl dies. The caster dies. The drawer guide dies. The bed motor dies on a different calendar. The wardrobe is still honest in year twelve and looks embarrassing next to a new mattress. A Medline-era package that was bought as a wedding is lived as a junkyard of anniversaries.

I have been asked to “refresh the wood” when the wood was the only thing still working. I have been asked to replace wood because a vinyl program changed and someone wanted the stain to match a new green.

## Three clocks

**The wipe clock.** Covers, tops, and edges. Infection-control intensity is the multiplier. A house that bathed everything in bleach has a short wipe clock. A house that used a named product and a film that met it has a longer one. The bleach-and-hardwood draft is the chemistry. This draft is the calendar. A conversion varnish that printed a pretty sample and then blushed under bleach is a residential finish on an institutional clock. A vinyl that cracked at the staple line is a cover clock, not a frame clock. Do not replace a frame because a cover died unless the frame died too.

**The mechanism clock.** Columns, motors, guides, casters, flip-top latches. Mechanisms do not refinish. They condemn. An overbed that will not rise is a med-pass problem at 6 a.m. A drawer guide that racks is a dignity problem when the only sweater is stuck. A caster that dies on a low bed is a fall program wearing a wheel. Write the mechanism as a line you will replace. Do not write it as a hope that maple will save it.

**The dignity clock.** The object still works and no longer looks like a house. F584 will not give you a year. A daughter will. Appendix PP names furniture that does not reflect a home-like environment or is uncomfortable as an institutional leftover. It does not say “replace in year seven.” Capital planning that only hears the dignity clock will waste money on honest wood that needed a new cover. Planning that only hears the mechanism clock will live in an ugly legal room.

Those three clocks do not start together except on a new-plant day. Every other day the building is a mix. A capital plan that treats the mix as a wedding will buy matching and then live in a junkyard again.

CMS tags give you a condition, not a schedule. F584 and F917 care whether the chair works, whether the closet reaches, whether the room is a house in the direction the guidance means. They do not care that the stain no longer matches the new mattress border. Match is a narcotic. The room-package draft already said so. Heights and edges are the match that matters.

## Beds vs. case goods

Beds are devices with service contracts and a fear file. They get money. Nightstands are furniture and get leftovers. That is how you end up with a new low bed next to a 1998 cabinet that is now a cliff.

FDA’s 2006 guidance is about systems: frame, rails, mattress. From January 1, 1985, to January 1, 2006, the Agency’s files held about 691 entrapment reports, 413 of them deaths. I keep putting those numbers on the table because a capital plan that changes the bed and forgets the mattress, or changes the mattress and forgets the rail, is how Zone 3 is manufactured after the fact. Furniture is outside the diagram. Furniture can still kill the program if it blocks the working side or becomes a canyon next to a floor-level deck.

Write a **interface review** whenever the bed clock rings. Mattress match, Zone 3, working side, nightstand height. The mattress draft is the long version. CapEx that forgets the interface is how a safety program buys a canyon.

Beds outlive nightstands when the house has a service contract and a biomed who will replace a motor. Nightstands outlive beds when the house bought honest boxes and a cheap fleet. I have seen both. The order is not a law. The interface is the law you can write without a statute.

A low-bed program is a furniture program. The chair and the deck are one specification. A high chair next to a floor-level bed is a cliff. A low chair next to a high bed is a slide. If the bed clock rings and the chair clock does not, you still have a cliff. Budget the chair.

## Vinyl is not a capital object

Treat covers as operating. Attic stock. A chair frame can be a capital object if it is a frame. A foam sculpture is a consumable wearing a capital invoice.

Bradley Brand’s argument for wood frames was always this clock. A maple chair can take a new cover. A stapled foam blob cannot. If the house will never re-cover, the argument dies. Ask before you romance the grain.

Medline-era vinyl programs were a binder of swatches that changed names every two years. The chair died when the vinyl died. A capital plan that treats the chair as maple and then throws the chair away because the teal is gone has paid capital money for an operating death. Attic stock a bolt. Write the vinyl grade that will still exist, not the picture.

Laminate faces have a wipe clock and an edge clock. The peel is the tell. Once moisture gets in, the core swells and the piece is done. That is not a refinish. That is a landfill. Hardwood plywood or solid hardwood with a finish system can take a new top or a new film. The bleach draft is the negotiation. The calendar is: **repairable objects belong on a longer clock if the house will actually repair.**

Cubicle curtains have a flame clock and a laundry clock. A panel that met 701 when it was new and failed after a bleach career is a replacement object. Attic stock a panel. Do not put curtains on a ten-year capital cycle and then wonder why the double is a ward.

## How houses actually buy

Four ways. I am naming types, not SKUs.

**Flood / outbreak / survey.** Panic punchout. No leftover measurement. Twenty nightstands after a pipe burst. Four chairs after a wound-care program changed the foam. The punchout’s honest work, and the punchout’s dishonest work when someone uses the same button to buy a whole floor at midnight because the capital meeting was yesterday.

**Wing renovation.** A package, a color, a photographer. New case goods, maybe new chairs, beds as existing or a mixed fleet. This is where Zone 3 is born if you also change mattresses. This is where the dignity clock spends money. This is where matching becomes a narcotic.

**One-off.** A condemned chair, a condemned column. A single door crushed on an elevator. A lock the care plan just changed. The channel was excellent at this when the page still existed. It was helpless when the special-buy had ended and there was no attic stock.

**New plant.** The only time the clocks start together. Everything. This is where people forget the closet because the architect drew one and the value-engineer deleted it. Count rods on the drawing you will build from, not the rendering.

The Medilodge-era channel was excellent at the first and the third. It was average at the second. It was as good as the drawing at the fourth.

If you are writing a capital plan, name which of the four you are in. Do not use a new-plant drawing for a flood. Do not use a flood punchout for a new plant. Do not use a photographer’s wing kit to replace one overbed.

Named geography on the public list — Medilodge of Montrose; Byrd Haven, Searcy; Green Acres, Paragould — sat in those four types at different times. I will not assign a type to a name. I will say the type matters more than the operator.

## What to replace first

I walk condemned piles. The order I usually see, not a law:

1. Sinking overbeds and dead casters (function).
2. Chairs no one can stand from (function and dignity).
3. Torn vinyl and open foam (infection).
4. Wardrobes that fail reach (tag).
5. Ugly honest wood (dignity, last).

Owners often invert the list because ugly photographs. Surveyors and hips do not invert it.

A capital plan that starts with the wardrobe crowns because the photograph is tired, while the dining chairs are 17-inch holes and the overbeds will not rise, has spent the year on a costume. F917’s chair sentence and F584’s uncomfortable-furniture sentence are not photograph sentences. They are use sentences.

Bariatric rooms and memory-care rooms should break out of the standard cycle. A + on a chair is not a 500-pound program. A bird print is not a memory program. The room-package draft already told you to break those types out. A capital plan that replaces “the floor” as one SKU will punish the rooms that were already a different job.

Personal belongings are not on this list. A resident’s own chair is a rights object until it is a fire, a tip, or a blocked egress. Do not condemn it because it does not match the new teal.

## The two-inch fund

Leave money for Thursday. The punch list, the sprinkler crown, the rod that was wrong, the chair that did not match the low bed. A capital plan with no two-inch fund will steal from attic stock, then from next year’s vinyl.

I have called this install. It is also CapEx. Call it something so it exists.

The channel-economics draft already said after-hours, liftgate, haul-away, and protection of vinyl are lines. The two-inch fund is the line for the collision between the drawing and the live ceiling. I have cut two inches off a crown more times than I will admit in a catalog. That cut is not a failure of the mill. It is the building telling the truth.

A plan that spends every dollar on matching and nothing on Thursday will look good in the board packet and bad on Vienna Road. I will not invent the dollar. I will invent the category so a later owner cannot say nobody mentioned it.

## What I write in a capital note

“Clocks: wipe / mechanism / dignity. This buy is (flood / wing / one-off / new plant). Bed interface review: mattress, rail, working side, nightstand height. Covers are operating; frames are capital if they are frames. Attic stock: pulls, vinyl, one bedside, one curtain panel. Two-inch fund: named. This note is not a depreciation schedule.”

That paragraph will not please a finance committee that wants a seven-year law. There is no seven-year law. There is a condition, a tag, a hip, and a daughter.
