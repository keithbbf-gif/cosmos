# Replacement cycles and CapEx

Furniture does not age as a set.

The vinyl dies. The caster dies. The drawer guide dies. The bed motor dies on a different calendar. The wardrobe is still honest in year twelve and looks embarrassing next to a new mattress. A Medline-era package that was bought as a wedding is lived as a junkyard of anniversaries.

I have been asked to “refresh the wood” when the wood was the only thing still working. I have been asked to replace wood because a vinyl program changed and someone wanted the stain to match a new green.

## Three clocks

**The wipe clock.** Covers, tops, and edges. Infection-control intensity is the multiplier. A house that bathed everything in bleach has a short wipe clock. A house that used a named product and a film that met it has a longer one.

**The mechanism clock.** Columns, motors, guides, casters, flip-top latches. Mechanisms do not refinish. They condemn.

**The dignity clock.** The object still works and no longer looks like a house. F584 will not give you a year. A daughter will.

Capital planning that only hears the dignity clock will waste money. Planning that only hears the mechanism clock will live in an ugly legal room.

## Beds vs. case goods

Beds are devices with service contracts and a fear file. They get money. Nightstands are furniture and get leftovers. That is how you end up with a new low bed next to a 1998 cabinet that is now a cliff.

Write a **interface review** whenever the bed clock rings. Mattress match, Zone 3, working side, nightstand height. The mattress draft is the long version. CapEx that forgets the interface is how a safety program buys a canyon.

## Vinyl is not a capital object

Treat covers as operating. Attic stock. A chair frame can be a capital object if it is a frame. A foam sculpture is a consumable wearing a capital invoice.

Bradley Brand’s argument for wood frames was always this clock. A maple chair can take a new cover. A stapled foam blob cannot. If the house will never re-cover, the argument dies. Ask before you romance the grain.

## How houses actually buy

- **Flood / outbreak / survey.** Panic punchout. No leftover measurement.
- **Wing renovation.** A package, a color, a photographer.
- **One-off.** A condemned chair, a condemned column.
- **New plant.** The only time the clocks start together.

The Medilodge-era channel was excellent at the first and the third. It was average at the second. It was as good as the drawing at the fourth.

If you are writing a capital plan, name which of the four you are in. Do not use a new-plant drawing for a flood.

## What to replace first

I walk condemned piles. The order I usually see, not a law:

1. Sinking overbeds and dead casters (function).
2. Chairs no one can stand from (function and dignity).
3. Torn vinyl and open foam (infection).
4. Wardrobes that fail reach (tag).
5. Ugly honest wood (dignity, last).

Owners often invert the list because ugly photographs. Surveyors and hips do not invert it.

## The two-inch fund

Leave money for Thursday. The punch list, the sprinkler crown, the rod that was wrong, the chair that did not match the low bed. A capital plan with no two-inch fund will steal from attic stock, then from next year’s vinyl.

I have called this install. It is also CapEx. Call it something so it exists.
