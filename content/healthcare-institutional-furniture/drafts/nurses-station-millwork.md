# Nurses’ station millwork

The nurses’ station is the last honest ward object in a building that wants to be a house.

It is high enough to write on, low enough to talk over if someone was kind, deep enough to hide a printer, and sharp enough to catch a hip. Culture change wanted it gone or wanted it to be a table. Survey and staffing wanted a place to see the corridor. The Medline era split the difference: a **bunker with a corbel**.

I have built these. They are millwork wearing a furniture invoice. If you treat them as a desk from an office catalog, the charting will win and the resident will lose the sightline, or the reverse.

## Sightline is the program

A station that cannot see the corridor is a back office. A station that is a fortress is a ward. The furniture question is the **transaction height** and the **cut-through**.

A high front that hides a seated nurse from a seated resident is a wall. A low front that leaves a computer in a lap is a theft and a HIPAA story. A window that is a real opening is how a person in a wheelchair asks for a glass of water without ringing.

Write the seated-to-seated conversation as a dimension. I will not invent the inch for your state. Measure a wheelchair arm and a nurse’s chair and the binder.

Glass: glare, infection control, and whether a person with no depth perception will hit it. Open: sound and the meds. There is no free choice. There is a choice you can name.

Nightingale’s leftover is a nurse who can see a row. The postwar plant kept the bridge. Culture change wanted the bridge to become a kitchen table. Furniture cannot invent a staffing ratio that makes a table enough. Furniture can refuse to build a wall and call it a desk.

I sit both sides. If I cannot see a face from a wheelchair, the station is a costume for a ward that has not admitted it. If I can read a screen from the corridor, the station is a costume for privacy that has not admitted it. Those two failures can live in the same piece of oak.

## Work surface vs. furniture

Charting, phones, meds, pizza, a Christmas cactus. The top is a lab bench. The public face wants to be a sideboard.

Shop practice: a sealed, wipeable work top on the staff side; a face on the public side that can take a hit from a wheelchair and a wipe from EVS. Do not put raw wood on the work side and call it homelike. Homelike is for the resident. The work side is a shop.

Cable management is a fire and a trip. A station without a defined cable path will grow a nest in a month. The nest is not charming.

The staff side fails first. Coffee rings, tape residue, a label gun, a hole saw for a monitor arm that was not in the drawing. If the core is particleboard and the edge is a sticker, the first hole is oatmeal. If the top is a solid surface or a phenolic or a sealed hardwood with an honest film, the hole can be a hole you fill. I will not tell you wood is always better. I will tell you the work side is a chemistry, not a corbel.

Printers want a cave. The cave wants ventilation and a path for paper that is not a trip. A printer under a counter that a wheelchair user cannot see past is how you buy a wall of plastic noise. Put the cave where the sightline can survive it, or admit the printer owns the corridor.

## Heights: standing, sitting, rolling

A station is three heights pretending to be one object.

Standing height is for a signature, a quick note, a person who will not sit because sitting is how you lose the corridor. Seated height is for a charting shift that lasts. Wheelchair-conversation height is for the resident who came to the opening. Those three numbers do not agree. A single 42-inch barrier with a 30-inch well behind it is a common shop pattern, not a federal dimension. Write the pair you actually built. Measure a nurse’s chair and a wheelchair arm before you freeze a section.

Geometry is still a furniture job. If a person in a wheelchair cannot see a face or sign a paper without putting the paper in the pizza, the section is wrong.

Knee clearance on the public side is a kindness and a bruise. A solid kick that meets a wheelchair footplate is a fight. A void that collects trash is a hygiene fight. A recessed toe with a real bumper is the boring answer that lasts.

## Meds, privacy, and the lock

If meds live here, you are in a different cabinet standard than a wardrobe. I will not fake a USP or a state board of pharmacy citation I have not opened. I will say: **locks, counts, and who stands where** are clinical. A pretty hutch is not a med room.

HIPAA is a sightline too. Screens that face the corridor are a furniture problem. Screens that face the wall are a staffing problem because the nurse cannot see. Recessed screens, privacy filters, a height that works — write them. Do not write “desk.”

The med cart is the mobile station. Household models that “got rid of the bunker” often moved the bunker onto wheels and parked it in the dining room. If you specify a table as the station, specify where the cart lives that is not a meal. Otherwise you have renamed the ward and kept the metal.

Controlled substances and a furniture invoice should not share a sentence without a clinician in the room. I can build a lockable box. I cannot tell you whether that box is legal in your state. I can tell you a glass door with a pretty grille is not a count.

## The table that wanted to replace the bunker

Household models put a table in a kitchen and called it the station. Sometimes it worked: a staff member could sit with a resident and still see. Sometimes it was a table with a laptop and a purse, and the med cart became the station.

If you specify a table as the station, specify **power, lockable storage, a sightline, and a place the cart can live that is not the dining room**. Otherwise you have moved the bunker on wheels.

Culture-change writing — Pioneer Network and the people who measured traditional plants — treated the institutional desk as a leftover to work off. `[CITE NEEDED: a named Pioneer Network station or desk paragraph an editor wants quoted.]` I agree with the direction. I do not agree that a residential dining table with a power strip is a specification. Power without a cable path is a nest. Storage without a lock logic is a purse. A sightline without a seated-to-seated opening is a hope.

A table that works is often two heights and a place to stand. A table that fails is a 30-inch four-top in the middle of a corridor crossing. Residents will use it as a lean. Staff will use it as a dump. Survey will use it as a photograph of clutter.

## Bumpers, corners, and the vinyl base

Wheelchairs hit the station every day. That is not abuse. That is occupancy.

A square corner at hip height is a bruise. A corbel that looks like a house is a hip-catcher with a stain. Radiused corners and a bumper that actually meets the vinyl base are millwork, not decoration. A bumper that stops an inch above the cove is a dirt line and a peel.

The public face can be hardwood if the film is a system and the house will not bathe it in bleach. The public face can be laminate if the edge is real. The public face cannot be a raw veneer and a prayer. The bleach draft sits with edges. Here: the station is the most-wiped wood in the building after the dining table, and the most-hit wood after the doorway.

Undersides and interiors want a wipe too. A station with a finished face and a raw cabinet box is a residential kitchen in a costume. Spills go down. Dust goes back. EVS will tell you if you ask. Ask.

## Sound, light, and the climate around the desk

F584 cares about lighting, sound, and a layout that does not pose a safety risk. The station is a noise source: phones, printer, conversation, a television leftover from the day room. A hard bunker in a hard corridor is a shout. Glass that glares is a lighting failure wearing a privacy story.

Temperature lives in a **71–81 °F** band for facilities first certified after October 1, 1990. Vinyl seats on the staff side and a west-facing glass front are a climate, not a color. A nurse who cannot sit because the well is a greenhouse will stand in the corridor. Then the sightline you built is empty and the bunker is a shed.

I will not specify a lamp as a federal object. I will say a high-gloss top that reflects a fluorescent troffer is an F584 problem a mill can avoid by picking a sheen. Matte shows every wipe streak. Gloss shows every tube. Pick the failure you can live with.

## Channel notes

Medline-era stations were often local millwork, not a punchout, because every plant is a different wreck. That is correct. The punchout tried anyway: modular reception desks. Modular reception desks are for dentists.

A chain that wanted the same corbel in twelve buildings wanted a face, not a work surface. Give them a face if you must. Do not let the face steal the transaction height.

Bradley Brand’s public list includes buildings whose stations I will not describe as case studies. I will say the station was always the piece that told me whether the house was still a ward. If the station owned the corridor like a bridge, the rooms could wear all the cherry they wanted.

I will not invent a millwork SKU or a purchase order. I will say the channel treated the station as architecture when the bid was large and as leftover office furniture when the bid was late. Leftover office furniture is how you get a 29-inch desk, a 17-inch task chair, and a computer that faces the hall.

Repair is the hardwood argument here too. A station is expensive to rip out. A film you can sand and a bumper you can replace are how a 2009 special-buy lives into the next survey cycle. A modular plastic desk with a broken latch is a landfill that still occupies the corridor while you wait for a truck.

## Walkthrough

I sit in the nurse’s chair. I sit in a wheelchair on the public side. Can I see a face? Can I see a screen I should not? Can I find a place to sign a paper without putting it in the pizza? Does the corner have a radius? Does the bumper at the base actually meet the vinyl?

I look for the cable nest. I look for the printer cave. I look for the med cart’s night address. I run a hand under the public edge.

If I cannot see a face, the corbel is a costume.
