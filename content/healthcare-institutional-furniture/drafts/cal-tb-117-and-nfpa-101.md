# CAL TB 117 and NFPA 101

A flammability label is not a fire program.

The Medline-era binder loved “meets CAL 117.” Sometimes the speaker meant the old open-flame 117. Sometimes they meant **TB 117-2013**, the smolder test California moved to, the one that later became the federal upholstered-furniture floor under 16 CFR part 1640. Sometimes they meant TB 133, the full-scale open-flame test that California later walked away from. Sometimes they meant nothing.

If you specify seating for a skilled-nursing dining room and you cannot name **which test, which edition, and which occupancy**, you are specifying a sticker.

## 117-2013 is a smolder test

California Technical Bulletin 117-2013 is *Requirements, Test Procedure and Apparatus for Testing the Smolder Resistance of Materials Used in Upholstered Furniture*. It is a component / mockup smolder test in the ASTM E1353 family, a cousin of the old UFAC cigarette work. It is **not** an open-flame room test.

Furniture manufactured after January 1, 2015, for the California market had to carry the 117-2013 label language the Bureau published. The federal story: the COVID-19 Regulatory Relief and Work From Home Safety Act (Pub. L. 116-260) made TB 117-2013 a flammability standard under the Flammable Fabrics Act, effective as a federal floor on **June 25, 2021**, now 16 CFR part 1640.

That is a big deal for a sofa factory. It is **not** a healthcare occupancy analysis. A SNF chair that meets 117-2013 has met a smolder floor. It has not thereby met the Life Safety Code your fire marshal is holding.

The old California 117 — the pre-2013 bulletin people still say out loud — was a different creature in the popular mouth. Specifiers used “117” for years as if it were one religion. It was not. If you inherit a 2009 special-buy that says “CAL 117,” do not inherit the words without inheriting the report. The report will tell you whether anyone burned a mockup, a component, or a sentence.

I will not recite Bureau label sentences from memory as if they were eternal. Open the Bureau FAQ and the label language that was in force when the chair was made. A chair made for California in 2016 and a chair made for a Michigan SNF in 2008 are not the same paper even if both say 117 in a binder.

## NFPA 101 is a different house

NFPA 101, the Life Safety Code, as adopted by a state or a CMS survey, has its own furniture language. Newly introduced upholstered furniture is expected to resist a cigarette ignition (NFPA 260 Class I or NFPA 261 char-length language, depending on the edition and the sentence). In some occupancies, in buildings **without** a sprinkler system, a heat-release test (ASTM E1537, the TB 133 cousin) still shows up: peak heat release and a ten-minute total that people quote as **80 kW** and **25 MJ**. In a fully sprinklered building the open-flame furniture requirement often drops. I am paraphrasing a moving code. **Open the edition your building is surveyed on.**

CMS uses NFPA 101 for life safety in certified houses. The edition has moved. I will not tattoo 2012 or 2015 or 2021 on this page as if it were eternal. Ask the life-safety guy which edition is on the last survey.

Healthcare occupancy in 101 is not a furniture catalog chapter. It is a building chapter that sometimes points at furniture. A mill that stamps “NFPA 101” on a chair sheet without an edition and an occupancy is stamping a book title. Book titles are not tests.

Sprinklered versus not is the fork people skip. A house that is fully sprinklered may live under a different furniture sentence than a house that is not. “May” is doing work. I will not invent a waiver. I will not tell you to skip a report because the ceiling is wet. The AHJ and the adopted edition get that sentence. Furniture people who treat sprinklers as a religion will ship a foam that the next surveyor still wants a paper on.

## 133 is a ghost that still talks

TB 133 was the California full-scale open-flame furniture test. Specifiers still say “133” the way they say “homelike.” California repealed that regime for its own public occupancies and pointed back at 117-2013. The **test method** lives on in ASTM E1537 and in 101’s unsprinklered sentences.

If a vendor says “meets 133,” ask for the report, the edition, and whether they mean E1537. If they say “we don’t need it, we’re 117,” ask whether the building is sprinklered and which 101 edition applies. Those are not the same answer.

I have sat through bids where “133” was a checkbox next to a lounge chair that had never seen a full-scale burn. Checkboxes are cheap. Full-scale reports are not. A vendor who cannot produce a report is not shy. They are selling an adjective.

LegalClarity and industry explainers of the repeal are secondary. Confirm in primary code and in the Bureau’s own record if an editor wants the repeal paragraph to carry a date stamp beyond “California walked away from that regime.” I will not invent the effective date of a repeal I have not opened this week.

## Mattresses are another book

Mattresses have 16 CFR 1632 / 1633 in the residential world and other documents in the healthcare world. Do not put a chair’s 117 label on a mattress. Do not put a mattress label on a cubicle curtain. Textiles are a stack of books.

The curtain draft will sit with NFPA 701 and the track. This draft stays with upholstered furniture, because that is what a mill and a vinyl program get blamed for.

A bedspread, a floral throw, a memory-care doll pile — F584 wants belongings. Fire wants a fuel inventory. Those two sentences share a chair. I will not tell you a throw is fine. I will not tell you to confiscate a belonging because a mill is nervous. I will tell you a throw is not a 117-2013 report and not a 101 sentence. Someone in the building has to own that object as a textile, or admit nobody does.

Pillows that came with the lounge group are furniture foam wearing a gift-shop cover. Ask whether they are in the mockup. Loose pillows are how a component test becomes a fiction on Tuesday afternoon.

## Component tests are not the built chair

I have seen vinyls that met a smolder mockup and chairs that failed a house because the foam was a different foam than the mockup. Component tests are component tests. Ask whether the **built chair** is in the report.

A vinyl mill will give you a swatch report. An upholsterer will give you a different foam than the swatch report. A mill will change a welt or a barrier and keep the same cover name. The name is not the assembly.

Shop practice: I want the report to name the cover, the barrier, the foam, and the date. If any of those four moved, the report is a cousin. Cousins are useful. They are not twins.

Dining foam and day-room foam often share a punchout page and not a density. A banquet chair with thin foam and a sofa with a sinkhole are not one test. Write the object. Do not write “seating.”

## What a specifier should write

Write three lines:

1. 16 CFR 1640 / TB 117-2013 (smolder floor for covered upholstered furniture).
2. NFPA 101, **named edition**, furniture ignition / heat-release sentences as they apply to **this occupancy and this sprinkler status**.
3. Any state or AHJ extra.

Then ask for reports, not adjectives.

Do not write “healthcare fire rated.” That is not a test.

A fourth line if textiles other than the chair cover live on the object: 701 or the mattress book or “belonging, not specified.” Silence is how a quilt becomes a surprise.

## Channel notes

A punchout that says “CAL 117” in 2026 without an edition is a time traveler. A special-buy from 2009 that said “117” might mean the old bulletin. Do not inherit the words without inheriting the report.

Medline-era binders stacked fire words because fire words photograph. The grown-up special-buy asked for 117-2013 or its ancestor, a 101 sentence with an edition, and a report that named the assembly. The late punchout asked for “all applicable fire codes.” All applicable is a lawyer’s nap.

I will not invent a Medline fire SKU. I will say a mill like ours was in the wood and the cover conversation, not in the foam chemistry lab. When we specified a vinyl, we wanted the mockup to match the chair we would ship. When we could not get that match, I wanted the fire sentence off our page or onto the upholsterer’s.

## What I will not do

I will not tell you a floral throw is fine. I will not tell you to skip a report because the building has sprinklers unless the AHJ and the edition say that sentence. I will not turn a label into a sermon.

I will not tattoo an NFPA 101 edition on this page. The edition moves. CMS’s adopted edition has moved. A 2012 sentence and a 2021 sentence are not interchangeable just because both books are red.

Fire is a program. Furniture is a component. Keep them in that order.

## Walkthrough

I read the label. I ask for the report. I ask which 101 edition the last survey used. I ask whether the building is fully sprinklered. I look at the throw on the chair and the foam in the loose pillow.

If the binder says 117 and the report is missing, the binder is a sticker.
