# Wardrobes and the Reachable Rod

*Educational history of healthcare and nursing-home furniture. Not a purchasing specification for a named building, not a survey opinion, and not clinical advice about beds, rails, or seating for a particular resident.*

The rod sits at 66 inches over a wheelchair. That is a typical wardrobe height, not a statute, and it is the whole problem in one number `[VERIFY]`. A seated person looks up at hangers they cannot work. A standing aide uses the rod without thinking. Out-of-season coats migrate upward because someone can reach. The resident’s everyday shirt lives on the back of the visitor chair, or on the bed, or in the one drawer (chapter 20), which is how a closet becomes a survey sentence and a hallway fact that do not agree.

42 CFR §483.90(e) wants, among other things, an individual closet space with clothes racks and shelves *accessible to the resident*. CMS files that under F917; older packets said F461. The regulation does not publish 66 inches. It does not publish a laminate. It does not settle built-in versus freestanding. It says the closet is individual and the racks and shelves can be used by the person whose clothes they are. Out-of-season storage may live elsewhere. That permission is how buildings hide the tall wardrobe’s real job: it holds the roommate’s unused coats and the extra blankets, and the reachable storage is a low shelf someone remembered to specify, or a rod someone lowered after a survey, or neither.

## What the tag asks, and what the carpenter built

Nightingale wards did not give each bed a closet. A locker, a kit bag, a hook. American rest homes used the bedroom closet the house already had. Purpose-built nursing homes after the war put a shallow closet in the room or a wardrobe at the foot because the corridor plan did not leave a walk-in. The furniture book then sold a tall laminate box with a single rod, a high shelf, a lock that failed the same way the nightstand lock failed, and doors that needed a swing the double room did not have. Sliding doors saved the swing and ate the opening. Bifold doors saved both and failed at the hinge.

F917’s “individual” is the word that fights the double. Two people, one built-in with a divider, or two freestanding wardrobes, or one wardrobe and a claim that the closet in the hall is “theirs.” Surveyors can accept a variety of arrangements if each person has space and can reach it. They can ding a beautiful oak wardrobe that is only usable by staff. Homelike (F584) will like the oak. Accessible (F917) will like the low rod. Those tags can disagree on the same object, which is why a Medilodge-era color board that standardized 66-inch rods was a finish decision wearing a rights problem.

ADA reach ranges are a cousin, not a copy-paste. The 2010 Standards talk about forward and side reach on an accessible route, with numbers that designers already know and that this essay will not reprint as if they were an F-tag. A SNF resident room is not automatically an ADA toilet-room diagram. Some residents stand. Some sit. Some will not use a hanger even if it is at 48 inches. The regulation’s word is still *accessible to the resident* — that resident — not accessible to a theoretical wheelchair user in a new public building. Furniture history can say the tall rod is a bad default for a seated person. It cannot write the resident’s assessment.

Shelves matter as much as rods. Shoes, a folded sweater, a box of cards, the extra briefs that will not fit in the nightstand. A high shelf over a 66-inch rod is a staff shelf. A low shelf behind doors that open onto a wheelchair’s footplates is a shelf nobody can get to. Open cubbies look homelike and look messy; they also let a roommate’s hand in. Doors with locks return us to the nightstand myth. The reachable, individual, reasonably private volume is the actual spec. The tall box is what the book photographed.

## Freestanding versus built-in, and the stolen hanger

Freestanding wardrobes are furniture. They freight in (chapter 42), they match the nightstand for a year, they tip if they are tall and unloaded and not secured — a stability fact, not a how-to. They can be moved when a room is painted. They can be replaced in gray when the oak program dies. They also eat floor that the expanded bed (chapter 13) and the visitor sleeper (chapter 22) both want. A Medilodge-class package that included a wardrobe per bed was selling two tall boxes in a double that was already short of turning radius. One of them ended up in the hall “until we see.” It stayed.

Built-ins are architecture. A carpenter’s closet with a rod, a shelf, a bifold, a light that burned out in 2004. They do not match the later gray bed. They do have a chance of a lower rod if someone specified it. They are hard to change after a survey without a construction punch (chapter 41). Older F461 letters and later F917 guidance both allow out-of-season clothes to be stored outside the room. That sentence is how a building keeps a tall built-in full of winter coats and still claims the low rod is the “resident” rod. Sometimes that is honest. Sometimes the low rod holds two hangers and a broken belt.

Hangers are inventory. They walk. Wire hangers from the laundry, plastic hangers from a family’s first weekend, a wooden hanger that looks like home and does not fit the rod’s diameter. Shared rooms make the hanger a small theft and a large feeling. A wardrobe without hangers is a closet that fails F917 in practice even if the rod is at a good height. Purchasing does not like to reorder hangers because they are not furniture. They are the hardware the furniture assumes, like the pulls on chapter 40.

Memory-care rooms (chapter 31) sometimes removed closet doors or used open rods because doors were a cue problem, or they locked closets because clothing was a rummage problem. Those are unit decisions this essay will not prescribe. They show that “reachable” and “usable” and “safe” are not one finish. A household-model Green House closet can look like a house closet and still need a rod a seated person can work. The purpose-built corridor closet can look like a locker and, if the rod is low and the person’s, satisfy the tag better.

Lighting inside a built-in is a forgotten accessibility fact. A dark closet at 66 inches is a closet a seated person cannot see into even if they could reach. A burned-out puck light from 2004 is how most Medilodge-era built-ins actually look. Freestanding wardrobes rarely had lights. The aide’s flashlight, or the hall light, did the work. F917 does not mention a lamp. It mentions racks and shelves the person can use. Seeing is part of using. The package photograph was always lit.

Hanger diameter and rod diameter are a hardware mismatch the book never lists. A thick wooden “homelike” hanger will not slide on a thin house-brand rod. A thin wire hanger slides and then dumps the shirt. Plastic hangers from a family’s first weekend are the ones that stay, until they walk. Purchasing that will not reorder hangers is purchasing that has decided the wardrobe is architecture, not a kit. The kit includes the hook the clothes hang on.

## Michigan sameness, and the rod that never came down

Corporate spec likes one wardrobe drawing. Fifteen MediLodge-era buildings, a Washington Township desk, a color board: oak, a certain width, a certain height, a lock option nobody keyed. The drawing was a motel wardrobe. Motel rods are for standing guests. SNF residents are often not standing guests. The mismatch survived because families hang the extra coats on the high rod and the room looks “full” on tour, which F584 can read as personalization. The everyday shirt still never makes it onto a hanger the person can reach. Surveyors who open the door and see a working low rod with the person’s clothes are looking at the rare room. Surveyors who see a stuffed high rod and an empty chair with a sweater are looking at the common one.

Wheelchair reach is not only height. It is approach. A wardrobe in the corner behind the hi-lo, with the overbed table parked in front of it, is a closet that exists on the plan. A wardrobe whose doors need 90 degrees in a room that has a geri chair “for a minute” (chapter 25) is a closet that opens on Tuesdays. Sliding doors help the swing and collect dirt in the track. EVS hates the track. Infection control after 2020 hated the track more. The cheap answer was to leave the doors open. The open wardrobe is then a homelike mess or a dignity problem, depending on who is walking in.

House-brand wardrobes arrived as part of the room package (chapter 23) and as replacements after a flood or a door that came off. The replacement’s rod was still high. Lowering a rod is a maintenance job: new holes, a shelf moved, a finish that does not match. Some buildings did it after a citation. Some put a second rod in, low, and left the high one for staff. The second rod is the furniture version of the regulation’s sentence. It is also a photograph this series would rather print than another oak rendering.

Width of the box is the other reach problem. A wardrobe so deep that a seated person cannot see the back wall is a wardrobe whose far hanger is staff-only. Shallow closets, the postwar built-in kind, are ugly and more usable. Deep armoires, the culture-change kind, are handsome and can hide a season of clothes behind a season of clothes. F917’s “accessible” does not grade beauty. It grades whether the person whose name is on the door can get a shirt. The Medilodge-era color board graded beauty. The shirt stayed on the chair.

Culture change asked for armoires that looked like grandmother’s. Some of those had rods at grandmother’s height. The wish and the tag parted. A beautiful armoire a person cannot use is a lobby object in a bedroom. Nemschoff-class and Kwalu-class casegoods (chapter 29’s cousin in the room) sometimes offered adjustable rods. The Medline-era book sometimes offered a “ADA wardrobe” as a separate SKU, which is a catalog noun, not a finding. Buildings bought the tall one because it was on the package line and because the photograph looked like more storage. More storage at 66 inches is staff storage.

## The hanger that stayed

The residual is not the tag number. It is the sweater on the chair. As long as the everyday piece lives on the visitor chair, the wardrobe is a seasonal warehouse and F917 is a file. Lower the rod, clear the approach, give the person hangers, and the chair can go back to being a chair (chapter 22). Leave the rod where the motel left it, and the next essay’s sleeper will have a coat on it at 6 a.m. as well as a family member. The closet was supposed to prevent that. The height did not.

## Notes

- 42 CFR §483.90(e): individual closet space; clothes racks and shelves accessible to the resident. CMS Appendix PP F917; older F461 language in pre-renumbering letters.
- Out-of-season storage outside the room is contemplated in survey guidance — a permission, not a command to empty the room closet.
- Rod height ~66 inches: typical tall wardrobe / motel habit `[VERIFY]` against a named shop drawing. Not an F-tag number.
- 2010 ADA Standards reach ranges: cousin language for designers; do not treat as the text of F917.
- Built-in vs freestanding, hanger inventory, second low rod: channel and maintenance practice, composite, no named citation or PO.
- MediLodge Group as a multi-building Michigan buyer type: *Provider*, May 2011; 2013 Prestige sale. Not a closet audit of a named home.
