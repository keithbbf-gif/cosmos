# Used and refurbished

The Facebook listing says “hospital bed, works great, rails included.” The rails are one. The mattress is a camping pad. The nightstand in the next photo is a residential three-drawer with a cup ring.

Used institutional furniture is a **history problem**. The object may be honest. The system is usually a mutt.

I have refinished honest wood that came out of a condemned wing. I have refused soup boxes. I have watched a used bed go into a house because capital was a rumor.

## Why this market exists

Houses dump packages when a wing changes color, when a flood finishes a floor, when a chain sells a building, when a vinyl program ends and someone wants the stain to match a new green. The dump is how a good nightstand got to a small house that could not buy new. That is not a sin. The sin is the small house buying the dump’s **bed** without the dump’s mattress.

The Medline-era channel was good at sameness and therefore good at surplus. Twelve buildings on one vinyl produce a pile when the thirteenth wants a different teal. Auctioneers, refurb shops, Facebook groups, a warehouse with a spray gun — those are the faces. I will not invent a shop name. I will say the educational fact: **the label tells you a route, not a factory, and the listing tells you even less.**

Capital is a rumor in a lot of small houses. Used is how they stay open. I am not going to moralize about that. I am going to teach you which objects can carry a missing history and which objects become a gap.

FDA’s 2006 guidance is about hospital bed **systems**: frame, rails, mattress. It discusses replacement components and legacy beds. It includes nursing homes and private homes in the settings. From January 1, 1985, to January 1, 2006, the Agency’s files held about 691 entrapment reports; 413 people died. A used bed is not a bargain if it is how you shop-build Zone 3.

## Beds are the dangerous bargain

A used hospital bed is a device with an unknown maintenance file, an unknown mattress match, and an unknown rail. The 2006 guidance is about systems. A system you assemble from three listings is a system you invented.

If you are a family taking a bed home, you are in FDA’s home-care sentence. If you are a facility putting a used bed on a certified floor, you are in a biomeds and a liability conversation I will not fake. I will say: **do not mix a used rail, a used deck, and a new cheap foam** and call it thrift. That is how Zone 3 is shop-built.

Refurbishers exist. Some of them are grown-ups with a zone assessment. Some of them are a spray gun. Ask for the assessment, not the paint. A powdercoat that makes a 1998 frame look like a 2014 frame has not measured the opening inside the rail. Zones 1 through 4 had recommended dimensional limits in that guidance — 120 mm on 1–3, 60 mm on 4, with the angle condition in the table. Zones 5–7 are named without the same FDA limits. I will not recertify a used bed from a photograph. Open the PDF. Measure. Or hire the person who will.

Rails that “come with” the bed may be one rail. They may be a rail from a different deck. They may latch. They may not. The side-rail draft already refused to let you solve a fall with a word like assist. A used rail is that refusal wearing rust.

Motors die on a different calendar than paint. A bed that “works great” in a garage may not work great after a two-hour transfer through a stair. Service manuals vanish. Parts vanish. A fleet of one unique used bed is a fleet that will be a fixed-height bed by winter.

Mattresses on used beds are the mutt’s heart. A camping pad, a residential pillow-top, a healthcare foam from a different width — those are how the canyon is manufactured. The mattress-fit draft is the long version. Here: if the mattress is not the mate, you do not have a bed. You have a frame and a hope.

I will not tell a family they cannot take a bed home. I will tell them the 2006 document already imagined them, and that a missing rail plus a soft foam is not thrift. It is a gap.

## Wood can be honest

A sealed maple bedside with a tired top and a good box is a candidate. A laminate box with a swollen core is a landfill. Smell the drawer. Look at the edge. Look for a shop mark. Look for a distributor sticker so you know the route if you need a pull.

Bradley Brand’s repair argument lives here. Wood that was finished as a system can be a system again. A mill in Warren can recut a top, reseal an interior, hang a new pull. Wood that was a showroom silk cannot. A conversion varnish that never saw bleach will blush when it finally does. The bleach draft is the chemistry. This draft is the accept/reject.

Do not “homelike” a used piece with a floral contact paper. Contact paper is a seam and a peel. Do not fill a swollen particleboard edge with putty and a marker. The swell will continue. Do not call a residential three-drawer with a cup ring a bedside because it is the right width. A residential drawer is an open box. An institutional drawer is a sealed story. Soup is the word I use for the smell. Soup is the reject.

Locks: who holds the key. A used bedside with a lock and no key is a box. A used bedside with a lock only staff can work is the dignity fight we already had. A used wardrobe with a 68-inch rod is a staff closet no matter how pretty the refinish.

Tip and anchor still apply. A tall box that walked out of a condemned wing will walk again. Especially if a drawer becomes a step. Especially in memory care. Physics does not care that you saved money.

Interiors that were never finished will smell like the last decade. Finish the interior or reject the box. I have opened a drawer that was raw in year twelve and still raw. That is not a patina. That is a reservoir.

## Chairs

A used healthcare chair might be a frame with a dead cover — good. It might be a foam sculpture with a hidden reservoir — bad. Zip the cover if you can. If you cannot, assume the reservoir.

Weight stickers lie or vanish. Sit and rock. A racked stretcher is a story. A chair that walks when you stand is a chair that will walk when an 86-year-old stands.

Flame labels may be the old 117. Treat a missing label as a missing label, not as a pass. TB 117-2013 is a smolder test; the federal floor under 16 CFR part 1640 arrived June 25, 2021. A chair from 2009 that says “117” might mean the old bulletin. A chair with no label might mean a spray gun. The fire draft is the long version. On a listing, a missing label is a question mark. Question marks are allowed. Adjectives are not.

Skirts hide the reservoir. I have refused skirts on new chairs. I will refuse them harder on used ones. Welts, buttons, a thousand seams — those are residential costumes that become maps of someone else’s void.

Sit-to-stand does not improve with age. Foam that was a 20-inch firm seat is a hole now. Measure compressed. If you cannot sit and stand in the seller’s warehouse, you are buying a JPEG.

Geri chairs and recliners are devices wearing furniture. A used recliner with a tray that will not come off is a restraint conversation. Do not buy a hold because it was cheap.

## Curtains and textiles

Used cubicle curtains are a flame and an infection story. I would rather buy new panels and used track than the reverse, or new both. I would not buy a used curtain because the color matched.

701 is a vertical textile test. Laundry can undo it. A panel with no date and no label is a rumor. Infection control has been pointing at cubicle curtains as high-touch objects for years. A used panel is a high-touch object with an unknown interval. The curtain draft already said the interval is the specification. A listing does not have an interval.

Track can be honest. Carriers die. Bends lie. Pull the panel through the bend before you buy the track. A dead track with a pretty panel is the flag I already named.

Window drapes from a hotel liquidation are not cubicle panels. Damask is not a method.

## Channel notes

Medline-era houses dumped packages when a wing changed color. The dump is how a good nightstand got to a small house that could not buy new. That is not a sin. The sin is the small house buying the dump’s **bed** without the dump’s mattress.

A refurb shop that only paints metal and does not talk about zones is a paint shop. A refurb shop that will show you a zone assessment, a mattress match, and a rail that latches is a grown-up. Ask which one you are in.

House-brand labels complicate the route. The drawer may say a distributor. The box may say a mill. The complaint used to go to the face. The face may no longer take the call. Photograph the label anyway. A pull that can be matched is a pull that can be matched. A ghost with no label is a guess, and guesses are how you buy a second door that does not hang.

I have taken honest wood back into a shop and sent it out again. I have refused soup. Both decisions are mill knowledge. Neither is a warranty I will invent.

## How I look at a listing

- Is it a system or a piece?
- Is the box soup?
- Is there a label?
- Is the rail present and latched?
- Is the mattress the mate?
- Can I sit and stand?
- Can I smell the drawer?
- Who will assess zones if this is a bed?
- Is the flame label present, and which bulletin?
- Who holds the key?
- Will this object take the wipe the house actually uses?

If the seller cannot answer, you are buying a mystery. Mysteries are expensive in this category.

A photograph of a styled room with a residential lamp and no roommate is a fiction on a new sheet. It is a louder fiction on a used listing. Look at the object in isolation. Look at the back. If they will not show the back, the back is unfinished. A smiling model is not a zone assessment.

## What I will not do

I will not tell a small house they must buy new. I will not tell a family that a used bed is fine because a stranger on the internet said it works great. I will not certify a refurbish from a JPEG. I will say: **wood can be honest, chairs can be frames, beds are systems, curtains are flame and infection.** Buy the history you can see. Refuse the history you cannot.

If the only history is “works great,” you do not have a history.
