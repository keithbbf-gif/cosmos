# Medilodge-Era Purchasing

*Educational history of healthcare and nursing-home furniture. Not a purchasing specification for a named building, not a survey opinion, and not clinical advice about beds, rails, or seating for a particular resident.*

The corporate spec sheet says oak laminate, teal vinyl, electric hi-lo, overbed table, bedside cabinet, wardrobe. The chair that just came off the truck is gray. Not a scandal. A year. The punchout refreshed. The wing did not. On a Medilodge-era corridor the book and the floor disagreed every time a single replacement crossed a color-board revision. That disagreement is the purchasing problem. Not a named purchase order. Not a DON’s invented memo. A type: a multi-building Michigan operator buying rooms the way a chain buys rooms, with corporate taste in one office and fifteen inventories on the ground.

MediLodge Group — also styled Medilodge in running copy after the first mention — sat in Washington Township, Michigan. Around 2011, *Provider* magazine’s “On The Road” piece on Michigan long-term care put Gail Clarkson as CEO, about fifteen skilled-nursing facilities and one assisted living community, something like 2,500 beds and 3,000 employees. In 2013, Crain’s Detroit Business reported a sale of fifteen homes to Prestige Healthcare of Louisville. Those are the dated nouns. They are here because they name the *kind* of buyer the Medline-era book was written for: enough buildings to need a spec, enough local exceptions to ruin it, enough freight to make the dock a second purchasing office.

Do not merge this operator with Ciena Healthcare. Ciena is a separate Southfield company, founded by Mohammad Qazi in 1998. The same *Provider* piece could mention both as Michigan employers without making them one firm. This essay is not an ownership exposé. Prestige on the lawn after 2013 is a sign, not a shop (chapter 44). The furniture habit — corporate chip, building leftover, distributor in the middle — outlived the letterhead.

## Five seats at one chair

Who buys a replacement armchair in a Medilodge-class building is not a single noun. The administrator hears survey and family. The DON hears falls, restraints, and whether the chair will be used as a geri-chair cousin in the hall. Maintenance hears casters, a missing pull, a welt that will be a work order forever. Corporate purchasing hears the spec, the GPO, the punchout, the freight claim. Therapy hears seat height and arms (F558, chapter 5, chapter 26). Five seats. One object. The object that arrives is a compromise that none of the five would have drawn.

Making, from this buyer’s side, is not making. It is issuing a color board and a bed class and calling that a standard. Honey oak TFL. Teal or mauve vinyl in the 2000s leftover, then whatever the book is printing. A Carroll- or Invacare-class electric, or a house-brand MDS-prefix frame when the number shrinks. The standard exists so that fifteen buildings do not invent fifteen rooms. It also exists so that a surveyor walking more than one building in a portfolio sees a decision. Homelike, as chapter 5 argued, can be faked with repetition. Chains like repetition. Homes do not.

Specifying is where the five seats write on the same sheet and cancel each other. Corporate writes finish and price. Therapy writes arms. Maintenance writes “same caster as last time,” which the new SKU will not have. The DON writes “no tray” on a recliner. The administrator writes “looks residential.” The Medline book can satisfy any three of those on a good day. The fourth and fifth become the mismatched chair in the hall, or the chair that sits in a carton because no one will take the freight claim (chapter 42).

Buying is a weekly motion, not a renovation. Renovations happen — a wing, a dining room after a bad letter, a lobby before a sale — and they are capital (chapter 41). The Medilodge-era texture is the other motion: one nightstand, one overbed table, two dining chairs, a bed for a room whose last frame died. Punchout. House brand versus name brand on facing pages. Drop-ship for the bed, DC stock for the chair. The corporate spec is a PDF. The building’s inventory is a closet of teal. The clerk keys the current SKU because the old one is gone. Gray arrives. Someone puts it at the table next to teal. The surveyor, later, may call the mix institutional. The purchasing office called it Tuesday.

Wearing is how the portfolio becomes a timeline. A 2005 oak box beside a 2011 gray overbed beside a recliner that predates the color board. Chapter 44 will walk that corridor as an afterlife. Here the point is that multi-building buying *produces* the afterlife on purpose. Standardization is a hope at the spec. Attrition is what the buildings do. Prestige’s 2013 purchase of a block of homes bought that attrition along with the licenses. A new owner can issue a new chip. The nightstands do not read.

## Corporate color, local floor

The color board is an object worth taking seriously. Chips in a binder, or a laminate fan deck, or a PDF with hex values no one on the floor can use. Oak. Cherry. A “neutral” vinyl that is still a color. The board is how Washington Township-class corporate taste traveled to a building an hour north without anyone driving. It is also how a 1998 teal day room became a problem the board would not name. You cannot punch out teal if teal has left the mill. You can punch out the neighbor and live with the argument.

Local exceptions are not rebellion. A therapy director who refuses the low-seat lounge package is doing F558. A maintenance supervisor who refuses a thermofoil nightstand because the last six peeled is doing chapter 37 before the series gets there. An administrator who keeps a donated wooden rocker in a private room is doing the personal-item half of F584 and creating a wipeability fight with EVS. Corporate can forbid the rocker. Corporate cannot sit in the survey. The Medilodge-era pattern is a written spec plus a quiet pile of exceptions that become the building’s real furniture list.

Ciena’s 1998 founding is in this essay only as a fence. Southfield is not Washington Township. Qazi is not Clarkson. A reader who has worked Michigan long-term care knows both names and may have walked both portfolios. The furniture channel did not care. Medline’s punchout would sell the same oak to either. That is the channel rule from chapter 7 applied to a map: the book is statewide; the operators are not interchangeable. Collapsing them is sloppy history. It is also how a writer starts inventing a single “Michigan chain” that never existed.

Assisted living, the one AL in the 2011 *Provider* count, is a different furniture brief that shared a corporate office. Less medical deck, more residential bedroom, still a wipeable dining chair if the building was honest. The SNF rooms paid for the hi-lo habit. The AL rooms paid for the story that the company also did home. Purchasing staff who bought for both learned to keep two mental catalogs and one login. The login always won when the week got short.

## Freight, thresholds, and the hall

A chain the size of fifteen SNFs generates freight like a small retailer. Beds on pallets. Chairs in pairs. Casegoods with corner damage that is concealed until the carton is opened in a resident room because there was no receiving cage. The dent has three owners (chapter 42). The Medilodge-era purchasing office is the place that has to decide whether to refuse, photograph, or put the dinged wardrobe in a storage alcove “until we hear.” Hearing can take longer than a census hold. The object in the alcove is a purchasing decision that looks like clutter.

Capital versus supply thresholds — $500, $1,000, $5,000 as typical facility habits, `[VERIFY]` any named operator — decided which of the five seats had to sign. A visitor chair might be supply. A bed might be capital. A package of ten rooms was always capital and always late. The same silhouette on two invoices is chapter 41. The Medilodge-class fact is that corporate liked supply because it was fast, and buildings liked capital because it came with a project manager, and the chair in the hall did not know which it had been.

After 2013 the letterhead on some lawns said Prestige. The punchout did not automatically change. House-brand prefixes already in the rooms stayed in the rooms. A new owner can renegotiate a GPO and still inherit a closet of teal vinyl and a spec PDF that someone forgot to take off the shared drive. This essay stops at the sale as a dated fact, not as a tour of the next decade. The purchasing type remains: multi-building, Michigan, book in the middle, floor as a museum of last year’s chip.

Making a standard for fifteen buildings also meant deciding what *not* to standardize. Wet-room seating (chapter 32) stayed a maintenance bin in a lot of plants: rusted shower chairs replaced one at a time, never on the color board. Therapy-gym mats and stacking chairs (chapter 33) were a therapy budget, not a room package. Courtyard resin (chapter 34) was seasonal and forgotten in a January shed. The corporate sheet looked like a room. The operator was buying a campus. Specifying only the room is how a chain gets a matched headboard and a rusted commode in the same photograph if anyone opens the bathroom door.

Buying at ~2,500 beds is a volume that changes the representative’s week. A Medline-era rep who already walked the buildings for gloves could add a furniture walk without a new badge. That convenience is the channel’s pitch and the buyer’s trap. The same person who knows the GPO glove tier may not know that last year’s pendant will not plug into this year’s house-brand actuator. Corporate purchasing liked the one-throat-to-choke. Maintenance liked a name-brand parts list. The five seats again. Volume does not settle them. It only makes the mismatch repeat.

Wearing at portfolio scale is a spreadsheet someone meant to start. Serial numbers on beds. Finish codes on casegoods. A photo of the dining chair so the next punchout matches. Medilodge-class offices that kept that spreadsheet could replace teal with teal until the mill died. Offices that did not kept chapter 8’s gray chair. *Provider*’s 2011 employee count — about 3,000 — is a reminder that the people who knew the wing’s real inventory were not always the people who keyed the SKU. Knowledge lived in maintenance and in the DON’s eye. The login lived in an office that might be in Washington Township. Distance is a furniture finish. It looks like the wrong chip.

## Residual mismatch

The gray chair next to the teal chair is the whole method in one glance. Corporate specified a room. The building needed a seat. The book sold what it had. Clarkson’s 2011 *Provider* numbers — fifteen SNFs, one AL, 2,500 beds — are the scale at which that glance repeats until it is a style. Prestige’s 2013 purchase is the date the style changed owners. Ciena, 1998, Southfield, stays on the other side of the road.

Nightingale counted beds. Batesville stained wood. OBRA counted rights. Medline counted SKUs. A Medilodge-class office counted buildings and then tried to make the SKUs behave as if the buildings were one building. They were not. The mismatched replacement is not a failure of the spec. It is proof the spec was doing chain work in a world of wings. The next essay opens the binder to the chip itself: mauve, teal, honey oak, the later gray, and the cream wall that made all of them look like a year.
