# Channel economics, GPO, freight, install

A nightstand has four prices, and only one of them is wood.

There is the mill’s shop rate. There is the distributor’s program price. There is the GPO tier the business office thinks is a law. There is the **landed, installed, wiped, punched** number that is the only number a building should use and the number a punchout will not print.

I will not invent a multiplier. I will invent even less a Medline margin. I will walk the stack so a reader can see why a “cheap” package got expensive on Vienna Road.

## GPO is a memory, not a discount fairy

A group purchasing organization is a way a 121-bed house borrows a chain’s price file. The tier is a political object: membership, compliance, a vendor’s willingness to live with the fee.

If a mill sells around the GPO, the business office may not be allowed to hear it. If a mill sells through the GPO, the mill may not be allowed to hear the end price. The Medline-era silence between those two phones is how a drawing froze wrong.

Educational use: when someone says “we have to use Medline,” they sometimes mean a GPO contract, sometimes a habit, sometimes a rep they like. Ask which. Habit can be broken. A contract has a term.

## Freight is a geography

A wardrobe is not a parcel. It is LTL: a pallet, a liftgate argument, a residential-delivery lie, a limited-access fee if the dock is a story, a inside-delivery that was never on the BOL.

Michigan winter and a trailer with no heat is a finish check. Texas heat and a trailer with no air is a vinyl stick. Arkansas humidity and a rush crate is a swell.

Shop knowledge: **the BOL is part of the specification**. Who unloads, whether a liftgate is on the truck, whether a crew is there, whether the elevator is reserved. A punchout that says “freight included” without those sentences has included a fight.

Damage at the corner is not a moral failure. It is a carton and a dock. Write the carton. Write the inspection window. A house that signs a clean BOL in the rain has bought the crush.

## Install is clinical

I have said this in other drafts because it is the economics. A crew that works around lunch, oxygen, and a resident who is not leaving is not a Saturday handyman. After-hours is a multiplier. Protection of vinyl is a material. Haul-away of wet mattresses is a waste story. Union or not is a local politics.

If install is “by others,” name the others. If there are no others, the extended price is a fiction.

A mill that includes install is selling a completed room. A mill that drop-ships is selling a pile. Both can be honest. Only one is a package.

## Attic stock is cheap

A spare pull, a spare vinyl bolt, a spare bedside, a spare curtain panel — these are the cheapest insurance in the stack. A house that buys exact count will pay rush freight on a single door and then hate the mill.

Write attic stock as a line, not as a leftover hope.

## Capital vs. operating

A nightstand can be a capital object or an operating replacement. The punchout is built for operating. The new-plant kit is capital. When a house uses the operating tool to buy the capital job, they get operating behavior: no drawing, no leftover measurement, no install plan.

When a house uses a capital designer to buy a replacement bedside, they get a six-week delay and a pretty sketch. Match the tool to the job.

## What I will not do

I will not tell you Medline is expensive or cheap. I will not tell you to break a contract. I will not give you a secret mill-direct pitch. I will tell you to **add the lines** until the number is landed and installed. If the vendor will not add the lines, they are selling a JPEG.

The Medilodge-era lesson is not a price. It is a stack. Wood is the small part. Memory, truck, and Thursday’s two-inch cut are the rest.
