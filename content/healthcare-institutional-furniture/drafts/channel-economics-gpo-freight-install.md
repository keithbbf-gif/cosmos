# Channel economics, GPO, freight, install

A nightstand has four prices, and only one of them is wood.

There is the mill’s shop rate. There is the distributor’s program price. There is the GPO tier the business office thinks is a law. There is the **landed, installed, wiped, punched** number that is the only number a building should use and the number a punchout will not print.

I will not invent a multiplier. I will invent even less a Medline margin. I will walk the stack so a reader can see why a “cheap” package got expensive on Vienna Road.

## The stack is older than the punchout

Hospital purchasing learned to borrow volume before nursing homes had a catalog page. Group purchasing organizations grew up as a way for a house that is not a chain to buy like a chain: membership, a contract file, a fee someone pays, a vendor who agrees to live with the file. I am not writing a GPO history paper. I am naming the memory — account number, tier, punchout, Tuesday rep, truck that already carried gloves. The [Medline as a channel](medline-as-a-channel.md) draft is where that memory lives on a dock; this draft is where it becomes a price stack.

The Medilodge-era lesson is not a price. It is a stack. Wood is the small part. Memory, truck, and Thursday’s two-inch cut are the rest. Medilodge of Montrose is a 121-bed house on Vienna Road. I will not invent what the stack cost there. I will say what any 121-bed Midwest SNF of that era was buying: a route, not a JPEG.

## GPO is a memory, not a discount fairy

A group purchasing organization is a way a 121-bed house borrows a chain’s price file. The tier is a political object: membership, compliance, a vendor’s willingness to live with the fee.

If a mill sells around the GPO, the business office may not be allowed to hear it. If a mill sells through the GPO, the mill may not be allowed to hear the end price. The Medline-era silence between those two phones is how a drawing froze wrong.

Educational use: when someone says “we have to use Medline,” they sometimes mean a GPO contract, sometimes a habit, sometimes a rep they like. Ask which. Habit can be broken. A contract has a term.

I will not tell you to break a contract. I will not give you a secret mill-direct pitch. I will tell you that **a mill-direct quote and a punchout price are different animals.** One is wood and finish. The other is wood, finish, memory, truck, and someone to call when the drawer sticks. If you compare them as if they were the same number, you will pick the smaller one and then discover install, liftgate, after-hours, and a crushed crown.

Compliance is a word purchasing uses to mean the file. A house that is “off contract” may still be allowed to buy a special-buy drawing. A house that is “on contract” may still be allowed to buy a mill drawing if the face opens the door. The politics are local. The statistic I will own is shop knowledge: **a punchout screenshot forwarded as a spec is not a drawing.** It has no rod height, no freight sentence, and no liftgate plan.

National accounts and corporate bids are cousins of the GPO. A chain that wants one vinyl in twelve buildings can get it. A chain that wants one vinyl and twelve leftovers cannot get the leftovers from the file. The file does not walk the room.

## Freight is a geography

A wardrobe is not a parcel. It is LTL: a pallet, a liftgate argument, a residential-delivery lie, a limited-access fee if the dock is a story, an inside-delivery that was never on the BOL.

Michigan winter and a trailer with no heat is a finish check. Texas heat and a trailer with no air is a vinyl stick. Arkansas humidity and a rush crate is a swell. I have opened a crate in January and found a finish that had been a different finish in November. I have opened a crate in August and found vinyl that had kissed itself into a permanent shine. Those are not moral failures. They are geography.

Shop knowledge: **the BOL is part of the specification**. Who unloads, whether a liftgate is on the truck, whether a crew is there, whether the elevator is reserved, whether the dock is a dock or a hillside with a door. A punchout that says “freight included” without those sentences has included a fight.

Damage at the corner is not a moral failure. It is a carton and a dock. Write the carton. Write the inspection window. A house that signs a clean BOL in the rain has bought the crush. A house that photographs the crush on the dock still has a fight, but it has a fight with evidence.

Residential delivery is a phrase trucking uses for a house. A SNF is not a house. It is a limited-access building with a med cart in the corridor and a rule about when a pallet can sit. If the carrier thinks they are delivering a sofa to a driveway, they will leave the pallet at the wrong door. Name the building type on the BOL. Name the hours. Name whether a liftgate is required because there is no dock.

Inside delivery and install are not the same sentence. Inside delivery is a pallet in a corridor. Install is a room that still has a person in it. A quote that collapses them has collapsed a clinical job into a trucking job.

Stair carries, elevator reservations, union halls, a smoke-detector that will not let you lean a wardrobe on a wall — those are local. I will not invent a fee. I will say they exist, and that a capital plan that does not name them will steal from attic stock.

## Install is clinical

I have said this in other drafts because it is the economics. A crew that works around lunch, oxygen, and a resident who is not leaving is not a Saturday handyman. After-hours is a multiplier. Protection of vinyl is a material. Haul-away of wet mattresses is a waste story. Union or not is a local politics.

If install is “by others,” name the others. If there are no others, the extended price is a fiction.

A mill that includes install is selling a completed room. A mill that drop-ships is selling a pile. Both can be honest. Only one is a package.

The punch list is the specification that actually met the resident. On Tuesday they will ask you to cut two inches off a wardrobe because the sprinkler clearance was a rumor. They will ask you to change a lock because the daughter is the thief in someone’s head. They will ask you to add a shelf because the closet tag just became real. A price that cannot take a punch list is a product, not a room.

After-hours exists because the room is occupied. Occupied is the default. A capital plan that assumes empty rooms is a new-plant plan wearing a renovation hat. New-plant is the only time the clocks start together. The replacement-cycle draft will sit with the four ways houses actually buy. Here the point is: **install in a live wing is a clinical shift**, and you should pay it like one.

Protection of floors, corners, and the one clean wall the photographer will use is not decoration. It is how you avoid a second invoice from EVS. Haul-away of the old stuff is a dignity conversation if a resident is still attached to it, and a waste conversation if it is wet. I will not invent a dumpster price. I will say the old nightstand does not vanish because the new one arrived.

## Attic stock is cheap

A spare pull, a spare vinyl bolt, a spare bedside, a spare curtain panel — these are the cheapest insurance in the stack. A house that buys exact count will pay rush freight on a single door and then hate the mill.

Write attic stock as a line, not as a leftover hope. Pulls die. Vinyl programs change names every two years. A crushed crown on an elevator is not a rare event. A spare bedside that matches year three is how a double stays kin.

The channel was good at memory for consumables and, by extension, for the furniture that failed like a consumable. It was bad at telling a mill what the bleach was, and it was bad at stocking a pull for a special-buy that had already ended. If you freeze a drawing for a chain, freeze the attic-stock count in the same buy.

I will not tell you how many extras. I will tell you that zero is a decision.

## Capital vs. operating

A nightstand can be a capital object or an operating replacement. The punchout is built for operating. The new-plant kit is capital. When a house uses the operating tool to buy the capital job, they get operating behavior: no drawing, no leftover measurement, no install plan.

When a house uses a capital designer to buy a replacement bedside, they get a six-week delay and a pretty sketch. Match the tool to the job.

42 CFR 483.90 does not set a price. It sets a room that the price must still satisfy. A cheap pile that fails F917 is not a savings. It is a tag with a dock date. A pretty capital package that cannot be installed around lunch is not a savings. It is a rendering in a crate.

The room-package draft already said the extended price is a story about objects and the real cost is a story about a live building. This draft is the stack under that sentence. Freight, install, attic stock, after-hours, haul-away, the two-inch fund — those lines are how a JPEG becomes a room. If the vendor will not add the lines, they are selling a JPEG.

## What I will not do

I will not tell you Medline is expensive or cheap. I will not tell you to break a contract. I will not give you a secret mill-direct pitch. I will tell you to **add the lines** until the number is landed and installed.

I will not invent a GPO fee, a liftgate charge, or a mill margin. Those numbers exist. They are not mine to publish, and they are not yours to learn from a magazine essay that made them up.

The Medilodge-era lesson is not a price. It is a stack. Wood is the small part. Memory, truck, and Thursday’s two-inch cut are the rest.

## Walkthrough of a quote

I look for the shop rate hiding inside a program price. I look for freight language that names a dock or admits it does not. I look for install by name, or “by others” with a name. I look for attic stock as a line. I look for after-hours as a possibility, not a surprise. I look for haul-away. I look for a punch-list sentence.

If those lines are missing, I do not have a price. I have a JPEG with a number on it.
