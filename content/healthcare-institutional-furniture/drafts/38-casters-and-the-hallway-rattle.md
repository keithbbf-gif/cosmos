# Casters and the Hallway Rattle

*Educational history of healthcare and nursing-home furniture. Not a purchasing specification for a named building, not a survey opinion, and not clinical advice about beds, rails, or seating for a particular resident.*

The caster looks locked. The pedal is down. The bed still walks — a half-inch on each shift of a resident’s weight, enough that the nightstand has a new bruise and the wall bumper has a black comma. Total-lock was the word on the sheet: wheel and swivel both supposed to die when the pedal goes. What arrived is a pedal that clicks and a wheel that has hair and tape in the tread so the lock pad cannot find rubber. It is 2 a.m. The hallway already has a language for this. The overbed table ticks. The med cart’s stem bearings announce the pass. A dining chair, dragged instead of carried, chatters its 3-inch twins. Mobility is a caster spec. The building hears it as a rattle.

Beds, tables, chairs, and carts do not share a caster. They share a floor. Vinyl composition tile, later sheet goods, a transition strip at every bathroom, a threshold that was never in the furniture drawing. Three-inch wheels fall into the grout of an older tile. Five-inch wheels roll the crack and still transmit the bearing. Soft tread quiets some of that and picks up hair. Hard nylon lasts and clicks. A Medline-era punchout could sell all of those options under one photograph of a bed. The wing got whatever was on the frame when the freight door opened (chapter 42).

## What the pedal is supposed to do

Hospital and SNF beds learned to roll because wards and rooms had to be washed, because a resident had to move to a shower, because a fall-program low-low still had to come out from the wall (chapter 12). Casters made the bed a vehicle. Brakes made it a piece of furniture again. IEC 60601-2-52, the medical-bed standard that succeeded the older powered-bed document 60601-2-38, treats mobility and braking as device language `[VERIFY the clause a given year cited]`. It is a manufacturer and test-lab standard, not a nursing protocol. The 10 March 2006 FDA entrapment guidance (chapter 14) maps openings in a bed system. It assumes the bed can be placed. Placement is casters plus a person plus a lock that holds.

Total-lock: one pedal, or a linked bar, that stops both rolling and swiveling. Directional lock: the swivel dies so the bed tracks down a hall like a shopping cart with a good moment; the wheel still rolls. Some frames offer both, on opposite corners, and the aide who learned which corner is which is doing furniture literacy, not clinical magic. Barrel or plate casters bolt under a wide bed leg. Stem casters — grip-ring or threaded — press into a chair or an overbed-table column. The names matter because replacement parts do not interchange. Maintenance with a coffee can of stems cannot fix a barrel that has lost its pad.

Floor lock is the other idea: a center foot or a pair of plungers that take the weight off the wheels. Some SNF beds advertised it as a stability story. Some chairs copied a version. A floor lock on a dirty tile is a slipper on a wet floor. The spec sheet’s photograph is always clean. The 2006 zone drawing does not include the black comma of rubber the lock leaves when it finally bites.

Capacity tags (chapter 43) and caster ratings are cousins that purchasing sometimes treats as twins. A 450-pound bed class `[VERIFY model]` with four casters does not mean each wheel was tested as a quarter of a resident plus a mattress plus a trapeze (chapter 18). Dynamic load — rolling over a threshold — is harsher than a static number. Bariatric frames (chapter 13) asked for reinforced casters and then met a 36-inch door. The caster that survived the door still has to lock on a floor that slopes to a drain.

## Three inches, five inches, hair, tape

Dining chairs and many visitor armchairs roll, when they roll, on 2-inch or 3-inch stems. The point is to scoot to a table (chapter 27) or to park a sleeper (chapter 22). The rattle is high-pitched. Soft thermoplastic rubber quiets it until the tread picks up a hair rat and becomes a drum. Hard composition wheels last through a bleach year (chapter 10, chapter 36) and announce every tile. Hoods — the little plastic fenders — trap the hair they were meant to shed. Someone pops a hood off. The stem looks naked. The chair still moves.

Overbed tables (chapter 19) are caster essays on their own. An H-base or a U-base lives in the same hair the mop leaves along the baseboard. The table that will not stay up is a gas-spring story. The table that will not stay put is a caster story. Two locks on a table are common and commonly ignored. A table that creeps during a meal is how a cup meets a welt. Pneumatic columns and cheap stems arrive on the same house-brand SKU because the punchout sold a height range, not a bearing.

Five-inch casters belong to beds and to many carts. They clear more debris. They still wind a telemetry wire, a discarded sock, a piece of cubicle-curtain tape. Night aides know the sound of a bed that needs a scissors session. That session is not in the fall program. It is not in IEC 60601-2-52. It is maintenance with a pocket knife, or nursing with bandage scissors, and a pile of black string on the soiled-utility sill. Soft treads on a 5-inch wheel can be kind to a newly waxed hall and cruel to a lock pad: the pad wants a firm place to press. Hard treads on a 5-inch wheel are loud at 2 a.m. and honest in January when grit from a Michigan entry (chapter 34’s weather, indoors) has been tracked the length of the corridor.

Med carts and WOWs (chapter 35) live between those sizes. Dual-wheel stems on a cart are supposed to spread load and swivel easier. They also double the hair collection. A cart with one locked caster and three free ones is a vehicle that wants to rotate around a pin. That is the turning-radius problem from the other side. The bed cannot come around because the cart is parked; the cart cannot leave because one stem is a rat’s nest. Furniture that moves is furniture that stops.

## Quiet as a specification that was never written

SNF nights are a soundscape the Life Safety Code does not score. NFPA 101 cares whether the corridor is a corridor and whether the door latches. It does not care whether a directional-lock caster chirps. Homelike language (F584) can ding a stack of vinyl chairs and has no decibel line for a table. Staff write the quiet spec themselves: wrap a stem in tape, swap a hard wheel for a soft one from the extra recliner, put a folded washcloth under a nightstand foot so the resident’s family thinks the building is still. None of that is a protocol. It is what a wing does when the book sold mobility and the census bought sleep.

Beds with roller bumpers — the horizontal wheels that are supposed to kiss a wall instead of gouging paint — add another rattle and another thing to wrap with hair. Headboards that want to look like furniture (chapter 17) still travel on steel. The wood-look panel is a crash point when the caster does not swivel and the corner does. A locked caster that is not locked is how a “residential” headboard earns a hospital scar.

Therapy gyms (chapter 33) collect the loudest leftover casters: mat tables, stacking chairs on cheap stems, an overbed table that migrated and never got locks. The gym can tolerate noise. The corridor that the gym chair is rolled down at 6:30 a.m. cannot. Purchasing codes those chairs as furniture and those tables as equipment and never writes “hallway” on either line.

A Medilodge-era multi-building buy could standardize a bed frame and still inherit four caster vendors, because replacements came as parts and because one home’s maintenance man liked a certain stem. Corporate spec sheets name a lock type. They rarely name a durometer. The rattle is the durometer reporting for work.

Shower chairs and commodes (chapter 32) belong in this hallway even when they live in water. Their casters rust, seize, and then hop — a different rattle, a worse one, because a seized stem turns a transfer bench into a sled. Soft treads swell with disinfectant. Hard treads crack. The rusted crossbrace in chapter 32 has a cousin in the rusted caster fork that stains the tile a permanent comma. Wet-room wheels are still a stem-or-plate decision. They are still hair magnets, with a lint that is not hair. Purchasing that specified a 350-pound shower chair `[VERIFY model]` and ignored the wheel was specifying half an object, the same way a 750-pound bed with a 300-pound dining chair is a half purchase (chapter 30).

Geri chairs and medical recliners (chapter 25) add a tray and a long wheelbase. Four casters, sometimes five if a fifth was meant to steer. Hallway speech parks them “for a minute,” which is how a directional lock that was never understood becomes a recliner drifting toward a fire door. The rattle is slower. The risk is not noise. The furniture fact remains a lock that does not hold and a tread that has polished itself on the same twenty feet of corridor.

## The unlocked lock, again

Return to the pedal that is down. Total-lock is a mechanism: a pad or a pin, a linkage, a spring. Hair is a mechanism too. So is a bent linkage from the time the bed was forced over a threshold with the pedal already down. So is a missing pedal cover that lets a foot click something that is not the lock. The catalog photograph shows a clean pedal and four identical wheels. The wing shows a bed with two replacements, one hood missing, one stem from a different color family, and a lock that holds on the right foot and not the left. The resident’s nightstand creeps. The aide, at 2 a.m., kicks the pedal again. Furniture history is that kick.

House-brand and name-brand frames fail the same way when the floor is the same. Claims desks argue about a dent in a headboard more readily than about a caster that walked in month eight (chapter 42). Wear is excluded. Hair is not a factory defect. The chair that sat in the hall because no one would take a freight claim has a cousin: the bed that stays on the wing because the lock “mostly works.” Mostly is how a rattle becomes a culture.

The residual fact is mechanical and small. Mobility is not a vibe. It is a wheel diameter, a tread, a stem or a plate, a lock that is total or directional or a rumor, and a floor that was poured without asking the furniture. Beds, tables, chairs, carts — four objects, four caster classes, one hallway. ## Notes

- IEC 60601-2-52 (medical beds) and the older IEC 60601-2-38 (powered hospital beds) are manufacturer/test-lab standards. Braking language is not a transfer lesson.
- FDA, *Hospital Bed System Dimensional and Assessment Guidance to Reduce Entrapment* (10 March 2006): guidance, not statute; casters are implied in placement, not drawn as zones.
- Typical 3-inch chair stems and 5-inch bed/cart wheels are channel ranges, not a universal code. Model-specific diameters `[VERIFY]`.
- BIFMA X5.1 / X5.4: seating performance cousins; no 2 a.m. corridor test.
- NFPA 101: corridor and door language; not a caster-noise standard.
- Composite hallway speech (“the aide,” “maintenance”) — no named living persons, no invented work-order numbers.
