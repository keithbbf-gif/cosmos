“Geri chair” is a nickname that does too much work.

Sometimes it means a high-back recliner on casters with a tray. Sometimes it means a tilt-in-space seating system. Sometimes it means the vinyl thing in the hallway that has become a person’s address. Sometimes it means a family La-Z-Boy that staff hate and the resident loves.

I will not police the nickname. I will police the **job**. If the object is a place a person lives for hours, it is a clinical seating program wearing furniture language. If it is a place a person sits after dinner, it is a chair. If it is a place a person is parked because the building is short a staff member, it is a restraint conversation that this series will not launder.

## History: a ward chair that learned a tray

The object predates the word the punchout loved. Mid-century wards and postwar nursing homes needed a sit that could recline a person who was not going back to bed, and a surface that could hold a meal when the dining room was a tray line. Chrome, vinyl, a high back, casters, a tray that flipped up — that kit is older than OBRA. It is hospital leftover seating that stayed because it wiped and because it moved.

OBRA 1987 and the later dignity language made the hallway address harder to defend. F584 wants a layout that maximizes independence and does not pose a safety risk. A corridor is an egress. A recliner in an egress is a furniture object doing architecture’s job badly. Culture change wanted people in living rooms and at tables. The geri chair census did not fall just because the bedspread got a print. It fell when the day-room chair could stand a person, the dining table could take a wheelchair, and the staff ratio could walk someone to a room. Furniture can do the first two. It cannot do the third.

Medline-era catalogs mixed “medical recliners,” “clinical recliners,” “geri chairs,” and “lift chairs” in a way that trained buyers to think they were one object. A lift chair is a motor that stands a person. A clinical recliner is often an infusion cousin. A geri chair is often a long-term sit. They share vinyl and a back. They do not share a job.

I have been asked to make a hallway “look nicer” around a row of matching recliners. I cannot. Maple does not turn an egress into a living room. Pioneer Network writing about traditional plants is secondary here, but it matches the mill’s walk: the traditional nursing home is not traditional in any domestic sense. A hallway of trays is the proof.

## Shop geometry: tilt, recline, tray, leftover

A recliner that opens is a different machine than a chair that sits.

Tilt-in-space keeps the hip angle and changes the gravity. Recline opens the hip and invites a slide. A tray can be a table, a restraint, a crumb trap, and a place to clip a mitt. Casters can make the object a transport, which is how you buy a tip and a pinched foot.

I am not going to specify a tilt angle. Therapists and seating clinics exist. What I will say as a furniture person: **a slick vinyl plus a recline plus a tired foam is a slide to the floor**. The catch, the belt, the tray are how houses try to fix a cover and an angle they already bought.

If you are on the punchout, “geri recliner” is not a spec. Write capacity, tilt vs. recline, tray yes/no, caster lock, cleanout, moisture barrier, and who owns the seating assessment.

Shop observations I will own. A tray in the up position all afternoon is not a table setting. It is a wall in front of a torso. Whether that wall is a restraint is a clinical and regulatory question I will not launder with a finish. Open the current SOM before you use a tag number in a plan of correction. The geometry is prior: a tray that cannot come down without two hands is a tray that will stay up.

Casters want locks that someone actually uses. A recliner that rolls when a person leans to reach a cup is a fall. A recliner that rolls down a corridor to dining is a device conversation. Wheelchairs have a seating culture, a cushion culture, a footrest culture. Geri chairs on casters often have none of that and still get pushed to the dining room.

Width is leftover. CMS’s **80** square feet in a double and **100** in a single do not include a recliner that opened. An open recliner is a bed that did not stay in the bed envelope. In a double, the roommate’s aisle dies. In a single, the visitor dies or the toilet door dies. Draw the open footprint, not the catalog side view.

Seat height still matters when the chair is closed. A geri chair that cannot sit-to-stand is how a person becomes a resident of the chair. The resident-chair draft’s 19- to 21-inch compressed neighborhood is not a geri-chair law. It is a reminder that a hole you cannot leave is a different object than a chair. Foam that collapses into a bucket is a hole.

Cleanout and moisture barrier are the same fight as the room chair, worse, because the sit is longer. A welt museum under a tray is a smell that outlasts a shift. Skirts do not belong here either.

Weight rating: write it. A plus-sign recliner is the bariatric draft’s cousin. A wide recliner that will not go through the door is a hallway address with a nicer back.

## The hallway address

The scar I will not decorate: a person in a recliner in a corridor, tray up, television down the hall, because the room is a double and the roommate has visitors.

Dignity tags have opinions about people who live in hallways. I am not the surveyor. I am the mill who was asked to stain the handrail so the recliner looked intended. Intended is a parking dimension.

If your package includes recliners, write **where they park**. If the answer is the corridor, you do not have a package. You have a staffing plan.

Egress is not a furniture tag, but a furniture object can fail it. A row of recliners is a wall. A tipped recliner is a barricade. I will not quote a life-safety clause I have not opened this week. I will say the fire marshal and the fall program will get there before F917 does.

Temperature belongs because vinyl and a long sit in an **71 to 81 °F** room — facilities first certified after October 1, 1990 — is a moisture and a slide story. West corridors at 80 degrees are how a slick cover becomes a sled.

## The family recliner

Personal belongings are in F584 on purpose. The floral recliner from the living room is a belonging. It is also a weight, a fabric that will not take bleach, a motor cord, a fire story, and a tip story.

I have seen houses write a policy that looks like a ban and then keep the chair because the daughter is louder than the policy. I have seen houses take the chair because a fire marshal was louder than the daughter. Both are the homelike tag doing its fight.

If the chair stays, someone has to own flame, egress, and whether the motor cord is a trip. If the chair goes, someone has to own the grief. Furniture people who pretend this is a swatch comparison are lying.

A lift chair from a consumer store is a motor in a room that already lost outlets to the bed. The cord is a trip. The mechanism is not a mill object. The leftover still has to take the open footprint.

## Transport vs. lounge

Wheels make a recliner a wheelchair’s cousin. If you are going to transport people in a recliner, you are in a device and a safety conversation I will not fake. If you are not, take the casters off or lock them so the object stops being a hallway vehicle.

The dining draft will sit with whether a recliner can pull to a table. Usually it cannot. Usually the person eats at the tray. The tray is then the dining program. Be honest.

A day-room chair that fails sit-to-stand is how the geri-chair census goes up. That is a package problem, not a vinyl problem. Mills like ours were rarely in the recliner mechanism. We were in the wood chair that was supposed to keep people out of the vinyl one. When the wood chair was a 17-inch lounge, the hallway filled.

FDA’s bed-entrapment file — **691** reports, **413** deaths, January 1, 1985, to January 1, 2006 — is a different house. Do not mix it into a recliner unless the object is being used as a bed with rails and a mattress story. Seating devices have their own house. Do not mix without a clinician. I mention the bed file only so a specifier does not treat a tray-and-recline sit as if it had been blessed by a zone diagram. It has not.

## Channel notes

Medline-era catalogs mixed the nicknames on facing pages. Buyers ordered “geri chairs” by count, the way they ordered overbeds. Count is not a seating assessment. A special-buy that froze a tray style for a chain froze a restraint conversation if the tray stayed up. Care plans do not freeze.

Attic stock the vinyl. A long-sit cover dies faster than a room-chair cover. A dead cover on a frame that still works is a recover. A foam sculpture with a reservoir is a landfill.

Freight: a recliner is an LTL object that arrives with a bent caster horn and a tray that will not latch. The latch is the spec. Install: where it parks. If the punch list discovers the corridor, the punch list is late.

I will not invent a Medline SKU or a Medilodge PO for a recliner count. The public project list names buildings, not chair tallies. The hallway I am talking about is a type, not a street number.

## What I will not call kindness, and what I look for

A tray in the up position all afternoon is not a table setting. A recliner tilted so far that the person cannot see a face is not rest. A hallway of matching recliners is not a living room.

Kindness would be a chair a person can leave, a room they can be in, a table they can get to, and a staff ratio that is not a furniture problem. I can build the chair. I cannot staff the floor.

On a walkthrough I look for the open footprint, the caster lock, the tray latch, the slide mark on the vinyl, the weight sticker if any, and the parking story. I look at whether the day-room chair can stand a person. I look at noon: who is eating at a tray. I look at the corridor: who lives there.

If the object is a person’s address, stop talking about stain.
