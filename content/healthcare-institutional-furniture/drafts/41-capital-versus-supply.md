# Capital versus Supply

*Educational history of healthcare and nursing-home furniture. Not a purchasing specification for a named building, not a survey opinion, and not clinical advice about beds, rails, or seating for a particular resident.*

Two invoices, same silhouette. An electric hi-lo with a wood-look headboard, a house-brand cousin of the Carroll- and Joerns-class frames that already sit on the wing (chapter 11). One invoice is a construction punch: a line on a renovation application, a depreciation schedule, a delivery that arrived with the new vinyl and the cubicle track. The other is a punchout release: a supply account, a distributor drop-ship, a bed that replaced the one whose motor died on a Tuesday. The motors do not know which invoice they are. The oak-look panel does not know. The person who signed knew, because a number on a facility habit sheet said so. Typical habits in this era clustered around five hundred, a thousand, or five thousand dollars as the line between a supply buy and a capital project `[VERIFY any named operator; these are not a Medilodge policy and not a Prestige policy]`. The silhouette did not change at the line. The file did.

This essay is about that file. Medline-era punchout made a bed look like gloves: orderable, house-branded, on the same screen as wipes. Construction made a bed look like a sink: specified, color-matched, delayed until the wing closed a pair of rooms. Replacement and renovation are both how furniture arrives. They are not the same job, and they leave different objects on the same corridor (chapter 44).

## A threshold is a habit, not a statute

Facilities invent lines so that an administrator, a maintenance director, and a corporate office can stop arguing every time a recliner fails. Below the line: the building buys, often from the book, often from a house brand, often without a board packet. Above the line: someone writes a justification, waits for a capital cycle, and may be told to keep the broken recliner until next year. The dollar figures that show up in hallway speech — $500, $1,000, $5,000 — are common American small-operator and chain habits, cousins to the IRS tangible-property conversations of the 2010s, not a published Medilodge rule and not a number to hang on a named home `[VERIFY a specific board policy before quoting one]`. A reader who wants a tax lecture is in the wrong magazine. A reader who wants to know why two identical overbed tables have different owners in the ledger is in the right room.

Five hundred dollars, in the 1990s and 2000s, could be a nightstand, a visitor chair, a pair of shower chairs, an overbed table, or a med-cart drawer repair that somehow became a cart. One thousand could be a recliner, a good HPL table, a WOW if the screen was leftover. Five thousand could be a handful of electric beds, a dining-room run, or a nurses’ station millwork that then got cut back to laminate (chapter 35). Inflation moved the objects under the number. The habit of having a number stayed. When a GPO or a distributor put a bed on a punchout below a building’s line, the bed became supplies even if last year’s identical bed had been capital. That is not fraud. It is a screen.

CMS does not publish the line. F917 wants a bed of proper size and height, a mattress, functional furniture, a closet. It does not care whether the invoice coded 1500 or 6200. Survey is not accounting. Accounting is not the fall program (chapter 12). A low-low bought as a program from a therapy budget can sit next to a low-low bought as a renovation from a construction budget. Both are furniture. Only one of them had a color board.

MediLodge Group of Washington Township, around the 2011 *Provider* portrait — about fifteen SNFs, one assisted living community, Gail Clarkson as CEO, a few thousand beds — is the *type* of buyer who needs a habit. So is any peer chain. The 2013 Crain’s report of a sale of fifteen homes to Prestige Healthcare of Louisville changed letterhead. It did not invent the difference between a punchout chair and a project chair. Do not treat those names as a store. Treat them as the kind of multi-building office that kept two files for one silhouette.

## Punchout as supply, punch as capital

The Medline book, from the 1979 glossy forward into the later punchout screen (chapter 7), trained a generation of buyers to think of furniture as a SKU. MDS-class prefixes on later house-brand beds and tables made the objects rhyme with gauze. Drop-ship meant the factory sent a carton to a Michigan dock and the distributor never touched the dent (chapter 42). Supply culture is fast: a motor fails, a release is keyed, a bed is promised. What supply culture skips is the room as a system. Mattress height, wall clearance, the outlet behind the nightstand (chapter 11) — those are renovation thoughts. They are easy to skip when the silhouette is “the same bed.”

Construction punch is slow. A wing closes two rooms, or a floor gets new sheet vinyl, or a memory-care household is declared (chapter 31). The furniture is a package (chapter 23): bed, mattress, overbed, nightstand, wardrobe, visitor chair. Color-matched laminate. A millwork station if the number holds. The package is where the chair gets cut when the capital shrinks. The bed almost never gets cut, because the room cannot survey without it. The visitor chair becomes a leftover from the day room. Ten years later the room looks like a renovation that ran out of money, because it was.

Therapy, activities, and dietary each keep smaller supply habits. A mat table (chapter 33) can be a therapy capital ask or a used equipment buy. Dining chairs fail one at a time and get replaced from the book in a stain that is almost the teal (chapter 9, chapter 26). Courtyard chairs (chapter 34) are supplies until a set is specified for a smoking-era patio renovation, at which point they become a line that winter will destroy anyway. The object does not change categories. The signature does.

House brand versus name brand tracks the two invoices more than it tracks quality. A Joerns-class or Invacare/Carroll-class frame bought on a construction punch can be the same factory family as a house-brand frame bought on a Tuesday, or it can be a different travel and a different caster (chapter 38). The ledger cannot tell. The aide who knows which bed walks when the pedal is down can tell. Capital paperwork likes a name. Supply paperwork likes a prefix. The wing likes a lock that holds.

## Replacement is not renovation, even when the SKU matches

Replacement is a hole: this bed, this chair, this table, this pull (chapter 40). The hole is filled with what the book still has. Colors discontinue. A 2005 honey oak nightstand dies and the 2014 gray woodgrain arrives, same footprint, different decade. The roommate’s box stays honey. That is how a corridor becomes a timeline without anyone declaring a project. Chapter 44 will sit in that corridor. This chapter owns the reason: replacement is allowed to mismatch because it is coded as maintenance of census, not as a design act.

Renovation is a promise of sameness. It often fails the promise — the chair is cut, the thermofoil is the cheaper cousin of the HPL top (chapter 37), the cubicle curtain is a remaindered fabric — but it fails as a set. Surveyors walking homelike (F584) after a renovation are looking at a decision. Surveyors walking a replacement chair are looking at a stain. Both can ding. Only one had a board packet.

Fall-program beds (chapter 12) and bariatric frames (chapter 13) expose the habit painfully. A low-low may be demanded on a Tuesday by a care plan and refused by a capital calendar. The building then rents, or borrows from the next wing, or puts mats around a high deck and writes a note. None of that is a protocol for the reader to copy. It is what a threshold does when it meets a person. A 750-pound frame that will not turn a 36-inch door is a construction problem even if the invoice was supplies. The door is capital. The frame, depending on the habit, might not be. The resident is in the room either way.

Warranties follow the invoice more than the silhouette (chapter 42). A construction package may carry a one-year furniture rider in a contractor’s binder. A punchout bed may carry a motor year and a frame year on a distributor PDF. When both beds fail a caster in month fourteen, one claim goes to a general contractor who has already left and one goes to a desk that asks for photos of a dent that is not the problem. Wear is excluded. Habit is why the paperwork does not match.

## What the two files cannot see

Neither file sees the swollen corner, the split welt, the faded capacity sticker, except as a later replacement trigger. Neither file sees HIPAA sightlines at the station (chapter 35) unless a renovation drew glass. Neither file sees the fire hangtag (chapter 39) unless a consultant put a bulletin on a lounge spec. Accounting is a poor ethnographer of furniture. It is a precise ethnographer of signatures.

A reader walking a Michigan wing can often guess the invoice. Matched laminate and a station that rhymes with the nightstand: punch. A gray overbed at an oak box: supply. A recliner whose vinyl is the wrong decade: supply, or a donation, or a therapy leftover. A whole dining room in one poly skin: capital, and someone won an argument. The guess is not a named building’s audit. It is literacy.

Wet-room seating (chapter 32) and courtyard sets (chapter 34) live almost entirely on the supply side of the habit until a renovation writes them into a punch. Then they become capital for a season and supplies again the first time rust or winter takes a frame. Hardware (chapter 40) is the purest supply object — a bag of pulls, a bumper by the foot — except when “upgrade hardware” is a line in a package meant to make old boxes photograph as new. That line is capital theater. The coffee can remains the real budget.

Fire cards and bleach cards (chapters 10, 36, 39) do not have a dollar threshold. They have a sleeve. A lounge that fails 2020 disinfection can still be too expensive, in habit terms, to replace as a set, so the building orders one chair from the book and files both cards beside the old 133 letter. The ledger says supply. The room says afterlife (chapter 44).

The residual tension is the silhouette that can be either. Electric hi-los became cheap enough, in channel terms, to slip under some habits and remain expensive enough, in construction terms, to headline a renovation. That double life is why a Medline-era bed is still on the floor next to a later gray one, why a Medilodge-era oak package still defines a room that a Prestige-era letterhead now occupies as history, and why a purchasing office can tell you the bed is “just a supply” while maintenance tells you it is the only capital that matters because nothing else moves the resident. The next essay opens a crushed carton on a Michigan dock and asks who owns the dent: factory, carrier, or the distributor who never saw the box.

## Notes

- Dollar thresholds ($500 / $1,000 / $5,000) are described as typical facility habits in the 1990s–2010s U.S. SNF office. They are not a named operator’s published policy. Flag any specific attribution `[VERIFY]`.
- Medline is a channel (chapter 7): punchout and house-brand prefixes, not the inventor of the hospital bed and not a capital-policy author.
