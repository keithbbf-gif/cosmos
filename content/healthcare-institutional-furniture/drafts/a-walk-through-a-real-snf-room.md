# A walk through a real SNF room

This is a composite. It is not Medilodge of Montrose’s room 12, and it is not Chenal Heights, and it is not a resident. It is the room I kept walking in the era: a double, a corridor, a package that almost fit.

I will not decorate it. I will not give it a number. I will not put a face in it. If you recognize a house on the public list — 9317 W. Vienna Road, or Searcy, or Pearland — that is geography leaking through a type. The type is the subject.

## The corridor first

You do not teleport into a nightstand. You come down a corridor that still wants to be a ward. Handrail. Bumper. A med cart the width of a decision. The nurses’ station is either a bunker or a table pretending not to be a bunker. I look from a wheelchair height, or I squat, because a lot of the people who live here see the world from there. If I cannot see a face at the station, the station still owns the sightline. Ward habits die hard.

The lobby behind me wanted to be a living room. If the lobby chair was a soft 16-inch seat with a skirt, the house is talking to families. If it was a firm healthcare vinyl with a cleanout, the house has already lost a fight and learned. Neither chair is this room. Both chairs are the sentence the room inherited.

## The door

Thirty-two or thirty-six. The wardrobe in the carton wants thirty-six and a straight shot. The plant gives you thirty-two and a turn. Already the crown is a rumor.

I measure the door before I bless the carton. A mill that trusts a plan dimension for a 1968 plant will learn the dimension on the elevator. The elevator is the other door. A wardrobe on its back may fit. A wardrobe standing may not. That is a dock fact that becomes a room fact.

The bathroom is shared or it is not. The toilet is a later essay. The grab bars are not ours. The nightstand will try to live in the swing. I move it on the drawing before I move it on the floor. A bedside in a door swing is how a legal 80 square feet becomes a bruise.

## The leftover

Two beds on old centers. A track in the ceiling. Eighty square feet each if the plan is kind. The leftover for wood is a strip.

I put the working side on the aisle and the bedside on the wall. Families will hate the photograph. The lift will not. If I put nightstands on both sides because the rendering was balanced, I have already stolen the working side. Shop range, not a standard: a lot of the bedsides that actually fit this leftover lived in the 18-to-22-inch depth class. A 28-inch “homelike” cabinet is a luxury the aisle cannot pay.

The overbed has no parking place in the JPEG. I steal a sliver at the foot and admit the sliver is also a trip. An overbed that cannot nest under the bed is a barricade wearing casters. I have watched staff throw a visitor chair into the toilet when the lift arrived. That throw is a specification. The leftover did not include the lift, so the lift took the chair.

Oxygen tubing, if this room has it, is another leftover user. Furniture feet should not be a snag garden. I look at the floor before I look at the crown.

## The bed

A device from a different page. Low or not. A rail up or a rail gone. A mattress that may not be the mate.

I look for daylight at the rail and at the headboard. I do not perform a test. I write “assess” on the punch list if the daylight is a canyon. Daylight is not 120 mm. Daylight is a reason to get the person whose job is 120 mm. I keep our headboard off the system unless the bed-maker wants it. A cherry panel that matches the nightstand and sits an inch off the frame is a Zone 6 kit with stain on it.

The plug is behind where a wardrobe wants to sit. I move the wardrobe or I move the bed. I do not pinch the cord. A pinched cord is a fire and a function problem. The headwall draft is the long version. Here I only need to see the outlet before I see the wood.

If the bed is low, I look at the nightstand height again. A cabinet drawn for a mid-height deck looks drunk when the deck is a fall program. If the rail is gone “to look like home,” I do not applaud. An incomplete device is not a house.

## The second bed

This is a double. The second bed is not a copy in the way a catalog thinks. It shares a track, a toilet door, a leftover, and a staff member who needs to see two faces from the door. The roommate’s working side may be the first resident’s window wall. I do not solve that with a matching stain.

I look at whether the two bedsides are the same object. Sameness is how you stock a pull. Sameness that ignores the window wall is how one resident owns the only outlet. I would rather write one nightstand SKU and two placements than two pretty twins that fight.

## The curtain

I pull it through the bend. It sticks. The panel is short. The roommate’s face is available at the toilet. The crown we shipped will catch the mesh if I leave the cathedral on. I cut the cathedral. That is Thursday.

A cubicle curtain is the honest object of the American double: a wall you can wash and a wall you can hear through. If the track dies two feet short of the toilet, privacy is a rumor from the bed in. I write the track on the punch list even though the mill did not sell the track. The room package is a fiction. The fiction has to include the textile wall or it is a ward.

## The wardrobe

A rod at a residential height. A seated person cannot hang a shirt. I write a lower rod. I write a divider because this is a double and the clothes were becoming communal. I write an anchor because the box is tall and the floor is slick.

The interior is finished because I have smelled the other kind. An unfinished interior in a wet program is a smell that becomes a tag.

If this box is standing in front of the only outlet or under a sprinkler that wanted two more inches, Thursday gets longer. I measure the ceiling politics before I freeze a crown. Track, sprinkler, TV bracket, smoke detector — the tall object has to sit in that convention, not on a JPEG.

## The chair

Seventeen inches of fashion or twenty inches of function. I sit. I stand. If I need a lunge, the chair is a restraint. I look at the arm cap. I look at the cleanout. I look at the vinyl map.

The visitor sits on the bed or on a folding chair in the closet. F917 imagined better. The leftover did not. I would rather one honest resident chair and a named place for a folding visitor than two lounge objects that steal the aisle.

If therapy has already condemned this seat, I do not argue with a finish. I write the height they asked for, or I write that we are not the chair vendor and the leftover still needs the height.

## The bedside

One drawer, a sealed box, a top with a lip. A lock the DON has not decided. I leave the lock off the default and on the options. The top is not glossy enough to be a glare under the only lamp. F584’s lighting sentence includes glare. A high-gloss top under a hard fluorescent is a glare kit.

A cup ring from the last package is still in the laminate of the condemned pile in the courtyard. That pile is the spec. I walk the pile if the house will let me. The pile knows the wipe. The pile knows the staff hip. The room knows the leftover. You need both.

## The clock

There is a television. There is not a clock. I put a surface where a clock can live that is not the overbed. It is a small sentence in a tag and a large sentence in a night. A television is a program. A clock is how a person knows it is still night. Appendix PP bothered to name the clock. Vendors skip it because they do not sell it. I do not skip it.

Personal belongings to the extent possible will try to live on this top: a picture, a card, a comb. If the top is too small because I stole width for the aisle, I say so. Honesty about a small top is better than a brochure top that never shipped.

## Night

I try to see the room at the hour the resident actually owns. Night light, or none. Call button reachable, or behind a panel. Chair in the aisle because a daughter stayed. Overbed parked as a barricade because no one named a park. The leftover changes after visiting hours. A package that only works at 10 a.m. on a Tuesday install is a package for a photograph.

I do not stay. I am not staff. I write what the night is doing to the objects and I leave.

## The sequence out

Back to the corridor. The station is a bunker with a corbel. I can or cannot see a face from a wheelchair. The day room has a sofa I will not sit in twice. The dining room’s apron will hit a footplate at noon. The overbeds will be busy.

This is one room. The floor is a hundred of them and a staff ratio. Furniture cannot staff the floor. Furniture can stop stealing the leftover.

## Why a composite

Because a named room would be a person. Because a named invoice would be a boast. Because the era was a route, not a showroom.

If you walk a house on the public project list, or a house like them, and you can still see these fights, the series has done its job. If you only see cherry, start again at the door. The door is where the carton meets the plant. Everything after that is a leftover.
