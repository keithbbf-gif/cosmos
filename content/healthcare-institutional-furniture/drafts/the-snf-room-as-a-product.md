# The SNF room as a product

A skilled-nursing resident room is sold like a kitchen.

Not in the showroom sense. In the **package** sense. Bed, mattress, overbed, bedside, wardrobe, chair, sometimes a second chair for the daughter, cubicle curtain if it is a double, a tackboard that will become a family wall. The catalog calls it a “room package” or a “resident-room grouping.” The bid tab has one extended price. The install crew has one punch list. The resident has to live inside a SKU.

I learned this the way a mill learns it: you do not quote a nightstand. You quote a floor.

## What the regulation thinks the room is

42 CFR 483.90(e) is not a furniture catalog, but it is a packing list.

The room must be designed and equipped for adequate nursing care, comfort, and privacy. It must measure at least **80 square feet per resident** in a multiple bedroom and at least **100 square feet** in a single. Each resident gets a separate bed of proper size and height, a clean comfortable mattress, bedding appropriate to the weather, **functional furniture appropriate to the resident’s needs**, and **individual closet space** in the bedroom with clothes racks and shelves the resident can reach.

Appendix PP, when it explains “functional furniture,” is more talkative than the CFR. Furnishings include a place to put clothing away so it stays clean, unwrinkled, and accessible, and protected from casual access by others; a place for personal effects such as pictures and a bedside clock; furniture suitable for the comfort of the resident **and visitors** (a chair is the example). Private closet space means your clothes are not your roommate’s clothes. If you cannot reach the rod, the facility owes you help or another place you can reach.

That is the product, in law. Everything else is sales.

## What the channel thinks the room is

In the Medline / Medilodge era the channel thought the room was a **repeatable kit**.

A 120-bed house does not want 120 conversations about nightstand hardware. It wants a drawing, a finish, a fabric, and a dock date. Corporate or a GPO blesses two or three packages: standard, bariatric, maybe a “premium” for the private-pay wing. The Medline punchout or the special-buy packet is how the DON and the business office see it. The mill sees a cut list.

The kit is a compromise among four budgets that do not like each other.

1. **Capital.** Who is writing the check — owner, lender, HUD, a refinance, a civil-money-penalty plan of correction that suddenly needs chairs.
2. **Operations.** EVS and maintenance will live with the edge banding. Nursing will live with the chair height.
3. **Survey.** Someone in compliance is imagining F584 and the closet tag while you are talking about crown molding.
4. **Marketing.** The website wants a room that looks like a hotel. The resident is 86 and wants her clock where she can see it without her glasses.

When those four are not in the same meeting, you get a beautiful wardrobe with a rod at 68 inches and a chair that photographs well and will not let a person stand.

## The double is the real product

The single room is how brochures think. The double is how America still houses a lot of Medicaid long-stay.

A double is not two singles sharing a toilet. It is a privacy problem with a track in the ceiling. The cubicle curtain is furniture even if the purchasing file files it under textiles. The nightstands have to land so a staff member can work both beds. The chairs have to nest or they become a fall line in the aisle. The wardrobes, if the building did not give you two closets, are the entire dignity program for clothing.

80 square feet per person is the federal floor, not a design. I have stood in doubles that met the number on a plan and still felt like a ward because every object was shoved to the walls to keep a four-foot aisle. Furniture that is “to scale” in a rendering becomes a trip when the overbed is parked.

If you are specifying a package and you only drew the single, you did not specify the product. You specified the photograph.

## The bed is the expensive stranger

In a lot of Medline-era packages the bed did not come from the same shop as the case goods. The bed is a medical device. The nightstand is furniture. The mattress might be a third vendor. The rail might be a fourth, years later, after a fall program changed its mind.

That split is how Zone 3 is born. The 2006 FDA guidance is explicit: a mattress that is not the one the deck was designed for, a rail from a different year, a foam that compresses, and you have a gap the drawing never showed. The room package as a sales object hides that split. The room as a lived object is the split.

I will not pretend a hardwood mill is a bed factory. When Bradley Brand was in a package, we were usually the wood: bedside, wardrobe, sometimes the dining and the day-room seating, sometimes a nurses’ station that was millwork wearing a furniture invoice. The bed brand on the same floor might have been Hill-Rom, Joerns, Invacare, Medline house, or whoever the GPO was pushing that year. `[CITE NEEDED: I will not name a bed SKU I cannot pin to a public invoice.]` The educational point is the **joint**, not the logo. Ask who is responsible for the gap between their objects.

## Install is part of the product

A room package that exists only on a truck is not a room.

Install in a live SNF is not a Saturday kitchen job. You work around oxygen. You work around a resident who is not moving to a hotel for the week. You pad the vinyl. You find out the elevator will not take a wardrobe on its back. You find out the door is 32 inches and your drawing assumed 36. You wipe the piece because infection control will not sign a dirty top. You come back for the punch list and the drawer that stuck because the building is dry in January.

None of that is romance. It is why “drop ship to dock” is not a specification. A Medline-era job that looked cheap on the extended price got expensive when the house had no one to stage 80 cartons and a liftgate that was not on the BOL.

Shop knowledge: if you cannot name the installer, you do not yet have a package. You have a pile.

## What a honest package list looks like

If I were still writing a bid tab for a teaching file, not a sale, it would name:

- Bed (manufacturer, model family, low height, rail type, weight capacity) as a **separate** line from case goods.
- Mattress (width, length, thickness, cover, whether it is the bed-maker’s match).
- Overbed (base type, height range, casters, whether the column is sealed).
- Bedside (width, drawer count, sealed box, lock or not, finish system, glide spec).
- Wardrobe or closet fit-out (rod height, shelf heights, lock, whether it is built-in).
- Resident chair (seat height, depth, arm, vinyl grade, moisture barrier, weight rating).
- Visitor chair if the room is supposed to have one.
- Cubicle curtain and track, if a double, with a flame standard named.
- Lighting the furniture will not fight (the headwall draft).
- Who installs, who wipes, who owns the punch list, who owns the gap between bed and rail.

If a quote is one lump called “rooms,” ask for that list. If they cannot give it, they are selling a costume.

## The resident is not a line item

The regulation keeps saying “appropriate to the resident’s needs.” That phrase is how a package becomes illegal without being ugly.

A bariatric resident in a standard chair is not a style mismatch. A memory-care resident with a lockable drawer she cannot open, and a roommate who can, is not a hardware story. A person who eats in the room because the dining program failed her, at an overbed that will not lock, is not a “resident preference.”

The product is the room **as inhabited**. The later drafts will keep coming back to that. The package is how the channel talks. The inhabitant is how the surveyor and the family talk. You have to be bilingual.
