# OBRA Did Not Specify the Chair

*Educational history of healthcare and nursing-home furniture. Not a purchasing specification for a named building, not a survey opinion, and not clinical advice about beds, rails, or seating for a particular resident.*

The Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1987 is a long statute. Ronald Reagan signed it. The nursing-home pieces — the Nursing Home Reform Act — talk about quality of life, quality of care, rights, and the reduction of restraints. They do not talk about seat height. They do not name a vinyl grade, a wardrobe rod, or an overbed table. The silence is the furniture fact. Congress changed what a resident could demand of a building. It did not publish a bill of materials. Someone still had to buy the chair.

Implementation came in a wave around 1990, not as a single morning when every mauve stacker was rolled out. Survey, conditions of participation, and later the *State Operations Manual* Appendix PP became the places where furniture entered by the side door: environment, accommodation, bed, closet, rails. Those F-tags are chapters 5 and 15 and 21. This chapter stays with the gap. A reform that spoke dignity without speaking oak left the Medline book and the Medilodge-class buyer to invent a room package and then argue, for thirty years, whether the package was what the statute had meant.

## What the statute actually said, and did not

OBRA 1987 is budget law with a nursing-home title inside it. The reform language that furniture people later treat as scripture is about residents as people with rights: to be free from unnecessary restraints, to participate in care, to have an environment that supports quality of life. Those sentences are real. They are also empty of SKUs. A restraint can be a vest, a tied sheet, a geri chair with a tray locked in, or a full rail in the raised click. The statute can forbid a practice without describing the replacement object. The replacement object is where purchasing lives.

Dignity, in the statutory mood, is a way of being treated. Dignity, on a wing, is also a chair a person can stand from, a closet a person can reach, a bed that is not a cage. The law did not do the translation. Surveyors, advocates, and operators spent the 1990s doing it, unevenly. Pioneer Network and Eden language (chapter 6) did another translation: plants, animals, children, household. The purchase order often stayed a hospital PO. That is not hypocrisy as a personality flaw. It is what happens when a statute changes the *why* and leaves the *what* to a distributor catalog that was already on the desk.

Restraints are the loudest furniture-adjacent piece of OBRA and the easiest to misuse in a chair essay. The reform asked facilities to reduce physical restraints. It did not say “buy this recliner.” Facilities that took the trays off the geri chairs, or stopped tying people into lounge seating, still had to seat people. Some bought dining chairs with arms. Some bought the same vinyl recliner and called the tray “for meals only.” Some bought nothing and used wheelchairs as dining furniture. The statute could ding a practice. It could not specify a seat. F700 / §483.25(n) later wrote bed-rail language that still refuses to be a catalog (chapter 15). The pattern is the same: care-plan words, then an object that has to be procured.

Quality of life is even thinner as a specification. It can mean a day room that is not a television tomb. It can mean a private room. It can mean a curtain that closes (chapter 24). It can mean a color that is not jailhouse beige. None of those meanings arrives with a model number. A Medilodge-era corporate spec sheet that listed “homelike casegoods, oak laminate, teal vinyl” was doing work the statute refused to do. Whether that work satisfied a surveyor is chapter 5. Whether it satisfied a resident is not a furniture measurement. The chair was still bought.

## How furniture entered through the manual

CMS did not leave the gap completely empty. Conditions of participation and Appendix PP accumulated sentences about the environment and the room. §483.10(i) / F584: safe, clean, comfortable, homelike. §483.10(e)(3) / F558: reasonable accommodation of needs and preferences, including seating that a person can use. §483.90(e) / F917 (older F461): a bed of proper size and height, a mattress, functional furniture, an individual closet with accessible racks and shelves. Those are still not a catalog. They are closer. They give a surveyor something to point at when the only chair in the room is a broken stacker, or when the rod is at 66 inches over a wheelchair.

The timing matters. OBRA is 1987. The 1990 implementation wave is when facilities felt the new survey. Appendix PP is a living manual; tag numbers move. A 2009 S&C letter still talked F246 and F461. Later revisions talk F558 and F917. A writer who treats “OBRA required oak nightstands” is inventing a PO. A writer who treats “OBRA required a homelike environment” is closer, and still has to say that *homelike* became a survey word, not a finish chip (chapter 5, chapter 9). The manual is where furniture got its hooks. The statute is where the hooks were justified.

Buying after 1990, for a multi-building Michigan operator, meant a new kind of nervousness in the purchasing office. The administrator heard survey. The DON heard restraints and falls. Maintenance heard casters. Therapy heard seat height. Corporate purchasing heard a color board and a GPO price. The chair had to answer all five and still wipe. The Medline book was willing. It had vinyl dining chairs with arms, medical recliners, visitor armchairs, and a “residential” line that was the same frame with a wood-look finish. None of those pages cited Public Law 100-203. They did not need to. They sat on the desk because the law had made the old day-room stacker look legally thin.

What did not happen is a national furniture standard for nursing homes. CAL TB 117 traveled as a hangtag (chapter 39). BIFMA seating tests are cousins, not healthcare law. IEC 60601-2-52 is a bed-device standard, not a dining-chair standard. The chair remained ordinary commercial seating asked to do extraordinary work: sit-to-stand all day, bleach at night, a surveyor in the morning. OBRA created the demand that the chair be *about* something — dignity, accommodation, home — without creating a test the factory could fail. Factories failed other tests. Edges peeled. Welts split. Arms came off. Those are wear. They are not statutory.

Making a “post-OBRA” chair, if a factory used that language at all, meant adding arms to a stacker, thickening a foam, printing *healthcare* on a vinyl card that already claimed bleach. It did not mean a new joint. Specifying meant a corporate sheet that said “arms required in dining” and a building that already had two hundred armless chairs in the closet. The mismatch is chapter 8’s purchasing problem. It starts in 1987 as a legal mood that never quite became a takeoff.

## The chair that still had to be bought

Walk a Medilodge-era dining room that was furnished in the decade after implementation and you will not find a plaque that says *OBRA 1987*. You will find a mix. Some chairs have arms. Some do not. Seat heights wander between a typical 18-inch stacker and a 19- or 20-inch sit-to-stand hope `[VERIFY as ranges, chapter 26]`. Vinyl is mauve or teal or a later burgundy. Tables may or may not take a wheelchair under the apron (chapter 27). The statute did not arrange this room. A buyer did, under a new fear and an old budget.

The geri chair in the corridor is the ugly cousin of the same story. Hallway speech for a medical recliner on casters, it can be a place to sit, a place to sleep, or a restraint look-alike if the tray locks and the care plan is thin (chapter 25). OBRA made the look-alike dangerous as a survey object. It did not invent a replacement that purchasing loved. Facilities kept buying recliners because residents still needed to recline, and because a dining chair does not roll to the nurse station. The reform changed the *excuse*. It did not change the caster.

Capital versus supply (chapter 41) decided which chair arrived how. A renovation after a bad survey might be a capital package: new dining seating, new lounge, a matching laminate. A single replacement after a welt split was a punchout line, often a near-match that did not match (chapter 8). The statute had nothing to say about either path. The channel had everything to say. Medline-era house brands and name brands both sold “healthcare seating” that existed to be wiped and stacked and, after 1987, to be pointed at if a surveyor asked whether the environment supported dignity. Pointing is not the same as specifying. The surveyor still had to look at the actual seat.

Wearing after OBRA is not a different vinyl chemistry. It is a different argument when the chair fails. Before the reform, a broken armchair was a maintenance ticket. After, it could be described as an accommodation failure if it was the only chair a person could stand from. That description is available. It is not automatic. Facilities that documented preferences and still had one decent armchair per room were doing the translation the statute skipped. Facilities that bought a truck of armless stackers because they nested in a closet were doing another translation: cleanability and storage over sit-to-stand. Both could quote quality of life. Only one of them had arms.

## Residual silence

The 1987 Act is easy to load with furniture it does not contain. Writers do it when they want a date for the oak-look nightstand or the end of the restraint tray. The nightstand has other dates: laminate fashion, Medline punchout, a corporate color board in Washington Township. The tray has other dates: a DON’s memo, a fall, a family complaint. OBRA sits behind those dates as a change in what could be *said* about a chair. It does not sit on the packing list.

Reagan’s signature, the 1990 implementation wave, the later F-tags — those are real. The chair in the dining room is also real, and it was specified by people who had a catalog and a budget and a survey window. Nightingale specified air. Batesville specified wood on a deck. Congress specified rights. The Medline book specified a SKU. Between the third and the fourth of those sentences is the entire purchasing history of the American nursing-home chair. The statute’s silence is not a defect in the furniture. It is the reason the furniture had to be argued, building by building, for the rest of this series.

Chapter 5 takes the surveyor’s word *homelike* and stays in the room until a mauve vinyl stacker and an oak-laminate box have both been asked to answer it.

- Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1987 (Nursing Home Reform Act); Reagan signature; nursing-home provisions on quality of life, rights, restraint reduction. No furniture catalog in the statute.
- Implementation wave commonly dated around 1990; facilities felt survey change then, not on the signing day.
- CMS *State Operations Manual*, Appendix PP: F584 / §483.10(i); F558 / §483.10(e)(3); F917 / §483.90(e) (older F461); F700 / §483.25(n). Later hooks, not 1987 SKUs. Tag numbers move; 2009 S&C language used F246 / F461.
