# Therapy-Gym Furniture

*Educational history of healthcare and nursing-home furniture. Not a purchasing specification for a named building, not a survey opinion, and not clinical advice about beds, rails, or seating for a particular resident.*

The mat table has a wooden box step. The table is a wide, padded slab on a steel frame, the height of a low bed or a high bench depending on who last cranked it. The box is plywood, painted the same cream as a 1990s corridor, a cut-out handhold, scuffed on the nose from shoes that were not supposed to wear it down. Parallel bars run to the window. A stacking chair from dining waits at the end of the bars. An overbed table — house-brand, gas spring tired — holds a gait belt, a clipboard, and a cup of coffee that is not a resident’s. This room looks like equipment. It is full of furniture. Purchasing still codes the chairs, the mats, and the borrowed overbed as furniture. Therapy still codes them as tools. The wooden box is the tell: someone built what the book did not sell.

This essay is not a treatment lesson. It will not write a parallel-bar protocol or a transfer count. The problem is the buyer seat. A Medilodge-era gym had a therapist who could specify a mat, a chair, a set of stairs that were really a piece of casegoods, and a corporate purchasing office that heard “furniture” and opened the dining page. The objects that arrived decided what the room could do. Parallel bars are architecture. Everything else is movable, and therefore argument.

## Parallel bars as architecture

Parallel bars are the gym’s built-in. Floor-mounted or bolted, a length of chrome or powder-coat, a height that may adjust and then freeze because the pin rusted. They occupy a rectangle no sofa can use. They are closer to a handrail than to a chair, and they are the reason the room is a gym rather than a day room with a mat. Construction punched them. Capital signed. After that, furniture filled the leftover floor: mat tables, treatment mats, stacking chairs, a high-low treatment table if the budget had a medical-equipment line, a set of training stairs that look like a miniature porch.

The bars make a corridor inside the room. Chairs collect at the ends the way geri chairs collect at a nurses’ station (chapter 25). A person sits before and after. The sitting is furniture even when the walking is therapy. F558’s sentence about arms and seat height belongs here as a cousin: the chair at the end of the bars is a sit-to-stand object. Therapy knows that and asks for a chair with arms. Purchasing sends a stacker because the gym “already has chairs.” The stacker came from dining when dining bought too many, or too few, and the leftover migrated. Migration is the gym’s supply chain.

Medicare-era skilled therapy — the PPS gym as a room type, not a billing guide — gave this space a status the day room never had. It had to exist. It had to look busy. It had a door families were shown. Furniture was part of the show: bolsters rolled the way a retail bed is rolled, a clean mat, a poster of a muscle. The wooden box step was not on the tour if someone remembered to hide it. The box was the working object. It was also furniture in the oldest sense: a made thing that holds a body at a height.

## Mats, chairs, the overbed that moved

Treatment mats are foam in vinyl, the same family of skins as the day-room sofa and the geri chair, with a different pitch. They live on the floor or on a table. They split at the welt the same way (chapter 36). They take bleach arguments the same way (chapter 10). They are furniture when they are on a platform with legs. They are supplies when they are a pad in a closet. The platform — mat table, hi-lo mat, “treatment table” on a medical-equipment page — is a bed cousin that IEC 60601-2-52 does not govern. It is not a hospital bed. It is a table that a person lies on. Purchasing that had just learned bed-system language sometimes over-specified it. Purchasing that had not learned sometimes bought a folding banquet table and a gym mat from a sporting-goods punchout. Both rooms existed. The folding table failed first.

Stacking chairs are the gym’s census. Groups. Families in a teaching session. Staff meetings after hours. The armless banquet stacker of chapter 26 is at home here because the room must empty. Therapy still wants a few chairs with arms for the people who will stand from them after a session. The mix is the spec. The uniform stack is the freight. A Medilodge-class operator buying for fifteen buildings could specify one gym package: bars, two mats, a dozen stackers, a set of stairs, a mirror. The package photographed. The wooden box appeared later, building by building, because the package stairs were the wrong height or the wrong place.

The overbed table is the migrant. Chapter 19 gave it a resident-room life: 15-by-30 or 18-by-32 tops `[VERIFY]`, a travel often advertised near 28–45 inches `[VERIFY]`, a gas spring that fails. In the gym it becomes a work surface: evaluations, water, the therapist’s notes, a resident’s glasses. U-base or H-base, whichever was surplus. It is the wrong height for a wheelchair at a mat and the right height for a standing clinician, or the reverse. It is furniture that therapy did not order and will not give back. Room overbeds disappear into the gym the way pens disappear into pockets. Housekeeping knows. Purchasing, looking at a room inventory, does not.

Stairs and boxes are casegoods. A training staircase with two or three risers and a rail is a small piece of architecture on casters, or a heavy thing that lives against a wall. The wooden box is the folk version: maintenance cut it, painted it, replaced it when the nose crushed. Culture-change gyms that wanted to look less clinical sometimes hid these objects. Clinical gyms left them out because they were the work. Either way they are furniture. They have a height, a load, a finish, a failure. They are closer to the wardrobe (chapter 21) than to a parallel bar: someone decided a dimension with a saw.

## Therapy as a buyer seat

Chapter 8 listed buyer seats in a Medilodge-era building: administrator, DON, maintenance, corporate purchasing, therapy. Therapy is the seat that sees the chair as a tool and still has to ask purchasing for a SKU. That double vision produces the gym’s mixed inventory. A named therapist — not a named person here, a role — writes “armchair, 20-inch seat, firm, with arms” and receives a lounge chair from the day-room page, too soft, too low (chapter 28). Writes “bariatric chair for gym” and receives one, which then never leaves the gym, which is how chapter 30’s dining room stays empty of width. Writes “transfer bench” and receives a shower chair (chapter 32). The mis-orders are not stupidity. They are hallway speech on a requisition, the same speech that turned a medical recliner into a geri chair.

Hospitals had rehabilitation departments with medical-equipment budgets and specified tables. SNFs had a gym that shared a punchout with the dining room. The difference shows in the overbed. A hospital gym does not need to steal a resident’s table. A SNF gym does, or it orders the cheap house-brand table as a consumable and treats it as one. After 2020 the gym emptied and filled like dining. Mats were wiped to a shine that split them. Stackers stood six feet apart and then nested again. The wooden box did not care. It was already the wrong century of object, and it worked.

Mirrors are furniture that pretends to be architecture. A wall mirror with a rail is a specified object; a wheeled mirror is a piece that lives in the turning radius and then in a claim if it tips. This page will not write a therapy cue. It will say that a person sitting in a stacker facing a mirror is sitting in dining-room furniture that migrated, and that the migration is why the gym looks thrown together even when the bars are straight. Bolsters and foam rolls are the soft goods: vinyl skins, zippers that EVS hates, a bleach argument. They sit on the mat table the way pillows sit on a bed, and they fail the same way, at the welt. Purchasing coded them as supplies. Therapy counted them as inventory. Both were right.

Parallel bars, again, are the architecture that furniture cannot replace. Buildings that converted a day room into a gym for a census change bolted bars to a floor that had been vinyl for sofas. Buildings that converted a gym into a memory-care living room (chapter 31) left bolt holes under a club chair. The holes are a record of which buyer seat won. Furniture covered the record until EVS moved the chair.

Dignity in the gym is a curtain or a screen (chapter 24) around a mat, or the absence of one. It is a chair that does not dump a person in front of a mirror. It is a bariatric mat table that exists, or a standard mat that does not, and a session that happens in a room instead. It is not a finish palette. Gyms kept the oak-look out, mostly. They kept chrome and cream and a blue vinyl that hid nothing. Honesty is the gym’s accidental style. The wooden box belongs to that honesty.

Hi-lo treatment tables, when the medical-equipment line paid, introduced a crank or a motor into a room that otherwise ran on surplus. They are cousins of the SNF bed (chapter 3) without being devices under IEC 60601-2-52. The pendant, if there is one, gets taped. The height someone wanted for a mat session stays until the next person. A table that does not reset is the adjustable dining table of chapter 27, only padded. Buildings that could not fund the hi-lo kept the wooden box and a slab that did not move. Both rooms could do the work. Only one looked like a brochure.

## The box under the slab

The residual fact is the homemade step. The book will sell a staircase, a mat table, a stacker, an overbed, a parallel bar that is really a construction item. The floor will still need a height the book missed. Maintenance will still build it. Therapy will still use it until the nose crushes and the plywood goes where broken plywood goes. A new box appears, painted the current cream.

A Michigan SNF gym on a Lower Peninsula corridor — not a named room, a type — still reads as a furniture store that therapy runs: surplus dining chairs, a stolen overbed, two mats in different vinyl years, bars at the window, a mirror, a staircase, a box. The next essay opens the courtyard door and finds the chairs stacked in a January shed, salt on the runners, a locked door between the day room and the weather.
