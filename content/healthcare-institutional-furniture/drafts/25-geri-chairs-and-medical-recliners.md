# Geri Chairs and Medical Recliners

*Educational history of healthcare and nursing-home furniture. Not a purchasing specification for a named building, not a survey opinion, and not clinical advice about beds, rails, or seating for a particular resident.*

The recliner is in the corridor “for a minute.” Vinyl the color of a 1990s waiting room. Four casters, two of them locking if anyone remembers which pedal. A tray table clipped across the arms, a pillow sliding toward the floor, a blanket that is not the bed’s blanket. The aide who parked it is in a room two doors down. The resident in the chair is watching the med cart go by because the day-room television is already claimed. Hallway speech has a name for this object that the catalog never printed: geri chair. The spec sheet said medical recliner, or lounge chair on casters, or Trendelenburg-capable seating. The floor said geri. The word is not a diagnosis. It is furniture slang that stuck because the object stuck — in doorways, at nurses’ stations, at the end of a wing where a person can be seen and still not be in bed.

This essay is not a seating prescription. It will not tell a building which tilt, which tray, or which resident belongs in which frame. The problem is historical and commercial. A padded chair learned to roll, learned to flatten, learned to carry a meal, and then learned to look like a restraint even when the care plan said otherwise. OBRA 1987 reduced restraints as a statute theme (chapter 4). F700 later asked questions about bed rails (chapter 15). The recliner arrived beside those arguments with a different geometry and the same dignity problem. A Medline-era book sold it next to the visitor armchair and the dining stacker. A Medilodge-class operator put one on a wing and then discovered there were six, because a chair that moves accumulates.

## What hallway speech named

“Geri chair” is short for geriatric chair, a phrase that already tells you the industry was talking to itself. Manufacturers preferred medical recliner, clinical recliner, or mobile lounge. Therapy departments said “the Broda-class” or “the Champion-class” the way beds are Carroll-class or Joerns-class: a silhouette, not a current SKU, not a shop link. The object is a wide vinyl seat with a reclining back, often a drop-down or flip-down footrest, often a removable tray, always casters. Some frames go to a full flat. Some advertise Trendelenburg or reverse-Trendelenburg as catalog language — head down, feet up, a hospital verb that migrated onto a chair. Treat those angles as sales geometry. Do not treat them as a protocol. The same sentence on a spec sheet can describe a nap and a position a nurse used in an ICU in 1975.

The chair is a cousin of the day-room lounge (chapter 28) and of the visitor sleeper (chapter 22), and it is not either. The lounge stays in the day room until an activity calendar forces it to move, which it often cannot. The sleeper lives in the resident room and becomes a bed for a family member. The geri chair lives wherever the last person left it. That is the furniture fact. Mobility was sold as a kindness: take the person to the dining room, to the courtyard, to the shower waiting area, without transferring again. Mobility is also how a day-bed appears in a fire lane. A castered recliner with a tray is a small room. It has a table, a back, a footrest, and a person. The corridor did not ask for another room.

Dignity is the word the statute used and the word the chair keeps failing. A person eating under a vinyl tray in a hallway is a picture families remember. A person asleep in Trendelenburg-look geometry outside the nurses’ station is another. The chair did not invent those pictures. Staffing, distance, and a dining room that closes between meals invented them. The chair made them portable. Purchasing, looking at a photograph in the book, saw a resident in a living-room pose. The photograph did not include the corridor handrail or the med-cart turning radius.

## Recline, tray, caster

The catalog pages of the 1990s and 2000s — Medline house-brand MDS-class prefixes among them, and name-brand pages the punchout could also reach — broke the object into options. Manual recline versus gas-spring versus motor. Trendelenburg as a checkbox. Tray that slides, tray that flips, tray that locks. Lateral supports. Drop-arms for a transfer that this essay will not describe. Capacity numbers in the 250–350 lb neighborhood on standard frames, higher on bariatric cousins (chapter 30); `[VERIFY]` any model year. Vinyl grades with a bleach card that chapter 10 will treat as history and chapter 36 as construction. CAL 117 hangtags. A head pillow on a snap.

What the page treated as options the floor treated as failure modes. The tray that locks becomes the tray that looks like a restraint when it is left across the arms after the meal. Furniture cannot settle that word, any more than a half-rail can (chapter 15). A tray is a table. A tray left on is a barrier. The care plan, not the SKU, is supposed to know the difference. Surveyors walking a Medilodge-era wing could see the object before they saw the chart. That is why the chair became a look-alike problem. It photographed as confinement even when it was a place to sit that was not the bed.

Casters are a later chapter’s whole subject (chapter 38). Here they are the reason the recliner is a corridor citizen. Total-lock versus directional lock. Hair and tape in the wheel. The pedal that says locked and is not. A chair that drifts toward the nurses’ station on a sloped 1970s slab. Soft tread that quiets the 2 a.m. roll and then flats out. Hard tread that announces every trip to the day room. The bed has a floor lock. The recliner often has a lever someone can miss with a shoe. The difference matters because the geri chair is used as a bed that is not a bed. A sleeping person on casters is a furniture sentence the 2006 FDA bed-system guidance never wrote. That guidance is about hospital bed systems, not lounge chairs. Do not import zone drawings onto a recliner. Do import the habit: if it holds a body for hours, people will ask what it can trap. The honest answer is that this object was sold as seating and used as a location.

Vinyl is the skin because cloth does not survive the tray, the meal, and EVS. The mauve and teal of chapter 9 sat on these frames first, before they sat on dining stackers. A medical recliner is a large field of upholstery. It shows every split welt (chapter 36). It shows every bleach map after 2020. The chair that survived that year and the chair that did not were sometimes the same silhouette from different decades of foam. Purchasing replaced the wrecked ones from the book and kept the survivors until the casters screamed.

## Day-bed, restraint look-alike, not a protocol

OBRA’s restraint-reduction theme did not name the geri chair. It named rights. Appendix PP later gave surveyors language about accommodation — F558 still wants seating with height, depth, firmness, and arms that help a person stand (chapter 26). A deep recliner with a folded footrest is a poor stand-up chair. That is a furniture observation, not a therapy note. Buildings bought recliners for people who could not sit in a dining stacker and then discovered those people could not leave the recliner without help. The object that was meant to increase the day’s geography could shrink it to a vinyl patch and a tray.

Some frames were specified by therapy. Therapy is a buyer seat the dining-room stacker rarely had (chapter 33). A therapist looking at a person who slid out of a lounge sofa might ask for a tilt-in-space silhouette, a different class again, closer to a wheelchair than to a living-room recliner. Purchasing heard “geri chair” and ordered the book’s medical recliner. The two objects share vinyl and casters and almost nothing else. That mis-order is a Medilodge-era classic: hallway speech on a requisition, a SKU on the dock, a therapist unhappy in the gym. The chair still got used. Unused furniture does not survive a wing that is short of places to park a person during a shower, a room cleaning, or a roommate’s family visit.

Hospitals had their own recliner culture — sleeper chairs for families, infusion recliners, Nemschoff-class waiting-room benches (chapter 29). The SNF borrowed the silhouette and not the capital. A hospital might specify a healthcare-furniture house and a cleanable but expensive cover. A nursing home more often specified the distributor page: a price that could come from supply rather than from a construction punch (chapter 41). The geri chair is what that price bought. It is not a failed hospital chair. It is a successful supply chair that took on jobs the bed, the dining chair, and the day-room sofa did not want.

Dignity, again, is not a finish. A cherry-look headboard on a medical bed (chapter 17) is the same argument in woodgrain. The recliner tried to look like a living-room La-Z-Boy and then accepted a tray that no living room uses. Families recognized the pose and not the hardware. Staff recognized the hardware and not the pose. The gap between those recognitions is where the word “geri” lives. It is slightly unkind and entirely accurate about the market. The market was older bodies, wipeable skins, and a need to move the sitting person as a unit.

## The minute that lasted

The corridor minute is the residual fact. A recliner that is easy to roll will be rolled to where the work is. The work is in rooms and at the station and in the dining room at noon. Between those nodes the chair idles in the hall because there is no alcove that fits a reclined footprint. Fire-rated corridors have rules about storage. A chair with a person in it is not storage. A chair waiting for a person can be. Buildings learned to keep a body in the chair if they wanted the chair to stay legal in that spot. That is a grim piece of furniture logic and it is not a care instruction. It is why the object photographs as it does.

On a Medilodge-era Lower Peninsula wing you can still find the stack: one recliner that belongs to a room, one that belongs to nobody and therefore to everybody, one with a broken tray that maintenance wired up, one whose Trendelenburg lever has been zip-tied because someone was tired of the unexpected tilt. House-brand and name-brand frames side by side, same vinyl era, different caster pedals. The book will still sell a cleaner version of the same idea. The floor will still call it a geri chair. The catalog will still avoid the word.

This series does not tell you how to seat a person. It tells you that hallway speech named a class the purchase order could not. The next essay leaves the castered recliner and sits down in the dining room, where the chair is not allowed to roll and is required, by a survey sentence, to help a person stand.

## Notes

- “Geri chair” is hallway speech for a medical recliner / lounge chair on casters; say so in running copy. Not a clinical term and not a single manufacturer’s model.
- Trendelenburg / reverse-Trendelenburg on recliner spec sheets is catalog geometry. Not a positioning protocol and not an FDA bed-system claim.
- OBRA 1987: restraint reduction as a statutory theme; furniture not enumerated. F700 / §483.25(n) is bed-rail language — do not paste it onto trays as if it were a chair regulation.
- F558 / §483.10(e)(3): seating height, depth, firmness, arms that assist standing — more naturally a dining-chair tag (chapter 26), but it is why a deep recliner can fail as the only chair a person is offered.
- Capacity ranges vary by model year; `[VERIFY]` any number attached to a named frame.
- Medline house-brand and name-brand recliner pages: channel classes, not current SKUs.
