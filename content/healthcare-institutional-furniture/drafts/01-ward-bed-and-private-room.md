# The Ward Bed and the Private Room

*Educational history of healthcare and nursing-home furniture. Not a purchasing specification for a named building, not a survey opinion, and not clinical advice about beds, rails, or seating for a particular resident.*

Florence Nightingale’s 1859 book is not a furniture catalog. It is a set of sentences about air, water, light, and the bed as a place that can make a person worse. *Notes on Nursing* keeps returning to the bed because the bed was the room. In a Nightingale ward the beds stood in rows under high windows. The furniture problem was spacing, not oak grain. A screen might hide a dressing. A locker might hold a kit. The architecture did the rest: a long hall, a stove, a nurse who could see a line of faces.

The American skilled-nursing room that a Medline-era book later furnished is not that hall. It is usually a double, sometimes a private, with a cubicle curtain on a ceiling track, a window that may or may not open, and a pair of electric beds whose headboards are trying to look like furniture. Between those two rooms sits a century of hospital building, a century of converted houses called rest homes, and a late-twentieth-century statute that talked about dignity without naming a nightstand. This essay is about that inheritance. The later chapters will take the bed’s motors, the survey word *homelike*, and the punchout SKU. Here the problem is simpler. Institutional furniture begins as a ward problem and arrives in Michigan as a private-room package that is still two beds and a shared toilet.

## What the ward thought a bed was

Nightingale’s sentences are easy to quote and easy to misuse. She wanted ventilation. She wanted the sick not piled. She wanted the bed placed so that a person was not breathing the stagnant air of a corner. She did not specify a hi-lo travel or a vinyl grade. The bed in her pages is a platform with bedding that can be washed, a place from which a patient can be lifted, a piece that must not become a damp trap. The famous pavilion wards that took her name — Nightingale wards in English and later American hospitals — turned those sentences into a plan: beds perpendicular to the long walls, windows between, a stove or a nurse’s station in the axis. The furniture was repetitive on purpose. A ward is a system. Variety would have been a failure of the system.

Nineteenth-century hospital inventories, when they survive, read like ship lists: iron bedsteads, hair mattresses, commode chairs, a table that could be rolled. White tubular steel later became the American hospital look — enamel that could take carbolic and then bleach, a crank at the foot if the deck was lucky. That steel is the object William Hillenbrand walked away from in 1929 when he put wood and metal on the same frame (chapter 2). The ward did not need the wood. The ward needed a count. How many beds, how many feet between them, how many screens. Privacy was a textile if it existed at all.

The screen is worth naming because it is the ancestor of the cubicle curtain. A folding screen in a ward is furniture. It has hinges, a fabric or a painted panel, a footprint. It can be stolen, broken, or left in the corridor. Nightingale’s world used screens for dressings and for dying. The later American double room uses a ceiling track and a flame-rated cloth that does not quite meet in the middle. Chapter 24 takes the curtain as a subject. The point here is that the private room did not abolish the ward’s privacy problem. It hung it from the ceiling and sold a second bed.

## The house that became a home, and then a facility

American “homes for the aged” and rest homes were often houses. A bedroom already had a bed, a dresser, a chair. The institutional turn was not the invention of the dresser. It was the multiplication of the bedroom until the house needed a nurse’s call, a diet kitchen, and a fire exit. Furniture from the family’s life mixed with hospital iron. That mix never entirely left the industry. A Medilodge-era room in the 2000s still tried to look like a bedroom while specifying a medical deck. The oak-look laminate nightstand is a motel object. The bed is a device. The visitor chair is a waiting-room leftover. The package pretends they were designed together.

Purpose-built nursing homes after the war — the long double-loaded corridors that culture-change writers later hated — copied the hospital more than the house. Semi-private rooms, a nurses’ station with a view of the hall, a day room at the end, a dining room that could take trays. The furniture followed the plan. Beds had to roll. Chairs had to stack or at least wipe. Casegoods had to survive a cart. The “private room” in that building is a marketing sentence. Architecturally it is a box with one or two beds and a toilet that may be shared. The furniture has to serve two people who did not choose each other. That is not a Nightingale ward. It is also not a bedroom. It is a third type, and the catalog pages that later called it a “resident room collection” were selling a fiction that purchasing needed.

Medicare and Medicaid, after 1965, paid for a great deal of that building. They did not publish a furniture list. They published conditions of participation that later surveyors turned into F-tags. The bed had to be of proper size and height. The closet had to be reachable. The environment had to be homelike. Those sentences arrive in chapters 4, 5, and 21. They are downstream of the room type. You cannot understand a Medline punchout suite until you understand that the suite is being asked to furnish a box that was designed like a cheap hospital and named like a house.

## What the SNF room actually holds

Walk a double room that was furnished in the Medline/Medilodge era — not a named wing, a type — and the inventory is stubbornly short. Two electric beds. Two mattresses, sometimes not the ones that came with the frames. Two overbed tables. Two bedside cabinets. One or two wardrobes, or a built-in closet with a rod that a seated person cannot reach. Two visitor chairs if the capital number held; one if it did not. A cubicle curtain. A wall-mounted television that is not furniture and still occupies the only wall a picture might have used. A bathroom with a shower that may hold a bench that rusts.

That list is the series. Each object has a history and a spec sheet and a failure mode. The bed is a medical device that wants to be a four-poster. The overbed table is a tray on casters that fails at the gas spring. The cabinet is a bumper. The wardrobe is a survey item. The chair is how a person gets to the toilet without a lift, or fails to. Nightingale would have recognized the bed and the screen. She would not have recognized the pendant control or the vinyl dining stacker waiting in the hall for a meal that is also a transfer.

The private room — one bed, one toilet, a door that closes — is the reform object of the last thirty years. Operators advertised it. Culture-change programs demanded it. Construction cost fought it. When it exists, the furniture list shrinks by half and the remaining pieces are asked to do more emotional work. The headboard must look residential. The chair must look like a living room. The nightstand must take a lamp that is not a hospital goose-neck. The Medline-era book had pages for that look. It also had pages for the double room that most census still occupied. Purchasing ordered both from the same binder. The difference was a SKU suffix and a curtain that was no longer needed.

## Rows, then pairs, then a package

Hospitals, for their part, spent the same decades moving the other way: Nightingale rows to semi-private to private, then to acuity rooms whose “headwall” is a utilities panel and whose furniture is a recliner for a family member. The SNF did not get that capital. It got the leftover geometry. A Medilodge-era wing in Michigan could be fifteen miles from a hospital that had already rebuilt its medical-surgical floor around single rooms, while the nursing home still opened every door onto two beds and a shared sink. The furniture book served both buildings. It had to. The distributor did not print a separate metaphysics for long-term care. It printed a bed, a table, and a chair, and let the building decide which century they were living in. The later chapters stay inside that decision: what the sheet claimed, what the dock delivered, and what the wing actually used.

The historical motion is easy to caricature: ward to private room, restraint to dignity, steel to oak. The furniture does not obey the caricature. A 2008 Michigan double room can hold a low-low bed bought for a fall program (chapter 12), a crank-era overbed table that someone refused to surplus, and a mauve vinyl chair from a 1990s day room. The ward’s logic — repeatable, wipeable, countable — never left. The private room’s logic — personal, homelike, one person’s things — was laid on top like a bedspread. Surveyors were told to look for the bedspread (F584 still talks about personal items and drapes). Maintenance was told to keep the casters working. Both instructions are true. They describe two different jobs.

Nightingale’s ward made the bed the unit of planning. The American SNF made the *room* the unit of buying. That is why the “resident room package” exists as a sales sentence (chapter 23). A package is a ward idea wearing a bedroom name: a set of objects that can be repeated down a corridor so that the building looks decided. Medilodge-class operators, buying for fifteen buildings, needed that decision. A house that became a home did not. The converted Queen Anne with six residents had furniture the way a family has furniture. The 120-bed purpose-built home had furniture the way a hospital has furniture, with a laminate finish that let everyone say otherwise.

This series stays with the purpose-built room and the channel that stocked it. The Nightingale ward is here because it is the first time a modern English sentence treated the bed as a public-health object. The private room is here because it is the sentence American operators sold to families. Between them is the double room with a curtain, which is where most of the furniture actually lived. The next essay leaves Scutari and London and goes to Batesville, Indiana, in 1929, where a casket-maker’s son decided the white tubular bed was the wrong piece of wood.

## Notes

- Florence Nightingale, *Notes on Nursing: What It Is, and What It Is Not* (London: Harrison, 1859). Beds, ventilation, and crowding are recurring; do not treat the book as a specification.
- Nightingale / pavilion ward plans: standard hospital-history accounts; a named plan plate belongs in the figure pass.
- 42 CFR §483.90(e) and CMS Appendix PP (F917; older F461): bed of proper size and height, mattress, functional furniture, individual closet — later survey language, not 1859.
- OBRA 1987: dignity and quality of life as statutory themes; furniture not enumerated. See chapter 4.
- Medline/Medilodge-era room inventory: composite of channel practice, not a named building’s 2008 PO.
