# OBRA 1987 and homelike

In 1987 Congress told the American nursing home to stop being a warehouse.

The Nursing Home Reform Act — tucked into the Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1987 — is why surveyors still talk about quality of life as if it were a measurable object. It is also why a furniture catalog learned the word **homelike**. The statute did not specify a nightstand. The industry heard a finish.

I care about this year because every Medline-era package I saw was an argument with 1987. Some arguments were honest: a chair you could stand from, a closet you could reach, a bedspread that was not a hospital sheet. Some were a cherry print on a box that still had a ward’s heart.

## What the statute actually did

OBRA 87 did not write a furniture code. It wrote a **resident-centered** nursing-home code and left the Secretary and the survey system to turn that into tags.

The living descendants you can open today are in 42 CFR Part 483. Quality of life. Dignity. A safe, clean, comfortable, **homelike** environment, including the right to use personal belongings to the extent possible. A physical environment with a bed of proper size and height, a mattress, functional furniture, a closet.

If you want the original bill language on your desk, get the public law and the early SOM, not a blog that says “OBRA required wooden furniture.” It did not. `[CITE NEEDED: a quoted sentence from Pub. L. 100-203’s nursing-home sections if an editor wants the statute on the page rather than the CFR descendant.]`

What it did, in practice, was make **warehouse aesthetics a compliance problem**. A metal locker, a stacking chair in the dining room, a bare mattress, a missing clock — those became things a surveyor could write. Vendors learned to sell the remedy.

## Homelike is a legal word with a list

Appendix PP, F584, is the paragraph I make people read out loud.

The intent of “homelike” is an environment as close to that of a private home as possible. A personalized, homelike environment recognizes individuality and autonomy. The guidance then names institutional leftovers a facility should work off, and furniture is on the list: furniture that does not reflect a home-like environment or is uncomfortable; missing window treatments; missing textures; missing bedspreads or personal items.

It also says a facility is not automatically non-compliant because it still has some of those features. It is expected to do what it can within fiscal constraints. That sentence is how a 1972 plant with a 2009 furniture package still passes, and how a new plant with ugly chairs can fail.

Notice what is **not** on the list. A species of wood. A brand. A rail height. Homelike is a **direction**, not a SKU.

## Personal belongings are furniture too

The same tag wants the resident to use personal belongings to the extent possible. That is how a floral recliner from 1994 ends up next to a healthcare vinyl chair from the package.

Staff hate the recliner because it is heavy and the fabric holds odor. Families love it because it is hers. The regulation is on the family’s side until the piece becomes a fire, a tip, or a blocked egress. Then you are in a different conversation — NFPA, the fire marshal, the fall program — and “homelike” is not a trump.

Shop knowledge from install days: the worst fights were not about our nightstand. They were about whether the daughter’s lamp could keep its shade under a sprinkler head and a plastic-bag policy. I will not invent a particular fight. I will say the tag creates the fight on purpose. A room with no personal objects is easier to wipe and easier to fail.

## What 1987 did to a mill

A hardwood shop in Warren did not need Congress to tell it that wood looks more like a house than a steel locker. We already knew that. What 1987 did was create a **buyer** for that knowledge inside a medical distributor.

Before the reform era, a lot of SNF purchasing was hospital leftover plus whatever the local office-supply house had. After the reform era, you could sell a case-good that looked like a bedroom if you could also survive bleach and a 400-pound crash onto a drawer. Medline and its peers needed pages that said “homelike” and “healthcare” in the same paragraph. Mills that could do both got into the channel. Mills that could only do pretty died on the first EVS complaint.

Bradley Brand’s public record puts Medilodge of Montrose and the Arkansas ConvoCare houses in that window. I will not pretend OBRA wrote our shop drawings. I will pretend even less that a cherry crown made a double a home.

## The abuse of the word

By the late 2000s “homelike” was a finish name.

I have seen bid language that said “homelike package” and then specified a 17-inch lounge chair, a wardrobe with no rod, and a bedside that locked from the staff side only. I have seen marketing that used the word to mean “not beige.” I have seen a rail pad in a floral print described as homelike. A pad can be kind. It is not a house.

The test I use is Appendix PP’s own examples, turned into questions.

- Is there a chair the resident can use, and a chair a visitor can use, without stealing the aisle?
- Is there a place for a clock and a picture that is not the overbed?
- Can the resident reach hanging clothes?
- Are the clothes separate from the roommate’s?
- Is the furniture comfortable **as inhabited**, not as photographed?

If the answers are no, the word on the catalog page is decoration.

## What 1987 did not fix

It did not fix the double. It did not fix the 80-square-foot floor. It did not fix Medicaid rates that treat a wardrobe as a luxury. It did not fix a bed that is a medical device sitting next to a dresser that is a piece of furniture, with a gap between them that can kill.

It did not fix fire. A homelike wingchair in an unsprinklered occupancy is a heat-release conversation, not a quilt conversation. The 117 / 101 draft will sit with that. Do not let a floral throw stand in for a test report.

It did not fix staffing. Furniture cannot be the CNA. A beautiful chair that a tired aide cannot move is a beautiful obstacle.

## How to read a “post-OBRA” package

Ask for the year of the plant, the year of the furniture, and the year of the bed. If those three years do not match, you are looking at a palimpsest. The 1987 word may be on the bedspread and the 1965 ward may still be in the centers of the beds.

Ask whether anyone moved a wall after 1987 or only bought objects. Objects are what a channel can ship. Walls are what an owner has to want.

Then sit in the chair and open the closet. The statute, if it is working, will be in your knees and your shoulder, not in the brochure.
