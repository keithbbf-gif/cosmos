# Eden, Green House, and the Room

*Educational history of healthcare and nursing-home furniture. Not a purchasing specification for a named building, not a survey opinion, and not clinical advice about beds, rails, or seating for a particular resident.*

At the end of the corridor there is a living-room grouping. A sofa, two chairs, a table that is not a dining table, a lamp that is not a night-light. The carpet changes color. A silk plant, or a real one if someone is still watering. The grouping is there because someone read that the building should feel like a house. The corridor is still a corridor: double-loaded, nurse-call lights, a med cart parked in the turning radius, a handrail that is not a banister. Culture change asked the room to stop looking like a hospital. The purchase order often stayed a hospital PO. The furniture at the end of the hall is where those two sentences meet and fail to agree.

Bill Thomas named the Eden Alternative in 1991, working in a New York nursing home — Chase Memorial is the house the origin story uses. Plants, animals, children: the three companions against loneliness, helplessness, boredom. That triad is the myth that traveled. It is a good myth. It is also almost silent about the nightstand. Pioneer Network gathered the wider culture-change conversation in the years after: household models, consistent assignment, the end of the double-loaded corridor as an ideal. The Green House Project, early 2000s, put the household into a building type. First houses associated with Tupelo, Mississippi. Small scale, a hearth, bedrooms off a common space, a cook who is not a trayline. What actually changed on a Medilodge-era Michigan wing was sometimes the chair. Sometimes it was only the language.

## What Eden asked, and what the shop received

Eden’s early literature is about life, not laminate. Bring the world in. Stop treating the building as a hospital that happens to keep people. The unpaid bill is furniture. A birdcage does not replace a geri chair. A visiting child does not lower a closet rod. A plant on the nurse station does not give the dining chair arms. Facilities that took Eden seriously still had to buy objects, and the objects available in the Medline book of the 1990s were the same objects the book had been selling to people who had never heard of Thomas: vinyl lounge seating, oak-look casegoods, electric beds with residential headboards, overbed tables.

Making a “culture-change chair,” if a factory used any such phrase, usually meant a slightly more domestic silhouette in the same wipeable cover. A club chair instead of a stacker. A wooden arm. A skirt that EVS would hate. Specifying meant a DON or an administrator who had been to a conference and a purchasing office that had not. The conference slide showed a sofa. The punchout showed a sofa in healthcare vinyl with a CAL 117 hangtag. Both could be called Eden if the press release needed a noun. The resident who could not stand up from the sofa was living in a different document (chapter 28).

Buying for a multi-building Michigan operator made the gap wider. Corporate could endorse culture change as a philosophy and still issue a room package that was a hospital package with a floral drape. Fifteen buildings cannot each invent a household. They can each hang a different print. Medilodge-class sameness — the reason chapter 8 exists — is not an insult to Eden. It is what a chain does with a catalog. The living-room grouping at the end of the corridor is often the entire budget for the myth: one purchased tableau, repeated, while the rooms themselves stay doubles with cubicle curtains.

Wearing is unkind to tableaus. The sofa cushions flatten. The lamp disappears into a room. The silk plant grayed out. The chairs get pulled to the nurse station for a family meeting and do not come back in the same arrangement. Staff need the corridor more than they need the grouping. A cart will not go around a coffee table that was placed for a photograph. The household idea assumes that furniture can be moved by people who live there. The SNF corridor assumes that furniture will stay out of the way of people who work there. Both assumptions can be true at 10 a.m. on a Tuesday. They are not true at med-pass.

## Green House as architecture, then as a purchase

The Green House Project tried to change the building so the furniture would have a different job. Early 2000s. Tupelo as the named first cluster in the movement’s own telling. A dozen or so elders, private rooms, a common hearth and table, staff as shahbazim in the project’s vocabulary — household workers, not a hallway aide assignment. The dining table is a real table. The living room is not at the end of a double-loaded corridor because the corridor is gone. That is a different furniture problem. Knee clearance still matters (chapter 27). Wipeability still matters. Capacity tags still matter (chapter 43). What changes is the *set*. You are buying for a house, not for a wing.

What did not automatically change is the bed. Green House bedrooms still needed a medical deck for many residents. The hi-lo, the mattress, the rail question — those traveled into the little house because bodies traveled. A hospital PO for the bed and a furniture-store PO for the hearth chairs is a split a lot of projects actually ran. Culture-change writing likes to photograph the hearth. The bed is in the other room with the pendant. Batesville’s argument (chapter 2) shows up again: the deck still wants to look like furniture. In a Green House it has a better chance, because the room is private and the headboard is not competing with a roommate’s curtain. Chance is not a spec.

Pioneer Network’s documents are movement documents, not statutes. They do not amend Appendix PP. They do not void a Medline punchout. They gave administrators a language in which to ask for a grouping, a household, a consistent assignment. Surveyors could hear that language and still write F584 for missing drapes in the old building. Operators could adopt the language and keep the old building because construction is a different capital number than a sofa. The honest Michigan story, for a Medilodge-era portfolio, is mostly the old building plus a conference and a furniture refresh. A few operators built or expanded toward households. Most dressed the corridor.

Specifying a household in a double-loaded plant is how you get the living-room grouping that opened this essay. The architect is not coming. The catalog is. Purchasing orders a “residential lounge package”: sofa, two chairs, table, maybe a bookcase that will hold puzzles and infection-control wipes. Finish: oak or cherry laminate, vinyl that claims a bleach number. The package photographs. It is not a Green House. It is not Eden. It is a purchase that lets the building say the words.

## What actually changed, and what the book kept

Sometimes the chair changed. Dining rooms after culture-change visits acquired arms. Day rooms acquired a mix instead of a row. A few buildings put real wood tables in and then discovered the apron (chapter 27). Memory-care units acquired “living rooms” of identical vinyl club chairs (chapter 31) — household language, unit look. The bed stayed an electric hi-lo. The overbed table stayed a castered tray. The wardrobe stayed too tall. Those objects are the series because they are what the PO would not release.

Sometimes only the language changed. The nurses’ station was called a neighborhood desk. The geri chair was called a lounge chair. The double room was called a suite. The Medline book did not need to reprint. SKUs stayed. Staff stayed. The silk plant arrived. A reader who treats 1991 as the year American nursing-home furniture became domestic is dating a press release. A reader who treats 1991 as the year a new *argument* entered the purchasing meeting is closer. The argument was: stop buying the hospital set. The invoice was often: hospital set, plus a sofa.

Making, after Eden, includes the aftermarket of accessories that are not quite furniture and not quite clinical: a birdcage stand, a fish tank on a laminate base, a child-sized chair in the lobby for visiting grandchildren. Those objects wear out or get banned after a fall or an infection-control month. They are the myth’s hardware. They rarely appear on the capital list next to the beds. They appear on a supply punchout next to the wipes. That is a correct classification and a sad one.

Wearing, after Green House photographs had circulated, includes a particular disappointment: the sofa no one can stand from, bought because it looked like the hearth in the picture. F558 does not care that the picture was from Tupelo. Seat height and arms still apply in a household. Culture change that ignores the stand is décor. Culture change that specifies the stand is furniture work. The second kind is rarer in the book because the book sells the picture.

A Medilodge-era administrator who wanted both — the survey word *homelike* and the Eden sentence about life — had to do the translation OBRA had also refused (chapter 4). Some did it with drapes and personal items and left the SKUs. Some did it with a real table and chairs and a fight with EVS about wood. Some did it by sending staff to training and buying nothing. All three can be found in Michigan without naming a building. The corridor grouping is the compromise object. It is furniture that performs the myth for visitors and for a surveyor walking the long hall. The rooms to the left and right still hold the hospital package.

Making a hearth chair that EVS will accept and a resident can leave is the actual culture-change shop problem. Few punchout pages posed it that way. They posed a sofa. Specifying seat height and arms on the grouping (F558 again) is how a living room stays a living room after the photograph. Buying only the photograph is how the grouping becomes a second day-room failure at the end of the hall.

## Residual hearth

The living-room grouping at the end of a corridor that is still a corridor is not a failure of good will. It is what a furniture channel can deliver when the building type does not change. Thomas asked for a different life. Tupelo tried a different plan. Pioneer wrote a different vocabulary. The Medline book offered a different photograph of the same vinyl. A Medilodge-class buyer could purchase the photograph. Purchasing the plan required a construction loan and a census the old double rooms already held.

Nightingale’s ward was honest about being a system of beds. Batesville was honest about wanting the bed to look like a dresser. Eden was honest about wanting the building to stop being a ward. The SNF that put a sofa under the exit sign is trying to hold all three honesties at once. The sofa will flatten. The exit sign will stay. The next essay leaves the hearth and opens the 1979 glossy catalog — the channel that priced the sofa, the bed, and the silk-plant dish in the same binder.

- William H. Thomas; Eden Alternative, 1991; Chase Memorial Nursing Home, New York, in the movement’s origin story. Treat plants/animals/children as the published triad, not as a furniture list.
