# Mattress fit is a gap

The cheapest way to build an entrapment zone is to buy a mattress on Tuesday that does not know the deck from Monday.

FDA’s 2006 guidance is blunt about components. A bed system is frame, rails, mattress. Replacement parts change openings. Compressible foam changes openings. Articulation changes openings. The Medline-era punchout was equally blunt in the other direction: mattresses are a consumable. Consumables get the glove treatment — price, dock date, a width that is a nickname.

I do not make mattresses. I have to live next to them. A nightstand specified to a 36-inch deck looks drunk next to a 32-inch foam that has slumped. A rail that was honest on the original pillow-top is a canyon on a thin replacement.

## History: the consumable that joined a death file

For most of the hospital-bed century the mattress was bedding. You bought a frame. You bought foam. You flipped the foam when it smelled. Hill-Burton plants and the nursing homes that inherited their supply treated the surface the way they treated sheets: a line item that turned over.

The 1995 safety alert and the Hospital Bed Safety Workgroup changed the job description. The March 10, 2006, guidance — the availability notice is 71 Fed. Reg. 12369 — treats the mattress as a **system component**. Not a comfort story. A dimension that decides whether a body can go into Zone 3 and not come out. The Agency’s file from January 1, 1985, to January 1, 2006, held about **691** reports: **413** deaths, **120** injuries, **158** near-misses. I will not invent a mattress-only subset. I will say the guidance is explicit that a replacement mattress changes openings, and that long-term care is in the settings.

CMS still writes the mattress as comfort and hygiene. 42 CFR 483.90(e)(2)(ii)–(iii) wants a clean, comfortable mattress and bedding appropriate to weather. Necessary. Not sufficient for fit. A surveyor can like the quilt and still be standing next to a canyon. The temperature band for facilities first certified after October 1, 1990 — **71 to 81 °F** — is why a foam that was firm in a 72-degree storeroom is a different edge in an 80-degree west room. Heat and moisture are how edges die.

Pressure programs in the same years made the mattress a clinical device wearing a cover: overlays, low-air-loss, alternating surfaces, rental contracts that swapped a surface on a Thursday and told furniture nothing. Wound care and gap care did not share a meeting. The punchout loved that silence, because silence lets you sell width as a name.

I have walked rooms where the bed label, the rail kit, and the mattress zipper were three companies. Nobody in that room had done a wrong thing on purpose. They had done the ordinary thing: replace the part that stains. Ordinary is how Zone 3 gets manufactured after the factory.

## Shop geometry: edge, length, height

**Width is a name, not a measurement.** “36-inch mattress” is a trade name. The foam, the cover, the zipper, the way it is stuffed, and the way it is centered on the deck decide the **edge**. Zone 3 lives at the edge.

A cover that is slick will let a person slide toward the rail. A cover that grabs will let a person dent the edge and open a gap anyway. A waffle or air surface that is proud of the deck will change the rail’s lower relationship. A foam that is tired in the middle will crown the edge and then collapse it.

I will not give you a brand ranking. I will give you a procedure: **measure the gap with the mattress that will be on the bed at 2 a.m.**, not the mattress in the cut sheet.

Shop practice: I want the deck width written, the mattress width written as a measured edge after the cover is on, and the rail type written on the same scrap. If those three numbers do not have owners, the nightstand height I pick is a guess. A 36-inch nickname on a deck that is honest at 35 and a foam that finished at 34 is how you get daylight that photographs as a “generous” bed.

Centering matters. A mattress that has walked toward the working side opens a canyon on the wall side, or the other way around. Articulation walks it more. A person who scoots toward a rail to stand walks it more. The gap you measured at noon on a fresh make is not the gap at 2 a.m.

**Length and Zone 7.** A short mattress on a long deck is a Zone 7 kit. Pillows get stuffed in the vacancy. Feet find the vacancy. A decorative footboard that was “close enough” becomes a trap.

A long mattress that bunches at the foot is a different Zone 7. Articulation will bunch it more. If the house uses an overlay, you have a third thickness no one measured.

Shop knowledge from rooms I walked: the overlay is the forgotten component. Pressure programs love overlays. Gap programs hate surprises. Write the overlay on the same line as the mattress or admit you are guessing.

A pillow stuffed in a vacancy is not a filler the guidance blessed. It is a compressible object that moves. I have seen two pillows and a folded blanket doing the work of a mattress that was ordered short because the nickname was “standard length.” Standard is not a tape.

**Height and the rail’s lower edge.** Zone 2 and Zone 3 care about how high the sleeping surface is relative to the rail’s lower edge. A thicker mattress can close a space or create a new one. A thinner mattress can open a bay that the original set closed.

The 2006 dimensional recommendations assume you know which surface you are measuring from. Zones 1 through 3: smaller than **120 mm (4.75 inches)** where the Agency put a recommended limit. Zone 4: smaller than **60 mm (2.375 inches)**. A tired foam is a different surface than the sample. Compressibility is in the guidance on purpose. A fist in a new pillow-top is not a fist in a three-year foam.

Low-air-loss and similar powered surfaces are devices on devices. They have their own hoses, their own power, their own way of failing flat at night. I am not going to specify one. I am going to say that a furniture height chosen for a six-inch foam will be wrong for a powered surface that sits higher and sounds like a fridge. The hose is a trip. The brick is a heat source in a room that already lives in a 71-to-81 band. The nightstand that was set for a hand reach on foam is a stretch or a cliff on air.

Read the mattress as leftover, too. In an 80-square-foot double, a thicker surface that cannot compress at the rail still has to leave a working side. Width nicknames that grow “for comfort” steal aisle the same way a 24-inch-deep bedside steals aisle.

## Covers, wipes, and the edge that dies

Infection control will wipe the cover. Some covers survive. Some coat themselves in a film that changes friction. Some crack at the zipper and become a moisture story and a gap story at once.

A cracked cover is how foam soaks and slumps. A slumped edge is Zone 3. EVS did not mean to design a zone. The spec that said “wipeable” without a replacement interval did.

Write a replacement interval if you are writing a real program. I will not invent the month. The house’s wound-care and infection-control people know when their surfaces die. Ask them, not the punchout.

Friction is a clinical number wearing a swatch. Slick plus a rail is a slide into Zone 3. Grabby plus a thin foam is a dent that opens Zone 3 anyway. Moisture-barrier covers that shine under a west window at 80 degrees are a slide in the afternoon and a grab at 6 a.m. after a void. I am not writing a wound protocol. I am writing why a furniture person should refuse to pick a nightstand height from a JPEG of a quilted top.

Zippers fail at the corner first. The corner is the edge. The edge is the zone. A taped repair is a ridge. A ridge is a new measurement.

## The punchout crime

A distributor can sell you a bed from vendor A, a rail kit from a later year, and a mattress from vendor C, all on one statement, all “healthcare.” That statement is not a system.

The honest channel move is a **matched set** from the bed-maker, or a written assessment after a mix. The dishonest move is a JPEG of a quilted top and a width nickname.

If you are a mill being asked to “match the bed,” ask which bed and which mattress. If they cannot name both, you are matching a ghost.

Medline-era consumable culture trained buyers to bid mattresses like gloves: count, dock date, a width column. Gloves do not make Zone 3. Foam does. GPO tiers that made the mattress the cheap line were not evil. They were inattentive. Inattention is how a 2006 guidance document sits in a binder while a 32-inch foam goes onto a 36-inch nickname deck.

Special-buy beds with “house” mattresses were a chain habit. Freeze the frame, shop the foam. I will not invent a SKU or a Medilodge PO. I will say the habit showed up in more than one state on our public project list, because the habit was the channel, not a building.

Used mattresses are worse. A used foam on a used deck is two unknown compressions. A camping pad on a hospital frame is a Facebook listing I have already complained about in the used draft. Do not “save money” in Zone 3. The death file is not a place to be thrifty.

Rental surfaces that rotate through a building are a program if someone owns the gap after each swap. They are a lottery if the nurse who called the rental company is not the person who called the mill about the nightstand.

## What I write next to a bedside

On a teaching drawing I write: “Bedside height and setback assume mattress X on deck Y. If mattress changes, revisit Zone 3 and the seated reach.”

That sentence has saved me from a pretty nightstand that became a cliff next to a thin foam. It will not save you if no one owns the mattress.

The mattress is not furniture. It is the reason furniture looks wrong, and the reason a rail becomes a death. Treat it as a dimension, not as bedding.

I also write the overlay, if there is one, and whether a powered brick lives on the floor where a chair wants to sit. I write that a decorative footboard is forbidden from closing a short-mattress vacancy. I write that attic stock, if the house is grown-up, includes a surface that matches the deck, not a close-enough foam from the punchout’s other page.

## What to look for on a floor

From the door: daylight between mattress and rail, between mattress and headboard, between mattress and footboard. Daylight is not 120 mm. It is a reason to get a tape and a person whose job is the tape.

If you are allowed to look closer: is the foam crowned in the middle and collapsed at the edge? Is the cover cracked at the zipper? Is there an overlay no one mentioned? Is the mattress short, with pillows stuffed at the foot? Has it walked off center? Does the nightstand height still meet a seated hand on this surface, or only on the surface in the binder?

Look at the label on the bed and the zipper on the mattress. If they disagree, you are in the guidance’s warning. Look at the rail in intermediate, not only up. Look at head-up, not only flat.

Smell the edge. A soaked foam has a smell. A soaked edge is a slump waiting for a census.

I will not perform the HBSW method in a stranger’s room. I will not tell you a brand to buy. I will tell you that **a width nickname is how Zone 3 gets a purchase order.**
