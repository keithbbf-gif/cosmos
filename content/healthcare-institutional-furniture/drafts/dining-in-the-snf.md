# Dining in the SNF

A nursing-home dining room is a restaurant that cannot fire its guests.

It is also a therapy gym, a church, a family visiting room, and a place a person in a recliner cannot get to. Furniture that only understands “four-top, oak, 30 inches” will fail before dessert.

I have built tables for these rooms. I have watched a beautiful apron hit a wheelchair footplate and turn a meal into a hallway tray. The apron is the spec.

## Height is two numbers

Wheelchair dining and chair dining do not share a height.

A standard table at 29–30 inches can work for a person in a chair who can sit-to-stand. A person in a wheelchair often needs a top that clears the arm and a base that disappears. Some houses run two heights. Some run a high-low table. Some pretend.

Shop practice: if more than a sliver of the room is on wheels, **specify a wheelchair-clear height and a base that is not a slab**. A four-leg table with a deep apron is a residential object. A T-base or a C-base with a measured knee/footplate clearance is an institutional object. Write the clearance. Do not write “accessible” as a vibe.

ADA dining surfaces in public accommodations have a number people remember — a 28-to-34-inch top height band and knee clearance rules in the 2010 Standards. A SNF dining room is not automatically that chapter in the way a restaurant is, and I will not fake a scoping opinion. `[CITE NEEDED: the scoping conversation for a particular dining room under ADA / state accessibility / §504.]` Measure the chairs you have. Measure the wheelchairs you have. Write the inch.

A mixed-height room is honest work. A single height that “sort of works” is how you buy a slump at one end and a shrug at the other. Height without a path is a JPEG.

## Bases, tips, and the grab

People will pull themselves up on the table. They will. Your base either accepts that or becomes a tip.

A light pedestal with a pretty top is a residential dining idea. A SNF table wants weight, a wide foot, or a way to fix to the floor that maintenance will forgive when the program changes. Flip-tops are for EVS and for the room that is also bingo. Flip-tops have latches that pinch and fail.

Corners: radiused. I will keep saying this until the catalog stops shipping a square corner at hip height.

The grab is not only a tip story. It is a finish story. A person who loads the edge will find the raw veneer you hid on the underside. A person who parks a wheelchair will find the stretcher you put where a footplate lives. I want the underside finished, the stretcher set back, and a foot that will not skate when someone uses the top as a parallel bar. If the table walks, the meal walks.

Pedestals that photograph as one column tip when the plate is small and fight shins when the plate is wide. I will take four honest legs with clearance before I take a pretty mast that lies about physics.

## Chairs that can leave the table

Dining chairs in a SNF are sit-to-stand chairs that have to live under a top.

Arms that do not go under the top are how you buy a six-inch gap and a slump. Arms that do go under the top are how you buy a width problem. Casters on a dining chair are how you buy a slide-away when a person loads the arms to stand. Glides that mark the floor are how you buy an EVS war.

I want a chair that is stable when pushed on, a seat height that matches the table, a vinyl that wipes, and a weight rating that matches the room. I want enough chairs that a wheelchair can take a place without becoming a fire-lane problem.

Stacking banquet chairs are a tell. They say the room is an event space. A resident who eats three meals a day is not an event.

The resident-chair draft already argued 19-to-21 inches compressed for a lot of the bodies I saw. Dining does not get a fashion exception. A 17-inch banquet seat at a 30-inch top is a child at an adult table. A 21-inch chair at a 28-inch wheelchair top is a person eating with their shoulders in their ears. Match the pair or admit you bought two programs and stacked them.

Weight ratings belong on the dining chair the same way they belong on the room chair. A plus-sign “bariatric seat” parked at one table is a stigma with a napkin. Mix capacities. Do not paint the wide chair a different color so purchasing can find it.

## The country kitchen and the tray line

Culture change wanted a table that looked like a house and a pot that was not a steam table. Some buildings did it. Some buildings put a lamp on the steam table.

Furniture cannot invent a staffing ratio. A beautiful eight-top that needs two aides to serve is a beautiful eight-top that will be abandoned. Smaller tables are kinder to noise and to a person who startles. They are meaner to staff who want one pass.

I do not pick a model for you. I ask whether the table size matches the staff you actually have at 8 a.m., not the staff in the brochure.

The overbed is waiting for the people this room fails. If you care about dining, count the overbeds in use at noon. That count is the dining spec.

Household models wanted a table you could sit at like a relative. `[CITE NEEDED: a named Pioneer Network or CMS culture-change dining paragraph an editor wants quoted, not paraphrased.]` The mill version is a table that is not a cafeteria slab and a chair you can leave. The dishonest version is a corbel on the same 30-inch four-top and a steam table with a tablecloth.

A tray line is an honest machine. It feeds a lot of people with a few hands. Furniture cannot abolish it. Furniture can refuse to pretend it is a dining room. If the plates leave on overbeds, write that as a program.

## The room that is also chapel and bingo

A SNF dining room is rarely only a dining room. Tuesday it is bingo. Sunday it is a service. Saturday it is a birthday with balloons that EVS will hate. Monday it is a therapy walk-through because the corridor is a fire drill.

That calendar is a furniture specification. Flip-tops exist because the room has to empty. Folding tables exist because someone bought an event, not a meal. Stacking chairs exist because the closet is the real dining program.

If the room must convert, write the conversion. Write how long it takes. Write who moves the tables. Write where the chairs go so they are not a trip in the pantry. A conversion that takes two aides and forty minutes will not happen at lunch. The tables will stay in the “event” position and the residents will eat around a gap.

Storage is the forgotten object. Bingo blowers, hymnals, a cake box, the extra leaves — if they live on the serving line they are a hygiene problem. If they live in the egress they are a life-safety problem. A mill can build a closed cabinet that takes a wipe. A cardboard pile in the corner is not a cabinet.

## Aisle, egress, and the wheelchair that is a person

A wheelchair at a table is not leftover seating. It is a person at a meal. The place has to be a place: a gap in the chair ring, a base that does not fight the footplates, a path in and a path out that is not a fire-lane argument.

I will not tattoo a corridor width onto this page as if it were furniture code. Life safety and the adopted NFPA 101 edition own aisle and egress language, and the edition moves. Ask the life-safety person which book the last survey used. Then walk the path with a chair, not a tape on a drawing.

What I will say as a mill: a table that grows toward the door because someone wanted two more seats is how you buy a blocked egress and a tray in the hallway. A chair parked behind a wheelchair “just for family” is how you buy a person who cannot leave the table without a scene. Count the seats after you count the paths.

CMS still cares that furniture is functional and appropriate to needs — 42 CFR 483.90 — and F584 still cares about layout, sound, and a room that does not pose a safety risk. A dining layout that photographs as full and lives as a jam will fail the person before it fails the tag.

## Temperature, vinyl, and a two-hour sit

Facilities first certified after October 1, 1990, live in a **71–81 °F** band. That is not a dining note in the SOM, and it still changes the chair. Vinyl at 80 degrees on a west wall is a slick. A two-hour sit after lunch — the program that is really a staffing hold — turns that slick into a slide and a heat rash conversation.

Foam that was a showroom pillow becomes a hole. A moisture barrier that was a hope becomes a crinkle. If people stay at the table because there is nowhere else to go, the dining chair is a day-room chair and should be specified as one: cleanout, barrier, a cover that takes the wipe EVS actually uses.

Hard rooms fail sound. F584’s comfortable-sound paragraph is not a suggestion you can finish your way out of. Hard tables, hard floors, hearing aids, and a television leftover from activity hour are a glare and a shout.

## Flame, sound, and the hard room

A dining room is a lot of foam and a lot of noise. CAL 117 / NFPA 101 belong on the chairs. If you can add one soft thing, add it where the noise lives, not a decorative runner EVS will hate.

TB 117-2013 is the smolder floor now — 16 CFR part 1640 as of June 25, 2021. It is not a healthcare occupancy stamp and it is not NFPA 101. The fire draft sits with editions. A dining chair with a floral throw from home is a belonging and a fuel. Do not let the throw stand in for a report. Do not invent a sprinkler waiver. Open the edition. Ask the AHJ.

## Channel notes

Medline-era dining packages were often a different vendor than the resident-room wood. That is fine. The crime was a finish that did not match and a height that did not match the room chairs.

A chain special-buy that froze a 30-inch four-top for twelve buildings froze a height. Check whether those buildings still have the same wheelchair census they had when the buy froze.

Bradley Brand’s southern hardwood table is a good object in a house that will maintain it. It is a bad object if the edge is raw and the bleach is a bath. The bleach draft sits next door.

I will not invent a Medline dining SKU or a Medilodge purchase order. Dining was the room purchasing treated as hospitality, and hospitality is how you buy a pedestal that tips. Ask for the apron dimension on the same page as the finish name.

Attic stock a top or a vinyl. A dining program that lasts ten years will outlive a cover that lasts two.

## What I do on a walkthrough

I sit. I pull a wheelchair to the apron. I stand using the arms. I listen. I look for the condemned-chair pile in the pantry. I count trays leaving for rooms. I look at the corners. I look at the flip-top latches.

I walk the path from the corridor to a window table without asking anyone to move. I check whether a base plate is a shin. I check whether the underside is finished. I check whether the only wheelchair gap is in front of the steam table.

If the apron hits me, the table is a tray line that has not admitted it.
