# Dining in the SNF

A nursing-home dining room is a restaurant that cannot fire its guests.

It is also a therapy gym, a church, a family visiting room, and a place a person in a recliner cannot get to. Furniture that only understands “four-top, oak, 30 inches” will fail before dessert.

I have built tables for these rooms. I have watched a beautiful apron hit a wheelchair footplate and turn a meal into a hallway tray. The apron is the spec.

## Height is two numbers

Wheelchair dining and chair dining do not share a height.

A standard table at 29–30 inches can work for a person in a chair who can sit-to-stand. A person in a wheelchair often needs a top that clears the arm and a base that disappears. Some houses run two heights. Some run a high-low table. Some pretend.

Shop practice: if more than a sliver of the room is on wheels, **specify a wheelchair-clear height and a base that is not a slab**. A four-leg table with a deep apron is a residential object. A T-base or a C-base with a measured knee/footplate clearance is an institutional object. Write the clearance. Do not write “accessible” as a vibe.

ADA dining surfaces in public accommodations have a number people remember — a 28-to-34-inch top height band and knee clearance rules in the 2010 Standards. A SNF dining room is not automatically that chapter in the way a restaurant is, and I will not fake a scoping opinion. `[CITE NEEDED: the scoping conversation for a particular dining room under ADA / state accessibility / §504.]` Measure the chairs you have. Measure the wheelchairs you have. Write the inch.

## Bases, tips, and the grab

People will pull themselves up on the table. They will. Your base either accepts that or becomes a tip.

A light pedestal with a pretty top is a residential dining idea. A SNF table wants weight, a wide foot, or a way to fix to the floor that maintenance will forgive when the program changes. Flip-tops are for EVS and for the room that is also bingo. Flip-tops have latches that pinch and fail.

Corners: radiused. I will keep saying this until the catalog stops shipping a square corner at hip height.

## Chairs that can leave the table

Dining chairs in a SNF are sit-to-stand chairs that have to live under a top.

Arms that do not go under the top are how you buy a six-inch gap and a slump. Arms that do go under the top are how you buy a width problem. Casters on a dining chair are how you buy a slide-away when a person loads the arms to stand. Glides that mark the floor are how you buy an EVS war.

I want a chair that is stable when pushed on, a seat height that matches the table, a vinyl that wipes, and a weight rating that matches the room. I want enough chairs that a wheelchair can take a place without becoming a fire-lane problem.

Stacking banquet chairs are a tell. They say the room is an event space. A resident who eats three meals a day is not an event.

## The country kitchen and the tray line

Culture change wanted a table that looked like a house and a pot that was not a steam table. Some buildings did it. Some buildings put a lamp on the steam table.

Furniture cannot invent a staffing ratio. A beautiful eight-top that needs two aides to serve is a beautiful eight-top that will be abandoned. Smaller tables are kinder to noise and to a person who startles. They are meaner to staff who want one pass.

I do not pick a model for you. I ask whether the table size matches the staff you actually have at 8 a.m., not the staff in the brochure.

The overbed is waiting for the people this room fails. If you care about dining, count the overbeds in use at noon. That count is the dining spec.

## Flame, sound, and the hard room

A dining room is a lot of foam and a lot of noise. CAL 117 / NFPA 101 belong on the chairs. Hard tables plus hard floors plus hearing aids is F584’s sound paragraph wearing a finish.

If you can add one soft thing, add it where the noise lives, not a decorative runner EVS will hate.

## Channel notes

Medline-era dining packages were often a different vendor than the resident-room wood. That is fine. The crime was a finish that did not match and a height that did not match the room chairs.

A chain special-buy that froze a 30-inch four-top for twelve buildings froze a height. Check whether those buildings still have the same wheelchair census they had when the buy froze.

Bradley Brand’s southern hardwood table is a good object in a house that will maintain it. It is a bad object if the edge is raw and the bleach is a bath. The bleach draft sits next door.

## What I do on a walkthrough

I sit. I pull a wheelchair to the apron. I stand using the arms. I listen. I look for the condemned-chair pile in the pantry. I count trays leaving for rooms. I look at the corners. I look at the flip-top latches.

If the apron hits me, the table is a tray line that has not admitted it.
