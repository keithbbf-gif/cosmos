# Headwalls and overbed light

A headwall is not furniture. It is a **vertical dock** for oxygen, vacuum, power, nurse call, and a light that will glare on a glossy top.

A mill that ignores it will put a wardrobe on the only outlet and a headboard over the only gas outlet. I have moved both. I have also been asked to build a “wood headwall” that was a panel with holes. Holes in a gas wall are not a finish.

I am writing this from the leftover, not from an electrical license. If the panel is a true medical-gas wall, the authority is the adopted NFPA 99 and the people who own that stamp. `[CITE NEEDED: pin the NFPA 99 edition and the medical-gas sentences an editor wants quoted.]` My job is to keep a case-good from making that stamp a Thursday emergency.

## The hospital leftover

Headwalls grew up in acute care. A hospital bed is a machine that wants gases, vacuum, power, and a light a nurse can slap at 3 a.m. without hunting a lamp. The American ward taught that those services should live in one vertical strip so a staff member could work a row. Nursing homes inherited a cheaper cousin: a few outlets, a call, a light, sometimes gases, sometimes a strip that is trying to look like a hotel sconce.

Hill-Burton and the postwar bed set made the habit national. The 1965 payment category bought the habit for houses that were not hospitals. OBRA 1987 then asked those houses to look like homes. The headwall did not get the memo. It is still a dock. You can put a wooden surround on a dock. You cannot make a dock a headboard without owning the valves.

In a lot of Medline-era SNFs the “headwall” is not a manufactured hospital unit at all. It is a few boxes and a fluorescent that someone called a program. That cheaper cousin is the one I walked most. It still wins fights with furniture, because two outlets and one light are enough to ruin a wardrobe plan.

## Light is a tag, not a sconce

F584 wants adequate and comfortable lighting, resident control where feasible, less glare. Adequate means the tasks the resident or staff must do. Comfortable means glare is minimized and the resident can do something about the light when that is feasible. Those are survey sentences. They are also mill sentences, because a high-gloss nightstand under a hard overbed fluorescent is a glare kit I shipped if I specified the gloss.

Write the light as a program: ambient, task, night. Furniture either helps or fights.

Ambient is the room at rest. A tall wardrobe that shadows the only ceiling can will make a single look like a cave on the pillow side. I have seen packages that photographed as bright because the photographer stood in the aisle, where the fixture actually throws.

Task is reading, eating, a wound, a med pass. The overbed light — when the house still has one — was built for the med pass. A resident trying to read under it gets a slap of cool white and a top that becomes a mirror. A lamp on the bedside is a belonging and a tip and a bulb EVS will not dust. Personal belongings to the extent possible includes that lamp until it becomes a fire or a fall. Then you are in a different conversation, and “homelike” is not a trump.

Night is the hour the resident owns. A fixture with no dim, no low, no separate night lamp, is a staff tool. If the room is dark at the pillow and bright at the aisle, the lighting program is for staff. F584 imagined the resident too.

I will not invent a footcandle table. `[CITE NEEDED: if an editor wants IES or Appendix PP lighting numbers beyond the glare / control language, pin the edition.]` I will say a mill can ruin a legal fixture with a glossy top and a tall box.

## Power is a leftover

Beds want a plug. Air surfaces want a plug. Chargers want a plug. A lift may want a plug. The family lamp wants a plug. The room has two, if it is lucky, and one of them is behind the bed.

If the wardrobe eats one and the bed eats the other, the family lamp is a power strip and a fire story. Specify outlet locations on the furniture plan the way you specify a rod height. I have cut a notch I hated because the alternative was a cord under a door. A notch is ugly. A cord under a door is a pinch and a trip and a call-light failure.

Power strips and cube taps show up when the plan lied. I do not specify them. I specify enough outlets, or I specify which object loses. Usually the wardrobe loses a few inches of width, or it moves off the wall that owns the receptacle. Matching grain is not a reason to cover a duplex.

Cables are furniture-adjacent. A bed cable pinched by a plinth, a nurse-call cord trapped in a drawer, an air-surface hose under a chair sled — those are function failures that look like mill problems when the house calls. Look at the cord path before you freeze a toe-kick.

## Oxygen, tubing, and the snag garden

Some SNF rooms have gases. Many have concentrators and tubing instead. Both turn the floor into a map.

Oxygen tubing is a trip. Furniture feet should not be a snag garden. Casters should not roll over a hose as a habit. An overbed base that looks like a wishbone can be kind to a tube or cruel to a tube, depending on the radius and where staff park it. I look at the floor around the headwall before I look at the crown.

If the headwall is a true gas panel, a wooden cover that has to come off with a millwright is a clinical failure. A valve that is late because a decorative panel was screwed into a stud is not a finish complaint. Keep the panel off the gases. If you must have wood in that plane, make it removable by a nurse with the tool that is already on the floor, or do not make it.

I will not write a medical-gas procedure. I will write a mill refusal: holes in a gas wall are not a furniture option.

## The decorative headboard again

A wood panel that covers the headwall is how you buy a service call and a Zone 6. FDA’s 2006 guidance names the space between the end of a rail and the side of a head or foot board, and the space between a board and the mattress end. A mill panel that “matches the suite” and sits off the frame is those zones with stain on it.

If the headwall has to be reached, the panel has to come off without a millwright. If the panel is furniture, keep it off the gases and write whether it joins the bed system. Matching the nightstand is not a reason to cover a valve. Matching the nightstand is not ownership of a 120 mm or 60 mm opening.

The ethical drawing says one of two things, in words a later installer can find:

- This panel does not join the bed system; clearance is a decision; here is the clearance.
- This panel is part of the bed-maker’s assembly; here is their drawing.

A third sentence — “decorative only” — is how Zone 6 gets built. I will not sign “decorative only.”

## Cubicle track, sprinklers, TVs

The ceiling is a political convention. Track, sprinkler, TV bracket, curtain mesh, a smoke detector that was already there. Furniture that wants height — a tall wardrobe, a tall headboard, a “wood headwall” that suddenly grew a cornice — has to sit in that convention.

I measure the ceiling politics before I freeze a crown. Thursday’s two inches usually live here. A cathedral that photographs as homelike will catch the mesh at the bend. A wardrobe that photographs as a closet will sit under a sprinkler that wanted a clearance nobody wrote on the punchout page.

The television is a late addition in a lot of older plants: a bracket on the wall the wardrobe also wanted, a cord to the only outlet the bed also wanted, a speaker that F584’s sound sentence will eventually meet. I do not specify televisions. I specify that the furniture plan and the bracket plan cannot occupy the same stud without a meeting.

## Nurse call is a reach

The call device has to be reachable from the bed. Furniture that looks intended can still put it behind a pillow ledge or under a decorative rail cover. I look at the call before I look at the wood grain. If a panel I ship will hide the jack, the panel is wrong.

This is not romance about independence. It is the same leftover math as the rod. A device you cannot reach is a staff closet. The tag language lives in other numbers. The mill failure is the same shape.

## Channel notes

Medline-era furnishings pages did not include the headwall. Facilities pages sometimes did. The split is the interface problem again: device and utility on one binder, wood on another, one PO pretending they met.

A special-buy wood package that never saw the electrical drawing was a pretty fight. Get the electrical drawing. Get the reflected ceiling if anyone has one. Get a photograph of the actual head of the actual bed in the actual room, because 1968 plants do not match the 2009 JPEG.

The punchout can sell you a lamp. It cannot sell you an outlet. If the job is a flood replacement of nightstands, walk the plugs anyway. The condemned pile in the courtyard will show you cup rings. It will not show you that the only duplex is now behind the new taller box.

## How I walk the head of the bed

I look at the head of the bed before I look at the wood.

How many plugs, and which objects have already claimed them. Where the tube goes, and whether a foot or a caster is a snag. Whether the light is a slap, a dim, a night lamp, or a rumor. Whether a panel is covering a service. Whether the wardrobe is a shadow on the pillow. Whether a decorative board is sitting off the frame where a rail ends. Whether the call is in a hand or in a crack.

If I am allowed to sit where the resident sits, I sit. Glare is different from a standing aisle. If I am not allowed, I do not perform a show. I write what I can see from the door and I ask nursing the rest.

A mill that starts at the crown is decorating a dock. Start at the dock. The crown can wait until Thursday, when the sprinkler tells you the truth.
