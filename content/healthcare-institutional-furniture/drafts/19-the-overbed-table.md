# The Overbed Table

*Educational history of healthcare and nursing-home furniture. Not a purchasing specification for a named building, not a survey opinion, and not clinical advice about beds, rails, or seating for a particular resident.*

The table will not stay up. That is the failure everyone on the wing can demonstrate without a work order: a top that sinks under a meal tray, or under a chart, or under a pair of elbows, the gas spring having given up its last inch sometime in the night. The crank versions fail differently. They bind, or the handle spins, or the column rotates so the top is no longer square to the bed. Either way the object is the same class — a castered tray with a column and a base that is supposed to slide under a deck — and it is the piece the aide uses twenty times a shift. Purchasing replaces it from the book the way EVS replaces a mop head. Capital never quite claimed it. The resident uses it as a desk, a dining table, a nightstand overflow, and a place to put the water the bedside cabinet will not hold.

Nightingale’s ward had a table that could be rolled. The American hospital made it a chrome H-base and a bakelite top. The Medline-era SNF made it a house-brand consumable with a cup holder and a vanity drawer that sticks. This essay is about that consumable. Chapter 23 will put it back in the room package. Chapter 38 will take its casters. Here the problem is the piece that fails first.

## A top, a column, a base that has to miss the chair

Typical tops in the channel sit around 15 by 30 inches or 18 by 32 inches `[VERIFY]`. Those rectangles are meal-tray sized, not desk sized. A laptop, later, hung over the edge. A basin hung over the edge. An elbow hung over the edge and taught the gas spring what fatigue is. Raised lips and “spill rims” were sold after someone counted the carpets. Cup holders and a small molded well were vanity. The vanity is how a medical tray pretended to be a bedside table. It is also how liquid found the column.

Height travel is advertised, in a great many sheets, in the neighborhood of 28 to 45 inches `[VERIFY]`. That range is trying to serve a low-low deck (chapter 12), a conventional hi-lo, a recliner, and a wheelchair. It cannot serve all of them with one spring. Set too high, the top becomes a chin rest. Set too low, it hits the mattress and will not slide over the expanded frame (chapter 13). Pneumatic columns are quick and they sink. Crank columns are slow and they last longer if anyone will crank them. A Medilodge-era wing mixed both because replacements came from whoever had stock. The resident who could crank, cranked. The resident who could not waited for the aide, which is how a table becomes a staffing object.

H-base versus U-base is the wheelchair sentence. An H-base has a stretcher between the feet and will not accept some chair frames. A U-base or a C-base opens to one side so a wheelchair can nest. Catalogs printed both and then printed a “universal” that was neither. The overbed table is often the dining surface for a person who will not go to the dining room. Knee clearance (chapter 27) belongs to the dining table. The overbed’s version is whether the base misses the chair’s footplates and whether the top clears the person’s knees when the column is at the only height that still holds. When it does not, the table sits beside the bed instead of over it, and then it is just a bad nightstand on casters.

Weight on the top is the unwritten spec. Meal, pitcher, chart binder, elbows, a Bible, a phone, a rolled brief, a pulse-ox that someone will come back for. House-brand sheets sometimes listed a top load — 50 pounds is a number that appears in the class `[VERIFY]` — and then the gas spring failed at a lower number because springs fail by cycle, not by a single afternoon’s tray. Name-brand tables lasted longer and cost enough to be missing when the capital number shrank. The package (chapter 23) cut the chair first and the table second. The bed stayed. People ate off the nightstand.

## Pneumatic, crank, and the vanity that stuck

The pneumatic column is a gas spring in a tube with a trigger, usually under the top, sometimes on a lever the resident cannot reach. Hair, tape, and thickened drink find the trigger. Bleach finds the labels. After 2020 the card in the file and the bottle on the cart (chapter 10) made the top a nightly chemical argument; the column was never on the vinyl card. Sticky triggers are how tables get left at one height for a year. A table that will not go down will not slide over the bed. It becomes a side table that still has casters, which is how it walks into the bathroom and then into chapter 42 as a dent.

Crank tables are older and, on some wings, preferred by maintenance because a stripped crank is a visible failure. The handle lives under the top or at the column. It catches a gait belt. It catches a catheter bag. It is chrome or plastic and it bends when a lift rolls over it. The column on a crank table can rotate if the keyway wears, so the top is no longer parallel to the deck. A rotated top is a spill. It is also a dignity problem: the cup holder now pours into the person’s lap. Maintenance squares it with a wrench until the next wear.

The vanity drawer — a shallow flip-out or a pencil drawer — is the table’s attempt to be casegoods. It holds glasses, a comb, a menu, a remote, a hearing-aid battery card. It sticks because the plastic runner was never a drawer slide. When it sticks, someone yanks, and the top rackets, and the spring takes another cycle. Some house-brand tops omitted the drawer and molded a recess instead. The recess fills with crumbs and with the thickened drink. EVS can wipe a recess. EVS cannot easily wipe the inside of a stuck vanity that nobody admits is a furniture part.

Casters on overbed tables are small, cheap, and always hair-packed. Two lock, or one locks, or none lock and the table walks when the person writes. Soft treads mark the vinyl floor; hard treads rattle at 2 a.m. A table that will not stay up and will not stay still is two failures in one SKU. Chapter 38 will generalize the caster. The overbed is the object that proves the generalization before breakfast.

## Channel: the consumable that looks like equipment

Medline’s furniture pages — house-brand prefixes, punchout photographs, a price that lived in supply rather than in the construction punch — treated the overbed table as a repeat buy. That is accurate. A 120-bed Medilodge-class home might keep a stack of boxed tables in the basement the way it kept boxes of foam mattresses. Drop-ship from the factory and warehouse stock from Mundelein-era distribution were both used; the table is light enough to warehouse. Freight claims on a bent column are harder than claims on a crushed nightstand carton, because the column looks straight until it sinks. Concealed-damage windows close (chapter 42). The table then lives in the hall “for the next room” because no one will take the claim and no one will put a sinking top over a meal.

Name-brand tables — the ones hospitals specified, with better springs and a U-base that actually cleared a wheelchair — appeared in rehab gyms and in a few private rooms. The double rooms got the house brand. That split is the lobby-vinyl-versus-Nemschoff split (chapter 29) in a smaller object. Who walks in the front door sees the lobby. Who eats in bed sees the table. Surveyors under F917 want functional furniture. A table that will not hold a meal is not functional. It is also not the thing a surveyor photographs first. The bed is. The table fails in private, twenty times a shift.

Hospitals moved some of this work to a bedside arm or a boom. The SNF did not. The SNF kept the rolling tray because the bed moves, the chair moves, and the person who eats in the recliner (chapter 25) still needs a surface. One table per bed was the package sentence. One table per two beds was the austerity sentence. The roommate then ate at a height that belonged to someone else.

Infection control discovered the overbed table every decade and again in 2020. The top is wipeable if it is a solid HPL or a molded plastic. The underside is a map of gum, labels, and spilled supplement. The column is a handhold. The base is a foot-stop that EVS mops around. Outdoor visits and plastic on the lounge (chapter 10) had a cousin here: a bag over the top, or a table left in the hall because it had been in an isolation room and nobody knew which disinfectant the spring seals could take. The vinyl card never covered the spring seals.

## What the aide knows and the spec forgets

The spec lists top size, height range, base style, color, maybe a cup holder. It does not list the number of cycles a spring will take with elbows on it. It does not list the outlet the table will block when it is parked at the head (chapter 11’s omitted wall). It does not list the trapeze boom it will hit (chapter 18). It does not list the visitor chair it will pin against the wardrobe. Those are room facts. The book sells a tray.

Therapy borrowed tables for the gym (chapter 33) and sometimes did not return them. A column that still held height was too valuable to leave over a bed whose occupant ate in the dining room. Purchasing then saw a “missing table” on a room list and ordered another house-brand spring. The gym kept the one that worked. The room received the one that sank. That is not theft. It is how a consumable migrates toward the user who will fight for it.

On a Medilodge-era corridor the tables dated the rooms the way the headboards did. A chrome crank from the 1990s, still working, painted with nail polish on the handle so it would not walk to another wing. A 2000s oak-look top that matched the nightstand for a year, then yellowed. A later gray molded top with a cup holder, already sinking, boxed spare in the basement. The package had promised a match. The replacement came in the color that was in stock.

The residual is the sink. A table that will not stay up is a small object and a complete theory of this channel: the most used piece is the one specified like a consumable, worn like equipment, and replaced like a mop. The next essay opens the drawer of the cabinet that was supposed to be the table’s partner — hairbrush, form, a life in one slide — and that was specified as storage while being used as a bumper.

## Notes

- Top sizes ~15×30 or 18×32 inches and height travel often advertised near 28–45 inches are channel ranges `[VERIFY]` by model year. Top-load numbers such as 50 lb are class claims `[VERIFY]`.
- H-base vs U-base / C-base: wheelchair nesting as a catalog distinction, not an accessibility statute copied onto the tray.
- F917 / §483.90(e): functional furniture. A sinking top is a function failure; the tag does not name a gas spring.
- Medline as channel (1966; later house-brand furniture pages), not as inventor of the overbed table. See chapter 7.
- Medilodge-class spare stack and mixed crank/pneumatic inventory: composite habit, not a named building’s basement list.
- Casters, vinyl chemistry, and freight claims are taken up in chapters 38, 10/36, and 42.
