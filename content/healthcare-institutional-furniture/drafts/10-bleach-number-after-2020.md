# The Bleach Number After 2020

*Educational history of healthcare and nursing-home furniture. Not a purchasing specification for a named building, not a survey opinion, and not clinical advice about beds, rails, or seating for a particular resident.*

The vinyl card says 1:10 bleach. Or it says a sodium-hypochlorite dilution that a mill treats as equivalent. The card is older than the pandemic. It lived in a purchasing file, in a binder behind the chips of chapter 9, in a PDF a representative forwarded once. Healthcare vinyl had been advertising bleach-cleanable for years because EVS was already pouring something like bleach on something like a chair. COVID did not invent the number. It made the card a nightly argument. Isolation rooms, outdoor visits, plastic on the lounge, a List N bottle on a cart that was not written for furniture. The chair that survived 2020 and the chair that did not are the same SKU on two wings, or two welts on one wing.

EPA List N is a disinfectant class. It names products for use against SARS-CoV-2 on hard surfaces, with contact times, in a public health emergency. It is not a furniture standard. It does not know a welt. It does not know a thermofoil edge. It does not know a pendant membrane. A Medilodge-era building that “followed List N” was following a bottle. The bottle met a chair whose hangtag had been written for a different pour, a different frequency, and a different fear. Chapter 36 takes construction: face, foam, stitching. This chapter takes the year the file card and the night bottle stopped pretending they were the same document.

## What the card already claimed

A healthcare vinyl card, pre-2020, is a small piece of sales literature. Cleaners allowed. Cleaners forbidden. A bleach sentence — commonly 1:10, sometimes with a dwell, sometimes with a rinse — printed as if it were a lab report. `[VERIFY]` any particular fabric. Quote the card as a card. Wyzenbeek double-rubs, when they appear, are the same kind of number: a mill’s marketing of a test, not a night’s work on a Michigan wing. The card’s job was to get the vinyl onto a punchout page next to CAL 117 (chapter 39) so that purchasing could say the chair was healthcare.

Making a bleach-claim vinyl is a coating and a plasticizer story. Too hard and it cracks. Too soft and it stains. A topcoat that will take hypochlorite for a while and then chalk. The welt is a different material, often, and the thread is a third. The card rarely spends sentences on the welt. Wearing starts there. A Medilodge-era teal chair that had already shone through on the arms by 2018 was not waiting for COVID. It was waiting for a higher frequency.

Specifying from the card meant attaching a PDF to a capital package and never opening it again. EVS had its own bind: a house disinfectant, a dilution, a cart. Infection control had a third. The three documents agreed in the meeting and diverged at 9 p.m. What EVS actually poured — a stronger bleach, a different quaternary, a wipe that was not the wipe the card pictured — is the wearing that no mill photographs. Outdoor furniture (chapter 34) had its own pour, or none, because no one wanted to bring cushions in. 2020 put plastic over the indoor lounge and called that infection control. The vinyl underneath still got it when the plastic came off.

Buying after the cards already existed was ordinary. Medline-era punchouts listed “bleach-cleanable” the way they listed oak. House-brand chairs and name-brand chairs used mills that printed similar sentences. A buyer who thought they had purchased a List N chair in 2018 was reading backward. They had purchased a card. The list did not exist yet in that form. The bottle did.

## The year the bottle won

2020 in a skilled-nursing building was a disinfectant year before it was a furniture year. Isolation rooms that had been rare became a plan. Chairs went in and did not come out, or came out and could not be used in the dining room until someone decided they could. Visitor chairs sat in halls. Sleepover chairs (chapter 22) became infection objects. Outdoor visits, when they happened, put seating in weather it was not bought for, then brought it back in, then wiped it as if the wipe were architecture. Plastic sheeting on lounge sofas: a cover that trapped moisture against a foam the card had never mentioned.

Staff poured more. That is the only furniture sentence that does not need a citation. Frequency is a dose. A 1:10 claim written for an occasional isolation is not a 1:10 claim written for a winter of every chair, every shift, sometimes without the rinse the card hoped for. Topcoats chalked. Dark vinyl went white in streaks. Light vinyl went yellow. Threads melted in a way that looked like dirt and was not. Thermofoil on the nightstand, which had never been on the vinyl card, got the same bottle because it was in the same room. The nightstand failed as chapter 37 said it would, only faster.

List N bottles that were quat-based, or hydrogen-peroxide-based, or something else the vinyl card had listed as forbidden, arrived because they were on the list and in stock. Forbidden is a mill word. In-stock is a pandemic word. The argument in the file — “our chairs are bleach 1:10” — met a cart that was not bleach that week. Some chairs survived the wrong bottle better than the right one at the wrong frequency. Survival is not a recommendation. It is a wear pattern. This essay is not a cleaning protocol.

Making, in 2020, included improvisation that purchasing did not order: tape on seams, garbage bags on seat cushions, a shower curtain as a sofa cover, dining chairs stacked in a room because dining had closed. Those are not SKUs. They are how a building uses furniture when the furniture’s card has nothing to say about a virus. Specifying stopped. No one was choosing a new teal. Buying, when it happened, was replacement of what had split, often the current gray woodgrain chair (chapter 9) because that was the punchout, not because gray survived better.

Wearing sorted the herd. A tightly stapled, well-topcoated vinyl on a simple seat without a welt stood a chance. A tufted back, a contrasting piping, a moisture-barrier fabric that was not vinyl and had been specified as “residential” in a culture-change year — those gave up. Crypton-class barriers (chapter 36) have their own cards and their own forbidden lists; some of them hated bleach before 2020 and hated it louder after. The geri chair in the corridor, wiped as a high-touch object, lost its arm tops. The overbed table’s vanity, not vinyl at all, clouded. The bed pendant, wiped because a hand had been on it, died as a switch and lived as a plastic shell.

## Michigan, plastic, and the chair that did not come back

A Medilodge-class wing — Prestige-era sign on the lawn or not — had the same channel furniture as 2019 and a different night. Outdoor visits in a Lower Peninsula winter put resin chairs and a blanket in a courtyard that chapter 34 will take as a season. Indoor vinyl went outside and came back stiff. Isolation rooms accumulated a chair that was “the isolation chair,” a leftover whose card no one could find. When the room cleared, the chair sometimes went to the dumpster because no one would sign that it was done being an isolation chair. That dumpster is a purchasing event. It does not appear on List N.

The lounge under plastic is the image that stays. A sofa bought to be homelike (chapter 5, chapter 6), wrapped so it could stay in the photograph without being sat on. Condensation. A smell. A vinyl that had been bleach-cleanable and was now wet in a different way. When the plastic came off, some sofas were fine and some were not. Foam that had wicked did not send a card back to the mill. The building ordered a replacement or lived with a soft spot. Soft spots are F558 problems if anyone is still thinking about standing. In 2020 many people were thinking about the bottle.

What EVS poured versus the hangtag is not a gotcha. EVS was trying to keep a building open. The hangtag was trying to keep a mill out of a warranty. Those jobs diverged. A house-brand chair whose warranty desk wanted photographs of the cleaners used was a chapter 42 claim dressed as chemistry. A name-brand chair with a clearer card still lost if the welt had been bad in 2018. 2020 did not invent bad welts. It invented a reason to look at every chair in one season.

Do not write that bleach-cleanable vinyl “saved” a building. Do not write that a chair is survey-proof because its card mentioned 1:10. Cards are literature. Buildings are pours. The educational fact is the mismatch, dated: List N as a public disinfectant class; furniture cards as mill claims; a year when frequency and substitution made the claims look smaller than they had in the file.

Making a replacement chair in 2021, for a mill, meant the same staple line and a print that had gone gray. Specifying, for a Medilodge-class office, meant “whatever is bleachable and in stock,” which was the punchout’s whole vocabulary by then. Buying meant accepting a six-week chair while dining used folding seats that had never had a card. Wearing meant those folding seats took the same bottle and died faster, then went to the courtyard and died of weather (chapter 34). The vinyl dining armchair that had been “the good chair” in 2019 was, if it survived, the only chair whose mill sentence had been even slightly about this pour. Survival still was not a specification. It was a remainder.

The file thickened. Infection-control memos, List N printouts, a mill’s updated PDF that now said *rinse after bleach* in a bolder face, a warranty denial that asked which product codes had been used. Chapter 42’s claim desk learned chemistry it had not staffed for. House-brand and name-brand desks did not learn it at the same speed. A chair that split in March sat in a hall until August because no one would own the pour. That hall chair is 2020’s furniture as surely as the isolation chair in the dumpster. It is also an older story: the object no one will take. The year only added a bottle number to the argument.

## Residual dilution

The card is still in the file. The bottle changed, then changed back, then became a habit at a frequency the mill did not price for. Mauve and teal that had already shone through shone through faster. Gray replacements arrived with the same 1:10 sentence and a shorter life because they were wiped like 2020 even in 2022. The overbed table and the pendant, which never had a vinyl card, took the same cart.

Nightingale wanted washable bedding. Batesville wanted a finish that would wipe. Medline printed “bleach-cleanable” on a page. A Medilodge-era EVS cart believed the page, then believed List N, then believed the shift. The chair that survived and the chair that did not are both true. They are not a product comparison. They are a year. The next essay leaves the bottle and opens a one-page bed spec that looks complete until you ask where the outlet is.

## Notes

- EPA List N: disinfectant products for use against SARS-CoV-2; a public-health listing with contact times. Not a furniture, vinyl, or welt standard.
- Healthcare vinyl bleach 1:10 (or named hypochlorite dilutions): common *card* language before 2020. `[VERIFY]` per fabric. Do not treat a card as a lab report unless a lab is named.
- Wyzenbeek and other wear numbers: marketing-on-a-card unless a test report is cited. Not used as invented counts here.
- Isolation rooms, outdoor visits, plastic-covered lounge: composite of 2020 SNF practice, not a named building’s log.
- Chapter 36 returns to construction (face, foam, stitch). This chapter is the year the file and the bottle argued.
