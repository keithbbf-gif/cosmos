# Hardware That Survives Med-Pass

*Educational history of healthcare and nursing-home furniture. Not a purchasing specification for a named building, not a survey opinion, and not clinical advice about beds, rails, or seating for a particular resident.*

The pull is missing. The wardrobe door has a pair of dirty screw holes and a dark oval where a plastic cup used to sit. An aide opens the door with two fingers in the gap, which is how laminate chips and how a resident who was supposed to reach a rod (chapter 21, F917) now needs help with a door that was meant to be the easy part. The med cart has already kissed this corner on a hundred passes. The bumper strip is cracked. The hinge still holds, in a tired way. Hardware is the furniture that is not in the photograph. The Medline-era book sold an oak grain. The wing lives on a hinge cup, a slide, a pull, and a screw that did not come from the factory.

This essay is about those small objects. Casegood skins (chapter 37) fail when water finds an edge. Hardware fails when a cart finds an edge, when a soft-close damper dies, when a shower chair’s rusted bolt (chapter 32) is asked to be a hinge. Purchasing rarely writes a BHMA number. Maintenance writes a work order that says “door off” and then hunts a screw that is almost the right length.

## Exposed, concealed, and the door that sags

European-style concealed hinges — the cup bored into the door, the plate on the cabinet side, the little screws that claim to adjust overlay — showed up on healthcare casegoods when kitchens had already normalized them. They look clean. They let a wardrobe door sit with a tight gap that photographs as quality. They also need a particleboard that has not swollen (chapter 37). Once the cup’s bore goes oval, the door sags. Adjustment buys a month. Then the door hits the frame, then it does not latch, then someone wedges a sock. F917’s functional furniture is that sock.

Exposed hinges — a piano hinge down a tall door, or a pair of institutional butts with exposed knuckles — take a cart better and look like a janitor’s closet. Homelike language (F584) does not love them. Maintenance does. A piano hinge distributes the load of a wardrobe door that holds, against the spec, a winter coat and a boxed fan. Concealed cups concentrate that load on a few millimeters of board. The Medilodge-era color board preferred the cups because the photograph preferred the cups. A multi-building buy in the 2000s could specify “residential overlay” on a wardrobe that faced a med-pass corridor. The overlay was a hinge choice pretending to be a finish choice.

Soft-close is a damper, a piston, a clip-on bit that selling language treated as a kindness. In a resident room it is also a failure mode. The damper dies and the door slams. The damper dies open and the door never quite shuts, which is how a roommate’s light becomes a fight and how a surveyor walking dignity can find a closet that will not stay closed. Soft-close slides on a nightstand drawer (chapter 20) fail the same way: a resident who can pull a drawer cannot always push a damper. F558’s accommodation talk is about seating, but the principle leaks. Hardware that requires a particular strength is furniture that has chosen a user.

Piano hinges rust in wet rooms. Shower benches and commodes (chapter 32) use bolts more than hinges, and those bolts rust first at the crossbrace. A rusted fastener is hardware. So is the replacement from the hardware-store blister pack that is not stainless and will rust faster. The factory’s sheet-metal screw and maintenance’s drywall screw are not interchangeable. The door that then strips is not a resident problem until it is. It is a thread problem first.

## Pulls, bumpers, the oval of dirt

Plastic cup pulls were cheap and kind to hips. They also snapped. A Medilodge-era wardrobe run could lose every third pull in five years, not because residents were violent but because a cart’s corner found the same height as the cup, over and over, at med pass. Metal bar pulls survive the cart and bruise the hip. Recessed pulls — a routed finger scoop — survive both and collect the same dirt the missing cup left as a ghost. Recessed scoops also demand a core that has not swollen. Once the scoop’s lip crumples, it becomes a sharp.

Screw-on pulls require a backer or a thick enough door. Thermofoil doors (chapter 37) do not love a through-bolt. The foil splits around the hole. The pull spins. Someone puts a dab of filler and a larger screw. The oval of dirt becomes an oval of filler. House-brand casegoods and name-brand healthcare houses fail this the same way when the door is a thin wrapped panel. Nemschoff-class lounge seating (chapter 29) is not immune; it simply hid the fastener better and charged the lobby for the privilege.

Bumper edges — PVC T-molding, glued strips, bolted corner guards — are hardware even when the catalog files them under “finish.” They are the med-pass spec. A cart (chapter 35) is a lockable dresser with its own bumper; the two bumpers meet and one of them loses. Beds with roller bumpers (chapter 38) are supposed to take the wall instead. They take the wardrobe when the caster does not swivel. Replacement bumper is sold by the foot at a shop that has never seen a punchout. The color does not match the 2005 oak. The new strip is a gray comma on a honey box. That comma is a finish era meeting a hardware era, the same mismatch as a teal chair beside a gray one (chapter 36).

Magnetic catches, roller catches, brass-colored tips that were already tired in 1998 — the wardrobe that will not stay shut is often a fifty-cent catch. The punchout did not sell catches as a line. It sold a door. Maintenance buys catches in bags. A door that slams after a soft-close death will also kill a catch. The sock returns.

## Slides, screws, the coffee can

Drawer slides are the nightstand story (chapter 20) in motion. Epoxy-coated 3/4-extension slides were the Medline-era default on a cheap box: they stick when the side panel swells, they rust in a room that shares a wet wall, they sag when the one drawer that holds the life is asked to hold too much life. Full-extension ball-bearing slides showed up when a package had money or when a maintenance man installed what he could get. Soft-close slides add the damper problem to the rust problem. A resident with a tremor can open a wooden drawer that an old runner has worn smooth and cannot manage a damper that wants a firm shove. That is not a care-plan sentence. It is a slide sentence.

Screws are the unwritten specification. Factory confirmation screws in a hinge plate are one length and one thread. Maintenance’s coffee can holds drywall screws, wood screws, a roofing screw, a stainless bolt from the last shower chair, and a stripped hex that will be reused. The wrong screw splits a thermofoil door, misses the insert in a particleboard side, or is long enough to come out the back into a wall that hides an outlet the bed spec also forgot (chapter 11). BHMA and ANSI A156 documents exist for people who write hardware schedules. A SNF wing writes a coffee can.

Inserts — brass or plastic threaded sleeves pressed into particleboard — are the grown-up answer to a hinge plate that would otherwise chew the core. They are also what a factory skips on a house-brand wardrobe. Once skipped, every adjustment of a concealed hinge is a little more oval. The door sags. The pull, if it still exists, hits the next door in a pair. Shared rooms steal hangers (chapter 21). They also steal the remaining pull, because a roommate’s visitor needed a handle for a suitcase. Composite hallway speech, not a named thief. The holes remain.

Shower hardware is a separate climate. Chrome that is not chrome, zinc that gives up, a plastic seat on a rusted frame, a commode lid bolt that has become one with the frame. Chapter 32 takes the wet-room object. The fastener is this chapter’s claim on it. Stainless specified and zinc delivered is a freight-and-claim story (chapter 42) as much as a hardware story. The dent on the dock is easy to photograph. The alloy that will rust in steam is not.

## What med-pass actually hits

Med-pass is a route. Cart, drawers, a pause at each door, a bumper height that is remarkably consistent because carts are remarkably consistent. Casegoods that face the corridor take that height as a belt of scars. Pulls in the belt disappear. Bumper in the belt cracks. Hinges above the belt live longer, which is why a tall wardrobe looks fine at the top and ruined at the hand. A charting alcove shelf (chapter 35) takes the same belt when a WOW parks too close. An overbed table (chapter 19) takes it on the column. Hardware is a height.

Renovation packages sometimes “upgrade hardware” as a line that sounds cheaper than replacing boxes. New pulls on old doors, new bumpers on swollen corners, new soft-close clips on cups whose bores are already oval. The photograph improves. The door still sags. Capital versus supply (chapter 41) will decide whether that line is a work order or a project. The coffee can does not care. It will fill the new pulls’ screws with the old mix.

Culture-change rooms that wanted a residential knob — a glass mushroom, a ceramic flower — learned that a knob is a med-pass target and a pocketable object. Memory-care specifications that already feared pocketable cues (chapter 31) went back to recessed scoops. The scoop is uglier and more honest. Ligature language stays in hospital psychiatry; the SNF fact is that a knob comes off and a scoop does not travel.

The residual object is the pair of holes at hand height. The grain around them is intact enough to photograph. The door opens by the gap. F917 still wants a closet the resident can use. A closet without a pull is a closet that has chosen an aide. The factory will sell another cup in a bag of twenty if the color still exists. Maintenance will use a screw from the can and a pull from a different oak. The cart will be back at 8 a.m. and at 8 p.m. Hardware that survives med-pass is hardware that was specified as a hit, or hardware that has already been replaced by a person who walks the belt. The next essay leaves the screw and opens two invoices for the same silhouette — capital on one, supply on the other, a threshold that was a facility habit and not a named operator’s law.

## Notes

- F917 / §483.90(e): functional furniture and an accessible closet; the tag does not name a hinge type or a pull.
- F584 / §483.10(i): homelike environment. Exposed institutional hinges can look “un-home” and still be the hardware that works.
- ANSI/BHMA A156 cabinet-hardware performance: factory cousin, not a CMS document. Do not invent a grade on a house-brand wardrobe.
- Soft-close, concealed cups, piano hinges, PVC bumpers: construction classes, not current SKUs.
- Composite maintenance (“the coffee can,” “the aide”) — no named living persons, no invented work-order numbers.
- Wet-room fasteners: see chapter 32 for the object; this chapter owns the rusted bolt as hardware.
