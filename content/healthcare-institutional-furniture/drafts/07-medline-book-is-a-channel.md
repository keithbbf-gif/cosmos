# The Medline Book Is a Channel

*Educational history of healthcare and nursing-home furniture. Not a purchasing specification for a named building, not a survey opinion, and not clinical advice about beds, rails, or seating for a particular resident.*

The 1979 Medline catalog cost about three hundred thousand dollars to produce. Company histories treat the glossy book as a turning object: a hospital-supply house that had been selling textiles and sundries deciding to look like a department store of care. A later punchout furniture page — MDS-class prefixes on a nightstand, a house-brand overbed table, a vinyl chair in the current chip — is the same decision in another decade. Medline is how a chair got a SKU. It is not how a hospital bed was invented. Batesville already existed (chapter 2). Gatch already existed (chapter 3). The book is a channel. Confusing the channel with the inventor is how a purchasing office starts to believe a distributor designed the room.

A. L. Mills opened a garment shop in 1910 — Northwestern Garment, meat-packing aprons for Chicago’s yards. In 1912 the line includes Mills Hospital Supply. Irving Mills continues the hospital-supply habit. The firm that Jim and Jon Mills found in 1966 as Medline, after a Cenco sale of the earlier business, is a second act with the same family memory: cloth, then sundries, then everything a hospital would reorder. Furniture arrives late in that everything. It arrives because a customer who already buys wipes will, if the book is thick enough, buy a chair from the same representative.

## From aprons to a binder

The 1910 shop is not a romance. Aprons are industrial textiles. Hospital supply, two years later, is still cloth and the kind of object that wears out on a schedule: gowns, underpads, the ancestors of the SKUs that still pay the distributor’s lights. Irving Mills’s years are the mid-century hospital as a repeat customer. Cenco’s ownership is a corporate interruption the family histories treat as a wound. 1966 is the refounding: Jim and Jon Mills, Medline as the new name. 1972, public. 1977, private again. Those dates matter to capital structure. They matter to furniture only because a private midwestern distributor that wants to own a relationship will print a book thick enough to keep the relationship indoors.

The 1979 glossy catalog is the furniture essay’s object even when the pages that year are still mostly not furniture. Three hundred thousand dollars, in the company-history telling, buys photography, paper, and the claim that Medline is a full-line house. A full-line house can add casegoods later without changing its self-description. 1984 is associated with the move toward Mundelein, Illinois — a campus instead of a Chicago address, warehouse logic instead of a garment loft. Drop-ship versus warehouse stock becomes a furniture fact: a bed that ships from a factory in another state, a chair that ships from Mundelein or a regional DC, a nightstand that arrives on a different truck than the mattress. Chapter 42 takes the dent. Chapter 7 stays with the page that made the trucks possible.

Making, for Medline-as-channel, is often not making. It is specifying a house brand that someone else welds. MDS-class prefixes on later furniture punchouts are badges. The actuator may be the same actuator in a Carroll-class frame. The vinyl may be the same vinyl on a name-brand chair. The difference is the claim desk, the price, the GPO tier, and the representative who already walks the Medilodge-era purchasing office for gloves. A writer who says Medline designed the first hospital bed is inventing a Batesville. A writer who says Medline taught a generation of Michigan buyers to order a room from a punchout is describing a channel habit.

The warehouse era after Mundelein made that habit physical. A carton of chairs can sit in a DC next to underpads. A bed cannot, not at volume, not in every finish. So the channel split its body: stock the small, drop-ship the large, print both on one invoice. Maintenance learned the split when a replacement caster for the drop-shipped bed had to be ordered from a factory the representative did not stock, while a replacement visitor chair arrived in three days with a different arm. The book presented one company. The dock presented two logistics. Chapter 23’s package price is this split wearing a smile.

Specifying through the book means the buyer sees a photograph and a column of attributes, not a shop drawing. Width, capacity, finish chip, “assist rail compatible,” a bleach sentence on the vinyl card. The photograph is a room that does not exist. The attributes are a mix of test language and hope. House brand and name brand sit on facing pages. The GPO contract may prefer one. The DON may prefer the other. Corporate purchasing prefers the one that punches out without a second quote. That preference is how a channel becomes a furniture style without ever being a factory town.

## Punchout, GPO, and the Medilodge-class desk

By the 1990s and 2000s the glossy book has a digital twin. Punchout catalogs inside a hospital or SNF purchasing system let a clerk click a nightstand the way they click a case of wipes. Group purchasing organizations put Medline-era contracts on the desk next to Cardinal- and Owens-class peers. Furniture is a strange passenger on those contracts. It is bulky, freight-sensitive, finish-sensitive, and slow to return. Wipes are none of those things. The channel’s power is that the same login buys both. The channel’s weakness is that a chair is not a wipe, and the return window will prove it.

A Medilodge-class Michigan operator — fifteen buildings, a corporate spec, a local exception — used the book the way a chain uses a book. Corporate picked a finish and a bed class. Buildings ordered replacements when something broke. The punchout did not know that the wing was still teal. It knew the current SKU. Drop-ship beds arrived on their own appointment. Warehouse chairs arrived on another. The package logic of chapter 23 is this chapter’s checkout: one price on a line, six trucks on the dock. Medline did not invent the package. It printed it.

Buying through a distributor also means the representative is a furniture person on Tuesdays and a textile person on Thursdays. That is not a slight. It is why the overbed table is treated as a consumable (chapter 19) and why the bed is treated as a capital exception or a supply exception depending on the threshold (chapter 41). The representative’s binder, later laptop, later portal, is the object that replaced Batesville’s traveling salesman and never replaced Batesville’s shop. Hill-Rom still sold beds. Invacare and Carroll and Joerns still sold beds. Medline sold *access* to beds, and then sold beds with its own prefix when access was not enough margin.

Wearing, through the channel, is a paperwork wear as much as a vinyl wear. A house-brand chair with a split welt goes back through Medline’s claim path. A name-brand chair on the same wing may go through a factory desk the distributor only forwards. Freight claims split three ways (chapter 42). The building that waits for an answer parks the dented carton in a hall. The book is silent about the hall. The book has a new photograph.

Do not collapse this into a villain story. A cold-start nursing home in the 2000s needed a way to furnish a hundred rooms without becoming a furniture expert. The channel did that. It also flattened difference: the same oak, the same teal, the same pendant hook, from Monroe to Muskegon. Culture-change buyers (chapter 6) who wanted a hearth still found themselves on a furniture page that had a hearth-shaped sofa in bleachable vinyl. Survey buyers (chapter 5) who wanted homelike found a drape and a picture frame in another section of the same portal. The channel is good at adjacency. It is not good at invention. Invention happened in Batesville, in Elyria, in Sheboygan seating shops, in a Tupelo plan. Medline happened in the login.

## What the furniture page is, and is not

A later punchout furniture page is worth describing as an object. Thumbnail. SKU. House-brand prefix or a national name. Finish drop-down: oak, cherry, mahogany, then the gray woodgrains. A sentence about CAL 117. A sentence about bleach 1:10 that is the vinyl mill’s sentence, not Medline’s research (chapter 10). Dimensions that may be deck or overall. A capacity number that is a test, not a census (chapter 43). “Ships in 3–4 weeks” next to “ships from DC.” The page looks like a store. It is a routing slip.

Making the house-brand object behind that page is a contractor’s job. Weld the frame, wrap the foam, staple the vinyl, band the TFL, drop the carton on a truck. Quality varies by year and by plant in ways a prefix cannot confess. Specifying from the page means trusting the prefix the way a grocery buyer trusts a store brand. Sometimes that trust is correct. Sometimes the actuator is orphaned in eighteen months. The channel will sell you the next prefix.

What the page will not say: Medline did not invent the hospital bed. Hillenbrand’s 1929 wood-and-metal deck and the 1950 motor claim are someone else’s shop. The Gatch joint is someone else’s geometry. The 2006 FDA zone drawing is a government document. The F-tags are CMS. The book cites none of them and does not need to. It needs a photograph and a price and a truck. A Medilodge-era purchasing clerk who thought the book *was* the industry was not foolish. The book had become the industry’s front door. Front doors are not factories.

The 1910 apron is still the honest ancestor. Mills made a textile that a workplace destroyed on a schedule and reordered. Hospital furniture, once it entered the binder, was asked to behave like that textile: standard, replaceable, punchable. Some of it can. Overbed tables, visitor chairs, plastic-liner nightstand drawers. Some of it cannot. A bed is a device. A wardrobe is a survey object. A dining chair is an F558 problem. The channel’s habit of treating them all as lines on a punchout is the habit this series keeps walking around. It is also why a Michigan wing can still be identified, in 2010, as a Medline-era wing without a single stencil saying so. The finishes agree. The SKUs rhyme. The inventor of the bed is not in the room.

## Residual prefix

Jim and Jon Mills’s 1966 name is now a national distributor-manufacturer. The 1979 catalog is a dated object. Mundelein is an address on a packing list. MDS-class prefixes still show up on furniture that has outlived the representative who keyed them. A Medilodge-class buyer used that channel because it was already on the phone for the rest of the building. That is the whole story and the only story that does not steal Batesville’s work.

The next essay leaves the catalog and sits in the purchasing office that had to use it: Washington Township, a corporate spec, a mismatched replacement chair, and a Michigan operator that is not Ciena.
