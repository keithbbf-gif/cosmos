# What the SNF Bed Spec Hides

*Educational history of healthcare and nursing-home furniture. Not a purchasing specification for a named building, not a survey opinion, and not clinical advice about beds, rails, or seating for a particular resident.*

The one-page spec looks complete. Electric hi-lo. Deck width 35 or 36 inches. Capacity 350 to 450 pounds on a standard frame — a class, `[VERIFY]` any model. Pendant. Assist-rail compatible. Battery backup, or a dash where the backup would be. Floor lock, or total-lock casters. A finish: oak insert. A photograph cropped above the outlet. A Medilodge-era purchasing clerk could key the SKU from that page and believe the room had been thought through. The mattress height is not on the page. The wall clearance when the head is up is not on the page. The outlet behind the nightstand is not on the page. Travel, capacity, and a rail sentence are not the same as the bed that arrived.

Typical SNF electrics in the Medline/Medilodge years were a class, not a showroom. Carroll-class and Invacare-class frames — Elyria in the Invacare story, Carroll as the bed line that sat on so many skilled-nursing decks — and Joerns-class frames in buildings that paid for a different spare-parts habit. Medline house-brand class with an MDS-prefix stencil when the number had to come down. Drive and GF Health as other catalog nouns. None of those names is a current shop link. They are the silhouettes a Michigan wing actually held. Hill-Rom was the ancestor argument (chapter 2), not the usual packing list. The spec page treated them as interchangeable if the inches rhymed. The floor did not.

## What the page is willing to say

Width first, because it is the honest number. A 35- or 36-inch deck is common on standard SNF electrics. That is the sleeping surface, not the outside of the rails, not the mattress if the mattress is a replacement. Expanded widths belong to chapter 13. The standard width is why a double room can hold two beds and a curtain and still fail a wheelchair turn. It is also why a 42-inch mattress on a 36-inch deck is a Zone 3 problem waiting for chapter 16. The spec says 36. It does not say what you will put on it.

Capacity next: often 350–450 lb on the standard frame, a test number, `[VERIFY]` by model year. Chapter 43 takes the faded sticker and the census. The spec uses the number as a comfort. Static versus dynamic is not on the page. Shared use is not on the page. A 400-lb tag on a bed that will be cranked, sat on the edge of, and hit by an overbed table is a lab sentence. Purchasing reads it as a census sentence. Those are different.

Controls: pendant versus siderail buttons, or both. A pendant is a thing that falls, gets wiped (chapter 10), gets trapped in a rail, gets a juice cup on it. Siderail controls are a thing a person can use without finding a cord, and a thing a confused hand can use at 2 a.m. Lockouts exist. The spec may say “pendant included.” It may not say where the holder is, or that the holder is a hook on a headboard that is also trying to look like furniture (chapter 17). Battery backup is a line that sounds like safety and means: this bed may still move when the outlet dies, for a while, if the battery was maintained. Many SNF frames in the house-brand class omitted it. The dash on the spec is a later 3 a.m.

Floor lock versus casters is a mobility sentence. Some frames drop a center lock. Some have total-lock casters at the corners. Some have a directional lock that no one understands (chapter 38). The spec says “locking casters” and the aide says the bed walked. Making the lock is a pedal and a linkage. Specifying it is a word. Wearing it is hair and tape in the wheel and a pedal that is not the pedal the last bed had.

IEC 60601-2-52 may appear as a compliance boast. It is a manufacturer and test-lab standard for medical beds. It is not a drawing of a Michigan double room. F917 wants a bed of proper size and height. Proper is not 36 inches. Assist-rail compatible is a sales phrase that means sockets or channels exist. It does not mean the rail you already own will fit, or that the 2006 FDA zones will close when the mattress is from another book (chapter 14, chapter 15, chapter 16). The page is willing to say compatible. Compatible is not a measurement.

## What the page will not say

Mattress height is the first omission that changes the room. Deck height is the frame. Top-of-mattress is deck plus foam, air, overlay, or a low-air-loss rented surface. A hi-lo sold on a 7-to-30-inch deck neighborhood `[VERIFY travel]` becomes a different transfer when the mattress is seven inches of replacement foam. The 2006 FDA guidance treats the mattress as part of the system. The Medline-era spec often treated the mattress as a second line, a second vendor, a second truck. Channel habit: bed from one page, mattress from another. The gap you can put a fist in is chapter 16. The spec’s completeness is the fist’s parent.

Wall clearance is the second. Head up, the deck shortens in plan or the headboard arcs toward the wall. A frame that needs four inches behind it will not get four inches if the nightstand and the outlet and the overbed table have already used the box. Crash marks on cream paint (chapter 9) are the record. Making a bed that articulates in a small room is a shop problem Batesville knew. Specifying “residential headboard” without a plan dimension is a punchout problem. Buying two beds for a double without measuring the remaining aisle is a Medilodge-era Tuesday.

The outlet behind the nightstand is the third. Electric beds need a receptacle. Battery backup, if present, still wants a charge. The nightstand wants to sit where the receptacle is, because that is where the lamp sat in the photograph. Cords go under casters. EVS mops around a tangle. Maintenance gets a call that the bed is dead and the outlet is live. The spec photograph cropped above this. The packing list did not include a relocation of the receptacle. Construction punch would have. Supply punchout does not (chapter 41).

Other omissions sit in the same family. Pendant length. Whether the emergency crank — if the model still has one — is on the frame or in a bag. Whether the side-rail sockets take the half-rails in the storeroom. Whether the floor lock pedal is on the same side as the last bed, which is the side staff already learned. Whether the finish insert is the oak on the color board or the oak the factory had. Whether the capacity tag will still be readable after the first year of wipes. A one-page spec cannot hold these. A one-page spec that looks complete is doing a different job: it is getting a PO through a threshold.

## Buying a class, wearing a room

A Medilodge-class buyer specified a class: Carroll/Invacare-class electric, oak insert, 36-inch, 450-lb neighborhood, pendant. Buildings received what the GPO and the warehouse had. House-brand substitutions happened without a meeting. The class is real. The substitution is real. The room is where they stop being the same. Therapy, as a buyer seat, may have asked for a high-high for transfers and been given a low-low because the fall program was louder that quarter (chapter 12). Maintenance asked for the actuator they still had parts for. Corporate asked for the price. The spec page smiled at all of them.

Making the later house-brand frame is a contractor weld with a badge (chapter 7). The travel may match the name-brand neighborhood. The pendant connector may not. Wearing starts when the first pendant dies and the spare in the bin is for last year’s prefix. Specifying “same as last time” on a punchout that has refreshed the SKU is how a wing gets two control dialects. Staff learn both. Residents learn neither if the lockout is on.

Freight hides on the spec as “ships in 3–4 weeks.” What arrives may need assembly: headboard insert, rails in a second carton, a mattress that was not on this truck. The bed that looks complete on the page is three appointments on a Michigan dock. Concealed damage on the insert is a finish problem that does not void the travel number. The dented headboard still goes to the low click. The spec is unhurt.

Making a one-page sheet is a marketing job: crop, rhyme the inches, put oak in the finish line. Specifying from it is a clerical job: key the SKU, hope the GPO price holds. Buying the class is a chain job: fifteen buildings, one silhouette. Wearing the omissions is a room job. The aide who cannot plug the bed in because the nightstand has the only receptacle is doing the missing line. The maintenance worker who drills a new pendant hook because the headboard insert has none is doing the missing line. The surveyor who writes that the bed is not a proper height because last year’s mattress is still on this year’s low-low is doing the missing line. F917 does not read punchout pages. It reads the box.

Width’s cousin, length, hides too. Extended decks for taller residents exist as a class and rarely as a double-room plan. A 80-inch sleep surface in a room drawn for 76 inches puts the footboard on the heat register or the door swing. The spec may list length. The photograph will not show the register. Bariatric widths (chapter 13) make the same omission louder: the door, the elevator, the roommate. Standard 36-inch frames hide it because they almost fit. Almost is the SNF’s favorite dimension. The page prints a clean number. The room prints a scrape.

What a surveyor can see that the spec cannot: a bed of improper height because the mattress is wrong; a bed that cannot be reached because the nightstand ate the aisle; a pendant the resident cannot use; a rail that is a restraint conversation (chapter 15) hanging on “compatible” sockets. F917’s “proper” and “functional” are the after-the-fact spec. They are not inches. The one-page sheet had inches and still hid the room.

## Residual outlet

The complete-looking page is not a lie exactly. The frame may well be 36 inches, 400 pounds, electric, oak. Those numbers can be true and the bed can still be the wrong object for the box it entered. Nightingale specified air around a platform. Batesville specified wood on a moving deck. IEC specified a lab path. Medline specified a SKU. The Medilodge-era clerk specified a class. The outlet behind the nightstand specified nothing and still ran the night.

A reader who has stood in that room knows the next omission before the next chapter names it: the low click. The spec’s travel range includes a number that looks like a fall program. The program includes a person who cannot get up. The sheet does not. Chapter 12 puts the bed at its lowest click and stays there.

## Notes

- SNF electric bed *classes* (Carroll / Invacare-class, Joerns-class, Medline house-brand class, Drive / GF as catalog nouns): channel silhouettes, not current SKUs or shop links.
- Deck width 35–36 inches: common standard class. Capacity 350–450 lb: typical catalog neighborhood. `[VERIFY]` model and year. Deck ≠ mattress top.
- Pendant vs siderail controls, battery backup, floor lock vs casters: features that appear as words on a page and as habits on a floor.
- FDA 10 March 2006 guidance: mattress is in the system. IEC 60601-2-52: lab standard. F917: proper size and height — survey, not a takeoff.
- Outlet, wall clearance, mattress height: composite omissions of Medline/Medilodge-era one-page specs, not a named PO.
