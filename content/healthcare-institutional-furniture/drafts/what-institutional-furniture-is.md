# What institutional furniture is

Institutional furniture is not a style. It is a **job**.

A house chair has one body, most nights, and a floor that does not get a bleach wipe on a schedule. A nursing-home chair has a hundred bodies in a year, a gait belt, a slide board, a hip that does not bend the way the seat expects, and a night nurse who will move the thing with one hand because the other hand is carrying a basin. If your definition of furniture starts with taste, you will specify the wrong object and then blame the residents for wrecking it.

I use “institutional” the way the bunk-bed rule uses it — as a habitat, not as an insult. Prisons, barracks, hospitals, and skilled-nursing floors all buy density, maintenance, and a SKU they can replace. The costume changes. The job does not.

## Three tests that still work

I walk a piece with three questions. They are shop questions. They are not a standard.

**Who else has to use it besides the person sitting or lying on it?** If the answer is “a CNA, a therapist, EVS, and a midnight crash cart,” you are in the institution. A residential nightstand does not have to clear a Hoyer or take a knee when someone drops.

**What chemistry is allowed on it?** If the answer is a quat, a bleach wipe, and whatever the infection-control binder added after the last outbreak, the finish is part of the structure. A conversion varnish that prints a pretty sample and then blushes under bleach is a residential finish in a costume.

**What happens when it fails at 2 a.m.?** If the failure mode is “call the mill in the morning,” you specified a house piece. If the failure mode is a fall, an entrapment, a missing med pass because the overbed will not roll, or a survey tag because the closet rod is a rumor, you specified an institution and then forgot.

Those three questions will sort a room faster than a brand name.

## The costume problem

After OBRA 1987, the American nursing home was told to look like a house. CMS still says the intent of “homelike” is an environment as close to a private home as possible. Appendix PP lists, among the institutional leftovers a facility should work off, furniture that does not reflect a home-like environment or is uncomfortable.

That sentence did honest work. It also created a market for **cherry print**.

A laminate that photographs like a Pennsylvania highboy and machines like a lab bench is not a lie if you say what it is. It becomes a lie when the catalog calls the print “residential oak” and the buyer thinks they have specified dignity. Dignity, in the furniture tags, is a chair you can stand up from, a closet you can reach, a place for a clock and a picture, and clothing that stays clean and unwrinkled. It is not a cathedral arch on a headboard that still has a four-inch gap at the mattress.

The Medline-era catalog was very good at costume. That was not malice. A punchout page has four inches and a JPEG. The mill still has to decide whether the drawer box is sealed, whether the back is finished because the piece will float, whether the glides will mark a vinyl floor. If you only read the JPEG you will buy a costume.

## Lineage, not branding

A short lineage, so the later drafts have a floor.

Hospital furniture as an American factory product is a twentieth-century object. Hill-Rom’s Batesville story starts in 1929 with a wood hospital bed. The overbed table, the bedside cabinet with a cup holder stamped in the top, the cubicle curtain on a track — those are ward inventions. They assume a bed that moves, a staff that works both sides, and a roommate.

The nursing home inherited the ward and then lived long enough to be embarrassed by it. Private rooms, “neighborhoods,” country kitchens, and memory-care wings are later costumes over the same job: many bodies, fixed walls, a budget, a survey.

Assisted living inherited the hotel and then discovered it had nursing-home hips. That is why an ALF lobby can look like a Marriott and still need a chair with a 20-inch seat and arms you can push on. The residential lie gets its own draft.

Medline, founded in 1966 by Jim and Jon Mills out of a Chicago garment-and-hospital-supply family that goes back to 1910, is not a furniture factory in the sense Batesville is. It is a **face**: manufacturer and distributor, punchout and truck, the binder the DON already has open for gloves. When furniture rode that face, a mill in Arkansas or a metal shop in Indiana became a line item next to underpads. That is a channel fact, not a compliment and not a slur.

## What the object is made of when it is honest

Honest institutional case goods are usually one of four constructions. I am speaking as a mill, not as a materials-science paper.

1. **Particleboard or MDF core, laminate faces, PVC or hardwood edge.** Cheap, stable, wipeable, and ugly when the edge peels. The peel is the tell. Once moisture gets in, the core swells and the piece is done.

2. **Hardwood plywood or solid hardwood, conversion or 2K finish, sealed interiors.** Heavier, repairable, more honest about dents. Bleach is still a negotiation. The bleach-and-hardwood draft sits with that fight.

3. **Polymer or urethane “wood.”** Kwalu-class and its cousins. The grain is a mold. The corner does not dent the way maple dents. Infection control loves it. A woodworker can dislike it and still admit why it won day rooms.

4. **Steel and powdercoat, with a wood or laminate top if anyone still cares.** Overbeds, med carts, food-service tables. The institution showing its metal.

Upholstery is a fifth construction pretending to be a fabric choice. Healthcare vinyl, polyurethane, Crypton-class woven, and the old wet-look Naugahyde are different animals. The cover is only half the chair. Foam density, moisture barrier, and whether the deck can be wiped after a void matter more than the swatch.

None of those four-plus-one is “better” in the abstract. A memory-care dining chair in molded urethane can be the right object. A solid-maple bedside in a private-pay suite can be the right object. The sin is specifying one with the language of the other.

## Weight, height, and the body that changed

Institutional furniture carries numbers that residential furniture treats as marketing.

Seat height is not a vibe. A 17-inch seat that looked “lounge” in a catalog is a trap for a person who needs 19 to 21 inches and a firm front edge to stand. Arm height and arm shape decide whether sit-to-stand is possible. Seat depth that is honest for a 5-foot-2 woman is wrong for a 6-foot man; a day room that buys one SKU has already chosen who will slump.

Weight ratings are not decorations. A 250-pound chair in a building that has bariatric rooms is a future incident. The bariatric draft will not pretend a + on a catalog page is a test.

Bed height is a clinical number wearing a furniture costume. Low beds and floor mats are a fall program, not a style. Rails are a geometry problem that CMS and FDA refuse to let you solve with a word like “assist.”

## What I refuse to call institutional

I will not call a conference table in an administrator’s office institutional because it is in a nursing home. That is just office furniture that happens to share a parking lot.

I will not call a resident’s own recliner institutional because it rolled in from a daughter’s garage. Personal belongings are in the regulation on purpose. The facility still has to think about tip, flame, and whether the piece blocks a fire exit. That is a different essay than “we specified the wrong chair.”

I will not call a hospital-grade overbed a dining table because someone ate on it. Use is not typology. The overbed’s job is a rolling surface at variable height over a mattress. When you ask it to be a dining program, you get crumbs in the column and a base that fights wheelchairs.

## A working definition for the rest of the series

Institutional furniture, in this folder, is furniture whose primary duty is to serve **bodies in a regulated occupancy**, under **staff handling** and **scheduled chemistry**, at a **density** a house does not run, with a **failure mode** that can become a survey tag, a fall, or an entrapment.

The costume may be oak. The job is still the job.

If you can hold that definition, the later drafts will not surprise you. If you cannot, start with a chair in a hallway that has no business being in a hallway, and ask the three tests. The hallway will answer.
