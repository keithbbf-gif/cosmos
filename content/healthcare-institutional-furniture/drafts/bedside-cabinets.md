# Bedside cabinets

The bedside is the object I have built the most of, and the object people notice the least until the drawer falls out.

It is not a bedroom nightstand. A bedroom nightstand is a lamp stand with a novel in it. A SNF bedside is a **staff workstation the resident also owns**. Gloves, wipes, a basin, a cup, a hearing-aid dish, a Bible, a wallet if the lock story has not gone bad, a phone charger fighting the bed’s plug. If you specify it like a bungalow bedroom, the aisle will teach you.

## History: ward locker to “homelike” box

The ancestor is a hospital cabinet: metal or a hard laminate, shallow, a top for a basin, a drawer that could take a leak. Ward geometry made it tall and thin because the aisle was the real room. Hill-Burton supply made that cabinet a set with the bed and the overbed. When Medicare and Medicaid paid for nursing-home beds, the same cabinet walked into a residential zip code.

OBRA 1987 did not specify a nightstand. It specified a homelike environment and, in the living CFR, functional furniture and a place for personal effects. Appendix PP’s F917 language — a place for pictures and a bedside clock, furniture for the resident and a visitor — is why a mill could sell wood into a medical distributor. The industry heard a finish. We heard a box that had to wipe and still hold a clock.

Medline-era packages put a wooden bedside next to a metal bed and called the room a bedroom. The box was still a staff workstation. The top was still a spill plane. The depth was still an aisle argument in an **80**-square-foot double or a **100**-square-foot single. Culture change wanted a lamp and a photograph. EVS wanted a sealed interior. Both were right. The catalog usually photographed the lamp.

I have built these boxes for houses on our public list — Montrose, Searcy, Pearland, the Arkansas names — and I will not invent a room count or a PO. I will say the object did not change its job when the crown got a cathedral arch. The job is leftover, leak, lock, and a height that still meets a hand when the deck moves.

## Shop geometry: leftover first

Shop ranges I will own: **18–22 inches deep, 20–24 inches wide**, height set to a seated reach from the bed, not to a designer’s lamp. Taller than a bungalow nightstand is common because storage has nowhere else to go. Deeper than 22 is how you steal the working side in a double.

The square-footage draft is the why. This draft is the object.

A double that gets two 24-by-24 residentials has already lost the lift. A single that gets a tiny hospital cabinet in a 12-by-12 room has lost the clock surface F917 is imagining.

Measure the leftover. Then pick a width. Do not pick a JPEG.

Height is a moving target if the house has a low-bed program. A bedside that matches a 10 a.m. boost is a cliff at 10 p.m. when the mattress is near the floor. The low-bed draft already said this. Here the shop consequence is: write the height against a named deck and a named mattress, or write two heights and admit you cannot serve both with one box. A gallery rail on the top — a tiny wood fence — is a snag and a climb. I have refused those rails. I will refuse them again.

Pulls are rungs if you look at them from the floor in a rail-free room. Full-extension drawers are stairs. Three-quarter drawers are uglier to use and harder to stand on. There is no free choice. Write the room.

Corners: radiused. A sharp laminate corner at hip height is a bruise factory. Soft edges photograph less “architectural.” Soft edges are why people can move in the leftover. In an 80-square-foot double the leftover is a strip. The strip does not forgive a square corner and a 24-inch depth.

Setback from the bed matters for Zone 6 and for staff hips. A box that kisses a rail is a hanger and a pinch. A box that sits so far away the resident cannot reach a cup is a staff cabinet. Measure the reach from a seated position on the mattress that is actually on the deck.

## The box is the specification

A drawer that is a staple-and-hope box will swell, stink, and fail. A drawer that is a sealed box — plywood or a decent composite, finished inside, a bottom that can take a leak — will live.

I care about this more than I care about the crown. EVS will pour. A cup will tip. A void will happen. If the interior is raw particleboard, the piece is done and the smell will outlast the resident.

Guides: full-extension is kind to a seated person and kind to a climb. Three-quarter is uglier to use and harder to stand on. In a rail-free low-bed room I have preferred drawers that do not become stairs. In a room with a person who lives in the drawer I have preferred full-extension. Write the room, not the hardware brand.

The top is a spill plane. A raised edge is honest. A splash of caulk is a maintenance confession. A leather-look pad is a sponge. Cup holders and recessed dishes are hospital leftovers that still earn their keep. A perfectly flat pretty top is a dining-room idea. High gloss in a west room that lives in the **71 to 81 °F** band is glare. F584 already knows how to write glare.

A back panel that is a staple-on afterthought is how a charger cord disappears and a void soaks the wall. Finish the back if the box will sit off the wall. Finish the interior if the box will hold a cup. Those two sentences are more of a specification than a species name.

## Locks are a dignity machine

F917 wants clothing and effects protected from casual access by others. It also wants the resident to use their things. A lock only staff can open is a staff cabinet. A lock the resident cannot work is a taunt. A lock with a key on a string around a neck is a 1998 story.

I have built lockable drawers. I have also left them off. The conversation belongs to the DON and the resident, not to a mill default. What I will not do is hide the lock on the drawing so it becomes a surprise on the punch list.

If the house’s theft story is a roommate, separate the wardrobes and stop using the bedside as a vault. If the theft story is a visitor, the lock will not save you. If the theft story is staff, you have a different problem than hardware.

Memory-care locks are a care plan. Hidden latches that treat the person as the problem are how you fail dignity while passing a punchout checkbox. Write who holds the key.

## Which side of the bed

The working side is for staff until nursing says otherwise. The pretty bedside on the working side is a mill’s gift to a future complaint.

In a double, one bedside per person, on the wall side if the working side must stay clear. Families will hate the asymmetry. The lift will not.

In a private, you can have two if the leftover exists. Two is not a moral improvement if the second one blocks the toilet door.

Outlets are part of this paragraph. A bedside that owns the only receptacle the bed wants is a fire and a function problem. The headwall draft will sit with devices. Here: do not park a 22-inch box in front of the plug and then blame the electrician.

## Channel notes

The Medline punchout loved a three-drawer stack because it photographed as storage. Three drawers in 22 inches of height are pencil drawers. A person with arthritic hands will not use three pencil drawers. A staff member will jam them.

A one-drawer-over-open or one-drawer-over-door is the adult version. The open is for basin and shoes. The door is for the things that should not be on display. The drawer is for the clock and the glasses.

Special-buy runs for a chain — the Medilodge-era pattern — froze a drawing so the warehouse could stock a pull. That sameness is a virtue if the drawing was measured in a leftover. It is a vice if the drawing was measured in a showroom.

Bradley Brand’s public list is full of buildings that wanted the wood to look like a house and wipe like a lab. The bedside is where that fight is most local. You cannot hide a failed drawer in a lobby. Arkansas hardwood can be recut and refinished if the box was sealed. A swollen particleboard core cannot. The bleach draft is the chemistry. This draft is the leak.

Freight crushes corners. Specify a radius that can take a crush, or a carton that is real. Install crews will slide the box into a leftover that shrank when the mat arrived. If the drawing never showed the mat, the install is the first honest measurement.

## What to look for on a used piece, and on a live floor

Pull the drawer. Smell it. Look at the bottom for swell. Look at the back for a shop mark or a distributor sticker. Look at the top for a burn from a lamp that was never specified. Look at the side for a crash from a bed rail.

If the box is honest and the top can be refinished, the piece can live. If the box is soup, stop. A pretty crown on soup is how a channel sells a replacement that will fail the same way.

On a live floor: can a seated person on tonight’s mattress reach a cup? Is the box on the working side? Does a pull read as a rung from the vinyl? Does the drawer become a stair? Is there a lock, and who holds the key? Is there a clock surface that is not the overbed? Does the depth leave a lift path in an 80-square-foot double?

Sit on the floor if the room is rail-free and low. The bedside will tell you whether it is furniture or a wooden rail.
