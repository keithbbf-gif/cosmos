# Bedside cabinets

The bedside is the object I have built the most of, and the object people notice the least until the drawer falls out.

It is not a bedroom nightstand. A bedroom nightstand is a lamp stand with a novel in it. A SNF bedside is a **staff workstation the resident also owns**. Gloves, wipes, a basin, a cup, a hearing-aid dish, a Bible, a wallet if the lock story has not gone bad, a phone charger fighting the bed’s plug. If you specify it like a bungalow bedroom, the aisle will teach you.

## Size is leftover, not taste

Shop ranges I will own: **18–22 inches deep, 20–24 inches wide**, height set to a seated reach from the bed, not to a designer’s lamp. Taller than a bungalow nightstand is common because storage has nowhere else to go. Deeper than 22 is how you steal the working side in a double.

The square-footage draft is the why. This draft is the object.

A double that gets two 24-by-24 residentials has already lost the lift. A single that gets a tiny hospital cabinet in a 12-by-12 room has lost the clock surface F917 is imagining.

Measure the leftover. Then pick a width. Do not pick a JPEG.

## The box is the specification

A drawer that is a staple-and-hope box will swell, stink, and fail. A drawer that is a sealed box — plywood or a decent composite, finished inside, a bottom that can take a leak — will live.

I care about this more than I care about the crown. EVS will pour. A cup will tip. A void will happen. If the interior is raw particleboard, the piece is done and the smell will outlast the resident.

Guides: full-extension is kind to a seated person and kind to a climb. Three-quarter is uglier to use and harder to stand on. There is no free choice. In a rail-free low-bed room I have preferred drawers that do not become stairs. In a room with a person who lives in the drawer I have preferred full-extension. Write the room, not the hardware brand.

## Locks are a dignity machine

F917 wants clothing and effects protected from casual access by others. It also wants the resident to use their things. A lock only staff can open is a staff cabinet. A lock the resident cannot work is a taunt. A lock with a key on a string around a neck is a 1998 story.

I have built lockable drawers. I have also left them off. The conversation belongs to the DON and the resident, not to a mill default. What I will not do is hide the lock on the drawing so it becomes a surprise on the punch list.

If the house’s theft story is a roommate, separate the wardrobes and stop using the bedside as a vault. If the theft story is a visitor, the lock will not save you. If the theft story is staff, you have a different problem than hardware.

## Tops, edges, and the cup

The top is a spill plane. A raised edge is honest. A splash of caulk is a maintenance confession. A leather-look pad is a sponge.

Cup holders and recessed dishes are hospital leftovers that still earn their keep. A perfectly flat pretty top is a dining-room idea.

Corners: radiused. A sharp laminate corner at hip height is a bruise factory. Soft edges photograph less “architectural.” Soft edges are why people can move in the leftover.

## Which side of the bed

The working side is for staff until nursing says otherwise. The pretty bedside on the working side is a mill’s gift to a future complaint.

In a double, one bedside per person, on the wall side if the working side must stay clear. Families will hate the asymmetry. The lift will not.

In a private, you can have two if the leftover exists. Two is not a moral improvement if the second one blocks the toilet door.

## Channel notes

The Medline punchout loved a three-drawer stack because it photographed as storage. Three drawers in 22 inches of height are pencil drawers. A person with arthritic hands will not use three pencil drawers. A staff member will jam them.

A one-drawer-over-open or one-drawer-over-door is the adult version. The open is for basin and shoes. The door is for the things that should not be on display. The drawer is for the clock and the glasses.

Special-buy runs for a chain — the Medilodge-era pattern — froze a drawing so the warehouse could stock a pull. That sameness is a virtue if the drawing was measured in a leftover. It is a vice if the drawing was measured in a showroom.

Bradley Brand’s public list is full of buildings that wanted the wood to look like a house and wipe like a lab. The bedside is where that fight is most local. You cannot hide a failed drawer in a lobby.

## What I look for on a used piece

Pull the drawer. Smell it. Look at the bottom for swell. Look at the back for a shop mark or a distributor sticker. Look at the top for a burn from a lamp that was never specified. Look at the side for a crash from a bed rail.

If the box is honest and the top can be refinished, the piece can live. If the box is soup, stop. A pretty crown on soup is how a channel sells a replacement that will fail the same way.
