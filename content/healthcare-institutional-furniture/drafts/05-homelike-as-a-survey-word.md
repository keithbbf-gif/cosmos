# Homelike as a Survey Word

*Educational history of healthcare and nursing-home furniture. Not a purchasing specification for a named building, not a survey opinion, and not clinical advice about beds, rails, or seating for a particular resident.*

The surveyor is looking at a chair. Not sitting in it. Looking. The seat is mauve vinyl, or teal, or a later gray that still wipes. The legs are oak-look laminate or powder-coated steel. There may be arms. There may not. Appendix PP has given this person a word to carry into the room: *homelike*. The word can ding a stack of identical vinyl chairs that “do not reflect home.” It can also pass an oak-laminate nightstand that never lived in anyone’s house. Homelike is a CMS word. It is not a finish color. The chair does not know that. Purchasing sometimes does not either.

§483.10(i) asks for an environment that is safe, clean, comfortable, and homelike. F584 is the current tag. The interpretive examples that travel with the tag — furniture that does not look like home, missing drapes, missing personal items — are how a regulation about atmosphere becomes a furniture argument. They are still not a catalog. A Medilodge-era Michigan wing that hung floral drapes and put a framed print over a honey-oak box was answering the examples. A wing that bought the same box and called the vinyl “residential” was answering the distributor. Both could meet a surveyor who had six minutes in the room. Neither is a proof that the statute of 1987 specified oak.

## What the tag is asking the room to hold

Safe, clean, comfortable, homelike: four adjectives that are not the same job. Safe is the caster lock, the rail assessment, the wet-floor chair that should not be in the shower (chapter 32). Clean is EVS and the vinyl card (chapter 10, chapter 36). Comfortable is closer to F558 — seat height, depth, a firmness a person can leave, arms that help a resident stand. Homelike is the leftover adjective, the one that sounds like décor and behaves like a citation when the room is only a box.

Surveyor guidance has long used ordinary pictures: a room that looks like a waiting room; furniture that is obviously institutional and nothing else; a window without drapes; a dresser without a photograph. Personal items are part of the tag’s imagination of home. Furniture is the other part. The trap is to treat the two as interchangeable. A family photograph on a laminate top does not make the laminate a sideboard. A missing photograph does not make a decent armchair a deficiency by itself. The tag is about the *environment*. Purchasing, under time, reduces environment to a color board.

F558, accommodation of needs and preferences, is the furniture tag people forget when they argue about oak. Seat height. Seat depth. Arms. A chair a person can use, not a chair a corridor can store. Appendix PP language here is closer to a spec than F584 ever is. It still refuses inches. Typical dining seats wander around 18 inches; sit-to-stand hopes wander 19–20 `[VERIFY as ranges]`. A surveyor can notice that every chair in the dining room is a stacker without arms. A surveyor can also walk past a good armchair in a private room and write up the drapes. The tags do not rank themselves for the furniture buyer. The buyer has to.

F917 / §483.90(e) — older F461 — is the room’s hardware list: a bed of proper size and height, a mattress, functional furniture, an individual closet with racks and shelves a resident can reach. Functional is doing a lot of work. A nightstand with a swollen corner (chapter 37) is still a nightstand until the drawer will not open. A wardrobe whose rod sits at 66 inches over a wheelchair is a closet that fails the reach the tag imagines (chapter 21). Homelike is not on that list. Proper and functional are. The oak grain on the wardrobe door is extra. It is also what corporate sent in the package because someone thought extra was the tag.

Making a “homelike” object, in the factory sense, meant changing the skin. Wood-look TFL on particleboard. A headboard insert on a medical frame. Vinyl printed with a small floral. Thermofoil wrapped around a profile edge until the edge peeled. The joints stayed institutional: a bumper edge, a plastic pull, a caster. Specifying meant a corporate sheet that said *residential finish, oak, mauve accent* and a punchout that had those chips in stock. The making and the specifying agreed on color. They did not agree on home.

What a factory could not make, and a surveyor could still ask for, was the rest of the environment. A drape requires a track or a rod and a flame rating and someone who will rehang it after a stain. A personal item requires a surface that is not already full of a water pitcher, a meal ticket, and a half-used barrier cream. Functional furniture, in F917’s sense, is the cabinet that still opens after the cart has hit it. Homelike furniture, in F584’s sense, is that cabinet plus a reason to look at it. The two tags share a room and do not share a purchase order. Capital buys the cabinet. The family brings the photograph. EVS moves the photograph. The surveyor notices the empty top. Purchasing, asked to “fix homelike,” orders another cabinet in a newer grain.

Therapy’s version of the same word is blunter. Homelike seating that a person cannot leave is not accommodation. A wing that passed F584 on drapes and failed a sit-to-stand conversation in the dining room has not misunderstood CMS. It has ranked the adjectives. Comfortable and homelike are neighbors in §483.10(i). They are not synonyms. A soft sofa photographs as home and behaves as a trap (chapter 28). A firm armchair with arms photographs as a waiting room and behaves as a way to stand. The tag will not pick for you. A Medilodge-era color board almost always picked the photograph.

## Buying the word, wearing the ding

A Medilodge-class operator buying for fifteen buildings needed *homelike* to be repeatable. Repeatable is the enemy of home and the friend of survey consistency. Corporate issued a color board. Buildings already had what they had: a 1990s teal recliner in one day room, a 2005 honey-oak nightstand in another, a cream wall that made both of them look like a motel. The replacement chair that arrived from the book was the current chip, not the old one. The surveyor, looking at the chair, saw a mismatch that the tag does not name and a vinyl that the tag might. Chapter 8 takes that purchasing mess as a subject. Chapter 9 takes the chips. Here the subject is the word that licensed both.

Who walks in with the surveyor matters. An administrator hears F584 as a décor problem and orders drapes. A DON hears F558 as a therapy problem and wants arms. Maintenance hears F917 as a work-order problem and fixes the rod with a lower closet pole that was not in the package. Therapy, invited or not, will say the lounge sofa is too soft to stand from (chapter 28). Corporate purchasing hears all of that as noise around a GPO price. The Medline book had pages for each hearing. It did not have a page titled Homelike. It had *resident room collections* and *healthcare seating* and a photograph of a room that had never been surveyed.

Wearing is how the word ages. Drapes fade and get taken down for a stain and not put back. Personal items go into a drawer when a roommate is admitted or when EVS needs the top. The oak-look box stays. The mauve chair cracks at the welt. A later gray woodgrain replacement arrives and the room now has two centuries of “home” in one box. Surveyors who write that furniture does not reflect home are sometimes looking at that pile. They are not always wrong. They are also not looking at a finish that would have saved the citation. A nicer laminate on the same box is still a box.

Hospitals have a different relationship to the word. A medical-surgical private room can be homelike in the brochure and still be a headwall and a recliner. SNFs were asked to be homelike as a condition of remaining what they are: long-stay rooms where people live. That is a harder ask of a double-loaded corridor. Culture-change programs (chapter 6) tried to change the corridor. Purchasing tried to change the chip. The tag sat between them, available to both, owned by neither.

The 2022–23 Appendix PP revisions did not invent F584 or F917. They restated and renumbered a habit. Writers who treat a 2023 tag number as a new furniture law are dating a reprint. The word was already in the room. The chair was already mauve or oak or both. What changed, when it changed, was how often a survey team had been trained to look at drapes versus how often it looked at seat height. Training is not a statute. It is why two buildings with the same nightstand could have different letters.

## Residual oak

Homelike can ding a mauve vinyl stacker and still accept an oak-laminate box because the tag is not a taste test with a published palette. It is an environment test with examples. The stacker fails the example about furniture that does not reflect home when there is nothing else in the room — no drapes, no objects, no variation. The box passes when it sits under a lamp and a photograph and a window treatment, even if the box is the same particleboard the corridor has always had. That is not hypocrisy in the manual. It is the manual admitting it cannot specify a chair.

A Medilodge-era room that “passed” homelike was often a room that had been dressed. The furniture underneath was still the hospital deck, the motel dresser, the waiting-room chair. Nightingale wanted air. Batesville wanted wood on a deck. OBRA wanted rights. CMS wanted an adjective. The Medline book wanted a SKU that could be repeated down a wing so that the adjective would photograph. The surveyor looking at the chair is the only person in that chain who has to decide, in a particular minute, whether the photograph worked.

The decision will not be a finish chip. It may be a missing drape. It may be a chair without arms that a person cannot leave. It may be a closet rod no one can reach. Those are furniture facts that happen to live under a soft word. Keep the word soft in the essay and hard in the room. The next chapter walks to the end of the corridor, where a living-room grouping has been arranged to look like Eden, and the corridor is still a corridor.

## Notes

- 42 CFR §483.10(i) / F584: safe, clean, comfortable, homelike environment. Interpretive examples (institutional furniture, missing drapes, missing personal items) are survey guidance, not a finish schedule.
- 42 CFR §483.10(e)(3) / F558: reasonable accommodation, including seating geometry (height, depth, arms). Inches in this essay are ranges. `[VERIFY]` model-specific seats.
- 42 CFR §483.90(e) / F917 (older F461): bed of proper size and height, mattress, functional furniture, individual closet. 2022–23 Appendix PP revisions restated numbering; they did not invent the room list.
- Homelike is not a color. Post-OBRA oak laminate and mauve/teal vinyl are fashion answers (chapter 9), not statutory ones.
- Medline/Medilodge-era dressed rooms: composite of channel practice, not a named survey letter.
