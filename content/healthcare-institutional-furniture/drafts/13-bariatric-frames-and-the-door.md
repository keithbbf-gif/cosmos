# Bariatric Frames and the Door

*Educational history of healthcare and nursing-home furniture. Not a purchasing specification for a named building, not a survey opinion, and not clinical advice about beds, rails, or seating for a particular resident.*

The frame will not turn the corner. That is the first fact, and it arrives before anyone reads the capacity line on the spec. A Medilodge-era corridor in a purpose-built Michigan home is a hospital leftover: a double-loaded hall, a ninety-degree turn into a double room, a bathroom door that was drawn for a standard shower chair. The expanded deck that came off the Medline-era truck is honest about its width. The building is not. Maintenance backs the bed up, kisses the door casing, tries the other diagonal, and then parks the carton in the hall while purchasing argues about whether the order was “42-inch sleep surface” or “42-inch overall.” Those are different numbers. The casing does not care which one the binder meant.

Capacity is a catalog class. The doorway, the elevator, and the toilet are the building. This essay is about that mismatch. Chapter 11 already took the one-page spec that looks complete. Chapter 30 will take the dining chair that still says 300 pounds next to a 750-pound bed. Here the object is the frame itself — the reinforced deck, the fat casters, the sleep surface that ate the roommate’s floor — and the room that was never redrawn for it.

## What the book meant by a class

The late Medline-era furniture pages, and the DME pages that sat in the same punchout, talked in hundreds of pounds the way paint chips talk in names. Five hundred. Six hundred. Seven-fifty. A thousand. Those numbers are not a building code. They are marketing bins that grew as manufacturers welded heavier tubes, added a center leg, and printed a larger sticker. A Carroll-class or Joerns-class expanded frame in the 2000s might be sold as a 600-pound bed with a 42-inch deck, or a 750 with a 48, or a 1,000-pound “heavy duty” with a sleep surface that looked, on the dock, like a small raft `[VERIFY]`. House-brand MDS-class prefixes followed the same bins a season later, cheaper, with a warranty desk that answered through the distributor.

What the class did *not* say was overall width with siderails up, or the turning radius with a helper bar hanging off the head end, or whether the floor-lock pedals cleared a vinyl transition strip. Purchasing ordered a capacity. The wing received a geometry. On a Medilodge-class buy — fifteen buildings, one corporate color board, a corporate purchaser who had never walked that particular corner — the geometry was assumed to be the same as last year’s standard 35- or 36-inch deck. It was not. An expanded frame is not a standard frame with a thicker sticker. It is a different piece of furniture that still has to share a toilet with a roommate who did not order it.

Reinforced casters were the quiet half of the class. Five-inch, sometimes six, dual-wheel, a stem that could take a side load when an aide steered from the foot. Chapter 38 will stay with casters. The point here is that the heavier wheel made the bed *feel* medical at the moment the laminate headboard was trying to feel residential. The rattle in the hall at 2 a.m. announced the class before anyone read a tag. Soft tread helped until hair and tape packed the fork. Then the bed that had been bought as dignity furniture became the loudest object on the wing.

Width was the number that fought the architecture. Catalog sleep surfaces stepped in even inches: 39, 42, 48, 54 `[VERIFY]`. Overall width with rails and headboard ears was larger. A 42-inch deck in a room drawn for two 36-inch hospital beds is not a substitution. It is a renovation that nobody budgeted. The cubicle curtain still hung on the old track. The nightstand still sat where the carpenter put the blocking. The overbed table’s H-base, sized for a standard deck, clipped the new frame’s side rail and would not nest. The package (chapter 23) had been sold as a set. The bariatric line item broke the set on the first afternoon.

## The 36-inch door and the elevator that was already a problem

Older skilled-nursing doors in the United States are often 36 inches in the clear, sometimes less after the closer and the latch-side stop. That dimension is a habit of the era that built the corridor, not a furniture standard. A standard electric bed, rails down, usually makes it. An expanded frame, even with rails collapsed, often does not — or makes it only if someone removes the door, the closer, or the helper bar that therapy clipped on after admission. The toilet room is worse. The bathroom door on a 1990s double is a residential leftover wearing a commercial hinge. A wheeled shower chair already argues with it. A 42-inch sleep surface does not go through. The resident who needs the expanded bed also needs the bathroom. The furniture solution stops at the casing.

Elevators on converted buildings and on early purpose-built homes were sized for a stretcher and a standard gurney, not for a 54-inch deck standing on its casters. A Medilodge-era Lower Peninsula building that began as a 1960s hospital wing might have a cab that takes a standard hi-lo and a tech, and nothing else. When the expanded frame is ordered for a second-floor room, the receiving story becomes a freight story (chapter 42) and then an occupancy story. Does the person move to a first-floor bed? Does the frame come in through a patio and down a corridor that was never a receiving dock? Those are building decisions dressed up as a SKU.

Shared rooms make the math unkind. F917 still wants a bed of proper size and height, a mattress, functional furniture, an individual closet. It does not redraw the square footage. Two people, one toilet, a curtain that does not meet (chapter 24), and now one sleep surface that occupies the floor the roommate used to reach the sink. Dignity is a furniture problem here, not a poster in the lobby. The expanded bed is the correct class for one body and an insult to the other. Operators who could afford it converted the double to a private and ate the census. Operators who could not left the curtain and the apology.

The toilet itself is a piece of porcelain the furniture book never listed. Raised seats, drop-arm commodes, and transfer benches live in chapter 32. They matter in this chapter because a 750-pound class on the sleep surface does not automatically produce a 750-pound class in the wet room. The bedside commode that maintenance pulled from the closet was a 350-pound chrome frame with a rusted crossbrace. The shower chair was whatever the last survey had not tagged. Half a purchase is a pattern in this series. The bed arrived. The building did not.

## How a multi-building buyer actually got one

On a Medilodge-class desk the expanded frame was rarely a package. It was an exception. Corporate spec said oak-look laminate, a standard electric, a house-brand mattress, the overbed table that failed at the gas spring (chapter 19). The exception said: this admission, this weight, this week. Therapy might have asked. The DON might have asked. The administrator signed because the hospital discharge would not wait for a construction punch. Capital versus supply (chapter 41) decided whether the bed was a project or a punchout line. Either way it was a one-off in a building that ran on sameness.

Sameness is why the door lost. Fifteen homes, roughly 2,500 beds in the 2011 *Provider* snapshot of MediLodge Group under Gail Clarkson, did not get fifteen architectural reviews when a 48-inch deck was added to the book. They got a freight appointment and a hope that the wing “had one of those rooms.” The room that “had one” was often the private at the end, or the former office, or the double whose other bed had been taken out after a death and never put back. That is not a bariatric program. It is an inventory accident that purchasing then had to repeat, because the next admission could not use a bed that was already occupied.

House brand versus name brand mattered at the claim desk, not at the corner. A Medline-era house-brand expanded frame and a Joerns-class or Invacare-class frame both stuck on the same casing. The name-brand sheet might have listed an overall width. The house-brand sheet might have listed a sleep surface and a capacity and left the ears of the headboard to surprise. Chapter 11 is the general case of that omission. The bariatric case is only louder, because the surprise does not fit through the door.

Dignity, again, is not a finish. A wood-look panel on a steel expanded frame (chapter 17) does not make the object smaller. A floral comforter does not change the turning radius. Families saw a bigger bed and heard “special.” The wing saw a piece that blocked the med cart, clipped the overbed table, and left the roommate walking sideways to the toilet. Survey language about accommodation — §483.10(e)(3), F558’s cousin in the chair — can be read as a reason to have the right size. It can also be read, in a double, as a reason the right size does not fit. Furniture cannot settle that fight. Architecture can. Architecture was not on the punchout.

## What the sticker could not do

The capacity tag is a test number, not a census. Chapter 43 will stay with that sentence. For the frame, the tag’s other failure is social. A 600-pound class in a building whose dining chairs are still the 1990s stacker is a bed that works at night and a day that does not. The person who needed the expanded deck also needed a way to sit in the day room, a way to sit at a table whose apron was cut for a standard wheelchair (chapter 27), a way to sit in a shower that still had a rusted bench. Buying the frame without the rest is how a Medline-era package pretended to be complete.

Low-low travel on an expanded frame was a rarer, later, more expensive sentence in the book `[VERIFY]`. Chapter 12 took the low deck as fall furniture. Combining low travel with a 750-pound class and a 48-inch surface produced a piece that solved two catalog problems and created a third: a transfer height that therapy hated and a width that the room could not swallow. Not every operator bought that combination. Some kept the expanded frame at a conventional hi-lo range and put a mat on the floor, which is a program, not a specification, and not a recipe this essay will write.

The afterlife of these frames is still visible on mixed-decade corridors (chapter 44). A 2000s oak-look expanded bed parked in a private that used to be a double; a later gray house-brand 600-pound class in a room whose door was planed by maintenance; a standard 36-inch deck still doing the work because the wide one is on the other wing and nobody will move it through the elevator. Prestige-era signs on a Medilodge lawn did not change the casing. The door is older than the sticker. The sticker is older than the mattress that later opened a gap the 2006 zone drawing did not imagine (chapters 14 and 16). The frame remains what it was on the afternoon it would not turn: a correct catalog class in an uncorrected building.

That is the residual. You can buy a thousand-pound deck from a punchout. You cannot buy a new corner. The next essay leaves the doorway and opens the federal drawing that tried to measure the openings *inside* the bed — seven zones, and numbers for only four of them.

## Notes

- Catalog capacity bins (500 / 600 / 750 / 1,000 lb) and expanded widths (39–54 inch sleep surfaces) are channel language and typical ranges, not a single year’s SKU list. Model pairings `[VERIFY]`.
- Door leaves near 36 inches and tight toilet rooms are architectural habits of late-twentieth-century SNF stock, not a furniture standard.
- MediLodge Group snapshot: *Provider*, May 2011 (Gail Clarkson; about 15 SNFs + 1 AL; ~2,500 beds). 2013 Crain’s Detroit Business: sale of 15 homes to Prestige Healthcare. Do not merge with Ciena.
- 42 CFR §483.90(e) / F917: bed of proper size and height — survey language, not a doorway dimension.
- 42 CFR §483.10(e)(3): reasonable accommodation. Not a bill of materials for an expanded frame.
- FDA 2006 entrapment guidance and IEC 60601-2-52 belong to the *system* (mattress, rails, deck) and are taken up in chapters 14–16; they do not resize a door.
