# Shower Chairs, Benches, Commodes

*Educational history of healthcare and nursing-home furniture. Not a purchasing specification for a named building, not a survey opinion, and not clinical advice about beds, rails, or seating for a particular resident.*

The crossbrace is rusted. Not surface rust — a brown scale at the weld where the aluminum or chrome-plated tube sits in standing water and urine and whatever EVS last poured. The shower chair is still in the stall. Someone has wrapped a towel over the back. The feet, once rubber, are now hard and cracked; one is missing, so the frame rocks on tile. This is furniture. It lives in water. It is also a supply item that arrived from the same Medline-era book that sold the nightstand, on a different page, with a different capacity sticker and no laminate to hide behind. This essay will not teach a transfer. It will not write a shower protocol. It will stay with the object and the three names the book used for it: shower chair, transfer bench, bedside commode.

Privacy is the other wet-room furniture. A vinyl curtain on a rod, hooks that rust independently of the chair. A door that does not close because the bench sticks out. A roommate (chapter 24) who can hear everything because the cheap private room never reached the toilet. The rusted brace is what purchasing sees when they finally walk the stall. The curtain is what the person in the stall sees. Both are the inventory.

## Three objects, one wet job

A shower chair is a seat with holes or slats, four legs or a frame on suction feet or small rusting casters, a back that may drop, arms that may flip. It stays in the stall or it is carried. A transfer bench is a longer frame that spans the tub or stall threshold — part in, part out — so that a person can sit and move along a slat without the essay describing how. The two are not interchangeable. Buildings that ordered chairs and used them as benches, or benches and used them as chairs, learned that in the wet. A bedside commode is night furniture: a chair over a bucket or a liner, arms, a back, sometimes a drop-arm the catalog mentions and this page will not demonstrate. It lives by the bed like a nightstand and smells like the reason it is there. In the morning it may roll to the toilet or it may not. It is the overbed table’s cousin in use-count and in failure.

Durable medical equipment language treats these as a class of devices. Furniture language treats them as seating that got wet. The SNF used both languages. Therapy specified. Purchasing punched out. Maintenance painted over rust or threw the frame in a dumpster and opened the book again. House-brand MDS-class prefixes sat next to name-brand DME pages — Drive-class, GF Health-class, Invacare-class, as silhouettes, not as a store. Capacity ratings were printed on stickers that faded in steam. Typical catalogs offered 250-, 300-, 350-pound neighborhoods on standard frames and higher on bariatric cousins `[VERIFY` any model]. Chapter 30’s half purchase lives here: an expanded bed and a standard shower chair. The sticker is not a census (chapter 43). The rust does not care what the sticker said.

Materials are the history. Chrome-plated steel rusted first and honestly. Aluminum rusted at the hardware — screws, rivets, the steel bolt in the aluminum tube. Plastic seats yellowed and cracked. Composite frames promised that the rusted brace would end and then grew a biofilm in every hollow. Wood commodes existed in older houses and in culture-change photographs; they are rare on a Medilodge-era wet-room punchout because wood and urine are a short marriage. Stainless appeared on pages that cost more than supply wanted to spend. The building bought aluminum and replaced it.

Feet are a small object with a large life. Rubber caps walk off. Suction cups let go on a floor that EVS waxed. Casters on a shower chair are how the chair leaves the stall and becomes a dining-room refugee (chapter 26) or a therapy extra (chapter 33). A caster in a wet room is also how rust finds a bearing. Chapter 38 will take hallway casters. Here the wheel is a mistake that keeps being specified because carrying a wet chair is a job no one wants.

## Rust, rating, the night commode

Rust is not a metaphor. It is the wet-room finish. The crossbrace sits in the puddle that the drain does not take because the floor was pitched to a corner the bench now occupies. Urine finds the weld. Bleach finds the weld. The card on a vinyl seat, if the seat is padded, is the same bleach argument as the day-room sofa (chapter 10), only steeper. Many wet-room seats are not padded. They are slats. The slat that cracks is a different failure: plastic fatigue, a person who exceeded a faded sticker, a cleaner that was not on any card. Maintenance wires a slat. Then they replace the chair. Then the new chair rusts in the same puddle because the puddle is architecture.

Ratings belong on the frame and in a file. They do not belong in a how-to. A commode that says 300 pounds and sits under a person who needs the 600-pound bed is a half purchase you can hear. A bench that says 400 and has lost a foot is a rating that has already failed. Shared wet-room seating — one chair for a stall that serves a double — wears like the shared bariatric armchair of chapter 30. Everyone’s weight, everyone’s cleaner, one sticker.

The bedside commode is the piece families remember and catalogs treat as ordinary. It is a chair, a pail, a lid that is also a seat. It is night furniture the way a urinal bottle is night furniture, except it has arms and a back and a place in the room package’s overflow. Some frames drop a pan and become, in daylight, a chair that should not be in the dining room and is. Some stay by the bed and become the nightstand’s rival for the only aisle a lift can use. Privacy is a curtain (chapter 24) that does not reach this corner. The roommate is the other user of the air. Homelike (F584) has very little to say that is useful at 2 a.m. Functional furniture (F917) has a little more. The commode is functional. It is also the object that makes the private room a reform people can smell as well as see.

After 2020 the wet room took on isolation’s extra chemistry. Chairs were bagged, wiped, or thrown away as if they were disposable. Some were. Disposable commode liners were already a supply line. Disposable chairs were a mood. The rusted brace survived the mood because capital did not replace every stall. The book sold the same aluminum. EVS poured the same night bottle, or a stronger one. The weld recorded it.

## Privacy, the curtain, the stall that is a room

A shower stall in a purpose-built double is a room that is not a room. A curtain, a rod, a bench that crosses the threshold and holds the curtain open. The cheap private room of chapter 24 has a wet cousin. Fire notes on shower curtains differ from cubicle-curtain 701 language; Life Safety pages often carve shower curtains out. `[VERIFY]` the edition before citing a K-tag. The furniture fact is that the curtain is short, the bench is long, and privacy is a gap again.

Culture-change and private-room construction gave some people a toilet with a door. The shower chair still rusted. The commode still appeared at night if the walk was the night’s fight. A better room did not abolish the objects. It gave them a door. Medilodge-era wings that remained doubles gave them a roommate and a rod.

Therapy is a buyer seat for these frames more than for dining stackers. A therapist who wants a bench rather than a chair, or a chair with arms that flip rather than a bench that occupies the whole stall, is specifying furniture. Purchasing that hears “shower chair” and orders the cheapest punchout is repeating the geri-chair mis-order (chapter 25). The gym (chapter 33) may hold a clean extra. The stall holds whatever was left. The difference is rust.

Dignity is a towel on a rusted back. It is a commode lid that is also a seat when a visitor is in the room. It is a bariatric bench that will not turn in the bathroom whose door was drawn for a 36-inch bed (chapter 13). It is not a finish. There is no oak-look for a slat. There is a plastic that tries to be white and becomes cream. There is a padded vinyl that tries to be kind and becomes a bleach map. The honest object is a frame you can see rust on before it fails.

Shared stalls multiply the wear. A double room with one shower is two people’s chemistry on one brace. A wing with a central bath is a dozen. The chair that lives in a central bath is a public object with a private job. It disappears into rooms and returns rustier. Maintenance paints the brace and calls it new. Paint on rust is a finish era, like mauve vinyl: it dates the last time someone walked the stall. A Medilodge-era punchout replacement looks bright for a month. The puddle does not care which building’s logo is on the lawn.

## The puddle that is the spec

Spare parts are a fantasy the punchout does not support. A missing foot is not a SKU anyone keeps in the maintenance cage except as a bag of mixed rubber caps. A cracked slat is a new chair. A rusted brace is a new chair. That is why the wet room looks like a graveyard of almost-useful frames: one without a foot, one with a wired slat, one whose arms no longer flip, parked behind the working one. The working one is last month’s shiny replacement. The graveyard is the true inventory.

The residual fact is the puddle. The chair is specified as seating. The stall is specified as tile. The meeting is a brace in water. Aluminum versus chrome versus composite is a materials argument the puddle settles. Capacity is a sticker the puddle does not read. Privacy is a curtain the bench holds open. A Medilodge-era Michigan wet room — not a named stall, a type — still holds a rusted crossbrace, a missing foot, a commode in the bedroom aisle, a spare chair in the tub that no one uses as a tub. The book will still sell the three names. The floor will still replace the rusted one with a shiny one that knows where to rust.

This series will not tell you how to shower a person. It will tell you that wet-room seating is furniture the purchase order kept trying to treat as a consumable, and that the consumable left a weld behind. The next essay walks down the hall to the gym, where the chairs are dry, the mats are furniture, and a wooden box step sits under a mat table as if it were always part of the building.
