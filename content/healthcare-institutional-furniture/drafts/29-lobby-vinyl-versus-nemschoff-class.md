# Lobby Vinyl versus Nemschoff-Class

*Educational history of healthcare and nursing-home furniture. Not a purchasing specification for a named building, not a survey opinion, and not clinical advice about beds, rails, or seating for a particular resident.*

Two chairs in two lobbies. The first is a vinyl barrel with a wood-look cap, a Medline-era punchout silhouette that also sits in the day room and the admissions office. It arrived on a truck with nightstands. It wipes. It stacks in no one’s memory because it does not stack; it just waits. The second is a heavier healthcare lounge chair with a replaceable cover, a frame a hospital specifier could defend to a facilities committee, a name that in the 1990s and 2000s meant a class: Nemschoff. Sheboygan, Wisconsin. Later a Herman Miller healthcare association. Not a SKU on this page. Not a shop. A way of buying a waiting-room chair that most skilled-nursing lobbies never used.

The problem is the split market. Hospitals, especially after the late 1980s, could specify furniture houses that had decided healthcare was a design problem: cleanable but not cheap-looking, replaceable covers, benches that could take a night, guest chairs that did not pretend to be banquet stackers. SNFs, especially Medilodge-class multi-building operators, bought the book. The front door was supposed to look like home (F584). The book’s vinyl set was what “home” cost. Who walked in the front door — a hospital family with a parking garage, or a Michigan family with a folder of Medicaid papers — did not change the wipe test. It changed the invoice.

## Sheboygan as a class

Leonard Nemschoff founded a chair company in Sheboygan in 1950, in a town that still called itself Chair City. Early work was commercial seating — libraries, colleges — the kind of contract furniture that lived next to Steelcase and Herman Miller without being either. In the late 1980s and early 1990s the firm leaned into hospitals: patient rooms, treatment areas, public waiting. A 2009 *Milwaukee Journal Sentinel* piece, written at the sale, put then-recent revenue above ninety million and the customer list in the neighborhood of a thousand hospitals `[VERIFY the newspaper’s figures if you quote them as more than period color]`. On 24 June 2009 Herman Miller completed the acquisition of Nemschoff Chairs, LLC — cash and stock, Sheboygan and a Sioux Center, Iowa plant remaining in the filing. That date is the afterlife. The class is older. It means a healthcare seating house that sold to specifiers, not only to distributors; that talked about replaceable components; that showed up on hospital finish boards next to flooring and wall protection.

Peers belong in the same sentence without becoming a catalog. Steelcase Health is the hospital-furniture face of a Grand Rapids contract giant. Wieland, founded in 1977 in Indiana, built a reputation first in renewable institutional seating and later in acute-care motion furniture; treat it as a peer class, not a current line. Kwalu-class molded polyurethane (chapter 37) sat nearer casegoods and restaurant-proof chairs. The point is not a brand ranking. The point is that a hospital waiting room in 2005 could be a specified interior. A SNF lobby in 2005 was more often a punchout page: two vinyl chairs, a loveseat, a table that took magazines and a bowl of wrapped mints.

Medline is the channel that stocked the second room (chapter 7). House-brand MDS-class prefixes, drop-ship, a GPO price. The lobby set coordinated with the resident-room oak (chapter 9) because coordination was the package’s last argument (chapter 23). Cleanability was real. Price was real. Who sat in the chair was a short-stay family, a hospice visitor, a salesperson, a surveyor waiting for an entrance conference. The chair had to wipe because all of those people brought weather. It did not have to last a decade of third-shift sleeping. That job belonged to the visitor sleeper in the room (chapter 22) and to the hospital’s night bench.

Specifier culture is the other channel. A hospital interior designer in the 1990s worked from a binder that included healthcare-furniture houses, a finish board, and a conversation with infection control about covers that came off. Steelcase Health pages sat next to Nemschoff pages because both spoke that language: replaceable components, a finish that could take a corridor’s traffic without looking like a banquet hall. Wieland’s 1977 shop story — renewable institutional seating, later acute-care motion furniture out of Indiana — belonged in the same binder as a peer, not as a rival ad. The SNF administrator who also walked a hospital for a relative saw those chairs and then went home and signed a punchout. The difference was not taste. It was who had a specifier and who had a password.

## Price, wipe, the door

A Nemschoff-class chair cost more than a distributor barrel. That is the least interesting fact and the one that decided most SNF lobbies. Capital thresholds (chapter 41) sent the lobby set to supply. Supply sent it to the book. The book sent a vinyl that could claim a bleach ratio (chapter 10) and a CAL 117 hangtag (chapter 39). The hospital specifier, working from a different budget and a different visitor-hour, could pay for a cover that came off, a frame that accepted a replacement, a bench that did not look like a bus station. Both buyers were right about their invoices. They were not buying the same door.

Cleanability is not a monopoly of the cheap chair. Healthcare-furniture houses sold wipeable skins as hard as any distributor. The difference was construction and the replacement story. A punchout chair that splits at the welt is a freight claim or a new SKU (chapter 42). A specified chair with a renewable cover is a maintenance event. SNFs had maintenance. They did not always have a parts relationship with a Wisconsin factory. They had a punchout password. That password is why the lobby still matches the day room’s leftover mauve when the rest of the building has gone gray.

Who walked in the front door is the social fact inside the furniture. A hospital lobby seats a public that includes people who will write to a board. A SNF lobby seats people who are about to walk a corridor of doubles with cubicle curtains (chapter 24). The chair is an introduction. Operators knew that. They put a chandelier, a fireplace surround that did not work, an aquarium. They still put the book’s vinyl in front of the fireplace because the fireplace was construction and the chair was supply. F584 could smile at the fireplace and frown at the furniture, or the other way around. Homelike is not a finish, as chapter 5 said. It is also not a brand. A Sheboygan frame in a cherry stain can look as institutional as a barrel if the vinyl is wrong. A distributor barrel in a living-room fabric can look like a house if the room is small and the lamp is not a torchiere from 1994.

Medilodge-era Michigan doors make the split visible. Washington Township corporate offices, around 2011, were buying for roughly fifteen SNFs and an assisted living community — Gail Clarkson’s *Provider* snapshot, about 2,500 beds. Those lobbies were a system. A system wants a repeatable chair. Repeatable chairs come from the book. Prestige Healthcare’s later name on a lawn, after the 2013 Crain’s report of a sale of fifteen homes, did not automatically change the seating. Chairs outlast letterhead. The vinyl barrel that sat under a Medilodge sign sat under a Prestige sign with a different throw pillow. The Nemschoff-class chair, if it existed, was probably in the hospital fifteen miles away, in a waiting room that had been renovated with a bond issue.

## Two lobbies, one wipe test

Hospitals learned, over the same decades, to treat the waiting room as a product. Family sleepers. Bariatric mix. Infection-control surfaces that still tried to look like a hotel. Healthcare-furniture houses existed because that product had a specifier. SNFs learned to treat the lobby as a first impression and a traffic island. Admissions sat to the left. A wheelchair parked to the right. A med-cart shortcut used the rug. The chair took a coat, a purse, a grandchild who should have been in a different seat. EVS wiped it at 6 a.m. with whatever the night bottle was (chapter 36). The wipe test is the same test. The object that meets it can come from Sheboygan or from Mundelein. The purchase path is what differs.

Bariatric mix and sleepers belong to the hospital lobby more often than to the SNF door. A specified waiting room in 2005 might include a wider seat in the same cover and a bench a family could spend a night on. The SNF lobby had a vinyl loveseat that became a bed for a grandchild and then a problem for EVS. Chapter 22 takes the sleeper in the room. Here the point is that the healthcare-furniture house sold the lobby as a stay. The distributor sold it as a wait. Stays need a different frame. Waits need a wipe and a price. Medilodge-era admissions wanted the wait to look like a stay — fireplace, aquarium, a lamp — and then sat people in the wait’s chair. The contradiction is the object.

There is a third chair that confuses the story: the donated living-room recliner. Families bring what they have. Staff park it by the door because it looks like home and because it is soft. F558 will not love the stand-up. Infection control will not love the cloth. Fire will want a hangtag that the recliner never had. The donated chair is not Nemschoff-class and not the book. It is the house entering through the lobby, which is what culture change asked for and what survey sometimes dinged. Buildings that had a policy against donations still had one recliner that someone could not bear to surplus. Buildings that had no policy had a flea market. The specified healthcare chair and the punchout vinyl are both, at least, countable.

This essay will not tell you which lobby is better. It will tell you that the industry ran two waiting rooms on two invoices and called both healthcare seating. The SNF did not fail to buy Nemschoff. It bought a channel. The hospital did not invent dignity by specifying a Wisconsin frame. It spent a different budget on a different visitor-hour. After 2009, when Herman Miller’s filing put Nemschoff inside a larger contract company, hospital catalogs shifted their letterhead. SNF punchouts did not. The vinyl barrel did not notice.

On a Lower Peninsula wing you can still do the comparison in a morning. Drive past the hospital’s porte-cochère and look at the benches through the glass. Drive to the nursing home and sit in the chair by the fish tank. Both will wipe. One will have a story about a factory in Chair City and a 2009 acquisition. The other will have a story about a freight appointment and a color board. The next essay goes past both lobbies into the room, where a 750-pound-class bed sits beside a dining chair that was never told about the bed.
