# Headboard as Furniture and Device

*Educational history of healthcare and nursing-home furniture. Not a purchasing specification for a named building, not a survey opinion, and not clinical advice about beds, rails, or seating for a particular resident.*

The cherry-look panel sits on a steel frame. That is the whole argument, visible from the doorway: a laminate insert with a molded edge, a pair of holes or a hook for the pendant, a crash strip at the height a nightstand will hit, and behind it the welded rectangle that actually moves when the deck articulates. William A. Hillenbrand’s 1929 idea — wood and metal on the same hospital bed, against the white tubular steel of the ward — never died. It became a finish. A Medilodge-era private room in Michigan could advertise a “residential headboard” and still be selling a medical device with a homelike sticker. The pendant hangs on the furniture. The furniture is the device.

Chapter 2 took Batesville and the founding. Chapter 5 took *homelike* as a survey word. This essay stays with the panel: the object that has to look like a bedroom, take a hit from a cart, hide a cable, and remain part of the openings the 2006 FDA drawing numbered as Zone 6 and Zone 7.

## Batesville’s compromise, repeated in laminate

John Hillenbrand’s Batesville work was timber and then caskets. William’s hospital-bed company, Hill-Rom, 1929, Depression, a year of walking into hospitals, put a wood-and-metal deck against the enamel ward look. Company histories treat that hybrid as the point of the firm: a bed that did not look only like a cot. The electronic bed commonly dated 1950 in those same histories `[VERIFY]` added motors without giving up the idea that the head and foot could be furniture. Later Hillrom, and Baxter’s 2021 acquisition, are afterlife. The SNF floor did not buy that afterlife. It bought the compromise in cheaper materials.

By the Medline-era book the wood was usually not wood. It was TFL or a thermofoil wrap or an HPL insert in a steel or aluminum channel (chapter 37 will stay with skins). Honey oak in the 1990s. Cherry and maple looks in the 2000s. Gray woodgrain after the oak tired. The panel clipped in, or rode on hooks, so that EVS could wipe the frame and so that a damaged insert could be replaced without scrapping the bed. Replacement inserts were a supply item. The frame was capital. That split — already the mattress story in chapter 16 — made the “headboard” a consumable face on a durable device.

Residential headboards that were actually bedroom furniture appeared in culture-change rooms and in some private-pay wings: a bolted-to-the-wall oak board, a fabric panel, a shelf. They looked more like home and they failed the device tests the electric bed still had to live with. The deck moved. The wall board did not. A gap opened at the mattress end — Zone 7 — that a factory-matched footboard would have closed. Or the bed rolled and the wall board became a crash point at a new height every time someone changed the hi-lo. F584 can like the look. IEC 60601-2-52, the later medical-bed particular standard, is looking at a device. The two papers do not share a finish schedule.

Hospitals, for their part, often gave up the furniture fiction at the head: a utility headwall, gases, a boom, a bed that docks. The SNF rarely got that capital. It got a duplex behind the nightstand and a panel that had to do the emotional work of a headwall and the mechanical work of a bumper. The Medilodge-era “homelike” headboard with a pendant hook is that double job in one SKU.

## The panel as a crash point, a hook, and a zone

Beds roll. Even with floor locks, they walk when an aide boosts a person or when a floor buffer kisses a caster. The headboard is what hits the wall. Rubber bumpers, molded ears, a strip of vinyl at the height of the outlet — those are furniture details that are also wall-protection details. A thin steel headboard on an older electric dings the gypsum and chips its own paint. A thick laminate insert dings the gypsum and delaminates at the corner. Maintenance files both as “the bed hit the wall.” Architects who put a chair rail in the room were answering the same object.

The pendant hook is a small, stubborn fact. Hand control on a cord, sometimes a rail control as well (chapter 11). The hook or the slot in the headboard keeps the pendant off the floor and out of the caster. It also announces, to anyone who thought the panel was a bedroom board, that a device is in the room. Some house-brand inserts omitted the hook; the pendant then hung on the rail or on the nightstand or on a cup hook someone screwed into the laminate. That screw is a later hole in a finish that was trying to be furniture.

Zone 6 in the 2006 guidance is the opening between the end of the rail and the side edge of the headboard or footboard. A thick residential panel narrows that opening; a thin steel plate widens it; a decorative post or a turned-look finial — rare on SNF electrics, common in the wishful catalog rendering — changes the radius. Zone 7 is between the board and the mattress end. A panel that sits high, or a board that is more ornament than stop, leaves a shelf a pillow can fall into and a space a later short mattress can open. The guidance maps both zones and does not write recommended inches for them (chapter 14). The headboard is why those two zones exist as furniture, not only as rail geometry.

Footboards are the quieter twin. They take the crank on older beds (chapter 3), the chart slot, the name card, the drainage-bag hook. They take kicks. A “residential” footboard with a laminate insert will still have a steel inner and a place where the insert pops when a lift’s knee pad hits it. Some low-low programs removed footboards to get the deck closer to a mat. The removal is a fall-program choice this essay will not script. It is also a Zone 7 change and a loss of the bumper at the foot.

## Homelike as a panel, not a room

F584’s homelike language can ding a mauve vinyl stacker and still accept an oak-laminate box (chapter 5, chapter 9). The headboard is where that sentence becomes a photograph for the family tour. Cherry-look, a little profile on the cap, maybe a “panel bed” silhouette that quotes a domestic four-poster without the posts. The electric hi-lo still shows its scissors underneath. The assist rail still clicks. The overbed table still rolls into the shot. Homelike, in this object, is a three-eighths-inch insert.

Culture-change programs (chapter 6) asked for more: a real wood board, a reading lamp that was not a hospital gooseneck, a bed that did not look like a device. Green House rooms and household models sometimes got a residential bed or a disguised frame. The purpose-built double-loaded corridor that most Medilodge-class census occupied got the insert. Purchasing could point at the panel and say the building had heard the wish. The wish was a room. The spend was a face.

Color boards made the panel a corporate decision. Washington Township–era MediLodge Group, about fifteen homes, needed the oak to match the nightstand and the wardrobe so that a replacement bed did not announce itself as a different decade. It still did, because the insert’s grain drifted by lot and because a later gray program (chapter 9) put a new bed in an oak room. The panel is the piece families notice. The caster is the piece the night aide notices. Both are the same bed.

Batesville’s sister story — the casket line that made the Hillenbrand name before the bed did — is not a joke in this chapter. It is why a wood-and-metal hospital bed could be imagined in a furniture town. The shop already knew how to veneer a panel that had to take a hand and a glance. The SNF insert is a distant cousin of that skill, pressed in a factory that never saw Indiana timber. The glance is what survived. The timber did not. When a later catalog said “real wood headboard,” it sometimes meant a veneer on a device frame and sometimes meant a board on a wall. Buyers heard furniture. The serial number on the frame still said device.

IEC 60601-2-52 and its older cousin 60601-2-38 are why the panel cannot only be décor. A medical bed is a device with particular requirements — electrical, mechanical, stability, the sleep surface. The headboard, if it is part of the device, is in that particular standard. If a facility bolts a bedroom board to the wall and rolls a frameless-looking electric under it, the device and the furniture have been separated in a way the catalog rendering hid. This is not a prohibition. It is a genre split. Surveyors walk in with F584. Test labs walk in with IEC. The cherry-look insert tries to satisfy the first without reminding anyone of the second.

Footboards deserve a longer look than the twin sentence above. On a crank-era bed the footboard is where the handles live. On an electric it is a chart slot, a name card, a place to hang a drainage bag, a kick plate for a lift’s knee pad. Some Medilodge-era “residential” feet were low profile so the bed would photograph like a motel queen. Low profile is a Zone 7 invitation when the mattress walks. High profile is a crash point when a walker comes in at the foot. Neither profile is in F584. Both are in the room every time someone makes the bed.

Pendant generations mark the panel the way latches mark the rail. Older pendants were hard-wired bricks with a coiled cord. Later ones were membrane pads. Rail-mounted controls, when they existed, let a person who could not find the hook still raise the head. House-brand inserts sometimes had a slot that fit last year’s brick and not this year’s pad. The pad then lived on the mattress, under the person, which is how buttons get pressed at 2 a.m. by a knee. The hook is a small design. The wrong hook is a night.

## What still shows from the doorway

Walk a mixed-decade Michigan corridor (chapter 44) and the headboards date the rooms the way the vinyl dates the chairs. A 1990s honey-oak insert with a cracked cap. A 2000s cherry with a pendant hook worn to bare plastic. A later gray board on a low-low frame whose footboard was omitted. A steel headboard painted beige because the insert was lost and the house-brand replacement did not clip onto that generation. Hillenbrand’s 1929 hybrid is in all of them: the refusal to let the hospital bed be only white tube, and the inability to let it be only a dresser.

The residual is the hook. As long as the pendant hangs on the panel, the panel is a device. Take the hook away and the bed still articulates, still rolls, still opens Zone 7 when the mattress walks. The furniture fiction is a finish schedule. The next essay leaves the board and looks at the hardware that hangs *over* the empty deck — trapeze, helper bar, floor pole — objects sold next to furniture that are not furniture, and that will not become a chair no matter what the transfer wish was.
