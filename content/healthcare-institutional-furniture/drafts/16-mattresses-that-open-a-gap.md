# Mattresses That Open a Gap

*Educational history of healthcare and nursing-home furniture. Not a purchasing specification for a named building, not a survey opinion, and not clinical advice about beds, rails, or seating for a particular resident.*

You can put a fist in it. That is the test nobody wrote on the purchase order and everybody performed in the doorway: knuckles between the rail and the mattress, a slit that was not there when the bed came off the truck. The frame is the same Carroll-class or Joerns-class or house-brand electric that purchasing specified. The rail is the same assist panel that the spec called standard. The mattress is a later foam from a different page, or an air overlay the wound-care vendor left, or a low-air-loss surface whose pump hums and whose edge collapses when a person sits. The 10 March 2006 FDA guidance already knew this could happen. It put the mattress inside the *system*. It treated that surface as a contributor to an opening or a mitigator of one. It did not know which SKU a Medilodge-era buyer would drop on a deck bought three years earlier from another book.

Zone 3 is the long opening between rail and mattress. Chapter 14 mapped all seven zones and the fact that dimensional limits attach to 1–4 only. This essay stays with the object that moves after the drawing is filed: the sleep surface. Compression, width, depth. A specialty mattress that was never the original. A channel habit that still treats the bed and the foam as two commodities.

## The system the guidance wrote, and the two invoices the dock saw

FDA’s 2006 page is not a mattress standard. It is a bed-system document. Deck, rails, headboard, footboard, mattress. An opening is a relationship. If the mattress is narrower than the deck, Zone 3 grows. If the edge compresses, Zone 3 grows even when the advertised width matches. If the surface is shorter than the deck, Zone 7 — headboard or footboard to mattress end — grows, and that zone never received a recommended inch in the 2006 table. If a therapeutic overlay sits on top of a foam and the rail height was set for the foam alone, the slit changes again. The companion FDA thinking about modifying bed systems and using accessories exists because facilities already did this. They did it from the book.

The Medline-era channel made the split official. Beds were a capital or a durable line: Carroll, Invacare, Joerns, a house-brand frame with an MDS-class prefix. Mattresses were a supply line: foam replacement, “pressure redistributing” marketing language, heels, fire socks, a vinyl ticking that EVS could wipe. Wound care had its own vendors and its own rental pumps. A Medilodge-class operator with fifteen buildings could standardize the frame and still let each building’s wound-care contract put a different surface on it. Corporate spec said oak-look headboard and a half-rail. The surface was whoever answered the phone after a stage-number argument this essay will not clinicalize.

House-brand foam was cheap enough to treat as a consumable. That is not an insult. It is a purchasing fact. Foam takes urine, bleach, and compression. Ticking splits at the welt the way a chair splits (chapter 36). A mattress that has been flipped, if it was a flipper, and bleached nightly after 2020 (chapter 10) is not the mattress in the 2006 still picture. The guidance’s Zone 3 dimension assumes a surface that still has a height and a square edge. A rounded, collapsed edge is a different geometry wearing the same work order number.

Name-brand therapeutic surfaces had the opposite problem: they arrived with their own width and height, and they were proud of both. A low-air-loss mattress built for a 36-inch deck may be slightly narrower than the deck on purpose, for a cover zipper or a pump hose. A mattress built for a 42-inch expanded frame (chapter 13) may be placed, in a hurry, on a 36-inch deck because it was the one in the closet. The fist finds that mistake without reading a tag.

## Compression, width, depth — three ways the slit appears

Compression is the everyday opening. A person sits on the edge to stand. The foam at the rail drops an inch or two `[VERIFY]`. The rail does not drop. Zone 3, which might have been acceptable in a factory photograph with an unused mattress, becomes a slit at the only place a body actually is. High-density foam holds an edge longer. Soft “homelike” pillow-tops — a late residential fashion that some SNF buyers tried — give the edge away on week one. The 2006 drawing does not grade foam. It grades an opening. The opening is a function of the edge that remains.

Width is the purchasing opening. Sleep-surface widths on SNF electrics cluster around 35–36 inches for the standard class (chapter 11). Mattresses are sold to those numbers and to the expanded bins: 39, 42, 48. A 35-inch mattress on a 36-inch deck is a small, even slit all the way down if the mattress is centered — and a large slit on one side if it has walked. Raised edges and “bolster” designs were sold as fall and entrapment answers. They are also new Zone 1 and Zone 3 geometries. This essay will not pick a design. It will say that a raised edge is not invisible to the 2006 page.

Depth — thickness — is the forgotten opening. Rails and headboards have a relationship to the top of the mattress, not only to the deck. Chapter 3 already separated deck height from top-of-mattress. A six-inch foam and an eight-inch foam on the same deck put the rail in two different places relative to a person’s chest. A low-air-loss surface that inflates to a taller section at the torso and a shorter section at the foot is a moving depth. Zone 2 (under the rail between supports) and Zone 3 both change when the surface is a bladder. The pump is furniture in the only sense that matters here: it is an object purchasing rented that altered the openings on a bed the building already owned.

Length is the quiet cousin. A mattress that has migrated toward the foot opens Zone 7 at the head. A shorter therapeutic surface, ordered because the longer one was out of stock, does the same work in one afternoon. Headboards that are trying to look like furniture (chapter 17) hide that gap in shadow. The fist still fits.

## Specialty surfaces versus the original deck

The original mattress, when a frame shipped as a package (chapter 23), was often a modest foam with a vinyl cover and a fire barrier good enough for the hangtag. It was sized to the deck because the factory packed them together. The first replacement was where the system broke. Replacement foam from the punchout was “for 36-inch beds” in a column next to washcloths. It might have been 35 inches wide and 6 inches thick and 80 inches long `[VERIFY]`. The deck might have been 80 or 82 or a “longer length” special for a tall resident. Nobody on the dock measured. The carton said compatible.

Air overlays — the egg-crate’s expensive cousin, then the pump pad — sat on top of that foam. They were thin when deflated and uneven when inflated. They slipped. They were the object a night aide folded back to find the wet spot, and then did not get square again. Zone 3 on an overlay is a nightly variable. The 2006 still picture cannot follow it. The FDA accessory guide exists because people kept adding things.

Low-air-loss and alternating-pressure systems came with their own frames sometimes, and sometimes they were mattresses only. Rental agreements made them temporary, which is another way of saying the openings were temporary and therefore nobody owned them. A Medilodge-era wound-care closet could hold two pumps and one extra cover. The cover’s width was a guess. When the rental went back, the old foam went back on, thinner than it had been, and the rail stayed where it had been set for the taller rental. The slit was the leftover.

Bariatric surfaces (chapter 13) have to match the expanded deck or they create the largest Zone 3 in the building. A standard 36-inch foam on a 42-inch deck is not a substitute. It is an opening you could put a forearm in. Buildings did it anyway when the wide mattress was in the wash or had been tagged out for a split cover. Dignity and entrapment share that afternoon: the correct class was on order, and the wrong surface was on the deck.

IEC 60601-2-52, the later medical-bed particular standard, treats the bed as a device with a sleep surface in mind. It is a test-lab genre. It does not inspect a Michigan wing six years after the original foam left. F917 wants a mattress. It does not specify a Zone 3. F700 wants an entrapment assessment when rails are in play (chapter 15). The assessment, if it is real, has to look at the surface that is actually on the deck, not the surface in the 2008 invoice. Furniture history can say that. It cannot write the assessment.

Ticking and the fire sock are the mattress’s other two skins. A vinyl or coated-fabric ticking is the wipeable face EVS sees. Under it, on many SNF foams, a barrier fabric exists because someone asked for a hangtag (chapter 39). Neither skin is in the 2006 zone table, and both change how the edge behaves. A ticking that has been bleached until it is brittle will split at the corner; the foam then rounds faster. A barrier that has wadded after a wash no longer holds a square. Replacement covers — sold separately, another channel habit — rarely restore the original width. They restore a color.

Rental paperwork is the other split invoice. A low-air-loss or alternating-pressure surface can be a monthly line on a wound-care contract while the frame is a ten-year asset. When the contract ends, the pump leaves and the old foam returns, thinner, sometimes with a different cover from the closet. Nobody updates the rail height. Nobody re-reads the 2006 page. The system the guidance described has been disassembled by accounting. A Medilodge-class operator with one corporate wound-care vendor and fifteen maintenance closets could run that cycle in every building without a single furniture meeting.

## The channel will not stop selling them separately

There is no moral to the split invoice. There is a habit. Beds last. Foams do not. Overlays are a therapy or wound-care budget. House-brand ticking is a supply cart. The 2006 guidance asked the industry to see a system. The Medline-era book kept seeing SKUs. A Medilodge-class purchaser could file the FDA PDF and still approve a mattress substitution because the frame was “the expensive part.” The expensive part is only half the opening.

The residual is the fist. It is a crude, unscientific, completely ordinary gesture, and it is how the floor learned that a later surface had undone a factory photograph. The next essay leaves the slit and looks at the board at the head — the cherry-look panel on a steel frame — which is trying to be furniture while remaining a piece of a device, and which can change Zone 6 and Zone 7 without anyone re-measuring the mattress.

## Notes

- FDA, *Hospital Bed System Dimensional and Assessment Guidance to Reduce Entrapment* (10 March 2006): mattress as part of the bed system; contributor or mitigator of openings; dimensional criteria for zones 1–4 only. Guidance, not statute.
- FDA, *A Guide for Modifying Bed Systems and Using Accessories to Reduce the Risk of Entrapment*: accessories and later surfaces as known modifiers. Not a hallway protocol here.
- Typical SNF deck widths ~35–36 inches; expanded classes 39–54. Mattress SKU dimensions `[VERIFY]` by year and house brand. Compression amounts are class behavior, not a named foam’s lab sheet.
- IEC 60601-2-52: manufacturer / test-lab particular standard for medical beds. Not a CMS mattress tag.
- F917 requires a mattress; F700 requires entrapment thinking when rails are used. Neither enacts a foam density.
- Channel split (frame vs. foam vs. rental surface): composite Medline/Medilodge-era habit, not a named PO.
