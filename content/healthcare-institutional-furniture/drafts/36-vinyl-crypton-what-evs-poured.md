# Vinyl, Crypton, What EVS Poured

*Educational history of healthcare and nursing-home furniture. Not a purchasing specification for a named building, not a survey opinion, and not clinical advice about beds, rails, or seating for a particular resident.*

The welt is split. A pale line of foam shows where the vinyl turned the corner of a dining-chair seat and the thread gave up. The face still looks like the 2005 card: a teal that chapter 9 already dated, a wipeable grain, a hangtag that claimed a bleach ratio. The failure is not the grain. The failure is the stitch, the boxed edge, the place a hip lands twice a meal. Environmental services has a bottle in the closet that does not match the card. The card is a manufacturer’s wish with a dilution printed on it. The bottle is whatever the night cart held after 2020 made every surface an argument (chapter 10). This essay is about construction. Chapter 10 is the bleach number as history. Here the problem is how a chair is skinned, what a moisture barrier is when it is a class rather than a miracle, and why a Wyzenbeek count on a sell sheet never met a welt.

Healthcare vinyl, polyurethane, and Crypton-class moisture-barrier fabrics are not three SKUs. They are three ways a seat tries to be a floor. A Medline-era punchout could offer all three on the same page, in the same mauve-to-gray march, and a Medilodge-class buyer could specify “bleach-cleanable” without naming a polymer. The wing received whatever the package still funded after the bed and the nightstand took the capital (chapter 23). Upholstery is where that shrinking number shows. It is also where EVS, not purchasing, finishes the spec.

## What the face is made of

Healthcare vinyl, in the late-twentieth-century SNF, is usually a PVC skin over a knit or a nonwoven, often with a foam backing, sometimes with a printed grain meant to look like something a living room would have owned. It wipes. That is the job. Polyurethane skins — the “PU leather” language of later cards — promised a softer hand and a different chemical story. Some of them hydrolyze. Some of them survive. A card that does not name a polymer is not a specification; it is a color. Crypton, as a brand begun in the 1990s, and the class of fabrics that copied its pitch, sold a woven face with a moisture barrier laminated or coated on the back: the cup tips, the barrier holds, the fabric can still be a tweed. In a hospital lobby that fabric could look like furniture. In a SNF dining room it still had to take a gait belt and a nightly wipe.

The card is the document purchasing files. Typical healthcare vinyl cards in the 2000s claimed a 1:10 bleach dilution as a cleanable boast `[VERIFY per fabric and year]`. That ratio is household language, not a statute. EPA List N, when it arrived as a public COVID disinfectant list, named chemistries that could be used against SARS-CoV-2 on hard surfaces. It was not a furniture standard and it did not bless a welt. Chapter 10 already walked the nightly argument: isolation rooms, plastic on the lounge, the chair that survived 2020 and the chair that did not. Construction predicts some of that. A vinyl with a thick skin and a well-adhered backing can take more of a bleach wipe than a thin PU that was specified because it felt residential. A Crypton-class barrier can keep a spill out of the foam and still stain at the stitch holes. The face is not the chair.

Wyzenbeek double-rubs — ASTM D4157, an oscillatory-cylinder abrasion test — appear on cards as a large number. 30,000. 100,000. 250,000. Treat those as marketing counts unless a lab report for that lot is in the file `[VERIFY any named figure]`. The test rubs a dry or wet abradant across a taut specimen. It does not sit a 200-pound person on a welt four times a day. It does not catch a name-bracelet. It does not stand in for a bleach dwell time. BIFMA seating standards (X5.1, X5.4) are cousins: they cycle a seat and an arm. They are not healthcare law. A dining chair that passed a double-rub boast and failed F558’s sit-to-stand common sense (chapter 26) was never tested for the job the wing actually assigned.

Mauve and teal vinyl of the 1980s–90s, honey-oak adjacent in the 2000s, then gray woodgrain and “contract sage” after the culture-change catalogs learned a new palette (chapter 9) — those are finish eras. They are not construction eras. The same boxed welt was sewn through all three. The same staple line held a vinyl to a plywood seat. Thermofoil on the casegoods changed (chapter 37). The chair’s problem stayed a textile problem: a skin, a foam, a thread, an edge.

## The stitch is the hole

A welt is a cord in a bias wrap, sewn into a seam so the edge looks decided. It is also a capillary. Liquid follows the stitch. Bleach, urine, thickened drink, the spray that was supposed to stay on the face for the contact time on the bottle — they find the needle holes. The foam behind a split welt is not a mystery. It is the interior the card never photographed. Once the foam is open, EVS is no longer wiping a vinyl. It is wiping a sponge that lives under a skin.

Staples along a seat underside are the other hole. They rust. They pull. A chair that is flipped for a “deep clean” shows a forest of rusted crowns and a dust cover that was torn off in 2011 and never replaced. Healthcare vinyl that “can be wiped” was often stapled to the same particleboard or plywood that swells when the wipe is actually a pour (chapter 37). The upholstery card and the casegood spec are different binders. They meet at the seat deck.

Piping around an arm, a decorative button, a welt that is a different color from the field — those are waterlines. Memory-care seating that wanted a homelike silhouette (chapter 31) often kept the welt because a club chair without one looked like a waiting-room reject. The wipeable club chair is a contradiction sewn into an edge. Ligature talk belongs in hospital psychiatry, not as a SNF fashion; the relevant furniture fact is simpler. Anything that can be picked, soaked, or used as a pocket will be. A moisture-barrier fabric with a good back still has a seam. A molded polyurethane seat (Kwalu-class skins, chapter 37) has fewer seams and a different failure: gouge, fade, a joint where the shell meets a frame.

Thread choice is rarely on the punchout line. Polyester versus cotton-wrap, UV, bleach resistance — factory defaults. The thread that failed on the teal dining chair was not specified by the DON. It was specified by a price. Replacement chairs arrived with a different thread and the same card claim. A Medilodge-era multi-building buy could put three stitch qualities on one corridor because replacements came one seat at a time from whoever still had the color. Corporate finish boards match faces. They do not match needle holes.

## What EVS poured, and what the foam drank

Environmental services in a SNF is a cart, a bottle, a rag or a wipe, and a schedule that does not read ASTM. The hangtag might say use quaternary ammonia, or a 1:10 bleach, or “see care card.” The closet might have a concentrated bleach, a hospital disinfectant with a long name, and a leftover bottle from the last GPO cycle. After 2020 the closet had more bottles and less patience. List N gave administrators a public list to point at. It did not tell anyone how to dry a welt.

Dwell time is the quiet variable. A card that survives a wipe does not survive a soak. Vinyl that craze-cracks — a fine network of lines in the skin — often did so where the sun hit a day-room arm and the wipe was bleach, repeated. Polyurethane that got sticky often did so where hydrolysis and cleaner met. Crypton-class fabrics that matted often did so where a brush was used because a stain sat in the weave. None of those sentences is a product recall. They are wear. The chair that survived 2020 in chapter 10 was sometimes just the chair that was reupholstered in 2018 with a heavier vinyl and fewer buttons. Construction, not virtue.

Foam is part of the fire system (chapter 39) and part of the moisture system. A barrier fabric is supposed to keep the foam dry. A split welt undoes the barrier without asking the card. Cal 117-2013 is a smolder test for the assembly; it is not a urine test. Purchasing that specified “moisture barrier” and then approved a stapled vinyl seat without a deck liner got half a chair. The other half is the plywood drinking what the welt admitted.

Bariatric seating (chapter 30) and geri-chair vinyl (chapter 25) take the same chemistry at higher load and longer dwell. A recliner tray that is wiped in place drips into a hinge. A dining chair that is stood on its seat for mopping — it happens — puts cleaner on the underside staples. The card assumed a seated wipe. The building assumed a floor. Between them the welt split.

## Cards, closets, and the chair that is still in the room

The Medline-era book sold cleanability as a finish, the way it sold oak as a homelike story. A punchout line that read “healthcare vinyl, bleach cleanable” was doing channel work: it let a corporate buyer in Washington Township or any peer city check a box that would survive a later DON question. It did not bind EVS. It did not bind the night bottle. House-brand chairs and Nemschoff-class lounge pieces (chapter 29) could carry equally confident cards and fail in different places — the house brand at the staple, the healthcare-furniture house at a zippered cushion someone put in a washer. Class is not destiny. Sewing is.

A faded capacity sticker (chapter 43) and a split welt often live on the same chair. The sticker is a test number. The welt is a history of wipes. Families see the foam and think neglect. Surveyors see the foam and may think infection control or dignity, depending on the day and the room. Maintenance sees a chair that can be stapled one more time or tagged for the courtyard shed (chapter 34). Purchasing sees a line that is easier to replace than to reupholster, until the color is discontinued and the replacement is gray. The wing then has a teal chair with a split welt and a gray chair with a welt that has not split yet. That is a finish era meeting a construction era in the same dining room.

The residual fact is small enough to hold. The upholstery card and the night bottle are different documents. They always were. 2020 made the difference loud. It did not invent it. A reader who wants a chair that “can take bleach” is still asking the wrong object. The chair can take a wipe if the skin, the stitch, the deck, and the bottle are in the same story. Most Medline-era packages bought the skin and hoped. The welt is where hope opened. The next essay leaves the textile and goes to the casegood skin — HPL, TFL, thermofoil, a molded poly that tried to retire the edge band — and to the nightstand corner that has already swollen.

## Notes

- EPA List N is a disinfectant class for a named virus era; it is not a standard for vinyl, polyurethane, or moisture-barrier fabrics.
- ASTM D4157 (Wyzenbeek) and BIFMA X5.1 / X5.4 are test methods / seating-performance cousins. Double-rub numbers on cards are marketing unless a lot-specific report is cited. Flag figures `[VERIFY]`.
- Bleach 1:10 as a “common card claim” is a channel habit of the 2000s healthcare-vinyl file, not a universal factory setting. Verify per fabric.
- Crypton is a brand (1990s origin in trade histories) `[CITE NEEDED]` for a dated founding sentence; “Crypton-class” here means woven face plus moisture barrier as a construction type.
- CAL TB 117-2013: smolder, cover-and-filling as a system. See chapter 39. Do not treat a bleach card as a fire card.
- Chapter 10 holds the 2020 disinfection history. This chapter holds construction: skin, stitch, foam, deck.
