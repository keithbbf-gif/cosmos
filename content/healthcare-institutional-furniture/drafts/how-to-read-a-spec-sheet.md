# How to read a spec sheet

A spec sheet is a short story with an agenda.

It will tell you a width. It will tell you a finish name. It will tell you a list of standards that may be cousins of the job. It will not tell you the rod height unless someone made it. It will not tell you the wipe. It will not tell you the leftover in your double.

I read sheets the way I read a punchout: **start with what is missing.**

## Two documents that never quite match

The Medline-as-a-channel draft explained the route. The catalog-versus-mill draft explained the marriage. This draft is the reading method.

A distributor sheet is a sales object. Title, JPEG, three finishes, a vinyl name that will change, a width, a lead time written by a hopeful person, a logo stack. It cannot carry a leftover. It cannot carry a lift. It cannot carry Thursday’s two inches. It cannot carry the wipe you actually use. It cannot carry who holds the key.

A mill shop drawing is a build object. Rod height. Interior finish. Edge radius. A note that the headboard does not join the bed. A lock option. A crown that can be cut. A finish system, not a color word. A scrap to test. It cannot carry the GPO memory. It cannot carry the truck. It cannot carry the account.

The grown-up Medline-era job used the page as a door and the drawing as a contract. The dishonest job used the page as the contract. The mill found out the rod was wrong when the daughter hung a dress on a chair.

I would rather have a shop drawing with rod heights than a beautiful sheet with a lifestyle photo. If you only have the sheet, write the missing inches in the margin and send it back. The margin is the beginning of a specification.

Hill-Rom’s Batesville story and the postwar hospital cabinet trained the industry to ship a set: bed, cabinet, table, a page that looked like a page. Nursing homes inherited the set and then asked the page to also say homelike. OBRA 1987 made warehouse aesthetics a compliance problem. The sheet learned the word. The inches did not always follow. That is the history under every laundry list of standards that does not name an edition.

## Dimensions: which ones

I want:

- Overall W / D / H.
- Seat height **compressed**, or a note that the printed height is uncompressed.
- Arm height, seat depth, seat width.
- Rod height, shelf heights, door swing or slider.
- Table height, apron clearance, base type.
- Bed: low height, high height, deck width, capacity — on a **device** sheet, not a furniture sheet.
- Weight capacity and how it was made.

If seat height is missing on a chair sheet, the sheet is a picture. If rod height is missing on a wardrobe sheet, the sheet is a picture.

Catalog seat heights are often uncompressed foam in a showroom. A 19 that becomes a 16 after six months is a hole. The chair draft already owned a shop range in the 19-to-21-inch neighborhood, compressed. Sit. Measure. Write the compressed number. If the vendor will not let you sit, you are buying a JPEG.

Wardrobe depth is a closet or a cubby. A 12-inch box will hold folded things and a hook. It will not hold a hanging shirt without crushing. The wardrobe draft already owned a shop range of 20–24 inches if it is truly hanging. A sheet that says “closet” and shows a 12-inch elevation is a 12-inch lie.

Door swing is a leftover number. A sheet that shows handsome hinged doors and no swing radius has not met an 80-square-foot double. CMS still writes 80 per person in a multiple and 100 in a single. Those numbers live in 42 CFR 483.90. They are why the leftover is already spent.

Knee clearance on a table is a number or it is a rendering. The ADA draft already taught the 28-to-34 dining band as a teaching band. Whether your room is in that chapter is scoping. Whether the apron hits a knee is furniture. Write the apron.

Bed sheets that live on a furniture page are how Zone 3 gets a matching stain. Force the bed onto a device sheet: low height, high height, deck width, rail type, mattress match. Then force the case goods onto their own line. Then write who owns the interface.

## Materials: the inside

“Hardwood solids and veneers” is a start. I want the **core**, the **interior finish**, the **edge**. “Healthcare laminate” is a start. I want the substrate and the edge band.

“Healthcare vinyl” is a color. I want the product name, the chemical chart, the moisture barrier, the foam density, the cleanout.

If the inside is unnamed, assume raw.

Four constructions show up on honest institutional case goods, as the definition draft already named: particleboard or MDF with laminate and an edge; hardwood plywood or solid hardwood with a finish system and sealed interiors; polymer or urethane “wood”; steel and powdercoat. A sheet that will not say which one you are buying is a sheet that wants you to hear “wood” and see a print.

Edges are the wipe clock. A PVC edge that peels is a swell waiting. A hardwood edge that was never finished on the underside is a swell wearing maple. Radius is a shin and a wipe. A sharp laminate corner in a memory-care room is a bruise.

Upholstery is a fifth construction pretending to be a fabric choice. Healthcare vinyl, polyurethane, Crypton-class woven, the old wet-look — different animals. The cover is only half the chair. Foam density, moisture barrier, and whether the deck can be wiped after a void matter more than the swatch. A sheet that shows a swatch and no cleanout has specified a costume.

Chemical-resistance charts are object-specific. A chart that does not name **your** product is a costume. The infection-control draft already said the wipe is part of the bill of materials. Ask EVS what they spray, what they spray when they are busy, and what they sprayed during the last outbreak. Those are three chemistries. The sheet has to survive the third.

## Standards: the laundry list

A good list names editions. A bad list says “CAL 117, BIFMA, NFPA, ADA, CMS.”

Translate:

- 117 → which bulletin, 2013 or a ghost. TB 117-2013 is a smolder test. The federal floor is 16 CFR part 1640 as of June 25, 2021. A 2009 sheet that says 117 might mean the old open-flame bulletin. Ask for the report.
- BIFMA → which standard, which report. X5.1, X5.4, X5.5 are office and contract durability. They are not a SNF occupancy stamp. The BIFMA draft is the long version. A chair that survived an office cycle has survived an office cycle.
- NFPA → 101 edition? 701? 260? 261? E1537? Cubicle curtains live in 701 more often than in 117. Upholstered furniture lives in 260/261 and sometimes E1537 depending on occupancy and sprinklers. Open the edition on the last survey.
- ADA → a number or a vibe. Reach, clear floor, operable parts, dining height. Scoping is a legal job. A sheet that says “ADA compliant” without an inch is a vibe.
- CMS → a tag or a wish. F584 and F917 talk about furniture as furniture. They do not say maple. They do not say this SKU. A sheet that says “CMS approved” has said a sentence CMS did not write.

The fire draft and the BIFMA draft are the long versions. On the sheet, I put a check or a question mark. Question marks are allowed. Adjectives are not.

IEC 60601-2-52 is a bed-system book, not a nightstand book. Do not put it on a wardrobe sheet to make the wardrobe look clinical. Do not leave it off a bed sheet because the stain matches.

## Capacity and the plus sign

A number without a test is a plus sign. A plus sign is not a number.

A 250-pound chair in a building with a bariatric program is an incident. The bariatric draft will not pretend a + on a catalog page is a test. How was the number made? Who sat? How many times? Is it a static sticker or a cycle? If the vendor cannot say, you have a plus sign.

Beds have capacity on a device sheet. Furniture that will be used as a grab — a headboard, an arm, a wardrobe door — needs a refusal or a rating. I will not give you a load I have not tested. I will not let a sheet imply that a decorative rail is an assist.

## Options that are actually the spec

Lock yes/no, who holds the key. Casters vs. glides. Cleanout. Moisture barrier. Headboard that joins the bed or does not. Attic stock. Install.

If those are in a separate “call for details,” the sheet is a teaser.

Memory-care hardware is not a finish. If the option is a hidden latch, write who holds the key and how the latch becomes a drawer again when the care plan changes. The memory-care draft already refused to hide hardware so a surveyor has to discover it.

Finish names are not finish systems. A cherry print and a conversion varnish are not cousins. A sheet that lists four color words and no film chemistry has listed a photograph.

Attic stock and install belong on the sheet or on the quote that the sheet pretends to be. The channel-economics draft already said the landed, installed, wiped, punched number is the only number a building should use. A sheet without those lines is a JPEG.

## Photographs

A styled room with a residential lamp and no roommate is a fiction. Look at the object in isolation. Look at the back. If they will not show the back, the back is unfinished.

A hand on the arm for scale is useful. A smiling model is not.

I will not use a photograph of a resident. The style guide already said so. A sheet that uses one is a sheet with an agenda I do not share.

Matching grain across a wardrobe, a bedside, and a headboard is cheap to photograph and expensive to maintain. Vinyl programs change. Laminate lots change. A replacement bedside two years later will be “close.” Close in a double is a roommate story. The honest sheet matches heights and edges, not mythology.

## Channel sheets vs. mill sheets

A Medline-era distributor sheet is a sales object. A mill shop drawing is a build object. I would rather have a shop drawing with rod heights than a beautiful sheet with a lifestyle photo.

Bradley Brand could live in the honest marriage: the page as a door, the drawing as a contract. We could not live in a JPEG. A lot of wood still got into buildings because someone, once, asked for the drawing.

If the drawer says a distributor and the box says Warren, both sentences can be true. Read the label as a route. The sheet you are holding may be the face’s sheet. The inches may live on a drawing the face never posted. Ask for the drawing.

House brand, named mill, special-buy — three ways a mill’s work showed up. The sheet’s logo is the face the DON already had a number for. It is not a factory. Do not read a logo as a species of wood.

## A one-page checklist

I keep this on a card:

1. Compressed seat / rod / table / low-bed heights.
2. Capacity + test.
3. Interior finish / barrier / cleanout.
4. Wipe named or “to be tested.”
5. 1640 / 117-2013; 101 edition; 701 if textile.
6. Lock logic.
7. Whether any wood joins the bed system.
8. Install, freight, attic stock.

If I cannot tick those, I do not have a sheet. I have an ad.

Write the missing inches in the margin. Send it back. If they will not fill the blanks, you have a rendering. Renderings are how a double loses a rod and a bed gains a gap.

The series will keep returning to this move: start with what is missing. Architecture first, then furniture, then fabric. A sheet that starts with fabric and never arrives at an inch has told you its agenda. Believe it.
