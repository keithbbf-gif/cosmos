# Mauve Vinyl and Oak Laminate

*Educational history of healthcare and nursing-home furniture. Not a purchasing specification for a named building, not a survey opinion, and not clinical advice about beds, rails, or seating for a particular resident.*

The color chip lives in a binder. A vinyl fan deck, or a laminate sample the size of a playing card, or both clipped to a spec that says *resident room, oak, accent seating*. The mauve is not quite pink and not quite gray. The teal is the one that photographed as “healthcare” for a decade. The honey oak is a printed wood that never grew. Flip forward and the chips go pewter, driftwood, “weathered” gray that is still a vinyl you can bleach. Finish eras date a wing the way wallpaper dates a kitchen. A 2005 Medilodge-era room — oak box, teal seat, cream wall — is a year you can still walk into without a name on the door.

Post-OBRA *homelike* (chapter 5) arrived as a survey adjective and left as a laminate story. No F-tag names honey oak. Factories and distributors did. They printed wood on particleboard, wrapped profile edges in thermofoil that would peel, and called the result residential. The mauve and teal were the seating half of the same sentence: colors that hid wear, matched a border, and told a family this was not a 1970s orange day room. Later gray woodgrain is not an escape from the sentence. It is the sentence in a colder ink.

## Making a year you can wipe

Healthcare vinyl in the 1980s and 1990s was a chemistry and a fashion. The chemistry is chapter 36: a face, a foam, a backing, a welt that fails first. The fashion is this chapter. Mauve, dusty rose, teal, jade, a burgundy that was supposed to be dignified. Patterns ran from faux tweed to a tiny floral that died at ten feet. The point of the palette was not home. It was *not hospital green* and *not motel rust*. It was also not a sofa you would take from a relative’s house. It was a chip that a mill would still be running when the next chair was ordered.

Oak laminate — TFL, thermally fused, a paper print under a melamine wear layer — is how casegoods joined the palette without paying for HPL on every nightstand (chapter 37). Honey, golden, “traditional.” A cathedral grain that repeats every few boards if you look. Edge band in a matching or almost-matching PVC. Thermofoil, the wrapped vinyl-like skin over an MDF profile, made the drawer front look thicker and the bullnose look expensive. Then the thermofoil lifted at the sink splash, at the bleach wipe, at the cart corner, and the MDF went to pulp. Making a homelike box was a print and a wrap. Wearing it was water.

Headboards learned the same print. A medical frame with a laminate insert in oak or cherry is Batesville’s argument (chapter 2) at a Medilodge-era price. The cream wall — builder beige, healthcare cream, a yellow that is not yellow — is the third chip, the one nobody specified because the painter already had it. Oak plus teal plus cream is a 2000s room even when the bed is a later low-low. Gray plus gray plus white is a 2010s refresh that still wipes. The wall is why both look like facilities. Change the wall and the same oak becomes a different decade. No one changed the wall.

Specifying a palette for a multi-building buyer meant locking three chips and hoping the mills would honor them for five years. They would not. Vinyl houses discontinue a color the way paint houses do, only the chair is still on the floor. Laminate printers shift a grain. The binder’s chip is then a lie. Chapter 8’s mismatched replacement is this chemistry plus this fashion. Corporate can freeze a board. The mill will not freeze a run for a Michigan portfolio’s leftover teal.

## Buying the era, wearing the peel

A Medline-era punchout page sold finish as a drop-down. Oak, cherry, mahogany, maple; then the grays. Vinyl: grade A, grade B, a pattern name that sounded like a suburb. CAL 117 on the hangtag (chapter 39) traveled with the chair regardless of mauve or teal. The fire card is not a color. Buyers treated the drop-down as the homelike decision. It was the cheap decision. Real wood, real textile, a painted dresser from a family — those were the other decision, and EVS would not sign them.

Medilodge-class purchasing used the drop-down as a standard and the wing as a remainder bin. New rooms, when capital existed, got the current oak and the current vinyl. Old rooms kept the last oak and the last vinyl. A 2005 double that still had its original package in 2012 was a matched set: honey box, teal seat, cream wall, a cubicle curtain in a coordinating print if the textile had not been replaced after a stain. A 2005 double that had lost two chairs and a nightstand was a museum. The replacement gray woodgrain nightstand next to the honey dresser is the date stamp.

Wearing the palette is particular. Mauve vinyl browns in the seat well and shines on the arms. Teal goes black in the welts. Oak TFL does not fade so much as chip, and then the particleboard line is a dark crescent. Thermofoil peels in a flap you can pick. HPL, when someone paid for it on a high-use top, loses its edge band first. The cream wall takes the hip print of every cart. None of that is a Wyzenbeek number you can invent. It is what a corridor shows. `[VERIFY]` any double-rub count against a named card; do not put a number here that was not read.

Culture-change buyers (chapter 6) tried to leave the palette by buying “residential” colors: a warmer brown, a leaf print, a sofa that was gold instead of teal. The vinyl still shone. The laminate still repeated. The living-room grouping at the end of the corridor was the same era in a different silhouette. Green House hearths that used real wood and fabric were the exception that proved the channel: those objects did not come from the punchout drop-down. They came from a different store and a different EVS argument.

Hospitals ran a parallel fashion with more money. A Nemschoff-class waiting room (chapter 29) could do a quieter textile and a better wood, or a better fake. SNF lobbies often bought the book: the same mauve as the dining room, a ficus, a console in oak TFL. The lobby was supposed to be the homelike advertisement. It was the chip at a larger scale. Families who said “it smells like a nursing home” were sometimes smelling disinfectant and sometimes seeing teal on cream and calling the combination a smell.

Making the print itself is a mill schedule. Cathedral oak is a cylinder. Repeat it across a truck of nightstands and the grain marches if you line the drawers up. Specifying “random plank” later was a hospitality fashion that SNF punchouts only half adopted; many house-brand tops stayed the old cathedral because the plant still had the paper. Buying a “cherry” insert next to an “oak” cabinet was a drop-down accident that a 2005 room could not hide under a cream wall. Wearing that accident is a family comment: *why don’t they match?* The honest answer is the binder had two cards and the clerk keyed one.

Hardware took the palette personally. Oak-look plastic pulls tried to disappear. Brass-look pulls tried to look like a house and went dull in bleach. Black pulls arrived with the gray woodgrain and made the leftover honey box look suddenly orange. Bumper edges in a “wood” brown scuffed to a cream core. Chapter 40 will take the missing pull. Here the pull is a chip. It dates the drawer as surely as the TFL. A Medilodge-era wardrobe with a honey door and a black replacement pull is a sentence about years, not about taste.

CAL 117 hangtags (chapter 39) and bleach cards (chapter 10) traveled through every color. Mauve was not less fire-tested than gray. Teal was not more bleachable than pebble. Fashion likes to imply improvement. The hangtag does not. A later “performance vinyl” in fog gray could be the same face chemistry with a new print cylinder. Purchasing that paid a premium for the new color as if it were a new construction was buying a year. Construction is a different chapter. The year is this one.

## What 2005 looked like, and what replaced it without replacing it

A composite, not a named wing: two electric beds with oak-look inserts. Two overbed tables, maybe not the same height class. Two bedside cabinets, honey TFL, one drawer, a plastic liner. A wardrobe or a closet with a too-high rod. Teal vinyl visitor chairs, or one chair and a leftover. Cubicle curtain in a floral that shares a pigment with the chairs. Cream wall, wood-look overbed tray in a printed oak that does not match the cabinet by a shade. Television on a bracket. That is a 2005 Medilodge-era room. It is also a 1998 room and a 2010 room if the capital never came.

What replaced it, when something replaced it, was often a single object in the new chip. Gray woodgrain overbed. “Driftwood” nightstand. A chair in a pattern called pebble or fog. The cream wall stayed. The beds stayed until a fall program bought a low-low (chapter 12) whose headboard insert was the new gray. The room then contained three finish years and one adjective, homelike, that had licensed all of them. Surveyors who wrote that furniture did not reflect home were looking at a catalog history, not a house.

Making the later gray is not a moral improvement. It is a mill following hospitality and office furniture into a cooler print. Specifying gray as “more residential” is a 2010s meeting. Wearing gray is the same welt split, the same edge peel, the same cream wall. Buying gray on a punchout next to a still-listed oak is how a chain gets both eras in one order. The binder’s chip, by then, has a gray card clipped in front of the mauve. No one throws the mauve away. Someone might need to match a recliner that will not die.

## Residual cream

The chip in the binder is more honest than the room. It admits it is a sample. The room pretends the sample is a home. Mauve and teal were the seating years when OBRA’s dignity talk needed a color that was not restraint-ward chrome. Honey oak was the casegood year when homelike needed a wood and would not pay for one. Gray is the current year until it is not. The cream wall is the constant that makes the fashion readable.

Nightingale did not specify a pigment. Batesville specified stain on real wood. The Medline book specified a drop-down. A Medilodge-class spec sheet specified oak and teal and then met a truck of gray. The peel at the thermofoil edge is the date that will not scrape off. The next essay takes a different card out of the same binder: the vinyl mill’s bleach ratio, already printed before 2020, and the nightly argument that year made of it.
