# Seven Zones, Four Limits

*Educational history of healthcare and nursing-home furniture. Not a purchasing specification for a named building, not a survey opinion, and not clinical advice about beds, rails, or seating for a particular resident.*

The 10 March 2006 FDA guidance draws seven openings in a hospital bed system and then writes dimensional limits for only four of them. The drawing is a plan view and a side view of a deck, a mattress, a rail, a headboard, a footboard. The openings have numbers. Zone 1 sits inside the rail. Zone 2 sits under the rail between the supports. Zone 3 is the long slit between rail and mattress. Zone 4 is the space under the rail at the ends. Zones 5, 6, and 7 continue around the split, the rail end, and the mattress end — and then the document stops giving inches. That stop is the essay. The federal page is not a statute, not a furniture catalog, and not a complete map of every gap a later foam or a later low-low deck would open. It is a guidance document that tried to make a death file into a picture a manufacturer and a facility could share.

The file behind the picture is dated. The guidance works from about 390 reports, January 1985 through March 2000, of people caught, trapped, entangled, or strangled in hospital beds. The Hospital Bed Safety Workgroup — HBSW in the footnotes — sat with FDA, manufacturers, clinicians, and advocates and sorted those reports into the seven openings. Zones 1 through 4 accounted for about 80 percent of the reports. That is why the dimensional criteria attach to 1–4 and why 5–7 wait. Later FDA pages added later years. The 2006 document is the one that put the seven zones on a page that a Medline-era spec sheet could point at without becoming law.

## A workgroup, a drawing, a word that is not “approved”

HBSW is not a brand. It is a 1990s–2000s table. Hospital-bed entrapment had been a known class of adverse event for a generation; ECRI and FDA notices had already told hospitals that rails and mattresses and headboards made a *system*. What the workgroup added was a shared geometry and a pair of tools — a cone and a cylinder — that could be used, for zones 1–4, to ask whether an opening was small enough to keep a head out or large enough not to catch a neck. This essay will not write a measurement recipe. The tools exist in the guidance as a method for people who already have a reason to assess a particular bed. Magazine history only needs the fact that the method was written for four zones, not seven, and that it treats the mattress as part of the opening (chapter 16).

FDA’s own sentence at the front of such documents is easy to skip and expensive to skip. Guidance represents the agency’s current thinking. It does not bind the public the way a statute binds, and it does not “approve” a furniture line. Beds in this story are medical devices; most casegoods are not. A punchout page that said “meets FDA 2006” was making a manufacturer’s claim about a dimensional assessment, not waving a federal sticker. A Medilodge-era buyer who filed the PDF next to the CAL 117 hangtag (chapter 39) was filing two different kinds of paper. One is a California smolder test on a chair. The other is a federal *thinking* about openings in a bed system. Neither one is a purchase order.

The *Federal Register* notice is 71 FR 12369. The document title is *Hospital Bed System Dimensional and Assessment Guidance to Reduce Entrapment*. The date is 10 March 2006. Those three facts are the ones to keep when a later catalog collapses them into “FDA approved rails.” There is no such sentence in the guidance. There is a drawing, a percentage, a report count, and a set of recommended dimensions for four openings.

## The seven, in the order the page numbered them

Zone 1 is within the rail — the space inside the perimeter of a single rail panel or between the bars of an older open rail. If the opening is large enough for a head, the rail is not a fence. It is a window. Older full-length rails with widely spaced bars made Zone 1 obvious. Later “assist” panels with smaller cutouts moved the problem into the other zones without abolishing it.

Zone 2 is under the rail, between the supports, or next to a single support. It is the long cave a person can slide into if the mattress compresses or if the rail sits high relative to the deck. Hi-lo travel (chapter 3) and low-low decks (chapter 12) change the relationship between rail and floor; they also change how a body meets Zone 2. The guidance is a still picture. The SNF bed is not still.

Zone 3 is between the rail and the mattress — the slit the fist fits. This is the zone that made mattress purchasing into safety purchasing. A foam that is too narrow, too soft at the edge, or too short for the deck opens Zone 3 on a frame that “passed” when it shipped with its original surface. Chapter 16 stays there. The 2006 page already knew the mattress was a contributor or a mitigator. It did not know every overlay a wound-care vendor would later drop on a Carroll-class or Joerns-class deck from a different book.

Zone 4 is under the rail at the ends — the space where the rail stops and the mattress or the deck continues. Half-rails and three-quarter rails, the language of the later spec sheet, live here. The end of the rail is a corner. Corners catch. The guidance gives Zone 4 a dimensional pair: small enough, or large enough, with a dangerous middle. The exact inches belong to the document, not to a hallway recipe.

Zone 5 is between split rails — the gap in the middle when a head-end rail and a foot-end rail both sit on one side. Split rails were sold as a transfer convenience and as a less “caged” look after OBRA made full rails a restraint argument (chapter 15). The opening between them is Zone 5. The 2006 page maps it and does not write a limit.

Zone 6 is between the end of the rail and the side edge of the headboard or footboard. Wood-look panels and laminate inserts (chapter 17) change the thickness and the radius of that board. A thin steel headboard and a thick “residential” panel are not the same Zone 6.

Zone 7 is between the headboard or footboard and the end of the mattress. A mattress that has migrated, compressed, or been replaced with a shorter therapeutic surface opens Zone 7 on a bed whose rails were never the problem. Again the page draws the opening and withholds the number.

The withheld numbers are not an accident. HBSW’s report file did not give the workgroup the same confidence about 5–7 that it gave them about 1–4. About 80 percent of the 390 reports sat in the first four openings. The guidance says so, in prose that catalogs later shortened to “FDA zones.” A Medilodge-era in-service poster that showed all seven and then listed inches for all seven was already past the document. The document is more modest than the poster.

## What a standard did later, and what a wing did anyway

IEC 60601-2-38 had been the particular standard for powered hospital beds — an electrical and mechanical path, not a seven-zone poster. IEC 60601-2-52 is the later particular standard for medical beds. A 2015 consolidated edition (Ed. 1.1) is the one FDA-recognition tables commonly point at `[VERIFY]`. 60601-2-52 is a manufacturer and test-lab document. It is not a CMS F-tag. It is not the 2006 guidance. A bed can be discussed under all three papers — guidance, particular standard, survey tag — without those papers being the same genre.

The SNF floor did not wait for the IEC number. Carroll-class, Invacare-class, Joerns-class, and Medline house-brand frames already sat on Michigan wings when the PDF landed. Some arrived with full rails in a box. Some arrived with half-rails as a default, because the word *restraint* had been in the building since OBRA 1987. Some arrived with “assist bars” that were neither the 2006 rail in the drawing nor a transfer handle a therapist would sign. Purchasing filed the guidance because surveyors had started asking whether anyone had thought about entrapment. Filing is not assessing. Assessing a named resident is a clinical act this series will not script.

What the wing actually did with the drawing was smaller and more furniture-like. Maintenance looked at a later mattress that had shrunk from the deck. Someone ordered rail pads from the same book that sold the overbed table. Someone took the foot-end rail off and left the head-end rail up, inventing a Zone 5 that the original shipment had not had. Someone put a low-low deck under an old rail kit and opened a Zone 2 the 2006 still picture did not show at that height. The guidance is a system document. The channel is a parts channel. Those two facts do not dissolve each other. They explain why a Medilodge-class corridor in 2008 could hold a bed that “knew about FDA” in the file and still showed a fist-wide slit at the mattress (chapter 16).

Hillenbrand’s wood-and-metal idea (chapter 2, chapter 17) complicates the drawing in a way the workgroup could not freeze. A decorative panel changes Zone 6 and Zone 7. A pendant hook on the headboard is not a zone, but it is a protrusion the drawing does not inventory. A trapeze socket (chapter 18) adds hardware at the head end. The 2006 page is a bed *system* — deck, mattress, rails, head, foot — and still not every object the book sold to hang on that system.

## Guidance on a clipboard, statute in another binder

The temptation, after 2006, was to treat the PDF as a rule. It is not a rule. It did not rewrite 42 CFR. It did not publish a furniture list for F917. CMS later wrote F700 / §483.25(n) in a different voice: alternatives, assessment, consent, correct size and weight, manufacturer instructions (chapter 15). That tag can point a surveyor toward the *idea* of entrapment. It does not enact the HBSW inches. A facility can fail F700 without failing a Zone 3 measurement, and the reverse can be imagined. This series keeps the papers in their genres. Guidance thinks. A particular standard tests. A tag surveys. A punchout sells.

The residual is the withheld four-fifths of the drawing. Seven openings. Numbers for four. A report file that stopped, for the purpose of those numbers, in March 2000. Everything after — low-low decks as a fall program, air overlays, house-brand mattresses from a different SKU family, wood-look boards, split rails sold as dignity — had to live in the three zones that never got inches and in the four that did, on mattresses the original shipment no longer had. The next essay takes the rail as a word problem: assist, half, three-quarter, restraint. The drawing will still be on the wall. The care plan will not be a dimension.

## Notes

- FDA, *Hospital Bed System Dimensional and Assessment Guidance to Reduce Entrapment* (10 March 2006); 71 FR 12369. Guidance represents FDA’s current thinking; it is not a statute and does not “approve” a bed line.
- About 390 reports, January 1985 through March 2000, cited in the HBSW / 2006 apparatus. Later FDA public pages add later years; do not collapse the windows.
- Dimensional criteria in the 2006 document attach to zones 1–4 only, described there as about 80 percent of the reports. Zones 5–7 are identified without those recommended limits.
- Zone list (guidance language, condensed): (1) within the rail; (2) under the rail between supports or beside a single support; (3) between rail and mattress; (4) under the rail at the ends; (5) between split rails; (6) rail end to headboard or footboard; (7) headboard or footboard to mattress end.
- HBSW cone and cylinder tools are assessment methods for zones 1–4 in the document — not a hallway protocol in this essay.
- IEC 60601-2-38 (historical powered beds); IEC 60601-2-52 (medical beds; 2015 Ed. 1.1 commonly cited; FDA-recognized consensus standard). Manufacturer / test-lab genre, not an F-tag.
