# Curtain, Screen, Roommate

*Educational history of healthcare and nursing-home furniture. Not a purchasing specification for a named building, not a survey opinion, and not clinical advice about beds, rails, or seating for a particular resident.*

The curtain in a Michigan double room does not close. One carrier has jumped the aluminum track. The hem has caught on the overbed table’s U-base. The last eighteen inches hang short of the wall because the laundry returned the panel a size smaller than the room, or because the room was always a size larger than the panel. Between the two electric beds there is a gap you can see a television through. Privacy, in this building type, is a textile hung from the ceiling. It is not a wall. It never was.

That is the furniture problem this essay stays with. Chapter 1 treated the cubicle curtain as the ward screen that moved overhead and stayed. Chapter 23 sold a resident-room package that still assumed two beds. Here the object is the cloth, the folding screen it replaced, and the roommate who is the other half of every purchase. A Medline-era punchout will list a flame-rated cubicle curtain as a line under “room accessories,” as if it were a wastebasket. On the floor it is the cheapest private room the operator ever bought, and the one that fails every night in the same place.

## Nightingale’s curtains and the ward’s screens

Florence Nightingale’s 1859 book is hostile to bed curtains. *Notes on Nursing* calls the old four-post bed with hangings “utterly inadmissible, whether for sick or well.” She wanted air through the mattress and light on the face. A textile around a bed was, in her sentences, a dust trap and a stagnant pocket. The iron bedstead she preferred was narrow, springy, and unenclosed. Anyone who quotes her as a patron of the cubicle curtain has not read the chapter on bed and bedding.

The ward still needed a visual break. Dressings, dying, a body being washed — those were not public events even in a hall of thirty beds. The answer was not a drape on the bed. It was a folding screen: hinges, a fabric or painted panel, a footprint on the floor. A screen is furniture. It can be counted on an inventory. It can be stacked in a closet, stolen for a wedding in the chapel, or left in a corridor where it becomes a fire-load and a trip. Nightingale’s world used screens because the architecture would not give a room. The later American hospital and the purpose-built nursing home used the same logic with a cheaper geometry. Hang a track. Clip a cloth. Call the pair a semi-private room.

The screen did not disappear when the track arrived. Some converted houses kept a wooden tri-fold in the linen closet into the 1980s. Some hospitals kept mobile screens for procedures that a hanging curtain could not surround. The Medline/Medilodge-era SNF mostly stopped buying the floor object. Floor objects occupy turning radius. A ceiling track occupies nothing a surveyor measures with a tape until the cloth is missing. That is why the curtain won. It is also why the curtain is always a little wrong. A screen has a width. A curtain has a track length that someone estimated from a reflected ceiling plan, plus a mesh header for the sprinklers, plus a body panel that shrank, plus a roommate’s furniture that was never in the drawing.

## The cheap private room

A purpose-built double on a Michigan wing — not a named building, a type — is a box with two medical decks, two nightstands, a shared toilet, and a ceiling track that runs between the beds or around one of them. F584 still talks about drapes and personal items as part of a safe, clean, comfortable, homelike environment. F917 still wants a bed, a mattress, functional furniture, and a closet. Neither tag names the cubicle curtain as a finish SKU. Visual privacy while a person occupies a bed is the expectation the cloth is asked to meet. The statute did not publish a hem length.

The construction is stubbornly consistent across distributors. An aluminum or painted-steel track, surface-mounted or recessed. Carriers that click and then wear oval. A mesh or open-weave header, often specified so that a sprinkler can see the fire — NFPA 13 coordination language that shows up in Life Safety notes as an eighteen-inch drop below the deflector or a seventy-percent-open top panel `[VERIFY the edition a given building was surveyed under]`. A body fabric sold as flame-resistant. NFPA 101 §10.3.1 points loosely hanging curtains at NFPA 701, the textile flame-propagation test. That is the fire card for this object, not CAL TB 117, which is a smolder test for upholstered seating (chapter 39). The hangtag on a cubicle curtain and the hangtag on a day-room sofa are different documents. Purchasing sometimes filed them in the same binder because both said “fire.”

Colors followed the vinyl eras of chapter 9. Mauve and teal prints in the 1990s. Then a beige “residential” leaf that still read as hospital. Then a gray weave that photographed better for the website. The cloth was always wipeable in the catalog and always a laundry problem on the floor. A panel that goes out stained comes back shorter, or does not come back, and the room lives with a borrowed curtain from a vacant bed. Mesh yellows. Snaps fail. The carrier that jumped the track is the one nearest the door, because that is where the overbed table and the visitor chair fight the hem.

What the book never quite said is that the curtain is the room divider for two people who did not choose each other. Every other piece in the package can be specified as a pair: two beds, two cabinets, two chairs if the capital number held. The curtain is singular. It has two faces. One face is the resident whose bed is nearer the window. The other is the resident whose bed is nearer the toilet. The furniture plan is not complete until you know which side the track favors and where the gap will open. A private room abolishes the object. A Medilodge-class operator buying for fifteen buildings in the years around 2011 still had a census that was mostly doubles. The curtain stayed in the package. It was cheaper than a wall.

## The roommate as a furniture specification

Walk the double as a purchasing problem and the roommate appears in every dimension. The wardrobe rod that F917 wants reachable (chapter 21) is reachable for one person and a coat-storage for the other. The visitor chair that became a sleeper (chapter 22) blocks the gait space on the window side and leaves the door side with a stacking chair from dining. The television is on the only wall a picture might have used, which means one person looks at it in profile and the other looks at the curtain. The nightstand drawer that holds the life (chapter 20) is on the aisle or against the wall depending on who was admitted first. None of this is a care-plan aside. It is the reason the cubicle curtain exists. The building sold two beds into one box and then asked a textile to finish the architecture.

Culture-change writing after Eden and the Green House Project (chapter 6) treated the double as the problem. Household models and private rooms were the reform objects. Furniture sales followed the language more than the plan. A Medline-era “residential” headboard and an oak-look nightstand could be ordered for a double as easily as for a private. The curtain was the tell. If the room still needed one, the package was still a ward idea wearing a bedroom name. Surveyors were told to look for personal items and drapes. Maintenance was told to rehang the carriers. Families were told the room was semi-private, a word that means the opposite of what it sounds like. Semi-private is two privacies sharing a track.

Folding screens returned in small ways. A therapy gym used one around a mat. A shower room used a vinyl curtain on a rod that rusted at the hooks (chapter 32). A memorial in the chapel used a wooden screen that looked like furniture because it was. The resident room did not get those objects back. There was no floor left. The electric bed’s hi-lo travel, the overbed table’s H-base, the wheelchair parked for a visit — those footprints ate the place a screen would have stood. The ceiling was the only unused plane. So the cheap private room hung from it.

Fire and infection argued over the same cloth. A 701-rated panel is a Life Safety object. After 2020 the same panel was a surface EVS wiped or a textile they bagged. Some buildings moved to disposable curtains for isolation and then discovered that disposable was a supply line with a different punchout and a different claim desk (chapters 10 and 42). Some kept the reusable panel and argued about bleach on a print that was never sold as vinyl. The roommate, again, was the specifier. One isolation put both beds on the same decision. The curtain could not be a wall. It could only be a policy.

## What the gap is for

The gap at the end of the curtain is not always a failure. Sometimes it is how the aide sees a face without entering. Sometimes it is how a call light is noticed. Sometimes it is the only way two overbed tables and a lift can occupy the same twelve feet. Nightingale wanted the nurse to see a line of faces; the pavilion ward was a surveillance plan as much as a ventilation plan. The American double kept a piece of that plan and called it privacy. The cloth that does not quite meet is the compromise in hardware. A wall would have been a different building. A Medilodge-era capital budget did not buy a different building. It bought a track.

On a Michigan wing furnished from the book, you can still read the history in the ceiling. A surface-mounted track that stops short of a later soffit. A mesh header that does not match the body because they were replaced in different years. A hook-and-eye at the wall that no longer meets the hem. A second, shorter curtain in the toilet doorway, a different SKU, a different fire note, a different roommate problem. The private rooms that were carved out later — a wall where a track had been — leave a painted scar on the gypsum. The furniture list shrinks by half. The remaining chair is asked to look like a living room. The nightstand is asked to take a lamp. The curtain is surplus, folded in a box in the basement with the Christmas decorations, still tagged NFPA 701, waiting for a double that the census may fill again.

The object that remains on most corridors is the one that does not close. That is not a metaphor for the industry. It is a measurement. The track is longer than the cloth, or the cloth is longer than the patience of the person who has to pull it. The roommate is on the other side either way. The next essay leaves the ceiling and goes to the corridor, where a medical recliner parks “for a minute” and hallway speech gives it a name the catalog never printed.

## Notes

- Florence Nightingale, *Notes on Nursing* (London: Harrison, 1859), “Bed and Bedding”: the four-post bed with curtains is “utterly inadmissible”; iron bedsteads preferred. Do not cite her as a designer of cubicle curtains. Ward screens are a separate furniture class in hospital inventories.
- NFPA 101 §10.3.1 points draperies, curtains, and loosely hanging furnishings at NFPA 701 flame-propagation criteria. Edition and K-tag numbering vary by survey year; treat as a class of Life Safety documentation, not a single citation that covers every building.
- NFPA 13 sprinkler-and-curtain coordination (mesh headers, drops below deflectors) is construction language; `[VERIFY]` the edition on a named plan.
- CMS Appendix PP: F584 / §483.10(i) (homelike environment, drapes and personal items as survey examples); F917 / §483.90(e) (bed, furniture, closet). Visual privacy while occupying a bed is a recurring survey theme; tag numbers have been renumbered — do not invent a letter.
- Medline/Medilodge-era curtain-as-accessory: composite of channel practice, not a named 2008 PO.
