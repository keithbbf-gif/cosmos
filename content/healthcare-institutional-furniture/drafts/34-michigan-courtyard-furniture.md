# Michigan Courtyard Furniture

*Educational history of healthcare and nursing-home furniture. Not a purchasing specification for a named building, not a survey opinion, and not clinical advice about beds, rails, or seating for a particular resident.*

The chairs are stacked in a January shed. Resin arms locked into resin seats, a tower of eight that leans toward a bag of ice melt. Aluminum frames, if the building had those, are hung on nails or laid on the dirt floor because the shed was a pump house first. Cushions — there are never enough hooks for the cushions — sit in a garbage bag that EVS will not carry back in April. Snow has drifted under the door. The courtyard on the other side of the locked wing door is a table bolted to a pad, a smoker’s coffee can from a former era, a bird feeder someone forgot. This is not a product list. It is a weather problem. Lower Peninsula skilled-nursing courtyards live eight months of the year as an idea and four as a room. The furniture has to survive the shed, the salt, and the door that does not open from the inside without a badge.

A Medilodge-era operator buying for a string of Michigan homes — Washington Township corporate offices around 2011, Gail Clarkson’s *Provider* snapshot, fifteen or so SNFs — was buying climate as much as chairs. Prestige Healthcare’s name later on a lawn, after the 2013 Crain’s report, did not change the lake-effect week or the March freeze. Aluminum versus resin versus wood that rots is a materials sentence the winter grades. Cushions are a labor sentence EVS already answered. Smoking-era courtyards versus the 2010 indoor ban is a use sentence. The catalog’s patio page is the least important document in the shed.

## Weather is the spec

Michigan outdoor seating fails in a short list of ways. Water sits in a tube and splits it when it freezes. Salt — parking-lot salt walked in on chair tires and staff boots, ice melt thrown on the pad — eats a fastener. Ultraviolet light chalks a resin arm and fades a vinyl strap. A table top holds a lake, then a sheet of ice, then a crack. Wood — teak fantasies, pressure-treated benches, a picnic table from a donor — rots at the foot where the pad stays wet. None of that requires a brand name. It requires a shed, or it requires furniture that can stay out and look like it stayed out.

Aluminum frames with slings were the healthcare-adjacent page: light enough to stack, honest about rust at the hardware, cold to sit on in October. Resin stackers were the volume buy: cheap, ugly after two seasons, replaceable from a punchout that also sold them to hotels. Strap-and-tube chairs held water in every vinyl tape. Wood was the homelike ask (F584’s cousin outdoors) and the maintenance complaint. A Medilodge-class color board could pick a resin that “matched” the honey oak inside. By March the match was a fiction. Replacement chairs arrived in a slightly different beige and sat among the chalked ones like the dining-room gray among mauve (chapter 26).

Weight and wind matter on a courtyard that is also an air shaft between wings. Light resin walks. A table that is not bolted becomes a cart. Bolted tables solve the wind and create a plow problem: snow cannot be pushed if the pad is a forest of pedestals. Maintenance then shovels by hand or does not, and the first chair out in April sits in grit. Casters on outdoor chairs are rare and wise; casters bring the hallway rattle (chapter 38) into the wet and then into the shed, where they freeze.

Storage is the real purchase. A building that has a shed, a garage corner, or a basement stair can buy slings and cushions. A building that has a locked courtyard and no shed can buy only what it will leave out. What it will leave out is resin or bolted metal with no pad. Purchasing that orders a patio set from a photograph without walking the January shed has bought a spring project for EVS. EVS, asked to bring cushions in every night, will not. The sentence is not a complaint about a named aide. It is a labor fact. Night bottles and linen already lose. A cushion that must migrate is a cushion that lives in the bag in the shed, mildewed, and then in the dumpster.

## The locked door and the smoking year

The courtyard is a room whose door is a policy. Memory-care courts lock (chapter 31). Other courts lock because elopement is a furniture problem only in the sense that a bench under a low wall is a step. This essay will not write that protocol. It will say that a locked door makes outdoor furniture into a scheduled object. Staff open the door at 10 and 2 when the weather allows. The chairs have to be ready. If the chairs are in the shed, the schedule includes a carry. If the chairs stayed out, the schedule includes a wipe of snow. Either carry is why the set is small. A dining room can hold forty stackers. A courtyard holds six chairs and a table because six is what two people will move.

Smoking wrote an older courtyard. Before 1 May 2010, Michigan nursing homes could still have indoor smoking rooms; many had already pushed the habit outside. After the Dr. Ron Davis Smoke-Free Air Law, indoor smoking in nursing homes was prohibited. Outdoor smoking did not disappear. LARA and ombudsman fact sheets treated the patio as a policy the home could allow, supervise, or, for new admissions, close. The coffee can, the canister of sand, the vinyl chair with burn moons — those were furniture. A resin arm with a melt is a finish spec. Ash is a cleaner the vinyl card never listed. Buildings that went smoke-free on the whole property still had a chair with moons, because chairs outlast policies. Buildings that kept a supervised outdoor time kept a cluster of chairs that smelled like the time. Either way the courtyard was not only a garden. It was an assigned seat.

Oxygen and smoking are a care-plan fight this page will not enter. The furniture fight is the chair that must be wipeable after a cigarette and stackable after a storm. Healthcare vinyl from the interior book does not like ultraviolet or frost. Outdoor vinyl likes them a little more and still fails at the stitch. A sling dries faster than a cushion. A cushion is what families want to see in a photograph of “our courtyard.” EVS wants the sling. The photograph wins the PO. The sling wins the April carry.

Culture-change gardens — raised beds, a path, a birdbath — asked for benches that looked like park furniture. Park furniture in Michigan is a winter object. Iron that rusts. Wood that slivers. A memorial plaque that lifts in a freeze. The bench that survives is often the one nobody loved in the rendering: a bolted aluminum, no pad, ugly in July, present in October. Present is the spec. A beautiful bench in the shed is a day-room sofa that cannot be moved (chapter 28), only colder.

## Aluminum, resin, wood — graded by the shed

This is as close as the essay comes to materials, and it is not a catalog. Aluminum: honest rust at screws, light to stack, cold, sometimes a welded table that stays out if the pad drains. Resin: stackable, chalks, cracks at the arm in a bad freeze, cheap enough that replacement is the maintenance plan. Wood: homelike for a season, rot at the foot, splinters that make it a therapy objection, donors who do not want to hear that the picnic table was a mistake. Strap: holds water, holds dirt, holds salt. Cushions: indoor labor, outdoor fantasy. Bolt-down: wind-proof, plow-proof in the wrong direction.

Bariatric mix (chapter 30) does not stop at the door. A resin stacker is a width and a rating the sticker forgot to weatherproof. The wider outdoor chair is rare. The person who needed the expanded bed sits on what is there, or does not go out. Dignity is a January fact: the shed’s stack is one size.

Therapy likes the courtyard when the door is unlocked and the pad is dry. A wooden box step (chapter 33) should not live outside and sometimes does. An overbed table should not live outside and has been seen, house-brand casters in grit, because someone wanted a surface for a game. Those migrants die faster than the resin. They are indoor furniture in a climate that was not in their card.

After 2020 the courtyard became a visiting room. Plastic on the lounge, then chairs in the weather, then a tent, then a memory of a tent. Furniture that had been a smoke-break cluster became a family cluster. The same resin. The same shed the next January. Infection control wanted wipeable. Winter wanted empty. The pad held both instructions and a bolt.

Medilodge-era Lower Peninsula weather is specific enough to name without a forecast. Lake-effect snow on the west side, Grand Rapids to Muskegon, a week of horizontal weather that fills a courtyard pad faster than maintenance can shovel around bolted pedestals. A Detroit-side freeze-thaw that works fasteners out of resin and then refreezes them at a tilt. A March that looks like April and then is not. A July courtyard that is actually used, mosquitoes, a shade umbrella that becomes a sail and then a broken pole in the shed. The umbrella is furniture. It has a base that no one filled with enough sand. It is in the January stack, bent.

Salt is the quiet finish. Parking lots get treated. Boots carry it. Chair feet sit in a white crust that looks like spilled sugar and behaves like a rust assignment. Aluminum pitting at the screw is a salt story. Resin does not pit; it holds the crust in the texture of the arm until April’s first wipe. Indoor vinyl chairs that migrated to the court for a visit — the 2020 habit — took the salt back inside onto the day-room floor (chapter 28). EVS then had two rooms to argue with. The shed, at least, keeps the argument in one place.

## What April finds

The residual fact is the stack. Not a patio collection. A count of what two people could carry and what the shed could hold. April opens the bag of cushions and throws them away. April unstacks the resin and finds a crack. April puts six chairs around a bolted table and leaves two in the shed as spares, which is the only spare-parts program outdoor furniture has. The locked door opens on a good day. The coffee can is or is not still there, depending on the smoking year. Salt dust is on everything. The bird feeder swings.

A Michigan wing — not a named court, a type — still shows the year in the shed more clearly than in the courtyard. The courtyard is a photograph. The shed is the inventory. The book will still sell a patio page. The floor will still buy what it can store. The next essays leave the weather and go back inside, to the nurses’ station and the cart, where furniture moves again and the turning radius is the spec.

## Notes

- Materials (aluminum, resin, wood, sling, cushion) are graded here by winter storage, salt, and freeze — not as a recommended patio collection and not as current SKUs.
- Dr. Ron Davis Smoke-Free Air Law, Michigan, effective 1 May 2010: indoor smoking prohibited in nursing homes. Outdoor smoking remained a facility policy question (LARA / long-term-care ombudsman fact sheets). Not a care-plan instruction.
- MediLodge Group ~2011: *Provider*, May 2011. 2013: Crain’s Detroit Business, sale of fifteen homes to Prestige Healthcare. Climate does not change with letterhead.
- Cushions as labor: composite EVS habit, not a named aide or a named building’s policy.
- Elopement and locked courts: named as a door policy only. No protocol.
