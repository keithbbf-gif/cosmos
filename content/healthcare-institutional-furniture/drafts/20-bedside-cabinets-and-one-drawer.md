# Bedside Cabinets and One Drawer

*Educational history of healthcare and nursing-home furniture. Not a purchasing specification for a named building, not a survey opinion, and not clinical advice about beds, rails, or seating for a particular resident.*

The drawer is open. A hairbrush, a folded form, a pair of glasses on a chain, a photograph facing the mattress, a roll of mints, a pen that does not work. That is the inventory this essay will allow itself — composite, unnamed, the ordinary contents of one slide on a Medilodge-era wing. The cabinet was specified as storage: a drawer, a door, sometimes a flip-up vanity, a plastic liner in the drawer so EVS could pull the crumbs. It is used as a bumper for the bed, a pantry for crackers, a chart shelf when the overbed table has sunk (chapter 19), a place to park the pendant, a surface for a lamp that is trying not to look like a hospital gooseneck. The one drawer holds the life. The door holds the overflow and the things a roommate should not see. The top holds whatever will not fit in either.

F917 wants functional furniture. F584 wants personal items and a room that does not look only like a ward. The cabinet is where those two sentences share a piece of TFL. It is also where the hi-lo deck (chapter 3) makes a height argument the millwork drawing never had: when the bed is at its lowest click, the drawer is at a standing person’s knee and a sitting person’s shoulder, or the reverse, depending on the cabinet. There is no “right” height for a moving deck. There is only the height purchasing bought.

## Drawer, door, vanity — the three-part lie of a complete bedside

The American hospital bedside locker was a metal box: one drawer, one door, a towel bar, enamel that took carbolic. The SNF cabinet inherited the plan and put oak-look or cherry-look skin on it (chapter 9). Drawer at the top, door below, sometimes an open niche for a basin. Flip-up vanities — a hinged panel with a mirror on the underside — appeared in the 1980s–2000s books as a homelike extra. They broke at the hinge. They pinched. They became a shelf that would not stay up, which is the overbed table’s failure in wood. A Medilodge-era spec that still listed “vanity” was listing a wish. The floor had often screwed the flap shut.

Plastic liners in the drawer are a small, correct idea. Urine, powder, spilled supplement, a leaking lotion. The liner pulls. The particleboard underneath still swells when the liner is missing or cracked (chapter 37). Wood drawers without liners look more residential and fail faster. Metal drawers rattle and look like 1974. House-brand cabinets used the liner and a thermofoil edge that peeled at the first wet wipe. Name-brand healthcare casegoods — Kwalu-class molded skins, HPL with a real edge band — showed up in hospital rebuilds and in a few SNF privates. The double rooms got the TFL box.

Locks are a myth that will not die. Families ask for a locking drawer. Corporate risk asks whether a lock is a ligature conversation this pack will not import as fashion (chapter 31). Keys go to the station and do not come back. The lock cylinder is empty by year two, or the drawer has been pried and the particleboard around the strike is a crater. Some cabinets shipped with a fake lock — a plug in the drawer face — because the rendering looked secure. Surveyors looking for personal items (F584) are not looking for a lock. They are looking for a photograph and a hairbrush that have a place. The open drawer in the doorway is, in that sense, compliance and a privacy problem at once.

The door hides the bedpan that is supposed to be in the bathroom, the extra briefs, the purse, the snack that dietary does not know about, the roommate’s charger that migrated. Soft-close hinges appeared late and failed early (chapter 40). Exposed hinges last and catch a hip. A door that will not latch is a cabinet that is always open, which is how the life spills onto the top.

## Height versus the deck, and the cabinet as a bumper

Hi-lo travel made the nightstand a moving target. A conventional 30-inch-high cabinet `[VERIFY]` sits reasonably next to a deck in the middle of its travel and looks absurd next to a low-low at seven to ten inches (chapter 12). The drawer then becomes a thing you reach *down* into from a standing position, or a thing you cannot see into from the mattress. Residents who eat from the cabinet top because the overbed table has failed are eating at a height that belongs to a dresser, not a table. Elbows on that top are how the edge band lifts.

The bed hits the cabinet. That is not abuse. That is a castered device (chapter 17’s crash point, chapter 38’s wheels) living next to a box. Bumpers on the cabinet — vinyl corners, a rubber T-mold, a field-applied strip — are the honest hardware. Cabinets without bumpers show the particleboard by year three. Expanded frames (chapter 13) hit sooner, because the deck is wider and the nightstand was placed for a 36-inch sleep surface. The outlet behind the cabinet (chapter 11) is why nobody will move it out of the bed’s path: the lamp, the pump, the phone, the air overlay’s compressor all share one duplex. The cabinet is furniture and a utilities closet.

Carts hit it too. Med-pass, meal, linen. The corridor side of a cabinet in a tight double is a sacrificial corner. Hardware that survives med-pass (chapter 40) is a later essay; the cabinet is the object that proves the need. A missing pull is a later essay’s opening. Here the missing pull is only why the one drawer is harder to open for a person who already has one shot at it from the mattress.

Shared rooms split the cabinet problem. Two cabinets is the package. One cabinet and a shelf is the austerity. A built-in between the beds is the carpenter’s answer and the surveyor’s question about whose drawer is whose. Stolen hangers belong to the wardrobe (chapter 21). Stolen drawer space is quieter. The form in the open drawer — a menu, a therapy schedule, a copy of something with a name this essay will not invent — is how you know the drawer is still a person’s and not a supply niche.

The hospital locker that the SNF cabinet inherited had a towel bar and a basin ring because the ward expected wash water at the bed. The nursing home expected a bathroom down a shared door. The towel bar stayed on some house-brand cabinets as a chrome memory. It caught the pendant cord. It caught a sweater that would not go on the 66-inch rod. It was useful and it was a snag. Removing it left two holes in the TFL that maintenance filled with caulk the color of last year’s oak.

Millwork shops that still built for hospitals in the 1990s offered a “bedside” with a deeper door and a removable plastic tub. SNF buyers took the tub option because incontinent supplies are a volume. The tub then became a pantry: crackers, a can of supplement, a family bag of candy that dietary was not to see. Infection-control rounds found the pantry and wrote a note. The cabinet did not change. The note went in a binder. F584 likes personal items. A bag of candy is a personal item until it is a pest item. The drawer is not a policy. It is a volume with a liner.

Roommate politics live in the gap between two cabinets. In a tight double the cabinets face each other across a curtain that does not meet (chapter 24). A drawer that opens toward the aisle is a drawer a cart will hit. A drawer that opens toward the bed is a drawer the person in the bed can work. Package drawings often put both cabinets the same way for the photograph. The floor turned one 180 degrees and then the door no longer cleared the wardrobe. Functional furniture, in F917’s mouth, includes a door that opens. The package photograph does not test the swing.

## What the top became

The top is HPL if the building was lucky, TFL if it was ordinary, a painted thermofoil if it was cheap and late. Water rings, bleach haze after 2020, a Bible that left a tan line, a fan that ran all summer. F584’s personal items live here because the walls are already taken by the call reset, the TV bracket, and the cubicle-curtain track. A lamp with a residential shade is the homelike spend. The cord runs to the duplex the cabinet hides. When EVS pulls the cabinet to mop, the lamp dies and the overlay pump alarms. The cabinet is then not furniture. It is the lid on the room’s electricity.

Flip-up vanities, when they still work, put a mirror at a height that helps a person in a chair and blinds a person in a low bed. Mirrors in memory-care rooms are a different argument (chapter 31). In a standard SNF double the mirror is a dignity object and a thing that shows the roommate. Some doors had a mirror on the inside. That was better and it got wet.

House-brand tops came in the same oak as the wardrobe and the headboard insert so the package would photograph. Replacement cabinets, years later, came in the gray program. A 2005 Medilodge-era room then held an oak box, a teal chair (chapter 9), and a later gray overbed table. The drawer still held the hairbrush. The finish schedule had given up. The life had not.

Outlets and pumps turn the cabinet into a utilities closet, and that job grew after the 1990s. An air overlay’s compressor (chapter 16) wants a duplex. A tube-feeding pole wants one. A phone charger wants one. The lamp that makes the room homelike wants one. The cabinet sits on the only wall that has them. Pull the cabinet to mop and four plugs come out. Leave the cabinet and the mop never reaches the baseboard, which is how the TFL swells from the wet edge. Maintenance added a power strip behind some cabinets. Surveyors and fire walkers have opinions about power strips that this furniture essay will not settle. The strip is evidence that the nightstand is doing electrical work the headwall never arrived to do.

## One slide

Purchasing thinks in units: cabinet, lock option, vanity option, color. The floor thinks in one drawer. That is the residual. You can specify a three-part bedside and still only have one slide that a person can work from the mattress, at the height the deck happens to be, with a pull that is still there. The door is for other people. The top is for the room. The drawer is for the hairbrush and the form. The next essay looks at the taller box — the wardrobe — and at a rod at 66 inches over a wheelchair, which is how F917’s individual closet becomes a reach problem instead of a storage problem.

## Notes

- F917 / §483.90(e): functional furniture. F584 / §483.10(i): homelike environment, personal items. Neither specifies a drawer count or a lock.
- Typical cabinet height near 30 inches is a casegood habit `[VERIFY]` against a named line. Low-low decks change the relationship; see chapter 12.
- Plastic liners, thermofoil edges, HPL vs TFL: construction taken up in chapter 37. Bumper and pull hardware: chapter 40.
- Locking drawers: channel option and hallway myth; not a security specification for a named building.
