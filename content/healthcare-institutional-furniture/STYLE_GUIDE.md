# Style Guide

This series talks to people who specify, buy, install, survey, or live with furniture in a skilled nursing facility, an assisted-living house, or a small hospital. Bradley Brand built and installed case goods for that world. The series teaches how the objects got their shapes and how the channel moved them. It does not sell a line.

## Voice

Human. Mine. Magazine voice, not catalog voice. Write as a person who has stood in a resident room with a tape and a bleach bottle, then gone home and opened the Federal Register.

Speak to the reader as a peer. Do not coach. Do not cheerlead. Do not close with a moral.

First person is allowed when it is a shop, dock, or room observation. It is not a diary and it is not a war story. Do not invent a conversation with a director of nursing. Do not invent a Medline SKU.

A sentence may be short. A sentence may run if it is carrying a fact.

## Banned phrasing

Do not use: delve, landscape (metaphorical), robust, leverage, unlock, cutting-edge, game-changer, “In today’s,” “It’s important to note,” Moreover, “Whether you’re,” “In conclusion,” “At the end of the day,” “rich tapestry,” “journey,” “elevate,” “empower,” “seamless,” “holistic,” “unpack,” “nuanced” as filler, “trusted partner,” “continuum of care” as decoration, “solutions,” “optimize.”

Do not open with a dictionary definition. Do not close with a recap list of “key takeaways.” If a list is a working table (CFR cites, zone dimensions, square footage), keep it. Decorative bullet stacks are out.

No COSMOS. No AI. No process talk about how the draft was written.

## Facts and citations

A dimensional claim, a death count, a temperature band, or a square-footage rule needs a named public source: the eCFR, CMS Appendix PP, an FDA guidance, a Federal Register notice, NFPA 101 as adopted, a California Bureau bulletin, IEC 60601-2-52.

If the fact is mill or channel knowledge and not pinned to a public artifact, mark it as shop knowledge or `[CITE NEEDED]`.

Do not invent a purchase order, a Medline catalog number, a Medilodge internal spec, a warranty term, or a dollar amount. The public project list (Medilodge of Montrose; Byrd Haven, Searcy; Tuscany Village, Pearland; Chenal Heights; Green Acres, Paragould; GreenBrier Rehab; Delta Memorial Clinic, Dumas; Little Rock Healthcare & Rehab; Baywind Village, League City) may be named as geography. Do not turn those names into case studies with invented room counts.

Hunt-board-style dealer romance has a cousin here: “homelike” as a finish chip. Name it when it is a later story.

## Structure of an article

1. Open on an object, a room, a gap, a label, or a failure — something a reader can see.
2. Name the form (bed, overbed, wardrobe, nightstand, chair, table, curtain, station).
3. Give the geometry or the code that actually governs it.
4. Walk the history, including the institution that wanted density and the reform that wanted a house.
5. Place the piece in the Medline / Medilodge-era channel when that is true: who signed, who punched the catalog, who freighted, who wiped it down.
6. Leave the reader with one way to look at a piece on a floor.
7. End with sources and cross-links, not a sermon.

One unified essay. No recap that restates the lede.

Target 1,400–2,200 words of body text. Frontmatter and source lists do not count toward the band.

## Brand and SEO staging

These drafts are staged for BBF / Bradley Brand institutional-furniture SEO. They teach. They do not sell chairs.

Bradley Brand / BBF may appear where Arkansas hardwoods, finish chemistry, install, or the distributor channel are the actual subject. One or two sentences. No product names, no “our craftsmen,” no call to action.

Primary keywords must not cannibalize. One slug owns one query. Cross-link siblings; do not repeat the same lede in two files.

## Names and spelling

American spelling in the running text. Keep the trade name first: overbed table, geri chair, cubicle curtain, punchout, GPO, DON, EVS, SNF, ALF.

Homelike is one word when CMS uses it that way in Appendix PP. Home-like is allowed when you are talking, not quoting.

Quartersawn, flatsawn, riftsawn: one word each after first explanation.

## Numbers

Dates: 2006, not “the mid-aughts,” unless the year is honestly unknown. Dimensions from the cited guidance in the units published (millimetres first when FDA leads with mm; inches when CMS or ADA leads with inches). Do not convert a label you have not seen.

FDA’s 2006 bed guidance: **691** entrapment reports from January 1, 1985, to January 1, 2006; **413** deaths; **120** injuries; **158** near-misses. Zones 1–4 get dimensional limits; 5–7 are named without FDA limits in that document. Do not “update from memory.”

CMS physical environment: **80** square feet per resident in a multiple room; **100** square feet in a single. Temperature **71–81 °F** for facilities first certified after October 1, 1990. Those numbers live in 42 CFR 483.90 and 483.10(i).
