# Free amino acids and nitrogen-spiking literacy

> **Pack:** Protein powder label literacy — staged draft.  
> **Jurisdiction:** United States (FD&C Act as amended by DSHEA, 1994).  
> **Class:** Consumer education. Not a product label. Not medical advice. Not an advertisement.  
> **Lock:** This draft does not claim that any protein powder diagnoses, treats, cures, or prevents any disease.  
> **Statute (teaching):** Under DSHEA, a dietary supplement label may not claim to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease.

## The move

“Amino acid spiking” or “nitrogen spiking” is shopper slang for a formulation choice: add inexpensive **free amino acids** (or other nitrogen-rich ingredients) so a **nitrogen × 6.25** test (PSL-17) prints a larger protein gram number than the intact protein the front of the tub implied. The slang is older than any one YouTuber. The literacy is not “every brand is guilty.” The literacy is **can the scoop’s named intact protein plus the named extras fit the serving and the protein line without magic?**

## Free amino acids are legal dietary ingredients

L-glutamine, glycine, taurine, alanine, leucine — these can appear on a supplement because they **are** dietary ingredients. A product that is *honestly* an amino-acid powder should not pretend to be a whey steak (21 CFR 101.36 even refuses a protein declaration on products that contain only individual amino acids, aside from technological ingredients). The problem is the **costume**: a tub that says WHEY and 25 g PROTEIN when a material slice of the 25 g is glycine.

Glycine is cheap, nitrogen-rich, and easy to hide in a blend if the firm wants to. That sentence is food chemistry, not an indictment of a named company.

## A kitchen-table fit test (not a lab)

You will not prove a spike at home. You can find **tension**:

1. Serving weight: _____ g  
2. Named intact proteins (isolate/concentrate/casein/pea/…): listed first?  
3. Protein declared: _____ g  
4. Room for everything else: serving − protein = _____ g  
5. Named free amino acids and “amino matrix” grams (if given): _____ g  
6. Flavors, gums, sweeteners, lecithin, moisture: they need **some** of that room.

If the amino matrix is listed as 5 g and the room left is 3 g, the protein line and the blend cannot both be ordinary. If the amino acids are only names with no amounts, inside a **proprietary blend**, you have an **opacity** problem (stage 5 will meet blends again) even if the math could still be innocent.

If room left is 8 g on a 32 g / 24 g concentrate, there is room for lactose, fat, flavor, *and* a small amino addition. Tension is weaker. Do not invent a scandal from leftover room.

## What an amino-acid-sum test would show (teaching)

A lab that hydrolyzes intact protein and sums amino acids (with proper methods) will not count some non-protein nitrogen the way N × 6.25 will, and it will treat added free amino acids as themselves — which may or may not be what you thought you bought. Independent testers use this difference. A brand COA that only shows N × 6.25 cannot settle a spike argument (PSL-40).

This pack is not a testing lab. It will not publish a hit list.

## Creatine and other nitrogen neighbors

Creatine monohydrate contains nitrogen. A “protein + creatine” tub that folds creatine into the **protein** line would be a category error. Creatine should have its own line if it is a featured dietary ingredient. If it does not, and the protein-by-weight looks miraculous next to a long extra list, you have another fit-test input — not a diagnosis of fraud, a reason to stop trusting the costume.

## Proprietary blends and spike fog

A blend that says “Whey protein isolate, glycine, taurine, alanine” without amounts is **legal in many designs** and **hostile to the fit test**. The order still helps: if glycine is second, glycine is not a dusting. If glycine is last of twelve, it may be a dusting. Fog is a finding.

## Label drill

Run the fit test on one flavored protein. Write the six lines above. Then choose one:

- A. Math fits a simple flavored isolate/concentrate.  
- B. Math is tight; extras could still be ordinary flavor.  
- C. Math cannot fit the named extras.  
- D. Proprietary fog; cannot tell.

If you chose C or D, your next move is **not** a social post. It is “I will not treat this protein line as a precise intact-protein measurement.” That sentence is enough literacy for a grocery aisle.

## What this draft will not say

It will not accuse a living brand. It will not say free amino acids are unsafe. It will not say you should buy an amino-acid-sum tested tub as a medical device. It will not claim that avoiding spikes treats a condition.
