# Collagen peptides — what they are not

> **Pack:** Protein powder label literacy — staged draft.  
> **Jurisdiction:** United States (FD&C Act as amended by DSHEA, 1994).  
> **Class:** Consumer education. Not a product label. Not medical advice. Not an advertisement.  
> **Lock:** This draft does not claim that any protein powder diagnoses, treats, cures, or prevents any disease.  
> **Statute (teaching):** Under DSHEA, a dietary supplement label may not claim to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease.

## The move

Collagen powders are protein in the **nitrogen-method** sense. They are not a drop-in synonym for whey, egg, or soy in the **completeness** sense. They are also the category where marketing most often leans toward body-part promises (skin, joints, bones) that sit one adjective away from a disease claim. Literacy holds two facts at once: **the panel can show grams**, and **the grams are not a complete protein pattern**.

## What collagen is, as a grocery object

Collagen is a structural protein family from animal connective tissue — hide, bone, fish skin, depending on the source. As a supplement it is usually **hydrolyzed** (“peptides”) so it dissolves. The source should appear somewhere: bovine, porcine, marine. The allergen/identity issues follow the source (fish collagen is a **fish** allergen story). “Unflavored collagen” can still be an animal product; it is not a plant powder with better PR.

## The tryptophan hole

Collagen is **low in tryptophan** and is treated as an **incomplete** protein in ordinary food science. That is why a collagen tub can declare 20 g protein and then show a %DV that is not 40%, or show no %DV, or sit next to a quiet “not a significant source of protein” type statement when the quality rules require it (21 CFR 101.9(c)(7) has a “not a significant source of protein” path for very low quality scores). Shoppers who add collagen grams to their mental “protein total” as if they were whey grams are mixing two different objects.

This pack will not say collagen is worthless. Bone broth culture and candy-chew culture both exist. It will say: **do not use collagen grams as if they were a complete-protein food unless the rest of the diet is doing that work.** That is a reading instruction, not a menu.

## What the front is selling instead of completeness

Collagen marketing talks about hair, skin, nails, joints, and “mobility.” Some of that speech can be shaped as structure/function (“helps support hair, skin, and nails”) with a disclaimer on a supplement. Some of it slides into **characteristic signs of a disease** or into pictures of damaged joints. PSL-31 is the claim-class lesson. This lesson’s local rule: **a body-part photograph plus a pathology word is not a protein-identity statement.**

This pack will not finish a skin or joint claim for a brand. It will not say collagen treats a dermatologic or rheumatologic condition. It will not say skipping collagen causes one.

## Collagen in a “whey” blend

Some tubs add collagen to a whey or plant blend to cheapen the nitrogen line or to print a beauty word on a gym tub. Ingredient order and the amino chart (if any) will show a pattern that looks less like whey (lower tryptophan, higher glycine/proline/hydroxyproline). If the amino chart is a highlight reel of glycine, you are looking at collagen’s signature, not at a mystery peptide technology.

Hydroxyproline is a useful **identity** hint in a lab. You will not see it on most consumer lids. You will see “collagen peptides” in the list if the brand is being ordinary. If the brand is not being ordinary, PSL-25’s fit-test still applies.

## Bovine vs marine as a label issue

The source changes:

- religious and diet-identity marks (PSL-30),
- allergen class (fish),
- and sometimes the heavy-metal conversation people have about marine products (PSL-38).

It does not change the completeness problem. Marine collagen is still collagen.

Classify the body-part sentence: *structure-function-shaped / disease-shaped / decoration*. Do not rewrite it to make it “better marketing.”

## What this draft will not say

It will not say collagen erases wrinkles or rebuilds a joint. It will not say collagen is a scam as a protein source — it is a **specific** protein source. It will not tell a pescatarian that bovine collagen is a fish. It will not recommend collagen to anyone as care.
