# Complete protein, limiting amino acids, and blends

> **Pack:** Protein powder label literacy — staged draft.  
> **Jurisdiction:** United States (FD&C Act as amended by DSHEA, 1994).  
> **Class:** Consumer education. Not a product label. Not medical advice. Not an advertisement.  
> **Lock:** This draft does not claim that any protein powder diagnoses, treats, cures, or prevents any disease.  
> **Statute (teaching):** Under DSHEA, a dietary supplement label may not claim to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease.

## The move

“Complete protein” is **food-science slang** that escaped onto lids. It means the protein provides the indispensable (essential) amino acids in a pattern that is not badly limited relative to a reference pattern. It does not mean the powder is a complete food, a complete diet, or a complete answer to a medical question. U.S. labeling translates a version of this idea into **PDCAAS** when %DV is required (PSL-13). The lid word “complete” is not itself a PDCAAS certificate.

## Indispensable amino acids, without a sermon

Humans cannot synthesize nine amino acids in amounts that cover need: histidine, isoleucine, leucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan, valine. Food proteins contain them in different ratios. A protein that is very low in one of them has a **limiting amino acid**. The score used for labeling (PDCAAS) cares about that limit and about digestibility, then caps at 1.0.

This is **composition**. It is not a command to eat animal products. It is not a command to eat powder. Diets made of ordinary foods routinely cover the pattern without a tub. The tub is a grocery item that may be complete, incomplete, or blended toward completeness.

## Typical patterns you will meet on lids (typical, not destiny)

- **Whey, casein, egg, soy:** commonly treated as complete, with PDCAAS at or near 1 when processed as food proteins.  
- **Pea:** often lower in methionine.  
- **Rice:** often lower in lysine.  
- **Pea + rice (or similar):** a blend that can raise the score by covering each other’s gaps.  
- **Collagen:** incomplete; tryptophan is the usual hole (PSL-23).  
- **Hemp:** often discussed as limited; read the specific panel, not a meme.

Those are **why blends exist** as a labeling and formulation practice. They are not a ranking of human worth.

## How a blend appears on a label

A honest blend names the proteins **in order of predominance** in the ingredient list: “pea protein, brown rice protein.” A less honest blend says “proprietary protein matrix” and hides the ratio. You can still learn something: if pea is first, pea is the majority of the protein ingredients. You cannot learn the exact PDCAAS from the order alone.

Some Supplement Facts lines say “Protein blend 30 g” and then list the proteins. 30 g of blend is not 30 g of protein. The **protein gram line** is the protein. The blend weight is the powder-of-those-ingredients, which may include moisture and leftover carbohydrate.

## “Complete” as a claim

If “complete protein” is used in a way that implies a nutrient-content or quality claim, it can drag **%DV** and ordinary truth-and-misleading rules into the room. This pack does not draft the claim for a brand. It tells the shopper: **ask whether the panel’s %DV agrees with the adjective.** A “complete” plant blend with a %DV far below grams ÷ 50 is telling you the score is not 1.0, or the %DV is missing, or the serving math is odd. That is a reading, not a lawsuit.

## Complementing at the table vs in the scoop

Rice-and-beans is the classroom example of complementary proteins **across a diet**. A pea-rice scoop is complementary proteins **inside a serving**. Both are food science. Neither is a prescription. A person who eats a varied diet may not need the scoop to “complete” anything. A person who wants the scoop to stand in for a protein food may care more about the pattern. This pack will not choose for them. It will not say an incomplete scoop causes a disease.

## What this draft will not say

It will not say incomplete proteins are worthless. It will not say vegans must buy blends. It will not say complete proteins prevent a deficiency disease (that would need the classical-deficiency claim machinery, which this pack will not run for a powder). It will not rank cultures by dairy.
