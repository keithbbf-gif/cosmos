# Whey — concentrate, isolate, hydrolysate

> **Pack:** Protein powder label literacy — staged draft.  
> **Jurisdiction:** United States (FD&C Act as amended by DSHEA, 1994).  
> **Class:** Consumer education. Not a product label. Not medical advice. Not an advertisement.  
> **Lock:** This draft does not claim that any protein powder diagnoses, treats, cures, or prevents any disease.  
> **Statute (teaching):** Under DSHEA, a dietary supplement label may not claim to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease.

## The move

Whey is the liquid leftover when milk is made into cheese or casein. As a powder, it is sold under three process nicknames: **concentrate**, **isolate**, **hydrolysate**. Those nicknames are **not statutory grades**. They are industry words for how much non-protein material was removed, and whether some peptide bonds were broken. The facts panel still wins. A sloppy isolate can lose a tidy concentrate on **protein grams per serving** and on **protein by weight**.

## Concentrate

Whey protein concentrate (WPC) is filtered less aggressively than isolate. Typical trade specs talk about protein-by-weight bands (you will hear “WPC80” as a roughly 80% protein dry powder). The consumer lid may only say “whey protein concentrate.” Expect more leftover **lactose**, **fat**, and **minerals** than an isolate from the same stream (PSL-16). Expect a serving that is a bit heavier for the same protein grams.

Concentrate is not “impure” as a moral word. It is a **different fraction**. Some people prefer the taste. Some people are watching lactose. Those are personal food choices. This pack does not medicalize lactose. If lactose matters to you, read sugars and ingredients, and take medical questions to a clinician.

## Isolate

Whey protein isolate (WPI) has had more of the non-protein material removed. Trade talk often starts around 90% protein by dry weight. The consumer signal is a serving that can show **high protein grams in a small scoop** and a short leftover map. Isolate still usually carries **milk** as a major allergen (PSL-29). “Lactose free” is a separate claim with a separate truth problem — look for the claim **and** the sugar line, not the word isolate alone.

Isolate is not a drug. “Fast” on the lid is marketing about emptying the stomach, not a disease claim we will finish for the brand.

## Hydrolysate

Hydrolyzed whey has been treated with enzymes or acid so some bonds are already broken. The powder can taste bitter. The lid will say “hydrolyzed” or “peptides.” That is a **process** statement. It is not proof of a clinical outcome. It is not a reason to print a disease claim. If a brand says hydrolysate “is pre-digested so it treats” a condition, they have left process talk and entered the cliff in PSL-05.

## Blends of the three

“Whey protein blend” often means concentrate + isolate, sometimes with a dusting of hydrolysate so the marketing can say three words. Ingredient order tells you which one you mostly bought. A blend that lists concentrate first is a concentrate tub with isolate in the chorus.

## What the front word must survive

Do this every time the front says ISOLATE:

1. First ingredient is actually whey protein isolate?  
2. Protein grams ÷ serving grams is in isolate territory (high), not gainer territory?  
3. Allergen line still says milk?  
4. Is there a second protein (casein, collagen, plant) quietly in the list?

If (1) fails, the front word is costume. If (4) is yes, you are holding a blend (PSL-19) whether or not the front admitted it.

## Cross-contact and “whey” on a plant tub

A plant powder manufactured on shared equipment may say “contains milk” or “manufactured in a facility that processes milk.” That is not whey protein as a first ingredient. It is an allergen/facility statement (PSL-29). Do not sort that product into this lesson’s dairy stack.

## What this draft will not say

It will not say isolate is healthier. It will not say hydrolysate is required after training. It will not say concentrate causes a diagnosis. It will not tell people with a milk allergy that isolate is fine — the allergen line, not this file, is the document, and a clinician is the person.
