# Casein, milk protein, and micellar language

> **Pack:** Protein powder label literacy — staged draft.  
> **Jurisdiction:** United States (FD&C Act as amended by DSHEA, 1994).  
> **Class:** Consumer education. Not a product label. Not medical advice. Not an advertisement.  
> **Lock:** This draft does not claim that any protein powder diagnoses, treats, cures, or prevents any disease.  
> **Statute (teaching):** Under DSHEA, a dietary supplement label may not claim to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease.

## The move

Milk’s two famous protein families are **whey** (the soluble leftover in cheese-making) and **casein** (the curd). A tub can sell casein, a **milk protein concentrate/isolate** (casein + whey in milk’s native ratio, more or less), or a night-time story about “slow protein.” The story is marketing about digestion rate. The label duties are the same as whey’s: grams, serving, allergens, no disease claims. Literacy keeps the clock-story in a smaller font than the panel.

## Casein as an ingredient name

You will see **micellar casein**, **calcium caseinate**, **sodium caseinate**, and **casein hydrolysate**.

- **Micellar** points at a filtration process that tries to keep casein in its micelle form. It is a **process** word, not a clinical outcome.  
- **Caseinates** are casein treated with alkali to make a more soluble salt. They show up in RTDs and in some powders. The mineral in the name (sodium, calcium) can move the leftover map (PSL-16).  
- **Hydrolyzed casein** is the same process idea as hydrolyzed whey (PSL-20): bonds broken, bitter risk, not a therapy.

The allergen is still **milk** unless the product is something else entirely.

## Milk protein concentrate and isolate (MPC / MPI)

These ingredients keep whey and casein together, closer to milk’s own ratio (casein-heavy). They are popular in ready-to-drink shakes and in “milk protein” tubs. The consumer lid may say “milk protein isolate” and still be a different object from “whey protein isolate.” **Read the words in the middle.** Whey isolate and milk isolate are not synonyms.

MPC/MPI can carry more lactose than a whey isolate, depending on the spec. The sugar line and the ingredient list, not the word isolate, tell you.

## “Night time” and “slow”

Casein gels in the stomach more than whey. Brands turn that food-science observation into a bedtime ritual. This pack will allow itself one dry sentence: **digestion rate is not a disease claim, and it is not a reason this curriculum will tell you when to eat.** If a brand says casein “prevents overnight breakdown” in a way that sounds like a pathology, they are walking toward 21 CFR 101.93(g). If they say “digests slowly,” they are still in process/physiology marketing and still need truth, and, on a supplement, the structure/function machine if they go further.

You will not find a clock on the facts panel. There is no “hours of amino acids” line in 21 CFR 101.36. Treat clock language as **speech**, then return to grams.

## Blends that hide casein

“Protein blend” on a whey-forward tub sometimes includes casein or milk protein to change texture (thicker shake) or to print “fast + slow.” Ingredient order still tells the majority. A “whey” tub with sodium caseinate in the middle of the list is a dairy-protein mix. That can be fine. It is not what a milk-allergic shopper should discover with their mouth.

## What this draft will not say

It will not say you must eat casein at night. It will not say whey is “fast medicine.” It will not say casein prevents a wasting condition. It will not tell a milk-allergic reader that caseinate is different enough to try — it is not, for labeling purposes, a non-milk food.
