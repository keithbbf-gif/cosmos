# How protein is counted — nitrogen × 6.25

> **Pack:** Protein powder label literacy — staged draft.  
> **Jurisdiction:** United States (FD&C Act as amended by DSHEA, 1994).  
> **Class:** Consumer education. Not a product label. Not medical advice. Not an advertisement.  
> **Lock:** This draft does not claim that any protein powder diagnoses, treats, cures, or prevents any disease.  
> **Statute (teaching):** Under DSHEA, a dietary supplement label may not claim to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease.

## The move

The gram number on a U.S. protein line is usually not “we weighed the protein molecules.” It is **we measured nitrogen, then multiplied**. Proteins are roughly sixteen percent nitrogen, and 100 ÷ 16 = 6.25. 21 CFR 101.9(c)(7) allows that factor unless an AOAC method requires another. The method is old, cheap, and good enough for a lot of food. It is also why **anything rich in nitrogen** can flatter a protein line. Literacy starts by respecting the method, then asking what else in the scoop contains nitrogen.

## What the lab is actually doing

Kjeldahl and combustion (Dumas) methods convert nitrogen in the sample into a number. The firm, or its contract lab, multiplies by 6.25 (or a food-specific factor when required) and rounds to the nearest gram for the panel. That pipeline is **normal**. It is how cheese, flour, and pet food get protein numbers too.

What the pipeline cannot see:

- whether the nitrogen sat in a folded whey protein or in glycine powder,
- whether the amino acid pattern matches a complete-protein reference,
- whether the nitrogen sat in creatine, ammonium salts, or other non-protein nitrogen (less common in a simple whey, more imaginable in a “matrix”).

PSL-25 is the spike lesson. This lesson is the **method** lesson. If you skip the method, “spiking” sounds like a conspiracy theory. If you own the method, spiking is just arithmetic.

## Why 6.25 is a compromise

Not every protein is 16% nitrogen. The true factor varies by amino acid composition. Dairy, soy, and wheat have histories of specific factors in some official methods. The regulation’s default 6.25 is a **harmonizing stick**. A one-gram argument between brands can be method plus rounding plus humidity, not a morality play.

Do not “correct” a panel at home with a factor you liked on a blog. The legal number is the legal number. Do use the method to interpret **amino acid lists** and **proprietary blends** later.

## What a shopper can see without a lab

You cannot rerun Kjeldahl in a kitchen. You can:

1. Compare **protein grams** to **serving grams** (PSL-14). A 30 g scoop declaring 28 g protein has little room for non-protein material — and little room for a pile of free amino acids unless the “protein” *is* those amino acids.
2. Read the ingredient list for **individual amino acids** named after the protein (glycine, taurine, alanine, glutamine).
3. Read the facts box for a **separate amino acid blend** with its own milligrams.
4. Look at an amino acid profile (PSL-18) that does not add up like the intact protein you were promised.

Those are **label** moves. They do not prove a crime. They tell you when the nitrogen method has extra friends in the scoop.

## Third-party tests still use a method

A YouTuber’s “lab test” that reports protein % is only as good as the method plus the sample. A nitrogen method on a spiked powder will faithfully report the spike. An amino-acid-sum method (adding the amino acids after hydrolysis, with proper handling of tryptophan and recovery) tells a different story. If a brand publishes a COA (PSL-40), see whether it says **protein (N × 6.25)** or something more specific. The parentheses are the literacy.

## What this is not

It is not a reason to call protein labels fake.  
It is not a reason to treat amino acids as poison.  
It is not a clinical assay of your blood.  
It is not a disease story.

It is a **metrology** story that happens to be printed as “Protein 24g.”

## Label drill

On one tub, write a method card:

- Protein grams:  
- Serving grams:  
- Room left for non-protein material (serving − protein):  
- Named free amino acids in the ingredient list (or “none visible”):  
- Amino acid profile present? (yes/no)  
- Any COA link, and if so, does it say N × 6.25?

If room left is 2 g and you still see a long amino-acid blend, you have a **math tension**. You will resolve it — or fail to — in PSL-25. Do not resolve it with a brand accusation today.

## What this draft will not say

It will not name a living brand as a spiker. It will not say nitrogen methods are a scam. It will not say you should only eat proteins measured by mass spectrometry. It will not connect nitrogen to a medical condition.
