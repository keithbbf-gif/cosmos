# Plant proteins — pea, rice, soy, hemp, blends

> **Pack:** Protein powder label literacy — staged draft.  
> **Jurisdiction:** United States (FD&C Act as amended by DSHEA, 1994).  
> **Class:** Consumer education. Not a product label. Not medical advice. Not an advertisement.  
> **Lock:** This draft does not claim that any protein powder diagnoses, treats, cures, or prevents any disease.  
> **Statute (teaching):** Under DSHEA, a dietary supplement label may not claim to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease.

## The move

A plant protein tub is not “whey without milk” as a single object. It is a **named seed or bean fraction** (or a blend of them) with its own amino pattern, leftover minerals, flavor problems, and allergen story. Literacy starts with the **first ingredient**, not with the leaf icon.

## Pea

Pea protein isolate/concentrate is the current default in many “vegan” tubs. It is a **legume** protein. Typical limiting talk is **methionine** (PSL-19). Typical leftover talk is **sodium** (PSL-16). Typical allergen talk: pea is **not** one of the nine major food allergens, but a person can still react to pea, and the facility may process soy or milk. “Plant-based” does not mean “allergen-free.”

Pea has a taste and a texture. Gums and flavors exist to hide them (PSL-27). A short ingredient list on an unflavored pea isolate is a different grocery item from a dessert-flavored blend.

## Rice

Rice protein is often the **lysine-low** partner in a pea-rice blend. Alone, it can look fine on a protein gram line and weaker on a quality-adjusted %DV. If a tub is **only** rice protein and the front says “complete,” do the PSL-13 arithmetic before you believe the adjective.

Rice is not a major allergen. Facility statements still matter.

## Soy

Soy protein isolate/concentrate is the old plant workhorse. It **is** a major food allergen. The Contains line should say **soy**. Soy is commonly treated as a complete plant protein in food-science teaching. That does not make it appropriate for someone with a soy allergy, and it does not make it a hormone drug. This pack will not do endocrine storytelling. It will say: **read the allergen line; ignore the forum.**

## Hemp, pumpkin, sunflower, and the rest

These show up as “something else” proteins, often in blends, sometimes alone in a niche tub. They can be limited in one or another indispensable amino acid. They can carry more fat (hemp) and more fiber. They are not automatically “more natural” in a legal sense (PSL-37). They are **different panels**. If the protein-by-weight is low, you are paying for the rest of the seed.

## Blends and the leaf icon

A leaf, a “plant-based” banner, and a brown-rice photograph can sit on a tub whose protein is 90% pea and 10% a seed you were meant to feel good about. Ingredient order is the adult document. If you want a blend for complementarity, you want **two proteins with complementary limits actually present in meaningful amounts**. A dusting of hemp for the photograph does not do PDCAAS work.

Some “plant” tubs add **watermelon seed** or **pumpkin** and then add **algal oil** or **MCT**. That is a calorie story (PSL-14), not a protein-identity upgrade.

## Dairy ghosts on plant tubs

Shared equipment, whey in a flavor system, or a “contains milk” line on a vegan-marketed product is a **trust and allergen** problem. It is also a labeling problem if “vegan” is a claim (PSL-30). Do not resolve it by tasting. Resolve it by the Contains line and, if you need a guarantee, by not buying the product.

## What this draft will not say

It will not say plant proteins cannot “build muscle.” It will not say soy is dangerous. It will not say pea is a treatment for a dairy allergy — a clinician plus the allergen line own that question. It will not say vegan powders prevent a disease that animal powders cause.
