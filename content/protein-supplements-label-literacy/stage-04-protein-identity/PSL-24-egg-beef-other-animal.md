# Egg, beef, and other animal proteins

> **Pack:** Protein powder label literacy — staged draft.  
> **Jurisdiction:** United States (FD&C Act as amended by DSHEA, 1994).  
> **Class:** Consumer education. Not a product label. Not medical advice. Not an advertisement.  
> **Lock:** This draft does not claim that any protein powder diagnoses, treats, cures, or prevents any disease.  
> **Statute (teaching):** Under DSHEA, a dietary supplement label may not claim to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease.

## The move

Not every animal protein tub is whey. Egg white, beef isolate, cricket, and bone-broth powders exist because someone cannot or will not use dairy, or because a brand wanted a story. The literacy method does not change: **first ingredient, serving math, leftover nutrients, allergen line, claim class.** The stories change. Do not let a ranch photograph do the panel’s job.

## Egg white protein

Dried egg white (albumen) is a complete food protein in ordinary teaching. The lid may say “egg white protein” or “albumin.” The major allergen is **egg**. People who avoid egg because of allergy, or because of a clinician’s advice, do not get a workaround from the word isolate.

Watch the **sodium** line; some egg white powders are high. Watch **added sugars** if the flavor is “meringue dessert.” Unflavored egg white used in baking may wear **Nutrition Facts** and live as a food (PSL-05, PSL-11). The same powder in a gym tub may wear Supplement Facts. Category follows representation.

## Beef protein isolate

Beef protein isolate is often made from **gelatin-family** raw material that has been processed into a more soluble powder. That means some “beef isolate” products behave more like **collagen** (PSL-23) than like a steak you chewed. The amino pattern and the %DV, not the ranch logo, tell you which object you bought.

If the amino chart is glycine- and proline-heavy and tryptophan is missing, you are in the collagen neighborhood even if the front says BEEF. If the product is truly a muscle-meat isolate, the pattern should not look like gelatin. This pack will not certify a living brand. It will tell you to **run the completeness checks you already have**.

“Hormone-free” and “grass-fed” on beef protein are PSL-37 problems. They are not protein grams.

## Cricket and other insect proteins

Insect powders appear as specialty items. Allergen cross-reactivity with **crustacean shellfish** is a known counseling topic; this pack will not play clinician. It will say: read any **shellfish** statement, read the ingredient’s common name, and if you have a shellfish allergy, this is a **clinician and a skip**, not a taste test.

Protein-by-weight and leftover fat/fiber on insect powders vary. The panel still wins.

## Bone-broth powders

Bone-broth protein is usually a **collagen-forward** product with a soup story. It may show sodium that looks like a bouillon cube. It may wear food labeling. Treat it as collagen-plus-salt until the amino pattern proves otherwise.

## Goat, sheep, and “A2” dairy

These are still **milk** proteins for allergen purposes unless the product is something else. “A2” is a marketing claim about beta-casein type. It does not delete the Contains: milk line. It does not become a disease claim in this pack.

## What “paleo” and “carnivore” are doing on a lid

Those words are **diet-identity** (PSL-30), not protein classes. A paleo-branded egg white and a paleo-branded collagen are different panels that share a font. Sort by ingredient, not by tribe.

If you cannot find one in the house, use a product page and write “page only.” The drill is the sort, not the purchase.

## What this draft will not say

It will not say beef isolate is steak. It will not say egg white is the “perfect protein” as a sales line. It will not say insect protein treats a food-system problem. It will not tell allergic readers to try a little.
