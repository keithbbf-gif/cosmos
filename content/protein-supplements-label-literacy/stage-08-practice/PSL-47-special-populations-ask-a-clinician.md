# Special populations — ask a clinician

> **Pack:** Protein powder label literacy — staged draft.  
> **Jurisdiction:** United States (FD&C Act as amended by DSHEA, 1994).  
> **Class:** Consumer education. Not a product label. Not medical advice. Not an advertisement.  
> **Lock:** This draft does not claim that any protein powder diagnoses, treats, cures, or prevents any disease.  
> **Statute (teaching):** Under DSHEA, a dietary supplement label may not claim to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease.

## The move

A facts panel is written for a **labeled serving**, usually represented for adults. It is not written as a pediatric monograph, a prenatal protocol, or a renal handbook. When the shopper is a child, pregnant, lactating, older and under care, or living with a condition or a medication list, this pack’s job **ends** and a **licensed clinician’s** job begins. The label still has to be readable — allergens, category, disease-claim junk — but **readability is not a care plan**.

## What the panel might still tell a caregiver (without becoming advice)

- Whether **milk, soy, egg, wheat, fish, sesame, peanut, tree nut, shellfish** are ingredients (PSL-29).  
- Whether the product is a **food or a supplement** (PSL-05).  
- Whether the serving is a 30 g isolate or a 333 g gainer (PSL-45).  
- Whether the lid is making a **disease claim** a caregiver should not have to see (PSL-31).  
- Whether a **lot** can be named if something goes wrong (PSL-39).

Those are **documents**. They are not “give this scoop to a child.”

## Children

Infant and young-child representations change protein Daily Values and can trigger “not a significant source of protein” language (21 CFR 101.9(c)(7)). A gym tub with a cartoon or a “family” flavor is still usually an **adult product**. This pack will not give a gram number for a child. It will say: **if the product is not represented for that age, do not invent a pediatric use from an adult panel.** Ask the clinician who knows the child.

## Pregnancy and lactation

The same paragraph of 101.9 lists a different protein RDI for pregnant and lactating women **as a labeling stick**, not as this pack’s menu. Prenatal care is a clinic. Powder marketing that targets pregnancy with disease or deficiency theater is a claim-class problem. This pack will not recommend a collagen or a whey for pregnancy. It will recommend the **person who is already providing prenatal care**, if that care exists, and a clinician visit if it does not.

## Older adults and people under clinical care

Protein as a nutrient is a normal part of food. Turning a tub into a protocol for a diagnosis is a disease-claim path we will not walk. Medications, swallowing problems, kidney or liver care, and allergen immunotherapy are all **outside** this curriculum. Bring the **label** to the clinician if they want to see it. Do not bring this pack as a substitute chart.

## Athletes in tested sport

That is a **seal and lot** problem (PSL-36, PSL-39), plus the sport’s current list. It is still not medical advice. A coach is not a substitute for a clinician when the question is a diagnosis. A clinician is not a substitute for the sport’s banned-substance program when the question is eligibility.

## What “ask a clinician” is not

It is not a dodge to sound responsible while still dosing you in the next sentence. There is no next sentence. It is not a claim that protein powder is dangerous as a class. It is not a claim that protein powder is required. It is a **jurisdiction line**: this pack does not hold a license.

## Label drill

If you are buying for someone other than a healthy adult self, write a **handoff card** (you may keep it private):

- Who the product is for (role, not a diagnosis you do not have a right to publish):  
- Allergen rows (ingredient / facility):  
- Category (food / supplement):  
- Serving size in grams:  
- Any disease-shaped sentence you found (quote):  
- Clinician you will ask (name or clinic):  

If you cannot name a clinician and the product is for a child or a pregnancy, the drill’s result is **stop and find one**, not “pick a flavor.”

## What this draft will not say

It will not say children should or should not use powder. It will not say pregnancy requires a scoop. It will not say older adults must supplement. It will not interpret a lab result. It will not tell you to start or stop a medication with a shake.
