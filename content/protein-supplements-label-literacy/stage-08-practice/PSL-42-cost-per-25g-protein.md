# Cost per 25 grams of declared protein

> **Pack:** Protein powder label literacy — staged draft.  
> **Jurisdiction:** United States (FD&C Act as amended by DSHEA, 1994).  
> **Class:** Consumer education. Not a product label. Not medical advice. Not an advertisement.  
> **Lock:** This draft does not claim that any protein powder diagnoses, treats, cures, or prevents any disease.  
> **Statute (teaching):** Under DSHEA, a dietary supplement label may not claim to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease.

## The move

Price per tub is a sales number. Price per **scoop** is a slightly better sales number. Price per **25 g of declared protein** is a comparison stick that survives scoop-size games. It is still only as honest as the **protein line** (PSL-17, PSL-25). Literacy uses the stick and keeps the caveats.

25 g is a **convenience stick**, not a recommended dose from this pack. It is close to many front-of-tub claims and to half of the 50 g adult DV. It is not a clinician’s number.

## The formula

1. Price you actually pay (after the subscription discount you will really use).  
2. Net grams (PSL-41).  
3. Protein grams per serving ÷ serving grams = **protein fraction**.  
4. Total declared protein in the bag ≈ net g × protein fraction.  
   - Or: protein g per serving × computed servings.  
5. Cost per gram of protein = price ÷ total declared protein g.  
6. Cost per 25 g = that number × 25.

Use **computed** servings if declared servings look wrong. Use **panel** protein grams, not the front, if they disagree.

## Worked fictional comparison

**FICTIONAL** prices and panels, September 2026, no living brand:

| | Isolate tub | Plant blend | Mass gainer |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Price | $42 | $38 | $36 |
| Net | 907 g | 907 g | 4500 g |
| Serving | 30 g | 36 g | 150 g |
| Protein / serving | 27 g | 20 g | 25 g |
| Protein fraction | 0.90 | 0.56 | 0.17 |
| Total protein g | 816 | 508 | 750 |
| $ / 25 g protein | $1.29 | $1.87 | $1.20 |

The gainer “wins” money per declared protein gram because you are also buying a sack of carbohydrate. If you did not want the carbohydrate, the $1.20 is a **false economy**. That is why PSL-14 sits behind this lesson. Cost-per-protein is a stick for **protein shoppers**, not for people who wanted a dessert mix and should use cost-per-calorie instead.

## Adjustments that are honest

- **Shipping** belongs in the price if you would not have bought in-store.  
- **A second scoop in the directions** belongs in cost-per-use, not in cost-per-25 g, unless you change the protein math too.  
- **Collagen grams** can be cheap and still be the wrong object (PSL-23). You may compute the number; do not call it the same stick as whey without a note.  
- **Seals and COAs** do not go into the formula. They are a different axis (stage 7). A more expensive sealed isolate can be the rational grocery item for a tested athlete and still lose the spreadsheet.

## What the stick cannot see

Flavor you will actually drink. Allergens. A clinician’s advice. The value of not supporting a disease-claim brand. Those are **outside** the formula. A spreadsheet that ignores them is not literacy; it is a hobby.

## Label drill

Run the formula on two real products you can price without buying (shelf tags or pages). Fill the isolate/plant/gainer table’s columns for your two. Write one sentence that starts with “The cheaper protein gram is _____ because _____” and names **fraction or extras**, not a diagnosis.

## What this draft will not say

It will not say the cheapest powder is the right powder. It will not say expensive means pure. It will not say 25 g is what you should eat. It will not say protein powder is cheaper than food as a general law — run the same stick on a carton of dairy or a bag of lentils if you want that argument, and still do not turn it into medical advice.
