# Claims lock — protein powder label literacy

**Binding on every draft in this pack.** If a later sentence fights this file, this file wins and the sentence is wrong.

## Statutory floor (United States)

The Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act of 1994 (DSHEA) did not turn powders into drugs. It created a food subcategory — **dietary supplements** — with its own panel, its own claim class, and a bright line:

A dietary supplement label may not claim to **diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease**.

That line is not a slogan we added for tone. It is the price of staying out of the drug definition in section 201(g) of the FD&C Act, as conditioned by section 403(r)(6) and 21 CFR 101.93.

The official disclaimer, when a structure/function (or related) statement appears, is:

> This statement has not been evaluated by the Food and Drug Administration. This product is not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease.

This pack **quotes** that disclaimer to teach it. This pack **does not** attach it to a product we are selling, because we are not selling one.

## What this pack may do

- Teach how to **read** a label: serving size, grams, % Daily Value, ingredient list, allergen line, lot code, seals.
- Explain **claim classes**: nutrient-content claims, structure/function claims, authorized health claims, qualified health claims, and disease claims.
- Describe **protein as a nutrient** in food-science terms (grams, amino acids, nitrogen methods, completeness scores) without promising a clinical outcome.
- Tell the reader to take personal medical questions to a **licensed clinician**.
- Show **fictional** labels and mark them fictional.

## What this pack may not do

- Say or imply that whey, casein, collagen, pea, rice, soy, beef, egg, or “mass gainer” powders diagnose, treat, cure, mitigate, or prevent any disease or class of diseases.
- Name a disease and then recommend a powder as the response.
- Use “clinically proven,” “doctor formulated,” or a before/after body as a substitute for a permitted claim class.
- Give dosages, stacking protocols, or “take this if you have …” instructions.
- Invent FDA “approval” of a brand, a tub, or this curriculum.
- Tell a manufacturer what illegal claim they should print. Teaching the **prohibition** is allowed. Drafting a disease claim “for example, to avoid” as if it were usable copy is not.

## Disease-claim shapes this pack refuses (21 CFR 101.93(g), teaching summary)

FDA looks at **context**, not magic words. A statement can be a disease claim if it says or implies the product:

1. Has an effect on a specific disease or class of diseases.
2. Has an effect on characteristic signs or symptoms of a disease.
3. Belongs to a product class understood as intended for disease.
4. Is a substitute for a therapy.
5. Augments a therapy for a disease.
6. Has a role in the body’s response to a disease or to a vector of disease.
7. Treats, prevents, or mitigates adverse events of a therapy for disease.
8. Otherwise suggests an effect on a disease (including pictures, names, and citations used that way).

We teach those shapes so the reader can **spot** them. We do not **use** them on behalf of a powder.

## Structure/function is not a loophole we will abuse

A permitted structure/function statement describes a nutrient’s role in **normal** structure or function (FDA’s example class: “calcium builds strong bones”) or a documented mechanism for **maintaining** that structure or function. It still needs:

- substantiation that it is truthful and not misleading,
- a 30-day notification to FDA after first marketing, and
- the bold disclaimer above.

This pack is not a notification. When a lesson mentions “supports muscle” language, it is showing a **class of marketing** the reader will see on tubs, and it immediately returns to the disclaimer and the disease-claim ban. It is not authorizing a brand to copy the sentence onto a label.

## Nutrient-content claims are not disease claims — and they still have math

“High protein,” “excellent source of protein,” and “good source of protein” are **nutrient-content** claims under 21 CFR 101.54. They are about **quantity relative to Daily Value**, not about treating a condition. Lessons in stage 6 walk the 10–19% and ≥20% DV thresholds. They do not convert those percentages into health outcomes.

## Voice bans

Do not write, in this pack, any of:

- “as a doctor / clinician / dietitian” (this pack has no license),
- “miracle,” “hack your hormones,” “detox,” “as seen on,”
- “prevents aging,” “anti-cancer,” “cures obesity,” “treats diabetes,”
- a recommendation that a person with a named diagnosis buy a powder.
