# Glossary — protein powder label literacy

Short definitions for the series. Not label copy. Not medical advice. Disease claims stay forbidden.

**% Daily Value (%DV).** On adult U.S. labels, protein %DV uses a Daily Reference Value of **50 grams** and, when required, a **PDCAAS-corrected** amount (21 CFR 101.9(c)(7)). Grams on the panel and %DV are not the same math.

**Amino acid spiking (nitrogen spiking).** Informal name for adding inexpensive free amino acids or other nitrogen-rich ingredients so a nitrogen × 6.25 test reads a higher “protein” number than the intact protein the shopper thinks they bought. A literacy topic, not a diagnosis of a named brand.

**Authorized health claim.** A claim about a substance–disease relationship that FDA has authorized by regulation after significant scientific agreement. Rare on protein tubs. Not a structure/function claim. This pack does not invent one.

**cGMP (21 CFR 111).** Current good manufacturing practice for dietary supplements. About how a firm makes and holds product. A “GMP” badge on a lid is marketing unless it names a real, current, third-party program you can look up.

**Contains statement.** Optional-format but all-or-nothing allergen line naming the food source of each major food allergen present (FALCPA / FASTER). On some supplements it sits next to Supplement Facts when there is no separate ingredient list.

**Conventional food.** A food that is not marketed as a dietary supplement. May carry a Nutrition Facts panel. Structure/function claims on conventional foods have a different disclaimer rule than supplements.

**DIAAS.** Digestible Indispensable Amino Acid Score. A scientific protein-quality score. **Not** the method 21 CFR 101.9 uses for %DV.

**Dietary ingredient.** Vitamin, mineral, herb or other botanical, amino acid, dietary substance for use by humans to supplement the diet, or a concentrate/metabolite/constituent/extract of those (FD&C Act § 201(ff)).

**Dietary supplement.** A product intended to supplement the diet, intended for ingestion, not represented as a conventional food or sole item of a meal, and containing a dietary ingredient. DSHEA’s category. Not a drug by virtue of being a powder.

**Disease claim.** A statement (words, pictures, citations, product names) that claims or implies the product diagnoses, mitigates, treats, cures, or prevents disease, as elaborated in 21 CFR 101.93(g). Forbidden on dietary supplement labeling unless it is a qualifying authorized/qualified health claim.

**DSHEA.** Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act of 1994.

**DSHEA disclaimer.** The boldface statement that FDA has not evaluated the structure/function (or related) statement and that the product is not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease (21 CFR 101.93).

**FALCPA / FASTER.** Federal allergen-labeling statutes. Nine major food allergens: milk, eggs, fish, Crustacean shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soybeans, sesame.

**Hydrolysate.** Protein treated with enzymes or acid to break some peptide bonds. A **process** word. Not a disease-treatment word.

**Isolate / concentrate.** Process classes for how much non-protein material was removed. Typical marketing bands (not statutory definitions): concentrates often lower protein-by-weight than isolates. Read the **grams per serving**, not the class word alone.

**Label / labeling.** The panel on the tub **and** written, printed, or graphic matter accompanying the product (including much of what a brand hosts as “product education”). Literacy that stops at the ink on the canister is incomplete.

**Major food allergen.** Statutory list in FD&C Act § 201(qq), including sesame as of 2023.

**Mass gainer.** Marketing name for a powder whose calories often come more from carbohydrate (and sometimes fat) than from protein. A Nutrition or Supplement Facts panel will show this if you read past the word “protein” on the front.

**New dietary ingredient (NDI).** A dietary ingredient not marketed in the United States before October 15, 1994. Many common food proteins are not NDIs. Novel extracts can be.

**Nitrogen × 6.25.** Default calculation converting measured nitrogen to a “protein” gram figure, because proteins are roughly 16% nitrogen. Free amino acids and some non-protein nitrogen also light up this math.

**Nutrient content claim.** A claim about the *level* of a nutrient (“high protein,” “good source”). 21 CFR 101.54. Not a disease claim.

**PDCAAS.** Protein digestibility-corrected amino acid score. Used in U.S. labeling math for protein %DV when that %DV is required.

**Proprietary blend.** A listing that names ingredients in a blend but may hide individual amounts. Legal in many cases; hostile to dose literacy.

**RACC.** Reference amount customarily consumed. Nutrient-content claims are judged per RACC, which may not equal the scoop the brand printed.

**Structure/function claim.** A statement about a nutrient’s role in normal structure or function, or a documented mechanism for maintaining that structure or function, that is **not** a disease claim. On supplements: substantiation, 30-day notification, disclaimer.

**Supplement Facts.** The mandatory box for dietary supplements (21 CFR 101.36). Different rules from Nutrition Facts (21 CFR 101.9), including how (b)(2) nutrients appear.

**Third-party seal.** A mark from NSF, USP, Informed-Sport, BSCG, or similar. At best, it speaks to identity, contaminants, or banned-substance panels the program actually ran. It is not FDA approval and not a disease claim.
