# Food, dietary supplement, or drug

> **Pack:** Protein powder label literacy — staged draft.  
> **Jurisdiction:** United States (FD&C Act as amended by DSHEA, 1994).  
> **Class:** Consumer education. Not a product label. Not medical advice. Not an advertisement.  
> **Lock:** This draft does not claim that any protein powder diagnoses, treats, cures, or prevents any disease.  
> **Statute (teaching):** Under DSHEA, a dietary supplement label may not claim to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease.

## The move

The same white powder can be sold as a **conventional food** (a protein drink mix with Nutrition Facts) or as a **dietary supplement** (a protein powder with Supplement Facts). It cannot lawfully be sold as a **drug** unless it is a drug — application, monograph, or other drug pathway. Category is not a vibe. Category is intended use plus the statutory definitions, and **the words on the label are how intended use shows its face**.

## Conventional food

A conventional food is food that is not being marketed as a dietary supplement. Think of a boxed drink mix that looks like cocoa, a baking protein, or a powder whose label says it is a food. The panel is **Nutrition Facts** (21 CFR 101.9). Ingredient lists, allergen rules, and nutrient-content claims still apply.

Structure/function language can appear on foods, but the **DSHEA disclaimer-and-notification machine** in 21 CFR 101.93 is a **dietary-supplement** machine. FDA has said conventional-food structure/function claims are about effects from **nutritive value**, and that those foods do not use the supplement disclaimer. If you are holding a tub with Nutrition Facts and no DSHEA disclaimer, you may be holding a food — or you may be holding a confused label. PSL-11 is where the two boxes are compared line by line.

## Dietary supplement

FD&C Act § 201(ff) is the definition. The pieces a shopper can actually use:

- It is intended to **supplement the diet**.
- It contains a **dietary ingredient** (protein as a dietary substance; amino acids; etc.).
- It is intended for **ingestion**.
- It is **not** represented as a conventional food or as the sole item of a meal or diet.
- It is not a tobacco product, and it is not (with listed exceptions) already approved or investigated as a drug in a way that blocks the supplement path.

The panel is **Supplement Facts** (21 CFR 101.36). If the label also bears a structure/function or related statement under § 403(r)(6), the disclaimer and the 30-day notification apply (PSL-31, PSL-32).

A protein powder is a common supplement. That does not make every protein powder a supplement. The **representation** matters. “Meal replacement,” “use as your lunch,” or a recipe that is obviously a food can pull the object back toward conventional food. Brands sometimes want supplement-claim tools and food-claim tools on the same lid. The statute does not owe them both.

## Drug

A drug includes articles intended for use in the diagnosis, cure, mitigation, treatment, or prevention of disease, and articles (other than food) intended to affect the structure or function of the body (FD&C Act § 201(g)). DSHEA carved a **conditional** path so that a dietary supplement may make certain structure/function statements **without** becoming a drug — if it stays inside § 403(r)(6) and 21 CFR 101.93, including the disclaimer and the ban on disease claims.

If a powder’s labeling claims to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent a disease (and it is not an authorized/qualified health claim that actually applies), FDA will treat that product as an **unapproved drug**, not as a spicy supplement. That is the cliff. This pack stands on the supplement/food side of the cliff and points at it. It does not jump.

## Intended use is a pile of evidence

Category is not only the word “supplement” on the front. Intended use is read from:

- the name,
- the panel type,
- the claims,
- the pictures,
- the directions (“take two capsules” vs “stir into oatmeal”),
- and sometimes what the same brand says off the tub.

A shopper’s private hope (“I bought this because of a diagnosis”) does not, by itself, turn the powder into a drug. The **seller’s** intended use does. Literacy still matters: if *you* are using a food as if it were a therapy, you have left the label’s job and entered a clinician’s.

## Why protein sits on the line so often

Protein is an ordinary nutrient in food. It is also packaged in scoops, sold next to creatine, and advertised with gym lighting. That combination tempts disease-adjacent speech (“joint,” “metabolic,” “immune”) that the category cannot bear. The correct response is not to whisper the disease claim. The correct response is to stay with **grams, ingredients, and permitted claim classes**.

## Label drill

Find two powders, or two product pages:

- One with **Nutrition Facts**.
- One with **Supplement Facts**.

Write, for each:

1. How the product names itself (food, drink mix, dietary supplement, other).  
2. Whether a DSHEA disclaimer is present.  
3. Whether directions sound like food (“mix in a glass”) or like a dosing ritual.  
4. One claim sentence, copied verbatim.

Do not decide which is “better.” Decide which **category** the label is trying to occupy. If a Nutrition Facts product is using the supplement disclaimer, or a Supplement Facts product is calling itself a meal, circle it. Stage 3 and stage 6 will tell you why that circle matters.

## What this draft will not say

It will not say supplements are safer than drugs or the reverse. It will not say foods are “just as regulated as drugs.” It will not recommend crossing categories. It will not claim a powder is appropriate for any diagnosis.
