# What a protein powder label is (and is not)

> **Pack:** Protein powder label literacy — staged draft.  
> **Jurisdiction:** United States (FD&C Act as amended by DSHEA, 1994).  
> **Class:** Consumer education. Not a product label. Not medical advice. Not an advertisement.  
> **Lock:** This draft does not claim that any protein powder diagnoses, treats, cures, or prevents any disease.  
> **Statute (teaching):** Under DSHEA, a dietary supplement label may not claim to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease.

## The move

A protein-powder label is a **regulated document**, not a personality. It is ink, type hierarchy, and a facts panel that has to survive a compliance reading. The tub is also a piece of packaging designed to be picked up in three seconds. Literacy starts when you can hold both facts at once: the panel is doing legal work; the front is doing sales work. They are allowed to live on the same canister. They are not required to be equally useful to you.

## Three objects people collapse into “the label”

**1. The principal display panel (the front).**  
Brand, flavor, a large gram number, a silhouette, a seal, sometimes a claim. This is where “25g PROTEIN” and “ISOLATE” earn their rent. It is still labeling. A number on the front is not a different species from a number on the back. If it is false or misleading, it is false or misleading *here*.

**2. The information panel (often the back or side).**  
Supplement Facts or Nutrition Facts, serving size, ingredients, allergen line, manufacturer or distributor, lot/best-by, and — if the product is a dietary supplement making a structure/function statement — a disclaimer you will learn to find with your thumb in PSL-32.

**3. Labeling that is not on the tub.**  
The FD&C Act’s idea of “labeling” is broader than shrink-wrap. A product page, a QR-linked “education” booklet, a card in the box, and some of what a brand emails after checkout can be labeling. Literacy that stops when the plastic ends will miss the sentence that actually makes the claim.

This series spends most of its pages on objects 1 and 2 because those are what you can photograph in a store aisle. Object 3 is why “the tub is clean” is not the end of the story.

## What the label is obligated to do

Depending on whether the powder is sold as a **dietary supplement** or as a **conventional food**, the box on the back follows different rules (PSL-05, PSL-11). Either way, the label has a short list of jobs:

- Identify what the product *is* (common or usual name, not only a fantasy brand).
- Identify who stands behind it (manufacturer, packer, or distributor, with a U.S. place of business).
- State net quantity.
- Declare a serving and the nutrients the applicable panel regulation requires.
- List ingredients in the required order and format.
- Declare major food allergens when they are present as ingredients (PSL-29).
- Avoid disease claims unless an authorized or qualified health-claim pathway actually applies — which, on a typical protein tub, it does not.

Notice what is missing from that list: “convince you this powder is a good idea.” Persuasion is allowed only inside the truth-and-not-misleading rule. It is not a separate duty to educate you well.

## What the label is not

**It is not a drug label.**  
A drug label exists because the product is a drug. A protein powder that stays a food or a dietary supplement does not get to borrow drug language. That is the whole DSHEA bargain, and it is why this pack repeats the disease-claim ban until it is boring.

**It is not a lab notebook.**  
You will not find the HPLC chromatogram, the heavy-metal raw data, or the nitrogen-method worksheet on the canister. Those live, if they live at all, in a certificate of analysis (PSL-40) or a quality-system file you cannot see. The label is a *summary with legal duties*, not the underlying measurements.

**It is not a meal.**  
“Protein powder” names a **form**. It does not tell you whether the calories in the scoop are mostly protein, mostly carbohydrate, or a dessert. Stage 3 is the stage that refuses to take the front of the tub at its word.

**It is not medical advice, and neither is this lesson.**  
If you are deciding what to eat because of a condition, a medication, an allergen, or a life stage, the right document is not a flavor name. It is a conversation with a licensed clinician who can see *you*. This series will say that again in PSL-47 and will not pretend a glossary replaces that conversation.

## A useful first posture

Pick up a tub. Cover the front with your hand. Read only the facts panel and the ingredient list. Say out loud:

1. What is one serving in **grams of powder**, not in scoops of folklore?
2. How many **grams of protein** are declared for that serving?
3. What else in the serving is large enough to show up as calories, sugar, or fat?
4. What is the **first ingredient**?
5. What does **Contains** say?

If you cannot answer those five, you do not yet have a label. You have artwork. Artwork is fine. It is just not literacy.

## Label drill

Find any protein powder you already own, or photograph one at a store without buying it. Write five lines, nothing more:

- Front gram claim:  
- Panel serving size (household **and** metric):  
- Panel protein grams:  
- First ingredient:  
- Supplement Facts or Nutrition Facts? (circle one)

If the front grams and the panel grams disagree, do not “average” them. Note the mismatch. You will use it in PSL-12 and PSL-13.

## What this draft will not say

It will not say a powder builds muscle, burns fat, “heals recovery,” or is required for health. It will not tell you to switch brands. It will not treat a missing seal as proof of danger or a present seal as proof of virtue.
