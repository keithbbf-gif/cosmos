# Who regulates the tub

> **Pack:** Protein powder label literacy — staged draft.  
> **Jurisdiction:** United States (FD&C Act as amended by DSHEA, 1994).  
> **Class:** Consumer education. Not a product label. Not medical advice. Not an advertisement.  
> **Lock:** This draft does not claim that any protein powder diagnoses, treats, cures, or prevents any disease.  
> **Statute (teaching):** Under DSHEA, a dietary supplement label may not claim to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease.

## The move

Shoppers say “Is this FDA approved?” as if one door exists. For a protein powder sold as a food or dietary supplement in the United States, **approval of the finished tub is the wrong door**. Several chairs share the room. Literacy is knowing which chair owns which sentence.

## Chair 1 — FDA (most of what is *on* the tub)

The Food and Drug Administration administers the FD&C Act for foods and dietary supplements. For a typical protein powder that stays in those categories, FDA’s world includes:

- Whether the product is misbranded or adulterated.
- Whether the correct panel is used (Nutrition Facts vs Supplement Facts).
- Whether nutrient-content claims meet 21 CFR 101.54.
- Whether a structure/function statement is actually a disease claim (21 CFR 101.93).
- Whether major food allergens are declared (FALCPA / FASTER).
- Whether the firm’s manufacturing, packaging, labeling, and holding meet 21 CFR 111 if the product is a dietary supplement.
- New dietary ingredient notification issues when they apply (PSL-08).

FDA does **not**, for an ordinary dietary supplement, pre-approve the label the way it pre-approves a new drug application. “Not pre-approved” is not the same as “not regulated.” PSL-07 exists to keep those two sentences from collapsing.

## Chair 2 — FTC (much of what is *around* the tub)

The Federal Trade Commission Act reaches **advertising**. A claim that never made it onto the canister can still live in a creator’s script, a search ad, an influencer’s story, or a brand’s comparison chart. FTC’s Health Products Compliance Guidance (2022) is the document counsel actually reach for when the question is “what evidence do you have for that outcome sentence?”

A practical literacy split:

- **On the panel and the legally required statements** — start with FDA’s labeling rules.
- **On the promise that made you click** — ask whether that promise is on the tub at all, and assume FTC cares even if the panel is quiet.

This pack teaches the tub first. It will still nod at ads when the ad is doing the claim work the tub was careful not to do (PSL-33, PSL-35).

## Chair 3 — States (warnings and unfair-practice laws)

California’s Proposition 65 is the warning shoppers actually see: a sentence about a listed chemical. It is a **state** regime with its own safe-harbor numbers and its own plaintiff’s bar. It is not an FDA potency rating (PSL-38).

Other states run consumer-protection statutes that can reach deceptive supplement marketing even when a federal inspector has never touched that lot. “Legal in the U.S.” is not a single stamp.

## Chair 4 — USDA (only when the claim actually invokes USDA)

An **organic** claim that uses the USDA organic mark is living in the National Organic Program, not in DSHEA. A powder can be a dietary supplement *and* an organic food. The organic mark does not move the product into a drug category and does not waive the disease-claim ban (PSL-37).

## Chair 5 — Sport bodies and retailers (private rules)

NSF Certified for Sport, Informed-Sport, a college athletic department, a military exchange, or a retailer quality program can all refuse a lot that is lawful to sell at a gas station. Those are **private** gates. They matter to the people they bind. They are not FDA approval (PSL-36).

## What “FDA approved” would have meant

When people say they want an FDA-approved protein powder, they are usually asking one of four different questions:

1. Did a drug division approve this as a treatment? **No — and if the brand talks that way, the product has a category problem.**
2. Did FDA pre-clear this exact label? **Generally no for a routine supplement.**
3. Could FDA take the product off the market if it is adulterated or misbranded? **Yes, that is the ordinary food/supplement enforcement world.**
4. Did a facility register or list something? **Registration and listing are not the same as a blessing of your scoop.**

Write the question you actually have. Then the chair is easier to name.

## Label drill

On one tub or product page, make a four-column card:

| Sentence or mark | Where it lives | First chair to ask | Why |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| “25g protein” on the front | | | |
| Supplement Facts box | | | |
| “Supports recovery” on the site | | | |
| Prop 65 warning | | | |
| NSF or Informed-Sport mark | | | |
| “USDA Organic” | | | |

If you put every row under FDA, redo the card. The drill is the split, not the brand.

## What this draft will not say

It will not say FDA is “asleep.” It will not say FTC is the real FDA. It will not say a state warning means the powder is illegal to sell. It will not say a sport seal makes a powder appropriate for a medical condition.

## Carry forward

PSL-05 draws the category triangle: conventional food, dietary supplement, drug. A protein powder can sit in the first or the second. The third is a different object — and disease claims are how a tub tries to climb there without the drug dossier.
