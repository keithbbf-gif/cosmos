# Structure/function claims vs disease claims

> **Pack:** Protein powder label literacy — staged draft.  
> **Jurisdiction:** United States (FD&C Act as amended by DSHEA, 1994).  
> **Class:** Consumer education. Not a product label. Not medical advice. Not an advertisement.  
> **Lock:** This draft does not claim that any protein powder diagnoses, treats, cures, or prevents any disease.  
> **Statute (teaching):** Under DSHEA, a dietary supplement label may not claim to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease.

## The move

Stage 6 is the speech stage. This file is the hinge. A dietary supplement may, if it follows the rest of the machine, describe a nutrient’s role in **normal structure or function**. It may not claim to **diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent disease**. FDA wrote the second sentence into criteria at 21 CFR 101.93(g) because people kept trying to do the second thing with the first thing’s adjectives. Your job as a reader is to sort sentences into piles. Your job is not to invent a clever third pile for a brand you like.

## What a structure/function statement is allowed to be

FDA’s teaching examples are deliberately boring: “calcium builds strong bones,” “fiber maintains bowel regularity,” “antioxidants maintain cell integrity.” The pattern is:

- a **nutrient or dietary ingredient**,
- a **normal** structure or function, or a **documented mechanism** for maintaining it,
- **no** disease, **no** characteristic signs of a disease used as the target.

On a protein tub, the analog is speech about protein as a nutrient in ordinary muscle and ordinary diet — not speech about a wasting diagnosis, not speech about a joint disease, not speech about an immune disease.

This pack will **not** hand a brand a ready-to-file structure/function line. Notification, substantiation, and counsel exist for that. We will only show the **shape** so you can recognize it.

## What a disease claim is (criteria in shopper language)

21 CFR 101.93(g) says FDA will find a disease claim if the statement, in context, claims the product:

1. Has an effect on a **specific disease or class of diseases**.  
2. Has an effect on **characteristic signs or symptoms** of a disease.  
3. Belongs to a **class of products** understood as intended for disease.  
4. Is a **substitute** for a therapy.  
5. **Augments** a therapy for a disease.  
6. Has a role in the body’s **response to a disease** or to a vector.  
7. Treats, prevents, or mitigates **adverse events of a therapy**.  
8. Otherwise **suggests** an effect on a disease.

Context includes **pictures**, **citations**, **product names**, and **testimonials**. A citation to a paper about a disease, next to a buy button, is not a neutral bibliography.

## Protein-aisle sentences, sorted (teaching only)

These are **illustrative sorts**, not approvals.

| Sentence shape | Typical pile |
| --- | --- |
| “25 g protein per serving” | Nutrient-content (PSL-34), if true |
| “Protein contributes to the maintenance of muscle mass” (as a class of EU-style nutrient speech you will also see imported onto U.S. sites) | Structure/function-shaped; still needs the U.S. machine if used on a U.S. supplement |
| “Supports recovery after exercise” | Structure/function-shaped **if** it stays with ordinary exercise, not with a diagnosis |
| “Treats wasting,” “for sarcopenia,” “prevents osteoporosis” | Disease-claim-shaped — **out** |
| “Use instead of your prescription” | Disease-claim-shaped (substitute) — **out** |
| “Take with your therapy for [disease] to help the therapy” | Disease-claim-shaped (augment) — **out** |
| A swollen joint photo + “mobility” | Context can pull “mobility” into signs/symptoms |

This pack does not use the out-pile as copy. It uses it as a **recognition drill**.

## Authorized health claims are a different door

A true **authorized health claim** is a substance–disease relationship FDA has authorized by regulation after significant scientific agreement. They are rare, specific, and usually carry required wording. They are **not** the DSHEA structure/function path. A protein tub that says “heart disease” or “cancer” without that machinery is not being creative. It is being a drug in costume.

This pack will not invent an authorized health claim for protein powder.

## Why “implied” is the whole game

Nobody prints “cures diabetes” on a national whey brand’s canister in 2026 if counsel got a vote. They print a glucose photograph, a testimonial about a lab number, a doctor’s coat, and the word “metabolic.” 101.93(g) is built for that. PSL-35 is the picture lesson. Start here: **if the only way the sentence is interesting is that it points at a disease, it is not a structure/function sentence.**

## Label drill

Reuse the page you saved in PSL-03. Sort **every** claim sentence into:

- N — nutrient content  
- S — structure/function-shaped  
- D — disease-claim-shaped  
- X — decoration / identity (vegan, NSF, chocolate)

You need at least one S and you should hunt for a D. If you cannot find a D on the tub, check the product **page**. Ads are where D hides when the canister was cleaned.

## What this draft will not say

It will not offer a “compliant” disease-adjacent rewrite a brand can paste. It will not say structure/function claims are proven. It will not say protein powder maintains muscle as **this pack’s** claim — we are teaching a class of speech, not filing a notification. It will not recommend a powder for any diagnosis.
