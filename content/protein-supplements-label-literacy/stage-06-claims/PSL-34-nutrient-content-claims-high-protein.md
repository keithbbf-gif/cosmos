# Nutrient-content claims — “high protein” and friends

> **Pack:** Protein powder label literacy — staged draft.  
> **Jurisdiction:** United States (FD&C Act as amended by DSHEA, 1994).  
> **Class:** Consumer education. Not a product label. Not medical advice. Not an advertisement.  
> **Lock:** This draft does not claim that any protein powder diagnoses, treats, cures, or prevents any disease.  
> **Statute (teaching):** Under DSHEA, a dietary supplement label may not claim to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease.

## The move

“High protein,” “excellent source of protein,” “rich in protein,” “good source of protein,” “contains protein” are **nutrient-content claims**. They talk about **how much** of a nutrient is there relative to Daily Value, not about treating a condition. 21 CFR 101.54 is the rule. They still have to be **true**. They still can **trigger** protein %DV on the panel (PSL-13). They are the rare claim class on a tub you can check with a pencil.

## The thresholds (ordinary foods and supplements, shopper-level)

Per **reference amount customarily consumed (RACC)**, not per whatever scoop looks good:

- **“High,” “rich in,” “excellent source of”** — 20% or more of the DV.  
- **“Good source,” “contains,” “provides”** — 10% to 19% of the DV.

For adult protein, DV is **50 g**. So, as a back-of-envelope **if** the RACC equals the declared serving and **if** quality correction is 1.0:

- 20% DV ≈ 10 g quality-adjusted protein,  
- 10% DV ≈ 5 g.

A 25 g whey isolate serving is not the hard case. The hard case is a **small RACC**, a **plant protein with PDCAAS < 1**, or a **gainer** whose front says HIGH PROTEIN because 25 g is a lot next to a candy bar but not next to the 150 g scoop’s calories.

## Why RACC is the adult unit

Brands choose serving sizes. Claims are judged against **RACC** (and related 101.13 rules), so a tiny serving cannot always manufacture “excellent source” by shrinking the denominator, and a huge serving cannot always manufacture it by dumping two meals into a scoop without consequences. This pack will not reprint the entire RACC table. It will say: **if the claim looks too easy, ask whether the serving is the RACC or a marketing cup.**

## Synonyms and implied nutrient-content claims

“Packed with protein,” “protein-rich,” a giant “25g” on the front — these can be nutrient-content claims even without the magic words. The front gram number **is** a claim about level. That is why it can force %DV inside the box.

A picture of a bodybuilder is not, by itself, a 101.54 claim. Combined with “25g,” it is still the 25 g that you check.

## What nutrient-content claims are not

They are not structure/function claims. They do not need the DSHEA disclaimer **because of 101.54**. (If the same lid also has a structure/function sentence, the disclaimer is there for **that** sentence.)

They are not disease claims. “High protein” does not mean “treats protein-energy malnutrition.” If a brand makes that leap, they have left 101.54.

They are not a personal target. 20% DV is a **labeling threshold**, not this pack telling you to eat 10 g of powder.

## The quality trap, again

If PDCAAS is 0.5, 20 g of nitrogen-method protein is 10 g quality-adjusted — 20% DV, not 40%. A plant tub can be an **excellent source** on the corrected number and still disappoint someone who divided 20 by 50 in their head. A collagen tub can fail to be an excellent source even with a large gram line. The %DV is the tie-breaker when it is printed.

## Label drill

On one tub that says high / excellent / good / rich / “25g protein”:

- Exact claim words:  
- Declared serving g and protein g:  
- Printed %DV:  
- Raw g ÷ 50:  
- Does printed %DV meet 20% (high/excellent) or 10–19% (good)?  
- Does the serving look like a snack scoop or a meal bucket?  

If there is no %DV but there is a front gram claim, write “trigger may apply — %DV missing.” That is a finding, not a lawsuit you are filing from this file.

## What this draft will not say

It will not say high-protein claims make a powder good for a diagnosis. It will not say you should chase 20% DV from a tub. It will not call a good-source product a failure. It will not draft a claim for a brand.

## Carry forward
