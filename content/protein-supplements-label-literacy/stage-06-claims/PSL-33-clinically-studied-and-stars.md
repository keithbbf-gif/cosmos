# “Clinically studied,” stars, and borrowed authority

> **Pack:** Protein powder label literacy — staged draft.  
> **Jurisdiction:** United States (FD&C Act as amended by DSHEA, 1994).  
> **Class:** Consumer education. Not a product label. Not medical advice. Not an advertisement.  
> **Lock:** This draft does not claim that any protein powder diagnoses, treats, cures, or prevents any disease.  
> **Statute (teaching):** Under DSHEA, a dietary supplement label may not claim to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease.

## The move

When a brand cannot or will not print a disease claim, it borrows **the furniture of medicine**: the word clinical, a star rating, a person in a coat, a university name, a graph with no axis. FTC’s advertising world cares whether the furniture is **substantiated**. FDA’s labeling world cares whether the furniture is a **disease claim in costume**. Literacy asks a ruder question first: **studied what, in whom, at what dose, compared to what, and is that object this scoop?**

## “Clinically studied” is not “clinically proven to treat”

**Studied** means someone ran a study. It does not mean:

- the study was in humans,
- the study used this formula,
- the study used this serving,
- the study won,
- the study was published,
- or the endpoint was a disease (which this pack would not want on a supplement anyway).

A whey protein can be “studied” as a food. A flavor system can be unevaluated. A brand can cite a 1990s paper on whey generally and print the words on a 2026 dessert tub. That is **borrowed literature**, not a monograph.

If the citation’s endpoint is a **disease**, the citation is trying to climb the cliff. Do not help it.

## Stars and “#1 doctor recommended”

Stars are **retailer or survey theater** unless the method is named. “Doctor recommended” needs a **who** (which doctors, how many, paid or not, recommended for what). A nameless doctor is an actor. A named endorsement is still not a 101.93 notification and still not a reason this pack will recommend a powder.

## University and lab names

A university can license a logo, rent a lab, or employ a scientist who once wrote a paper. None of those events is FDA approval. If the university mark is load-bearing for you, look for the **actual relationship** (sponsored research, trademark license, alumni founder). If you cannot find it, the mark is furniture.

## Graphs without numbers

A rising bar labeled “performance” with no units, no time, no N, and no comparator is a **picture** (PSL-35). It is not a facts-panel line. It cannot become protein grams.

## “Patented”

A patent is a **property right**, not a finding of effectiveness, and not a finding of lawfulness as a supplement. Patented blending methods exist for powders that still misdeclare serving size. Read the panel.

## How to deflate the halo in one pass

Ask, in order:

1. Is there a **citation** you can open?  
2. Is the studied object **this product** or “whey protein as a substance”?  
3. Is the endpoint a **nutrient/intake/body-composition food-science** endpoint, or a **disease** endpoint?  
4. Is the dose **this serving**?  
5. Who **paid**?  
6. Does the tub still have the **disclaimer** pair if the halo is structure/function-shaped?

If (3) is a disease endpoint, stop. The halo is not for this category.

## Label drill

Find one “clinically” / “doctor” / “university” / star cluster on a protein page. Write a six-line deflation card answering the questions above. If you cannot open a citation, write “citation: unopenable” and treat the cluster as **furniture**.

## What this draft will not say

It will not say all studies are fake. It will not say you should ignore evidence as a life practice. It will not say a study of protein as a food is a reason to use powder as a therapy. It will not “clinically prove” anything in this curriculum.

## Carry forward
