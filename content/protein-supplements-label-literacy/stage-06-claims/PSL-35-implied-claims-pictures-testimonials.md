# Implied claims — pictures, testimonials, and vibe

> **Pack:** Protein powder label literacy — staged draft.  
> **Jurisdiction:** United States (FD&C Act as amended by DSHEA, 1994).  
> **Class:** Consumer education. Not a product label. Not medical advice. Not an advertisement.  
> **Lock:** This draft does not claim that any protein powder diagnoses, treats, cures, or prevents any disease.  
> **Statute (teaching):** Under DSHEA, a dietary supplement label may not claim to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease.

## The move

21 CFR 101.93(g) says FDA looks at **context**. FTC looks at **net impression**. Both ideas punish the clever lid that never uses the verb “treat” and still sells a treatment. This lesson is a picture lesson. The facts panel cannot save a disease claim that the **photo** made.

## Pictures that do work they should not do

- A diseased or post-surgical joint next to “mobility.”  
- A glucometer, a heart, a pink ribbon used as a product story.  
- Before/after bodies with dates and a disease story in the caption.  
- A prescription bottle in the same frame as the tub.  
- Organs with inflamed color palettes.

Any of these can pull an otherwise soft adjective into **signs, symptoms, or disease class**. This pack will not use those pictures. When you see them on a product, sort them as **context**, not as decoration.

## Testimonials

A testimonial is labeling or advertising if the brand uses it. “This finally fixed my [diagnosis]” is a disease claim **in a customer’s mouth**. Putting it on the product page does not make it a customer’s private diary. The brand chose it.

“I recovered faster after my workouts” is softer and still needs to be truthful as a typical result if it is an ad. This pack will not police every athletic adjective. It will say: **if the testimonial names a diagnosis, the tub has named a diagnosis.**

## Hashtags and QR codes

A QR code that opens a blog titled for a disease is **labeling that you have to walk to**. Literacy includes scanning once. If the QR is a recipe, fine. If the QR is a condition protocol, you have left the canister’s careful back panel.

## Names

A product name that is already a therapy nickname, a disease nickname, or a drug look-alike is context. “Whey” is a name. A name that sounds like a clinic is a name doing claim work. You do not need to be humorless. You need to notice.

## What is usually *not* a disease picture

- A person lifting a barbell.  
- A glass of chocolate milk.  
- A cow, a pea, a kitchen.  
- A nutrition-facts-style callout of grams.

Those can still be misleading (a gainer photographed as an isolate), but they are not, by themselves, 101.93(g) objects. Sort honestly.

## Net impression as a shopper tool

Cover the facts panel. Look at the front and the first screen of the product page for five seconds. Say out loud what the product is **for**. If you hear a diagnosis, the net impression is a diagnosis — even if the back panel has a perfect disclaimer. The disclaimer cannot un-picture a glucometer.

## Label drill

Take five screenshots or photos: front, back, a testimonial, a lifestyle image, and the first product-page screen. For each, write the **job** the image is doing in one noun: *identity / quantity / structure-function / disease / dessert / seal / nothing*.

If any noun is *disease*, write the lock sentences under that screenshot. Do not rewrite the brand’s caption into a “safer” version. This pack does not produce safer disease claims.

## What this draft will not say

It will not say athletic photos are illegal. It will not say testimonials are always false. It will not say you should report a picture you dislike. It will not use a before/after as a reason to buy or avoid a powder for a medical condition.

## Carry forward

Stage 6 is done. Stage 7 is the sticker aisle: seals, “natural,” metals warnings, lot codes, and COAs. PSL-36 opens third-party marks.
