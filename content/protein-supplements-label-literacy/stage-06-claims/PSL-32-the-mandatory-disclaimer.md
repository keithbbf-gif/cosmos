# The mandatory DSHEA disclaimer

> **Pack:** Protein powder label literacy — staged draft.  
> **Jurisdiction:** United States (FD&C Act as amended by DSHEA, 1994).  
> **Class:** Consumer education. Not a product label. Not medical advice. Not an advertisement.  
> **Lock:** This draft does not claim that any protein powder diagnoses, treats, cures, or prevents any disease.  
> **Statute (teaching):** Under DSHEA, a dietary supplement label may not claim to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease.

## The move

When a dietary supplement bears a statement under FD&C Act § 403(r)(6) — structure/function, general well-being, or a qualifying classical deficiency statement — 21 CFR 101.93 requires a **disclaimer** in boldface. The disclaimer is not a vibe. It is two jobs in two sentences: **FDA did not evaluate this statement**, and **this product is not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease**. If you cannot find those jobs, the statement is missing its legal pair — or the product is not using that path.

## The words (teaching quotation)

The regulation’s disclaimer is:

> This statement has not been evaluated by the Food and Drug Administration. This product is not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease.

You will also see **“These statements”** when the brand has more than one. That plural is ordinary. What is not ordinary is a rewrite that drops “diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent,” or that hides the sentences in 6-point gray on a foil bag.

## Where it has to live

101.93 is picky about **placement and type**. The shopper version:

- It must be **prominent** and **bold**.  
- It must sit **with the claim** or be connected by a **symbol** that actually leads you to it.  
- A claim on the **front** with a disclaimer only on a website is not the pair.  
- A disclaimer on the tub with the real claim only in a creator’s video is a split that FTC will still understand as advertising (PSL-04).

Literacy: when you see a structure/function-shaped sentence, **your thumb should already be looking for the bold pair.**

## What the disclaimer does not do

It does **not**:

- legalize a disease claim (you cannot disclaim your way into “treats diabetes”),
- mean FDA reviewed and shrugged,
- mean the statement is true,
- replace substantiation,
- replace the 30-day notification,
- or apply to a conventional **food** the same way (PSL-05, PSL-11).

A brand that prints the disclaimer **and** a disease claim has printed a contradiction. Believe the disease claim as the intended use. The disclaimer cannot un-ring it.

## What the disclaimer does do, for a reader

It is a **category signal**. Its presence says: this firm is trying to occupy the supplement structure/function path. Combined with Supplement Facts, it is a strong hint you are not holding a Nutrition Facts drink mix.

Its absence on a tub that is still shouting about muscles and metabolism says: either this is a food, or the speech is being treated as something else (nutrient content, puffery, or a claim that forgot its pair). Sort it; do not assume silence is innocence.

## Notification is the sibling you cannot see

The 30-day notification to FDA is not printed on the lid. You will not get a tracking number in the scoop. The disclaimer is the **public** sibling. The notification is the **file** sibling. Neither sibling is a scientific paper.

## Label drill

On one supplement protein:

1. Copy every structure/function-shaped sentence (front, back, lid).  
2. Copy the disclaimer verbatim (or write “none”).  
3. Note type size / bold / color as best you can (e.g. “bold, back panel, under ingredients”).  
4. Note whether a **symbol** on the front points to the back.  
5. Check the **website** for additional sentences that never appear on the tub.

If (1) is full and (2) is none, you have a pair failure. If (5) is where the interesting sentences live, you have an **ad** at FTC’s table that the canister was too careful to hold.

## What this draft will not say

It will not say a disclaimer makes a powder safe. It will not say you can ignore claims because a disclaimer exists. It will not say this pack’s own lock sentences are a 101.93 disclaimer — they are **curriculum locks**, not product labeling. It will not recommend a product that “had a nice disclaimer.”

## Carry forward

PSL-33 is the halo that tries to outrun both the claim class and the disclaimer: “clinically studied,” stars, and doctors in white coats.
