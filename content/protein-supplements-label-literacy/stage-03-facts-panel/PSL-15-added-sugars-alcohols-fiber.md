# Added sugars, sugar alcohols, and fiber lines

> **Pack:** Protein powder label literacy — staged draft.  
> **Jurisdiction:** United States (FD&C Act as amended by DSHEA, 1994).  
> **Class:** Consumer education. Not a product label. Not medical advice. Not an advertisement.  
> **Lock:** This draft does not claim that any protein powder diagnoses, treats, cures, or prevents any disease.  
> **Statute (teaching):** Under DSHEA, a dietary supplement label may not claim to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease.

## The move

After protein, the next argument on a tub is sweetness. The panel has more than one line for that argument: **total carbohydrate**, **total sugars**, **added sugars**, sometimes **sugar alcohol**, sometimes **fiber**. Brands pick a front slogan — “no added sugar,” “keto,” “low carb” — that may be true of one line and false of the feeling in your mouth. Literacy reads the cluster, then the ingredient list (PSL-26), and does not stop at the slogan.

## Added sugars vs total sugars

**Total sugars** includes sugars that were already in the dairy or the fruit powder and sugars that were added. **Added sugars** is the 2016-era line that is supposed to show sugars put in during processing (plus certain concentrated sugars). A whey concentrate can show a gram of sugar that is mostly lactose and **0 g added sugar**. A “cake batter” whey can show 6 g added sugar and a dessert’s worth of calories from carbohydrate (PSL-14).

“No added sugar” is a **nutrient-content style** claim with its own conditions in 21 CFR 101.60. This lesson does not litigate every condition. It tells you to **look at the added-sugars line and the ingredient list together**. Cane sugar, brown rice syrup, molasses, and many syrups are obvious. Fruit-juice concentrate used for sweetness is the line people argue about; the panel and the rulebook, not a forum, are the place that argument ends.

## Sugar alcohols

Erythritol, xylitol, maltitol, sorbitol, and friends can appear as a voluntary or required line when a claim is made about them. They often exist to keep “sugar” low while keeping sweetness high. They still appear in the **carbohydrate** neighborhood and they still have caloric rules that are not “zero because Twitter said so.”

This pack will not give GI-tract advice. It will say: if the ingredient list is a parade of -itol and the serving is large (gainers, bars, thick shakes), the panel’s sugar line can look saintly while the carbohydrate line does not. Read both.

## Fiber

Some protein powders add inulin, chicory root fiber, soluble corn fiber, or gums that the firm counts toward **dietary fiber**. Fiber grams change the carbohydrate story and sometimes the texture. They do not turn the powder into a vegetable.

If a brand makes a fiber claim, the declaration rules tighten — the same family of “you talked about it, now you must show it” that protein %DV uses. A quiet fiber line with no claim is still worth a glance when you are doing the 4-4-9 energy sketch, because fiber is not fully 4 kcal/g in the way sugar is. Do not over-correct in your head. Use the **calorie line** as the energy authority and the fiber line as composition.

## Stevia, monk fruit, sucralose, acesulfame potassium

These are **not** the added-sugars line. They are high-intensity sweeteners. They can produce a “0 g added sugar” panel that still tastes like a milkshake. That is legally coherent and emotionally surprising. PSL-26 walks them as ingredients. Here, the panel lesson is: **0 g sugar is not “unsweetened.”** Unsweetened is an ingredient-list fact (and sometimes a claim with its own meaning).

## Supplement Facts can omit a zero

If added sugars round to zero, a Supplement Facts panel may omit the line (PSL-11). Nutrition Facts is more likely to show the skeleton you are used to. Do not infer a hidden syrup from a missing line on a supplement without reading the ingredients. Do not infer purity from a zero that is only rounding.

## What this draft will not say

It will not say sugar is a toxin or that non-nutritive sweeteners are a therapy. It will not promise a powder will help someone “control blood sugar.” It will not tell you to fear fiber additives. It will not treat “keto” as a medical protocol.

## Carry forward

PSL-16 is the quiet nutrients that ride along with the protein source: sodium, calcium, cholesterol, potassium, iron — the lines that tell you what the powder **used to be** (milk, pea, soy) after the marketing forgot.
