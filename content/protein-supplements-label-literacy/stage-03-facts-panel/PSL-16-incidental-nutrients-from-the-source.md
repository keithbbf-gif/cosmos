# Incidental nutrients — what rode along with the protein

> **Pack:** Protein powder label literacy — staged draft.  
> **Jurisdiction:** United States (FD&C Act as amended by DSHEA, 1994).  
> **Class:** Consumer education. Not a product label. Not medical advice. Not an advertisement.  
> **Lock:** This draft does not claim that any protein powder diagnoses, treats, cures, or prevents any disease.  
> **Statute (teaching):** Under DSHEA, a dietary supplement label may not claim to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease.

## The move

Protein powders are **fractions of foods**. Whey and casein remember milk. Soy remembers the bean. Pea remembers the seed. When a firm does not strip a mineral or a fat to zero, the facts panel is supposed to show the leftovers that cannot be declared as zero. Those leftovers are not “bonus medicine.” They are **composition**. Reading them is how you notice that two 25 g protein scoops are not the same object.

## Milk-derived powders

Whey concentrates often carry more **lactose**, **fat**, **cholesterol**, **calcium**, **phosphorus**, and **sodium** than isolates from the same dairy stream. Isolates are the same idea with more of the non-protein material washed out — not a different mythology. Casein and milk-protein blends can show a calcium line that looks large next to an isolate. That calcium came from milk’s ordinary mineral load, or from an added calcium salt. The ingredient list tells you which.

Cholesterol, when present above rounding, is a **declaration**, not a morality play. This pack will not turn a cholesterol milligram into a cardiac story. That would be a disease claim in a curriculum costume.

## Plant-derived powders

Pea protein often shows **sodium** that surprises people who think “plant” means “unsalted.” Some of that sodium is process. Some is flavor. Soy can show isoflavone chatter **off** the panel (in blogs) that the panel itself will not settle — believe the panel for sodium, calcium, iron, and potassium; treat blog hormone stories as off-scope (and not as medical advice).

Rice protein is often low in some minerals the pea is not. Blends exist partly to fix **amino** gaps (PSL-19) and accidentally average the mineral picture. Read the panel in front of you, not a generic “pea vs whey” chart from 2018.

## Added minerals vs inherited minerals

A line that says calcium 200 mg could be:

- leftover from milk protein, or  
- calcium carbonate / calcium phosphate added to a plant blend so the panel looks more “dairy-like,” or  
- both.

The **ingredient list** is the tie-breaker. “Calcium phosphate” in the list means someone put it there. No calcium salt in the list plus a milk protein first ingredient means you are probably looking at the dairy’s own mineral.

Neither version is a treatment for a bone condition. Both versions are reasons two scoops are not interchangeable if a clinician has you watching a mineral.

## Sodium is the leftover shoppers skip

Flavors, sodium caseinate, processing salts, and “salted caramel” will move sodium more than a philosophy about isolation. If you compare powders on protein grams alone, you can “upgrade” to a product that added 250 mg sodium per serving for taste. That is not illegal. It is a **different grocery item**.

## What is supposed to be absent

If a (b)(2) nutrient rounds to zero, Supplement Facts may omit it (PSL-11). Do not invent a deficiency because a line is missing. Do not invent a purity halo either. Missing cholesterol on a pea powder is expected. Missing sodium on a salted-caramel whey is suspicious — look again.

## Fortification creep

Some tubs add vitamin D, a B-vitamin blend, or creatine and then talk like a multivitamin. Those added lines are **dietary ingredients** with their own claim rules. They do not make the protein a drug. They do make the product a worse comparison target if you thought you were buying “just whey.” When you cost-compare in PSL-42, decide whether you are paying for protein grams or for a bundled vitamin story.

## Label drill

Build a leftover map for one dairy and one plant powder (pages are fine):

| Iron (mg or absent) | | | |

Write one sentence that starts with “These are not the same scoop because…” and names a leftover, not a disease.

## What this draft will not say

It will not say dairy minerals “build bones” as a product claim. It will not say plant sodium is dangerous. It will not say cholesterol in whey is a reason to use or avoid whey for a cardiac condition. It will not recommend a powder as a mineral supplement.
