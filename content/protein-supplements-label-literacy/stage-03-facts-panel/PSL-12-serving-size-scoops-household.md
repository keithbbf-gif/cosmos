# Serving size, scoops, and the household measure

> **Pack:** Protein powder label literacy — staged draft.  
> **Jurisdiction:** United States (FD&C Act as amended by DSHEA, 1994).  
> **Class:** Consumer education. Not a product label. Not medical advice. Not an advertisement.  
> **Lock:** This draft does not claim that any protein powder diagnoses, treats, cures, or prevents any disease.  
> **Statute (teaching):** Under DSHEA, a dietary supplement label may not claim to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease.

## The move

Every number on a facts panel is a number **per serving**. If you do not know what a serving is, you do not know the protein grams, the sugar grams, or the calorie count. Brands know this. Scoops are convenient. Scoops are also the easiest place to hide a comparison. Literacy treats **metric weight** as the adult unit and the scoop as a kitchen hint.

## The serving has two faces

U.S. panels declare a serving in a **household measure** (1 scoop, 2 scoops, 1/4 cup) and in a **metric amount** (usually grams for a powder). The metric amount is the one you can check with a scale. The household measure is the one marketing will repeat (“one scoop”).

A scoop is not a legal instrument. Scoops nest, warp, get replaced from another tub, and get heaped. Two brands’ “1 scoop” can be 25 g and 42 g. That is not a conspiracy by itself. It becomes a problem when a shopper compares **front-of-tub gram claims** as if the scoops were the same object.

## Directions can disagree with the panel

Some tubs say:

- Panel: 1 scoop (30 g) — 24 g protein  
- Directions: “For best results mix two scoops”

“Best results” is not a serving-size definition. If you drink two scoops, you have eaten **two servings** unless the panel already defined the serving as two scoops. Double the calories, the sugar, the sodium, and the cost. Do not double only the protein in your head and leave the rest behind.

This pack does not tell you how many scoops to use. It tells you to **multiply the whole serving**, not a favorite line.

## Heaping, packing, and “the scoop in the photo”

Powder density changes with settling, humidity, and how the scoop was filled at the studio. A nutrition facts panel is not recalculated for your humidity. If you care about the gram number — because you are comparing products, or because a clinician asked you about intake — **weigh the serving the panel named**. If you do not care, admit you are using a flavor delivery system and stop arguing about 2 grams.

## Servings per container is a check, not a promise

Net weight divided by grams per serving should be close to the stated servings per container. Rounding, settling, and the way firms handle “about X servings” create slack. Slack of a serving or two on a large tub is ordinary. Slack that looks like the tub is 30% short is a **net-contents** question (PSL-41), not a protein-quality question.

## Reference amounts vs the scoop the brand printed

Nutrient-content claims (“high protein,” “excellent source”) are judged against a **reference amount customarily consumed (RACC)** and the rules in 21 CFR 101.13 / 101.54, not against whatever scoop makes the front of the tub look muscular. A brand can print a small scoop so the calorie number looks tiny, or a large scoop so the protein number looks huge. The claim rules are supposed to stop the worst of that game. Shoppers still have to look at **grams of powder per serving** before they fall in love with a front number.

This lesson does not do the full RACC walk — PSL-34 does. Here you only need the suspicion: **serving size is a chosen denominator.**

## Ready-to-drink is a different serving problem

A carton that is “one bottle = one serving” is easier. A carton that is “2.5 servings per bottle” is the smoothie-shop trick in plastic. If you drink the bottle, you drink 2.5 servings. Protein powders mixed at home inherit the same trick whenever the brand’s “recipe” uses more powder than the panel serving.

## Label drill

Using a scale if you have one (or the metric line if you do not):

1. Write the panel serving: household + grams.  
2. If a scoop is present, weigh one **level** scoop.  
3. Subtract: your scoop grams minus panel grams.  
4. Write the front-of-tub protein claim and the panel protein grams.  
5. Note the directions’ recommended number of scoops.

You now have a five-line serving card. Keep it. PSL-13 and PSL-42 will reuse the denominator.

If you have no scale, do steps 1, 4, and 5 only, and write “scale: none.” Honesty about missing tools is literacy. Inventing a weight is not.

## What this draft will not say

It will not say a larger scoop is better. It will not say you should eat a certain number of scoops. It will not treat a heaping scoop as “more gains.” It will not accuse a brand of fraud because a plastic scoop is not a metrology lab.
