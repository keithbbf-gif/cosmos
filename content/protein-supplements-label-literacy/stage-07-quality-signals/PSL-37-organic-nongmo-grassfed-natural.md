# Organic, non-GMO, grass-fed, “natural,” hormone language

> **Pack:** Protein powder label literacy — staged draft.  
> **Jurisdiction:** United States (FD&C Act as amended by DSHEA, 1994).  
> **Class:** Consumer education. Not a product label. Not medical advice. Not an advertisement.  
> **Lock:** This draft does not claim that any protein powder diagnoses, treats, cures, or prevents any disease.  
> **Statute (teaching):** Under DSHEA, a dietary supplement label may not claim to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease.

## The move

These words sell **origin stories**. Some of them have rulebooks (USDA organic). Some have private standards (Non-GMO Project). Some are ordinary English that FDA has spent decades not fully defining on processed foods (“natural”). None of them is a protein gram. None of them is a disease claim this pack will allow a brand to smuggle (“organic whey prevents …”). Literacy is matching the word to a **rulebook or to fog**.

## USDA Organic

The USDA organic mark is a **National Organic Program** claim. It has a certifier, a percentage logic (100% / organic / made with organic), and a look-up culture. It does **not** move the product into a drug category. It does not waive DSHEA’s disease-claim ban. It does not mean the %DV math is different. An organic gainer is still a gainer.

If the mark is load-bearing, name the **certifier** on the small print, the same way you did for kosher (PSL-30).

## Non-GMO

**Non-GMO Project Verified** is a private standard with a directory. A brand-drawn butterfly is not that standard. “Non-GMO” as self-speech on a whey protein is a claim that still has to be truthful; the verification mark is the thing you can look up. Plant proteins (soy, corn-derived sweeteners) are where the claim usually has a story. Whey’s GMO story is often about cow feed, which is a **different** story than “this isolate molecule was engineered.” Read what the claim is actually asserting.

## Grass-fed / pasture

On dairy and beef proteins, grass-fed is an **animal-diet** claim. Programs differ. A recognized standard plus a certifier is stronger than a green field photograph. Grass-fed does not delete milk as an allergen. It does not make casein a night medicine. It does not change nitrogen × 6.25.

## “Natural” and “clean”

FDA’s “natural” history on food is a mess of policy and guidance, not a shopper-friendly checkbox. On a protein tub, “natural” often means “we would like you to stop reading the sucralose.” Pair the adjective with PSL-26. If the list is short and the adjective is “natural,” you have a consistent costume. If the list is a flavor lab and the adjective is “natural,” you have dialect (PSL-30).

“Clean” is not a CFR nutrient.

## Hormone language on dairy

“No added hormones,” “rBST-free,” and similar lines are **production claims** with their own FDA/FTC truth problems (including required contextual statements in some dairy advertising histories). They are not a finding that the powder treats an endocrine condition. This pack will not do hormone-disease storytelling. It will say: **production claim ≠ panel protein, and production claim ≠ medical advice.**

## When origin stories become implied disease claims

“Organic because chemicals cause [disease]” is a disease claim in a grocery costume. “Grass-fed to prevent [disease]” is the same. Sort those sentences with PSL-31, not with this lesson’s origin map.

## What this draft will not say

It will not say organic protein is healthier. It will not say conventional whey causes a disease. It will not say non-GMO is a therapy. It will not tell you to pay more for a field photograph.
