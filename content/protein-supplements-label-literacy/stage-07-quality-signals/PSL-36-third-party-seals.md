# Third-party seals — NSF, Informed-Sport, USP, BSCG

> **Pack:** Protein powder label literacy — staged draft.  
> **Jurisdiction:** United States (FD&C Act as amended by DSHEA, 1994).  
> **Class:** Consumer education. Not a product label. Not medical advice. Not an advertisement.  
> **Lock:** This draft does not claim that any protein powder diagnoses, treats, cures, or prevents any disease.  
> **Statute (teaching):** Under DSHEA, a dietary supplement label may not claim to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease.

## The move

A third-party seal is a **private checklist** someone else ran, or claims to have run, on a facility or a lot. It is one of the few quality signals that can be **looked up**. It is not FDA approval. It is not a disease claim. It is not a reason this pack will tell you a powder is appropriate for a medical condition. Literacy is naming the program, checking that the **product** is in the program’s directory, and knowing what the program **does not** test.

## What the common sport and supplement marks are aiming at

Program rules change. Believe the current public standard. As a shopper map:

- **NSF Certified for Sport / NSF Dietary Supplement:** identity, contaminants, and (in the sport program) a banned-substance screen plus facility process. Two different NSF marks exist; the **sport** mark is the one athletic programs usually mean.  
- **Informed-Sport / Informed-Choice (LGC):** lot or product banned-substance testing with a registry.  
- **USP Dietary Supplement Verification:** identity, potency, contaminants, and GMP-style process against USP’s program.  
- **BSCG Certified Drug Free:** another banned-substance / contaminant family of marks.

These programs are built because **21 CFR 111 does not print a consumer medal** (PSL-09) and because sport has a list of substances a protein tub should not contain. They are not built to prove a structure/function sentence.

## The directory is the adult document

A seal on a 2024 bag can outlive a 2026 delisting. If the mark matters to you — because a team, a job, or your own risk tolerance said so — **open the certifier’s search box and type the product**. A screenshot on a brand site is not the directory. A “style” of the logo that is slightly wrong is a reason to look twice.

Write down the **certificate number** if there is one. Lot-level programs mean the **lot** matters (PSL-39).

## What seals do not do

They do not:

- pre-approve a disease claim,
- set your protein target,
- guarantee the flavor system will agree with you,
- guarantee the amino-acid-sum equals N × 6.25 (unless that test is actually in the program — usually it is not the headline),
- or replace allergen labeling.

A sealed collagen is still incomplete protein (PSL-23). A sealed gainer is still a carbohydrate tub (PSL-14).

## Retailer and in-house marks

A grocery chain’s “quality tested” badge can mean anything from a real spec to a design system. Treat unnamed marks as **PSL-09 fog** until a standard and a directory appear.

- One sentence on what the program says it tests:  
- One sentence on what you wanted it to test that it doesn’t:  

If you cannot find the product in the directory, the seal on the page is **unverified furniture**.

## What this draft will not say

It will not say unsealed powders are adulterated. It will not say a seal makes a powder safe for an allergic person or for a diagnosis. It will not rank certifiers as medical devices. It will not tell a tested athlete which mark their sport requires — their sport’s current list does.
