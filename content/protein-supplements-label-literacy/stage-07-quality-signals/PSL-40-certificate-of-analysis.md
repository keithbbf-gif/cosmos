# Certificates of analysis — reading one beside the label

> **Pack:** Protein powder label literacy — staged draft.  
> **Jurisdiction:** United States (FD&C Act as amended by DSHEA, 1994).  
> **Class:** Consumer education. Not a product label. Not medical advice. Not an advertisement.  
> **Lock:** This draft does not claim that any protein powder diagnoses, treats, cures, or prevents any disease.  
> **Statute (teaching):** Under DSHEA, a dietary supplement label may not claim to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease.

## The move

A certificate of analysis (COA) is a **lab page** for a lot or a raw material. It is the closest thing a shopper gets to the notebook the facts panel summarized. It is also a **PDF a brand chose to show you**. Literacy is reading it **next to the tub**, not instead of the tub, and noticing which methods were used.

## What a useful COA names

Look for:

- Product name and **lot / batch** that matches PSL-39.  
- Date of test and date of the certificate.  
- The **lab** (in-house vs named third party).  
- **Protein** — and whether it says N × 6.25, Dumas, Kjeldahl, or an amino-acid sum.  
- **Microbiology** (total plate count, yeast/mold, pathogens the program chose).  
- **Metals** (lead, cadmium, arsenic, mercury) with units and detection limits.  
- **Identity** (does it even say whey vs pea?).  
- A signature or a quality-unit name.

If three of those are missing, you have a flyer.

## Method is the whole protein story

You already know why (PSL-17, PSL-25). A COA that reports **protein (N × 6.25): 84%** on a powder whose ingredient list is an amino festival has faithfully reported nitrogen. A COA that reports an **amino acid profile** you can sum is a different conversation. If both appear, compare them. If only nitrogen appears, do not pretend the spike question was closed.

## Units and serving math

Labs love **mg/kg** and **ppm**. Labels love **per serving**. Convert before you gasp.

- 50 µg/kg lead on a 30 g serving is 1.5 µg per serving.  
- A Prop 65 safe harbor is a **different** stick than a lab’s reporting limit.

This pack will not run your risk assessment. It will insist on **units** so you do not compare µg/kg to µg/serving in a caption.

## Raw-material COA vs finished-product COA

A whey isolate **ingredient** COA from a dairy processor is not a COA of the **birthday-cake tub** after flavors, lecithin, and a second blend tank. Finished-product testing is the one that matches the scoop. Raw-material testing is still better than a vibe. Know which you were handed.

## What a COA will not do

It will not:

- make a disease claim lawful,
- replace Contains,
- prove a structure/function sentence,
- prove the website image matches this lot,
- or guarantee the next lot.

A beautiful COA plus a disease claim on the same page is still a disease claim.

## When there is no COA

Most grocery-aisle proteins will not email you a PDF. That is **normal**, not proof of dirt. Third-party seals (PSL-36) are a substitute *kind* of third look. Your remaining tools are the panel, the ingredient list, and the refusal to treat a missing PDF as a morality play.

## Label drill

If you can obtain a COA (brand site, QR, email), fill:

- Lot match to your bag?  
- Protein method:  
- Protein result vs panel (same units?):  
- Metals table present? (units)  
- Micro table present?  
- Finished product or raw material?  
- Third-party lab named?  

If you cannot obtain one, write “COA: none” and list which **other** stage-7 signal you do have (seal directory / Prop 65 / nothing).

## What this draft will not say

It will not say a COA makes a powder safe. It will not interpret a metal number as a diagnosis. It will not say in-house labs are always liars or that third-party labs are saints. It will not tell you to eat or avoid a lot.
