# Lot codes, best-by dates, and storage statements

> **Pack:** Protein powder label literacy — staged draft.  
> **Jurisdiction:** United States (FD&C Act as amended by DSHEA, 1994).  
> **Class:** Consumer education. Not a product label. Not medical advice. Not an advertisement.  
> **Lock:** This draft does not claim that any protein powder diagnoses, treats, cures, or prevents any disease.  
> **Statute (teaching):** Under DSHEA, a dietary supplement label may not claim to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease.

## The move

A protein powder is not one eternal object. It is a **lot**: a batch that was mixed, filled, and labeled together. Seals, COAs, metal tables, and adverse-event reports are meaningful only when they point at a lot. Literacy is finding the code, finding the date, and reading the storage line as a **quality-system hint**, not as folklore about a garage.

## Lot or batch codes

You will find them ink-jetted on the bottom, crimped into a bag, or printed on a sticker that also has the best-by date. There is no single federal font. There is a **quality** reason they exist (21 CFR 111’s record and labeling-control world): so a firm can recall, so a tester can match a sample, so you can tell two identical-looking bags apart.

If you will email a brand, report a problem, or compare a COA, the lot code is the first line. A flavor name is not a lot.

## Best-by, use-by, and “expiry”

On foods and supplements, date phrases are a mix of **quality** dating and, in some categories, stricter rules. A protein powder’s “best by” is usually a **quality** date: flavor, clumping, the firm’s stability file. It is not, by itself, a sterility guarantee, and it is not a disease claim.

A swollen, wet, or rancid-smelling powder is a **food** problem you do not solve with a date argument. Throw-out decisions are ordinary kitchen judgment. This pack will not medicalize a stale scoop.

## Storage lines

“Store in a cool, dry place. Close the lid. Do not use if seal is broken.” That is manufacturing and holding talk. Protein powders take up **humidity**. Humidity changes scoop weight (PSL-12) and can cake the bag. A storage line is a hint that the firm’s stability assumption was a closed container in a dry cupboard, not an open bag in a steamy bathroom.

This is not a reason to invent a pathogen story. It is a reason your **weighed serving** can drift if you treat the bag like a sugar jar for six months.

## Seals and inner bags

A broken inner seal at purchase is a **retail** problem. Part 111 cares about packaging control; you care about not buying a bag that already lived open on a shelf. Photograph the lot if you return it.

## Why website images lie about lots

Product-page Supplement Facts are often a **template**. Reformulations happen. The bag in your kitchen is the document. If you compare a 2024 review’s panel to a 2026 bag, you may be comparing two lots and two formulas. Stage 8’s worked examples are fictional **so** they cannot go stale as a brand’s live panel. Your real-tub drills should be redone when the lot changes.

## Label drill

On one bag in your kitchen (or a store photo):

- Lot / batch code (copy exactly):  
- Best-by or other date:  
- Where printed (bottom / crimp / sticker):  
- Storage sentence:  
- Inner seal intact? (if you own it):  

Then write where you would look for a COA **for that code** (brand site, QR, email). If the answer is “nowhere,” that is a stage-7 finding, not a moral verdict.

## What this draft will not say

It will not say an expired powder causes a disease. It will not say a fresh lot is clinically superior. It will not tell you to eat expired powder or to discard it on a medical protocol. It will not treat a lot code as a pedigree of purity.

## Carry forward

PSL-40 is the document the lot code unlocks when a firm is willing: the certificate of analysis.
