# Allergen declarations — Contains and facility statements

> **Pack:** Protein powder label literacy — staged draft.  
> **Jurisdiction:** United States (FD&C Act as amended by DSHEA, 1994).  
> **Class:** Consumer education. Not a product label. Not medical advice. Not an advertisement.  
> **Lock:** This draft does not claim that any protein powder diagnoses, treats, cures, or prevents any disease.  
> **Statute (teaching):** Under DSHEA, a dietary supplement label may not claim to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease.

## The move

If you remember one non-protein line from a tub, remember **Contains**. FALCPA (2004) and the FASTER Act (sesame, effective 2023) require the **food source** of each major food allergen present as an ingredient to be declared in plain language. Protein powders live in milk, soy, egg, and sometimes wheat, fish, tree nut, and sesame. Literacy is reading that line **every time**, including the tub you “always buy,” because formulas change.

This lesson is not allergy treatment. It is **how the declaration is supposed to look**.

## The nine major food allergens (statutory list)

Milk, eggs, fish, Crustacean shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soybeans, sesame.

The name of the **source** must appear either:

- in parentheses after the ingredient (“lecithin (soy)”), or  
- in a **Contains** statement immediately after or next to the ingredient list.

If a Contains statement is used, it must name **all** major allergens in the product, not a favorite subset. On some dietary supplements, when there is no separate ingredient list, FDA’s allergen Q&A places Contains **next to Supplement Facts**.

## Protein powders: the usual hits

- **Whey / casein / MPC:** milk.  
- **Soy protein, soy lecithin:** soy.  
- **Egg white:** egg.  
- **Fish collagen:** fish (and the species story belongs on the label in the fish/shellfish style the rules require).  
- **Cookie inclusions:** wheat, maybe soy, maybe milk.  
- **Flavor systems:** sesame, tree nut, milk — the parentheticals matter after FASTER.  
- **Peanut butter** flavor that actually uses peanut: peanut. A “peanut butter” flavor that is only flavoring may still need a careful read; do not assume.

## Facility statements are a different object

“Manufactured in a facility that also processes milk, soy, and tree nuts” is **advisory** language about cross-contact. It is not a substitute for declaring an allergen that is an **ingredient**. It is also not a standardized federal line with one meaning. Some firms use it generously. Some use it instead of better controls. Some use it because a retailer asked.

A vegan-marketed pea powder with “facility: milk” is a **cross-contact** warning, not a whey ingredient — unless milk also appears as an ingredient or in Contains as an ingredient allergen. Read both sentences. Do not collapse them.

This pack cannot tell you your personal cross-contact risk. That is clinician + your own history. The literacy is: **ingredient allergen and facility allergen are two rows.**

## What “may contain” is

Voluntary precautionary allergen labeling (PAL) is common and **not** a uniform federal code. “May contain peanuts” is not the same as “contains peanuts” as an ingredient. People with allergies treat PAL in different ways; this pack will not choose for them. It will say: do not use PAL as proof that the product **does** contain the allergen as a formulation choice, and do not ignore it if your clinician has told you to treat PAL as a stop.

## Sesame after 2023

Sesame is a major allergen now. Flavor and spice mixes that once hid it have less room. If you avoid sesame, re-read old favorites. FDA has also noted that some firms **added** sesame to recipes after FASTER so they could use a Contains line instead of keeping a line clean. That industry behavior is a reason to read the list, not a reason this pack will name a villain.

## Label drill

On one protein tub, copy **verbatim**:

- Ingredient-list allergen parentheticals:  
- Contains statement (or “none”):  
- Facility / may-contain statement (or “none”):  

Then write two rows:

1. Allergens that are **ingredients** (from Contains or parentheticals).  
2. Allergens that are **only** in a facility/PAL sentence.

If you have an allergen in your household, this drill is the one you repeat when the lot code changes (PSL-39).

## What this draft will not say

It will not say a powder is safe for an allergic person. It will not say isolate is “lactose only” and therefore fine for milk allergy — that is false as a general rule. It will not treat allergy as a structure/function opportunity. It will not tell you to try a little to see.
