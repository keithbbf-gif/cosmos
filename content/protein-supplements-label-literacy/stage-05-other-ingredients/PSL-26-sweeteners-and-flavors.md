# Sweeteners and flavors

> **Pack:** Protein powder label literacy — staged draft.  
> **Jurisdiction:** United States (FD&C Act as amended by DSHEA, 1994).  
> **Class:** Consumer education. Not a product label. Not medical advice. Not an advertisement.  
> **Lock:** This draft does not claim that any protein powder diagnoses, treats, cures, or prevents any disease.  
> **Statute (teaching):** Under DSHEA, a dietary supplement label may not claim to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease.

## The move

Most people do not drink unflavored isolate for pleasure. The scoop is a **flavor system** wrapped around a protein fraction. The facts panel told you sugars and calories (PSL-15). The **ingredient list** tells you how the sweetness and the “birthday cake” were built. Literacy is being able to name the builders, not having a moral ranking of stevia versus sugar.

## How to read the tail of the list

Ingredients are listed in **descending order by weight**. After the protein(s), a flavored tub typically shows:

- a texture agent (lecithin, gums — PSL-27),
- salt,
- a flavor line (“natural and artificial flavors”),
- one or more sweeteners,
- sometimes color.

The flavor line can be a **bundle**. “Natural flavor” is a legal category, not a field of vanilla beans. It can include carriers. That is why allergen literacy does not stop at the word flavor (PSL-29). FASTER made sesame a reason to look at spice and flavor parentheticals.

## Sugar and syrups

Cane sugar, beet sugar, brown rice syrup, molasses, honey, maple, coconut sugar — these will move **added sugars** if they are added. They are easy. If they are in the list and added sugars is zero, you have a rounding or a serving-size story, not a philosophy.

## Polyols

Erythritol, xylitol, maltitol, sorbitol, isomalt. They exist to cut the sugar line. They still belong to the carbohydrate neighborhood. This pack will not give tolerance advice. It will say: **large servings + polyols** is a composition fact you should notice before you treat “0 g sugar” as “this is a small food.”

## High-intensity sweeteners

**Sucralose**, **acesulfame potassium**, **aspartame**, **steviol glycosides**, **monk fruit** (mogrosides), sometimes **saccharin**. They can produce dessert sweetness at milligram amounts, which is why they sit at the **end** of the list even when they dominate the taste.

“Stevia” on the front and “sucralose” in the list is a common split: the leaf is the story, the sucralose is the work. Both can be in the same tub. Read the list.

This pack will not say these sweeteners cause or treat a disease. It will not do internet toxicology. Personal tolerance and clinician advice are outside the file.

## “Naturally flavored” vs “natural flavor”

Those phrases are cousins, not twins. A product can be naturally flavored and still sweetened with sucralose. A product can use natural flavor and artificial color. Claims about “natural” are PSL-37. Here: **do not let the flavor adjective hide the sweetener nouns.**

## Unflavored is a claim you can test

Unflavored whey still tastes like whey. The list should be short: protein, maybe lecithin, maybe sunflower lecithin, maybe a flow agent. If “unflavored” includes sucralose, the word failed. If it includes cocoa, it is chocolate.

## Label drill

Copy the ingredient list from one flavored protein (or type it). Highlight:

- Proteins  
- Sweeteners (sugar, polyol, high-intensity)  
- The word “flavor” / “flavors”  
- Colors  

Write the front sweetness slogan (“no sugar,” “stevia sweetened,” “not too sweet,” or none). Note any slogan that names a sweetener that is **not** the first sweetener in the list.

## What this draft will not say

It will not say artificial flavors are unsafe or that natural flavors are wholesome. It will not say sucralose is a toxin. It will not say honey-sweetened powder is a therapy. It will not tell you to detox from sweetener.
