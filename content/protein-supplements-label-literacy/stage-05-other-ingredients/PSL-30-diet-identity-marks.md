# Vegan, kosher, halal, and other diet-identity marks

> **Pack:** Protein powder label literacy — staged draft.  
> **Jurisdiction:** United States (FD&C Act as amended by DSHEA, 1994).  
> **Class:** Consumer education. Not a product label. Not medical advice. Not an advertisement.  
> **Lock:** This draft does not claim that any protein powder diagnoses, treats, cures, or prevents any disease.  
> **Statute (teaching):** Under DSHEA, a dietary supplement label may not claim to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease.

## The move

A leaf, a hechsher, a halal mark, “keto,” “paleo,” “carnivore” — these are **identity claims** about a dietary pattern or a certifier’s rulebook. They are not protein grams. They are not FDA drug approvals. They can still be **false** if the ingredient list fights them. Literacy is checking the fight, then deciding whether the mark is a certifier you recognize or a font.

## Vegan / plant-based

**Plant-based** is softer. It can mean “the protein is from plants” while honey, dairy flavor, or a milk facility statement still exists. **Vegan** is harder in ordinary speech: no animal ingredients. A vegan claim next to **Contains: milk** is a fight. A vegan claim next to “facility: milk” is a cross-contact fight that different vegan shoppers resolve differently.

Collagen cannot be vegan. Whey cannot be vegan. A “vegan collagen builder” is usually a **different product** (vitamin C, amino acids, a story) wearing collagen’s marketing. Read the facts box. If there is no collagen, the word collagen is speech.

This pack will not referee vegan ethics. It will say: **the ingredient list is the adult vegan document; the leaf is the poster.**

## Vegetarian

Egg white can be vegetarian and not vegan. Whey is not vegetarian in some personal definitions (rennet/cheese-making) and is in others. Marks that say “vegetarian” without a certifier are a brand’s own word. If the distinction matters to you, you need the **certifier’s rulebook**, not a green stripe.

## Kosher

A kosher mark (OU, Kof-K, OK, Star-K, and others) is a **private religious certification**. Dairy powders may be kosher-dairy (and should not be treated as pareve). A “K” a brand drew in a word processor is not the same object as a recognized hechsher. Literacy: **name the certifier, then look the product up if the mark is load-bearing for you.**

Kosher does not mean allergen-free. Kosher does not mean no disease claims. Kosher does not mean FDA approved.

## Halal

Same structure: a recognized certifier vs a crescent a designer added. Gelatin-family and some enzyme sources are the usual reason a protein powder would fail a shopper who cares. The certifier’s site, not the flavor name, is the check.

## Keto, paleo, carnivore, “clean”

These are **marketing dialects**. They sometimes correlate with a panel shape (low carbohydrate for keto-marketed isolates; egg or collagen for paleo-marketed tubs). They have **no** single federal definition on a protein tub. A “keto” gainer is a contradiction you can catch with PSL-14. A “clean” tub with sucralose is a dialect problem, not a CFR violation by itself.

Do not let a dialect become a nutrient-content claim you forgot to check (“high protein,” “no added sugar”) — those have rules (PSL-34, PSL-15).

## When identity marks become misleading

Truth-and-not-misleading is still the floor. A halal mark on porcine gelatin is false. A vegan mark on whey is false. A kosher-pareve claim on a milk protein is false. Those are not DSHEA disease claims. They are **ordinary false labeling**. Your drill is to catch the contradiction before checkout.

## Label drill

On one tub with at least one identity mark:

- Marks/words:  
- Named certifier (or “self-declared”):  
- Ingredient-list tensions:  
- Contains / facility tensions:  
- Would the mark still be true if the front art were deleted?  

If you cannot name a certifier, write “font only.”

## What this draft will not say

It will not say one diet identity is healthier. It will not say kosher powders treat a condition. It will not say vegan powders prevent a disease associated with animal foods. It will not rank religions.
