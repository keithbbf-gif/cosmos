# Lecithin, gums, and flow agents

> **Pack:** Protein powder label literacy — staged draft.  
> **Jurisdiction:** United States (FD&C Act as amended by DSHEA, 1994).  
> **Class:** Consumer education. Not a product label. Not medical advice. Not an advertisement.  
> **Lock:** This draft does not claim that any protein powder diagnoses, treats, cures, or prevents any disease.  
> **Statute (teaching):** Under DSHEA, a dietary supplement label may not claim to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease.

## The move

A protein fraction does not automatically mix into cold milk like a commercial. Brands add **emulsifiers**, **thickeners**, and **anti-caking** agents so the scoop pours and the shake photographs. These ingredients are usually a small slice of the serving by weight. They punch above that slice in **allergens**, **texture**, and **fiber lines**. Literacy names them so they stop looking like a mysterious “chemical stack.”

## Lecithin

Sunflower lecithin and soy lecithin are the common emulsifiers. They help powder wet and disperse. **Soy lecithin** can matter to someone avoiding soy; FALCPA’s soy story is about the food source. Many highly refined oils have a specific allergen history; this pack will not play counsel on every exemption. It will say: **if the list says soy lecithin, the word soy is on the label — look at Contains and decide with your own risk and clinician, not with a forum that says lecithin “doesn’t count.”**

Sunflower lecithin exists partly so a brand can keep soy off the tub. That is a formulation choice, not a virtue badge.

## Gums and thickeners

Xanthan, guar, cellulose gum, carrageenan, gellan, acacia. They make a shake viscous. They can appear on the **fiber** line (PSL-15). They can make a 25 g protein scoop feel like a meal in the glass without being a meal on the panel.

“Thick” is not “more protein.” A gainer is thick because of carbohydrate mass (PSL-14). An isolate can be thick because of 200 mg of xanthan. Your mouth is not a Kjeldahl apparatus.

## Silicon dioxide, tricalcium phosphate, calcium silicate

These are **flow agents**. They keep powder from turning into a brick in humid kitchens. They can move a **calcium** or **silicon** conversation in blogs. The panel, if it shows calcium, may be counting an added phosphate (PSL-16). Flow agents are a reason a “clean” list still has a mineral salt. That is manufacturing, not a secret therapy.

## Cocoa, inclusions, and cookie pieces

Some tubs add cocoa powder (which brings its own fiber and bitterness) or cookie crumbs (which bring flour, sugar, and **wheat**). Inclusions are how a “protein” becomes a dessert mix. They belong in this lesson because they are **other ingredients** that change allergen class and protein-by-weight. If wheat cookie pieces are in a whey tub, Contains should not be only milk.

## Proprietary “texture systems”

A blend that says “mouthfeel matrix” without names is fog. You already met fog in PSL-25. Same move: if you cannot name the gum, you cannot map the fiber or the allergen.

## Label drill

On one powder, list every ingredient that is not a protein, a sweetener, or a vitamin/mineral fortification. Classify each as:

- emulsifier  
- gum / thickener  
- flow agent  
- color  
- inclusion / cocoa  
- other  

Note any of those names that also appear as a **major allergen** or in a **Contains** line.

## What this draft will not say

It will not say gums are inflammatory or that lecithin is brain food. It will not say anti-caking agents are unsafe. It will not say a short list is medically superior. Those sentences would be either disease claims or nutrition-therapy theater.
