# DSHEA 1994 in plain English

> **Pack:** Protein powder label literacy — staged draft.  
> **Jurisdiction:** United States (FD&C Act as amended by DSHEA, 1994).  
> **Class:** Consumer education. Not a product label. Not medical advice. Not an advertisement.  
> **Lock:** This draft does not claim that any protein powder diagnoses, treats, cures, or prevents any disease.  
> **Statute (teaching):** Under DSHEA, a dietary supplement label may not claim to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease.

## The move

The Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act of 1994 is a **category statute**. It did not declare protein powders safe. It did not declare them effective. It did not tell FDA to stop existing. It wrote dietary supplements into the FD&C Act as a kind of **food** with special labeling tools — and with a door that slams if the label talks like a drug.

If you remember one sentence from 1994 for a protein tub, remember this: **supplements got a panel and a claim class; they did not get a license to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent disease.**

## What Congress was trying to settle

Before DSHEA, the fight was about whether vitamins, herbs, and similar products would be squeezed into the drug definition whenever a seller said anything about the body. After DSHEA, a product that meets § 201(ff) can be a dietary supplement even though it is intended to affect structure or function — **provided** the speech stays inside the § 403(r)(6) box.

That box has three related statement types FDA lists together:

1. **Structure/function** — the role of a nutrient or dietary ingredient in normal structure or function, or a documented mechanism for maintaining that structure or function.
2. **General well-being** — general well-being from consuming the ingredient.
3. **Classical nutrient-deficiency disease** — a narrow statement about a deficiency disease that also says how widespread that deficiency is in the United States.

Protein-powder marketing lives almost entirely in (1), sometimes dresses itself as (2), and almost never honestly occupies (3). This pack will not draft a deficiency-disease claim for a powder. Protein deficiency in the United States is not a slogan you get to print because a gym ad felt dramatic.

## The bargain, itemized

**Sellers received:**

- A statutory definition of dietary supplement.
- A path to talk about normal structure/function without automatically becoming a drug.
- A Supplement Facts system instead of forcing every canister into a drug insert.

**Sellers gave up (or never had):**

- The right to disease claims on that path.
- A world without substantiation. The statement still must be truthful and not misleading, and the firm must have substantiation.
- Silence toward FDA. A notification with the text of the claim is due **no later than 30 days after first marketing** the supplement with that claim.
- A disclaimer-free panel. The boldface disclaimer is the public price of the structure/function path.

**FDA retained:**

- Adulteration and misbranding authority.
- The power to treat disease-claim labeling as drug labeling.
- Manufacturing-practice authority (today, 21 CFR 111).
- The ordinary tools of inspection, warning letters, and court.

That is not “unregulated.” It is **differently gated** than a new drug application. People who say “DSHEA means anything goes” are describing a vibe, not the United States Code.

## What DSHEA did *not* do to protein

It did not set a federal maximum for lead in whey.  
It did not pick PDCAAS versus DIAAS as a consumer-education campaign.  
It did not define “isolate” or “hydrolysate.”  
It did not bless “25g protein” as a health outcome.  
It did not move advertising law out of the FTC.

Protein was already a nutrient in food law. DSHEA is the reason a tub can call itself a dietary supplement and use Supplement Facts plus a structure/function sentence. The **grams** still come from 21 CFR 101.9 / 101.36 math (stage 3). The **identity** of the protein still comes from ingredients and food science (stage 4).

## How to hear “DSHEA compliant” on a sales page

Brands use the phrase as a halo. All it can honestly mean is: *we are trying to stay in the supplement category and not make disease claims.* It does not mean:

- FDA reviewed the formula,
- the scoop matches the panel,
- the flavor system is allergen-clean,
- or the powder is appropriate for you.

If a page says “DSHEA compliant” **and** then names a disease, the halo has already cracked. Believe the disease name, not the halo.

## Label drill

Copy the structure/function disclaimer off a supplement tub, word for word. Compare it to the official pair of sentences (PSL-32 quotes them). Note:

1. Is it bold?  
2. Is it on the same panel as the claim, or linked by a symbol?  
3. Did the brand rewrite “This statement” as “These statements”? (Both appear in the wild; the regulation discusses the wording.)  
4. Is there a claim on the **front** with no disclaimer in sight — only on the back?

You are not scoring the brand for sport. You are training your eye for the bargain: claim and disclaimer are a pair. A claim without the pair is a different legal object.

## What this draft will not say

It will not say DSHEA was a victory or a disaster. It will not say 1994 “protected your right to whey.” It will not say a powder is safe because the statute exists. It will not use DSHEA as a reason to recommend a scoop.

## Carry forward

PSL-07 is the sentence shoppers get backward: FDA does not pre-approve most supplement labels — and that fact is a **burden on the seller**, not a gold star on the lid.
