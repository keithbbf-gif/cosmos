# What FDA does not pre-approve

> **Pack:** Protein powder label literacy — staged draft.  
> **Jurisdiction:** United States (FD&C Act as amended by DSHEA, 1994).  
> **Class:** Consumer education. Not a product label. Not medical advice. Not an advertisement.  
> **Lock:** This draft does not claim that any protein powder diagnoses, treats, cures, or prevents any disease.  
> **Statute (teaching):** Under DSHEA, a dietary supplement label may not claim to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease.

## The move

For a routine protein powder sold as a dietary supplement, FDA does **not** stamp the finished label before it ships. That is the design of DSHEA’s structure/function path: notify after marketing, keep substantiation in the file, stay out of disease claims. Shoppers hear “not pre-approved” and add a second, false sentence: “so nobody is responsible.” The second sentence is the one this lesson deletes.

## Pre-approval is a specific machine

A new drug is not lawfully on the market because a founder felt earnest. It is on the market because an application was allowed, or because another drug pathway applies. That machine is expensive, slow, and built around **intended use as a drug**.

A dietary supplement that stays a dietary supplement does not enter that machine. The firm is still responsible for:

- the truth of every number and every claim,
- the lawfulness of every ingredient in that intended use,
- the manufacturing practice in 21 CFR 111,
- allergen declaration,
- and the decision **not** to make a disease claim.

Responsibility moved **upstream into the firm**, not **out of the legal system**.

## What “notification” is — and is not

A structure/function notification under 21 CFR 101.93 is a filing that includes the text of the claim and required identifying information. It is due no later than 30 days after first marketing the supplement *with that claim*.

Notification is not:

- a scientific review that ends in a love letter from FDA,
- permission to use the claim forever,
- a finding that the claim is substantiated,
- or a finding that the product is not a drug.

FDA can still object. FDA can still write a warning letter a year later when the same words, next to a new picture, become a disease claim. A quiet inbox is not a monograph.

## What FDA *does* look at, when it looks

Literacy fails if it pretends inspectors do not exist. FDA can inspect supplement facilities, sample products, read labels in the marketplace, and sue. The agency also issues warning letters that are public. Those letters are a teaching archive: they show which sentences, in context, the agency read as disease claims or as misbranding.

This pack will not turn warning letters into gossip about living brands. It will say: **the fact that a path is post-market does not mean the path is unwatched.**

## Halo phrases that launder “not pre-approved”

Watch for these on protein pages:

- “FDA registered facility” — many food and supplement firms register. Registration is not product approval.
- “FDA approved ingredients” — ingredients may be old dietary ingredients, food substances, or GRAS in some uses. That is not a blessing of the *formula* or the *claim*.
- “Manufactured in an FDA inspected facility” — inspections happen. They are not a gold seal you get to print as if a lot were certified.
- “Compliant with FDA guidelines” — which guidance, which edition, which sentence?

Each phrase tries to borrow the **prestige** of pre-approval without the **dossier**. Read them as location or process claims, then ask for the process.

## How this changes how you read a tub

Because the label was not pre-cleared:

1. **You** have to do the panel math. Nobody in Rockville added the scoops for you.
2. **The firm** has to be nameable. A P.O. box and a fantasy brand with no U.S. place of business is a literacy problem, not a vibe.
3. **Third-party seals** (stage 7) become more interesting — not as magic, but as *someone else* who at least ran a defined checklist.
4. **Disease-adjacent adjectives** are more dangerous, not less. The path only stays open if the speech stays in the box.

## Label drill

On a product page, search for the strings `FDA`, `approved`, `registered`, and `inspected`. For each hit, rewrite the sentence in boring English that cannot be mistaken for a new-drug approval. Example:

- Page: “FDA approved manufacturing.”  
- Boring: “The firm wants you to think an inspector liked the building.”

If you cannot rewrite it without using “approved,” the original sentence was doing halo work. Keep the rewrite in your notes for PSL-36.

## What this draft will not say

It will not say post-market regulation is enough, or that it is a scandal. It will not say you should only buy powders that went through a drug application. It will not say an inspected facility cannot ship a mislabeled lot. It will not recommend a brand because it avoided the word “approved.”

## Carry forward

PSL-08 is the other pre-market door people confuse with approval: **new dietary ingredients**. Most ordinary food proteins are old. The door still matters when a tub is not only protein.
