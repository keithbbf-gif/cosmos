# New dietary ingredients — why most proteins are old

> **Pack:** Protein powder label literacy — staged draft.  
> **Jurisdiction:** United States (FD&C Act as amended by DSHEA, 1994).  
> **Class:** Consumer education. Not a product label. Not medical advice. Not an advertisement.  
> **Lock:** This draft does not claim that any protein powder diagnoses, treats, cures, or prevents any disease.  
> **Statute (teaching):** Under DSHEA, a dietary supplement label may not claim to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease.

## The move

DSHEA split dietary ingredients into **old** and **new** using a calendar: October 15, 1994. An ingredient marketed in the United States as a dietary ingredient before that date is not a “new dietary ingredient” (NDI). A new dietary ingredient has a **notification** problem the old ones do not. Protein literacy needs this split because whey, casein, soy, egg, and many other food proteins are not exotic — while the *other* things in the same scoop sometimes are.

## Old is a marketing-history word, not a quality word

“Old dietary ingredient” does not mean:

- the farm is good,
- the heavy-metal test is clean,
- the flavor system is simple,
- or the claim is lawful.

It means the statute’s NDI notification trigger is aimed elsewhere. A 1993 whey concentrate can still be misbranded in 2026. An old ingredient in a dirty facility is still a 21 CFR 111 problem (PSL-09).

## What the NDI door is for

Congress did not want a brand to invent a novel extract on Tuesday and sell it as a supplement on Wednesday with no file. The NDI notification is a **premarket** information packet for ingredients that do not have the 1994 marketing history (with a recognized exception path when the ingredient is present in the food supply as an article used as food, in a form that has not been chemically altered — counsel live in that sentence; this pack will not pretend it is simple).

For a shopper, the useful version is:

- **Common food proteins in ordinary forms** are usually not the NDI story.
- **Novel fractions, exotic botanicals, designer “amplifier” blends, and newly isolated peptides** can be.

If the first ingredient is “whey protein isolate” and the rest is lecithin, salt, sucralose, and flavor, you are probably not holding an NDI puzzle. If the scoop is protein *plus* a proprietary blend of named extracts you cannot find in a 1994 grocery aisle, you might be.

## Why protein tubs still trip this wire

Modern protein powders are rarely only protein. They are flavor systems, color systems, “enzyme matrices,” mushroom powders, “focus” blends, and amino cocktails. Each extra dietary ingredient has its own history. The protein can be old while the companion is new.

Literacy move: **read the whole Supplement Facts and the whole ingredient list**, not the word WHEY on the front. Stage 5 exists because the extras are where category problems hide.

## What you will not see on the tub

You will almost never see “NDI notification number” printed in the same type as the flavor name. Absence of that phrase is not proof the firm filed. Presence of a marketing line like “novel peptide technology” is not proof they filed either. NDI status is a **file question**, not a front-panel question.

So why teach it? Because shoppers use “new” as a compliment. In DSHEA, **new** is a regulatory burden. A brand that boasts about novelty on a supplement is boasting about the door they are supposed to have walked through.

## Amino acids are not a free pass

Free amino acids are dietary ingredients. Some have long food and supplement histories; some uses are newer than the slogan on the lid. PSL-25 is about nitrogen math and spike literacy. This lesson only needs one sentence: **adding a pile of free amino acids does not make the product “more approved.”** It can make the identity of “protein” harder to read, and it can add NDI questions the whey itself did not have.

## Label drill

Take one flavored protein tub. Split the ingredient list into two columns:

| Likely old food-protein / ordinary processing aid | Ingredient I cannot place in a 1990s grocery aisle |
| --- | --- |
| | |

You will mis-sort some rows. That is fine. Circle the ones you would need a researcher to place. Those circles are your NDI-curiosity list. Do not treat the list as a verdict. Treat it as the moment you stopped assuming the word “protein” covers the scoop.

## What this draft will not say

It will not say old ingredients are safe and new ones are dangerous. It will not say an NDI notification is a safety approval. It will not tell a firm how to paper around a notification. It will not claim any powder treats a condition because the protein is “traditional.”

## Carry forward

PSL-09 is the manufacturing rule that applies whether the ingredient is old or new: 21 CFR part 111. A lid that says “GMP” is not a substitute for knowing what that part actually governs.
