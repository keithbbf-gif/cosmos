# Adverse-event duties — why the panel is almost silent

> **Pack:** Protein powder label literacy — staged draft.  
> **Jurisdiction:** United States (FD&C Act as amended by DSHEA, 1994).  
> **Class:** Consumer education. Not a product label. Not medical advice. Not an advertisement.  
> **Lock:** This draft does not claim that any protein powder diagnoses, treats, cures, or prevents any disease.  
> **Statute (teaching):** Under DSHEA, a dietary supplement label may not claim to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease.

## The move

A drug bottle often carries a thick insert because the product is a drug. A protein powder usually carries a serving suggestion and a flavor story. Shoppers infer: *if something could go wrong, it would be printed.* That inference is backward. Dietary supplements have **serious adverse event reporting** duties under the 2006 Dietary Supplement and Nonprescription Drug Consumer Protection Act. Those duties live in **firm files and FDA submissions**, not in a consumer monograph on the back of the tub.

This lesson teaches the silence so you do not mistake it for a safety rating.

## What the 2006 act added (shopper-level)

Domestic dietary supplement manufacturers, packers, and distributors must:

- have a way to receive adverse event reports,
- keep records,
- and report **serious** adverse events to FDA on a defined timeline,
- with the **label of the product** included in the report so FDA can see what was actually being sold.

The label you hold is, in that system, an **exhibit**, not a patient leaflet. That is why the canister can be almost mute about risks and the firm can still have a legal inbox.

## What “serious” means in this neighborhood

The statute uses a serious-event idea familiar from drug safety: death, a life-threatening experience, inpatient hospitalization, a persistent or significant disability, a congenital anomaly, or a **medical or surgical intervention needed to prevent** one of those. This pack names those categories so you understand why most stomach complaints never become a 15-day report. It does **not** invite you to diagnose an event. If something bad happens after you ate a food or a supplement, that is a **clinician and, if appropriate, FDA MedWatch** problem, not a label-literacy puzzle.

## What you might actually see on a tub

You may see:

- a U.S. address or phone number for the responsible firm,
- “questions or comments,”
- a QR code to a brand site.

You will rarely see a structured list of contraindications like a prescription insert. Absence of that list is **category-typical**, not proof the product is gentle. Presence of a scare-list that names diseases and then recommends the powder is the opposite problem — it can become a disease claim (PSL-31).

## How a shopper should use this fact

1. **Do not read silence as clearance.** The panel was not built to enumerate every way a powder can disagree with a person.

2. **Do read identity and allergens.** The duties that *are* on the label — what it is, what is in it, milk/soy/egg/sesame — are the ones that prevent a large class of ordinary disasters. PSL-29 is more useful in a kitchen than a fantasy insert.

3. **Do keep the lot code** if you will report a problem (PSL-39). A report without a lot is a fog.

4. **Do not treat this lesson as a reason to start or stop a powder for a medical condition.** That decision is outside the pack.

## MedWatch is a consumer door

FDA’s MedWatch system accepts reports from clinicians and from individuals. This pack is not telling you that a protein powder is dangerous. It is telling you that **the regulatory system assumes reports exist off-label**. If you need that door, you use FDA’s current MedWatch instructions, not a flavor name from this file.

## Why brands stay quiet (and why that is not your loyalty test)

Counsel fear that a long voluntary warning list will be read as an admission, or that naming a disease in a warning will be read as implied intended use. That fear produces **thin labels**. Thin labels are a reason for **your** caution, not a reason to punish a brand in a caption. The literacy move is: *I will not outsource risk-listing to a marketing department that is trying not to become a drug.*

## Label drill

On one supplement tub, find the **responsible firm’s** name and a way to contact them (address, phone, web). Write:

- Firm name as printed:  
- U.S. place of business (city/state at least):  
- Contact path:  
- Lot code location (describe, do not invent a number if you cannot find one):  

If you cannot name the firm, you cannot imagine who would file a report. That is a harder problem than a missing halo seal.

## What this draft will not say

It will not say protein powders are risky or safe as a class. It will not list symptoms and pair them with scoops. It will not tell you to report a flavor you disliked. It will not claim that reporting duties mean FDA has studied your tub.
