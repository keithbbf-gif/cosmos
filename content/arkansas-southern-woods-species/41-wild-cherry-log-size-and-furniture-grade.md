Furniture-grade cherry in the catalogs is a wide, clear, mostly heart board. Furniture-grade cherry in the Arkansas woods is a shorter log with a dark center and a pale jacket.

Second-growth *Prunus serotina* puts on sap. The handbook says the sapwood is narrow in old-growth and wider in second-growth. We are in the second-growth world. A twelve-inch log may give you eight inches of heart if you are lucky, and the heart may have a pith you have to lose. The math of a tabletop becomes a glue-up of narrows, not a single-plank fantasy.

I like that math. A cherry top of four or five matched boards, rift-ish, sap steered to the underside or the ends, is a stronger object than a pith-in wide board that will split along the first check. The scarcity forces better work. The 1912 writers called the state’s cherry unimportant in quantity and good in quality. Quantity is a design constraint. Quality is the reason to accept it.

Defects particular to local cherry: gum spots, small black knots from old spurs, bird peck, a sweep in the stem from growing toward a gap in the canopy. Gum spots are brown-black flecks that some people call character and some people call cause to discount. I call them the tree. They will telegraph under a highgloss film. Under oil they are part of the face.

Because the commercial supply is elsewhere, a lot of “cherry furniture” sold in Arkansas is not Arkansas cherry and may not be *P. serotina* at all. Stained poplar, stained alder, a tropical lookalike. If you care, look at the pore, the luster, the way a fresh cut smells faintly of cyanide and almond — the cherry family tell. If you do not care, enjoy the color and do not write a species essay about it.

National-forest cherry in the Ozarks has been treated carefully for a long time — small volume, good quality, not a pulp species. The 1912 Part II note on the forests said the wood was scattered, small in size and amount, valuable for furniture and interior finish. That is still the cruise. You are not going to buy a truck of it. You are going to buy a log, or a few boards from a sawyer who likes you.

When I get a local log, I saw for length first and width second. A pair of short, clear legs is better than one long, pithy stretcher. Aprons can be scarfed in the unseen. Tops are glue-ups. The tree was never going to be a Pennsylvania veneer log. It can be a good Arkansas table if you stop asking it to be wide. The grade is the length you can honestly use. Everything else is a catalog from another state.
