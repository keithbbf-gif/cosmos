Heart pine is what people say when they mean the floor in their grandmother’s hallway.

They do not mean *Pinus echinata* or *P. palustris*. They mean color, hardness, a tight grain, a smell when you sand it, a story about a mill. The leftover boards from that story — the ones pulled from a Crossett-era warehouse, a Warren mill house, a Cotton Belt depot — are real wood with real properties. The name is a memory that got loose.

Botanically, heartwood in southern pine is just the inner wood that has darkened and filled with extractives after the tree is about twenty years old. In a slow virgin tree that heartwood was most of the log and it was dense. In a twenty-five-year-old plantation tree the heartwood is a small reddish core inside a lot of sap, and it is not automatically hard. Salvage “heart pine” from an old building is usually the first thing. Big-box “heart pine” is sometimes the second thing with a stain.

Arkansas has a lot of the first thing still hiding in plain sight. The cut-out-and-get-out years left towns full of dense pine that was too ordinary to save on purpose and too heavy to wear out. When those buildings come down, the boards enter a trade that calls them heart pine and prices them like a relic. Some of them are relic-quality. Some of them are ordinary old pine with nail holes. You sort with a fingernail and a scale, not with the adjective.

Furniture from salvage SYP is a Southern genre: tables with bolt holes left visible, benches with round-shank nails sawn through, mantels that still have the soot. I like it when the maker does not pretend the wood was a cabinet hardwood. I do not like it when a soft, wideringed salvage joist is sold as if it were longleaf heart and then finishes like butter under a fork.

There is also a leftover-board tradition that never went to the antique trade. Mill workers took home what the yard would not ship. Kitchen tables in Bradley County and Ashley County were built from that take-home — shortleaf, a little resin, a breadboard end if someone knew the trick, oil or nothing. Those tables are heart pine in the family sense. They are not a product line.

If you want the memory, say so. Buy salvage, test density, pull the metal, kiln or air the wood again because a hundred years in a dry attic is not the same as a hundred years in a damp crawl. Then build. If you want a new table that will look like that memory in thirty years, you will not get there with a pale plantation 1×12 and a can of “heartwood” stain. You might get partway with selected dense shortleaf, an oil that is allowed to amber, and the patience to let the UV do the rest. The rest of the memory is the mill whistle, and you cannot buy that by the board foot.
