Before there was a furniture factory there was a clearing, and the clearing had both trees.

A settler in the Ozarks or on a south-Arkansas creek did not choose pine or oak from a catalog. He chose what would come down with the tools he had and what would last in the job he had. Oak for sills and posts, because it lasted in the ground and at the weather line. Pine for anything that had to be sawn or split in a hurry and did not sit in the dirt — puncheon floors in a pinch, later board floors, doors, shelves, the table. Cypress, if he was in the bottoms, for the parts that stayed wet. Walnut if he had a tree and a reason to look rich. Cherry almost never, because the stems were scarce and small.

This is not a romantic inventory. It is a gravity inventory. Oak is heavy and hard to work with a dull axe. Pine is lighter and the resin gums the saw. You use both. The furniture that comes out of that choice is pine more often than the museum labels admit, because the show pieces that survived were the walnut and cherry that someone later decided to keep. The pine tables went to the smokehouse and then to the dump. Survival bias is how we got a “Southern antiques are hardwood” story.

Hernando de Soto’s men walked through these woods when the trees were an obstacle. The Encyclopedia of Arkansas is right that from that century through the Civil War the forest was mostly in the way of travel and crops. A handful of people made objects from it. Most people made fields. The small water-powered mills and, after the war, the circular mills in places like Little Rock, turned the obstacle into a product. Joseph Brown’s circular sawmill in Little Rock after 1865 is one named example among unnamed ones. Those mills cut what was nearest. Nearest was often pine.

So the cabin’s furniture wood is a sequence: what you can work, what you can spare from the fire, what you can trade. Pine wins the sequence until a town has a factory and a fashion. Oak wins the parts that fail if they rot. The properties line up with the history so neatly that people forget they are properties. Softness, weight, decay resistance, tannin, resin — these are why the sill is oak and the shelf is pine, not because someone read a handbook.

When I build a “cabin table” now, I do not fake distress. I pick pine or oak for the same reasons the clearing would have. If the table will live on a porch, I think about cypress. If it will live in a dining room that wants to look like 1910 Fort Smith, I think about oak and gum. The frontier choice is still a good choice. It just has to be a choice, not a decorating theme.
