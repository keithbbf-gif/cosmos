People outside the state know Fort Smith for a courthouse and a gallows and a border. People who built furniture know it for beds.

The 1878 arrival of Ott, Meier & Co. is a small date with a long shadow. Indiana machinery, Indiana habits, Arkansas hardwood, a river, rails west into what was still Indian Territory. The first company stumbled. Edward Ballman did not. He went from turner to owner to a one-man industrial policy: if the town was going to have furniture, it should have specialized companies that could be sold together. Folding beds and tables, 1902. Couches and bedding, 1904. Kitchen cabinets under the Border Queen name, 1906. Metal products in the war year 1917. A manufacturers’ company in 1921 to market the lines as a city, not as a scramble.

That is a furniture town. Not a craftsman myth. A town that made objects other towns sat on and slept in.

The woods under those objects, statewide, were mostly red gum and white oak. Pine was the air and the housing, not the show face. Walnut and cherry were too scarce to be the volume. So a Fort Smith kitchen cabinet in oak is the central artifact of Arkansas furniture woods, more than a walnut highboy, more than a cypress chest. It is local hardwood, factory skill, and a market that ran to Texas and Oklahoma as easily as to Little Rock.

I care about this because species essays drift toward the tree and away from the town. The tree does not become furniture by itself. A turner on a train and a cabinet name on a hardware plate do that. When you handle an old Border Queen bin, you are handling white oak (or gum stained to look like it) that was chosen because it would take a finish and survive a freight car. The properties in the oak essays — tannin, ray, hardness, movement — are the properties that factory had to industrialize. They did not have a handbook on the wall. They had a production problem.

The town is still on the river. The factories are not the same. The woods on the Ozark side are still oak-hickory. A maker working here now is in the after of Ballman’s habit: you can still specialize, you can still use the local hardwood, you just cannot pretend the volume is 1878. That is fine. Volume was never the only virtue. The virtue was a town that knew what a bed post was.

I have looked for Ballman’s name on pieces the way people look for a maker’s mark. Sometimes it is a company name. Sometimes it is only a hardware pattern and a way of turning a post that feels like a shop that had to make quota. That is enough. A furniture town leaves habits in the objects.

If this series has a civic home, it is Fort Smith for the furniture and Warren-Crossett-Fordyce for the pine that surrounded it. Two economies. One state. The species sit in both. A dining table made now in Sebastian County is still in that civic home, whether it is oak or walnut or a pine someone finally allowed on the show face.
