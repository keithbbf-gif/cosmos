In 1912 the men counting Arkansas’s wood-using industries wrote a short, cold paragraph about cypress.

Manufacturers in the state wanted twelve million feet a year. The sawmills were cutting four times that much. The tree grows very slowly. It was evident, they said, that demand would shortly exhaust the supply.

They were looking at old-growth swamp timber in a state that had just learned to industrialize pine. Cypress was still being treated like a mine. You do not grow a mine. You empty it. The pine-belt towns would later hire foresters and invent a crop. The swamps were harder to turn into a crop. They were also being drained for cotton and soybeans. A slow tree in a disappearing wet woods is a supply curve with one direction.

Furniture was not the main mouth. Sash, tanks, boats, coffin stock, the general millwork of a wet climate — those were the mouths. Furniture took what the culture wanted for a porch piece or a stand-in mahogany. The exhaustion warning still belongs in a furniture series because the wood we call cypress now is the after of that sentence. We are working the recovery, the second growth, the pockets of the Cache and the White that were not ditched, the salvage.

I read that 1912 line the way I read a moisture meter: it is a number attached to a time. Twelve million in, forty-eight million out. The ratio is the scar. A modern maker who treats cypress as an endless Southern generic is repeating the ratio in miniature. Use the wood. Do not use it like the mine is still full.

Bragg’s work on antebellum cypress logging sits behind the bulletin. The cut did not start in 1912. 1912 is just when a federal bulletin said the quiet part in a table and a paragraph. The tables in that book are full of such tells — shortleaf as three-quarters of manufacturing, walnut already “not the important furniture wood that it once was,” cherry “too scarce to be of much importance” but good. Cypress is the one they said would run out.

Compare the pine paragraph in the same bulletin. Shortleaf was three-quarters of manufacturing wood, and the worry was grade and heartwood width, not extinction. Cypress was already a special case: slow, overcut, wet-site, not a crop yet. A century of drainage made the case specialer. When we say “Arkansas has cypress,” we should say where, and how old, and whether the stand is a remnant or a ditch bank.

If I am choosing a porch table tomorrow, I can still choose cypress. I choose it knowing I am on the far side of a warning. That changes the design more than the species. I build it to last, because the replacement forest is slow, and because the bulletin already told me what hurry looks like. Hurry is forty-eight million feet against a twelve-million appetite. I try not to be the small version of that ratio.
