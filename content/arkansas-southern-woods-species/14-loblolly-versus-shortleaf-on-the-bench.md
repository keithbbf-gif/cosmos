The trade calls them both Southern Pine. The grading rules
allow it. A lot of invoices never say which tree.

On the bench they are not the same board, even when the
handbook averages look like twins. Both sit near 0.51
specific gravity and 690 Janka if you take the group
number. Both shrink in the same neighborhood. Loblolly
—*Pinus taeda*—grows faster on the sandy, warm sites of
south Arkansas. Shortleaf takes the thinner, drier, more
western and northern pine ground and used to own more of
the state. The 1912 writers said not one manufacturer
reported loblolly, though a comparatively large amount was
cut; it was marketed as shortleaf. The hiding is old.

What you see in a plantation loblolly from the pine belt
is wide rings, a thick band of pale earlywood, a latewood
line that can be hard enough to chip a plane iron, and a
lot of sapwood. The contrast between soft earlywood and
hard latewood is the whole machining problem. The iron
wants to dive in the soft and skip on the hard. A
card-scraper or a high-angle plane helps. Sanding through
the earlywood to “clean up” a tear will dish the ring.

Shortleaf from a slower stand is often more even. That
was the 1912 “higher grade” remark. Not always. A fast
shortleaf on a good site can look like loblolly. A
suppressed loblolly can look refined. You read the board,
not the common name. But if I am buying for furniture and
I can choose, I ask for shortleaf from the Ouachitas or
from mixed stands, and I am suspicious of the cheapest
wide bunk out of a south-Arkansas chip-and-saw culture.
That bunk was grown for weight and volume. Furniture is a
side hustle for that tree.

Resin is another difference in practice, not in the table
of averages. Loblolly plantation wood can bleed pitch
around knots for a long time, especially if you put it
near a window or a heat vent. Shortleaf does it too.
Anyone who has finished a pine table and then found
amber tears in July knows the species group. Kiln
schedules and a sealer over the knots help. They do not
make the tree into white pine.

Longleaf is the third cousin people drag in. Harder,
around 870 Janka, heavier, scarcer in Arkansas — the 1912
note said it was not abundant and three-quarters of what
was used came in from Louisiana, Mississippi, and Texas.
If you have real longleaf, you know it by the narrow
rings and the weight. Do not call a heavy loblolly
heartwood “longleaf” to make a customer feel better.

I have glued up tabletops from both. The loblolly panel
needed more flattening after a season. The shortleaf
panel, from tighter stock, stayed closer to the winding
sticks. That is one shop’s weather, not a paper. It
matches the old reputation. Reputation is not a
substitute for a moisture meter, but it is a reason to
pick up the right phone when you call a mill.

If the invoice says SYP, believe the invoice and then
open the bundle. Count rings. Weigh a sample. Look at
sapwood width. Decide if this is furniture pine or
framing pine that wants to be a painted bench. Both can
be honest. Only one should be asked to look like a
dining room.
