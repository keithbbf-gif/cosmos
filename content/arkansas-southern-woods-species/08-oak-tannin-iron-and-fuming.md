Oak is full of tannin. That is a chemical fact with three shop
consequences, and they all get worse in a humid state.

The first is iron stain. Water plus steel plus oak tannin makes a blue-
black ink. It happens under a clamp pad, under a nail, under a wet
chisel left on a panel, under a wool blanket on a steel bar. White oak
is famous for it because there is so much tannin, but red oak will do it
too. In Arkansas in June the air is already a wet rag. You do not need
to spill a cup. A sweaty forearm and a cheap clamp are enough. The fix
is oxalic acid for the mark, or sandpaper and profanity if the mark is
deep, and then the habit of using dry pads, stainless or plastic cauls,
and no wet rags on bare oak. I keep a box of sacrificial pine cauls for
glue-ups. Pine does not write on oak with iron.

The second is fuming. Ammonia vapor reacts with the tannin and browns
the wood without a pigment stain. The Craftsman factories used it on
quartersawn white oak because the rays and the fiber took the color
differently and the flake stayed alive. You can fume in a plastic tent
with household ammonia and a scrap. You should not treat that as a
casual Saturday. The vapor is ugly to breathe. The color is not even if
the tannin is not even, and bottomland oak and upland oak will not fume
to the same shade in the same night. A test scrap from the same boards
is not optional. Southern humidity speeds the reaction and also rusts
everything else in the shop while you are doing it. People who fume in
July learn why the old shops had a shed for it.

The third is water-based finish blushing and tannin bleed. A waterborne
topcoat on raw oak can pull tannin to the surface and yellow or stain
the film. Sealers exist for this. Shellac is the old one. In a humid
week, waterborne finishes already fight blush. Oak gives them another
fight. I do not refuse waterborne. I refuse to pretend oak is maple.

Tannin is also why oak and ferrous hardware have a long, stained
marriage on porch furniture and old iceboxes. The black around the
screws is not dirt. It is the same reaction. Brass and stainless are
the peace treaty. If you want black iron hardware on a white oak piece
for the look, isolate it with a finish and accept that a scratch will
eventually write a halo.

History sits in that halo. A lot of Southern oak furniture was built in
shops that were not climate-controlled, with steel tools and hide glue
and water everywhere. The pieces that look “original dark” sometimes
are just a century of tannin and iron and hand sweat. You can clean
that back. You can also decide it is part of the object. I have left
iron stain in a breadboard end because it was the only record of a
particular clamp and a particular July. That is a choice, not a
default. A new dining table for a paying kitchen does not get to keep
my clamp marks.

If you remember oak as a tannin wood, you will stop being surprised by
the black streaks. You will plan the metal. You will test the ammonia.
You will not leave a wet belt-sander platen sitting on a finished
panel. The chemistry is old. The humidity is local. Together they are
the actual finishing curriculum for oak in this region, more than any
catalog of stain colors.
