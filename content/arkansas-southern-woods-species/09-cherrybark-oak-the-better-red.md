If you have ever handled a red oak board that felt like it had been to school, it may have been cherrybark.

The tree used to be listed as a variety of southern red oak. It has its own name now, *Quercus pagoda*, the pagoda oak, cherrybark for the dark, scaly hide that made people think of *Prunus*. It grows on better bottomland sites — well-drained second bottoms, the kind of ground that is wet enough to be rich and not wet enough to be a slough. In the South it is the red oak the timber cruisers smile at. The stem is straighter. The wood is heavier. Janka numbers get quoted up around 1,480, which puts it past white oak and well past the southern red that shares its old Latin. Specific gravity sits up with the serious oaks.

On the bench it still has open pores. It is a red oak. You can blow through it. You cannot make a barrel out of it. What you can make is flooring and furniture that does not feel soft under a chair leg. The grain is usually better behaved than *falcata*. Less tearout, more even density between earlywood and latewood, a color that can run a cleaner pink-brown. Quartersawn cherrybark is a sleeper. The ray is there, the hardness is there, and the board does not have the washed-out orange some southern reds take on.

Arkansas sold it as red oak. Of course it did. The 1912 grouping did not have a line for cherrybark. A modern small mill might know the tree in the woods and still stamp the lumber with the group name. If you want it, you buy from people who walk their own tracts and will say “that creek bottom, the ones with the dark bark.” You do not find it reliably in a home-center bunk.

I treat cherrybark as the red oak I will use for a show piece when white oak is spoken for or when the client wants a warmer color without going to cherry. It will not darken like cherry. It will not have walnut’s chocolate. It will look like oak that ate well. In a dining table with a hard oil, the pores still need filling if you want a glass-smooth top. I often leave them. A Southern table that looks like it has pores looks like wood.

There is a small historical cruelty in the name. Cherrybark is not cherry. People have ordered “cherry oak” from a yard and received neither. The bark fooled a common name. The wood does not fool a plane. It is oak, ring-porous, tannic, moving on the usual oak terms. The manners are in the density and the stem form, not in a change of family.

If a cruiser in the Delta or along a well-behaved stretch of the Ouachita or Arkansas bottoms tells you he has cherrybark, listen. That is one of the few times the red oak group is doing you a favor. Take the better stems for furniture. Let the rest of the group be pallets and the painted parts. Not every red oak is a compromise. This one is a reason the compromise exists.
