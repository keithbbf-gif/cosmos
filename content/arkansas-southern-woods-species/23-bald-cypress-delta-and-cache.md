If you have paddled the Cache in winter you have already met the furniture wood.

Bald cypress stands in water that would kill the upland oaks. The knees come up like a crowd of stumps. The butt swells. The needles drop — it is a deciduous conifer, a softwood that acts like it has seasons. *Taxodium distichum*. The handbook’s other names are a pile: southern-cypress, redcypress, yellow-cypress, white-cypress, tidewater red, gulf cypress. Inland yellow versus coastal red is an old trade split. Arkansas’s cypress is inland water: the Big Woods, the White, the Cache, the back swamps of the Delta.

The wood is moderately heavy, moderately hard. Janka in the low five hundreds, sometimes listed near 570. Specific gravity around 0.46. Softer than the pines of the belt, much softer than oak. Radial shrinkage about 3.8 percent, tangential about 6.2 — a friendly T/R for a Southern house. Color from light yellowish brown to a dark chocolate or pumpkin red. Sapwood narrow and nearly white. The smell when you saw it is soursweet, a swamp smell that people either want in the shop or open a door for.

Old-growth heartwood is one of the most decay-resistant woods in the country. That is the property that made tanks, vats, coffins, boats, sash, greenhouse benches, and the porch work that outlived the house. Second-growth heartwood is only moderately resistant. The handbook is careful on that point and furniture people should be too. A new cypress picnic table is not a 1910 cistern. It is a pretty, stable, relatively soft wood that will last better than untreated pine outdoors and worse than the myth.

Antebellum Arkansas already had a cypress trade. Don Bragg has written the swamp-logging side — the kind of work that does not look like a pinebelt spur. The 1912 bulletin said manufacturers wanted twelve million feet a year and the mills were cutting four times that. “The timber grows very slowly, and it is evident that the demand will shortly exhaust the supply.” They were not wrong about the old stuff. What came back is a younger, less immortal tree in a Delta that has also been ditched and farmed.

I treat Arkansas cypress as a place wood. A table that will live on a screened porch. A blanket chest that can take a wet boot. An interior piece where you want the pumpkin color and do not need oak’s dent resistance. I do not treat it as a magic outdoor hardwood. The Cache does not owe anyone a deck.

If you buy cypress, ask inland or tidewater, old or second, pecky or clear. Those four words are the species. “Cypress” alone is a catalog.
