Furniture and whiskey want the same tree.

A bourbon barrel has to be new oak, charred, tight. White oak is the wood that is tight without a liner. Tyloses again. The stave mills want clear, straight-grained *Quercus alba* with the right ring count — not too fast, not a knot factory. That description is also the description of a good furniture log. The difference is the checkbook. Cooperage has been willing to pay.

University of Arkansas extension (FSA5035) put the current tension in writing. White oak is often called the most important commercial hardwood in the Southeast. In Arkansas the white oak group was about twenty-one percent of hardwood volume to mills, thirty-eight percent in the Ozarks. The unique use is barrels. That use “has threatened the presence of quality white oak as a component of forests in the region.” They are talking about quality, not acres. You can have a lot of oak and still run out of the stems that make a stave or a tabletop.

This is not a new kind of competition. Tight cooperage has wanted white oak since before Arkansas was a state. What is newer is the scale of the whiskey revival and the global appetite for American oak barrels. A log that would have gone to a Fort Smith cabinet in 1910 can go to a Kentucky stave mill now and ride out as someone else’s mash bill. Arkansas grows the tree. The barrel may be assembled elsewhere. The furniture shop still shops the leftovers.

I do not begrudge the coopers. They are using the anatomy correctly. I begrudge the pretense that furniture-grade white oak is simply “available” because the FIA map is green. Green on a map is volume. Furniture is grade. Grade is a subset, and the subset has another industry sitting on it.

What does a maker do? Buy from mills that still saw for lumber, not only for staves. Take the widths the stave buyers reject — the ones with a small sound knot, a bit of sweep, a color variation. Design around shorter clears. Quartersaw smaller logs and accept narrower faces. Or stop insisting that every show surface be *alba* and use cherrybark, walnut, or an honest pine where the object can take it. The pull of bourbon is a fact of the woods. Fighting it with nostalgia does not grow a twelve-inch clear.

There is a forest side. White oak does not regenerate as a monoculture plantation the way loblolly does. It wants light at the right time, acorns that survive the deer and the hogs, a fire regime or a cutting regime that is not all-pine. The Ozark and Ouachita public lands hold a lot of the upland hardwood. What happens there in the next thirty years is the real supply story. A furniture essay that ends on a finish recipe and ignores the stand is decorating a shortage.

If you are writing a bill of materials in Arkansas today, put a line in your head: this top is in a contest with a barrel. Then choose a wood, or a grade, that can win.
