Here is a number worth building around: walnut shrinks about 5.5 percent radially and 7.8 percent tangentially, green to oven-dry. The ratio is about 1.4. White oak’s ratio is about 1.9, on larger absolute tangential numbers. Cherry is about 1.9 but on smaller absolutes. Cypress is friendly. Pine is middle.

What the ratio means in a house is cupping. A flatsawn board with a high T/R will cup harder for a given moisture swing. Walnut cupping is milder. Combined with a moderate hardness and a pore that does not act like a straw, you get a case wood that stays a case. That is why a walnut dresser from 1910 still opens in August in Little Rock when an oak one from the same year needs a hip check.

I still do not glue breadboard ends tight on walnut. The absolute movement is not zero. A wide flatsawn walnut top will grow. The point is that walnut gives you more forgiveness when the house is a Southern moisture machine. If you can only pick one solid wood for a chest of drawers that must live in Arkansas without central humidity control, walnut is the adult.

The handbook also calls it stable in use and easy to work. Those two sentences are why it became the furniture hardwood of reputation while oak became the furniture hardwood of volume. Volume needs acres. Reputation needs drawers that fit.

Design still matters. Quartersawn walnut is quieter and less showy than quartersawn oak because the rays are narrower. You do not buy it for flake. You buy it for the radial number. A walnut door on rift or quarter grain will stay flat in a way a flatsawn oak door will not. I use oak for the hard use and walnut for the moving parts when I can afford the mix. That mix is an old one. It just usually hid the oak.

A practical drawer number: on a twelve-inch-deep walnut side, flatsawn, a two-percent moisture swing might move the width on the order of a sixteenth or less. The same swing in white oak, same cut, is more. That sixteenth is the difference between a drawer that kisses the divider and a drawer that needs a hip. I leave a thirty-second to a sixteenth of side play on walnut drawers in Arkansas and a little more on oak. People who grew up with tight European fittings in a humidified apartment think that play looks sloppy. In a house without a humidistat it looks like you have built furniture before.

If a client wants a walnut table in a sunroom with glass on three sides, the UV will do more to the color than the humidity will do to the width. Different problem. Stability is not immunity to light. I say that before I say yes. The drawer-fit story is a humidity story. Keep it in the humidity chapter, where it earns its keep. The sunroom story is a cherry-and-walnut-together story: both will change color, walnut less dramatically. Pick your chapter before you pick the tree.
