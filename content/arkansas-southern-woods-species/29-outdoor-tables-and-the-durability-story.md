“Won’t rot” is a three-word finish that
has ruined more outdoor furniture than
weather has.

What cypress heartwood can do, at its
best, is resist the fungi that eat
untreated pine in a couple of seasons
in Arkansas. What it cannot do is
refuse standing water in a cup joint,
or sun on a film finish, or a steel
screw that rusts and then stains, or
a tabletop that is finished on top and
raw underneath so it cups and opens
the joints. Decay resistance is not
the whole outdoor problem. Movement,
UV, metal, and design are the rest.

A good outdoor cypress table is a
drainage problem that happens to be
made of wood. Slatted top. Gaps that
stay gaps in August. Legs that do not
sit in saucers of water. No box
aprons that hold leaves. Fasteners
that do not rust. A finish you can
renew — oil, not a peeling spar
varnish you will hate in year three —
or no finish and a willingness to go
gray. Gray cypress is honest. Peeling
cypress is a brochure that got tired.

Second-growth wood, again: moderate
resistance. I design as if the wood
will fail at the joints first. I keep
end grain off the ground. I would
rather a trestle that can be taken
apart than a glued lamination that
traps a wet core. The old tanks and
vats lasted because they were full of
water on purpose and made of thick
old heart. Your patio table is empty
and thin.

Compared with the other woods in this
series, cypress is the outdoor
candidate. Oak heartwood has
resistance, especially white oak, but
the movement and the tannin-iron
stain are a nuisance outside. Walnut
has some natural resistance and will
still check in the sun. Cherry does
not want this job. Pine wants pressure
treatment, which is not a furniture
finish I want on a dining surface.
So cypress wins the outdoor argument
by default, not by miracle.

White oak outdoor tables exist and
some of them are good. They are
heavier, they stain around the
fasteners, and they move more. I
build them when the client wants
the hardness and will accept the
halos. I do not build them when
the pitch is “something that can
live by the lake and never be
thought about.” That pitch is
either a teak budget or a lie.

Pressure-treated pine is the other
outdoor default in this state. It
is a construction product. The
chemicals are not a dining finish
I want under a plate, even after
the wet look has gone. If the
table is a picnic unit that will
be painted and abused, treated
pine is honest. If the table is
a family table that happens to
live on a porch, I still want
cypress or a well-designed oak,
not a green 2×6.

I have a cypress bench that has
been through six Arkansas summers.
The seat is gray. The joints are
still tight because they were
pinned and the water has somewhere
to go. That bench taught me more
than the handbook paragraph, and
the handbook paragraph is how I
knew to build it that way. Use
both. Do not use the phrase
“won’t rot.” Say “designed to
dry, moderately resistant
heartwood, stainless, renewable
oil.” If that sentence is too
long for the tag, the tag is
the wrong size.
