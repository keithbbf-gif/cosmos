Pecky cypress is a disease that learned to be a
style.

A fungus attacks the living tree and leaves
pockets — elongated cavities, sometimes filled
with a powdery residue, lined with a darker wood.
When you saw the log and dry the lumber, the
fungus stops. The handbook is explicit: the decay
does not continue in service once the wood is
dry. What you have left is a durable board with
holes in it. Restaurants and stores paneled whole
rooms with that look. Furniture makers inlay it,
or they make a chest front that looks like it
came out of a swamp with a story.

The pockets are not randomly pretty. They follow
the life of the infection. Some boards are
lightly pecked and still structural. Some are
lace. You do not make a chair rail from lace.
You do not make a tabletop that has to hold soup
from a board with open pockets unless you fill
them, and filling them is how you kill the
reason you bought the board. I use pecky as a
vertical face, a sliding-lid chest, a headboard,
a panel inside a frame. I do not use it as a
cutting surface.

Furniture-grade pecky is a sorting job. You want
pockets that are interesting and not so through-
going that the board is a rattle. You want the
surrounding wood sound. You want to be honest
that this is a novelty the handbook already
called a novelty: useful “where water tightness
is unnecessary, appearance is not important, or
a novel effect is desired.” That third clause is
the furniture clause. The first two are the
industrial ones.

There is a class problem hiding in pecky. Clear
old-growth cypress was the expensive, tight,
pumpkin-red wood for tanks and fine millwork.
Pecky was what you did with the infected stems.
Then the clear old-growth went away and pecky
became the thing people could still buy that
said “cypress” in a restaurant. The infection
outlived the luxury. I do not mind. I mind when
pecky is priced as if it were the clear
tidewater that no longer exists.

Working it: the pockets catch a plane iron. You
scrape and sand, or you use a wide belt and then
pick the fuzz out of the holes. Finish will puddle
in the pecks. Wipe it. A thin oil looks better
than a built-up film, which will bridge and then
crack over a cavity. Dust lives in the pockets
forever. If the piece is a kitchen, accept that
you have built a crumb trap. If that bothers the
client, do not use pecky.

I have a scrap of pecky on the wall because it
looks like a map of a slough. That is a
legitimate reason. It is not a strength reason.
Keep those two reasons in different pockets.
