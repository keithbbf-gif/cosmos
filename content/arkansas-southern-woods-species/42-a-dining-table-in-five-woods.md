If I had to make one dining
table for an Arkansas house
and I could pick from the
five, I would not pick the
same wood every time. I
would pick the house.

A house with kids, homework,
and a basketball in the
hall wants oak — white oak
if I can get it, cherrybark
if the oak is red and I
want manners. Hardness
around 1,300 and up. Open
or closed pores as the
group dictates. Movement
that I will design for
with a breadboard that
works. The table will look
like a table in ten years
instead of a relief map of
forks.

A house that wants the
kitchen to look like the
kitchen it actually is —
south Arkansas, mill memory,
no pretense — wants
shortleaf pine, selected
for density, knots sealed,
oil allowed to amber. It
will dent. That is the
point. I will not apologize
for a dent in a wood that
measures 690.

A screened porch that is
used as a dining room
wants cypress, second-growth
honesty, slatted or at
least able to dry, stainless
screws, a finish I can
renew. Soft. Stable. Not
immortal. The Cache is in
the grain if I did not
use the buttress.

A quieter, adult room with
good light and a budget
wants walnut from a river
bench, steamed or not
depending on whether we
like sap, drawers in the
apron if it is that kind
of table, a T/R ratio that
will not fight the
seasons. Formal without
being oak-formal.

A north room that needs
blood, or a client who
understands time, wants
cherry from the Boston
Mountains, pale at
delivery, no toner, a
warning about the runner.
Softer than oak. Kinder
than oak in the joints.
Scarce. I will glue the
top from narrows and not
pretend I had a veneer
log.

The wrong reasons: pine
because it is cheap and
then expecting oak
behavior. Cypress because
someone said it never
rots and then putting a
solid top in a rain
shadow. Cherry because
the catalog was red.
Walnut because it is
“the best” and the house
is a wrestling gym. Oak
because it is oak and
the house is a glass
sunroom with no
overhangs and a client
who hates gaps.

History sits in the pick.
Fort Smith would have
given you oak and gum.
The pine towns would have
given you a take-home
board. The bottoms would
have given you cypress.
The creek bench would
have given you walnut.
The cove would have given
you cherry if the birds
had been busy. You are
choosing among those
histories, not among five
paint chips.

I have made all five. The
ones that failed failed at
the reason, not at the
Janka number. Pick the
reason. The wood will
follow, and the handbook
numbers will tell you how
to joint it.
