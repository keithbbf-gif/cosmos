Straight walnut is a stem wood.
The famous walnut is a problem
the tree had.

A crotch is two stems arguing.
The fiber dives and crosses. On
a board or a veneer you get a
feather, a plume, a landscape
that looks designed. It is not
designed. It is stress. That
stress remains in the piece.
Crotch walnut moves in ways a
straight board will not. It
wants to twist. It checks along
the plume. I use it as a panel
inside a frame, or as a veneer
on a stable core, not as a
solid tabletop unless the client
likes risk and I like
disclaimers.

A burl is a mess of dormant buds
and swirling grain, usually on
the lower stem or a wound. Walnut
burls are dark, busy, full of
inclusions. They are not
structural. They are a surface.
Anyone who builds a chair seat
from a burl is making a sculpture
that will crack. Anyone who
makes a box lid from a well-
dried burl slab is in the
tradition.

Stump wood — the flare and the
root — has the most violent
figure and the most water and
dirt in its past. It must be
dried like you mean it. It will
still move. The figure can look
like a river map. Arkansas
stumps from creek benches
sometimes have mineral color
that people call “stone.” It is
not stone. It is the bench.

Purdue mentions “flash” or
“flare” in the grain of some
walnut. That is a milder figure,
a ribbon in an otherwise straight
board, the kind of thing a
factory might even like. I like
it for drawer fronts. It gives
the eye a place to land without
turning the piece into a trophy.

Historically the figured parts
were veneer if the industry was
paying, firewood if it was not.
The hayrack era did not slice
crotches. The later price era
sliced everything, including
stumps that should have stayed
in the ground. A modern small
shop in Arkansas is back to a
middle: we have the occasional
local crotch, we do not have a
veneer mill down the road, we
decide whether to saw it solid
and live with the movement or
send it to someone with a
slicer.

I once sawed a creek-bench
crotch in Warren’s orbit
and got two bookmatched
plumes and a pile of
firewood from the same
three feet. The plumes
became a box lid. The
firewood was the tuition.
That is the yield of
figure. Anyone who prices
crotch by the board foot
of the parent log is
lying about the waste.

If you buy figured walnut,
buy it dry, buy it
oversize, and buy enough
to lose some. Figure is
not extra straight wood.
Figure is a different
material that happens to
be attached to the same
tree. Design for the
argument in the fiber, or
the argument will happen
in the finish room. I
prefer it happen on a
scrap.
