The Forest Service said a thing about red oak that people skip because it is inconvenient: rapidly grown second-growth wood is generally harder and tougher than finer-textured old-growth wood.

That is backwards from the pine story people carry in their heads. In southern yellow pine, old and slow usually means dense latewood and the “heart pine” that dents less. In oak, a fast second-growth tree can put on wide rings with a lot of latewood and come out harder than a slow, tight-ringed patriarch. Tight rings look fancy. They are not automatically stronger.

Arkansas oak today is almost all second growth. The virgin hardwoods of the Ozarks and the bottoms were cut. What we mill now grew back in the twentieth century, sometimes in pasture going back to woods, sometimes under a high-grade, sometimes in a stand that was high-graded and left with junk. The rings tell you which. Wide, even rings and a healthy latewood band: the tree had light and food. Tight rings on a small log: the tree was suppressed, maybe on a ridge, maybe under a canopy. Either can be furniture. They will not machine the same.

Wide-ringed oak has earlywood pores you could lose a pencil tip in, then a band of hard latewood. A plane will chatter if you treat it like maple. You take a lighter cut, or you scrape, or you sand and stop pretending. Tight-ringed oak planes sweeter and may be a little less brutal on a chisel edge. It may also be the slower, less tough material the handbook warned about. I do not pick rings for romance. I pick them for the job. A workbench top can take wide rings and the extra hardness. A carved apron wants tighter, even texture.

Color follows the life. Fast oak from an open Ozark pasture edge can be paler, with a wide sapwood. Forest-grown oak often shows a narrower sap and a dirtier heart. Mineral streak is a bottoms signature. None of that is grade by itself. Grade is knots, wane, shake, and whether the board will hold a finish. The rings are the biography.

Old catalogs used “slow growth” as a compliment on oak because the figure looked refined and the European tradition prized tight oak. Southern second-growth does not owe that tradition anything. If the board is sound and dry and the rays are where you wanted them, it is good oak. If a dealer charges you extra for “old growth Arkansas oak,” ask to see the rings and the provenance. There is not much of that left that is not already a beam in a building. Most of what is for sale is twentieth-century wood wearing a nineteenth-century adjective.

I count rings on the end grain the way some people read a menu. Ten rings to the inch, twenty, four. I look at the latewood percentage. I look at whether the rings run true or wander, which is how you spot a tree that leaned. Then I decide if this board wants to be a top, a leg, or firewood. The handbook average cannot do that. Only the end of the board can.
