Cherry has a reputation for machining well. That reputation is true and incomplete.

Blotch is the finishing fight. The even texture people like is not even to a pigment stain. Some areas drink, some areas refuse, and you get a mottled face that looks like the board has a skin disease. Gel stains, dyes, a spit-coat of shellac, a conditioner — everyone has a religion. Mine is dye plus a film or oil, not pigment in the pore, and a sample board from the actual lot. Southern humidity makes blotch worse because people rush the wipe. If the stain flashes off at two speeds, you have already lost. Work smaller areas. Or skip stain. Cherry does not need it.

Grain reversal is the planing fight. Cherry likes to change direction in the same board, a gentle interlock or a wave that the raking light will show after you have already torn a patch. A sharp highangle plane, a scraper, a light cut. Dull tools burnish and then tear. I have ruined a drawer front by insisting the jack plane was fine. It was not fine.

Burn is the power-tool fight. Cherry burns in a router fence and on a saw blade that is a little pitchy. The burn is a dark line that sanding will not always lift because you have cooked the fiber. Then the cooked line finishes darker, and the darkening of the years makes it a pinstripe you did not ask for. Clean blades. Climbcut the last pass if you know how. Do not linger in a stopped cut. A burned edge on a cherry door is how you can date my impatience.

None of this makes cherry unfriendly. Walnut is friendlier. Oak is louder. Cherry is particular. The particularity is why the finished piece looks like it was made by a person. A wood that never fights also never shows the care.

Hand tools change the fights. A well-tuned scraper does not burn. A sharp chisel does not blotch. The blotch is a stain problem. The burn is a spinning-cutter problem. If you are willing to work slower, cherry is the pleasant wood the books promised. If you are routing a whole kitchen of cherry doors in July, it is a pitch-and-burn wood that happens to be expensive.

Arkansas cherry with wide sap adds a fourth fight: matching. You can dye sap. You can hide sap. You cannot pretend sap is heart after a Southern window has had a year. Work the first three fights on heartwood if you can. Save the sap for interiors and painted backs. The tree is scarce here. Do not spend it on a blotched, burned door with a lightning bolt of sap. That is how a good Boston Mountain log becomes a lesson instead of a piece. I have that lesson. I do not need another.
