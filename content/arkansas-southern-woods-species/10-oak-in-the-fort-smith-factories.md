Fort Smith did not become a furniture town because someone in a
catalog discovered oak. It became a furniture town because men who
already knew how to turn wood got on a train.

In 1878 William Ott came down from Indiana with partners and a
factory’s worth of machinery. Ott, Meier & Co. opened the Fort
Smith Furniture Factory — also called the Fort Smith Furniture
Manufacturing Company — and brought experienced woodworkers with
them. Edward Ballman was one. He was a turner. The first company
did not prosper. The building changed hands. By the early 1880s
Ballman was an owner, and over the next decades he helped make
Fort Smith a place that made beds, couches, kitchen cabinets,
folding tables, and the other objects a growing Western town
would buy. Fort Smith Folding Bed and Table Company, 1902. Fort
Smith Couch and Bedding, 1904. Border Queen Kitchen Cabinet
Company, 1906. A manufacturers’ cooperative in 1921 to sell the
lines together.

What did they build from? The 1912 statewide furniture numbers
are the closest honest snapshot: red gum and white oak together
were about ninety-five percent of the wood in Arkansas furniture
factories. White oak was “generally an outside wood,” finished
for appearance. Red gum — sweetgum — did inside and outside and
took any stain you pointed at it, including fake Circassian
walnut and fake oak. Shortleaf pine, which was three-quarters of
all manufacturing wood in the state, barely showed in furniture.
The factories had pine in the air and oak and gum on the show
faces.

So the Fort Smith oak was not a mystique. It was the local
hardwood that would take a finish and survive a railroad trip
to a customer. Some of it was quartersawn for the flake. Some
of it was flatsawn and stained dark so a kitchen cabinet looked
like the furniture in a magazine. Border Queen cabinets were
not trying to be a royal court. They were trying to be a
kitchen in a house that might be in Oklahoma or Texas as easily
as Sebastian County. Oak was the language those houses already
spoke.

I think about the turners. Ballman could make a bed post. Oak
turns, but it is not maple. The ring-porous earlywood can
crumble on a bead if the tool is dull or the speed is wrong.
Factory turners learned the wood as a production problem:
how many posts per hour, how much sandpaper, how much stain to
hide the tear. That is a different oak knowledge from the
one-off shop. Both are real. The factory knowledge is the one
that put Arkansas oak into other people’s rooms in volume.

The town sat on the river and the rails, at the edge of the
Ozarks and the edge of the Indian Territory that became
Oklahoma. Hardwood came off the hills. Pine came up from the
south if they wanted it. The factories did not need to invent
a supply. They needed skill, capital, and a way to ship.
Ott’s machinery and Ballman’s habit of starting specialized
companies were the skill and capital. The Frisco and the other
roads were the rest.

When you see an old oak bed with a Fort Smith mark, or a
kitchen cabinet with Border Queen hardware, you are not looking
at a craft myth. You are looking at a production hardwood
culture that used the state’s white oak as the respectable
face and sweetgum as the versatile understudy. The properties
of the oak — hardness, tannin, ray, movement — were the same
properties in this essay series. The history is that a river
town industrialized them.
