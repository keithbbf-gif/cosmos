Post oak got its name from the job, not from the furniture shop.

*Quercus stellata* takes the dry, thin soils — the ridges and south slopes of the Ouachitas, the rockier Ozark glades, the places a white oak would rather not linger. The cross-shaped leaf is the field mark. The tree is often shorter, limby, stubborn. Farmers split it for posts because the heartwood lasts in the ground. That durability is a white-oak-group trait plus a dry-site chemistry: denser latewood, high extractives, a life spent not being in a hurry.

The 1912 Arkansas list put post oak under white oak and moved on. Mills did not pile it separately. A furniture factory buying “white oak” could easily be buying *stellata* from a ridge that had been logged for ties and posts and had some sawlog left. The wood is in the group. It is not *alba*. The pores still plug. The color is a little dingier, the ray a little less regular, the grain often more cantankerous. You can build with it. People did. They built kitchen tables that nobody photographed, porch steps, wagon parts, and the kind of cupboard that stayed in the same house until the house came down.

I like post oak when I know that is what I have. It feels honest in a piece that will be used hard — a workbench skirt, a hall bench, a farm table with a breadboard end that is allowed to look like wood. I like it less when it is asked to be a Stickley settle with even flake. The tree did not grow for that. The limbs that made it a poor veneer log are the same limbs that made it a good post: low, strong, full of reaction wood and knots.

There is a conservation story under the shop story. Shortleaf pine woodlands on these same Ouachita ridges are being restored in places, which means fire and a more open understory and oak that has to live with pine again instead of a closed hardwood thicket. Post oak belongs in that picture. It is a woodland oak, not a bottomland oak. If the restoration holds, there will still be post oak. If everything is planted to loblolly, the furniture wood of those ridges becomes a memory and a fence line.

Hardness is in the white oak neighborhood. Do not expect a gift. The planes still dull. The tannin still stains iron. The movement is still that 2:1 tangential-to-radial trouble if you saw it flat and wide. What you gain is a local wood with a name that remembers work. “Post” is not a slur. It is a use-history. A lot of Southern furniture started as a use and only later became a style.

When a sawyer in the Ouachitas says he has some oak, ask which ridge and whether the leaves were lobed round and crossed. If he looks at you like you have time to waste, buy the board anyway and read the end grain. If the rays are there and the pores are closed and the color is a little tired, you may have post oak. Build the piece that can stand a wet boot. Leave the parlor to *alba* and walnut.
