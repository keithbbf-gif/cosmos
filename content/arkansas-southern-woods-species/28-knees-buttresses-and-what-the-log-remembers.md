A cypress log is not a pine log with
wet feet. The tree’s body is a response
to standing water, and the lumber keeps
the response.

The swollen butt — the buttress — is
lighter, often brash, full of the kind
of wood the handbook warns about on
other swamp trees too. Pumpkin ash and
water tupelo have the same note: wood
from the buttress is not the wood from
the upper stem. If you saw furniture
stock out of the flare because the
diameter is tempting, you will get
surprise density and surprise movement.
Take the stem above the swell when you
can.

Knees are not lumber. They are a folk
object and a tourist object and
occasionally a carved base. I have
seen knees turned into lamp stands that
looked like a joke the swamp played.
They are unstable, full of bark
inclusions, and they want to split. If
you use a knee, use it as itself, not
as a substitute for a joined box.

Reaction wood lives in trees that lean
toward light over water. Cypress leans.
The resulting compression wood shrinks
along the grain more than honest wood
and will bow a leg after you have
already cut the mortise. Sight down
every blank. If the rings are piled on
one side and the pith is off, pick
another blank.

Mineral stain and dark streaks are
common. They are not pecky. They are
the chemistry of wet soil. I leave
them. They are how you know the board
did not grow on a ridge.

The upper stem of a decent cypress can
be as straight and even as a good
softwood. That is the furniture wood.
The romantic parts of the tree — knees,
flare, the flared flutes — are the
parts that make photographs and bad
tables. I am not against romance. I am
against building a dining table out of
a photograph.

I have split a cypress blank after a
month in the rack and found the pith
off-center by an inch and a half, the
rings packed on the lean side, and a
shake that had been waiting since the
tree took a flood year. That blank
had looked fine on the mill cart.
The slough was still in it. I cut
it into turning squares and stopped
asking it to be a stile.

Tupelo and overcup from the same
water have their own versions of
this memory. The point is not that
cypress is uniquely deceitful. The
point is that a swamp tree writes
the swamp into the first eight
feet, and those eight feet are the
feet a sawyer hates to waste
because the diameter is there.
Waste them anyway, or waste them
into rustic work that can take a
split. Furniture that has to stay
flat should start above the flare,
the way a good cooper starts
above the swell on a white oak.

When you stand in a slough in
January and look at the knees, you
are looking at a tree that has
solved a problem you do not have
in the shop. Do not import the
problem. Import the stem. The log
remembers the water either way.
You get to choose how much of the
memory you can live with in a
joint. I choose less than the
postcard, more than a stud. That
is the furniture amount of swamp.
