Cherry in Arkansas is a mountain
tree that also shows up wherever
a bird sat still.

*Prunus serotina* — wild black
cherry, the only native cherry
that is a lumber tree — ranges
across the eastern half of the
country. The commercial center
has always been the Middle
Atlantic. The 1912 Arkansas
bulletin said the wood was too
scarce in the state to matter
much, and that the quality was
good. Both clauses are still
true. You can find it. You
cannot furnish a factory with
it. The Boston Mountains and
the moister Ozark coves are
where I look: a straight dark
bole in a mixed hardwood stand,
the bark like burnt cornflakes,
white flowers in spring that
the whole ridge can smell.

The wood is moderately heavy,
moderately hard. Janka about
950. Specific gravity about
0.50. Softer than walnut by a
little, much softer than oak.
Radial shrinkage 3.7 percent,
tangential 7.1. After it is dry
it is very stable, the handbook
says, and shops agree. Heartwood
is a light reddish brown with a
luster you can see when you
turn the board in the light.
Sapwood is pale and, in second
growth, wide. The texture is
even. The pores are not a
shout. It machines, as the
books say, very well — until
it doesn’t, which is a
different essay.

Arkansas cherry is not
Pennsylvania cherry in volume
or in log diameter. It can be
Pennsylvania cherry in quality
on a good stem. I have had
local boards with the same
luster and the same polite
grain, just shorter and with
more sap. You design for the
length you have. A six-foot
clear is a gift. A ten-foot
clear is a rumor.

Historically cherry was parlor
wood when someone had it —
the good bed, the drop-leaf,
the piece that sat away from
the wet. It was never the
state’s volume furniture wood.
That was gum and oak. Cherry
was a northern reputation that
a few Arkansas trees could
support. They still can. The
restriction is the forest, not
the species.

The flowers are not a
furniture property and I
mention them anyway. A
ridge in April smells like
the reason the wood has a
name people like. The fruit
is small, bitter-sweet, bird
food. The bark on a young
stem is smooth and banded;
on an old one it is those
cornflakes. Cruisers walk
past a lot of small cherry
because the stem never made
a log. The furniture wood
is the minority that did.

I do not stain cherry to
look like cherry. That
sentence should be illegal
and is not. A light oil,
time, and a southern window
will do the work. If the
sap bothers you, hide it
on the back or dye it
gently. If you flood a red
toner on Arkansas cherry to
make it look like a
catalog, you have wasted a
Boston Mountain tree on a
photograph. The ridge
already did the hard part.
