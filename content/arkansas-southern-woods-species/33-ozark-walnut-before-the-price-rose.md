Purdue’s walnut circular has a
sentence I keep in my pocket: as
late as the 1960s the author saw
walnut 1×12s sold for hayrack
boards because the wood would not
rot. By the 1970s the price had
gone up and the furniture industry
promoted other species. By the
mid-1990s pale hardwoods were
fashion and walnut looked old.

Arkansas had already lived a
version of that arc by 1912. The
bulletin said walnut was not the
important furniture wood it had
been. The Ozarks still had trees.
Farmers still had hayracks. The
factories had moved their volume
to gum and oak because volume
needs a volume tree. Walnut became
the special piece — the dresser
that stayed in the family, the
gunstock, the courthouse rail.

Before that rise, Ozark walnut was
a farm hardwood. You cut a tree
when it shaded the field or when
you needed cash. You sawed it at a
local mill. You built a wardrobe
or you sold the logs to a buyer
who knew St. Louis. Some of those
logs were beautiful. Some were
limby farm trees with a wide sap
and a crotch that should have been
sliced for veneer and instead
became firewood. Waste is a luxury
of later prices. When the wood is
ordinary, you waste it. When the
wood is dear, you invent steaming
and veneer and a market for the
stump.

I have repaired Ozark pieces from
that ordinary period. The walnut
is often lighter than modern
steamed stock, with more sap left
on, hide glue, simple frames,
panels that were allowed to float.
They were not trying to be a
showroom. They were trying to be a
place to put clothes. The
properties that make walnut easy
to work are the properties that
made it a farm luxury instead of a
farm chore: it planes, it holds a
profile, it looks finished with
almost nothing on it. Oak is a
chore. Walnut is a gift you could
still afford if you owned the
tree.

Fort Smith’s factories did not
build their volume on that gift.
They could not. There was not
enough. The 1912 furniture mix is
the evidence. So the Ozark walnut
story and the factory oak story
are neighbors, not the same story.
One is a tree on a bench above a
creek. The other is a production
hardwood culture on a river
railhead. When people say
“Arkansas furniture” they should
say which.

The price rise did a good thing
and a bad thing. Good: people
stopped using walnut for hayracks.
Bad: people started treating every
walnut board like jewelry and
every other wood like a
compromise. I would like a middle.
Use walnut where the hand will
see it. Use oak and pine where
the hand will work. That is an
older Ozark economy than the
current one, and it still makes
better rooms.
