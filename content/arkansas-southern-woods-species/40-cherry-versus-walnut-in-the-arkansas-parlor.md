Parlor wood is a social
category that happens to be
a botanical one.

Walnut and cherry were the
two native woods the
handbook says were used
“primarily for furniture and
cabinets” while oak was out
building ships and ties.
In an Arkansas house that
distinction landed as: oak
in the kitchen and the
factory cabinet, walnut if
the family had a tree or
money, cherry if they had
less of both and a taste
for red, or a northern
catalog.

Walnut reads darker, more
formal, more “we own land.”
Cherry reads warmer, more
domestic, more “we keep
this room for Sunday.”
Those are not properties.
Those are habits. The
properties underneath are
close enough that a shop
can substitute: both
machine well, both sit
around 950–1,010 Janka,
both are more stable than
oak, both darken or quiet
in light, both are scarce
in this state relative to
oak and pine.

The 1912 bulletin puts
them in their Arkansas
place. Walnut: small cut,
home-grown, mostly
furniture, not what it
had been. Cherry: too
scarce to matter, quality
good, commercial supply
elsewhere. So the parlor
in this state, if it had
a fine native wood, was
more often walnut when it
was local, and cherry when
it was bought in. That
split still hides in
estates. The cherry
drop-leaf may be
Pennsylvania wood that
rode the rails. The walnut
wardrobe may have been a
creek-bench tree.

I choose between them now
by the room and the light.
A north room that needs
warmth: cherry, and let
the window do what it can.
A room that already has
too much red brick or
cedar: walnut. A client
who will put a runner on
the table and then move
it: walnut, because the
ghost is quieter. A client
who likes a ghost: cherry.

Fort Smith’s volume did
not run on either. Gum and
oak did. The parlor woods
were the exception pieces
inside an industrial
hardwood town. When you
see a cherry or walnut
Fort Smith piece, you are
seeing the factory on a
day it was not making
kitchen cabinets. That is
worth knowing. It keeps
you from thinking the
state’s furniture story is
a cherry story. It is an
oak-and-gum story with a
parlor at the edge.

A mixed parlor — cherry
fronts, walnut sides — was
not unheard of when both
were scraps from different
trees. It looks intentional
if you commit. It looks
like a mistake if you were
trying to match a catalog
and ran out. I would
rather commit. The five-
wood series is full of
permissions to mix. The
parlor is allowed.

The two woods will both
outlast the fashion that
picked them. Pick for the
hand and the light. Let
the social category be a
history lesson, not a
hangtag. Sunday rooms
change. The boards do
not mind which day it is.
