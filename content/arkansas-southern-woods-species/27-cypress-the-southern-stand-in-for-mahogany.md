Mahogany did not grow in Arkansas in commercial quantity. The 1912 bulletin said so, and then mentioned that factories still knew the word because tropical wood arrived when someone paid. Most people did not pay. They wanted the reddish-brown look of a parlor wood and they had pumpkinhearted cypress, red gum that would take a walnut stain, and the occasional black walnut tree.

Cypress as a mahogany stand-in is a color story more than a strength story. A good red-cypress heart can look, across a room, like a quiet mahogany. Up close it has the softwood ring and none of mahogany’s interlocked ribbon unless you get lucky with figure. It dents if you look at it. It does not have the silky pore of *Swietenia*. What it had was being here, and being associated with durability, and being able to take an oil that warmed it into the mahogany neighborhood.

I have seen church furniture and courthouse trim in the Delta that was clearly cypress trying to be a tropical hardwood. Dark finish, brushed bronze, a solemnity that the wood did not ask for. Some of it is handsome. Some of it is a costume. The handsome pieces are the ones that let the grain stay visible and did not drown the board in red toner. The costume pieces are the ones that might as well have been painted.

Sweetgum was the other stand-in, and in Arkansas furniture factories it was the main one. Rotary gum veneer dressed as Circassian walnut. Stain recipes for oak, cherry, mahogany. Cypress shows up less in those factory tables and more in the millwork and the porch-adjacent parlor — the piece that might get weather, or might remember weather. A cypress wardrobe in a hallway off a porch makes sense. A cypress ball-and-claw dining set is a wish.

Today the stand-in instinct is back because mahogany is expensive and loaded with questions, and cypress is still Southern. If you are going to do it, do it as cypress. Oil, wax, maybe a dye that evened the sap, no pretense in the invoice. The history of Southern furniture is already full of woods pretending to be other woods. We do not need a new layer of pretending. We need the pumpkin board to be allowed to be a pumpkin board.

There is a finishing recipe I will not write down as a mahogany formula. Dark red dye, brown glaze, a name on the invoice that is not the tree. That recipe is how Southern factories taught customers not to trust their eyes. I will even a cypress sap streak. I will not turn cypress into a tropical hardwood on paper. If the client wants mahogany, we talk about mahogany, cost, and legality. If they want a Southern red-brown that can live near weather, we talk about cypress.

The property that makes the stand-in plausible — that warm heartwood color — is also the property that varies wildly from tree to tree. Do not buy one face board and assume the rest of the bunk will match. Cypress color is a harvest, not a paint code. Buy the set of faces at once, or design the piece to enjoy the mismatch, the way a swamp enjoys more than one brown.
