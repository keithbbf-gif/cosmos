Framing pine is dried for construction. Casework pine has to be dried for a box that cannot rack.

Those are not the same target. A stud can sit at nineteen percent and nobody cries. A drawer side at nineteen percent will shrink in a cooled house and leave a gap you can see from the hall. If you then glue a plywood bottom into a groove that was cut at the wet size, the drawer becomes a machine for splitting itself.

Southern yellow pine is not difficult to dry in the sense of collapsing like some gums. It is difficult in the sense of resin, knots, juvenile wood, and a steep gradient between sapwood and heart. The handbook calls shrinkage moderately high and the wood stable once properly dried. “Properly” is the word that eats weekends. A too-hot kiln will set pitch on the surface and leave a wet core. A too-fast schedule will check the knots and case-harden the wide stock. A construction kiln that dumps 2×12s for a truss plant is not thinking about your dovetails.

I like to take furniture pine down to eight or nine percent and then let it sit in the shop. The shop in Arkansas is not a laboratory. In July it may put moisture back. That is information. If the boards gain, your target for the customer’s house may need to be a point higher. If they stay, you got lucky with the week. Measure, do not assume the kiln ticket is the end of the story. I have met kiln tickets that were fiction.

Juvenile wood — the first several rings, the stuff around the pith — will twist no matter how pious the schedule. Leave the pith out of casework. Rip it out. Use those boards for internals that can be screwed and painted. The clear, pith-free, vertical or near-vertical grain is what you want for a door stile. That selection is more important than arguing loblolly versus shortleaf once the wood is in the rack.

Gluing SYP for casework is easy if the surfaces are fresh and the resin is not a glaze. Old planed pine that has sat can have a pitch film that glue does not love. Joint it again. Yellow glue holds. I have used hide glue on pine interiors for repairs because it reverses. For a new cabinet that will see kitchen water, I want a glue that does not reverse. The wood is the weak part anyway.

Pine casework in the Fort Smith era was not the main show. The 1912 numbers say so. When pine did show up in furniture it was often painted or hidden. That is still a good strategy. A painted pine cupboard is a Southern object with a long pedigree. A clear-finished pine cupboard will yellow, dent, and weep at the knots. Dry it right and those are patina. Dry it wrong and they are a callback.
