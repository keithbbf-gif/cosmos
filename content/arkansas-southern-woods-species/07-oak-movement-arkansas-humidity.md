People blame the wood when a drawer sticks in August. The wood is only
doing arithmetic.

Wood shrinks and swells across the grain as it sheds or takes water
vapor. Along the grain it barely moves. The handbook numbers are green to
oven-dry, which is a bigger swing than a house will ever see, but the
ratio is the useful part. White oak: about 5.6 percent radial, 10.5
percent tangential. Northern red: 4.0 and 8.6. Southern red often worse
on the tangent. Walnut: 5.5 and 7.8 — notice how close those two are.
Cherry: 3.7 and 7.1. Cypress: 3.8 and 6.2. Shortleaf pine: 4.6 and 7.7.

Oak is not the most stable wood in this series. Walnut and cypress and
cherry all have a kinder T/R story. Oak is the one people still build
wide tops from, because it is hard and local and they like the look. So
the furniture fails at the geometry, not at the species.

An Arkansas house through a year is a moisture machine. Summer air
conditioning pulls indoor relative humidity down. A closed, cooled house
in July can sit in the thirties or low forties RH if the system is
oversized and the doors stay shut. November without heat or with the
windows open can sit in the seventies. A board dried to 6 percent in a
kiln and then built into a table that lives at 11 percent in a damp
shoulder season will grow. The same table in a tight, cold-January house
with a gas heater may shrink back. The movement is mostly across the
width. If you glued a breadboard end on tight and pinned it like a
mortise that cannot slide, you split the top. If you framed a raised
panel without room, you buckle the panel or you open a gap that looks
like a mistake in February.

Equilibrium moisture content indoors here is not a New England number and
it is not a desert number. It wanders. The practical shop answer is to
dry the oak to the middle of the range the piece will see — often 8 to 10
percent for furniture that will live in a typical Arkansas house — and
then design the joints to let the rest happen. Quartersawn helps because
you are living on the radial number. Narrow boards in a glued-up top help
because each board cups less. Slotted screw holes, floating panels,
breadboards with elongated pins — these are not style. They are how you
keep oak from telling on you.

I have seen factory oak from the Fort Smith era that survived because the
tops were not too wide, the finish was on both faces, and the houses were
leaky. A leaky house is a kinder moisture environment than a sealed,
air-conditioned box. The old pieces did not have better oak. They had
more air. New work in a tight house needs more forgiveness in the
joinery, not more stain.

Red oak and white oak do not move the same, but they both move enough to
ruin a glued-on breadboard. Do not pick red because the chart said 8.6
instead of 10.5 and call that safety. Pick the cut. Pick the width. Pick
a finish that coats both faces so one side does not take water faster
than the other. And do not deliver a piece from a 6-percent kiln room
into a customer’s house the same week. Let it sit in the climate it will
die in. Oak will tell you what it wants if you give it a month and a
pair of winding sticks.
