In 1912, nearly three-quarters of all the wood used for manufacturing in Arkansas was shortleaf pine. The men who wrote that sentence thought some loblolly was hiding in the tally. They also thought Arkansas shortleaf was a higher grade than the same species grown elsewhere: softer, better color, wide rings that showed in the grain. Favorite for sash, doors, ceilings. Liked for floors, though a little soft next to longleaf.

That is the furniture-adjacent pine of this state. Not the romantic longleaf of the deep coastal plain. Not eastern white pine, which does not grow here and had to be imported if a factory wanted it. Shortleaf. *Pinus echinata*. The one that once covered millions of acres of native woodland and still holds the Ouachitas and the drier pine sites better than loblolly does.

It is a southern yellow pine. Sapwood yellowish white, heartwood reddish brown. Janka around 690 for the shortleaf and loblolly class. Specific gravity around 0.51. Radial shrinkage about 4.6 percent, tangential about 7.7. Those are not oak numbers. A shortleaf table will dent. A dropped canning jar will leave a memory. People who want a farmhouse table in “pine” and also want it to look new in ten years are asking for two different objects.

What shortleaf has, when it is sawn from a decent log, is workability and a color that takes oil like it was waiting. The 1912 compliment — softer, good color, rings that show — is a furniture compliment. Hard longleaf is a joist compliment. You can make furniture from either. The shortleaf will plane more kindly, nail with less splitting if you are doing country work, and look like the kitchens this region actually had.

Heartwood starts when the tree is around twenty years old, says the handbook for the southern pines as a group. In old, slow trees the sapwood may be only an inch or two. In the second-growth that filled the cutovers, sapwood is wide and the board is mostly pale. That is why a modern “yellow pine” dresser looks blond and a porch board pulled from a 1905 mill town building looks amber. Same species group, different life.

I do not treat shortleaf as a cheap substitute for oak. I treat it as the wood that built the state’s doors and then got snubbed by the furniture factories, who used it for almost nothing in 1912 while they dressed sweetgum to look like walnut. That snub was fashion. Pine furniture in a Southern house is an older fashion. It is also a truer one if the house is a house and not a showroom.

The tree is in restoration talk now — shortleaf woodlands, fire, the Ouachita National Forest, the mix that used to be pine and oak together instead of a loblolly row. If that work holds, the furniture pine of Arkansas might again be a woodland pine with knots and character instead of a plantation stud. I would rather build a table from the woodland tree. The knots are part of the species, not a defect the factory forgot to cut out.
