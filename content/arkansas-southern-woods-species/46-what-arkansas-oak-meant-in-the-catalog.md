Catalogs are liars that
sometimes tell a regional
truth.

“Arkansas oak” in a
late-nineteenth or early-
twentieth-century listing
could mean: oak from
Arkansas, which was
plausible because the
state had the trees and
the rails; oak of a
certain grade the seller
wanted to sound
specific; or just oak,
with a Southern adjective
to make it sound tougher
or cheaper depending on
the year. Buyers in
Chicago and St. Louis
already knew Arkansas as
a timber state. The
pine-belt names traveled.
Oak traveled with them,
even when the mill that
sawed it was not in
Warren.

What it could not mean,
as a property, was a
single Janka number or
a single species. The
state grows white oak
on the ridges, post oak
on the dry shelves,
overcup in the bottoms,
a red oak group that
is mostly not *Q.
rubra*, cherrybark on
the better second
bottoms. A catalog line
cannot hold that. It
holds a mood. The mood
was: Southern, plentiful,
a little cheaper than
Appalachian oak, good
enough for factory
furniture and floors.

Sometimes the mood was
fair. Ozark white oak
is a real product with
real rays and real
weight. Sometimes the
mood was a bunk of
mixed reds from a
Delta cut, stained dark,
sold north as a
regional brand. The
1912 bulletin’s refusal
to separate the white
oaks, and its list of
reds hiding under one
word, is the mill-end
of that catalog. The
catalog is the
consumer-end.

I read old advertisements
the way I read grade
stamps: as a claim that
wants a board behind
it. If I cannot find
the board, the claim
is decoration. “Arkansas
oak” on a surviving
piece is decoration
until you look at the
end grain. Closed pores
and gray-brown heart:
white group, maybe
*alba*, maybe not.
Open pores and pink:
red group, almost
certainly not the
northern tree on the
chart. That check is
the whole method of
this series.

The phrase is still
useful if we take it
back. Arkansas oak
should mean oak that
grew in this state’s
actual forests, sold
with a group name and
a site — upland or
bottom — and used in
a way that matches the
movement and the
tannin. It should not
mean a vibe. We have
enough vibes. We have
a limited number of
ridges.

This is the last draft
in the folder and it
is a naming draft
because naming is how
the five woods get
lost. Pine that is
really a density class.
Cypress that is really
an age class. Walnut
that is really a farm
tree. Cherry that is
really a short log.
Oak that is really a
pile of species under
a catalog word. Say
the smaller word. The
furniture gets better
when the word gets
smaller.
