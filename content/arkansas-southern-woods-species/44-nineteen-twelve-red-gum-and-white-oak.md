This series is about oak, pine, cypress, walnut, and cherry. The 1912 furniture table in Arkansas is about two woods, and one of them is not on the list.

Red gum and white oak together were ninety-five percent of the wood in the state’s furniture factories. White oak on the outside, finished to look like itself. Red gum — sweetgum, *Liquidambar* — inside and out, stained to look like Circassian walnut, oak, cherry, mahogany, whatever the buyer had seen in a picture. Shortleaf pine’s tiny showing, the bulletin said, was remarkable given how much pine the rest of the state’s industry demanded.

I am not going to pretend sweetgum is one of the five. I am going to say that any honest history of the five has to admit the understudy. Sweetgum was the reason walnut and cherry could stay scarce and oak could stay the respectable face. It is moderately hard, often interlocked, a nightmare if you dry it fast, a ribbon figure when you quarter it. Arkansas led the country in red gum lumber. Europe called it satin walnut. The name is a confession.

Why include this in a folder named for the five? Because clients still say “they used walnut” when they mean “they used gum that was stained to look like walnut.” Because a lot of “oak” factory furniture is oak faces and gum sides. Because pine’s absence from the 1912 furniture line is the fact that makes the pine essays in this series a correction, not a period reconstruction.

The five woods are the ones a shop now actually reaches for when it wants a Southern furniture language that is not a stain recipe. Gum is still around. It is still a good wood. It is also the wood that taught Arkansas factories they could fake the others. This series is against the fake, not against the tree.

If you are dating an old Arkansas piece, look at the pores. Gum is not ring-porous like oak. It is not the semi-ring of walnut. It is a softer, more even hardwood with a tendency to ribbon. The finish may say cherry. The pore will not. Learn that and the 1912 table becomes a tool instead of a trivia item.

Sweetgum still grows in the bottoms like it owns them. It still interlocks. It still dries like a grudge. A modern shop that wants an Arkansas volume hardwood after oak is looking at gum whether this folder named it or not. I left it off the title list because the assignment was the five. I will not leave it out of the history. History does not care about the title list.

The bulletin writers were not sentimental. They were counting feet and dollars. Their ninety-five percent is the ground truth this series stands on when it says Fort Smith ran on oak and gum, the pine towns ran on pine, and walnut and cherry were the parlor exceptions. Count first. Then tell the tree stories. The count is why the stories do not float.
