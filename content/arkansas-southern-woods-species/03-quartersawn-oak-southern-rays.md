Quartersawing is a way of wasting a log on purpose.

You stand the annular rings so they run nearly vertical on the end of the
board. The face then shows the medullary rays as flakes — the “tiger” or
“fleck” people point at in Arts and Crafts catalogs. You lose yield. You
gain a board that moves less in width and a face that is not a set of
cathedrals waiting to cup. In oak the rays are wide enough that the waste
is worth arguing about. In cherry they are not. That is why nobody
quartersaws cherry for the show.

The 1912 Arkansas bulletin already knew the furniture reason. White and red
oaks, it said, were valued in furniture and interior finish for figure and
grain, and the grain was “best brought out by quarter sawing, which exposes
the flat sides of the medullary rays, the bright clear streaks or flakes so
prominent in the oaks.” The factories in the state were not waiting for
Gustav Stickley to explain this. They were already paying more for the
better-figured stock.

A Southern shop in a humid climate has a second reason that the catalogs
underplay. Tangential shrinkage in white oak is roughly twice the radial.
A flatsawn top is a tangential face. It will grow and shrink across its
width with the seasons, and it will cup if the two faces see different
humidity. A quartersawn top is a radial face. It still moves. It moves
less, and it stays flatter. In an Arkansas house that is shut up with air
conditioning from May to October and then opened to a damp November, that
difference is the difference between a table you live with and a table you
shim.

The flake itself is not decoration glued on. It is a ribbon of ray tissue
running from pith toward bark. When you slice parallel to that ribbon you
see its width. When you slice across it you barely notice it. People who
stain quartersawn oak dark and then wonder where the figure went have
buried the ray in pigment. A clear oil, a light dye, or a fumed finish
that does not fill the ray with mud will leave the flake visible. The old
ammonia-fumed quartersawn oak of the Craftsman period worked because the
tannin in the ray and the fiber darkened at slightly different rates. You
can still do it. You do not have to. A raw, well-quartersawn Arkansas white
oak under a hard wax already looks like it belongs in a room.

Small Southern mills did not always want to quartersaw. The grade rules and
the yield argued against it. A lot of “quartersawn” that reached local
shops was rift-sawn or near-quarter — rings at forty-five to sixty degrees,
some flake, better stability than flat, not the full show. That is honest
stock if you pay rift prices. It is a cheat if you pay for fleck and get
cathedrals at the edges. Look at the end grain. The rings should stand up
like a palisade. If they arc across the end, you bought flat.

Red oak quartersaws too. The rays are there. The color is pinker. The
pores stay open, so the flake sits in a wood that still drinks finish. I
like it for floors more than for tabletops. White oak is the one that
earned the style. Cherrybark will quartersaw into something handsome and
hard. Post oak often has a coarser, less regular ray. You learn this by
ruining a few boards, or by asking the sawyer which log he would take home.

There is a Southern Arts and Crafts echo that is not a museum copy.
Mission furniture went through Fort Smith and every other factory town as a
catalog style. Some of it was quartersawn oak. Some of it was flatsawn oak
stained to look serious. The good pieces that survived in Arkansas houses
are usually the ones that were actually quartered, because they did not
tear themselves apart in the humidity. Style is what you see in a
photograph. Sawing is what you see in a hundred-year-old apron that is
still flat.

If you only remember one thing: quartersawing is not a finish. It is a
geometry. The geometry spends log and buys movement and light. In this
climate, buy the geometry.
