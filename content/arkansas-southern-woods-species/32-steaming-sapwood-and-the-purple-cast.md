Walnut sapwood is a problem the mills
solved with steam, and the solution
became a taste.

Open-grown trees — the farm walnuts
of Arkansas creek edges — can carry
three inches of cream sap. In a
narrow board that sap is a stripe.
In a wide one it is a map. Furniture
customers for a long time called it
a defect. Mills began steaming the
lumber green. The heat and moisture
darken the sap toward the heart,
even out the heart, and, as Purdue’s
walnut note says, knock back the
purplish cast that fresh heart
sometimes shows. After steaming, the
sap is no longer graded as a defect
in the same way. You have bought a
more uniform brown.

I have mixed feelings, which is a
useless shop emotion unless you turn
it into a choice. Steamed walnut is
easier to match in a set of chairs.
It looks like the walnut in a
catalog. It also looks a little
cooked. The purple in fresh heart is
a living color. It fades toward
brown in light anyway, but the
unsteamed purple-to-chocolate shift
is a different life than the steamed
even-brown. If I can get air-dried,
unsteamed Arkansas walnut, I take
it, sap and all, and I design the
sap as a line — a breadboard, a
panel edge, a drawer front with the
cream left as a border. If I am
matching a dining set for someone
who wants “walnut-colored,” I take
the steamed and stop arguing.

The purple cast worries people who
think the wood is dyed. It is not
dyed. It is fresh. Give it sun and
time. The color will settle. If you
put a UV-blocking finish on too
early you may keep more of the
purple than you meant to. Test a
scrap in the window. Walnut and
cherry are both time-woods. Cherry
goes darker. Walnut goes quieter.

Sapwood without steam is also a
durability stripe. Heartwood has
the extractives. Sapwood does not.
Indoors this is academic. On a
windowsill planter it is not. I do
not put sap-heavy walnut outdoors
and then complain.

I have glued a steamed rail next
to an air-dried stile and watched
the pair argue in a year’s light.
The steamed piece had already
spent its purple. The air-dried
piece spent it in the house. They
met later, mostly. The first six
months were a conversation I had
to explain. Match the process
inside a piece. Mix processes
only if you want a record of the
mill’s decision on the object.

There is a small honesty issue in
the trade. A steamed board can
hide how much sap the tree had.
You are entitled to know whether
you are buying heart or a cooked
compromise. Ask. A mill that will
not say is selling the compromise.
The compromise can still be a
good table. It should just be
named. “Walnut” is not a process.
“Steamed walnut” is.
