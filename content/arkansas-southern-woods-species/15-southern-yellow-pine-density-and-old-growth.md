“Pine” on a furniture blog is a mood. Southern yellow pine
is a density class.

The four major species — longleaf, shortleaf, loblolly,
slash — are sold together under grading rules that also
allow some minor pines. Georgia, Alabama, North Carolina,
Arkansas, and Louisiana have led production. The woods
look alike: pale sap, reddish-brown heart, abrupt
transition in the ring. They do not weigh alike. Longleaf
and slash are the heavy ones. Shortleaf and loblolly are
usually lighter. The handbook says so in one sentence and
then, in the next breath, says you can write a density
rule for structural timbers that specifies latewood
percentage and growth rate. In other words, even inside
one species the board can be a joist or a cream puff.

Old-growth southern pine is the extreme of that rule.
Slow rings, thick latewood, a heart that is amber and
resinous and hard enough that people quote Janka figures
in the 1,200s for “heart pine.” That is not the 690 of
plantation shortleaf. It is a different material that
happens to be the same genus. Most of it is already in
buildings. The rest is salvage. Calling a new 2×12
“heart pine” because the pith is showing is a sales
voice. The pith is juvenile wood, often the worst wood
in the tree — unstable, weak, prone to twist. Heartwood
color is not the same as old-growth density.

Furniture from dense SYP is a different proposition from
furniture from light SYP. The dense stuff dents less,
takes a crisper profile, and will wear like a cheap oak.
It is also heavier to hang as a door and harder on
cutters. The light stuff is the kitchen table of the
last forty years: blond, soft, knotty if you paid for
character, full of the earlywood that mushrooms under a
finish. Both are “yellow pine.” If you do not weigh them
or look at latewood, you will design for the wrong one.

Arkansas’s old mills — Crossett, Warren, Fordyce — cut
virgin pine that was mostly shortleaf on many of those
tracts, with the density of a forest that had not been
taught to grow like corn. The towns were built from that
cut. Walk a 1910 mill-house floor in south Arkansas and
you are walking the argument. Then walk a new-home
subfloor from the same county and you are walking the
other argument. Same trade name. Different century.

I keep a pair of offcuts on a shelf, labeled with a
pencil: “Ouachita shortleaf, 2019, 18 rings/in” and
“belt loblolly, 2022, 4 rings/in.” Visitors who tap them
stop saying pine is pine. That is the whole lesson. The
species group is a marketing convenience. Density is the
property. History is the reason the density used to be
higher in the boards this region shipped north.

If you are specifying pine for a piece, specify a weight
or a ring count or a source stand. “SYP” is how you buy
a surprise.
