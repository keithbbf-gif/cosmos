Cypress machines like a softwood that has
opinions.

The earlywood is tender. The latewood is a
harder line. A dull plane will fuzz the
tender and skip the line. A sharp plane with
a tight mouth will take a shaving that looks
like wet silk and then, on the next board,
hit interlocked grain and tear a crater. I
keep a scraper on the bench when I am on
cypress. I also keep the blades sharper than
I do for pine, because the fuzz of cypress
is a particular embarrassment under a clear
finish — pale nap that no stain will hide.

The wood has an oily feel in some hearts,
especially the darker ones. Glue still
holds if the surface is fresh. If you joint
on Monday and glue on Thursday, wipe or
joint again. Oil finishes sink in and look
right. Film finishes can sit on the oil
like water on a duck if you do not let a
first coat dry hard and then scuff. I like
a wiping varnish cut thin, or a hard wax
over oil, more than a plastic film. The
wood wants to look like it can breathe,
even if that is a feeling and not a
building-science paper.

Tearout around knots and around pecky
pockets is the other tax. Take a light
climbing cut if you are brave and the
cutter is in a good mood, or go to
abrasives and stop chasing a perfect
shaving. Sanding through 180 is enough
for oil. Past that you are burnishing
and the oil will sit on top.

The smell. Some people come into the shop
and smile. Some people ask if something
died. It is the smell of the slough, a
little sour, a little sweet, stronger when
the saw is in the cut and on a humid day.
It fades in the finished piece. It does
not fade in the dust collection. If you
are sensitive, this is not your everyday
wood. I am not sensitive and I still open
the door.

Nails go in easily and hold poorly if the
piece will rack. Screws need a pilot in
the harder latewood or they will split a
thin stile. Cypress is not a structural
furniture wood in the chair sense. It is
a box wood, a top wood, a panel wood. The
old industrial uses — tanks, vats, cooling
towers — were thick and often wet on
purpose. A chair is thin and dry and full
of leverage. I have seen cypress chairs.
I have also repaired them.

Dust is fine and gets in the pecks and in
your teeth. It is not walnut-league
chemically, but I still wear a mask
because I like having a nose. The shop
rule is the same as the woods rule: treat
cypress as a swamp tree that agreed to
come inside, not as a tame pine.
