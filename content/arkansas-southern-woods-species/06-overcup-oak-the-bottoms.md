Overcup oak grows where the white oak of the ridges would drown.

*Quercus lyrata* sits in the bottoms — sloughs, first bottoms, the edges
of water that stays too long in the spring. The acorn is almost swallowed
by the cap, which is the only field mark most people remember. The wood
goes to market as white oak. Sometimes that is fair. Sometimes you can
feel the difference as soon as the board comes off the truck: a little
more weight in some logs, a little more swamp in the color, a grain that
does not lie as straight as an upland *alba*.

Bottomland oaks live with flooding. The tree puts on wood in a different
rhythm. Tension wood, butt swell, mineral streak — the bottoms write in
the log. A furniture maker who loves clear, even stock will curse a
percentage of overcup. A maker who likes a board that looks like it came
from a place will not. I have seen overcup with a gray-green cast that no
stain should touch. I have also seen it honeycomb in a kiln that was
rushed because someone treated it like upland oak and cranked the heat.

It is still a white oak in the ways that matter for a table. Tyloses are
generally there. The ray is there. Decay resistance in the heartwood is in
the family. You can quartersaw it. You can make a case side that will not
drink the way red oak will. What you cannot do is assume the drying
schedule or the machining will be identical to a slow Ozark *alba*. Wet
site, different stresses, different defects.

Arkansas furniture history is full of this substitution because the
bottoms were logged hard for anything that would raft or ride a spur.
Overcup, Nuttall, willow, water oak, sweetgum — the Delta and the river
counties sent wood to mills that already had a bin labeled white or red.
The 1912 writers listed overcup under white oak and said the woods were
seldom sold separately. A century later the bin is still the bin.

If you are buying from a small mill on the White or the Cache or down
toward the lower Arkansas, ask whether the oak is upland or bottom. A
good sawyer knows which tract. He may not know *lyrata* from *michauxii*,
and he does not have to. Upland versus bottom is the split that predicts
the board. Upland: tighter rings, cleaner stems if the stand was managed,
color in the classic gray-brown. Bottom: faster or more irregular rings,
more stain, more of a chance the wide board you wanted has a hidden check
from the butt.

I do not treat overcup as a second-class white oak. I treat it as a
bottomland white oak. That is a species of place. A harvest table with
breadboard ends, a little mineral color left visible, an oil finish that
does not try to make it look like quartered English oak — that is a
correct use. A jewelry-box interior is not. Let the bottoms stay in the
piece the way the water stayed in the tree.
