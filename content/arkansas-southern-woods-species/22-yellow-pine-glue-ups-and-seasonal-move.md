A pine panel is a set of tangents glued into a
weather vane.

Each board wants to cup away from the bark side as it
dries, toward the bark side as it takes moisture —
or the other way, depending on who you learned from
and how the rings sit. The rule that matters is
simpler: the growth rings are curves, and the longer
arc of the curve will change length more. If you
glue all the bark faces the same way, the panel
moves like one board. If you alternate, it
corrugates. Neither is a miracle. Alternating is
what I do for a tabletop that has to stay in a
family. Same-way is what I do if I am going to
fasten the panel to a base that will hold it flat
and I want the cup predictable.

Southern yellow pine cups hard when the rings are
wide and the earlywood is weak. A flatsawn 1×12 from
a fast tree is almost designed to move. Rip it into
threes or fours, joint, and glue. You lose the
cathedral if you wanted the cathedral. You gain a
panel that does not turn into a trough by Labor Day.
If you must have the wide cathedral, make it a
plank top with battens or a breadboard, not a glue-
up you pretend is stable.

The shrinkage numbers are kinder than white oak —
roughly 4.6–4.8 radial, 7.4–7.7 tangential for
shortleaf and loblolly — but the boards are often
wider and more flatsawn, so the absolute movement
is still enough to open a breadboard joint. Finish
both faces. A pine top oiled on top and raw
underneath is a cupping machine in a humid week.
I have done that to myself. The underside looked
clean. The top looked like a boat.

Seasonal move in Arkansas is not theoretical. I mark
a pencil line on the end of a panel in January and
again in August. The difference is the lesson I
cannot get from a chart. If you do not want to wait,
clamp a scrap the same way, leave it in the
customer’s house for a month, and measure that. The
house is the kiln now.

A breadboard end on pine is a different animal
than a breadboard on oak. The pine top is often
wider, the stock is often flatsawn, and the
tongue you cut is in earlywood that will crush
if you pin it like hardwood. I cut the tongue
a little fat, I elongate the outer pin holes
more than my oak habit, and I do not glue
anything but the center two or three inches.
In August the top grows and the breadboard
stays. In January it shrinks and you see a
proud end or a gap. That gap is the joint
working. A customer who wants no gap wants
a different wood or a different climate.

Battens on the underside, slotted, are the
other Southern answer. They are ugly if you
look under the table. They are beautiful if
you look at the top in September. I would
rather a farm table with honest battens than
a glue-up I have to take back.

Pine will forgive a lot of finish mistakes
and zero movement mistakes. Design the panel
like you respect the rings. Then the August
cup is a rumor instead of a drive back to
the shop. If you ignore this essay and glue
a 1×12 cathedral panel in June, we will
meet again in August. I already know which
of us will be right.
