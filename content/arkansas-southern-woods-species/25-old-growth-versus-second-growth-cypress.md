The sentence in the Wood Handbook that should be taped to every cypress invoice is this: the heartwood of old-growth baldcypress is one of the most decay-resistant of U.S. species, but second-growth wood is only moderately resistant to decay.

That is a species split inside one Latin name. Old-growth *Taxodium* spent centuries laying down heartwood full of the extractives that fungi do not like. Second-growth *Taxodium* spent decades. The color can still be handsome. The smell can still be swamp. The knees can still be on the postcard. The rot resistance is not the same chemical fact.

Furniture people inherited the old sentence — “cypress doesn’t rot” — and applied it to whatever the mill had. Outdoor kitchens, garden benches, boat-adjacent coffee tables, planter boxes. Some of those pieces are already soft in the joints because the wood was young, the joints held water, and “moderately resistant” is not a spell. Untreated pine would have failed sooner. Cedar might have failed later. The myth fails on schedule.

How do you tell? Color is a hint, not a test. Old tidewater red-cypress had a deep, oily look and a weight that surprised people. Inland yellow-cypress was paler. Second-growth can imitate both. Ring count helps. A board with four rings to the inch is not a patriarch. Provenance helps more: a documented salvage beam from a 1900 vat is a different gamble from a new 1×8 out of a young Delta stand. Even salvage can be second-growth if the vat was late.

I build outdoor cypress as if it were moderately resistant, always. I keep water out of end grain. I do not trap moisture in a thick film on one face. I use stainless fasteners because tannin and iron are not only an oak problem. I tell the client the handbook sentence. If they want the myth, they can buy the myth from someone else.

Indoors, the split matters less. Decay is not the indoor enemy. Dents and scratches are. Old or young, cypress is soft. A dining table in cypress is a choice about atmosphere, not about armor. The old-growth indoor pieces I have seen — blanket chests, wardrobes, church millwork — survived because they were dry, not because they were immortal.

A lab durability rating is also not a climate. Arkansas heat plus a wet joint is a harder test than a northern shed. I have seen second-growth cypress fail at a mortise that held water against a lawn while the same species, same age, lasted on a covered porch twenty feet away. The cover was the treatment. The Latin was the same.

The trade used to mark the difference with those regional names: tidewater red, gulf, inland yellow. The names were half geography and half marketing. They at least admitted that cypress was not one thing. We would be healthier if every new board said “second-growth, moderate durability” in the same typeface as the species. Until then, the handbook sentence is the species. Read it to the client. If they still want a myth, they can buy a myth. They should not buy it from this shop.
