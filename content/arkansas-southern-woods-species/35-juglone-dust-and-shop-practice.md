Walnut does not only darken a
room. It darkens a garden.

Juglone is the naphthoquinone
the tree leaks into the soil.
Tomatoes hate it. A lot of
plants hate it. The furniture
maker meets a milder version
as the yellow-brown stain on
hands, the smell in the
husks, the dust that hangs
in a sunbeam. Some people
get a headache. Some people
get a rash. Some people get
nothing and then get cocky.
I wear a respirator on the
sander because cocky is not
a property of the wood.

The dust is fine and oily. It
sticks. It will contaminate a
blond maple finish if you
sand walnut in the morning
and maple in the afternoon
without cleaning the air and
the brushes. Cross-contamination
is how you get a gray ghost
in a clear coat. Shop
sequence: light woods first,
walnut last, or a real
cleanup in between. The same
rule applies to cherry, but
walnut is the one that
advertises itself on the
walls.

Fresh husks will stain
concrete and children. If you
mill your own logs in
September, plan the mess.
The hulls are not a charming
byproduct. They are a dye.

Toxicity talk gets sloppy on
the internet. Walnut is not
arsenic. It is also not
basswood. Treat the dust as
a nuisance dust with a
known plant poison in the
family, keep it out of your
lungs, do not burn scraps
in a poorly drafted stove
and stand in the smoke, and
do not let a client with a
horse-stall romanticism use
walnut shavings without
asking a vet. The shop
piece is simpler: mask,
collection, cleanup.

I like the smell anyway. That
is allowed. Liking the smell
is not a reason to dry-sand
a tabletop without a fan.
The properties that make
walnut a pleasure — oils,
color, the nut chemistry —
are the properties that make
it a shop discipline. Oak
tannin writes on the board.
Walnut chemistry writes on
you.

The garden side of juglone
matters if you mill on a
place that also grows food.
I do not dump walnut chips
on the tomato beds. I do
not store fresh slabs on
the grass I care about. The
tree is allowed to be
allelopathic. The shop is
not required to spread it.

People also ask about
burning scraps. A clean
outdoor fire of dry walnut
is a thing some families
do. A shop stove in a
closed room is a different
thing. I do not fill the
shop air with any hardwood
smoke on purpose. The
species is irrelevant next
to carbon monoxide and
creosote. Use the scraps
for cauls and clamping
pads. Walnut cauls do not
iron-stain oak, which is a
small kindness.

One more practical note.
Juglone and the related
compounds can inhibit some
finishes if the surface is
fresh and oily. A wipedown
with the solvent your
finish wants, or a day of
open-grain time after the
last cut, prevents the
occasional mysterious
fisheye. If your oil beads,
do not add more oil. Clean
and wait. The chemistry
that makes the garden fail
is the same chemistry that
makes the finish fail if
you rush. Walnut teaches
patience in two directions.
