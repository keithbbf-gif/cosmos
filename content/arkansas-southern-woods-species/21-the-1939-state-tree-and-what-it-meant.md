In 1939 Arkansas named the pine as the state tree and did not say which pine.

That is not a clerical error. It is a political description of the woods. Loblolly and shortleaf were both the state’s trees, and everyone who lived among them knew it. Shortleaf was the older woodland pine, the one the 1912 bulletin treated as the manufacturing wood. Loblolly was already the faster Coastal Plain pine, the one that would take the plantations after the virgin stands were gone. To pick one would have been to pick a region and a decade. The law left it as pine.

Furniture people can take a lesson from that cowardice, or that wisdom. “Arkansas pine” is a civic name. It is not a specification. The civic name got painted on school walls and 4-H posters while the mills were learning sustained yield and the paper plants were going up at Camden and Crossett. Pine meant payroll. It meant the largest manufacturing sector for a long stretch of the twentieth century. It did not mean a dining table.

I like that the state tree is a softwood that dents. Most state trees are a kind of boast — live oak, sugar maple, something that photographs. Arkansas boasted the tree that paid the bills and built the shotgun houses. The properties of that boast are the properties in this series: 690 Janka if you are in the shortleaf-loblolly class, resin, wide sap in second growth, a grain that shows the year. A child who grew up under that poster and then bought oak cabinets in 1998 was not betraying the state tree. The state tree was never asking to be a cabinet. It was asking to be a forest industry.

Shortleaf restoration people will tell you the unnamed species is a problem now, because loblolly ate the map. If the civic pine is mostly taeda in rows, the 1939 law has been fulfilled in the cheapest way. The woodland echinata is the one that makes a better furniture board and a better story. I would rather the next generation’s state-tree poster showed a shortleaf woodland with oak in the understory than a single geometric pine. That is a forest wish, not a shop spec.

There is a classroom version of this story that I distrust. It puts a single pine on a worksheet and asks a child to label needle, cone, and “products.” The products are lumber and paper. The tree is a silhouette. Nobody mentions that shortleaf needles come in twos, mostly, and loblolly in threes, and that you can tell them apart in a parking lot if someone taught you. The 1939 law made the silhouette official. Foresters spent the rest of the century trying to put the twos and threes back into the picture, because a woodland you cannot name you cannot manage.

I have used that parking-lot test with kids in Hot Spring County. Two-needle, shorter, a little twisted: shortleaf, or close enough to start a fight with a botanist. Three-needle, longer, the plantation look: loblolly. The furniture test is the same test with a sawkerf. Two-needle stands on thinner soil often give me the even board. Threeneedle rows on old cotton sand give me the stud. The law that refused to name a species still left us a way to name a board.

For the shop, then: write the Latin on the cut list when it matters, and let “pine” be enough when you are painting a cupboard for a mudroom. The state was willing to be vague. Your glue-up should not be. If you want a civic gesture in the piece, leave a small unpainted patch on the underside and write *P. echinata* or *P. taeda* and the county. That is more of a state tree than the poster.
