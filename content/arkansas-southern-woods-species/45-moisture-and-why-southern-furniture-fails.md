Species is a conversation.
Moisture is the argument
the piece loses.

I have been through the
five woods in this folder
as if the Latin were the
plot. The plot is
equilibrium moisture
content. A board at 6
percent in a kiln, built
into a table, dropped
into a house that sits at
11 percent in November,
will grow across the
grain. The same table in
a sealed, over-cooled
July house may sit nearer
7 or 8. The swing is the
enemy. The species only
chooses the amplitude.

Approximate green-to-oven-
dry tangential numbers,
the worst-case direction
for a flatsawn top:

- White oak: 10.5%
- Southern red oak: often
  worse than northern’s 8.6
- Shortleaf pine: 7.7%
- Loblolly: 7.4%
- Cypress: 6.2%
- Walnut: 7.8%
- Cherry: 7.1%

You will never see the
full green-to-OD swing in
a house. You will see a
slice of it, every year,
for as long as the piece
is together. Oak asks the
most of your joinery.
Cypress and cherry ask
less. Walnut is the
sleeper — moderate
absolute, kind ratio.
Pine is moderate and then
you glue a twelve-inch
flat and throw the
advantage away.

Southern failure modes I
keep seeing, species
almost irrelevant:

1. One-face finish. The
   top takes weather or
   oil, the underside
   takes the room. Cup.
2. Breadboard pinned
   like a mortise that
   cannot slide. Split.
3. Panel glued in the
   groove. Split or
   buckle.
4. Delivery from a dry
   shop into a wet week
   with no acclimation.
   Stuck doors, proud
   edges.
5. A wide cathedral
   glue-up with all
   rings nested the same
   way and no battens.
   Trough.

Air conditioning made
this worse, not better.
Old leaky houses sat in
a narrower indoor swing.
New tight houses with
oversized cooling sit
very dry in summer and
then, when the system
is off in a mild wet
spell, jump. The piece
has to live in both.
Design for both.

I dry to the middle of
the expected range, 8–10
percent for most
Arkansas interiors, and
I let the piece sit. I
finish both faces. I
let panels float. I do
not care how pretty the
species essay was if
the drawer does not
open in August.

A cheap moisture meter and
a notebook will teach more
than another species essay.
Pick a board from each of
the five. Weigh them or
meter them in January and
August in the same room.
Write the numbers on the
end grain. That is the
only local table that
matters. The handbook
table is a national
average from a different
century’s sample. Yours
is the house.

If you take one thing
from this whole folder,
take this one. The five
woods are a palette.
Moisture is the medium.
A good Southern maker
is a moisture maker who
also has taste in trees.
Taste without a meter is
how we get pretty
failures. I have made
those. I prefer not to
repeat them in oak, pine,
cypress, walnut, or
cherry. The Latin does
not save you.
