Northern red oak — *Quercus rubra* — is the red oak in the textbooks and
the Janka charts. About 1,290 pounds. Specific gravity around 0.63. Nice
pink-brown heartwood, open pores, a board the kitchen-cabinet boom of the
late twentieth century turned into a national default.

It is not the common red oak of Arkansas.

The 1912 bulletin is blunt. “The true red oak (*Quercus rubra*) is not
plentiful in Arkansas.” What the yards called red oak was a pile of other
trees: Texan or spotted oak (*Q. texana*, the Nuttall oak of later lists),
willow oak, black oak, water oak, Spanish oak. Today the group still
includes southern red (*Q. falcata*), cherrybark, Shumard, pin, willow,
water, Nuttall, black. You cannot sort those species from the lumber
alone. The Forest Service said so. The sawyer who tells you he can is
selling confidence.

Southern red oak as a named wood is usually *Q. falcata*. It is lighter
than northern red in the handbook tables — specific gravity nearer 0.59,
Janka nearer 1,060. The grain is often coarser. The color can run more
orange or russet. Interlocked grain shows up enough that a plane will tear
out in patches you did not expect. Shrinkage is not friendlier. Tangential
figures for southern red are often worse than northern red. So the board
you bought because “oak is oak” may be softer, wilder, and more willing to
move than the chart you memorized.

That does not make it junk. It makes it a Southern wood sold under a
Northern name. Furniture factories in Arkansas used the red oak group for
strength in vehicles and machinery and for figure in furniture, same as
white oak, and they quartered it when they wanted the ray. A lot of
serviceable chairs, tables, and casework in this state are southern-group
red oak that nobody ever called *falcata*. They called it oak. The finish
was dark. The pores showed. It lasted if it was dry and the joints were
honest.

Willow oak and water oak from the bottoms are a different mood again —
often used, often in the red oak bin, not what you want for a fine
tabletop unless you like a restless grain and a surprise in the moisture
meter. Black oak can be handsome. Shumard, on better sites, can look
almost like the northern wood people think they bought. Cherrybark is its
own essay. The point is the bin. The bin is a commercial convenience. The
tree is not.

I have ripped “red oak” from a big-box unit and found three densities in
one bunk. That is the modern version of the 1912 grouping. The grade stamp
says red oak. The Janka number on the website is northern red. The board
in your hands grew in a county that never had much *rubra*. If the piece
is painted, none of this matters. If the piece is clear-finished and
expected to behave like a Pennsylvania red oak dresser, you will learn the
grouping the hard way when a door starts to bind in August.

History here is a naming history. Arkansas grew many red oaks. The market
grew one word. Furniture followed the word north in catalogs and followed
the trees at home. A shop that wants to speak plainly will say “red oak
group, Arkansas, probably southern red or Nuttall, open-pored, not
cooperage, hardness in the low thousands if you are lucky.” That sentence
does not fit a hangtag. It fits a bench.

Use the wood. Just do not let a catalog from another region write its
properties onto your invoice.
