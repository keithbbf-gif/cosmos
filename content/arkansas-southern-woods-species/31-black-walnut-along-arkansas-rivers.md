Walnut in Arkansas is a river tree that also learned the farm.

*Juglans nigra* wants deep, welldrained soil — the benches above the flood, the creek bottoms that do not stay drowned, the edge of a field where a squirrel planted a nut and nobody cut the seedling because the nuts were useful. It is not an upland oak. It is not a swamp cypress. It is the hardwood of good ground, and good ground in this state is a strip along water or a farm that was worth keeping.

The wood is the one people mean when they say furniture without naming a species. Heartwood from pale brown to a dark chocolate, sometimes a purple cast when fresh. Sapwood creamy and sharp against the heart, wider on open-grown farm trees. Janka about 1,010. Specific gravity about 0.55. Hard enough for a table, soft enough to work without hating your tools. Radial shrinkage 5.5 percent, tangential 7.8 — that ratio is why walnut drawers still fit in houses that have wrecked oak doors.

It machines clean. It takes a finish like it was designed for oil. The pores are semi-ring-porous, a middle texture between oak’s shouting earlywood and cherry’s evenness. You can fill them or not. I usually do not. A walnut table that looks like plastic is a waste of a river tree.

The 1912 bulletin said the cut in Arkansas was small, all home-grown, and “not the important furniture wood that it once was,” though most of what was used in the state still went to furniture. That sentence is a whole history. There had been enough walnut to matter. Then there was not. The factories moved to gum and oak. The remaining walnut became a special wood, then a rich wood, then a gunstock and office-furniture wood, then, after the pale-hardwood fashion of the 1990s, a wood people wanted again.

Nationally the best stems have been the central states — Ohio to Iowa. Arkansas is the southern shoulder of that range. Our trees can be excellent. They are not the volume. If you find a clean, dark, twelveinch Arkansas walnut, you have found a farm or a bench that was left alone. Treat it like that, not like a commodity with a Janka number.

I knock walnut on the bench and it answers lower than oak, drier than pine. The plane shaving curls and the shop smells like a nut husk. That smell is juglone and oils. It is also the reason people forgive walnut every other fault. There are not many faults. The faults are price, sapwood, and the fact that everyone else wants the same board.
