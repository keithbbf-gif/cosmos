South Arkansas learned what pine was from company
towns.

Fordyce Lumber Company, 1892, Dallas County — Watzek,
Gates, Crossett, the Davenport men. Crossett Lumber
Company, 1899, Ashley County and over the line into
Morehouse Parish, Louisiana. First commercial timber
sold in 1902 after a million dollars in start-up.
Two band mills, dry kilns, a planer; a second pine
mill in 1905; on the order of eighty-four million
board feet a year in that early peak. Arkansas
Lumber Company at Warren, Bradley County, 1901,
Rittenhouse and Embree. Southern Lumber Company at
Warren, 1902, Weyerhaeuser. The Cotton Belt had
already given them a way out. Samuel Fordyce’s road
from St. Louis through Pine Bluff and Camden toward
Texas is the reason those names are towns and not
just tracts.

They came for virgin pine. A lot of it was shortleaf.
The method, for a generation, was cut out and get
out: buy the timber, build the mill, cut the parcel,
move the spur, leave a shaved county. Some towns died
when the mill moved — Graysonia, Mauldin, Forester.
Crossett did not die. Cap Gates called Yale. In 1926
H. H. Chapman told him to hire a forester. W. K.
Williams came. Sustained yield entered south Arkansas
as a business decision, not a poem. Later came kraft
paper, chemicals from waste, food board, flakeboard,
and in 1963 a pine plywood plant at Fordyce under
Georgia-Pacific. International Paper’s kraft mill at
Camden opened in 1928. Potlatch at Warren. The pine
stopped being only boards.

Furniture is a side character in that story and
should stay one. These towns made dimension, paper,
and later panels. They did not exist to feed Fort
Smith’s cabinet lines. But they set the price and the
idea of pine for the whole state. If pine was
endless and cheap and headed for a kiln and a boxcar,
a furniture factory would not spend it on a show
face. They spent white oak and sweetgum. The 1912
bulletin is the proof. The pine-belt towns are the
reason the proof looks like that.

I have stood in Warren and Crossett thinking about
the difference between a furniture wood and an
industrial wood. Same tree, different mouth. An
industrial wood can become furniture in a kitchen
because a mill hand took a board home. It does not
become a parlor wood until fashion or shortage says
so. Shortleaf had the properties for parlor work —
color, rings, a softness that planes. It did not have
the fashion. The towns had other plans.

When people now talk about Arkansas pine furniture
they are talking across that history. They want the
look of the mill house and the respect of the dining
room. You can build the object. You cannot rebuild
the virgin stand or the whistle. What you can do is
name the towns correctly and not pretend a plantation
stud is a 1905 Crossett board. The towns earned the
distinction the hard way, by cutting a forest and
then, late, learning to grow one.
