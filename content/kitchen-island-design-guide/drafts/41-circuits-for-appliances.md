A receptacle for a kettle is one
story. A cooktop is another. Do not
let them share a sentence.

Small-appliance branch circuits in a
dwelling kitchen are a code family
with their own manners. The island,
if it has receptacles serving the
top, usually sits in that family.
A microwave drawer, a wine fridge, a
dishwasher, an induction cooktop —
those want their own circuits as the
nameplate demands. I want the
nameplate before the box is ordered,
because the hole and the circuit and
the vent are a set.

Induction in an island is fashionable
and electrically greedy. It is also
cleaner than gas in an open room if
you were going to skip a real hood
anyway, which you should not. Still:
dedicated circuit, manufacturer
clearances, and a landing that is
not a diner’s notebook. Gas in an
island is a gas permit, a shutoff
you can reach, and a hood that is
not a joke.

Dishwashers in islands look clever
on a plan and steal both structure
and a circuit. They also steal the
place a person wanted to stand. I
allow them when the cleanup zone
truly lives in the island and the
aisles still walk. I do not allow
them as a way to “use the space.”

Garbage disposals, if the island has
a sink, are a circuit and a noise
into the living room. Instant-hot
taps are a circuit and a maintenance
item. Each gadget is a year-ten
conversation. I ask the household
to pick two, not five.

Lighting is not an appliance
circuit. Pendants and an under-fly
LED strip can share a lighting
story. They should not share the
toaster.

Bradley full-access drawers make it
easy to hide a wine fridge behind a
panel. Hidden is not the same as
ventilated. If the appliance wants
air, we leave air. A dead fridge
behind a beautiful door is a
warranty call I cannot help.

The sequence: pick the machines,
read the nameplates, tell the
electrician, then cut the boxes.
The other order is how you end up
with a 15-amp circuit and a 20-amp
cooktop and a week of silence.
