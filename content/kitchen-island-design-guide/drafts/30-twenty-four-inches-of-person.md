Twenty-four inches is not a generous seat.
It is the planning number that keeps two
people from eating off the same plate.

I have sat at 21-inch stations. You can do
it if you like the person and the meal is
short. You cannot do it for four adults
and a holiday. When a client asks for four
stools on a 7-foot edge, I divide. Eighty-
four inches of edge is 21 apiece if I
spend nothing on ends and nothing on a
sink. I tell them they have three seats,
or they have four skinny ones, and I make
them pick while we are still friends.

Stools with arms need more than 24. Stools
with a wide base need more than 24. A
backless stool can live at 24. Measure the
stool you will actually buy, not the ghost
stool in the rendering. I have had a
beautiful plan die because the client
fell in love with a 19-inch-wide seat
that had a 28-inch footprint at the floor.

End seats need a little air. If the
overhang stops at the end of the box, the
person on the end sits in a hallway. I
like to let the top run past the last
stool so a body can angle out. That past
is 3 to 6 inches, not a new waterfall
project.

Kids complicate the number in both
directions. They need less width and more
help getting up. A 30-inch table-height
stretch with real chairs may serve a
family better than four counter stools.
The island can still be the place. The
perch does not have to be.

Access seating wants 30 to 36 inches of
width and 17 to 25 of depth, with a knee
space around 36 wide, 27 high, 17 deep.
If someone in the house uses a mobility
aid, that station is not a nice extra. It
is the station. Put it at an end so the
approach is clean.

Bradley islands get stool marks on the
purchase order: count, width each, total
edge, leftover. If leftover is negative,
we are not ordering that count. I will
not let a family chat vote four stools
onto a three-stool edge after the boxes
are in production.
