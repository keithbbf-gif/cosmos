Stone is heavy in a way wood is not, and
islands concentrate that weight on four
corners and a couple of rails.

A 3-centimeter quartz or granite top on a
7-by-3 island is hundreds of pounds. Add a
waterfall end and you have a slab that wants
to peel the box over if you treat the end
like a decoration. Add a farm sink and you
have cut the box where it wanted a rail.
None of this is a surprise. All of it gets
forgotten when the elevation looks light.

What I want in a Bradley island that will
wear stone:

- A box that is not a row of legs with air
  between them unless we have a steel frame.
- Stretchers under the stone, especially
  under the seating fly.
- Plywood or furniture-board tops and bottoms
  at the thickness the line actually ships.
  Confirm the spec. Do not assume.
- Levelers that hit the floor we measured,
  not a hope.
- A waterfall tied back with a mechanical
  story, not only silicone.

Wood tops are kinder. They are not free. A
thick end-grain maple block is a solid
object. Two people should still plan the
lift. Clip it, do not glue it into a
forever.

Floors: an island in an old house may sit
on a bounce. Stone will tell you. If the
floor moves, I want a plywood spread or a
conversation with the carpenter before the
slab shows up. Cabinets can hide a dip.
Stone will pond a dip.

Delivery day is part of design. Measure the
turns. A waterfall that cannot make the
stair is a waterfall we should have split
or skipped. I have watched a crew stand in
a yard with a slab that had already lost.
The drawing was beautiful.

Appliances in the box steal structure. A
microwave drawer, a wine fridge, a
dishwasher in the island — each one is a
hole. Holes need frames. Frames need to be
on the order, not invented in the field
with scrap.

I write weight on the job like I write
aisles. If I cannot say where the pounds
go, the top is not chosen yet. Bradley
gives us a decent box. We still have to
use it like a box, not like a skirt.
