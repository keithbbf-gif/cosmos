Length is the friendly dimension. It is also where islands go
to get fat and useless in the middle.

Twenty-four inches of width per diner is the NKBA seating
number. Two stools want 48 inches of clean edge. Three want
72. Four want 96. That is before you put a sink or a cooktop
in the same edge. Appliances steal seats. A 96-inch island
with a prep sink in the seating side is not a four-seater.
It is a two-seater with a wet zone.

Prep wants length too. A primary prep stretch is 36 inches
wide and 24 deep, next to a sink if you have one. Landing
rules stack on top of that. If you try to give four diners
and a real prep zone and a sink, you are in 10-foot-island
country. Some rooms have that. Many rooms have a client who
thinks they do.

The dead middle is the failure mode of a long island. Past
about 8 feet, the center becomes a place where nothing
happens unless you put a job there: a second sink, a raised
eating tier, an overhead pot rack you will regret dusting.
A long empty top photographs well and works like a hallway
you are not allowed to walk on.

I break long Bradley islands into bays. Drawer stack, door
cabinet, seating knee wall, appliance garage. The bays keep
the carpenter honest and keep the homeowner from opening one
8-foot drawer that nobody built. Full-access drawers already
run wide. Use that. Do not specify a single cavern.

Corners of a long island are where hips and grocery bags
meet stone. If the traffic turns at an end, radius the top
or hold the stone back. A sharp waterfall on a circulation
corner is a chip waiting for a school backpack.

If the client wants four seats and the room only gives you
7 feet of leftover length, tell them they have three seats
or they have a table. Giving them four 18-inch stations is
how you get the Thanksgiving argument. I would rather lose
the argument in the showroom.
