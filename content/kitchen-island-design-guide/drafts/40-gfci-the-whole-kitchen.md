GFCI in the kitchen used to be a
treasure hunt. Six feet from a sink,
countertop only, measure the door,
argue about the fridge.

2023 widened 210.8(A)(6). The phrase
that limited protection to receptacles
serving countertops came out. The
direction of travel is: 125-volt through
250-volt receptacles in the kitchen
get GFCI, not only the pretty ones
at the backsplash. There is also
appliance-level GFCI language in
210.8(D) for a list of machines with
big metal skins. Your electrician
will know the current list. I want
the island included in that thinking,
not carved out because it is
“furniture.”

Nuisance trips are the fear. A fridge
on GFCI has a reputation. Some of
that reputation is old devices and
old refrigerators. Some of it is
real. I do not solve it by skipping
protection on an island circuit that
will see a kettle and wet hands. I
solve it by putting the fridge on
the conversation with the
electrician, not on a myth.

Test buttons exist so someone uses
them. I show the household where the
GFCI lives — at the device, or at
the breaker — and I tell them that a
dead island outlet is often a trip,
not a failure. This is boring. It
prevents a Saturday panic.

Damp stone, a wet wood sink rim, a
pop-up with a splash: these are why
the island is not a special innocent
zone. If anything on this box should
be protected, it is the thing a
person touches with wet hands while
barefoot on a winter floor.

Bradley islands do not ship electrical.
We ship a box with a chase if we
planned one. The protection story is
the house’s story. I still write
“GFCI per current kitchen rules” on
the dealer sheet so nobody thinks the
cabinet vendor is the code.

If the house is on an older edition,
do not take that as permission to be
sloppy. I spec protection as if the
next inspector were the one I like.
The one I like is the one who wants
people to go home after they boil
water.
