Finish is the contract.

Oil — mineral oil, a wiping oil, a hardwax oil if
we trust the product — stays in the family of a
cutting board. You can cut. You can sand a scar
and add oil after dinner. You will also add oil
when the top looks dry, which is more often in
year one. If that sounds like caring for a cast
iron, you will be fine. If that sounds like a
chore you will resent, do not buy this contract.

Film — conversion varnish, a good waterborne
topcoat, a factory finish on a Bradley-adjacent
wood top — is a jacket. Water beads. Stains sit
on top a little longer. You should not cut on it.
When it fails, it fails as a scratch in a skin,
and the repair is blending a coat, not wiping a
rag. Film is how I finish a walnut hosting island
that will see wine and homework, not a cleaver.

People want both: cut on it and never think about
it. That product is called stone, or it is called
a cutting board you pull from a drawer.

Food safety talk gets religious. A maintained oil
finish on a clean top is a normal kitchen surface.
A neglected oil finish that is black at the sink
rim is not. A film finish is easy to wipe and easy
to pretend is a cutting board. I would rather the
care card be blunt than poetic.

My pairing habits:

- Maple prep island: oil.
- Walnut serve island: film.
- Oak mixed-use: hardwax oil if they will maintain
  it, film if they will not.
- Any wood within a foot of a sink: film or a
  different material. I have oiled sink rims for
  people I trust.

Do not put a cutting-board oil on top of a film
and call it a refresh. You have made a smear. Do
not put wipe-on poly on an oiled top and call it
an upgrade unless you sand back to wood. The
contracts do not stack.

Write the finish on the dealer sheet next to the
species. “Oiled maple” and “filmed maple” are
different tops. They photograph similarly in the
first week. They do not live similarly. I want
the household to choose the life, not the photo.
