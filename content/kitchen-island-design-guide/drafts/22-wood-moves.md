A wood top is a tray of water that happens to be hard.

Across the grain it grows and shrinks with the house.
Along the grain it mostly stays. If you bolt it down
like a quartz slab — rigid, every hole, no slot —
you are asking the glue lines to be the movement
joint. Glue lines will take that job and then they
will split.

Figure-8 fasteners. Z-clips. Slotted brackets. A
wood shop has a favorite. I want a method that holds
the top down and lets it walk a little in the wide
direction. I want the screws in the box, not a
construction adhesive that turns the whole island
into one unhappy object.

Acclimate the top in the room. Not in the garage.
Not in the truck. Days, not hours. A winter top
dropped onto a humid summer job will move after
you leave, which is a mean trick to play on a
homeowner.

Seal all six sides. The underside is not a secret.
If you finish only the show face, the panel cups
toward the dry side. I have seen a beautiful walnut
top go shallow-bowl because someone saved a coat of
oil where nobody looks.

Sinks in wood are a special kind of optimism. If we
do it, the cutout gets sealed like a boat, the rim
is detailed so water cannot sit, and someone in the
house agrees to wipe the joint. Undermount in wood
is possible and fussy. A stainless top-mount with a
honest rim can be the better adult. Or put the sink
in stone and keep the wood for prep. That is the
mixed-top draft.

Heat vents, dishwashers, and a cooktop will locally
dry a top. Leave a little more room in the fasteners
near those appliances. Do not run a wood top tight
to a dishwasher steam plume and then blame the
species.

Bradley boxes have a top rail or a stretch of
cabinet top we can clip to. We do not screw through
a finished wood top from above unless we like plugs
that will telegraph later. The fastener is hidden.
The movement is not optional.

If a client wants the top “tight, like stone,” I
show them a photograph of a split glue line and we
pick stone. I will not fasten wood against its
nature to satisfy a sentence.
