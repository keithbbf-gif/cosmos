A prep island starts being an island at about 48 inches long and
24 inches deep. That is a landing you can flour. Below that you
have a cabinet in the way.

I will build smaller if a client understands they are buying a
staging perch: a place for the kettle, the mail, a bowl of
fruit. I will not call it a work island. Names keep us honest.

Add two seats and the floor changes. You want something closer
to 60 by 36 if the seating is on the long side. That 36 is not
luxury. It is 24 inches of box plus 12 to 15 inches of knee
space. People skip the knee space and then wonder why guests
sit like they are at a counter in a gas station.

Add a sink or a cooktop and you jump again. A useful minimum
with an appliance in the top is about 72 by 36, and that is
before you honor landing rules. A prep sink is not just the
cutout. It wants 24 inches of landing on one side and 18 on
the other, same height, top at least 16 in. deep. A cooktop wants
12 and 15 beside it and 9 behind if the burners are at the
same height as the rest of the top. Those landings eat length
faster than clients expect.

Bradley boxes help here because we can shrink a cabinet in
width without inventing a filler story. That does not create
space that does not exist. It just means the leftover inches
go into the aisle or into a drawer that still opens.

A rolling cart can be 24 by 36 and be the right object. It
leaves. It does not own the triangle. If the room is trying to
tell you it wants a cart, listen. A tiny fixed island is a
permanent apology.

My shop rule: if I cannot set down a half-sheet pan and a
cutting board without stacking them, the island is too small
for prep. If I cannot do that and also open the trash, it is
too small for prep with a sink. Measure with the objects, not
with the catalog.
