For a long stretch of the 20th century, the island was a peninsula
and nobody was embarrassed about it.

Postwar American kitchens were U-shapes and L-shapes punched into
small footprints. A peninsula gave you a second run of counter and
a place to eat without asking the room to grow four aisles. One
end tied into the wall or the cabinet run. You saved a walkway.
You also saved the marriage. Two people could pass if they were
paying attention.

The Frankfurt kitchen of 1926 — the one that still gets cited in
design school — was a galley. Tight, fitted, no monument in the
middle. Efficiency was the religion. The island as a status object
had to wait for houses that could waste a little floor.

What the peninsula taught, and what we keep forgetting:

- You only need three sides of clearance if one side is a wall.
- Seating on the living-room side works because the fourth aisle
  was never promised.
- Plumbing and power already live in the wall you tied into. An
  island has to invent a path through the slab or the crawl.

I still draw peninsulas. Not because I am cheap. Because a 12-by-12
kitchen with a 4-by-7 island is a kitchen where nobody opens a
dishwasher without asking permission. The peninsula keeps the extra
counter and gives the extra floor back.

When a client says “we have to have an island,” I ask what job they
are buying. If the job is seating that faces the couch, a peninsula
does that job and keeps a 42-inch work aisle. If the job is walking
around a roast, then we are in island country and the room has to
prove it.

Power is the other quiet argument for a peninsula. The wall you
tie into already carries cable. An island asks for a chase through
a slab or a joist bay you have not opened yet. I have killed more
islands on a plumber’s face than on a tape. If the waste line is
a fantasy, the peninsula keeps the second sink without a movie
about concrete.

Bradley boxes build both. The difference is a wall and an honest
conversation about aisles. Do not let a floor-plan fashion talk
you out of the piece that fits. If a client still wants the word
“island” on the resale sheet, write “peninsula seating” and move
on. Agents can translate. Cooks cannot cook in a caption.
