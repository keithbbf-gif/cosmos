If you skip the island receptacle under
2023, you do not skip the future.

The code wants a way to add an outlet
later without opening the house like a
fish. In practice that means a wiring
method — conduit, raceway, or cable —
run to the island and landed in a
junction box with a cover. The inspector
will tell you what they want to see.
The electrician will tell you what they
want to pull. I want it on the drawing
before the slab is poured or the floor
is closed.

This is the part remodelers skip because
the island “doesn’t need power.” Then
year three arrives with a kettle, a
laptop, and a holiday blender. Someone
runs a cord from the wall across the
aisle. That cord is the accident the
2023 change was trying to reduce.

Leave the path even if you think you
will never use it. A junction box in
the toe or in a drawer box (listed,
accessible, not a junk drawer full of
batteries) is uglier than a rendering
and prettier than a sawzall through
finished oak.

New slabs: this is cheap. A conduit
stub under the island footprint is a
conversation that lasts ten minutes
and saves a day later. Existing slabs:
we core, we go through a crawl, or we
come down from a ceiling and up a
leg. Each path is a design. A
waterfall end can hide a chase if we
say so before the stone is cut.

Bradley boxes need a bay that is not
only drawers. A chase cabinet, a false
panel, a tall back on a two-tier —
those are where the future lives. If
every inch is a dovetail drawer, we
have nowhere to be honest.

Write “future island receptacle
provision per 210.52(C)(2)” on the
electrical sheet when you omit the
outlet. If the house is not on 2023,
write the edition you are on and
still leave a path. Editions change.
Kettles do not get lighter.

I treat the provision like a plumbing
cleanout. Nobody photographs it.
Everybody is grateful when it exists.
