I keep a private list. Not names. Shapes.

The 1998 granite rectangle, 4 by 8, dropped into a kitchen that
had been a comfortable U. Aisles went to 32 inches. The couple
who cooked together stopped cooking together. They told friends
the island “changed the way we live,” which was true.

The cooktop island with stools on the same side as the controls.
A guest reached for the salt and turned a burner with a forearm.
Nobody was badly hurt. The stools moved to the basement.

The butcher-block beauty fastened down with rigid screws through
every hole. First heating season it cracked along a glue line
you could hear from the hall. The shop had told them wood moves.
They wanted it tight like stone. Wood is not stone.

The island with six outlets on the show side, 1999 NEC energy,
cords draping toward the walkway. A toddler pulled a kettle. The
2023 code change that made side outlets illegal as counter
service is not an aesthetic tantrum. It is a hospital-visit
count.

The seating overhang of 8 inches on a 36-inch top, sold as
“casual dining for four.” People sat sideways. They stopped
sitting. The overhang is still there, a little lip that does
nothing.

The waterfall-end island in a room with a 90-degree turn from
the pantry. Every grocery run clipped the stone. They put a
felt pad on the corner. It looked like a confession.

Failures share a pattern. Someone protected the photograph and
spent the aisle, the structure, or the truth. A Bradley island
fails the same way if we let it. Full-access drawers will not
save a 30-inch walkway. Soft-close hinges will not save a
cooktop in a diner’s ribs.

The mixed-top that was caulked like a tub. Dough found the
joint. Then water. Then a dark line the household blamed on
the wood species. The species was fine. The joint was a shrug.

When a plan feels famous, I walk the failure list. If I can
match the plan to a ghost, we redraw. That is the whole method.
The history that matters is the history of rooms that did not
work, told while there is still time to keep this room off the
list. Keep your own list. Date it. The next island you draw
will try to join it.
