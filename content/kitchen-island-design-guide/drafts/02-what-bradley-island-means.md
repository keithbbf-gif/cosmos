When I say Bradley island I do not mean a rolling cart from a
warehouse aisle, and I do not mean a European furniture piece that
arrives in three crates with an Allen key. I mean a run of full-access
cabinet boxes, built in the U.S. or Canada, sized to the room, set
on a finished floor, and capped with a top we chose on purpose.

Full-access is the old “frameless” story told without the accent.
There is no face frame eating an inch of every opening. Drawers go
wider. You feel it when you put a mixer away without turning it
sideways. Dealers who carry this line will tell you the same three
facts because they are the ones that survive a punch list:

- Size modifications are normal, not a change-order drama. Height,
  width, and depth can come down to fit a tight aisle or a low soffit.
- The upgrades people used to pay extra for — dovetail drawers,
  soft-close hardware, a thicker box — are the baseline, not a menu.
- The finish is a shop finish. You do not field-paint an island
  unless you like living with the difference.

None of that makes the island good. A full-access box in a 36-inch
aisle is still a bad island. The construction just means that once
the proportions are right, the storage and the doors will not be the
thing you regret.

A Bradley island is also a dealer object. You do not configure it
alone at midnight. Someone stands in the kitchen with a tape, marks
the dishwasher door swing, and argues with you about whether four
stools will fit. That argument is part of the product. If a drawing
arrives with four stools and a 30-inch walkway behind them, the
drawing is not finished.

What a Bradley island is not:

- A furniture island on bun feet that you can drag when you mop.
  Those exist. They are useful. They are a different sentence.
- A waterfall-everything monument. We can do a waterfall end. We
  should be able to say why.
- A second kitchen pretending to be a table. If you want a cooktop,
  a sink, a dishwasher, and four diners, you need a room that can
  take all four. Most rooms can take two.

I will keep saying “the box” in these drafts. The box is the cabinet
carcass: ends, top, bottom, shelves, the part that carries the stone
and hides the wiring path. Get the box right and the top has a
chance. Get the box wrong and you are choosing a prettier way to
fail.

Confirm the current collection book with the dealer before you quote
a finish or a door style. Collections move. The aisle does not.
