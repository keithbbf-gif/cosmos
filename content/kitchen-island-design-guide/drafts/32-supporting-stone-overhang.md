Stone does not bounce to warn you. It
cracks.

Fabricators have rules of thumb and they
do not all match. Mine, until the slab
and the shop say otherwise:

- Up to about 8 to 10 inches of 3-cm
  stone: often self-supporting.
- Around 12 inches: ask. Many shops want
  a hidden bracket.
- 15 inches: steel, corbels, or a
  dropped panel. Not a vibe.
- Past 15: we are building a table
  structure under a slab. Admit it.

Brackets should sit inside the knee
space without stealing it. A 2-inch
steel plate along the underside, set
back so thighs miss it, is the usual
adult. Corbels can be honest if the
house wants them. Decorative corbels
that start 8 inches down from the top
are shin-knockers wearing costume.

Waterfall ends are not seating support
unless the diner is at the end. A
waterfall on the short side does nothing
for a 15-inch fly on the long side. I
have been told otherwise by a rendering.
The rendering was not a beam.

Seams in an overhang are how you write
a warranty claim in advance. Keep seams
over the box. If the island is long
enough to need a seam, put it at a sink
or over a rail, not in the last foot of
a fly.

Adhesive is not structure. Silicone
holds a splash. It does not hold a
teenager leaning on a 16-inch granite
lip.

Bradley boxes give us a face to lag
steel into. I want wood or a plate
behind the finished end, not a screw
into a 3/4-inch panel and a prayer.
If the seating side is a finished
furniture back, we plan blocking
before the box is ordered. Field
blocking is how you miss a drawer.

Ask the fabricator to put the support
on the drawing. If it is not on the
drawing, it will not be in the truck.
I do not install stone on a promise.

Rodding — steel in a kerf on the
underside — helps some overhangs and
gives people a false peace on others.
It is not a license to skip brackets
on a 15-inch fly. Ask what the shop
will warranty. If they hedge, we add
steel you can see on a drawing. A
warranty that lives in a shrug is
not a support spec.
