I have sat at more islands that do not work than I have at ones that do.
That is not a flex. It is the reason this folder exists.

A kitchen island is the most expensive piece of furniture most people
will ever put in the middle of a room, and it is the piece they decide
on from a photograph. The photograph does not show the dishwasher door
hitting a stool. It does not show the 34-inch aisle that turns two
cooks into one cook and a person standing in the hall. It does not
show the granite overhang that rings like a bell when a kid leans on
it, because nobody put steel under the stone.

Bradley islands are not a catalog SKU. They are cabinet boxes, sized
to the inch, with a top you choose and a power story you finish with
an electrician. This guide is the conversation we should have before
anyone draws a pretty rectangle in the middle of a floor plan.

You will find numbers. Most of them come from the National Kitchen
and Bath Association planning guidelines, which are good practice and
not law, and from the National Electrical Code edition your town has
adopted, which is law until the inspector says otherwise. Where I
give a shop number — how far a 1.5-inch maple top can fly before it
needs a bracket — I will say it is a shop number. Do not treat a shop
number like a stamp on a permit.

You will also find refusals. Some rooms should not have an island.
Saying that early is cheaper than saying it after the boxes are on a
truck.

Read this like a person talking, because that is what it is. If a
sentence sounds like a brochure, it is wrong. Mark it. We will cut it
on the next pass.

The five fights this guide is built to win:

1. History, so you stop treating the island as a 2014 Pinterest
   object and start treating it as a worktable that learned to host.
2. Proportions, so the aisle wins over the photograph.
3. Butcher block versus stone, said as a trade-off, not a team sport.
4. Seating overhang, which is where comfort and structure meet and
   usually lose.
5. Electrical, because the 2023 NEC changed the island outlet story
   and a lot of drawings have not caught up.

If you only have twenty minutes, skip to the sequence draft and the
dealer spec sheet. If you have an afternoon, start here and keep
walking. The island will still be in the middle of the room in ten
years. It should earn the floor it occupies.
