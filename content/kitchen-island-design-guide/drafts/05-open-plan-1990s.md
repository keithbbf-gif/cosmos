The island became furniture when we knocked the wall down.

By the 1990s the great room had won. Kitchen, dining, and the TV
were one volume, and something had to hold the middle so the cook
was not facing a blank. The island did that work. It was a counter,
a bar, a homework desk, and a place to set down grocery bags. It
was also the object you saw from the front door, which is how it
picked up the granite, the turned legs, and the pot filler that
never got used.

That era taught two useful things and one bad habit.

Useful: the island is a social piece. People will stand at it with
a drink while you cook. Kids will do math on it. You should design
for elbows and backpacks, not only for a work triangle drawn on
trace.

Useful: once the wall is gone, the island is the only thing giving
the kitchen an edge. Without it the cook is in the living room.
A 36-inch-high mass, even a modest one, makes a room.

Bad habit: we started sizing islands to the photograph of the room,
not to the walking. Magazines loved a 10-foot slab. Houses loved
copying the slab into a room that had 9 feet of leftover floor.
The leftover floor is where the aisle used to be.

Open plan also changed the electrical and the light. Pendants
became a fashion, then a requirement in the client’s head. Outlets
hid on the side of the box so the stone could stay clean. That
side outlet is now a code fight — more on that in the electrical
drafts — but the instinct was visual. Hide the ugly. Show the
stone.

A Bradley island in an open room has to do furniture work. The
ends show. The back of the box faces the sofa. You do not get to
put an unfinished side toward the people you love. Specify the
finished end panels. Specify the seating side as a piece of
furniture, not as the back of a cabinet run. If the collection
can take a furniture base or a thicker end, use it. The 1990s
got one thing right: people will look at this thing more than
they look at the sink.
