Not every pretty board wants to be an island.

Hard maple is the shop default for a reason. Tight grain,
hard, pale, takes abuse, takes a resurfacing. It is the
wood butcher blocks were made of when the word still
meant work. It shows stains if you let lemon sit, and it
will amber a little. Clients who want “white forever”
should buy painted boxes and a stone top, not maple.

Walnut is the one people point at. Chocolate, expensive,
softer than maple, gorgeous next to painted Bradley
doors. It dents easier. It is a seating-and-serving wood
more than a daily chopping wood, unless the household is
gentle or proud of dents. I like walnut on a film finish
when the island is furniture. I like it oiled when the
client already talks like a wood person.

White oak is the current friend of the modern kitchen.
Ray fleck if you quartersaw it, tough, a little more
movement than maple. It pairs with the oak floors
everyone already has. It can look muddy if you stain it
on top of an already-warm floor. Samples in the room,
not in the showroom.

Teak and other oily exotics show up in outdoor-kitchen
talk. For an indoor Bradley island I ask why. If the
answer is water, we should talk about the sink detail
and maybe about stone. If the answer is the look, we
can get the look closer to home.

Cherry is a heartbreak wood. Beautiful, soft, it will
go red-brown in the sun while the underside of the
fruit bowl stays pale. I use it when the client wants
that story. I do not use it when they want control.

Soft woods — pine, fir, a “farm table” look — do not
earn a working island. They earn a table you can leave
to your kids. Different object.

I ask three questions before I lock a species:

1. Will anyone cut on this without a board?
2. Is there a sink or a pot-filler on this top?
3. Do you want year-one color or year-ten color?

Maple / oil / cut: yes. Walnut / film / host: yes.
Oak / oil / mix: often. Cherry / oil / sunny bay:
only if we all smile about the tan. Everything else
has to interview.

Match the box. A walnut top on walnut doors can go
monochrome and dead. A maple top on a painted Bradley
run is the combination I sell most, because the work
surface reads as work and the doors read as furniture.
That split is useful. It tells guests where to put
the wine and where to put the knife.
