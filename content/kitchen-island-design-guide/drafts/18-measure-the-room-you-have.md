Drawings lie in small, friendly ways. The room does not.

I measure finished floor to finished wall, then I measure
again after the plasterer has added an eighth and the
cabinet setter has found the hump. Islands land on the
floor that exists. A 1/4-inch twist in an 8-foot run is
enough to make a stone overhang see-saw.

What I want on the pad:

1. Overall walls, openings, and the swing of every door
   that can hit a stool or a hip.
2. Existing wall-cabinet depth and height, because the
   island will be judged against them every day.
3. Window stools and mullions, if pendants will line up
   with anything a person can see from the living room.
4. Slab or joist direction, and a guess at where a
   plumber and an electrician can travel. Guess is the
   honest word until someone opens a hole.
5. The high and low of the floor in the island footprint.
   Levelers can eat a little. They cannot eat a ramp.

Tape the island. Blue tape on the floor, both the box and
the top overhang. Then tape the stool footprints. Then
open the existing fridge with the tape down. I have lost
arguments to tape that I was winning on paper. Good. The
tape is cheaper than the stone.

Measure the people too. Not in a creepy way. In a “how
tall is the person who will chop here, and do they use a
wheelchair on Tuesdays” way. NKBA access numbers exist
for a reason: 60-inch turning, 27-inch-high knee space,
reaches that do not assume a 6-foot arm. If someone in
the house sits to work, the island can be the place we
honor that, not the place we ignore it.

Bradley size mods are only useful if the numbers we send
are the room’s numbers. I would rather send a 1/2-inch
reduction that we measured than a pretty 3-inch reduction
we invented so the plan looked balanced.

Bring the tape to the meeting. If a number on the plan
cannot be walked to a wall and touched, it is not a
number yet. We do not order boxes from a not-yet.
