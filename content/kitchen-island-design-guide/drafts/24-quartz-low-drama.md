Quartz is the stone I spec when the household wants
low maintenance and a surface that stays predictable.

It is not stone in the quarry sense. It is stone in
the kitchen sense: a slab, cold, heavy, seamed by a
fabricator, wiped with a cloth. The binder is resin.
That binder is why quartz is less porous than granite
and why it is more particular about heat and UV than
people expect.

What it gets right on a Bradley island:

- No sealing ritual.
- Color that matches the sample closer than natural
  stone will.
- A seam you can hide if the fabricator is good and
  the color is busy, or a seam you will see if the
  color is a white nothing.
- A seating overhang that behaves if we put steel
  in it.

What it gets wrong:

- A hot roasting pan can leave a mark. Trivets are
  not optional just because the brand name sounds
  like a laboratory.
- Direct sun on a dark slab can fade. Islands in
  a south window are not the place I put a cheap
  black quartz and forget.
- It feels like what it is. Some people miss the
  life of granite or wood and cannot say why. I
  ask them to touch a sample for a full minute,
  not a second.

Knives lose on quartz. Always. If the island is
the prep zone, I still want a board. At that
point the quartz is a wipeable field around the
board, which is a good job. Do not sell quartz
as a cutting surface. Sell it as a clean field.

Seams on an island are a design choice. I would
rather place a seam at a sink or at a logical
break than run a 10-foot white slab and pray.
Prayer is not a fabrication method.

Weight: plan it. A 3-centimeter island top is a
couple of hundred pounds before cutouts. The
Bradley box needs rails, and the floor needs to
be the floor we measured. Two people and a
suction cup are not a plan for a waterfall.

I like quartz on islands that host, do homework,
and see the Sunday paper. I like it less on
islands that want to be a butcher shop. That
island wants wood, or it wants a wood insert
in the quartz, which is a mixed-top and a
conversation about crumbs in the joint.
