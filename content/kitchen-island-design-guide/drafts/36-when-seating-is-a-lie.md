Seating is the first thing a client
asks for and the first thing a small
room should lose.

A lie looks like this:

- 8 to 10 inches of overhang at 36
  inches high, sold as two seats.
- Four stools drawn on an edge that
  is 72 inches including a sink.
- A walkway behind the stools that
  is 30 inches to a pantry door.
- Stone flying 16 inches with no
  steel on the shop drawing.
- Stools on the cooktop side.
- A “seat” at the end of the island
  in the work aisle.

People will sit on a lie once. They
will not sit on it in year two. The
stools become coat racks. The
overhang remains, a little hat brim
on the cabinet, reminding everyone
of the story.

I would rather specify zero seats
and a 24-deep prep island that
works than two fake seats that
make the aisle mean. A table two
steps away will do the meal. The
island can do the work.

If we must have one honest seat —
a perch for a kid with homework,
a place to put on a shoe — I will
build one 24-inch station with a
15-inch fly and a 32-inch back-up,
and I will not draw a second stool
“for symmetry.” Symmetry is not a
person.

Bradley dealers get pressure to
match the neighbor’s four-stool
photograph. Bring the tape. Sit in
the showroom island, then sit in
the taped kitchen. If the client
still wants the lie after both,
I write it down as their choice
and I still will not put stools on
the cooktop side. There is a limit
to what I will sign.

The test: would you serve soup
there to someone you like? If the
answer is a joke, it is not
seating. Take it off the plan
while the eraser is cheap.

A second test: leave the stools in
the aisle for a week of taped
living, if the room is empty enough.
If the household starts parking them
in a bedroom “just until the island
comes,” you have your answer. Seating
that cannot live in the room before
the island arrives will not live
there after. Believe the parking
spot, not the rendering.
