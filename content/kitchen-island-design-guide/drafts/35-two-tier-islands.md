A two-tier island is a 36-inch worktop
and a 42-inch wall that hides the
mess. It is also a crumb trap, a
second fabrication, and a place
where glasses knock.

I still build them. They earn it when
the living room looks straight into
the prep and the household wants a
little dignity, or when the room is
tight and 12 inches of bar overhang
recovers depth we cannot give at 36.

What I insist on:

- The upper tier is wide enough to be
  a plate zone, not a shelf you
  apologize for. Eight inches of
  upper is a ledge. Ten to twelve
  starts to be a bar.
- The step between tiers is detailed
  so a sponge can live there. A
  square inside corner of two stones
  will collect paste. A small radius
  or a metal bead is cleaner.
- Support for the upper fly is
  planned. A 42-inch top does not
  get to cheat just because it is
  thinner in the rendering.
- Power: if the code path wants a
  receptacle serving the work
  surface, the upper tier can host
  an on-or-above outlet within 20 in.
  above the work surface (210.52(C)(3)).
  That is one of the
  few remaining clean places after
  2023. Use it on purpose, not as
  an accident.

What I resist: a tiny 42-inch splash
that does not hide anything and
steals prep depth. If the mess-hiding
is the job, commit. If it is not,
stay one height and buy a tray.

Wood below and stone above works.
Stone below and wood above is
stranger — you lose the cutting
surface and keep the maintenance.
I will do wood above if the upper
is a serving rail we do not wet.

Bradley boxes for two-tier want a
tall seating back or a second box
on top of the first. I prefer a
designed tall back with blocking
for the upper slab. Stacked boxes
can look like we forgot. They can
also look like furniture if the
reveals are intended.

Sit at a two-tier before you buy
one. Your arms change. Conversation
with the cook changes. Some people
feel shut out. That can be the
point. Say it.
