Depth is the measurement that makes people lie to themselves.

A working box is 24 inches deep. The top hangs a little past
that — an inch, give or take, on the cook side — so you are
in the 25-to-26 range before anyone sits down. That is a
prep island. It is enough.

Add seating on the far side and you add the overhang. At 36
inches high, plan 15 inches of knee space. Twenty-four plus
fifteen is thirty-nine, plus the cook-side top hang, and you
are looking at a top around 40 to 42 inches deep. That is a
normal, comfortable, one-sided seating island. It is also
deeper than the 36-inch “standard” people recite from memory.

Clients will ask for a 36-deep island with seating. What they
get, if you obey them, is a 24-deep box and a 12-inch
overhang. Twelve can work at bar height. At counter height it
is tight. Tall people sit on the edge of the stool and look
like they are waiting for a bus.

Two-sided seating is how you invent a 5-foot-deep island.
Do not. If you need people on both long sides you need a
table. An island that thick becomes a continent. The triangle
has to walk around a continent.

A cooktop in a seating island wants even more depth because
of the 9-inch landing behind the burners. That 9 is safety,
not style. It keeps a diner from setting a sweater on a
grate. Add 9 behind, 24 of box, 15 of knees, and you are
past 48 inches of top. Measure the room again. Most rooms
say no.

Bradley full-access boxes can be reduced in depth. That is
useful when the aisle is the fight and seating is not. It is
a bad way to “make seating fit.” Shrinking the box to 18
deep and flying a 15-inch stone overhang is how you get a
top that needs a steel armature and a client who still
thinks they saved space.

Write depth as two numbers: box depth, and top depth. If
those numbers are more than an inch apart on the cook side
and more than the planned overhang on the seating side,
someone did the math in their head. Do it on paper.
