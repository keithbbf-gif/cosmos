**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not medical advice, not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Virginia Satir was the Mental Research Institute's first training director. That sentence should travel farther than the airport-book Satir who seems to have been born in a circle of open palms. She came out of social work and the Chicago tradition, then California. She could run a room. She could also write a manual that a 1964 supervisor could assign.

*Conjoint Family Therapy* (Science and Behavior Books, 1964) is a training object. It talks about interviews, about seeing more than one person at a time, about a therapist who uses the self. *Peoplemaking* (1972) is a public book. It taught a generation to look at stances — blaming, placating, computing, distracting — and at the way a family breathes when someone tells the truth. *The New Peoplemaking* (1988) is a late revision. Confusing these classes is how a brochure writes "Satir" as if it were a feeling.

She was experiential, sometimes theatrical, and allergic to the idea that a child should be "the problem" on the intake form. Theater is a method and a risk. A sculpture in a hotel ballroom can make a hidden coalition visible. It can also make a family perform their pain for strangers. This pack will describe the public workshops as a workforce event. It will not hand you a sculpture prompt.

Satir's warmth was a politics. In a field of men with theories of schizophrenia and strategies of paradox, she talked about self-worth and communication as if those were not soft words. Later feminist writers would both claim her and argue with her — claim the attention to voice, argue with any implication that better communication dissolves a hierarchy. The Women's Project (stage 5) is one later room. Satir is not that room. She is an earlier woman whose fame the later room had to live next to.

MRI's research culture and Satir's training culture were never the same object, even when they shared a building. Jackson wanted interactional rules. Haley wanted strategy. Satir wanted a person to show up in the session as a person. The split is ordinary. Institutes split. What is less ordinary is how completely the public memory kept Satir and filed Haley under a different brand. They had been colleagues. Colleagues disagree. Disagreement is not a scandal. It is a 1960s payroll.

Avanta and the later Satir Global network turned a clinician into a curriculum. Curricula outlive founders. They also smooth them. A historically literate reader keeps 1964 next to 1972 and refuses to let 1972 talk over the training book. The training book is dryer. Dryness is sometimes the more ethical object, because it is harder to turn into a weekend.

No workshop stills. No generated "earth mother therapist." Type: Virginia Satir, 1916–1988. Commons, as last opened, does not hand us a clean studio still. Graves are not thumbnails.

A small-city hour that still asks who is allowed to speak, and whether a child's silence is a loyalty, is standing in a hallway Satir helped furnish. Asking is not a Satir protocol. This page will not teach "parts" or "stances" as homework. It will say the public book made those words ordinary, and ordinary words still need a clinician who knows you.

She died in 1988. The new edition of the public book is the same year. Late books are often a founder trying to outrun the imitators. Imitators are part of the history. So is the social-work door she walked through, which this pack will not let a California workshop erase.

## Training book, public book, curriculum

Assign *Conjoint Family Therapy* in a 1965 supervision and you have a manual. Sell *Peoplemaking* in an airport and you have a public. Build Avanta after 1988 and you have a curriculum. Three social classes. A brochure that writes one word — Satir — over all three has already failed the title pages.

The stances she named (blaming, placating, computing, distracting) escaped into trainings that never opened 1964. Escape is a historical force. This page will not reprint the stances as a quiz. Quizzes are worksheets.

Feminist readers later wanted more than warmth. They wanted a hierarchy named. Satir is not the Women's Project. She is the earlier woman whose fame the Project had to live next to. Living next to a fame is a historical job. This pack gives that job a sentence instead of a smear or a halo.
