Pauline Boss is living at last verification (2026-09-14). Public books only. No campus photographs. No health speculation.

*Ambiguous Loss* (Harvard University Press, 1999) named a kind of grief the older family-therapy diagrams only partly knew what to do with: a person physically absent and psychologically present, or physically present and psychologically absent. Missing soldiers, missing migrants, dementia, a divorce that does not finish — the book gathers those as a single clinical object. Gathering is a historical act. It can help a household feel less mad. It can also become a label that travels without the book's caution.

This pack will not teach you to "treat" ambiguous loss. It will say a 1999 Harvard book made a phrase ordinary, and ordinary phrases still need a clinician who knows you. Disaster-response and military-family afterlives of the phrase are later political events. Afterlives are sloppy. The 1999 book is the object.

Boss wrote from a Minnesota and later California teaching life, and from a research interest in families under unclear absence. Unclear absence is not a synonym for every sad household. Stretching a term until it covers everything is how a good 1999 sentence becomes a 2026 slide. This page prefers the sentence.
