**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not medical advice, not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Michael White and David Epston published *Narrative Means to Therapeutic Ends* with Norton in 1990. The book is an Adelaide–Auckland object that the North Atlantic field had to import. Importing is not the same as inventing. American syllabi that start narrative therapy in a California weekend have already lost the map.

White had worked in Adelaide, including in a psychiatric hospital culture he later argued with. Epston's Auckland practice, letter-writing, and curiosity about documents as therapeutic objects are on the title page for a reason. This pack will not collapse Epston into a side credit. He is living (verified 2026). Public books only. No gossip. No scrape of a workshop still.

"Externalizing" is the word that escaped. The 1990 book treats a problem as something a person can be in a relationship with, rather than something a person *is*. That sentence has been useful for children who arrived already named as the trouble. It has also become a cartoon: "How is the worry bullying you?" printed on a handout. This page will not print the handout. Cartoons are afterlives.

White died in 2008. *Maps of Narrative Practice* (Norton, 2007) is a late teaching book. Late teaching books are how a school tries to survive its founder. Dulwich Centre in Adelaide is the shop. Shops have websites and photographs this pack will not steal.

Narrative work argued with structural and strategic certainty. It liked documents, audiences, and a politics of whose story gets to be official. That politics can be a justice practice. It can also become a style that never risks a direct sentence. Both possibilities are in the historical record. This pack will not pick a winner.

No Dulwich scrapes. No externalizing worksheet. Type: White and Epston, 1990.

A small-city clinician who still wonders whose description of a child has become the child's name is near this hallway. Wondering is not a narrative protocol. This series does not publish treatment protocols.

The Southern Hemisphere origin is not a flavor. It is a reminder that American family therapy is not the only mailbox. Milan had already said so in Italian. Adelaide said so in a Norton book the Americans could order without learning a city. Ordering is easy. Keeping the city in the caption is the work.

## Documents as objects

Epston's interest in letters and documents is on the 1990 title page for a reason. A letter a clinician writes to a household is a historical technology with a consent problem. This page will not teach you to write one. It will say 1990 made documents thinkable as therapeutic objects, and thinkable is not the same as free.

White's hospital years in Adelaide are the under-told door. Hospital cultures he later argued with are part of why narrative work sounds like a politics of naming. Politics of naming can serve a child. They can also become a style. Both possibilities stay in the record.

Dulwich Centre is a shop with a website this pack will not scrape. Shops have photographs. Photographs of living colleagues are not ours. Type: 1990, two cities, two names.

## Outsider witnesses as a later object

White's later teaching about outsider-witness practices is a 2000s object this writer will not turn into a guest-list how-to. Practices that need a community are not a blog. 2007 *Maps* is a late book trying to leave a map without a cult. Whether it succeeded is a shop argument. This URL is not the shop.

Adelaide remains in the caption. California weekends remain a misfile. Misfiles are how North Atlantic syllabi work. This pack exists to unfile.

## Letter as a technology with a consent problem

A clinical letter can honor a household. It can also travel into a file a court later reads. 1990 made letters thinkable. Thinkable is not free. This series will not teach a letter. It will keep Adelaide and Auckland in the caption so a California weekend cannot steal the map.

Epston living (2026): public books only. White dead 2008: type, 1948–2008. Two rules, one title page.
