**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not medical advice, not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

A claim about family therapy is usually four objects pretending to be one. The first is a **paper**: year, journal, authors, a sentence that later escaped. The second is a **book** a hospital could order. The third is a **workforce** — a license, a conference, a one-way mirror with a supervisor behind it. The fourth is a **headline** that has already decided the household is the patient.

This page is a reading method. It is not a method for your relatives.

Start with the paper. Bateson, Jackson, Haley, and Weakland, "Toward a Theory of Schizophrenia," *Behavioral Science* 1, no. 4 (1956): 251–264. Those numbers are the claim. "The family causes schizophrenia" is not those numbers. It is what happened to those numbers in a decade that already liked to prosecute mothers. A historically literate reader keeps the pagination and the later walk-back on the same table. The walk-back is part of the history. So is the damage the slogan did before anyone walked it back.

Then the book. *Families and Family Therapy* is Harvard University Press, 1974. *Conjoint Family Therapy* is Science and Behavior Books, 1964. *Family Therapy in Clinical Practice* is Jason Aronson, 1978. *Invisible Loyalties* is Harper & Row, 1973. *Narrative Means to Therapeutic Ends* is Norton, 1990. These are different social classes of object. A colleague's volume, a training shop's paperback, a public "peoplemaking" book, a Guilford life-cycle anthology — they do not weigh the same. A brochure that writes "Minuchin" as if it were one pamphlet has already failed the reading.

Then the workforce. Who paid for the room? Rockefeller and NIMH money sat under Palo Alto. A public-health monograph (Bell, 1961) sat under an early conjoint hour. Wiltwyck was a school for boys the analytic institutes of the same cities did not see. Philadelphia Child Guidance was a clinic with a later national reputation. AAMFT is a successor name to a 1942 marriage-counselors' association. AFTA dates itself to 1977. A license is a political-administrative object. It is not a finding about your Tuesday.

Then the headline. "Family therapy works." Works for whom, compared with what, for how long, in which room, paid by whom? A 1978 feminist essay in *Family Process* (Hare-Mustin) is a claim about gender inside the field, not a press release about outcomes. Virginia Goldner's 1988 essay on generation and gender is another. If a website flattens those into "we take a systems approach," it has used a journal as wallpaper.

## Four questions that keep a sentence honest

**Who is the unit?** A child-guidance clinic in 1924 invited a mother into an hour that was still about the child. Bowen admitted a mother and an adult daughter onto a ward in 1954 and later talked as if the unit were a multigenerational system. Minuchin moved the chairs so that a parent and a child had to speak while the room watched. Milwaukee asked what was already working and sometimes left the family-of-origin story on the floor. "Systems" is not one unit. Ask which one the sentence is smuggling.

**Who is being blamed?** If the sentence cannot be read without a mother becoming the villain, it is standing in 1948 whether it says so or not. Lidz's "marital schism and skew," Wynne's 1958 "pseudo-mutuality," Fromm-Reichmann's adjective — these are dated objects. A contemporary clinician may still read them as warnings. This pack will not reissue them as science.

**What is the glass doing?** Live supervision, a team, a tape, a one-way mirror: these are technologies of seeing. They have a consent history. Andersen's 1987 paper turned the seeing around. Boyd-Franklin's 1989 book asked who was allowed to be seen. A claim that does not mention the glass is often a claim that has forgotten power.

**Is this a protocol leaking?** If you can do the paragraph at the kitchen table, the paragraph has left history. A genogram printed as a blank form is a worksheet. A miracle question printed as a prompt is a worksheet. A circular-question list is a worksheet. This series names those objects. It does not hand them over.

## What "evidence" meant in these rooms

Family therapy did not grow up inside the same trial culture that later made Beck's 1979 manual easy to graph. Some later schools wrote manuals and sat for trials. Some earlier rooms treated a tape and a live audience as evidence enough. Neither fact is a license to invent a victory. When this pack mentions a study, it will try to keep year, journal, and comparison in the sentence. When the trail is thin, the sentence will say the trail is thin.

Managed care later asked family therapists for six sessions and a countable object. That request is a historical event. It is not proof that a 1974 structural hour "does not work." It is proof that a payer wanted a different object.

## What a small-city reader can do with this

You can notice when a sentence has changed jobs. "Bowen described triangles in the 1950s" is history. "You are in a triangle" is a clinical move, and it is not this page's move. "Minuchin worked at Wiltwyck" is history. "Let me unbalance your family" is a clinical move. The difference is the only literacy this folder is selling.

WOW Therapies does not, by publishing that difference, claim a family-therapy service line. The sister pack's survey essay (`families-systems-therapy`) already warned that a theory of families can injure as well as help. This page is the warning's method: keep the paper, the book, the workforce, and the headline from collapsing into one warm word.

If a later draft in this folder fails the method, the draft is wrong. The method is the fence.
