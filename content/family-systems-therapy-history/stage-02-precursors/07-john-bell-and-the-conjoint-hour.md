**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not medical advice, not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

John Elderkin Bell published *Family Group Therapy* in 1961 as Public Health Service Monograph No. 64. A government press is a particular kind of authority. It is not a Guilford workshop. It is not a Palo Alto paperback. It is a federal object a health officer could order.

Bell's own later accounts, and the secondary literature that repeats them, date his first family interviews earlier — often to the early 1950s, Clark University and then a California clinic trail. Historians argue about how clean that memory is and about how many other people were already seeing relatives in the same years. This pack will not crown a single inventor. It will keep the 1961 monograph on the table because a reader can still hold it.

The monograph's title is careful. *Family group therapy.* Group is the older technology. Family is the new unit. The sentence says: take the group method and point it at a household. That is a different claim from Bateson's communication theory, Bowen's ward, or Minuchin's structure. It is closer to a public-health hope that a method already in circulation could be aimed at a cheaper object than a long individual analysis.

"Conjoint" — everyone in the room at once — became the later advertising word. Bell is one of the people later textbooks mention when they need an early conjoint hour that is not Ackerman, not Bowen, and not Satir. Mention is not the same as a school. He did not leave a trademarked institute with a gift shop. He left a monograph and a career inside academic and public-health psychology.

Why does that matter on a clinic site? Because origin stories prefer a genius and a city. Palo Alto photographs well in memory even when we will not scrape the photographs. A PHS monograph photographs as a gray cover. The gray cover is the more honest American object: family therapy as something a government health service thought it might use. The later profession sometimes forgot that public-health sentence when it became a private-practice guild.

Bell's hour was still, in many descriptions, a series of meetings with a beginning, a middle, and an end, and with a therapist who talked more like a group leader than like a structural mover of chairs. This page will not reconstruct those meetings as a script. The historical claim is smaller. By 1961 a federal monograph had already said the unit could be the family group. The 1956 Palo Alto paper had said something else: that communication in a household might be a theory of a diagnosis. Those two sentences traveled together and then fought.

Secondary sources sometimes call Bell "the first." The style guide bans kinship titles unless we are quoting a myth. The myth is useful only as a myth: a field that wanted a first hour that was not psychoanalytic and not a ward. Bell is a convenient first for people who want a psychologist, not a psychiatrist, at the head of the table. Ackerman is a convenient first for people who want a New York analyst. Bowen is a convenient first for people who want a science. Satir is a convenient first for people who want a woman. The convenience is the tell.

A later collection, *Family Therapy* (1975), gathered Bell's papers for a profession that had already become a marketplace of schools. Reading 1961 next to 1975 is a way of watching a public-health object get absorbed into a brand war. This pack will stay with 1961 long enough to let the government press be strange. A monograph number is not a personality. It is a filing system.

If your graduate program skipped Bell for Haley, that is a syllabus choice, not a law of nature. If your workshop never mentioned a PHS number, that is a marketplace choice. The conjoint hour had more than one father, which is exactly why this series will not use the word.

## Why a monograph number matters

Public Health Service monographs were how a federal health service talked to officers who would never see a Palo Alto hotel. Number 64 is a filing fact. Filing facts are easy to skip in a field that prefers founders. Skipping them is how family therapy forgot it once looked like public health.

Bell's later 1975 collection is a different social class of book: a profession that now had schools, journals, and a market. Reading 1961 next to 1975 is a way of watching a government object get absorbed into a brand war. This pack stays with 1961 long enough for the gray cover to remain strange.

Historians who want a "first conjoint hour" will keep arguing. This page will not end the argument. It will say a 1961 federal press already thought the unit could be a family group, and that thought is older than a workshop's origin slide.
