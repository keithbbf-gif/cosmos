**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not medical advice, not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

*Family Process* volume 1, number 1, is dated March 1962. A first issue is a claim: that a field now has enough papers, enough quarrels, and enough mailing addresses to need a periodical of its own. Before 1962, family papers sat in *Psychiatry*, *Behavioral Science*, orthopsychiatric programs, and books. After 1962 they had a living room.

Jay Haley is the name most secondary sources put on the founding editorship. Nathan Ackerman and Don Jackson are the institutional parents in the usual telling: New York and Palo Alto agreeing, for a moment, that the new unit needed a journal. `[VERIFY]` the masthead titles against a library copy before anyone writes a caption that names an office. Mastheads get mythologized. A bound volume does not.

A journal is not a school. It is a marketplace of schools pretending to be a community. Strategic papers, Bowen papers, later Milan papers, feminist papers, qualitative papers, outcome papers — they did not live in the same institute. They lived in the same table of contents. That is a political arrangement. Who gets the lead article, who gets the book review, who gets ignored: those are historical facts a later issue can still reveal if you read the old numbers instead of the brand.

Ackerman's 1958 book had already treated the family as a psychodynamic object. Jackson's MRI (1958 in his institutional sentences; 1959 in some later encyclopedias) had already treated the family as an interactional object. The journal is the handshake and the later fight. Haley, as editor, was not a neutral postman. He had a strategic ear and a talent for making a field sound like a campaign. Campaigns produce enemies. Enemies produce better citations.

For a small-city reader, a journal name is easy to treat as an atmosphere. *Family Process* sounds like a method. It is a title. The method, if any, lives in a particular article: Hare-Mustin 1978; the Milan 1980 paper on hypothesizing, circularity, and neutrality; Andersen 1987; dozens of others this pack will not pretend to have held in every volume. Honesty is naming the issues we mean.

Copyright: the covers are not ours. Do not scrape volume 1 as a featured image. Type is enough: "Family Process 1(1), March 1962." A typographic poster that invents a fake mid-century cover is still a fake. A library photograph of a bound spine, if the library's license is clean, is a book, not a face. Most clinic websites should not bother. The heading can carry the date.

The journal later became an institution with boards, prizes, and a website. Institutions age. They also forget their first fight. The first fight was whether "family" was a serious enough object to leave the child-guidance conference and the psychoanalytic institute. Volume 1 is a yes. The yes did not settle who owned the object. Bowen, Satir, Minuchin, Selvini Palazzoli, White — they would all, in different decades, need that mailbox. Some of them would quarrel with it.

AAMFT and AFTA are neighboring objects (a later essay). A journal is not an association. An association is not a license board. A license board is not a school. American family therapy spent the rest of the century confusing those four on purpose, because confusing them is how a workforce is built. This page unconfuses one of them: March 1962, a first number, a field announcing it had process enough to paginate.

If a later draft in this folder cites "*Family Process*, sometime in the 1970s," the draft should go back to the library. Year and issue are the claim. "The journal" is a fog.

## What a first issue cannot settle

Volume 1 could announce a field. It could not decide whether the field was a science, a craft, or a men's research club with a mailbox. Those decisions took the rest of the century and are still unfinished. A first issue is a claim, not a constitution.

Later special issues — feminist, cultural, outcome — are corrections that used the same spine. Using the spine is how a revolt stays inside a profession instead of founding a second journal that nobody files. Hare-Mustin 1978 is one such use. Andersen 1987 is another. This page exists so "the journal" cannot become a fog that hides those years.

Importers: do not scrape the cover. Type the date. If a graphics agent wants a spine photograph, they need a library license, not a phone picture of a Guilford shelf.
