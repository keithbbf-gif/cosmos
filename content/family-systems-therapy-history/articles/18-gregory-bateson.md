**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Gregory Bateson was born on 9 May 1904 in Grantchester, near Cambridge, and died on 4 July 1980 in San Francisco. He was an anthropologist who wandered into a veterans' hospital town and gave American family therapy a set of words — context, logical types, double bind, ecology of mind — that the field still uses when it wants to sound older than a technique. He was not a family therapist. He disliked being drafted as one. That refusal is part of why the draft kept happening.

## Before Palo Alto

His father, William Bateson, was a geneticist who named genetics. The son took the name into another science. *Naven* (1936) is a book about ritual, gender, and schismogenesis among the Iatmul of New Guinea. Schismogenesis — complementary and symmetrical runaway processes — would later sound, to clinicians, like a couple. Bateson had been talking about runaway processes before he met Don Jackson.

The Bali years with Margaret Mead, in the 1930s, produced photographs and film that museums still show. Those images have named Balinese people in them. Library of Congress exhibition files often carry usage conditions. They are not Bateson's headshot and they are not clip art for "the family." This pack will not scrape them. A 1963 Imogen Cunningham portrait of Bateson at the Smithsonian National Portrait Gallery is © 1963 Imogen Cunningham Trust. Type: Gregory Bateson, 1904–1980.

The Macy Conferences on cybernetics, from the 1940s into the early 1950s, gave him colleagues (McCulloch, Wiener's orbit, a room that liked feedback) and a language of systems that did not begin in a clinic. He brought that language west.

## 1952–1962

The Palo Alto project — Rockefeller money, then other funds including NIMH, the VA Hospital as a site, Jackson, Haley, Weakland, Fry — is the era essay. This figure essay needs only the temperament. Bateson wanted to know how communication could be mapped the way a ritual could be mapped. He wanted logical types, play, paradox. He did not want a treatment manual. "Toward a Theory of Schizophrenia" (1956) was a research paper. Its afterlife as a story about households and psychosis is a fact he and the others had to answer in the 1962/1963 "Note on the Double Bind." *Steps to an Ecology of Mind* (1972) is the book in which he gathered the pieces and tried to keep them from becoming a slogan.

He married Mead in 1936; they divorced in 1950. Those are public dates. This page will not turn a marriage of two famous anthropologists into gossip. Their daughter, Mary Catherine Bateson, later wrote her own books; she is not this pack's subject.

## What clinicians did with him

They took context. A symptom lives in a dance of responses. They took the double bind and, too often, made it a parlor diagnosis of a mother. They took "the map is not the territory" (a Korzybski line Bateson liked) and used it to humble a file. They took ecology and sometimes meant, loosely, that everything is connected, which is true and not yet a method.

Good teachers use Bateson to slow a clinician who wants a villain. Bad teachers use Bateson to avoid a body: as if a psychosis were only a paradox. He is not responsible for every misuse. He is responsible for a 1956 paper that was available for misuse in a culture that already wanted to blame the house.

MRI was Jackson's institute, not Bateson's. The confusion is a later textbook habit. Bateson remained next door, then elsewhere — Hawaii, the Oceanic Institute, a California end — an epistemologist on the move. Students who want a guru will find one anyway. He is a poor guru. He is a good warning.

## Inheritance

A small-city clinician can steal one habit. When two messages disagree, and a child is being asked not to notice, notice. Then do not turn the noticing into an origin myth of an illness. Then stop.

Do not diagnose a relative with a double bind. Do not use Bali faces. Do not generate a "period photograph."

If a household is in trouble, this page is not the appointment.

The usable inheritance is a temperament: curiosity about pattern, suspicion of thin explanation, a willingness to say that a research paper is not a church. Family therapy needed that temperament in the 1950s because psychiatry's other temperament was a mother-noun and a closed file. It still needs it, for different reasons, whenever a manual or a brand gets loud.

Grantchester to New Guinea to Bali to Palo Alto to San Francisco is not a hero's journey. It is an anthropologist's itinerary that happened to pass through a hospital town at the right decade. The decade did the rest.

## 1936, Cambridge University Press, a ritual not a clinic

*Naven* is a 1936 anthropology book. Clinicians who start in 1956 have skipped the part where complementary and symmetrical runaway processes were about ritual and gender among the Iatmul, not about a California couple. Skipping is how a field steals a concept and forgets the people it was written about. This pack will not use Iatmul or Balinese faces as decoration. It will say: read 1936 as anthropology. Read 1956 as a hospital-town paper. Do not make 1936 a footnote that launders 1956.

Mary Catherine Bateson's later books are public. She is not this pack's subject. Mentioning her existence is enough to keep "the Batesons" from meaning only a marriage and a project.

He died on the Fourth of July, 1980, in San Francisco. Obituaries used the date. This page will not make a symbol of it. The work is the papers and the 1972 gathering. The refusal to be a clinician is the ethic. Family therapy drafted him anyway. Drafts are historical facts. They are not consent.
