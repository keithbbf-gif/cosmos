**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In June 1978 *Family Process* printed Rachel T. Hare-Mustin's "A Feminist Approach to Family Therapy." She was then at Villanova, writing from inside a field that had spent twenty years celebrating "the system" without asking who washed the system's dishes. The paper said the quiet part: family therapy recognized social context and then ignored the particular context called the status of women. Stereotyped sex roles were being reinforced in the hour and called neutrality, or structure, or strategy. Two years later she published "Family Therapy May Be Dangerous for Your Health" in *Professional Psychology*. The second title is not a joke. It is a research program.

## 1977, four women, a project

Betty Carter — a midlife social-work student who had trained at Ackerman with Olga Silverstein under Peggy Papp — co-founded the Women's Project in Family Therapy in 1977 with Papp, Silverstein, and Marianne Walters. Carter's own papers, now at Smith College, give the project's sentence: to explore the changing role of women in families and to raise those issues in a field where most of the practitioners were women and most of the leaders were men. She directed the Family Institute of Westchester from 1977 to 1997. She died in 2012.

The four wrote *The Invisible Web: Gender Patterns in Family Relationships* (Guilford, 1988; paperback catalogues often 1991). They took mother–daughter, father–daughter, mother–son, and husband–wife pairs and refused to treat gender as a side topic. They wrote about divorce, remarriage, and female-headed households without using those households as proof of decline. The Women's Project won the 1986 AFTA award for a distinguished contribution. Awards are not arguments. The book is the argument.

Stonehenge — the name later given to gatherings of women family therapists in 1984 and 1986 — belongs in this paragraph as a date, not as folklore. An international meeting followed in 1991. The field's official memory still prefers Palo Alto 1956. Official memories are a choice.

## What they were revolting against

A "complementary" couple, in MRI language, can be a polite name for a husband who talks and a wife who accommodates. A "parental child," in structural language, can be a polite name for a daughter who lost her childhood to a father's absence and a mother's load. A "paradoxical prescription" can tell a woman to do more of the labor that is already killing her week. A Bowen diagram can treat a mother's contact with her family of origin as "undifferentiation" when it is also the only childcare she has.

None of those schools is only that misuse. All of them were available for that misuse. Hare-Mustin named the contract, the tasks, the communication, the generational boundaries, the relabeling of deviance, the modeling, and the alliances as places a clinician could stop being casually sexist. This essay will not reprint her list as a checklist. A list is how a 1978 paper becomes a 2026 worksheet. Read the paper.

Virginia Goldner's mid-1980s *Family Process* essays on gender pushed the argument into the journal that Haley had once edited. The journal, to its credit, printed the fight. Printing a fight is not the same as changing a training institute's faculty.

## Race was not a footnote they all wrote

The Women's Project was four white women. That is a historical fact, not a cancellation. Nancy Boyd-Franklin's *Black Families in Therapy* (1989) and Celia Falicov's later work on Latino families are not sequels to *The Invisible Web*. They are corrections of a field that could discover gender and still imagine the family as white. Kenneth Hardy's later public work on race in the family hour belongs on the same long shelf. This pack gives those writers their own essays so this one does not end with a paragraph titled "diversity."

Hare-Mustin also wrote, with Paulette Moore Hines, on ethical concerns in family therapy (1978). Hines's presence in that citation is a reminder that Black women were in the argument early, even when the famous book covers were not.

## What not to do with this revolt

Do not treat feminism as a 1978 costume the field outgrew. Manuals and "evidence-based" couples protocols can hide the same old task distribution behind an outcome table.

Do not use this essay to diagnose your marriage. Do not use it to prosecute your mother. The revolt was against *clinical* habits, not an invitation to a new kitchen trial.

Do not scrape Ackerman or Westchester photographs. Carter died in 2012. Papp, Silverstein, and Walters have their own copyrights. Type only.

A small-city clinician can steal one habit. When you admire a "well-organized" family, ask who is doing the organizing, at what hour, and what it costs. Then look at your own hour: who do you face, who do you interrupt, who do you find "difficult." Then stop. The rest is supervision, not a heritage page.

If a household is in trouble, this page is not the appointment. If someone is in danger, call local emergency services.

The usable inheritance is a refusal. A system that cannot see gender will call a patriarchy a pattern and then try to "rebalance" it without naming the law the pattern is obeying. Family therapy's better decades after 1978 are the decades that kept that refusal in the room. Its worse decades are the ones that filed Hare-Mustin under "historical interest" and went back to the mirror.

## Villanova, 1978, a journal that had been a men's magazine

*Family Process* in 1978 was sixteen years old and had already had Haley's farewell. Printing Hare-Mustin was the journal doing what journals are for. It was also late. Women had been the practitioners since child guidance. The paper is late the way Boyd-Franklin 1989 is late: the field's clock, not the writer's.

She asked for feminist attention to the contract, the tasks, the talk, the boundaries, the labeling of deviance, the modeling, and the alliances. Seven places. This page still will not reprint them as a checklist. Seven is a reminder that "be less sexist" is not a mood. It is a set of ordinary clinical decisions.

Peggy Papp's Depression Project at Ackerman, Olga Silverstein's midlife training path, Marianne Walters's earlier social-work and institute work — each could be a figure essay. This pack gave the chair to Carter because Smith already organized her papers. The other three remain in the 1988 contents page and in this paragraph so the Project is not a one-woman myth.
