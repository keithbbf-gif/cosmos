**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Nancy Boyd-Franklin published *Black Families in Therapy: A Multisystems Approach* in 1989. The book treated kinship, church, agency, and racism as present-tense structure, not as a "cultural note" at the bottom of a genogram. She was alive at last verification in 2026. Living writers get extra care: public books, public academic posts, no gossip, no scraped photographs.

## 1989 is late, and that is the point

Family therapy had a journal in 1962, a structural bible in 1974, a feminist paper in 1978, an ethnicity anthology in 1982. A full book that put Black families at the center arrived in 1989. Lateness is a historical fact about the field, not about Boyd-Franklin. Minuchin had already noticed, in Philadelphia, that clients and staff did not match. Noticing is not the same as a literature.

Multisystems, in her usage, means the family is not only the people who share a last name on a form. Grandmothers, churches, schools, courts, and the racial order of the town are in the hour whether the clinician names them or not. This page will not teach you a multisystems interview. It will say the 1989 book exists so that a clinician who only knows Haley 1963 has no excuse to treat a Black household as a white suburban system with different food.

Later editions and later papers continued the work. A live "Sources" block should use the edition a library holds. This draft names 1989 as the event.

## Neighbors, not sequels

McGoldrick 1982 made ethnicity ordinary on syllabi. The Women's Project made gender a subject. Boyd-Franklin is not a sequel to either. She is a correction of a field that could discover gender and ethnicity and still imagine the family as white, or imagine Black kinship as "enmeshment." The structural essay in this pack already warned about that word. Her book is one of the reasons the warning has a citation.

Paulette Moore Hines, who wrote with Hare-Mustin on ethics in 1978, is a reminder that Black women were in the argument early. Boyd-Franklin's 1989 book is the one a general reader can hold.

Hardy's later public work on race in the family hour is a neighbor. Do not collapse them. Different books, different tones, same field that needed both.

## Inheritance

A small-city clinician can steal one habit. Ask which agencies already have a story about this household, and whether that story is the one the family would tell. Then stop. Do not assign a "strengths of the Black family" paragraph you memorized.

## Guilford, later books, a living writer

Guilford printed *Black Families in Therapy* in 1989 — twenty-seven years after *Family Process* 1.1. Lateness is the field's, not hers. A second edition followed in 2003; cite the spine on the desk you actually have. *Reaching Out in Family Therapy: Home-Based, School, and Community Interventions*, with Brenna Hafer Bry (Guilford, 2000), is the home-based wing. *Boys into Men: Raising Our African American Teenage Sons*, with A. J. Franklin (Dutton, 2000; later paperbacks), is a general-audience book from the same years.

Rutgers Graduate School of Applied and Professional Psychology is the academic address most public C.V.s name. Confirm before a live block. This draft will not invent a colorful employment history to look finished.

A small-city clinic in Arkansas is not her New Jersey-and-New-York training world. The usable remainder does not require it to be.

HIV-affected families appear in her public writing of the 1990s as a present-tense clinic fact, not as a historical curiosity. Multisystems here means school, church, hospital, child-welfare office, and street. It does not mean a second Haley 1963.

Paulette Moore Hines, who wrote with Hare-Mustin on ethics in 1978, is an earlier neighbor in the argument; Hardy's later race work is a later neighbor. Do not collapse them into a single "diversity" paragraph. *Reaching Out in Family Therapy* (2000) is the public-health wing of the same career — home, school, and community as places a family already lives, not as stages for a clever interview. The second edition of *Black Families in Therapy* (2003) is the expansion students should cite when a library only holds the later spine.
