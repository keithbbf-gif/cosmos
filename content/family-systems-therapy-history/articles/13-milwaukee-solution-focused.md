**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 1978, in Milwaukee, Steve de Shazer and Insoo Kim Berg founded the Brief Family Therapy Center. They had MRI in their rearview mirror: Weakland, Fisch, Watzlawick, a taste for shortness, a distrust of excavation. They spent the 1980s asking a different first question. Not "what maintains the problem?" so much as "when is the problem not happening, and what are you doing then?" *Patterns of Brief Family Therapy* (1982), *Keys to Solution in Brief Therapy* (1985), and *Clues* (1988) are the books that made "solution-focused" a phrase agencies could put on a flyer. The phrase is easy to parody. The parody exists because a miracle question, taken out of a supervised hour and dropped into a staff meeting, sounds like a greeting card.

## A Korean pharmacist, an American bricoleur

Berg was born in Korea in 1934, trained in pharmacy in Seoul, then in social work in the United States, then at MRI, then in Milwaukee. That path is the figure essay. This era essay needs it in one sentence because SFBT is sometimes told as de Shazer's epistemology with a coauthor. The Center was a partnership. Berg's clinical ear — especially with people agencies called "difficult" or "mandated" — is part of why the model survived contact with county buildings.

De Shazer (1940–2005) wrote like a man who had read Wittgenstein and wanted a therapy that did not pretend to see behind language. He liked exceptions, small changes, and the idea that you do not need a theory of the problem in order to build a solution. MRI had already said the attempted solution could *be* the problem. Milwaukee said: then stop studying the problem as if it were a cathedral. Look at the hours the cathedral is closed.

## What traveled, and what got cheap

What traveled was a stance. The person in the chair is the expert on their own week. The clinician is an expert on asking about that week without stealing it. Compliments, in Berg's usage, were not fluff. They were a way of marking a competence the file had ignored. Scaling questions, exception questions, the so-called miracle question — this essay names them as historical objects so a reader can recognize a training-room vocabulary. It will not teach you how to ask them. A numbered scale on a blog is a worksheet. This series does not publish worksheets.

What got cheap was the agency version: three sessions, a form, a supervisor who wants a "strengths-based" sentence in every chart. Solution-focused work can be serious with people in ugly corners. It can also become a cheerleader that cannot hear a bruise. Berg knew the difference. Manuals that dropped her name after she died in 2007 did not always know it.

Feminist and trauma clinicians asked the right rude question. If the problem is a violent partner, "what is already working" can become a way to keep a woman in a house. If the problem is racism at a school, exceptions can become a way to compliment a child for enduring. SFBT's better teachers built in safety and did not pretend a miracle question replaces a shelter. This pack will not pretend either. If you are in danger, call local emergency services.

## Cousins, not clones

MRI's Brief Therapy Center wanted to interrupt a pattern. Milwaukee wanted to grow an exception. Narrative therapy, a few years later and an ocean away, wanted to unmask a story that had recruited a person. All three are brief in mood and anti-excavatory in temperament. They are not the same church. De Shazer and Berg did not ask you to externalize a problem as a character. White and Epston did. Do not blur the addresses.

The Milwaukee Center closed as a place you could visit; the books did not. Solution-focused practice moved into schools, child-protection offices, and coaching — a migration that thrilled people who wanted therapy to be less medical and worried people who watched a clinical method become a corporate icebreaker. Both reactions are historical facts.

## What not to do with Milwaukee

Do not ask a miracle question at a dinner table and call it care. Do not tell a depressed person to "focus on solutions" as if the 1985 book were a slogan. Do not use "mandated client" techniques you learned from a slide deck on a family that has not consented to a county experiment.

Do not scrape BFTC photographs. De Shazer died in 2005. Berg died in 2007. Type only.

A small-city clinician can steal one habit. Ask when the trouble is *less*, and be genuinely interested in the answer. Then stop. The rest is training. The rest is not a heritage page.

If a household is in trouble, this page is not the appointment.

The usable inheritance is an ethic of attention. Files are written in the language of deficit because deficit is what agencies pay to see. Berg kept asking for the other hours. That question is older than 1978 — social workers have always known about the aunt who shows up — but Milwaukee gave it a literature. Honor the literature. Do not franchise the smile.

## 1982, 1985, 1988: three spines, one Center

Guilford 1982, Norton 1985, Norton 1988. The publishers tell you the work moved from a specialty-family list to the same house that printed *Pragmatics* and *Change*. Milwaukee wanted to be readable next to MRI, not only next to social-work handbooks. Readable is how a county supervisor meets a miracle question without having been to Palo Alto.

de Shazer liked language games more than growth stories. Berg liked people agencies had already decided were not games. The 1978 founding is their overlap. After 2005 and 2007 the overlap became a literature without its founders. Literatures outlive rooms. Rooms are where the mandate sat.

This pack's general-psychotherapy Berg essay is a shorter counseling-heritage object. This era essay is the family-systems deep lane: MRI as cousin, the mandate as ethic, the flyer as failure mode. Do not merge the two calendars. Do not skip safety. If you are in danger, call local emergency services.
