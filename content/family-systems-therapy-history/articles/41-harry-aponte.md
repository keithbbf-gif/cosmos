Harry J. Aponte published *Bread & Spirit: Therapy with the New Poor* in 1994, after years as a structural family therapist trained in the Philadelphia orbit. The book put poverty and faith back into an hour that could otherwise pretend it was only about boundaries. He was to be treated, at last verification, as a living or recently living writer: public books only, no gossip, no scraped institute photographs. ## Philadelphia's other line

Minuchin's clinic trained more than one temperament. Aponte's is the one that would not let structure float free of a neighborhood. Ecological family therapy, in his usage, means the family is in a street, a church, a welfare office, a labor market. Wiltwyck had already known that and had written it as "slums." Aponte wrote it as a present tense for the 1990s — the "new poor" of the title — without treating faith as an embarrassment the clinic had to wait out.

*Bread & Spirit* is the object a general reader can hold. This page will not teach an ecological interview. It will say: a structural hour that never asks about money or prayer has chosen a 1974 classroom over a 1994 street.

He has written, in public, about the person of the therapist — the idea that the clinician's own hunger and spirit show up in the hour. That sentence can be serious. It can become a workshop about self-disclosure. This pack will not run the workshop. Public books only.

## Poverty is not a metaphor

Family therapy's heroic decades used poor families as the sample that proved a theory (Wiltwyck) and then used professional families as the sample that traveled (Palo Alto workshops). Aponte is one of the people who kept the first sample from becoming a career-starting story you leave behind. Boyd-Franklin and Falicov are neighbors. Hardy is a neighbor. Do not collapse them into "diversity." Poverty is a class fact. Race is a race fact. Faith is a faith fact. His title named two of the three and implied the street.

Feminist readers will ask who, in a poor household, is doing the organizing. The revolt essay still applies. Bread and spirit can be a woman's unpaid week.

## 1976 *Family Process*; person-of-the-therapist as a later model

Aponte's "The Family-School Interview: An Eco-Structural Approach" appeared in *Family Process* 15 (1976): 303–311 — a Philadelphia-era paper eighteen years before *Bread & Spirit*. The school is in the hour, not only the people who share a last name. "The Negotiation of Values in Therapy" (*Family Process* 24, 1985) later named class, faith, and what a clinician will not pretend not to see. Values, in that essay, are not a workshop icebreaker. Later writing collected around *The Person of the Therapist Training Model* (with Karni Kissil; Routledge, 2016) made the clinician's own family and hunger a training subject. That is a training idea for licensed people. It is not homework for a WOW reader.

He was on staff at the Philadelphia Child Guidance Clinic in the Minuchin years and remained a Philadelphia teacher after the directorship changed. The clinic is the institutional ancestor. *Bread & Spirit* is not a second *Families of the Slums*. 1967 was Wiltwyck's sample in a Basic Books cloth. 1994 is a present-tense poor in a Norton cloth. Date both.
