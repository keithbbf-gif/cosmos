**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Ivan Boszormenyi-Nagy was born in Budapest in 1920, came to the United States in 1950, and spent the decades that made family therapy famous at the Eastern Pennsylvania Psychiatric Institute in Philadelphia and in a language that never quite sounded like Palo Alto. With Geraldine Spark he published *Invisible Loyalties* in 1973. With Barbara Krasner he published *Between Give and Take* in 1986. He died in 2007. The words he left — loyalty, entitlement, ledger, relational ethics, multidirected partiality — are easy to turn into a poster. They were meant as a claim about justice between generations, not as a poster.

## Not homeostasis, not a sculpture

Nagy's unit was not the pattern of talk (MRI) and not the map of who sits with whom (Minuchin) and not the weather of anxiety (Bowen), though he could hear all three. His unit was the *ethic* of the relationship: who has given, who has been owed, who is silently paying a parent's unpaid bill. "Invisible loyalties" named the way a child can remain faithful to a wounded parent by failing, by staying, by carrying a symptom that keeps the old account open.

That sentence can be profound in a room where an adult child is still organizing a life around a parent's injury. It can also become a way to tell a woman she owes her father more fairness. Contextual therapy's later teachers had to keep saying: a ledger is not an order to reconcile with someone who hurt you. Fairness includes the right to a balanced account, and sometimes the balance is distance. Pauline Boss's later work on ambiguous loss — a different essay — is one place the field learned to talk about absence without demanding a tidy ethic. Nagy is the place it learned to talk about obligation without only talking about power.

## EPPI, and a city that already had clinics

Philadelphia in the 1960s and 1970s was crowded with family-therapy addresses: Minuchin's Child Guidance Clinic, Nagy's EPPI, later Aponte's community work. Students could take a bus between dialects. Histories that treat the field as a sequence of California papers miss the city. Nagy's Hungarian training and the postwar migration are not color. They are why "loyalty" in his pages has a Central European gravity that Haley's Wyoming irony does not.

Multidirected partiality — the clinician's attempt to be on more than one person's side, in turn, without becoming a judge who has already ruled — is the technical name students underline. This essay will not teach you how to do it. Doing it badly is how you abandon a child to a parent's charm. Doing it well is a life's work. A heritage page can only say the name exists because Nagy thought a neutral technician was a moral evasion.

## What the ledger is not

It is not a spreadsheet for a reader to fill in. This series will not print a blank ledger. It is not a religious confession, though Nagy's language can sound like one and later writers have put it next to theology. It is not a demand that everyone forgive. Forgiveness-as-assignment is a misuse the field has had to name, especially when the person being asked to forgive is the one who was harmed.

Feminist readers asked, correctly, who keeps the books. If the unpaid labor is already a woman's, a therapy of "give and take" can become a second shift. The revolt essay in this pack is the place for that argument. Contextual therapy is not excused from it. Nagy's better students took the argument in. His worse slogans did not.

## Inheritance

Ordinary counseling still uses "loyalty" without the citation. A teenager who fails a grade the year a parent gets sick; an adult who cannot enjoy a marriage without feeling they have betrayed a lonely mother — those scenes are why the 1973 book stayed in print. The usable inheritance for a small-city clinician is a question, not a method: who would be hurt, silently, if this problem got better? Then stop. The answer may be "no one," and then you have learned something too.

Do not use loyalty language to keep a person in an unsafe house. A violent home is the problem. A hungry home is the problem. Ethics after those facts are named is the only ethics worth the word.

Do not scrape EPPI or institute photographs. Type: Ivan Boszormenyi-Nagy, 1920–2007.

If a household is in trouble, this page is not the appointment.

James Framo's later work on family-of-origin sessions, and other intergenerational writers who sat near Nagy without being him, belong on the same shelf and not in this paragraph as a blur. Intergenerational is a neighborhood. Contextual is a particular house in that neighborhood. Keep the addresses straight.

A Southeast Arkansas clinic that still answers its own phone does not need a Hungarian noun to notice that a child can be loyal in a way that costs them. It needs a clinician who will not call that loyalty cute, and who will not bill a blog as a session.

## 1973, Harper & Row, a word that sounds like church

*Invisible Loyalties* arrived from a trade-and-text house, not from a Palo Alto specialty imprint. The word "loyalties" already had a church and a nation in it. Nagy's usage is relational and intergenerational, not patriotic. Readers still hear the other meanings. That hearing is why the book can be misused as a demand to stay. Date the book. Keep the warning.

Geraldine Spark's name on the 1973 cover is not a courtesy. Barbara Krasner's name on the 1986 cover is not a courtesy. This pack's figure essay is Nagy's because the theory is filed under his hyphenated name. Filing is not the same as solo authorship.

Eastern Pennsylvania Psychiatric Institute was a state-facing research and training address, not a private lodge. The sample and the funding differ from Chestnut Lodge and from Wiltwyck. Addresses matter when a theory of justice between generations is being written next door to a structural clinic that moved chairs. Philadelphia could hear both in one week of supervision. Most towns cannot. A heritage page can at least keep both on the map.

James Framo's family-of-origin sessions, often cited next to Nagy, are a neighboring intergenerational practice and not a contextual clone. Keep the street addresses. A reader who wants "loyalty" in ordinary counseling has already met the 1973 book whether they know the title or not. Knowing the title is the difference between a noun and a demand.

Philadelphia's bus between EPPI and Child Guidance is a literal remainder. Students could hear loyalty on Monday and structure on Tuesday. Most towns hear one dialect. A heritage page can at least keep both stops on the map and refuse a blank ledger.
