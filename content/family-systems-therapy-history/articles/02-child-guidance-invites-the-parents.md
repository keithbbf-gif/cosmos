**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 1909, on the edge of Chicago's juvenile court, William Healy opened the Juvenile Psychopathic Institute. The court wanted to know why a particular child had stolen, run, or gone silent. Healy wanted a file that was thicker than a police blotter. Within a few years the institute's method — medical exam, psychological tests, a social history taken in the home — had a name that traveled: child guidance. The child was still the patient. The parents had already been invited, whether they knew the invitation was an examination.

## A clinic with three chairs before it had a theory

The American child-guidance team was a small invention with a long shadow. A physician looked for lesions and lumpy histories. A psychologist ran tests that the era trusted more than we do. A social worker went to the kitchen. That third chair is the one this series cares about. It is the ancestor of the family hour and of every later fight about who owns the story.

The Judge Baker Foundation in Boston (1917) copied and refined the model. So did clinics funded by the Commonwealth Fund in the 1920s, which planted child-guidance offices in cities that had never asked for a "psychopathic institute." The word *psychopathic* in Healy's title is a period word. It meant, in that usage, a clinic for trouble that the court could not classify. It did not mean the later pop insult. Say the 1909 name once, then say child guidance.

What the social worker wrote down was not a genogram. It was a moral geography: rent, alcohol, a missing father, a grandmother who kept the children, a priest who had opinions. Later family therapists would call some of those notes "context" and some of them "bias." Both readings can be true of the same paragraph. A home visit can see a wage. It can also see a mother as a type.

Healy's *The Individual Delinquent* (1915) is the book this era can put on the table without stealing a face. Little, Brown, Boston, 1915. The title page is public domain. The case photographs are not a blog header. The book's honesty, for 1915, is that the "individual" of the title keeps dissolving into streets and households. Its limit is the era's: it still wanted a cause that could be filed on one child.

## Parents in the lecture hall, then in the room

Adlerian clinics treated parent education as clinical work. Vienna's child-guidance lectures, and Dreikurs's later Chicago versions, put mothers and fathers in chairs and talked about encouragement, birth order, and the child's "goal." That vocabulary would later leak into ordinary American parenting talk until no one remembered it had a clinic. Family therapy's later diagrams of sibling position (Bowen) and of a child's nominated role (Minuchin, Satir) have cousins here. Cousins are not the same person. Adlerians still thought in a psychology of the person-in-the-family. They did not yet think the family *was* the patient.

John Bowlby's 1949 paper in *Human Relations* asked whether group tensions in a family could be studied and reduced. The paper sits on the hinge between war-nursery attachment work and the family hour. This pack will not retell the Strange Situation. It will say: by 1949 a respectable journal would print the family as a group with tensions, not only as a backdrop for a child's symptom.

Marriage counseling, again, ran beside this track. The Marriage Council of Philadelphia (1932) and the 1942 association of marriage counselors were not child-guidance clinics. They were offices that had already decided the *relationship* could be the object. When family therapy later claimed to have discovered the unit, it was claiming a discovery that social workers and marriage counselors had been practicing without the cybernetic vocabulary.

## Bell, and the habit of leaving him out

John Elderkin Bell started seeing families as a group at Clark University in 1951. He spoke in Boston in 1953. He published the method as a U.S. Public Health Service monograph in 1961. Histories that begin at Bateson 1956 or Jackson 1958 have to step over that monograph. They do so because Bell trained few famous students and founded no brand. The omission is a fact about how professions write origin stories. It is not a fact about 1951.

Bell treated the family as a group of members who needed turns. Later structural and strategic writers wanted something colder: a pattern that would continue if you swapped the people. Both gestures needed the child-guidance inheritance. You cannot invite a household into a room if the clinic has not already learned to ask for the parents.

Midelfort's 1957 book, *The Family in Psychotherapy*, shows the same invitation inside a hospital. Relatives were not only visitors. They were part of the treatment the chart had to account for. The Upper Midwest does not appear in as many textbooks as Palo Alto. The book exists. The rooms existed.

## The parent as defendant

Child guidance's gift to family therapy was the file that included the house. Its scar was the ease with which that file became a verdict on a mother. A social history that notes "cold," "overprotective," or "inconsistent" is one adjective away from a theory of harm. The next essay in this pack is about what happened when those adjectives hardened into an etiology of psychosis. This essay's job is to say the hardening did not begin in 1948. It had a clerical practice behind it: the home visit, the school report, the court letter.

A contemporary clinician who asks a parent to join a first hour inherits both the gift and the scar. Who is the client? Who signs the consent? What is said back to the school? Child guidance invented those as professional questions because it had already invented the parent as a source of data. Data is not the same as blame. The two have shared a clipboard for a century.

Do not use this history to run a family meeting in your kitchen. The 1915 book is a book. The 1961 monograph is a monograph. If a household is in trouble, the historical essay is not the appointment.

The usable inheritance for a small-city practice is plain. The intake form that lists one child is unfinished. The home — or the phone call to the person who keeps the calendar — is still where the file gets honest. Honesty is not a sculpture and not a paradox. It is the old social-work habit, stripped of the era's adjectives, and bound by a consent form child guidance did not yet have to write at this length.
