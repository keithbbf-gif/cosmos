**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 1948, in the journal *Psychiatry*, Frieda Fromm-Reichmann published "Notes on the Development of Treatment of Schizophrenics by Psychoanalytic Psychotherapy." She was writing from Chestnut Lodge, a private hospital in Maryland that took psychoanalysis into rooms other hospitals called hopeless. Buried in a paper about treatment was a phrase the next twenty years would not bury: the "schizophrenogenic" mother. A word that looked like Greek and behaved like a verdict.

## A phrase, a climate, a file

Fromm-Reichmann did not invent the habit of blaming mothers. Child-guidance social histories had been practicing the habit in plainer English. What she did was give the habit a medical-sounding noun at the exact moment American psychiatry was looking for a psychological cause of psychosis. The 1948 paper is about therapy with people the era called schizophrenic. Later popular memory reduced it to a mother. That reduction is itself a historical event. It traveled farther than the rest of the paper.

Theodore Lidz at Yale, with Stephen Fleck and Alice Cornelison, spent the 1950s and 1960s describing "schismatic" and "skewed" marriages in families of patients. *Schizophrenia and the Family* (1965) is a book you can still find in libraries. It is also a book that later readers, especially women who had been the "skew," learned to read as a prosecution file. Lyman Wynne and colleagues at NIMH published "Pseudo-Mutuality in the Family Relations of Schizophrenics" in *Psychiatry* in 1958. The word named a brittle, too-agreeable surface. It was a careful observation of some interviews. It became, in careless hands, another way to say the house was sick and the patient was its symptom.

Gregory Bateson, Don Jackson, Jay Haley, and John Weakland published "Toward a Theory of Schizophrenia" in *Behavioral Science* in 1956. That paper has its own essay in this pack. It belongs in this one too, because a communication theory that does not name a villain can still deliver one. A child caught in contradictory injunctions needs a person who delivers the injunctions. In the American mid-century, that person was almost always drawn as a mother.

Deborah Weinstein's later history, *The Pathological Family* (2013), is one place a reader can go to see the climate whole: postwar America, anxious about homes, funded a science of the pathological household. The science produced journals, films, and careers. It also produced shame that outlived the citations.

## What the later literature was not

In the 1960s and 1970s, George Brown, J. L. T. Birley, John Wing, and then Christine Vaughn and Julian Leff developed a different vocabulary: expressed emotion, or EE. They were asking a narrower question. After a person with a psychotic illness left hospital, did certain patterns of criticism, hostility, or emotional over-involvement in the household predict relapse? The studies were about *relapse*, not about who "made" the illness. That distinction is the whole ethical difference, and it is the distinction popular memory keeps collapsing.

This series will not collapse it. EE research has its own limits, its own cultural fights, and its own risk of being heard as "don't feel things around your relative." Those fights belong in a methods seminar. The historical point for a general reader is simpler. Mid-century family theories of *origin* and later studies of *course* are not the same literature. Using the second to launder the first is a misuse of both.

Biological psychiatry after DSM-III (1980) often treated the older family papers as an embarrassment and nothing else. That, too, is too neat. The older papers were wrong as etiologies of schizophrenia. They were not empty as documents of how a household talks under terror. Wynne spent later decades walking toward genetics and communication both. A career can revise itself. A phrase like Fromm-Reichmann's, once loose in the culture, does not revise itself.

## Injury has a gender

The person who paid for these theories was, disproportionately, a woman who had already been doing the unpaid work of the illness: the hospital visits, the nights, the neighbors. To be told that her love was a toxin is not a small insult. It is a clinical event. Feminist family therapists in the 1970s named that event without needing a new Greek word. Rachel Hare-Mustin's later warning that family therapy could be dangerous for a woman's health (1980) has a preface in 1948.

Fathers were not absent from the Yale and NIMH descriptions. "Schism" and "skew" are marriage words. The popular afterlife still chose the mother. Popular afterlives are historical facts. They tell you who was available to blame.

A contemporary clinician who meets a parent of a young person with a first psychotic episode inherits this file whether or not they have read Fromm-Reichmann. The parent may have read the internet's version. History's job is to say: that version is a mid-century theory, it injured people, and it is not the current medical account of schizophrenia. History's job is also to say: households still matter for *how an illness is lived*. Mattering is not causing. If you cannot keep those two verbs apart, do not use systems language in that room.

This essay will not teach you how to talk to a family in a first-episode clinic. It will not offer a psychoeducation script. If you or someone you love is in danger, call local emergency services. If you need care, you need a licensed clinician, not a heritage page.

## What to do with the papers now

Read them as dated documents. Name the hospitals (Chestnut Lodge, Yale, NIMH). Name the journals (*Psychiatry*, *Behavioral Science*). Name the year. Then name the women who had to live under the noun.

Do not use "enmeshment," "pseudo-mutuality," or "double bind" as a polite way to tell a mother she caused a psychosis. Do not use EE as a laundered version of the same sentence. Do not put a stock photograph of a woman and child under this title.

The usable inheritance is negative and useful. Family therapy's later honesty — Satir's refusal to let a child be the only problem; Minuchin's interest in poor families the institutes ignored; the feminist revolt inside the field — all had to work in a room this theory had already poisoned. They did not all succeed in cleaning it. A small-city practice that invites a parent in 2026 still has to decide whether the invitation is a collaboration or a hearing.

Chestnut Lodge, in Rockville, Maryland, was a private hospital that could afford long stays and a psychoanalytic staff. That privilege is part of why a 1948 paper from its rooms traveled so far: the readers were other psychiatrists, not the women who would later find the noun on a neighbor's tongue. Privilege does not make the paper false as a document of its rooms. It does make the popular afterlife less innocent. A theory born in a paying hospital became a story told about households that never saw the Lodge.
