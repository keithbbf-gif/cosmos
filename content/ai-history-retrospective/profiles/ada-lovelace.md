The portrait is a painting, not a photograph. Alfred Edward Chalon’s likeness, now widely circulated from the Science Museum Group’s line of images, shows Ada King, Countess of Lovelace, as a nineteenth-century public woman — dress, posture, the usual studio grammar. She was born Augusta Ada Byron in 1815, died in 1852, and would have vanished into the usual aristocratic footnotes if she had not written a set of notes that outgrew the paper they were attached to.

## The translation job that became a treatise

In 1842 Luigi Federico Menabrea, an Italian engineer, published a sketch of Charles Babbage’s Analytical Engine in French, based on lectures Babbage had given in Turin. Lovelace translated it for *Taylor’s Scientific Memoirs* in 1843. Then she added notes, signed A. A. L., that ran longer than the original.

Note G contains the Bernoulli-number computation: a sequence of operations the Engine would perform if the mill and store ever existed in metal. Later writers have fought, sometimes pointlessly, over whether this is “the first computer program.” It is certainly a published algorithm written for a general-purpose design that was not built. That is rare enough.

She also wrote the sentence later generations treat as a prophecy and a brake at once. The Engine, she said, has no pretensions to originate anything. It can do whatever we know how to order it to perform. She then allowed that if the fundamental relations of sounds or colors could be expressed as the Engine’s symbols, it might compose elaborate pieces. Reduction first; romance after.

## Mathematics under a married name

Lovelace studied with Augustus De Morgan. She corresponded with Babbage in a tone that is not hero-worship: impatient, technical, sometimes managerial. Victorian medical and financial disasters ate her later years. The public record is thinner than the myth that grew in the late twentieth century, when computer science needed a nineteenth-century ancestor who was not only Babbage.

The myth has done some harm. It invites us to treat her as a mascot. The notes are better than that. They are a working document about what it would mean for a machine to manipulate symbols that are not only numbers — and about the difference between a machine that follows a rule and a mind that invents one.

Babbage never got his Engine. Lovelace’s text waited in journals and then in histories of computing. When this series talks about programs before computers, it means her pages, in 1843, describing a mill that Parliament would not fund.
