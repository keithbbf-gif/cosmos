Geoffrey E. Hinton was born in London in 1947. The photograph is from 30 May 2013 at the University of British Columbia: a lecture on deep neural networks, a year after his students’ ImageNet result had already rewritten other people’s lectures.

## Cambridge, Edinburgh, Carnegie Mellon, Toronto

He studied at Cambridge and took a Ph.D. at Edinburgh (with Christopher Longuet-Higgins), then worked at Sussex, UCSD, Carnegie Mellon, and the University of Toronto. The geography matters because the 1970s–1980s weather was not the same in each place. He kept working on distributed representations when the fashion was leaner models.

The 1986 *Nature* letter with Rumelhart and Williams, the Boltzmann-machine work with Terrence Sejnowski, the later “fast” algorithms for deep belief nets (2006, with Simon Osindero and Yee-Whye Teh) are the documented spine. Not every spine bone is a household object. The 2006 paper is the one mid-2000s students photocopied.

## 2012

Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever, and Hinton’s NIPS 2012 ImageNet paper is a students’ engineering victory with a supervisor’s long bet behind it. Google’s subsequent hiring of the group is a public business fact. Hinton’s later departure from Google, announced in 2023, and his public warnings about future systems, are also public facts. This profile will not turn those warnings into a second career. It will say they were said, dated, and reported.

## Two official sentences

The 2018 ACM Turing Award, shared with Yoshua Bengio and Yann LeCun, cites conceptual and engineering breakthroughs that have made deep neural networks a critical component of computing. The 2024 Nobel Prize in Physics, shared with John Hopfield, is a different committee’s sentence about foundational work on neural networks. Both sentences are official. Neither replaces the 1986 letter or the 2012 contest.

Hinton’s public speaking style is plain and sometimes mischievous. The work is not mischievous. It is a decades-long refusal to treat a single-layer limit as the end of a subject.
