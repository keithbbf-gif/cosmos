Joseph Weizenbaum was born in Berlin in 1923, fled the Nazis as a teenager, and died in 2008 in Ludwigshafen. The 2005 photograph by Ulrich Hansen shows an old man who had been arguing with his own 1966 program for forty years.

![Joseph Weizenbaum, 11 February 2005.](../assets/portraits/joseph-weizenbaum.jpg)

*Credit: Ulrich Hansen. CC BY-SA 3.0. File:Joseph Weizenbaum.jpg.*

## SLIP and a script

Before the fame he wrote SLIP, a list-processing system, and worked in industry. At MIT he wrote ELIZA (*CACM*, 1966), a program that rewrote a user’s sentences according to scripts. The DOCTOR script is the one that escaped. It is not large. You can read it. The phenomenon — attribution of understanding — is larger than the code.

He watched people, including colleagues, treat the script as a companion. He did not treat that as a product opportunity.

## Judgment

*Computer Power and Human Reason* (1976) is the book. The distinction between deciding and choosing, between calculation and judgment, is the spine. He named tasks he thought should remain human even if a program could mimic the surface. The argument is dated in its examples and not dated in its structure.

He spent later years in Germany as well as at MIT, a public intellectual of a kind American computer science does not always keep. Interviews from the 1980s and 1990s show the same refusal: fluency is not a moral improvement.

## After the chat window

A 2022 interface made ELIZA’s social fact into a population-scale habit. Weizenbaum cannot be asked what he would have said. The 1976 text can. It does not ban compilers. It bans a certain kind of delegation. Whether a particular modern system is that delegation is a judgment — his word — and not a benchmark number.

This profile exists so the ethics essay has a person, not only a slogan. The person wrote a small program and then refused to be proud of what people projected onto it.
