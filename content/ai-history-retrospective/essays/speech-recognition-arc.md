Raj Reddy’s 1976 *Proceedings of the IEEE* review, “Speech Recognition by Machine,” is a map of a field that already knew it was hard. The acoustic waveform does not arrive as words. It arrives as a mess that varies with throat, room, and microphone. The 1970s answer at Carnegie Mellon was Hearsay-II: a blackboard architecture, multiple knowledge sources, a hope that phonetics, syntax, and pragmatics could negotiate.

## Harpy and the 1,000-word bet

DARPA’s Speech Understanding Research program (early to mid-1970s) set numerical targets. CMU’s Harpy, among other systems at BBN and Lincoln Laboratory, hit a 1,000-word connected-speech mark that the agency could put in a report. The trick was constraint: a finite vocabulary, a grammar that was not English, a room that was not a train station.

Reddy’s later career — robotics, the Turing Award shared with Feigenbaum in 1994 — grew from that same CMU habit: pick a performance number, then build the circus that might hit it.

## The statistical capture

By the 1980s and 1990s, hidden Markov models and n-gram language models, industrialized at IBM and at telephone-research labs, took the microphone. The linguistic rule bases did not vanish in a night. They lost bake-offs. Word error rate is a brutal editor.

The 2000s added more data and then, after 2010, deep nets: first as acoustic-model replacements, then as end-to-end objects. Public papers from Microsoft Research, Google, and academic groups document the drop in error rates on Switchboard and similar corpora. Voice assistants of the 2010s are the consumer shadow of that drop.

Telephone companies had a reason to care that the 1990s academic literature sometimes treated as vulgar: billable minutes, operator replacement, a digit string spoken over a bad line. AT&T and later industrial labs published enough that the vulgar reason is not a secret. A history that only cites CMU and IBM has still done better than a history that only cites chat.

## What did not get reduced

Speaker variation, code-switching, the ethics of always-on microphones, the fact that a transcript is not an understanding — these remained. Weizenbaum’s warning about fluent surfaces applies to speech systems that type what you said and do not know whether you meant it.

The speech arc is one of the cleaner public stories in this series because the metric was stable enough to lie about only a little. A history of AI that skips it in favor of chess and chat has skipped the place where machines first entered the ordinary ear.
