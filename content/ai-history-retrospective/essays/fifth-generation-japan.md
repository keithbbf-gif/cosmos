In 1982 the Japanese Ministry of International Trade and Industry stood up the Institute for New Generation Computer Technology — ICOT — in Tokyo. The Fifth Generation Computer Systems project, announced in the preceding year and scoped as a decade, was to leap past von Neumann machines toward parallel inference, knowledge bases, and a national answer to American computing.

The public face was logic programming. Prolog, already a European research language associated with Alain Colmerauer’s group in Marseille and with Robert Kowalski’s work in London, became a Japanese industrial dialect. Kazuhiro Fuchi directed the technical vision. Visitors flew in, wrote alarmed memos, and flew home.

## Why Washington and London startled

Edward Feigenbaum and Pamela McCorduck’s 1983 book *The Fifth Generation* told an American audience that Japan had chosen a national AI strategy and that the United States had not. The book is advocacy as history. It is also evidence of a mood: after VLSI and consumer electronics, another “Japan as number one” chapter seemed plausible.

ICOT’s technical reports and the FGCS conference proceedings are less cinematic. They show parallel inference machines, KL0 and later kernel languages, attempts at knowledge-base management, and a running negotiation between Prolog’s elegance and the ugliness of large programs. Some of the hardware was real. Some of the software was a summer school that never ended.

## What the decade produced

By 1992 the project closed on schedule, which is rarer than it sounds. It did not deliver a general thinking machine, and it did not hand Japanese industry a Prolog workstation that displaced UNIX. It did train a generation of researchers, leave a trail of papers on concurrent logic languages, and demonstrate that a ministry can buy a research culture for ten years.

Critics called it a failure because it missed its most inflated sentences. That verdict is too cheap. National computer projects routinely miss their most inflated sentences. The more precise failure is architectural: the 1980s knowledge-and-logic stack was not the stack that would later win at vision and language. Gradient descent on large corpora does not look like a fifth-generation inference machine.

## A different Japanese line

While ICOT was building parallel Prologs, Kunihiko Fukushima’s neocognitron (1980) sat in *Biological Cybernetics*, a hierarchical network for shift-tolerant pattern recognition. It was not the Fifth Generation’s mascot. It would matter more to the 2010s than most of ICOT’s racks.

The Fifth Generation remains useful as a historical instrument. It shows what a state thought intelligence was, in 1982: symbols, rules, parallelism, a language named for logic. A later state, anywhere, will pick a different mascot. The mistake is to treat the mascot as the science.
