Claude Shannon’s 1950 chess paper treated the game as a finite tree with an evaluation function at the leaves you can afford. That is still the honest description. Games are attractive because the rules do not argue back. A board position is public. A score is a score.

## From Shannon to a match in New York

Arthur Samuel’s checkers programs at IBM, in the late 1950s and after, showed that a machine could improve its own evaluation. Mac Hack VI, at MIT in the 1960s, played tournament chess. The 1970s and 1980s were an arms race of search tricks: alpha-beta, transposition tables, special-purpose hardware. In May 1997, IBM’s Deep Blue beat Garry Kasparov in a six-game match in New York. The public argued about whether a particular move was “human.” The engineering argument was simpler. Enough search, enough evaluation, a team that treated the match as an instrumented experiment.

## A television quiz

IBM’s Watson, on *Jeopardy!* in February 2011, was a different instrument. The game is language, timing, and a buzzer. The system described in the subsequent *IBM Journal* papers combined retrieval, many expert modules, and a betting policy. It was not a chess tree. It was a reminder that “winning on television” and “understanding” are not the same verb, a point Joseph Weizenbaum had already made about a much smaller program in 1966.

## Go, and a journal cover

David Silver, Aja Huang, and colleagues at DeepMind published “Mastering the Game of Go with Deep Neural Networks and Tree Search” in *Nature* on 28 January 2016. AlphaGo had already beaten Fan Hui in late 2015; the Lee Sedol match in Seoul followed in March 2016. The paper’s mix — policy and value networks, Monte Carlo tree search — is specific. The cultural aftershock was not. A game long used as a proverb about why computers would not soon be “intelligent” had a new score.

DeepMind’s later chess and shogi programs, and the AlphaZero paper, pushed the same family of methods toward learning from self-play with less human-featured evaluation. Those papers are public. They do not license a claim that every human practice is a board game.

## Why the laboratory keeps using toys

A game is a closed world with a metric. That is a scientific virtue and a philosophical vice. Newell and Simon used chess as a window on heuristic search. Hassabis’s group used Go as a window on deep reinforcement learning. The window shows the wall of the room. The street outside, as Winograd learned with blocks, is not obliged to have rules you can print on the box.

Demis Hassabis appears in this essay because the 2016 paper is a dated event, not because a company biography belongs here. The 2024 chemistry Nobel, for AlphaFold and related work, is a different instrument again: a scientific score, not a board.
