Slava Gerovitch’s *From Newspeak to Cyberspeak* (2002) is the book to keep on the desk for this essay. In the late Stalin years, “cybernetics” could be abused in the Soviet press as a bourgeois pseudoscience. After 1953 the weather changed. By the late 1950s and 1960s, the same word could be a vehicle for mathematicians, military planners, and economists to talk about control, information, and computers without always saying what they were doing.

## Institutes, not a Dartmouth

The Soviet story is not a summer in Hanover. It is institutes: in Moscow, in Kyiv, in Novosibirsk. Viktor Glushkov’s work on computers and automated management systems, the OGAS discussions about a nationwide economic information network, the long fights over whether such a network was a technical plan or a political threat — these are public in the archival and secondary literature. They are not a footnote to McCarthy.

Alexey Ivakhnenko, in Kyiv, published the Group Method of Data Handling in the late 1960s (*Soviet Automatic Control*, 1968; then *IEEE Transactions on Systems, Man, and Cybernetics*, 1971). GMDH grows polynomial models in layers. Later deep-learning retrospectives sometimes adopt Ivakhnenko as an ancestor. The adoption is fair only if we keep his context: inductive self-organization, cybernetics journals, a different argument about what “learning” was for.

## Translation and delay

English-speaking AI read Kolmogorov, sometimes, and Wiener, often. It read Soviet cybernetics late and through the Cold War’s distorting glass: as rivalry (Sputnik, then ICOT, then everything), or as absence. Papers existed. The citation graph did not always include them.

This series will not pretend to have restaged the Russian-language archive. It will insist on the structural fact. A history of AI that stays in Massachusetts and California is a history of a funding dialect, not of every machine that learned from data.

Academic visitors in the 1960s and 1970s — some of their trip reports later surfaced in archives — describe institutes that looked, to American eyes, like a parallel universe with different journals and the same Fourier transforms. Wiener himself had readers in translation. The 1948 book’s Soviet afterlife is one of Gerovitch’s subjects: a word that could be denounced and then required.

## After the word lost its heat

By the 1970s and 1980s, “cybernetics” in the USSR was sometimes a bureaucratic umbrella, sometimes a genuine research program, sometimes both. The word’s heat moved. What remains for this retrospective is a set of documented objects — GMDH, Glushkov’s institute, the Gerovitch narrative — that refuse the claim that intelligence research had a single capital city.
