MIT AI Laboratory memos, Stanford SAIL notes, and CMU technical reports were, for a long time, the true journal. A result could live for years as a stapled document with a number. *Artificial Intelligence* (the journal) and the IJCAI proceedings then gave the field a more public clock. NIPS, later NeurIPS, added a winter meeting that became, by the 2010s, a city.

The medium is not a side story. Priority fights, graduate careers, and what counts as a result all depend on what you can hold.

## The preprint bar

arXiv, born in physics in 1991, became the AI field’s front door in the 2010s. A CVPR or NeurIPS paper now often exists as a URL months before a stamp. The Transformer paper, the GPT-3 paper, the LLaMA paper — readers met them as PDFs with an identifier, not as a bound volume. Speed rose. So did the pile of near-duplicates.

OpenReview, for ICLR and then others, made reviews a public or semi-public object. That is a moral change as well as a logistical one. A rejected paper can still be a public argument.

Double-blind review, as NeurIPS and ICML practiced it, collided with arXiv’s speed. A paper could be famous and still “anonymous” to a reviewer who had a browser. The collision is documented in community blog posts and in program-chair letters. It is not a scandal so much as a medium refusing an older etiquette.

## Weights as a publication

Meta’s LLaMA (2023) and the later Llama 2 release, along with many subsequent open-weight models from other groups, turned a trained array into something that behaves like a paper: it circulates, it is forked, it is cited by hash and by Hugging Face card. Closed APIs turned a model into something that behaves like a utility. Both forms are now part of the historical record. Neither is identical to the 1960 *CACM* LISP paper, which you could retype.

## What to trust in a retrospective

This series prefers: a dated PDF with authors; a journal volume; a conference front matter; a government or prize citation. It treats company blogs as primary sources for product dates and as weak sources for scientific claims. It does not treat a keynote joke as a paper.

The 1955 Dartmouth proposal is itself a publication regime: a foundation document, later reprinted in *AI Magazine* in 2006 so that a new century could footnote it. We are doing a similar thing here, in staged Markdown, without pretending the staging is the archive.
