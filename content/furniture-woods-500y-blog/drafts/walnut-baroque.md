# Walnut in the Baroque rooms

When Charles II came back in 1660, a London chair changed color. Cane in the seat, scroll in the stretcher, and a thin skin of *Juglans regia* — grey-brown, streaked, sometimes an oyster of transverse slices — over a carcase of oak or deal. Macquoid later called the years to 1720 the Age of Walnut. French and Italian rooms had already been there. Dutch taste arrived with people and with prints. Adam Bowett’s *English Furniture 1660–1714: From Charles II to Queen Anne* is the construction-and-trade book that keeps the oysters attached to a shop, not to a mood. Open the drawer of a William-and-Mary card table. The sides are pale. The grain does not match the top. That is correct.

## Two walnuts, one fashionable skin

*Juglans regia* is the European or “English” walnut — also Persian, French, Circassian, Italian, depending on the dealer’s poetry. Heartwood grey-brown to chocolate with dark streaks. Figure can be wild. The tree is smaller and more crotched than American black walnut; Restoration shops used it as veneer because the log asked for that. Solid *regia* exists on provincial pieces and on parts that had to take a joint — a leg, a stile. The show surfaces of polite London work are often a slice.

*Juglans nigra*, American black walnut, is a taller, straighter gift. The *Wood Handbook* (FPL-GTR-190, table 5–3a) puts it at specific gravity 0.55 and Janka 1,010 lbf at 12 percent moisture — softer than white oak (1,360 / 0.68), hard enough for a chair, friendly to tools. FPL’s species note still reads like a love letter: straight grain, easy to work, stable, takes a natural finish, much valued for furniture. About three-quarters of the commercial wood, the handbook said, comes from the Central States. In the Baroque hour *nigra* is not yet London’s fashion wood. It is already a colonial primary. The Queen Anne draft takes the American solid chair. This draft stays with the European skin.

Butternut (*J. cinerea*) is the pale cousin, used for carving and cheap imitation, now a canker story `[CITE NEEDED for a current USFS status note]`.

Walnut is semi-ring-porous. The face has a gentle grain, not oak’s trenches. It planes like a courtesy. It carves without coarse red oak’s crumbling. Burls and crotches are structural chaos and visual wealth. Oyster veneer — transverse slices laid like shells — used walnut, laburnum, and olive. The technique is of this moment; the monopoly is not. Figure 3 is why a veneer shop cares about cut: a transverse oyster is not a flatsawn cathedral and not a quartered flake. It is the log as a stack of coins.

Movement is moderate for a hardwood. It is not teak. A badly made pine or oak ground will still telegraph. Hide glue gone brittle will let a burl check. Cross-banding and a proper substrate are the quiet half of the fashion.

## Restoration, William and Mary, the court color

Restoration cane-back chairs, high-back scrolls, twist-turned legs, the first polite card tables, seaweed marquetry on olive and walnut grounds — the literature is thick because the rooms wanted to look like the court. Macquoid’s second volume is the gallery walk. Bowett 1660–1714 is the one that ties a cane chair to a joinery shop and a veneer merchant.

Dutch taste came with a Dutch king in 1689 and had been coming in objects before that. French taste came because France was the factory of fashion; André-Jacob Roubo’s *L’Art du menuisier* (1769–75) is later, but the Parisian veneer habit is earlier than Charles II. English trees and imported *regia* fed a trade that treated the figured log as a skin. Oak did not leave the kingdom. It left the rooms that wanted to look current.

Seaweed marquetry is a fine end-grain and veneer drawing in pale and dark woods, often on a walnut field. It is a different contract from Chinnery’s pinned oak. The panel may still float. The picture does not like a ground that cups. Figure 4 places walnut’s hardness among the anchors: carvable, not soft as pine, not trench-pored as oak.

Do not invent a royal commission or a price per foot of oyster. Named pieces live in museum records — Wallace Collection notes help for later French marquetry; English Restoration chairs live in V&A and country-house lists `[CITE NEEDED before attaching a specific inventory number to a claim]`. The type is secure. The invoice is not, unless you have it.

## Joinery

Veneer on an oak or deal carcase is the Baroque walnut contract. The carcase is joined: mortise-and-tenon frames, pinned or glued, drawer runners, a dustboard of deal. The show face is hide-glued. A bookmatched pair of crotch leaves on a cabinet door is a sawyer’s flitch opened like a page. Oysters are laid in a field, often framed with a stringing line of holly or box. The edge of the carcase, if honest, shows the pale ground.

Cane seats are a woven panel in a grooved frame. The frame is walnut, solid, with stretchers that may be scrolled or twisted. The twist is a lathe job on a square blank; walnut takes it. Oak did it earlier and thicker. The cane fails first. Replacement cane is expected. Look at the frame’s joints to date the chair.

Twist turning, scroll brackets, and a carved crest sit on solid walnut or on a walnut-faced construction. Draw-boring is less the religion than it was in oak; glue and a tight shoulder do more work in a smaller section. A loose Restoration chair is often a failed glue line at a stretcher, not a lozenged pin.

Cane fails in a predictable order: the weave at the front rail, then the holes worn oval. A restorer who recanes and then stains the new cane dark to “match 1680” is matching a tea advertisement. Period cane was paler. The frame’s oxidized walnut is the old color; the seat was a light grid. Look at the groove that holds the cane spline. A later tacked-on sheet of machine cane is a different joint. The Baroque chair is a grooved frame. Keep the groove.

Drawer dovetails in this hour can be fine. Sides are oak or deal. Bottoms are planed, bevelled into grooves. A plywood bottom is a later marriage. The Queen Anne highboy with an MDF dustboard is a warning from another century.

Table tops in solid walnut exist on provincial pieces. Polite London card tables are often veneered, with a deal or oak core, rule joints on later leaves, hinged flaps. If the ground moves and the veneer does not, the oysters check. Humidity in a 1690 house went up and down with the fire. Central heating is crueler.

Seaweed and arabesque marquetry want a stable ground and a toothing plane. The cutter leaves a keyed surface for hide glue. Sanding that surface off in a modern refinish is how a picture lifts.

## Finish and light

Old French polish on *regia* can look golden. The wood darkens, then some of the red and the gold leave in a south window and you get a flat brown. Oil goes deeper. A thick polyurethane film looks sealed-in, like furniture afraid of itself. Stain is rarely required on heartwood and often muddies the grain. Sapwood is the nuisance: pale, left visible on some period pieces, steamed away in modern mills. Disclose the steam on a new board. A 1690 oyster does not have it.

Iron and walnut are kinder than iron and oak, but water stains and sun fade are the daily enemies. A card-table leaf that lived closed is darker than the one that faced the window. That is a record, not a mismatch to “correct.”

## Conservation

Black walnut is a managed American hardwood, not a CITES appendix tree. *J. regia* in parts of its range has been pressured by veneer markets `[CITE NEEDED for a current IUCN or national status]`. Stolen backyard *nigra* in the last twenty years is a real crime file `[CITE NEEDED: named prosecutions]`. A cheap wide slab from an unnamed seller has a past. This hour’s objects are mostly *regia* veneer. The ethical buy of a new “Baroque walnut” suite is a binomial and an edge: solid, veneered, or printed.

Hide glue is reversible. PVA floods and stays. A restorer who wants the oysters to be removable later keeps the period adhesive. Brittle glue and a checking burl want humidity first, not a flood of finish.

Lead in later overpaint. Worm in deal grounds. A carcase that is later than its veneer is a common marriage.

## What to look for

Pale drawer sides, oysters or burl on the show face, cane in a grooved frame, twist legs, seaweed on a box, a deal dustboard. A 1970s “Mediterranean” suite with printed walnut grain is a poster. A 1690 table with a pine drawer is a shop telling the truth. Heart versus sap, solid versus a 2 mm jacket, European streak versus a later American chocolate — the edge still ends the argument.

Macquoid gave the age a name. The drawer still gives the construction.
