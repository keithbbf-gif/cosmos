# Ebony, inlay, and the piano key

André-Charles Boulle’s cabinets in the Wallace Collection are brass and turtle cut as one picture, the black ground a *Diospyros* that takes a polish like stone. The name *ébéniste* comes from this wood. Most of the job, by the time Riesener was shading a flower in kingwood, was a tropical palette that was not ebony. The black that people mean when they say the word is still a small, heavy, fine-textured heartwood used for lines, keys, and the counterpart to brass — not for dining tables. Kukachka (FPL-125) put African commercial ebony at about 63 pounds per cubic foot and Ceylon *D. ebenum* at about 73. A solid Gabon chest would be a monster of weight. A piano’s sharp keys, when they were wood, were this genus in strips.

## Several blacks, one trade name

African ebony of commerce, Kukachka writes, is believed to be *Diospyros crassiflora* and *D. piscatoria* — small trees, West Africa, color from patchy to a uniform black, the best jet attributed to *D. crassiflora*. Very fine texture, very hard, about 63 pounds per cubic foot. Seasons rapidly in small sizes, tends to split or distort. Best worked with metal-working equipment. Specialty uses: small parts of musical instruments, carvings, turnery.

East Indian and Ceylon ebonies: *D. melanoxylon*, *D. ebenum*, Andaman *D. marmorata*. Ceylon ebony extremely heavy, fine, even, black, with irregular light-brown streaks less loud than in most East Indian woods; about 73 pounds per cubic foot. The black portions of *D. ebenum* develop long, fine, deep cracks if you try to season large dimensions. Convert small, store under cover. *D. melanoxylon* is the species some older notes say to girdle and stand before felling `[CITE NEEDED: confirm Kukachka’s girdling report against a forest-department source]`. Macassar (*D. celebica*) and Andaman woods are the striped brown-black that Art Deco treated as a modern luxury. Compiled Janka for Gabon-type ebony is commonly given near 3,080 lbf; Ceylon and Macassar compilations sit in the same brutal neighborhood. Figure 4 will not make ebony look like mahogany (about 800 lbf, SG 0.45–0.50). It is off the scatter’s polite cluster.

American persimmon (*D. virginiana*) is the temperate cousin: golf-club heads, shuttles, a pale wood with a dark heart when it has one. It is not a piano-key ebony. Do not sell it as one.

African blackwood is *Dalbergia melanoxylon* — Kukachka’s 70 pounds per cubic foot at 6 percent moisture, a clarinet wood. The genus is rosewood. The color is black. The name fools buyers. Keep the binomials apart.

## Boulle, stringing, and the black that is a finish

Boulle work is a sandwich: brass and turtle (and sometimes pewter) sawn together, laid into or onto an ebony or ebonized ground, the première-partie and contre-partie reversing which material is the field. The Wallace Collection’s notes are the place to see it without a romance. The ground must be stable and black through or black enough that a chip does not flash pear. Some period “ebony” grounds are ebonized pear, maple, or a softwood with a film. That is a historic finish, not a fraud, if named. A lab test is what a customs officer may want. A chip is what a restorer can get.

Federal American inlay used ebony stringing — or a black line that wants to be ebony. Holly and maple are the pale partners; satinwood the yellow; mahogany the field (Bowett’s Atlantic *Swietenia* after 1721/1722, now in New York and Philadelphia shops). A line a millimeter wide does not need a Gabon log. It needs a dense black that will not crumble under the scratch stock. Boxwood is the pale opposite number in many English lines. The boxwood draft in this series is that millimeter. This draft is the black one.

Empire furniture sometimes quotes Boulle with gilt-bronze mounts on crotch mahogany. The mounts are screwed. Boulle is sawn. The Regency-Empire draft makes that distinction. Do not flatten them.

Victorian ebonized suites — Eastlake and the factory cousins — are often beech or a pale hardwood under black varnish. Look at a worn arris. If the brown shows, you have a finish. If the black goes through a dense, fine pore and the piece weighs like a stone fragment, you may have *Diospyros*. Most parlor “ebony” is the finish.

## Piano keys and the piano case

The case and the keys are different contracts. A Victorian parlor piano may wear a Brazilian rosewood case (*D. nigra*, Appendix I) on a pine or poplar ground — the rosewood-Victorian draft. The keys, when they were wood and ivory, were ivory naturals and ebony sharps, or later celluloid and a stained hardwood. Kukachka’s “small parts of musical instruments” is the sharp key, the tailpiece, the frog, the fitting — not the case. The case is a carcase. The key is a strip.

Ivory is its own appendix story. This draft will not invent a date for the plastic takeover of the natural key `[CITE NEEDED: a dated industry shift from ivory/ebony to celluloid and later plastics]`. What a restorer can still say: a sharp that is *Diospyros* is a small, legal-in-place antique part and a paperwork part if you ship a set of raw key blanks. A sharp that is stained maple is a finish. Look at the end grain of a loose key.

The piano case in rosewood or mahogany is veneer on a ground, as crotch mahogany on yellow-poplar was in the Empire hour. Ebony as a case wood is rare and usually a skin or a stringing, not a slab. When Art Deco uses Macassar, it is a sliced face on a cheap core: a cabinet, a clock, a radio, a picture of stripe. The plywood draft is the industrial version of that skin.

Stringed-instrument fittings — tailpieces, frogs, tuning pegs, chinrests — are Kukachka’s “small parts” in another shop. A cello peg is a turning problem in a brittle, fine wood. A piano sharp is a planing problem in a short strip. Neither is a furniture carcase. The cabinet shop that keeps a bar of Gabon for stringing is closer to the ébéniste than to the piano factory. The scratch stock that cuts a one-millimeter groove in a mahogany rail is the Federal tool. The black line glued into that groove is this chapter. If the line is stained holly, say so on the repair ticket.

Antwerp and Paris ebony cabinets of the 17th century — architectural pieces with ripple moldings, often with painted or pietre-dure panels — are the large objects that seem to contradict the “no dining table” rule. Look again. Much of the black is veneer or a thin solid on a softwood carcase. The weight is still ugly. The show is a skin. Ripple moldings are a separate small-wood problem, often in ebony or an ebonized fruitwood, cut against a rocking molding plane or a dedicated engine. Do not invent a particular cabinet’s invoice. The Rijksmuseum and the V&A object records are the place to start.

## Workability: metal tools, chatter, a fine dust

Kukachka is blunt. African ebony: metal-working equipment. East Indian ebonies: extremely hard by hand and machine, considerable dulling, brittle, harsh cut, fine sawdust. Hold the work firmly in the planer or it chatters. A very smooth finish is possible. The cabinet trade used it primarily for inlay. That sentence should govern new design. You are not making a tabletop.

Seasoning cracks in Ceylon black are why furniture-sized boards are a bad idea even before the law. Convert small. Store covered. Do not start with a wide flatsawn plank and hope.

Glue is possible on a clean, fresh surface. Oil is less a teak problem than a finish-contamination problem. Dust collection is hygiene. The sawdust is fine enough to float.

## Figure 5 and the listings

Madagascar *Diospyros* populations have been listed (CoP16 and after) after documented illegal trade. Check Species+; annotations change. African and Asian ebonies sit in a mix of appendix and national-law controls that this draft will not pretend to freeze. Figure 5 is illustrative. Confirm at publication.

Brazilian rosewood (*Dalbergia nigra*) is Appendix I — the piano-case wood, not the key wood. The rest of *Dalbergia* went onto Appendix II on 2 January 2017, Annotation #15. Neotropical *Swietenia macrophylla* entered Appendix II on 15 November 2003. A single instrument can hold all three papers: rosewood case, mahogany veneer elsewhere, ebony sharps. The owner who ships the piano learns this the hard way.

The U.S. Lacey Act (2008 amendment) and APHIS Phase VII (1 December 2024) sit on top. Antique inlay already in the country is usually legal to own. A bag of new Gabon blanks from an unnamed seller is a different object.

## Substitutes the shop has always used

Ebonized pear, maple, holly, beech. Dyed walnut. Polymer keys. Macassar prints on lauan. The honest sentence is the finish name or the binomial. “Ebony piano” without a look at the keys and the case is two guesses.

Bowett’s mahogany lane taught Europe to spend a tropical hardwood in the solid. Ebony never got that hour at dining-table scale. It stayed a millimeter, a key, a ground for brass. That is not a lesser job. It is the job the density allows.

A restorer replacing a lost Federal stringing line should document the scrap. New Gabon is a permit question. Dyed holly is the old shop answer and the honest one if named. A restorer replacing a set of sharp keys on an 1880 piano is in the instrument trade, not the furniture trade, and should read the current annotation before ordering blanks. The case may be Appendix I rosewood. The keys may be a different paper. Treat them as two objects that happen to share a stand.

## What to look for

A Boulle field that is black through, or a film on pear. A Federal stringing line. A sharp key’s end grain. A Macassar stripe that stops at a ply edge. An ebonized Eastlake chair with brown in the wear. Weight that surprises you in a small box.

Arkansas does not grow furniture ebony. Persimmon grows; it is not a key wood in the Gabon sense. A shop that wants a black line can ebonize holly or maple and say so. The Wallace cabinet is the museum supply of the other look.
