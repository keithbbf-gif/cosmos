# White pine and the softwood economy

A mast pond at Portsmouth, New Hampshire, held *Pinus strobus* that the Crown had already claimed with a Broad Arrow blaze. The tree was a taper of clear wood tall enough to step a ship. Albion’s *Forests and Sea Power* is the English strategic reading of that mark; the Massachusetts charter tradition of 1691 is the colonial statute half `[CITE NEEDED for the exact 1691 wording]`. Furniture never ate those sticks first. It ate the boards that came after the mast trade, then the lake-state clears that built interiors from Boston to Chicago, then the knotty seconds that twentieth-century dens called rustic. White pine is a softwood economy that sometimes made a chest. The chest is not the point of the tree. It is the remainder that still furnishes rooms.

## The handbook tree

Eastern white pine is the light, even, low-resin pine of the Northeast and the Great Lakes. FPL-GTR-190, table 5–3a: specific gravity about 0.35, Janka about 380 lbf at 12 percent moisture. Shrinkage green to ovendry on the order of 2.1 percent radial, 6.1 percent tangential, 8.2 percent volumetric. Compared with white oak at 1,360 lbf and SG 0.68, or black walnut at 1,010 lbf and 0.55, it is the soft end of Figure 4. A buckle dents a tavern table. A dropped pot leaves a crescent. That is the species, not a defect label.

Heartwood is light brown with a red hint and darkens in air. Sapwood is paler and wide on second-growth trees. Old-growth clears — long, knot-free, slow enough to look like cream — were the interior-joinery grade: doors, wainscot in the American sense (wall boards, not Baltic oak), cupboard interiors, clock cases in New England that wanted light weight and a paint or veneer ground. The *Wood Handbook* still lists furniture among uses. The yard that can sell that clear today is selling a scarce remnant or a carefully graded modern piece, not 1850 ordinary stock.

Resin canals exist but are shy next to southern yellow pine. The latewood band is modest. The face can take a clear finish without the corrugated argument of *P. palustris*. Stain still blotches if you insist: earlywood drinks. Paint is the historic finish that understood the tree.

Michael Williams’s *Americans and Their Forests* is the geography book: the white-pine cut moved from New England inland and west as stands fell. Furniture factories in later decades used what the construction trade left. A Grand Rapids crate and a Boston chimney-breast can be the same species in different grades.

## Masts, boards, and the civilian remainder

England called the tree Weymouth pine. The navy wanted length and a clean taper. The Broad Arrow reserved the large stems. Smaller trees and the tops became boards. After independence the mark lost its Crown, not its mill. New England yards cut white pine for export and for domestic build. The colonial-pine draft is the painted chest of 1630–1780. This draft is the commodity: how a forest became dimension lumber, and how shops specified a furniture grade inside that flood.

Baltic deal — *P. sylvestris* and spruce — was Britain’s imported softwood. White pine was the North American answer to the same job: carcases, backs, painted rooms, the hide under a show wood. A London bill that says deal and a Boston bill that says pine are cousins in function. They are not the same ring. Repair an English dustboard with northern European pine; repair a Connecticut case with *strobus*. Southern yellow pine’s loud latewood is a third voice.

Do not invent a mast-price or a board-foot series. Albion and the customs books are where those figures live when a copy is open. The economy can be named without a fake invoice: reserved trees, then a lumber cut, then a furniture use on the grades that would not floor a mill or step a ship.

Clock shops liked white pine for cases because the movement is modest and the box is light on a wall. Case furniture in the Federal hour often hides pine or poplar behind mahogany or cherry. Open the drawer. The side that is pale and dented, with resin canals, is the economy talking.

## Joinery

Pine takes nails. That fact built more American rooms than dovetails did. The fiber crushes and grips. A rose-head nail in a boarded chest is period manners. Glue holds on a clean, low-resin face. Hide glue on a pine carcase under walnut veneer is the English and American sandwich of the walnut hour; the ground is often deal in London and white pine or poplar in the colonies.

Dovetails in pine must be cut fat. A slender mahogany tail in 380 lbf stock chips. Large, few tails on a colonial or country drawer are competence, not clumsiness. Later factory drawers in white pine can be machine-dovetailed and still correct for the wood.

Frame-and-panel in pine works if the panel floats. The wood moves less than oak, not zero. A wide flatsawn lid still cups. Battens, slot-screwed breadboards, and painted finishes that do not glue a film across a check are the old answers. A steel screw without a pilot splits a thin rail. Pegs are kinder.

Turned pine appears on cheap tables and some clock finials. It dents in the lathe if the tool is dull. Maple took the better turnings (Forman). Pine took the painted ones.

Interior joinery — doors, shutters, stair parts — uses the same mortise-and-tenon grammar as furniture, often with hide glue and a painted finish. A “furniture” table from house-joinery stock is a common afterlife. Look for hinge gains and a paint ghost.

Veneer on white pine is ordinary in the nineteenth century: a thin mahogany or walnut face on a light ground. The edge tells. A 12 mm pine core with a sliced face is a legal cabinet door if named. It is a slight if sold as a solid hardwood slab.

## The knotty century and the vanished clear

Twentieth-century American dens paneled in knotty pine made a fashion of the grade old-growth millers rejected for furniture. Colonial Revival factories added machine distress. Some of it is charming. Some of it is a cartoon of a six-board chest. Mid-century taste hid pine, then country taste brought it back. The clear old-growth door in a Federal house and the knotty rec-room panel are the same binomial. They are not the same material.

Lake-state logging left cut-over and second growth. Furniture from that second growth is knotty, wider-ringed, still 380 lbf, still willing to paint. Using remaining old-growth clears for a suburban dining table spends a structural and historic grade on a use pine already served in paint. Plantation and managed white pine for painted case work is the ordinary modern specification.

Walk a Saginaw or Muskegon mill town in the later nineteenth century and the product on the dock is dimension lumber first: boards for Chicago balloon frames, clears for doors, boxboards for freight. Williams maps that cut as a moving front. A furniture shop in the same town bought the grade that would not make a door stile — knots, pith, a waney edge — and painted it. A clockmaker in Boston or Connecticut still wanted the clear: light on a wall, stable enough that a painted or veneered case would not rack the movement. The two shops named the same tree and did not compete for the same board.

A counting-house interior in Salem or Boston — shutters, a desk, a railing — can be white pine throughout, painted or grained to look like oak. Graining is a skill. It is also how a softwood economy imitated a hardwood room without paying Baltic wainscot freight. Scrape a shutter’s bottom edge in a house you own. The cream wood and the shy resin canal are the tell. Leave a museum shutter alone.

Western pines enter later catalogs as “knotty pine” without a binomial. Ask. *P. strobus* is eastern. The handbook numbers above are for that species. A different pine is a different latewood.

## Conservation

Not CITES. Old-growth white pine in the East is a remnant ecology and a scarce lumber grade. Williams is the historical geography; living-forest policy is a separate literature `[CITE NEEDED before naming a particular remaining stand as a furniture source]`. Lacey still applies to imported “white pine” that may be mixed. Antique pine is reuse. New “reclaimed white pine” from a barn can be honest; a container of mixed softwood with a New England story is not.

Lead in old paint. Active powder-post in sapwood. A mast-pond history, if claimed, implies a waterlogged past; dry the board to the destination humidity before a glued joint.

## What to look for

Weight (low), dents, shy resin canals, a paint history, a carcase under a darker face, clock-case lightness, a clear door versus a knotty panel. A dealer’s “pumpkin pine” is a color word for old oxidized heartwood, not a species. End grain still has to be *strobus*.

The Broad Arrow was a navy claim. The blanket chest was what the household kept. Both were white pine. Only one of them was trying to be furniture. A mast pond in January and a painted cupboard in a heated parlor are the same species at two moisture contents. Dry the board to the room before you call it furniture stock.
