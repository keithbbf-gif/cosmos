# Birdseye maple and the factory pale

A mill in the Great Lakes belt opens a sugar-maple log and finds a constellation. The leaves go to a veneer dryer, then to a factory that will glue them onto a cheaper ground for a Federal-revival card table, a radio cabinet, a 1940s bedroom suite whose catalogue says “maple” and means a color. The same genus turns a Windsor leg that takes a crisp bead, and it floors a bowling alley because the Janka number is 1,450 lbf and the fiber does not fuzz under feet. Figure is not a species. Factory pale is not a forest.

## Hard, soft, and the English “sycamore”

Hard maple is *Acer saccharum* (sugar, rock) and *A. nigrum* (black). The *Wood Handbook* (FPL-GTR-190, table 5–3a) gives sugar maple specific gravity 0.63 and Janka 1,450 lbf at 12 percent moisture — among the hardest of the ordinary American furniture woods, in a class with white oak and above cherry and walnut. Figure 4 places that hardness among the series’ ten anchors. Heartwood is light reddish brown; sapwood is wide and white. Texture is fine and even. Grain is usually straight until it is not.

Soft maple is mostly *A. rubrum* (red) and *A. saccharinum* (silver). Red maple: SG 0.54, Janka 950 lbf — cherry’s neighbor. It machines well and takes paint and stain. It is not a failed hard maple. It is a different ticket. Factory “maple” bedroom suites of the Colonial Revival years were often a red-orange stain on whichever pale hardwood the plant had, soft maple among them. A lens on an unseen rail. End grain: diffuse pores, no oak vessels, no pine resin canals. Weight surprises people who thought pale meant pine.

European furniture “sycamore” is *Acer pseudoplatanus*, a maple. Quartered, it can be pale and ray-flecked. Dyed grey it became harewood for Hepplewhite and Sheraton inlay. American sycamore is *Platanus occidentalis*, a different family. The word is a trap already named in this series. Bigleaf maple (*A. macrophyllum*) on the Pacific coast has its own figured trade. Regional, not a substitute binomial for sugar maple.

## Figure is a cut and a defect, not a tree

Birdseye, curly (fiddleback), quilted, blistered — grain deviations, more common in hard maple, not exclusive to it. A shop pays for figure by the leaf. Catalogues that say “tiger maple” are selling curl. The Federal inlay tradition used the pale sap and the figure as a stand-in for satinwood when satinwood was a ship away. A Connecticut cherry chest with maple inlay is a conversation among species, not a single-wood object. The satinwood draft returns to that wish.

Figure 3 is the cut geometry. Birdseye reads on a flatsawn face as small circular disruptions; it is not a ray flake and not a cathedral. Curl shows as a washboard across the grain, louder on a quartered or rift face when the fiber undulates. Quilt is a rarer topographic figure, more a Pacific and a specialty-log story. A kitchen island in plain hard maple is still hard maple. It is allowed to be boring and hard.

Tear-out on curl is sharp blades and light cuts, a scraper, sometimes a back-bevel. Filling birdseye so it does not pit takes patience and a finish that does not shrink out of the eyes. The surface photographs like water. Veneer factories sliced the best logs because a solid birdseye board wide enough for a table is a trophy and a movement risk. Hide glue on a deal or yellow-poplar ground is the historic sandwich. A plywood ground is the later sandwich. A paper foil on particleboard is a photograph of birdseye. The edge tells you which contract you bought.

## Chairs, Shakers, and a wood that mostly refuses the steam box

Nancy Goyne Evans keeps finding maple in the turned members of American Windsors even when the seat is elm or pine and the bow is oak. That is a job list, not a species patriotism. Hard maple turns a crisp bead and wears like a tool. Chair shops in Philadelphia, New York, and New England bought it by the wagon from the seventeenth century on. It does not steam-bend like ash or beech. The fiber is hard and short-tempered in the box; shops that need a hoop use ash, oak, or hickory. Maple takes the lathe. Beech takes Thonet’s radius. Do not credit maple with the café chair.

Shaker work at Hancock, Sabbathday Lake, and Pleasant Hill uses maple where a piece must stay true: chairs, stands, sometimes peg rails, the understructure of tables (Kassay; Rieman and Burks). The slat-back with a tape seat and a pommel finial is often maple. Tape — cotton or wool, later a synthetic — weaves through holes in the seat rails. The holes want a wood that will not crumble. Maple is that wood. The oval box is the mixed-wood cousin: maple sides, steamed and fingered in a swallow-tail, copper tacks, a pine top and bottom. The maple takes a gentler bend than a Windsor hoop; the fingers and the tacks are the visible fasteners. A box in sapele from a weekend class is a shape. The cherry draft takes the face wood that darkens. This draft keeps the pale members.

Hard maple and nails are a fight. Predrill. Screws too. The wood has no tyloses story and no teak oil story. It does not like weather. Ambrosia streaks — beetle wound response — are a color fashion of the last twenty years, not a separate tree and not an antique grade.

## Factory blonde, gym floors, and the word on the tag

The twentieth century split the name. School furniture and bowling alleys and gym floors kept using hard maple because the Janka number is not a metaphor. Factory catalogues used “maple” as a color. Colonial Revival suites pushed a red-orange toner. Mid-century blonde was often a clear finish on sapwood, sometimes a light toner — the pale dining room next to the teak that got the magazine covers. Blotch in stain is worse on soft maple. Dye and a finish that does not pretend the wood is walnut will treat you better.

The white sapwood yellows in UV. A “white maple” kitchen in a south window will cream. Water-white finishes slow this; they do not repeal it. Thermal darkening is a cherry trick more than a maple one; maple’s drama is figure and stain, not russet.

Biedermeier used maple when birch was not the local tree. French *érable* appears in provincial work. English satinwood-age furniture used harewood as a grey field. None of these are the Great Lakes veneer mill, and all of them are the same genus doing a pale job.

Sugar maple is a managed northern hardwood. FPL still centers commercial lumber in the Middle Atlantic and Great Lakes, about two-thirds of the cut. Climate and sugar-bush economics affect the furniture log. Soft maple is abundant in many eastern stands. Neither is CITES. The fashion for wide live-edge slabs asks a sugar maple for a shape it does not want to give. That is ordinary forestry, not an appendix.

Factory conversion is the other half of birdseye’s life. Rotary and sliced veneer mills paid for the eyes. A leaf a millimeter thick on a yellow-poplar or basswood ground is how a 1920s radio cabinet and a 1940s suite got a constellation they could not have afforded in the solid. The glue line is the tell. Solid birdseye in a drawer front is a small-shop or a high-style choice. Solid birdseye in an eight-foot table is a trophy and a cupping risk; flatsawn figure still moves on the tangential number. Quartersawn curl (fiddleback) is the instrument-maker’s cut: stripe across the width, less width-movement than a flatsawn eye board. Figure 3 is that argument. A mill that sells “figured maple” as a mixed bundle is telling you to specify eyes or curl if you care which photograph you bought.

School desks and gym floors kept the plain hard maple because denting is a use, not a fashion. The factory bedroom used the name because pale hardwood was cheap and stain was cheaper. Those two objects share a binomial and nothing else in the room. A bowling alley is a wear surface. A Colonial Revival headboard is a color. Say which job. Inlay stringing in maple is the cheap pale line the boxwood draft will name as a substitute; it works on a Federal apron if you accept a softer fingernail test. Maple will not take Thonet’s café radius. It will take a gentle oval-box bend. Keep the jobs apart.

## What to look for

End grain first. Diffuse pores. Weight. Figure across the light — eyes, curl, or plain. On a Windsor: a turned leg that takes a bead. On a Shaker chair: tape holes that have not crumbled, a pommel that is crisp, not fat. On a Federal card table: birdseye or curl as a satinwood wish, often veneer. On a 1940s suite: a color called maple that chips to a different wood. On a gym floor: the real thing, dented, still pale.

Arkansas is not Vermont. Hard maple is a northern commercial center; soft maple and scattered hard maple still enter southern shops. A designer specifying maple in the South is often specifying shipped stock. Bradley Brand works that shipped hard maple when a pale, dent-resistant top is the job — the same *Acer saccharum*, not a local sugar bush. Say hard or soft. Say figured or plain. The word alone is fog.
