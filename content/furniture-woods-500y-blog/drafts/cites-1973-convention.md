# The 1973 Convention and a furniture crate

On 3 March 1973, in Washington, eighty-odd delegations signed a trade convention that did not mention Chippendale, a dining leaf, or a piano case. The instrument is the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora. Article XXII set entry into force at ninety days after the tenth ratification. That date is 1 July 1975. The United States had already ratified, on 14 January 1974; for the first ten Parties, including the U.S., the Convention bound them on the same July morning. A crate of sawn wood that cleared a dock in June 1975 and a crate of the same species that cleared in August 1975 are not the same legal object if the species had been placed on an appendix. This draft walks that paper. It is not a permit. It will not issue one. Species+ and the national Management Authority are the living pages.

## What the Convention actually is

CITES is a trade convention among Parties. It is not a forestry code and not a furniture statute. Article II splits listed species into three appendices. Appendix I is species threatened with extinction that are or may be affected by trade; commercial trade in wild specimens is not a furniture plan. Appendix II is species that may become threatened unless trade is regulated, plus look-alikes that customs cannot tell apart; trade is allowed with permits and a non-detriment finding. Appendix III is a species a Party asks others to help control. The text lives at cites.org. The lookup for a binomial on an invoice is Species+.

Each Party names a Management Authority and a Scientific Authority. In the United States the Fish and Wildlife Service holds the management seat for most CITES timber. The Scientific Authority is the one that must be able to say, in writing, that a proposed export will not be detrimental to the survival of the species. That sentence is the non-detriment finding. A factory in High Point cannot write it. A designer in Arkansas cannot write it. A magazine chapter cannot write it.

Annotations are the fine print that decide whether a finished chair is a specimen. Some listings cover the whole animal or the whole tree. Timber listings often name commodities: logs, sawn wood, veneer sheets, plywood. Annotation #6, which later attached to neotropical *Swietenia macrophylla*, is that list. Annotation #15, which later attached to *Dalbergia* (except *D. nigra*), is a different list with exemptions. The Convention of 1973 did not invent those numbers. Conferences of the Parties added them as furniture and musical-instrument trade forced the text to get specific. Figure 5 is a schematic of origin regions, not a current appendix. ## A crate, a code, and a binomial

Picture a crate on a Savannah or Long Beach dock in 1976. The packing list says “wood furniture” or “mahogany lumber.” The Harmonized System as we now use it did not yet rule every invoice; the point is the same. Customs needs a name. The Convention needs a species. “Mahogany” is a color word. *Swietenia macrophylla*, *Khaya ivorensis*, and a rotary face of *Shorea* are three different objects. CITES does not grade the chair. It asks whether the specimen in the crate is a listed taxon, from a listed population, in a listed form.

If the answer is Appendix I, commercial import of a new wild specimen is the wrong plan. Brazilian rosewood, *Dalbergia nigra*, went onto Appendix I later (CoP8, in force 11 June 1992). If the answer is Appendix II, the exporting Party’s Management Authority must have issued an export permit, backed by a non-detriment finding. Some Appendix I movements also need an import permit on the receiving side. If the answer is “not listed,” CITES is quiet. Other laws may not be. The U.S. Lacey Act as amended in 2008, Food, Conservation, and Energy Act §8204, makes illegally harvested plant product a crime whether or not the tree sits on an appendix. APHIS Phase VII declarations, 1 December 2024, reach more furniture HTS codes. Those instruments are later drafts. They sit on top of 1975, they do not replace it.

A pre-Convention certificate exists for specimens acquired before the listing bound the Parties. Antique annotations exist in some later texts. Neither is a postcard you write yourself. USFWS will tell you. A dealer’s shrug will not.

## What 1973 did not settle

The Washington text did not list big-leaf mahogany. It did not list the rosewood genus. It did not list Madagascar ebony. *Swietenia humilis* appears on Appendix II from the Convention’s own first day, 1 July 1975, with a parts annotation that has since been revised `[CITE NEEDED: original 1975 annotation wording versus current #4]`. *S. mahagoni* waited until 1992. Neotropical populations of *S. macrophylla* went onto Appendix II at CoP12 (Santiago, 2002), in force 15 November 2003, Annotation #6: logs, sawn wood, veneer sheets, plywood. The whole genus *Dalbergia* (except *D. nigra*, already I) went onto Appendix II at CoP17, in force 2 January 2017, Annotation #15, revised at CoP18 in 2019 so finished musical instruments and small finished products — wood of the listed species up to 10 kg per shipment — would not freeze concerts and jewelry boxes. Madagascar populations of *Diospyros* went onto Appendix II at CoP16, in force 12 June 2013, Annotation #5.

Those dates belong in the later slugs. The 1973 draft exists so a reader can see the machine before the species fall into it. Conferences of the Parties amend appendices. Reservations exist. A Party can enter a reservation and be treated, for that listing, as a non-Party, with the paperwork that implies. India entered a reservation on the 2017 *Dalbergia* genus listing `[CITE NEEDED: current status of that reservation]`. The Convention still runs.

National implementing statutes are where a crate actually stops. CITES “does not take the place of national laws,” the Secretariat’s own explainer says. It is a framework. The U.S. Endangered Species Act and the implementing regulations at 50 C.F.R. part 23 are the American teeth. A shop that treats a CITES.org printout as a license has misread both documents.

## Furniture language for the three appendices

Appendix I, said as furniture: do not commission a new dining table in a wild Appendix I timber. *Dalbergia nigra* is the famous case. Own the 1880 piano if it is already in the country. Ask before you export it. A new “Brazilian rosewood suite” from an unnamed mill is a fantasy or a crime.

Appendix II, said as furniture: trade is a permit path, not a ban. The mill that imports sawn *S. macrophylla* from a neotropical range state needs the export permit and the finding. The designer who specifies *Dalbergia latifolia* for a sideboard that weighs eighty kilograms of listed wood has written a permit problem, not a jewelry-box exemption. Finished furniture may sit outside a timber annotation. The sawn wood in the factory does not. Read the annotation, not the hangtag.

Appendix III, said as furniture: one country asked for help. Some cedars and other woods have sat here. The listing can be narrower than a genus. Always look up the current appendix. This chapter will not update itself when CoP20 moves a line.

Look-alike listings matter in a warehouse. Customs officers are not wood anatomists. The 2017 *Dalbergia* genus listing exists in part because a crate labelled “rosewood” could be any of dozens of species, and a look-alike rule is how you stop the laundering. TRAFFIC and Chatham House’s five-taxa paper is the trade-analysis neighbor of that decision. It is not a permit.

## A shop file that would have made sense in 1976 and still does

Keep the binomial. Keep the country of harvest. Keep the date you looked the taxon up, and the page you looked it up on. If you import, keep the permit or the declaration. If you do not import, keep the mill’s chain-of-custody sheet anyway. An edge that matches the name is the object-level check: tyloses in white oak, the oily pore of teak, the tight diffuse face of maple, the printed cathedral that does not turn the corner.

A crate that says only “wood” is a refusal. A salesperson who says “it’s all mahogany” is a refusal. A funny price on a listed genus is a refusal.

Arkansas white oak, walnut, cherry, and pine do not sit on the furniture-timber appendices this series cares about. That is a practical fact, not a halo. Stolen walnut trees exist. Lacey reaches domestic plants taken in violation of state or foreign law. The Convention of 1973 is silent on a Boone County log that never crossed a border. The 2008 amendment is not.

## How to look at a piece that predates the paper

A 1760 Jamaica chair is older than the Convention by two centuries. It is usually legal to own in the United States. It can be complicated to export. The same is true of a 1962 Danish rosewood credenza, which is younger than the Convention and older than the 2017 genus listing. Pre-Convention and antique rules are real. They are administered, not assumed. Photograph the grain. Keep the invoice. If the piece later wants to go to London, the file starts in the living room, not at the airport.

A new table claiming an old name is the opposite case. The invoice binomial is the first question. The harvest country is the second. The annotation is the third. Fashion without that file is how *Swietenia* and *Dalbergia* arrived at the appendices. The 1973 text did not cause the logging. It built a gate after the ships had already sailed for three hundred years.

Bradley Brand’s Arkansas hardwoods are a specification a 1973 delegate would have understood without an appendix: *Quercus alba*, United States. The Convention was written for the other crate — the one whose name was a color and whose origin was a shrug.

Look at the dock date and the binomial. That is the Convention in a shop. The later drafts — `cites-swietenia-permits`, `cites-dalbergia-2017`, `cites-ebony-africa`, `conservation-today` — put particular trees through the same gate. This page is the gate.
