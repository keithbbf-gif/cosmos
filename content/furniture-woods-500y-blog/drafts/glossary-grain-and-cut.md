# Grain, cut, and the words shops actually use

A log is a cylinder of tubes. How you slice the cylinder decides the face you stain, the way a table top cups, and whether an oak chair looks like a cathedral or like a rayed stone. Fashion has picked sides for five hundred years. The physics has not changed. This draft is the field kit for every later slug: the three cuts, the pore classes, the words that mean too many trees.

## Three faces from one tree

Flatsawn (plain-sawn) boards are cut tangent to the rings. On the face you get arches — the cathedrals of oak and ash. The width of the board is mostly tangential grain. Tangential shrinkage is the large number in the *Wood Handbook* tables (FPL-GTR-190, ch. 4). A flatsawn white-oak board will move more in width, and it will cup so the rings try to flatten. That is not a defect. It is geometry.

Quartersawn boards are cut so the rings run nearly perpendicular to the face. The face is radial. In oak, the medullary rays — ribbons of tissue that ran from pith to bark — show as flakes. English joiners and later Arts and Crafts catalogues called the look silver grain or tiger oak. Radial shrinkage is smaller. A quartersawn top still moves, but it stays flatter and wears its width change more evenly.

Riftsawn is the compromise: rings at about 30 to 60 degrees, straight stripe, fewer flakes. Chair shops like it for legs that must match. Yield is worse than flatsawing. A mill that sells “quartered and rift” as a mixed bundle is telling you to specify flake if you want flake.

Hoadley, *Understanding Wood*, is the shop book for this geometry. Figure 3 above is the same argument in one diagram.

## Before the circular saw

Medieval and early modern oak furniture often started as riven stock, not sawn. A wedge follows the rays. The board you get is naturally quartered. That is why a 16th-century linenfold panel can be thin, wide, and stable in a way a cheap 19th-century oak sideboard is not. Baltic wainscot, the *wagenschot* of the Gdansk trade, was typically cleft or sawn to keep the face radial (Haneca et al. 2005; KNOB wainscot literature). Buyers in London and the Low Countries paid for dryness and for that radial cut.

When pitsaws and later water-powered sash mills took over, flatsawing won on yield. You get more wide boards by slabbing the log. Walnut veneer in the late 17th century is a different conversion: the log is too precious to quarter for solidity, so you glue a thin face to a cheaper carcase and let the substrate argue with the weather.

Mahogany in the 18th-century solid — Bowett’s cheap Jamaica wood after 1721 — was often flatsawn because the logs were huge and the shops wanted width for dining tables. A two-board mahogany top with matching cathedrals is a flatsaw trophy. Quartersawn mahogany exists; it is quieter. The ribbon in some *Swietenia* and later in sapele is interlocked grain, not oak-like rays.

## Pores, rings, rays

Hardwoods have vessels. On the end grain they are holes. On the face they are grooves or dashes.

Ring-porous woods (oak, ash, elm, teak, hickory) lay a row of large earlywood pores each year. You can count rings with a thumbnail. The face shows a strong grain that filler and stain love. Diffuse-porous woods (maple, cherry, birch, yellow-poplar, true mahogany in a quieter way) spread smaller pores through the ring. The face can look satiny. Semi-ring-porous woods (walnut) sit between.

Softwoods have no vessels. They have tracheids. Pine shows resin canals and a jump from earlywood to latewood that is loud in southern yellow pine and shy in eastern white pine. If you think you are looking at maple and you see a resin canal, you are not looking at maple.

Rays are the other tell. Oak rays are large enough to be a design element when quartered. Beech and sycamore have crowded rays that speckle a quartered face. European “sycamore” in the furniture trade is *Acer pseudoplatanus*, a maple. American sycamore is *Platanus*. The word is a trap. Boxwood rays are fine; the wood looks almost like a pale meat.

A 10× lens and a cut end grain beat a phone photograph of a finished top.

## Joinery that assumes a cut

Frame-and-panel assumes the panel may shrink. The groove is deep enough that a flatsawn oak panel can lose width and still sit. The stiles and rails are often closer to quartered so the frame stays true next to glass or a door edge.

A breadboard end across a flatsawn top must slot-screw or pin in elongated holes. If the pins are tight, the top splits. If the breadboard is glued across the whole end, the top splits. Eighteenth-century shops knew this. Some nineteenth-century factories forgot.

Draw-boring in oak likes riven or quartered stiles: the pin crushes fibers in a predictable direction. In interlocked elm you do not draw-bore a seat; you drive a tapered leg into a bored hole and wedge it from above.

Veneer assumes a ground. Hide glue on deal or yellow-poplar is the historic sandwich. A plywood ground is the 20th-century sandwich: cross-bands cancel movement. A paper foil on particleboard is a photograph of grain. The edge tells you which contract you bought.

## Names that mean too many trees

Mahogany in a 1760 London bill is likely Jamaican *Swietenia*. In a 1960 American suite it may be *Khaya*, sapele, or a photograph on lauan. Rosewood may be *Dalbergia nigra*, East Indian *D. latifolia*, or a stained substitute. Cedar in a blanket chest is often *Juniperus virginiana*. Cedar in a Spanish colonial chest may be *Cedrela odorata*. Sycamore is a fight between continents. Fruitwood on an eight-foot table is a stain name.

CITES and the Lacey Act do not care about poetry. The later conservation drafts in this series use binomials on purpose.

## How to see it on a piece you did not make

Look at the end grain. Rings running across the thickness, parallel to the face, mean flatsawn. Rings running through the thickness, end-on, mean quartered. If you cannot see the end, look at the face: cathedrals versus straight stripe plus flake (oak) or ribbon (interlocked tropicals).

Look at wear. Chair seats that were flatsawn cup toward the bark side. Tabletops with breadboard ends: if the pins elongated into slots, someone understood tangential movement.

Look at the period. A 1600 joined chest with ray flake is doing its job. A 1905 sideboard with loud flake is announcing a movement. A 1970 oak suite with printed grain on a veneer of unclear species is announcing a factory. Printed grain has no end-grain rings.

## Cost

Quartering wastes the log. That is why Baltic wainscot was dear, why Gillows in Lancaster could pay nearly mahogany prices for Danzig oak in 1740 (Bowett, provincial woods paper), and why a true quartersawn white-oak dining table still costs more per foot than a flatsawn one from the same kiln charge.

Mills now cut quartered and rift as a mixed bundle. Designers who want flake must say flake. Designers who want straight grain for a run of doors should say rift.

A Windsor seat is a cut lesson you can sit on. English shops often used elm because the interlocked grain will not split out to the rim when four tapered legs go in (Evans). American shops used pine and poplar too. The seat is thick and usually flatsawn; it cups. The bow wants a riven or steam-bent stick — ash, oak, hickory, sometimes yew — because a flatsawn oak bow with a cathedral on the face will break toward the bark on a tight radius. The glossary’s three cuts are not museum talk. They are why a chair stays a chair.

Rotary veneer is the other conversion the figure does not draw. A log on a lathe, peeled like a paper towel, gives wide wild cathedrals and a cheap face. Sliced veneer — crown or quarter — is how you get a walnut crotch or an oak flake on a door. The plywood draft takes the sandwich. Here it is enough to say: the cut of the face and the cut of the ground are two decisions. A quartered oak flake on a particleboard core is a photograph of a radial board glued to a fiber that does not have rings.

Moisture content sits under every cut. FPL-GTR-190, chapter 4, is the handbook. Wood shrinks as it dries, more tangentially than radially, for most species about one-and-a-half to two times. A flatsawn panel in a heated apartment is the large number. A quartersawn stile next to glass is the small number. The moisture draft (`moisture-wood-movement`) walks a door through a winter. This page only needs the rule: name the cut before you name the finish.
