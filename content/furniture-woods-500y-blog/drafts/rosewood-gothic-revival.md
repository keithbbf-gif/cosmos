# Rosewood and the Gothic Revival parlor

A. W. N. Pugin wanted oak. Church furniture, the Houses of Parliament fittings, the moral argument that a pointed arch should sit in a northern hardwood — that is the English Gothic Revival most people can name. Walk into an American parlor of 1848 and the pointed arch is often rosewood. J. and J. W. Meeks, Alexander Roux, a dozen New York and Philadelphia shops carved quatrefoils and clustered colonnettes in *Dalbergia* veneer on pine or yellow-poplar. The wood is the same Bahia or East Indian rosewood the rococo parlor used. The silhouette is a cathedral quoted in a dark, oily leaf. Oak stayed in the hall and the church. Rosewood took the front room.

## Two Gothics, one genus

English ecclesiastical Gothic is a riven-and-sawn oak story: Pugin, the Cambridge Camden men, later Scott. American architectural Gothic — Alexander Jackson Davis, Downing’s cottage books — could live in painted pine. The furniture that filled those cottages and the city row houses next door split. Hall chairs and bookcases sometimes followed Pugin into oak. Parlor chairs, étagères, and center tables followed the luxury market into rosewood and mahogany.

The Meeks firm’s labeled Gothic suites (the type associated with houses such as Stanton Hall in Natchez) are the object to keep in your head: pointed-arch chair backs, crockets, a dark polish, a marble top on the table. Roux’s New York shop worked in the same vocabulary and also in the Louis XV revival. A single maker could sell you Gothic on Tuesday and rococo on Thursday. Both might be rosewood. The difference is the piercing, not the tree.

Do not invent a royal commission or a price for a Meeks suite. Museum labels and the surviving secondary woods are the evidence. Winterthur and the Met open-access records are the place to start `[CITE NEEDED: pin a specific Meeks object accession at publication]`.

John Henry Belter’s laminated rosewood (patent 1858) is usually rococo, not Gothic — compound curves, roses, not quatrefoils. The V&A plywood history uses Belter as a moulded-ply milestone. A Gothic Revival chair is more often a joined or doweled frame with applied tracery and a veneered splat. If the back is several leaves in a mould, you may have a Belter-type process wearing pointed ornament. Look at the edge of the back. Laminate shows as layers. A solid splat shows as one thickness.

## The tree does not know it is Gothic

The Victorian parlor chapter keeps Kukachka’s empty *D. nigra* table and the compiled hardness of both rosewoods. This hour only needs the joinery contrast. A pointed arch carved in oak is a pore-and-ray problem: earlywood trenches, flakes if the board is quartered, a member that wants thickness. A pointed arch veneered in rosewood is a leaf-and-ground problem: an oily skin on poplar or pine, a picture of stone that fails when the glue line lets go. Figure 4 is here so you do not confuse either wood with mahogany (about 800 lbf) or white oak (about 1,360 lbf). The numbers live in the other rosewood draft. The quatrefoil lives here.

Yellow-poplar grounds (FPL-GTR-190: SG 0.42, Janka 540 lbf) and pine grounds are the servants, as they were for Empire crotch mahogany. Hide glue, a sliced leaf, cross-banding around a Gothic panel that is trying to look like stone tracery. The joinery implication is the same sandwich with a different picture. Crotch mahogany on poplar is a flame. Rosewood Gothic on poplar is a window. Both fail when the glue line lets go at a water ring or when a belt sander meets the leaf.

Applied crockets and colonnettes are often separate turnings or carvings, glued or pinned to the veneered frame. They snap. Repair with the same species is now a permit question if the scrap is imported. Repair with dyed walnut or pear is how shops have always faked a missing crocket. Name the repair.

Mahogany Gothic exists. Bowett’s Jamaica-to-London lane (Naval Stores Act 1721, in force 1722) had already made *Swietenia* the default show wood of the previous century. A Gothic Revival library in mahogany is a real object. The parlor fashion of the 1840s–60s still preferred the darker, oilier *Dalbergia* when the client would pay. They are not substitutes for each other. They are two luxuries in the same revival.

## Why a pointed arch in an oily wood

Fashion, not theology. Downing and the pattern books made Gothic a domestic style: a cottage, a villa, a chair that announced taste. Rosewood announced money. Put them together and you get a chair that quotes a cathedral and smells like a jewelry box when you open a door. Oak would have been the Pugin-correct choice. Oak was also the hall-chair wood, the church-screen wood, the wood that read as old even when the suit was new. Rosewood read as now.

Eastlake, later in the century, pulls the dark parlor back toward oak and ebonized finishes and a flatter, incised ornament. That is a different hour. Do not call every pointed chair Eastlake. Eastlake is a book (1872 in America) and a factory look. Gothic Revival rosewood is earlier and glossier.

English shops made Gothic rosewood too — not only oak. A London chair with clustered columns and a palisander face is not a failed Pugin. It is a parlor object. Continental Gothic Revival has its own oak-and-softwood habits. This draft stays with the rosewood parlor because that is the species hour.

A bookcase with clustered columns and a pointed-arch glazed door is the large object in this vocabulary. The stiles may be solid mahogany or a cheaper hardwood, veneered. The tracery in the glass is often a separate fret, pinned or glued to the sash. Rosewood on the door face, oak or pine in the carcase, a dustboard of poplar: the piece is a map of what the shop would spend. Castellation along a cornice is applied. It snaps in a move. Do not invent a Davis drawing for a particular bookcase unless the object record says so. The geometry is enough.

Southern American houses that bought New York Gothic — Natchez, the river towns, Charleston parlors that could afford the freight — used the same Meeks-and-Roux language in rooms that also held Empire crotch mahogany. The woods sit together. The hours overlap. A house is not a museum label.

## Workability when the ornament is applied

Rosewood dulls tools. Calcareous deposits in some *D. latifolia*, Kukachka notes, blunt edges rapidly. A 25-degree cutting angle helped him get a smooth plane surface on the Indian wood. Gothic piercing in a veneered splat is often fret-sawn through the leaf and the ground together, then filed. If the ground is poplar, the file meets a soft wood under a hard skin. The arris crumbles on the poplar side first. That is why so many Gothic chair backs show pale wounds at the points of the tracery.

Finish is a high polish: filler in the coarse pore, French polish or a spirit varnish, a look like wet stone. Old pieces oxidize toward brown. The violet that catalogues praise is easier to see on a fresh scrape than on a hundred-year film. Do not sand a listed wood to find it.

A side chair in this vocabulary is a light object with a heavy name. The back looks like a window. The seat is often a slip, upholstered, on a frame that is mahogany or a cheaper hardwood with a rosewood face on the show rails. Turn the chair. If the side rails are pale under the seat, you have the sandwich. If the legs are solid rosewood, the chair will surprise you when you lift it. Most parlor Gothic chairs are not that surprise. They are a picture of a cathedral glued to a pine idea of a chair.

Dust collection is not optional. Sensitizing dust is a family trait of *Dalbergia*.

## Figure 5, Appendix I, and the church that is not the problem

Figure 5 is the later map. *D. nigra* is CITES Appendix I. Commercial new trade from the wild is not a furniture plan. The rest of *Dalbergia* went onto Appendix II on 2 January 2017, Annotation #15 as revised at CoP18 (finished musical instruments exempt; small finished products under a weight cap on the listed wood). A Gothic Revival chair is not a guitar. Sawn wood and veneer for a factory are not a casual import. Neotropical *Swietenia macrophylla* entered Appendix II on 15 November 2003 — relevant if the “Gothic” library is mahogany, not rosewood.

Pugin’s oak is not listed. That is not a moral ranking. It is a genus fact. A new oak Gothic chair can be a specification: *Quercus alba*, United States, quartersawn. A new rosewood Gothic chair is a paperwork object before it is a style object.

Antique pieces already in the country are usually legal to own. Export wants the Management Authority. The U.S. Lacey Act (2008 amendment) and APHIS Phase VII (1 December 2024) sit on top of the appendix.

Dean’s Atlantic-forest account still sits under the Bahia wood. Indian *D. latifolia* has its own colonial extraction story (the export draft). Do not tell the quatrefoil as a New York idea only.

## What to look for

Pointed arches and quatrefoils in a dark, oily face; a veneer line; poplar or pine inside; applied crockets that have snapped and been glued; pale wounds at the fret points. On a Pugin-type church chair: oak, often, and a different moral. On a Belter-type parlor chair: laminate, roses, not Gothic. On an Eastlake factory chair: flatter ornament, often oak or ebonized. On a new “Gothic rosewood” suite: the invoice binomial and the permit, or a walk away.

Arkansas grows oak that will take a pointed arch honestly. It does not grow *Dalbergia*. A shop that wants this hour’s silhouette without the appendix is looking at quartersawn white oak, or at walnut, and at ornament that does not pretend to be Bahia.
