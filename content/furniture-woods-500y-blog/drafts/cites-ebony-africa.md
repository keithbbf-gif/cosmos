# African ebony and the listed black

A piano key that is truly Gabon ebony is a small, dense, jet object, and a crate of the same wood is a customs argument. True ebonies are *Diospyros*. The furniture black of 17th-century Antwerp cabinets, of Boulle’s counterpart to brass, of piano sharps when the sharps were wood, and of Art Deco accents is this genus — or it is a stain on pear. Madagascar’s populations of *Diospyros* went onto CITES Appendix II at CoP16 (Bangkok, 2013), in force 12 June 2013, Annotation #5: logs, sawn wood, and veneer sheets. Gabon ebony, *Diospyros crassiflora*, is a West and Central African tree, IUCN Endangered, and a different listing question. Check Species+ the morning you specify it. This draft is the African black in shops and the paper that sometimes follows it. It is not a permit.

## Two Africas, one word

Madagascar is an African island with its own *Diospyros* flora — dozens of species, many poorly separated on a dock. In March 2010 Madagascar decreed a ban on cutting, exploiting, and exporting *Dalbergia* and *Diospyros* (CITES Notification 2013/039). In 2011 it listed those genera’ Malagasy species on Appendix III. CoP16 moved the Madagascar populations of both genera to Appendix II, annotated #5. The listing took effect 12 June 2013. The Secretariat spent the following years asking Parties to check shipments, because illegal rosewood and ebony kept leaving the island (SC65 Doc. 48.1). The Action Plan attached to Decision 16.152 is the paper trail of that failure. A container of “ebony” from Toamasina after June 2013 is a #5 question if it is logs, sawn wood, or veneer.

Gabon ebony — *D. crassiflora* (trade also says African ebony, Nigerian ebony) — grows in Cameroon, Gabon, the Republic of the Congo, the Central African Republic, and neighbors `[CITE NEEDED: current range-state list from a flora]`. It is the jet-black heartwood of the piano-key and inlay trade. It is not a Madagascar species. A CoP16 proposal synonym that looks like *D. crassiflora* is a Malagasy name now treated as *D. mcphersonii* (CoP16 Prop. 58). Do not conflate them. *D. crassiflora* has sat off the appendices while the Madagascar populations sat on them. That can change at a Conference of the Parties. Species+ is the living page. IUCN Endangered is not a CITES appendix. It is a conservation status. Both belong on a specification, and they are not the same stamp.

Ceylon ebony (*D. ebenum*) and Macassar (*D. celebica*, striped brown-black) are Asian trades. They appear here only so a reader does not call every black key Gabon. Figure 5 lumps origin regions as a schematic. Confirm.

## Weight, hardness, and a key

Ebony is why Figure 4’s scatter has an empty upper-right corner in this series’ ten anchors. Wood Database compilations put Gabon ebony’s Janka near 3,000 lbf and specific gravity near or above 1.0 — the piece sinks, or nearly does. Treat those figures as compilations until you have an FPL row. The shop fact does not need a decimal. A solid Gabon ebony chest would be a monster of weight and money. Furniture used the wood as stringing, as a key, as a banding, as a clock-case accent, as the black in a Boulle pair. Antwerp and Paris cabinets of the 17th century are the luxury objects. Victorian ebonized furniture is often a finish on pear, maple, or beech. Look at a chip. If the black is a skin, you have a stain. If the black goes through a dense, fine pore and the piece weighs like stone, you may have *Diospyros*. A lab test is what a customs officer may want.

The *Wood Handbook* is thin on African ebony as a structural furniture species because shops did not build tables from it. Hoadley will tell you it is hard, fine, and punishing to edges. It turns. It engraves. It does not forgive a dull gouge. Dust is nasty; collection is not optional `[CITE NEEDED: a named occupational-health note for *Diospyros* dust]`. Glue is ordinary if the surface is clean and not polished to a seal before the joint.

A piano with wooden sharps is a dated object. Later pianos went to plastic. Repair of an old key with true Gabon is now a supply and a paper problem. Repair with dyed hornbeam or a polymer is how most shops keep a keyboard even. Name the repair.

## Illegal trade, named as a crate

The Madagascar story after 2010 is not a sermon. It is a Standing Committee file. Notification 2013/039 asked Parties to inspect timber leaving the island. SC65 recorded “numerous reports of suspected illegal exports” after the 12 June 2013 listing. EIA and other NGOs published container-level accounts; treat those as advocacy documents and check the CITES notices before you quote a tonnage `[CITE NEEDED: a dated CITES or TRAFFIC tonnage you are willing to stand behind]`. The Chinese market for Hongmu and for black furniture woods is part of the demand side `[CITE NEEDED: a named trade-statistics paper, not a headline]`. A shop in Arkansas does not need the geopolitics. It needs to know that “Madagascar ebony” on an invoice after 2013 is a listed population, and that a funny price is a refusal.

West and Central African *D. crassiflora* has its own illegal-logging file. IUCN Endangered is the status. National harvest and export rules in Gabon and Cameroon exist and change `[CITE NEEDED: current Gabon / Cameroon export rule for ebony]`. Lacey does not wait for an appendix. The U.S. Lacey Act as amended in 2008, Food, Conservation, and Energy Act §8204, makes a plant illegal here if it was taken in violation of foreign law. APHIS Phase VII, 1 December 2024, reaches more furniture HTS codes. A crate of inlay stock that was illegal to cut in the source country is a Lacey crate whether or not Species+ shows an appendix.

The 1973 Convention (signed 3 March, in force 1 July 1975) is the machine. *Dalbergia nigra* went onto Appendix I on 11 June 1992. The *Dalbergia* genus went onto Appendix II on 2 January 2017, Annotation #15 revised at CoP18 in 2019 (musical instruments / 10 kg). Neotropical *Swietenia macrophylla* went onto Appendix II on 15 November 2003, Annotation #6. Madagascar *Diospyros* is the ebony date on that same wall: 12 June 2013, #5. Do not borrow the rosewood 10 kg exemption for a sawn ebony flitch. Different annotation. Different genus.

## Ebonized versus ebony, in a room

Victorian “ebony” suites are usually stained. The fashion was a black parlor, not a *Diospyros* forest. French polish over a black ground, or a spirit stain on pear, reads as ebony at ten feet and as a finish at a chip. Art Deco used Macassar and other striped ebonies as a modern luxury: a cabinet face, a clock, an accent, not a farm table. English Regency and French Empire used rosewood as the dark show veneer more often than solid ebony. Boulle’s brass-and-turtle cabinets at the Wallace Collection are the place to see the black as a counterpart, not as a table top.

Inlay and stringing are the honest furniture uses. A 1 mm line of Gabon in a satinwood panel is a historic craft. A new dining table advertised as solid African ebony is a weight and money claim that should make you pick up a corner. If the corner is light, the advertisement is a stain. If the corner is heavy and the invoice lacks a binomial and a harvest country, put the corner down.

## Shop file

Binomial. Harvest country. Date you checked Species+. If the wood is Madagascar *Diospyros* in logs, sawn wood, or veneer, you want Appendix II paper under Annotation #5, or a refusal. If the wood is *D. crassiflora*, you want the current appendix (or the absence of one) plus Lacey due care plus the range-state export rule. If the wood is a stain, write “ebonized pear” or “ebonized maple.” Do not write ebony.

Repair patches imported as sawn stock follow the annotation. A gram of inlay in a traveler’s pocket is not a dining table; it can still be a specimen if the listing covers that form. This draft will not draw that line for you. USFWS will.

Arkansas does not grow *Diospyros* of the jet furniture kind. Persimmon (*D. virginiana*) is a pale, hard, domestic cousin used for golf-club heads and some textile shuttles, not for Boulle. It will not be Gabon. It does not need to be. A dark domestic hardwood in the same shop is walnut. Name it.

## Keys, stringing, and a Boulle pair

Piano sharps were a volume market. A single keyboard is a handful of grams of jet heartwood, sawn, dyed if the sap streaked, glued to a pine or basswood carcass. Factories in the late 19th century ate *D. crassiflora* in those grams. They did not eat it as tables. When plastics took the sharps, the furniture and inlay trades remained the excuse to cut the tree. A 1 mm stringing line in a satinwood panel is the honest leftover of that trade. A “solid ebony console” on a website in 2024 is usually a stain, a hope, or a Lacey problem.

Boulle’s counterpart to brass is turtle and ebony in the same Paris luxury. The Wallace Collection pieces are the place to see the black as a line, not as a slab. English Restoration cabinets used ebony as a ground for japanning and as a banding. Dutch and Antwerp ebony cabinets of the 17th century are a different, heavier claim; some of that black is stained pear even there. A lens on an unseen edge ends the argument better than a catalogue.

Macassar (*D. celebica*) is striped and Indonesian. It is the Art Deco face people mean when they say “ebony” and show brown lightning. It is not Gabon and not Madagascar. Do not file it under Annotation #5. Do not file Gabon under #5 either unless Species+ has moved. The 2013 listing is a population listing: Madagascar. The word ebony is not a population.

Look at a chip and a scale. A skin of black on a light wood is a finish. A jet heartwood that sinks in the back of the mind is *Diospyros* or a hope. The invoice decides which. The 2013 Madagascar listing and the 1975 Convention are the paper. This page is not a stamp on that paper.
