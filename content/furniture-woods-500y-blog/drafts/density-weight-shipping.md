# Density, freight, and a crate that argues

A cubic foot of eastern white pine at handbook specific gravity 0.35 weighs on the order of 22 pounds at 12 percent moisture. A cubic foot of white oak at SG 0.68 weighs on the order of 47. A cubic foot of Gabon ebony, specific gravity near or above 1.0 in the compilations, is a crate that argues with the longshoreman and with the scale. Freight has always been part of furniture fashion. Thomas Ripley told the Admiralty that mahogany would be cheaper than deal or wainscot if Navy ships brought it home (Bowett, *Georgian Group Journal*, 1997). The wood was light enough, once the duty was gone, to carve in the solid. This draft is density as a shop number and as a shipping number. Figure 4 is the scatter. Figure 1 is the long trade that paid for the weight.

## What the handbook means by specific gravity

FPL reports specific gravity on an ovendry-mass / volume-at-stated-moisture basis. The furniture tables commonly use volume at 12 percent. Basic specific gravity uses green volume. They are not interchangeable. A designer who writes “density 0.68” without saying which basis has written a muddle. The series follows the handbook’s 12 percent convention unless a note says basic SG.

Pounds per cubic foot at 12 percent is roughly SG × 62.4 × (a moisture adjustment). Shop shorthand: multiply SG by 62 and you are close enough to lift a board in your head. White pine ~22 lb/ft³. Yellow-poplar ~26. Mahogany ~28–31. Cherry ~31. Walnut ~34. White oak ~42–47. Hickory ~45–50. Those are means. Your balk varies. Heartwood versus sap, ring width, moisture above or below 12 percent — the scale will tell you.

Janka travels with density on Figure 4. Green, Begel, and Nelson (FPL-RP-643) estimated hardness from specific gravity. The scatter is why we still print both axes. Mahogany is the useful outlier in furniture history: moderately light, moderately hard, carvable. Ebony and rosewood are the other end: heavy, hard, used as small objects and veneers because a solid table is a freight and money problem.

Moisture changes the weight more obviously than it changes the SG basis. A green oak balk can hold more water than its ovendry mass. Ship green and you pay for water. The Baltic wainscot trade paid for dryness as well as for the radial cut (Haneca et al. 2005; KNOB “driest wainscots” literature). That is a density story: water is dense. A kiln schedule is a freight schedule.

## Freight as style

English oak furniture of 1550 is not simply English oak. Gdansk wainscot moved west because the boards were worth the Sound toll: slow-grown, radially converted, dry enough. Weight per useful face was the product. Deal from the Baltic was the light, cheap cousin — pine and spruce for backs, painted furniture, and the insides of finer things. Ripley’s mahogany sentence is the Atlantic version. Jamaica wood after the Naval Stores Act of 1721 (in force 1722) rode Navy freight and a duty cut. A wood that is too heavy for its price stays a curiosity. Lignum vitae, already in the 1660 Book of Rates, made sheaves and mallets, not dining tables.

Rosewood pianos and ebony cabinets are luxury because the wood is scarce and because a little of it goes a long way by weight. A solid *Dalbergia* dining table that is truly the genus is heavy in the room and loud at the border after 2 January 2017. A solid Gabon ebony chest would be a monster. Veneer is how you ship the picture without shipping the cubic foot.

Thonet’s No. 14 (V&A W.31-2011) is six pieces of steamed beech. Beech is not light. The chair is light because the sections are small. Aalto’s birch laminate and the Eames plywood seats are density managed by thickness and by glue. Particleboard is dense in the wrong way: heavy, cheap, a kitchen wall that fights you when you move house. The engineered chapter takes the panel. This chapter is why the panel feels like a bag of cement.

## A crate on a dock, weighed

Picture two crates of equal outside volume. One is eastern white pine drawer stuff. One is white oak flooring. The oak crate can be double the pine on the scale. Ocean freight and now container weight limits care. So does a stair. So does a floor joist under a solid-oak library. Architects who specify solid rift-oak casework on a fourth floor without asking the structural engineer have written a density problem.

CITES and Lacey do not weigh the crate for biology, but the declaration asks for quantity. APHIS Phase VII (1 December 2024) wants scientific name, country of harvest, quantity, value. Quantity is often in kilograms or cubic metres. A crate that says “wood furniture” without a mass is the old habit. A crate of *Swietenia macrophylla* sawn wood after 15 November 2003 is an Annotation #6 specimen with a volume. A crate of *Dalbergia* after 2 January 2017 is an Annotation #15 problem if it is a dining table, and a 10 kg conversation only if it is a small finished object. Weight is legal language now, not only freight language.

Hide glue adds ounces. Urea-formaldehyde in a particleboard core adds pounds and a chemistry. The density of the tree is not the density of the object. Count the core.

## Shop uses of the number

Lift the drawer. Yellow-poplar and pine secondary wood make a tall chest possible. That is why Philadelphia high chests exist in the rooms they exist in. Solid oak sides would have changed the joinery and the market.

Weigh a “rosewood” suite. If it is light, it is not *Dalbergia*. If it is heavy and cheap, it is a problem. Weigh an “ebony” box. If it is light, it is ebonized pear. The Janka draft is the dent. This draft is the lift.

Shipping finished furniture inland: a solid-oak dining table, 1¼-inch top, 42 × 84, is on the order of a hundred pounds in the top alone before legs `[CITE NEEDED: show the arithmetic with your actual SG and thickness if you publish a single pound figure]`. A walnut table of the same size is less. A mahogany table less again. A veneered ply table is a different, often lighter object with a heavier carbon story in the glue. Specify the object, not the wood word.

Arkansas oak, walnut, cherry, and pine cover a useful density band without an ocean. Bradley Brand’s crates are domestic freight. That is not a halo. It is a shorter haul and a binomial you can spell. Stolen walnut still has a density. Lacey 2008 §8204 still applies if the log crossed a line.

## How to read a piece by lift

A 1760 mahogany chair is lighter than its oak country cousin of the same size. That is the fiber and the section. A 1962 Danish rosewood credenza is heavier than a teak one of the same outline. That is *Dalbergia* versus *Tectona*. A 2012 foil-wrapped dresser is heavy in the particleboard way and light in the face-veneer way. Lift, then look at the edge.

Figure 4 places the ten anchors. Figure 1 places the ships. Density is why pine painted furniture could be tall, why mahogany could be thin, why rosewood became a piano case and a permit, and why a crate of ebony is a scale event. ## Navy freight, a Sound toll, and a container limit

Ripley’s mahogany was a freight argument before it was a style argument. Navy ships already crossed. The duty cut of 1721 made the cubic foot worth carving. Baltic wainscot had paid the Sound toll for two centuries on a different cubic foot: oak, radial, dry. Albion’s *Forests and Sea Power* is the navy-oak neighbor of that sentence — masts and hulls, not dining tables, but the same scale. A wood that is heavy and cheap stays home. A wood that is light and dutied stays home. A wood that is heavy and precious travels as veneer or as a small object.

Modern containers have a payload limit. A factory that fills a box with solid white-oak casework hits the limit before it hits the volume. The same box filled with pine painted furniture hits the volume first. That arithmetic is why veneered MDF suites can be a wall of storage that still ships, and why a solid-oak library is a local mill’s job. Figure 1’s late years include the CITES cluster. Those years also include a freight market that made cheap tropical rotary faces cheaper than domestic solids for a generation. Density did not cause that. The price of a cubic foot on a ship did.

A specifier who writes a solid rift-oak reception desk, 2-inch top, 16 feet long, has written a hoist as well as a cut. Ask the stair. Ask the elevator. Ask the joist. The Janka number will not lift the desk.

Hide glue adds ounces. A UF particleboard core adds pounds. A solid oak library table, 1¼-inch top, 42 × 96, is a two-person lift before the base. The same outline in walnut is less; in mahogany less again; in veneered birch ply a different, often lighter object. Write the thickness and the species on the shipping ticket. “Wood table” is how a crate gets reweighed at the dock and refused. Phase VII wants a mass and a binomial anyway. The scale and the appendix arrived in the same decade for a reason. Weight was always money. After 1 December 2024 it is also a line on a declaration.

Look at the bill of lading and the corner of the piece. If they disagree, believe the scale.
