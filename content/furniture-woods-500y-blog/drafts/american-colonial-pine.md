# American colonial pine

A six-board chest in eastern white pine, painted oxblood, sits against a New England wall. Two sides, a bottom, a lid, a front and a back — nailed or pegged, often a till, light enough for one person to drag. It is not a failed oak chest. It is a different idea of furniture: cheap enough to own, soft enough to dent, honest about paint. Benno Forman’s *American Seating Furniture, 1630–1730* is the seating half of the same wood culture. Hurst and Prown’s *Southern Furniture 1680–1830* is the reminder that a Charleston parlor might show walnut or mahogany while the passage and the kitchen stayed pine. The colonies furnished more rooms in pine than in any hardwood the London books named.

## Three pines, two coasts of a colony

Eastern white pine (*Pinus strobus*) — Weymouth pine in English bills — is the New England and later Great Lakes furniture pine. The USDA *Wood Handbook* (FPL-GTR-190, table 5–3a) puts it at about specific gravity 0.35 and Janka 380 lbf at 12 percent moisture. Shrinkage green to ovendry is on the order of 2.1 percent radial, 6.1 percent tangential, 8.2 percent volumetric. Soft, even, low movement for a commercial wood, easy to work. Heartwood light brown with a red hint; it darkens in air. The handbook still lists furniture among its uses. Old-growth clears are a vanished ordinary grade. Second growth is knotty. Knots that hold are the tree. Knots that fall out are holes you plug or paint.

Southern yellow pines (*P. taeda*, *P. echinata*, and kin) are denser, with a loud latewood band. They appear in southern country furniture and as secondary wood under walnut. Longleaf heart pine (*P. palustris*) is a harder, resinous cousin — FPL about SG 0.59 and Janka 870 lbf — more floor and joist than chamber chest, though shops used what they had (Outland, *Tapping the Pines*; Earley, *Looking for Longleaf*). Do not call every pale southern board white pine. End grain shows the latewood jump.

Scots pine (*P. sylvestris*) is the European deal pine. It is not the colonial American default. A “pine” cupboard in Pennsylvania may also be yellow-poplar (*Liriodendron tulipifera*), greener and heavier. Basswood is even softer and more even. Resin canals tell pine from those hardwoods. Figure 4 places white pine at the soft, light end of the series anchors. Figure 1’s early milestones are Baltic oak and later mahogany; colonial pine is the American clock those London dates under-count.

The Broad Arrow reserved large white pines for masts (Albion, *Forests and Sea Power*; the Massachusetts charter tradition of 1691). The exact charter wording wants a page `[CITE NEEDED]`. Furniture shops used what the navy did not take: smaller stems, limbs, the later boards after a mast tree came down. A blanket chest is not a mast. It is the civilian remainder.

## Paint, and the rooms the histories skip

Painted pine is the furniture of people who had trees and brushes. New England blanket chests, Connecticut Valley cupboards, Pennsylvania work that is not all walnut, southern huntboards and pie safes that may be yellow pine or poplar or both. The Metropolitan and Winterthur hold the best of what survived. Most of it lived harder lives and did not.

Paint is linseed, milk, distemper — oxblood, blue, a green that goes black in the folds. Old layers may be lead. That is a health fact. Overpaint is a palimpsest. Stripping a cupboard to “natural pine” is often a 1960s taste, not an eighteenth-century one. Stain blotches on pine: earlywood drinks, latewood refuses. A “honey pine” finish is a later factory argument with the rings.

Shaker pine is a later, clearer subset — pale, chosen, maple and cherry beside it. Kassay and Rieman and Burks take that mix. The colonial chest is more willing to be red and dented.

Southern shops (Hurst and Prown) used yellow pine as a primary for painted and stained case work and as a secondary under walnut and mahogany. A Charleston parlor and a backcountry press are not one market. Forman’s seating survey shows turned chairs and joint stools that may mix maple, ash, oak, and pine in one object. Species follow the job: a post that must take a mortise, a seat that must take a turning, a panel that must take paint.

## Joinery

The six-board chest is the teaching piece. Nails hold because the fiber crushes and grips. Glue holds if resin is not on the surface. Pegs are older in some shops and better in a thin rail. A steel screw without a pilot — a later repair — splits. Battens across a lid, nailed or pegged, keep a wide board from cupping; if they are glued tight across a flatsawn top, the lid splits. Breadboard ends, when they appear on tables, must slot or pin in elongated holes.

Boarded construction is the colonial pine default for chests and many tables. Joined construction — mortise and tenon, frame-and-panel — appears when the shop has oak or when a pine cupboard wants doors that stay true. A pine panel in an oak frame is a North Sea habit translated: the panel floats, the frame is the grown-up wood. All-pine joined work exists; the tenons are fatter than a mahogany shop would cut, because 380 lbf is not 1,360.

Drawer work in better colonial case pieces may be pine sides with a walnut or maple front, or all pine, nailed or dovetailed. Early dovetails can be large and few. A plywood drawer bottom is a marriage. Hand-planed bottoms, bevelled into a groove, are the period contract.

Windsor production, already mixing species in the eighteenth century (Evans, *Windsor-Chair Making in America*), uses pine for some seats in later cheap work; elm is the seat that will not split. Do not assume a colonial “pine chair” is a Windsor. Turned post-and-rung chairs in maple and ash are Forman’s territory. Pine is more often the chest, the table top, the cupboard, the painted box.

A nailed six-board is allowed to be crude at the back and careful at the front. Look at the lid’s underside for plane tracks. A circular-saw skip is a nineteenth-century conversion or a later replacement.

## After the navy’s mark

Independence did not change the tree. It changed who could cut a mast pine without a Crown mark. The white-pine economy draft takes the later lumber hour: New England to the Lake States, clears for interiors, knotty grades for paint. Colonial furniture already shows the split: a clear boarded lid on a better chest, a knotty painted piece for the kitchen.

A southern pie safe with punched-tin panels is the humidity version of the same boarded idea. The case may be yellow pine or poplar; the tins keep air moving around pies and meat. Hurst and Prown teach the reader to look at the case species before the tin pattern. A Connecticut Valley cupboard with a bold grain-painted face is the northern cousin: the paint is architecture, the pine is the servant. Winterthur’s painted chests keep enough original film that a conservator can still sequence the colors. A house-owned safe that has been stripped to a gym-floor orange has lost the sequence. Leave a chip in the back if you need a resin-canal check. The show face is the document.

Macquoid’s English ages do not schedule this work. Figure 1 can be read without a colonial pine tick; that is a London map. A Winterthur blanket chest is still a 500-year furniture wood. Softwoods were never only poor. They were what a household could lift and paint.

## Conservation

*Pinus strobus* and the southern yellow pines are not CITES species. Old-growth white pine in commercial clears is scarce. Using remaining old-growth for a suburban table wastes a grade the seventeenth century already treated as building and mast material. Using managed and plantation pine for painted work is ordinary. The Lacey Act still applies if a container of “colonial pine” is imported mixed softwood. Ask for the country and the species.

Lead paint: do not sand a chest as if it were raw wood. Insect holes in sapwood are common. Active powder is a different problem from old flight holes.

Repair with *P. strobus* or a close northern pine. Southern yellow pine’s loud latewood shouts in a New England lid. The reverse is also true: a white-pine patch in a coastal yellow-pine safe reads as a pale stripe.

Arkansas shops work southern yellow pine more than *strobus*. A painted cupboard in that timber is a country object in the same boarded grammar. One sentence is enough.

## What to look for

Light weight, dents, paint layers, resin canals on an unseen end, a till, rose-head nails or later replacements, a lid that still has battens. A “mahogany” factory piece with a pine back is a later sandwich. A “Pilgrim” chest with machine distress is a Colonial Revival cartoon. Forman’s dates and Hurst and Prown’s rooms are the correctives.

Lift the chest. If two people are required, ask whether someone lined it with oak or filled it with lead paint. The first owner could move it.
