# Dalbergia, 2 January 2017

A rosewood dining table is not a jewelry box. That sentence is the whole of Annotation #15, said as furniture. On 2 January 2017 the genus *Dalbergia* — every rosewood and palisander except the one already on Appendix I — entered CITES Appendix II. The decision was CoP17, Johannesburg, 2016. The annotation that came with it was #15. CoP18, Geneva, 2019, revised #15 so that finished musical instruments, finished instrument parts and accessories, and finished products up to 10 kg of wood of the listed species per shipment would not freeze concerts and small boxes. A sideboard that holds eighty kilograms of *D. latifolia* is not in that sentence. This draft is the genus listing and the annotation. It is not a permit.

## The tree that was already shut

*Dalbergia nigra* — jacaranda da Bahia, Brazilian rosewood — went onto Appendix I at CoP8, in force 11 June 1992. TRAFFIC called it the first tree species on Appendix I. Commercial trade in wild specimens is not a furniture plan. Warren Dean’s *With Broadax and Firebrand* is the Atlantic-forest history behind the piano cases. An 1880 parlor piano with a *D. nigra* case is a legal object in an American living room and a paperwork object if you ship it across a border. A new dining table in wild *D. nigra* is a fantasy or a crime.

The rest of the genus kept moving. East Indian rosewood (*D. latifolia*), cocobolo (*D. retusa*), Honduras rosewood (*D. stevensonii*), Siamese rosewood (*D. cochinchinensis*), the Madagascar rosewoods, kingwood and tulipwood — a palette of dense, oily, fragrant hardwoods that Victorian pianos, French ébénisterie, and mid-century Danish sideboards all wanted. Some species sat on Appendix II or III before 2017, often limited to logs, sawn wood, and veneer. Customs still faced a crate labelled “rosewood.” Officers are not wood anatomists. CoP17 Prop. 55 said the quiet part: they are unlikely to tell the species apart. The genus listing is a look-alike rule as much as a biology rule.

Figure 5 is a schematic of origin regions. Confirm the appendix at publication. Figure 4 places hardness and density among the series’ ten anchors. *Dalbergia* sits off the right-hand edge of that scatter: Janka numbers commonly cited well above 2,000 lbf for *D. nigra*, specific gravities often 0.8–1.0 (Wood Database compilations; check FPL imported-woods notes before treating a single figure as law). They are the heavy, oily woods. They are why a “solid rosewood suite” that ships cheap is not rosewood.

## What 2 January 2017 changed in a shop

From that morning, *Dalbergia* spp. except *D. nigra* sat on Appendix II with Annotation #15. The first text of #15 included all parts and derivatives except leaves, flowers, pollen, fruits, and seeds; a non-commercial export cap of 10 kg per shipment; and special rules that pointed *D. cochinchinensis* at Annotation #4 and Mexican *Dalbergia* at Annotation #6 (CITES PC23 documents). Guitar companies and orchestras learned the first version the hard way. Taylor Guitars’ public CITES note is a clear industry walkthrough. Verify it against Species+. Furniture companies have less excuse. A dining table was never going to hide under a 10 kg non-commercial line.

CoP18, August 2019, revised #15. The text that entered into force on 26 November 2019 reads, in furniture English: all parts and derivatives except (a) leaves, flowers, pollen, fruits, and seeds; (b) finished products to a maximum weight of wood of the listed species of up to 10 kg per shipment; (c) finished musical instruments, finished musical instrument parts, and finished musical instrument accessories; (d) parts and derivatives of *D. cochinchinensis*, covered by #4; (e) parts and derivatives of *Dalbergia* spp. originating and exported from Mexico, covered by #6. The Interpretation section defines the instrument terms against Harmonized System Chapter 92, and defines “10 kg per shipment” as the weight of the listed wood in each item, not the gross weight of the crate (CITES PC25 Doc. 36). A jewelry box may pass. A guitar may pass. A rosewood dining table does not pass.

Three *Guibourtia* species — *G. demeusei*, *G. pellegriniana*, *G. tessmannii*, the bubingas — carry the same annotation. A “rosewood look” in bubinga is not a way around the paper.

India entered a reservation on the 2017 genus listing (CITES Notification 2017/010). Reservations are not a shop license in a Party that did not enter one. Check the current notice. This draft will not update itself.

## A sideboard at the airport

A 1962 Danish credenza in *D. latifolia*, bought in Milwaukee in 1998, is usually legal to own in the United States. It became more complicated to export after 2 January 2017. Owners who want to sell it to London need a permit path, not a dealer’s shrug. Photograph the grain. Keep the invoice. Pre-Convention certificates exist for specimens acquired before the listing bound the Parties. They are administered by the Management Authority. USFWS is the American office. A magazine chapter is not.

A new “rosewood suite” from an unnamed exporter is the opposite object. If the price is funny, the wood is funny. If the hangtag says rosewood and the piece is light, you are looking at stained walnut, printed foil, or a hope. Weight, pore, and a fresh-scratch smell help. Many *Dalbergia* are fragrant when cut. They do not replace a binomial on an invoice. A lab test is what a customs officer may want.

Sawn wood and veneer for a furniture factory are not a casual import. Annotation #15 as revised still covers those commodities. The mill that wants *D. latifolia* leaves for a run of doors needs the export permit and the non-detriment finding from the country of harvest. Finished furniture that is mostly rosewood is a permit problem unless it fits the 10 kg finished-product line — and a dining table does not.

## How the wood behaves, since the listing did not change the fiber

Rosewoods are hard, dense, and often oily. They dull edges. They can starve a glue joint if you treat them like mahogany. Dust is a sensitizer; some people do not get a second chance at a first exposure `[CITE NEEDED: a named occupational-health note]`. A scraper often beats a plane on interlock. The polish looks like stone. Victorian shops used them as veneer more than as solid tops, except in small work and in some mid-century Danish pieces. Solid *Dalbergia* dining tables that are truly the genus are heavy, expensive, and now legally loud.

Figure 4’s scatter is the contrast. Honduran mahogany sits near 800 lbf and SG 0.45–0.50. White oak near 1,360 lbf and 0.68. *Dalbergia* is the other end of the rack: you lift a drawer and the drawer argues. That is useful when you are trying to decide whether a dark brown suite is rosewood or a photograph.

Repair with the same species is a permit question when the patch is imported. Repair with walnut or dyed pear is how shops have always faked a missing molding. Name the repair. Do not strip an Appendix I piano case over a weekend to “brighten the rosewood.” The finish is part of the object’s market and legal identity.

## Lacey on top

The U.S. Lacey Act (16 U.S.C. §3371 et seq.), amended by the Food, Conservation, and Energy Act of 2008 §8204, makes it illegal to trade plants taken in violation of U.S. or foreign law. It does not care whether the crate is also a CITES specimen. A rosewood log illegal in the source country is illegal here. APHIS Phase VII, 1 December 2024 (Federal Register 31 May 2024), extends declarations to remaining plant-product HTS codes that are not 100 percent composite, including more furniture. Due care is the standard. A certificate on a website is not automatically due care. FSC is a useful risk tool. It is not a finding that Lacey has been met.

Stolen walnut trees exist. Domestic wood is not automatically innocent. *Dalbergia* does not grow in Arkansas. The local dark hard woods are walnut and locust. They will not be rosewood. They do not need to be.

## What to write on a specification

*Dalbergia latifolia*, country of harvest, permit number, Annotation #15 read and filed. Or: *Juglans nigra*, United States, quartersawn. Or: vintage *Dalbergia*, already in the country, invoice and photographs in the project file, export not contemplated. Those are three different objects. “Rosewood dining set” is not a specification.

Species+ is the lookup. The annotation text is the fine print. This series will not issue a permit. It will not update itself when CoP20 changes a kilogram line. It will say: a guitar is a Chapter 92 object; a jewelry box may be a 10 kg object; a dining table is a furniture object. The 2017 listing exists because the genus was a furniture and flooring trade, not because a concert hall needed a new tax.

Look at the weight and the invoice. If the crate is a table, do not quote the instrument exemption. If the crate is a guitar, read Taylor’s note and then read Species+. If the crate is an 1880 piano in *D. nigra*, you are on Appendix I and the living-room rule is not the airport rule.

## A factory leaf and a concert guitar

The 2017 listing hit two trades that had shared a genus and not a paperwork culture. Guitar shops already knew *D. nigra* as Appendix I. They did not all know that *D. latifolia* fingerboards and *D. retusa* bridges would become Appendix II specimens on 2 January. The first year of #15 produced a pile of industry notes and a pile of seized or delayed shipments. CoP18’s instrument exemption was the political answer to that pile. Furniture factories had less of a lobby and less of an exemption. A sliced rosewood leaf for a High Point door run is still sawn wood or veneer in annotation English. It is not a Chapter 92 object. It is not 10 kg of listed wood in a finished box. It is why the genus was listed.

Kingwood and tulipwood — *D. cearensis*, *D. decipularis*, the ébéniste’s small woods — sit in the same genus and the same 2017 net. A banding line on a French commode already in a museum is not a new import. A new banding stock from an unnamed supplier is.

The smell in a fresh kerf is a pleasure. The permit is the price of that pleasure crossing a border. This page does not sell either.
