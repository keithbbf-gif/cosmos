# Teak on the deck and in the garden

A teak weather deck is a joinery problem before it is a luxury. Planks are laid with a seam, oakum is driven into that seam with a caulking iron, and the seam is paid with pitch or a later marine compound. The heartwood’s oil and the silica that dulls a plane are the same facts that keep the seam from rotting out as fast as a softwood deck. Leave the same wood in a garden without a film and it goes silver. Oil is a look, not a duty. The grey bench in the rain is doing what the wood does.

## The same numbers, a different job

*Tectona grandis* is still the ring-porous tree of the dockyard chapter. The *Wood Handbook* (FPL-GTR-190) still gives excellent dimensional stability and a very high natural durability. Compiled Janka sits near 1,070 lbf, specific gravity near 0.55 — oak’s neighbor, not maple’s. Figure 4 places that hardness among the series’ ten anchors. Outdoor furniture does not need a higher number. It needs heartwood, a joint that drains, and fasteners that will not stain.

Figure 2 keeps the Indian Ocean lane in view. The garden bench of 1985 and the Bombay frigate of 1817 share a species and a port geography. They do not share a finish, a shop, or a labor system. Plantation teak from Africa and Latin America is a later supply on that same map (FPL already noted plantations). The plantation slug takes the papers. This draft takes the wet face.

Old Burma teak is the folklore grade: tight rings, dark, greasy. Plantation boards can be lighter, paler, wider-ringed, less oily. Both are *Tectona* if the anatomy agrees. Neither is “yacht grade” because a catalogue said so. Sapwood on a garden rail is the cheap cut; it will not last like heart. Look at the end grain. A wide pale band is a specification, not a surprise.

## Caulking, camber, and a bench that holds water

Deck caulking is the period outdoor joint. The plank edges are slightly beveled or left square depending on the yard; the seam is a controlled gap, not a hairline glue line. Oakum — hemp fiber, historically tarred — is driven in layers with a caulking iron and a mallet until the seam is tight. Paying (pitch, later butyl or polysulfide compounds) keeps water from sitting in the fiber. Teak’s stability is why the seam stays a seam through a season of sun and spray. A glued teak “deck” on a yacht interior is a different contract: veneer or thin stock on a stable ground, no oakum, a look. Do not restore one as if it were the other.

Garden furniture borrowed the durability and dropped the caulking. Mortise-and-tenon slats, through-tenons wedged, sometimes knockdown hardware so a steamer chair packs. The failure is almost never the heartwood. It is a joint that traps water against end grain, a steel screw that stains, a flat seat that ponds, a cushion that keeps the slats wet. Design that drains will outlive a finish schedule. Design that holds a puddle will fail in teak as surely as in oak.

Stainless or bronze fasteners outdoors. Teak is kinder to iron than oak is — the tannin story is not teak’s — but cheap steel still makes black halos and rust streaks. Washers that hold water against the face are a small cruelty. Predrill; the wood is hard enough to split a thin slat if you drive blind.

## Indoor honey, outdoor silver

After 1945, Danish and other Scandinavian shops used teak for dining chairs, sideboards, and low tables. Finn Juhl, Arne Vodder, a hundred factories. Indoor teak in the 1950s was oiled to stay honey. That oil can is a maintenance habit, the way a cutting board is a maintenance habit. A thick polyurethane on a 1958 sideboard is a different object. Some of the best Danish work is solid; much is veneer on cheaper cores. The Danish-modern chapter in this series returns to the mix with oak and rosewood. Teak was the tropical member of that triad.

American suburban “teak” of the 1960s includes true *Tectona*, and also print, and also other brown ring-porous woods. A lens on an unseen rail. A 1958 sideboard with an oiled face and a teak smell in a fresh scratch is one object. A 1968 suite with a printed grain that dies at the edge is another. Do not strip the first and paint it without a long thought. The finish and the wood were a pair.

Late-century garden furniture made plantation teak a patio staple. The grey bench is not neglected. It is the outdoor specification. Owners who want the honey color outdoors are choosing oil and a cloth, forever. Owners who want silver are choosing the weather. Both are honest if named. A film finish on a garden table will crack and hold water in the cracks. That is a finish failure, not a species failure.

Steamer chairs and plantation chairs of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries sit between the campaign chest and the patio set. A steamer chair is a knockdown: slats, a sliding footrest, hardware that lets the piece fold for a deck. Some are teak, some are mahogany, some are painted beech pretending. The joint that matters is the pivot and the stop, not a glued carcase. Look at the hinge metal. Look at whether the slats can drain. A 1920s P&O-deck romance in a catalogue is not a named ship; it is a form. Do not invent a liner.

Gluing and finishing may need the oil wiped back. Solvent on the joint faces, then the glue the shop trusts — often epoxy outdoors, hide or PVA indoors if the oil is cut. Silica still dulls edges. Sharp carbide. Collection of dust is not optional in a small shop. A caulking iron does not belong on a furniture seam unless you are actually laying a deck. Paying a garden-table joint with pitch is a confusion of contracts. Leave the furniture joints as joints. Leave the deck seams as seams.

## Lookalikes on the patio

Iroko (*Milicia*) is the African wood sold as “African teak.” Ring-porous, durable, a Janka often cited near 1,260 lbf. It is not *Tectona*. The pore and the color rhyme; the oil and the smell do not quite. Afrormosia (*Pericopsis elata*) is a closer visual cousin and already CITES Appendix II. Cumaru, ipe, and the South American deck woods are harder, heavier, more iron than oil. They make furniture that feels like a deck. Fine if wanted. Robinia (black locust) is a temperate outdoor hardwood, yellow-green when fresh, a true alternative for benches, not a teak clone. White oak is the domestic outdoor furniture wood of the tyloses story. It does not look like teak. It lasts if designed to drain.

Rubberwood (*Hevea*) with a toner is an indoor plantation wood. It is not outdoor teak. A “teak finish” on a big-box chair is a stain. The crate that says only “teak” fails Lacey the same way a crate that says only “mahogany” fails it. The binomial and the harvest country are the specification.

Kitchen worktops in European shops used teak and then iroko for the same oil-and-water story. An oiled teak counter next to a sink will stain and go grey in the wet zone if you let it. That is not a defect. A film-finished teak counter that delaminates at the sink is a finish and a substrate problem. Ask whether the top is solid, a thick laminate, or a veneer on a board that will swell. Outdoor manners indoors still want a drain and a cloth.

*Tectona grandis* is not a CITES appendix species in the rosewood sense. ## What the wet face tells you

At the bench, ring-porous brown plus a greasy feel plus a blunted iron is still the working ID. Outdoors the color goes toward silver-grey; a fresh cut or a sanded patch shows the old gold. Weight is oak’s neighbor. Smell is milder than juniper. A plantation board with wide rings will still weather; it may check more and hold less oil. Standing-dead or poorly stickered stock cups like any ring-porous hardwood. Teak’s stability is excellent, not magic.

On a yacht: look at the seam. Real caulking has a compound line and a thickness of plank. A printed teak floor in a condo has neither. On a garden bench: look at the end grain of the slats, the fastener metal, whether the seat can drain. On a 1950s dining chair: oil, a honey that has gone toward brown, joints that were made for a room. On a 1990s patio set: plantation rings, stainless screws, a price that may or may not match the papers.

Arkansas does not grow teak. A designer who wants the outdoor manners in a domestic hardwood is looking at white oak or cypress, not at a stain. A designer who wants the 1960s interior color is looking at a named *Tectona* with papers, or at walnut, or at an honest iroko. The caulked deck and the grey bench share a species with the campaign chest. They share a wet-face habit. They do not share a century. Name the tree. Then decide whether the job is a seam, a slat, or a wish.
