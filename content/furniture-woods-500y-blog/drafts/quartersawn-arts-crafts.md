# Quartersawn oak and Arts and Crafts

Gustav Stickley’s Craftsman Workshops at Eastwood, on the edge of Syracuse, hung white oak in a tent with aqueous ammonia. The tree’s tannin went brown in the fiber, not only in the pore. Ray flake — the silver grain English joiners already knew from riven wainscot — went darker or lighter than the ground, depending on the board. *The Craftsman* (1901–1916) published the settle and the library table as if the reader already knew the tent. William Morris, in *Hopes and Fears for Art* (1882) and in the practice of Morris & Co., had already set oak and the visible joint against ebonized mahogany pretence. The fashion of 1900 is a sawing choice and a chemistry choice. Figure 3 is the sawing. The tent is the chemistry.

## Why oak, why that face

They wanted a northern hardwood that was not mahogany’s empire and not walnut’s Victorian parlor. Oak was local or could be, tannic, visible in the grain, and old in the English floor. Quartersawn stock shows the medullary rays as flake and moves less in width than a flatsawn board (FPL-GTR-190, ch. 4: tangential shrinkage commonly about one and a half to two times radial). Settles and library tables wanted wide, quiet faces. Flatsawn Mission exists. It was the cheaper line. Collectors still sort by flake.

White oak (*Quercus alba* and the white group): specific gravity about 0.68, Janka about 1,360 lbf at 12 percent (FPL-GTR-190, table 5–3a). Tyloses plug the heartwood pores. That is why tight cooperage attaches to white oak, and why a fume has tannin to react with. Northern red oak (*Q. rubra*): SG about 0.63, Janka about 1,290 lbf. Open pores. You can blow through a straight red-oak end grain. Stain floods. Fuming is less dramatic. The shops that cared specified white. Figure 4 places both among the anchors. European *Q. robur* and *Q. petraea* have the same ray anatomy; a single FPL Janka wants a bench citation `[CITE NEEDED]`. Slow-grown English or Baltic oak is often finer than a wide-ringed second-growth American board. The binomial does not tell you the ring count.

Maple will not fume the same way. A “fumed” maple is a stain. Ash will take a brown; it is not oak’s tannin story.

Haneca et al. (2005) and the Gdansk wainscot trade explain why a radial oak face was already dear in 1565. Gillows could pay nearly mahogany prices for Danzig oak in 1740 (Bowett, provincial woods). Stickley did not invent quartering. He made the flake the badge of a factory that wanted to look like a guild.

## Eastwood, Sapperton, East Aurora

Eastwood made the American object most people mean by Mission: a settle or a library table in quartersawn white oak, through-tenons with visible pins, corbels under a top, a brown that photographed well. The mill was not a cottage. The catalogs are the proof: models, prices, a finish called fumed.

In the Cotswolds, Ernest Gimson and Sidney and Ernest Barnsley at Sapperton were closer to Morris and farther from a mail-order settle. English oak, visible joints, a finish that was not always a fume. C. F. A. Voysey’s designs — the owl suite is the postcard — used oak as a northern moral wood. Charles Limbert’s factory in Grand Rapids and then Holland, Michigan, cut cutouts into oak that was sometimes lighter, sometimes more Mackintosh than Eastwood. Elbert Hubbard’s Roycroft shops at East Aurora, New York, made a heavier, more hammered oak. The Prairie school overlapped: oak interiors that were architecture. Lesser Mission factories from Indiana to the Pacific coast flooded the catalogs after 1905. Collectors still sort by flake, by hardware, by the red decal on a drawer.

Golden oak of the 1890s — often quartersawn, often a yellow-brown varnish, pores filled — is a cousin, more factory, more Renaissance-revival leftover. Do not collapse it into Stickley. A filled golden-oak sideboard and a fumed 1910 settle are both oak. They are not the same finish religion.

Museum holdings — the Art Institute of Chicago, the Dallas Museum of Art, Winterthur’s Mission pieces — are where an unstripped surface still teaches. A dealer’s “refinished Stickley” is often a darker, thicker thing than Eastwood shipped.

## Joinery

Through-tenons with visible pins are the American Craftsman signature. Draw-boring is older than Stickley; Chinnery’s oak stools already pulled a shoulder with an offset pin. Eastwood made the pin a show face. The pin can be oak or a darker walnut. A steel dowel with a wood cap is a later cheat unless named. Historic tenons often sit flush or a hair proud. A tenon that looks like a hat is a reproduction flourish. Proud tenons get knocked.

Corbels under a top are brackets, often through-tenoned or screwed from above. The top itself must still move. Buttons, slots, or a cleat that does not glue across a wide quartered leaf — quartered moves less, not zero. A library table with a top screwed tight around the perimeter will split on a heating season the same as a Jacobean board.

Frame-and-panel doors on sideboards: groove, floating panel, often a quartered panel so the flake is the door. Raised panels are less the Mission language than a flat field and a square edge. Limbert’s cutouts are pierced flats; they want even, quartered or rift stock so the void does not wander across a cathedral.

Seat frames and settle rails are mortised. Glue blocks hide in corners. Hardness at 1,360 lbf lets a slender Mission rail span farther than a pine rail; it does not excuse a missing stretcher on a cheap factory cousin.

Hardware: hammered copper and iron on American pieces; quieter iron on many English ones. A bright brass knob on a 1910 settle is usually a replacement.

Fumed finish is not a joint, but it is shop work. Ammonia vapor, a tent or a chamber, color that keeps changing after the tent comes down. Sunlight still moves the brown. Clients who want a 1905 settle matched to a new leaf must be told that the next two years are part of the finish. Ammonia is dangerous. Ventilation is not a suggestion. Test boards from the same tree if you can. The latewood and the ray will not go the same brown. That is the point.

A morning in that tent is a chemistry shift you can smell before you can see. Boards hung on edge, a tray of aqueous ammonia, a wait measured in hours, not a wipe of pigment. When the flaps open, the oak is already brown and still not finished changing. Gimson’s bench at Sapperton was a quieter room: a visible mortise, a finish that might be oil, a piece that did not need a mail-order photograph. Both rooms used the radial face Figure 3 names. One broadcast it. One sat with it.

## The scratch test and the pore

Not every brown oak chair is fumed. Many were stained and shellacked. A true fume has color in the wood. A scratch that stays pale is a stain. A scratch that stays brown is in the fiber — or a deep dye. Original finishes are thin. Restoration that pours polyurethane on a 1905 settle is a different object. The pore Stickley left open is a 1910 idea, against the filled golden-oak pore of 1892.

Red oak pretending to be the 1905 catalog is a common floor. The pore argues. The ray is quieter. The water-drop test on an unseen end still sits on white and sinks on red if the finish has not sealed the question.

Printed “Mission oak” on a fiber core has no end-grain rings. Figure 3 cannot be faked by a photograph.

## Conservation

Oak is not a CITES appendix tree in these mains. The living-forest pressures are high-grading of wide clears, oak wilt and sudden oak death in some regions `[CITE NEEDED for a range map if counties are named]`, and a second-growth ring that will not look like Baltic wainscot. Specify quartered. Specify white. Specify ring if you care.

A 1910 settle is the ethical supply of that look. A new “Stickley” in mixed red oak, stained, is a finish. Arkansas white oak still quarters; the flake is the same anatomy Eastwood wanted, on a wider ring. One sentence is enough.

If you fume now, tell the client the color will keep moving. If you do not fume, do not call the stain a fume.

## What to look for

Flake on a radial face, through-tenons, a pin that is wood, a brown that survives a scratch, thin original film, hammered hardware, a decal, a filled golden-oak cousin that is not the same religion. Morris supplied the English sentence. Stickley supplied the American mill. The ray was already in the tree when a Gdansk splitter sold wainscot. The tent made it a catalog.
