# Workability, Hoadley, and the plane that chatters

The plane chatters on interlocked sapele. That is a shop observation, not a metaphor. R. Bruce Hoadley’s *Understanding Wood* is the book that puts a reason under the chatter: grain direction reversing in successive bands, a cutting angle that lifts instead of severs, an edge that was sharp enough for mahogany and is not sharp enough now. Janka on Figure 4 will tell you sapele is harder than *Swietenia*. It will not tell you the scraper is the next tool. This draft is workability as the furniture shop knows it — edge, glue, and the joint that assumes the fiber — not as a catalogue adjective.

## What Hoadley puts on the bench

Hoadley treats wood as a material you cut, not as a finish photograph. Cutting angle, sharpness, moisture, and grain direction are the four variables that decide whether a plane leaves a surface or a tear. A high effective cutting angle — a tight mouth, a back-bevel, a scraper’s hook — handles interlock and figured faces. A low angle on a block plane loves end grain on a clean pine or mahogany. The same low angle on quartered sapele ribbon is a tearout machine. Figure 3 matters here: a flatsawn cathedral changes direction on one face. You plane “up the hill” of the arch and then you turn the board, or you scrape.

Silica and other inclusions dull edges. Teak is the famous furniture case. The Janka is only moderate (~1,070 in imported notes). The edge dies anyway. Hone more often. Carbide on the machine side; a dedicated iron on the bench side. Oak tannin rusts planes if you leave the shavings. Cherry is kind until a gum pocket. Maple is fine-textured and hard; the plane must be sharp or the surface fuzzes and then polishes into blotch later. Mahogany, the *Wood Handbook*’s premier furniture tropical, planes, carves, and takes a moulding without oak’s trenches. That is why the splat got thin after 1722, once the duty was off (Bowett). The tool and the statute are the same story.

Moisture is a tool variable. Green wood cuts easier and then moves. Furniture-dry wood at 6 to 8 percent in a winter shop can crumble on end grain and burn on a saw. FPL chapter 4 is the moisture text. Hoadley is the reminder that the iron sees the moisture you actually have, not the 12 percent in the Janka table.

## Species as tool problems

Ring-porous oaks and ashes: the earlywood is a trench. A moulding plane leaves a dotted line unless you fill or you accept the dotted line. Quartersawn oak is easier to keep flat under the plane; the ray flake can chip if the iron is steep and dull. Rift is the even stripe (Figure 3) and a friend to a run of legs.

Diffuse-porous cherry, maple, yellow-poplar, true mahogany: the plane can leave a surface that looks like light under glass. Interlock, when it appears in *Swietenia* or more often in *Khaya* and sapele, ends that gift. Scraper. Then a cabinet scraper. Then a sanding sequence that does not dish the ribbon.

Softwoods: resin in pine gums the iron. Southern yellow pine’s latewood is a hard band that the plane rides up onto; the surface goes wavy. Eastern white pine is the opposite gift — 380 lbf on Figure 4, a joiner’s wood, dents under a buckle.

Rosewoods and ebonies: hard, often oily, dust that sensitizes. Do not machine *Dalbergia* without collection. Some people do not get a second chance at a first exposure `[CITE NEEDED: a named occupational-health note]`. Wipe oily faces before glue. A scraper often beats a plane. After 2 January 2017 the genus is Appendix II; after 11 June 1992 *D. nigra* is Appendix I. The tool problem is older than the appendix. The appendix is why you may be scraping walnut instead.

Beech takes a turning gouge and a steam bend (Thonet No. 14, V&A W.31-2011). Elm takes a seat boring and a wedge, not a draw-bore. Boxwood takes a stringing plane a millimeter wide. Lime (basswood) takes a carver’s gouge across the grain without crumbling. Each of those sentences is a tool choosing a tree.

## Glue is a tool

Hide glue — animal collagen, hot or liquid — is the historic furniture adhesive. It tacks. It creeps a little. It reverses with heat and moisture. A 1760 mahogany chair can come apart for repair because the glue was designed to. Hoadley and every conservator you trust will say some version of this. Hide glue on a veneer ground is the historic sandwich. It fails when the ground moves and the glue goes brittle: crotch mahogany on yellow-poplar, American Empire, the face checks.

Urea-formaldehyde is the 20th-century interior-furniture glue: cheap, gap-intolerant in the wrong way, not reversible. Klim’s 1990 WAG paper is the conservator’s timeline: protein, then casein (Aalto’s cold bends), then phenolics for wet work, then cheap ureas. A UF-glued breadboard that was glued across a flatsawn top has made a permanent split. Phenol-formaldehyde, the dark line of exterior ply, is a different chemistry: better wet behavior, still not a hide-glue repair culture.

Yellow PVA is the small-shop default. It creeps less than hide, reverses poorly, and grabs faster than a beginner wants on a complicated clamp-up. Choose the glue for the life of the joint. Then design the joint for movement: floating panels, slotted screws, buttons under a top. Glue will not repeal chapter 4. A perfect iron and a glued-in panel still give you a split door.

## Joinery as workability

Draw-bored mortise and tenon in riven oak holds because the pin, driven slightly offset, pulls the shoulder tight as the oak moves (Chinnery, *Oak Furniture*). You need a wood that crushes in a predictable direction. Interlocked elm does not get that pin in a seat; it gets a tapered leg and a wedge from above (Evans).

A mahogany splat takes a gouge across the grain without crumbling and a chisel along the grain without tearing into a pore line the way oak does. Hard maple will hold a bead and fight the same gouge. Sapele will chatter. Specify the wood for the tool in the plate, not only for the color in the plate.

Slotted screws in a breadboard or a table top are workability of a different kind: the screwdriver is the tool, the elongated hole is the joint. If you drive them tight at the ends of a wide flatsawn top, you have used the tool well and the design badly.

Hand-cut dovetails in pine are easy and look like it if you are careless. In oak they want sharp marking and a mallet. In maple they want patience. In plywood they want a different conversation: you are cutting veneers and glue lines. The veneer draft takes that sandwich.

## Machines, and what they hide

A wide-belt sander will flatten chatter and also flatten the conversation. You can sand sapele into submission and then watch the ribbon blotch under stain because the torn fibers and the dense bands drink differently. A sharp scraper and a plane, used first, leave a surface that finishes more evenly. Hoadley is not nostalgic about this. He is describing the fiber.

CNC routers like MDF and even-textured hardwoods. They do not like silica, voids, or a knife that has already died on teak. Carbide helps. Dust collection is not optional on rosewood, on MDF, or on western redcedar. The machine does not repeal the material.

Arkansas oak, walnut, cherry, maple, and pine cover a useful workability band. Interlock is rare. Tannin in oak will still rust a plane. Cherry will still blotch if you skip a conditioner or a scraper. Domestic is not a synonym for easy. It is a synonym for a known iron.

## A gouge, a scraper, and a glued panel

Chippendale’s splat is a gouge problem. Mahogany’s diffuse texture lets the tool cross the grain without the crumbling oak gives you in a thin piercing. Walnut can do some of it. Hard maple will hold the arris and fight the same cut. Sapele will chatter in the ribbon and then take a scraper to a finish that looks like the *Director* plate if you are patient. The plate does not name the iron. The wood does.

A cabinet scraper, sprung, is Hoadley’s answer to figure. It is also the answer to a tearout you already made. You are cutting a dust, not a shave. The surface can burnish. Stop before the polish seals the pore if you still have to glue. Hide glue wants a clean, slightly open surface. UF wants fit and clamp. Neither glue wants a surface you just waxed to see the color.

The split door returns here because workability includes the joint you chose after the plane was happy. A perfect sapele panel, scraped, oiled, and glued into a tight groove, is a January crack. Floating panels and slotted screws are tool work: a plow plane or a slot in a breadboard, a screwdriver that stops before it pins the width. The iron and the fastener are the same chapter.

Look at the surface under raking light. Tearout is interlock or a dull iron or a cathedral planed the wrong way. A dotted moulding is oak earlywood. A polished, slightly wavy pine board is latewood bands. A checked crotch veneer is a ground that moved under hide glue that went glass. Figure 4 is hardness. Figure 3 is cut. The iron is the third axis. Hoadley is the text. The split door is what happens when the iron was fine and the panel was glued.
