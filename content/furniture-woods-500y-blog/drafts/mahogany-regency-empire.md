# Mahogany in the Regency and Empire hours

A New York pier table of 1815 wears a crotch-mahogany face so loud the drawer front looks like a folded flame. Behind that leaf, if you chip a corner, the ground is yellow-poplar or pine. Charles-Honoré Lannuier brought Percier and Fontaine’s grammar across the Atlantic; Duncan Phyfe’s later shop learned the same heavier silhouette. English Regency and French Empire did not invent mahogany. They spent the figured log as a picture glued to a cheaper carcase. The Chippendale splat had been a tax story (Bowett’s 1721 Naval Stores Act, in force 1722). This hour is a veneer story.

## Darker, heavier, still the same lane

Adam Bowett’s thesis remains the English backbone. There is little hard evidence of a furniture trade in mahogany before 1700. After the duty came off British West Indian timber, Jamaica wood that had not been worth the freight became a solid carving wood. Ripley told the Admiralty it would undercut deal and wainscot (*Georgian Group Journal*, 1997). Honduras bay-wood later filled volume. Figure 2 keeps that Atlantic lane beside Baltic oak and Indian Ocean teak. By 1800 the lane is older than the style. Cuban and Hispaniola woods still carried quality reputations and Spanish-imperial politics. Commercial sizes of *Swietenia mahagoni* were already tightening. *S. macrophylla* was becoming the volume tree.

Macquoid’s Age of Satinwood (from about 1770) is a pale English hour that overlaps this one and then loses the argument. Thomas Hope’s *Household Furniture and Interior Decoration* (1807) wants mahogany, rosewood, gilt, and a classical archaeology that does not apologize for weight. Percier and Fontaine’s *Recueil de décorations intérieures* (1801) is the French pattern book. English Regency shops — Gillow still among them, now a London and Lancaster house — made the same dark turn. American Empire factories, a decade later, industrialized the crotch.

Do not invent a particular voyage or a price per foot of crotch. The port books, the pattern books, and the surviving grounds are the evidence `[CITE NEEDED: a named Lannuier or Phyfe object record for one crotch pier table]`.

Enslaved labor still sits in the island end of the lane. A gilt-bronze mount does not wash that out.

## What crotch wants from a ground

Crotch is the junction of stem and limb, sliced into leaves that book-match as a plume or a flame. The figure is unstable in the solid: short grain, a tendency to check, a waste of the log if you try to make a drawer front from the fork itself. Veneer is the honest use. Hide glue on a yellow-poplar or deal ground is the historic sandwich. FPL-GTR-190 puts yellow-poplar at SG 0.42 and Janka 540 lbf — light, wide, paint-and-glue friendly, a hardwood that behaves like a well-brought-up softwood. Eastern white pine (about 380 lbf, SG 0.35) does the same job in New England. The mahogany face, if it is Honduran *S. macrophylla*, sits near 800 lbf and SG 0.45–0.50 (FPL imported-woods chapter / Kukachka FPL-125). Kukachka notes that *S. mahagoni* and *S. humilis* run heavier, and that by 1970 those two were used almost entirely at home.

The joinery implication is not a metaphor. A solid Jamaica dining leaf of 1760 moves as one board; the rule joint hides the gap. A crotch face on poplar is two species with two shrinkage rates, tied by a protein glue. If the ground was dry and the leaf was hammered or caulled flat, the picture lasts through a heated apartment better than a wide flatsawn solid of the same figure would. If a later shop glues a breadboard across the sandwich, or belts through the leaf, the contract breaks. The green tulip streak that shows in a chip is *Liriodendron*, not a defect in the mahogany.

Cross-banding at the edges — a ribbon of straight mahogany or rosewood around the crotch — is both a frame and a stress break. French polish or a spirit varnish wants that frame to stay put. A water spill at the edge lifts the band first.

Piano cases in this hour begin to matter. Broadwood in London, then the American makers, wanted a stable box that could wear a figured skin. Mahogany and rosewood both appear. The case is a carcase problem before it is a music problem: a wide surface, a lockboard, a fall that must not twist. Veneer on a softwood or poplar ground is how you get a flame without a checking slab. The Victorian rosewood draft takes the later parlor piano. Here the point is that Empire shops already knew the sandwich.

Boulle work — brass and turtle against ebony, André-Charles Boulle’s Paris technique, revived under the Empire and the Restauration — is a different sandwich on a different ground. It belongs in the ebony draft. Empire mahogany sometimes wears gilt bronze that *looks* like Boulle from across a room. It is not. Look at the inlay line. If the “brass” is a mount screwed on, you have Empire furniture. If the brass is a leaf sawn with the turtle, you have a Paris luxury the mahogany shop is quoting.

## The silhouette

English Regency chairs can still be mahogany in the solid: saber legs, a scrolled tablet, a cane seat, a lightness that Hope’s heavier plates do not always share. French Empire seating is more often a dark show wood with gilt and a rectangular logic. American Empire goes thick: pillars with carved pineapple or acanthus, paw feet, a sofa with a rolled arm that wants a lot of wood or a lot of veneer. The secondary woods tell the town the way they did in the Federal hour. Open the drawer.

Sheraton’s *Cabinet Dictionary* (1803) is late enough to see the turn. The *Drawing-Book* of 1793 still has the tapered Federal leg. Twenty years later the fashionable New York shop is selling a pier table that would look overdressed in a 1790 dining room. Style changed. The genus did not.

Rosewood banding and rosewood chairs appear beside the mahogany. *Dalbergia nigra* is already a show veneer in this hour; the Victorian draft is when the parlor and the piano eat it in quantity. Do not collapse the two hours. A Regency sofa table with rosewood cross-banding on a mahogany skin is a palette, not a substitution.

## Workability when the figure fights the plane

Straight mahogany still planes like a gift. Crotch does not. Short grain around the plume tears. A scraper and a cabinetmaker’s scraper plane are the period tools; a later belt sander is how the leaf gets thin. Interlock in some *Swietenia*, and later in sapele sold as mahogany, wants the same scraper. Kukachka’s warning about tension wood — fuzzy or torn grain in planing — is a shop fact, not a period complaint.

Finish in this hour is darker on purpose. Spirit varnish, oil, then French polish; ormolu mounts that want a brown ground, not a pink one. Old oxidized Empire mahogany can go almost black in the recesses. Refinishers who “bring back the red” are arguing with the hour.

Movement is still modest for the density (FPL’s praise of mahogany’s stability). Wide solids still need to float. The veneered carcase moves less across the face and more at the edges if the ground was flatsawn poplar. Frame-and-panel interiors remain manners for a wide board. The industrial version of that manners is plywood, a century later. The plywood draft in this series is the place for Eames and Aalto. The ancestor is this sandwich.

## Exhaustion, already visible

Kukachka, writing in 1970, treats *S. macrophylla* as the commercial mahogany and the West Indian species as local woods. The exhaustion began earlier. By 1900 Cuban mahogany in commercial furniture sizes is a memory (the peak draft). Empire shops of 1820 were already taking what the islands and the Bay would send. Honduras wood can be coarser; Cuban, when you can still find it on a period piece, is often tighter. A lens and a weight test help. They do not replace a binomial on a new invoice.

African *Khaya* is not this hour. It is the 20th-century name-theft. A “Empire mahogany” suite of 1925 may be *Khaya* with a crotch print. Look at the edge.

French Restauration and Charles X shops kept the dark field and sometimes brought back lighter woods as a relief. English Regency can still surprise you with a cane seat and a saber leg that is mahogany in the solid, a lighter object than the New York pier table. American factory Empire of the 1820s–40s is the hour that floods the inland parlor: pillars turned by the hundred, crotch leaves matched by the dozen, poplar carcases stacked in a drying shed. The secondary wood remains the dating tool. A “New York” Empire bureau with tulip-streaked drawer sides is doing the Mid-Atlantic job. A New England cousin with white-pine interiors is doing the other job. Do not attribute by the flame alone.

Gilt bronze and gilt composition tell on each other. A Lannuier-quality mount has weight and a back that was meant to be seen by a workman. A later factory “ormolu” can be a stamped sheet or a composition that has lost its leaf. That is not a species test. It is a shop test sitting on the same crotch face.

## Figure 5 and the permits

Figure 5 is the later map. Neotropical *S. macrophylla* entered CITES Appendix II in 2003, in force 15 November 2003, typically for logs, sawn wood, veneer, and plywood. *S. mahagoni* and *S. humilis* are listed. Brazilian rosewood (*D. nigra*) is Appendix I. The rest of *Dalbergia* went onto Appendix II on 2 January 2017, Annotation #15 as later revised so finished musical instruments and small finished products would not freeze the concert hall. An Empire pier table already in the country is usually legal to own. Shipping it across a border is a Management Authority question. A new “crotch mahogany” slab from an unnamed exporter is a permit question before it is a decorating question.

The U.S. Lacey Act as amended in 2008, and APHIS Phase VII (1 December 2024), sit on top. Species+ is the living page.

## What to look for

A flame on a drawer, a poplar or pine interior, pillars, paw feet, gilt that is a mount and not a Boulle leaf, a cross-band that has lifted at a water ring. On an English Regency chair: saber legs, maybe still solid mahogany. On an American Empire sofa: veneer, a lot of it, and a ground you can see in a chip. On a Victorian revival: redder, louder, sometimes still *Swietenia*. On a 1960s “Empire” dining room: probably not.

Arkansas does not grow *Swietenia*. A shop that wants this hour’s picture in a domestic wood is looking at walnut crotch — a real figure, a different color — on the same poplar ground the 1815 shop used. Name the face. Name the ground. The flame is a leaf, not a miracle.
