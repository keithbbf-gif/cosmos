# Indian rosewood and the export lane

Kukachka’s FPL-125 entry on *Dalbergia latifolia* starts in the provinces. Native to most of India except the Northwest. Heartwood dark purplish-brown, denser blackish streaks terminating the growth zones, an attractive figure on flatsawn surfaces. Average weight about 53 pounds per cubic foot. Texture uniform and moderately coarse; grain narrowly interlocked, an inconspicuous stripe on the quarter, punched up by the dark zones. Similar in look to Brazilian and Honduras rosewood. That paragraph is a furniture specification written as a forest note. The export lane — Malabar, Bombay, later Java as sonokeling — made it a world wood. CITES made it a permit wood on 2 January 2017.

## What the handbook could measure

Unlike *D. nigra*, which Kukachka had to describe without a test table, Indian rosewood came with shop manners. It air-dries fairly rapidly with little degrade if you protect it; slight surface and end checking, shakes that lengthen, a central core that very commonly contains numerous shakes. Moderately refractory. Kiln-dries well but slowly; color said to improve in the kiln. Heavy, high strength, particularly hard for its weight once seasoned. Moderately hard to work by hand; fair resistance on machines. Calcareous deposits blunt tools. A 25-degree cutting angle gave him a smooth plane surface. Turns well; high screw-holding. Fill the pores if you want a glass top. In the United States of 1970, used primarily as veneer. “High-class furniture and cabinet work” is his phrase for the decorative job.

Compiled Janka is commonly given near 2,440 lbf, specific gravity near 0.83 at 12 percent (Wood Database beside FPL/Kukachka). Figure 4 places that hardness among the series’ anchors. White oak sits near 1,360 lbf and SG 0.68; Honduran mahogany near 800 lbf and 0.45–0.50; yellow-poplar, the historic American ground, near 540 lbf and 0.42. *D. latifolia* is the hard, oily, interlocked cousin of Bahia jacaranda, not a darker teak (teak compiled near 1,070 lbf, SG near 0.55).

Honduras rosewood and cocobolo (*D. retusa*) sit in the same genus palette. African blackwood (*D. melanoxylon*) — Kukachka’s 70 pounds per cubic foot at 6 percent moisture, a woodwind wood — is a *Dalbergia* that furniture shops almost never saw as a carcase. Do not collapse the genus into one parlor color.

## Ports, plantations, and a colonial ledger

Indian joiners used *D. latifolia* at home long before London wrote “rosewood” on a Regency invoice. The export is the European and later American and Danish appetite: veneer for case goods, solids for small work, then mid-century factory furniture that wanted a darker luxury than teak. Java and other Indonesian growth — sonokeling — entered the same trade name. The trees are not a single forest. The invoice often treated them as one.

Do not invent a price per ton at Bombay or a particular Company monopoly clause for rosewood the way teak had one. Teak’s Malabar monopoly (1807) is a documented dockyard story (the teak draft). Rosewood rode the same imperial shipping without the same navy excuse. Pin a dated export-statistic series at publication `[CITE NEEDED: a named Indian forest-department or customs series for D. latifolia]`. Figure 1 is the long clock: Baltic oak, Atlantic mahogany (Bowett’s 1721/1722 duty cut), tropical peak around 1900, then the CITES years. Indian rosewood sits on that clock as a 19th-century parlor wood and a 20th-century export, not as Macquoid’s London age.

Enslaved and coerced labor is louder in the Bahia and West Indian stories. Indian forest labor under Company and Raj administration is a different, still extractive, ledger. Do not tell a Copenhagen sideboard as a wood that appeared from nowhere.

Musical instruments took the species as seriously as furniture did. Sitars and related instruments at home; guitar backs and sides in the 20th-century West. Annotation #15’s later musical-instrument exemption is a concert-hall clause written after factories and guitar shops discovered they had been listed. Furniture is why the genus went on the appendix. A dining table is not a guitar.

## Joinery: shakes in the core, a leaf on a ground

The shake-prone core is a conversion fact. A sawyer who tries to get wide solid tops from the heart will write checks into the panel. Veneer from the outer, sounder wood is the historic furniture use Kukachka already recorded for the U.S. trade. A solid chair arm or a turned pull is a different, smaller contract.

Interlock means the plane will tear on the quartered stripe. Scraper. The 25-degree angle is his machine note; a hand plane with a tight mouth and a steep chipbreaker is the bench version. Calcareous specks — the “petrified” white flecks some boards show — are where a plane iron dies. Feel for them before you take a long finishing cut.

As a face on a case, *D. latifolia* wants the same sandwich the Empire shop used for crotch mahogany: a sliced leaf on yellow-poplar, pine, or, later, plywood or particleboard. Hide glue, then synthetics. The leaf follows the ground. If the ground is a cheap void-core, the figure telegraphs. If the ground is Baltic birch or a decent hardwood ply, the door stays a rectangle. Molded ply versus solid is not this species’ famous hour — that is birch and the Eames-Aalto line — but a rosewood face can ride a molded shell (Jalk and others). The structure is still the laminate. The name on the catalog is still the face.

Piano cases in the Victorian hour used both rosewoods. Indian wood appears on instruments that are not Bahia-colored enough for the romantic caption. Smell and streak, not a dealer’s country, decide. Boulle work is ebony and brass, not this chapter.

Bowett’s mahogany — Jamaica logs after 1722, cheaper than deal when the Navy carried them — is the Atlantic supply that taught Europe to carve a tropical hardwood in the solid. Indian rosewood rarely got that solid-carving hour in London. It arrived as a veneer luxury into a shop that already knew how to glue a leaf. That is why so much “rosewood furniture” is a picture on a pale carcase. Open the door.

Colonial and later Indian workshops used the same species for furniture that never left the subcontinent: carved chairs, carved screens, a box with a brass mount. Some of that work was made for export as “Bombay blackwood” or under other trade names that wander between *D. latifolia* and other dark hardwoods. Shisham in the north is often *D. sissoo*, a different species, a different furniture habit, now also inside the genus listing. Do not flatten every Indian “rosewood” bed into Malabar palisander. Look at the pore and the streak. Pin a named trade-name paper at publication `[CITE NEEDED: a furniture-trade note separating sissoo, latifolia, and blackwood in export lists]`.

Sonokeling from Java is *D. latifolia* grown and converted in a different forest administration. Mid-century European buyers took it as Indian rosewood. The papers, when they exist, should say the country of harvest. After 2017 that sentence is not pedantry.

## Workability and dust

Pore filler if you want the Victorian glass look. Oil if you want the mid-century feel. Neither will make this wood into teak. Silica is teak’s tax; calcium is sometimes this wood’s tax. Dust is a sensitizer across *Dalbergia*. Collect it. Some people do not get a second chance `[CITE NEEDED: named occupational-health note]`.

A dining table in exported Indian rosewood is usually a veneered top on a hardwood or ply ground, with solid edges if the shop was proud. A solid top from the shake-prone core is a future crack. Breadboard ends on that solid are the same geometry lesson as oak: slot-screw or watch it split. The mid-century oil finish wants renewal, not a later film that traps the oil and looks like a failed piano. Victorian London wanted the piano. Copenhagen wanted the grain. Same species, two sheens, two hours.

Glue: wipe, then glue, then wait. Screw-holding is high once you pre-drill. The wood will split a thin rail that oak would only bruise.

## 2017, Annotation #15, and the other appendix

*D. nigra* was already Appendix I. The rest of *Dalbergia* — including *D. latifolia* — went onto Appendix II on 2 January 2017, Annotation #15. CoP18 revised the annotation (in force 26 November 2019): finished musical instruments, parts, and accessories exempt; small finished products under a weight cap on the listed wood per shipment. Sawn wood and veneer for furniture factories are not a casual import. Neotropical *Swietenia macrophylla* entered Appendix II on 15 November 2003. A room can hold both papers. They are not interchangeable.

Figure 1’s last marks are those dates. Species+ is the living page. The U.S. Lacey Act (2008 amendment) and APHIS Phase VII (1 December 2024) sit on top. Vintage Indian-rosewood furniture already in the country is usually legal to own. Export is the question Danish-modern owners learned after 2017. This draft is not a permit.

New specification: another wood, or *D. latifolia* only with the permit path in the file and a named country of harvest. “East Indian rosewood” without a binomial is an ad.

Guitar shops learned the 2017 listing in public; furniture shops have less excuse. A crate of sawn *D. latifolia* for a factory is the object the annotation was written to catch. A finished sitar crossing a border may sit under the later instrument exemption. Verify. Do not guess from a blog. Species+ and the Management Authority are the living references.

## What to look for

Purplish-brown with blackish zone lines, weight, an oily feel, interlock tear-out on a quartered edge, shakes if someone used the core as a top, a veneer line on a case. On a sitar or a guitar: the instrument annotation, still verify. On a 1960s Danish sideboard: maybe this species, maybe *D. nigra*, maybe a print — look. On a new suite at a funny price: walk away.

Arkansas does not grow *Dalbergia*. Walnut will do a dark, hard furniture wood with papers you can read. It will not smell like a Malabar kerf. That is an honest loss.
