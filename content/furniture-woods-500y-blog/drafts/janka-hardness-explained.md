# Janka hardness, a steel ball, 12 percent

A pine tavern table dents under a buckle. A sugar-maple counter does not. The laboratory version of that observation is a steel ball, 0.444 inch in diameter, pressed into the side of a specimen to half its diameter. The load, in pounds-force, is the Janka side hardness. The USDA Forest Products Laboratory reports it at 12 percent moisture content (*Wood Handbook*, FPL-GTR-190, tables 5–3a and following; Green, Begel, and Nelson, FPL-RN-0303, on nonstandard specimens). The number is an average. Your board is a sample of one. This draft is what the ball measures, what it does not, and why a dining table and a floor cite the same test for different reasons.

## Side hardness, not a moral ranking

The test is a dent test. It is not modulus of rupture, not shear, not “strength” in the beam sense. A wood can be hard and brittle, or softer and tough. Elm is the furniture reminder: not the hardest seat, but a seat that will not split out to the rim when you drive a tapered leg (Evans, *Windsor-Chair Making in America*). Janka does not tell you that. Specific gravity does not fully tell you that. The job does.

FPL’s convention is side hardness at 12 percent MC. End hardness is a different, usually higher number; shops that quote one figure almost always mean side. Moisture moves the number. A wet board dents easier. A bone-dry antique pine table can feel harder than a damp oak board. The moisture draft is the first chapter. This one assumes 12 percent because that is how the tables were built.

Green, Begel, and Nelson (FPL-RP-643) showed you can estimate Janka from specific gravity for many species. The scatter is real. Figure 4 is that scatter for ten anchors in this series. Density and hardness travel together. They are not the same point.

Treat imported figures as handbook-or-compilation means. Brazilian rosewood and Gabon ebony sit off the right of Figure 4 — Janka compilations well above 2,000 and 3,000 lbf — and they are not structural table woods in the way oak is. A fingernail into an underside arris is crude and useful: pine yields, maple barely does, teak is oily and confusing, old finish lies.

## What a furniture shop does with the number

Dent resistance on a show top and a floor is the honest use. A maple butcher block and a pine blanket chest are different objects. A client who wants a dining table that takes a laptop and a belt buckle without a gallery of dents is asking for a number north of cherry, or for a finish that can be revived, or for a habit of using mats. The number does not choose. It ranks.

Chair stretchers and legs care more about section and grain than about a hundred Janka points. Mahogany at ~800 lbf made a slender splat possible not because it is hard but because the fiber is even enough to pierce and stable enough to stay thin (Symonds, quoted and complicated by Bowett). Hard maple at 1,450 will take a turned bead and fight a gouge. Soft maple is a different row. “Maple” on an invoice without hard or soft is a hardness problem.

Floors cite Janka more aggressively than furniture shops. A 2,000-lbf exotic and a 1,360-lbf white oak are sold as a ranking. Wide-plank flatsawn oak at 1,360 will still dent under a stiletto if the finish is thin and the heel is small. The test ball is larger than a heel point. Localized denting is not the table mean. Species with large earlywood pores (red oak, ash) can crush in the pore band even when the latewood is hard. The ball averages a small circle. A floor averages years of chairs.

Workability is not Janka. Silica in teak dulls edges on a wood that is only moderately hard. Interlocked sapele at a Janka near 1,410 (Wood Database compilation; check FPL before treating it as law) chatters under a plane. Hoadley is the shop book for that. The workability draft takes the tools. Hardness is why a pine plane shaving is easy and a hickory one is work. It is not why the sapele tears.

## History did not use the ball

Macquoid did not rank Ages by Janka. Shops ranked by the tool in hand and the dent in service. Oak for joined chests. Walnut for veneer and for American solids. Mahogany after 1722 because it was cheap enough to carve in the solid, not because a laboratory existed. Beech for Thonet because it steam-bends, with a Janka near 1,300 that nobody in 1859 wrote down. The ball is a 20th-century way to put a number on a 16th-century observation: some woods mark, some woods don’t.

Secondary woods are the other ranking. Yellow-poplar at 540 lbf lined Philadelphia drawers because it was cheap, stable enough, and light. You lift the drawer. Solid oak drawer sides make a chest that fights you. Janka predicts the dent on the arris. Density predicts the lift. Both belong on the specification.

## Limits, and a dent you can see

The test is on clear, straight-grained specimens when the handbook means what it says. Your knot, your interlock, your juvenile wood, your 8 percent or 14 percent moisture, your flatsawn face with a pore band — those are not the specimen. Green, Begel, and Nelson’s nonstandard-specimen note exists because people keep testing flooring scraps and small pieces. The correlation holds in the large. A single shop test with a homemade ball is a story, not a table.

Finish changes the felt hardness. A thick film hides a soft face until the film breaks. Oil on pine does not. A 1980s stain can make maple look like walnut; it does not make it dent like walnut. Fumed oak is a color reaction, not a hardness change.

CITES does not care about Janka. *Dalbergia* on Appendix II (2 January 2017, Annotation #15 revised 2019) and *Swietenia macrophylla* on Appendix II (15 November 2003, Annotation #6) are permit woods whether they dent or not. Lacey 2008 §8204 and APHIS Phase VII (1 December 2024) want a binomial, not a ball test. Hardness will not get a crate through Savannah.

## How to use Figure 4 in a room

Stand a pine tavern table next to a maple counter. Press a thumbnail into an unseen arris. That is the test without the machine. Then look at the job. A show top that must stay clean: maple, oak, hickory, or a finish you will renew. A carved splat: mahogany or walnut, and a section that forgives the gouge. A Windsor seat: elm, for split resistance you cannot read on Figure 4. A blanket chest: pine, and an honest expectation of dents.

Arkansas white oak, maple, hickory, cherry, and walnut cover most of the table. The number on the wall is FPL’s, at 12 percent, side hardness. Your board will vary. Specify the species and the job. Do not specify “hardwood” and hope the ball is on your side.

Flooring catalogues made Janka a consumer word. Furniture catalogues mostly did not. That is why a client now asks whether a cherry table is “hard enough” and expects a single integer. Cherry at 950 lbf is a fine dining top if the house will accept a few marks. It is a poor shipping counter. Maple at 1,450 will still mark under a dropped can, because the ball is not a can edge. Hickory at 1,820 is the domestic top of Figure 4 and a wood that fights a plane. Specify the abuse, then pick the row.

End hardness, when FPL reports it, runs higher than side hardness. Do not borrow the end number to sell a top. A floor at 6 percent in January will dent a little less and gape a little more; at 14 percent it will dent more and cup. The table does not replace the moisture chapter.

Look at the dent that is already there. A buckle mark in pine is the species doing its job. A crushed earlywood pore in red oak is the anatomy doing its job. Figure 4 is the map. The ball is 0.444 inch. The room is larger.
