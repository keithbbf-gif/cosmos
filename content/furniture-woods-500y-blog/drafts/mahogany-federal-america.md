# Mahogany in Federal America

Duncan Phyfe’s New York shop, on the street that became Fulton, sold a shield-back chair whose splat is thinner than a Chippendale splat and quieter. The wood is still mahogany. The statute that made Jamaica logs cheap enough to carve in London — the Naval Stores Act of 1721, in force 1722 — is Adam Bowett’s English story (*The English Mahogany Trade 1700–1793*; *Georgian Group Journal*, 1997). Federal America inherited the fiber and the Atlantic lane, not the duty cut. After independence the same *Swietenia* came in under American customs, to shops that already knew yellow-poplar as a servant. The chair is a Hepplewhite plate in a New World carcase.

## The same genus, a different coast

Bowett killed Macquoid’s 1733 and Cescinsky’s 1748 as the dates mahogany “arrived.” The English furniture trade in the wood is thin before 1700; the 1660 Book of Rates names brazeletto, ebony, fustic, lignum vitae, and not mahogany. After 1722 the customs values jump — Bowett’s RFS notes give £695 in 1723, £3,121 in 1725, £4,060 in 1729 — because duty came off British West Indian timber and Navy ships could carry it freight-free. Thomas Ripley told the Admiralty it would undercut deal and wainscot. Jamaica dominated the early furniture supply. Honduras “bay-wood” grew as a cheaper, often coarser later stream. Twenty to forty percent of the English intake after about 1730 entered through out-ports. Gillow of Lancaster bought Jamaica wood as a provincial firm in a port world.

Figure 2 puts that Atlantic lane beside Baltic oak and Indian Ocean teak. They are parallel clocks. An American Federal chair sits on the Atlantic clock. It does not sit on Macquoid’s London staircase as a late Age of Mahogany leftover. Hepplewhite’s *Guide* (1788) and Sheraton’s *Drawing-Book* (1793) are the pattern books the coastal shops already owned or pirated. The wood in the chair is the wood Bowett tracked through Kingston and the out-ports, now billed in dollars at Philadelphia, New York, Boston, Newport, Charleston, Baltimore, and Salem.

What the early United States charged on that wood is a customs story I will not invent. The published American duty schedules of the 1790s treat West Indian timber; pin the exact line at publication `[CITE NEEDED: the 1789/1790/1794 tariff language on mahogany]`. The point for the shop is simpler. Coastal towns could get *Swietenia*. Inland towns used walnut and cherry because the log was local. A “Federal” high chest in the Ohio valley with no mahogany is not a failed Boston piece. It is a different forest.

Enslaved labor logged and milled on the islands that filled those bills. That belongs in the caption, as it does in the Chippendale hour. A Phyfe chair can be beautiful and still be a receipt.

## Two *Swietenia*, and the numbers that explain a tapered leg

*Swietenia mahagoni* — Cuban, Santo Domingo, West Indian — is the finer, often darker, tighter mahogany of the early trade. Kukachka (FPL-125, 1970) says *S. mahagoni* and *S. humilis* are generally heavier than the big-leaf species and, by his date, used almost entirely within their growth areas because volume was already limited. *S. macrophylla* — Honduran, big-leaf, later “genuine mahogany” — ranges from southern Mexico through Central America into the Amazon. It supplied the major commercial volume by the time FPL wrote the imported-woods chapter.

The *Wood Handbook* (FPL-GTR-190, 2010) describes American mahogany as pale pink to dark reddish brown, generally straighter than African *Khaya*, easy to dry, dimensionally stable, durable, easy to work, a premier veneer wood. Janka is commonly cited near 800 lbf, specific gravity near 0.45–0.50 (imported-woods chapter / Kukachka FPL-125). Kukachka puts mahogany’s strength in the neighborhood of paper birch and black cherry. Figure 4 places that hardness among the series’ ten anchors. Softer than white oak (about 1,360 lbf, SG 0.68). Kinder than sugar maple. Strong enough, in the right section, for a square-tapered Federal leg that would look stingy in oak and glassy in hard maple.

Federal chairs are often still mahogany in the solid: legs, rails, splat, a shoe. Carcases are another contract. Open a Philadelphia or New York secretary of 1795. The show surfaces are mahogany. The drawer sides, dustboards, and backboards are frequently yellow-poplar — *Liriodendron tulipifera*, FPL-GTR-190 at SG 0.42 and Janka 540 lbf — or, in New England, eastern white pine (about 380 lbf, SG 0.35). Charleston mixed pine and sometimes cypress. Hurst and Prown’s *Southern Furniture 1680–1830* and the Winterthur catalogues make that geography an attribution tool. A “Salem” piece with tulip-streaked poplar sides wants an argument.

## Joinery: the splat thins, then the face becomes a leaf

The Chippendale splat was a carving problem. The Federal splat is a drawing problem. Shield, oval, and urn backs want a fiber that takes a fine piercing and a clean moulding without oak’s pore trenches. Mahogany’s diffuse-to-semi-ring texture is that fiber. Seat rails can be slighter than oak rails of the same span. The glue block in the front corner is still there. Stretchers often disappear on the high-style chair; they return on country cousins and on some painted Baltimore seating.

Inlay is the Federal tell that Chippendale shops used more sparingly. Holly and maple stringing, satinwood panels, ebony lines, bellflowers, eagle and urn paterae — a palette of pale and black against the red-brown ground. Much of that black is ebonized holly or a stained hardwood, not a bar of Gabon *Diospyros*. Look at a chip. The ebony draft in this series is the place for Boulle and piano keys. Here the black is usually a line a millimeter wide.

Veneer arrives in earnest on the larger surfaces. A card table with a book-matched mahogany top over a pine or poplar ground is already a sandwich. Hide glue, a hammer or a caul, the leaf sliced from a figured log that was too precious to waste as a solid slab. Late Federal and early American Empire push that sandwich into crotch. The flame figure of a crotch veneer on a yellow-poplar drawer front is a different contract from a solid Jamaica dining leaf. The poplar moves. The crotch wants to stay a picture. If the glue holds and the ground was dry, the picture lasts. If a later refinisher sands through the leaf, the green or purple tulip streak shows. That chip is the lesson.

A breadboard end glued tight across a solid mahogany top will split it the same as oak. The rule joint on a dining table is still the period answer for leaves that must meet and still move. Federal shops knew this. Some later revival factories forgot.

## Shops and plates

Phyfe is the name the market recites. The shop was larger than one pair of hands, and “Phyfe” on a label is not a species test. Charles-Honoré Lannuier’s New York work, with its French Empire grammar, already belongs to the next hour (`mahogany-regency-empire`). John and Thomas Seymour in Boston, Samuel McIntire’s carving in Salem, the Townsend-Goddard circle’s late Newport work, Philadelphia’s remaining high-chest shops, Baltimore’s painted and inlaid school — each town had a secondary-wood habit and a way of using the import.

Hepplewhite and Sheraton plates assume mahogany for the ambitious seating. They also assume satinwood and painted decoration. American shops used satinwood when they could import *Zanthoxylum flavum*; they used figured maple when they could not. A Connecticut piece with maple inlay is often a satinwood wish. Do not invent a particular Phyfe invoice or a royal commission. The Winterthur and MFA object records are the evidence; the splat and the drawer side are the residue.

Painted fancy chairs — Hitchcock and the factory line that follows — are a maple and beech story with a mahogany stain in the cheaper grades. The stain does not survive a worn arris. A lens on an unseen rail ends the argument.

## Workability and the brown that is not a dye

Mahogany planes and takes a bead without oak’s trenches. Interlock, when it appears, wants a scraper. Crotch and curl are show veneers; ribbon stripe is quieter in *Swietenia* than in later sapele. It finishes like a gift: oil, wax, a spirit varnish, later French polish. Old oxidized Federal mahogany is brown, sometimes with a golden cast in the early pieces. Refinishers who sand to pink and then dye a fire-engine red are making a 1900s dining-room idea, not a 1795 one.

Dust is milder than rosewood’s sensitizing dust. That is not a reason to skip collection. Glue is ordinary if the surface is clean.

## What the next hour does to the same tree

By 1815 the fashion darkens and thickens. Crotch veneer spreads over whole drawer fronts. Pillars, paw feet, gilt bronze, a heavier silhouette — English Regency and French Empire grammar on American poplar grounds. The Regency-Empire draft takes that hour. This one stops at the tapered leg and the stringing line.

By 1900 Cuban *S. mahagoni* in commercial furniture sizes is already a memory (the peak draft in this series). Honduran *S. macrophylla* keeps the name. Then *Khaya* and *Entandrophragma* borrow it.

## Paper, 2003, and a drawer you can open

Neotropical *S. macrophylla* went onto CITES Appendix II (decision 2002, in force 15 November 2003) for logs, sawn wood, veneer, and plywood (CITES press release, 11 November 2003). *S. mahagoni* and *S. humilis* are also listed. Finished furniture may fall outside that annotation; the sawn wood in the factory does not. Brazilian rosewood (*Dalbergia nigra*) is Appendix I — a different genus, a sharper brake — and the rest of *Dalbergia* followed onto Appendix II on 2 January 2017, Annotation #15. Those dates belong on the same shop wall as Bowett’s 1722, because a new “Federal mahogany dining set” from an unnamed exporter is a labeling problem before it is a style problem.

The U.S. Lacey Act as amended in 2008, and APHIS Phase VII declarations (1 December 2024), make a binomial on an invoice part of the piece. A 1795 chair in a collection is the ethical supply of that look.

## What to look for

A shield or oval back in the solid, oxidized brown, square-tapered legs, holly or maple stringing, a shoe you can feel. Open a drawer: poplar in the Mid-Atlantic, pine in New England, a mix in the South. On a late piece, crotch veneer over that same pale ground. On a Victorian “Federal”: redder finish, machine-cut inlay, maybe still *Swietenia*. On a 1960s suite: probably *Khaya* or a print. On a new table: the invoice binomial.

Arkansas does not grow *Swietenia*. A shop that wants Federal thinness in a domestic wood is looking at walnut or cherry, and at a splat that will not take the same open piercing. Yellow-poplar is still the right servant for a veneered face. Name the wood. Do not name the island you did not pay for.
