# The tropical hardwood peak around 1900

By 1900 a Chicago or London factory could put Cuban mahogany, Bahia rosewood, Burma teak, and a strip of African ebony on the same invoice, and the woods would still mean the trees. Twenty years later the word mahogany had begun to leak toward *Khaya*. Forty years later lauan cores wore walnut photographs. The years on either side of 1900 are the last hour when the fashionable tropical names and the fashionable tropical genera still mostly matched — and the hour when the match was already breaking. Cuban *Swietenia mahagoni* in commercial furniture sizes was already a memory. Honduran *S. macrophylla* held the name. Brazilian *Dalbergia nigra* still arrived, thinner in log and thicker in veneer. Figure 1 is that crest: after Baltic oak and Bowett’s 1722 mahogany duty cut, before the CITES years.

## What “peak” means here

Not a single tonnage number I will invent. Peak means the last decades when piano factories, mail-order suites, world’s-fair displays, and city shops could still specify *Swietenia*, *Dalbergia*, *Tectona*, and *Diospyros* as ordinary luxury, while the source forests were already writing the shortage Kukachka would record in 1970. Pin a comparable import series at publication `[CITE NEEDED: a named UK or US tropical-hardwood import series, 1880–1914]`. The objects are evidence enough to start: rosewood parlor pianos, mahogany dining rooms still in the true genus, teak campaign leftovers, ebony stringing, satinwood revival desks.

Bowett’s English mahogany story is the start of this crest, not the crest itself. The Naval Stores Act of 1721, in force 1722, took the duty off British West Indian timber. Ripley told the Admiralty the wood would undercut deal and wainscot (*Georgian Group Journal*, 1997). Jamaica dominated the early furniture supply; Honduras bay-wood grew as volume. Customs values jumped in the 1720s. That statute made a tropical hardwood cheap enough to carve in the solid. The 1900 factory made it cheap enough to veneer onto every sideboard in a catalogue. Same genus, different conversion, a thinner leaf on a larger production.

Macquoid’s *Age of Mahogany* (to 1770) was already a periodization, not a law, when the Edwardian dining room still said mahogany and meant, if you were lucky, *S. macrophylla*. The Age had overflowed its dates. The tree had not overflowed its range.

## Four woods on one factory floor

Mahogany. Kukachka (FPL-125): *S. macrophylla* the commercial volume, Mexico to the Amazon; *S. mahagoni* and *S. humilis* generally heavier, by 1970 used almost entirely at home. *Wood Handbook* (FPL-GTR-190): pale pink to dark reddish brown, stable, durable, easy to work, premier veneer wood. Janka near 800 lbf, SG near 0.45–0.50. Strength in the neighborhood of cherry and paper birch. Figure 4 places that mid-density carvable wood among the anchors. A 1900 “mahogany” suite may still be this. A 1925 suite may be *Khaya*. Look at the ribbon and the pore.

Rosewood. Kukachka had no mechanical table for *D. nigra* and said so. Bahia to Rio, oily, fragrant, barely floatable dry, veneer for decorative plywood, *Machaerium* already mixing in. *D. latifolia* at about 53 pounds per cubic foot, compiled Janka near 2,440 lbf. Piano cases and parlor sets of 1880–1910 are the objects. Dean’s Atlantic-forest history is the background. The Victorian and Gothic-revival drafts take the silhouettes. Here the point is volume: more cases, thinner leaves, a forest that Kukachka would call nowhere abundant.

Teak. FPL: properties generally on par with U.S. oaks; compiled Janka near 1,070 lbf, SG near 0.55–0.66; silica dulls tools; oily; a dockyard wood that furniture inherited (the teak drafts). 1900 is still imperial deck and campaign chest more than Danish oil. The suburb comes later.

Ebony. African commercial *Diospyros* about 63 pounds per cubic foot; Ceylon about 73 (Kukachka). Inlay, keys, turnery. Not a carcase. The ebony draft is the millimeter. On the 1900 floor it is a strip in a drawer of parts.

Yellow-poplar and pine are on that floor too, as grounds. Crotch mahogany on poplar is an Empire inheritance the factory never retired. A 1900 sideboard with a flame face and a green tulip interior is the same sandwich Lannuier’s shop knew, run through a larger glue press.

## Joinery at factory scale

Solid dining leaves still exist. They still need to float. The rule joint still hides a gap. What multiplies is veneer: sliced faces, rotary faces for the cheap sides, cross-banding, a poplar or basswood or early plywood core. Figure 3’s cut geometry (in the glossary and the rosewood-Victorian draft) decides whether the face shows cathedrals or stripe. The factory prefers yield. Flatsawn and rotary win. Quartersawn mahogany ribbon is a special, not a default.

Piano-case construction is the high-volume version of the Empire sandwich: a stable box, a lockboard, a figured skin, hide glue giving way to earlier synthetics `[CITE NEEDED: a dated adhesive shift in piano-case shops]`. Boulle is not a factory product in 1900; ebonized finishes and gilt transfers are. Molded ply is already in the building — Belter’s 1858 patent is fifty years old; the V&A history treats 1850–1890 as the molded-ply hour — but the 1900 parlor still hides the layers under carving. Aalto and Eames are not yet born as furniture names. The core-stock habit is.

Screws, dowels, and machine-cut mortises replace draw-boring. The wood still moves. A glued-down breadboard on a mahogany top still splits it. Factories that forgot the 18th-century manners left a trail of cracked leaves.

Carving quality is the other factory tell. A Chippendale splat of 1760 is a gouge in the solid, oxidized brown. A “Chippendale” dining chair of 1900 may still be *Swietenia*, machine-cut piercing, a redder film. The wood can be true and the hour still be the peak, not the statute. Bowett’s Jamaica chair and the 1900 catalogue chair share a genus and fight about the tool. Look at the back of the splat. Tear-out from a router is not a gouge.

## Substitutes waiting in the yard

African mahogany (*Khaya*), sapele and utile (*Entandrophragma*), Philippine “mahogany” (lauan/meranti), Spanish cedar (*Cedrela*), toon (*Toona*). Okoume as a European plywood face (Kukachka: the most important African timber by volume for that use). Fuma and later lupuna as corestock. None of these are sins if named. They become the 20th-century default when the invoice still says the 18th-century word.

Satinwood revival desks of the 1890s–1910s may be *Chloroxylon swietenia* (Kukachka: about 60 pounds per cubic foot, almost twice sugar maple in side hardness, veneer only in the U.S.) or a dyed pale hardwood. A lens.

Do not invent a world’s-fair prize or a price list. The genera on the invoice and the secondary wood in the drawer are enough.

Grand Rapids, High Wycombe, the German bentwood towns, the Paris Faubourg, the New York piano loft: different clocks, the same tropical bills of lading. A mail-order suite of 1905 could offer “genuine mahogany” and mean *Swietenia*, or mean a hope. The surviving leaf is the test. A world’s fair display of 1893 or 1900 is a good place to see the names still attached to the trees — and to see the plywood and the composition ornaments already standing next to them. I will not invent a particular exhibit number. The catalogues exist; quote one at publication `[CITE NEEDED: a named 1893/1900 exposition furniture-wood catalogue page]`.

Railroad and steamship freight made the crest possible. A Bahia log and a Rangoon teak balk could meet a Michigan carcase plant in the same season. That is not a romance of globalization. It is a freight ledger and a forest ledger written on the same crate. Enslaved labor is earlier in the mahogany and rosewood stories; wage and coerced colonial labor is the 1900 version. The dining table does not become innocent because the legal form of the labor changed.

## The later brake, already latent

Figure 1’s right-hand marks are the papers the 1900 factory did not file. Neotropical *S. macrophylla* entered CITES Appendix II on 15 November 2003 (decision 2002; press release 11 November 2003), typically for logs, sawn wood, veneer, plywood. *D. nigra* went onto Appendix I on 11 June 1992. The rest of *Dalbergia* followed onto Appendix II on 2 January 2017, Annotation #15 as later revised for instruments and small finished goods. Madagascar ebonies took their own listings. The U.S. Lacey Act as amended in 2008, and APHIS Phase VII (1 December 2024), make a binomial part of the crate.

A 1900 suite already in the country is usually legal to own. A new suite that borrows its names is a labeling problem. The peak is why the appendices exist. The appendices are not why the peak ended. Exhaustion and substitution ended it. The law arrived after the vocabulary had already slipped.

## What to look for

A dining room of 1900: oxidized mahogany that may still be *Swietenia*, a rosewood piano, oak in the hall, pine or poplar inside the cases, maybe an ebony line. A dining room of 1925: the same words, a different chance of *Khaya* and a print. A dining room of 1965: teak, a rosewood sideboard that may be *D. latifolia*, ply cores. Open an edge. Weigh a leaf. Read the invoice if there is one.

Arkansas was sending oak, gum, and pine into the same national catalogues. The tropical names on those pages were imports. A shop that works the domestic hardwoods now is not missing the peak. The peak was a matching of name and genus that lasted about two centuries after Bowett’s statute and then came apart. Name what you use.

A 1900 hotel lobby — mahogany counters, a rosewood piano, teak chairs from a leftover campaign vocabulary, oak wainscot that is still Baltic or American — is the mixed room this series keeps asking you to see. Macquoid’s staircase does not describe it. The invoice, if you could find it, would. The woods are still themselves. The peak is the last decade when that sentence was cheap to say.
