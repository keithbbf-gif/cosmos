# Swietenia, Annotation 6, and the mill permit

A dining table in “genuine mahogany” and a bundle of sawn *Swietenia macrophylla* on a mill floor are not the same CITES object. Neotropical populations of big-leaf mahogany entered Appendix II at CoP12 (Santiago, 2002), in force 15 November 2003. The annotation is #6: logs, sawn wood, veneer sheets, and plywood. Finished furniture may sit outside that list. The mill that imported the boards does not. The CITES press release of 11 November 2003 is the dated public notice. This draft is the permit path for the genus the Chippendale shop already used. It is not a permit. It will not issue one.

## Three *Swietenia*, three clocks

*Swietenia humilis*, the Pacific-dry-forest mahogany, sits on Appendix II from the Convention’s first morning, 1 July 1975. The parts annotation has been revised; current text is not the 1975 text `[CITE NEEDED: original annotation versus current #4]`. *S. mahagoni* — Cuban, Santo Domingo, West Indian, the fine early furniture mahogany — went onto Appendix II in 1992 (in force 11 June 1992, with *D. nigra*’s Appendix I day). Commercial sizes of *S. mahagoni* were already a memory in the shops. *S. macrophylla* — Honduran, big-leaf, the “genuine mahogany” of the later trade — ranges from Mexico to the Amazon. Range states put populations on Appendix III from 16 November 1995 (logs, sawn wood, veneer). CoP12 moved neotropical populations to Appendix II and added plywood to the commodity list. Fifteen November 2003 is the furniture date.

Populations outside the Neotropics — plantation *S. macrophylla* in Fiji, Bangladesh, India, Indonesia, the Philippines — sit outside that Appendix II listing. Kew’s *CITES and Timber* says so in one sentence. Species+ is the check. A plantation board from Fiji and a wild board from a Central American forest are the same binomial and different paper. Do not treat the Fiji board as a loophole you invented. Treat it as a harvest-country fact you can show.

Figure 5 is origin regions, illustrative. Figure 2 puts the Atlantic mahogany lane beside Baltic oak and Indian Ocean teak. Bowett’s Jamaica wood after the Naval Stores Act of 1721 (in force 1722) is the furniture beginning of that lane. The 2003 listing is the permit end of the wild neotropical run. They belong on the same wall.

## What Annotation #6 covers, in mill English

#6 designates logs, sawn wood, veneer sheets, and plywood. Resolution Conf. 10.13 (timber definitions) points those words at Harmonized System codes: logs as wood in the rough (HS 44.03); sawn wood normally thicker than 6 mm (HS 44.06, 44.07); veneer sheets usually 6 mm or less (HS 44.08). Plywood was the late add. The Plants Committee, August 2003, accepted the HS 44.12 definition as an interim: three or more sheets glued and pressed, grains of successive layers generally at an angle (PC14 Doc. 7.5.1). Preferred unit on a plywood permit: square metres.

A rough-sawn flitch of *S. macrophylla* from Guatemala is a #6 specimen. A sliced crown-cut face 0.6 mm thick is a #6 specimen. A five-ply sheet with a mahogany face and a mahogany core is a #6 specimen if the listed wood is what the annotation names. A finished dining table, already a piece of furniture with legs and a finish, may fall outside #6. The boards that became the table did not. The factory that imported the sawn wood needed the export permit from the range-state Management Authority and a non-detriment finding from that Party’s Scientific Authority. A High Point warehouse that buys the finished table still has Lacey due care. It may not have a CITES import permit for the table if the annotation truly excludes finished furniture. That “may” is why you read Species+ and USFWS, not this paragraph, before you ship.

This draft is not a permit. Annotations move. CoP20 can rewrite #6. The living page wins.

## The permit, said as a file

The exporting Party issues the export permit. The Scientific Authority must find the export non-detrimental. The Management Authority must be satisfied the specimen was legally obtained. For Appendix II timber, the importing Party does not always need a matching import permit; national rules differ, and the EU is stricter than the Convention’s floor `[CITE NEEDED: current EU Annex B mahogany import practice]`. In the United States, USFWS is the office. 50 C.F.R. part 23 is the regulation. A shop that wants new neotropical *S. macrophylla* in the solid or as veneer is buying a file: binomial, harvest country, permit number, finding date, quantity, and the annotation that applied on the day of export.

Re-exports need their own paper, tracing back to the original export. A bundle that lost its number in a kiln shed is a problem, not a story. Pre-Convention certificates exist for wood acquired before 15 November 2003 (or before the relevant earlier listing). They are not a shrug. They are a form.

TRAFFIC and Chatham House’s five-taxa paper tracked mahogany among other listed timbers. Illegal wild *S. macrophylla* exists beside legal plantation wood. A certificate on a website is not automatically due care. FSC is a risk tool. It is not a CITES finding and not a Lacey finding. APHIS and FSC both say versions of this.

## Lacey, 2008, and the crate that says MAHOGANY

The Lacey Act (16 U.S.C. §3371 et seq.), amended by the Food, Conservation, and Energy Act of 2008 §8204, makes it illegal to trade plants taken in violation of U.S. or foreign law. A mahogany log illegal in Peru is illegal in Savannah. The declaration — scientific name, country of harvest, quantity, value — is the import stamp. Phase VII, 1 December 2024 (Federal Register 31 May 2024), extends that stamp to remaining plant-product HTS codes that are not 100 percent composite, including more furniture. A crate that says only MAHOGANY fails the declaration the way a crate that says only CEDAR fails it. *Swietenia macrophylla*, Guatemala, is a line. *Khaya ivorensis*, Ghana, is a different line. *Shorea* spp. as a rotary face is a third line. “Genuine mahogany” is an ad.

Some furniture HTS codes were omitted from the first Phase VII list (APHIS noted 9403409040, 9403409060, 9403409080 and promised a later notice with six months’ lead). Check the current APHIS page. This chapter will not update itself.

## What the wood still is

The *Wood Handbook* describes American mahogany as pale pink to dark reddish brown, generally straighter than African *Khaya*, easy to dry, dimensionally stable, durable, easy to work, a premier veneer wood. Janka is commonly cited near 800 lbf, specific gravity near 0.45–0.50 (FPL imported-woods chapter / Kukachka FPL-125). Softer than white oak, kinder than maple, strong enough in section for a slender splat. The 2003 listing did not change the fiber. It changed the invoice.

Wide flatsawn leaves still move. The rule joint on an 18th-century dining table is still the period answer. A factory that glues a breadboard tight across a new mahogany top will split it the same as oak. Floating panels, slotted screws, and a plywood ground are still the manners. The permit does not float the panel.

*Khaya* and *Entandrophragma* (sapele, utile, sipo) filled the name after Cuban *S. mahagoni* ran out of commercial sizes. They are Meliaceae. They are not *Swietenia*. A ribbon-striped quartered sapele door is one of the best furniture faces of the last hundred years. It is a substitute name when the invoice says mahogany. Santos mahogany is *Myroxylon*. Philippine mahogany was lauan. The Chippendale draft (`mahogany-chippendale`) is the splat and the 1721 statute. This draft is the 2003 permit.

## How to specify, and how to look

Write *Swietenia macrophylla*, neotropical, permit path in the file — or *S. macrophylla*, plantation, country outside the Neotropics, papers that show it — or *Khaya ivorensis*, named, or *Juglans nigra*, United States. A 1760 Jamaica chair in a collection is the ethical supply of that look. A new “Chippendale mahogany dining set” from an unnamed exporter is a labeling problem before it is a style problem.

On the piece: oxidized brown on an 18th-century splat, oak or deal secondary wood, a shoe that is a separate piece. On a Victorian table: redder finish, maybe still *Swietenia*. On a 1960s suite: probably *Khaya* or a print. On a new table: the invoice binomial and the harvest country. Ribbon that looks like sapele: read the sapele chapter.

Arkansas does not grow *Swietenia*. A shop that wants that carved thinness in a domestic wood is looking at walnut or cherry, and at a splat that will not be as open as the *Director* plate. Name the wood. Do not name the island you did not pay for.

## A permit that names plywood

PC14 Doc. 7.5.1 is a dull file and a useful one. After CoP12 added plywood to the mahogany annotation, the Plants Committee had to say what plywood was. Three or more sheets, glued and pressed, grains of successive layers generally at an angle: HS 44.12. Square metres on the permit, not a furniture sketch. A five-ply door skin with a *S. macrophylla* face and a *S. macrophylla* core is that object. A five-ply door skin with a *S. macrophylla* face and a *Shorea* core is a mixed specimen; the listed face is still a veneer sheet under #6 if the leaf is what the annotation names. Do not invent a fraction. Ask USFWS. The mill that peels the leaf in a range state needed the finding before the knife hit the log.

Non-detriment findings for mahogany have a working-group history. The Bigleaf Mahogany Working Group, kept alive at CoP12 as Decision 12.21, is the paper trail of range states trying to make Appendix II mean something other than a stamp. TRAFFIC’s later five-taxa work is the outside audit. A finding that is a form letter is a finding a Scientific Authority may still have signed. Due care on the American side is not satisfied by assuming they meant it. Keep the document. Keep the quantity. Keep the date.

A 1760 Jamaica chair does not have that file. It has a collection record and a secondary wood. A 2004 bundle of Honduran sawn wood does have that file, or it should. Those are the two objects this series will not conflate.

Look at the mill ticket, not the hangtag. If the ticket is sawn wood from a neotropical range state, you want Annotation #6 paper dated after 15 November 2003, or a pre-Convention story that USFWS will accept. If the ticket is a finished table, you still want Lacey due care and a binomial. This page will not stamp either document.
