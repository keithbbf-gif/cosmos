# Satinwood in the Adam room

Macquoid’s fourth age, from about 1770, is named for a yellow wood that takes a polish like dry silk. A Hepplewhite or Sheraton card table — satinwood veneer, perhaps a harewood border, painted swags on a golden ground — is the object that taught English fashionable rooms to go pale after mahogany’s brown decades. Robert Adam’s interiors wanted classical light. Satinwood did what gilding did, with grain. Hepplewhite’s *Guide* (1788) and Sheraton’s *Drawing-Book* (1793) assume the color. Two trees, not one, carried the name.

## *Zanthoxylum* and *Chloroxylon*

West Indian satinwood (*Zanthoxylum flavum*) was the earlier prize, a Caribbean citrus-family wood that was cut until it became a rumor. East Indian satinwood (*Chloroxylon swietenia*), from southern India and Sri Lanka, took the trade in the nineteenth century. They are not the same genus. They both sit in Rutaceae. They are both hard, lustrous, and yellow when the finish is kind. They were used mostly as veneer because the logs were not American-walnut generous.

Eric Meier’s Wood Database comparison (secondary, but careful) notes similar pore sizes and a useful tell: East Indian satinwood often shows ripple marks from storied rays; West Indian does not, and looks slightly freckled. Hardness is in the maple-and-above class `[CITE NEEDED: FPL or Kukachka numbers for both species]`. Figure 4 will not give you a handbook row among the ten anchors; the wood sits with the imported specialists. They work with a brittle, sometimes interlocked grain. They take a polish that is the point of the fashion.

Figure 3 is why the face looks the way it does. A quartered leaf can show the ripple in *Chloroxylon* more clearly. A flatsawn leaf of *Zanthoxylum* reads as a quieter yellow with that freckle. Ribbon and cathedral are not oak’s here; the cut still decides whether you see stripe or a broader wash. Veneer factories sliced for figure and for yield. A whole commode in satinwood veneer is a luxury of the 1780s–1810s. Solid satinwood in wide members is scarce then and scarcer now.

*Chloroxylon swietenia* is IUCN Vulnerable (Asian Regional Workshop, 1998, still widely cited). *Z. flavum* is scarce in commercial sizes. Neither is a kitchen-island wood.

## Stringing, harewood, and a painted swag

Inlay stringing is the linear art of the same pale room. A rebate is cut in the ground — mahogany, satinwood, or a dyed sycamore field — and a slip of boxwood, holly, or satinwood itself is glued in and scraped flush. The line can be a millimeter wide. Boxwood is the hard pale; holly is the Federal white; satinwood stringing is a yellow line in a darker neighbor. Rosewood sat as the dark banding. Harewood — *Acer pseudoplatanus* dyed grey-green — sat as the cool field. The marquetry chapter in this series is the picture-making version of that palette. A trellis of stringing on a Sheraton table is a drawing. Do not sand through a painted Sheraton panel to “find the satinwood.” The paint is the design.

Adam’s interiors — plaster, pale paint, classical ornament — made a room in which a golden card table could look inevitable. The brothers did not invent the wood. They invented a demand for light after the brown mahogany decades. Gillow and a hundred unnamed shops made “Adam” and “Sheraton” in the woods they could get. Some of it is satinwood. Some of it is figured maple. Some of it is painted beech. A lens on an unseen rail.

French taste used lighter woods in the late Louis XVI and Directoire years; satinwood appears, and so do other yellows. American Federal shops used satinwood when they could import it, and figured maple when they could not. A Connecticut piece with maple inlay is often a satinwood wish. That wish is a complete design history. It does not require a Vulnerable log. The maple draft takes the birdseye factory. This draft keeps the imported yellow.

Victorian and Edwardian revivals brought satinwood back in display cabinets and Sheraton-revival desks. Some of that is *Chloroxylon*. Some is a dyed pale hardwood. A 1905 display cabinet may still be East Indian veneer over a deal or oak carcase. A 1930s bedroom in the same yellow is likelier avodiré or a toner. The date on the piece is half the identification. The other half is the ray ripple under a raking light.

## Ports, islands, and the plywood-age crowd

West Indian satinwood came from the same Caribbean basin that sent mahogany, in smaller quantities and for a different fashion. Do not invent a particular packet or a price. The species and the islands are the facts; a named customs line that separates *Zanthoxylum* from *Swietenia* is still a research note `[CITE NEEDED]`. East Indian satinwood rode the later imperial timber trade from southern India and Ceylon (Sri Lanka) into Victorian and Edwardian shops that wanted a Sheraton revival and had exhausted the island prize.

Avodiré (*Turraeanthus*), movingui (*Distemonanthus*), limba, obeche, even yellow birch with a toner — the twentieth century sold “satinwood” as a color. Meier’s “Sorting Out Satinwoods” is the practical key. If the piece is not at least mid-nineteenth-century and of quality, East Indian is the likelier true wood; if it is 1785 and English court-quality, West Indian is the romance and sometimes the fact. Density of the true satinwoods is high relative to that plywood-age crowd.

Check CITES and IUCN at publication; listings move. New satinwood furniture is a specialist and permit question. Antique satinwood is the ethical supply for anyone who is not a museum. Lacey still wants the name on a new import. “Satinwood” is not a binomial.

At the bench the wood is brittle compared with mahogany. Thin old veneer chips. Sun fades the yellow toward a sad maple. Keep a card table out of the blast, or accept the fade. A polish that looks like dry silk is the historic point; a thick plastic film is a different object. Hide glue on a deal or oak ground is the historic sandwich. A paper foil on particleboard is a photograph of yellow.

Stringing repair is a small-shop job that punishes haste. The old slip is often brittle. A new boxwood or holly line must match thickness and color; satinwood stringing yellows differently from holly. Cut the rebate clean, do not undercut the ground veneer, glue, scrape, stop. A filler stripe of pale putty is a confession. Painted decoration on a satinwood ground — swags, husks, a medallion — is a different layer: gesso or a thin paint on the veneer, sometimes with a black ink line. The Adam room wanted that drawing. A restorer who strips to “original wood” has destroyed the object the plate book asked for.

Card tables are the teaching form. Rule-jointed leaves, a hinge that hides, a top that may be satinwood on one face and mahogany or oak on the other. Open it. The underside is the truth: deal, oak, or a later plywood patch. Concertina-action frames exist on better tables; a simple pivot is commoner. The wood of the frame is often not the wood of the face. That is not a fraud. It is how a pale luxury was built on a cheaper carcase, the same contract walnut veneer had used a century earlier.

## Federal maple and the sentence for a designer

American Federal shops solved the pale-room problem with what they had. Figured maple — tiger, birdseye, a quiet curl — did the satinwood job in Connecticut, Massachusetts, and the Mid-Atlantic when the imported leaf was scarce or dear. Holly and a little imported exotic turned up as inlay. The object is still Federal. The wood is not a failed satinwood. It is the continental answer.

Biedermeier shops in the German-speaking lands made a virtue of pale birch, pear, and cherry after the Napoleonic wars. That is another pale-room tradition, not Macquoid’s Age of Satinwood, and it has its own chapter. A designer who likes classical light has more than one tree.

Arkansas has no satinwood tree. The Federal substitute on this continent was always maple. Specify *Acer saccharum*, or an antique *Chloroxylon* piece already in the country, or a named modern yellow — avodiré, maple, a dyed pear — with the name on the invoice. A new “satinwood” kitchen from an unnamed mill is a color. Ask which genus. Then look at the ripple, the freckle, or the missing both.

Painted Adam-style furniture on cheaper woods — beech, birch, pine — is the volume that outsold the satinwood commode. The pale room was a color scheme before it was a timber scheme. Sheraton’s plates assume satinwood for the ambitious pieces; they do not assume every provincial chair that now wears the name was built in a yellow veneer. Gillow bought what the ports sent. A painted fancy chair is not a failed satinwood. It is the seat that had to be sold in quantity, the same sentence the beech draft will use for a different factory. Harewood and satinwood on the same table are a pair, not a substitute. The grey field makes the yellow read. If the harewood has gone brown, the pair is broken and the satinwood looks louder than Sheraton drew it.

## What to look for

Yellow lustre, ripple or freckle, a 1790 card table, painted decoration, harewood beside it, stringing a millimeter wide. A Sheraton-revival cabinet of 1905: maybe *Chloroxylon*. A 1930s “satinwood” bedroom: maybe avodiré. A Connecticut Federal table with curl maple: the wish, completed. On a new yellow veneer: the edge, the binomial, the papers.
