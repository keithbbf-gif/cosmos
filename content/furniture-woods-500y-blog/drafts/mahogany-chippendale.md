# Mahogany and the Chippendale shop

Thomas Ripley told the Admiralty that mahogany would be cheaper than deal or wainscot if Navy ships brought it home (Bowett, *Georgian Group Journal*, 1997). That sentence still surprises people raised on Macquoid’s luxury. The Naval Stores Act of 1721, in force 1722, stripped the duty from British West Indian timber. Jamaica wood that had not been worth the freight became, in the port books, a furniture wood you could carve in the solid. The splat of a mid-century London chair is a tax story, a sugar-island story, and only then a style story.

## What the statute changed

Adam Bowett’s thesis, *The English Mahogany Trade 1700–1793*, is the backbone. There is little hard evidence of a furniture trade in mahogany before 1700. The 1660 Book of Rates lists other West Indian woods — brazeletto, ebony, fustic, lignum vitae, sweetwood, speckled wood — and not mahogany. After 1722 the customs values jump: Bowett’s RFS lecture notes £695 in 1723, £3,121 in 1725, £4,060 in 1729. Macquoid’s date of 1733 and Cescinsky’s 1748 are connoisseur dates. The statute is the date.

Jamaica dominated the early furniture supply. Honduras “bay-wood” became a large, cheaper, often coarser supply later in the century. Cuban and Hispaniola woods had quality reputations and Spanish-imperial politics. Figure 2 puts that Atlantic lane beside Baltic oak and Indian Ocean teak. They are parallel, not a relay race.

Gillow of Lancaster bought Jamaica wood as a provincial firm in a port world, not only as a London satellite (Bowett, Jamaica/Gillow paper). Twenty to forty percent of mahogany after about 1730 entered through out-ports, unlike walnut, which had been a London monopoly. Mahogany was a leveller. Provincial chairmakers could get it.

Harewood House paid Thomas Chippendale with money that sat on West Indian estates — Bowett’s opening image. The chair and the plantation are the same economy. Enslaved labor logged and milled. That belongs in the caption, not in a footnote the reader can skip.

## The trees

*Swietenia mahagoni* — Cuban, Santo Domingo, West Indian — is the fine, often darker, tighter mahogany of the early trade. Commercial sizes were exhausted early. *S. macrophylla* — Honduran, big-leaf, later “genuine mahogany” — ranges from Mexico to the Amazon. The *Wood Handbook* describes American mahogany as pale pink to dark reddish brown, generally straighter than African *Khaya*, easy to dry, dimensionally stable, durable, easy to work, a premier veneer wood. Janka is commonly cited near 800 lbf, specific gravity near 0.45–0.50 (FPL imported-woods chapter / Kukachka FPL-125). Softer than white oak, kinder than maple, strong enough in the right section for a slender chair.

Figure 4 places that hardness among the series’ ten anchors. Mahogany is not the hard wood. It is the carvable, stable, moderately light wood that made a pierced splat possible without the crumbling of coarse oak or the glass fight of hard maple.

## Joinery: why the splat got thin

R. W. Symonds argued that mahogany’s strength let chairmakers draw legs, rails, and backs more slender, with more accentuated curves, than walnut allowed. Bowett quotes him and complicates the credit: the style did not wait for a new fiber the way a myth wants. Shops already knew how to make a splat. What they got after 1722 was a wood cheap enough to waste in carving, even enough to pierce, and stable enough that a thin member stayed a thin member through a London winter.

A Chippendale splat in the *Director* (1754; third edition 1762) is a carved, often interlaced, openwork back. The joint at the shoe, the crest rail tenons, the carved knee on a cabriole — these want a fiber that takes a gouge across the grain without crumbling and a chisel along the grain without tearing into a pore line the way oak does. Mahogany’s diffuse-to-semi-ring texture is that fiber. Walnut can do some of it. Oak does it as a thicker, simpler splat, or as a splat you do not pierce.

Seat rails in mahogany can be slighter than oak rails of the same span. The glue block in the front corner is still there; the wood is not magic. Stretchers return when the design asks for them (country cousins, some Gothic plates). The high-style London chair often trusts the joints and the wood.

Dining tables in two or three wide flatsawn leaves, rule-jointed, want the stability FPL still praises. The leaves still move. The rule joint is the period answer: a molded edge that hides a gap as the leaf shrinks. A later factory that glues a breadboard tight across a mahogany top will split it the same as oak.

Carcase work in this hour is often still oak or deal inside. The show wood is mahogany. Open a drawer. The truth is pale.

## Workability and finish

Mahogany planes, carves, and takes a moulding without oak’s trenches. Crotch and curl are the show veneers of the next hour (`mahogany-regency-empire`). Ribbon stripe appears when the grain interlocks and the board is quartered — more famous later in sapele than in all *Swietenia*.

It finishes like a gift: oil, wax, French polish, a film that looks deep. Old oxidized mahogany is brown. Refinishers who sand to pink and then dye fire-engine red are making a 1900s dining-room idea, not a 1760 one.

## What Chippendale’s book does not say

The *Director* is a pattern book. It assumes mahogany for the ambitious plates. It does not assume every provincial chair that now wears the name was made in St Martin’s Lane. Gillow and a hundred unnamed shops made “Chippendale” in the wood they could get. Some of it is walnut. Some of it is oak with a mahogany stain. A lens on an unseen rail ends the argument.

A carved knee on a cabriole is a cross-grain argument. The gouge works downhill on one side of the knee and uphill on the other. Mahogany forgives that more than oak’s pore line and more than maple’s hardness. The knee block — a glued-on piece that thickens the rail-to-leg meeting — is still there on many London chairs. It is a joint, not a carving flourish. Country cousins skip it and keep the rail thicker. The *Director* plate does not show the block. The underside does.

Pierced Gothic and Chinese plates in the third edition (1762) waste more wood than a solid splat. That waste is the duty cut made ordinary. A shop that could not afford to throw half a splat on the floor stayed with walnut or with a simpler back. When you see an openwork back in oak, look at the thickness. The member is usually fatter. The piercing is usually simpler. The fiber is the reason.

Chippendale’s own *Director* plates are not a species catalogue. Plate after plate names the form — a “French chair,” a “Gothic chair,” a “breakfast table” — and leaves the timber to the shop. That silence is useful. The book sold a language of ornament that any timber able to take a gouge could speak. Mahogany won because it spoke that language at less risk than walnut. The plates that show pierced Gothic backs assume a wood that will hold a cusp after the fretsaw has removed half the splat. White oak can do that work if the member stays fat. Cherry can. Pine cannot. The *Director* is a joinery filter disguised as fashion.

Country shops used the same imported boards for slats and stretchers without carving a leaf. Those chairs survive in greater numbers than the gilt pier tables, and they tell you more about how the timber actually entered ordinary rooms.

The Federal American hour is the same genus on a different coast, with yellow-poplar and pine as the servants. The Regency and Empire hour is crotch veneer and a darker, heavier taste. The splat and the statute are the English hinge. The later rooms change the finish, not the fiber.

## Conservation, already latent

Cuban *S. mahagoni* in commercial furniture sizes is a memory. *S. macrophylla* is CITES Appendix II for neotropical populations (in force 15 November 2003). The permit draft (`cites-swietenia-permits`) takes the annotation. A new “Chippendale mahogany dining set” from an unnamed exporter is a labeling problem before it is a style problem. A 1760 chair in a collection is the ethical supply of that look.

## What to look for

A carved splat in the solid, oxidized brown, secondary wood of oak or deal, a shoe that is a separate piece, a crest rail tenon you can feel from the back. On a Victorian “Chippendale”: redder finish, machine-cut piercing, maybe still *Swietenia*. On a 1960s suite: probably *Khaya* or a print. On a new table: the invoice binomial.

Arkansas does not grow *Swietenia*. A shop that wants that carved thinness in a domestic wood is looking at walnut or cherry, and at a splat that will not be as open as the *Director* plate. Name the wood. Do not name the island you did not pay for.
