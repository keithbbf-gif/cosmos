# Moisture, movement, and a stuck door

A door that sticks in August and shows a line of raw wood in the groove in January is chapter 4 of the *Wood Handbook* hung on hinges. Wood is a sponge with a grain. Below the fiber saturation point — commonly near 30 percent moisture content — loss or gain of bound water changes the dimensions across the grain. Tangential change is the large number. Radial is smaller, often by a factor of 1.5 to 2. Longitudinal change is a rounding error, 0.1 to 0.2 percent (FPL-GTR-190, ch. 4). The door is flatsawn in the panel and closer to quartered in the stiles if the shop was paying attention. If the shop glued the panel in, the door is a split waiting for the heating season. This draft is the moisture math and the joinery that assumes it.

## What FPL chapter 4 actually says

Green wood can hold more water than its ovendry weight. Free water sits in the cell cavities. Bound water sits in the cell walls. Fiber saturation is the point where the cavities are empty and the walls are still saturated. Shrinkage and swelling, for practical shop work, begin there. The handbook’s mechanical tables — Janka, modulus, the numbers on Figure 4 — are reported at 12 percent moisture, a convention, not a magic room. A bone-dry antique pine table can feel harder than a damp oak board. Moisture first, then species.

Equilibrium moisture content is the moisture the wood will approach in a given relative humidity and temperature. FPL’s EMC tables are the reference. A shop at 70 °F and 50 percent RH is looking at something near 9 percent EMC `[CITE NEEDED: exact FPL table row if you quote a single pair]`. A January apartment with forced air and no humidifier can run much drier. A coastal August can run wetter. Period houses went cold at night and the average moisture in a chest sat higher than in a 2010 condominium. A flatsawn oak leaf glued up in 1780 will shrink across the grain in that condominium. It may split along a glue line or open a rule joint. Quartersawn leaves suffer less cup, not zero movement.

Published green-to-ovendry shrinkages, from the handbook’s species notes, are the working table:

| Species | Radial % | Tangential % |
| --- | --- | --- |
| White oak | 5.6 | 10.5 |
| Red oak | 4.0 | 8.6 |
| Sugar maple | 4.8 | 9.9 |
| Black walnut | 5.5 | 7.8 |
| Black cherry | 3.7 | 7.1 |
| Eastern white pine | 2.1 | 7.4 |
| Honduran mahogany | ~3.7 | ~5.1 |

Mahogany’s pair is the imported-woods / Kukachka tradition (FPL-125 and later handbook notes). Treat a single decimal as a mean, not your board. The ratio is the lesson. Flatsawn white oak is a wide traveler. Quartersawn white oak is a narrower traveler. Mahogany in the solid was prized in part because the pair is modest for a furniture hardwood. Wide tables still need to float. Figure 3 is why the cut multiplies whatever the species already wants to do.

Dimensional-change coefficients in chapter 4 let you estimate movement between two moisture contents without going all the way to ovendry. The shop version: a 24-inch flatsawn white-oak panel changing 4 percent moisture can move on the order of a quarter inch `[CITE NEEDED: show the handbook coefficient arithmetic if you publish a single fraction]`. That is a groove depth, or a split.

## Joinery that treats moisture as a fact

Frame-and-panel: the groove is deep enough that the panel can lose width and still sit. A spot of hide glue at the top center, or no glue, keeps the show face centered. Glue around the perimeter and you have invented a split door. The stiles and rails, closer to quartered, stay truer next to glass.

Breadboard ends: center fastener tight, outboard fasteners in elongated holes. Glue the tongue at the middle if you must. Glue the whole end and the top splits. Look at the underside of a 1790 table. Slots are evidence of manners. A crack in line with a tight pin is evidence of the other school.

Slotted screws through battens or pocket-screw patterns that all point the same way across a wide top are a factory shortcut that sometimes works and sometimes waits for February. Buttons and table clips are the cabinetmaker’s version: hold-down with travel.

Hide glue on a historic carcase can be taken apart with heat and moisture. The joint is a reversible contract. Urea-formaldehyde on a factory edge is not. A hide-glue mahogany chair can be repaired. A UF-glued breadboard that split the top is a different object. Choose the glue for the life you want, then design the joint for the moisture you will get.

Veneer assumes a ground. Hide glue on deal or yellow-poplar is the historic sandwich. If the ground moves and the glue goes brittle, the face checks — crotch mahogany on poplar in American Empire is the textbook failure. A plywood ground is the 20th-century sandwich: cross-bands cancel movement. A paper foil on particleboard is a photograph. The veneer draft takes the sandwich. This draft is why the sandwich was invented.

## Heat, air conditioning, and a finish

Finish slows moisture exchange. It does not stop it. A film on one face only — a table finished on top and raw underneath — is a cupping machine. Finish both sides. Hoadley says so. Old shops that waxed the underside were not being precious.

A finish that seals the end grain more than the face will still lose the argument at the ends first if the board was wet. End-grain sealer at the mill is a drying practice. It is not a substitute for acclimation. Let the wood sit at the humidity of the destination before you glue a wide panel. The 18th-century shop did this by having the boards in the building. The modern shop does it with a meter and a calendar. A pin meter in the middle of a 12/4 oak balk can lie if the shell is dry and the core is not. Read the core.

Kiln schedules exist because air-drying to furniture moisture takes time and still leaves a gradient. Second-growth Arkansas oak with wide rings dries differently from tight Baltic wainscot. The handbook’s species notes give starting points, not a recipe for your charge.

## Species on Figure 4, read as movers

Figure 4 is hardness and density, not shrinkage. Density and movement are related but they are not the same graph. Mahogany is moderately light and a modest mover. White oak is heavier and a large tangential mover. Ebony and rosewood are heavy and still move across the grain; they just do it as small objects. Pine is light; eastern white pine’s tangential number is not small (7.4 percent green to ovendry). A wide pine cupboard door is a traveler. Paint does not stop it. The panel still floats.

Interlocked tropicals (sapele, some elm) add tearout to the moisture story. The board may be stable enough on the quarter and still chatter under a plane. Workability is the next draft. Moisture is why you do not glue the panel even when the plane is happy.

## A walk around the stuck door

August: the flatsawn panel has swollen. The groove was not deep enough, or a bead of paint locked the edge, or the shop glued the panel. The door rubs the jamb. January: the panel has shrunk. A line of unfinished wood shows in the groove, or a split has opened down a cathedral, or a breadboard has torn the top. End grain on the panel: rings parallel to the face. End grain on a well-chosen stile: rings more nearly through the thickness. That is Figure 3 on a hinge.

Look at tabletops with breadboards. If the pins elongated into slots, someone understood tangential movement. If the breadboard split the top, someone did not. Look at a 2010 condominium dining table with pocket screws every four inches across a 42-inch flatsawn oak top. Look for the crack.

Arkansas shops send furniture into houses with air conditioning. The destination EMC is the design number, not the mill’s 12 percent sticker. Bradley Brand’s oak and walnut still move. Domestic is not a repeal of chapter 4. Specify cut, specify acclimation, specify the joint that travels. The Convention of 1973 will not float your panel. Neither will a Janka number.

## Hide glue, UF, and a panel that should rattle

A floating panel that rattles in January is doing its job. A bead of hide glue at the top center is enough to stop the rattle in a show door without imprisoning the width. A bead of UF around four sides is a split. The glue is a tool. Hoadley will say the joint is a moisture joint first. Conservators prefer hide on objects that should come apart. Factories prefer UF because the clamp time is short and the joint is “forever.” Forever on a wide flatsawn panel is a crack.

Veneer grounds are the other moisture object. A yellow-poplar drawer front moves. A crotch leaf on it does not want to. Cross-banding under the face — a historic ébéniste habit and later a factory habit — is a thin plywood idea before plywood. If you skip the cross-band and glue a leaf to a flatsawn solid ground, you are betting the hide glue will creep enough. Sometimes it does. Sometimes you get the Empire check.

Look at the groove and the slots. If they have room, the door will stick less and split less. If they do not, January will write the rest of this chapter on the face. FPL chapter 4 is the text. The door is the problem set.
