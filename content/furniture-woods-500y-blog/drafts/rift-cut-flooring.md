# Rift cut, matching legs, and a floor

A run of dining-chair legs that do not match is usually a flatsawn run. Two faces show cathedrals. Two faces show something quieter. The set looks like leftovers. Rift cut — rings at about 30 to 60 degrees to the face — gives a straight stripe on all four sides, or close enough that a shop can sell twelve chairs. Yield is worse than slabbing the log. Complaints are fewer. Floors that want a quiet, linear oak without flake are the same cut in a longer piece. This draft is the compromise face: furniture first, then the floor that borrowed the name.

## The geometry, without poetry

Figure 3 is the cylinder. Flatsawn is tangent: arches, maximum tangential width, the large shrinkage number. Quartersawn is radial: rings on end in the thickness, oak flake, the smaller shrinkage number. Rift is the sector between. Hoadley, *Understanding Wood*, is the shop book. Mills disagree about the exact degrees. Thirty to sixty is the useful band. Below about 30 you start picking up cathedral. Above about 60 you start picking up ray flake in oak. A mill that sells “quartered and rift” as a mixed bundle is telling you to specify flake if you want flake, and to specify rift if you want a run of doors to match.

True quartering wastes the log. Rift wastes less than honest quartering and more than through-and-through. You get narrower boards and more offcuts than a flatsawn pile from the same diameter. That is why Baltic wainscot was dear, why Gillows in Lancaster could pay nearly mahogany prices for Danzig oak in 1740 (Bowett, provincial woods paper), and why rift white-oak flooring still costs more per foot than a plainsawn grade from the same kiln charge. The money is the waste and the extra handling. The product is a stripe that does not argue with its neighbor.

White oak (*Quercus alba* and the white group) is the American furniture and floor wood that made rift famous in catalogs. European oak (*Q. robur*, *Q. petraea*) has the same anatomy. Red oak will rift; the flake you are avoiding is smaller anyway, and the pores are more open. A designer who wants Stickley’s silver grain and writes “rift oak” will get a quiet stripe and a disappointed client. A designer who wants a hotel corridor without flake and writes “QS oak” may get flake in the middle of the run. Say the face.

## Chair legs, door stiles, table aprons

Chair shops like rift for legs that must match. A flatsawn leg is a different picture on adjacent faces. Turned legs show the problem in the round: the rings walk around the cylinder and the turning goes from cathedral to stripe every ninety degrees. Rift stock, chosen so the rings sit on the diagonal of the square, keeps a more even stripe through the turning. You still read the end grain. You still reject a blank that is flatsawn dressed up as rift.

Door stiles in a run of kitchen or cabinet doors are the architectural version. One cathedral in the middle of a set of fifteen is the complaint. Rift stiles and rails, flatsawn or quartered panels if you want figure in the field, is a coherent specification. All-rift doors are quieter. All-flatsawn doors are a cathedral gallery. Mixed “Q&R” from the mill is a lottery.

Table aprons and bed rails want the same evenness if they show. Breadboard ends on a flatsawn top are often rift or quartered so the end cap stays straight while the top moves. The fasteners still need slots. A rift breadboard glued tight across a flatsawn top will split the top the same as a flatsawn breadboard glued tight. Cut does not repeal chapter 4.

FPL-GTR-190, chapter 4, gives the shrinkage ratio — tangential about one and a half to two times radial for many hardwoods. Rift sits between. It is not quartersawn stability and it is not flatsawn travel. A wide rift oak table still moves. Fasten it as if it will. Floating panels, slotted screws, buttons under a top: the manners do not change because the stripe is straight.

## The floor that took the name

“Rift and quartered oak flooring” is a mill grade and a sales phrase. Architects mean: straight grain, minimal flake, or a controlled amount of flake, depending on the sentence. Some specs want rift only — no flake. Some want quartered — flake as a feature. Some accept the mixed bundle and then reject boards on site. Write the face. Write the allowed percentage of cathedral. Write the allowed flake. A flooring bundle labelled Q&R that arrives half plainsawn is a purchasing failure, not a surprise of nature.

Floors move less in service than a wide door because the strips are narrow. A 2¼-inch rift oak strip has little tangential width to argue with. A 7-inch flatsawn plank has a lot. Wide-plank floors in heated apartments cup and gape for the same reason dining tables cup and gape. Species and cut still matter. So does moisture at installation. FPL’s EMC tables and the floor’s own installation instructions (a dated NWFA or mill sheet, not a memory) are the documents. This draft is not an installation manual.

Historic floors were often flatsawn because yield won. Heart-pine floors in the South show loud latewood bands and cathedrals. Quartersawn oak strip of the late 19th and early 20th centuries — the tight, straight, sometimes slightly rayed corridors of better houses — is the cousin of Arts and Crafts furniture. Stickley’s rooms and a rift-cut floor are the same fashion in two planes. Golden-oak factory floors with filled pores are the louder cousin.

Engineered floors with a 2 or 3 mm rift-sliced face on a plywood core are a veneer story. The face can be honest rift oak. The core is the movement control. A 0.6 mm face on MDF is a photograph of rift. Look at the edge. The veneer draft takes that sandwich.

## How mills actually cut it

A sawyer who wants rift can take boards from the sectors of the log that sit between the quartered pie and the flatsawn slab. Some mills quarter the log first and then saw each quarter at an angle. Some use a circular mill and sort after the fact. Some saw-and-sort operations call anything without a loud cathedral “rift.” The ticket should say how the mill defines the word. A 10× lens on the end grain ends the argument: rings at a slant, not parallel to the face, not square-on.

Before the circular saw, riven oak followed the rays and gave you quartered stock, not rift. Rift is a sawn idea. You do not split a log at 45 degrees with a wedge and get a furniture blank. You saw it. That is why rift furniture is a mill-age and factory-age product more than a 1600 joined-chest product. The chest wanted ray. The hotel corridor wants stripe.

Sapele and some *Swietenia* show ribbon when quartered — interlocked grain reversing, not oak flake. “Rift sapele” in a door shop often means a quieter stripe with less ribbon flash. Say what you want. You cannot get oak flake from *Entandrophragma*. You cannot get sapele ribbon from *Quercus*.

## A room, a set, a specification

Walk a dining room. If the chair legs match on all four faces and the table top is a two-board cathedral, someone specified two cuts and paid for both. If the chair legs are a jumble of arches and stripes, someone bought “oak” and hoped. If the floor under them is a straight, quiet oak without flake, it is probably rift or a mixed Q&R that was sorted. If the floor is a cathedral every board, it is plainsawn and cheaper, and it will cup more in a dry season if the boards are wide.

Arkansas white oak rifts well. The rays are there if you quarter; they stay shy if you rift. The same forests that filled American shops in 1800 still yield both cuts, second-growth rings and kiln schedules aside. Specify the face. Specify white if you want tannin and a quieter pore than red. The 1740 Danzig price Bowett recorded was a freight and dryness price as much as a cut price. The modern premium is the waste.

## Nail, tongue, and a dry apartment

Strip flooring hides some of the cut argument because the piece is narrow. It does not hide a moisture argument. Install at the destination EMC. A rift oak floor laid at 12 percent in a shop and moved into a 6 percent apartment will gape. The gaps will be even if the cut is even; they will still be gaps. Wide-plank rift is not a repeal. A 7-inch rift plank has less tangential travel than a 7-inch flatsawn plank and more than a 2¼-inch strip. Fasten it as a moving board: cleats, a floating engineered core, or a nail pattern the mill’s dated sheet allows. Glue-down of a solid wide plank on a concrete slab is a split-door idea in another plane.

Chair legs next to that floor should match each other, not the floor. A rift floor and flatsawn legs is a coherent room if you meant it. A mixed bundle in both is a room that happened. Specify twice.

A factory that grades rift by the face photograph, without an end-grain check, will ship you a flatsawn board whose cathedral is shy. Reject it on the dock. The 10× lens is cheaper than the callback. Hoadley’s cylinder does not care about the grade stamp. Figure 3 is the picture. This page is why a set of chairs and a corridor cost more when they match.
