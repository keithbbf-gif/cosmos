# Boxwood stringing and the pale line

A boxwood rule does not wear out in a hurry. Folding rules from the nineteenth-century tool trades — boxwood with brass joints — outlived the men who carried them in an apron. Thomas Bewick made end-grain box the ordinary wood-engraving block; the press then made it industrial. Furniture uses *Buxus sempervirens* the way a jeweler uses gold: stringing, banding, knobs, chessmen, the pale line in a dark walnut veneer. Nobody makes a press cupboard of box. The tree does not make the board. The line is the job.

## A shrub-tree, not a timber

*Buxus sempervirens* is a shrub-tree of tiny rings. Specific gravity can exceed 0.9. Secondary hardness tables put Janka near or above 2,800 lbf `[CITE NEEDED: a primary mechanical test; FPL-GTR-190 does not table European box as a commercial American lumber]`. Figure 4 will not give you a handbook row among the ten anchors; the wood sits off the commercial scatter and still draws the furniture. Pale yellow, closed, almost no visible pore on the face, a silky end grain. Diffuse-porous, so dense some pieces sink. Wide flats do not exist. Anyone selling a “boxwood” table top is selling a veneer or a different tree.

European box from the Mediterranean and the Caucasus, English garden box that took a century to make a billet, later East London “box” that was often *Buxus* from the Black Sea trade. African box (*B. macowanii*) and other hard pale woods filled the engraving trade when Turkish and European box ran short `[CITE NEEDED: 19th-c. engraving-trade supply notes]`. It turns like ivory’s cousin. Files, scrapers, a polish with its own dust. Hand planes can do small faces if the iron is sharp; the wood is abrasive in a fine way. It moves less than its density might scare you into thinking, in small sections. In a humid swing, a wide glue-up of box would still be a mistake. There are no wide glue-ups. There is no stock. Finish is often wax or oil or nothing. The wood is already a finish.

Do not confuse boxwood with “box elder” (*Acer negundo*), a soft maple with red stain streaks. The names share a syllable and nothing else. Holly (*Ilex*) is the other Federal inlay white; it is not as hard. Maple stringing is the cheap pale line. Box is the line that survives a fingernail.

## Stringing: the rebate, the slip, the scrape

Inlay stringing is the furniture joint this slug is for. A rebate — a shallow groove — is cut in the ground, following a line the designer drew. A slip of boxwood is planed thin, sometimes as little as a millimeter in section, glued into the rebate, and scraped flush. Hide glue is the historic adhesive. The ground may be walnut, mahogany, ebony, or a dyed harewood field. The line is a highlight the way a drawing uses a white stroke. William-and-Mary and later walnut furniture uses box lines that way. French ébénistes used it with ebony and brass. Roubo and the Wallace Collection marquetry notes put pale *Buxus* in the same kit as dyed greens and the dark woods. A seventeenth-century English table with box inlay is using the tree correctly: a line, not a field.

Sheraton and Hepplewhite plates assume stringing as part of the pale classical room. Satinwood can be the field; box or holly can be the line; rosewood the dark neighbor. Adam interiors wanted that drawing. A Baltimore Federal table with holly stringing is the continental cousin — often holly because box was an imported specialist and holly grew. The satinwood draft takes the yellow field. This draft keeps the pale hard line.

Parquetry and pictorial marquetry multiply the same idea: a saw, a knife, a bundle of dyed and natural veneers, hot sand for a shadow. The pale piece in a flower or a trellis is often box or holly. When the wood-engraving trade collapsed, the furniture inlay trade did not take up the slack. It had already moved to other whites. Fashion: always a minor wood by volume, a major wood by eye.

A wood-engraving block is cut on the end grain so the tool meets a field of tiny rings, not a long fiber. Bewick’s school and the later London trade depended on that cut. Furniture stringing is the long-grain cousin. The same density that held a thousand impressions on a press holds a fingernail on a drawer front. Chessmen, rule joints, turned knobs and finials, musical-instrument fittings (a recorder beak, a guitar bridge pin), textile printing blocks — boxwood’s main life was not seating. Keep the objects in scale.

Banding is stringing’s wider cousin: a ribbon of box, sometimes with a dark neighbor, set into a broader rebate. Parquetry floors and table tops used the pale hard wood for the light square. A boxwood knob on a mahogany drawer is the turning shop’s share — a piece you can feel as stone, a screw or a tang into the drawer front. Do not replace it with a maple knob and call the job done. The hardness is the specification. Federal shops that could not get box used holly for the line and maple for a knob. That is honest if named. A laser-cut “boxwood inlay” from a gift shop is often maple or a print. The edge and a fingernail end the argument.

Stringing around a curved Sheraton apron wants a slip that will take a gentle bend. Box will accept a large radius if the slip is thin and the grain is straight; it will snap on a tight cabriole knee. Shops steamed or kerfed, or they used holly, or they painted the line. Steam-bend radii here are jewelry radii, not Thonet’s café loops. Do not put a box slip into a steam box and expect beech manners. Heat, a form, a light restraint, or a kerf on the back of the slip: the small-work protocol. A broken line filled with putty is the tell of a repair that rushed.

## Neighbors on the appendix, box off it

*Dalbergia* — rosewood, the dark neighbor of so much stringing — went onto CITES Appendix II on 2 January 2017 (the whole genus; *D. nigra* was already Appendix I). Brazilian rosewood, East Indian rosewood, the banding woods that sat next to box on a Sheraton table: those are the listed neighbors. *Buxus* is not a CITES furniture listing. The pale line and the dark band do not share a legal file. That is the conservation sentence for this slug. Confirm Species+ at publication; listings move. Do not write a rosewood permit for a boxwood slip. Do not treat an unlisted *Buxus* knob as a free pass to ship a *Dalbergia* table.

*Buxus sempervirens* in European gardens has been hit by box blight and box-tree moth in recent decades — an ornamental crisis, not a CITES furniture listing `[CITE NEEDED: current CABI or forestry notes]`. Wild box in the Mediterranean and Caucasus has been pressured by historic trade. Buy small, old, or clearly garden-salvage stock. Do not specify box for surfaces that holly or maple stringing can do. A forestry monograph on the historic *Buxus* trade belongs on the source shelf when you can put a hand on it `[CITE NEEDED]`.

American furniture used imported box for the same inlay jobs because the tree is not an American forest species. Substitute pale woods did the cheap stringing. Garden box from a ripped-out hedge is full of dirt at the collar and small in diameter. Use it for what it is. Old engraving blocks are historical objects; do not cut them for knobs. Box dust is fine and can irritate.

Ebony inlay — the black key, the dark string — is the other specialist line in this series (`ebony-inlay-keys`). Box is the pale answer. Together they draw the furniture. Neither is a table wood.

## What to look for

A pale hard line in walnut or ebony. A turned knob that feels like stone. An engraving block, end-grain, nineteenth century. A folding rule with brass joints. A Sheraton table with stringing a millimeter wide. A “boxwood” large table: a lie or a veneer. On a Federal piece: maybe holly, not box — look at the hardness with a fingernail. On a new “inlay” from a gift shop: even, dead, often laser-cut maple or a print.

Arkansas does not grow *Buxus* as timber. A southern shop meets it as inlay stock. The local pale hard wood is maple. Different scale. Related job. The rule, the block, and the stringing line share a genus. They do not share a century. Name the tree. Then decide whether the job is a line, a knob, or a wish.

Repairing a missing line is a small, slow job. Match the species if you can: box to box, holly to holly. A maple patch in a boxwood trellis will dent first and yellow differently. Cut the rebate to the old depth; do not widen it to make the work easier. Scrape, do not sand through the neighboring veneer. On a William-and-Mary oyster or a seaweed-marquetry box the pale line is part of a picture; replacing it with a fat commercial stringing from a catalogue is a different drawing. Keep the scale. A millimeter was the point.

CITES officers meet boxwood’s neighbors more often than they meet box. A Sheraton table with rosewood banding and box stringing can need a *Dalbergia* paper to move across a border and no paper for the pale line. That asymmetry is the law, not a shop preference. Antique pieces already in the country are usually legal to own. They can be complicated to export if the dark neighbor is listed. Ask before you crate the table. The boxwood knob in your pocket is not the problem. The banding is.
