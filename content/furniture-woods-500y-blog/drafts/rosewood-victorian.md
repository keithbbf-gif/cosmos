# Rosewood in the Victorian parlor

A parlor piano from about 1880 with a Brazilian rosewood case is a legal object in a living room and a paperwork object if you ship it across a border. *Dalbergia nigra* — jacaranda da Bahia — is the dark, fragrant, violet-brown wood of that case, of rococo-revival parlor sets, and of a forest in the Brazilian Atlantic that Warren Dean wrote as a destruction story (*With Broadax and Firebrand*). B. F. Kukachka, writing for the Forest Products Laboratory in 1970 (FPL-125), said something a furniture writer should keep on the bench: amazingly, there are no technical data for this species. Its early acceptance by the makers of classic furniture “mitigated against the need for a thorough academic study.” The piano came first. The Janka number came later, from compilations, not from Kukachka’s table.

## What Kukachka could say without a test machine

He could say the range: eastern forests of Bahia to Rio de Janeiro, nowhere abundant after centuries of cutting. Color variable — brown, red, violet, irregularly streaked with black — with local names for the prevailing hue. Texture coarse, commonly overlooked because the color does the talking. Grain generally straight. Heartwood oily or waxy to the hand, odor fragrant and distinctive. Thoroughly air-dry wood “just barely floatable in water,” which is a shop way of saying the specific gravity sits near 1.0. Hardness “exceeds by far any of the native hardwood species used in the furniture and veneer field.” Machines and veneers well; glue if you take the oily-wood precautions. Excellent reputation for durability against fungus and insects, including termites, though furniture is not a termite test. Primary use in 1970: veneer for decorative plywood. Solids for cutlery handles, brush backs, cue butts, fancy turnery. Shortages already bringing in *Machaerium* — caviuna — of similar color and finer texture.

Compiled Janka figures for *D. nigra* are commonly given near 2,790 lbf, with specific gravity in the 0.8–0.95 band (Wood Database, citing the usual FPL-adjacent sources). Mark those as compilations beside Kukachka’s empty table. Figure 4 will not plot a number he refused to invent. Indian rosewood (*D. latifolia*), which Kukachka *did* weigh, averages about 53 pounds per cubic foot; compiled Janka near 2,440 lbf, SG near 0.83. The Victorian parlor used both. The name on a London or New York bill is often just “rosewood.” A lens and a smell in a fresh kerf help. They do not replace a binomial.

Mahogany, for scale, sits near 800 lbf and SG 0.45–0.50 (FPL imported-woods / Kukachka). White oak near 1,360 lbf and 0.68. Rosewood is the hard, oily, fragrant outlier in the parlor, not a darker mahogany.

## How the parlor ate a forest

English Regency and French Empire already used rosewood as a show veneer and as banding on mahogany. The Victorian hour industrializes the appetite. Rococo-revival suites — laminated and carved backs, marble-top tables, étagères, whatnots — wanted a wood that took a high polish and read as luxury under gaslight. John Henry Belter’s New York patent of 1858 (the V&A plywood history flags it) is a molded-laminate rosewood back: several leaves in a mold, carved after, produced in batches. That is plywood thinking inside a Victorian costume. The plywood draft in this series returns to Belter as a process. Here he is a consumer of *Dalbergia*.

Pianos ate more. Broadwood, Steinway, Chickering, a hundred provincial makers: a case is a large, stable box that must wear a figured skin and take a finish that looks like furniture, not like a crate. Rosewood veneer on a softwood or poplar ground is the standard sandwich. Hide glue, later animal-glue variants, then early synthetics. The fallboard and the lockboard are the places a restorer sees the leaf thickness. Do not belt through it. The finish is part of the object’s legal and market identity. Stripping an Appendix I case to “brighten the rosewood” is how a living-room piano becomes a lesser thing.

Solid rosewood seating exists — small chairs, some sofa frames — but the weight and the cost of the log pushed shops toward veneer. A dining table that is truly solid *D. nigra* in the wide top is heavy in the hand and loud in the paper now. Most “rosewood” tables are leaves on pine or poplar. Open the underside.

Dean’s Atlantic-forest history is the ecological background. Colonial extraction is the supply story. Enslaved and coerced labor sits in parts of that history as it does in mahogany. Do not tell the piano as a European object only. Do not invent a price per log or a particular imperial commission. The forest, the case, and the later appendix are enough.

East Indian *D. latifolia* — Kukachka’s purplish-brown with blackish growth-zone streaks, narrowly interlocked, a central core that often carries shakes — entered the same shops when Bahia wood tightened. The Indian-export draft takes that lane. Victorian London and New York mixed the two without always writing the coast.

## Joinery: an oily leaf on a pale ground

Figure 3 is the cut geometry. Rosewood veneer is usually sliced, not rotary: crown-cut cathedrals or a quieter stripe, book-matched on a fallboard. Rotary would waste the figure and widen the pore. The ground is flatsawn poplar or pine more often than not. Tangential movement in that ground is the large number in FPL-GTR-190, chapter 4. The leaf is thin enough that it follows the ground if the glue holds. If the ground cups, the figure cocks. If you glue a solid rosewood breadboard across a veneered top, you have misunderstood the sandwich.

Carving on Belter-type backs is carving after lamination. The gouge meets end grain and face grain in the same curve. Rosewood takes it because it is hard and fine enough in the right log; it also chips if the glue line is proud. A solid mahogany Chippendale splat is a different fiber problem (Bowett’s cheap Jamaica wood after 1722). Do not use the Chippendale hour as a gloss on a Victorian laminate.

Pore filler is part of the Victorian polish. Kukachka says filling is desirable if you want a very smooth surface on Indian rosewood; Brazilian’s coarse texture wants the same habit. French polish on a filled ground is the high parlor look. Oil alone leaves the pore open and the color quieter. Neither finish is a 1760 mahogany brown. Victorian rosewood is meant to look like stone with a smell.

Dust is a sensitizer. Kukachka’s cocobolo note — dermatitis like ivy, a collecting system essential — is the family warning. *D. nigra* and *D. latifolia* deserve the same hygiene. Some people do not get a second chance at a first exposure `[CITE NEEDED: a named occupational-health note]`.

Glue: wipe the oil, then glue, then clamp longer than your mahogany habit. A scraper often beats a plane.

## Substitutes already in the shop

Walnut can look like a tired rosewood in a dark room. Stained mahogany, ebonized beech, printed grain on later suites. *Machaerium* as Kukachka warned. Bubinga (*Guibourtia*; some species later listed with Annotation #15) as a 20th-century stand-in. The eye is easy to fool under a parlor lamp. Weight, pore, and a fresh-scratch smell help.

Bowett’s mahogany story is the earlier Atlantic luxury. By 1850 mahogany is still in the dining room; rosewood has taken the piano and the show parlor. They are not a relay. They sit in the same house.

The factory multiplied the leaf. A London or New York cabinet shop of 1840 might veneer a single étagère with care. A Grand Rapids or High Wycombe plant of 1880 could put a rosewood photograph — still a real leaf, then later a print — on a carcase that left the line every hour. Marble tops, casters, tufted seats, a whatnot that is more air than wood: the silhouette is Victorian even when the face is a millimeter. Open the back. If the secondary is poplar or pine and the glue blocks are machine-cut, you are in the factory hour. If the secondary is oak and the blocks are hand-shaped, you may be earlier, or you may be a shop that kept older manners.

Rococo-revival carving on a laminated back is not the same as carving on a solid Chippendale splat. Belter’s gouge meets a stack. A slip of the tool can pop a glue line. Repairs that inject hide glue and clamp in a mold are conservation. Repairs that screw a plywood patch behind a broken rose are a different object. Name the patch.

## The law that the 1880 piano did not know

*D. nigra* sits on CITES Appendix I. Commercial trade in wild specimens is effectively shut. The rest of *Dalbergia* went onto Appendix II on 2 January 2017, Annotation #15 — first with a 10 kg non-commercial shipment exemption, later revised (CoP18, in force 26 November 2019) so finished musical instruments, parts, and accessories are exempt, and small finished products sit under a weight cap on the listed wood. Furniture, especially new furniture in sawn rosewood, is why the genus was listed. A dining table is not a jewelry box. A piano is a musical instrument under the later annotation; verify the current text on Species+ before you ship. This draft is not a permit.

Neotropical *Swietenia macrophylla* entered Appendix II on 15 November 2003. A Victorian house can hold both woods. The papers are not the same.

The U.S. Lacey Act as amended in 2008, and APHIS Phase VII (1 December 2024), sit on top. Antique *Dalbergia* already in the country is usually legal to own. Export is the sharp question.

## What to look for

Smell in a kerf, weight, a violet streak, a veneer line at the lockboard, poplar or pine inside, a Belter-type laminated back that is several leaves, not a solid slab. On an ebonized Victorian suite: often a finish on a paler hardwood, not ebony and not rosewood. On a new “rosewood parlor set” from an unnamed exporter: walk away unless the papers are boringly complete.

Arkansas does not grow *Dalbergia*. The local dark hard woods are walnut and locust. They will not be rosewood. They do not need to be. Enjoy the old piano. Do not commission a new one from a genus that is now a legal third rail.
