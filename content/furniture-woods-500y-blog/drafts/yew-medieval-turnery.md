# Yew on the pole lathe

A medieval turned cup in yew is a small orange object with a cream rim if the turner left the sap. The pole lathe — spring pole, cord, a hook tool — is how a lot of that small work was made: bowls, cups, treen, the occasional fine box, a knob that feels like stone. The English longbow is the famous yew object. The furniture objects are closer to the ground. *Taxus baccata* is a conifer that behaves like a hard, elastic hardwood. Tight rings. No vessels. A shop that only knows oak and elm will miss it.

## Softwood numbers, hardwood manners

Closed grain, no pores. It is a conifer. Hardness in secondary compilations sits high for a “softwood,” around 1,500 lbf Janka `[CITE NEEDED: confirm a primary FPL or equivalent number; FPL-GTR-190 does not treat yew as a commercial furniture lumber in the domestic tables]`. Density is high. Figure 4 will not give you a handbook row for yew among the ten anchors; the wood sits off the commercial scatter and still belongs in the room. Heartwood is orange to russet, sometimes with a purple hint. Sapwood is sharply pale. That two-tone is the furniture look: a Windsor bow with a ribbon of cream sap, a slab stool with a white edge, a turned cup that keeps the sap as a lip.

It planes to a glass surface and tears where the grain swirls around knots — and yew is knots. It turns. It scrapes. It takes a polish that looks like a hard varnish even under oil. Dry yew moves less than its hardness suggests. Knots check. Design for the defects. They are the tree. The foliage and seed (not the red aril) are poisonous to livestock. The wood in a chair is not a poison story for sitters. Old churchyard logs hide metal and grit. Cut slowly.

Do not confuse yew with osage-orange (*Maclura pomifera*), another orange bow wood, American, a ring-porous hardwood. Osage shows up in rustic work and tool handles. Anatomy decides: vessels versus none. Do not confuse it with the hotel “yew” of the 1980s — burl veneer on a cheaper ground, a golden slice that is not a stick chair.

## The lathe before the parlor

Medieval and early modern England used yew for small joined work, for inlay, and for the occasional fine box. Treen — the household woodenware that rarely survives — is the volume that museums under-represent. A turned cup, a salt, a pulley, a needle case: the tree’s small diameter is a specification, not a disappointment. English garden and churchyard yew took a century to make a billet. The turner used what the tree offered. Wide flats do not exist. Anyone selling a “yew” dining top without a glue line is selling a veneer or a different tree.

Italian *tasso* appears in inlay. French and Alpine work used yew in small doses. It was never mahogany. Inlay slips of yew in a walnut or oak ground are the small high-style use: a warm line, not a field. Boxwood is the pale cousin of that line; yew is the orange one. The boxwood draft takes the stringing rebate. This draft keeps the turn and the bend.

Pacific yew (*T. brevifolia*) is a different history — a bow wood, a tool wood, then a taxol-bark story that had nothing to do with chairs. It was never a case-work supply. American furniture rarely shows *T. baccata*. A catalogue “yew” dining table is likely European yew veneer, or a stained impostor. Look at the edge.

## Steam-bend radii, the Windsor hoop, the Welsh arm

Bending is the bowyer’s art and the chairmaker’s. Steam or heat, a form, a restraint. The elasticity that made a war bow makes a Windsor hoop that comes back. English Windsors used yew when the shop could get it; ash did the same bending job more often. Not every Thames-valley or High Wycombe shop had churchyard thinnings and limestone-hill stools in the rack. Vernacular chair books are the right shelf. Chinnery’s oak volume is the wrong one. Cotton, Jenkins, and Dunn on Welsh and country seating are the names restorers reach for `[CITE NEEDED: pin the chair monograph you have on the bench]`.

A Windsor bow in yew wants a clean steam and a form that matches the stick, not a fight against a knot that will open on the outside of the bend. The usable steam-bend radius is kinder than maple’s and tighter than a clumsy oak flatsawn hoop — closer to ash when the grain is straight, worse than ash when a knot sits on the extrados `[CITE NEEDED: a bending-wood table that actually lists Taxus]`. Thickness still governs: a thin long span will snap where a new ash bow would complain first. Standing-dead churchyard wood can be brittle. Tap a dry stick. The good bow-grade rings. Bend with caution.

Welsh stick chairs used yew for arms and occasional sticks when the tree was local. Collectors pay for the color now out of proportion to the farmhouse status the chair had when it was new. That price is a modern taste. The original was a country seat. Ash, elm, and oak did more of the English seating volume. Yew is the minority that photographs well.

Churchyard trees and outdoor gates used the durability of the heart. Local felling rules and sentiment are the real limits. *T. baccata* is not a typical CITES furniture listing. Old trees are cultural monuments. Furniture should come from windthrows and managed cuts, not from a raid on a medieval yard. Pacific yew was pressured by the taxol harvest before synthesis. It was never a furniture supply.

## Two later objects that are not the cup

The main furniture tradition after the medieval turnery is country seating from the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries: the yew-wood Windsor, prized now, vernacular then. The late twentieth century made a third object: yew burl veneer on boardroom desks and hotel case goods in Britain, a golden sliced surface that is not a stick chair and not a pole-lathe cup. That veneer can be handsome. It is a different object. Do not restore a 1980s pedestal desk as if it were a Welsh armchair.

Fashion was always a minority taste. Price now is a collector’s taste. The romance of the churchyard tree is real and also a hazard. A 17th-century English table with yew inlay is using the tree correctly: a line or a small panel, not a field. A “yew suite” from a catalogue is using the word.

The pole lathe is worth a paragraph because the furniture word “turnery” is not a factory lathe. A spring pole above, a cord around the work, a hook or a gouge in the rest: the work spins toward the tool on the downstroke and idles back. Green wood turns cleaner. Yew can be turned drier than alder or birch because it is dense and oily enough to take a polish off the tool. Medieval and early modern turners made the cups and the knobs this way; later wheel-lathes and then industrial lathes took the volume. A yew chessman or a Windsor pommel from a nineteenth-century shop may be wheel-lathe work. A museum cup with a hook-tool facet may be earlier. Do not date a piece by the wood alone. Date it by the tool mark if you can see one `[CITE NEEDED: a named treen or lathe monograph — MacGregor, or Cotton on chairs]`.

At the bench the dust is fine. The polish is the historic point; a thick plastic film is a different object. Glue is ordinary hide or PVA on clean, dry heart. Knots want to be accepted or cut out, not filled and forgotten. A finish slows the fade from orange toward brown. Owners who want the two-tone should keep the piece out of a south blast, or accept the fade. Inlay stringing in yew is possible and rare; boxwood did more of that linear job because it is paler and more even. Yew’s inlay credit is a warm slip, not a trellis.

## What to look for

Orange heart, white sap, tiny rings, a turned cup or knob that feels like stone, a bent bow, a Welsh arm, a hotel desk with yew burl veneer. Weight. A polish like stone. On a cheap “yew” suite: the edge. On a southern bench: you are probably looking at an antique or a veneer, not an Arkansas log. The local elastic hardwoods for a bend are hickory and ash. Different color. Same job if the part must come back to shape.

If the invoice says yew and the panel is eighteen inches wide without a glue line, look again. The tree does not offer that board. If the invoice says yew and the end grain shows vessels, you are holding osage or a stained hardwood. If the invoice says yew and the face is a photographic burl on particleboard, you are holding a 1980s hotel. The pole-lathe cup, the Windsor hoop, and the veneered desk can share a showroom wall. They should not share a caption.

English longbow literature is useful for the fiber and dangerous for the furniture caption. A war bow wants a specific sap-and-heart cut — the cream sap in tension, the orange heart in compression. A Windsor bow wants a stick that will take a steam-bend radius without opening a knot. Those are related selections, not the same object. Do not hang a chair on Agincourt. Hang it on High Wycombe or a Welsh kitchen, and on the form the stick actually went around. A turned cup with a cream lip is the medieval object this slug started on. Keep it in the room when the collector only wants the hoop.
