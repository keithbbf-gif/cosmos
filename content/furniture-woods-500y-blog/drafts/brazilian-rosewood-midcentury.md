# Brazilian rosewood in the mid-century shop

A Copenhagen sideboard of 1962 with a rosewood face is a smaller, sharper luxury than the teak dining set in the same catalogue. The magazines flattened Danish modern to a honey brown. The shops did not. Oak, teak, walnut, and rosewood were four different prices of the same grammar: visible joinery, a thin oil or a thin film, a case that shows the leaf. *Dalbergia nigra* — jacaranda da Bahia, Appendix I — is the name Americans recite. Much of the rosewood that actually left Danish factories was East Indian *D. latifolia* and related stock. Still a listed genus. Still a forest with a colonial ledger. The binomial on a vintage piece is often a guess until someone looks.

## Bahia wood in a teak decade

Kukachka (FPL-125, 1970) wrote *D. nigra* while the mid-century trade was still moving. He had no mechanical table. Early furniture use, he said, had excused the academy from measuring it. He could still say the facts that matter to a specifier: eastern Brazil, Bahia toward Rio, nowhere abundant, color in brown-red-violet with black streaks, oily or waxy, fragrant, barely floatable dry, harder than native furniture hardwoods, veneer first, solids for small work, *Machaerium* already in the shipments as a lookalike. Compiled Janka near 2,790 lbf, SG in the high 0.8s to near 1.0, is the later compilation. Figure 4 will not pretend Kukachka plotted that point.

*D. latifolia*, which he did weigh at about 53 pounds per cubic foot, is the other historic furniture rosewood: purplish-brown, blackish growth-zone streaks, narrowly interlocked, shake in the core, veneer in the United States, compiled Janka near 2,440 lbf, SG near 0.83. Danish buyers took both when they could. Brazilian wood had the older prestige and the tighter supply. Indian and Indonesian (sonokeling) wood had volume. A 1960s label that says only “rosewood” is a color word. The Indian-export draft takes *D. latifolia* as a lane. This draft stays with *D. nigra* as the name that became the legal third rail, and with the furniture that wore either wood as if they were one fashion.

Mahogany, for scale, remains the carvable mid-density wood of Bowett’s 18th-century Atlantic lane — about 800 lbf, SG 0.45–0.50 (FPL / Kukachka). Mid-century shops still used *Swietenia* and *Khaya*. They did not confuse them with palisander except in advertising. Teak (compiled Janka near 1,070 lbf, SG near 0.55) was the export look. Rosewood was the third luxury: darker, heavier, rarer, a sideboard meant to look scarce.

## Who used it, and how the joint changed

Finn Juhl’s sculptural chairs are often teak or walnut; some production and some specials took rosewood. Arne Vodder’s cases, Ib Kofod-Larsen’s seating, a long list of factory sideboards from the Copenhagen and provincial plants — the rosewood pieces are the ones estate sales still photograph first. Hans Wegner’s named chairs are more often oak or walnut; rosewood appears in some production and in the market’s wish. Do not invent a particular Juhl invoice. Object records and the Guild exhibition catalogues are the evidence `[CITE NEEDED: pin one Guild-exhibition rosewood lot at publication]`.

Solid versus veneer is the joinery split. A solid rosewood chair seat or arm is a dense, oily member that wants sharp tools, a scraper, and a glue surface wiped of extractives. It moves less in width than a flatsawn oak seat of the same dimension, and it weighs more than the client expects when they lift the chair. A veneered case — the common sideboard — is a sliced face on a cheaper core: teak, oak, particleboard in the later cheaper lines, or a plywood that the catalogue hoped you would not ask about. The 18th-century ancestor of that sandwich is crotch mahogany on yellow-poplar (the Empire draft). The mid-century version may be a true *Dalbergia* leaf on a lauan or birch core. Look at the edge. Count plies. Weigh the door.

Molded ply versus solid is the other split. The V&A plywood history and the Eames DCM sit in the plywood draft; Aalto’s Paimio birch is not rosewood. Mid-century rosewood is rarely a compound-curved shell. It is a flat face or a solid sculpted member. Grete Jalk’s 1963 molded-ply chair (V&A exhibition lists) can wear a rosewood face; the structure is still laminate. Do not call a flat-faced Vodder sideboard an Eames problem. Call it a leaf-and-core problem.

Oil is the honest mid-century finish on these pieces. A thick film that looks like a piano is a later “restoration.” Victorian rosewood *wanted* that piano polish (the Victorian draft). Danish rosewood wanted grain you could still feel. The two hours share a genus and fight about sheen.

## Workability in a factory that also ran teak

Rosewood dulls knives. Dust sensitizes. Kukachka’s cocobolo dermatitis note is the family warning; collect the dust. Glue wants the oil wiped back; clamp longer than the teak habit. A scraper beats a plane on interlock and on the polish the wood will take if you let it. Calcareous deposits in some Indian boards blunt edges (Kukachka on *D. latifolia*). Brazilian boards, when you still meet them as offcuts in a repair shop, are the oily, fragrant ones that barely float.

Screws hold if you pre-drill. The wood is hard enough to split a rail that a teak rail would forgive. Dowels and mortise-and-tenon in solid members are the Copenhagen habit. Cam locks in a later veneered case are the factory habit. Look underneath.

A sliding-door sideboard is the characteristic large object. The door is a veneered panel in a groove, or a panel hanging from a fiber or metal track. Rosewood on a core that has gone out of flat will bind in January and rattle in July if the core was cheap particle. A decent ply core stays a rectangle. The pull is often a routed finger or a small metal bar; a rosewood pull that is a solid sticking is a nicer shop. Drawer sides in the better plants are teak or oak, not the face genus. That is honest if you can see it. A face of palisander and a side of lauan with a stain is the cheaper plant.

Upholstered seating in rosewood — a frame that shows at the arm, a loose cushion — is a sculptor’s use of the solid. The rail is thick because the wood is brittle compared with ash. You do not steam-bend *Dalbergia* the way Thonet steamed beech. You cut the curve from the solid or you laminate a few leaves for a gentle bow. The plywood draft is the place for compound shells. This hour’s famous chairs are still mostly cut and joined.

Bowett’s mahogany statute (1721/1722) is not this supply. The Bahia forest and the Malabar-to-Java *Dalbergia* lane are. Enslaved and coerced labor sits in the older Brazilian extraction the way it sits in mahogany. Mid-century invoices are quieter about that. Dean’s Atlantic-forest book is still the background for *D. nigra*. Do not tell the sideboard as a Scandinavian object only.

American and Italian factories ran the same darker luxury in the same years: a rosewood wall unit, a headboard, a dining table with a leaf that is too wide to be a honest solid without a price the catalogue would not print. Much of that is veneer on particleboard. The Copenhagen Guild pieces are the high end of a broader habit. A suburban “rosewood” suite of 1968 is more often the print or the cheap leaf. The mid-century draft in this series is not a license to baptize every brown wall unit *D. nigra*.

## Figure 5: Appendix I is not a style note

*D. nigra* is CITES Appendix I. Commercial trade in wild specimens is effectively shut. You do not specify new Brazilian rosewood furniture. You enjoy old pieces already in the country, or you specify another wood.

The rest of *Dalbergia* went onto Appendix II on 2 January 2017, Annotation #15 — in force with the CoP17 listing, later revised at CoP18 so finished musical instruments, parts, and accessories are exempt, and small finished products sit under a weight cap on the listed wood. A dining table is not a jewelry box. A 1962 sideboard being sold abroad is a permit conversation. Neotropical *Swietenia macrophylla* entered Appendix II on 15 November 2003; relevant if the same room holds a “mahogany” case that is actually big-leaf.

Figure 5 is the map. Species+ is the living page. The U.S. Lacey Act (2008 amendment) and APHIS Phase VII (1 December 2024) sit on top. This draft is not a permit.

Repair with the same species is a permit question when the patch is imported. Repair with walnut or dyed pear is the old shop fake. Name it.

## Substitutes the 1960s already knew

Walnut can impersonate a tired palisander. Teak with a dark toner. Printed foil on particleboard. Bubinga and other *Guibourtia* (some later listed under Annotation #15). *Machaerium*. A 1970s “rosewood wall unit” is often a photograph. A lens on an unseen edge ends the argument.

New work that wants this hour’s sharpness without the appendix: American black walnut (FPL: SG 0.55, Janka 1,010 lbf), smoked oak, a named plantation teak with papers. None of these is jacaranda. They do not need to be.

## What to look for

A dark, streaked face with an oily look; weight in a solid member; a veneer line on a case; an oil finish that has worn to pale at the pulls; a Copenhagen or provincial factory mark. Ask whether the wood is *D. nigra* or *D. latifolia* before you ship. On a print: no pore, no smell, a core that swells. On a new “Brazilian rosewood” suite: walk away.

Arkansas does not grow *Dalbergia*. Oak, walnut, and cherry will do the mid-century grammar of visible joints and a thin finish without borrowing a Bahia name. The point of this hour is to say what the vintage piece is, and what a new commission cannot be.
