# Reclaimed oak beams

A tithe-barn tie beam lands on a bandsaw. The first cut shows a square nail, then a black ring where the nail’s iron met tannin, then a mortise that once held a brace with a draw-bored pin. The mill is not making a beam. It is making a table top from a joint that already had a life. Reclaimed oak is a real furniture wood. It is also a salvage claim that can be a barn, a mill, a warehouse, or a container of mixed hardwood with a story stapled to it. Chinnery’s joined furniture and Albion’s naval oak are the earlier lives some of these balks actually had. Most barn beams in the American market are nineteenth-century house and farm carpentry: *Quercus alba* or a red-group cousin, pegged and notched, not a 1565 wainscot and not a man-of-war knee.

## What the handbook still says after a century in a roof

White oak: Janka about 1,360 lbf, specific gravity 0.68 at 12 percent moisture (FPL-GTR-190, table 5–3a). Northern red oak: 1,290 lbf, 0.63. European *Q. robur* / *Q. petraea* sit in the same band `[CITE NEEDED for a single FPL-equivalent]`. A beam that sat dry in a roof for a hundred years is not a different species. It is a different moisture history. The *Wood Handbook* (ch. 4) still owns the movement: tangential shrinkage larger than radial; a flatsawn face cups; a quartered face stays quieter. Figure 4 is the hardness scatter. Figure 1 is the long trade strip. Reclaimed stock is not on that strip as a milestone. It is a second use of woods the strip already named.

Tyloses in white-oak heartwood still plug pores. A water drop sits. Red oak still drinks. The test works on a freshly cut end if finish has not sealed it. Barn paint and linseed on the old surface will lie. Cut a hidden end.

Ring width tells the stand, not the romance. Slow, even rings can be an old-field American oak or a European import. Wide rings are second growth or a fast site. Dendrochronology can provenance a beam the way Haneca et al. (2005) provenanced art-historical panels. A hang-tag that says “European castle oak” without a ring study is a story. Some beams are European. Some are Ohio. The mortise does not speak Latin.

Metal is the shop fact that overrides romance. Square nails, wire nails, spikes, bolts, plaster keys, a wagon staple, a bullet. Scan or cut slowly. A bandsaw blade that finds a drift becomes a lesson. Iron stains in tannic oak are black and deep; they will not sand out to a clean blonde without taking the face with them. Leave them, or site the cut so they become an edge, not a lake in the middle of a dining leaf.

## Where the beams came from

American barn and mill frames of the nineteenth century are the common supply: white oak and chestnut in some regions (chestnut is a blight story after 1904; a wormy chestnut beam is a different chapter), pine in others. English and Low Countries barns can be *Q. robur*, sometimes with earlier reused ship or house timber in the same frame. Albion’s dockyard oak appears in the salvage market with a frequency that should make a buyer suspicious. A bolt hole and a shipwright’s mark that can be checked are evidence. A burned-in fouled anchor is not.

Heart-pine joists are often sold next to oak beams in the same warehouse. They are *Pinus palustris* or another yellow pine, FPL longleaf about 870 lbf and SG 0.59 — not oak. Loud latewood, resin, orange heart. Name them separately (Outland; Earley). A “reclaimed hardwood beam” pile that mixes the two is a pile, not a specification.

Do not invent a demolition date or a price per board foot. Documented salvage has a building address and a photo of the frame before it came down. Undocumented salvage has a truck.

A warehouse walk is the practical education. Meter the beam: a weathered top face can read several points wetter than the boxed heart after a rain week. Stickers between flitches, a month in the shop’s humidity, then a second reading. Glue on the first reading and the leaf will move after delivery. Smell creosote; if it is there, you are in a sleeper pile, not a barn pile. Sight down a mortise: a clean, unused mortise on all four faces of a “beam” is a machine decoration. An original mortise has crushed fibers, a peg stain, and a direction that made sense in a frame. Photograph it before you slice. The photograph is the provenance the hang-tag will not be.

Furniture use is a late fashion: dining tables from joists, mantel beams that never held a roof, “live-edge” slabs from boxed hearts that still want to check. Some of it is honest reuse. A mortise in the underside of a table is a good tell. Some of it is new oak stained with tea and iron to fake the tannin stain. End grain and a moisture meter end that argument faster than a story.

## Joinery

The beam’s original joints are carpentry: mortise and tenon with a peg, sometimes draw-bored; lap dovetails at a plate; a birdsmouth; a scarf. Those joints are large. They were cut for a load and a building movement, not for a dining chair. When you slice the beam into furniture, you cut through that grammar. The peg hole becomes a void in a table top. Fill it with an oak plug of similar ring, leave it as a witness, or site the leaf so the hole is off the plate. Do not fill it with epoxy tinted as “character” if you are claiming a clean furniture face — or do, and say so.

New furniture joints in reclaimed oak are ordinary joints in a harder, often more brittle material. Old heart can snap where a new kiln-dried board would bend. Predrill for screws. Draw-bore a new mortise and tenon if you want the old manners; the fiber still crushes, but a dry, case-hardened surface can split if the offset is greedy.

Frame-and-panel remains the polite way to use unstable flatsawn slices from a boxed heart. A slab table from a whole beam is a bet against Figure 3’s geometry: the boxed heart wants to check along the pith. Many “beam tables” keep the pith and then act surprised. Cut the heart out, or live with the check, or spline it and admit the spline.

Breadboard ends on a reclaimed leaf must slot. The beam may have a moisture gradient from a weathered face to a protected heart. Let the stock sit at the destination humidity. A barn in January and a kitchen in July are not the same kiln.

Veneering a slice of beam face onto a stable ground is a way to keep the iron stain and the peg witness without a 40 mm cupping slab. Name the ground. A beam photograph on MDF is not reclaimed oak.

Through-tenons and visible pegs on a new base under a reclaimed top are a choice. They are not proof the top is old. Stickley’s visible pin is an Arts and Crafts religion; a barn peg is a carpentry leftover. Do not confuse them on a hang-tag.

## Conservation

Taking a listed barn apart for dining tables is a loss of a building. Taking a collapsed barn apart after a survey is reuse. The difference is paperwork and a photograph, not a finish. Oak as a species is not CITES-listed in the American and European mains. Lacey still applies to imported “reclaimed oak” that may be mixed tropical or undocumented hardwood. Ask for the country, the species, and the building if the price depends on the story.

Insect holes: old flight holes in sapwood are common. Active powder is a pile of dust on the warehouse floor. Heartwood is less tasty. Do not buy a beam that is still raining frass.

Lead paint on a mill beam. Creosote on a railway sleeper sold as a “beam” — a different chemistry, a poor indoor furniture wood, a health problem. Sleepers are not barns.

Oak wilt and sudden oak death affect living trees `[CITE NEEDED for current range if you name counties]`. They do not make a dry beam safer or more valuable.

Arkansas white oak still quarters from new logs. A shop that wants flake without a barn story can buy kiln-dried quartered stock and skip the nail. That is the quieter local sentence.

## What to look for

Mortises, pegs, iron stains, a weathered face versus a protected one, metal on a scan, ring width, tyloses on a fresh end, a documented address. A perfectly even “aged” color on all four faces of a beam is a stain. A moisture reading above the room’s equilibrium is a wait, not a glue-up.

Haneca’s method can still be applied to a panel sliced from a European beam. A castle on the invoice cannot.
