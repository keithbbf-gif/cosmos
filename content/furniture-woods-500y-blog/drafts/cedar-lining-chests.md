# Cedar linings and the moth chest

Open a late-nineteenth-century Pennsylvania or Shenandoah Valley chest in January and the first fact is smell. Eastern redcedar heartwood carries a kitchen-spice note shops still sell as moth insurance. “Cedar” on a furniture label is a family reunion that invited strangers. The Pennsylvania moth chest is usually *Juniperus virginiana*, a juniper with purple heartwood. A Spanish colonial chest or a cigar-box lining may be *Cedrela odorata*, Spanish cedar, a Meliaceae cousin of mahogany. A Pacific blanket chest may be western redcedar, *Thuja plicata*, a true thuja, light, durable, not a parlor show wood. Three genera. One English word. Keep them in separate drawers.

## Eastern redcedar: the lining wood

The *Wood Handbook* (FPL-GTR-190, table 5–3a) lists eastern redcedar around specific gravity 0.47 and Janka 900 lbf at 12 percent moisture — harder than you think for a “cedar,” cherry’s neighborhood, not pine’s. Figure 4 places that hardness among the series’ ten anchors. Heartwood is dull red to purple-red; sapwood is cream and sharp. That hardness is why a lid can take a sitting child and why a knot, left proud, will print a shirt.

Whether the volatiles still deter moths in a forty-year-old chest with a worn surface is a question shops over-claim `[CITE NEEDED: a current entomology note]`. Fresh heartwood is the traditional belief. A film finish inside a chest is a decision against that habit. Owners who want the smell leave the interior unfinished. Owners who want a coffee-table top from a recycled chest should accept brown: the purple fades in sun.

The wood is knotty. Clear grades are small. It is used for chests, closet lining, small boxes, pencils (the pencil trade also used incense-cedar, *Calocedrus decurrens*). It works easily, takes a polish, and will stain a damp shirt if the finish is thin. The shirt test is crude and useful: a damp cotton rag rubbed on unfinished juniper heart will often pick up color. Hoadley’s anatomy pages will show you a juniper, not a mahogany: no vessels, a distinct heart, a smell you can still get off a fresh shaving.

American furniture: the cedar chest as a nineteenth- and twentieth-century factory object (Lane and others, Altavista, Virginia, and the catalogues that followed) is a cultural form — dowry, storage, a smell. Earlier, small southern and Mid-Atlantic boxes. Not a Chippendale wood. Chippendale never asked for it. A joiner making a small box in 1840 did. Closet lining nailed over pine studs is the same genus in a cheaper contract: aromatic slats, often knotty, a smell that fades.

Knots are not defects in this wood; they are the tree. Eastern redcedar on glades and old fields is often limby. Clear cuttings are short. A shop that wants a clean lid glue-up is selecting, not milling a forest sawlog. Shrinkage numbers in the handbook are modest for a wood of its density; the practical movement problem is not a table top — it is a lid that was flatsawn through a knot and then hung over a heat register `[CITE NEEDED: the exact radial/tangential pair from FPL table 4–3 or 5–3 if you quote them]`.

## *Cedrela*: the mahogany cousin in the lining

Kukachka’s imported-woods paper (FPL-125) and the *Wood Handbook*’s imported chapter treat *Cedrela odorata* as a light, fragrant utility hardwood, not as a structural oak. Compiled Janka figures sit well below Honduran mahogany’s commonly cited 800 lbf; specific gravity is often given in the high 0.3s `[CITE NEEDED: the exact FPL or Kukachka row for C. odorata]`. It machines like a mild *Swietenia*. Watch the softness on arrises. The fragrance is a selling point and a finishing complication; some film finishes trap it, some owners want it. It is not teak-durable in every grade. Interior work is the honest job: humidor linings, cigar boxes, drawer bottoms and sides in tropical and colonial furniture, the secondary wood that smells when you open the piece.

Colonial Spanish and Portuguese furniture in the Caribbean and Brazil used *Cedrela* and related woods because they grew in the same empires as mahogany. English fashionable shops used it less as a show face. When it appears in a London or Philadelphia piece it is often a drawer lining, a cigar cabinet, or a later repair. Ports that handled it sat on the same sugar-and-log routes Bowett mapped for *Swietenia*. Do not invent a particular ship. The customs category is the evidence, and a named port-book line that separates *Cedrela* from mahogany is still a research note `[CITE NEEDED]`.

*Cedrela* listings have moved. Populations have sat on Appendix III and have been the subject of later uplisting proposals. Species+ at publication is the living page. A Missouri redcedar mill and a furniture importer of tropical *Cedrela* do not share a legal file. Lacey wants the binomial. “Cedar” is not enough.

At the bench, *Cedrela* versus *Swietenia* is smell, softness, and a slightly more open, cedar-family fragrance rather than the flatter mahogany odor. A humidor that smells right and a dining table sold as “Spanish cedar” are not the same specification. Treat *Cedrela* as a secondary mahogany. Sharp tools, modest feed, a finish chosen for the job (oil if you want smell; a film if you want a box that wipes clean). Watch end-grain checking on thin box sides. The wood will take a stain that makes it look more like *Swietenia* than it is. Refuse that trick on a piece you will later have to name.

## *Thuja* and the other “cedars”

Western redcedar (*Thuja plicata*): specific gravity about 0.32, Janka about 350 lbf — a true light, durable, outdoor and shingle wood. Furniture use is porch, garden, the occasional chest, contemporary outdoor benches. The odor is different from juniper. The hardness is not a table-top hardness unless you like dents. Heartwood durability is a tropolone story in the wood-science literature — thujaplicins and related extractives that discourage fungi `[CITE NEEDED: the FPL durability class row and a named extractives paper]`. That chemistry is why a shingle lasts and why a garden bench can be left unfinished to go silver. It is not a reason to build a dining table and then be surprised by dents. Fasteners should be stainless or bronze if the piece lives in weather. End grain drinks finish; the flats can look dry a week later. That is the wood, not a failed product.

Old-growth *Thuja* on the British Columbia and Washington coasts is a conservation argument with its own literature. Second-growth and certified stock exist. A crate that says only “cedar” on an import invoice fails Lacey the same way a crate that says only “mahogany” fails it.

White cedar (*Thuja occidentalis*) is canoe-and-fence in the Northeast, almost no furniture. Port-Orford-cedar (*Chamaecyparis lawsoniana*) is a Pacific specialty, a Japanese-export wood, a boat wood, a mill rarity after root disease cut the commercial forest `[CITE NEEDED: a dated note on Phytophthora lateralis and the Port-Orford trade]`. Yellow-cedar (*Callitropsis nootkatensis*) is another Pacific specialty. Incense-cedar belongs in the pencil trade more than in the furniture rack. None of these should be sold as the Pennsylvania moth chest. A shop that lumps them on a sales tag is selling a smell.

## Linings, lids, and the joints that belong

A cedar lining is a secondary-wood contract. Thin slats or a veneer of juniper or *Cedrela* are nailed, glued, or slipped into a pine, poplar, or mahogany carcase. The lining is not structural. The carcase is. Tongue-and-groove aromatic slats, often run vertically in a closet or horizontally in a chest, are the factory lining joint: they can shrink and still cover. A flush-fitted lining board that was glued tight across a wide pine lid will split or buckle. Leave a gap. The smell does not require a glue-up. Do not restore a Lane chest as if the aromatic interior were the show face; the factory already decided which side faced the room. Campaign knockdown fittings — brass corners, split chests — turn up on travel trunks that may be lined with *Cedrela* or camphor cousins; they are not a juniper habit. Deck caulking is a teak habit. Do not caulk a moth chest.

Humidor work is *Cedrela*’s specialist shop. Spanish cedar linings in a mahogany or walnut box, sometimes a whole *Cedrela* box, a lid that seals, a gauge. The wood’s job is humidity buffer and smell. A film finish on the inside fights both. Oil or nothing on the lining; a finish on the show face. Cigar factories in Cuba and elsewhere kept the binomial for that reason, not for a parlor fashion `[CITE NEEDED: a named cigar-industry wood note]`. A dining table sold as Spanish cedar is using the lining word for a show-wood job. Ask why.

Juniper: glue is ordinary hide or PVA on clean, dry heart; knots want to be accepted or cut out, not filled and forgotten. Dust and splinters irritate some people. *Cedrela*: watch the softness. *Thuja*: design that holds water against end grain will fail. Do not put a western redcedar outdoor-bench finish on a juniper linen chest. Different jobs.

Eastern redcedar is a pasture invader in parts of the Midwest and a timber tree in others — not scarce. Spanish cedar is a tropical hardwood with a genuine harvest and permit story. Western redcedar old-growth is a Pacific conservation fight. The word “cedar” on an import invoice is not enough.

## What to look for

Smell, purple versus mahogany-tan versus chocolate-weathered grey. Knots in juniper. A cigar box. A Lane chest. A porch bench that is too light to be oak. A “cedar” dining table: ask which genus, then sit down. Arkansas grows *Juniperus virginiana* on glades and old fields. A chest from that wood is a local object. It is not Spanish cedar and does not need to be. The moth chest, the Havana box, and the porch bench can share a showroom wall. They should not share a species line.
