# Cherry in the Shaker room

Leave a newly planed board of American black cherry in a sunny shop for a month, half of it under a scrap of plywood. Take the scrap off. The exposed wood has gone toward russet. The masked wood is still the color of a peeled stick. Clients call the dark “cherry.” They mean the sunburn. Shaker communities put that wood on the pieces that faced the room: stands, some case fronts, the meetinghouse color that maple would not become. John Kassay’s measured plates and Rieman and Burks’s catalogue are the furniture fact. The theology of simplicity has been written to death.

## The short list

The United Society of Believers furnished rooms the way they furnished theology: a short list, repeated until it became a vocabulary. Maple for the parts that must stay true and take a turning. Cherry for the face that will darken in the window. Pine for the broad, painted or clear boards that do not need to be heroes. Occasional butternut, occasional walnut, a little figured maple. Mount Lebanon, New York — the central ministry — ran a chair industry that sold to “the world.” Hancock, Massachusetts, Sabbathday Lake, Maine, Canterbury, New Hampshire, Enfield, Connecticut, sat in the white-pine and hard-maple forest. Kentucky’s Pleasant Hill sat in a different mix: more walnut, more cherry, more southern secondary woods. The Shakers bought and sold timber. They were not only forest hermits.

*Prunus serotina* is the furniture cherry of North America. The *Wood Handbook* (FPL-GTR-190, table 5–3a) lists black cherry at specific gravity 0.50 and Janka 950 lbf — walnut’s neighbor, softer than hard maple, harder than poplar. Figure 4 places that hardness among the series’ ten anchors. Machining is “very good.” Shrinkage is moderately high, but the wood is “very dimensionally stable after drying.” Cabinetmakers forgive the gum streaks for that clause. Heartwood is light to dark reddish brown with a luster. Sapwood is nearly white, narrow in old trees, wide in second growth. Production, FPL notes, has been centered in the Middle Atlantic States; the range runs from southeastern Canada through the eastern United States.

Hard maple (*Acer saccharum*): SG 0.63, Janka 1,450 lbf. The chair wood. Eastern white pine (*Pinus strobus*): SG about 0.35, Janka about 380 lbf. The cupboard wood. European cherry (*P. avium* — cerisier, Kirsche, gean) is the kitchen and Biedermeier cousin, often a bit paler and pinker in fresh stock. English fashionable shops in the eighteenth century did not make it their mahogany. Neither species is the ornamental flowering cherry of lawns, which is useless as timber.

## Tape, swallow-tails, and thin legs

The Mount Lebanon slat-back with a tape seat and a pommel finial is the commercial object. Chair tape — cotton or wool, later a synthetic — weaves through holes in the seat rails, over and under, a seat you can renew without re-caning. The holes want maple. A 20th-century factory “Shaker” is often a stain and a tape seat on mixed hardwoods: some decent maple, some beech or birch from a hotel-chair plant. Look at the turning. Look at the underside. A pommel that is fat and vague is not a Mount Lebanon pommel. Kassay’s plates are the comparison. Numbers stamped or written under a slat — a #7 rocking chair as the famous size — are commercial marks, not monastery poetry. Communities sold chairs by the dozen.

The oval box is the mixed-wood object that sits beside the Windsor in this series. Maple sides, steamed and fingered in a swallow-tail, copper tacks, a pine top and bottom. The swallow-tail is a finger joint cut so the overlapping ends lock like a bird’s tail; the tacks are the fasteners you can see. The maple takes a gentler steam bend than an ash hoop — a radius the thin side will accept without splitting if the grain is straight and the fingers land on sound wood. A steel nail in place of a copper tack is a different object. A box in all walnut is a different community or a later fancy. Hide glue is the historic adhesive for a lot of the case work. Ask before you soak a joint.

A Hancock-quality cherry stand has thin, exact legs and a top that moved and was allowed to. The historic stands are lighter than the reproduction houses want them. A thick leg is the tell of a factory that insured itself against freight damage. Peg rails are not a decorative row. They are how a chair leaves the floor so the floor can be cleaned. Look at the wear on the peg and at the hole in the chair finial. A cupboard interior is as careful as the face: shelves that fit, a pin that holds, no slush of leftover stain.

Mahogany appears when they made goods for “the world” or when a piece is not as Shaker as the dealer hopes. Heavy Renaissance Revival walnut is not their hour. Tropical inlay is not their hour. The absence is a specification.

## Inland Federal, gum, and the runner’s pale ring

Cherry is also the show wood of a certain American house that was never Shaker: Connecticut, Pennsylvania, western Massachusetts, later Ohio. High-style coastal shops preferred mahogany when they could get it (Bowett’s statute story belongs in the Chippendale draft). Inland shops had cherry. A Lancaster County chest of drawers can be as exact in its inlay as a Philadelphia mahogany piece, with maple and holly instead of satinwood. Hepplewhite sideboards, Pembroke tables, tall clocks with white-pine or poplar works-boxes and cherry hoods — the type fills Winterthur and a hundred town historical societies. The piece is polite. It never saw a Jamaica wharf.

Gum pockets and pitch flecks are handwriting, not rot. A shop that fills every one with color-matched putty is making a different product from a shop that leaves them. Second-growth sapwood is wide. A sap streak on the edge of a contemporary board is ordinary. Cherry and steel make marks if you leave a damp chisel. It is not oak’s tannin black. It is enough to curse. Secondary wood in cherry case pieces is often pine or poplar. A cherry drawer side is a luxury or a small-shop choice.

Blotch is the finishing quarrel, shared with pine and sometimes maple (Hoadley). A washcoat, a dye chosen for evenness, or an oil-varnish that does not shove pigment into the soft stripes: each is a school. The other school waits. Light and oxygen will do more than a pad of Van Dyke brown, and they will do it in the direction the wood already wants to go. UV finishes slow the darkening. A kitchen of even pink is a choice. A buyer who wants the twelve-year color should be told to keep objects off the top for the first seasons or to accept pale rings forever. Two leaves of a table that do not match are often a runner and a window, not two logs. Thermal darkening — heat and light boxes — works, and it can go too far toward brown-black. Tell the client before the box does.

The orange “Shaker finish” of late reproductions is often a toner the historic piece did not wear. Cherry was allowed to darken. Maple was allowed to stay pale. Pine was painted or left, depending on the room. Milk paint, distemper, later industrial paints, clear varnish, oil. Do not distress. The originals wore.

## Factory red and the 1990s dining room

American Colonial Revival factories stamped “cherry” on suites that were gum, birch, or maple under a red finish. Sweetgum (*Liquidambar*) has been a stand-in: interlocked, more movement, a different anatomy under a lens. An unseen edge ends the argument. Late-century trade names — “Oregon cherry” and the rest — need binomials. Orchard trees for fruit are not a timber supply.

The 1990s made a cherry decade in American dining rooms: reddish tables, clear finishes, a reaction to oak kitchens. Those tops are now in their oxidation middle age. South-window pieces and apartment-blind pieces no longer look like the same wood. Reproductions still push a red dye that sits in the grain and does not match the inside of the drawer. Look there.

French provincial armoires in cherry are a tourist sentence that happens to be true. German Biedermeier used cherry beside birch and pear when a warmer pale was wanted. Alpine painted furniture sometimes hides cherry and more often hides pine. Do not trust color. Southern shops used cherry where it grew. It was never the coast’s defining wood the way walnut and heart pine were (Hurst and Prown). It shows up. It does not brand the tidewater.

Black cherry is a commercial U.S. hardwood, not a CITES species. Clear wide stock from a tree that also has mast and wildlife value is ordinary pressure. Black knot and gum are grading issues. *P. avium* in Europe is managed and planted; it can still be stolen from hedgerows. Provenance is a habit. The species is not mahogany’s emergency.

## What to look for

Gum flecks. Fine diffuse pores. A luster under oil. On a Shaker stand: thin legs, a top that was allowed to move, cherry that darkened without a red toner. On a Mount Lebanon chair: maple, tape, a crisp pommel, a number under a slat. On an oval box: swallow-tail fingers, copper tacks, pine lid. On a Federal chest: holly or maple stringing, poplar or pine inside. On a reproduction: thick legs, orange finish, a tape seat on beech.

Arkansas grows cherry in the Ozarks, not in the volumes of Pennsylvania, but the species is the same *P. serotina*. Bradley Brand works that Ozark cherry when the log is there — a Federal wood that never needed a port. The sun will still finish the job after the truck leaves.
