# Plantation teak and the papers

A plantation board of *Tectona grandis* can be *Tectona* and still disappoint a buyer who paid for a Burma story. Wide rings, paler heart, less oil, a specific gravity that sits at the light end of the handbook range. The anatomy is the same ring-porous brown. The folklore grade — tight, dark, greasy — is a different growing year and a different forest politics. “Sustainable” on a hang-tag is not a binomial and not a permit. The papers are the piece.

## What the tree still is

The *Wood Handbook* (FPL-GTR-190) already treated teak as an imported furniture and decking wood with properties “generally on par with those of U.S. oaks,” excellent stability, very high natural durability, silica as the shop tax. Compiled Janka near 1,070 lbf, specific gravity near 0.55. Plantation stock can run lighter. That is a grading fact, not a moral one. Figure 2 keeps the Indian Ocean lane next to Baltic oak and Atlantic mahogany. Plantations in Africa and Latin America are later dots on that map, not a new species.

*Tectona grandis* is not a CITES appendix species in the rosewood sense. Figure 5 is the contrast: *Dalbergia*, *Swietenia macrophylla* (neotropical populations, Appendix II in force 15 November 2003), African ebony annotations — the listed neighbors. Teak’s legal life is national export law, bilateral sanctions, Lacey declarations, and whatever certificate a mill bought. Confirm Species+ and the national instrument at publication. Listings move. A hang-tag does not.

Dutch Java planted teak because the tree was already a state timber in the East Indies. That planting is older than the late-twentieth-century patio boom and older than FSC. The VOC and then the Netherlands-India forest service treated *Tectona* as a crop with a rotation, not only as a wild log `[CITE NEEDED: a named Java teak-plantation monograph — Boomgaard or a forestry-service history]`. British India and Burma ran reservations and a forest department that mixed conservation language with a dockyard appetite (the colonial-dockyard draft). A plantation is not automatically a virtue. It is a land use. Whose land, whose labor, what rotation, what chemical, what mill — those are the questions a certificate is supposed to answer and sometimes does not.

West African and Central American farms of the late twentieth century were a response to Asian export controls and to a patio fashion that had outrun the folklore grade. FPL already noted plantations as a real supply. The rings on those boards are the evidence a shop can see without a certificate. The certificate is the evidence a customs officer can see without a board. Both belong on the piece.

## Rings, oil, and the folklore grade

Old-growth or long-rotation teak from the Burma and Siam folklore is tight-ringed because the tree grew slowly. Heartwood is darker, oilier, the face that restorers still call “greasy.” Plantation rotations in Indonesia, West Africa, Central and South America, and later farms in Central America and Brazil can be twenty to thirty years or a little more `[CITE NEEDED: a named plantation-rotation survey, not a vendor blog]`. Wider rings. More sapwood in the log. Less extractive in some stems. The wood still weathers silver outdoors. It still dulls a plane. It may check more, glue a little easier (less oil), and look paler under the same oil can.

A shop that pretends plantation and Burma are the same product is selling a story. A shop that pretends plantation is not teak is selling a different story. Both are *Tectona* if the pore and the silica agree. Specify rotation and origin the way you specify quartersawn. The board will tell you the rings. The invoice must tell you the country.

Indoor Danish-modern teak of the 1950s and 1960s was often the older supply — Burmese and Thai logs into Copenhagen factories — and also, as the years went on, whatever the mills could get. Some of the best is solid; much is veneer. The garden furniture of the 1980s–2000s is the plantation object most buyers now own. Do not restore a patio armchair as if it were a Juhl. Do not oil a grey bench and call it a sideboard.

## Papers, not poetry

The U.S. Lacey Act as amended in 2008, and APHIS Phase VII declarations (1 December 2024), make a binomial on an invoice part of the piece. “Teak” is not enough. *Tectona grandis*, harvest country, and the rest of the declaration fields are the specification. A crate that says only “teak” fails the same way a crate that says only “mahogany” fails.

FSC and PEFC chain-of-custody are private certificates. They are not a legal finding and not a CITES permit. They can be useful. They can be bought and gamed. Read the certificate number. Ask whether the claim is forest-management or mixed-credit. A mixed-credit sofa can be mostly something else with a teak face. That is honest if the invoice says so.

National export regimes matter more than a magazine adjective. Myanmar’s teak export rules have tightened, loosened, and been sanctioned in cycles `[CITE NEEDED: dated instrument — ban, partial ban, or sanctions list at publication]`. Indonesia’s plantation export is a different paper. Ghana, Côte d’Ivoire, Tanzania, Panama, Costa Rica, Brazil — each has a forest law and a mill culture. Illegal logging in mainland Southeast Asia is documented by TRAFFIC and others `[CITE NEEDED: a named report year]`. “Burma teak” as a sales name without a legal path is a story. Treat it as one.

CITES is the wrong panic and the right habit. Teak is not *Dalbergia*. Afrormosia (*Pericopsis elata*), the closer visual cousin, is Appendix II. Iroko (*Milicia*) is the African lookalike, not listed like rosewood, still a Lacey name problem if the crate says teak. A specifier who writes “sustainable teak” and means iroko has written a false binomial. A specifier who writes *Tectona* and attaches country plus certificate has written a sentence a customs officer can read.

## What a plantation shop can and cannot do

Silica still dulls. Oil still wants wiping before glue, even when there is less of it. Outdoor design still fails if it ponds. Stainless or bronze still belong in weather. The plantation board does not repeal the dockyard manners. It changes the color, the oil, and the politics.

Wide plantation flatsawns will move like other ring-porous hardwoods. Teak’s stability is excellent, not magic. A glued-up table top still wants a finish on both faces and a base that lets it shrink. Breadboard ends still need slotted screws. Campaign knockdown fittings still make sense for a piece that will travel; they are not a green badge. Deck caulking still wants heartwood and a seam, not a plantation veneer on a yacht-interior plywood. A “sustainable teak deck” that is a 3 mm face on a foam core is a photograph of a deck. Name the core.

Plantation sawyers often slab for yield. You get more wide boards by flatsawing. The cathedrals on a plantation table are a cut, not a grade. Quartersawn plantation teak exists; it is quieter and dearer, the same geometry the glossary already explained for oak. Specify the cut if the face matters. Specify heartwood if the weather matters. Sapwood on a plantation rail is common because the rotations are short. A wide pale band on the end grain is the mill leaving money in the board.

Substitutes belong on the invoice. Rubberwood (*Hevea*) with a toner is an indoor plantation wood, often a retired rubber tree, useful and honest if named. It is not outdoor teak. White oak is the domestic outdoor furniture wood. Robinia is the temperate bench wood. Neither looks like teak. Both last if designed to drain. A designer who wants the honey interior color without tropical papers is looking at walnut, or at an antique *Tectona* already in the country, or at a named veneer with a core you can see.

Reclaimed dock and warehouse teak — demolition beams, old decking — is a third supply that vendors fold into the sustainability sentence. Some of it is the folklore grade. Some of it is painted, nailed, and oily in ways that fight glue. Metal in old decks ruins saws. A detector pass and a named demolition, not a mood. Reclaimed is not plantation and not a CITES workaround. It is a used board. Say so. A “reclaimed Burma teak” coffee table at a funny price may be new plantation with a wire-brush. Look at the fastener holes. Look at whether they go through or stop at the face.

## How to read a tag

Look at the rings. Count them on the end grain if the mill left you an end. Look at the oil in a fresh scratch. Look at the sapwood band. Then look at the paper: binomial, country, certificate, and whether the claim is solid, veneer, or mixed. Figure 5 will not show teak in a CITES color. That absence is information. It is not a free pass.

On a 1960 Copenhagen sideboard: older supply, oil, a honey that has gone brown; papers may be a later reconstruction. On a 1995 patio set: plantation, stainless screws, a grey face if anyone left it out. On a new “Burma teak” dining table at a funny price: ask which forest, which year, which permit. On a crate of “sustainable outdoor furniture”: open the declaration. Arkansas does not grow teak. The local outdoor ethic is white oak or cypress. The plantation ethic, if you want this tree, is a named farm and a paper trail, not a mood.
