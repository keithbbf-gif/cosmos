# Conservation as a crate, 2024

A container lands at Savannah. The packing list says “wood furniture.” The HTS code is a furniture code. Until Phase VII, a lot of those codes did not require a Lacey declaration. On 1 December 2024 they did, unless the product is 100 percent composite or sits in a remaining exemption APHIS still lists. The Federal Register notice of 31 May 2024 is the dated instrument. USDA APHIS’s Lacey Act declaration pages are the living ones. Check the date. This chapter is the present tense of the timber appendices, the 2008 plant amendments, and a dining table on a drawing. It is not a permit.

## The wall of dates

Figure 1 is the long trade. The conservation end of that line is a cluster, not a single year.

CITES, signed Washington 3 March 1973, entered into force 1 July 1975. Appendix I is not a furniture plan for new wild specimens. Appendix II is a permit and a non-detriment finding. Appendix III is a Party asking for help. Annotations decide whether a finished chair is a specimen.

Neotropical *Swietenia macrophylla* went onto Appendix II on 15 November 2003, Annotation #6: logs, sawn wood, veneer sheets, plywood. *Dalbergia nigra* had already gone onto Appendix I on 11 June 1992. The rest of *Dalbergia* went onto Appendix II on 2 January 2017, Annotation #15, revised at CoP18 in 2019 so finished musical instruments and small finished products — wood of the listed species up to 10 kg per shipment — would not freeze concerts and jewelry boxes. Madagascar populations of *Diospyros* went onto Appendix II on 12 June 2013, Annotation #5. Figure 5 is a schematic of those origin regions. Confirm at publication.

The Lacey Act (16 U.S.C. §3371 et seq.), amended by the Food, Conservation, and Energy Act of 2008 §8204, makes it illegal to trade plants taken in violation of U.S. or foreign law. It is the first comprehensive U.S. ban of that kind on illegal wood. Importers file a declaration: scientific name, country of harvest, quantity, value. Enforcement of declarations was phased. Phase VII, 1 December 2024, extends declarations to remaining plant-product HTS codes that are not 100 percent composite, including more furniture. APHIS later noted that furniture codes 9403409040, 9403409060, and 9403409080 were omitted from the first list and would need a later notice with six months’ lead. Check the current page.

Due care is the standard. A certificate on a website is not automatically due care. FSC is a useful risk tool. It is not a finding that Lacey has been met — APHIS and FSC both say versions of this. Composite exemptions exist for some mixed or unidentifiable cases. They are not a blanket for a solid “mahogany” table.

Stolen walnut trees exist. Domestic wood is not automatically innocent. A Boone County *Juglans nigra* log with a cloudy chain of custody is a Lacey problem if it crossed a line to get to the mill. The Arkansas chapter is a preference, not a halo.

## What a specifier can put on a sheet

Write binomials. *Quercus alba*, United States. *Juglans nigra*, United States. *Prunus serotina*, United States. *Tectona grandis*, plantation, country, certificate number. *Swietenia macrophylla* only with the permit path in the file — neotropical sawn wood is Annotation #6 paper after 15 November 2003, or a pre-Convention story USFWS will accept. *Dalbergia latifolia* as a finished sideboard is not a 10 kg jewelry box. *Shorea* spp. as a core only if you know the harvest country and you are willing to defend due care.

Prefer domestic hardwoods when the job allows: oak, walnut, cherry, maple, hickory, ash (while the dry stock lasts), pine. That is a specification, not a sermon.

For tropicals: old furniture already in the country, or new wood with boring paperwork. If the price of a “solid rosewood suite” is funny, the wood is funny. If the hangtag says mahogany and the pore looks like *Shorea*, the lauan chapter already told you.

For engineered goods: still ask the face species and the core species. Lauan cores have a forest. Foil has a name. CARB / TSCA Title VI is an emission stamp, not a harvest stamp. You want both conversations on the same purchase order.

## Antiques, exports, and the sideboard that cannot fly

Antique *Dalbergia* and *Swietenia* pieces are usually legal to own in the U.S. They can be complicated to export. Ask before you ship a 1960s rosewood sideboard to another country. Appendix I objects are the sharpest case. An 1880 Brazilian rosewood piano is a living-room object and a paperwork object at the border. Pre-Convention certificates and antique annotations exist; they are not a postcard you write yourself. USFWS will tell you. A dealer’s shrug will not.

Within the country, a 1962 Danish rosewood credenza can be bought and sold. Take a photograph of the grain. Keep the invoice. If you later want to send it to London, the file starts then, not at the airport.

A 1760 Jamaica chair in a collection is the ethical supply of the Chippendale look. A new “Chippendale mahogany dining set” from an unnamed exporter is a labeling problem before it is a style problem.

## Shop behavior that is not a halo

Keep a Species+ printout in the project file, dated. Keep the harvest country on the invoice. If you import, keep the APHIS declaration and any CITES permit. If you do not import, keep the mill’s chain-of-custody sheet anyway. An edge that matches the name is the object-level check: tyloses in white oak, the oily pore of teak, the tight diffuse face of maple, the printed cathedral that does not turn the corner.

A crate that says only “wood” is a refusal. A salesperson who says “it’s all mahogany” is a refusal. A funny price on a listed genus is a refusal.

Hide glue on a historic carcase can be reversed. Urea-formaldehyde on a particleboard carcase cannot. That is a conservation fact about objects, not about forests: repair culture is how a 1760 chair stays a chair. A cam-lock suite that swells under a sink is landfill. Design for a life that matches the glue. The engineered chapter takes the panel. This chapter takes the name on the panel.

Plantation teak and certified *S. macrophylla* from outside the Neotropics are real supplies. They are not automatically innocent. Ask for the country and the papers. IUCN status is not a CITES appendix. A Forest Stewardship Council claim is not a Lacey finding. The three stamps can sit on one invoice. None of them replaces the others.

## What this series will not do

It will not issue a permit. It will not update itself when CoP20 changes an annotation. It will not treat FSC as a halo. It will not treat domestic wood as automatically innocent. It will say: names, countries, papers, edges.

TRAFFIC and Chatham House’s five-taxa paper is the trade-analysis neighbor. Taylor Guitars’ CITES note is the instrument-industry walkthrough of Annotation #15; verify it against Species+. Furniture companies have less excuse than guitar companies. A dining table is not a Chapter 92 object.

Emerald ash borer (Detroit / southeastern Michigan, 2002) and Dutch elm disease (U.S. introduction, 1930s) are domestic conservation clocks that do not wait for an appendix. Ash chairs while the dry stock lasts. Elm Windsor seats from trees that are already down. Those are shop facts. They belong next to the tropical appendices so a reader does not think conservation is only a southern-hemisphere story.

## A drawing and a dock

On the drawing: *Quercus alba*, United States, quartersawn, floating panels, slotted screws in the breadboard, hide glue if the piece should come apart in a hundred years. On the dock: a crate that used to say “wood furniture” and now, after 1 December 2024, often needs a binomial and a harvest country. Those two sentences are the same job. Fashion without a paper trail is how *Swietenia* and *Dalbergia* got to the appendices. The 1973 Convention built the gate. The 2008 amendment and Phase VII put a declaration on more of the crates that walk through it.

## A kitchen door and a stolen walnut

Walk a 2012 kitchen. The door may be walnut veneer on MDF, or a foil, or a solid frame-and-panel in white oak. The carcase is often particleboard. CARB / TSCA Title VI may be stamped on the edge tape. That stamp does not name a forest. The face, if it is *Juglans nigra*, still wants a harvest country if the leaf was imported. If the face is domestic, it still wants a mill that can say so. Phase VII is an import declaration. It is not a domestic chain-of-custody law. Lacey still reaches interstate commerce in plants taken in violation of state law. A walnut log stolen from a fencerow and sold across a state line is the example APHIS trainers use `[CITE NEEDED: a named APHIS or DOJ case, not a training-room memory]`. The Arkansas preference does not wash that log.

Reclaimed oak beams and “barnwood” suites are a conservation fashion with their own lies. A beam can be a beam. A beam can be new oak beaten with a chain. Ask for the building and the date. The reclaimed-oak draft in this series takes that object. This chapter only says: old wood already in the country is usually the cleanest tropical look you can buy, and a new barnwood crate from an unnamed port is still a crate.

Plantation teak (*Tectona grandis*) and plantation *S. macrophylla* outside the Neotropics are the supplies that let a specifier write a tropical name without pretending the 18th century is still in force. They need country, certificate, and a will to defend due care. They do not need a sermon about “sustainability.” They need a file.

Look at the invoice the way you look at an end grain. Rings and a binomial are evidence. A color word is not. The interesting trees are in the other chapters. The dullness here is the point.
