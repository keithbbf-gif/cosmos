# Teak in the colonial dockyards

HMS *Trincomalee* left the Bombay Dockyard in 1817 as a 46-gun frigate of Malabar teak. The Wadia yard had already sent *Salsette* (1807) and the seventy-four *Minden* (1810) into Admiralty service. Home oak was short; teak did not splinter like English oak under shot, and it did not eat iron the way oak tannin does (Albion, *Forests and Sea Power*; the Bombay experiment after 1802). The same heartwood that framed a hull lined a campaign chest: oily, siliceous, indifferent to a hold. Furniture inherited a dockyard wood. It did not invent one.

## A ring-porous tree on three clocks

*Tectona grandis* is a ring-porous tree of India, Burma (Myanmar), Thailand, and the old East Indies. The *Wood Handbook* (FPL-GTR-190, imported-woods chapter) describes the heartwood as yellow-brown to golden brown, aging richer, coarse, usually straight, with excellent dimensional stability and a very high natural durability. Silica in the fiber dulls tools. Workability is moderate for that reason. Density sits on the order of American oak; compiled Janka is commonly given near 1,070 lbf, specific gravity near 0.55, plantation stock sometimes lighter and oil-poor. Figure 4 puts that hardness among the series’ ten anchors. Teak is not the hard wood. It is the oily, stable, outdoor-and-hold wood that a navy and a campaign joiner could both name.

Figure 2 puts the Indian Ocean lane beside Baltic oak and Atlantic mahogany. They are parallel, not a relay race. Macquoid’s London clock never ran this timber. A Bombay keel, a Rangoon export boom, and a Java plantation are a different clock.

Indian shipwrights used Malabar and Canara teak long before the East India Company tried to monopolize it. The Company annexed Malabar, set up a crude forest management on that coast in 1802, and proclaimed a timber monopoly over teak and other specified trees in 1807. The well-organized Malabar merchant trade resisted the monopoly the way it had resisted the pepper monopoly (relinquished 1793). The dockyard still got logs. The forest department became a principal branch of public service because the yard could not live on hope [CITE NEEDED: Mann’s Malabar timber essay is the modern account; pin the archive citation at publication].

## Bombay, then Rangoon and Moulmein

The Napoleonic emergency is the documentary hinge. Oak in the home yards was a known shortage (Albion). Officers had reported favorably on teak-built ships in action. A first teak-constructed ship sailed up the Thames in 1795; the Bombay Marine’s *Cornwallis* impressed the Admiralty enough that they bought her and then asked for a ship of the line and a frigate a year from Bombay. The stock of timber and plank at the Presidency, collected since the 1802 order to build men-of-war, was said to equal three years’ consumption, replenished from Canara and Malabar `[CITE NEEDED: the Admiralty or Bombay yard minute that states the three-year figure]`. Do not invent a price per load. The species, the yard, and the statute-and-contract paper are enough.

Parsi Wadia master builders — Lowjee the founder of the yard, later Jamsetjee Bomanjee and his line — built the Admiralty teak ships. *Trincomalee*’s keel went down in the upper old dock on 25 April 1816, after *Amphitrite* and the Company ship *Buckinghamshire* floated out. She is still afloat as a museum hull. That longevity is the furniture argument in naval clothes: heartwood that does not sulk in salt and weather.

Burma became the later export engine. The 1911 *Encyclopaedia Britannica* teak article, still a useful period snapshot, puts the chief European export on the Burma coast, principally Rangoon and Moulmein. Between 1786 and 1825, 111 European vessels were built at Rangoon, aggregating 35,000 tons, teak the chief timber. Moulmein became British after the first Burmese war (1826); from 1829 to 1841 more than 50,000 loads of teak left, and 68 vessels were built there. When the British took the country in 1852, teak was already the chief article of export. The forests that first fed Moulmein sat on the Attaran, a feeder of the Salween; by 1841 more distant forests were coming down-river. Bangkok sent quantities to Europe after 1882. Java exported a little. The furniture trade later inherited those ports as names on a grade: Burma teak, Siam teak, Java teak. A grade is not a permit.

Extraction is a political and military story as well as a forest one. Later sanctions and logging bans belong in a buying note, not in a romance of the deck `[CITE NEEDED: a dated Myanmar export-ban / sanctions instrument at publication]`. Enslaved and conscripted labor sit in other imperial timber records; Burmese teak labor is its own archive. Do not flatten it into a caption about “craft.”

## Campaign knockdown, brass, and a hold

Campaign furniture is a military-administrative style: brass corners, flush handles, a chest that splits into two stacking cases, fittings that let a piece come apart for a march or a ship. Mahogany did a lot of this work in the Atlantic world. Teak did it on the Indian Ocean stations because the wood was already in the dockyard and because it would not sulk in a hold the way an under-dried oak or a soft deal carcase would. The knockdown is the joinery, not the romance. Screws, bolts, hooks, and interlocking battens — not glue as the primary contract — so a clerk or a batman could take the piece down without a bench. Recessed brass keeps the faces from tearing each other in a crate. A secret drawer is a period taste. A flush hinge is a survival habit.

Nicholas Brawer’s survey of British campaign furniture is the modern catalog habit `[CITE NEEDED: confirm edition and the teak-versus-mahogany split in the plates]`. Morgan & Sanders and later campaign shops sold the form to officers who were already living in a teak climate. Some chests are mahogany with teak slats; some are teak throughout; some are camphor or *Cedrela* cousins sold under a cedar word. A lens on an unseen rail ends the argument. The dockyard wood and the parlor wood share a binomial. They do not share a finish schedule.

Deck work taught the same manners. Teak planks on a weather deck are caulked: oakum driven into the seam with a caulking iron, then paid with pitch or marine glue. The oil in the heartwood and the silica that dulls a plane are the same facts that keep a seam from rotting out as fast as a softwood deck. The furniture chapter that follows (`teak-deck-and-garden`) takes the grey bench and the patio. The yard and the chest that left it are this hour’s objects.

## Java planted; China used; London barely named it

Dutch colonial furniture in Java used teak because the tree was there, and because the Dutch planted it. The plantation story is the later slug (`sustainable-teak-plantations`). The colonial fact is older: a VOC and then a Netherlands-India administration that treated *Tectona* as a state timber, the way Britain treated Malabar and Burma. Chinese shops in Southeast Asia used teak for chests and tables that never saw a Copenhagen oil can. The furniture is older than Danish modern. Danish modern is why most buyers can spell the word.

English fashionable shops in Macquoid’s mahogany and satinwood hours did not make teak their show wood. A London bill of the 1780s that says teak is worth a second look; it may be a dockyard leftover, a curiosity, or a later mis-label `[CITE NEEDED: a named London cabinet-maker’s bill that specifies teak before 1850]`. Provincial and colonial shops are where to look. Anglo-Indian furniture — the caned chairs, the carved Bombay blackwood cousins, the teak tables with turned legs that look a little too thick for Hepplewhite — sits in that provincial-imperial room. Do not call it Chippendale because the splat is pierced.

## At the bench: silica, oil, iron

A plane iron that just finished a cherry job will feel tired after twenty feet of teak. That is the shop tax FPL names. Carbide helps. So does accepting that teak is not mahogany under the iron. Wipe the oil back before glue. Stainless or bronze fasteners if the piece will see weather or a damp hold; teak and cheap steel can still make stains even if the wood is kinder than oak. Sanding dust is an irritant for some people. So is the oil.

Ring-porous brown plus a greasy feel plus a blunted iron is the working ID. Smell is milder than juniper, not the kitchen-spice of eastern redcedar. Weight is oak’s neighbor, not western redcedar’s. A plantation board with wide rings is still *Tectona* if the anatomy agrees; it is not “Burma grade” because the tag says so. Iroko (*Milicia*) is the later African lookalike, sold as “African teak,” not this tree. Afrormosia (*Pericopsis elata*) is a closer visual cousin and already CITES Appendix II. White oak is the domestic outdoor furniture wood of the tyloses story. It does not look like teak.

## What to look for

A campaign chest with brass corners and knockdown screws, a teak smell in a fresh scratch, secondary wood that may be deal or a local tropical. A Bombay or Rangoon ship-carpenter’s box that migrated into a house. A Dutch-Javanese kast in teak, heavy, architectural. A Victorian Anglo-Indian table with turned legs and a coarse pore. On a 1960s sideboard: you have left this chapter. On a crate that says only “teak”: Lacey wants the binomial and the harvest country. *Tectona grandis* is not a CITES appendix species in the rosewood sense; some populations and some export regimes are tightly controlled.

Arkansas does not grow teak. A designer who wants the dockyard manners in a domestic hardwood is looking at white oak, not at a stain. The campaign chest and the Wadia frigate share a species. They do not share a century, a port, or a finish. Name the tree. Then decide whether the job is a hull, a knockdown case, or a wish.
