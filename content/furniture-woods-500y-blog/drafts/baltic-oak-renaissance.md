# Baltic oak and the Renaissance panel

A linenfold chest in a London hall can look as local as the parish. Lift a panel and send a slice of the end grain to a dendrochronologist and the rings often refuse England. They match the slow-grown oaks of the south-eastern Baltic — the hinterlands that fed Gdansk, then Königsberg and Courland — the boards the trade called wainscot and the Dutch called *wagenschot*. Haneca and colleagues, writing in the *Journal of Archaeological Science* in 2005, showed how art-historical oak can be provenanced by those ring patterns. In 1565, they note, 85 percent of the wainscots that passed the Danish Sound shipped from Gdansk. The carving may be English. The fiber is a North Sea cargo.

## The tree the ports sold

European furniture oak is mostly *Quercus robur* (pedunculate) and *Q. petraea* (sessile). Dealers later sold it as Danzig, Riga, Polish, Slavonian, French, or English oak depending on the quay and the century. The USDA *Wood Handbook* does not give a single FPL-equivalent Janka for *Q. robur* `[CITE NEEDED for a bench-copy imported-woods number]`. American white oak, the nearest handbook neighbor, sits near 1,360 lbf and specific gravity 0.68 at 12 percent moisture; northern red oak near 1,290 lbf and 0.63 (FPL-GTR-190, table 5–3a). Figure 4 puts those two among the series anchors. Slow-grown Baltic stock is often finer and more even than a wide-ringed second-growth American board. The binomial does not tell you the ring count. The Sound does not either. A splitter in the Vistula hinterland did.

Both European species are ring-porous. Large earlywood vessels open each spring; latewood closes the year. Tyloses plug many heartwood pores in the white-oak group, which is why tight cooperage and ship planking follow the same trees that made panels. Red-group oaks keep the pores open. A Renaissance wainscot is a white-group story: liquid stays out, the board takes a radial face, and the medullary rays — ribbons from pith to bark — show as flake when the cut is true.

The *Wood Handbook* (ch. 4) treats tangential shrinkage as commonly about one and a half to two times radial for many hardwoods. A radially split or quartersawn Baltic board moves less in width than a flatsawn slab from the same log. That is why a thin linenfold panel can stay quiet in a groove for four centuries, and why a cheap 1890 oak sideboard, steam-sawn tangent to the rings, cups toward the bark.

## Gdansk, the Sound, and the driest boards

Wainscot is a cargo word, not a mood. Tomasz Wazny’s work on Gdansk export oak, cited throughout the Haneca paper, is the port-side half of the dendro story. The Danish Sound Toll at Helsingør taxed the boards as they left the Baltic. Western buyers in London, Antwerp, Amsterdam, and the Hanse towns paid for dryness and for a radial face. The KNOB bulletin literature still quotes the period instruction: the driest wainscots you know how to get. That is a specification, not a proverb.

Figure 2 draws the Baltic oak lane beside Atlantic mahogany and Indian Ocean teak. They are parallel supplies, not a relay. In the sixteenth century the oak lane is the one that matters to a joiner. Königsberg and Courland rose as Gdansk’s share fell; Riga’s name stuck to a grade long after the quay mix changed. Home oak built frames, posts, and anything a shop could hide. Imported boards often made the faces — the linenfold, the carved frieze, the panel a guest would touch.

A London joiner in 1550 did not need a botanist. He needed a balk that had already been cleft or sawn to keep the rings on end in the thickness. A wedge follows the rays. The board you get is naturally quartered. Baltic *wagenschot* was typically converted that way (Haneca et al. 2005; the KNOB wainscot essays). Pitsaws could still rotate a balk and take radial cuts. They usually did not unless the buyer paid. Width is money. The radial face was the dear face.

Do not invent a named voyage or a price from a blank register. The Sound Toll books exist; a writer who has not opened a year-page should say so. Later, in 1740, Gillows of Lancaster could still pay nearly mahogany prices for Danzig oak (Bowett, provincial woods paper). That sentence is a late echo of the same grade, not a Renaissance invoice.

## What Renaissance shops asked the board to do

Macquoid’s *Age of Oak* (1500–1660 in *A History of English Furniture*, 1904–08) is a London clock. The Baltic trade is older than his first date and wider than England. Netherlandish and French joiners used the same cargo. A Flemish linenfold and an English one share a fiber before they share a carver. The Renaissance in this draft is the hour when radially split oak, imported in quantity, met a fashion for framed panels, carved folds, and joined chests that replaced the boarded box in polite rooms.

Oak planes if the grain is straight and the irons are sharp. Quartersawn stock picks up at the rays. Interlocked or wavy boards tear. Tannin is the daily chemistry: iron and damp oak write a black stain — the old ink, the ruined clamp mark. Water-based finishes raise the grain into needles. Oil and wax leave the pores open and the flake bright. A shop that wants the sixteenth century back skips the Victorian filler that hid the structure on purpose.

The wood is hard enough to take a draw-bored pin and still be carved. It is not the carvable evenness of later mahogany. A linenfold is a gouge-and-veiner job on a radial face; the fold reads because the ray flake catches light differently from the ground. That is anatomy doing the work of ornament.

## Joinery

The joint that holds this hour together is the draw-bored mortise and tenon. The pin is driven through a hole in the tenon that is offset a hair toward the shoulder. As the pin goes home it pulls the shoulder tight. Oak crushes in a predictable direction if the stile is riven or quartered. Years on, the pin lozenges in the hole as the wood dries. Victor Chinnery’s *Oak Furniture: The British Tradition* is still the long walk through that joint and the forms that use it: the joined stool, the press, the court cupboard, the chest with stiles and rails around a floating panel.

Frame-and-panel is not a style. It is manners for a wide board. The groove is deep enough that a panel can lose width and still sit. The stiles and rails stay closer to quartered so the frame remains true. A boarded six-board chest — nailed or pegged, no frame — is the older, cheaper cousin. Both exist in the same decades. The joined chest is the one that wants Baltic faces.

Linenfold sits on that panel. The carver works the radial face; the back can be riven-rough. Dowelled or grooved into the rails, the panel must float. Glue it tight and the next dry winter splits the fold down a vessel line.

Haunched tenons at the corners of a frame keep the mortise away from the end grain of a stile. A muntin between two panels is a third stile with two grooves. Pins are oak, sometimes a slightly darker piece so they read. Iron nails appear in boarded work and in later repairs; they stain.

A chest lid that later becomes a table top is a common afterlife. Look at hinge scars and a leftover till. The Baltic panel does not care what century reused it. The ring pattern still points east.

## Conservation

Oak in the American and European mains is not a CITES appendix story. It is a forestry story: high-grading of slow-grown clears, oak wilt and sudden oak death in some regions `[CITE NEEDED for a current range map if counties are named]`, and a market that still asks second growth to behave like 1565 wainscot. Plantation oak exists. It is not Gdansk hinterland oak. Tight rings must be written on the purchase order, or the buyer pays for antique stock and accepts iron stains and sapwood worm.

A Renaissance panel in a museum is a documented object. A “linenfold chest” from an unnamed warehouse may be a Victorian Gothic revival with steam-sawn oak and a stain. End grain tells: rings through the thickness and a fine ring count support the Baltic claim; wide rings and a cathedral face do not. Dendrochronology is the only honest provenance for “Baltic.” A dealer’s country of origin on a tag is not.

Repair a split panel with a spline or a butterflied patch in oak of similar ring and cut. Do not flood the face with a film finish that fills the pores the first shop left open. Iron stains at old hinges are history; bleaching them for a kitchen island is a different object.

Arkansas white oak still quarters, and the flake is the same anatomy a Gdansk splitter sold. The rings are wider. The kiln ticket is new. That is the whole local sentence.

## What to look for

On an old piece: ray flake on a thin panel, a draw-bored pin that has lozenged, sapwood worm, a black line where a clamp or a nail met tannin. On a new “Renaissance oak” suite: printed grain with no end-grain rings, or a 0.6 mm oak face on a fiber core. On a claim of Baltic: ask for a ring study or be quiet. Haneca’s method is available. A port name on a hang-tag is not a Sound Toll page.

Macquoid named an English age. The panel in the chest may have paid a Danish clerk on the way.
