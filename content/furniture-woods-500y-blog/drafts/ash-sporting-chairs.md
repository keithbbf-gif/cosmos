# Ash, the hoop, and the sporting chair

A Windsor bow coming off a form is ash doing the work fashion used to ignore. The piece was in the steam box until the fibers would move. It was pulled around iron or timber, clamped, and left to set. The same long fiber made tennis frames, tool handles, wagon gear, and the white-ash baseball bat until maple and aluminum took shares. Furniture treated ash as a servant until Mission shops and later pale interiors noticed the open grain. Then a beetle from Asia arrived in Michigan in 2002 and the servant’s tree began to die in streets and woodlots across the eastern United States.

## Handle grade and a quiet oak face

White ash (*Fraxinus americana*) sits at specific gravity 0.60 and Janka 1,320 lbf in the *Wood Handbook* (FPL-GTR-190, table 5–3a) — oak’s neighbor in hardness, a little lighter. Figure 4 places that hardness among the series’ ten anchors. Ring-porous: a bold earlywood pore line, a face like a quieter oak. Heartwood light brown; sapwood wide and nearly white. That pale sap is the restaurant-table ash of the 2010s. Black ash (*F. nigra*) is lighter (basic SG in the 0.45–0.48 range, FPL), a basket-splint and swamp wood, not handle grade. European *F. excelsior* is the English and continental chair ash, never abandoned by designers who needed a pale, tough bend.

Ash has no tyloses. You do not build a barrel from it. Durability in the ground is poor. Durability in a stretcher is excellent. Springy long fiber survives rungs that snap in a short-fiber species. Chair shops and bat factories used to buy from the same grade stories. Tap a board. Handle-grade rings. Kiln-dried stock from standing-dead trees can be brittle. Bend with caution.

Ash planes well, with pore-edge tear if you rush. Dark stain hits the earlywood first and makes a striped, oak-revival face. Clear finish on pale sapwood is the later taste: Scandinavian-adjacent interiors, the “white oak” look for people who wanted more bounce in the grain. Open pores want filler if you want glass. Most shops now leave them. Rays are finer than oak’s. The flake will not flash. Red oak’s open pores resemble ash; quartered red oak still shows an oak ray if you look. The tyloses test still separates white oak from this tree.

## Steam-bend radii: the hoop, not the café loop

Evans’s American Windsor research and the English vernacular literature agree on the job: bend and toughness. English country chairs — ash and elm in the Midlands and the Thames valley — are older than Macquoid’s parlor ages. High-style eighteenth-century London did not make ash its mahogany. Painted Hepplewhite-style chairs sometimes used beech or birch; ash shows up in the cheaper painted seat. Provincial work is where to look. A continuous-arm bow in ash is the American sentence. Oak will bow too; quartersawn oak was used; hickory will bow and come back like a tool. Maple mostly refuses the box. Beech takes Thonet’s tight café radius in Moravian volume. Ash takes the Windsor hoop and the sporting frame.

Steam-bend radius still follows thickness and grain. A straight-grained ash bow, steamed and pulled around a form, will take the hoop radii High Wycombe and New England shops needed — tighter than a clumsy flatsawn oak, not as tight as the smallest Thonet beech loops on a café back `[CITE NEEDED: a bending-wood table that lists Fraxinus next to Fagus]`. A knot on the outside of the bend will open. A standing-dead urban log after the beetle may snap where a green handle-grade stick would complain first. The sporting-goods shop knew this before the furniture shop wrote it down. Tennis frames, hockey sticks, the bat: the same selection. A chair back that must flex and return is that selection in a parlor.

Mission and Arts and Crafts shops used ash when oak was dear or when a paler open grain was wanted. Some American factory “oak” of the golden-oak years is ash under a stain. A lens on the end grain. Mid-century bent-plywood ash faces and pale dining furniture were a fashion return, not a new species. European designers never left *F. excelsior* for chairs.

The sporting chair is not only a metaphor. Club chairs and porch rockers in ash, gymnasium seating, the pale restaurant chair of the 2010s, the tennis-club bench: shops specified ash because it flexes and shows a grain that reads as honest. A tennis frame of laminated ash, before graphite, is the same selection as a Windsor bow — long fiber, a radius, a return. Baseball bats were white ash until maple (the hard, dense *Acer saccharum* of the birdseye draft) and aluminum took shares. The bat factories and the furniture shops bought from the same grade stories. That is a purchasing fact, not a romance of sport.

Shaker tape seats are usually maple rails, not ash; the oval-box swallow-tail is maple. Ash’s joinery credit is the hoop and the stretcher, not the tape hole. Campaign knockdown fittings are a teak and mahogany habit. Ash knockdown is a Windsor you can ship in parts, or a later factory chair with screws. Do not confuse the contracts. A through-tenon on a Mission ash arm, pinned, is the visible joint that style wanted. A steam-bent continuous arm is the joint the fiber wanted. Both are ash chairs. They are not the same chair.

## 2002, and the other thinning

Emerald ash borer (*Agrilus planipennis*) was identified in the Detroit area in 2002 (USDA / APHIS and Forest Service timelines). It has since killed ash across a huge part of the species’ North American range. The furniture implication is not that ash has left the earth. It is uneven salvage, a question over future clear wide stock, and a warning to designers who treated ash as infinite pale oak. Urban and woodland salvage has flooded some markets with dead standing timber of uneven quality. Metal in street trees ruins saws. Ask how long the tree stood dead, and whether anyone ran a detector.

Europe has its own ash disaster: *Hymenoscyphus fraxineus*, ash dieback, a different organism, a similar thinning of *F. excelsior* (noted as a parallel introduced-pathogen story in the chestnut-blight review literature). English chair woods are not what they were. Use what is already down. Dry it. Do not sell stained poplar as ash. Planting and breeding programs exist. They are not a catalogue grade yet.

*Fraxinus* species are not CITES furniture woods in the mahogany sense. The conservation issue is pest and disease. Street-tree furniture is a reasonable use if the wood is identified and dried. A pale ash table from domestic hardwood is still one of the more readable American buys: no appendix, anatomy you can see, a history in chairs that does not need a tropical port. The beetle makes that sentence sadder than it was in 1999. It does not make it false.

Salvage drying is the shop problem the extension bulletins underplay. Standing-dead ash can be dry on the outside and wet at the core, or the reverse, or checked so a steam bend is a gamble. Sticker it. Weigh it. Bend a sample. A board that rings like handle grade may still have a hidden shake. Urban nails and fences are the other tax. A detector pass is cheaper than a saw. The furniture that comes out of that pile can be honest and beautiful. It cannot be specified as if the 1999 forest still stood.

A designer who specifies “ash chair” without saying hoop, splat, or restaurant slab has specified a color. Say the part. Then say whether the log was living or standing dead, and how it was dried. The Janka number will not save a brittle bend.

Through-tenons and pinned stretchers in Mission ash want the spring and the open pore. A tight glue-only stretcher in a short-fiber species snaps; ash complains first and often holds. Predrill for pins. The earlywood will take stain in stripes if you darken the piece — the golden-oak costume. A clear finish on sapwood will yellow a little, like maple sap, less dramatically than cherry. That pale restaurant table of 2014 is now in its cream middle age. Leave it, or accept a refinish that will not stay white. A steam-bent arm that was forced past its radius will show a raised grain on the extrados; that is the fiber talking, not a finish fault.

## What to look for

Ring-porous, no big oak rays, spring in a thin member, a bend without a break. On a Windsor: the bow. On a 1900 “oak” rocker: maybe ash. On a 2010s table: sapwood that will yellow a little. On a tennis frame or a bat: handle grade, the same tree. On a new wide slab: the standing-dead question. On a stained factory chair: the end grain, not the catalogue word.

Arkansas is ash country in the same way it is oak country — mixed hardwood stands, white ash in the better soils. A shop that works it now is working a wood whose living future is being argued in extension bulletins. The boards already in stick are still boards. The hoop, the stretcher, and the sporting frame share a fiber. They do not share a century. Name the tree. Then decide whether the job is a radius, a pale top, or a salvage.
