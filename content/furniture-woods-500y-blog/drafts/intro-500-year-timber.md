# Five hundred years of furniture timber

Percy Macquoid, opening *A History of English Furniture* in 1904, split the fashionable English house into four timber piles: oak to 1660, walnut to 1720, mahogany to 1770, satinwood after that. Walk a country-house corridor and the scheme still works on the hand — blackened joined oak, faded oyster walnut, a red-brown splat, a yellow card table. It fails as soon as you leave that corridor. A Gdansk wainscot in an English chest is a Baltic object. A Philadelphia high chest is mahogany on yellow-poplar. A Thonet café chair is Moravian beech. A Ming yoke-back is huanghuali. Macquoid named a London clock. The trade ran several clocks at once.

## What a furniture wood is asked to do

A furniture wood is a species a shop will dry, face, joint, and either show or hide. Beech made Thonet’s café empire and also laundry pegs. Yellow-poplar lined Philadelphia drawers and also the cores of cheap veneered suites. The difference is the job, not a moral ranking of trees.

Three behaviors decide whether a wood stays in the rack.

Hardness and density. The USDA Forest Products Laboratory reports side hardness (Janka) and specific gravity at 12 percent moisture in the *Wood Handbook* (FPL-GTR-190, 2010, tables 5–3a and following). White oak sits near 1,360 lbf and SG 0.68; black cherry near 950 lbf and 0.50; eastern white pine near 380 lbf and 0.35; Honduran mahogany near 800 lbf and 0.45–0.50 (imported-woods chapter / Kukachka FPL-125). Those numbers explain why a pine tavern table dents under a buckle and a maple counter does not. They do not explain fashion. Fashion is why cherry was a secondary wood in one decade and a show wood in the next.

Movement. Wood shrinks across the grain as it dries, more tangentially than radially (FPL-GTR-190, ch. 4). Quartersawn stock moves less in width. A door flatsawn in 1710 and hung in a heated apartment in 2010 will tell you this without a hygrometer. Frame-and-panel is not a style. It is manners for a wide board. Breadboard ends, floating panels, slotted screws, and later plywood grounds are all answers to the same geometry.

Workability. Interlocked grain (elm, sapele) tears under a plane. Silica (teak) dulls edges. Open pores (oak, ash) drink filler. Fine texture (maple, boxwood, pear) takes a polish that looks like light under glass. Shops chose woods for the tool in hand as much as for the color in the plate book.

## Trade is the hidden style

English oak furniture of 1550 is not simply English oak. Dendrochronology on panels and chests keeps pointing to the south-eastern Baltic — the hinterlands of Gdansk, then Königsberg and Courland — for the slow-grown, radially split boards sold as wainscot (*wagenschot*). Haneca and colleagues (2005) showed how art-historical oak can be provenanced by ring patterns. In 1565, they note, 85 percent of wainscots passing the Danish Sound shipped from Gdansk. London joiners mixed that imported stuff with home oak. A linenfold chest can be a North Sea object even when the carving looks local.

Walnut’s English fashion after 1660 rode Dutch and French taste and a veneer technology that wasted less of a scarce, figured log. American black walnut, *Juglans nigra*, did a different job in the colonies: it was a primary solid wood, wide and available, and it still is in the central hardwood belt.

Mahogany’s English story is a statute. Adam Bowett killed the old connoisseur dates (Macquoid’s 1733, Cescinsky’s 1748) for the duty change. The Naval Stores Act of 1721, in force 1722, took the import duty off British West Indian timber. Jamaica wood that had not been worth the freight became, in the port books, cheap enough to carve in the solid. Thomas Ripley, advising the Admiralty, called mahogany cheaper than deal or wainscot when Navy ships carried it freight-free (*Georgian Group Journal*, 1997). The rococo splat did not invent mahogany. A tax cut and a sugar island did. Logging gangs and enslaved labor sit in those records. A dining table can be beautiful and still be a receipt from an extractive system.

## Joinery follows the fiber

Draw-bored mortise and tenon in riven oak holds because the pin, driven slightly offset, pulls the shoulder tight as the oak moves; Chinnery’s *Oak Furniture* is still the long walk through that joint. A mahogany splat can be thinner than a walnut one because the fiber is long and even enough to take a carved piercing without crumbling — the claim R. W. Symonds made and Bowett complicated. An elm Windsor seat takes four tapered legs because the interlocked grain will not split out to the rim (Evans, *Windsor-Chair Making in America*). Beech takes Thonet’s steam bend; maple takes a turned bead; boxwood takes a stringing line a millimeter wide.

When the fiber changes, the joint changes. Veneer on a deal carcase (walnut age) is a different contract from a joined oak stool. A crotch-mahogany face on yellow-poplar (American Empire) is a different contract from a solid Jamaica dining leaf. Plywood’s cross-band is the industrial version of the floating panel. The glossary and the cut chapters in this series are not technical asides. They are half of period style.

## The other clocks

In the German-speaking lands after the Napoleonic wars, Biedermeier shops made a virtue of pale birch, pear, and cherry. In Denmark a century later, oak and teak and a little rosewood became an export look. In the United States the Shakers kept maple, cherry, and pine while Renaissance Revival factories drowned parlors in black walnut. Gustav Stickley and the English Arts and Crafts men dragged quartersawn oak back into the polite room and fumed it with ammonia so the tannins went brown.

Michael Thonet’s No. 14 chair, 1859, is six pieces of steamed beech (V&A W.31-2011). Alvar Aalto’s Paimio seating is Finnish birch laminate. Charles and Ray Eames’s plywood chairs learned their compound curves on a 1942 wartime splint (V&A plywood history). None of these fit an Age of Mahogany.

## Substitutes and paper

By 1900 Cuban mahogany (*Swietenia mahagoni*) was already a memory in commercial sizes. Honduran *S. macrophylla* took the name, then African *Khaya* and *Entandrophragma*. Brazilian rosewood (*Dalbergia nigra*) went onto CITES Appendix I; the rest of *Dalbergia* followed onto Appendix II on 2 January 2017. Big-leaf mahogany’s neotropical populations entered Appendix II in 2003 (in force 15 November 2003). The U.S. Lacey Act as amended in 2008, and APHIS Phase VII declarations (1 December 2024), make a binomial on an invoice part of the piece.

Particleboard and veneered MDF are furniture woods in the only sense that counts: they are what most people now own. They move less than solid flatsawn oak. They fail in water. They can be named without apology. They cannot be named as walnut.

## How to walk the house

Start with the cut. A linenfold panel that is still flat after four centuries is usually riven or quartered. A two-board mahogany top with matching cathedrals is flatsawn and proud of it. A run of dining-chair legs that do not match is usually a flatsawn run. The glossary that follows is the field kit: three faces from one log, pore classes, and the shop words that name too many trees. Figure 3 lives there.

Then take a wood you already own, or walk Macquoid’s English stairs — oak, walnut, mahogany, satinwood — and keep going into the rooms he barely named: Baltic wainscot, Dutch shipping, navy reserves, American pines, campaign teak, Thonet beech, factory maple, CITES paper. Figure 1 and Figure 4 above are the map and the scatter. The later figures sit where the lane, the cut, or the appendix is the subject.

Open a drawer wherever you are. Secondary wood is the honest half of the piece: yellow-poplar under Philadelphia mahogany, deal under a Dutch *kast*, pine under a painted New England chest. The show face is the fashion. The interior is the mill.

Arkansas sits in this history without a costume. White oak, red oak, cherry, walnut, hickory, ash, and pine are the same genera that filled American shops in 1800. The difference is second-growth rings, kiln schedules, and a legal paper trail. Bradley Brand works those hardwoods. The useful question is still the shop question: what will the board do — swell, dent, darken, take a finish, outlive a fashion — and what joint did someone cut to live with that.

Macquoid gave a staircase. The house has more rooms than he drew.
