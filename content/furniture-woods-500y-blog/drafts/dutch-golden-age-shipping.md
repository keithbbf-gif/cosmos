# Dutch shipping and Golden Age timber

Stand on the Zaan in the seventeenth century and the air is sawdust and tar. Wind-powered mills convert Baltic balks into boards. Timber auctions move oak and deal that never grew in Holland’s wet fields. Amsterdam’s warehouses hold *wagenschot* for local joiners and for re-export west. The Republic’s furniture — the great *kast*, the linen press, the boarded chest that went to a farm in Friesland — sits on a shipping economy, not on a native oak forest of English size. The Golden Age is a taste in pictures. It is also a quay.

## A republic that imported its furniture wood

The Low Countries had oaks. They did not have enough slow-grown, radially useful furniture oak to feed a century of urban joinery and a shipbuilding industry at the same time. Baltic oak — *Quercus robur* and *Q. petraea* from the hinterlands of Gdansk, then Königsberg and Courland — filled the gap. Haneca and colleagues (2005) provenanced art-historical oak by ring width and pointed at that south-eastern Baltic. In 1565, they note, 85 percent of wainscots passing the Danish Sound shipped from Gdansk. Dutch buyers stood in that stream with the English and the Flemish. The KNOB bulletin literature on *wagenschot* — including the driest-boards instruction — is a Dutch-language half of the same specification.

Deal travelled the same sea: Scots pine (*Pinus sylvestris*) and spruce (*Picea abies*) as yellow, red, and white deal, sawn boards for floors, backs, and painted work. Bowett’s provincial-woods essays keep *wainscot* and *deal* from collapsing into moods. Different cargoes. Sometimes the same hull. Sound Toll registers and later timber-auction records at the Zaan and in London are the paper trail. Do not invent a named voyage or a price from a blank year. Ports in the ordinary timber literature include Riga, the Prussian quays, and the Norwegian outlets that fed the softwood side `[CITE NEEDED: pin a named cargo series to a year only with a register page]`.

Tropical woods appear in small work and in VOC-adjacent luxury — ebony stringing, later a tropical veneer — but the *kast* that holds the linen is oak, or oak show on a deal hide.

American white oak in the *Wood Handbook* (FPL-GTR-190, table 5–3a) is the mechanical neighbor: Janka about 1,360 lbf, specific gravity 0.68. Northern red oak: 1,290 lbf, 0.63. European oak sits nearby; a single FPL Janka for *Q. robur* wants a bench citation `[CITE NEEDED]`. Eastern white pine, 380 lbf and SG 0.35, is not the Dutch furniture pine. The Republic’s softwood is Baltic *sylvestris* and spruce — harder in many northern grades than *P. strobus*, with a stronger ring. Furniture shops used the joinery grade. Masts and spars were another ticket (Albion’s naval book is the English strategic pair; Dutch yards had their own mast trade `[CITE NEEDED for a named Dutch mast-port series]`).

## What the Golden Age shop built

The Dutch *kast* is the object to stand beside. Tall, framed, often with heavy cornice and cushioned frieze, doors on floating panels, interior of deal or oak, sometimes ebonized moldings that are stained fruitwood or true ebony in small section. It is a linen and plate cupboard for a merchant house. The wood is tannic, ring-porous, radial on the better doors. A cheaper *kast* can be painted or grained. Graining is a skill and a disguise.

Joined stools, gateleg tables, boarded chests, and wall paneling use the same grammar as English work because the shops shared a North Sea joinery culture. Distinctions are in proportion, in the profile of a molding, in the hardware — not in a different physics. A Haarlem panel and a Southwark panel can be the same Gdansk hinterland tree.

After mid-century, French and Italian walnut taste and a veneer technology reach Amsterdam as they reach London. The walnut drafts take that hour. The Golden Age oak *kast* does not vanish. It becomes the conservative object in the same house that bought a veneered cabinet for the voorhuis.

Painted furniture of the farms — greens, reds, later the Frisian and North German cousins — is often deal. Identification on a painted antique is a scraped hidden edge and a resin-canal search. Do not scrape a museum piece.

## Joinery

Draw-bored mortise and tenon on the *kast* stiles. The pin pulls the shoulder. Oak likes a riven or quartered stile so the pin crushes along a predictable fiber. Frame-and-panel doors: groove deep enough for seasonal shrink, haunched tenons at the corners, a muntin if the door is wide. Raised panels appear; the raise is a bevel to a field, not a glued-on sheet.

Interior fittings — shelves on cleats, a hanging rail, small drawers — are often deal, nailed or pegged. A walnut-veneered cabinet of the next generation will still hide deal or oak. Open the door. The truth is pale.

Boarded chests for export and for farms: nailed six-board construction, iron straps, a lock plate. The fiber crushes around a nail and holds. Glue is secondary. A till inside is the same habit as an English chest.

Floor construction and stair joinery in a canal house use the same imported oak and deal. Furniture is the portable subset. A restorer who treats a *kast* as if it grew in a Utrecht coppice will buy the wrong repair board. Match ring width and cut, not a romantic “Dutch oak” label.

Veneer, when it arrives, is a different contract: hide glue, a ground of oak or deal, oysters and burls that check if the ground moves. That is the Baroque walnut hour. This draft stops at the solid *kast* and the quay that fed it.

Ship carpentry overlaps the furniture shop in tools and in timber grades, not in joints. A knee is a grown crook. A *kast* stile is a straight quartered balk. Albion’s English navy story and the Dutch admiralty yards both ate compass timber and straight oak; furniture took the leftovers and the joinery boards. Do not collapse a hull and a cupboard into one romance.

## Trade, war, and the end of an easy Baltic

The Sound Toll made Denmark a clerk on every Baltic board. Wars in the eastern Baltic and competition from English buyers shifted shares among Gdansk, Königsberg, Courland, and Riga (Haneca; Wazny). The Dutch were entrepôt merchants: buy, convert at the Zaan, sell on. A board in a London chest can have passed a Dutch mill. Dendrochronology names the forest. It does not always name the last ship.

Figure 1’s later milestones — mahogany after 1721, CITES in the twentieth century — are other clocks. The Golden Age clock is the Sound, the Zaan mill, and the *kast*. Tropical listings do not apply to *Quercus* or *Pinus sylvestris*. The conservation problem here is old-growth boreal and temperate oak, not an appendix number.

When the Republic’s great shipping hour tightens after 1672, the furniture trade does not stop. It changes suppliers and tastes. Walnut and later mahogany enter the polite room. Oak stays in the *kast* that already stood in the house. That is the same two-room story Macquoid told for England, told from a quay instead of a country-house corridor.

A Zaan mill’s sash saw set a balk on a carriage and took boards a Haarlem joiner could plane into a door stile. The *kast* that results can stand in a canal house with a still-life of lemons on the wall while its interior smells of deal and linen. Open the lower door. Shelf cleats are nailed. Backboards run horizontal, often unsmoothed. Iron lock plates and drop handles are the hardware of a merchant cupboard, not a church press. A small ebony string on a later cabinet in the same room is a tropical accent on a Baltic body. Do not let the string rename the *kast*.

## Conservation

Boreal and Baltic forestry is a climate and old-growth argument. Antique *wagenschot* in a *kast* is reuse by definition. New “reclaimed Baltic oak” should have a story that is not a container of mixed hardwood. FSC and PEFC appear on Nordic pine for a reason; they are certificates, not a legal finding.

Lead in old paint on a Frisian cupboard is a health fact. Overpaint is a palimpsest. Stripping to “natural pine” is often a twentieth-century taste.

Repair a *kast* door with quartered oak of similar ring. A wide-ringed American red oak will take stain and still look like a patch: open pores, a different flake. White-group European or American oak is the closer neighbor. Tyloses and ray size still argue if the ring count is wrong.

## What to look for

A tall framed *kast* with floating panels, deal interiors, ebonized small moldings, iron locks. Paint layers on a farm chest. A radial face on a door and a wilder ring on a hidden stile. A London bill that says wainscot or deal — the same words a Dutch auction used in another language.

The Golden Age picture on the wall may be a calm sea. The furniture in the room paid a mill on the Zaan and a clerk at Helsingør.
