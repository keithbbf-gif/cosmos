# Plywood and the core

The V&A’s short history of plywood in ten-ish objects opens with a correction. It has always been assumed that the history of plywood was mainly that of the flat board. That is not the case. Between about 1850 and 1890 molded plywood was the common innovative form, and furniture design drove it. John Henry Belter’s New York patent of 1858 molded chair backs in batches of eight. Charles and Ray Eames’s 1942 US Navy leg splint taught compound curves that became the DCM. Alvar Aalto’s Paimio seat is a birch scroll between two frames. The face can be walnut, teak, rosewood, or birch. The core can be a tree the catalog hoped you would not name. This draft is the sandwich: what the layers do that a solid board will not, and what they hide.

## Before the mold: a leaf on a ground

Cross-banded veneer is older than the factory. An Empire pier table with a crotch-mahogany face on yellow-poplar is already a two-species contract. Hide glue, a hammer or a caul, a flame figure that would check if you tried to use the fork as a solid drawer front. FPL-GTR-190 puts yellow-poplar at SG 0.42 and Janka 540 lbf — wide, glue-friendly, the Mid-Atlantic servant under Federal and Empire mahogany (Bowett’s Atlantic *Swietenia*, the same genus the 1721/1722 duty cut had made cheap enough to carve in the solid). The leaf follows the ground. If the ground cups, the picture cocks. Plywood is that idea with the grain of each inner leaf run the other way so the cup argues with itself.

Figure 3 is why. A flatsawn solid is mostly tangential grain; tangential shrinkage is the large number in the *Wood Handbook* (FPL-GTR-190, ch. 4). A quartersawn solid moves less in width. A three-ply or seven-ply sheet cancels the width change by stacking contradictions. The 18th-century frame-and-panel was manners for a wide board. The cross-band is industrial manners for a wide sheet.

Rotary veneer — a log on a lathe, peeled like a paper towel — makes the cheap cathedrals of a 1950s dresser side. Sliced veneer makes the crown-cut and quarter-cut faces that want to look like show wood. Furniture that wants to look like a solid uses sliced faces. Furniture that wants to be cheap uses rotary faces. Furniture that wants to be honest modern sometimes shows the ply edge.

## 1850–1890: the mold before the badge

Belter’s technique, the V&A notes, increased speed and cut cost: chair backs in batches from a single mold, rosewood leaves laminated, then carved. That is *Dalbergia* in a process, not only in a parlor fashion (the Victorian draft). Gardner & Company’s doll’s chair of about 1875 and Isaac Cole’s 1874 all-plywood chair are the other V&A witnesses. Industrial flat boards expand in the 1880s. Prejudice against ply as cheap kept much of it hidden under other materials — Christopher Wilk’s point in the V&A exhibition texts.

Thonet’s No. 14 (V&A W.31-2011) is not plywood. It is six pieces of steamed solid beech. The parallel factory problem was how to make a seat or a back from leaves glued in a mold when you did not want, or could not get, a steam-bent solid. Molded ply and bent solid are cousins, not twins. Beech takes Thonet’s bend. Birch peels and glues for Aalto’s stack. Mahogany and rosewood take a mold if the leaves are thin and the glue holds. A solid flatsawn oak seat of the same compound curve would split.

## Aalto, then the splint, then the DCM

Aalto’s Paimio chair (1930, tuberculosis sanatorium) suspends a thin, curved birch seat between two frames. The V&A tells it as a plywood milestone; Artek’s factory made the bends a product; Finmar sold them into Britain in the 1930s. Casein glue in some of Artek’s cold-bend processes (Klim, WAG 1990) is a reminder that “plywood glue” is not one chemistry. Northern birch peels well and glues well. It is not silica-hard like teak, not tannic like oak. Thin leaves, stacked, take a bend a solid oak of the same thickness will not.

Charles and Ray Eames, with Eero Saarinen, tried compound-curved shells for MoMA’s 1940 Organic Design competition. The war interrupted, then supplied the 1942 molded-plywood leg-splint contract for the US Navy (Engelbrecht on the 1946 chair; Kirkham). Later in the war they made plywood aircraft parts. The DCM (dining chair metal), 1946–47, wears a three-dimensionally molded seat. Robin Day, via the V&A: every designer he knew had a picture of that Eames chair pinned to the drawing board. The face might be walnut, ash, teak, or a calico birch. The core is the process.

Wilk is careful, and so is this draft: the Eameses did not pioneer plywood, or plywood molding, or the inflated rubber bag (the Loughead brothers used a bag in a cement mold for aircraft in 1919 `[CITE NEEDED: pin Wilk’s Loughead citation]`). They were the most influential furniture designers of the second half of the century, and they domesticated the molded seat. Aalto had started the modern furniture argument. The Eames shop industrialized the compound curve.

Molded ply versus solid is a structural sentence. A solid member has long grain you can trust for a slender splat (mahogany after Bowett’s statute) and a movement you must allow across the width. A molded shell has no single “across.” It has layers, a glue line, a thickness that can be thin because the curve is the stiffness. You cannot draw-bore it. You cannot plane a shaving off a compound seat and call it a refinish without changing the section. Repair is specialist work. A pine block sistered onto a laminate scroll is not conservation.

## Core stock: the tree you were not sold

Kukachka’s imported-woods paper is a core-stock document as much as a show-wood one. Fuma (*Ceiba pentandra*) had been used primarily for corestock and was being replaced by lupuna (*Ceiba samauma*), firmer and more readily peeled. Cuangare (“virola” in the trade, *Dialyanthera*) for corestock, with a warning: “brownheart” wet zones collapse and make wavy panels; do not use that material for hot-press cores. Okoume as the great African plywood volume. Yellow-poplar as the American rotary core and crossband. Later, lauan and meranti as the tropical cores under walnut-look faces. Particleboard and MDF as the cores that are not veneer at all.

Void-core cheap ply telegraphs and loses a screw. Baltic birch and the many-ply clear cores do not. Count plies. Weigh the board. Interior furniture ply is often a cheaper glue than the phenolic films of 1930s aircraft and boat ply (Klim). Water still finds the cheap glue. Old furniture ply delaminates when the protein glue gives up in a damp room. Clamping and the right adhesive save some of it. A belt sander does not.

Screws in the edge of a cheap ply have nothing to hold but glue and a void. Screws in the face of a seven-ply birch hold if you do not oversize the hole. Confirmat screws and barrel nuts are the late-factory answers. A 1760 mahogany chair does not need them because the fiber is long in the right direction. A molded seat does not take a traditional corner glue-block the way a square rail does. The metal loop of a DCM is the joint. The birch knee of an Aalto stool is the joint. Do not treat those as failed mortises. They are different contracts.

Veneer matching — book, slip, radial — is a design choice as old as the 17th-century ébéniste. Plywood factories automated it and then abandoned it for random rotary. A radio of 1932 with a burl face that is book-matched and a back that is rotary lauan holds both habits in one box.

Boulle work is a different sandwich — brass, turtle, ebony — and belongs in the ebony draft. The piano case is a veneered box on a softwood or poplar ground, sometimes with ply in later instruments. Do not call a Steinway fallboard an Eames shell. Call it a leaf on a box. Do not call an Eames seat a Belter back. Call both molded leaves, a century apart, different genera on the face.

## Faces that sit under appendices

Decorative faces in *Dalbergia* and *Swietenia* sit under CITES when the species does. Neotropical *S. macrophylla* entered Appendix II on 15 November 2003 for logs, sawn wood, veneer, and plywood. *D. nigra* is Appendix I. The rest of *Dalbergia* went onto Appendix II on 2 January 2017, Annotation #15 as revised so finished musical instruments and small finished products would not freeze the concert hall. A rosewood-faced molded chair is not a jewelry box. A vintage Eames or Aalto already in the country is reuse of an already-cut tree. A new “walnut ply” is a specification: face species, core species, glue, certification.

Figure 1 puts the molded-ply hour and the Eames year on the same long clock as Bowett’s 1722 and the CITES marks. They are not a relay. A 1760 solid Jamaica leaf and a 1946 DCM are both furniture wood. The joint changed when the fiber was stacked.

Lacey (2008 amendment) and APHIS Phase VII (1 December 2024) still want names. “Plywood” is a process. *Betula*, Finland, or *Shorea*, unnamed, is the content.

## What to look for

An edge. A void. A printed grain that dies at a corner. A molded seat whose underside shows layers. A Belter-type back that is several leaves. A Paimio scroll. A splint in a museum. A 1930s radio with a burl face and a cheap back. A 1950s dresser with rotary cathedrals on lauan. A poplar interior under an Empire flame — the ancestor, still at work.

Arkansas yellow-poplar is still core stock. Solid oak, walnut, and cherry are a different contract. Both can be named without apology. The sin is the single word “walnut” on a door that is 0.6 mm of face and a tropical core. The 500-year series needs this chapter because more people own ply than own joined oak. The physics is old. The factory is new. The label is where the trouble lives.
