# Veneer, solid, and the ground that moved

A crotch-mahogany face on a yellow-poplar drawer front, American Empire, that has checked into a thousand hairlines is a veneer lesson. The leaf wanted to stay put. The ground moved across the grain. The hide glue went brittle. The picture of the tree opened. Solid wood would have moved as one piece — cupped, shrunk, maybe split along a glue line — but it would not have been a skin riding a different species. Veneer is not a sin. It is a contract: a thin face, a ground, an adhesive, and a weather. This draft is that contract, from oyster walnut to foil on particleboard.

## Two conversions of the same log

Solid furniture is a board thick enough to hold a joint: a splat, a dovetail, a mortise. Veneer is a leaf, historically sawn, later sliced or rotary-peeled, glued to a cheaper or more stable ground. Walnut after 1660 in England is the fashion that made the leaf ordinary: oyster and burl, seaweed marquetry, a deal or oak carcase. The log was too precious and too figured to quarter into solids for every surface. Mahogany after 1722 went the other way — cheap enough, after the Naval Stores Act, to carve in the solid (Bowett). Then Empire and the factories swung back to crotch veneer over pine and poplar. The fiber did not change. The price of the face did.

Figure 3 is the cut of a solid board. Veneer has its own geometry. Sliced crown-cut repeats a cathedral more politely than rotary. Rotary, the log on a lathe, peels a continuous sheet: wild arches, cheap, the 1950s dresser side. Quarter-sliced oak shows flake; rift-sliced oak shows stripe. A 1910 Stickley door with ray flake is usually sliced, not peeled. Look at the repeat. Nature does not clone a ray every foot.

Hoadley’s veneer pages still teach the difference between a leaf and a board. Thickness matters. A 3 mm sawn veneer on a historic piece behaves more like a thin board. A 0.6 mm sliced face is a skin. A paper foil is a photograph. The edge tells you which contract you bought.

## The ground is the furniture

Hide glue on deal or yellow-poplar is the historic sandwich. The ground moves as FPL chapter 4 says it will: more tangentially than radially, below fiber saturation, toward the room’s EMC. If the leaf’s grain does not run with the ground’s grain, the leaf is a cross-band by accident and will try to split or check. If the grains run together, they move together until the glue fails. Hide glue reverses with heat and moisture; it also goes glass-brittle with age and central heating. The Empire crotch that checked is that failure. Conservators relay the leaf. A belt sander is not a relay.

Plywood as a ground is the 20th-century answer. Cross-bands cancel movement. A walnut face on a birch core is a stable door in January and in July. The 18th-century frame-and-panel was the old answer to the same physics: the panel floats, the frame stays. Plywood is the industrial floating panel, except the “panel” is the whole slab. An Eames LCW can be a shaped seat without the splits you would expect from a solid flatsawn slab steamed into a compound curve (V&A plywood history; Engelbrecht on the 1946 chair).

Particleboard and MDF as grounds stay flatter still and die in water. A walnut-veneered MDF kitchen door is a respectable object if the invoice says so. A paper-foil “walnut” on chip is a costume. Formaldehyde in old ureas is an indoor-air specification (CARB / TSCA Title VI), not a harvest stamp. You want both conversations.

Urea-formaldehyde as the veneer adhesive is the factory default for interior work. It does not reverse. A delaminated 1950s dresser with protein glue can sometimes be reclamped. A UF bubble under a casserole is a different repair, often a replacement leaf. Phenol-formaldehyde, the dark line, belongs to exterior and aircraft ply (Klim 1990). Name the glue when you can.

## Solid manners that veneer does not need — and some it still does

A solid flatsawn oak door needs a floating panel or it splits. A veneered MDF door does not float; it is a rectangle. Do not apply solid-wood manners to a stable panel and call it honesty, and do not apply panel manners to a solid top and call it modern. Buttons, slotted screws, and breadboards with elongated holes are for solids that move. A veneered-ply top can be screwed more rigidly. A veneered-ply top with a solid-wood breadboard glued across the end has invented a new split: the solid cap moves, the ply does not.

Solid edges on a veneered top — a lipping — are a good shop habit. They take the dent (Figure 4: the face may be walnut at 1,010 lbf, the core yellow-poplar at 540). They also take the moisture at the end grain. Finish the lipping and the underside. A finish on one face only is a cupping machine even on ply, less so than on solid, still real on cheap cores.

Matching — book, slip, radial — is a design choice as old as the 17th-century ébéniste. The Wallace Collection’s Riesener notes are the handmade version. Factories automated matching and then abandoned it for random rotary. A pair of doors that book-match are a specification. A pair that do not are a price.

## Law on a thin face

Decorative faces in rosewood and mahogany sit under CITES when the species does. Neotropical *Swietenia macrophylla* went onto Appendix II on 15 November 2003, Annotation #6: logs, sawn wood, veneer sheets, plywood. The leaf is named. *Dalbergia* went onto Appendix II on 2 January 2017, Annotation #15, revised at CoP18 in 2019 (finished musical instruments; finished products up to 10 kg of listed wood per shipment). A rosewood-faced sideboard is not a jewelry box. *D. nigra* is Appendix I (11 June 1992). Madagascar *Diospyros* veneer after 12 June 2013 is Annotation #5. Lacey 2008 §8204 and APHIS Phase VII (1 December 2024) want the face species and, when you can know it, the core species. A lauan core has a forest. This draft is not a permit.

## How to look, and what to specify

Look at the edge. A leaf that turns the corner with the grain is veneer or solid. A photograph that dies at a 0.4 mm ABS is foil. A void behind a 0.6 mm face is cheap ply. Thin dark lines, many of them, are ply. Homogeneous brown fuzz is MDF. Chips in pale glue are particleboard. End-grain rings through a thick top are solid; rings that stop at a glue line under a skin are a sandwich.

Look at the failure. Checks in a crotch that follow no board joint: veneer on a moving ground. A split down a cathedral in a thick top: solid, probably flatsawn, probably fastened tight. A bubble under a hot dish: foil or a failed urea. A panel that has shrunk in a groove: solid, and the shop that let it float was right.

Specify: face species, cut of the face (crown, quarter, rift, rotary), thickness of the leaf, core species, glue, and whether the edges are solid. Or: solid *Quercus alba*, flatsawn, floating panels, slotted screws in the breadboard, hide glue if the piece should come apart in a hundred years. Those are different objects. “Walnut dining table” is not a specification.

Arkansas shops meet veneer as imported faces on imported cores, and as domestic walnut and oak leaves on domestic ply or on yellow-poplar for paint-grade work. The solid side of that rack is the older contract. The useful sentence is the long one. The Eames DCM and a printed suburban suite share a century. They do not share a glue, a mould, or a noun.

Oyster veneer — end-grain slices of walnut or laburnum, laid like overlapping coins — wastes less of a small figured stem than a solid board would. Movement in the oysters is a different geometry (end grain, not a long face). The failure is lifting at the edges, not a cathedral split. Seaweed marquetry is the cousin: two woods, a knife, a ground.

Foil under a casserole blisters. There is no plane that fixes a bubble. A solid top would have a white ring you might revive. Choose the life before you choose the leaf. A veneered-ply top screwed rigid is acceptable physics. A solid top screwed rigid is a split. If the picture of the tree has opened and the core is a different color, you are in this chapter. If the whole thickness opened along a glue line, you are in the flatsawn and moisture chapters.
