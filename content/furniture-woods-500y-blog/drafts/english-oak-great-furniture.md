# English oak and the great furniture

A joined stool in a parish church still does the job it was made for. Four legs, four rails, a seat that may have been replaced, and at each corner a mortise and tenon pinned with a draw-bored oak peg. The peg has lozenged in the hole. The shoulder is still tight. Victor Chinnery walked that joint until it stopped being a mystery and became manners — court cupboard, press, tester bed, the great chest with a till. Macquoid, opening the *Age of Oak* in 1904, treated this furniture as the first of four English timber ages and closed the age at 1660, when walnut veneer took the polite room. The kitchen did not read him. The church did not. The farm press did not. What changed in 1660 was fashion in a few rooms, not the fiber that held the house together.

## Home oak, and what “English” hides

English furniture oak is *Quercus robur* and *Q. petraea*, the same pair the Baltic ports sold. The difference is the stand and the conversion. Home oak could be quicker grown, wider-ringed, used where a joiner could hide a wilder face — a stile, a back rail, the underside of a seat. Imported wainscot often took the show panels. “English oak furniture” is that sandwich more often than a gallery label wants to say. The joinery tradition is English, Netherlandish, and French. The fiber in a linenfold may have paid the Sound Toll. The Baltic draft takes that cargo. This one stays with the shop that assembled the house’s heavy furniture.

The USDA *Wood Handbook* (FPL-GTR-190, table 5–3a) puts American white oak near 1,360 lbf Janka and specific gravity 0.68, northern red oak near 1,290 lbf and 0.63. European oak sits in the same neighborhood; a single FPL-equivalent number for *Q. robur* is still a library errand `[CITE NEEDED]`. Figure 4 is the scatter. Figure 3 is the cut: flatsawn cathedrals, quartersawn flake, rift stripe. English joined work of 1550–1660 prefers riven or radial stock for stiles and panels. A wedge follows the rays. The board is naturally quartered. That is why a thin panel can be wide and still stay quiet.

Ring-porous, tannic, hard enough to hold a pin. Iron and damp oak make black stains. Steam-bending is a historic use for boat frames and some chair bows; ash and hickory often took the extreme bends. Oak planes if the irons are sharp and the grain is straight. Quartersawn rays pick up. A wavy board tears no matter what the catalogue promised.

John Evelyn’s *Sylva* (1664) is a planting book written as the fashionable rooms were already turning toward walnut. He wanted oaks for the navy and for the kingdom’s future timber. Furniture shops lived on what the yards and the tanbarks did not take, and on the imported boards the navy did not want for knees. Albion’s *Forests and Sea Power* is the strategic pair; the navy draft takes the reserved forest. The great furniture is the civilian half of the same trees.

## Macquoid’s rooms, Chinnery’s joints

Percy Macquoid’s first volume is still the walk through the fashionable English house: blackened joined oak, linenfold, bulbous legs on a court cupboard, inlay of holly and bog oak on a Nonsuch chest. Treat it as a periodization, not a law. Provincial England kept cutting oak while London laid *Juglans regia* oysters on deal after 1660. Church benches, farm presses, kitchen tables — the Age of Oak did not end. It left the parlor.

Chinnery remains the construction book. The court cupboard is a display machine: open shelves, a closed cupboard below, heavy stiles, rails pinned through. The press is a clothes cupboard with doors on frame-and-panel. The tester bed is a four-post frame that must not rack; the joints are the architecture. The joined stool is the teaching piece because there is nowhere to hide a bad shoulder.

Inlay and carving sit on that structure. A Nonsuch chest with architectural marquetry is still an oak box with floating panels. Bog oak and holly are the dark and light strings. They are not a different furniture system. The system is the pinned frame.

Bowett’s later work on provincial woods matters even after 1700: Gillows could pay nearly mahogany prices for Danzig oak in 1740 because quartered Baltic was still a specified grade. English shops never forgot oak. They stopped showing it in the rooms Macquoid cared about.

## Joinery

Draw-bored mortise and tenon first. The hole in the tenon is offset toward the shoulder by a fraction. The pin, often riven oak, pulls as it drives. Chinnery’s photographs of lozenged pins are the aging proof. Do not confuse this with a later factory dowel glued in a bored hole. The offset is the whole trick.

Frame-and-panel is the carcase language. Stiles and rails mortised; panels grooved and free to shrink. Haunched tenons keep the mortise out of the end of a stile. A muntin splits a wide opening into two panels so no single board has to be heroic. Grooves are ploughed; a stuck moulding on the inner edge of a rail is cut in the solid, not planted, on better work.

The boarded chest is the other English grammar: six boards, nailed or pegged, sometimes a till, sometimes chip-carved. It is not a failed joined chest. It is a different contract — faster, cheaper, more willing to split along a board if the house dries. Both grammars run through the same century.

A table with trestle ends and a loose top is older than the joined frame table. The joined table — four legs, four rails, stretchers when the span asks, a top pegged or cleated — becomes the great dining board of the hall. Tops are often two or three boards, edge-jointed, sometimes with a breadboard that must not be glued across the end. If the pins in a breadboard are tight and the top is flatsawn, the next heating season splits the leaf. Figure 3 is why.

Floor-standing cupboards pick up hinges that split stiles if the screw or nail sits too near the edge. Replaced hinges are a common scar. Look for a second set of holes and an iron stain.

Wainscot as wall lining uses the same panel grammar as the chest, only taller. A room lined in Baltic boards and a chest made in the same shop can share a conversion habit. The furniture is the portable version of the room.

Pins are oak. Trenails in heavier work can be the same. Glue, when it appears, is hide glue on a shoulder that already has a mechanical lock. Glue alone is not the period trust.

## After 1660, the rooms that did not change

Walnut veneer took the polite London room. Oak stayed in the kitchen, the church, the farm, the college hall, the court cupboard that was already heirloom. Gothic Revival and Renaissance Revival in the nineteenth century hauled carved oak back into the dining room as a costume. Those suites are steam-sawn, often stained black-brown, sometimes competent, often louder than a 1620 press. The Arts and Crafts shops later asked for quartered oak again and meant it; that is another draft. The great furniture of 1500–1660 is the joined, pinned, tannic object that still sits in a nave or a farmhouse passage.

American joined oak exists in the seventeenth century — the so-called Pilgrim-century chests of New Haven and the Connecticut valley (Forman, *American Seating Furniture, 1630–1730*, is the seating half; the chests live in the same wood culture). Colonists also had pine, poplar, and walnut in quantity. English oak furniture is a joinery export as much as a timber export. The joint travelled. The Baltic board sometimes travelled with it. Home American oak did the rest.

## Conservation

Not CITES. Worm in sapwood is ordinary; heartwood holds. Iron stains at hinges are ordinary. A top that was a chest lid is ordinary. Strip a blackened church pew to “natural oak” and you have a 1960s taste, not a 1620 one. Original surfaces are thin oil, wax, soot, and hand. A thick polyurethane film is a different object.

Replacement seats on stools are expected. Look at the rails and pins to know the stool. A “Jacobean” suite from a 1920s factory has machine-cut mouldings, even pins, and a stain that sits in the pores. The lozenged pin is the tell that time, not a distressing wheel, did the work.

Repair a loose shoulder by drawing a new pin if the old one is gone; do not flood the joint with PVA and call it saved. Keep the panel floating. If a flatsawn replacement panel is the only board you have, leave it room in the groove.

## What to look for

Lozenged pins, a floating panel, ray flake on a thin face, iron stains, a till inside a chest, hinge scars, a seat rail that still has its original pegs. Macquoid’s corridor still works on the hand: blackened joined oak before the faded oyster walnut. Walk out of that corridor into the kitchen and the same joints are still there, only cleaner from soap and worse from cooks.

The great furniture is great because it is joined, not because it is courtly. A stool that still holds is the argument.
