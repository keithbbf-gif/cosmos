# Flatsawn cathedrals and the floating panel

A dining-room door that split down a glued-up cathedral in January is a flatsawn lesson. The rings on the end grain run parallel to the face. The width of the door is mostly tangential grain. Tangential shrinkage is the large number in the *Wood Handbook* tables (FPL-GTR-190, ch. 4). The shop that glued the panel into a tight groove, or glued a breadboard across the whole end, asked the wood to be plywood. It is not. Frame-and-panel is not a style. It is manners for a wide board. This draft is the cathedral face: how you get it, what it does in a heated room, and why a panel that can shrink is the old answer.

## The cut

Flatsawn — plain-sawn, slash-sawn, through-and-through — means the saw runs tangent to the rings. On the face you get arches. Oak and ash make the loudest cathedrals because they are ring-porous; you can count years with a thumbnail. Maple and cherry make quieter ones. Mahogany in the 18th-century solid, Bowett’s cheap Jamaica wood after the Naval Stores Act of 1721 (in force 1722), was often flatsawn because the logs were huge and the shops wanted width for dining tables. A two-board top with matching cathedrals opening away from a glue line is a flatsaw trophy. You can still see the pair on drop-leaf tables in museum collections: the arches are the middle of the tree, split and opened.

Figure 3 is the geometry. Quartersawn boards put the rings nearly perpendicular to the face; the face is radial; oak shows flake. Riftsawn sits at about 30 to 60 degrees: straight stripe, fewer flakes. This chapter is the tangent. The rift and moisture drafts take the other two faces and the numbers.

When pitsaws and later water-powered sash mills took over from riving, flatsawing won on yield. You get more wide boards by slabbing the log. A 16th-century linenfold panel that started as a riven, naturally quartered board can be thin, wide, and stable in a way a cheap 19th-century oak sideboard is not. Baltic wainscot, the *wagenschot* of the Gdansk trade, was typically cleft or sawn to keep the face radial (Haneca et al. 2005). Buyers paid for that. Fashion and thrift later aligned on the cathedral. The circular saw left skip marks on backboards. Rotary veneer is the flatsawn idea taken to a lathe: the log turns, the knife peels a continuous sheet, and the cathedrals repeat at the circumference. That is how a 1950s bedroom suite can have the same arch every eighteen inches. Nature does not clone a ray every foot. A mill does.

## Movement, named as a door

FPL chapter 4 treats tangential shrinkage as commonly about 1.5 to 2 times radial for many hardwoods. White oak’s published green-to-ovendry figures sit near 10.5 percent tangential and 5.6 percent radial (FPL-GTR-190, tables in ch. 4 / species notes). Red oak is quieter on the tangential number and still a problem in a wide door. Hard maple, black walnut, black cherry — each has its own pair. Longitudinal change is a rounding error, on the order of 0.1 to 0.2 percent. You do not design for length. You design for width.

Shrinkage happens below fiber saturation, commonly near 30 percent moisture content. A board at 12 percent, the handbook’s mechanical-test moisture, still has travel left if the room goes to 6 percent in a January apartment. Equilibrium moisture content tracks relative humidity and temperature; FPL’s EMC tables are the shop reference. Period furniture was built for rooms that went cold at night. Central heating and air conditioning reset the average. A flatsawn oak leaf glued up in 1780 at a higher average humidity will shrink across the grain and may split along a glue line or open a rule joint. The door that split in January is that math in paint-grade pine or in show oak.

Cup is the other flatsawn habit. The rings try to flatten. The bark-side face becomes convex as the board dries, in the usual telling; Hoadley walks the geometry in *Understanding Wood*. Chair seats that were flatsawn cup toward the bark side. A table top without battens or breadboards will dish. That is not a defect in the tree. It is the cylinder remembering it was a cylinder.

## Joinery that assumes a cathedral

The groove in a frame-and-panel door is deep enough that a flatsawn oak panel can lose width and still sit. The panel is not glued around the perimeter. A spot of hide glue at the top center, or no glue at all, is the historic habit: the panel can move in the groove, and the show face stays centered. The stiles and rails are often closer to quartered so the frame stays true next to glass or a door edge. If you glue the panel in, you have made a wide flatsawn board with a stiff frame around it. The panel splits, or the frame rackets, or the door goes diamond. The split door in the lede is usually that mistake, or the cousin mistake: a breadboard glued across the whole end.

A breadboard end across a flatsawn top must slot-screw or pin in elongated holes. The center pin or screw can be tight. The outboard fasteners must ride. If the pins are tight at the ends, the top splits. If the breadboard is glued across the whole end, the top splits. Eighteenth-century shops knew this. Some nineteenth-century factories forgot. Some twenty-first-century box stores never learned. Look at the underside. Elongated holes and screws that can slide are evidence. A tight glue line at the breadboard shoulder and a crack in the top are evidence of the other school.

Buttons, table clips, and slotted metal fasteners are the same idea in a carcase: the top is held down and allowed to grow and shrink across its width. Pocket screws in a rigid pattern across a wide flatsawn top are a split waiting for February. Hide glue on a historic joint can be reversed with heat and moisture. Urea-formaldehyde on a factory edge cannot. If you must glue a wide top, glue long-grain to long-grain along the joints and fasten the assembly as if it were still a moving board — because it is.

Draw-boring in oak likes riven or quartered stiles. A flatsawn stile will still take a pin. The cathedral in a door stile is a visual problem more than a joint problem: the arch runs off the edge and the pair of doors will not match. Chair shops that want matching legs buy rift. Door shops that want matching stiles do the same, and they let the panel be the cathedral if the client wants figure.

## Fashion for the arch

Matching cathedrals on a mahogany dining leaf were a pride mark. The two boards came from one flitch, opened like a book. Walnut oyster veneer is a different conversion: end-grain slices, not a flatsawn face, but the same appetite for a picture the tree did not draw as a quartered stripe. Golden-oak factory sideboards of the 1890s often showed loud flatsawn figure under a yellow varnish, pores filled. Stickley’s catalogues wanted the other face — flake — and charged for it. A flatsawn Mission chair is a cheaper cousin. Collectors still sort by the arch versus the ray.

Paint hides species and cut. A pine cupboard with cathedrals under milk paint is still a flatsawn object; the panel still wants to float. Country shops knew. Some reproduction shops glue the panel “so it won’t rattle.” It will not rattle. It will split.

Rotary-cut lauan and printed foil are the industrial cathedral. The repeat gives them away. A 1970 oak suite with the same arch every eighteen inches and no end-grain rings is a photograph. Look at the edge.

## How to see it on a piece you did not make

End grain: rings running across the thickness, parallel to the face, mean flatsawn. If you cannot see the end, look at the face. Cathedrals versus straight stripe plus flake (oak) or ribbon (interlocked tropicals). Wear: cup toward the bark side on a seat; a breadboard whose pins elongated into slots; a door panel that has shrunk and left a line of raw wood in the groove in winter, or swollen and stuck in August.

Period: a 1600 joined chest with ray flake is doing a different job. A 1760 two-board mahogany top with matching arches is this chapter. A 1905 flatsawn oak sideboard is a factory yield choice. A 1970 print is a factory costume.

Cost: flatsawing wastes less of the log than quartering. That is why it won. A true quartersawn white-oak dining table still costs more per foot than a flatsawn one from the same kiln charge. Designers who want the arch should say flatsawn and then say how the width will move. Designers who write “solid oak” and get a cathedral they did not expect have not specified a cut.

Arkansas white oak flatsaws into loud earlywood pores and a stain problem if you wanted quiet. It also flatsaws into a handsome kitchen table if the breadboard is slotted and the finish is honest. Bradley Brand’s hardwoods include that table. The cut is a choice. The split door is what happens when the choice is the face without the manners.

## A glue line that was the middle of the tree

Matching cathedrals are a book-match in the solid. The sawyer opened a flitch and the two faces that had been neighbors became a pair of arches opening left and right. Hide glue along that long-grain joint is the right glue if the piece should come apart. UF along that joint is a factory habit. Either glue will hold the pair as one panel. Neither glue will stop the pair from shrinking as one panel. The split that follows the glue line in January is often the joint starving as the two boards pull. The split that ignores the glue line and runs down an arch is the board itself, usually a too-wet core or a fastener that pinned the width.

Rule joints on drop-leaf tables are the period answer for a flatsawn leaf that will shrink: a molded edge that hides a gap. A later factory that drops a straight butt and a metal hinge on a mahogany leaf will show a light line in winter. The 18th-century shop was not more moral. It had already lost arguments with heated rooms that were less dry than ours.

Look at the groove. If the panel can move, the cathedral can stay. If the panel is a prisoner, the rings will open a crack and tell you so. Figure 3 is the picture. Keep it in your head when a catalogue says “solid oak” and the edge is 0.6 mm of something on a fiber core.
