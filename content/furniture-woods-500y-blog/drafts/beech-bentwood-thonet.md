# Beech, steam, and the No. 14

In 1859 the Gebrüder Thonet put a café chair on the market that was six pieces of steamed beech, ten screws, two nuts, and a cane seat. The V&A’s early example (W.31-2011) is thin and exact; later No. 14s grew thicker. Philadelphia Museum of Art notes the shipping arithmetic: thirty-six dismantled chairs to a crate. Some fifty million are said to have been made by 1930 (V&A texts). The chair is famous. The wood is why the chair exists. *Fagus sylvatica* in Moravian and Austrian forests bent in iron molds after a steam bath, dried, and packed flat. Oak would not have done this at that price. Walnut would not have grown in those stands. Beech did.

## The wood that takes a radius

European beech and American beech (*F. grandifolia*) are close cousins in the shop. The *Wood Handbook* (FPL-GTR-190) calls American beech heavy, hard, strong, high in shock resistance, “highly suitable for steam bending.” It shrinks a lot and must be dried with care. It machines smoothly, turns, wears. Color runs from nearly white sap to reddish-brown heart, sometimes without a clear line. Little figure. Uniform texture. Janka about 1,300 lbf, specific gravity about 0.64 at 12 percent moisture — maple-and-oak country. Figure 4 places that hardness among the series’ ten anchors. Greatest lumber production, FPL notes, in the Central and Middle Atlantic States. European beech is the larger furniture story because the factories were there.

Rays on a quartered beech face are a small, even fleck — not oak’s mirrors. The wood looks dull until a finish wakes the pink. It has almost no outdoor durability. A bench left in weather is a short story. It moves. A wide panel is a risk. Frame-and-panel, or laminate, or stay narrow. Dry it thoroughly and let it sit at house humidity before glue-up. It will move after you think you are done. Glue holds. Nails hold if you predrill; the wood is hard.

Steam-bend radii are the industrial secret in plain sight. A straight-grained, defect-free stick, steamed until plastic, pulled around an iron mold, clamped, dried in the mold. Popular accounts of the No. 14 give factory times — steam, molds, about twenty hours drying around 70 °C `[CITE NEEDED: pin Ostergard or a factory manual for the temperature and hours]`. Thickness governs the tightest radius: shops talk in multiples of thickness, and beech will take a tighter loop than flatsawn oak and a far tighter loop than hard maple, which mostly refuses the box. The No. 14’s back is two loops; the legs are also bends. The usable radius is why the chair can be six pieces instead of a joined walnut carcass. A small shop can bend a chair part. A small shop cannot pretend a flatsawn beech slab will stay flat in a steam-heated room.

Michael Thonet began with laminated veneer bends in Boppard, then moved to solid steam-bent beech and to Koritschan in Moravia. Competitors (Kohn and others) used the same stands. Café chairs from Lisbon to Chicago are beech objects with local stains. Alvar Aalto’s laminates are birch. The idea of a bent seat is cousin to Thonet, not the same mill. The Finnish chapter takes that. What belongs here is the species credit: European beech made the café century possible.

## Before the café, and under the stain

Before the café chair, beech was already in the shop as a toolmaker’s wood: molding-plane bodies, workbench parts, mallets, anything that must wear smooth and take a precise cut. Medieval and early modern Europe used it for trenchers, bowls, cheap furniture, the insides of things. English shops in the eighteenth century put it under paint — the cheaper Sheraton and Hepplewhite lines, then the fancy-chair factories. It is not Macquoid’s oak and not his mahogany. It is the seat that had to be sold in quantity.

American beech never became a high-style show wood. Floors, tool parts, furniture frames, food-related wood (little taste). Some Windsor shops used it. It was never elm’s equal as a seat in the English sense, nor maple’s equal as a New England turning wood. Thonet is not a maple story. Maple would have turned some parts; it was not the steam-bent price point in Moravian forests.

Beech takes stain too well. Factory “walnut,” “mahogany,” and “cherry” chairs from the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries are often beech underneath. The Thonet red-brown is a finish, not a heartwood, and it is honest because the form is honest. A carved “Renaissance” parlor chair in stained beech is a costume. Chip the finish. The pore is not oak’s. The weight and the even texture remain.

The cane seat in the No. 14 ring is a different contract from a Shaker tape seat. Cane is woven through holes in a steamed beech hoop; tape weaves through maple rails. Both are seats you can renew. A later plywood or pressed-seat No. 14 is a factory revision. Look at the ring. The V&A early example is the thin type; hotel stock grew thicker because freight and abuse punish a slender bend.

The six pieces of an early No. 14 are worth naming because the count is the design. A circular seat ring; a front-leg hoop that is also the front rail; a rear-leg and back-loop assembly (or two loops, depending how you count the continuous bends); stretchers that are themselves bent sticks; cane in the ring. Later catalogs vary the count. The V&A object record is the place to look before you argue with a dealer `[CITE NEEDED: confirm the exact part list on W.31-2011 if you quote it as six]`. Screws through the bends replace the mortise. The radius of the back loops is the dimension that made the chair cheap: one mold, many sticks, no carved splat. Maple would have turned a pommel. It would not have given that loop at that price in those forests.

## Knockdown as industry, not campaign

Thonet’s screws and nuts are a knockdown, but not a campaign knockdown. Campaign fittings — brass corners, flush handles, interlocking chests — are a military-administrative style in mahogany and teak (the dockyard draft). Thonet’s knockdown is a shipping style: thirty-six chairs in a crate, assembled at the café with a screwdriver. The joint is a screwed socket, not a draw-bored mortise. The wood makes it possible because the bend replaced the joint. That is the industrial sentence. A restorer who glues a No. 14’s screwed joints as if they were eighteenth-century chairs has misunderstood the object.

Beech stayed the European chair wood through school seating, dining sets, pale postwar German and Czech factories, kitchen tables in a thousand flats. Some Danish work used it when oak was not the point. American taste called it “cheap imported chair.” That snobbery missed the Thonet achievement and the tool-wood quality. It correctly identified a lot of stained hotel furniture.

Steaming wants straight grain, no defects, adequate time — a bending-wood protocol, not a slab-table fantasy. A flatsawn beech stick with a cathedral on the face will break toward the bark side on a tight radius. Rift or a near-quartered stick is the bending stock. The glossary’s cut diagram is the same argument in a café factory. Beech is not CITES. European forests are managed; mast years and climate affect increment. American beech has beech bark disease in parts of the range `[CITE NEEDED: USFS current map]`. Neither is a rosewood emergency. Both can be exhausted locally if factories want only the large, clear stems.

Tool-wood beech — the plane body, the mallet, the workbench dog — is the other life that never got a café. It wears smooth. It takes a precise cut. It is why a restorer who only knows Thonet still meets *Fagus* in a molding plane. Food-related wood (little taste) is a third life: bowls, spoons, a butcher’s block in some European shops, though maple took more of that American job. The species is a utility that fashion occasionally noticed. The No. 14 is the notice that stuck.

## What to look for

Even texture, small rays, a pink-brown, stain that chips to pale. On a café chair: the bend, the screw, the cane, a radius that oak would not have given for the money. On an early No. 14: thin members, V&A’s W.31-2011 as the comparison. On a later hotel chair: thicker bends, the same screws. On a “walnut” parlor chair: pale wood under the chip. On a plane body: the right species in the right job.

Arkansas grows little commercial beech. A southern shop meets *F. grandifolia* as a tool wood or a shipped chair part, not as a Moravian forest. The chair most people in modern cities have sat on, without knowing, was this one. Name the tree. Then decide whether the job is a radius, a stain costume, or a wish.

Repairing a broken bend is not a mortise repair. The fiber is already plastic-set in a curve; a scarf in the middle of a loop is a visible confession unless you hide it under a later stain. Better to replace the stick and steam a new one to the old mold, or to a tracing of the surviving mate. Hide glue in a screwed Thonet socket is a misunderstanding. The screw is the joint. Cane replacement is ordinary: soak, weave, peg or glue the ends as the ring requires. Tape is the Shaker seat, not this one. Keep the seats on their woods. A No. 14 that has lost its cane and gained a plywood disc is a factory or a kitchen revision. Name it. Do not call the disc original 1859.
