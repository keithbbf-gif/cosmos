# Series Map

Wood fashion is not a single parade. London, Paris, Philadelphia, Boston, Charleston, Copenhagen, Kyoto, and Beijing ran on different clocks. Percy Macquoid’s four “Ages” (*A History of English Furniture*, 1904–08) still help a reader walk an English room. They do not describe an Arkansas shop, a Ming cabinet, or a Thonet café.

This map is a scaffold. Article slugs sit in parentheses.

## Before 1500 (roots only)

- Oak as the joinery wood of northern Europe: chests, settles, church fittings. Radial riven boards and early wainscot from the Baltic already moving west (Haneca et al. 2005; Salzman citations in KNOB wainscot literature). → `oak-european-and-american`, `quartersawn-versus-flatsawn`
- Walnut in Italy and France earlier than in England. → `walnut`
- Chinese hardwoods: huanghuali and zitan already in Ming scholar furniture. → `huanghuali-zitan`
- Boxwood and fruitwood for small work and carving. → `boxwood`, `fruitwoods`

## 1500–1660 — Macquoid’s Age of Oak

- English and Netherlandish oak: linenfold, draw-bored mortise and tenon, boarded chests. Baltic *wagenschot* mixed with home oak. → `oak-european-and-american`, `five-centuries-of-furniture-wood`
- Country pine and deal in Scandinavia and the Alps; painted surfaces hide the species. → `pine-country-furniture`, `baltic-deal-painted-pine`
- Yew and fruitwood in Welsh and English country chairs. → `yew`, `fruitwoods`
- Elm for seats that must not split. → `elm`, `windsor-mixed-wood`

## 1660–1720 — Age of Walnut (England); oak still in the farmhouse

- Restoration and William-and-Mary: walnut veneer, oyster and burl, seaweed marquetry, caned chairs. French and Dutch taste after 1660. → `walnut`, `marquetry-picture-of-trees`
- American black walnut in the colonies as a primary wood, not only a veneer. → `walnut`, `arkansas-hardwoods-500y`
- Olive, kingwood (“princes wood”), ebony stringing in Paris and Amsterdam. → `kingwood-tulipwood-ebeniste`, `rosewood-ebony-cites`
- The walnut blight years and frost in Europe push shops toward other woods. [CITE NEEDED for the often-repeated “walnut famine” of the 1720s — Bowett is cautious.]

## 1721–1770 — Mahogany becomes cheap enough to carve

- Naval Stores Act 1721 (in force 1722) removes duty on British West Indian timber. Jamaica mahogany in London and the outports (Bowett). → `mahogany-and-substitutes`
- Chippendale *Director* (1754): mahogany in the solid, not only as a skin. → `mahogany-and-substitutes`
- American shops: walnut, cherry, maple, yellow-poplar secondary wood. Southern heart pine in lesser rooms. → `cherry`, `maple`, `yellow-poplar`, `heart-pine-longleaf`
- Quartersawn oak does not go away; it just leaves the fashionable parlor. → `quartersawn-versus-flatsawn`

## 1770–1820 — Pale woods and the classical shop

- Satinwood, harewood (sycamore dyed), rosewood banding, painted decoration. Hepplewhite and Sheraton plates. Macquoid’s Age of Satinwood. → `satinwood`, `sycamore`, `biedermeier-pale-woods`
- Federal America: mahogany still the show wood; cherry and figured maple in New England and Pennsylvania. → `cherry`, `maple`
- French late ébénisterie: Riesener’s pictorial marquetry, then a turn toward plainer mahogany under Louis XVI and the Empire. → `marquetry-picture-of-trees`, `kingwood-tulipwood-ebeniste`

## 1815–1860 — Empire, Restauration, Biedermeier, Greek Revival

- Dark mahogany and rosewood in high Empire. → `mahogany-and-substitutes`, `rosewood-ebony-cites`
- Biedermeier: birch, pear, cherry, maple — pale, figured, local. → `biedermeier-pale-woods`, `birch`, `fruitwoods`
- American Empire and late Federal: crotch mahogany veneer over pine and poplar. → `yellow-poplar`, `plywood-veneers-twentieth` (veneer logic starts here)
- Windsor production industrializes; species stay specialized. → `windsor-mixed-wood`

## 1830–1900 — Bentwood, revival, and the piano

- Thonet steam-bent beech, No. 14 from 1859. → `beech`, `finnish-birch-aalto` (laminate cousin)
- Gothic and Renaissance revivals bring oak back in carved suites. → `oak-european-and-american`
- Rosewood and ebony on pianos, whatnots, and parlor sets. Brazilian *Dalbergia nigra* already under pressure. → `rosewood-ebony-cites`
- Black walnut’s American peak: Renaissance Revival, Eastlake. → `walnut`
- Chestnut as a quiet secondary wood — then the 1904 blight. → `chestnut-and-the-blight`
- Longleaf heart pine floors and some furniture in the South. → `heart-pine-longleaf`

## 1875–1915 — Arts and Crafts, Mission, fumed oak

- Morris, Voysey, Stickley, Limbert, Roycroft: oak again, often quartersawn, sometimes fumed. → `arts-and-crafts-oak`, `quartersawn-versus-flatsawn`
- Shaker communities still in maple, cherry, pine. → `shaker-woods`
- Early plywood seats and molded veneer already in factory chairs (V&A plywood history). → `plywood-veneers-twentieth`

## 1900–1945 — Pale modern, tropical faces, war

- Wiener Werkstätte, early modern: beech, walnut, painted wood.
- Aalto and Finnish birch laminate, 1930s. → `finnish-birch-aalto`, `birch`
- Teak in British colonial and campaign furniture; early Danish experiments. → `teak`
- Okoume aircraft ply and later decorative cores. → `lauan-meranti-okoume`
- Chestnut commercially gone; elm heading toward Dutch elm disease (U.S. 1930s). → `chestnut-and-the-blight`, `elm`

## 1945–1975 — Teak, rosewood, molded ply

- Danish modern: oak, teak, rosewood, some walnut. Wegner, Juhl, Mogensen, Jacobsen. → `danish-modern-woods`, `teak`
- Eames molded plywood (DCW, LCW, DCM) after wartime splints. → `plywood-veneers-twentieth`
- American blonde maple and walnut dining rooms; knotty pine dens. → `maple`, `pine-country-furniture`
- Tropical face veneers over cheap cores: lauan, meranti. → `lauan-meranti-okoume`
- Particleboard and early MDF enter casework. → `engineered-wood-honest`

## 1975–2000 — Substitutes, regulation, nostalgia

- African mahogany group (Khaya, sapele, utile) fills the “mahogany” tag. → `african-mahogany-sapele-khaya`
- Iroko and plantation teak as outdoor and kitchen fashion. → `iroko-teak-lookalikes`, `teak`
- Oak and pine country reproductions. → `oak-european-and-american`, `pine-country-furniture`
- *Swietenia macrophylla* to CITES Appendix II (2003, decided 2002). → `mahogany-and-substitutes`, `cites-lacey-buying`
- Ash still common in steam-bent and Mission-descended chairs — before the borer. → `ash`

## 2000–present

- Emerald ash borer, North America, 2002. → `ash`
- *Dalbergia* genus to Appendix II, 2017; Brazilian rosewood already Appendix I. → `rosewood-ebony-cites`
- Lacey Act plant amendments (2008) and APHIS Phase VII declarations (1 Dec. 2024) for more furniture HTS codes. → `cites-lacey-buying`
- Honest engineered wood in kitchens and flat-pack; veneered MDF as the default case. → `engineered-wood-honest`
- Domestic hardwoods (oak, walnut, cherry, maple, hickory) as a readable choice, not a nostalgia act. Arkansas and the southern hardwood belt sit in that present. → `arkansas-hardwoods-500y`, `reading-furniture-wood`

## How to use the map

Read `five-centuries-of-furniture-wood` first, then `reading-furniture-wood` and `quartersawn-versus-flatsawn`. After that, species chapters can be taken in any order. Style chapters (`windsor`, `shaker`, `arts-and-crafts`, `biedermeier`, `danish-modern`, `marquetry`) assume you have already met the woods they name.
