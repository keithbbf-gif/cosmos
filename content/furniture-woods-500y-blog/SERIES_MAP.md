# Series Map

Wood fashion is not a single parade. London, Paris, Philadelphia, Boston, Charleston, Copenhagen, and the Indian Ocean yards ran on different clocks. Percy Macquoid’s four “Ages” (*A History of English Furniture*, 1904–08) still help a reader walk an English room. They do not describe an Arkansas shop, a Ming cabinet, or a Thonet café.

This map is a scaffold. Draft slugs sit in parentheses. Figure 1 in `intro-500-year-timber` is the same story as a timeline.

## Before 1500 (roots only)

- Oak as the joinery wood of northern Europe: chests, settles, church fittings. Radial riven boards and early wainscot from the Baltic already moving west (Haneca et al. 2005; Salzman citations in KNOB wainscot literature). → `baltic-oak-renaissance`, `english-oak-great-furniture`, `glossary-grain-and-cut`
- Walnut in Italy and France earlier than in England. → `walnut-baroque`
- Yew and boxwood for small work, bows, and carving. → `yew-medieval-turnery`, `boxwood-inlay`

## 1500–1660 — Macquoid’s Age of Oak

- English and Netherlandish oak: linenfold, draw-bored mortise and tenon, boarded chests. Baltic *wagenschot* mixed with home oak. → `english-oak-great-furniture`, `baltic-oak-renaissance`, `intro-500-year-timber`
- Dutch shipping: Sound Toll, Zaan mills, the *kast* with a deal interior. → `dutch-golden-age-shipping`
- Navy demand for compass timber and masts already competing with the joiner. → `navy-oak-reserves`
- Country pine in Scandinavia and the Alps; painted surfaces hide the species. → `american-colonial-pine` (New World cousin), `white-pine-softwood-economy`
- Yew and fruitwood in Welsh and English country chairs. → `yew-medieval-turnery`

## 1660–1720 — Age of Walnut (England); oak still in the farmhouse

- Restoration and William-and-Mary: walnut veneer, oyster and burl, seaweed marquetry, caned chairs. French and Dutch taste after 1660. → `walnut-baroque`
- Queen Anne: cabriole, solid walnut, a thinner splat than oak allowed. → `walnut-queen-anne`
- American black walnut in the colonies as a primary wood, not only a veneer. → `walnut-baroque`, `walnut-queen-anne`
- The often-repeated “walnut famine” of the 1720s: Bowett is cautious. [CITE NEEDED]

## 1721–1770 — Mahogany becomes cheap enough to carve

- Naval Stores Act 1721 (in force 1722) removes duty on British West Indian timber. Jamaica mahogany in London and the outports (Bowett). → `mahogany-chippendale`
- Chippendale *Director* (1754; 3rd ed. 1762): mahogany in the solid, pierced splats. → `mahogany-chippendale`
- American shops: walnut, cherry, maple, yellow-poplar secondary wood. Southern and New England pine in lesser rooms. → `cherry-shaker`, `maple-birdseye-factory`, `american-colonial-pine`
- Quartersawn oak does not go away; it leaves the fashionable parlor. → `quartersawn-arts-crafts`, `glossary-grain-and-cut`

## 1770–1820 — Pale woods and the classical shop

- Satinwood, harewood (sycamore dyed), rosewood banding, painted decoration. Hepplewhite and Sheraton plates. Macquoid’s Age of Satinwood. → `satinwood-adam-style`
- Federal America: mahogany still the show wood; cherry and figured maple in New England and Pennsylvania. → `mahogany-federal-america`, `cherry-shaker`, `maple-birdseye-factory`
- Bombay teak frigates (*Trincomalee*, 1817) and campaign knockdowns. → `teak-colonial-dockyards`

## 1815–1860 — Empire, Restauration, Greek Revival

- Dark mahogany and rosewood in high Empire; crotch veneer over pine and poplar. → `mahogany-regency-empire`, `veneer-versus-solid`
- American Empire and late Federal: the same *Swietenia* on a different coast. → `mahogany-federal-america`, `mahogany-regency-empire`

## 1830–1900 — Bentwood, revival, and the piano

- Thonet steam-bent beech, No. 14 from 1859 (V&A W.31-2011). → `beech-bentwood-thonet`
- Gothic and Renaissance revivals bring oak and rosewood back in carved suites. → `rosewood-gothic-revival`, `english-oak-great-furniture`
- Rosewood and ebony on pianos, whatnots, and parlor sets. Brazilian *Dalbergia nigra* already under pressure. → `rosewood-victorian`, `ebony-inlay-keys`, `tropical-hardwood-peak-1900`
- Black walnut’s American peak: Renaissance Revival, Eastlake. → `walnut-baroque`
- Factory maple: birdseye veneer, gym floors, school desks. → `maple-birdseye-factory`
- Longleaf and white-pine commodity lumber; furniture as a grade inside the flood. → `white-pine-softwood-economy`

## 1875–1915 — Arts and Crafts, Mission, fumed oak

- Morris, Voysey, Stickley: oak again, often quartersawn, sometimes fumed. → `quartersawn-arts-crafts`
- Shaker communities still in maple, cherry, pine. → `cherry-shaker`, `maple-birdseye-factory`
- Early plywood seats and molded veneer already in factory chairs (V&A plywood history). → `plywood-and-core-stock`, `veneer-versus-solid`
- Tropical hardwoods still specified as ordinary luxury. → `tropical-hardwood-peak-1900`

## 1900–1945 — Pale modern, tropical faces, war

- Teak in British colonial and campaign leftover; garden and deck uses grow. → `teak-deck-and-garden`
- Aalto and Finnish birch laminate, 1930s. → `plywood-and-core-stock`
- Eames wartime splint, 1942, then molded ply chairs. → `plywood-and-core-stock`

## 1945–1975 — Teak, rosewood, molded ply

- Danish modern: oak, teak, rosewood, some walnut. → `teak-deck-and-garden`, `brazilian-rosewood-midcentury`, `indian-rosewood-exports`
- Padauk and other *Pterocarpus* as modernist accents. → `padauk-modernist-accents`
- Tropical face veneers over cheap cores. → `veneer-versus-solid`, `plywood-and-core-stock`

## 1973–2000 — Convention, substitutes, nostalgia

- CITES signed 1973; in force 1975. → `cites-1973-convention`
- *Dalbergia nigra* to Appendix I, 11 June 1992. → `brazilian-rosewood-midcentury`, `cites-dalbergia-2017`
- African “mahogany” group and plantation teak fill catalogue names. → `sustainable-teak-plantations`, `conservation-today`
- *Swietenia macrophylla* (neotropical populations) to Appendix II, in force 15 November 2003. → `cites-swietenia-permits`

## 2000–present

- Emerald ash borer, North America, 2002. → `ash-sporting-chairs`
- Madagascar *Diospyros* annotations, 12 June 2013. → `cites-ebony-africa`, `ebony-inlay-keys`
- *Dalbergia* genus to Appendix II, 2 January 2017; Annotation #15 revised CoP18 (2019). → `cites-dalbergia-2017`
- Lacey Act plant amendments (2008) and APHIS Phase VII declarations (1 December 2024). → `conservation-today`
- Reclaimed oak as a furniture fashion; kiln-dried quartered stock as the quieter alternative. → `reclaimed-oak-beams`, `quartersawn-arts-crafts`
- Domestic hardwoods (oak, walnut, cherry, maple, hickory) as a readable choice. Arkansas and the southern hardwood belt sit in that present. → `intro-500-year-timber`, `conservation-today`

## How to use the map

Read `intro-500-year-timber` first, then `glossary-grain-and-cut`. After that, species hours can be taken in any order. Cut and science chapters (`quartersawn-arts-crafts`, `flatsawn-panel-figure`, `rift-cut-flooring`, `moisture-wood-movement`, `janka-hardness-explained`, `density-weight-shipping`, `workability-hand-tools`, `veneer-versus-solid`) assume you have already met the woods they name. CITES chapters are not permits.
