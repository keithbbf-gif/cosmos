# Style Guide

This series talks to people who buy, specify, restore, or just stare at furniture and want to know what they are looking at. Bradley Brand works Arkansas hardwoods. The series teaches species behavior. It does not sell a line.

## Voice

Write as a person who has planed boards and stood in front of old pieces. Prefer names, dates, ports, and tool marks over general claims. A sentence may be short. A sentence may run if it is carrying a fact.

Speak to the reader as a peer. Do not coach. Do not cheerlead. Do not close with a moral.

First person is allowed when it is a shop observation (“the plane chatters on interlocked sapele”). It is not a diary.

## Banned phrasing

Do not use: delve, landscape (metaphorical), robust, leverage, unlock, cutting-edge, game-changer, “In today’s,” “It’s important to note,” Moreover, “Whether you’re,” “In conclusion,” “At the end of the day,” “rich tapestry,” “journey,” “elevate,” “empower,” “seamless,” “holistic,” “unpack,” “nuanced” as filler.

Do not open with a dictionary definition. Do not close with a recap list of “key takeaways.” If a list is a working table (Janka numbers, CITES appendices, Macquoid’s ages), keep it. Decorative bullet stacks are out.

No COSMOS. No AI. No process talk about how the draft was written.

## Facts and citations

Mechanical numbers (specific gravity, Janka, shrinkage) come first from the USDA Forest Products Laboratory *Wood Handbook* (FPL-GTR-190, 2010; FPL-GTR-282, 2021 where it supersedes). Secondary compilations (Wood Database, Hoadley) may be used if the FPL figure is named.

Trade history needs a named source: Bowett, Macquoid, Haneca, port books, a museum object record. If the fact is traditional shop knowledge and not pinned, mark `[CITE NEEDED]`.

Conservation claims (CITES appendix, Lacey Act phase, IUCN status) need a dated instrument. Species names use binomials on first mention in an article.

Do not invent a voyage, a price, or a royal commission. If a story is too good and the paper is thin, say so.

## Structure of an article

1. Open on an object, a port, a mill, or a failure — something a reader can see.
2. Name the tree(s).
3. Say how the wood behaves: density, grain, movement, tools, finish.
4. Walk the furniture history, including colonial and imperial extraction where it is true.
5. Say what is scarce, regulated, or substituted now.
6. Leave the reader with one way to look at a piece in the room.
7. End with sources and cross-links, not a sermon.

Target 1,400–2,200 words of body text. Frontmatter and source lists do not count toward the band.

## Brand

Bradley Brand / BBF may appear where Arkansas or southern hardwoods are the actual subject (oak, ash, cherry, hickory, pine). One or two sentences. No product names, no “our craftsmen,” no call to action. Preferred photo fills from the BBF shop are listed in `PHOTO_CAPTIONS.md`, not in the article body as ads.

## Names and spelling

American spelling in the running text. Historical British terms stay British when they are terms: wainscot, deal, mahogany-bay, ébéniste. Use the furniture-trade name first, then the binomial.

Quartersawn, flatsawn, riftsawn: one word each after first explanation.

## Numbers

Janka in lbf at 12% moisture, with SI in parentheses if you give one. Specific gravity on the ovendry-mass / 12% volume basis unless you say “basic SG.” Dates: 1721, not “the early eighteenth century,” unless the year is honestly unknown.

## Photos

Caption in `PHOTO_CAPTIONS.md`. Prefer public-domain plates, museum collection images with rights stated, and (as fills) BBF oak and pine shop shots. Do not paste copyrighted magazine interiors.
