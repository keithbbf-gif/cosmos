# Shaker woods

The Shaker list is short on purpose. Maple for the parts that must stay true and take a turning. Cherry for the face that will darken in the meetinghouse light. Pine for the broad, painted or clear boards that do not need to be heroes. Occasional butternut, occasional walnut, a little figured maple. John Kassay’s *Book of Shaker Furniture* and Rieman and Burks’s *Complete Book* are the measured catalogues. The theology of simplicity has been written to death. The species list is the furniture fact.

## Why these trees

New England and New York communities sat in the white pine and hard maple forest. Kentucky’s Pleasant Hill sat in a different mix and the woods shift — more walnut and cherry, more southern secondary woods. The Shakers bought and sold timber; they were not only forest hermits. They still preferred a small set because a small set can be finished with one vocabulary: a clear wash, a light stain, a red or yellow paint on pine, a chair that can be hung on a peg.

Maple chairs: the slat-back, the tape seat, the pommel. Cherry stands and cases. Pine cupboards and built-ins. The famous oval boxes are often maple sides with pine tops, fingers swallow-tailed, copper tacks — a mixed-wood object like a Windsor, quieter.

## What they did not use

Mahogany appears when they made goods for sale to “the world” or when a piece is not as Shaker as the dealer hopes. Heavy Renaissance Revival walnut is not their hour. Tropical inlay is not their hour. The absence is a specification.

## Finish

Milk paint, distemper, later industrial paints, clear varnish, oil. The orange “Shaker finish” of late reproductions is often a toner that the historic piece did not wear. Cherry was allowed to darken. Maple was allowed to stay pale. Pine was painted or left, depending on the room.

## The reproduction problem

20th-century factory “Shaker” is a stain and a tape seat on mixed hardwoods. Some is decent maple. Some is beech or birch from a chair plant. Look at the turning and the underside. A Hancock-quality cherry stand has thin, exact legs and a top that moved and was allowed to.

Do not distress. The originals wore.

## What to look for

Maple, cherry, pine. Tape. Pegs. A cupboard interior as careful as the face. Lightness. A reproduction’s thick legs.

The series includes Shaker woods as a comparative frame so the American hardwood chapters have a shop that chose them as a set, not as leftovers from mahogany.

## Mount Lebanon, Hancock, Pleasant Hill

The United Society of Believers furnished rooms the way they furnished theology: a short list, repeated until it became a vocabulary. Mount Lebanon, New York — the central ministry — ran a chair industry that sold to “the world.” The slat-back chair with a tape seat and a pommel finial is that industry’s object. Hancock, Massachusetts, and Sabbathday Lake, Maine, and Canterbury, New Hampshire, and Enfield, Connecticut, sat in the white-pine and hard-maple forest. Kentucky’s Pleasant Hill sat in a different mix: more walnut, more cherry, more southern secondary woods. The Shakers bought and sold timber. They were not only forest hermits. They still preferred a small set because a small set can be finished with one vocabulary: a clear wash, a light stain, a red or yellow paint on pine, a chair that can be hung on a peg rail.

John Kassay’s *The Book of Shaker Furniture* (1980) is the measured-drawing book. Timothy Rieman and Jean Burks’s *The Complete Book of Shaker Furniture* (1993) is the catalogue raisonné habit: community, wood, date range, construction. Read them before you believe a dealer’s “Shaker.” A lot of country slat-backs are just country slat-backs.

The oval box is the mixed-wood object that sits beside the Windsor in this series. Maple sides, steamed and fingered in a swallow-tail, copper tacks, a pine top and bottom. The maple takes the bend. The pine stays light. The tacks are the fasteners you can see. A box in all walnut is a different community or a later fancy. A box in sapele from a weekend class is a shape.

## Why these numbers

Hard maple (*Acer saccharum*): SG 0.63, Janka 1,450 lbf (FPL-GTR-190). The chair wood. It turns a pommel that stays crisp. It stays true if it was dried. It takes a tape seat’s holes without crumbling. Figured maple appears; it is not required. The theology of simplicity has been written to death. The Janka number is why the slat does not fuzz.

Black cherry (*Prunus serotina*): SG 0.50, Janka 950 lbf. The *Wood Handbook* still says machining is “very good” and the wood is “very dimensionally stable after drying.” Heartwood goes from light to dark reddish brown in the sun — the meetinghouse window is a finish. Gum flecks stay. A Hancock-quality cherry stand has thin, exact legs and a top that moved and was allowed to. The orange “Shaker finish” of late reproductions is often a toner the historic piece did not wear.

Eastern white pine (*Pinus strobus*): SG about 0.35, Janka about 380 lbf. The cupboard wood, the built-in, the painted case. Soft enough to dent, cheap enough to be a wall of storage, clear enough in old-growth boards that a door can be one leaf. Second-growth pine is knotty. Paint was not a cover-up. It was the specification for a lesser room or a wet one.

Occasional butternut (*Juglans cinerea*), occasional black walnut (*J. nigra*), a little yellow-poplar as a secondary wood — the list grows at Pleasant Hill and in pieces made for sale. Mahogany appears when they made goods for the world or when a piece is not as Shaker as the dealer hopes. Heavy Renaissance Revival walnut is not their hour. Tropical inlay is not their hour. The absence is a specification.

## Pegs, tape, and the underside

A Shaker cupboard interior is as careful as the face: shelves that fit, a pin that holds, no slush of leftover stain. That is a shop habit you can still read. The peg rail is not a decorative row. It is how a chair leaves the floor so the floor can be cleaned. Look at the wear on the peg and at the hole in the chair finial.

Chair tape — cotton or wool, later a synthetic — is a seat, not a brand. A 20th-century factory “Shaker” is often a stain and a tape seat on mixed hardwoods. Some is decent maple. Some is beech or birch from a chair plant that also made hotel stock. Look at the turning. Look at the underside. A pommel that is fat and vague is not a Mount Lebanon pommel. Kassay’s plates are the comparison.

Milk paint, distemper, later industrial paints, clear varnish, oil. Cherry was allowed to darken. Maple was allowed to stay pale. Pine was painted or left, depending on the room. Do not “age” a new piece with a chain. The originals wore.

## Shop behavior

If you build in this vocabulary, use the list. A “Shaker” table in sapele is a confusion. A Shaker-style table in Arkansas cherry and maple is a geographic shift of the same idea — Pleasant Hill already made that shift, and the walnut belt of Kentucky is not a Massachusetts maple grove. Name the community you are thinking of, even if you are only thinking of it.

Keep the legs thin. The historic stands are lighter than the reproduction houses want them. A thick leg is the tell of a factory that insured itself against freight damage.

Do not distress. Do not add a fake peg rail to a piece that never hung. Do not stain maple to cherry and call it Hancock.

When you repair an oval box, match the swallow-tail and the tack. A steel nail in place of a copper tack is a different object. Hide glue is the historic adhesive for a lot of the case work; later shops used what the hardware store sold. Ask before you soak a joint.

The Mount Lebanon chair lists — numbers stamped or written under a slat, a #7 rocking chair as the famous size — are commercial marks, not monastery poetry. Communities sold chairs by the dozen. A surviving label or a stencil on the underside is worth more than a dealer’s adjective. Sabbathday Lake still holds a living community and a furniture memory; Hancock is a museum village. Walk the rooms before you copy a finial from a photograph.

## What the series needs this frame for

The American hardwood chapters — maple, cherry, pine, walnut — need a shop that chose them as a set, not as leftovers from mahogany. The Shakers are that shop. They sat in the forest this series keeps recommending to a southern designer: local woods, a short list, a finish that can be renewed, a chair you can hang on a wall. Fashion went to *Swietenia* and to teak. They mostly did not. That is the furniture fact. The rest is theology, and it has been written.
