# Five centuries of furniture wood

In 1904 Percy Macquoid opened *A History of English Furniture* by splitting the craft into four piles of timber. Oak until 1660. Walnut until 1720. Mahogany until 1770. Satinwood after that, into the Regency. The scheme still hangs on gallery labels. It is a useful lie.

It is useful because English fashionable furniture did change color and species in that order, and a buyer walking a country-house corridor can still feel the shift under the hand: the blackened joined oak, the faded oyster walnut, the red-brown chair that wants to be called Chippendale, the yellow card table with a harewood border. It is a lie because farmhouses never got the memo, because Paris ran ahead of London and Philadelphia ran beside it, because Chinese hardwood cabinets were already old when Henry VIII was buying wainscot, and because the wood in a piece is often not the wood on the label.

This series starts here so the later chapters have a room to sit in.

## What a furniture wood is

A furniture wood is a species a shop will spend time on: dry it, saw it to a face, joint it, and show it or hide it. Ship timbers, mine props, and firewood overlap the list. They are not the same list. Beech made Thonet’s café empire and also laundry pegs. Yellow-poplar lined the drawers of Philadelphia high chests and also the cores of cheap veneered suites. The difference is intention and finish, not a moral ranking of trees.

Three behaviors decide whether a wood stays in the shop.

Density and hardness. The USDA Forest Products Laboratory reports side hardness (Janka) and specific gravity at 12 percent moisture in the *Wood Handbook* (FPL-GTR-190, 2010, tables 5–3a and following). White oak sits near 1,360 lbf and SG 0.68; black cherry near 950 lbf and 0.50; eastern white pine near 380 lbf and 0.35. Those numbers explain why a pine tavern table dents under a belt buckle and a maple counter does not. They do not explain fashion. Fashion is why cherry was a secondary wood in one decade and a show wood in the next.

Movement. Wood shrinks across the grain as it dries, more tangentially than radially. Quartersawn stock moves less in width and shows rays in oak. A door that was flatsawn in 1710 and hung in a heated apartment in 2010 will tell you this without a hygrometer. The sawing chapter in this series is not a technical aside. It is half of period style.

Workability and finish. Interlocked grain (elm, sapele) tears. Silica (teak) dulls edges. Open pores (oak, ash) drink filler. Fine texture (maple, boxwood) takes a polish that looks like light trapped under glass. Shops chose woods for the tool in hand as much as for the color in the book.

## Trade is the hidden style

English oak furniture of 1550 is not simply “English oak.” Dendrochronology on panels and chests keeps pointing to the south-eastern Baltic — the hinterlands of Gdansk, then Königsberg and Courland — for the slow-grown, radially split boards sold as wainscot (*wagenschot*). Haneca and colleagues (2005) showed how art-historical oak can be provenanced by ring patterns. London joiners mixed that imported stuff with home oak. A linenfold chest can be a North Sea object even when the carving looks local.

Walnut’s English fashion after 1660 rode Dutch and French taste and a veneer technology that wasted less of a scarce, figured log. American black walnut, *Juglans nigra*, did a different job in the colonies: it was a primary solid wood, wide and available, and it still is in the central hardwood belt.

Mahogany’s English story is a statute. Adam Bowett has spent a career killing the old connoisseur’s date (Macquoid’s 1733, Cescinsky’s 1748) for the duty change. The Naval Stores Act of 1721, in force 1722, took the import duty off British West Indian timber. Jamaica wood that had not been worth the freight became, in Bowett’s reading of the port books, cheap enough to use in the solid. Thomas Ripley, advising the Admiralty on a new building, called mahogany cheaper than deal or wainscot when Navy ships carried it freight-free. The rococo splat did not invent mahogany. A tax cut and a sugar island did.

That island story is not a romance. Logging gangs, enslaved labor, and a forest that could be mined like a seam — Bowett’s thesis and the Jamaica trade paper sit on those records. A dining table can be beautiful and still be a receipt from an extractive system. The series will not sand that off.

## The other clocks

Macquoid stopped, more or less, when satinwood and painted decoration had their London hour. Furniture did not.

In the German-speaking lands after the Napoleonic wars, Biedermeier shops made a virtue of pale birch, pear, and cherry. In Denmark a century later, oak and teak and a little rosewood became a national export look. In the United States the Shakers kept a short list — maple, cherry, pine — while Renaissance Revival factories drowned parlors in black walnut. Gustav Stickley and the English Arts and Crafts men dragged quartersawn oak back into the polite room and fumed it with ammonia so the tannins went brown.

Michael Thonet’s No. 14 chair, 1859, is a beech object. Alvar Aalto’s Paimio seating is a birch-laminate object. Charles and Ray Eames’s plywood chairs are a glue-and-veneer object that learned its compound curves on a wartime splint. None of these fit an “Age of Mahogany.” They are still furniture woods.

China’s classical hardwood furniture — huanghuali (*Dalbergia odorifera* and related trade names) and zitan (often *Pterocarpus santalinus*) — belongs in a 500-year survey because European and American collectors spent the late twentieth century treating those pieces as a parallel canon. The trees and the shops are older than that fashion.

## Softwoods were never only poor

Country furniture in pine is not a failed hardwood piece. Scots pine and Baltic deal furnished northern Europe in paint. Eastern white pine furnished New England in paint and in clear varnish. Southern longleaf, the heart pine of floors and some case work, is a different creature: dense latewood, old-growth resin, a naval-stores economy. The Crown’s Broad Arrow reserved large white pines for masts (Albion, *Forests and Sea Power*, and the Massachusetts charter tradition). Furniture shops used what the navy did not take, and later what the cut-over left.

Paint changes the ethics of identification. A blue cupboard from the Connecticut River valley may be pine, poplar, or a mix. The species still matters for repair. It did not always matter for the first owner.

## Substitutes are the modern style

By 1900 Cuban mahogany (*Swietenia mahagoni*) was already a memory in commercial sizes. Honduran mahogany (*S. macrophylla*) took the name, then African *Khaya* and *Entandrophragma* (sapele, utile, sipo) took the sales tag. Teak from Burma and later from plantations filled a mid-century dream of oil and weather. Brazilian rosewood (*Dalbergia nigra*) went onto Appendix I of CITES; the rest of *Dalbergia* followed onto Appendix II in 2017. Big-leaf mahogany’s neotropical populations entered Appendix II in 2003.

The honest chapter in this series is the engineered one. Particleboard, medium-density fiberboard, and veneered panels are furniture woods in the only sense that counts: they are what most people now own. They move less than solid flatsawn oak. They fail in water. They can be named without apology. They cannot be named as walnut.

U.S. buyers of imported wood goods also live under the Lacey Act as amended in 2008 and under APHIS’s phased declaration rules (Phase VII, 1 December 2024, sweeping more furniture codes). A species chapter that skips the permit is a period piece.

## How to use what follows

Read the sawing essay next, then the short method piece on how to look at a board. After that, skip. If you own a cherry chest, read cherry. If you are about to specify “mahogany” for a dining table, read mahogany and then sapele. If you like Stickley, read oak and the Arts and Crafts chapter together.

Arkansas sits in this history without needing a costume. White oak, red oak, cherry, walnut, hickory, ash, and pine are the same genera that filled American shops in 1800. The difference is second-growth rings, kiln schedules, and a legal paper trail. Bradley Brand works those hardwoods. The point of the series is to say what the wood will do — swell, dent, darken, take a finish, outlive a fashion — so a designer or a buyer can choose with open eyes.

Macquoid gave us a staircase. The house has more rooms than he drew.
