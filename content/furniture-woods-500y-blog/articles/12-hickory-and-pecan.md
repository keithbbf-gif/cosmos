# Hickory and pecan

Hickory is the American wood that furniture fashion walked around. Toolmakers did not. A struck adze handle, a skid pole, a ladder rung, the bow of a chair that had to come back to shape — *Carya* is the hardest of the ordinary eastern hardwoods (shagbark hickory Janka about 1,820 lbf, SG about 0.72, FPL-GTR-190). Pecan, *Carya illinoinensis*, is hickory’s sweeter-named cousin, a bit more furniture-facing, still a tough, ring-porous wood with a grain that looks like a brown ash.

The 500-year story is mostly tools, wheels, and vernacular seating, then a 20th-century dining-room try, then the rustic and “pecan finish” factory years. A designer who wants a wood that will dent less than oak and still show a bold grain is looking here. A designer who wants easy planing is not.

## The woods

True hickories (shagbark *C. ovata*, mockernut, pignut, shellbark) are the impact woods. Pecan-hickories (pecan, bitternut, water, nutmeg) are slightly less famous for shock but still hard. The *Wood Handbook* treats them as a group for lumber. Heartwood is pale to reddish brown; sapwood is wide and white. Ring-porous, like ash. Rays are not oak’s mirrors.

Pecan as a furniture name in Texas and the lower Mississippi valley is both the nut tree and the cabinet wood. Factory “pecan” of the 1960s–80s was often a finish color on mixed hardwoods. Real pecan has a varied, sometimes streaked heart.

Hickory’s shrinkage is high. Dry it like you mean it. Movement in service is not polite. Frame-and-panel still applies.

## Workability and finish

Tools suffer. Carbide helps. Hand-plane work is a workout; the wood can be straight and still hard. It nails poorly without holes. It bends when steamed — the other half of the handle-and-chair story.

Grain is open. Stain will stripe. Clear finishes on sap-and-heart mixed boards give a two-tone that rustic shops sell and formal shops hide. Filling pores is a Victorian idea that sits oddly on hickory.

The wood has a reputation for splitting in the ring if the piece is thin and the climate dry. Design around that, or stay with thicker sections.

## History in furniture

Native and colonial tool culture used hickory constantly. Furniture used it for parts: Windsor bows and spindles in some shops (alongside oak and ash), rustic chairs, southern vernacular. It was not the Philadelphia show wood. Mahogany, walnut, and cherry took the parlor.

19th-century factories made some hickory seating — porch rockers, hoop chairs, the Indiana and Appalachian “hickory” furniture that used bark-on or peeled rods in the Adirondack and Old Hickory vein (Martinsville, Indiana, from the 1890s) `[CITE NEEDED: company date from a catalogue]`. That is a furniture tradition: rustic, resort, national-park lodges. It is not Chippendale.

20th-century American dining rooms in “pecan” were a Mediterranean-revival and then a suburban style: heavy suites, dark finish, carved backs. Some of it was pecan. Some was hickory. Some was printed grain. The better pieces have the weight.

Contemporary shops use hickory for floors, for rustic tables, for steam-bent parts, for people who want an American hardwood that is not oak again. Live-edge hickory is a hard live edge.

## Compared with ash and oak

Ash bends as well and is easier to work. Oak quarters with theater. Hickory out-hardens both and out-shocks them. If the job is a rung that will be stood on, hickory. If the job is a show panel of rays, oak. If the job is a pale restaurant table, ash (while the trees last).

Pecan sits closer to furniture than shagbark: a little more color variation, a little more southern identity. A pecan table from the lower Mississippi is a place story.

## Shop notes

Green hickory is a different animal from dry. Chairmakers who work green (post-and-rung, some rustic) use the shrink to lock joints. Dry hickory furniture is kiln furniture. Do not mix the methods without a plan.

Sawmills dislike hickory a little — hard on saws, the logs are heavy. You pay for that.

## Conservation

Hickories are not CITES species. They are part of the oak-hickory forest type under pressure from land use, gypsy moth / spongy moth, and the usual high-grading. Pecan orchards are food first; furniture pecan is often wild or mixed stands. Ordinary forestry. No appendix.

## What to look for

Extreme hardness for a pale ring-porous wood. A rustic chair with hickory rods. A 1970s suite with “pecan” on the label and a veneer edge. Tool handles in the barn that outlived the tools. On a new floor: dent resistance you can feel next to a pine board.

Arkansas grows hickory and pecan in the same hardwood mix as oak. A shop that works those woods is working the tool-forest, not the mahogany pier. The series includes them so a buyer can recognize the grain and not mistake it for dirty ash, and so a designer can specify shock resistance on purpose.

On a rustic chair: hickory rods. On a 1970s suite: the word “pecan” on the label and a veneer edge. In the barn: handles that outlived the tools. On a new floor: dent resistance. Fashion walked around this wood for most of five hundred years because it is contrary to plane and loud in the grain. Specify it when you want shock resistance on purpose, not when you want an easy walnut substitute. The tool-forest is still a furniture forest if the ticket says the binomial.
