# Yellow-poplar

Open a Philadelphia high chest and look at the drawer sides. The wood is often a pale tulip-streaked board with a hint of green or purple in the heart. That is *Liriodendron tulipifera*, yellow-poplar, tulip poplar, tulipwood in the American shop (not the Brazilian *Dalbergia* tulipwood of the ébéniste). It is the cheap-good wood of the eastern United States: light, stable enough, wide, paint-loving, carvable. High-style mahogany would have been impossible at the scale of 18th-century production without a secondary wood like this.

The *Wood Handbook* lists it at specific gravity 0.42 and Janka 540 lbf — chestnut’s neighbor, twice the hardness of eastern white pine, half of cherry. It is a hardwood that behaves like a well-brought-up softwood.

## The wood

Diffuse-porous, fine texture, heartwood that can be brown, green, purple, or streaked — “tulip” in the old name. Sapwood is creamy and wide. The tree grows straight and tall in the Appalachians and the Ohio valley. Boards can be wide without knots. That fact made carcases.

It is not a poplar in the *Populus* sense (aspen, cottonwood). The name is a settler’s error that stuck. Cottonwood is weaker and fuzzier. Specify *Liriodendron*.

Durability outdoors is poor. Durability as a painted porch column is a paint story, not a heartwood story.

## Workability and finish

It planes, carves, and takes a gouge without oak’s fight. Grinling Gibbons is a lime story; American architectural carving is often poplar. It glues. It nails. It dents.

Paint is the historic finish on show surfaces when poplar was primary: blanket chests, interiors, the backcountry piece that never saw mahogany. Bare poplar oxidizes toward brown and can look like a poor walnut if stained. The green heart, once called “whitewood” or sold separately, scares some clients and pleases people who like a mineral streak.

Veneer: rotary-cut poplar is a core and a crossband. A lot of 19th- and 20th-century plywood starts here.

## The hidden century

From the late 17th century through the Federal period, Mid-Atlantic shops used yellow-poplar as the default secondary wood. New England preferred white pine for many of the same jobs. The South mixed pine, poplar, and sometimes cypress. Hurst and Prown’s southern furniture catalogue and the Winterthur literature make this geography a dating and attribution tool. A “New England” highboy with poplar drawer sides wants an argument.

Poplar was also a primary wood: painted chests, kitchen cupboards, the interiors of houses. It never sat in Macquoid’s London ages. It sat in the American shop every day.

Empire and Victorian factories used poplar as the ground for mahogany and rosewood veneer. The crotch on the front; the tulip on the inside. When the veneer chips, the green shows. That chip is a lesson.

## 20th century

Millwork, paint-grade doors, core stock, inexpensive furniture sold as “whitewood.” School projects. The wood is still one of the most useful in an American hardwood yard. It is unfashionable as a show surface except in painted work and in a few contemporary shops that like the mineral color.

## Shop notes

Weight tells poplar from pine (heavier) and from maple (much lighter). No resin canals. Diffuse pores. Green heart is not decay.

It moves more than the old chestnut it sometimes replaced as a secondary wood. Still kinder than beech. Still wants floating panels in wide widths.

Do not sell it as basswood. Basswood is softer and even more the carver’s friend. Do not sell it as mahogany. The stain will not survive a drawer opening.

## Conservation

*Liriodendron* is a common, fast, managed American hardwood. Not CITES. The conservation issue is ordinary land use. Using poplar for hidden parts instead of a threatened tropical is a correct substitution that shops have been making, without virtue language, for two hundred years.

## What to look for

Green or purple streaks. Wide, clear paint-grade boards. Drawer sides in a mahogany case. A veneered Empire bureau with a chip. Painted Pennsylvania chests. On a new paint-grade kitchen: the right wood if the door is solid and the finish is a film.

Arkansas and the southern Appalachians grow tulip poplar in quantity. A shop that uses it for dustboards and for painted work is in the 18th-century habit. The series includes it so buyers stop calling every pale interior “pine” and so designers remember that show woods need a servant.

## The Philadelphia habit

Open a high chest labeled Philadelphia, 1760–1780, and the drawer sides are a dating tool before they are a romance. Winterthur studies and the Mid-Atlantic literature treat yellow-poplar as the default secondary: dustboards, drawer bottoms, the backboards that never saw a parlor lamp. New England shops in the same decades often reached for eastern white pine for those jobs. The South mixed yellow pine, poplar, and sometimes cypress. Hurst and Prown’s *Southern Furniture 1680–1830* makes that mix an attribution argument, not a mood. A “New England” highboy with tulip-streaked drawer sides wants a better story than a dealer’s map.

The tree is *Liriodendron tulipifera*, a magnolia relative, not a *Populus*. Settlers called it poplar because the leaf and the pale wood suggested one. Cottonwood and aspen are weaker and fuzzier. Specify the binomial on a mill ticket if the yard also sells “poplar” as mixed whitewood. Anatomy still holds: diffuse-porous, fine texture, no resin canals, a heart that can run brown, green, or purple in the same log.

FPL-GTR-190 puts yellow-poplar at specific gravity 0.42 and Janka 540 lbf at 12 percent moisture — chestnut’s hardness neighbor, about twice eastern white pine, about half of black cherry. Shrinkage green to ovendry is on the order of 4.6 percent radial, 8.2 percent tangential, 12.7 percent volumetric in the handbook tables. That is more movement than the old chestnut it sometimes replaced as a lining. It is still kinder than beech. Wide tops still want floating panels. A glued-down breadboard on a poplar lid is a future crack.

Durability outdoors is poor. A painted porch column that lasts is a paint story. Bare heart in the weather is a short story.

## Paint, gouge, and the green that scares a client

It planes. It takes a gouge without oak’s fight. Grinling Gibbons is a lime story; American architectural carving — mantel drops, capitals, the applied ornament on a Federal chimney breast — is often poplar or basswood. Dull tools bruise it. Sharp ones leave a surface that paint covers without a ring telegraph. White pine carves too, and then the resin and the latewood bands show through a thin film. Poplar is the even cut with a mineral surprise.

Paint is the historic finish when poplar was primary: blanket chests, kitchen cupboards, the backcountry case that never saw mahogany. Bare wood oxidizes toward brown. A stain can fake a poor walnut until a drawer opens and the green heart laughs. Some clients want that streak. Some call it stain and ask you to bleach it. It is not decay. It is the tree.

Empire and Victorian factories used poplar as the ground for mahogany and rosewood veneer. Crotch on the front; tulip on the inside. When the veneer chips, the green shows. That chip is a lesson in what a show wood costs and what a servant wood does. Rotary-cut poplar is still a plywood core and a crossband. A lot of nineteenth- and twentieth-century panel product starts in this genus.

## How a shop tells it from the neighbors

Weight tells poplar from eastern white pine (heavier) and from hard maple (much lighter). No resin canals on the end grain. Diffuse pores, not the spring ring of oak or chestnut. Basswood is softer, paler, even more even; a fingernail decides faster than a speech. Sweetgum can share a mineral look and then move worse. Do not sell poplar as mahogany. The stain will not survive a drawer opening. Do not sell it as basswood to a carver who wanted *Tilia*. The gouge will tell them you lied.

Clamps bruise the arris. Use pads. Glue holds. Nails hold. Screws want a pilot in thin rails or you split the end. The dust is fine and pale. A wide paint-grade board from the southern Appalachians or the Ohio valley can still come clear of knots; that fact made carcases when mahogany was the skin.

School shops still buy it because it is cheap and kind to a block plane. Millwork shops still buy it because a paint-grade door does not need cherry’s luster. Contemporary makers who leave the mineral color as a show face are in a minority taste. They are not wrong. They are asking the wood to do a job Macquoid never wrote down.

## Southern rooms and the servant wood

Hurst and Prown’s Williamsburg pieces show poplar where a northern reader expects pine: cupboard interiors, the hidden members of a huntboard, painted case work that the parlor mahogany never had to explain. Coastal shops mixed it with yellow pine. Piedmont shops used what the cove and the bottomland grew. The point for a buyer is not a moral ranking. It is a repair fact. A split poplar dustboard wants poplar, not a southern yellow pine with a loud latewood band that will telegraph through a thin bottom.

A designer specifying hidden parts in 2026 can still write *Liriodendron* without a CITES appendix or a virtue paragraph. Shops have been making that substitution, without a speech, for two hundred years — first against the cost of show woods, later against the scarcity of tropical names. The conservation issue is ordinary land use. The labeling issue is calling every pale interior pine.

Arkansas yards stock tulip poplar as a local and regional hardwood. A painted cupboard in it is an eighteenth-century habit in a second-growth ring. The green heart is not a flaw. It is how you know you are not looking at pine.
