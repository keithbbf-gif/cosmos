# Danish modern woods

Hans Wegner’s Wishbone (CH24) is usually oak. Finn Juhl’s more sculptural chairs are often teak, sometimes walnut. A rosewood sideboard from a Copenhagen factory in 1962 is a third luxury. Danish modern is not “teak furniture.” It is a small set of woods — oak, teak, rosewood, walnut, a little beech — used with joinery that shows and a finish that is oil or a thin film. ## Oak

Northern oak, often pale, sometimes smoked, the chair wood of Wegner and of many dining sets that were never photographed. It connects Danish work to a local forest and to the Arts and Crafts idea of a visible hardwood, without fuming as a religion. A 1950s oak chair is as Danish as a teak one.

## Teak

The tropical member, the export look, the suburban American living room. Solid and veneer. The teak chapter covers the tree and the plantations. Danish factories made teak a Scandinavian word, which it is not botanically. They also made some of the best teak furniture in the record.

## Rosewood

Sharper, darker, rarer, now a CITES object. The rosewood chapter is required reading before you ship a 1960s sideboard. Danish rosewood was often *Dalbergia latifolia* and related, not only Brazilian. Still a listed genus.

## Shop notes

If you specify “Danish modern” in a new commission, pick a wood. Oak is the local-ethic choice in a hardwood region. Teak is a paper-trail choice. Rosewood is a no for new work. Walnut is the American cousin.

Arkansas oak and walnut sit comfortably in this vocabulary. Teak does not grow there. The style does not require it.

## What to look for

A Wishbone in oak, a teak sideboard with a thin oil, a rosewood grain that is not a print, dowels and tenons, a copy’s rounded nothing.

## Copenhagen, the Guild, and a chair with a name

The Copenhagen Cabinetmakers’ Guild exhibitions, from the 1920s into the 1960s, are the public record of the shops: Kaare Klint’s teaching at the Furniture School, then the generation that magazines flattened to a honey brown. Hans J. Wegner’s Round Chair — “The Chair” after the 1960 Nixon–Kennedy broadcast sat it in American living rooms — is usually oak or walnut, sometimes teak, a joined crown and a woven seat. The Wishbone, CH24, designed 1949 and put into production by Carl Hansen & Søn in 1950, is usually oak: a steam-bent back, a Y of splats, a paper-cord seat. Finn Juhl’s Chieftain (1949) and the more sculptural chairs are often teak, sometimes walnut, a carver’s attitude toward a slab. Børge Mogensen’s hunting chair and his oak cases are the sober line. Arne Jacobsen’s wood is often the secondary story beside the metal and the leather; the Series 7 is not this chapter. Poul Kjærholm is even less a wood story.

Danish modern is not “teak furniture.” It is a small set of woods — oak, teak, rosewood, walnut, a little beech — used with joinery that shows and a finish that is oil or a thin film. The shops did not flatten it. The magazines did.

## Oak as the local ethic

Northern oak, often pale, sometimes smoked, the chair wood of Wegner and of many dining sets that were never photographed. European oak (*Quercus robur*, *Q. petraea*) and, in later and export work, American white oak. The *Wood Handbook* puts white oak at SG about 0.68, Janka about 1,360 lbf. The Wishbone in oak is the object to keep in your head when a catalogue tries to sell you “Danish” as a color.

Smoked oak — ammonia or a related fume, a darker brown — appears in later and in revival work. It is chemistry the Stickley chapter already explained. It is not a different tree.

## Teak as the export look

*Tectona grandis*, the tropical member, the suburban American living room of 1962. Solid and veneer. FPL puts teak’s properties “generally on par with those of U.S. oaks”; Janka is commonly cited near 1,070 lbf, SG near 0.55–0.66 depending on plantation versus old-growth. Silica dulls tools. Oil in the fiber wants wiping before glue and finish. Indoor teak in the 1950s was oiled to stay honey. Outdoor teak goes silver if you let it.

The logs, in the years the Copenhagen factories wanted them, came out of Burma (Rangoon as the old port name), Thailand, and later Indonesian plantations. Danish factories made teak a Scandinavian word, which it is not botanically. They also made some of the best teak furniture in the record. The teak chapter covers the tree, the imperial deck, and the plantation. This chapter covers the oil can on a Copenhagen bench.

Paper-trail teak is the only new teak a specifier should write. Plantation, country, certificate number. A “solid teak suite” at a funny price is the meranti problem in a honey costume.

## Rosewood, and the 2017 line

A rosewood sideboard from a Copenhagen factory in 1962 is a third luxury. Sharper, darker, rarer, now a CITES object. Danish rosewood was often East Indian *Dalbergia latifolia* and related, not only Brazilian *D. nigra*. The whole genus went onto Appendix II on 2 January 2017, Annotation #15 later revised so finished musical instruments and small finished products (wood of the listed species up to 10 kg per shipment) would not freeze concerts and jewelry boxes. A rosewood dining table is not a jewelry box. *D. nigra* was already Appendix I.

Densities in the *Dalbergia* group often sit above 0.8–1.0. They dull tools, they can be oily to glue, they take a polish that looks like stone. Dust is a sensitizer. The rosewood chapter is required reading before you ship a 1960s sideboard. Antique pieces already in the country are usually legal to own. They can be complicated to export. Ask.

## Walnut, beech, and the mix

American black walnut (*Juglans nigra*, SG 0.55, Janka 1,010 lbf) appears in some lines, especially those aimed at the U.S. Beech (*Fagus sylvatica*) appears in lesser chairs and in parts. Teak-on-oak mixes exist: a teak face and oak where the shop saved. That is honest if you can see it. A teak print on a beech carcase is the engineered chapter.

## Oil, copies, and the underside

Oil that can be renewed is part of the object. A thick polyurethane on a Wegner is a mistake you can see from the door. The paper cord on a Wishbone is a seat, not a brand; it wears and is meant to be replaced. Look at the joint: a dowel and a tenon that meet as if someone cared, or a rounded nothing from a copy mill.

Taiwanese and later global copies used rubberwood (*Hevea brasiliensis*), printed veneer, and a shape. Rubberwood is a plantation utility hardwood. It is not oak. It is not teak. Look at the underside. Look at the tool marks on the inside of a rail. Carl Hansen’s and the other named shops’ model woods are in the catalogues. Fiell’s *Scandinavian Design* is a picture book with a spine; use it to find the object, then go to the maker’s sheet for the species.

## Shop behavior

If you specify “Danish modern” in a new commission, pick a wood. Oak is the local-ethic choice in a hardwood region. Teak is a paper-trail choice. Rosewood is a no for new work. Walnut is the American cousin.

Arkansas oak and walnut sit comfortably in this vocabulary. Teak does not grow there. The style does not require it. A new dining chair in quartersawn white oak with a paper-cord seat and a thin oil is a correct sentence. A new “Danish” credenza in foil-wrapped MDF with a honey print is a catalogue crime.

When you restore, clean and re-oil before you sand. The thin film is the object. A white ring from a glass is often recoverable. A belt sander is how a 1962 teak top becomes a 1990s one. Keep the original oil can’s logic: wipe, wait, wipe again. Do not pour a gel stain into the pore and call it teak.
