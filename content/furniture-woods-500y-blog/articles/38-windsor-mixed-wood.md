# Windsor chairs

A Windsor is a mixed-wood machine. The seat is a thick plank, often elm in England, often pine or poplar or elm in America. The legs are turned, often maple in America, ash or beech in England. The bow and the spindles want a wood that bends and does not snap — ash, oak, hickory, sometimes yew. Nancy Goyne Evans’s *Windsor-Chair Making in America* is the long proof that the label “maple Windsor” is a half-sentence. The chair is a system of jobs.

This chapter is a comparative frame. The species chapters (elm, ash, maple, oak, pine, yew, hickory) are the parts list.

## Why mix

No one wood wants every job. Elm’s interlocked seat does not split when you drive four tapered legs and a row of spindles into it. Maple turns a crisp bead and wears at the foot. Ash takes a steam bend for a continuous-arm bow. Oak will bow too, and quartersawn oak was used. Pine seats are light and cheap and split more readily; American shops used them anyway, and painted.

Green woodworking — shrinking a dry tenon in a wetter seat, or the reverse depending on the shop — is part of the vernacular. Factory Windsors of the 19th century used drier stock and glue and paint.

## England and America

English Windsors from the Thames valley and the Midlands are a type with elm seats as a cliché that is often true. Yew-wood Windsors are the collector’s prize (yew chapter). Painted beech and ash chairs sit in the cheaper line.

American Windsors, from Philadelphia outward, become a national industry: Rhode Island, New York, New England, later the Midwest. Evans tracks woods by part and by region. Paint is common. Fancy Windsors pick up bamboo turnings and color. The species mix remains a job mix.

## Fashion

18th-century garden and tavern seating; then the polite American parlor; then the 19th-century factory; then the Colonial Revival; then the craft revival of the late 20th century (Dunbar and others) that taught a new generation to rive oak and steam ash. The Windsor never needed Macquoid’s mahogany age. It outlived it.

## Shop notes

Do not replace an elm seat with a flatsawn oak plank and expect the same life. If you restore, match the job, not the romance.

Paint layers are history. Stripping a Philadelphia Windsor to “show the maple” is a 1960s idea.

A new craft Windsor in white oak and maple is a correct American sentence. A new “Windsor” in stained rubberwood from a container is a shape.

## What to look for

Seat thickness, interlocked grain, turned legs that do not match the seat species, a bent bow, paint in the spindle holes, a wedge through a through-tenon. Weight. A pine seat’s dents.

Arkansas woods — oak, ash, hickory, maple if shipped or found, pine, elm if any remains — are a complete Windsor palette. A southern shop can build the system without a tropical crate. That is the point of a mixed-wood chapter in this series.

## High Wycombe, and a Philadelphia shop in 1765

English Windsors gather around the Thames valley and the Chiltern beech woods, with High Wycombe as the factory town of the 19th century. The cliché is an elm seat, ash or yew bow, ash or beech sticks, turned legs in beech or fruitwood. It is often true. Victor Chinnery’s oak book is not the Windsor book, but the English vernacular chair literature still sits thin in American shops `[CITE NEEDED: name the English monograph the editor wants beside Evans]`. Yew-wood Windsors — the bow a ribbon of orange heart and pale sap — are the collector’s prize. The yew chapter is the anatomy. Painted beech and ash chairs sat in the cheaper line and still do in the leftovers of pub rooms.

In Philadelphia, by the 1740s and 1750s, the same job-mix had become an urban export. Joseph Henzey, the Tracys of Connecticut (Ebenezer Tracy’s shop in Lisbon is the named New England case), and a long list Evans tracks by account book and by wood sample, made chairs that went south and west in coasting vessels. The seat might be eastern white pine, poplar, or elm. The legs were often hard maple. The spindles and the bow wanted hickory or oak or ash — whatever would rive and bend. Paint was not an apology. Philadelphia green, a mustard, a black, a red — the chair was a painted object. Fancy Windsors of the early 19th century picked up bamboo turnings and more color. The species mix remained a job mix.

Nancy Goyne Evans’s *Windsor-Chair Making in America* (2006) is the long proof that “maple Windsor” is a half-sentence. She sorts woods by part and by region. Forman’s earlier seating book (*American Seating Furniture, 1630–1730*) is the prehistory: joined chairs and board chairs before the stick chair takes the tavern.

## Why each wood, in numbers

No one wood wants every job. That is the whole chapter.

Elm (*Ulmus* spp.): interlocked grain, a seat that does not split when you drive four tapered legs and a row of spindles into it. The *Wood Handbook* puts American elm at SG about 0.50 and Janka about 830 lbf at 12 percent — softer than oak, tough rather than hard. Dutch elm disease (U.S. introduction in the 1930s) emptied the urban elm. English elm seats of the 18th century are a different forest that also thinned. A restorer who puts a flatsawn red-oak plank in place of an elm seat has changed the physics.

Hard maple (*Acer saccharum*): SG 0.63, Janka 1,450 lbf (FPL-GTR-190). It turns a crisp bead and wears at the foot. American shops used it for legs because the lathe likes it and the floor does not erase it in a year. Soft maple will turn. It will not wear the same.

Ash (*Fraxinus americana*, and European *F. excelsior*): white ash at SG 0.60, Janka 1,320 lbf. Ring-porous, pale, the textbook steam-bend. A continuous-arm bow in ash is the American sentence. Oak will bow too; quartersawn oak was used; hickory will bow and come back like a tool. Shagbark hickory sits at Janka about 1,820 lbf, SG about 0.72 — the hardest of the ordinary eastern hardwoods. Spindles in hickory can be thinner than spindles in oak for the same nerve.

Pine seats (*Pinus strobus* in the northeast): SG about 0.35, Janka about 380 lbf. Light, cheap, they dent and they split more readily than elm. American shops used them anyway, and painted. Poplar (*Liriodendron tulipifera*) is the southern and mid-Atlantic cousin of that cheap seat: a servant wood, as in 1760.

Green woodworking — a drier tenon in a wetter seat, so the seat shrinks onto the leg — is part of the vernacular. The reverse, or a fully dry joint with glue, appears as shops industrialized. Factory Windsors of the 19th century used drier stock and glue and paint. Michael Dunbar and the late-20th-century craft revival taught a new generation to rive oak and steam ash and to argue about moisture again. Use Dunbar as practice, not as an archive.

## Paint, wedges, and the 1960s mistake

Paint layers are history. Pigment in the spindle hole, a drip under the seat, a wear-through on the crest that shows an earlier green — those are the object. Stripping a Philadelphia Windsor to “show the maple” is a 1960s idea. The maple was not the face. The paint was the face.

Through-tenons on the legs, wedged from above, are a tell if the seat is thick enough. A wedge in a different wood is common. A seat that is too thin for the tradition is often a later replacement or a factory economy.

Weight is a tell. A pine-seat chair feels like a pine-seat chair. An elm seat has a duller heft. A container “Windsor” in stained rubberwood has a slick turnery and no job-mix at all: one species trying to be four. Look at the underside. Look at the bow: steam-bent long grain, or sawn from a wide board and hoping.

## Shop behavior

If you restore, match the job, not the romance. Do not replace an elm seat with a flatsawn oak plank and expect the same life. Oak will split along the ring if you bore a row of spindle holes across the growth like a dotted line. Quartered oak is kinder. Elm is kinder still.

If you build new, a craft Windsor in white oak and maple is a correct American sentence. Hickory spindles and an oak bow are a correct American sentence. A continuous arm in ash is a correct American sentence. Arkansas woods — oak, ash, hickory, maple if shipped or found, pine, elm if any remains — are a complete palette. A southern shop can build the system without a tropical crate.

Steam-bending is not a mystery and is not a weekend. The stick wants straight grain. A run-out bow is a broken bow. The box wants a thermometer you believe. Spring-back is a number you learn on scrap.

Do not stain a new maple leg to “look old” and then sell it as a 1790 Philadelphia chair. The turning profile and the tool marks will argue with you. Evans is full of profiles. Use them.

## Fashion without mahogany

18th-century garden and tavern seating; then the polite American parlor; then the 19th-century factory; then the Colonial Revival; then the craft revival. The Windsor never needed Macquoid’s mahogany age. It outlived it. That is why this series gives it a chapter among the style frames: it is the mixed-wood machine that kept running while fashion went to Jamaica, to Burma, and to a printed lauan.
