# CITES, Lacey, and a clean buy

A dining table is a tree with a paper trail, or it is a tree without one. This chapter is for the buyer and the specifier, not for a lawyer. The living references are Species+ (CITES), the U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service’s timber pages, and USDA APHIS’s Lacey Act declaration pages. Listings and annotations change. Check the date.

## CITES in furniture language

Appendix I: commercial trade in wild specimens is not a furniture plan. Brazilian rosewood (*Dalbergia nigra*) is the famous I. Some ebonies and other woods join it.

Appendix II: trade is allowed with permits and a non-detriment finding. Big-leaf mahogany (*Swietenia macrophylla*, neotropical populations) went on II in 2003 (in force 15 November 2003), typically for logs, sawn wood, veneer, plywood — Annotation #6. Finished furniture may sit outside that annotation; the mill that imported the sawn wood does not. The whole *Dalbergia* genus went on II on 2 January 2017, Annotation #15, later revised so finished musical instruments and small finished products (wood of the listed species up to 10 kg per shipment) would not freeze concerts and jewelry boxes. A rosewood dining table is not a jewelry box.

Appendix III: a country asks others to help control a species. Some cedars and others have sat here. Always look up the current appendix.

Annotations decide whether a finished chair needs a permit. They are the fine print. Guitar companies learned this the hard way (Taylor’s public CITES note is a clear industry walkthrough; verify against the text). Furniture companies have less excuse.

## Lacey, 2008 and Phase VII

The Lacey Act (16 U.S.C. §3371 et seq.), amended by the Food, Conservation, and Energy Act of 2008 §8204, makes it illegal to trade plants taken in violation of U.S. or foreign law. It is the first comprehensive U.S. ban of that kind on illegal wood. Importers file a declaration: scientific name, country of harvest, quantity, value. Enforcement of declarations was phased. Phase VII, 1 December 2024, extends declarations to remaining plant-product HTS codes that are not 100 percent composite, including more furniture (APHIS).

Due care is the standard. A certificate on a website is not automatically due care. FSC is a useful risk tool. It is not a finding that Lacey has been met (APHIS and FSC both say versions of this).

Composite exemptions and special-use designations exist for some mixed or unidentifiable cases. They are not a blanket for a solid “mahogany” table.

## What a designer can actually do

Write binomials on the specification. *Quercus alba*, United States. *Tectona grandis*, plantation, country, certificate number. *Swietenia macrophylla* only with the permit path in the file.

Prefer domestic hardwoods when the job allows: oak, walnut, cherry, maple, hickory, ash (while it lasts), pine. The Arkansas chapter is that preference without a sermon.

For tropicals: old furniture already in the country, or new wood with boring paperwork. If the price of a “solid rosewood suite” is funny, the wood is funny.

For engineered goods: still ask the face species and the core species. Lauan cores have a forest. Foil has a name.

## Antiques

Antique *Dalbergia* and *Swietenia* pieces are usually legal to own. They can be complicated to export. Ask before you ship a 1960s rosewood sideboard to another country. Appendix I objects are the sharpest case.

## What this series will not do

It will not issue a permit. It will not update itself when CoP20 changes an annotation. It will not treat FSC as a halo. It will not treat domestic wood as automatically innocent (stolen walnut trees exist). It will say: names, countries, papers, edges.

## What to look for

A binomial on an invoice. A harvest country. A Species+ printout in a project file. An APHIS declaration if you import. An edge that matches the name. A crate that says only “wood.”

The 500-year story ends, for a buyer, in this dull chapter. The interesting trees are in the other chapters. The dullness is the point. Fashion without a paper trail is how *Swietenia* and *Dalbergia* got to the appendices.

## A crate on a dock, and a dining table in a drawing

A container lands at Long Beach or Savannah. The packing list says “wood furniture.” The HTS code is a furniture code. Until Phase VII, a lot of those codes did not require a Lacey declaration. On 1 December 2024 they did, unless the product is 100 percent composite or sits in a remaining exemption APHIS still lists. The Federal Register notice of 31 May 2024 is the dated instrument. USDA APHIS’s Lacey Act declaration pages are the living ones. Check the date. This chapter will not update itself when the next phase or the next Conference of the Parties moves a line.

## CITES, said as furniture

CITES is a trade convention. Species+ is the lookup. The U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service’s timber pages are the permit office in American language.

Appendix I: commercial trade in wild specimens is not a furniture plan. Brazilian rosewood (*Dalbergia nigra*) is the famous I. Some ebonies and other woods join it. A new dining table in *D. nigra* is a fantasy or a crime.

A specifier who writes “*Dalbergia latifolia*, finished sideboard, 80 kg” has written a permit problem. A specifier who writes “*Quercus alba*, United States, quartersawn” has written a dining table.

TRAFFIC and Chatham House’s five-taxa paper is the trade-analysis neighbor of this chapter. It is not a permit. It is why the permits exist.

## What a designer can actually put on a sheet

Write binomials. *Quercus alba*, United States. *Juglans nigra*, United States. *Prunus serotina*, United States. *Tectona grandis*, plantation, country, certificate number. *Swietenia macrophylla* only with the permit path in the file. *Shorea* spp. as a core only if you know the harvest country and you are willing to defend due care.

Prefer domestic hardwoods when the job allows: oak, walnut, cherry, maple, hickory, ash (while the dry stock lasts), pine. The Arkansas chapter is that preference without a sermon.

For tropicals: old furniture already in the country, or new wood with boring paperwork. If the price of a “solid rosewood suite” is funny, the wood is funny. If the hangtag says mahogany and the pore looks like *Shorea*, the lauan chapter already told you.

For engineered goods: still ask the face species and the core species. Lauan cores have a forest. Foil has a name. CARB / TSCA Title VI is an emission stamp, not a harvest stamp. You want both conversations on the same purchase order.

## Antiques, exports, and the sideboard that cannot fly

Antique *Dalbergia* and *Swietenia* pieces are usually legal to own in the U.S. They can be complicated to export. Ask before you ship a 1960s rosewood sideboard to another country. Appendix I objects are the sharpest case. A 1880 Brazilian rosewood piano is a living-room object and a paperwork object at the border. Pre-convention certificates and antique annotations exist; they are not a postcard you write yourself. USFWS will tell you. A dealer’s shrug will not.

Within the country, a 1962 Danish rosewood credenza can be bought and sold. Take a photograph of the grain. Keep the invoice. If you later want to send it to London, the file starts then, not at the airport.

## Shop behavior

Keep a Species+ printout in the project file, dated. Keep the harvest country on the invoice. If you import, keep the APHIS declaration. If you do not import, keep the mill’s chain-of-custody sheet anyway. An edge that matches the name is the object-level check: tyloses in white oak, the oily pore of teak, the tight diffuse face of maple, the printed cathedral that does not turn the corner.

A crate that says only “wood” is a refusal. A salesperson who says “it’s all mahogany” is a refusal. A funny price on a listed genus is a refusal.
