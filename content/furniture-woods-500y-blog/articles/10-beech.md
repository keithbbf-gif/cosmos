# Beech

In 1859 the Gebrüder Thonet put a café chair on the market that was six pieces of steamed beech, ten screws, two nuts, and a cane seat. The V&A’s early example (W.31-2011) is thin and exact; later No. 14s grew thicker. Some fifty million are said to have been made by 1930. The chair is famous. The wood is why the chair exists. *Fagus sylvatica* in Moravian and Austrian forests bent in iron moulds after a steam bath, dried, and packed flat. Oak would not have done this at that price. Walnut would not have grown in those stands. Beech did.

## The wood

European beech and American beech (*F. grandifolia*) are close cousins in the shop. The *Wood Handbook* calls American beech heavy, hard, strong, high in shock resistance, “highly suitable for steam bending.” It shrinks a lot and must be dried with care. It machines smoothly, turns, wears. Color runs from nearly white sap to reddish-brown heart, sometimes without a clear line. Little figure. Uniform texture. Janka about 1,300 lbf, SG about 0.64 at 12 percent — maple-and-oak country.

European beech is the larger furniture story because the factories were there. It steams, it stains (the Thonet red-brown is a finish, not a heartwood), it is pale under a clear coat. It has almost no outdoor durability. A beech bench left in weather is a short story.

Rays in beech are visible on a quartered face as a small, even fleck — not oak’s mirrors. The wood can look dull until a finish wakes the pink.

## Workability and finish

Beech is a toolmaker’s wood: plane bodies, moulding-plane stocks, workbench parts, mallets. It wears smoothly and takes a precise cut. In furniture it is the same: chair parts, frames, kitchen tables in Central Europe, turned members.

It moves. A wide beech panel is a risk. Frame-and-panel, or laminated, or stay narrow.

It takes stain too well. Factory “walnut,” “mahogany,” and “cherry” chairs from the late 19th and early 20th centuries are often beech underneath. The Thonet stain is honest because the form is honest. A carved “Renaissance” chair in stained beech is a costume.

Glue holds. Nails hold if you predrill; the wood is hard.

## Before Thonet

Beech was a medieval and early modern utility wood in Europe: trenchers, bowls, cheap furniture, the insides of things. English shops used it for painted chairs in the 18th century — Sheraton and Hepplewhite designs in the cheaper line, the “fancy chair” factories later. It is not the Age of Oak and not the Age of Mahogany. It is the age of the seat that had to be sold in quantity.

American beech grew in the eastern third of the country. FPL notes greatest lumber production in the Central and Middle Atlantic States. It never became a high-style show wood. Floors, tool parts, furniture frames, food-related wood (it has little taste). Some Windsor shops used it. It was never elm’s equal as a seat in the English sense, nor maple’s equal as a turning wood in New England.

## The bentwood century

Michael Thonet began with laminated veneer bends in Boppard, then moved to solid steam-bent beech and to Koritschan in Moravia. The No. 14, designed 1859, is the type: circular seat, two back loops, legs that are also bends. Philadelphia Museum of Art’s notes emphasize the shipping: thirty-six dismantled chairs to a crate. The industrial story is beech’s story — a species that grew where the factory could be built, that bent, that was light enough to ship, cheap enough to undercut joined walnut.

Competitors (Kohn, later others) used the same forests. Café chairs from Lisbon to Chicago are beech objects with local stains.

Alvar Aalto’s laminates are birch; the bentwood idea is cousin to Thonet, not the same mill. The Finnish chapter takes that.

## 20th century to IKEA’s ancestors

Beech remained the European chair wood: school seating, dining sets, the pale postwar German and Czech factories. Some Danish work used beech when oak was not the point. It is a kitchen-table wood in a thousand flats.

American taste associated beech with “cheap imported chair.” That snobbery missed the Thonet achievement and missed the tool-wood quality. It correctly identified a lot of stained hotel furniture.

## Shop notes

Dry beech thoroughly and keep it in the house’s humidity before glue-up. It will move after you think you are done.

Steaming: follow a bending-wood protocol (straight grain, no defects, adequate time). Thonet’s factory times — heat to steam, moulds, twenty hours drying around 70 °C in popular accounts of the No. 14 — are industrial. A shop can bend chair parts without a factory. A shop cannot pretend a flatsawn beech slab will stay flat in a steam-heated room.

## Conservation

Beech is not a CITES species. European beech forests are managed; mast years and climate affect increment. American beech has beech bark disease in parts of the range `[CITE NEEDED: USFS current map]`. Neither is a rosewood-level emergency. Both are ordinary woods that factories can exhaust locally if they only want the large, clear stems.

## What to look for

Even texture, small rays, a pink-brown, stain that chips to pale. On a café chair: the bend, the screw, the cane. On a “walnut” parlor chair: the weight and the pore (or lack of oak pore). On a plane body: the right wood in the right job.

A 500-year series cannot treat beech as a footnote to oak. The chair most people in modern cities have sat on, without knowing, was beech.
