# Marquetry

Marquetry is a picture whose pigments grew. A saw, a knife, a bundle of dyed and natural veneers, a ground of oak or deal or poplar, hide glue, hot sand for a shadow, an engraving tool and a mastic for a vein. The Wallace Collection’s notes on Riesener are exact: a flower of a dozen pieces, a trellis of stringing, knife marks where a leaf breaks a border. The brown you see on a 1780 secrétaire now is not the garden it was. Dyes fade. Some pink tulipwood goes quiet. The technique does not.

This chapter is a comparative frame for the woods already met: box, holly, walnut, kingwood, tulipwood, satinwood, pewter, brass, turtle in Boulle, straw in poorer work, later celluloid in sad copies.

## Kinds

Marquetry: pictorial or floral veneers assembled like a puzzle. Parquetry: geometry — cubes, diamonds, the tumbling-block floors of a table top. Inlay: a recess cut into a solid and a piece let in (Federal holly in mahogany). Boulle: brass and turtle (and pewter, and ebony) sawn together and swapped — a première-partie and contre-partie. Oyster veneer: transverse slices, not a picture but a related luxury of the walnut age.

English seaweed marquetry of the 1690s is fine, dark, acanthus-like, often on walnut. Dutch and Flemish floral marquetry is a garden. French pictorial work is the court. American Federal inlay is a quieter, often linear art. Islamic and Indian inlaid furniture are other schools; this series notes them and does not pretend to cover them.

## Woods as colors

Pale: box, holly, maple, satinwood, bone. Dark: ebony, ebonized pear, walnut burl. Red: cherry, some dyed woods, padauk in later work. Green: dyed sycamore (the famous faded greens). Purple: kingwood, plum. The ébéniste’s palette chapter is the shopping list. Roubo describes the shop.

Sand-shading: dip the edge of a leaf-shaped veneer in hot sand, get a burn that reads as volume. Engraving: cut a vein, fill it. None of this is a printer’s trick. It is a wood trick.

## Grounds and time

The picture is as stable as the ground and the glue. A deal or oak ground that moves will split a face. Central heating has cracked more marquetry than bad taste. Protein glue lets go in damp. A later PVA repair is a conservation event.

Do not sand a picture. Do not “oil it back to life” with a dark polish that fills the engraving. Photograph, support, a specialist.

## Law

A new floral panel in *Dalbergia* tulipwood and kingwood is a CITES object. Antique marquetry is the ethical picture. Holly and walnut still make a Federal fan without a permit.

## What to look for

A flower that has escaped its panel, a sand-shaded edge, a fade from green to brown, oysters on a William-and-Mary box, holly stringing on a Baltimore table, a Boulle clock. A laser-cut “marquetry” from a gift shop: even, dead, often paper.

The series ends its style frames here because marquetry is the place all the minor woods were doing a job at once. A buyer who can read a Riesener flower can read a kitchen edge.

## A flower on a secrétaire, 1780

Jean-Henri Riesener’s pictorial marquetry — the Wallace Collection’s notes are the exact public version — is a flower of a dozen pieces, a trellis of stringing, knife marks where a leaf breaks a border. The brown you see on a 1780 secrétaire now is not the garden it was. Dyes fade. Some pink tulipwood goes quiet. A green sycamore leaf goes brown. The technique does not. Stand in front of the piece and count the woods before you count the ormolu.

André-Jacob Roubo’s *L’Art du menuisier* (1769–1775) is the shop manual: the saw, the donkey, the bundle of dyed and natural veneers, the ground, the hot sand, the engraving tool, the mastic. Paris is the court school. André-Charles Boulle, a century earlier, had already made the metal-and-turtle version: brass and turtle (and pewter, and ebony) sawn together and swapped — a première-partie and a contre-partie. English seaweed marquetry of the 1690s — Gerrit Jensen’s circle, the William-and-Mary walnut age — is fine, dark, acanthus-like. Dutch and Flemish floral marquetry is a garden. American Federal inlay is a quieter, often linear art: holly in mahogany on a Baltimore table, a fan, a string. Islamic and Indian inlaid furniture are other schools; this series notes them and does not pretend to cover them.

Oyster veneer — transverse slices of walnut, laburnum, olive — is not a picture. It is a related luxury of the walnut age: a cut across the branch that makes a ring like a shell. Parquetry is geometry: cubes, diamonds, the tumbling-block floors of a table top. Inlay is a recess cut into a solid and a piece let in. Marquetry is a puzzle assembled on a sheet and then laid. Keep the words apart. Dealers do not.

## Woods as a palette

Pale: boxwood (*Buxus sempervirens*), holly (*Ilex*), maple, satinwood, bone. Dark: ebony (*Diospyros*), ebonized pear, walnut burl. Red: cherry, some dyed woods, padauk in later work. Green: dyed sycamore maple (*Acer pseudoplatanus*) — the famous faded greens of French work. Purple: kingwood (*Dalbergia cearensis*), plum. The ébéniste’s palette chapter is the shopping list. Tulipwood (*D. decipiens* and kin) is the pink stripe. None of these is a stain catalogue. They are trees, or they are trees plus a dye.

Sand-shading: dip the edge of a leaf-shaped veneer in hot sand, get a burn that reads as volume. Engraving: cut a vein, fill it with mastic. None of this is a printer’s trick. It is a wood trick. A laser-cut “marquetry” from a gift shop is even, dead, often paper. The sand-shaded edge is the tell you want. The fade from green to brown is the tell that the piece lived.

Boxwood and holly are the pale line-woods because they are fine, hard, and take a cut. The boxwood chapter and the holly-in-the-Federal-shop habit are the anatomy. Walnut is the dark ground of the 1690s. Mahogany is the dark ground of the 1780s. Oak or deal or poplar is the hidden ground under the picture.

## Grounds, glue, and central heating

The picture is as stable as the ground and the glue. A deal or oak ground that moves will split a face. Hoadley’s movement numbers — tangential shrinkage in oak on the order of 8–10 percent from green to ovendry, radial less — are why an 18th-century shop used a quartered or a framed ground when it could, and why a 20th-century apartment with the heat on splits a flower at the border. Protein glue (hide) lets go in damp and can go brittle in dry. A later PVA repair is a conservation event. It does not reverse with the same heat and moisture as hide.

Do not sand a picture. The veneer is thin. A leaf is a leaf. Do not “oil it back to life” with a dark polish that fills the engraving. Photograph, support, a specialist. ## Law on the palette

A new floral panel in *Dalbergia* tulipwood and kingwood is a CITES object. The whole genus went onto Appendix II on 2 January 2017. Brazilian rosewood, if someone still tries it as a ground or a flower, is Appendix I. Antique marquetry is the ethical picture: the trees are already cut, the dyes have already faded, the object is already a museum or a living room. Export is a different question from ownership. Holly and walnut still make a Federal fan without a permit. Boxwood is not a *Dalbergia*. Dyed sycamore is a maple.

Ivory and turtle are not woods and are their own legal and ethical wall. This chapter names them because Boulle and some Federal work used them. It does not recommend a new supply.

## Shop behavior

If you commission a new picture, write the binomials. Holly, walnut, maple, cherry, pear: a Federal fan or a simple floral that does not need a permit. If the designer wants kingwood and tulipwood, the file needs the permit path. If the designer wants a lasered paper on MDF, that is a poster. Charge poster money.

When you restore, do not replace a faded green with a bright new dye and call it original. The fade is the 1780–2020 sentence. Infill in kind, slightly quieter than the old, is the conservation habit. A dark wax rubbed into every joint is how a picture becomes a brown rectangle.

Look for a flower that has escaped its panel — a lift at the border, a sand-shaded edge still readable, oysters on a William-and-Mary box, holly stringing on a Baltimore table, a Boulle clock with a contre-partie still paired somewhere in the literature. A gift-shop “marquetry” chessboard with even squares and a glossy film is parquetry’s distant cousin, often stained birch.

The series ends its style frames here because marquetry is the place all the minor woods were doing a job at once. A buyer who can read a Riesener flower can read a kitchen edge: which leaf is a tree, which leaf is a print.
