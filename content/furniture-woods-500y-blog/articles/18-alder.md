# Alder

Red alder (*Alnus rubra*) is the Pacific Coast’s paint-grade and stain-grade hardwood: light, even, a little like a soft cherry when finished, a little like birch when left pale. European alder (*A. glutinosa*) is an older furniture and clog and pile wood, a riverside tree whose timber turns from white to orange when you cut it and then to a dull brown. Neither alder furnished a court style. Both furnished a lot of chairs and a lot of kitchen doors, and American factory catalogues in the late 20th century made “alder” a bedroom-suite word.

## The wood

Red alder: the *Wood Handbook* puts it near SG 0.41 and Janka 590 lbf — between poplar and cherry, closer to poplar. Fine, even texture. Heart and sap not strongly parted. It takes stain with a willingness that made it a cherry impersonator in western factories. It is not durable outdoors. It is not a structural oak.

European alder is similar in hardness, famous for underwater piles (Venice’s folklore and some fact `[CITE NEEDED: a measured durability note]`) and for smoking, clogs, and cheap furniture. The fresh-cut color change is a shop tale every mill knows.

## Workability and finish

Alder machines easily. It dents. It glues. It is a dream for painted millwork and a temptation for stained “cherry” doors. The blotch is milder than cherry’s, the hardness lower, the pores still diffuse.

A clear finish on red alder is a warm, quiet brown-pink. It will never have cherry’s luster or oxidation drama. Selling it as cherry is a stain crime. Selling it as alder is a correct kitchen.

## History

European alder in furniture is vernacular: painted rural pieces, interiors, the hidden parts. It does not have a Macquoid chapter.

Red alder’s furniture hour is 20th-century West Coast: plentiful, fast-growing after logging, a hardwood where the other commercial trees were Douglas-fir and hemlock. Cabinet shops in Oregon and Washington used it as their poplar. Then nationwide factories discovered it as a stain-grade suite wood. “Alder bedroom” in a 1990s catalogue is a real specification.

Arts and Crafts shops in the Pacific Northwest sometimes used alder when oak had to be shipped in. It is not quartersawn white oak. It should not be asked to be.

## Shop notes

Weight and hardness separate alder from cherry (lighter, softer) and from maple (much softer). No gum flecks. Even grain. Pin knots in lower grades are the character some kitchen lines sell.

It is a poor outdoor wood and a poor wet-bath wood unless painted and maintained.

## Conservation

Red alder is an abundant, nitrogen-fixing pioneer on the Pacific Coast. Using it is ordinary forestry, often a feature of the stand, not a raid. European alder is common on wet ground. Not CITES. The ethical issue is labeling: alder as alder, not as cherry, not as “solid hardwood” hiding an MDF core with an alder frame.

## A western hardwood where the woods were fir

Red alder (*Alnus rubra*) is the Pacific Coast’s answer to yellow-poplar: light, even, paint-grade and stain-grade, a hardwood in a forest that sold Douglas-fir and hemlock to the structural trade. After logging, alder comes in as a pioneer. Nitrogen-fixing roots, fast growth, a mill log in decades rather than centuries. Cabinet shops in Oregon and Washington used it as their default secondary and then as a show wood when a kitchen wanted paint or a mild stain. Nationwide factories in the late twentieth century made “alder bedroom” a catalogue specification. That is a real species line, not a mood.

FPL-GTR-190 puts red alder near specific gravity 0.41 and Janka 590 lbf at 12 percent moisture — between poplar and cherry, closer to poplar. Shrinkage in the handbook tables is moderate for a hardwood (radial about 4.4 percent, tangential about 7.3 percent, volumetric about 12.6 percent green to ovendry). Fine, even texture. Heart and sap not strongly parted. It takes stain with a willingness that made it a cherry impersonator in western factories. It is not durable outdoors. It is not a structural oak. It is not cherry.

Every mill that has opened a log knows that color change. ## How the factory found it

The furniture hour is twentieth-century West Coast first, then national. Plentiful, fast, a hardwood where the other commercial trees were softwoods. Then stain-grade suites: bedroom collections, entertainment walls, the 1990s catalogue page that said alder and meant it. Some of those pieces are honest solid frames. Some are an alder face on a particleboard box. The engineered chapter takes the box. The ethical issue here is labeling: alder as alder, not as cherry, not as “solid hardwood” hiding an MDF core with an alder stile.

European painted country furniture that is not pine is sometimes alder. Interiors, hidden parts, a rural chair. It does not have a court style. It has a river.

## A southern shop and a shipped board

Arkansas does not grow red alder. A southern shop using it has paid freight, which is fine. Local paint-grade equivalents are yellow-poplar and soft maple. ## A mill ticket and a kitchen stile

Walk a Portland or Tacoma millwork shop in the 1970s and the rack that is not fir is often alder. Door stiles, drawer fronts, a face frame for a painted box. The plane takes a long shaving. The arris bruises if you stack boards without stickers. Fresh-cut European alder flashes orange on the saw and then settles; red alder is quieter about that change but still pinks as it oxidizes. Hoadley would send you to the end grain: diffuse pores, no gum, no oak ray. A client who wants “cherry grain” will be disappointed. A client who wants a painted door that machines clean will not.

Turned alder legs show in factory suites more often than in high-style work. They dent at the foot where a vacuum hits. That is the hardness number doing what FPL said it would do. Birch is paler and a little harder in many grades; poplar has the mineral streak; maple fights the stain. Alder sits in the middle and takes the spray booth’s cherry toner without a fight. The honest shop writes the species on the ticket. The dishonest one writes the toner name.

Clogs and smoked fish are the European vernacular pair that never needed a parlor. Pile work in wet ground is the other old job. Furniture from *A. glutinosa* in a North German painted cupboard is the same riverside tree doing a drier task. Do not force a Venice-foundation speech onto a kitchen door.
