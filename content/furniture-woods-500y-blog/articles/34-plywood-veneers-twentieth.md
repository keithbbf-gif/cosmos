# Twentieth-century plywoods and face veneers

The V&A’s plywood history makes a point that flat-board people forget: between about 1850 and 1890 the innovative plywood was molded. Chair seats, backs, the factory furniture that was not Thonet’s solid beech bend — laminated veneer in curves. Then modernists in the 1920s and 1930s took the industrial sheet as a badge. Then the war taught compound curves to a couple in Los Angeles, and the Eames DCM went onto a thousand drawing boards (Robin Day’s remark, via the V&A). The species on the face could be walnut, teak, or birch. The species in the core could be something the catalogue hoped you would not ask about.

This chapter is the 20th-century veneer economy. Finnish birch and the tropical cores have their own essays. Engineered particle panels have theirs. Here is the habit of hiding a tree behind a tree.

## Rotary versus sliced

A log on a lathe, peeled like a paper towel: rotary veneer. Wide, wild cathedrals, cheap, the look of a 1950s dresser side. Lauan, yellow-poplar, Douglas-fir. A log sliced on a leaf: crown-cut or quarter-cut decorative veneer. Walnut crotch, quartered oak flake, mahogany crown. Furniture that wants to look like solid show wood uses sliced faces. Furniture that wants to be cheap uses rotary faces. Furniture that wants to be honest modern sometimes shows the ply edge.

Adhesives moved from animal glue to casein (Aalto’s factory still used casein for some cold bends, Klim 1990) to urea-formaldehyde and phenol-formaldehyde. Phenolic films in the 1930s made waterproof ply for aircraft and boats (Klim). Interior furniture ply is often a different, cheaper glue. Water still finds it.

## Faces of fashion

Art Deco liked exotic sliced faces on cheap cores: Macassar, burl, palisander. The core might be alder or gaboon. Bauhaus and early modern liked the idea of ply even when the famous chairs went to metal. Aalto made the ply the chair. Eames and Saarinen’s 1940 MoMA “Organic Design” experiments wanted a compound-curved shell; the war interrupted, then supplied the splint contract (1942) that taught the moulds.

Postwar American suites: a walnut-look rotary face on lauan, a printed grain that is not even veneer. The printed-and-papered board is the ancestor of foil-wrapped MDF. Look at a chip.

## Why ply is not a sin

Cross-banding cancels movement. A shaped seat that would split in solid flatsawn oak can live in five layers of birch. A table top that stays flat in a heated apartment is often ply. The 18th-century frame-and-panel was the old answer to the same physics. Plywood is the industrial answer.

The sin is the name. “Walnut dining table” for a 0.6 mm face on a void-core of mixed tropicals is a sentence that needs more nouns.

## Shop notes

Void-core cheap ply will telegraph and will lose a screw. Baltic birch and apple-ply grades will not. Count plies. Weigh the board.

Old furniture ply delaminates when the protein glue gives up in a damp room. Clamping and the right adhesive save some of it. A belt sander does not.

Veneer matching — book, slip, radial — is a design choice as old as the 17th century. Plywood factories automated it and then abandoned it for random rotary.

## Conservation

Decorative faces in rosewood and mahogany sit under CITES when the species does. Cores in lauan and okoume sit under the tropical-forest argument of those chapters. Formaldehyde in old urea glues is an indoor-air story (the engineered chapter). Buying vintage Eames and Aalto is reuse of an already-cut tree. Buying new “walnut ply” is a specification: face species, core species, glue, certification.

## What to look for

An edge. A void. A printed grain that does not continue around a corner. A moulded seat. A splint in a museum. A 1930s radio with a burl face and a cheap back.

## Why the name is the sin

“Walnut dining table” for a 0.6 mm face on a void-core of mixed tropicals is a sentence that needs more nouns. Cross-banding is honest physics. The sin is the name. Void-core cheap ply will telegraph and will lose a screw. Baltic birch and apple-ply grades will not. Count plies. Weigh the board.

Decorative faces in rosewood and mahogany sit under CITES when the species does. *Dalbergia* went onto Appendix II on 2 January 2017, Annotation #15; neotropical *Swietenia macrophylla* went onto Appendix II on 15 November 2003 for logs, sawn wood, veneer, plywood. Cores in lauan and okoume sit under the tropical-forest argument of those chapters. Formaldehyde in old urea glues is an indoor-air story the engineered chapter takes up. Buying vintage Eames and Aalto is reuse of an already-cut tree. Buying new “walnut ply” is a specification: face species, core species, glue, certification.

Hoadley’s veneer pages still teach the difference between a leaf and a board. A shop that repairs a delaminated 1950s dresser is doing hide-or-PVA work on a protein or early synthetic glue line. A shop that specifies a new table is doing a bill of materials. Both jobs fail when the only noun is walnut.

## An edge in the room

Look at an edge. A void. A printed grain that does not continue around a corner. A moulded seat. A splint in a museum. A 1930s radio with a burl face and a cheap back. The 500-year series needs this chapter because more people own ply than own joined oak.

Arkansas shops meet ply as imported faces on imported cores, and as domestic yellow-poplar and pine rotary for paint-grade work. Bradley Brand’s hardwoods are the solid side of that rack. The useful sentence for a designer is still the long one: face *Juglans nigra*, core birch, phenolic, FSC — or face rotary lauan, void, urea, no papers. Those are different objects. The Eames DCM and a printed suburban suite share a century. They do not share a glue, a mould, or a noun.

The V&A’s ten-object history is the reading. Klim 1990 is the conservator’s adhesive timeline. Kirkham and Engelbrecht are the Eames file. Ostergard is the bent-wood shelf. None of them will save a specification that says only wood. The physics is old. The factory is new. The label is where the trouble lives.
