# Arts and Crafts oak

Gustav Stickley put quartersawn white oak in the American living room and fumed it. Ammonia vapor and the tree’s tannin made a brown that is not a pigment sitting in the pores but a reaction in the fiber. The ray flake went darker or lighter than the ground, depending on the board, and the catalogues called it honest. William Morris had already argued, in *Hopes and Fears for Art* and in the practice of Morris & Co., that oak and the visible joint were a moral alternative to ebonized mahogany pretence. English shops (Barnsley, Voysey’s designs, Gimson) and American ones (Stickley, Limbert, Roycroft, the lesser Mission factories) made oak fashionable again for the first time since Macquoid’s first age — and they made the quartered face the badge.

This chapter is a style frame. The oak chapter and the sawing chapter are the anatomy.

## Why oak, why quartered

They wanted a northern hardwood that was not mahogany’s empire and not walnut’s Victorian parlor. Oak was local (or could be), tannic, visible in the grain, and old in the English floor. Quartersawn stock showed the ray — “silver grain” — and moved less in the wide boards they liked for settles and library tables. Flatsawn Mission exists; it was the cheaper line. Collectors still sort by flake.

Fuming works because oak is tannic. Maple will not fume the same way. A “fumed” maple is a stain. Red oak fumes less dramatically than white; the shops that cared specified white.

## England and America

Morris & Co. and the English craft furniture makers used oak in a range of finishes, not only fume. The American Craftsman movement industrialized a version: mortise-and-tenon through-tenons with visible pins, corbels, a brown that photographed well in *The Craftsman* magazine (1901–1916). Limbert’s cutouts and the Michigan factories made a broader, sometimes lighter oak. The Prairie school overlapped.

Golden oak of the 1890s — often quartersawn, often a yellow-brown varnish — is a cousin, more factory, more fill. Do not collapse it into Stickley, or vice versa.

## Finish myths

Not every brown oak chair is fumed. Many were stained and shellacked. A true fume has color in the wood, not only in the pore. A scratch that stays pale is a stain. A scratch that stays brown is in the fiber — or a deep dye.

Original finishes are thin. Restoration that pours polyurethane on a 1905 settle is a different object.

## Shop notes

If you fume now: ammonia is dangerous, the color keeps changing after the tent, and clients must be told that sunlight will still move the brown. Test boards.

If you build Mission in Arkansas white oak, you are in the right genus and the right cut. Specify quartered. Specify white. The 1905 mill in Eastwood, New York, was not the only oak forest.

## What to look for

Flake, through-tenons, a brown that is a reaction, a 1910 settle, a factory Mission with decals, a golden-oak sideboard with filled pores. Red oak pretending to be the catalogue.

## Eastwood, Sapperton, East Aurora

Gustav Stickley’s Craftsman Workshops at Eastwood, New York — Syracuse’s edge — made the American object most people mean by Mission: a settle or a library table in quartersawn white oak, through-tenons with visible pins, corbels under a top, a brown that photographed well in *The Craftsman* (1901–1916). The mill was not a cottage. It was a factory that wanted to look like a guild. The catalogues are the proof: models, prices, a finish called “fumed” as if the reader already knew the tent.

In the Cotswolds, Ernest Gimson and Sidney and Ernest Barnsley at Sapperton were closer to the Morris argument and farther from a mail-order settle. English oak (*Quercus robur*, *Q. petraea*), visible joints, a finish that was not always a fume. C. F. A. Voysey’s designs — the owl suite is the postcard — used oak as a northern moral wood. Morris & Co. had already, in *Hopes and Fears for Art* (1882) and in the firm’s practice, set oak and the visible joint against ebonized mahogany pretence. William Morris’s sentence is the English one. Stickley’s magazine is the American broadcast.

Charles Limbert’s factory in Grand Rapids and then Holland, Michigan, cut cutouts into oak that was sometimes lighter, sometimes more Mackintosh than Eastwood. Elbert Hubbard’s Roycroft shops at East Aurora, New York, made a heavier, more hammered oak. The Prairie school overlapped: oak interiors that were architecture, not only furniture. Lesser Mission factories from Indiana to the Pacific coast flooded the catalogues after 1905. Collectors still sort by flake, by hardware, by the red decal on the drawer.

Golden oak of the 1890s — often quartersawn, often a yellow-brown varnish, pores filled — is a cousin, more factory, more Renaissance-revival leftover. Do not collapse it into Stickley, or vice versa. A filled golden-oak sideboard and a fumed 1910 settle are both oak. They are not the same finish religion.

## Why oak, in the handbook’s numbers

They wanted a northern hardwood that was not mahogany’s empire and not walnut’s Victorian parlor. Oak was local (or could be), tannic, visible in the grain, and old in the English floor.

White oak (*Quercus alba* and the white group): SG about 0.68, Janka about 1,360 lbf at 12 percent (FPL-GTR-190, table 5–3a). Tyloses plug the heartwood pores. That is why tight cooperage and a certain outdoor manners attach to white oak, and why a fume — ammonia vapor plus the tree’s tannin — has something to react with. The brown is a reaction in the fiber, not only a pigment sitting in the pore.

Northern red oak (*Q. rubra*): SG about 0.63, Janka about 1,290 lbf. Open pores. You can blow through a straight red-oak end grain. Stain floods. Fuming is less dramatic; the shops that cared specified white. Red oak pretending to be the 1905 catalogue is a common floor.

European oak sits in the same hardness neighborhood; published Janka figures vary by source and ring width `[CITE NEEDED for a single FPL-equivalent on Q. robur]`. Slow-grown English or Baltic oak is often finer than a wide-ringed second-growth American board. The binomial does not tell you the ring count. The quartersawn chapter tells you why the ray — “silver grain” — became the badge. Flatsawn Mission exists. It was the cheaper line.

Maple will not fume the same way. A “fumed” maple is a stain. Ash will take a brown; it is not oak’s tannin story.

## The tent, and the scratch test

Ammonia vapor and tannin. The shop hung the piece, or the boards, in a tent or a chamber with aqueous ammonia. The color kept changing after the tent came down. Sunlight still moves the brown. Clients who want a 1905 settle matched to a new leaf must be told that the next two years are part of the finish.

Not every brown oak chair is fumed. Many were stained and shellacked. A true fume has color in the wood. A scratch that stays pale is a stain. A scratch that stays brown is in the fiber — or a deep dye. Original finishes are thin. Restoration that pours polyurethane on a 1905 settle is a different object. The pore that Stickley left open is a 1910 idea, against the filled golden-oak pore of 1892.

Museum holdings — the Art Institute of Chicago, the Dallas Museum of Art, Winterthur’s Mission pieces — are where you go to see an unstripped surface. A dealer’s “refinished Stickley” is often a darker, thicker thing than Eastwood shipped.

## Shop behavior

If you fume now: ammonia is dangerous. Ventilation is not a suggestion. Test boards from the same tree if you can. The latewood and the ray will not go the same brown. That is the point, and also the complaint.

If you build Mission in Arkansas white oak, you are in the right genus and the right cut. Specify quartered. Specify white. Specify the pin in the through-tenon as a piece of oak or of walnut, not as a steel dowel with a wood cap unless you say so. Draw-boring is older than Stickley; he made it visible on purpose.

Through-tenons that are proud of the post will get knocked. Historic ones often sit flush or a hair proud. A tenon that looks like a hat is a reproduction flourish.

Do not stain red oak with a yellow-brown toner and sell it as fumed white oak. The pore will argue. The ray will argue. The weight may not.

Hardware: hammered copper and iron are part of the American object. English pieces often used quieter iron. A bright brass knob on a 1910 settle is usually a replacement.

## Fashion as a sawing choice

The series places this chapter after the species essays so the fashion of 1900 can be seen as a sawing choice and a chemistry choice, not as a vague return to craft. Quartersawn white oak, tannin, a visible pin: that is the specification. Morris supplied the English sentence. Stickley supplied the American mill. The tree was already here. Arkansas still grows it.
