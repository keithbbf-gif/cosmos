# Fruitwoods

An orchard dies a furniture death. When the apple tree stops bearing or the pear is blown over, the trunk is short, often hollow, often figured at the root, and hard in a way that has nothing to do with a forest sawlog. European country shops and American farm shops used that wood because it was there: apple for a turned bowl and a chair seat, pear for a drawing instrument and a Biedermeier drawer front, plum for a small box with a purple heart. The 500-year story is local, small, and repeatedly mistaken for cherry.

This is a comparative chapter on purpose. No single fruit species furnished a Macquoid age. Together they furnished the pieces that never needed a port.

## Apple (*Malus*)

Dense, hard, fine-textured, often twisted. Specific gravity commonly cited near 0.65–0.75 and Janka in the hard-maple neighborhood, but orchard wood varies so wildly that a handbook average is a rumor `[CITE NEEDED: FPL does not table orchard apple as a commercial lumber]`. It turns like a friend. It splits like an enemy if you try to rive it. It moves. It is excellent for mallets, screws in wooden vises, saw handles, and small chairs.

English and French country furniture in “fruitwood” is often apple or pear. American farm chairs sometimes have apple seats or turnings. The wood oxidizes to a warm brown that people call cherry. The pores are finer; the weight is often higher; the log is shorter.

## Pear (*Pyrus*)

The printer’s and patternmaker’s wood: even, hard, almost no grain telegraph, takes a cut like boxwood’s cousin. German and French shops used pear for drawing tools, for carving, and for Biedermeier show surfaces when a silky pale brown was wanted. Stained black it became ebonized line-work and sometimes fake ebony. The Biedermeier chapter leans on pear as much as on birch.

Pear is more mannerly than apple in the plane. It is still orchard-sized. Wide boards are glue-ups or a lie.

## Plum and the other *Prunus*

Plum (*P. domestica* and relatives) can have a startling purple-brown heart. Small boxes, knife handles, inlay. Wild cherry is in the cherry chapter; orchard cherry is usually too small and too valuable as fruit to be a timber. Apricot and peach appear in southern European small work. None of these are a dining table unless the table is a fraud or a mosaic.

## How shops thought

Fruitwood is a farm and provincial category. Inventories say “fruitwood” because the clerk did not care which tree in the hedge. Attribution now wants a lens: cherry vs apple vs pear vs alder (often painted and thrown in the same bin). Weight, pore, and smell in the cut help. Pear is even; apple is wilder; cherry has gum flecks and a luster.

High-style Paris used fruitwoods in marquetry as colors: light brown, pink, a stained green. The Riesener chapter of this series (marquetry) is the court version of the orchard.

American high-style used cherry, which is a *Prunus* and a forest tree, not an orchard leftover. Do not collapse *P. serotina* into “fruitwood.” It is a commercial lumber.

## Finish and aging

Fruitwoods take a polish to a closed, almost metallic surface. Oil darkens apple toward mahogany-in-miniature. Pear stays paler unless stained. Old French polish on pear is a Biedermeier signature.

Worm likes some fruit sapwood. Orchard trees are often full of metal: nails, fence wire, a forgotten hook. The first cut is a saw-risk.

## Fashion

Never a London decade of apple. Always a countryside constant. Biedermeier made pear polite. Arts and Crafts sometimes used fruit for inlay and small cabinets. Mid-century ignored them except as bowls. Contemporary makers pay orchardists for a trunk and make a bench that is one tree wide and four feet long. That is the honest scale.

## Conservation

Orchard fruitwoods are agricultural waste or a second crop. Using them is not a forest threat. Wild *Pyrus* and *Malus* in hedges are another matter if you strip a hedgerow for “character wood.” Buy trees that were coming down. CITES is silent here.

Watch for tropical woods sold as “fruitwood finish.” That is a stain name from factory catalogues, often on rubberwood (*Hevea*) or mixed hardwoods. Rubberwood is a plantation rubber tree, a legitimate furniture wood with its own story, not an apple.

## What to look for

Short billets, wild grain, hardness, a provincial chair, a German drawer front like satin, a purple plum box. A catalogue that says “fruitwood” on an eight-foot dining table: read the edge.

Arkansas orchards are not Normandy, but apple wood still turns up from farm removals. A shop that uses it is in the old habit: the tree that fed people then furnished them. Cherry from the woods remains the forest *Prunus*. Keep the words apart.

## Apple at the bench

It turns. Bowls, mallet heads, the wooden screw in a vise, saw handles, a chair seat that will take a lot of sitting. It splits if you ask it to be a riven panel. It moves. Oil darkens it toward a mahogany-in-miniature that people then call cherry. The pores are finer than *P. serotina*; the weight is often higher; the log is shorter.
