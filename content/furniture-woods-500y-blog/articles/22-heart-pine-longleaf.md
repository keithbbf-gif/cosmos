# Heart pine and longleaf

Before the longleaf pine was a floor, it was a tar tree. The naval-stores belt of the American South — North Carolina to Texas, a fire forest of *Pinus palustris* — fed pitch, tar, turpentine, and masts to Atlantic shipping (Outland, *Tapping the Pines*; Earley, *Looking for Longleaf*). The heartwood of old trees, dense with latewood and resin, became the “heart pine” of southern houses: joists, floors, the occasional press and table. Furniture was a side use. The side use is what this chapter is for.

## The wood

Longleaf is a southern yellow pine. The *Wood Handbook* lists longleaf at about SG 0.59 and Janka 870 lbf at 12 percent — more than twice the hardness of eastern white pine, in the neighborhood of teak’s number and below oak. The face is a loud stripe of hard latewood and softer earlywood. Old-growth heartwood is red-orange to brown, resinous, and heavy. Sapwood is paler. What dealers call heart pine is that old heartwood, often reclaimed from mills and warehouses.

Slash pine (*P. elliottii*) and other yellow pines overlap in the salvage market. Anatomy does not always give you the species on a painted joist. The grade is the density and the heart.

Resin will bleed through finishes for years if the kiln work was shy. Old heart is more stable than a wet new yellow-pine 2×10. It is still a pine: it dents less than white pine, more than maple.

## Workability and finish

Hard latewood and soft earlywood make a corrugated plane feel if the iron is dull. Carbide and sharp handsaws. Nails in old heart can bend. Predrill.

Oil finishes wake the red. Film finishes sit on the resin and sometimes peel. Shellac as a sealer is an old answer to bleed. Water-based finishes can fail on resinous stock.

The grain is too strong for a delicate stain. People who want “oak” should buy oak. Heart pine looks like itself or it looks like a gym floor.

## Southern furniture

Coastal and backcountry shops used yellow pine as a primary wood for painted and stained case work and as a secondary wood under walnut and mahogany. Huntboards, safes, cupboard interiors, bedsteads. Hurst and Prown’s *Southern Furniture 1680–1830* is the place to see the mix. A Charleston parlor might show mahogany; the passage and the kitchen did not.

19th-century mills cut longleaf into a national structural lumber. Furniture factories used it when they wanted cheap and strong. School desks, pews, mill-town tables.

20th-century depletion of old-growth longleaf (agriculture, logging, fire suppression that stopped the forest’s own regeneration) made heart pine a salvage fashion. Flooring companies pulled warehouses. Dining tables from 19th-century joists became a late-century product. Some of those tables are honest reuse. Some are new yellow pine stained orange.

## Naval stores and the Crown

The Naval Stores Act tradition and the Broad Arrow are white-pine and Baltic stories as much as longleaf, but the southern pitch industry is the economic pair to New England masts. Longleaf was a strategic tree. Furniture shops lived on the leftovers and on the domestic build. Reading a heart-pine table without the tar kiln is like reading mahogany without Jamaica.

## Shop notes

Metal in reclaimed joists: nails, plaster keys, a bolt. Scan or cut slowly.

Old heart can be brittle. A thin long span will snap where a new oak board would bend.

Insect holes in sapwood are common; heart is less tasty. Do not buy powder that is still coming out.

## Conservation

Longleaf restoration is one of the serious American forest projects: fire, wiregrass, red-cockaded woodpecker. Earley’s book is the public narrative. Furniture should use certified new longleaf if you want new wood (the tree is being planted again), or documented salvage. It should not use the last old trees for a coffee table.

Not CITES. The Lacey Act still applies to imported pine that might be mixed in a container of “heart pine.” Ask for the country and the species.

## What to look for

Loud latewood, orange heart, weight, resin, a southern house floor, a joist mortise in the underside of a table. A light, even “heart pine” from a big-box aisle: probably not.

Arkansas is on the edge of the old longleaf map; shortleaf and loblolly are the more local yellow pines. A shop that works southern pine is in the right region. True old-growth longleaf is a salvage or a specialty order. Name it honestly.

True old-growth longleaf is a salvage or a specialty order. Name it honestly. Weight, resin, loud latewood, orange heart — those are the tells. A joist mortise under a table is better evidence than a hang-tag.

A fire forest wants fire. Earley’s narrative and the restoration literature agree on that much: without periodic burn, hardwoods and loblolly take the ground, and longleaf fails to recruit. Furniture does not restore a stand. It can refuse to spend the last old boles on a coffee table and it can name salvage as salvage. Turpentine faces and boxed hearts in a reclaimed joist are industrial marks, not rustic decoration. Leave them if they are stable. Do not sand them into a gym-floor cartoon.
