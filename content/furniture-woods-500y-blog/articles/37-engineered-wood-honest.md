# Engineered wood, told honestly

Most of the furniture alive in the world right now is not a joined oak chest. It is a panel: veneered plywood, particleboard, medium-density fiberboard, a paper foil, a plastic edge. This chapter will not sneer. It will say what those panels do well, where they die, and how a buyer can tell a named tool from a costume.

Bradley Brand works solid hardwoods. That is a choice, not a license to lie about the other choice.

## What the panels are

Plywood: layers of veneer, grain crossed. Already treated in the ply chapters. The good stuff (Baltic birch, proper hardwood ply) is a furniture wood with a species list. The cheap stuff is a void and a smell.

Particleboard (chipboard): chips in glue, pressed. Heavy, cheap, swells in a wet kitchen like a bread sponge. Holds a cam lock if you do not sit on the shelf. Invented for a reason: small wood, waste wood, a flat sheet. European kitchens after the 1950s ran on it. So did a lot of “walnut wall units.”

Medium-density fiberboard (MDF): refined fiber, usually with urea-formaldehyde or a newer resin, pressed smooth. Machines like a dream. Edges are the tell: fuzzy, brown, homogeneous. Takes paint. Swells if you leave a cup on a raw edge. A walnut-veneered MDF door is a standard, respectable kitchen door if the invoice says so.

OSB: oriented strand, a sheathing board. Rare in show furniture, common in the back of something you should not have bought.

LVL and other structural laminates: beams. They appear as live-edge-look tables when someone slabs a laminated beam and calls it rustic. That is a look. Name the glue.

## What they do better than solid wood

They stay flat. A six-foot cabinet door in flatsawn oak is a season of movement. The same door in MDF with a veneer is a rectangle in January and in July. The 18th-century shop used frame-and-panel to get that rectangle. The 21st-century shop uses a press.

They use trees that solid furniture would reject: small stems, waste, plantation thinnings. That can be a conservation good if the glue and the forest are decent.

They are cheap enough that more people have a place to put a plate.

## What they do worse

Water. A leak under a sink is a particleboard obituary. Solid oak would have stained and lived.

Screws in the edge. MDF holds a well-designed fastener and drops a careless one. Particleboard is worse.

Repair. A chipped foil cannot be planed. A broken cam-lock carcase is often landfill. Hide-glue mahogany can be taken apart. That is not nostalgia. It is a joinery fact.

Heat and cheap foil: the dining table that blisters under a casserole.

Formaldehyde: urea-formaldehyde resins emit. Standards (CARB, TSCA Title VI in the U.S., E1/E0 in Europe) exist because people live with these boards. Old unlabelled particleboard in a closed room is the worst of the story. Ask for certified low-emission boards. This is not a scare paragraph. It is a specification.

## History

Klim’s 1990 WAG paper still helps: protein glues, then phenolics for wet work, then cheap ureas for interior furniture. Particleboard’s European industrial rise is postwar. MDF’s American and European rise is late 20th century. IKEA and its peers did not invent the panel; they perfected the cam lock and the flat box.

Printed grain and vinyl wraps are the late costume. A 1970s lauan print and a 2010s paper-foil oak are the same idea with better ink.

## How to buy without being a snob

Read the edge. Ask for the core. A “solid walnut front, engineered carcase” is a normal, honest kitchen. A “solid walnut collection” that is foil on particleboard is a catalogue crime.

Pay for the right panel: moisture-resistant MDF in a bath, plywood in a wet-adjacent cabinet, particleboard only where the budget and the dryness agree.

If you want repairable furniture that can be recut, buy solid or honest ply. If you want a wall of cheap storage you will not move, buy the cam lock and do not wet it.

## Conservation

Engineered wood can be the least romantic and the most efficient use of a log. It can also be a tropical core with a temperate photograph on top (lauan chapter). Certification and the resin matter. Landfill of failed particleboard is the end of the efficiency story. Design for a life that matches the glue.

## What to look for

Homogeneous brown edges. Cam locks. Foil that does not turn a corner with the grain. A CARB label. A veneered door with a plywood core (good) versus a paper-wrapped chip (know it). A swollen kickboard.

The 500-year series ends its materials run here so that oak and mahogany do not pretend they are the only furniture left. They are not. The honest shop names the panel. The honest buyer reads the edge.

## A kitchen in 1962, and one in 2012

Walk a West German or Swedish kitchen from the early 1960s. The doors may be painted, or a thin teak face, or a printed wood. The carcase is particleboard: chips in urea glue, pressed flat, edged in a strip of real wood or a painted banding. The plates live. The sink leak is the enemy. Max Himmelheber and the German postwar mills did not invent waste-wood board as a thought experiment; they industrialized a sheet that could be a wall of storage for people who were not going to commission a joined oak dresser. IKEA did not invent the panel either. Gillis Lundgren’s 1956 flat-pack idea — take the legs off a table so it fits in a car — and the later cam-lock carcase are distributions of a board that already existed.

Walk the same kitchen type in 2012. The door is often MDF, veneered or foil-wrapped, machined on a CNC as if it were cheese. The edge is a 0.4 mm ABS or a thicker wood lipping. The core is brown and fuzzy and homogeneous. A confirmat screw holds if the hole was drilled for it. A drywall screw in the edge does not. The physics did not change. The costume got better ink.

A shop that works solid hardwoods has made a choice about repair and about a forest it can name. That is not a license to sneer at the wall of storage. This chapter keeps that line.

## What the Wood Handbook and Hoadley actually give you

The *Wood Handbook* is a book about trees as engineering material. Engineered panels have their own chapters and their own ASTM and EN standards; they do not get a Janka the way *Quercus alba* does. What you can steal from FPL and from R. Bruce Hoadley’s *Understanding Wood* is the reason the panel was invented: solid wood moves. Tangential shrinkage in oak is on the order of 8–10 percent from green to ovendry; radial is less; longitudinal is a rounding error. A six-foot flatsawn oak door is a season of cup and a year of argument. Frame-and-panel was the 18th-century answer. Cross-banded plywood is the 20th-century answer. MDF is the 21st-century answer if you want a painted rectangle and you accept the water rule.

Hoadley is also the reminder that glue is a material. A hide-glue mahogany carcase can be taken apart with heat and moisture. A urea-formaldehyde particleboard carcase cannot. That is not nostalgia. It is a joinery fact. Phenol-formaldehyde, the dark glue of exterior ply and of some older marine sheets, is a different chemistry: darker line, better wet behavior, still not a solid-wood repair culture. Methylene diphenyl diisocyanate (MDI) and other newer resins show up in some moisture-resistant and no-added-formaldehyde boards. Ask. The invoice should say.

Formaldehyde emission is a specification, not a ghost story. Urea-formaldehyde resins emit. The California Air Resources Board’s ATCM, then the U.S. EPA’s TSCA Title VI, set composite-wood formaldehyde limits for hardwood ply, particleboard, and MDF sold in the United States. Europe’s E1 and the tighter E0 / CARB NAF / ULEF labels are the same conversation in different stamps. Old unlabelled particleboard in a closed room is the worst of the story. Ask for the stamp. This is not a scare paragraph.

## How a panel dies

Water. A leak under a sink is a particleboard obituary. The chips swell, the glue lets go, the edge crumbles into a brown pastry. Solid oak would have stained and lived. Moisture-resistant MDF (often green-dyed in the core, a factory habit) lasts longer in a bath than standard MDF and still hates a standing puddle on a raw edge. Plywood in a wet-adjacent cabinet is the better of the cheap choices if the glue is the right glue.

Heat. A foil-wrapped dining table under a casserole blisters. The photograph of oak lifts in a bubble. There is no plane that fixes a bubble. A solid top would have a white ring you might revive. The foil table is a different object.

Fasteners. MDF holds a well-designed fastener — confirmat, insert, a screw into the face rather than the edge — and drops a careless one. Particleboard is worse. Cam locks are honest if you do not sit on the shelf. They are not a mortise and tenon. A broken cam-lock carcase is often landfill because the holes have gone oval and the board has no long fiber to grab.

Repair. A chipped foil cannot be planed. A dented MDF edge can be filled and painted if the door was painted to begin with. A walnut veneer on MDF can be patched by a specialist; the patch will be a leaf, not a recut of the door. Hide-glue mahogany can be taken apart. Choose the life you want before you choose the board.

## Shop behavior

Read the edge before you read the catalogue. A homogeneous brown fuzz is MDF. Chips in a pale glue are particleboard. Thin dark lines, many of them, are ply. A void behind a 0.6 mm face is cheap ply. A paper that does not turn the corner with the grain is a print. A veneer that does turn the corner, with book-matched leaves on a door pair, is a different invoice.

When you machine MDF, the dust is fine and the cut is clean. Seal the raw edge the same day if the door will see a kitchen. When you machine particleboard, the chips tear. When you machine cheap lauan ply, the core crumbles and the screw story begins.

LVL and other structural laminates appear as “live-edge” tables when someone slabs a laminated beam and calls it rustic. Name the glue. Name the species in the lamstock if you can. A beam is not a walnut tree.

OSB is a sheathing board. It shows up as the back of something you should not have bought, or as a “rustic” headboard. It is oriented strand, waxed, structural. It is not furniture grade. Say so.

## Conservation, without romance

Engineered wood can be the least romantic and the most efficient use of a log: small stems, mill waste, plantation thinnings, a flat sheet. That can be a conservation good if the glue and the forest are decent. It can also be a tropical core with a temperate photograph on top — the lauan chapter — and a landfill of failed particleboard at the end of the efficiency story. Design for a life that matches the glue.

Certification (FSC, PEFC) is a risk tool for the face and the core. It is not a finding that the Lacey Act has been met. CARB / TSCA Title VI is an emission stamp, not a forest stamp. You want both conversations on the same purchase order if the board is imported or mixed.
