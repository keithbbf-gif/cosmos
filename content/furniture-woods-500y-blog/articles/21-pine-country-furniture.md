# Pine and country furniture

A six-board chest in eastern white pine, painted oxblood, is not a failed oak chest. It is a different idea of furniture: light enough to move, cheap enough to own, soft enough to dent, and honest about paint. Northern Europe and eastern North America furnished more rooms in pine than in walnut. The fashionable histories under-count it because pine was often painted out of the story.

This chapter is the country habit. Heart pine and Baltic deal have their own essays. They overlap. They are not the same trees.

## The woods people meant

Eastern white pine (*Pinus strobus*) — Weymouth pine in England — is the New England and Great Lakes furniture pine: SG about 0.35, Janka about 380 lbf (FPL-GTR-190). Soft, even, low shrinkage, easy to kiln. Heartwood light brown with a red hint; it darkens in air. The *Wood Handbook* still lists furniture among its uses. Old-growth clear white pine is a vanished grade; second growth is knotty and fine for paint.

Scots pine (*P. sylvestris*) is the European furniture and joinery pine, the redwood or red deal of the British trade when it came from the Baltic and Scandinavia. Harder than white pine in many grades, with a stronger ring.

Southern yellow pines (*P. taeda*, *P. echinata*, and kin) are denser, with a loud latewood band. They appear in southern country furniture and in later factory work. Longleaf heart pine is the next chapter.

Knots are not a defect in country pine. They are the tree. A knot that holds is a feature; a knot that falls out is a hole you plug or paint.

## Workability and finish

Pine planes easily and bruises easily. Resin in yellow pines gums cutters and finishes. White pine is kinder. All pines take paint as if they were born for it. Stain blotches: the earlywood drinks, the latewood refuses. A “honey pine” finish is often a toner and a varnish arguing with the rings.

Nails hold in pine as well as in any furniture wood; the fiber crushes and grips. Glue holds if the resin is not on the surface.

Movement is modest in white pine compared with oak. A wide pine board still cups. Breadboard ends and battens are old answers.

## Country, not “primitive” as an insult

Painted pine furniture is the furniture of people who had trees and brushes: New England blanket chests, Pennsylvania schranks that are not all walnut, Scandinavian peasant cupboards, Alpine painted armoires, English boarded pine of the farmhouse. The Metropolitan, Winterthur, and Nordic museums hold the best of it. Most of it lived harder lives and did not survive.

Shaker pine is a subset: clear finish or light paint, maple and cherry beside it. The Shaker chapter takes the mix. The pine there is a chosen pale, not a last resort.

Southern pine country pieces — huntboards, pie safes, cupboard tops — may be yellow pine, poplar, or both. Hurst and Prown teach the reader to look.

## The knotty-pine century

20th-century American dens and kitchens paneled in knotty pine, often *P. strobus* or western pines, made a fashion of the grade that old-growth millers would have rejected for furniture. Colonial Revival “Early American” factories made pine dining rooms with machine distress. Some of it is charming. Some of it is a cartoon of a 17th-century board chest.

Mid-century taste hid pine, then country taste brought it back, then oak and cherry took the 1990s. Pine never left the painted cupboard.

## Shop notes

A steel screw in pine without a pilot can split a thin rail. Glue and a peg are older and often better.

Old paint may be lead. That is a conservation and health fact, not a style note.

Do not call every pale American secondary wood pine. Poplar is greener and heavier. Basswood is even softer and more even.

## Conservation

The pines in this chapter are not CITES species. White pine’s old-growth was taken for masts and for clear lumber (Albion; the Broad Arrow tradition). Second-growth furniture is a different material. Using plantation and managed pine for painted work is ordinary. Using remaining old-growth for a suburban table is a waste of a scarce grade.

## What to look for

Dents, paint layers, knots, resin canals on the end grain, light weight. A chest you can lift. A “mahogany” factory piece with a pine back. A Shaker stand that is maple and pine together.

Bradley Brand works pine among other Arkansas woods. A painted pine cupboard from southern yellow pine is a country object in a line that does not need to apologize to walnut. It needs a finish that expects dents, and a buyer who does not mistake softness for a mistake.

## Six boards and a brush

A six-board chest is a lesson in what pine is for. Two sides, a bottom, a lid, a front and back — nailed or pegged, often a till inside, often painted oxblood or blue or a green that has gone black in the folds. Eastern white pine (*Pinus strobus*) made the New England version light enough to drag to a wall. You can lift a chest that would be a two-person oak object. That is not a failed hardwood piece. It is a different idea of furniture: cheap enough to own, soft enough to dent, honest about paint.

FPL-GTR-190 puts eastern white pine at about specific gravity 0.35 and Janka 380 lbf at 12 percent moisture. Shrinkage is low for a commercial wood: on the order of 2.1 percent radial, 6.1 percent tangential, 8.2 percent volumetric green to ovendry. That stability, and the old-growth clears that used to come off the lake states and New England, made furniture and interior joinery. The *Wood Handbook* still lists furniture among its uses. England called *P. strobus* Weymouth pine. The Broad Arrow reserved large white pines for masts (Albion, *Forests and Sea Power*; the Massachusetts charter tradition). Furniture shops used what the navy did not take, and later what the cut-over left. Old-growth clear white pine is a vanished grade for ordinary yards. Using remaining old-growth for a suburban table is a waste of a scarce clear. Using managed and plantation pine for painted work is ordinary.
