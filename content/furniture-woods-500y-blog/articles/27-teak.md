# Teak

Teak feels oily because it is. *Tectona grandis*, a ring-porous tree of India, Burma (Myanmar), Thailand, and the old East Indies, lays down a heartwood that the *Wood Handbook* describes as yellow-brown to golden brown, aging to a richer brown, coarse, usually straight, with excellent dimensional stability and a very high natural durability. Silica in the fiber dulls tools. The same heartwood does not corrode iron the way oak’s tannin does, which is why shipbuilders and the fittings on deck wanted it. Furniture wanted it for the same oil and for a color that mid-century Denmark turned into a world export.

## Numbers and manners

Density is on the order of American oak (FPL: properties “generally on par with those of U.S. oaks”). Janka commonly cited near 1,070 lbf, SG near 0.55–0.66 depending on source and plantation versus old-growth. Plantation teak can be lighter, paler, with wider rings and less oil. Old Burma teak is the folklore grade: tight, dark, greasy.

Workability: moderate, says FPL, with silica as the tax. Gluing and finishing may need the oil wiped back. Outdoor teak is often left unfinished to go silver; indoor teak in the 1950s was oiled to stay honey.

It is ring-porous. The face has a teak look — coarse pore, brown — that stain catalogues have chased onto other species for seventy years.

## Imperial furniture and the ship

Indian and Burmese teak was a British imperial timber: railways, harbors, the deck of a warship, the campaign chest that went to a hot station. Campaign furniture in mahogany and teak is a military-administrative style: brass corners, knockdown fittings, a wood that would not sulk in a hold. Dutch colonial furniture in Java used teak because the tree was there (and planted). Chinese shops in Southeast Asia used it. The furniture is older than Danish modern. Danish modern is why most buyers can spell the word.

## Conservation

*Tectona grandis* is not a CITES appendix species in the rosewood sense; some populations and some export regimes are tightly controlled, and illegal logging in Southeast Asia is documented. Plantation teak in Africa and Latin America is a real supply (FPL already noted plantations). FSC and national export papers matter. “Burma teak” as a sales name should come with a legal path or be treated as a story.

Substitutes: iroko (*Milicia*), sometimes sold as “African teak,” is the next chapter. It is not *Tectona*.

## What to look for

Oil, ring-porous brown, silica-blunted planes in a shop, a campaign chest, a 1958 sideboard, a grey garden bench, a plantation board with wide rings. A “teak finish” on rubberwood: a stain.

Arkansas does not grow teak. A designer who wants teak’s outdoor manners in a domestic hardwood is looking at white oak or cypress, not at a stain. A designer who wants the 1960s interior color is looking at a named *Tectona* with papers, or at walnut, or at an honest iroko.

## Working an oily, siliceous board

Wipe with solvent before glue if the joint is oily. Sharp carbide. Stainless or bronze fasteners outdoors; teak and cheap steel can still make stains even if the wood is kinder than oak. Sanding dust is an irritant for some people. So is the oil. Collection is not optional in a small shop.

Indoor oil finishes want maintenance the way a cutting board wants maintenance. A film finish is possible if the oil is cut back; it is a different object than a 1950s Danish piece. Outdoor design that traps water against end grain will fail in teak as surely as in oak. The durability is the heartwood’s, not the joint’s.

At the bench, ring-porous brown plus a greasy feel plus a blunted iron is the working ID. Smell is milder than juniper, not the kitchen-spice of eastern redcedar. Weight is oak’s neighbor, not western redcedar’s. A plantation board with wide rings is still *Tectona* if the anatomy agrees; it is not “Burma grade” because the tag says so.

Arkansas does not grow teak. A designer who wants teak’s outdoor manners in a domestic hardwood is looking at white oak or cypress, not at a stain. A designer who wants the 1960s interior color is looking at a named *Tectona* with papers, or at walnut, or at an honest iroko. The campaign chest and the Juhl chair share a species. They do not share a century, a port, or a finish schedule. Name the tree. Then decide whether the job is a deck, a sideboard, or a wish.
