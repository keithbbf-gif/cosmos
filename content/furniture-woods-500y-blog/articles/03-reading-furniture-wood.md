# How to read a furniture wood

A dealer says mahogany. The drawer sides are a greenish poplar. The back is pine. The beading is a blackened fruitwood. The piece is still “a mahogany chest” in the way a book is “cloth” — by its jacket. Learning to read the woods is learning to read the shop.

This chapter is a method, not a key to every species. The species chapters do that work. Here is how to look so those chapters have somewhere to land.

## Start with the job, not the color

Color is a poor first question. Cherry starts as salmon-pink and goes to liver-brown in the sun. Mahogany ranges from pale salmon to a red so deep the Victorian finishers barely needed pigment. Walnut fades toward chocolate and then toward mud if the windows are cruel. Teak goes silver outdoors and honey under oil. A 1980s stain can make maple look like walnut and oak look like mahogany. Ask instead: what was this part asked to do?

Show surfaces — top, drawer front, splat, door — want a wood that planes, carves or turns, and takes a finish. Secondary woods — drawer sides, dustboards, backboards, glue blocks — want cheap, stable, or both. Chair seats may want a wood that will not split when you drive in legs (elm). Bows want a wood that bends (ash, oak, hickory). Carving wants a wood that holds a cut (lime, close-grained mahogany). Clock cases in New England often want white pine because it is light and takes paint or veneer.

If you name the job first, the short list of species shrinks before you argue about pores.

## Pores, rings, rays

Hardwoods have vessels. On the end grain they are holes. On the face they are grooves or dashes.

Ring-porous woods (oak, ash, elm, teak, hickory) lay a row of large earlywood pores each year. You can count rings with a thumbnail. The face shows a strong grain that filler and stain love and that a fine polish has to negotiate. Diffuse-porous woods (maple, cherry, birch, yellow-poplar, true mahogany in a quieter way) spread smaller pores through the ring. The face can look satiny. Semi-ring-porous woods (walnut) sit between.

Softwoods have no vessels. They have tracheids. Pine shows resin canals and a jump from earlywood to latewood that is loud in southern yellow pine and shy in eastern white pine. If you think you are looking at maple and you see a resin canal, you are not looking at maple.

Rays are the other tell. Oak rays are large enough to be a design element when quartered. Beech and sycamore have crowded rays that speckle a quartered face (European sycamore in the furniture trade is *Acer pseudoplatanus*, a maple; American sycamore is *Platanus*). Boxwood rays are fine; the wood looks almost like a pale meat.

A 10× lens and a cut end grain beat a phone photograph of a finished top.

## Weight and the dent test

Lift the drawer. Yellow-poplar and pine secondary wood make a tall chest possible. Solid oak drawer sides make a chest that fights you. Janka hardness is a laboratory dent test (a steel ball, a measured load). You do not need the machine. A fingernail into an underside arris is crude and useful: pine yields, maple barely does, teak is oily and confusing, old finish lies.

The *Wood Handbook* numbers are averages at 12 percent moisture. A bone-dry antique pine table can feel harder than a damp oak board. Moisture first, then species.

## Tool marks and shop habits

Hand-plane tracks on a drawer bottom, a pit-sawn backboard, a circular-saw skip from a 19th-century mill, a rotary-cut veneer with a wide, repeating cathedral — these date the conversion of the log. They also hint at species. Rotary veneer loves big, cheap logs: lauan, yellow-poplar, Douglas-fir. Sliced veneer is how you get a crown-cut walnut or a quartered oak flake on a door.

Look at the unseen edges. American 18th-century high-style work often uses yellow-poplar (*Liriodendron tulipifera*) for secondary wood in the Mid-Atlantic; New England leans white pine; the South mixes pine and poplar and sometimes cypress. English work uses oak and deal. A “Queen Anne” highboy with MDF dustboards is a marriage, not a birth.

## Finish is a mask and a record

Shellac on mahogany can be French-polished to a depth that looks like standing water. Oil on teak can look like nothing. Milk paint on pine is a crust. Later varnish ambered. Later polyurethane sat on top like a plastic tray.

Cherry’s darkening is oxidation and light, not a finish trick, though finishers have always helped it. If two leaves of a cherry table do not match, ask which one lived under a runner. Walnut’s brown is heartwood color plus time; steamed walnut is a mill practice to even sap and heart, and it is not a sin unless it is undisclosed in a “solid walnut” claim that is mostly sap.

Fumed oak is a chemical reaction (ammonia and tannin), a true finish of the Arts and Crafts shop. A stain trying to fake it sits in the pores and looks dirty.

## Names that mean too many trees

“Mahogany” in a 1760 London bill is likely Jamaican *Swietenia*. In a 1960 American suite it may be *Khaya*, sapele, or a photograph of grain on lauan. “Rosewood” may be *Dalbergia nigra*, East Indian *D. latifolia*, or a stained substitute. “Cedar” in a blanket chest is often *Juniperus virginiana* (eastern redcedar, a juniper). “Cedar” in a Spanish colonial chest may be *Cedrela odorata* (Spanish cedar, a Meliaceae cousin of mahogany). “Sycamore” is a fight between continents.

The species chapters give binomials. Use them when money or law is involved. CITES and the Lacey Act do not care about poetry.

## What you cannot see

Plantation teak and old Burma teak are both *Tectona grandis*. The rings and the oil differ. Second-growth red oak from a fast Arkansas stand is still *Quercus rubra*; the earlywood pores may be wider and the latewood weaker than a tight old tree. The handbook averages still apply. Your board is a sample of one.

Glued-up tops hide the sawing pattern unless you find an end. Engineered cores hide everything except the 0.6 mm face. Reading those pieces is reading an edge. The engineered chapter is written for that edge.

## A short walk around a table

Walk a dining table as a practice.

Top: species and cut. Matching cathedrals — flatsawn pair from one log. Ribbon stripe — quartered interlocked grain, often sapele if the table is postwar and the price was not mahogany-grade. Flake — oak, quartered.

Edge: solid or a lip over ply? A breadboard end? Slot-screwed?

Underside: plane tracks or wide-belt scratches? Pocket screws (late)? Wood shrinks across the width; if the battens are glued tight across a wide flatsawn top, look for splits.

Legs: same species? Often not. A maple leg under a cherry top is a New England habit. A beech leg under a stained “walnut” seat is a factory habit.

Then open a drawer. That is where shops tell the truth.

## Why this comes third

The five-century essay gave you the parade. The sawing essay gave you the geometry. This essay is the field kit. The rest of the series is what you take into the room: oak’s tyloses and the Baltic trade, walnut’s two continents, mahogany’s statute and its substitutes, cherry’s sunburn, maple’s figures, the softwoods that painted the countryside, the tropical woods that law now follows, the plywoods that bent, the fiberboards that do not, and the shops — Windsor, Shaker, Arts and Crafts, Biedermeier, Danish, marquetry — that mixed species because each wood had a job.

Arkansas hardwoods read the same way a Rhode Island shop’s boards did. You look at pores, rays, weight, and the part they play. The brand that mills them is irrelevant to the anatomy. It is not irrelevant to the fact that those species are still in the woods.

## The drawer as a confession

Open the top drawer and do the work the label will not do. Sides first. Yellow-poplar in a Mid-Atlantic chest is a green-tinged, even, diffuse-porous wood that planes like a courtesy and takes a nail. White pine in a New England case is softer, with resin canals if you find an end, and a smell that old varnish cannot quite kill. Oak sides in English work are a different ambition: heavier, ring-porous, sometimes a leftover from the same Baltic parcel that faced the carcass (Bowett’s provincial geography still helps). Cypress in a southern piece is a shop that had a wet-climate wood and used it (Hurst and Prown). MDF or a printed vinyl wrap is a marriage. The dovetails, if they exist, will tell you whether a person or a machine spent the morning here: slight irregularity and a scribe line, or a perfect pin from a spindle jig after the First World War.

Bottoms next. A hand-planed bottom, bevelled to fit a groove, with tracks that catch a fingernail, is older than a plywood panel stapled into rabbets. A rotary-cut veneer bottom with a repeating cathedral is 20th-century conversion. Dustboards, if present, are the same test one storey down. A “Queen Anne” highboy with a fiberboard dustboard was assembled after the form was already a reproduction style.

Then the back. Pit-sawn kerf, a slightly irregular band, is a date before the circular mill was ordinary. Circular-saw arcs are 19th century onward. A single sheet of lauan plywood nailed on is a repair or a factory. Glue blocks in the corners — pine, poplar, sometimes oak — are the shop’s handwriting. They are rarely the wood on the tag.

## Light, a thumbnail, and the name on the tag

Take the piece to a window if you can. Rake light across the show face. Pores that are trenches (oak, ash, elm, hickory, teak) catch the light as lines. A satiny diffuse-porous face (maple, cherry, birch, yellow-poplar, a quiet mahogany) will not. Beech and trade sycamore (*Acer pseudoplatanus*) speckle when quartered — a crowded fleck, not oak’s mirrors (Hoadley; Panshin and de Zeeuw). Softwoods announce themselves with latewood bands and, in pines, resin canals. If you came looking for maple and found a canal, stop. You have a conifer.

The thumbnail on an unseen arris is crude and better than a brochure. Pine yields. Hard maple barely does. FPL-GTR-190’s Janka figures (table 5–3a, 12 percent moisture) are laboratory averages. Your fingernail is not that machine. It still sorts the pile. Moisture lies in both directions. A soaked oak offcut dents. A bone-dry pine tavern table can feel proud of itself until July.

A 1760 London bill that says mahogany is a species claim with a port behind it — Jamaican *Swietenia* in Bowett’s reading of the years after the 1721 duty change, unless the bill is more specific. A 1960 American suite that says mahogany may be *Khaya*, sapele, or a photograph on lauan. CITES and the Lacey Act want binomials. Poetry does not move a container. Glued-up tops hide the cut unless you find the end. A 0.6 mm face hides everything except that edge. Read the edge. The species chapters have a place to sit after that walk.
