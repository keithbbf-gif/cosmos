# Chestnut and the blight

Herman Merkel found dying American chestnuts in the New York Zoological Park in 1904. W. A. Murrill described the fungus. The name moved from *Diaporthe parasitica* to *Endothia* to *Cryphonectria parasitica*. Within fifty years the overstorey tree that had been perhaps a quarter of some eastern hardwood stands was gone as timber (Anagnostakis; Prospero and Rigling review, PMC6638123). Furniture shops did not cause the blight. They lost a secondary wood that had been easy, stable, and everywhere from Maine to Georgia.

What you buy now as chestnut is almost always dead already: wormy beams pulled from barns, tannin-plant leftovers, a drawer side in an 18th-century Newport case. The living sprouts still come from old roots and still die back when the canker girdles them. Restoration breeding and a transgenic line at SUNY ESF are forest stories. They are not a mill story yet.

## The wood that was

American chestnut (*Castanea dentata*) is ring-porous, like oak, but lighter and softer. FPL’s older species leaflet and the *Wood Handbook* tradition put it near specific gravity 0.43 and Janka about 540 lbf — poplar’s neighbor in hardness, with a grain that reads as a gentle oak. It split easily, worked easily, held a nail, took stain, and — important for furniture — moved less nastily than some secondary woods. A Fine Woodworking anatomy note (1992) cites volumetric shrinkage around 11.6 percent green to ovendry, kinder than sweetgum or yellow-poplar in that comparison.

Heartwood was durable in the weather for a hardwood of that density: rails, shingles, poles, railroad ties. Tannin extract for leather was an industry. The same tannin made the wood useful and made the blight’s orange cankers a chemical rhyme.

European chestnut (*C. sativa*) is a separate furniture wood: Italian and southern French country pieces, some English work, a ring-porous pale brown. It has blight problems too, and ink disease, but it did not vanish from Europe the way *C. dentata* vanished from American commerce.

## How shops used it

Chestnut was a secondary wood of the first rank in American high-style work. Drawer sides and interior parts of some Newport Townsend and Goddard pieces are chestnut (Fine Woodworking, 1992). That is not a slight. Secondary woods are chosen for stability and cost. Chestnut was both, and it had figure enough to look like a choice when a drawer opened.

It was also a primary wood for cheaper case work, kitchen tables, and the kind of New England and Mid-Atlantic utility furniture that never made Macquoid’s ages. Painted chests. Casket stock. Core stock for plywood, FPL said, when the tree still stood.

Wormy chestnut — powder-post beetle holes in the sap and in dead wood — is a salvage grade. The holes happened after death or in dying trees. Selling “wormy chestnut” as a decorative fashion is honest if the wood is reclaimed. Hunting living small sprouts for that look is vandalism.

## The blight as material culture

The fungus is East Asian. American and European chestnuts had no coevolved peace with it. Spread from New York was on the order of tens of kilometers a year. By the 1920s it was in the southern Appalachians, where the tannin industry and the biggest trees were. By the 1940s the commercial species was finished.

Furniture after that used the dead: standing snags, then barn wood, then the last beams. Mid-century “wormy chestnut” paneling is a grief product. It can be beautiful. It is a cemetery fence.

European furniture in *C. sativa* continues in a smaller way. Do not confuse a Provençal chestnut armoire with Appalachian salvage. The trees are cousins. The histories are not the same crash.

## Workability and finish (reclaimed)

Old chestnut is soft. You dent it with a belt buckle. You sand through an arris. The worm holes collect finish and dirt. A filling of wax or a leave-alone are the two schools. The wood glues if the surface is sound. It is dusty. Nails in old beams may be the only steel left in a house.

Color is brown-tan, sometimes with mineral stain. It does not look like walnut unless you push it. It looks like itself, which is why people want it.

## Conservation and the future tree

The American Chestnut Foundation and university programs breed for resistance (Chinese chestnut crosses, backcrosses) and, separately, SUNY ESF has developed a transgenic tree with a wheat oxalate-oxidase gene; regulatory status has been a moving target (Connecticut DEEP summary of the debate). Hypovirus biocontrol works better in Europe than in North America, a biology story about vegetative compatibility.

None of this returns 18th-century drawer stock this year. A designer specifying chestnut specifies salvage, European *C. sativa*, or a different species. Oak is the lookalike that still grows. It is heavier and harder. Poplar is the workalike for hidden parts. It lacks the tannin durability.

Do not buy “chestnut” carvings from unnamed tropical exporters. The word is a market.

## What to look for

Ring-porous, softer than oak, no big oak rays, worm holes in salvage. Inside a Newport or Philadelphia case: a brown secondary wood that is not poplar’s green. In a 1950s den: paneling with holes. In a nursery catalogue: a sprout, not a board.

The blight belongs in a furniture series because the shop list changed. American makers lost a wood the way they later lost elm and are losing ash. The pieces that remain are the last wide, easy, ring-porous brown boards of a tree that used to be ordinary.

## A drawer that is not the show

Pull a drawer from a Townsend-and-Goddard secretary in the Newport literature — mid-eighteenth century, a façade that wants to be called mahogany — and the sides may be chestnut. Fine Woodworking’s 1992 anatomy note is still the shop paper restorers quote: ring-porous, no oak-sized rays, a brown that is not poplar’s green streak. Winterthur files and the Goddard-Townsend corpus treat that lining as ordinary. A shop that could buy Cuban or Jamaica mahogany for a door still boxed the drawer in what grew on the next ridge.

Hurst and Prown’s *Southern Furniture 1680–1830* is thinner on chestnut as a named show wood. The Mid-Atlantic and southern New England are the documented furniture geography. *Castanea dentata* grew south of the Potomac; the counted case list in southern collections is smaller `[CITE NEEDED: a survey of chestnut secondaries in Williamsburg and related catalogues]`. Do not invent a Charleston parlor in chestnut because the tree map includes the Carolinas. Attribution wants a board, not a range map.

Secondary wood is not a slight. Shops chose it for movement, cost, and the way a nail held. Chestnut split for a riven panel if you asked it to, and it planed for a drawer side if you asked it that instead. The same tree made kitchen tables that never entered Macquoid’s ages and casket stock that left no connoisseur’s essay. FPL’s older uses list — furniture, caskets, core stock for plywood — is a mill list from when the overstorey still stood.

## Rings without the oak mirrors

Oak and chestnut share a ring-porous habit. That is why a flatsawn chestnut board fools people at ten feet. Get closer. White oak and red oak throw broad rays: the flake on a quartered face, the dark lines on a flat one. Chestnut’s rays are fine. The earlywood pores make a spring ring. They do not make a cathedral of ray fleck. Hoadley would send you to the end grain with a hand lens. You need light and the refusal to call every brown ring-porous board oak.

The *Wood Handbook* tradition and FPL’s chestnut leaflet put American chestnut near specific gravity 0.43 and Janka hardness about 540 lbf at 12 percent moisture — yellow-poplar’s neighbor, not oak’s. The dent test will not separate chestnut from tulip poplar. Anatomy and tannin will. Volumetric shrinkage green to ovendry around 11.6 percent is the Fine Woodworking comparison figure, kinder in that table than sweetgum or yellow-poplar. Radial movement still runs below tangential, as with every hardwood. A wide chestnut panel wants a frame. It just fights the frame less than some secondaries.

Heartwood durability outdoors is the other number shops used without saying it. Rails, shingles, poles, railroad ties. The same extract that fed leather tanneries in the southern Appalachians made the wood last in weather better than its density predicted. That is why a barn beam cut about 1890 can still be a table top if you accept beetle holes and a nail.

## The tannin belt and the fifty-year vanishing

The blight did not start in a furniture shop. Herman Merkel found dying American chestnuts in the New York Zoological Park in 1904 — the Bronx Zoo, if you want the later civic name. W. A. Murrill described the fungus. The name moved from *Diaporthe parasitica* to *Endothia parasitica* to *Cryphonectria parasitica* (Anagnostakis; Prospero and Rigling, PMC6638123). East Asian chestnuts had lived with it. American and European trees had no coevolved peace.

Spread from New York was on the order of tens of kilometers a year in the reviews. By the 1920s the southern Appalachians were in it — tannin towns, the biggest boles, extract plants that had been pulling bark and wood for leather. Then they pulled dying trees. Then snags. By the 1940s the commercial species was finished as a mill item. Furniture after that used the dead: standing timber that would not leaf, then barn wood, then the last beams sold as “wormy chestnut” paneling to mid-century dens.

The holes are powder-post work in sap and in dead wood, not a living-tree ornament. Selling reclaimed wormy chestnut is honest if the wood came out of a building. Cutting living root-collar sprouts for that look is a small vandalism against a species that is already a shrub of dieback.

A Provençal armoire in *C. sativa* is a living-wood object. An Appalachian mantel pulled from a cattle barn is a cemetery object. Cousins. Not the same hour.

## At the bench with dead wood

Old chestnut is soft. A belt buckle dents it. A random-orbit sander rounds an arris if you linger. The worm holes collect finish and dirt. Two schools: wax the holes and leave the topography, or fill and sand toward a false smoothness. Glue holds on sound fiber. The dust is fine. Nails left in beams may be the only steel in the house; run a magnet or cut as if every joist has a cut nail and a lag.

Tannin plus water plus a steel fastener makes a blue-black stain. That mark is a chemical rhyme with the blight’s orange cankers, not a defect to bleach out of a salvage top unless the client asks. Color is tan to medium brown, sometimes a grey or mineral cast. It does not look like walnut unless you push a stain. People want it because it looks like itself.

Do not buy nameless “chestnut” carvings off a tropical invoice. The word is a market. Oak is the lookalike that still makes logs. Poplar is the hidden-part workalike. Neither is the tannin tree that used to be ordinary from Maine to Georgia.

The American Chestnut Foundation’s backcross breeding and the SUNY ESF transgenic line (wheat oxalate-oxidase gene; regulatory status a moving target in Connecticut DEEP summaries) are forest programs. Hypovirus biocontrol works better in Europe, a vegetative-compatibility story. None of it is drawer stock this year. A specifier writes salvage, European *C. sativa*, or a different species.
