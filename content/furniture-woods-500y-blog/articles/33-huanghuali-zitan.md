# Huanghuali and zitan

A Ming yoke-back chair in huanghuali is not an English oak chair in another climate. The joinery (mitered bridle joints, floating panels, no glue as a religion), the finish (wax and hand, not French polish), and the trees belong to a Chinese classical tradition that was already mature when Macquoid’s Age of Oak was young. ## Huanghuali

The word names a look as much as a species: a warm, translucent brown-orange, a ghostly figure, a sweet smell in the cut. *Dalbergia odorifera* is the Hainan rosewood at the center of the modern botanical identification. Trade has also used related *Dalbergia* and lookalikes; connoisseurship (Wang Shixiang; Handler; the Museum of Classical Chinese Furniture literature) argues from objects more than from bills of lading.

It is a rosewood in the CITES sense. Appendix II (or I if a given species is listed separately — check). New furniture in true huanghuali is a legal and ethical knot. Antique furniture is the supply that scholars trust.

## Zitan

Zitan is the dark, almost black-purple, extremely dense imperial wood of Qing court taste, earlier used more sparingly. *Pterocarpus santalinus* (red sandalwood) is the species most often attached; other heavy dark woods have been sold under the name. *P. santalinus* is listed (CITES — verify appendix and annotation). The furniture is late, courtly, and heavy in the hand.

Do not confuse zitan with ebonized beech or with wenge. Weight and pore tell.

## Conservation

## What a North Atlantic shop can borrow

Because American and European designers now specify “rosewood” and meet Chinese classical pieces in the same auction week as a Stickley chair. Because the conservation lesson is the same genus story as Brazilian rosewood, with a different shop tradition. Because the joinery is a lesson.

Arkansas walnut and oak will not be huanghuali. They can still teach a floating panel, a mitered frame that lets a panel move, a chair that is hardwood without upholstery. That is the useful borrow. The idea of a hardwood chair that is all joinery and wax is older than the New York collecting boom. It does not require a raid on Hainan.

In a museum: the chair, the incense stand, the wax, the join. In a market: papers, weight, smell, a price that makes sense only if the wood is true — or a price that makes sense only if it is not. This draft will not invent those prices. In a design studio: specify *Juglans nigra* or *Quercus alba* for a new chair in that spirit, or buy an antique with a scholar’s file. The 2017 *Dalbergia* listing and the red-sandalwood files are the legal third rail. Species+ is the living page.

Handler keeps returning to luminosity: a hardwood that reads as light in the room even when the species is dense. That is a finish and a figure fact, not a metaphor. Wang’s connoisseurship pages teach the same objects from construction — the miters, the floating panel, the absent glue as a habit. A reader who has just come from the oak and mahogany chapters will recognize the physics and miss the proportions if they only look for a splat.

Macquoid gave English furniture four ages of timber. China’s classical hardwoods were already a canon. The series puts them in the same 500 years so a reader walking from a yoke-back to a splat-back can see two answers to the same physics.
