# Iroko and the teak lookalikes

Iroko (*Milicia excelsa*, *M. regia*) is a West and Central African hardwood that shops sell when they want teak’s outdoor manners without teak’s price or papers. Ring-porous, yellow-brown to brown, durable, a Janka often cited near 1,260 lbf — oak’s neighbor. It has been called African teak in catalogues. It is not *Tectona*. The pore and the color rhyme; the oil and the smell do not quite.

This chapter is the honest substitute: what iroko does, what other brown outdoor woods do, and when the substitution is a good shop decision rather than a dodge.

## Iroko in the shop

It works with a moderate fight, sometimes with a dust that irritates (chlorophorin and related compounds are shop-lore allergens `[CITE NEEDED: a medical or FPL dust note]`). It glues better than oily teak. It finishes well. Outdoors it weathers grey. Indoors it can look like a quieter teak or a brown ash.

Furniture: garden chairs, kitchen worktops in European shops, church pews, lab benches, the late-20th-century “teak” patio set that was not. Some contemporary indoor furniture uses it as a show wood on purpose.

## Other lookalikes

Afrormosia (*Pericopsis elata*) is a closer visual cousin to teak for furniture, finer, and CITES Appendix II. Do not treat it as an unlisted bargain.

Cumaru, ipe, and the South American deck woods are harder, heavier, and a different aesthetic — more iron than oil. They make furniture that feels like a deck. Fine if wanted.

Robinia (black locust, *Robinia pseudoacacia*) is a temperate outdoor hardwood of high durability, yellow-green when fresh, grown in Europe and America. It is a true alternative for benches, not a teak clone.

White oak is the domestic outdoor furniture wood of the tyloses story. It does not look like teak. It lasts if designed to drain.

Rubberwood (*Hevea*) is a plantation indoor wood, often stained “teak.” It is not outdoor-durable.

## History

Iroko enters European trade with the same colonial concession map as *Khaya*. It was a decking and joinery wood, then a furniture wood as teak fashion outran teak supply. It has no 18th-century London age. It has a 1960s–2000s garden-center age.

## Conservation

*Milicia* has been logged hard in parts of West Africa. IUCN and national statuses vary by species and country. Not as legally famous as *Dalbergia*. Still a Lacey name problem if the crate says “teak.” FSC exists. Ask.

Afrormosia is the lookalike that can get you a seizure at the border if you ignore Appendix II.

## What to look for

Teak color without teak oil, African origin, garden furniture, a worktop, a crate that says African teak. A domestic outdoor bench in locust or white oak: the better named choice if you live in Arkansas.

## Working a tannin-brown outdoor wood

Iroko can stain metal and skin in the same tannin family as oak. Wet cloth and steel leave marks. Stainless or bronze outdoors. Quartered stock is less wild. The ring-porous face will telegraph through a thin paint.

Dust collection is the same rule as for the allergenic tropicals. Some shops treat iroko dust as they treat western redcedar or cocobolo: not a wood for a first-day apprentice at an open sander. Glue is kinder than teak’s oily habit. Finish: oil indoors if you want a quieter teak look; leave it raw outdoors if you accept grey. Design that drains beats a finish that claims immortality.

At the bench, ring-porous brown without teak’s grease, African origin if the tag is honest, a hardness near oak — those are the tells. A crate that says African teak is a crate that failed the noun test. Write *Milicia excelsa* or *M. regia*, country, cut.

## An Arkansas bench and the wish

The series includes iroko so teak’s chapter does not have to carry every brown garden chair, and so a buyer can say the binomial instead of the wish. Arkansas does not grow *Milicia*. It grows white oak and, in places, black locust. Those are the better named choices for a bench that will sit in rain beside a Delta cypress or an Ozark oak house.

A designer who wants teak’s outdoor manners without teak’s papers is looking at iroko (named), locust, white oak, or cypress — not at a stain. A designer who wants the 1960s interior honey is looking at a named *Tectona* or at walnut, not at iroko pretending. The garden-center age made iroko ordinary. Ordinary is fine. The word teak on that ordinary chair is not.

A European kitchen worktop in iroko — the late-century fashion in British and Dutch shops — is a useful object lesson. The wood takes the knife and the wet cloth better than beech; it is not a butcher-block maple and it is not a teak oil advertisement. Church pews and lab benches used the same durability without asking the board to look like a 1958 Danish sideboard. Those jobs are why a mill kept *Milicia* on the rack after the patio set went out of fashion.

Ports that handled the export — the same West African coast that sent *Khaya* and sapele — do not make the wood Jamaican mahogany and do not make it Burmese teak. Bowett’s 1721 Act is a different statute. CITES 2017 is a different genus. Iroko lives in the gap those two famous dates do not cover. The gap still wants a binomial.
