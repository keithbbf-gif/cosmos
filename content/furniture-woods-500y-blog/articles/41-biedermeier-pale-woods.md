# Biedermeier pale woods

After Napoleon, the Central European bourgeois interior went pale. Birch with a butter polish, pear like a closed silk, cherry a little warmer, maple, a black inlay line, columns and lyres and a commode that looked expensive without looking imperial. The name Biedermeier is later and half-mocking. The woods are local. This is Macquoid’s satinwood age translated off the ship and onto the northern tree.

## The palette

Birch — often *Betula pendula* and its figured forms, including Karelian masur — is the frequent face. Pear is the finer, even face. Cherry when a rose heat was wanted. Ebonized pear or stained hardwood for the black. Walnut appears, especially later and in darker rooms, but the postcard Biedermeier is pale.

## Vienna after the Congress, Berlin after the army

The Congress of Vienna closed in 1815. The Central European bourgeois interior, for a generation, went pale. The name Biedermeier is later — a half-mocking coinage from the 1850s satirical figure Gottlieb Biedermaier (the spelling wandered) — and it stuck to furniture that was already thirty years old. The dates shops use, 1815–1848, run from the peace to the revolutions. The woods did not wait for the name.

Berlin, Prague, Munich, and a string of smaller residences made the same argument with local trees: birch, pear, cherry, maple, a black line, columns and lyres and a commode that looked expensive without looking imperial. Museums in those cities hold the type. A Viennese commode in figured birch with ebonized colonnettes is the postcard. A Prague secretary in pear is the quieter cousin.

They used veneer on cheaper grounds, like everyone else — pine, beech, a local oak. They also used solid in smaller forms: chairs, a work table, a sewing stand. The polish is high. The grain is quiet compared with oak. The room wanted silk, not a ray flake.

## The pale list, with numbers

Birch — often *Betula pendula* and its figured forms, including Karelian masur from Finnish and Russian Karelia — is the frequent face. The *Wood Handbook* tables the American cousins (yellow birch SG about 0.62, Janka about 1,260 lbf). European furniture birch sits in that neighborhood: fine, diffuse-porous, pale, a butter color under a spirit polish. Karelian figure is a growth peculiarity, almost marble, a luxury veneer from particular trees. You cannot order it by the cubic meter and mean it.

Pear (*Pyrus communis* and related orchard woods) is the finer, even face — closed silk, almost no pore to the fingernail. Fruitwood chapters in this series give the shop manners: dense, often twisted, specific gravity commonly cited near 0.65–0.75, Janka in the hard-maple neighborhood, but orchard wood varies so wildly that a handbook average is a rumor `[CITE NEEDED: FPL does not table orchard pear as a commercial lumber]`. Ebonized pear is the black line and the black capital. Repair in kind. A painted maple line is a different sentence.

Cherry, here mostly European wild cherry (*Prunus avium*, Kirsche, cerisier), when a rose heat was wanted. American black cherry (*P. serotina*, SG 0.50, Janka 950 lbf) is the New World cousin, not the Viennese default. Both darken in light. A Biedermeier cherry that is still pale may have lived behind a curtain, or may have been refinished.

Maple (*Acer* spp., including the European sycamore maple *A. pseudoplatanus*) for a colder pale. Walnut appears, especially later and in darker rooms. The postcard Biedermeier is still birch and pear.

Black is usually ebonized local wood, not Gabon ebony. Pewter and brass turn up as line inlay. The ébéniste’s tropical palette — kingwood, tulipwood, satinwood — is not this room’s shopping list. That is the political and money fact: mahogany after the Napoleonic wars was a problem of supply and of taste. Local pale woods were the answer a Berlin shop could write on an invoice.

## Satinwood’s cousin, not satinwood

English satinwood rooms of 1780–1810 are the fashionable cousin: more yellow, more imported (*Chloroxylon* and the West Indian woods of that chapter), more painted swag. Macquoid’s *Age of Satinwood* is the English periodization. Hepplewhite and Sheraton plates are the English grammar. Biedermeier is what a Viennese or Berlin shop did with the forest it had when mahogany was a political and a money problem.

American Federal pale is often figured maple and cherry, mahogany still at the top of the house. A Baltimore card table with holly stringing is a linear art, not a Viennese commode. The birch chapter and the fruitwood chapter are the anatomy. This chapter is the room.

Do not sand a faded Biedermeier veneer to “fresh gold.” The surface is the object. The butter color is a polish and a century of light, not a stain you can get back with a belt. Black lines are often ebonized pear. A later PVA fill in a missing line is a conservation event.

## Shop behavior

A true pear commode has weight and a surface like stone. Reproductions in pale birch ply are a shape. A new “Biedermeier” in stained maple from a container is a poster: thick legs, a lyre laser-cut, a finish that looks like candy. Look at the underside. Look at the ground. A pine ground with hide glue and a birch face that has moved and been allowed to is the historic sandwich. An MDF ground with a paper birch print is the engineered chapter wearing a column.

When you repair, support the ground first. Central heating has cracked more Biedermeier faces than bad taste. Protein glue lets go in a damp room and in a dry one it can go brittle. A specialist. Not a kitchen-table refinisher with a dark wax.

Karelian figure is expensive and often veneer for a reason. Solid masur birch in large sizes is a unicorn. Do not plane it to “even out” the marble. The marble is the point.

Spirit varnish and a French-polish habit gave the butter surface. A later polyurethane is a different object: thicker, colder, a plastic skin on a pale face. When a client wants “that Viennese glow,” they want the thin film and the wood’s own yellowing, not a candy coat. Columns and lyres are the fashionable hardware of the type; a shop that machines them from a CNC file and glues them onto a box has made a poster. Look at how the capital meets the frieze. A knife-cut mitre in ebonized pear is the historic join. A blob of black filler is not.

## Afterlife

20th-century collectors made the type expensive. Dealers in Vienna and New York taught a generation to say Biedermeier the way an earlier generation said Chippendale. Scandinavian pale modern — Aalto’s birch, later IKEA’s better lines — is not Biedermeier, but a northern taste for light wood runs through both. Danish teak is a refusal of that pale as much as a continuation of the craft. The Aalto chapter and the Danish chapter return to that argument.

The series includes this frame so satinwood does not stand as the only pale classical story, and so birch and pear have a room to sit in. A designer who specifies a pale dining room in Arkansas maple and cherry is not making Biedermeier. They are making the American Federal and Pleasant Hill version of the same wish: local trees, a quiet grain, a dark line if you want one. Name the wish. Do not name the wrong city.
