# Baltic deal and painted pine

English joiners said “deal” the way they said “wainscot”: a traded size and a quality, not a botanical mood. Deal was a sawn softwood board, often Scots pine (*Pinus sylvestris*) or spruce (*Picea abies*), shipped from Norway, Sweden, Finland, and the Russian Baltic. It built floors, shop fittings, the carcases under walnut veneer, and whole painted interiors. The furniture of northern Europe that is not oak is, a great deal of the time, this wood with a brush on it.

## The trade

The Dutch and the English ran on Baltic timber as they ran on Baltic oak. Sound Toll registers and later timber-auction records at the Zaan and in London are the paper trail (the same KNOB and Haneca world as wainscot, with different cargoes). “Yellow deal” and “red deal” were color and port grades of pine. White deal was often spruce. A London cabinetmaker’s bill that says deal means the cheap, stable, imported board — the yellow-poplar of Britain.

Gillows and other northern English firms bought Baltic oak and Baltic deals on the salt-and-cloth return voyages (Bowett, provincial woods). Furniture in Lancaster could be mahogany show and deal hide, or painted deal throughout.

## The wood

Scots pine in northern, slow-grown grades is an even, resinous, workable furniture pine, harder than *P. strobus*. Spruce is lighter, less resinous, a painter’s friend and a soundboard wood. Both dent. Both take nails. Both move less than oak.

Identification on a painted antique is a scraped edge and a resin-canal search. Do not scrape a museum piece. On a house-owned cupboard, a chip in the back tells you.

## Paint as the species

Scandinavian and North German painted furniture — rosemaling, blue-grey Gustavian, the peasant cupboard — is a finish tradition on local and traded pine and spruce. The Gustavian pale grey of late-18th-century Sweden is a classical fashion in paint, a Baltic answer to satinwood that does not need satinwood. Russian and Finnish painted work belongs here too.

English painted pine of the 18th and early 19th centuries — dressing tables, cheap chairs, later the “fancy chair” — used deal and beech and birch. Sheraton’s cheaper cousins lived here.

The paint is linseed, milk, distemper, later industrial enamel. Lead in old layers. Overpaint is a palimpsest. Stripping a Gustavian cupboard to “natural pine” is a period error unless you like 1960s taste.

## Under the veneer

William-and-Mary and Georgian carcases: oak for strength, deal for backs and dustboards. A walnut oyster box with a deal inside is correct. An English “oak coffer” that is deal throughout with oak graining is a different, later, and common object. Graining is a skill. It is also a disguise.

## 19th and 20th centuries

Steam-sawn Nordic pine became the whitewood of Europe’s factories. Thonet is beech; the packing crates were deal. IKEA’s ancestors are this forest: painted, stained, then foil-wrapped. The engineered chapter takes the foil. The tree under the early cheap furniture was still *Pinus* and *Picea*.

## Shop notes

Deal in old English houses can be wide and slow-grown — a grade you cannot buy new. Repair with the closest northern pine, not with southern yellow pine’s loud latewood, unless you want the patch to shout.

Knots in furniture-grade Baltic were once selected against. Knotty Nordic is a modern taste.

## Conservation

Boreal forestry is a climate and old-growth argument, not a CITES appendix. FSC and PEFC appear on Nordic pine for a reason. Antique deal is reuse. New “reclaimed Baltic pine” should have a story that is not a container of mixed softwood.

## What to look for

Paint layers, light weight, a London carcase, a Swedish grey cupboard, yellow/red deal in a joiner’s account. Graining that follows a fantasy oak. A spruce drawer bottom that is still bright after two hundred years in the dark.

The series splits pine into three chapters because a Connecticut blanket chest, a Savannah heart-pine floor, and a Stockholm painted cupboard are not one material. They share dents and paint. They do not share ports.
