# Lime, basswood, and the carver

Grinling Gibbons cut lime. The cascades of lacewood flowers at Hampton Court and St. James’s — the ones that look like they would bruise — are *Tilia*, the European lime, not the citrus. American basswood is the same genus, *Tilia americana*: pale, light, even, a wood that holds a knife cut and does not fight the grain. Furniture uses it where the show is the carving or the paint, not the pore.

The *Wood Handbook* puts basswood at about SG 0.37 and Janka 410 lbf — among the softest commercial hardwoods, pine’s neighbor, softer than yellow-poplar. It is not a chair-seat wood unless the chair is painted and the sitter is kind. It is a carver’s wood, a Venetian-blind wood, a honey-section wood, a piano-key (some grades) and drawing-board wood.

## The wood

Diffuse-porous, almost invisible grain, sap and heart both pale. Soft enough to dent with a fingernail. Straight logs. Cheap in the American yard relative to walnut. European lime (*T. cordata*, *T. platyphyllos*) is the historic carving timber of Britain and the Low Countries. German *Linde* is the same idea.

It dries with little trouble for a hardwood. It moves less dramatically than beech. It has no outdoor life.

## Workability and finish

Gouges love it. It cuts across the grain without crumbling like a cheap pine and without the muscle of maple. Undercuts stay crisp if the tool is sharp. Dull tools bruise it.

Paint and gesso were the court finishes: frames, applied ornaments, the carved drops that were glued onto oak or pine carcases. Bare lime yellows. Stain is blotchy and pointless. Gilding over gesso is the high finish.

In American factories basswood was a core, a painted bedroom-suite wood, a cheap drawer side. It still is a carving-class wood and a model-maker’s wood.

## History

Medieval polychrome sculpture in northern Europe is often lime or oak, depending on region. Baroque decorative carving in England after the Restoration made lime famous because Gibbons and his shop used it at a scale that still stops people in doorways. The V&A and the royal palaces hold the evidence. The carcase behind the lace may be oak or pine. The lace is lime.

American architectural carving — capitals, mantel ornaments — used basswood and yellow-poplar. Ship carvers used whatever was light and available; white pine is the other half of that sentence.

Furniture in the solid basswood that shows the wood is rare in high-style work. Painted country pieces exist. A “solid walnut” Victorian bed with basswood applied carvings is a factory ordinary.

## Compared with poplar and pine

Yellow-poplar is harder and has mineral color; it carves well but not as indulgently. White pine carves well and is even softer in some grades; it has resin and rings that telegraph through paint. Lime/basswood is the even, ringless cut. Boxwood is the opposite: hard, tiny, for the finest detail. Carvers keep both.

## Shop notes

Basswood dust is fine. The wood bruises in clamps. Use pads. Glue holds. Nails hold too well and crush.

Do not use it for a table top that will see keys and plates. Do use it for a carved crest that will be gilded.

European lime from urban trees can hide nails and a life of pollution. Same as orchard fruitwood: cut carefully.

## Conservation

*Tilia* species in commerce are not CITES. American basswood is a managed northern hardwood. European limes are street and park trees as much as forest trees. Using a taken-down civic lime for carving is a good death. Stripping a double row of limes for a kitchen island is a bad joke; the wood is wrong for the island anyway.

## What to look for

Pale, even, soft, carved lace, gesso, a painted suite that dents. Gibbons-style drops. A model airplane. A drawer side that is too soft to be poplar. On an invoice: basswood vs aspen vs poplar — weight and a fingernail.

The series includes the carver’s woods so a reader who only thinks in oak and mahogany can see why a palace door has a different genus glued to it. Arkansas yards may stock basswood as a northern import. The carving habit does not require a local tree. It requires a cut that holds a leaf.

## Lime, poplar, pine, box

Yellow-poplar is harder and has mineral color; it carves well but not as indulgently. White pine carves well and is even softer in some grades; resin and rings telegraph through paint. Lime and basswood are the even, ringless cut. Boxwood is the opposite pole: hard, tiny, for the finest detail and for a knob that must survive a hand. Carvers keep both. Roubo’s carving-wood remarks belong on the same shelf as the V&A Gibbons labels.

Basswood dust is fine. The wood bruises in clamps; use pads. Glue holds. Nails hold too well and crush the fiber. Do not use it for a table top that will see keys and plates. Do use it for a carved crest that will be gilded. European lime from urban trees hides nails and a life of street grit, the same saw-risk as orchard fruitwood. Cut slowly.

A drawer side that is too soft to be poplar may be basswood. Weight and a fingernail decide faster than a speech. Aspen is in the same pale, soft conversation and then felts under a tool. Specify *Tilia* if the job is a leaf.
