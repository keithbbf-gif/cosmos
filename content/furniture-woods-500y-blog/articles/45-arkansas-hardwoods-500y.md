# Arkansas hardwoods in a five-hundred-year frame

The Ozark and Ouachita hardwood stands are not a footnote to London. They are the same genera that furnished American shops when Philadelphia still bought mahogany at the wharf and the backcountry used what grew. White oak and red oak, hickory and pecan, black walnut, cherry in the cooler coves, ash where the beetle has not finished, shortleaf and loblolly pine, baldcypress in the Delta, eastern redcedar on the glades, yellow-poplar in the east of the state. Bradley Brand works those woods. This chapter is a map, not a catalogue.

## Where the 500 years sit

Before 1800 the timber of this region was a Native woodland economy and then a settler one: buildings, fences, a press, a table, tanbark, fuel. It does not have a Chippendale archive. It has the same physics as a Rhode Island shop: oak that quarters, walnut that planes, pine that paints.

In the 19th century the lower Mississippi and the railroads took the best logs toward cities. Heart pine and cypress from the wet south, oak and hickory from the hills. Furniture factories elsewhere ate some of it. Local furniture stayed vernacular: pine and poplar painted, oak when the piece had to last.

In the 20th century Arkansas was a hardwood state in the national tally — flooring, pallets, the unglamorous grades — and a pine state in the south. Furniture-grade walnut and cherry were always the minority log. They still are.

In the present, a shop that quarters white oak here is doing what a Gdansk splitter and a Stickley mill did, on a second-growth ring count. The Baltic wainscot story does not have to be imported for the ray to be real.

## What the woods want

White oak: tyloses, flake, outdoor manners if designed to drain, the Arts and Crafts face. Red oak: open pores, stain, the 1980s kitchen, still a valid furniture wood if you like it. Hickory: tools and shock, a hard table, not a delicate moulding. Walnut: the Central States gift, sap and heart, no permit. Cherry: the sun will finish it; gum flecks stay. Ash: use the dry stock, do not pretend the living forest is what it was in 1990. Pine: paint, dents, country. Cypress: wet sites, pecky honesty. Cedar: chests, knots, a smell.

Yellow-poplar: the servant wood, as in 1760.

## What not to pretend

This is not Honduran mahogany and should not be stained to lie. It is not Burma teak. It is not huanghuali. It can do Windsor, Shaker, Mission, a Danish-modern oak chair, a walnut dining table, a painted pine cupboard. It cannot do a Riesener flower without imported scraps, and it does not need to.

Second-growth rings are wider than the old Baltic oak. Say so. Kiln schedules are better than a 1710 shed. Say so. The forest is managed and also pressured — oak decline, land use, the ash borer. Say so.

## Buying from this belt

Binomials still help: *Quercus alba* versus *Q. rubra*. Cut: quartered, rift, flat. Grade: the clear wide board is the expensive one because the trees are not old-growth cathedrals. A knot in a country piece is correct. A knot in a formal table is a conversation.

No CITES on these mains. Lacey still applies if the shop ever mixes in an imported ply core. The engineered chapter still applies if a “solid oak collection” is a foil.

## A last walk

Walk a new white-oak table from this timber with the series in your head. End grain: rings and tyloses. Face: flake or cathedral. Finish: is the pore filled like 1890 or open like 1910 or 2020. Movement: how is the top held. Secondary wood: poplar or pine, as in Philadelphia. Compare it, in memory, to a linenfold chest, a fumed settle, a Wegner oak chair. Same genus. Different rooms.

That is the whole education the series was for. Fashion moved. The trees in this state kept making furniture wood. A designer who knows the difference between a ray and a print, a *Swietenia* and a *Khaya*, a cam lock and a draw-bored pin, can use Arkansas hardwoods without costume and without apology.

## Ozarks, Ouachitas, and the river

The Ozark Plateau and the Ouachita Mountains are oak-hickory country with pine on the south slopes and in the plantations. The Mississippi Alluvial Plain — the Delta — is where baldcypress and the wet-site hardwoods sat, and where the best 19th-century logs went onto the water toward New Orleans and the railheads. Michael Williams’s *Americans and Their Forests* is the geography book. Hurst and Prown’s *Southern Furniture 1680–1830* is the reminder that the backcountry used what grew, and that a Chippendale archive is a coastal and a money fact, not a physics fact. The same genera that furnished Philadelphia when the wharf still took Jamaica mahogany furnished a press and a table in a highland county: oak that quarters, walnut that planes, pine that paints.

## The list, with the handbook open

White oak (*Quercus alba* and the white group): SG about 0.68, Janka about 1,360 lbf (FPL-GTR-190). Tyloses, flake on the quarter, outdoor manners if designed to drain, the Arts and Crafts face. A shop that quarters white oak here is doing what a Gdansk splitter and a Stickley mill did, on a second-growth ring count. The Baltic wainscot story does not have to be imported for the ray to be real. Say the rings are wider than the old Riga oak. Say so.

Red oak (*Q. rubra* and the red group): SG about 0.63, Janka about 1,290 lbf. Open pores, stain, the 1980s kitchen, still a valid furniture wood if you like it. Do not fume it and call it Eastwood.

Hickory (*Carya* spp.): shagbark Janka about 1,820 lbf, SG about 0.72 — the hardest of the ordinary eastern hardwoods. Tools and shock, a hard table, not a delicate moulding. Pecan (*C. illinoinensis*) is the sweeter-named cousin, a bit more furniture-facing, still a tough ring-porous wood. Factory “pecan” of the 1960s was often a finish color.

Black walnut (*Juglans nigra*): SG 0.55, Janka 1,010 lbf. The Central States gift. FPL still said about three-quarters of the commercial wood comes from that belt. Arkansas sits on it. Sap and heart, no permit, a plane that behaves. The walnut chapter is the love letter. This chapter is the map.

Black cherry (*Prunus serotina*): SG 0.50, Janka 950 lbf. Cooler coves, a minority log. The sun will finish it. Gum flecks stay. Pleasant Hill already used the southern version of this idea.

Ash (*Fraxinus americana*): SG 0.60, Janka 1,320 lbf. Use the dry stock. The emerald ash borer, found near Detroit in 2002, did not spare this state. Do not pretend the living forest is what it was in 1990.

Eastern white pine is not the Arkansas default. Shortleaf (*Pinus echinata*) and loblolly (*P. taeda*) are the southern pines: louder latewood, paint, dents, country. Longleaf heart pine is a different, older grade, mostly reclaimed. Baldcypress (*Taxodium distichum*): SG about 0.46, Janka about 510 lbf — wet sites, pecky honesty. Eastern redcedar (*Juniperus virginiana*): SG about 0.47, Janka about 900 lbf — chests, knots, a smell, the glades. Yellow-poplar (*Liriodendron tulipifera*): the servant wood, as in 1760, in the east of the state.

## What a shop here can build

Windsor: oak, ash, hickory, maple if shipped or found, pine, elm if any remains. Shaker: cherry, maple, pine, walnut at the Pleasant Hill end of the list. Mission: quartersawn white oak, fumed if you have a tent and a warning. Danish-modern oak chair: yes. Walnut dining table: yes. Painted pine cupboard: yes. Aalto’s birch laminate: the lesson is the bend, not the species. Marquetry of Riesener’s kind: not without imported scraps, and it does not need to.
