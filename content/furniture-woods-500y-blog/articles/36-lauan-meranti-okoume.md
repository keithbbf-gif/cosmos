# Lauan, meranti, okoume

Open a hollow-core door from 1974. The skin is a pink-brown rotary veneer that the hardware store called Philippine mahogany. The tree was likely a *Shorea* or a cousin — lauan, meranti, seraya, the dipterocarp forest of the Philippines, Malaysia, Indonesia. Open an older aircraft or a cheap French panel: the pink might be okoume (*Aucoumea klaineana*) from Gabon, the peelers’ favorite, light, cooperative, the core of a lot of decorative ply. These woods furnished the hidden 20th century. They are why “mahogany” on a motel dresser is a joke, and why Southeast Asian and Central African forests took a furniture-shaped hole.

## Lauan and meranti (*Shorea* and allies)

A cluster of species, not one. Density varies from light utility to something that can pretend to be a show wood. Rotary-cut, they built the postwar world’s cheap faces and cores. “Philippine mahogany” was a trade name that Bowett’s Jamaica wood would not recognize.

Furniture: the entire middle market of 1950–1990. Drawer bottoms, case backs, the veneer under a printed walnut photograph. Some solid meranti doors and outdoor furniture exist; durability varies by species and sapwood. Do not treat meranti as teak.

Conservation: dipterocarp forests are one of the planet’s serious loss stories — logging, oil palm, fire. CITES listings have applied to some *Shorea* (e.g. ramin is *Gonystylus*, a different genus, Appendix II; do not confuse). Many merantis are unlisted and still a Lacey/due-care problem if illegally cut. FSC and national bans matter. The TRAFFIC five-taxa paper’s world is next door.

## Okoume / gaboon (*Aucoumea klaineana*)

Light, salmon-pink, peels into long, even veneers. Aviation in the early 20th century and decorative ply afterward. Gabon and neighboring forests. It works like a dream and dents like a softwood. A show surface in okoume wants a finish and a gentle life.

## What to look for

Pink rotary faces, “Philippine mahogany,” a 1970s credenza, an aircraft ply, a void. A new meranti garden bench sold as teak: weigh it and look at the pores.

Arkansas shops use these woods as imported ply, not as local logs. The local substitute for a cheap core is poplar ply or, if you want the good core, birch. The tropical peeler forest is not a southern hardwood story. It is the story of the furniture most people already own.

## A name that never was a tree

“Philippine mahogany” was a hardware-store sentence, not a binomial. Adam Bowett’s work on Jamaica wood and the 18th-century London trade is the reminder: mahogany, in English, once meant *Swietenia* from the Caribbean. By 1974 the word had been stretched over a pink rotary leaf from a dipterocarp. The pores are different. The weight is different. The smell is different. A motel dresser that says mahogany on the hangtag and shows a wild cathedral grain on a paper-thin face is this chapter, not the Chippendale one.

Lauan (also lauan, red lauan, white lauan in older U.S. yard talk) and meranti and seraya are trade clusters inside *Shorea*, *Parashorea*, and their neighbors — the dipterocarp canopy of the Philippines, Borneo, Sumatra, the Malay Peninsula. Light red meranti, dark red meranti, white meranti, yellow meranti: four colors that are not four species. Density runs from a light utility peeler you can dent with a thumbnail to a heavier board that can pretend to be a show wood. The USDA imported-woods sheets and Kukachka’s FPL-125 tradition treat them as a range, not a single Janka. Secondary compilations put light red meranti near SG 0.40–0.54 and a soft Janka; dark red meranti sits higher `[CITE NEEDED: pin one FPL/Chudnoff line per trade group if the editor tables it]`. Do not treat meranti as teak. Teak is oily, silica-hard, and a different family. Meranti outdoors is a species-and-sapwood question. Some heartwoods last. A lot of garden-center “meranti benches” do not.

The logs left through Manila, then through Sandakan and Tawau on the east coast of Sabah, through Balikpapan and Samarinda, through Singapore as a sorting port. Postwar American and Japanese mills wanted wide, cheap, peelable stems. The furniture factory wanted a face it could print. The two wants met in a rotary lathe.

## Okoume at Port-Gentil

*Aucoumea klaineana*, okoume or gaboon, is a Burseraceae tree of Gabon and neighboring forests, not a dipterocarp. The Chudnoff / FPL African sheet gives a basic specific gravity (ovendry weight / green volume) of 0.37 and an air-dry density around 28 pounds per cubic foot — a lightweight hardwood, salmon-pink, that peels into long, even veneers. Janka numbers in the compilations sit in a soft band, often cited near 270–400 lbf; confirm against a primary FPL table before you publish a single figure `[CITE NEEDED]`. It works like a dream and dents like a softwood. Silica is low compared with teak, but the face can still go woolly under a dull knife.

Aviation wanted that combination before furniture did. Early 20th-century aircraft ply — and later a great deal of decorative European panel — used okoume because the leaf was wide, the glue line behaved, and the sheet was light. Port-Gentil and Libreville were the names on the bills of lading. French and later multinational mills made “gaboon ply” a specification. A show surface in okoume wants a finish and a gentle life. A core of okoume under a walnut face is the 20th-century sandwich in its polite form.

## The factory as a sandwich machine

Cheap, large, peelable logs. Glue likes them. The face could be anything: walnut, a photograph of walnut, a paper foil. Klim’s 1990 WAG paper is again the glue timeline: protein, casein, phenolics for wet work, cheap ureas for interior furniture. Interior lauan ply is often a urea sandwich. Water finds it. A kitchen splash that swells a 1970s drawer side is that chemistry, not a mystery.

Hollow-core doors and void-core ply made the filling thinner. The lauan skin got thinner too. A chip on a 1974 door shows a paper, then air, then a cardboard honeycomb or a few slats. The “Philippine mahogany” was never the door. It was the costume.

Drawer bottoms, case backs, the unseen sides of a credenza — this is where most people already own *Shorea*. Some solid meranti doors and outdoor furniture exist. Durability varies by species and sapwood. A new meranti garden bench sold as teak should be weighed. Look at the pores. Teak’s ring-porous face and oily feel are not meranti’s.

Ramin (*Gonystylus* spp.) is a different genus, a pale utility wood that went onto CITES Appendix II, and a standing confusion in yard talk. Do not fold ramin into meranti. Some *Shorea* have carried listings or national bans; many merantis remain unlisted and are still a Lacey due-care problem if illegally cut. The TRAFFIC / Chatham House five-taxa paper sits next door. FSC and national export bans matter. A certificate on a website is not automatically due care — that argument belongs to the Lacey chapter, but it starts in this forest.

## Shop behavior

Screws in cheap lauan ply crumble the core. The rotary leaf is thin; the inner plies are often voids dressed as wood. Use inserts, or go to birch ply, or design the joint so the fastener lives in a solid rail. A confirmat screw in furniture-grade birch is a different conversation from a drywall screw in a 1970s stereo cabinet.

Okoume dust is fine and pink. It stains clothes. Finish soon; the salmon shifts toward brown in light. A clear film yellows. Oil makes it look like a cheap mahogany, which is how a lot of boat interiors were sold.

Do not restore a printed-lauan suite by sanding. There is nothing under the print. A 0.4 mm rotary face under a walnut photograph is not a veneer you can “take down to fresh wood.” You will take it down to glue.

When you specify new ply, write the face species and the core species. “Walnut ply” is not a specification. *Juglans nigra* face, birch core, interior glue, CARB/TSCA Title VI, is a specification. *Shorea* core, paper-foil oak, urea glue, is also a specification. The honest shop names both.

## What the forest paid

Dipterocarp forests are one of the planet’s serious loss stories — logging, oil palm, fire, the conversion of a peeler stand into a plantation that will never make a 1974 door again. Central African okoume stands have their own pressure and their own certification politics. Buying vintage furniture in these woods is reuse. Buying new meranti with no harvest country on the invoice is how the Lacey Act got a furniture phase.

Arkansas did not grow these trees. Southern shops meet them as imported sheets and as the insides of furniture clients already own. The local substitute for a cheap core is yellow-poplar ply or, if you want the good core, Baltic birch. The tropical peeler forest remains the hidden 20th century. Most living rooms still sit on it.
