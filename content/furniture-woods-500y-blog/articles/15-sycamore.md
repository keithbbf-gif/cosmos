# Sycamore

Say “sycamore” in a London shop in 1790 and you mean *Acer pseudoplatanus*, a maple, pale, sometimes quartered into a lace of small rays, sometimes dyed in iron liquor until it went grey-green and took the name harewood. Say “sycamore” in an Ohio mill in 1890 and you mean *Platanus occidentalis*, the American planetree, a different family, a wood with crowded broad rays that look like oatmeal on the quarter and a reputation for butcher blocks and cheap furniture. The word is a fork. This chapter holds both tines so a buyer does not buy the wrong tree.

## English sycamore: a maple in costume

*Acer pseudoplatanus* is native to central Europe and long naturalized in Britain. It is not *Acer saccharum*. It is softer than hard maple, pale, clean, a turning and veneer wood. Quartered faces show a fleck. The furniture trade loved it when pale woods were the fashion: late 18th-century satinwood rooms, painted furniture, the insides of drawers, food-related wood (it does not scent butter).

Harewood is sycamore (or sometimes other pale maples) dyed. Sheraton and Hepplewhite plates want grey fields and fine inlay. The dye is historic iron-and-tannin chemistry, not a gel stain. Old harewood can fade or go muddy. Conservators treat it as a colored wood, not as a species.

Biedermeier shops used the same pale maple-sycamore group. The Biedermeier chapter overlaps. The English name is the confusion.

Workability: friendly, like a mild maple. Finish: blotch possible, clear coats yellow the white. Movement: ordinary hardwood manners.

## American sycamore: *Platanus*

*Platanus occidentalis* is a river-bottom tree with a mottled bark people know from streets. The wood is reddish-tan, interlocked, with rays that dominate a quartered face — a lace more aggressive than beech. The *Wood Handbook* puts American sycamore around SG 0.49 and Janka 770 lbf. It is not a hard maple. It is not a weak cottonwood.

Interlocked grain tears. Quartered sycamore was sold as a decorative veneer and as drawer sides. It was a cheap furniture wood in the Midwest: painted, stained, hidden. Butcher blocks used it because the end grain and the rays wear in a useful way `[CITE NEEDED: period block-maker catalogues]`.

London plane (*Platanus × acerifolia*) is the street tree of Europe; its wood appears in small work. It is not English furniture “sycamore.”

## Fashion

Harewood is the high-fashion story: 1770–1820 in England, Macquoid’s satinwood age, a grey that set off rosewood banding and yellow satinwood. American *Platanus* never had that hour. It had a utility hour and a brief Arts-and-Crafts-adjacent interest in quartered fleck, then floors and crates.

Contemporary designers sometimes quarter American sycamore for a loud ray face. It photographs well. It moves and tears. Specify quartered if you want the look, and allow for waste.

## Shop notes

If a U.S. invoice says sycamore, ask *Acer* or *Platanus*. The density and the ray size will tell you if the seller cannot. *Platanus* rays are large and crowded; *Acer pseudoplatanus* is a maple anatomy.

Harewood on an antique: do not sand to “fresh wood.” The color is in a shallow layer.

Food-safe reputation of European sycamore is traditional for rolling pins and worktops. Finish choice is the modern half of that tradition.

## Conservation

Neither furniture sycamore is a CITES species. American sycamore is common on wet sites. English sycamore is often treated as a weed tree in British conservation and as a timber tree in the same breath. Plane trees in cities are civic trees, not logs, until they come down.

## What to look for

Grey inlay fields on a Federal or Sheraton piece: harewood, an *Acer*. Oatmeal quartered fleck on a Midwestern drawer: *Platanus*. A pale solid “maple” that is too cheap and too rayed: ask. A butcher block with busy end grain: maybe sycamore, maybe maple — weight and rays.

The series keeps this chapter short on romance and long on names because more furniture has been misidentified under “sycamore” than under almost any other English word except cedar and mahogany.

## Two genera, one invoice

A London cabinetmaker in 1790 who wrote “sycamore” meant *Acer pseudoplatanus*: a maple, pale, sometimes quartered into a small-ray lace, sometimes sent through iron liquor until it went grey-green and took the name harewood. Sheraton’s *Drawing-Book* (1793) and Hepplewhite’s *Guide* (1788) want those grey fields against satinwood and rosewood banding. Macquoid’s Age of Satinwood is the gallery name for that hour. The dye is iron-and-tannin chemistry, a shallow color, not a gel stain. Conservators treat old harewood as a colored wood. Sanding to “fresh wood” erases the fashion.

An Ohio or Indiana mill in 1890 that wrote “sycamore” meant *Platanus occidentalis*, the American planetree, a different family. Crowded broad rays. Oatmeal on the quarter. Interlocked grain that tears under a dull plane. The *Wood Handbook* (FPL-GTR-190) puts American sycamore around specific gravity 0.49 and Janka 770 lbf at 12 percent moisture — harder than yellow-poplar, not a hard maple, not a cottonwood. It was cheap furniture wood, drawer sides, painted case work, and the butcher-block reputation that period catalogues repeat `[CITE NEEDED: a named block-maker’s list]`.

London plane (*Platanus × acerifolia*), the street tree of European cities, is a *Platanus*. Its wood appears in small turned work when a civic tree comes down. It is not English furniture “sycamore.” If a U.S. invoice says sycamore, ask *Acer* or *Platanus*. Ray size decides when the seller cannot. *Platanus* rays are large and crowded. *Acer pseudoplatanus* is maple anatomy: finer fleck, a milder lace.

Biedermeier shops used the pale maple-sycamore group for the same reason English satinwood rooms did: a field that would take polish and sit next to a darker band. The Biedermeier chapter overlaps. The English name is the confusion, not the furniture.

## Harewood in the room

The grey is the high-fashion story: roughly 1770–1820 in England, painted furniture and inlay, a color that set off yellow satinwood and dark rosewood without asking for another tropical log. Old harewood fades or goes muddy. A conservator’s note will say “colored sycamore” or “harewood” and will forbid a scrape. The color lives in a shallow layer. A furniture dealer who “refreshed” a Sheraton card table by sanding the grey fields has destroyed the object’s fashion and left a pale maple.

Food-related wood is the other English habit. *A. pseudoplatanus* does not scent butter. Rolling pins, worktops, the dairy interior — the tradition is real even when the modern half of it is a finish choice (oil, a film, nothing). `[CITE NEEDED: a standard UK timber monograph on sycamore as a dairy wood]`. American *Platanus* got the butcher-block job instead: end grain, busy rays, a wear that does not polish to a closed maple face.

Workability on the maple side is friendly, like a mild *Acer*. Blotch is possible. Clear coats yellow the white. Movement is ordinary hardwood manners. Workability on the *Platanus* side is a fight where the grain interlocks. Quarter it if you want the oatmeal photograph. Allow for waste and for tear-out. Specify the cut. A flatsawn American sycamore board is a different, quieter, less fashionable face.

## What a Midwestern drawer remembers

Quartered *Platanus* as a drawer side is a Midwest and river-town habit: cheap, stable enough, a ray fleck that looks like decoration if you leave it unfinished in the dark of a case. Painted, it disappears. Stained, it can look like a muddy beech. Arts-and-Crafts-adjacent taste had a brief interest in that fleck, then the wood went back to floors and crates. Contemporary designers who quarter it for a loud ray face are buying a photograph. The board will still move. The plane will still tear if the iron is lazy.

Street-tree plane in Europe is a civic tree until it is a log. Furniture from it is opportunistic: a bowl, a small box, a turning. Do not write a dining table in London plane unless you like glue-ups and a story about a storm on a boulevard.

Neither furniture sycamore is a CITES species. American sycamore is common on wet sites. English sycamore is a weed tree in one British conservation sentence and a timber tree in the next. The ethical issue is the name on the ticket, not an appendix.

A butcher block with busy end grain: maybe sycamore, maybe maple — weight and rays. Harewood on an antique: do not sand. Food-safe talk: the species tradition is European sycamore; the finish is the modern half.

The word is a fork. This chapter holds both tines so a buyer does not pay for the wrong tree and so a restorer does not bleach a dye that was the point of the room.

A London plane log from a storm-felled street tree is opportunistic turning stock, not a Sheraton field.
