# Finnish birch and the laminated chair

Alvar Aalto designed a chair for the Paimio tuberculosis sanatorium with a seat that looks like a scroll of paper and is a bent laminate of Finnish birch. The V&A tells the object as a plywood milestone; Artek’s factory (Korhonen, then the Artek system) made the bends a product. Finmar sold them into Britain in the 1930s, and the UK was Finland’s largest furniture export market in those years (Finmar study). The wood is the same genus as Biedermeier birch. The technology is Thonet’s cousin and Eames’s elder. The forest is boreal.

## Why birch

Northern birch peels well, glues well, and is pale in a way that modern interiors wanted. It is not as silica-hard as teak, not as tannic as oak. Thin leaves of it, stacked with the grain alternated or aligned depending on the part, will take a bend that solid oak of the same thickness will not. Aalto’s “bent knee” legs and scroll seats are that fact made polite.

Solid Finnish birch furniture exists too — stools, tables — but the famous objects are laminates. Baltic birch plywood, the many-ply clear core, is the shop descendant: jigs, modern cabinets with exposed edges, the honest ply aesthetic.

## The line from Thonet

Thonet bent solid beech in moulds. Aalto bent stacks of veneer. Both used heat, moisture, and a form. Both needed a forest next to a factory. Moravia had beech; Finland had birch. The café chair and the sanatorium chair are answers to shipping and to sitting, a century apart.

Casein’s slow, water-resistant glue in Artek’s cold-bend processes (Klim) is a reminder that “plywood glue” is not one chemistry. Later production moved. Conservators of Aalto have to ask which.

## Influence

Charles and Ray Eames knew this work. The 1940 MoMA shell and the 1942 splint are American wartime industrializations of a laminated idea. English designers (Day and others) pinned up the DCM. The Finnish chair taught a generation that wood could be modern without being painted metal.

It also taught a market that “Scandinavian” meant pale wood, even when Danish factories were shipping teak.

## Shop notes

Exposed birch ply edges want a finish that does not leave them fuzzy. Water raises the grain of those end-plies with enthusiasm.

Repairs to a broken Aalto knee are specialist work. Do not sister a pine block onto a laminate scroll and call it conservation.

New “Aalto-style” chairs in mixed ply from unnamed mills are a shape, not a forest. The birch chapter and this one both ask you to count plies.

## Conservation

Boreal birch is a managed industrial forest, tied to paper as much as to Artek. Old-growth arguments in Finland and Russia are real and are not solved by a stool. Certification exists. Vintage Artek is reuse. The species is not CITES.

## What to look for

A scroll seat, a bent knee, pale laminates, a 1930s Finmar label, a many-ply edge. A stained “walnut” version of the same shape: a different sentence.

Arkansas has no *Betula pendula* forest. The lesson a southern shop takes is the laminate: poplar and oak veneers can bend too. The species credit for the famous chair stays Finnish.

## The Paimio chair in a room

The Paimio tuberculosis sanatorium, in the pine and birch country southwest of Turku, opened in 1933. Alvar Aalto and Aino Aalto designed the building and the furniture as one argument: pale rooms, washable surfaces, a chair that would not feel like hospital iron. The armchair now called 41, or the Paimio chair, was drawn in 1931–32 and made by Otto Korhonen’s Huonekalu- ja Rakennustyötehdas in Turku. Two closed birch loops form the arms and the runners. Between them a single scroll of laminated birch — seat and back in one sheet — hangs like a piece of paper that learned to hold a person. The V&A’s plywood history treats that scroll as a milestone: molded ply as furniture, not as a hidden core. The museum’s own Aalto holdings (among them a Finmar-imported birch chair, W.41-1987, and related Korhonen-Aalto stacking types) let you walk around the grain instead of reading a caption.

Patients sat in those loops. The wood is cool in a Finnish winter room and warm compared with tubular steel. That is not a slogan. It is why Aalto kept arguing with Marcel Breuer’s metal chairs in the early 1930s. Birch was the local answer to metal fashion.

## Korhonen, Artek, Finmar

Otto Korhonen had been bending and stacking chairs in Turku since the 1910s. Aalto met him on the Southwestern Agricultural Cooperative building and then on Paimio. The factory, not the drawing board, is where the closed loop and the later “bent knee” or “Doric” leg were made repeatable. Aalto later called the standard L-leg a column. The shop called it a sawn and bent laminate that had to come out of the mould without spring-back that would ruin a stool.

Artek was founded in Helsinki in 1935 by Alvar Aalto, Aino Aalto, Maire Gullichsen, and Nils-Gustav Hahl, to sell the furniture as a system and to keep the art-and-industry talk attached to actual invoices. Korhonen kept making the pieces. The brand and the benches were not the same thing.

Britain saw the work before Artek existed as a company. Philip Morton Shand, who wrote for the *Architectural Review* and had met Aalto at the Stockholm Exhibition of 1930, put Aalto’s plywood furniture into Fortnum & Mason in November 1933 under the title *Wood Only*, timed with an English-Finnish trade week. Orders from that show were enough that Shand and Geoffrey Boumphrey formed Finmar Ltd in 1934 as the UK importer. Heal’s and Bowman’s sold the chairs. Jack Pritchard of the Venesta plywood company walked the Korhonen shop and Paimio in 1935 and went home to found Isokon. The Finmar study still used by architectural historians says the UK was Finland’s largest furniture export market in those years. A stamp on the underside — FINMAR LTD / MADE IN FINLAND — is the object-level proof. Look for it before you believe a dealer’s “1930s Aalto.”

## What birch is, as a number

The peeler logs behind those chairs are European silver birch and downy birch, *Betula pendula* and *B. pubescens*: pale sap, fine diffuse-porous texture, a tree that paper mills already knew how to peel. The USDA *Wood Handbook* (FPL-GTR-190) tables the American cousins, not a single Finnish mill run. Yellow birch (*B. alleghaniensis*) sits at specific gravity about 0.62 and Janka about 1,260 lbf at 12 percent moisture — near hard maple, a little kinder, and it “shrinks considerably” in the kiln. Paper birch (*B. papyrifera*) is lighter (SG about 0.55, Janka about 910 lbf). Finnish furniture birch behaves in that neighborhood: hard enough for a stool rail, pale enough for a modern room, and — this is the industrial fact — it peels into long, even leaves. A published FPL-equivalent Janka for *B. pendula* as a Finnish furniture grade is not the number shops quote `[CITE NEEDED]`. The genus credit is safe.

Birch is not oak. It has no tyloses story and no ray flake. It is not teak: no oil, no silica tax of that kind. It glues. It takes a clear film that yellows toward cream, which is why mid-century owners either loved the honey or fought it with water-white varnish. Water raises the end-grain of a plywood edge as if the board were a brush.

Karelian or masur birch — the marble figure of Biedermeier luxury — is a growth peculiarity, not a separate order. Aalto’s factory chairs are ordinary, clear birch, stacked. The luxury is the bend, not the figure.

## Glue, heat, and the bent knee

Thonet’s No. 14, from 1859, is solid beech, steamed and forced into an iron mould. Aalto’s scroll and L-leg are stacks of veneer, glued, then bent. Both need heat, moisture, and a form. Both need a forest next to a factory. Moravia and the Austrian beech woods fed Thonet; the Finnish boreal belt fed Korhonen. The café chair and the sanatorium chair are answers to shipping and sitting, a century apart.

Susan Klim’s 1990 Wooden Artifacts Group paper still helps a conservator more than a catalogue essay: protein glues, then casein, then the cheap ureas. Artek’s cold-bend work used casein — slow, reasonably water-resistant, a dairy chemistry, not a formaldehyde one. Later production moved. A broken knee on a 1930s stool and a broken knee on a 1980s reissue are not the same repair. Ask which glue. The V&A plywood history is the public version of that timeline; Klim is the bench version.

The L-leg, standardized on Stool 60 in 1933, is a closed birch loop sawn so that the grain of the upright turns into the grain of the seat rail. It looks like a solid piece that changed its mind. It is a laminate that was taught a corner. Later Y-legs (1947) and X-legs (1954) are the same idea cut into a fan. A shop that sisters a pine block onto a failed knee has not conserved the chair. It has changed the sentence.

## Shop behavior

Count the plies on an exposed edge. Baltic birch of the jig-and-cabinet grade is many thin, clear leaves, few voids — often thirteen plies in an 18 mm sheet, though mills vary and you should count, not recite. A “birch” plywood with a pretty face and a core of mixed rotary tropicals is a different product. Weigh the board. Tap it. A void sounds like a drum.

When you cut Baltic birch, the saw fuzzes the end-plies. A light sanding and a finish that does not raise the grain again is the whole edge ritual. Water-based finishes are enthusiastic about that fuzz. Oil darkens the edge lines and makes the sandwich readable, which is the honest modern look.

Do not belt-sand a vintage Aalto scroll. The face veneer is thin. The curve is the object. A white ring from a wet glass is a finish problem; a delaminated leaf is a glue problem. Protein and casein failures can sometimes be re-adhered with the right adhesive and a form. A home-center yellow glue and a C-clamp across a scroll will telegraph forever.

New “Aalto-style” chairs from unnamed mills arrive in mixed ply, often a tropical core, often a stain that wants to be walnut. The shape is not a forest. The birch chapter and this one both ask you to count plies and to read the underside stamp.

## After 1940

Charles and Ray Eames knew this work. The 1940 MoMA “Organic Design in Home Furnishings” experiments with Eero Saarinen wanted a compound-curved shell; the war interrupted, then supplied the 1942 molded-plywood splint contract for the U.S. Navy that taught the moulds. The 1946 DCM and LCW are American industrializations of a laminated idea that Aalto had already made polite. Robin Day and other English designers pinned up the DCM. The Finnish chair had already taught a generation that wood could be modern without being painted metal.

It also taught a market that “Scandinavian” meant pale wood, even when Danish factories in the 1950s were shipping teak. Aalto’s birch and Wegner’s oak are cousins. Juhl’s teak is a refusal of that pale as much as a continuation of the craft. The Danish chapter returns to that argument.

Vintage Artek and Finmar pieces are reuse of an already-cut tree. The species is not CITES-listed. Boreal birch is a managed industrial forest, tied to paper as much as to furniture. Old-growth arguments in Finland and in Russian Karelia are real and are not solved by a stool. Certification exists. A new birch ply from a named mill with a grade stamp is a specification. A crate that says only “wood” is the lauan problem in a northern accent.
