# Maple

A Windsor chair’s legs are often maple because maple turns clean and wears like a tool. A Federal card table’s apron may be birdseye because someone opened a log and found a constellation. A bowling alley is maple because the Janka number is 1,450 lbf and the fiber does not fuzz under feet. Same genus. Three jobs. Fashion only sometimes noticed.

## Hard, soft, and the English “sycamore”

Hard maple is *Acer saccharum* (sugar, rock) and *A. nigrum* (black). The *Wood Handbook* (FPL-GTR-190) gives sugar maple specific gravity 0.63 and Janka 1,450 lbf — among the hardest of the ordinary American furniture woods, in a class with white oak and above cherry and walnut. Heartwood is light reddish brown; sapwood is wide and white. Texture is fine and even. Grain is usually straight until it is not.

Soft maple is mostly *A. rubrum* (red) and *A. saccharinum* (silver). Red maple: SG 0.54, Janka 950 lbf — cherry’s neighbor. It machines well and takes paint and stain. It is not a failed hard maple. It is a different ticket.

European furniture “sycamore” is *Acer pseudoplatanus*, a maple. Quartered, it can be pale and ray-flecked. Dyed grey it became harewood for Hepplewhite and Sheraton inlay. American sycamore is *Platanus occidentalis*, a different family; see that chapter. The word is a trap.

Bigleaf maple (*A. macrophyllum*) on the Pacific coast has its own figured trade. It is a regional story.

## Figure is not a species

Birdseye, curly (fiddleback), quilted, blistered — these are grain deviations, more common in hard maple but not exclusive to it. A shop pays for figure by the leaf. A kitchen island in plain hard maple is still hard maple. Catalogues that say “tiger maple” are selling curl. The Federal inlay tradition used the pale sap and the figure as a substitute for satinwood when satinwood was a ship away.

Figured maple is difficult. Tear-out on curl is a religion of sharp blades and light cuts. Filling birdseye so it does not pit takes patience. The reward is a surface that photographs like water.

## Workability and finish

Plain hard maple is tough on tools and clean in the cut when the irons are right. It turns: tool handles, chair parts, kitchenware. It resists denting. It is a butcher-block wood because it is tight and does not flavor food the way oak’s tannin can.

Blotch in stain is the finishing quarrel, worse on soft maple. Dye and a finish that does not pretend the wood is walnut will treat you better. The mid-century “blonde” maple look was often a clear finish on sapwood, sometimes with a light toner. Colonial Revival “maple” bedroom suites were often a red-orange stain on whatever pale hardwood the factory had.

Maple does not like outdoor life. It has no oak-tyloses story and no teak oil story.

## America: chairs, Shakers, and the blonde dining room

Turned maple is an eastern North American habit from the 17th century on. Chair shops in Philadelphia, New York, and New England bought maple by the wagon. Nancy Goyne Evans’s Windsor work keeps finding maple in the turned members even when the seat is elm or pine and the bow is oak.

Shaker work — Hancock, Sabbathday Lake, Pleasant Hill — uses maple for the pieces that must stay true: chairs, stands, peg rails sometimes, the understructure of tables. Cherry and pine share the load. The aesthetic of pale wood in a clear finish is partly maple’s sapwood.

Federal and Empire shops used figured maple as a show veneer and as solid for smaller forms. A Connecticut cherry chest with maple inlay is a species conversation, not a single-wood object.

The 20th century split. Factory “maple” became a stain color. School furniture and gym floors kept using the real hard maple. Then Scandinavian influence and American blonde modern put pale maple back in the dining room, next to the teak that was getting the magazine covers.

## Europe: pale, dyed, and quiet

Biedermeier used maple when birch was not the local tree. English satinwood-age furniture used harewood (*A. pseudoplatanus* dyed) as a grey field for inlay. French *érable* appears in provincial and in some Parisian work, never as the mahogany of the house.

Thonet is beech. Do not credit maple with the café chair. Maple would have done some of the turning; it would not have been the steam-bent price point in Moravian forests.

## Shop notes

Hard maple and nails are a fight. Predrill. Screws too.

The white sapwood yellows in UV. A “white maple” kitchen in a south window will cream. Water-white finishes slow this; they do not repeal it.

Ambrosia maple — beetle streaks — is a color fashion of the last twenty years. It is wound response, not a separate tree. Fine if you like it. Not an antique grade.

## Conservation

Sugar maple is a managed northern hardwood. Climate and sugar-bush economics affect the furniture log. Soft maple is abundant in many eastern stands. Neither is a CITES species. The conservation conversation is ordinary forestry, and the fashion for wide live-edge slabs that a sugar maple does not want to give.

## What to look for

End grain: diffuse pores, no vessels like oak, no resin canals. Weight: hard maple surprises people who thought “pale means pine.” Figure: look across the light. On Windsors: turned legs that take a crisp bead. On 1940s suites: a color called maple that chips to a different wood.

Arkansas is not Vermont. Hard maple is a northern commercial center (FPL: Middle Atlantic and Great Lakes, about two-thirds of lumber). Soft maple and scattered hard maple still enter southern shops. A designer specifying maple in the South is often specifying shipped stock. Say hard or soft. Say figured or plain. The word alone is a fog.

## School desks, gym floors, and the word on the tag

The 20th century split the name. School furniture and floors kept using hard maple because the Janka number is not a metaphor. Factory catalogues used “maple” as a color. Scandinavian influence and American blonde modern put pale maple back in the dining room next to the teak that got the magazine covers. End grain still settles arguments: diffuse pores, no oak vessels, no pine resin canals. Weight surprises people who thought pale meant pine.
