# Kingwood, tulipwood, and the ébéniste

Paris in the 17th and 18th centuries bought color by the leaf. The men who glued those leaves were *ébénistes* — the name from ebony, the job from a whole tropical palette. Kingwood (*Dalbergia cearensis*), once “princes wood,” is a violet-brown striped Brazilian rosewood of small dimension, almost only an inlay and banding wood. Tulipwood (*D. decipularis*, *D. frutescens*, and the trade’s overlapping names) is pink and yellow and loud. Together with bois de rose, violetwood, holly, box, stained sycamore, and pewter or brass, they made furniture that was a picture. The Wallace Collection’s Riesener notes are the place to see how many pieces of veneer sit in a single flower.

This chapter is the ébéniste’s palette, not a dining-table species. The marquetry chapter is the technique. The rosewood/CITES chapter is the law. Read the three together.

## Kingwood

Restricted to a small Brazilian range, *D. cearensis* is dense, hard, fine-striped, spectacular under a polish, and available only in small sizes (the old encyclopedias and the trade agree). Seventeenth-century Paris used it when it was the most expensive furniture wood in general use — the “princes wood” of the inventories. It turns for small objects. It blunts tools. It is a banding on a secrétaire, not a carcase.

## Tulipwood

Not *Liriodendron*. The Brazilian *Dalbergia* tulipwoods are streaked pink-cream, used for parquetry grounds, for small boxes, for the loudest edges on a Louis XV piece. They are still rosewoods in the legal sense. Appendix II, Annotation #15. Small finished objects may fall under the weight exemption; a factory’s sawn stock does not.

## The shop

André-Jacob Roubo’s *L’Art du menuisier* (1769–75) is the period encyclopedia of the French wood shop. Jean-Henri Riesener’s pictorial marquetry for the French crown is the high-water mark: sand-shaded edges, engraved detail, mastics, a palette that has since faded to brown (Wallace Collection). When new, the furniture was a garden. Time and light ate the dyes and some of the natural pinks.

Boulle work is brass and turtle and ebony more than kingwood; it belongs in the same Paris. German and English shops imported the taste and the woods. English seaweed marquetry of the 1690s is a related, quieter cousin, often in walnut and holly.

## America

Federal inlay used holly, maple, cherry, and a little imported exotic. It is not Riesener. A Baltimore painted-and-inlaid table is a different school. Southern and New England shops did not keep kingwood in the rack.

## Shop notes

These woods are oily, hard, and small. They are also listed. A restorer replacing a lost band should document the antique source of the scrap or use a legal substitute (dyed pear, walnut, a documented old piece). Do not order a new log of *D. cearensis* as if it were walnut.

Faded marquetry should not be “brought back” with aniline on a guess. Conservators photograph, then stop.

## Conservation

All *Dalbergia*: CITES as in the rosewood chapter. Brazilian Atlantic forest history (Dean) is the ecological background. New kingwood and tulipwood furniture is a luxury that now has to justify itself on paper. Antique Paris furniture is the museum and the market that already exists.

## What to look for

Violet stripes in a 3 mm band. Pink parquetry. A Riesener flower that is now brown. A small Brazilian box. A “tulipwood” American chest that is poplar: the other tulip.

Arkansas has no *Dalbergia*. The ébéniste’s lesson for a domestic shop is the idea of a palette — holly and walnut and cherry as colors — not a hunt for princes wood.

## How an ébéniste’s leaf behaves in a modern shop

These woods are oily, hard, and small. They are also listed. A restorer replacing a lost band should document the antique source of the scrap or use a legal substitute (dyed pear, walnut, a documented old piece). Dust collection is the same rule as for the larger rosewoods: sensitizing dust, no casual sanding at an open bench `[CITE NEEDED: a named occupational-health note]`.

Glue wants a wiped surface and patience. A scraper and a fine polish are the historic finish, not a thick film that wants to be a piano. Book-matching a tulipwood ground is a design choice; the pink will not stay the pink you saw on the day you opened the packet. Plan for fade the way you plan for mahogany’s shift from pink to brown.

## Palette without a Brazilian log

The ébéniste’s lesson for a domestic shop is the idea of a palette. Holly and walnut and cherry are colors. Pear, stained, can stand in for a lost black line. Maple can stand in for a lost yellow. None of these are kingwood. They do not need to be. A shop that thinks in leaves — a 3 mm band, a flower of a dozen pieces, a ground that will fade — is thinking like Roubo’s reader even when the rack is Arkansas hardwood.

CITES for all *Dalbergia* is the rosewood chapter’s law: Appendix II, Annotation #15 as revised; *D. nigra* separately on Appendix I. Species+ at publication. Lacey on top. A small antique box already in the country is one object. A new factory parquetry floor in unnamed “tulipwood” is another.

Density in these Brazilian *Dalbergia* banding woods sits well above mahogany; compiled figures put kingwood and tulipwood in the 0.9–1.2 specific-gravity range, hard enough to blunt a moulding plane that just finished a walnut job `[CITE NEEDED: Kukachka or a measured table for *D. cearensis* and *D. decipularis*]`. That hardness is why a 3 mm band holds a polish like stone and why a shop does not treat a leftover offcut as kindling for a practice cut without dust collection.

Violet stripes in a 3 mm band. Pink parquetry. A Riesener flower that is now brown. Those are the things to look for. Arkansas has no *Dalbergia*.
