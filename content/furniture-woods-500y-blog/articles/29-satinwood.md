# Satinwood

Macquoid’s fourth age, from about 1770, is named for a yellow wood that takes a polish like a dry silk. Hepplewhite and Sheraton plates assume it: card tables, satinwood commodes, painted decoration on a golden ground, harewood and rosewood as the neighbours. Two trees, not one, carried the name. West Indian satinwood (*Zanthoxylum flavum*) was the earlier prize, a Caribbean citrus-family wood that was cut until it became a rumor. East Indian satinwood (*Chloroxylon swietenia*), from southern India and Sri Lanka, took the trade in the 19th century. They are not the same genus. They are both hard, lustrous, and yellow when the finish is kind.

## Two anatomies

Eric Meier’s Wood Database comparison (secondary, but careful) notes similar pore sizes and a useful tell: East Indian satinwood often shows ripple marks from storied rays; West Indian does not, and looks slightly freckled. Both sit in Rutaceae. Density is high relative to the later “satinwoods” of the plywood age (avodiré, movingui, obeche), which are lighter and were sold into the name.

Hardness is in the maple-and-above class `[CITE NEEDED: FPL or Kukachka numbers for both species]`. They work with a brittle, sometimes interlocked grain. They take a polish that is the point of the fashion. They were used mostly as veneer because the logs were not American-walnut generous.

*Chloroxylon swietenia* is IUCN Vulnerable (Asian Regional Workshop, 1998, still widely cited). *Z. flavum* is scarce in commercial sizes. Neither is a kitchen-island wood.

## Fashion

The English neoclassical shop — Adams’ interiors, Hepplewhite’s *Guide* (1788), Sheraton’s *Drawing-Book* (1793) — wanted pale, bright, classical. Satinwood did what gilding did, with grain. Painted swags sat on it. Marquetry sat in it. A whole commode in satinwood veneer is a luxury of the 1780s–1810s.

French taste used lighter woods in the late Louis XVI and Directoire years; satinwood appears, and so do other yellows. American Federal shops used satinwood when they could import it, and figured maple when they could not. A Connecticut piece with maple inlay is often a satinwood wish.

Victorian and Edwardian revivals brought satinwood back in display cabinets and Sheraton-revival desks. Some of that is *Chloroxylon*. Some is a dyed pale hardwood. A lens.

## Substitutes and the word

Avodiré (*Turraeanthus*), movingui (*Distemonanthus*), limba, even yellow birch with a toner — the 20th century sold “satinwood” as a color. Meier’s “Sorting Out Satinwoods” is the practical key. If the piece is not at least mid-19th-century and of quality, East Indian is the likelier true wood; if it is 1785 and English court-quality, West Indian is the romance and sometimes the fact.

## Shop notes

Old satinwood veneer is thin and brittle. It fades. A sun-bleached satinwood table looks like sad maple. Keep it out of the blast, or accept the fade.

Do not sand through a painted Sheraton panel to “find the satinwood.” The paint is the design.

## Conservation

*Chloroxylon* is vulnerable because it was good. *Z. flavum* was island-cut. Check CITES and IUCN at publication; listings move. New satinwood furniture is a specialist and permit question. Antique satinwood is the ethical supply for anyone who is not a museum.

Lacey still wants the name on a new import. “Satinwood” is not a binomial.

## What to look for

Yellow lustre, ripple or freckle, a 1790 card table, painted decoration, harewood beside it. A 1930s “satinwood” bedroom: maybe avodiré. A new yellow veneer: ask.

## Ports, islands, and the later mill

West Indian satinwood came from the same Caribbean basin that sent mahogany, in smaller quantities and for a different fashion. Do not invent a particular packet or a price. The species and the islands are the facts; a named customs line that separates *Zanthoxylum* from *Swietenia* is still a research note `[CITE NEEDED]`. East Indian satinwood rode the later imperial timber trade from southern India and Ceylon (Sri Lanka) into Victorian and Edwardian shops that wanted a Sheraton revival and had exhausted the island prize.

## Federal maple and the Arkansas sentence

American Federal shops solved the pale-room problem with what they had. Figured maple — tiger, bird’s-eye, a quiet curl — did the satinwood job in Connecticut, Massachusetts, and the Mid-Atlantic when the imported leaf was scarce or dear. Holly and a little imported exotic turned up as inlay. The object is still Federal. The wood is not a failed satinwood. It is the continental answer.

Biedermeier shops in the German-speaking lands made a virtue of pale birch, pear, and cherry after the Napoleonic wars. That is another pale-room tradition, not Macquoid’s Age of Satinwood, and it has its own chapter. A designer who likes classical light has more than one tree.

Arkansas has no satinwood tree. The Federal substitute on this continent was always maple. That is a complete sentence for a designer who likes the pale classical room and does not want a Vulnerable citrus-family log. Specify *Acer saccharum*, or an antique *Chloroxylon* piece already in the country, or a named modern yellow — avodiré, maple, a dyed pear — with the name on the invoice. A new “satinwood” kitchen from an unnamed mill is a color. Ask which genus. Then look at the ripple, the freckle, or the missing both.

A Sheraton-revival display cabinet of 1905 may still be *Chloroxylon* veneer over a deal or oak carcase; a 1930s bedroom in the same yellow is likelier avodiré or a toner. The date on the piece is half the identification. The other half is the ray ripple under a raking light.
