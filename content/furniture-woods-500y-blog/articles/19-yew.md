# Yew

The English longbow is the famous yew object. The Welsh stick chair and the country Windsor that used a yew bow or a yew stick are the furniture objects. *Taxus baccata* is a softwood that behaves like a hard, elastic hardwood: tight rings, a warm orange heart, a cream sap, a willingness to bend and return. It grows slowly, often in churchyards and on limestone, and the furniture that came from it is almost always vernacular, small, and now expensive.

Pacific yew (*T. brevifolia*) is a different history — a bow wood, a tool wood, then a cancer-drug bark story (taxol) that had nothing to do with chairs. European yew is the furniture species of this chapter.

## The wood

Closed grain, no vessels (it is a conifer), hardness often compared to hard maple in anecdotal shop tests; published Janka figures sit in a high range for a “softwood,” around 1,500 lbf in secondary compilations `[CITE NEEDED: confirm a primary FPL or equivalent number]`. Density is high. The heartwood is orange to russet, sometimes with purple streaks; sapwood is sharply pale. That two-tone is the furniture look: a Windsor bow with a ribbon of sap, a slab stool with a white edge.

It is durable. Churchyard trees and outdoor gates have used that fact. The foliage and seed (not the aril) are poisonous to livestock; the wood in a chair is not a poison story for sitters.

## Workability and finish

Yew planes to a glass surface and can tear where the grain swirls around knots — and yew is knots. It turns beautifully. It scrapes. It takes a polish that looks like a hard varnish even under oil.

Bending: the bowyer’s art and the chairmaker’s. Steam or heat, a form, a restraint. English Windsors and Welsh chairs used yew for the parts that had to be tough and pretty. Not every shop could get it. Ash did the same job more often.

The dust and the sawing of old churchyard yew can include metal and grit. Cut slowly.

## Furniture history

Medieval and early modern England used yew for small joined work, for inlay, and for the occasional fine box. The main furniture tradition is country seating from the 18th and 19th centuries: the yew-wood Windsor, prized now out of proportion to its original farmhouse status. Collectors pay for the color and for the romance of the churchyard tree. Chinnery’s oak book is the wrong shelf; vernacular chair books are the right ones `[CITE NEEDED: Cotton, Jenkins, or Dunn on Welsh chairs]`.

French and Alpine work used yew in small doses. Italian *tasso* appears in inlay. It was never mahogany.

American furniture rarely shows *T. baccata*. Pacific yew is too scarce and too small for case work. A “yew” dining table from a catalogue is likely veneer of European yew on a cheaper ground, or a stained impostor.

## Fashion

Always a minority taste. The late 20th century made yew veneer a boardroom and hotel-desk fashion in Britain — a burled, golden surface that is a slice, not a stick chair. That veneer can be handsome. It is a different object from a 19th-century Windsor.

## Shop notes

Dry yew moves less than its hardness suggests, but knots check. Design for the defects; they are the tree.

Do not confuse yew with osage-orange (*Maclura*), another orange bow wood, American, with a different anatomy (a hardwood, ring-porous). Osage shows up in American rustic work and tool handles.

## Conservation

*Taxus baccata* is not a typical CITES furniture listing; local felling rules and churchyard sentiment are the real limits. Old trees are cultural monuments. Furniture should come from windthrows and managed cuts, not from a raid on a medieval churchyard. Pacific yew was pressured by the taxol harvest before synthesis; it was never a furniture supply.

## What to look for

Orange heart, white sap, tiny rings, a bent bow, a Welsh chair, a hotel desk with yew burl veneer. Weight. A polish like stone. On a cheap “yew” suite: the edge.

The series includes yew so the country chair is not collapsed into “oak or elm,” and so the longbow’s tree gets its domestic life. Arkansas does not grow *T. baccata*. A southern shop meets it as an antique or as a veneer. The local elastic hardwoods are hickory and ash. Different color, same job in a bend.

## Two yew objects that are not the same

The main furniture tradition is country seating from the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. The late twentieth century made a third object: yew burl veneer on boardroom desks and hotel case goods in Britain, a golden sliced surface that is not a stick chair. That veneer can be handsome. It is a different object. Do not restore a 1980s pedestal desk as if it were a Welsh armchair.

## How to look

Orange heart, white sap, tiny rings, a bent bow, a Welsh chair, a hotel desk with yew burl veneer. Weight. A polish like stone. On a cheap “yew” suite: the edge. On a southern bench: you are probably looking at an antique or a veneer, not an Arkansas log. The local elastic hardwoods for a bend are hickory and ash. Different color. Same job if the part must come back to shape.

Tap a dry stick. The good bow-grade rings. Standing-dead churchyard wood can be brittle. Bend with caution. A thin long span will snap where a new ash bow would complain first.

A Windsor bow in yew wants a clean steam and a form that matches the stick, not a fight against a knot that will open on the outside of the bend. High Wycombe and other English chair districts used ash when yew was dear or short; the mixed-wood Windsor chapter takes that shop list. Inlay slips of yew in a walnut or oak ground are the small high-style use: a warm line, not a field. Italian *tasso* in a cabinet is the same idea. If the invoice says yew and the panel is eighteen inches wide without a glue line, look again. The tree does not offer that board.
