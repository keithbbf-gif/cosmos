# Rosewood, ebony, and CITES

A piano from 1880 with a Brazilian rosewood case is a legal object in a living room and a paperwork object if you ship it across a border. *Dalbergia nigra* sits on CITES Appendix I. The rest of the rosewoods — the whole genus *Dalbergia* — went onto Appendix II on 2 January 2017, with Annotation #15 later revised so that finished musical instruments and small finished products (under a weight cap) would not freeze the concert hall (Taylor Guitars’ summary of the annotation; CITES notifications). Furniture, especially new furniture in sawn rosewood, is why the genus was listed. This chapter is species behavior and a trade history that ends in a permit.

## Rosewoods (*Dalbergia*)

Brazilian rosewood (*D. nigra*, jacaranda da Bahia) is the dark, fragrant, violet-brown wood of high Victorian pianos, of some French and English cabinet work, and of a forest in the Brazilian Atlantic that Warren Dean wrote as a destruction story. Appendix I: commercial trade in wild specimens is effectively shut.

East Indian rosewood (*D. latifolia*) is the other historic furniture and instrument wood: slightly more even, still a true palisander. Cocobolo (*D. retusa*), Honduras rosewood, Madagascar rosewoods, kingwood and tulipwood (separate chapter, same genus) — the family is a palette of dense, oily, colorful hardwoods.

Hardness is high; densities often above 0.8–1.0. They dull tools, they can be oily to glue, they take a polish that looks like stone. Dust is a sensitizer. Shop hygiene is not optional.

Worked as veneer more than as solid table tops, except in small pieces and in some mid-century Danish work (the Danish chapter). Solid rosewood dining tables that are truly *Dalbergia* are heavy, expensive, and now legally loud.

## Ebonies (*Diospyros*)

True ebonies are *Diospyros*: Ceylon ebony (*D. ebenum*), African ebonies including Gabon (*D. crassiflora*), Macassar (*D. celebica*) with its striped brown-black. They are the black of piano keys (when they were wood), of 17th-century cabinets, of Boulle’s counterpart to brass, of Art Deco accents. Madagascar *Diospyros* populations have been listed (CoP16 and after). Check Species+.

Much “ebony” furniture is ebonized pear, maple, or beech — a black stain or paint. That is a historic finish, not a fraud, if named. Victorian ebonized furniture is often a finish. A solid Gabon ebony chest would be a monster of weight and money.

## Furniture history

Ebony cabinets of the 17th century, Antwerp and Paris, are a luxury trade. Rosewood in English Regency and French Empire is a show veneer. Victorian parlor sets and pianos ate Brazilian rosewood in quantity. Art Deco used Macassar and other striped ebonies as a modern luxury.

Danish modern used rosewood for a smaller, sharper luxury than teak: a sideboard that was meant to look rare. After the 2017 listing, those pieces became more complicated to export. Owners of vintage *Dalbergia* furniture should learn the permit path before they sell abroad.

Colonial extraction — Brazil, India, Madagascar, West Africa — is the supply story. Enslaved and coerced labor sits in parts of that history as it does in mahogany. Do not tell the piano as a European object only.

## Substitutes

Walnut can look like a tired rosewood. Bubinga (*Guibourtia*, some species also listed with Annotation #15) was a stand-in. Wenge, stained oak, printed foil. The eye is easy to fool in a dark room. A lens and a weight test help. A lab test is what a customs officer may want.

## Shop notes

Do not machine rosewood without dust collection. Do not wash oily boards and then complain about glue. Do not refinish an Appendix I piano case as a casual weekend.

Repair with the same species is a permit question when the patch is imported. Repair with walnut or dyed pear is how shops have always faked a missing moulding.

## The law, short

- Appendix I: *D. nigra* (and other I species). Commercial new trade from the wild is not a furniture plan.
- Appendix II: *Dalbergia* spp. (except I), with Annotation #15 as revised — finished musical instruments exempt; finished products under 10 kg of the listed wood per shipment exempt; sawn wood and veneer for furniture factories are not a casual import.
- Madagascar ebonies and rosewoods: extra scrutiny, documented illegal trade.
- U.S. Lacey Act sits on top: illegal under the source country’s law is illegal here.
- Species+ and the national Management Authority are the living references. This draft is not a permit.

## What to look for

Smell (many *Dalbergia* are fragrant when cut), weight, pore, a piano, a 1960s Danish sideboard, ebonized vs ebony (look at a chip). A new “rosewood suite” from an unnamed exporter: walk away unless the papers are boringly complete.

Arkansas does not grow *Dalbergia*. The local dark hard woods are walnut and locust. They will not be rosewood. They do not need to be. The series includes this chapter so a buyer can enjoy an old piano and not commission a new one from a genus that is now a legal third rail.

## How a shop cuts an oily, listed wood
