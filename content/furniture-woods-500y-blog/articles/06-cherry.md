# Cherry

Leave a newly planed board of American black cherry in a sunny shop for a month, half of it under a scrap of plywood. Take the scrap off. You now have a demonstration that no stain catalogue will beat. The exposed wood has gone toward russet. The masked wood is still the color of a peeled stick. Clients call the dark “cherry.” They mean the sunburn.

*Prunus serotina* is the furniture cherry of North America. *Prunus avium*, wild cherry or gean, is the European cousin. Both are rose-family hardwoods with a fine, diffuse-porous texture and a talent for looking older than they are.

## Numbers and manners

The *Wood Handbook* (FPL-GTR-190) lists black cherry at specific gravity 0.50 and Janka 950 lbf — about walnut’s neighbor, softer than hard maple, harder than poplar. Production, FPL notes, has been centered in the Middle Atlantic States; the range runs from southeastern Canada through the eastern United States. Heartwood is light to dark reddish brown with a luster. Sapwood is nearly white, narrow in old trees, wide in second growth. Machining is “very good.” Shrinkage is moderately high, but the wood is “very dimensionally stable after drying.” That last clause is why cabinetmakers forgive the gum streaks.

Gum pockets and pitch flecks are the species’ handwriting. They are not rot. A shop that fills every one with color-matched putty is making a different product from a shop that leaves them as evidence.

European cherry (*P. avium*) is often a bit paler and pinker in fresh stock, used in French provincial furniture and in German and Alpine work. Trade names wander: cerisier, Kirsche. Neither species is the ornamental flowering cherry of lawns, which is useless as timber.

## Workability and finish

Cherry planes to a surface that wants to be touched. It teases if the grain rolls, as maple does. It turns well. It glues well. It takes a cut for mouldings without oak’s pore-and-splinter argument.

The finishing problem is blotch. Like pine and sometimes maple, cherry can take dye in patches. A washcoat, a dye chosen for evenness, or an oil-varnish that does not push pigment into the soft stripes — each is a school. The other school waits. Light and oxygen will do more than a pad of Van Dyke brown, and they will do it in the direction the wood already wants to go.

UV finishes slow the darkening. Some buyers want that: a kitchen of even pink. Others want the twelve-year color and should be told to keep objects off the top for the first seasons or to accept pale rings forever.

## America: the Federal wood that was not mahogany

Cherry is the show wood of a certain American house: Connecticut, Pennsylvania, western Massachusetts, later Ohio. High-style coastal shops preferred mahogany when they could get it. Inland shops had cherry. Hepplewhite sideboards, tall clocks, Pembroke tables, Shaker stands — cherry reads as polite without being imported.

Winterthur and other collections are full of the type. It was never only “country.” A Lancaster County chest of drawers in cherry can be as exact in its inlay as a Philadelphia mahogany piece, with maple and holly instead of satinwood.

Shaker communities used cherry for the pieces that faced the room. The Shaker chapter comes back to this. The species list is short; cherry is on it because it behaves and because it darkens into dignity.

Southern shops used cherry where it grew. It was never the southern wood the way walnut and heart pine were. It shows up. It does not define the coast.

## Europe: kitchen and Biedermeier, not the court

French provincial armoires in cherry are a tourist truth that happens to be real. German Biedermeier used cherry beside birch and pear when a warmer pale was wanted. English fashionable shops in the 18th century did not make cherry their mahogany. Fruitwood chairs exist; they are not the main London story.

Alpine painted furniture sometimes hides cherry, more often hides pine and fir. Do not trust color.

## The 20th century

American Colonial Revival factories stamped “cherry” on suites that were gum, birch, or maple under a red finish. Sweetgum (*Liquidambar*) has been a stand-in; it is a different anatomy (interlocked, more movement). A lens on an unseen edge ends the argument.

Late-century “Oregon cherry” and other trade names need binomials. Food-industry cherry (the orchard tree) is not a timber supply.

Contemporary American furniture made a cherry decade in the 1990s: reddish dining rooms, clear finishes, a reaction to oak kitchens. Those tables are now in their oxidation middle age. The ones that lived with south windows look like different wood from the ones that lived in apartments with blinds.

## Shop notes

Cherry and steel make marks if you leave a damp chisel. It is not oak’s tannin black, but it is enough to curse.

Thermal darkening: some finishers use heat and light boxes to pre-age. It works, and it can go too far toward brown-black. Tell the client.

Secondary wood in cherry case pieces is often pine or poplar. A cherry drawer side is a luxury, or a small-shop choice.

## Conservation

Black cherry is a commercial U.S. hardwood, not a CITES species. The furniture trade’s pressure is ordinary: clear wide stock from a tree that also has a wildlife and mast value. Black knot and gum are more common in some stands. That is a grading issue, not a moral one.

*P. avium* in Europe is managed and also planted. It is not the conservation emergency that mahogany and rosewood are. It can still be stolen from hedgerows. Provenance is a habit worth keeping.

## What to look for

Gum flecks. Fine diffuse pores. A luster under oil. Sap streaks on the edges of second-growth boards. A color mismatch between leaves of a table (sun). On antiques: oxidized tops and pale interiors. On reproductions: a red dye that sits in the grain and does not match the inside of the drawer.

Arkansas grows cherry in the Ozarks, not in the volumes of Pennsylvania, but the species is the same *P. serotina*. A shop that works it is working a Federal wood that never needed a port. The sun will still finish the job after the truck leaves.

## Inland shops, holly stringing, and a clock case

A Lancaster County chest of drawers in cherry can be as exact in its inlay as a Philadelphia mahogany piece. The difference is the forest out the door. Coastal shops bought *Swietenia* when the duty and the freight allowed (Bowett’s statute story belongs in the mahogany chapter). Inland Connecticut, Pennsylvania, western Massachusetts, later Ohio, had *Prunus serotina*. Hepplewhite sideboards, Pembroke tables, tall clocks with white-pine or poplar works-boxes and cherry hoods — the type fills Winterthur and a hundred town historical societies. Maple and holly stand in for satinwood. The piece is polite. It never saw a Jamaica wharf.

Shaker communities put cherry on the pieces that faced the room: stands, some case fronts, the wood that darkens into a quiet brown while maple stays pale and pine takes paint (Kassay; Rieman and Burks). Cabinetmakers forgive the gum streaks for that clause. Southern shops used cherry where it grew. It was never the coast’s defining wood the way walnut and heart pine were (Hurst and Prown). ## Gum, blotch, and the runner’s pale ring

Leave a newly planed board in a sunny shop for a month, half under plywood. The demonstration is older than any stain chip. Clients call the dark “cherry.” They mean oxidation and light. UV finishes slow it. A kitchen of even pink is a choice. A buyer who wants the twelve-year color should be told to keep objects off the top for the first seasons or to accept pale rings forever. Two leaves of a table that do not match are often a runner and a window, not two logs.

Gum pockets and pitch flecks are handwriting, not rot. A shop that fills every one with color-matched putty is making a different product from a shop that leaves them. Second-growth sapwood is wide and nearly white; old trees ran narrower sap. A sap streak on the edge of a contemporary board is ordinary.
