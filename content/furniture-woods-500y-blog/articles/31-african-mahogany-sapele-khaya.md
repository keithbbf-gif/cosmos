# Khaya, sapele, and African “mahogany”

By the time a 20th-century American factory stamped MAHOGANY on a dining suite, the tree behind the stamp was often West African. *Khaya* (true African mahogany to the trade) and *Entandrophragma* — sapele, utile, sipo — filled the hole that Cuban *Swietenia mahagoni* had left and that Honduran *S. macrophylla* could not fill at every price. They are Meliaceae, like *Swietenia*. They are not *Swietenia*. A ribbon-striped quartered sapele door is one of the best furniture faces of the last hundred years. It is still a substitute name when the invoice says mahogany.

## Khaya

*Khaya ivorensis* and its cousins are the woods FPL compares to American mahogany: similar color range, often a bit more interlocked, easy enough to work, a staple of the 20th-century trade. Janka commonly near 830 lbf. They carve. They veneer. They were the “mahogany” of a great deal of interwar and postwar case work.

## Sapele (*Entandrophragma cylindricum*)

Sapele is the designer’s African wood of the last generation: a pronounced ribbon stripe on the quarter, a cedar-ish smell, Janka often cited near 1,410 lbf — harder than *Swietenia*, closer to hard maple. Interlocked grain is the price. A plane chatters. A scraper and a sharp belt save the face.

It finishes well. It is more stable than its hardness suggests if quartered. It has been used for doors, boat interiors, guitar necks, and furniture that wanted mahogany’s color with more flash.

Utile (*E. utile*) and sipo (*E. utile* / *E. cylindricum* trade confusion — use the binomial) are related, often a bit quieter. The shop that wants ribbon asks for quartered sapele.

## How the name slipped

Philippine mahogany was lauan (next chapter but one). Gaboon was okoume. “Santos mahogany” is *Myroxylon*, a different, denser wood. The word mahogany became a color and a hope. Bowett’s 18th-century Jamaica wood did not consent.

Honest specification: *Khaya ivorensis*, *Entandrophragma cylindricum*, country, cut (quartered / crown). A client who wants Chippendale should hear that the splat will be harder to carve in sapele and historically wrong in species, even if the color sings.

## Furniture history

African mahoganies enter European and American shops in volume in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, as colonial concessions opened West African forests. The furniture is Edwardian, then factory Colonial Revival, then mid-century commercial. Some excellent work is in *Khaya*. A lot of mediocre veneer is too.

Late-century architectural millwork made sapele a default “modern mahogany.” Hotels, offices, the boardroom table that is a quartered ribbon. That is a legitimate aesthetic. Name it.

## Shop notes

Interlocked grain: read the direction, take light cuts, do not chase tear-out with stain. Sapele dust irritates some people.

Color: fresh sapele can be salmon; it oxidizes toward brown-red. Like cherry, it will change after installation.

## Conservation

West African hardwood concessions are a long controversy: legality, community rights, old-growth. FSC and national documents are the minimum. Some *Entandrophragma* and *Khaya* have been discussed for CITES; afrormosia (*Pericopsis elata*), sometimes in the same showroom, is already Appendix II. Do not assume “African mahogany” is unlisted. Look up the binomial.

Lacey Phase VII wants the scientific name on more furniture. This is exactly the category where “mahogany” on a crate fails.

## What to look for

Ribbon stripe, hardness above true mahogany, a cedar smell, a 1920s suite, a 2010s office door. A Chippendale reproduction in sapele: say so. A “solid mahogany” that is *Khaya*: say so.

## Working interlock

Read the grain direction. Take light cuts. Do not chase tear-out with stain; you will only darken the torn fibers. A scraper, a high cutting angle, sometimes a belt with a fresh grit — those are the tools. Sapele dust irritates some people. Collection is not optional.

*Khaya* is kinder, closer to *Swietenia* under the plane, still more interlocked than a straight Honduran board. Moulding profiles will take. A rococo splat in sapele is a fight the 1754 *Director* did not ask you to have.

Glue is ordinary if the surface is clean. Finish: oil and film both work; the ribbon will flash under a polish the way quartered sapele is supposed to flash. That flash is why architects specify it. It is also why a flatsawn sapele table looks busier than the door you saw in the millwork catalogue.

## The Arkansas label problem

Arkansas shops meet these woods as imports. The local lesson is labeling. Cherry and walnut will not be sapele. They also will not require a West African concession map. A southern shop that wants mahogany’s color in a domestic wood is looking at cherry after ten years of sun, or at walnut, or at a named *Khaya* with papers.

On a 1920s suite: probably *Khaya* or a mix, a redder factory finish, secondary poplar or pine. On a 2010s office door: probably quartered sapele, and that is fine if the invoice says so. On a Chippendale reproduction in sapele: say so. On a “solid mahogany” that is *Khaya*: say so. The ribbon, the hardness above true mahogany, a cedar smell in a fresh cut — those are the bench tells.

The 2003 Appendix II listing for neotropical *Swietenia macrophylla* did not automatically list *Khaya*. That gap is why the word mahogany kept working as a sales tag after the true genus needed a permit. The gap is not a reason to keep the tag. Write the binomial. The door will still stripe.
