# Walnut

When Charles II came back in 1660, London’s polite furniture changed color. Oak’s black-brown joinery did not vanish from the kingdom. It vanished from the rooms that wanted to look like the court. Walnut veneer — burl, crotch, oyster, a thin skin over oak or pine — took the light. Macquoid called the years to 1720 the Age of Walnut. French and Italian rooms had already been there. American rooms would stay there longer, because *Juglans nigra* grows like a gift in the central hardwoods and does not need to be sliced to look like money.

## Two walnuts

*Juglans regia* is the European or “English” walnut, also Persian, French, Circassian, Italian, depending on the dealer’s poetry. The heartwood is grey-brown to chocolate with dark streaks. Figure can be wild. The tree is smaller and more crotched than American black walnut; shops used it as veneer because the log asked for that.

*Juglans nigra*, American black walnut, is a taller, straighter gift. Heartwood from light brown to deep chocolate, sapwood creamy and wide on open-grown trees. The *Wood Handbook* (FPL-GTR-190) puts it at specific gravity 0.55 and Janka 1,010 lbf at 12 percent moisture — softer than white oak, hard enough for a chair, friendly to tools. The handbook’s species note still reads like a love letter: straight grain, easy to work, stable, takes a natural finish, “much valued for furniture.” About three-quarters of the commercial wood, FPL said, comes from the Central States. Arkansas sits on that belt.

Butternut (*J. cinerea*) is the pale cousin. It stains toward walnut and has never had walnut’s strength. Shops used it for carving and for cheap imitation. It is scarce now, a canker story `[CITE NEEDED for current FPL/USFS status note]`.

## How it behaves

Walnut is semi-ring-porous. The face has a gentle grain, not oak’s trenches. It planes like a courtesy. It carves without the crumbling of coarse red oak or the glass-hard fight of maple. It turns. It glues. The sapwood is the nuisance: pale, and in modern mills often steamed to blend. Purists argue. A board with a sap streak in a 19th-century American piece is ordinary.

The wood darkens, then some of the red goes out in strong sun and you get a flat brown. Old French polish on European walnut can look golden. American oil finishes go deeper. The same species under polyurethane looks sealed-in, like furniture afraid of itself.

Movement is moderate for a hardwood. It is not teak. Wide solid tops still want a proper attachment to their aprons. Veneer on a badly made ground will still check.

Burls and crotches are structural chaos and visual wealth. Oyster veneer — transverse slices laid like shells — used walnut, laburnum, and olive in the late 17th century. That is a walnut-age technique, not a walnut monopoly.

## England: veneer as a worldview

Restoration chairs with cane seats, William-and-Mary high chests, seaweed marquetry on olive and walnut grounds, the first polite card tables — the literature is thick (Bowett, *English Furniture 1660–1714*; Macquoid vol. 2). Dutch taste came with a Dutch king. French taste came because France was the factory of fashion. English walnut trees and imported *regia* fed a veneer trade. The carcase underneath stayed oak or deal. Look at the inside of a drawer from 1690. The truth is pale.

The “walnut famine” of the early 18th century is a connoisseur’s chestnut: frost, or overcutting, or both, and then mahogany. Bowett has been cautious about neat famines. What the port books show is mahogany becoming cheap after the 1721 duty change, and walnut remaining in provincial work. Fashion moved. Supply stories should not be tidier than the ledgers.

## America: walnut in the solid

In the colonies and the early republic, black walnut was a primary wood. Philadelphia and the backcountry, the Shenandoah, Kentucky, later Indiana and Missouri — walnut furniture in the solid, not only as a skin. Queen Anne and Chippendale forms in walnut sit in Winterthur and in county museums. Southern furniture (Hurst and Prown) uses walnut beside cherry and southern pine.

The 19th century made walnut the American parlor wood of the Renaissance Revival and Eastlake years. Factories in Grand Rapids and Cincinnati carved it into a darkness that still fills antique malls. Then taste went oak, then mahogany stain, then blonde. Walnut came back after the Second World War as a mid-century American show wood: dining suites, office desks, the corporate modern that was not teak.

Gun stocks ran parallel the whole time. A furniture shop and a rifle shop want the same dry, stable, figured heartwood.

## Europe after the Age

French *noyer* never needed Macquoid’s permission. Provincial armoires, Parisian veneer work, Italian *noce* — walnut is a European default. German and Austrian shops used it next to oak and pine. Scandinavian modern mostly walked past it toward oak and teak, then rediscovered darker woods later.

Circassian walnut, from the Black Sea and the Caucasus, kept a luxury veneer market into the 20th century. Pianos and dashboards ate the best of it.

## Shop notes

Walnut dust is an irritant for some people; the species is in the same gossip as teak and western redcedar. Work with air.

Stain is rarely required on heartwood and often muddies the grain. If a client wants “even color,” steaming at the mill and a careful sap selection beat a pad of pigment.

Figured walnut veneer is where the law and the ethics get ordinary: know the ground. A 12 mm walnut-veneered MDF panel is a fine kitchen door if named. It is a slight if sold as a solid slab.

## Conservation

Black walnut is a managed American hardwood, not a CITES appendix tree. Demand spikes when interior fashion goes dark; high-grading of figured logs is the quiet harm. *J. regia* in parts of its native and long-naturalized range has been pressured by veneer markets `[CITE NEEDED for a current IUCN or national status]`. Butternut’s decline is the sharper North American conservation story in this genus.

Stolen walnut trees — the backyard-tree-at-night trade — have been a real crime story in the eastern United States in the last twenty years `[CITE NEEDED: specific prosecutions]`. A cheap wide walnut slab from an unnamed seller is a board with a past.

## What to look for

Heart versus sap. Steamed versus raw. Solid versus veneer (the edge, always the edge). European versus American: *regia* often greyer and more streaked; *nigra* often more chocolate and even — until it isn’t. A William-and-Mary piece with a pine carcase and walnut oysters is correct. A 1970s “Mediterranean” suite with printed walnut grain is a poster.

In Arkansas and the Central States the tree is still a neighbor. A new walnut table from that timber sits in a line that does not have to pretend to be English 1680 or Danish 1960. It only has to be dry, honest about sap, and finished so the grain can still be felt.

## Oysters on a card table, pine in the drawer

A William-and-Mary card table is a useful object to stand beside. The show faces are *Juglans regia* — grey-brown, streaked, maybe an oyster of transverse slices laid like shells, maybe a burl that was waste to the sawyer and wealth to the veneer shop. The carcase underneath is oak or deal. Open the drawer. The sides are pale. The grain does not match the top. That is correct (Bowett, *English Furniture 1660–1714*; Macquoid’s second volume). Restoration cane-back chairs, seaweed marquetry on olive and walnut grounds, the first polite high chests: the literature is thick because the rooms wanted to look like the court after 1660, and because Dutch and French taste arrived with people and with prints. English trees and imported *regia* fed a trade that treated the log as a skin. The Age of Walnut is a veneer worldview. It is not a claim that the kingdom stopped owning oak stools.

Oyster work used walnut, laburnum, and olive. The technique is of that moment; the monopoly is not. Crotch and burl are structural chaos. They check if the ground moves and the hide glue goes brittle. A badly made yellow-poplar or pine ground will telegraph. Cross-banding and a proper substrate are the quiet half of the fashion.

The early-18th-century “walnut famine” — frost, overcutting, a neat prelude to mahogany — is a connoisseur’s chestnut. Bowett has been cautious. Port books after the Naval Stores Act of 1721 (in force 1722) show mahogany becoming cheap enough to use in the solid. Walnut stays in provincial shops. Fashion moved. Ledgers are messier than a famine story.

## America in the solid, Grand Rapids in the dark

*Juglans nigra* did not need to be sliced to look like money. Philadelphia and the backcountry, the Shenandoah, Kentucky, later Indiana and Missouri, built Queen Anne and Chippendale forms in the solid. Winterthur holds the high-style end. County museums hold the rest. Southern shops used walnut beside cherry and southern pine (Hurst and Prown). The wood is a primary, not a jacket. A sap streak on a 19th-century American piece is ordinary, not a defect label.

Then the factories. Renaissance Revival and Eastlake parlors in Grand Rapids and Cincinnati carved black walnut into a darkness that still fills antique malls. Gun stocks ran parallel the whole time: the same dry, figured heartwood, a different customer. After the Second World War, walnut came back as American corporate modern — dining suites, office desks — the dark wood that was not teak. Circassian walnut from the Black Sea and the Caucasus kept pianos and dashboards in veneer. ## Steaming, sun, and the edge

Heartwood darkens, then some of the red leaves in a south window and you get a flat brown. Old French polish on *regia* can look golden. American oil goes deeper. Polyurethane looks sealed-in. If a client wants even color, mill steaming and sap selection beat a pad of pigment. Disclose the steam. “Solid walnut” that is mostly sap is the slight. Wide tops still want buttons or slots at the aprons. Figured veneer on MDF is a fine kitchen door if named. It is a problem if sold as a slab. A 1970s “Mediterranean” suite with printed grain is a poster. A 1690 oyster table with a pine drawer is a shop telling the truth. The honest buy is still the edge: heart versus sap, steamed versus raw, solid versus a 12 mm jacket.
