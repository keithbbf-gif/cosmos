# Mahogany and the substitutes

Thomas Ripley told the Admiralty that mahogany would be cheaper than deal or wainscot if Navy ships brought it home (Bowett, *Georgian Group Journal*, 1997). That sentence still surprises people who were raised on Macquoid’s luxury. The Naval Stores Act of 1721, in force 1722, stripped the duty from British West Indian timber. Jamaica wood that had not been worth the freight became, in the port books, a furniture wood you could carve in the solid. The splat of a mid-century London chair is a tax story, a sugar-island story, and only then a style story.

## Three *Swietenia* and a crowd of names

*Swietenia mahagoni* — Cuban, Santo Domingo, West Indian — is the fine, often darker, tighter mahogany of the early trade and of the Spanish islands. Commercial sizes were exhausted early. *S. macrophylla* — Honduran, big-leaf, “genuine mahogany” in later trade — ranges from Mexico to the Amazon. The *Wood Handbook* describes American mahogany as pale pink to dark reddish brown, generally straighter than African *Khaya*, easy to dry, dimensionally stable, durable, easy to work, a premier veneer wood. Plantations exist. Wild old trees of furniture grade are a different market.

*S. humilis* is a Pacific-dry-forest species, listed and scarce.

Janka for Honduran mahogany is commonly cited near 800 lbf, SG near 0.45–0.50 (FPL imported-woods chapter / Kukachka tradition). It is softer than white oak, kinder than maple, strong enough in the right section for a slender chair — the quality R. W. Symonds and others credited with English rococo (Bowett quotes and complicates this).

“African mahogany” is *Khaya*. Sapele, utile, sipo are *Entandrophragma*. Philippine “mahogany” was lauan/meranti. Spanish cedar is *Cedrela*. Toon is *Toona*. The substitutes chapter in the series (`african-mahogany-sapele-khaya`, `lauan-meranti-okoume`) picks them up. This chapter is the true *Swietenia* and the moment the word escaped the genus.

## Workability and finish

Mahogany planes, carves, and takes a moulding without oak’s pores or maple’s hardness. Crotch and curl are the show veneers. Ribbon stripe appears when the grain interlocks and the board is quartered — more famous later in sapele than in all *Swietenia*.

It finishes like a gift: oil, French polish, a film that looks deep. Old oxidized mahogany is brown; refinishers who sand to pink and then dye fire-engine red are making a 1900s dining-room idea, not an 1760 one.

Movement is modest for a tropical hardwood of this density. Wide tables still need to float.

## Jamaica, the Bay, and Cuba

Bowett’s thesis (*The English Mahogany Trade 1700–1793*) is the backbone: little evidence of a furniture trade before 1700; the 1660 Book of Rates lists other West Indian woods (ebony, lignum vitae, fustic) and not mahogany; after 1722 the values in the customs accounts jump. Jamaica dominated the early furniture supply. Honduras “bay-wood” became a large, cheaper, often coarser supply later in the century. Cuban and Hispaniola woods had quality reputations and Spanish-imperial politics. The 1795 duty schedules treated sources differently (Bowett, Jamaica/Gillow paper).

Gillow of Lancaster bought Jamaica wood as a provincial firm in a port world, not only as a London satellite. Chippendale’s *Director* (1754) assumes mahogany. Harewood House paid Chippendale with money that sat on West Indian estates — Bowett’s opening image. The chair and the plantation are the same economy.

Enslaved labor logged and milled. That belongs in the caption, not in a footnote the reader can skip.

## America

Coastal American shops used mahogany when they could get it: Newport, Philadelphia, Charleston, New York. Inland shops used walnut and cherry. A high chest with mahogany drawer fronts and poplar sides is the type. Later, Empire factories drowned the country in crotch mahogany veneer over pine and poplar.

20th-century “mahogany” suites are where the word breaks. *Khaya*, sapele, stained birch, printed lauan — see the other chapters.

## Conservation

Neotropical *S. macrophylla* went onto CITES Appendix II (decision 2002, in force 15 November 2003), annotation covering logs, sawn wood, veneer, plywood (CITES press release 11 Nov. 2003). Finished furniture may fall outside that annotation; the sawn wood in the factory does not. *S. mahagoni* and *S. humilis* are also listed. Plantation mahogany exists; illegal wild mahogany also exists. TRAFFIC and Chatham House have tracked the trade.

U.S. Lacey Act (2008 amendment) makes illegally harvested plant product a crime regardless of the appendix. APHIS declarations now reach more furniture HTS codes (Phase VII, 1 Dec. 2024).

Buyers: Species+, a named country, a named species, a chain of custody. “Genuine mahogany” is an ad. *Swietenia macrophylla*, Guatemala, FSC, is a specification.

## What to look for

On an 18th-century chair: carved splat, oxidized brown, secondary wood of the region. On a Victorian table: redder finish, maybe still *Swietenia*. On a 1960s suite: probably not. On a new table: the invoice binomial. Ribbon that looks like sapele: read the sapele chapter.

Arkansas does not grow *Swietenia*. A southern shop that wants that color in a domestic wood is looking at cherry after ten years of sun, or at walnut, or at a named African substitute with its own manners. The honest sentence is the species name.

## What the handbook measures

The *Wood Handbook* describes American mahogany as pale pink to dark reddish brown, generally straighter than African *Khaya*, easy to dry, dimensionally stable, durable, easy to work, a premier veneer wood. Janka for Honduran mahogany is commonly cited near 800 lbf, specific gravity near 0.45–0.50 (FPL imported-woods chapter / Kukachka FPL-125). It is softer than white oak, kinder than maple, strong enough in the right section for a slender chair — the quality R. W. Symonds and others credited with English rococo. Bowett quotes and complicates that credit. The wood made the carving possible. The duty cut made the wood ordinary enough to carve in the solid.

Movement is modest for a tropical hardwood of this density. Wide tables still need to float. Crotch and curl are the show veneers. Ribbon stripe appears when the grain interlocks and the board is quartered — more famous later in sapele than in all *Swietenia*. It finishes like a gift: oil, French polish, a film that looks deep. Old oxidized mahogany is brown. Refinishers who sand to pink and then dye fire-engine red are making a 1900s dining-room idea, not a 1760 one.

At the bench the plane likes mahogany. Moulding profiles stay crisp without oak’s pore and without maple’s fight. Interlock, when it appears, wants a scraper. Dust is milder than rosewood’s sensitizing dust, which is not a reason to skip collection. Glue is ordinary if the surface is clean; oily patches are less a teak problem than a finish-contamination problem.

## Newport, Philadelphia, Charleston, New York

Coastal American shops used mahogany when they could get it. Newport’s Townsend-Goddard circle, Philadelphia’s high-chest shops, Charleston’s humid rooms, New York’s later Empire factories: the secondary woods tell the town. A high chest with mahogany drawer fronts and yellow-poplar sides is the Mid-Atlantic type. A Charleston piece may hide cypress or pine where Philadelphia hid poplar. Inland shops used walnut and cherry because the log was local and the import was a coastal luxury.

Later, Empire and then factory suites drowned the country in crotch mahogany veneer over pine and poplar. That is still *Swietenia* when the face is true. It is already a substitute economy when the face is *Khaya*, sapele, stained birch, or printed lauan. The 20th-century stamp MAHOGANY is where the word breaks. See `african-mahogany-sapele-khaya` and `lauan-meranti-okoume`.

Bowett’s 18th-century Jamaica wood did not consent to those later names. Honest specification now is the binomial, the country, the cut. *Swietenia macrophylla*, Guatemala, FSC, quartersawn, is a specification. “Genuine mahogany” is an ad.

## 2003, Lacey, and the warehouse aisle

Neotropical *S. macrophylla* went onto CITES Appendix II (decision 2002, in force 15 November 2003), annotation covering logs, sawn wood, veneer, plywood (CITES press release, 11 November 2003). Finished furniture may fall outside that annotation; the sawn wood in the factory does not. *S. mahagoni* and *S. humilis* are also listed. TRAFFIC and Chatham House have tracked the trade. Illegal wild mahogany exists beside legal plantation wood. Species+ is the living page.

The U.S. Lacey Act as amended in 2008 makes illegally harvested plant product a crime regardless of the appendix. APHIS declarations now reach more furniture HTS codes (Phase VII, 1 December 2024). A crate that says only MAHOGANY fails the declaration the way a crate that says only CEDAR fails it.

In a warehouse aisle the substitutes stand next to the true boards. *Khaya* is often a bit more interlocked, a staple of interwar and postwar case work. Sapele (*Entandrophragma cylindricum*) shows the ribbon that architects now mean when they say mahogany. Utile and sipo sit in the same genus confusion. Philippine “mahogany” was lauan. Spanish cedar is *Cedrela*. Toon is *Toona*. Santos mahogany is *Myroxylon*, denser, a different family. The client who wants a Chippendale splat should hear that sapele is harder to carve and historically wrong in species, even if the color sings.

Arkansas does not grow *Swietenia*. A southern shop that wants that color in a domestic wood is looking at cherry after ten years of sun, or at walnut, or at a named African substitute with its own manners. The honest sentence is still the species name. The 1721 Act made Jamaica wood cheap enough to carve. The 2003 listing made big-leaf mahogany a permit wood. Those two dates belong on the same shop wall.
