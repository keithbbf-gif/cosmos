# Ash

Ash is the wood that bent. Tool handles, wagon gear, tennis frames, the hoop of a Windsor, the back of a country chair that was steamed in a box and pulled around a form — *Fraxinus* has long fibers and a willingness to move while hot that oak only partly shares and maple mostly refuses. Furniture fashion treated ash as a servant until Mission shops and later pale modern interiors noticed the open grain. Then a beetle from Asia arrived in Michigan in 2002 and the servant’s tree began to die in streets and woodlots across the eastern United States.

## The wood

American white ash (*Fraxinus americana*) and its white-ash group cousins are the furniture and handle woods. The *Wood Handbook* puts white ash at specific gravity 0.60 and Janka 1,320 lbf — oak’s neighbor in hardness, a little lighter in weight. It is ring-porous: a bold earlywood pore line, a face that looks like a quieter oak. Heartwood is light brown; sapwood is wide and nearly white. That pale sap is the “white ash” of table tops in the 2010s.

Black ash (*F. nigra*) is lighter (basic SG in the 0.45–0.48 range, FPL). It is a basket-splint wood, a swamp wood, a veneer wood. It is not the handle grade.

European ash (*F. excelsior*) is the English and continental furniture ash: pale, tough, used for painted chairs and for the bent parts of country seating. Japanese and other ashes exist in trade; the binomial still matters.

Ash has no tyloses story like white oak. It is not a barrel wood. It is not a boat-plank wood of the oak kind. Durability in the ground is poor. Durability in a chair is excellent.

## Workability and finish

Ash planes well, with pore-edge tear if you are careless. It steam-bends in the textbook. It takes stain in the earlywood first, so a dark stain makes a striped, almost oak-revival look. A clear finish on pale sapwood is the modern taste: restaurant tables, Scandinavian-adjacent interiors, the “white oak” look for people who wanted more bounce in the grain.

Open pores want filler if you want a glass top. Most shops now leave them.

The wood is springy. Chair stretchers in ash survive rungs that would snap in a short-fiber species. Baseball bats were white ash until maple and aluminum took shares; the bat factories and the furniture shops bought from the same grade stories.

## Furniture history

English country chairs — the ash and elm combinations of the Midlands and the Thames valley — are older than Macquoid’s parlor ages. Windsor production on both sides of the Atlantic used ash for bows and sometimes for legs. Evans’s American Windsor research and the English vernacular literature agree on the job: bend and toughness.

High-style 18th-century London did not make ash its mahogany. Painted Hepplewhite-style chairs sometimes used beech or birch; ash shows up in the cheaper painted seat. Provincial work is where to look.

Arts and Crafts and Mission shops used ash when oak was dear or when a paler open grain was wanted. Some American factory “oak” of the golden-oak years is ash under a stain. A lens on the end grain: ash rays are finer than oak’s. The flake will not flash the same way.

Mid-century and after: pale ash dining furniture, bent plywood ash faces, the 2010s restaurant boom in ash tables. European designers never abandoned *F. excelsior* for chairs.

## The beetle

Emerald ash borer (*Agrilus planipennis*) was identified in the Detroit area in 2002 (USDA / APHIS and Forest Service timelines). It has since killed ash across a huge part of the species’ North American range. The furniture implication is not “ash is gone from the earth.” It is that urban and woodland salvage has flooded some markets with dead standing timber of uneven quality, that future clear wide stock is a question, and that designers who treat ash as an infinite pale oak substitute are not reading the forest.

Europe has its own ash disaster: *Hymenoscyphus fraxineus*, ash dieback, a different organism, a similar thinning of *F. excelsior* (noted in the chestnut-blight review literature as a parallel introduced-pathogen story). English chair woods are not what they were.

Use what is already down, dry it properly, and do not pretend a stained poplar is ash. Planting and breeding programs exist. They are not a furniture catalogue yet.

## Shop notes

Ash and oak look alike to the hopeful. Rays and the tyloses test (white oak) separate them. Red oak’s open pores resemble ash; the ray fleck on a quartered red-oak face is still an oak tell.

Kiln-dried ash can be brittle if it was standing dead too long. Bend with caution. Tap a board. The good handle-grade rings.

## Conservation and buying

*Fraxinus* species are not CITES furniture woods in the mahogany sense. The conservation issue is pest and disease. Salvage furniture from street trees is a reasonable use if the wood is identified and dried; metal in urban logs will ruin saws. Ask.

A pale ash table from domestic hardwood is still one of the more readable American buys: no appendix, anatomy you can see, a history in chairs that does not need a tropical port. The beetle makes that sentence sadder than it was in 1999. It does not make it false.

## What to look for

Ring-porous, no big oak rays, spring in a thin member, a bend without a break. On Windsors: the bow. On 2010s tables: sapwood color that will yellow a little. On “oak” rockers from 1900: maybe ash. On a new wide slab: ask if it was standing dead, and how long.

Arkansas is ash country in the same way it is oak country — mixed hardwood stands, white ash in the better soils. A shop that works it now is working a wood whose living future is being argued in extension bulletins. The boards already in stick are still boards.
