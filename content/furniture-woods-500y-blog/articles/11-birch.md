# Birch

Biedermeier commodes in Vienna and Berlin went pale after the wars with Napoleon. The wood that made many of them look like butter and silk was birch. A century later, Finnish factories rolled birch into plywood and Alvar Aalto bent the plies into a sanatorium chair. Between those moments, American yellow birch impersonated mahogany in Grand Rapids and then impersonated nothing, as a honest blonde. Birch is a northern genus that furniture uses when it wants light, a fine texture, and a price below walnut.

## The woods

Yellow birch (*Betula alleghaniensis*) is the American furniture birch. The *Wood Handbook*: heavy, hard, strong, good shock resistance, fine uniform texture. SG about 0.62, Janka about 1,260 lbf — near hard maple, a little kinder. Heartwood light reddish brown; sapwood white. It shrinks considerably in the kiln. Sweet birch (*B. lenta*) is similar. Paper birch (*B. papyrifera*) is lighter, for turned ware, toothpicks, and the canoe, not for a dining table of ambition.

European silver and downy birches (*B. pendula*, *B. pubescens*) are the Scandinavian and Russian woods: pale, used solid and as the classic Baltic / Finnish plywood. Karelian birch — a figured, masur, almost marble grain — is a luxury veneer from particular trees, not a separate species you can order by the cubic meter.

Birch is diffuse-porous, tight, and takes a polish. It also takes paint as if it were made for it. Country and factory chairs in painted birch are legion.

## Workability and finish

Yellow birch machines well and dulls edges less than hard maple. It turns. It glues. It has a reputation for moving if drying was rushed — dry it, then wait.

Stain blotch is possible but less famous than cherry’s. The 19th-century American factory habit was to stain birch red-brown and sell it as a mahogany suite for people who could not buy *Swietenia*. The pores do not really match. The color can fool a dark room.

Clear finishes on pale birch yellow with the wood. Water-white formulas delay the cream. Oil darkens it toward honey, which is a Biedermeier cousin if you want it.

## Biedermeier and the pale north

After 1815, Central European bourgeois furniture turned from imperial mahogany toward local pale woods: birch, pear, cherry, maple. Black inlay, columned forms, a polished birch face that looks almost like satinwood without the ship. Museum collections in Vienna, Munich, and Berlin hold the type. The Biedermeier chapter returns to the mix. Birch is the frequent star.

Russian and Baltic birch went west as logs and as veneer. The later plywood empire — Finland, Russia, the Baltics — starts in that forest.

English fashionable shops used birch for painted late-18th and early-19th-century chairs and for cheap cabinet work. It was not Macquoid’s satinwood. It was what the factory could finish with paint and sell.

## America: the impersonator and the honest blonde

New England and the Lake States cut yellow birch as a primary furniture wood. Turned chairs, tables, factory suites. Colonial Revival “maple” and “mahogany” both ate birch. A lens and an unseen surface (the inside of a rail) will show a tight diffuse-porous wood that is not oak and not true mahogany.

20th-century American blonde furniture used birch and maple together in the public mind. School desks, dinette sets, the postwar kitchen. Then teak and walnut took the magazines, and birch went back to the plywood mill and the dowel bin.

## Plywood

Baltic birch plywood — many thin, clear plies, few voids — is a shop standard for jigs and for exposed-edge modern work. It is a furniture wood. Aalto’s factory story and the Eames story belong in their chapters; the species credit is birch’s. Cheap “birch” plywood with a nice face and a core of something else is a different product. Count the plies. Look for voids.

## Shop notes

Birch dust is fine and irritating. Paper birch is a poor outdoor wood. Yellow birch in wet use is not teak.

Karelian figure is expensive and often veneer for a reason. Solid masur birch in large sizes is a unicorn.

## Conservation

Birch species used in furniture are not CITES-listed. Northern forestry, peatland issues, and the paper industry affect the same genus. Karelian figure is inherently scarce. Ordinary yellow birch is a managed American hardwood. The conservation conversation is dull on purpose. Keep it that way: buy the grade you need, do not dye a threatened tropical to look like birch.

## What to look for

Tight grain, pale color, small pores, a stained “mahogany” that is too fine in texture. On Biedermeier: a buttery face and black line inlay. On a plywood edge: even brown lines, many of them. On a Shaker-looking stand that is too cheap: maybe birch, maybe not maple — weigh it.

Arkansas is not birch country of the Lake States kind. A southern shop that uses birch is usually using shipped yellow birch or plywood. That is fine if named. The series includes birch because the last 200 years of pale furniture cannot be told without it.
