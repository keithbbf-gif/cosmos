# Quartersawn versus flatsawn

A log is a cylinder of tubes. How you slice the cylinder decides the face you stain, the way a table top cups, and whether an oak chair looks like a cathedral or like a rayed stone. Fashion has picked sides for five hundred years. The physics has not changed.

## Three faces from one tree

Flatsawn (plain-sawn) boards are cut tangent to the rings. On the face you get arches — the “cathedrals” of oak and ash. The width of the board is mostly tangential grain. Tangential shrinkage is the large number in the *Wood Handbook* tables. A flatsawn white-oak board will move more in width, and it will cup so the rings try to flatten. That is not a defect. It is geometry.

Quartersawn boards are cut so the rings run nearly perpendicular to the face. The face is radial. In oak, the medullary rays — ribbons of tissue that ran from pith to bark — show as flakes. English joiners and later Arts and Crafts catalogues called the look “silver grain” or “tiger oak.” Radial shrinkage is smaller. A quartersawn top still moves, but it stays flatter and wears its width change more evenly.

Riftsawn is the compromise: rings at about 30 to 60 degrees, straight stripe, fewer flakes. Chair shops like it for legs that must match. Yield is worse than flatsawing, better than true quartering if the mill is sloppy; worse than both if the mill is honest about the waste.

The USDA *Wood Handbook* (FPL-GTR-190, ch. 4) treats tangential shrinkage as commonly about 1.5 to 2 times radial for many hardwoods. Species differ. The sawing pattern multiplies whatever the species already wants to do.

## Before the circular saw

Medieval and early modern oak furniture often started as riven stock, not sawn. A wedge follows the rays. The board you get is naturally quartered. That is why a 16th-century linenfold panel can be thin, wide, and stable in a way a cheap 19th-century oak sideboard is not. Baltic wainscot, the *wagenschot* of the Gdansk trade, was typically cleft or sawn to keep the face radial (Haneca et al. 2005; KNOB wainscot literature). Buyers in London and the Low Countries paid for dryness and for that radial cut. “The driest wainscots you know how to get” is a period instruction, not a metaphor.

When pitsaws and later water-powered sash mills took over, flatsawing won on yield. You can get more wide boards by slabbing the log. Fashion and thrift aligned. Walnut veneer in the late 17th century is a flatsawn or rotary-ish slice story of a different kind: the log is too precious to quarter for solidity, so you glue a thin face to a cheaper carcase and let the substrate argue with the weather.

Mahogany in the 18th-century solid — Bowett’s cheap Jamaica wood after 1721 — was often flatsawn simply because the logs were huge and the shops wanted width for dining tables. A two-board mahogany top with matching cathedrals is a flatsaw trophy. Quartersawn mahogany exists; it is quieter, and the ribbon in some *Swietenia* and later in sapele is interlocked grain, not oak-like rays.

## Oak’s public life is a quartered life

Whenever oak is the polite wood, quartering comes back.

Tudor and Jacobean joinery shows ray flake on panels because the stock was riven or chosen. Victorian Gothic revival cut oak any way the steam mill allowed, then stained it black-brown; the flake is there if you look, under the dirt. The Arts and Crafts shop made the flake the point. Gustav Stickley’s *Craftsman* furniture, the English oak of Voysey and Barnsley, the Mission catalogues — they specify quartersawn white oak because the ray is a badge. Fuming (ammonia on tannin) darkens the ray and the fiber at different rates. A flatsawn Mission chair is a cheaper cousin. Collectors still pay the difference.

American white oak (*Quercus alba* and the white group) shows rays more loudly than most red oaks. European oak (*Q. robur*, *Q. petraea*) has the same anatomy. Red oak will quarter; the flake is usually smaller and the pores more open. A designer who wants “that Stickley flash” and specifies red oak flatsawn will get cathedrals and a stain problem.

## Movement in a heated house

Period furniture was built for rooms that went cold at night. Central heating and air conditioning reset the moisture content. A flatsawn oak leaf on a drop-leaf table, glued up in 1780 at a higher average humidity, will shrink across the grain and may split along a glue line or open a rule joint. Quartersawn leaves suffer less cup, not zero movement.

The practical rule in a modern shop is old: allow wood to sit at the humidity of the destination, do not trap a wide flatsawn panel in a rigid groove without room, and prefer quartered stock for frames, stiles, and anything that must stay flat next to glass. The 18th-century frame-and-panel already knew this. The panel floats. The frame is the grown-up wood — often closer to quartered — and the panel, even flatsawn, can shrink inside the groove.

Veneer changes the politics. A crotch mahogany veneer on a yellow-poplar ground (common in American Empire) will check if the ground moves and the glue is hide glue gone brittle. Plywood grounds in the 20th century were a stability invention: cross-banded plies cancel movement. That is why an Eames LCW can be a shaped seat without the splits you would expect from a solid flatsawn slab steamed into a compound curve. The sawing chapter and the plywood chapter are the same argument in two centuries.

## How to see it on a piece you did not make

Look at the end grain. Rings running across the thickness, parallel to the face, mean flatsawn. Rings running through the thickness, end-on, mean quartered. If you cannot see the end, look at the face: cathedrals versus straight stripe plus flake (oak) or ribbon (interlocked tropicals).

Look at wear. Chair seats that were flatsawn cup toward the bark side. Tabletops with breadboard ends: if the pins elongated into slots, someone understood tangential movement. If the breadboard split the top, someone did not.

Look at the period. A 1600 joined chest with ray flake is doing its job. A 1905 sideboard with loud flake is announcing a movement. A 1970 “Mediterranean” oak suite with printed grain on a veneer of unclear species is announcing a factory. Printed grain has no end-grain rings.

## Cost, then and now

Quartering wastes the log. You get narrower boards and more offcuts. That is why Baltic wainscot was dear, why Gillows in Lancaster could pay nearly mahogany prices for Danzig oak in 1740 (Bowett, provincial woods paper), and why a true quartersawn white-oak dining table still costs more per foot than a flatsawn one from the same kiln charge.

Mills now cut “quartered and rift” as a mixed bundle. Designers who want flake must say flake. Designers who want straight grain for a run of doors should say rift, or they will get a surprise cathedral in the middle of a set.

Arkansas white oak quarters well. The rays are the same anatomy they were when a Berkshire joiner opened a Baltic board. The difference is the kiln, the ring width of second growth, and the fact that you can still buy it as a domestic hardwood without a CITES permit. That is not a small fact. It is also not a reason to quarter everything. A flatsawn oak breadboard, a painted pine cupboard, a mahogany table with matching cathedrals — each is a correct use of the cut.

The fashion for one face or the other will swing again. The rings will not.

## A pit, a sash mill, and a pencil line

Picture a 17th-century saw pit first, because the geometry is older than the circular saw. One man stands in the hole. The other walks the log. The saw is a long ribbon of iron with a handle at each end. They are not thinking “quartered.” They are thinking: keep the kerf on the line the joiner scribed, and do not waste a Baltic board that cost freight through the Sound. If the log was riven first, the line already follows the rays. If it was a sawn balk, the men can still rotate the piece and take radial cuts. They usually do not. Width is money. A linenfold panel that is already cleft does not need their help.

A water-powered sash mill changes the arithmetic. The frame saw drops, the carriage advances, and the cheapest way through a cylinder is to slab it. Cathedral faces pile up on the barrow. A shop in Lancaster or Philadelphia in 1760 could still special-order quartered oak. Gillows did, and paid for it (Bowett, provincial woods). Most dining-table mahogany after the 1721 duty change did not get that courtesy. The logs were too wide and too useful as two-board tops. Matching cathedrals from one flitch became a kind of signature: you can still see the pair on a drop-leaf in Winterthur, the arches opening away from a glue line that was once the middle of the tree.

The circular saw, once it is common in the 19th century, leaves its own witness. A skip mark on a backboard — a shallow arc where the blade wandered — tells you the conversion was mill, not pit. Rotary veneer is the flatsawn idea taken to a lathe: the log turns, the knife peels a continuous sheet, and the cathedrals repeat at the circumference. That is how a 1950s bedroom suite can have the same arch every eighteen inches. Quartersliced veneer is a different knife path. The flake in a 1910 Stickley door is usually sliced, not peeled. Look at the repeat. Nature does not clone a ray every foot.

## How a shop decides, piece by piece

FPL-GTR-190, chapter 4, gives the shrinkage ratio — tangential about one and a half to two times radial for many hardwoods. The species notes give hardness and tyloses. They do not choose your cut. If a panel must stay flat, prefer rings on end in the thickness. If a top must show a cathedral, flatsaw and fasten it so the width can move: buttons, slots, a breadboard with pins in elongated holes. If a run of doors must match, rift, and say so on the ticket. “Quartered and rift” as a mixed bundle is a mill convenience, not a specification.

A designer who writes “QS oak” and means Stickley’s silver grain, then accepts red oak from a mixed charge, will get a quieter flake and a stain that floods the open pores. That is the binomial, not the mill cheating. Ribbon stripe in sapele or some *Swietenia* is interlocked grain reversing, not a medullary ray. You can quarter those logs. You cannot get oak flake from them.

Chair legs are the practical case. Rift gives a straight stripe on all four faces, or close enough. A flatsawn leg shows cathedral on two faces and something quieter on the others. Sets do not match. A shop that makes a dozen dining chairs will pay for rift even when the table top is a flatsawn trophy. Yield is worse. Complaints are fewer.

Stand a piece on end when you can. Rings parallel to the face: flat. Rings through the thickness: quartered. No rings, only a photograph: factory. The physics does not care which century you prefer.
