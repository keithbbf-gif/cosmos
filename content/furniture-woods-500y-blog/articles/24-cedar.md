# Cedar

“Cedar” on a furniture label is a family reunion that invited strangers. A moth chest in Pennsylvania is usually eastern redcedar, *Juniperus virginiana*, a juniper with purple heartwood and a kitchen-spice smell. A Spanish colonial chest or a cigar-box lining may be *Cedrela odorata*, Spanish cedar, a Meliaceae cousin of mahogany, soft, fragrant, the wood Cuban cigar factories still want. A Pacific blanket chest may be western redcedar, *Thuja plicata*, a true thuja, light, durable, not a furniture show wood of the parlor kind. Three genera. One English word. This chapter keeps them in separate drawers.

## Eastern redcedar (*Juniperus virginiana*)

The *Wood Handbook* lists eastern redcedar around SG 0.47 and Janka 900 lbf — harder than you think for a “cedar,” a match for cherry’s neighborhood. Heartwood is dull red to purple-red; sapwood is cream and sharp. The odor is the point of the linen chest. Whether the volatiles still deter moths in a 40-year-old chest with a worn surface is a question shops over-claim `[CITE NEEDED: a current entomology note]`. Fresh heartwood is the traditional belief.

The wood is knotty. Clear grades are small. It is used for chests, closet lining, small boxes, pencils (the pencil trade also used incense-cedar, *Calocedrus*). It works easily, takes a polish, and will stain a damp shirt if the finish is thin.

American furniture: the cedar chest as a 19th- and 20th-century factory object (Lane and others) is a cultural form — dowry, storage, a smell. Earlier, small southern and Mid-Atlantic boxes. Not a Chippendale wood.

## Spanish cedar (*Cedrela odorata*)

Soft, mahogany-like grain, fragrant, the traditional lining of humidors and the secondary wood of some tropical and colonial furniture. It is in the mahogany family. It machines like a mild *Swietenia*. It is not durable in all grades the way teak is; it is durable enough for many interiors.

*Cedrela* species have moved onto CITES listings (populations and annotations have changed; check Species+ at publication — *C. odorata* has been App. III and subject to later uplisting proposals). A humidor factory and a furniture importer live under different paperwork than a Missouri redcedar mill.

Colonial Spanish and Portuguese furniture used *Cedrela* and related woods because they grew in the same empires as mahogany. English shops used it less as a show wood.

## Western redcedar (*Thuja plicata*)

SG about 0.32, Janka about 350 lbf — a true light, durable, outdoor and shingle wood. Furniture use is porch, garden, the occasional chest, contemporary outdoor benches. The odor is different from juniper. The hardness is not a table-top hardness unless you like dents.

## Other “cedars”

Aromatic “Spanish cedar” is not incense-cedar. White cedar (*Thuja occidentalis*) is a northern white, canoe and fence, almost no furniture. Port-Orford-cedar (*Chamaecyparis lawsoniana*) is a Pacific specialty, a Japanese-export wood, a boat wood. Yellow-cedar (*Callitropsis nootkatensis*) is another Pacific specialty. None of these should be sold as the Pennsylvania moth chest.

## Shop notes

Juniper dust and splinters irritate some people. The purple heart will fade in sun toward brown. A finish slows the smell and the fade; a chest meant to smell should not be plastic-coated inside.

Do not put a western redcedar outdoor bench finish on a juniper linen chest. Different jobs.

## Conservation

Eastern redcedar is a pasture invader in parts of the Midwest and a timber tree in others — not scarce. Spanish cedar is a tropical hardwood with a genuine harvest and permit story; look up the current appendix. Western redcedar old-growth is a Pacific conservation fight; second-growth and certified stock exist. The word “cedar” on an import invoice is not enough for Lacey. The binomial is required.

## What to look for

Smell, purple vs mahogany-tan vs chocolate-weathered grey. Knots in juniper. A cigar box. A Lane chest. A porch bench that is too light to be oak. A “cedar” dining table: ask which genus, then sit down.

Arkansas grows *Juniperus virginiana* on glades and old fields. A chest from that wood is a local object. It is not Spanish cedar and does not need to be. The series includes all three so a buyer can follow a nose to the right tree.

## A linen chest in a January room

Open a late-nineteenth-century Pennsylvania or Shenandoah Valley chest in January and the first fact is smell. Eastern redcedar heartwood carries a kitchen-spice note shops still sell as moth insurance. The *Wood Handbook* already put the species near specific gravity 0.47 and side hardness 900 lbf at 12 percent moisture — cherry’s neighborhood, not pine’s. That hardness is why a lid can take a sitting child and why a knot, left proud, will print a shirt.

The object is a household form before it is a timber form. Farm boxes in the Mid-Atlantic, factory Lane chests after the First World War (Altavista, Virginia, and the catalogues that followed), closet lining nailed over pine studs: American shops treated juniper as a smell and a color, not as a parlor show wood. Chippendale never asked for it. A joiner making a small box in 1840 did. The purple-red heart and the cream sapwood make a striped interior that owners learned to leave unfinished so the volatiles could move. A film finish inside a chest is a decision against that habit.

Knots are not defects in this wood; they are the tree. Eastern redcedar on glades and old fields is often limby. Clear cuttings are short. A shop that wants a clean lid glue-up is selecting, not milling a forest sawlog. Hoadley’s anatomy pages will show you a juniper, not a mahogany: no vessels, a distinct heart, a smell you can still get off a fresh pencil shaving if the pencil was the older incense-cedar trade (*Calocedrus decurrens*) rather than this one.

## Havana lining and the mahogany cousin

Kukachka’s imported-woods paper (FPL-125) and the *Wood Handbook*’s imported chapter treat *Cedrela* as a light, fragrant utility hardwood, not as a structural oak. Compiled Janka figures sit well below Honduran mahogany’s commonly cited 800 lbf; specific gravity is often given in the high 0.3s `[CITE NEEDED: the exact FPL or Kukachka row for *C. odorata*]`. It machines like a mild mahogany. Watch the softness on arrises. The fragrance is a selling point and a finishing complication; some film finishes trap it, some owners want it. It is not teak-durable in every grade. Interior work is the honest job.

Colonial Spanish and Portuguese furniture in the Caribbean and Brazil used *Cedrela* and related woods because they grew in the same empires as mahogany. English fashionable shops used it less as a show face. When it appears in a London or Philadelphia piece it is often a drawer lining, a cigar cabinet, or a later repair. Ports that handled it sat on the same sugar-and-log routes Bowett mapped for *Swietenia*: Kingston and the Spanish islands, then the continental entrepôts. Do not invent a particular ship. The customs category is the evidence, and a named port-book line that separates *Cedrela* from mahogany is still a research note `[CITE NEEDED]`.

*Cedrela* listings have moved. Populations have sat on Appendix III and have been the subject of later uplisting proposals. Species+ at publication is the living page. A Missouri redcedar mill and a furniture importer of tropical *Cedrela* do not share a legal file.

At the bench, *Cedrela* versus *Swietenia* is smell, softness, and a slightly more open, cedar-family fragrance rather than the flatter mahogany odor. A lens on an unseen rail still helps. A humidor that smells right and a dining table sold as “Spanish cedar” are not the same specification.

## Puget Sound boards and the porch

Old-growth *Thuja* on the British Columbia and Washington coasts is a conservation argument with its own literature. Second-growth and certified stock exist. A crate that says only “cedar” on an import invoice fails Lacey the same way a crate that says only “mahogany” fails it. The binomial is the specification.

Port-Orford-cedar (*Chamaecyparis lawsoniana*) left Coos Bay and related Pacific ports as a specialty: boat wood, a Japanese-export timber, a mill rarity after root disease cut the commercial forest `[CITE NEEDED: a dated note on *Phytophthora lateralis* and the Port-Orford trade]`. Yellow-cedar (*Callitropsis nootkatensis*) is another Pacific specialty. White cedar (*Thuja occidentalis*) is canoe-and-fence in the Northeast. None of these are the Pennsylvania moth chest. A shop that lumps them on a sales tag is selling a smell.

Western redcedar heartwood’s durability is a tropolone story in the wood-science literature — thujaplicins and related extractives that discourage fungi `[CITE NEEDED: the FPL durability class row and a named extractives paper]`. That chemistry is why a shingle lasts and why a garden bench can be left unfinished to go silver. It is not a reason to build a dining table and then be surprised by dents.

## How a shop works each genus

Juniper: glue is ordinary hide or PVA on clean, dry heart; knots want to be accepted or cut out, not filled and forgotten. *Cedrela*: treat it as a secondary mahogany. Sharp tools, modest feed, a finish chosen for the job (oil if you want smell; a film if you want a humidor that wipes clean). Watch end-grain checking on thin box sides. The wood will take a stain that makes it look more like *Swietenia* than it is. Refuse that trick on a piece you will later have to name. *Thuja*: fasteners should be stainless or bronze if the piece lives in weather. End grain drinks finish; the flats can look dry a week later. That is the wood, not a failed product.

Incense-cedar belongs in the pencil trade more than in the furniture rack. If a pencil and a hope chest share a word, they do not share a genus. A shop that keeps one bin labeled CEDAR will eventually sell the wrong tree to a careful customer. Three bins, three binomials.

## Movement, fade, and the shirt test

Eastern redcedar’s shrinkage numbers in the *Wood Handbook* are modest for a wood of its density; the practical movement problem is not a table top — it is a lid that was flatsawn through a knot and then hung over a heat register `[CITE NEEDED: the exact radial/tangential pair from FPL table 4–3 or 5–3 if you quote them]`. Spanish cedar, being a tropical hardwood of low density, is often well behaved in small boxes and less so if you treat a wide board as if it were quartersawn oak. Western redcedar moves, but the bigger outdoor failure is design that holds water against end grain.

Fade is a juniper fact. A chest left under a south window will lose the purple and keep the smell a little longer than the color. Owners who want the 1910 interior should keep the lid down. Owners who want a coffee-table top from a recycled chest should accept brown.

The shirt test is crude and useful: a damp cotton rag rubbed on unfinished juniper heart will often pick up color. *Cedrela* is less of a dye and more of a scent. *Thuja* can leave a tea-colored wet mark on stone. None of this is a laboratory ID. It is a shop habit that keeps the three words from collapsing.

A “cedar” dining table on a container invoice is still the question this chapter opened with. Ask which genus. Then sit down. The moth chest, the Havana box, and the porch bench can share a showroom wall. They should not share a species line.
