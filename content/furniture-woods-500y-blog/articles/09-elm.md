# Elm

Turn a Windsor seat over. If the underside is a broad, interlocked, slightly greasy-looking hardwood with pores in bands and a grain that will not split out to the rim when four legs are driven in, you are often looking at elm. Chairmakers used it because it refuses to cleave cleanly. That vice for the splitter is a virtue for the seat.

The tree that gave those seats has had a bad century. Dutch elm disease — *Ophiostoma ulmi*, then the more aggressive *O. novo-ulmi* — was described in the Netherlands after the First World War and found in the United States in the 1930s, introduced on logs (USDA / NISIC). American elm (*Ulmus americana*), the street-tree cathedral, collapsed as a mature timber. English elm (*U. procera* and related taxa — the names have been argued) vanished from hedges and from the chair counties. Furniture elm after that is salvage, rock elm, slippery elm, imported stock, or a label that means “we wish.”

## The wood

The *Wood Handbook* divides American elms into soft and hard. Soft elm (American, slippery / *U. rubra*): moderately heavy, high shock resistance, excellent bending. American elm: SG about 0.50, Janka about 830 lbf at 12 percent — softer than oak, tough rather than hard. Hard elm (rock elm, *U. thomasii*, and others) is heavier and was a specialized timber.

Heartwood is light brown, often with a red tinge. The grain is typically interlocked. That is the whole plot. You do not rive elm into tidy boards. You saw it, and you still fight tear-out. You do not make a straight-grained show table unless you like a wild face. You do make seats, hubs, coffins, keels, and the parts of a chair that must take a wedge.

European elm furniture — English country, French provincial *orme*, Dutch — uses the same interlocking. Burl elm and “Carpathian elm” burl were veneer fashions: 19th-century and Art Deco surfaces with a storm in them.

Water resistance in the log is part of elm’s folklore and part of its use in piles and pipes `[CITE NEEDED for a measured durability class versus the folklore of water pipes]`. In furniture, the point is toughness and the refusal to split.

## Workability and finish

Elm is contrary under a plane. Sharp, steep, and sometimes a scraper. It steam-bends well (FPL). It takes nails without splitting, which is why wagon and chair shops kept it.

The face can be handsome — a cascading interlock, a fiddle — or merely busy. Stain makes it busier. Country pieces were often painted or left alone. Burl veneer was polished like a luxury.

Odor in green elm is famously sour. Dry furniture does not smell. If an old seat smells, you have another problem.

## Seats, coffins, and the vernacular

English elm seats in Windsors and in Midlands chairs are a textbook pairing with ash bows and beech or ash legs. American Windsors used elm when they could get it; they also used pine and poplar for seats (Evans). The species is a clue to shop and region, not a rule.

Coffins kept elm in the trade after chairs had other options. So did some factory rockers. So did veneer mills that sliced burl for dressing tables and radio cabinets.

High-style 18th-century work rarely showed elm. It is not a mahogany substitute. It is a job wood that sometimes became a look when burl was in fashion.

## Disease and the street

The American elm’s loss is a civic and forest grief that furniture only inherits. Remaining elms are often younger, sprout-grown, or resistant cultivars that are not timber yet. Rock elm was always scarcer. Slippery elm has a medicinal-bark story that did the standing tree no favors.

Europe’s elm story is similar: a vernacular furniture wood tied to hedges and riverbanks, then a pathogen, then a museum of old seats.

Planting resistant elms is a civic project. It is not a mill project this decade.

## Shop notes

If you restore a Windsor, do not replace an elm seat with flatsawn oak unless you enjoy splits between the leg holes. If you must substitute, a well-chosen ash or a pine seat of the regional type is more honest than a pretty oak plank.

Burl elm veneer on antiques is thin and brittle. Do not sand through a swirl to “clean it up.”

## Conservation

Elms are not CITES show woods. They are disease woods. Buying old elm furniture is reuse. Buying new “elm” wants a binomial and a country. Chinese elm (*Ulmus parvifolia* and trade “elm” that may be other genera) enters the carved-furniture market. Identify it. Some of that trade is ordinary; some of it is a name pasted on something else.

## What to look for

Interlocked grain on a thick seat. Leg holes that have not split to the edge. A wild veneer on a 1930s vanity. On a street, a vase-shaped tree that may be a cultivar. On a bill of lumber, a species name, not a wish.

The Ozarks grew elm. The furniture shops used it less than oak and walnut. The chairs that have it are doing a job the better-known hardwoods do worse. That is enough to keep the species in a 500-year series after the boulevards went bare.

## Four holes in a seat that will not cleave

A Windsor seat is a thick plank with four tapered holes and a lot of sideways force. Drive the legs in, wedge them, and the fiber around the rim has to hold. Oak, riven or flatsawn, will often split out to the edge along the rays or the ring. Maple is hard and can be brittle in a wide seat. Pine and poplar were used in American shops (Evans) and they work if the seat is thick enough and the grain is kind. Elm is the species that refuses to cleave cleanly. Interlocked grain, a vice for the splitter, is the virtue. Turn the seat over. Broad, slightly greasy-looking hardwood, pores in bands, a wild face: that is the tell.
