# Cypress

Baldcypress (*Taxodium distichum*) stands in water and makes a furniture wood that southern shops used for pieces that had to live with damp: pie safes, cupboard bottoms, plantation shutters, the occasional chest, later a porch and garden furniture. The *Wood Handbook* puts baldcypress around SG 0.46 and Janka 510 lbf — soft, light, a cousin to the cedars in manners if not in family. Heartwood is brown to reddish, sometimes with a greasy look; it has a reputation for decay resistance that made it a tank, flume, and greenhouse wood as much as a parlor wood.

It is not the Italian cypress of graveyards (*Cupressus*). It is not the northern white-cedar. It is a deciduous conifer of the Mississippi valley, the Gulf Coast, and the drowned forests that salvage loggers still pull from river mud.

## The wood

Even, relatively straight, with a grain that can be plain or gently figured. Old-growth “sinker” cypress from river bottoms is darker, oilier, and sold as a precious grade. Second-growth is paler and milder. Pecky cypress — fungal pockets in the living tree — is a decorative salvage look, like wormy chestnut, with the same need for honesty.

Workability is easy. It planes, nails, takes paint. It can feel greasy to glue; wipe and use the right adhesive. Finish: oil on old heart is the current fashion; paint is the historic one for a lot of utility pieces.

The odor is mild, not juniper. The durability is the selling point for outdoor and wet-adjacent interiors. It is not teak. It will still weather grey and it will still fail if designed as a sponge.

## Southern furniture

Cypress appears in Hurst and Prown’s world as a secondary and sometimes primary wood: coastal and riverine shops, painted case work, pieces that sat on brick floors. New Orleans and the sugar-country houses used it because it grew there and because the climate punishes maple.

It never became a Federal show wood. Mahogany and walnut took those rooms. Cypress took the closet, the safe, the shutter, the cistern cover, then the Craftsman porch.

20th-century pecky cypress paneling is a Florida and Gulf fashion. Some of it is handsome. All of it is a fungus pattern, not a species you can plant for pecky on purpose in a short rotation.

## Shop notes

Baldcypress and yellow-poplar can look similar in a painted piece. Weight and the softwood anatomy (no vessels) separate them. Cypress has no oak pores.

Outdoor cypress furniture wants design that drains, not a finish that claims immortality.

## Conservation

Old-growth baldcypress swamps are a conservation habitat (birds, water, the trees themselves). Furniture should prefer certified second-growth or documented sinker. Not CITES. Lacey still wants the right name on imports of “cypress” that may be other *Cupressaceae* from other continents.

Mexican and Central American “cypress” in trade may be *Cupressus* or mixed softwoods. Binomials.

## A pie safe on a brick floor

## Anatomy against poplar, pine, and “cypress”

Baldcypress is a deciduous conifer. Under a hand lens you see earlywood and latewood, no pores, a softness that dents with a thumbnail more readily than white oak and less romantically than eastern white pine. Yellow-poplar (*Liriodendron*) in a painted piece can look similar in color once the paint is on. Weight, the vessel lines on an unseen edge, and the way a chisel crushes the end grain separate them. Pine shows resin canals; cypress does not, not in the pine sense.

Italian cypress (*Cupressus sempervirens*) is a graveyard and garden tree, not this wood. Mexican and Central American “cypress” in trade may be *Cupressus* or mixed softwoods. Northern white-cedar is *Thuja occidentalis*. A container dining set stamped CYPRESS is a binomial question. Lacey wants the right name. The series keeps this chapter so the hardwood belt is not the only southern story, and so a Gulf shutter is not sold as a Mediterranean tree.

Durability of baldcypress heartwood is real enough that the *Wood Handbook* and older naval-stores and tank literature treat it as a wet-service timber `[CITE NEEDED: the FPL durability class listing for baldcypress heartwood]`. Sapwood is another matter. A shop that uses a wide sap streak on a porch floor is building a shorter story than the heartwood catalogues promised.

## Southern shops, shutters, and the cistern

Hurst and Prown keep returning to secondary woods and to pieces that sat on brick. Cypress appears as a primary wood more often in coastal and riverine work than in Piedmont walnut country. Plantation shutters — louvered, painted, swollen in August — are a cypress job when the shop had the tree. So are cupboard bottoms that faced a damp floor, cistern covers, and later greenhouse benches. None of these objects needed a splat carved in the solid. They needed a board that would not sulk after a flood week.

20th-century pecky paneling and porch furniture pulled the wood into a Craftsman and then a Florida-resort vocabulary. Some contemporary outdoor furniture still specifies baldcypress. The honest comparison is white oak (tyloses, heavier) and western redcedar (lighter, different odor), not teak. A designer who wants teak’s outdoor manners in a domestic wet-site wood is looking at cypress or white oak, not at a stain called “cypress finish” on rubberwood.

Arkansas grows baldcypress in the Delta and in wet bottoms. A Bradley Brand shop that uses it is using a southern wet-site wood, not an Ozark oak. The rings will be second-growth unless the slab is a documented sinker. That is enough history for a porch bench. It is not enough history to call a container table cypress and stop.

## Working wet-site wood

Sharp tools, a light cut, nails that do not split the way oak splits. Glue: wipe the grease, then ordinary PVA or a polyurethane if the piece will see weather. Outdoor fasteners should be stainless or hot-dip; tannin-family stains are milder than oak’s but wet steel still leaves a mark `[CITE NEEDED: a measured extractives or fastener-stain note]`. End grain in old heart can look oily and still drink a first coat.

Paint was the historic finish for utility pieces because paint is a climate tool in the South. Oil on sinker heart is a late fashion that shows the grain and the grease. Neither finish will save a joint that holds water. Design the bench to drain; then choose a color.

If you restore a painted safe, do not strip to “find the cypress” unless the client has asked for a different object. The paint is often the period surface. If you must patch, match softness as well as color: a hard-maple dutchman in a cypress stile will read as a scar in a year.

Identification in a mixed southern cupboard is a lens and a weight test, not a romance of the swamp. No vessels, light weight, a soft dent, sometimes a greasy heart, sometimes pecky pockets. A “cypress” dining set from a container: ask. The drowned forest is finite. The word on the crate is not.
