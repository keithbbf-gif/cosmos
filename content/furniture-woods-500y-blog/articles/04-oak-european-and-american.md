# Oak, European and American

The first piece of furniture most northern Europeans owned that was not a board on trestles was oak. Chests hold that history: boarded six-board chests, then joined chests with panels floating in grooves, then drawers that learned their manners from the chest. The wood was local, or it was Baltic, or it was both in one carcass. Five hundred years later the same genus still makes dining tables in Arkansas. The tree did not become fashionable again. Fashion left and came home.

## Two groups, many trade names

European furniture oak is mostly *Quercus robur* (pedunculate) and *Q. petraea* (sessile), sold as English, French, Polish, Slavonian, Riga, or Danzig oak depending on the port and the century. American furniture oak splits into the white group — *Q. alba* and its cousins (bur, chestnut oak, post, overcup) — and the red group — *Q. rubra*, *Q. falcata*, and the rest. The USDA *Wood Handbook* (FPL-GTR-190) treats them as groups for good reason: white-oak heartwood pores are usually plugged with tyloses. Liquids stay out. Tight cooperage, ship planking, and a certain kind of outdoor gear follow. Red oak’s pores stay open. You can blow through a red-oak end grain if the piece is straight. Stain floods. Finishers either fill or celebrate the pits.

Hardness is close. White oak: specific gravity about 0.68, Janka about 1,360 lbf at 12 percent moisture. Northern red oak: SG 0.63, Janka about 1,290 lbf (FPL-GTR-190, table 5–3a). European oak sits in the same neighborhood; published Janka figures vary by source and ring width `[CITE NEEDED for a single FPL-equivalent number on Q. robur]`. Slow-grown Baltic oak is often finer and more even than a wide-ringed second-growth red oak from a warm hillside. The binomial does not tell you the ring count.

Both groups are ring-porous. Both quarter to show rays. White oak’s rays are the ones Arts and Crafts catalogues wanted. Red oak will show flake; it is usually less theatrical.

## Workability and finish

Oak planes well if the grain is straight and the irons are sharp. Quartersawn stock can pick up at the rays. Interlocked or wavy boards tear. Steam-bending is a historic use (boat frames, some chair bows) though ash and hickory often took the extreme bends.

Tannin is the chemistry that matters in the shop. Iron and oak make black stains — the old ink, the ruined clamp mark, the fumed Mission finish when ammonia vapor is the iron’s cousin in effect. Water-based finishes can raise the grain into a field of needles. Oil and wax leave the pores open and the flake bright. Filler and pigment stain were the Victorian answer: hide the structure, even the color. A contemporary shop that wants the 17th century back will skip the filler.

Oak moves. Tangential shrinkage is high. Frame-and-panel is not a style choice in oak; it is manners. A wide flatsawn oak slab table in a house with air conditioning is a bet.

## The Baltic board and the English joint

From the late Middle Ages into the 17th century, western Europe imported radially split oak from the south-eastern Baltic. Gdansk (Danzig) dominated the wainscot trade in the 16th century; Haneca et al. (2005) cite Sound Toll figures — 85 percent of wainscots passing Denmark in 1565 shipped from Gdansk, falling as Königsberg and Courland rose. Riga’s name stuck to a grade. Dendrochronology keeps finding that oak in English panels and some furniture. Home oak built the frames; imported boards often made the faces.

This is why “English oak furniture” is a phrase that needs a second sentence. The joinery tradition is English (and Netherlandish, and French). The fiber may be Polish. Victor Chinnery’s *Oak Furniture: The British Tradition* remains the long walk through forms: the court cupboard, the press, the joined stool, the draw-bored mortise and tenon that still holds when the pins have lozenged with time.

Oak did not vanish in 1660 because Macquoid turned a page. It left the fashionable parlor and stayed in the kitchen, the church, the farm. Provincial England kept making oak furniture while London veneered walnut.

## America: the same genus, a different forest

Colonists met a hardwood forest that still had chestnut, and oaks of a size that made the European eye tired. American joined oak exists in the 17th century — the so-called Pilgrim century chests of New Haven and the Connecticut valley — but the colonies also had pine, poplar, and walnut in quantity. Oak became a utility wood: floors, barns, wagon gear, then furniture again when revival and Arts and Crafts wanted it.

Southern and central white oak, including the Ozark and Ouachita timber that Arkansas shops still buy, is cooperage country as much as furniture country. The same tyloses that hold bourbon hold a porch bench if the finish is honest. Red oak became the cheap hardwood of the 20th-century American factory: stained, filled, sold as “golden oak” in the 1890s and as kitchen cabinets in the 1980s. Golden oak was often a finish fashion on quartersawn stock. The 1980s oak kitchen was often flatsawn red oak under a yellow-orange film. Same word. Different ambition.

## Fashion’s returns

Gothic Revival and Renaissance Revival hauled carved oak back into the dining room. Then Morris and the English craft shops, then Stickley, Hubbard, Limbert, made oak the moral wood — honest, quartersawn, fumed or lightly hung with a finish that did not pretend to be mahogany. The Arts and Crafts chapter takes that story. The anatomy is here: they needed white oak’s rays and tannin.

Mid-century taste dropped oak for teak and walnut, then country oak came back as a reproduction style. Danish modern used oak as much as teak; Wegner’s Wishbone is usually oak. The pale northern oak of a 1950s Copenhagen chair and the blackened Jacobean court cupboard are the same genus in different lights.

## Conservation and the living tree

Oak as a furniture species is not a CITES story in the American and European mains. It is a forestry story: high-grading, oak wilt and sudden oak death in some regions, the replacement of oak-hickory stands, the market’s hunger for wide clear boards that second growth does not want to give. European oak prices follow storms and French export policy as much as fashion `[CITE NEEDED for a current price series]`.

Plantation oak exists. It is not Baltic slow-grown wainscot. A designer who wants tight rings must say so on the purchase order, or pay for antique stock.

## What to look for

On an old piece: worm in sapwood, iron stains, replaced hinges that split the stile, a top that was once a chest lid. On a new piece: white versus red (end-grain tyloses, or a water drop that sits versus a water drop that sinks), quartered versus flat, how the finish treats the pore. On a “solid oak” suite from a container: look at the edge. A 0.6 mm oak face on rubberwood or MDF is a legal description in some catalogues. It is not a joined chest.

Arkansas white oak still quarters. The flake on a new table can be the same structure a Gdansk splitter sold to a London joiner. The difference is the kiln ticket and the fact that the tree grew here. That is enough history to be going on with.

## A joined chest and a clamp stain

Start with a 17th-century joined chest because the wood and the joint grew up together. Stiles and rails are mortised. The panel floats in a groove. Pins are draw-bored and, years on, lozenge in the hole as the oak dries. Chinnery walks the forms — court cupboard, press, joined stool — until the construction stops being a mystery and becomes manners. The panel can be Baltic wainscot, radially split, the *wagenschot* Haneca and the KNOB literature keep finding in English work. The frame can be home oak, quicker grown, used where a joiner could hide a wilder ring. “English oak furniture” is that sandwich more often than a gallery label wants to say.

Macquoid’s *Age of Oak* ends, as a fashion story, when walnut veneer takes the polite room after 1660. The kitchen did not read him. Church benches did not. Farm presses did not. Provincial shops kept cutting oak while London laid *regia* oysters on deal. That is not a fall from grace. It is two rooms.

In the shop, tannin is the daily chemistry. A steel clamp left on a damp stile writes a black line that finish will not fully lift. Old iron ink is the same reaction. Fuming — ammonia vapor on the same tannin — is the Arts and Crafts version, a controlled darkening that treats ray and fiber differently (Stickley, *The Craftsman*). Water-based finishes raise the grain into needles. Oil and wax leave the pores open and the flake bright. Victorian filler and pigment hid the structure on purpose. A shop that wants the 17th century back skips the filler and keeps the irons sharp, because quartersawn rays pick up and a wavy board will tear no matter what the catalogue promised.

## What a water drop still tells you

Haneca et al. (2005) and the Sound Toll figures already named above are the port story. Gillows paying nearly mahogany prices for Danzig oak in 1740 (Bowett) is why quartering was never a folk default once mills could slab a log. You waste width. You get flake and a quieter cup. You pay. Albion’s naval oak is context: furniture shops used what the yards and the tannin plants did not take, and later what the steam mill made cheap.

On an old piece, sapwood worm, iron stains at the hinge, a lid that was once a chest top and now a table. On a new piece, the water-drop test on an unseen end: it sits on white oak, it sinks on red, if the finish has not already sealed the question. A container suite stamped “solid oak” wants the edge looked at. A 0.6 mm face on rubberwood or MDF is a legal sentence in some catalogues. It is not a draw-bored stool.

Plantation oak exists. It is not 1565 wainscot. If you want tight rings, say so on the purchase order, or buy antique stock and accept the iron stains. Oak wilt and sudden oak death are forestry facts in some regions `[CITE NEEDED for a current range map if you name counties]`. High-grading of wide clear boards is a market fact everywhere second growth is asked to be old growth. The tree is not a CITES appendix story in the American and European mains. It is a manners story: frame-and-panel, room to move, and a finish that either hides the pore or tells the truth about it.
