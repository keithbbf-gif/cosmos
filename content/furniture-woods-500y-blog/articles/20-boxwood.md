# Boxwood

A boxwood rule does not wear out in a hurry. Neither does a wood-engraving block cut on the end grain, the kind Thomas Bewick made ordinary and the 19th-century press made industrial. *Buxus sempervirens* is a shrub-tree of tiny rings, a specific gravity that can exceed 0.9, and a hardness that secondary tables put near or above 2,800 lbf `[CITE NEEDED: primary mechanical test]`. Furniture uses it the way a jeweler uses gold: stringing, banding, knobs, chess pieces, the pale line in a dark walnut veneer. Nobody makes a press cupboard of box.

## The wood

Pale yellow, closed, almost no visible pore on the face, a silky end grain. European box from the Mediterranean and the Caucasus, English garden box that took a century to make a billet, later East London “box” that was often *Buxus* from the Black Sea trade. African box (*B. macowanii*) and other hard pale woods filled the engraving trade when Turkish and European box ran short `[CITE NEEDED: 19th-c. engraving-trade supply notes]`.

It is a hardwood, diffuse-porous, so dense it sinks in some pieces. It does not like wide flats. The tree does not make them.

## Workability and finish

Turns like ivory’s cousin. Files, scrapers, a polish with its own dust. Hand planes can do small faces. Tools must be sharp; the wood is abrasive in a fine way.

It moves less than its density might scare you into thinking, in small sections. In a humid swing, a wide glue-up of box would still be a mistake. There are no wide glue-ups; there is no stock.

Finish is often wax or oil or nothing. The wood is already a finish.

## Furniture and the trades

Marquetry and parquetry: the pale string. William-and-Mary and later walnut furniture uses box lines the way a drawing uses a highlight. French ébénistes used it with ebony and brass. The marquetry chapter returns to this.

Turned knobs, finials, chess sets, musical-instrument fittings, textile-block printing, the engraving trade — boxwood’s main life was not chairs. A 17th-century English table with box inlay is using the tree correctly.

American furniture used boxwood less because the tree is not an American forest species. Imported box served the same inlay jobs. Substitute pale woods (holly, maple, sometimes holly’s lookalikes) did the cheap stringing. Holly (*Ilex*) is the other Federal inlay white; it is not as hard.

## Fashion

Always a minor wood by volume, a major wood by eye. When pale classical furniture wanted a sharp line, box or holly provided it. When Victorian engravers wanted a block that would print a thousand times, box provided it. When the block trade collapsed, the furniture inlay trade did not take up the slack; it had already moved to other whites.

## Shop notes

Box dust is fine and can irritate. Old engraving blocks are historical objects; do not cut them for knobs. Garden box from a ripped-out hedge is full of dirt at the collar and small in diameter. Use it for what it is.

Do not confuse boxwood with “box elder” (*Acer negundo*), a soft maple with red stain streaks. The names share a syllable and nothing else.

## Conservation

*Buxus sempervirens* in European gardens has been hit by box blight and box-tree moth in the last decades — an ornamental crisis, not a CITES furniture listing `[CITE NEEDED: current CABI or forestry notes]`. Wild box in the Mediterranean and Caucasus has been pressured by historic trade. Buy small, old, or clearly garden-salvage stock. Do not specify box for surfaces that holly or maple stringing can do.

## What to look for

A pale hard line in walnut or ebony. A turned knob that feels like stone. An engraving block, end-grain, 19th century. A “boxwood” large table: a lie or a veneer.

The series includes boxwood because 500 years of fine furniture are drawn in pale lines, and those lines have a genus. Arkansas does not grow *Buxus* as timber. A southern shop meets it as inlay stock. The local pale hard wood is maple. Different scale, related job.
