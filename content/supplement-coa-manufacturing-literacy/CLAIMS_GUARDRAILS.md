## The statute, not the vibe

Under DSHEA (Pub. L. 103-417) and 21 U.S.C. § 321(ff), a dietary supplement is a food category. It is not a drug. A product is a drug if it is intended to diagnose, cure, mitigate, treat, or prevent disease (21 U.S.C. § 321(g)(1)(B)). Intended use is read from labels, websites, social posts, influencer scripts, and customer-service macros — not from what the formulator meant.

Structure/function claims are allowed if they are truthful, not misleading, and substantiated, and if FDA is notified within 30 days and the DSHEA disclaimer appears (21 CFR 101.93). "Supports immune function" is a different legal object from "prevents flu."

FTC's *Health Products Compliance Guidance* (December 2022) is the advertising rule. Competent and reliable scientific evidence, usually well-controlled human studies, for the claim as a reasonable consumer would read it.

A lot that fails 21 CFR 111 is adulterated under 21 U.S.C. § 342(g)(1) whether or not anyone got sick. A label that the COA cannot support is misbranded under 21 U.S.C. § 343. Quality theater does not cure either.

## Never say (or imply)

### Disease and infection

- Prevents, treats, cures, mitigates, or diagnoses any disease.
- "COVID protocol," "anti-viral stack," "natural vaccine," "replaces your medication."
- "Clinically proven to lower your risk of [disease]" on a supplement SKU.
- Heavy-metal or micro testing presented as "this bottle treats [condition]." Testing is quality. It is not an efficacy claim.

### Quality theater

- "FDA approved" on a supplement. FDA does not pre-approve supplement SKUs.
- "Pharmaceutical grade" as a consumer claim. That phrase is not a defined USP mark for dietary supplements.
- "cGMP certified by FDA." FDA inspects. It does not certify plants the way a registrar issues ISO 9001.
- "USP / NSF / Informed-Sport certified" unless the *current lot* and *exact label* are in the program database. A factory tour in 2019 is not a mark.
- A supplier COA treated as a finished-product COA.
- "Heavy-metal free." The honest sentence is "tested; results below [limit] [method] [lab] [lot]."
- "Every bottle tested" when the program is skip-lot, composite, or incoming-only.
- "ND" without an LOQ presented as zero.
- "Third-party tested" with no named lab, no method, and no lot.

### White-label costumes

- Inventing a science partner, a lab director, or "our Utah facility" that is a contract manufacturer's floor.
- Showing another company's COA, lot number, or plant photo as if it were ours.
- "Made in an FDA-registered facility" as if registration were a quality mark. Registration is a list.
- Promising a retailer or 3PL that a PDF will clear Prop 65, Amazon, or a sports-org screen when the serving-day math has not been done.

### Personalized and drug-adjacent

- "This kit tells you which supplements to take."
- SARMs, BPC-157, unapproved peptides, or "research chemicals" described as dietary ingredients.
- CBD as an interstate dietary supplement. FDA's 26 January 2023 conclusion still stands unless a later dated page says otherwise. Check the date.

## Allowed shapes (still need substantiation)

- "Contains [dose] of [ingredient], as [form], lot-tested for identity, potency, and [named contaminants]."
- "Third-party tested to [named standard]. Certificate [ID]. Lab [name]. Method [ID]."
- "Manufactured under 21 CFR 111. The brand owner remains responsible for the labeled product."
- "Supports [normal structure/function]" with a notified, evidence-backed claim and the DSHEA disclaimer.
- "Not evaluated by FDA. Not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease."

## Process

1. If a sentence would be illegal on a label, it is illegal in a blog caption next to a cart button.
2. Influencer briefs get the same list. A Reel is labeling if you paid for it.
3. Cite the CFR, the USP chapter, the warning-letter number, or flag `[CITE NEEDED]` / `[VERIFY]`. Do not invent an LOD, a pass rate, or a market-share figure.
4. Empty seats stay empty: science/QA partner and counsel are not named here. Do not invent them on-site to launder a claim.
