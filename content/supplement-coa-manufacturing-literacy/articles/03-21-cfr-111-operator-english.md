**Disclaimer.** Educational only. Not medical advice. Dietary supplements are not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease (DSHEA; 21 U.S.C. § 321(ff); 21 CFR 101.93). This is not legal advice and not an audit.

On 25 June 2007 FDA published the dietary supplement cGMP final rule at 72 FR 34752. The codified text is 21 CFR Part 111. Compliance staggered by firm size: 2008, 2009, 2010. The rule is not new. The founders who treat it as optional are new.

This piece is a map, not a substitute for the eCFR. Read the sections. The small-entity compliance guide is the plain-language twin. Use both.

## Who the rule talks to

Part 111 applies to people who manufacture, package, label, or hold dietary supplements. "Hold" is doing real work in this sentence. A 3PL that stores your bottles is in the conversation. A brand that never touches a blender and still puts its name on the label is in the conversation as the person who caused the labeled product to exist.

The rule is written in "you." You establish specifications. You test. You keep records. You do not get to point at a contract and disappear (piece 05, piece 29).

A lot prepared, packed, or held under conditions that do not meet these requirements is adulterated under 21 U.S.C. § 342(g)(1). No outbreak required. No hospital chart. The condition of the operation is the violation.

## The spine: specifications, then proof

**111.70** is the spine. You establish specifications for:

- incoming components (identity of dietary ingredients; purity, strength, composition as needed; contaminant limits that matter);
- in-process points where control is necessary;
- labels and packaging;
- the finished product (identity, purity, strength, composition, contaminant limits);
- product you receive already made, if you only pack or label;
- the packaged and labeled product you will distribute.

**111.73** is one sentence long and people skip it: you must make sure those specifications are met.

**111.75** is how. Tests and examinations that are appropriate and scientifically valid. Identity of each dietary ingredient by at least one such test. Supplier COAs only where the rule allows them, and only after you qualify the supplier. Finished batches verified every time or on a sound statistical sampling plan. Exemptions from finished-batch verification only when there is no scientifically valid finished-batch method *and* you document why component and in-process work still get you there (111.75(d), 111.95(b)(5)).

If you remember one triangle from this pack: **write the spec, run a valid test, keep the record.** A COA is one shape of the third side.

## Subparts you will actually live in

The rule is chunked. Operators hit these rooms every week:

**Personnel (Subpart B).** Hygienic practices, disease control, training. A hairnet photo is not a training record.

**Physical plant (Subpart C).** Design, sanitation, pest control. This is why a garage-with-a-capsule-machine story dies on inspection.

**Equipment (Subpart D).** Suitable, clean, calibrated when measurement matters. A balance that has not seen a weight in two years is a decoration.

**Production and process control (Subpart E).** Specifications and the 111.75 tests. This is the COA subpart.

**Quality control (Subpart F).** The unit that reviews and approves. Disposition decisions sit here (111.87). Sales does not release a lot.

**Records of the batch.** Master manufacturing record (111.205–111.210): the recipe and the instructions. Batch production record (111.255–111.260): what this run actually did, who did it, what was issued, what was yielded, what was tested, who released it. If those two documents do not match, you do not have control. You have a story (piece 19).

**Holding and distributing (Subpart M).** Quarantine, rejects, reserve samples (111.83, 111.465), returned product.

**Product complaints (Subpart Q, 111.553).** A written procedure, a review, a file. Instagram DMs are not a complaint system.

**Records and recordkeeping (Subpart P).** How long you keep what. If the investigator asks and you cannot produce it, the record did not exist for the purpose of this rule.

## What Part 111 is not

It is not Part 211. Drug GMPs are a different book. Do not put "pharmaceutical GMP" on a supplement cart page because someone at the CMO used to work in a drug plant.

It is not Part 117, though the same building may have to satisfy both if you also make conventional food (piece 30).

It is not a consumer seal. FDA does not mail you a cGMP certificate when you comply. Registration of a food facility is a list. Inspection is an event. A passing inspection is not a lifetime badge.

It is not permission to make disease claims. Intended use lives in 21 U.S.C. § 321(g) and in 21 CFR 101.93. You can have a beautiful 111 file and still be selling an unapproved drug because the website said the capsules treat something (piece 04).

## Compliance dates people still get wrong

Large firms: 25 June 2008. Firms with 20–499 employees: 25 June 2009. Firms with fewer than 20 employees: 25 June 2010. Those dates are in the 2007 preamble. "We are a startup" is not a stay.

The identity-testing piece of 111.75(a)(1) had its own history, including an interim final rule and a petition path for limited exemptions. Do not assume you have an exemption because a consultant mentioned one in 2011. If you think you have FDA's written response to a petition under 111.75(a)(1)(ii), put that letter in the file. If you do not have the letter, you do not have the exemption.

## How to use this map this week

1. Print 111.70, 111.75, 111.80, 111.83, 111.205, 111.260. Six pages of work.
2. For one SKU, write the specifications you actually use. If they only exist as "whatever the CMO sent," you do not have 111.70.
3. For one lot, collect the identity test, the finished-product results, the batch record, and the reserve. If any of those are missing, that is the gap. Not a vibe. A gap.
4. Read one warning letter from `BIBLIOGRAPHY.md`. Notice how often the citation is "you failed to establish" or "you failed to verify," not "a customer got sick."

Part 111 is a manufacturing rule. The COA is how a lot talks. If the lot cannot talk, you should not ship it.
