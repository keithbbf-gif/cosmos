Identity is the first job. If you do not know what is in the drum, potency is a number about the wrong plant.

21 CFR 111.75(a)(1) requires at least one appropriate test or examination to verify the identity of any component that is a dietary ingredient. You cannot meet that sentence by filing a supplier PDF. Western Innovations (679737, 13 November 2024) handed FDA supplier CoAs whose methods did not have the specificity to identify the ingredient. LXR Biotech (714568, 23 December 2025) produced an identity report where the "reference standard" carried the same lot number as the sample. That is a mirror, not a test.

## What identity means here

Identity answers: is this material the dietary ingredient we named, in the part and form we named?

*Withania somnifera* root is not leaf. Root extract 10:1 is not root powder. Cholecalciferol is not ergocalciferol. Magnesium glycinate is not magnesium oxide. Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG is not "a probiotic blend."

A method is appropriate when it can tell those pairs apart. A method is a costume when it cannot.

## Methods you will actually see

**HPTLC (high-performance thin-layer chromatography).** The workhorse for many botanicals. You run the sample against a botanical reference material and look at the band pattern. A plate that does not match is a fail, even if a marker assay later says "2% withanolides." Marker assays without identity are how spiked material travels: someone adds a cheap withanolide source to sawdust and sells you a number.

Ask: which reference material? Which method ID? Who read the plate? Is there an image in the file?

**FTIR (Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy).** Useful for many single-entity vitamins, minerals as salts, amino acids, and some excipients. A library match is only as good as the library and the sample prep. FTIR on a four-herb blend will give you a soup. Do not accept FTIR as identity for a complex botanical blend unless someone can show you why that soup is specific.

**HPLC / UPLC with a reference standard.** Identity-by-retention-time plus a spectrum (DAD, MS) can be identity for a defined chemical: caffeine, melatonin, a vitamin. It is weaker as the sole identity for a powdered herb, because many plants can be made to show a marker peak.

**Microscopy.** Still real for some intact botanicals. A trained eye on a root powder is not romantic nostalgia. It is how you see rice husk. It does not work on a highly processed extract with no tissue left.

**DNA barcoding / NGS.** Useful on some unprocessed plant material. Often useless on extracts, because processing destroys the DNA. A CMO that offers "DNA tested" on a 50:1 extract is selling a sentence. Ask whether the lab had amplifiable DNA. If they did not, the test did not happen.

**Organoleptic plus a document.** Color, odor, taste, appearance. 111.75 allows examinations, not only instrumental tests. Organoleptic alone is rarely enough for a high-value botanical that is commonly substituted. Combine it with something that can fail.

## The reference-standard trick

LXR's 2025 letter is the teaching case. If the supplier sends the lab both the sample and the "standard," and they are the same lot, the chromatogram will match. Of course it will.

You want a reference that is independent: a pharmacopeial standard, a vouchered botanical reference material, a lot from a second supplier you already trust, a synthesized standard with a certificate that is not this drum. If the lab cannot name that independent object, you do not have identity. You have a selfie.

## Finished-product identity is a different sentence

Incoming identity: is this drum *Withania somnifera* root extract?

Finished-product identity: is this bottle the product you specified? That may be a set of tests — appearance, a marker, a capsule size, an FTIR of a simple formula — that together say you made the SKU you wrote down.

A multi-ingredient blend will not give you a clean HPTLC of every herb. That is why 111.75(d) exists: some finished-product specifications cannot be verified at the finished-batch stage with a scientifically valid method. You document why incoming identity plus in-process control still get you there. You do not use that paragraph as a shrug. FDA reads the shrug.

## What to put on the spec

For each dietary ingredient:

- The name you will put on the panel (common name and Latin binomial for botanicals; salt or form for minerals and vitamins).
- The plant part, if botanical.
- The identity method, or the method family plus the SOP number.
- The reference material.
- Fail: does not match. Not "atypical, released by sales."

For the finished product: the identity tests you can actually run, and the 111.75(d) file for the ones you cannot.

## A Friday script

1. Pick one botanical SKU.
2. Find the incoming identity record for the last lot. Not the assay. The identity.
3. Name the method and the reference.
4. If either is missing, that lot was released on a hope.
5. Pay for one split identity test at a lab that keeps botanical reference materials. Compare.

If the plate does not match, you have a supplier problem and a label problem. You do not have a marketing problem. Do not "explain" a failed identity into a passing one.

Pull the incoming identity row before you read assay.
