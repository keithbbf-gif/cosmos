Founders say "our formula" about a house blend they picked from a PDF. Then they try to move plants and discover they have a brand and a purchase history. The recipe stayed.

Ownership is a contract fact. The MMR (111.205–111.210) is a manufacturing fact. You need both. An NDA is neither.

## Three states of a recipe

**They own it.** White-label house formula. You may get a panel-level ingredient list. You may not get percentages, suppliers, or the MMR. You cannot take it down the road unless the agreement says you can — and it will not, at catalog prices.

**You own it.** You specified the formula, or you bought an assignment. The CMO executes. You can, in principle, move. You still need the files: MMR, specs, supplier list, methods. If those live only on their server, you own a hope.

**Co-developed, unspoken.** The worst state. You asked for "something like brand X but with glycinate." They tweaked a house recipe. Both of you think you own it. A later lawyer will enjoy this. Write the assignment *before* the first PO.

## What an NDA does

A mutual NDA says you will not tell others what you saw. It does not transfer copyright, trade secret, or the right to the MMR. It does not make a house blend yours because they showed you a percent.

Do not confuse a signed NDA with a bill of sale for IP. Do not post "proprietary formula" on a PDP because an NDA exists.

## What you need in order to move

If the plan is to own a formula you can take to a second CMO:

- Written assignment or work-for-hire language that names the recipe, specs, and process instructions.
- Delivery of the MMR, spec pack, and supplier part numbers (or equivalents you may use).
- A non-exclusive license back to them if they still run it for you, or an exclusive if you paid for exclusive.
- Residual-knowledge language that does not let them sell your exact panel to the next founder next week — if you paid for that restriction.

Counsel writes this. This pack flags the empty seat. The operator point: if you did not pay for ownership, you did not get it.

## House blends and "proprietary blend" on the panel

21 CFR 101.36 has rules for proprietary blends: you declare the blend weight and list ingredients in descending order, without having to disclose each amount. That is a labeling permission. It is not a quality permission to skip assay of ingredients you elsewhere claim by number.

It is also not a reason to hide a house formula as if it were a trade secret you invented. If four brands share the tank, the secret is a marketing word.

Do not use "proprietary blend" to dodge a weak identity story. The blend still contains dietary ingredients that 111.75(a)(1) wants identified incoming.

## Why this belongs in a COA pack

You cannot interpret a COA if you do not know what the article is. A house blend COA that lists "blend 800 mg" and no methods is a brick. Ownership of the recipe is how you demand the method list. If you do not own it, your option is to leave, not to rewrite their MMR.

When you *do* own it, put the revision on the spec and on the COA (piece 06). A fight about "which formula" is a fight about the first match.

## A conversation to have before the photoshoot

1. Who owns the formula after I pay the NRE?
2. May I receive the MMR and spec pack?
3. May I take them to another facility in 12 months?
4. Is this blend run for other brands?

## Tooling and molds are a cousin

A custom bottle, a scoop, a blister tool. Who owns it when you leave? If they own the mold, your "owned formula" still cannot be packed the same way at the next plant without a buyout. Write tooling the same day you write the recipe assignment. Otherwise you own a recipe and rent a bottle.

## What "we can tweak it for you" usually means

They will change a flavor, a capsule color, or a minor excipient and still keep the formula. That tweak is valuable and still not ownership. If you later need a different magnesium salt, you are asking for a favor on their object.

If the brief is "same panel, we need to own it in 12 months," say that in the first email. Price the assignment now. A year of invoices does not accrue IP. A year of invoices accrues habit.

If the answers are no, no, no, and yes, you are buying a label, not a recipe. Price it that way. Test it that way. Do not build a five-year brand plan on a recipe you cannot hold.
