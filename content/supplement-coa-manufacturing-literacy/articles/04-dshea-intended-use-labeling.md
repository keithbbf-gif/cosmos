**Disclaimer.** Educational only. Not medical advice. Dietary supplements are not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease (DSHEA; 21 U.S.C. § 321(ff); 21 CFR 101.93). This is not legal advice and not an audit.

DSHEA is Public Law 103-417, signed in 1994. It created the dietary-supplement category at 21 U.S.C. § 321(ff). It did not create a permission slip to talk like a drug ad.

A product is a drug if it is intended to diagnose, cure, mitigate, treat, or prevent disease (21 U.S.C. § 321(g)(1)(B)). Intended use is not a feeling in the formulator's head. It is read from the label, the website, the Amazon bullet, the Reel you paid for, the script your customer-service person uses at 9 p.m., and the blog post that sits next to an add-to-cart button.

This pack is about COAs and plants. It still has to stay on the safe side of that line. A sentence about lead testing that slides into "protects you from heavy-metal disease" has left manufacturing literacy and entered drug territory.

## What DSHEA actually did

It defined dietary supplements and dietary ingredients. It set a path for statements of nutritional support — structure/function claims — if you notify FDA within 30 days, you have substantiation, the claim is truthful and not misleading, and the disclaimer appears (21 CFR 101.93).

The disclaimer is not decoration. The familiar sentence — that the statement has not been evaluated by FDA and the product is not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease — is the rule's own English.

DSHEA also left FDA the adulteration and misbranding tools. Part 111 arrived later, in 2007, as the manufacturing book. New dietary ingredients still have 21 U.S.C. § 350b. None of that is "the Wild West," which is a phrase people use when they have not read the file.

## Structure/function versus disease

**Allowed shape (still needs evidence):** "Helps fill a dietary gap for [nutrient] when intake is low." "Supports [normal structure or function]."

**Not allowed:** "Prevents flu." "Treats anxiety." "Reverses aging." "COVID protocol." "Natural vaccine." "Clinically proven to lower your risk of [disease]" on a supplement SKU.

The difference is not tone. "Supports immune function" and "prevents COVID-19" are different legal objects. Vitamin D, zinc, vitamin C, elderberry, quercetin, NAC, and silver all showed up in FDA and FTC COVID warning letters in 2020–2021. The ingredient was not the violation. The intended-use claim was.

This pack will mention those ingredients when they show up in testing or labeling math. It will not tell a reader to take them for a disease.

## FTC sits on the same URL

FDA looks at labeling and intended use. FTC looks at advertising. A blog that is doing work for a brand is advertising. FTC's *Health Products Compliance Guidance* (December 2022) is the current long form: competent and reliable scientific evidence, usually well-controlled human studies, for the claim as a reasonable consumer would read it.

A rat paper is not that. A podcast quote is not that. A CMO's "customers love it for sleep" is not that.

If you paid an influencer, the Reel is labeling for your purposes. The 2023 endorsement guides and the wave of FTC notices are the adjacent file (the R&D pack covers that scar). Do not put a disease hashtag under a bottle you also describe as "third-party tested," as if the COA washed the hashtag.

## Why a manufacturing blog is still labeling

These drafts will live, if they ever clear staging, on a site that will also sell SKUs. A reader will move from "how to read a metals row" to a product page in two clicks. Investigators and plaintiff's lawyers know how to do that too.

So:

- Do not use a COA row as an implied disease claim.
- Do not say "heavy-metal free." Say "tested; results below [limit] [method] [lab] [lot]."
- Do not say "FDA approved."
- Do not say "pharmaceutical grade."
- Do not put "cGMP certified by FDA" on a plant you do not operate.
- Do not invent a science partner to launder a sentence.

`CLAIMS_GUARDRAILS.md` is the never-say list. If a caption would be illegal on a label, it is illegal next to the cart.

## What you *can* say about quality

Quality language is allowed when it is true and specific.

"Contains 125 mcg vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol) per serving. Lot [ID] assayed by HPLC at [lab]; result [X] mcg/serving against a spec of [Y–Z]."

"Third-party tested for identity, potency, and [named contaminants] to [named standard]. Certificate [ID]."

"Manufactured under 21 CFR 111. The brand owner remains responsible for the labeled product."

Those sentences are boring. They are also the sentences a 3PL and a retailer can live with. They do not treat, cure, or prevent.

## Notification and the empty seat

If you eventually make a structure/function claim on a live SKU, 101.93 wants a notification to FDA within 30 days of first marketing. The disclaimer on the label. Substantiation in a file you can produce.

Counsel and a science/QA partner are empty seats in this pack on purpose. Do not fill them in public copy with a made-up PharmD. Do not treat this folder as the notification, the substantiation, or the legal opinion.

## A reading habit

When you draft a sentence, ask: if this sat on a Supplement Facts panel, would FDA read a disease? If yes, cut it. If you are teaching a method or a CFR section, stay with the method and the section.

Manufacturing literacy is a structure/function of a company, not of a body. Keep it there.
