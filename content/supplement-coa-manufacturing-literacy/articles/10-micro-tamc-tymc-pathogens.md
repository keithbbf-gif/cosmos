**Disclaimer.** Educational only. Not medical advice. Dietary supplements are not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease (DSHEA; 21 U.S.C. § 321(ff); 21 CFR 101.93). This is not legal advice and not an audit. A micro row is a quality result, not an infection-treatment claim.

A dry capsule plant and a liquid shot plant are different microbiological objects. Founders treat the COA row as if it were one object: "micro passed." Ask which tests, which limits, which method, and whether the product can grow anything after it leaves the lab.

## The three families

**Enumeration.** TAMC (total aerobic microbial count) and TYMC (total combined yeasts and molds). These are bioburden numbers. USP \<2021\> is the chapter people mean when they are being precise about enumeration for this category.

**Absence of specified organisms.** USP \<2022\> is the procedures chapter. The specified list depends on the article. You will commonly see *Salmonella*, *E. coli*, *S. aureus*, and sometimes others. "Pathogen absent" without naming the organism is a vibe.

**Attributes / limits.** USP \<2023\> discusses microbiological attributes of nonsterile nutritional and dietary supplements — the thinking about what limits belong on which kind of article. If you adopt a USP-style limit set, write the numbers on *your* spec. "Meets USP micro" with no numbers is a slogan.

21 CFR 111.70 still wants *your* contaminant specifications. USP chapters are how many labs talk. They are not automatically your 111.70 file until you adopt them.

## Dry powders are not liquids

A low-water-activity powder in a sealed bottle is a hostile place for many organisms to grow. That does not make incoming bioburden irrelevant. It does change what "pass" means after a month in a hot warehouse.

A water-based shot, a gummy, a liquid emulsion, or anything you keep in the fridge "just in case" is a growth conversation. Preservative systems, pH, water activity, and a stability micro pull belong in the file (piece 26). A TAMC from day 0 is not a 12-month story.

If you are making a liquid and your CMO's COA only has TAMC/TYMC from a powder template, you are reading the wrong costume.

## Incoming versus finished

Incoming botanicals can carry high counts. That is not automatically a fail. It is a reason to write a limit that matches the ingredient and the process. A tea-cut herb and a 50:1 extract are different.

Finished-product micro is about the lot you will ship. Blending can dilute. Water and a flavor system can amplify. A passing incoming *E. coli* row does not bless the tank.

Probiotic SKUs are a special case: you are *selling* organisms. Enumeration of the labeled strains is an assay problem (piece 08). Contamination with organisms you did not name is a micro problem. Do not let a CFU claim hide a pathogen row you never ran.

## Method and sample

Ask:

- USP \<2021\>/\<2022\>, or a validated in-house equivalent, or BAM, or another named method?
- Sample size and how it was taken (piece 17). A swab of the lid is not a lot sample.
- Dilution and inhibition. Botanicals and preservatives interfere. A "zero" that is actually inhibition of the test is a known failure mode. The lab should have checked recovery.
- Time-to-result. A lot released on Friday because "plates looked clean" before the incubation finished is not a released lot.

## What a pass looks like

Numbers at or under the spec, organisms named, method named, lab named. "Conforms" can be acceptable on an absence test if the spec is "absent in 10 g" and the method is named. "Conforms" on TAMC without a number is weaker; ask for the count.

OOS micro is a hold (piece 22, piece 23). Retest theater — same sample, new number, no investigation — is how you ship a problem with a prettier PDF.

## Water, air, and the plant

Micro on a COA is downstream of water and air (piece 25). A tank rinsed with pretty water that was not tested, a hose that lives on the floor, a drain that backs up under the blender: those do not appear on the customer PDF. They appear in the 483.

If you walk the plant, look at the water system and the drain before you look at the lobby certificate wall.

## A liquid-shot example

You buy a botanicals-and-vitamin shot. The CMO sends a COA with TAMC 80 CFU/g, TYMC 10 CFU/g, *Salmonella* absent, *E. coli* absent. The template footer still says "powder, 10 g sample." Your shot is 60 mL of liquid with water activity you have never seen written down.

Ask for:

- The method as applied to *this* liquid, including any neutralization of the preservative.
- Water activity or pH on the same lot, if the spec requires it.
- A hold-time: how long the bulk sat after the test and before fill.
- Whether the absence tests used a 10 g equivalent of *liquid* or a leftover powder SOP.

If they cannot answer, you have a powder costume on a liquid lot. Hold it. A passing number on the wrong method is not a pass. It is a category error with a CFU attached.

## What to write on the spec this week

For each SKU family (dry capsule, powder, gummy, liquid):

- TAMC limit and method.
- TYMC limit and method.
- Named organisms and the mass in which they must be absent.
- Whether the test is incoming, finished, or both.
- What happens if the lab reports "inhibition, test invalid."

Then pick last month's lot and see whether the COA can fill those cells. The empty cells are the program.

## What this is not

A passing pathogen row is not "supports immunity" and it is not "prevents food poisoning." Do not put infection language next to a micro table.

It is also not sterility. Nonsterile dietary supplements are not sterile products. Do not advertise them as such.

Name the organism. Name the limit. Name the method. If you cannot, you do not have a micro story. You have a checkbox.
