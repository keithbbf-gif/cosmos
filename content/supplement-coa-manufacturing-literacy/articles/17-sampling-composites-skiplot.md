"Every bottle tested" is a claim. Most plants do not do that. Part 111 does not require that. What it requires is a representative sample and, for finished batches, either every batch or a subset chosen by a sound statistical sampling plan (111.75(c), 111.80(c)).

Those are adult sentences. They get dressed up as "100% tested" on a cart page. That adjective is the problem, not the skip-lot plan.

## Representative is a word with a job

111.80 tells you which representative samples to collect: each unique lot (and each unique shipment) of components, packaging, and labels; in-process materials at the control points in the MMR; a subset of finished batches (or every finished batch); product you receive for pack/label; each lot of packaged and labeled product.

A representative sample is not the scoop from the top of the first drum because it was easy. It is a sample taken so that the test can speak for the lot. Stratify. Sample from beginning, middle, and end of a fill if the process can segregate. Do not test the retain from a previous lot and type this lot's number in the header (piece 39).

If you cannot describe how the sample was taken, you cannot defend the result.

## Skip-lot is allowed. Skip-thinking is not.

111.75(c) lets you verify finished-batch specifications on a subset of batches identified through a sound statistical sampling plan, *or* on every finished batch.

A sound plan has:

- A written SOP.
- A statistical reason (not "we test Mondays").
- Quality-control approval.
- Documentation that the tests you *do* run, plus in-process and component work, support the specifications you did not run this time (111.95(b)(4)).
- A trigger to go back to every-batch when a lot fails, a process changes, or a supplier changes.

What is not a plan:

- "We tested the first three lots in 2019."
- "The CMO says they test enough."
- "It's a simple vitamin, we skip metals forever."
- Testing the flavor system and calling it the SKU.

111.75(d) is the exemption for a *specification* you cannot verify at the finished-batch stage because there is no scientifically valid finished-batch method. That is a per-spec document, not a blanket "botanicals are hard." If you use it, file why component and in-process work still get you there (111.95(b)(5)).

## Composites

A composite is several increments blended into one test sample. It can be honest: you wanted a better picture of a drum. It can be a smear: one hot increment diluted below the spec by nine clean ones.

If you composite, write the rule: how many increments, from where, and whether a fail on the composite fails the lot (yes) or triggers increment testing (also yes, then disposition). Do not composite across lots. Do not composite a fail away.

Retailer "every lot tested" programs usually mean every finished lot, not a composite of the quarter. Read the portal. Then read your SOP. Then do not put both sentences on the same PDP.

## Incoming is not skip-lot in the same breath

111.80(a) wants representative samples of each unique lot of components — and of each unique shipment, and each unique lot within each unique shipment. Identity of dietary ingredients is every lot of that ingredient (111.75(a)(1)), not a Monday club.

You may qualify a supplier and rely on their COA for *some* non-identity specs (piece 02, piece 28). That is not the same as skip-lot of finished product. Keep the words in different drawers.

## What you may say on a site

Allowed when true: "Finished lots are tested on a documented skip-lot plan under 21 CFR 111.75(c). Lot 24A was in the tested subset; certificate [ID]."

Allowed when true: "Every finished lot is tested for [named rows] by [lab]."

Not allowed when the plan is skip-lot: "Every bottle tested." Not allowed when you tested incoming only: "Third-party tested" as if it were the bottle.

Adjectives are labeling (piece 04).

## A Friday check

1. Ask the CMO: every finished batch, or a subset?
2. Get the SOP. If there is no SOP, there is no plan.
3. See whether *this* lot was in the subset. If not, what finished-product data exist for this lot?
4. Look at the sample description on the COA. "Client sample" with no mass and no increment count is a flag.

## A skip-lot that forgot a change

SOP says: test finished metals every fifth lot, unless the extract supplier changes. Lot 24A tested. Lots 24B–24E skipped. Lot 24F is due. In week 3 of 24C–24E, purchasing swapped the ashwagandha house and did not tell quality.

24F's metals look fine. 24C already shipped. A split on a retain from 24C is now the only way to see whether the new house moved the lead number (piece 38, piece 43). If nobody pulled a retain, 24C is a story.

The "unless the supplier changes" clause only works if purchasing and quality share a list. Put supplier changes on the same deviation log as a missed in-process check (piece 27). Skip-lot is a privilege you earn with that list.

## What to attach to the SOP

- Which specs are every-batch, which are subset, which are 111.75(d).
- The statistical or interval rule, in one paragraph a founder can read.
- The reset triggers: OOS, process change, supplier change, pack change.
- Who signs that *this* lot was in or out of the subset.

If that one-pager does not exist, you do not have 111.75(c). You have a habit.

Sampling is how a test earns the right to speak for a lot. If the sample is a convenience, the result is a convenience. Do not ship a convenience.
