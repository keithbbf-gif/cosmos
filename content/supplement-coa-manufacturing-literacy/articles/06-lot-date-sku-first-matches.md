Do not start on the pretty header. Start on the nouns that say whether you are looking at this lot.

A finished-product COA, a label PDF, and a batch production record should agree on five things before you spend time on a metals row. If any of the five fail, the rest of the PDF is someone else's story.

## 1. Product name and SKU

The COA product name has to be the bottle name, or a code your quality agreement maps to the bottle name in one hop.

House codes are fine. Unmapped house codes are how last year's formula ships under this year's label. A CMO that runs fifty private-label variants of "Sleep Blend" will reuse a template. Your job is to see *your* SKU, *your* formula revision, *your* label version.

Put the formula revision on the spec and on the COA. "Sleep Blend" is not a revision. "Sleep Blend / SPEC-014 / label v3" is a revision.

If the COA says 150 mg magnesium (as oxide) and the panel says 150 mg magnesium (as glycinate), stop. That is not a typo you fix in Canva after the truck leaves. That is a different product (piece 35).

## 2. Lot number

The lot on the COA equals the lot on the bottle, the case, the batch record, and the reserve (111.83 wants the reserve identified with the batch, lot, or control number).

Watch for:

- A component lot sitting in the header where the finished lot should be.
- A "lot" that is a date code for the week, shared across three SKUs.
- Two finished lots on one PDF with one set of results.
- A lot that exists on the COA and does not exist in the batch-record log.

111.260 wants the batch, lot, or control number on the batch production record. If you cannot find that number in both places, you cannot release.

## 3. Dates that can be true at the same time

You want a manufacture date, a test date, and (if used) a pack date that a human can believe.

A test date *before* the manufacture date is a recycled PDF or a component COA in a finished-product costume (piece 39).

A test date two years after manufacture, presented as the release test, wants an explanation: stability pull? Retest after a complaint? OOS investigation? "We found a better PDF" is not an explanation.

A 2021 test on a 2026 lot is a costume. Say that out loud in the quality meeting. People get polite about it. Do not.

Labeling rules on expiration dating are their own file (piece 26). The COA date problem is simpler: the test has to be about the lot you are releasing.

## 4. Label claim versus assay, with units

Open 21 CFR 101.36 and your panel. Then open the assay row.

If the panel says 125 mcg vitamin D3 (as cholecalciferol) per serving, the assay row should be D3, as cholecalciferol or with a conversion you can show, in mcg or IU with the factor written down (40 IU = 1 mcg D3). A row that says "vitamin D 5000" with no unit is not a result.

Watch IU versus mcg. Watch mg versus mcg. Watch "as the salt" versus elemental. Magnesium glycinate at 150 mg of *compound* is not 150 mg elemental magnesium. Calcium citrate is the same trap. Folate as mcg DFE is a third trap (piece 16).

A spec of 90–110% of claim, or the USP range you actually adopted, belongs in the spec column. "Conforms" on potency is a rumor (piece 08, piece 15).

Do the multiplication yourself. Assay is often per capsule or per gram. The panel is per serving. Two-capsule servings are how a "correct" per-capsule number becomes a wrong panel.

## 5. Serving size math that matches the bottle

The serving size on the COA (if the lab converted anything to "per serving") has to be the serving size on the panel and the serving size you used to write contaminant limits.

If metals are reported per gram of powder and you scoop 12 g, the daily number is not the per-gram number. If the CMO changed the capsule count from 60 to 90 and kept the same assay-per-capsule COA template, someone will ship the wrong story.

Prop 65 desks and retailer portals live in mcg/day. Your COA may live in ppm. Piece 09 and piece 16 do that conversion. The first-match step is only: *which serving did they use?*

## Do this with three files open

Label PDF. COA. Batch record. Three windows. Not memory.

If you only received the COA, you do not have a first-match review. You have a PDF. Ask for the other two. A CMO that will not send a redacted batch-record header (product, lot, date, yield, QC sign-off) for your own lot is telling you who they think owns the file.

## What changed since 2020

More founders launched without walking a plant. CMOs got better at sending pretty PDFs. The five matches did not get easier. The founders who treat a COA like a bank statement — match the account, match the date, match the amount — still have a brand after the first retailer audit.

If the first five fail, do not negotiate the metals row. Send it back.
