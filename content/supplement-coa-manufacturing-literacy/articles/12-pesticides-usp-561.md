Botanicals grow outside. Outside has insects, fungi, and a chemical industry. A pesticide panel is how you see one part of that. USP \<561\> (Articles of Botanical Origin) is the chapter that includes pesticide limits among other botanical tests. It is not a romantic monograph about "clean herbs." It is a set of numbers and methods.

If your formula has a plant in it — powder, extract, essential oil, tea cut — the pesticide conversation is open until you close it.

## What the panel is

A multi-residue screen looks for a defined list of pesticides, often by GC-MS/MS and LC-MS/MS. The list is not "all pesticides." It is the list in the method. A pass means: nothing on *that* list above *those* limits in *this* sample.

Ask:

- Which method and which residue list?
- Which limits — USP \<561\>, a customer spec, EPA tolerances copied from food, or the lab's default?
- LOQ for the residues that matter to you (piece 14).
- Plant part and species, because limits and likely residues differ.

A row that says "pesticides: conforms" with no list is a costume. A row that says "organic, therefore not tested" is a different costume.

## Organic is a certification. A COA is data.

USDA National Organic Program certification (7 CFR 205) is a process and a paper trail about how a crop was produced and handled. It is valuable. It is not a multi-residue chromatogram of your lot.

Organic lots can still carry residues: drift, old soil, a dishonest handler, a lab list that includes substances allowed in organic production at some levels. If you need a number for a retailer or a 3PL, you need a number. The seal does not print the number.

Do not put "pesticide-free" on a cart page. The honest sentence is "tested against [list]; results below [limits]; lot [ID]; lab [name]."

## Incoming versus finished

Pesticides live on the plant. Incoming botanical testing is the usual place for the panel. A finished-product blend of ten herbs can be hard to interpret and expensive to run every batch. That is a 111.75(d) / skip-lot / qualification design problem (piece 17, piece 28), not a reason to never test.

If you rely on the extract house's pesticide COA, qualify them. Confirm. InnoMark (657518, 1 September 2023) is the public example of "we filed the supplier COA" without reliability. Pesticides were in that sentence.

A solvent extract can concentrate some residues and leave others behind. The starting-material COA and the extract COA are not interchangeable.

## Limits are a choice you have to write down

USP \<561\> limits are one defensible set for many botanicals. Food EPA tolerances are a different set, written for different eating patterns. A customer (a European retailer, a sports program, a private-label buyer) may hand you a third list.

Write *your* spec. If you adopt \<561\>, say so. If you adopt a customer list, say so. If the lab used its default and you never approved it, you do not have 111.70. You have whoever answered the phone at the lab.

Prop 65 lists some pesticide actives. That is a fourth machine. Do not mix it into the USP row without counsel.

## What to do with a hit

A residue above your spec is OOS (piece 22). Hold the drum. Do not blend it into a "dilution will save us" story unless quality control has a written disposition that you would show an investigator.

A residue below your spec but above a customer's list is a commercial problem. Know which list you sold against before the PO.

## Organic plus a residue, and the argument that follows

A USDA organic ashwagandha extract arrives with a seal and a pesticide COA that lists 42 residues, all ND, LOQs in the file. A split on your nickel finds a low level of a residue that was not on the 42. The supplier says "organic, so it cannot be." The lab says "our list is 42." Your retailer says "we use 250."

Three true sentences. They do not cancel. The seal did not look for the 251st. Your split did. The retailer has a list you did not adopt when you wrote 111.70.

Decide, in writing, which list you sell against. If you cannot afford the 250-residue screen on every lot, write a confirmation schedule and a customer-list exception. Do not put "pesticide-free organic" on the PDP while you sort it out.

## What to ask the extract house this week

1. Which residue list and which lab.
2. The last two lots' full reports, not a "typical" sheet.
3. Whether they changed farms or processors in the last year.
4. Whether they will accept a split from your retain.

If they send a seal and no list, you have certification theater. Useful as a purchasing filter. Not a finished thought.

## What this is not

A passing pesticide panel is not "non-toxic," "detox," or a treatment for pesticide exposure. Do not put those words near this paragraph.

It is not a substitute for identity. The cleanest pesticide panel on the wrong species is still the wrong species.

Plants grow outside. Measure what came inside. If you did not measure it, do not advertise the absence.
