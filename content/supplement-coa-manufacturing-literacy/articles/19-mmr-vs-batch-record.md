The master manufacturing record (MMR) is the recipe and the instructions for a product. The batch production record (BPR) is what this run did, who did it, what was issued, what was yielded, what was tested, and who released it. 21 CFR 111.205–111.210 and 111.255–111.260 are those two objects.

If they do not match, you do not have process control. You have a story and a blender.

White-label founders ask for a COA and never ask for the BPR header. That is how a formula revision ships under last month's name.

## What the MMR has to be

111.205: you must have an MMR for each unique formulation of dietary supplement that you manufacture, and for each batch size.

111.210 is the contents list. In operator English:

- Name, strength or concentration of the product, and the batch size this MMR is written for.
- A complete list of components and the amount of each (with the identity specs you need to weigh correctly).
- A statement of any intentional overage.
- Theoretical yield at each stage and the acceptable range.
- A description of the packaging and a representative label, or a cross-reference to the approved label.
- Written instructions, in order, including specifications for each step that needs one, sampling, in-process tests, and what to do if a spec is not met.
- Manual and special instructions that are not obvious from the equipment.

An MMR that says "blend and encapsulate per operator skill" is not an MMR. An MMR that exists only as a CMO's house recipe you have never seen is not *your* MMR. You can contract the writing. You cannot contract away the need to know what is being written (piece 05, piece 32).

Batch size is not a detail. A 50 kg MMR is not a 500 kg run. Scale-up is a new record or a controlled change, not a "we multiplied by ten" in the margin.

## What the BPR has to be

111.255: you prepare a BPR every time you manufacture a batch. It follows the MMR. Quality control reviews and approves it.

111.260 is the contents list. The parts founders actually need to see:

- The unique batch, lot, or control number.
- The identity of equipment and lines used, when that matters.
- The date and time of each maintenance, cleaning, and sanitation that belongs to this run — or a cross-reference that an investigator can follow.
- The unique identifier of each component, packaging, and label issued, and the quantity issued.
- A statement of the actual yield and a comparison to theoretical.
- The results of any testing or examination performed, or a cross-reference to those results (this is where the COA lives).
- Documentation that quality control reviewed the BPR and released or rejected the batch.
- The actual label used, or a cross-reference.
- Any deviations, and the disposition.

If the BPR yield is 62% and the MMR said 95–105%, someone owes a deviation (piece 27). If the BPR lists a magnesium oxide lot and the MMR and the panel say glycinate, someone owes a hold (piece 06, piece 23).

## How to read them in fifteen minutes

You will not audit a full BPR on a sales call. You can ask for:

1. The MMR cover: product name, revision, batch size, formula list.
2. The BPR header for *your last lot*: lot number, date, yield, QC sign-off.
3. The issue table: component lots issued.
4. The cross-reference to the finished-product tests.

If the CMO will not show a redacted version of those four to the person whose name is on the bottle, that is a walk-away input (piece 41).

Do not accept a "batch record" that is three weigh tickets and a smile. Do not accept an MMR that is a spreadsheet titled "recipe_FINAL_v7_USE_THIS.xlsx" with no revision control.

## Change control is the third document

A flavor swap, a capsule color change, a new extract supplier, a new batch size: each of those can make the old MMR a lie. Write who may change the MMR, who approves, and whether a change forces every-batch testing for a stretch (piece 17).

Private-label catalogs that share a house blend across twelve brands are especially sloppy here. Your label version is not their house revision unless the quality agreement says so and the files match.

## What this is not

An MMR is not a marketing brief. Do not put structure/function poetry in the manufacturing instructions. The BPR is not a COA. The COA is one attachment. The BPR is the day.

## A yield that should have stopped the run

MMR theoretical yield: 10,000 capsules, acceptable range 95–105%. BPR actual: 7,400. QC signed release because the HPLC "looked fine" on a composite.

7,400 is a 26% hole. Capsules went somewhere: floor, reject, a second unlabeled tub, a miscount, a fill-weight that ran light for an hour. The HPLC on a composite will not find the light hour if you sampled the end of the run (piece 17, piece 21).

A yield outside the MMR range is a deviation before it is a COA conversation (piece 27). Hold. Count. Then decide. A pretty assay does not fill a 2,600-capsule hole.

## What "revision" means in practice

MMR-014 and label v3 must be cited on the BPR. If the BPR still says MMR-011 and you changed D3 from 50 mcg to 125 mcg two revisions ago, the issue table may still be weighing the old beadlet amount. That is not a clerical typo. That is a different product with this year's panel (piece 35).

Ask for the revision log: who changed what, when, and which lot was first on the new MMR. If there is no log, there is no change control.

If the recipe and the day disagree, believe the disagreement. Then stop the lot.
