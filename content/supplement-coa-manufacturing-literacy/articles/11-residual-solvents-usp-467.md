If the process used a solvent, the solvent is part of the material until you prove otherwise. Ethanol, methanol, hexane, acetone, ethyl acetate, dichloromethane — they are tools. They are also residues. USP \<467\> and ICH Q3C are how grown-up labs talk about that residue.

A botanical "10:1 extract" without a solvent story is an incomplete object. The ratio tells you a concentration legend. It does not tell you what was left behind.

## When the row is required in practice

You want a residual-solvent panel when:

- The dietary ingredient is an extract, oleoresin, or isolate made with organic solvents.
- An excipient or coating was solvent-processed and the supplier's file says so.
- You used a solvent in *your* process (rare on a simple blend; real on some liquid and softgel operations).

You may not need a full \<467\> screen on a mineral salt made in water, or on an unextracted dried herb, if you have a reason in the file. "We never thought about it" is not a reason.

Write the decision on the spec. Incoming extract: solvents. Finished product: solvents if the matrix and the process still need the check, or a documented 111.75(d) story that incoming plus process control is enough.

## Classes, not vibes

ICH Q3C / USP \<467\> group solvents:

**Class 1** — solvents that should be avoided (benzene, carbon tetrachloride, and the rest of that short, ugly list). If a Class 1 shows up, you have a supplier and process problem, not a marketing sentence.

**Class 2** — solvents to be limited (methanol, hexane, dichloromethane, many others). Numeric limits exist. Use them. Convert to your serving if your spec is written that way.

**Class 3** — solvents with low toxic potential (ethanol, acetone, ethyl acetate, among others). Higher limits. Still limits. "It's just ethanol" is how a sloppy extract house skips the row.

Do not recite the entire class tables from memory in a blog caption. Point at the current USP / ICH text. They are updated. This draft is a map.

## Method

Headspace GC is the usual instrument. The method should be named. The list of solvents actually looked for should be named. A "residual solvents: conforms" row that does not say *which* solvents is a costume.

If the extract house used hexane and the lab screened a Class 3 ethanol set, the test did not look at the tool that was used. Ask the supplier for the process solvent. Then ask the lab if that solvent is in the method.

LOQ matters (piece 14). A Class 2 solvent "ND" with an LOQ above your limit is not a pass. It is a method that cannot see the spec.

## Extracts, ratios, and the sales sheet

A 10:1 extract means, in honest use, that ten parts of starting material were used to produce one part of extract. It does not mean the product is ten times "stronger" in a clinical sense. It does not mean the solvent is gone.

Ask:

- Plant part and identity (piece 07).
- Solvent system.
- Marker assay and method (piece 08).
- Residual-solvent results.
- Carrier (maltodextrin and such) — because "extract" is sometimes mostly carrier.

The R&D pack already warned about ratio poetry. This piece only adds: the solvent row is how you see the process that made the poetry.

## Finished-product surprises

Softgels, flavor systems, and some coatings bring their own solvents. A powder blend of solvent-free minerals can still pick up a solvent from a flavor. If the formula has an extract *or* a solvent-processed excipient, the finished-product conversation is open until you close it on paper.

Do not advertise "solvent-free" unless you measured the solvents that would have been used and they are below a written limit. Absence of a row is not absence of a solvent.

## A hexane extract that arrived as "ethanol only"

The sales sheet said "water/ethanol extract." The incoming COA screened ethanol, acetone, ethyl acetate. All ND. You released the drum. Six months later a retailer asks for a hexane row because their EU desk uses a different list. A second lab finds hexane above *their* limit.

Nobody forged the first COA. The first COA looked at the solvents the extract house advertised, not the solvents the extract house used on a Tuesday when ethanol was expensive. Ask for the process solvent in the purchasing spec. Put "notify on solvent change" in the supplier file (piece 28). Then make sure the lab's list includes that solvent.

If the extract house will not name the solvent, you do not have a 111.70 contaminant specification. You have a story about "clean extraction."

## What to put in the purchasing spec

- Allowed process solvents, by name.
- Residual-solvent method (headspace GC or the named equivalent) and the list that must be reported.
- Numeric limits, Class 1 = reject-and-call.
- LOQ at or below each limit (piece 14).
- Change notification if the plant or the solvent system moves.

Attach last lot's chromatogram summary, not only the "conforms" line, until you trust the house.

## What this is not

A passing \<467\> panel is not "detox" and it is not "cleans the liver." Do not put organ-disease language next to a hexane number.

It is also not a consumer seal. "USP 467 tested" is a method claim. Make it true and specific, or do not make it.

If there was a solvent, look for the residue. If you cannot name the solvent, you cannot name the test.
