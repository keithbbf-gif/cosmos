A date on a bottle is a claim about the future. The assay on the release COA is a claim about last Thursday. Those are different sentences. If you print "Best by 06/2028" you owe a reason that this formula, in this pack, still meets the specifications that matter at that date.

21 CFR 111.127 is the quality-control neighbor for expiration dating and related decisions. 111.70 still wants specifications. 101.36 still wants the panel to be truthful. 111.83 keeps reserves so you can look backward when the future argues.

This is not a drug ICH Q1A lecture. It is the operator version: do you have data, or do you have a date someone liked?

## Real-time is the adult study

Same formula, same pack, stored at the labeled conditions (or the ordinary conditions you wrote if the label is silent). Pull at planned points. Assay the ingredients that die. Check micro and physicals that can fail (disintegration, moisture, oxidation on oils). Compare to the spec you will still owe at that point — which may be the same 90–110% or a different through-expiry rule you wrote in advance.

A 24-month date wants 24-month data on *this* article, or a written extrapolation you would show a buyer and an investigator. Extrapolation is allowed in grown-up programs. A guess from a different capsule, a different bottle, or a different extract is not this article.

## Accelerated is supporting evidence

Higher temperature and humidity can show you a problem fast: a gummy that weeps, a vitamin that dies, a desiccant that was a sticker. It does not automatically equal 24 months on a kitchen shelf. Use it to design overage and to kill bad packs early. Do not use a 40 °C month as a personality.

Oils want oxidation numbers (PV, p-AV, TOTOX), not only EPA/DHA milligrams. Probiotics want CFU at the pull points, not only a release count (piece 08). Botanicals want the marker you actually claimed, and a look at moisture.

## Overage is not a study

You put 20% extra D3 in the MMR because someone told you D3 dies. Fine as a hypothesis. The study tells you whether 20% was enough in *this* bottle with *this* headspace and *this* liner.

If release is 115% of claim and twelve months later you are at 82% with a 90% through-expiry spec, the date is wrong or the overage is wrong or the pack is wrong. Changing the cart-page adjective will not lift the HPLC.

## Pack is part of the formula

A blister and a bottle are different products for stability purposes. A cotton coil, a desiccant, an induction seal, a clear jar in a window — they change the curve. If you switch pack and keep the date, you owe a reason.

Ship stress is the cousin (piece 44). A passing 25 °C study does not bless a July trailer.

## What to ask a CMO

1. Do you have real-time data on *this* formula in *this* pack?
2. Which lots, which pull points, which tests?
3. Is the date on my label their house date or a date I chose?
4. What happens if a pull fails — do you notify me (piece 27)?

If the answer is "we use two years for everything," you have a calendar, not a program.

## What you may print

Allowed when true: "Best by date based on real-time stability of this formula in this bottle; lot [ID] release assay [X]."

Not allowed: a date you copied from a competitor.

Not allowed: "expires 2029" on a probiotic with only a manufacture-time CFU.

Not allowed: converting a stability pass into a disease claim ("stays potent so it can treat…").

## Reserves and arguments

When a customer says the lot "doesn't work" eleven months later, you will want *your* retain, stored under the labeled conditions (piece 43). The CMO's retain in a hot closet is a different object.

## Probiotic CFU is a clock

If the panel says CFU at expiry, release CFU must be high enough that the curve can land there. A release that already sits at the expiry claim has no room. Ask for the curve, not only the day-0 count. If they have no curve, you do not have an expiry claim. You have a manufacture-time count. Print that, or start the pulls.

## A house date on a house pack you changed

The CMO prints "Best by 24 months" because that is their catalog default. You swapped their bottle for a clear PET the designer liked. You kept the date.

Their real-time file, if it exists, is on HDPE, opaque, different headspace. Your PET is a new article for light and maybe for moisture. The date is now a guess.

Either put the date back to a conservative number you can defend while you start *your* pulls, or keep the opaque pack. Do not keep the pretty bottle and the borrowed date. The customer who opens a faded oil at month 18 will not care that the catalog default was 24.

The date is a promise. Measure the promise in time, not only on Thursday.
