A finished-product COA is a photograph at the end. In-process controls are whether the batch stayed itself while it was being made. 111.70(c) wants specifications at the points, steps, or stages where control is necessary. 111.75(b) wants you to monitor those points. 111.80(b) wants representative samples there. 111.210 wants those points written into the MMR.

If the only number you have is a lab PDF from Thursday, you do not know what Wednesday did.

## The usual points

**Blend / mix.** Time, speed, order of addition. A uniformity check when the formula can segregate (a tiny D3 dose in a large powder bed; a heavy mineral in a light botanical). If you skip uniformity on a low-dose vitamin, you are hoping the beadlets went everywhere.

**Moisture / water activity.** After a mill, after a wet granulation, before a gummy set, before a bottle that will sit in Houston. Micro and stability both care (piece 10, piece 26).

**Fill weight / count / volume.** Capsules, tablets, scoops, shot bottles. Individual weights, not only an average. An average can hide a bimodal fill that puts one customer at 60% of claim and another at 140%.

**Tablet / capsule physicals.** Hardness, thickness, disintegration when those are how the article does its job as a *dosage form*. Disintegration is not a disease claim. It is a form spec.

**Metal detection / magnets / sieves.** Process hygiene. Document the challenge. A magnet that has not been pulled in a month is furniture.

**Line clearance.** The last SKU's capsules are not a component of this SKU. Check the hopper, the bowl, the printer, the carton stack.

Not every product needs every point. The MMR names the ones this formula needs. Copy-pasting a tablet MMR onto a powder fill is how you invent fake control.

## In-process data is not a finished-product COA

A passing fill-weight chart does not assay D3. A passing blend-time log does not identify the herb. Do not let a CMO hand you in-process sheets when you asked for finished-product metals and potency (piece 02).

The reverse is also true. A beautiful HPLC on Friday does not prove the first 20 minutes of fill were in weight. If you only retain finished-product data, you will lose the argument about the bottles from the start of the run.

## What you can ask to see

You will not stand at the blender for every lot. You can ask, for one recent lot of yours:

- The in-process page of the BPR.
- Fill-weight or volume results versus the spec.
- Any blend-uniformity or marker check the MMR requires.
- Deviations at those points (piece 27).

If those pages are blank and the COA is pretty, the pretty is a photograph of a process nobody recorded.

## Scale and the "simple vitamin" story

A single-ingredient ascorbic acid capsule is simpler than a twelve-herb night blend. It still has a fill weight and a line clearance. "Simple" is not a 111.75(b) exemption. The points can be few. They cannot be imaginary.

Liquids have tank weights, pH, and a hold-time. Gummies have cook temperature and water activity. Softgels have seam and leak checks. Write the ones you have. Walk the ones they claim.

## What this is not

In-process control is not "small-batch artisanal energy." It is a named step with a number.

## Printers are an in-process point

Lot codes and dates printed on the bottle are specifications for the packaged product (111.70(g), 111.80(e)). A printer that smears or that still holds yesterday's lot is a line-clearance failure. Check first bottle, a middle bottle, last bottle. Keep the examples with the BPR.

A beautiful HPLC does not fix a lot code that is unreadable at the 3PL (piece 36).

## Low-dose D3 in a large bed

You are putting 125 mcg of D3 into a 12 g scoop via beadlets. The blend is 200 kg. A few grams of beadlets have to find every scoop. If you only record "blend 12 minutes, speed 2" and never check uniformity, the HPLC on a finished composite can still pass while a subset of bottles is light.

A marker check on three locations in the blender — or a finished-product assay on start/middle/end of fill — is how you see segregation. It does not have to be a pharmaceutical blend-uniformity saga. It has to be a written point that can fail.

If the CMO says "we have never had a problem," ask for the last uniformity or start-middle-end data on a low-dose SKU. If those data do not exist, the first time you see a problem will be a customer who opened a light bottle.

If the step is necessary, name it in the MMR. Sample it. Write the result on the BPR. Then take the Friday photograph. Not instead of it.
