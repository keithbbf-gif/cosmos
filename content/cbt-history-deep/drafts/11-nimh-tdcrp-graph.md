Irene Elkin and colleagues, writing in the *Archives of General Psychiatry* at the end of the 1980s, reported the National Institute of Mental Health Treatment of Depression Collaborative Research Program. Cognitive behavior therapy, interpersonal psychotherapy (the Klerman–Weissman school), imipramine plus clinical management, and a placebo-plus-management condition had been run across sites. The papers (design 1986; general effectiveness 1989) are the historical object. This page will not re-litigate every secondary analysis. It will say what it meant that a federal graph had three psychosocial and pharmacological cousins on it.

A third school on a federal graph is a political fact. It is also a reminder that "the empirical turn" was never only Beck's.

## Why the graph needed more than Penn

If the only talk therapy that could be studied was cognitive therapy, the study would have looked like a coronation. IPT — a time-limited therapy built around grief, disputes, transitions, and deficits in a social field — gave the graph another talking column. Medication gave it a pill column. Clinical management gave the pill a bedside. Placebo made the argument expensive and, to some readers, devastating.

Sites had to agree on what they were treating. DSM-III had just made that agreement possible in official English. Manuals made the hours describable. Training and adherence checks made the word "cognitive" mean roughly the same thing in Oklahoma and in Washington. That "roughly" is doing a lot of work. Multi-site studies are empires of roughly.

What the public heard, later, was often a winner. What the papers showed was messier: severity matters; sites differ; follow-up arguments never end. Historians who need a simple "CBT arrived" slide have to stop reading before the letters. This pack will not stop.

## What IPT is doing in a CBT deepen pack

This is not an IPT history. The survey pack's house is the place for Klerman and Weissman as figures if a later writer takes them. They appear here because Beck's school is unintelligible as a 1980s event without the neighbor on the same page. A therapy becomes "the" empirical therapy only if the other columns are blank. The columns were not blank.

IPT also kept a social field on the graph while cognitive therapy kept a sentence. Rural hours that hear a fight with a partner as the week, not a cognition about the fight, are standing nearer IPT's column than they may know. The graph did not ask them to choose a religion. Later training cultures sometimes did.

## After the graph

STAR*D, in the 2000s, is a different federal object: sequenced antidepressant strategies, Rush's name again, a controversy about how "remission" was counted. It is not TDCRP's sequel so much as a reminder that the graph-making appetite did not die. IAPT, in the UK, is a still later appetite: a government program that treated NICE-recommended protocols as a public utility. The 1989 papers are an American ancestor of that appetite.

Managed care recognized the graph's cousin: something you could authorize in a limited dose. University clinics argued about dose. County clinics argued about who could get in the door. The graph did not resolve those arguments. It gave them a picture to point at.

WOW Therapies will not, here, declare a winner among the 1989 columns. If a later service page names a method, that page needs its own limits sentence. This page names an event: the 1980s federal government paid to see whether a manualized cognitive therapy, a manualized interpersonal therapy, and a pill could be compared as if they were the same kind of object. The "as if" is the history.

A contemporary reader who meets "evidence-based" on a website is meeting a grandchild of that as-if. Grandchildren forget grandparents. Elkin's name is less famous than Beck's. The graph is why Beck's school could speak federal. Speaking federal is a mixed gift. It funds. It also teaches a profession to ignore work that cannot get on the graph. Community, bilingual, and feminist work spent years in that ignore. The critiques essay in this pack picks up there.

The design paper (1986) is worth as much as the results paper (1989) for a historian. Design is where a profession shows what it is willing to count. Results are where it shows what it got. Slide decks skip to results. This page keeps the design on the table: sites, manuals, a pill arm, a management arm, a definition of depression that DSM-III had just made official. That furniture is the 1980s. Cognitive therapy sat down in it. It did not build the table alone.

## Elkin's name, and why slide decks drop it

Irene Elkin is less famous than Beck and less famous than a pill. Coordinating a multi-site federal program is unglamorous work: manuals, adherence, a statistical plan, a fight about who is an author. Histories that drop the coordinator drop the fact that "CBT arrived" was a managed arrival. Someone scheduled it.

Secondary analyses of TDCRP never ended. Severity, site, therapist effects — the letters columns of the 1990s are a literature. This page will not pick a reanalysis as the truth. It will say the argument is the point. A graph that ends argument is propaganda. A graph that starts argument is a 1980s federal object doing its job. CBT's prestige after 1989 is partly the prestige of having sat still for that job.

A rural reader does not need the p-values. They need to know that "evidence-based" has a 1989 picture behind it, and that the picture had more than one talking column. IPT is not a failure to be CBT. It is a neighbor who also learned to be filmed. Neighbors keep a street from becoming a church.

Oklahoma and Washington, in the design, had to mean roughly the same "cognitive." Roughly is the cost of a multi-site empire. A rural hour that does not mean the same thing as a 1980s site is not therefore a failure. It is a different room. The graph measured rooms it could staff. It did not measure the country.

Letters-to-the-editor in 1990 and 1991 are primary sources a later historian should read before declaring a winner. This pack will not declare one. It will leave Elkin's name on the plaque so a slide that says only Beck cannot stand as the 1980s federal story.

A 1987 site visit — the unglamorous kind, with a binder and a question about adherence — is the federal graph's real romance. Romance is the wrong word. Labor is the right one. Elkin coordinated labor. Labor made "roughly the same cognitive" possible in two cities. Roughly is still the cost. This URL will not sand it smooth.
