**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Albert Ellis liked to tell Friday-night audiences that a slave-teacher in Nicopolis had already said the useful thing. Men are disturbed not by things, but by the views they take of them. The sentence is from the *Enchiridion* of Epictetus, or from a long English habit of rendering it. Ellis put it in *Reason and Emotion in Psychotherapy* (1962) the way a carpenter puts a reused beam in a new house: visible, load-bearing, not his timber.

This page is about that beam. It is not a class in Stoic spiritual exercise. It is not an invitation to treat a panic attack with a Roman handbook. The historical claim is smaller and stranger. When mid-century American clinicians wanted a lineage that was not Freud's, some of them reached for a philosophy that had already treated judgments as the place a life can break.

## A handbook, not a consulting room

Epictetus taught in the late first and early second centuries. He did not keep office hours. The *Enchiridion* is a short manual assembled by a student, Arrian, from a larger set of *Discourses*. The voice is abrupt. It tells a reader to distinguish what is up to them from what is not. It does not offer a diagnosis. It does not film a session.

Marcus Aurelius, writing *Meditations* as notes to himself, belongs in the same cabinet: a powerful man talking himself down from the day's impressions. Later centuries bound these books with Christian and then secular covers. Victorian readers found consolation. Early twentieth-century readers found a psychology of attitude before "attitude" was a scale.

None of that is cognitive therapy. Cognitive therapy needs a clinic, a supervisor, and, later, a trial. Stoicism had a school and a reputation. The two objects got confused in the 1990s and 2000s, when airport books discovered both "CBT" and "the Stoics" and sanded them into one product. A rural Arkansas hour that asks where a *must* is sitting is using Ellis. It is not recreating Nicopolis.

## Why Ellis needed a dead teacher

Ellis had Adler and a New York quarrel with respectability. He also needed, in 1955, a way to say that a belief could be argued with without waiting for a childhood scene to surface. The Stoic line did that work in public. It made REBT sound older than the Eisenhower decade. It made the attack on "musts" sound like hygiene rather than rudeness.

He was not the first modern clinician to like the ancients. Adler's individual psychology already talked as if a person organized a life around a fiction. Kelly, in 1955 — the same year Ellis began naming his therapy — treated constructs as hypotheses. The Stoic citation was Ellis's particular stagecraft. Beck, quieter and more medical-school, rarely needed a toga in the paragraph. His 1963 and 1964 papers on thinking and depression cite patients' sentences, not Arrian.

That difference still matters on a practice site. One founder advertised a lineage. The other advertised a note. Both were building a therapy that could interrupt a sentence. Only one of them wanted the interruption to sound like a classical education.

## What the borrowing left out

Stoic ethics is a whole life: cosmopolitan duty, a physics of the cosmos, a training of attention that is closer to a monastery than to a six-session authorization. Ellis took the cognitive fragment — the view, the judgment, the demand — and left the cosmology on the floor. Later popularizers left even more: no mention of slavery, no mention of what "not up to us" meant for a person who was not a free male citizen, no mention of the parts of Epictetus that would fail a modern clinic's ethics board.

Feminist and liberation readers have said the obvious thing. A philosophy that tells you to adjust your view can be medicine. It can also be a muzzle. The sibling essay on critiques the worksheet could not hear takes that quarrel into the late twentieth century. Here the historical fact is earlier: the sentence arrived in American therapy already cut down to a slogan.

A second loss is quieter. Stoic writing is often addressed to the self. REBT Friday nights were addressed to a stranger on a folding chair. Beck's tapes were addressed to a supervisor. The audience changed the sentence. What had been a midnight note became a public correction. Some people could stand that. Some people heard another demand.

## How not to illustrate this

There is no photograph of Epictetus. The busts that circulate are later inventions, the way Robert-Fleury's Pinel canvas is an allegory. If a graphics agent wants a lead image, a pre-1930 title page of an English *Enchiridion* — with a named dead engraver and a clean Commons file — is a book, not a face. An AI toga is a lie. A lotus is a different religion.

Type is enough: "Epictetus, *Enchiridion*." Dates on the teacher are approximate. Honesty about approximation is part of the style.

## What a contemporary clinician is still reading

A clinician who notices that a patient is suing the universe for a preference is standing in a long hallway. Ellis hung a sign on that hallway in 1955. The sign quoted a dead teacher. The hallway includes Adler, Kelly, and a medical psychiatrist in Philadelphia who wrote the sentences down instead of quoting Nicopolis.

WOW Therapies does not, on this page, teach a Stoic exercise or an ABC form. The sibling figure essays on Ellis and Beck stay with the men and their institutes. This page only names the timber: an old claim that a view can wound, imported into a noisy American clinic, later flattened into an app that had never read Arrian.

If you came here for a morning practice, this series will not give you one. If you came here to understand why a 1962 book sounds briefly like a Roman handbook, the 1962 book is still the object. Read it as a mid-century American argument that needed an ancestor. Then notice how much of the ancestor did not survive the import.

The later "Stoic revival" of the 2010s — blogs, military briefings, Silicon Valley breakfasts — is a third career for the same sentence. It is not REBT and it is not Beck. It is a culture that likes portable maxims. A history site can admit the rhyme without handing out the maxim as care.

## A Friday night, and a translation problem

Ellis's public demonstrations on East 65th Street — the ones the 2005 board fight later canceled — treated the Epictetus line as a warm-up. The volunteer sat. Ellis named a demand. The audience laughed, or winced, or learned. That room is a New York object. It is not a Roman school, and it is not a rural hour in Southeast Arkansas. The transplant problem is the same one the Ellis figure essays name: theater travels badly if you copy the volume and not the ear.

There is also a translation problem historians should not sand down. The English Ellis liked is one rendering among several. W. A. Oldfather's Loeb (1928) is a convenient scholarly English, not Ellis's stage Bible. Popular websites now print the line as if it were a single authorized verse. It is not. A clinic that treats a translation as a protocol has made a category error this series exists to refuse.

A last dated neighbor: cognitive therapists in the 1980s and 1990s sometimes listed " stoic philosophy" on slide one of an introductory workshop, then spent the day on inventories. The slide was ancestry theater. The day was workforce training. Both can be true. Only one of them belongs on a history page as the thing that happened. The slide is the thing that happened to the sentence. The inventories are the thing that happened to the profession.

This pack's later essays on IAPT and on apps show the sentence's fourth career: a government program and a phone that can display a maxim without a teacher. Epictetus had students. Ellis had a folding chair. Beck had a tape. The phone has a tap. Each step shed context. The shed context is the history.
