Hoeber issued Aaron T. Beck's *Depression: Clinical, Experimental, and Theoretical Aspects* in 1967. Later printings and the retitled *Depression: Causes and Treatment* are a publishing history. The 1967 object is the one this page keeps on the table: a thick, sometimes dry book that treated a depressed person's sentences as findings rather than as foam on a drive.

It is not yet the 1979 Guilford manual. It is not yet a franchise. It is the year a set of papers became a volume a colleague could assign. Kelly died that year. The coincidence is editorial. One psychology of constructs closed. One psychology of depressive thinking opened a door the hospitals would later walk through.

## What the book actually is

The 1963 and 1964 *Archives of General Psychiatry* papers had already said that thinking in depression has idiosyncratic content and that a therapy might address it. The 1967 book gathered clinical description, experimental bits, and a theory of a triad — a bleak view of self, world, and future — into a single spine. Readers who know only the triad from a workshop poster have not read the book. The book is slower. It argues with the idea that depression is only inverted hostility. It takes dreams and experiments as data, not as ornaments.

It is also a medical book. Beck writes as a psychiatrist among psychiatrists. The later popularization — Burns's 1980 *Feeling Good*, the waiting-room BDI, the insurer's six sessions — is a different social class of object. Confusing them is how a history becomes a brochure. This pack keeps the classes separate. 1967 is for colleagues. 1979 is for a workforce. 1980 is for a bookstore.

A person who quotes "cognitive triad" without the 1967 argument is quoting a sticker. Stickers travel. Books wait.

## What 1967 still is not

It is not a protocol this page will reprint. It is not a claim that every sadness is a distortion. It is not a claim that social conditions are imaginary. Feminist and community clinicians said that at the time and said it louder later. Beck, to his later credit, kept publishing limits. The sticker did not.

It is not Ellis. The 1962 *Reason and Emotion* is loud because Ellis was loud. The 1967 *Depression* is quiet because Beck wanted a department to take him seriously. American hospitals preferred quiet. That preference is political as well as stylistic. A therapy that sounds like a lecture hall can be granted. A therapy that sounds like a roast is a boutique.

It is not DSM-III. The 1980 manual would later make "major depression" a checklist a trial could count. Beck's 1967 depression is still a clinical picture with edges. The later marriage of checklist and thought-as-data is the empirical-turn story. 1967 is the data-before-the-checklist moment.

## How a school reads a book

Students after 1979 often met Beck through the manual and through Judith Beck's *Basics and Beyond* (1995). The 1967 volume became, for many of them, the book you were supposed to have read. Supposed-to-have-read is a status, not a practice. A deepen pack exists to put the status back into a year.

What a contemporary clinician is still doing when they take a sentence seriously — not as a symbol of something else, as a claim about Tuesday — is standing in the 1967 building. What they are doing when they treat the sentence as the whole house is standing in a later annex the book did not draw.

## The Hoeber object, and who could buy it

Hoeber was a medical imprint. The 1967 book's first life was on hospital and medical-school shelves, not on a chain-bookstore table. That matters for class. Ellis had already published a public book (1961) that a non-clinician could take home. Beck's 1967 volume assumed a reader who knew what a ward sounded like. The later *Cognitive Therapy and the Emotional Disorders* (1976) opened the door wider. The 1979 Guilford manual opened it onto a training shop. Each widening changed the voice.

A librarian's accession stamp in 1967 is part of the history. So is the colleague who wrote a cool review and the colleague who stole the triad for a lecture. Schools form in those small thefts. By the time the 1970s depression trials wanted a psychosocial arm they could describe, the 1967 book had already made "cognitive" a word a psychiatrist could say without sounding like a philosopher.

Rural clinics did not buy Hoeber volumes in bulk. They bought, later, whatever a supervisor photocopied. Photocopy culture is how 1967 reached places Penn did not. It is also how the book became a chapter, then a page, then a triangle on a whiteboard. This essay exists to put the triangle back on a spine with a year.

If you have only met the sticker, the 1967 book will feel long. That is a feature. Length was how Beck asked a department to take thinking seriously as a medical object. The later culture asked for shorter. Shorter won the waiting room. The long book is still the better honor.

## 1967 beside 1962 and 1976

Ellis's professional book was five years old when Hoeber issued *Depression*. A colleague could have read both in one season and heard two Americas: a New York argument with a demand, a Philadelphia argument with a picture of sadness. The 1976 *Cognitive Therapy and the Emotional Disorders* would later open Beck's door beyond depression. 1967 is still the depression door. Doors have years. Brochures hate years because years prevent a single invention story.

A library copy with an accession stamp from 1968 is a better lead image than a face. If the scan of the *page* is clean, use it. If it is not, type the title and the year. Either way, keep the book longer than the sticker. This pack's job, on this URL, is that one refusal.

Hoeber's medical readers were not Friday-night volunteers. They were psychiatrists who needed a volume that sounded like their journals. Beck wrote them one. The later bookstore objects — Burns 1980, a waiting-room form — should not be allowed to talk over that volume. 1967 is still a colleague's book. Keep it that way on this URL.

A 1968 journal club that assigned the Hoeber volume was already doing the work a later workshop would flatten into a triangle. Journal clubs leave notes in margins. Margins are a better archive than a sticker. If a later editor finds a contemporaneous review, add it. Until then, keep 1967 longer than the poster.
