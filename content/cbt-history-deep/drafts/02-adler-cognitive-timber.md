In 1957 Albert Ellis published "Rational Psychotherapy and Individual Psychology" in the *Journal of Individual Psychology*. The venue was a confession. He was not pretending to have invented a psychology of mistaken ideas out of the Manhattan air. He was telling Adler's remaining readers that a noisier American clinic could use their timber.

Alfred Adler had broken with Freud in 1911 and spent the rest of his life talking as if a person were organizing a life around a guiding fiction: inferiority, compensation, a private logic about how the world must be met. *Über den nervösen Charakter* (1912) is the early book. *What Life Should Mean to You* (1931) is the American public object. The sibling therapy-history pack already has a figure essay on Adler. This page does not retell Vienna. It follows the plank that ended up in REBT and, more quietly, in the cognitive therapies that preferred Beck's manners.

## A fiction that can be argued with

Adler's "fictional finalism" — the borrowed Vaihinger word for a as-if goal — treated a life as aimed. The aim might be hidden. It was not therefore sacred. A clinician could ask what a symptom was *for* in a person's movement through a social world. That question is not a thought record. It is older and more social than Beck's 1963 papers. It is also the reason Ellis could later attack a *must* without waiting for a dream.

Individual psychology was a therapy of encouragement and of community feeling (*Gemeinschaftsgefühl*). American popular Adlerianism sometimes flattened that into pep. Ellis flattened it differently. He kept the argument with the private logic and turned up the volume. He left much of the socialist and educational Adler on the table. The 1931 book still reads like a civic lecture. The 1962 Ellis book reads like a cross-examination.

A history that says "Ellis came from Adler" and stops has not said enough. He came from Adler the way a renovation comes from a house: some walls stay, the staircase moves, the neighbors complain.

## Why Beck did not need the same citation

Aaron Beck trained as an analyst in Philadelphia and left that training by writing sentences down. His published debts in the 1960s run toward depression research, inventories, and a quarrel with the idea that hostility must sit under sadness. Adler is not the name on the 1967 spine. The cognitive triad — self, world, future — can be read as a cousin of Adler's organizing fiction. Cousin is not citation.

Donald Meichenbaum, writing *Cognitive-Behavior Modification* in 1977, sat closer to the behavioral lab than to Adler's lecture hall. George Kelly's 1955 constructs were another American cognitive psychology that did not owe Adler a footnote in every chapter. The mid-century "cognitive" turn had more than one door. Ellis used Adler's door and then slammed it so the room would notice.

On a practice site this matters because readers collapse names. "CBT" in a waiting-room brochure may mean Beck's manuals, a grandchild of Ellis, a Kendall protocol for a child, or a worksheet an insurer recognizes. Adler is rarely in the brochure. He is in the 1957 paper. The paper is the historical object this pack wants on the shelf.

## What Adler warned that REBT sometimes forgot

Adler insisted that a person is unintelligible outside a social field: birth order as a folk map, yes, but more seriously class, gender, and the school. A private logic is private *in* a city. Ellis, on his better days, knew that a violent home is not a pebble plus a belief. On his worse days the volume treated the situation as if it were imaginary. The Adlerian remainder is the social field. A rural hour that hears only the *must* and not the landlord has used half the timber.

Adler also died in 1937, on a lecture tour in Scotland, before the war that would scatter his students. American individual psychology after 1945 was a smaller guild than psychoanalysis and a quieter one than the later CBT workforce. Ellis's 1957 paper is, among other things, a raid on a shrinking archive. He took what he could carry into a therapy that hospitals would eventually film. They filmed Beck first.

## How this pack will not use him

No birth-order quiz. No "encouragement" script. No list of lifestyle types. The sibling figure essay on Adler in the therapy-history pack is the biographical door. The sibling Ellis essays here and next door are the noisy American door. This page is the hinge: a 1957 journal that still had "Individual Psychology" on the masthead, and a 1962 book that mostly did not.

WOW Therapies does not practice "Adlerian therapy" by naming Adler in a history post. If a later service page ever names a school, that page needs its own review. Here the claim is only: when Ellis needed an ancestor who was not Freud and not a Stoic, he had one, and he said so in the journal that still belonged to that ancestor's readers.

The later cognitive-behavioral house forgot the citation more often than it forgot the move. Asking what a life is organized *around* is older than a BDI score. The score won the hospital. The question did not die. It just changed clothes.

## 1935, 1956, and the shrinking archive

Adler spent his last American years lecturing to teachers and to child-guidance audiences. The New York institute that carried his name after 1935 was a civic project as much as a clinical one. Rudolf Dreikurs, in Chicago, made a classroom version that school counselors still half-remember. Ellis, training as a clinician in that same city-system, could hear Adler without becoming a Dreikursian. The 1957 paper is the sound of a man taking the private-logic piece and walking it into a different building.

Heinz and Rowena Ansbacher's English editions — *The Individual Psychology of Alfred Adler* (1956) is the fat reader many Americans actually used — sit on the same shelf as Ellis's paper. A generation that could not read the 1912 German could still steal the 1956 English. That is how timber moves in a bilingual profession: not by pilgrimage to Vienna, by a thick paperback.

Beck's Philadelphia was a different library. The analytic institutes taught Freud and, if you were lucky, some ego psychology. Adler was a footnote about a split. When Beck later described "rules" and "assumptions," he was not quoting *Gemeinschaftsgefühl*. He was describing what a depressed intern said on a Tuesday. The resemblance is real. The citation trail is not. This pack will not invent a meeting that did not happen.

A rural clinic that still owns a Dreikurs paperback and a later CBT workbook is holding two American translations of a European quarrel. They do not cancel. They also do not add up to a protocol this page will print. The historical useful sentence is only this: Ellis admitted the debt in 1957, in the journal that could still hear the name, and then built a therapy loud enough that many of his students never opened Adler at all.
