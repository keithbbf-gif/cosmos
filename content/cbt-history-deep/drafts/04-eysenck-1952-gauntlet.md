**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 1952 the *Journal of Consulting Psychology* printed H. J. Eysenck's "The Effects of Psychotherapy: An Evaluation." The paper compared published outcomes with estimates of spontaneous remission and concluded that the evidence then available did not show that psychotherapy worked. Clinicians hated it. Behavior therapists later loved to quote it. Historians now treat it as a gauntlet: a thrown glove, not a final score.

This page is about the glove. It is not a rehabilitation of Eysenck the later controversialist. It is not a claim that talking never helped anyone in 1952. It is a claim about what a profession does when someone says, in public, that its results look like the weather.

## What he actually threw

Eysenck assembled other people's numbers. He did not run the trial the 1980s would later want. His remission estimates were argued at once. The categories of "psychotherapy" he lumped together would not survive a modern methods seminar. All of that is true and does not cancel the event. The event was a psychologist at the Institute of Psychiatry in London telling an American journal that the emperor's improvement rate might be the base rate.

Hans Jürgen Eysenck (1916–1997) is a difficult ancestor. German-born, British-working, later entangled in arguments about race and intelligence that this pack will not launder. The 1952 paper is separable as a professional-history object the way a knife is separable from a later crime. Separable is not innocent. A history site names the later record and still uses the 1952 date, because the date is when the clinic's confidence was asked to show its work.

Behavior therapy in Britain and South Africa — Eysenck's Maudsley, Wolpe's Johannesburg — heard a permission structure: if insight therapy could not prove itself, a therapy built on exposure and inhibition might. Wolpe's 1958 book is the clinic that walked through that door. Beck's later manuals walked through a neighboring door with better bedside manners. Both doors are easier to open after someone has kicked the old one.

## What the gauntlet did not do

It did not end psychoanalysis in 1952. American institutes were still filling. It did not create CBT. Beck was a young psychiatrist; Ellis was still closer to sexology and advice than to a named school. It did not settle the spontaneous-remission fight. That fight is a graveyard of reanalyses.

It did change the *tone* of a certain kind of ambition. A therapy that wanted to answer Eysenck would have to count. Counting needs an object. DSM-III (1980) later manufactured objects by checklist. The NIMH Treatment of Depression Collaborative Research Program (Elkin et al., 1989) put three schools on one graph. Those are descendants of a mood, not of a single table in 1952. Moods have lineages too.

A rural clinician who has never read Eysenck still lives in a world the gauntlet helped make: progress notes, outcome measures, a supervisor who wants a graph. That world has costs this pack names elsewhere (the worksheet that never looks up; the six-session cousin). The costs do not make the 1952 question dishonorable. They make it incomplete.

## How later slide decks abused him

Introductory CBT workshops in the 1990s sometimes opened with Eysenck as a cartoon villain of psychoanalysis and a cartoon hero of science. The cartoon skipped his later work's worst controversies and skipped every methodological objection to the 1952 paper. This pack will not reprint the cartoon.

It will also not use Eysenck as a stick with which to beat every talking therapy that cannot produce a 1989-style graph. Some work is local, bilingual, unfunded, and still a craft. The gauntlet asked for evidence. It did not decree that only one genre of evidence exists. When Division 12 later wrote lists, it chose a genre. That choice is the Chambless essay. 1952 is the earlier noise.

## A London address, an American journal

The Maudsley and the Institute of Psychiatry were already a behavior-therapy city by the late 1950s. American readers of the 1952 paper sat in a different city: New York, Boston, the Menninger, the analytic institutes. The paper's power was partly geographic. A British psychologist used American pages to scold an American (and international) guild. Transatlantic scolding is a genre. This instance had numbers attached.

Eysenck's later textbooks taught personality dimensions and a biological story that many clinicians never needed. The 1952 paper is the piece that leaked into psychotherapy's argument with itself. Leakage is how a methods complaint becomes a founding myth. Founding myths need editors. The editor's job here is to keep the date and drop the halo.

WOW Therapies does not, on this page, declare that "the evidence" now belongs to one school. If a later service page names a method, that page needs its own review and its own limits sentence. This page names a 1952 event: a journal article that made it harder to treat improvement as self-evident. Beck's inventories and Linehan's 1991 trial are later answers in a conversation that article made louder.

The honest remainder is uncomfortable. Some of what Eysenck wanted — counting, comparison, a refusal of unfalsifiable guild talk — was a reform. Some of what followed — a profession that treats an unlisted therapy as a superstition — was a new guild. Both grew from the same thrown glove. A history site can hold both without selling either.

## After 1952, before the manual

The 1950s and 1960s filled with replies: better follow-ups, arguments about what "recovery" meant, the first generation of controlled studies that were still too small. By the time Beck, Rush, Shaw, and Emery wrote the 1979 depression manual, the gauntlet was a grandfathered insult. You did not have to agree with Eysenck to write a book a trial could see. You only had to live in a profession that had been dared.

That dare is why this deepen pack exists beside the survey pack's shorter cognitive-turn essay. The survey names the turn. This page names one of the insults that made the turn feel necessary — and names the insult's author as a man whose later record a reader must not be asked to swallow whole.

## 1952 in the same decade as a naming

Put Eysenck's paper on a table with 1955. Ellis is naming a therapy. Kelly is issuing two volumes. Beck is still an analyst. Wolpe's book is unwritten. The gauntlet does not create those objects. It makes a weather in which a therapy that can count will later feel like an answer. Weather is not destiny. Psychoanalysis continued. Humanistic work had not yet had its American decade. The 1952 paper is one loud British sentence in an American journal, and the later CBT house learned to quote it as if it had been a founding charter.

It was not a charter. It was a methods complaint with numbers that were argued at once. This pack keeps the complaint and drops the charter. Readers who need Eysenck's later controversies will find them in any honest encyclopedia; they will not find them laundered here. The 1952 date is enough for the furniture: after someone asks whether talking does anything, someone else will try to write talking down.

A rural clinician who has never read the paper still writes notes as if someone might ask. That "someone" is a descendant of 1952, of DSM-III, of a reviewer, of a list. Descendants stack. This essay names the first stack that CBT histories like to start with, and then refuses to let it be the only stack.
