**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 2004 *Behavior Therapy* printed Steven C. Hayes's paper "Acceptance and Commitment Therapy, Relational Frame Theory, and the Third Wave of Behavioral and Cognitive Therapies." The same year, Guilford issued *Mindfulness and Acceptance: Expanding the Cognitive-Behavioral Tradition*, edited by Hayes, Victoria Follette, and Marsha Linehan. A nickname and an anthology arrived together. People had already been doing DBT, ACT, and MBCT. Now they had a geology.

First wave: stimulus, response, Wolpe's kind of clinic. Second wave: cognition, Beck's kind of sentence. Third wave: the *relationship to* thoughts and feelings — acceptance, mindfulness, values — without giving up data. The nickname is marketing as much as geology. The work is real.

## What the 2004 paper actually did

Hayes described ACT and relational frame theory, then offered a characterization of a "new generation": function over form, contextual change, repertoires instead of elimination of a narrow symptom, issues that matter to the clinician as well as the client. He placed DBT and other packages in the weather. He was not inventing Linehan's 1991 trial or the 1993 Guilford book. He was giving a family a last name.

Families argue about last names. Stefan Hofmann and Gordon Asmundson, in *Clinical Psychology Review* (2008), asked whether the new wave was old hat: acceptance and exposure with better manners, not a rupture. The argument is intra-family. A practice site does not need to pick a champion. It needs to know the fight is about how much "acceptance" is a new school and how much it is a second-wave shop in new clothes.

Linehan's dialectic — accept the person as they are *and* insist that they change — was already a 1990s staffing pattern: skills group, individual hour, consultation team, a phone-coaching idea administrators would fund or gut. Hayes's 1999 ACT book was already a landmark. Segal, Williams, and Teasdale's 2002 MBCT book was already a relapse-prevention object borrowed from a medical mindfulness clinic Kabat-Zinn had opened in 1979. 2004 named the cluster. Naming is power. It is also a product.

## What the nickname flattened

It flattened dates. DBT is a 1991–1993 object. ACT as a named therapy is 1990s with 1980s behavioral roots. MBCT is 2002 with a 1979 medical cousin. A "wave" arriving in 2004 is a historian's convenience.

It flattened politics. Acceptance, taught badly, can sound like "tolerate your oppression." Linehan's and Hayes's better students treat acceptance as a way to *move*, not as a muzzle. The critique still has to be answered in rooms where a woman's "value" has been defined by someone else. A nickname does not answer it.

It flattened training. A clinician who says "just notice that" after a weekend workshop is not doing DBT or ACT. They are doing a vibe. The Association for Contextual Behavioral Science grew around ACT the way DBT grew around a research clinic. Workshops, listservs, a journal: a school becoming a workforce. The historical question is not whether acceptance "works." The question is what a school does when its vocabulary leaves the room where someone is still at risk.

## How this pack will use the wave

As a filing cabinet, not as a law of nature. Era essays on behavioral activation, process-based CBT, and the critiques the worksheet could not hear sit beside this one. Figure essays on Linehan, Hayes, Segal, Kabat-Zinn, and Hofmann stay with people. The survey pack's third-wave essay is the shorter public door. This page is the 2004 door: a journal, an anthology, a fight about geology.

WOW Therapies will not list skill modules or print a values card. If the clinic ever offers a named third-wave service, that belongs on a clinical page with training, supervision, and consent. Here the claim is only: at the start of the twenty-first century, a behavioral journal gave a cluster of therapies a nickname, and the nickname stuck harder than some of the authors wanted.

A rural hour that asks a person to sit with a feeling without immediately fixing the sentence is standing in weather 2004 named. A rural hour that uses "mindfulness" as yogurt branding is standing in the boom the nickname helped sell. Both hours exist. This series is for telling them apart.

The 2004 anthology's table of contents is a cast list: Linehan, Hayes, the MBCT trio's weather, functional analytic psychotherapy, others. Cast lists are how a generation recognizes itself. They are also how a generation excludes. Behavioral activation, metacognitive therapy, schema therapy — neighbors that sometimes get waved in and sometimes left in the hall — have their own essays in this pack. The nickname does not own them. It tried to weather them.

## Reno, a presidential address, a reprint life

Hayes's paper began as a presidential address for the Association for Advancement of Behavior Therapy (the society's name in that era). Addresses are theater. Theater becomes a PDF that workshops cite without reading the middle. The middle is RFT, a basic-science claim about language that most clinicians never needed in order to say "third wave" at a cocktail hour. This pack will not teach RFT. It will say the 2004 object had a science story and a branding story bound together. Binding is why Hofmann could later call the wave old hat without denying the papers.

A rural hour that has never heard "relational frame" may still be standing in the weather 2004 named. Weather does not require the address. The address required a journal willing to print a geology. *Behavior Therapy* printed it. The reprint life did the rest. Nicknames that find a reprint life become furniture. Furniture is what this URL is for: a year, a journal, a fight, no lotus.

Follette's name on the 2004 anthology is easy to drop. Do not drop it. Anthologies have editors who make a cast list possible. Hayes wrote a geology. Linehan's work was already staffed. Follette helped bind a book. Binding is how nicknames find a Guilford spine as well as a journal.

A 2005 workshop flyer that used "third wave" before the attendee had read the 2004 paper is already the reprint life. Flyers are not journals. This URL keeps the journal so the flyer cannot be the origin story.

A 2006 journal special issue that treated the nickname as settled geology was already the reprint life's cousin. Special issues recruit. This URL keeps the 2004 presidential-address origin so a special issue cannot be the birth certificate.

A 2004 reprint request that turned an address into a PDF is the object workshops later cited. PDFs shed rooms. This URL keeps the room: a society presidency, a journal, a nickname.

Geology is a metaphor printed in *Behavior Therapy* that year. Metaphors recruit. We filed the year. We will not staff the metaphor.
