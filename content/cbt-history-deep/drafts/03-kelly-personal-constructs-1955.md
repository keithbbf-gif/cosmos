In 1955 W. W. Norton issued George A. Kelly's *The Psychology of Personal Constructs* in two volumes. The same year, in New York, Albert Ellis began practicing the therapy he would name Rational Emotive. Aaron Beck was still an analyst in Philadelphia. Three cognitive doors opened, or almost opened, within months of each other. American textbooks later kept two and wallpapered over the third.

Kelly taught at Ohio State. He was not a New York showman and not a Penn depression researcher. He treated a person as a scientist of their own life: constructs, predictions, revisions when the world refused the forecast. The Role Construct Repertory Test is the instrument students remember. This page will not teach it. The historical object is the 1955 book and a claim that arrived too early for the later CBT workforce to absorb without translation.

## A person as a scientist, not a patient as a score

Kelly's fundamental postulate — that a person's processes are psychologically channelized by the ways they anticipate events — is a mouthful because he wanted a psychology, not a slogan. Constructs come in pairs. They have a range of convenience. They can be tight or permeable. A clinician's job, in his telling, was to understand the construct system well enough to invite an experiment.

Fixed-role therapy, the method attached to his name, asked a person to live for a short time as if they were someone written on a sheet. That sounds, to a later ear, like a technique. In 1955 it was also a philosophical dare: identity as a draft. The dare frightened some departments and bored others. It did not become a Guilford manual with competence tapes.

Beck's later "collaborative empiricism" is a cousin one can hear across the hallway. Both men asked a person to treat a belief as a hypothesis. Kelly got there from a construct theory that wanted to replace trait psychology. Beck got there from listening to depressions that did not match the hostility-under-sadness script. The resemblance does not make Kelly a grandfather. This style guide usually bans the kinship word anyway. Call him a neighbor who published first.

## Why the later house forgot the address

Cognitive therapy, once it could be filmed and granted, needed objects a supervisor could point at on a tape: a hot thought, a missed reframe, a score that moved. Kelly's two volumes are long, metaphorical, and light on the kind of trial the 1980s learned to want. Personal construct psychology kept a society, a journal, and a British afterlife (Bannister, Fransella) larger than its American textbook share. CBT workshops in the 1990s mentioned Kelly, if they mentioned him, as a curiosity on slide two.

Ellis could have used Kelly. He used Adler and Epictetus instead. Kelly's voice is dry; Ellis's is wet. A Friday-night demonstration is a poor place for a range of convenience. Beck could have used Kelly and mostly used his own notes. The 1976 *Cognitive Therapy and the Emotional Disorders* builds a clinic out of thoughts as data. It does not pause for a repertory grid.

The forgetting is not a conspiracy. It is what happens when a profession chooses a workforce technology. Kelly wrote a psychology. Beck's students wrote a training shop. The shop won the hospital. The psychology kept a smaller room.

## 1955 as a crowded year

Put the three objects on one table. Kelly's Norton volumes. Ellis beginning to interrupt musts. The American psychological mainstream still largely Freudian in the clinic and behavioral in the lab. Wolpe's *Psychotherapy by Reciprocal Inhibition* is three years away (1958). DSM-I (1952) is the official dialect and already unsatisfying. Eysenck's 1952 gauntlet is still ringing.

A historian who starts "CBT" in 1979 has started at the manual. A historian who starts in 1967 has started at Beck's depression book. A historian who starts in 1955 has to admit that "cognitive" was already a quarrel among Americans who did not yet share a guild. That is the use of this page on a practice site: to stop the brochure from sounding as if one man flipped a switch.

Kelly died in 1967, the year Beck's *Depression* appeared. The coincidence is editorial, not mystical. One book closed a career. One book opened a school. The school did not inherit the career. It inherited a mood that Kelly had already named: a person might be wrong in a patterned way, and the pattern might be testable.

## What this series will not do

No grid. No fixed-role script. No "try on a new self this week." Those objects, if they are used at all, belong in a trained room with consent. A history page that reprints them would be doing the thing this pack exists to refuse: turning a 1955 psychology into a handout.

WOW Therapies does not, by publishing this essay, claim a personal-construct service. The sibling figure essay on Kelly stays with the man. The sibling Beck and Ellis essays stay with the rooms that won. This page only restores the year: 1955 had more than one cognitive door, and the door that later clinics walked through was not the only one that opened.

If a graduate seminar still assigns Kelly, it is often as "context." Context is a polite word for a book the workforce did not need. Need is not the same as truth. The 1955 volumes remain a way to see what CBT might have sounded like if it had stayed a psychology of anticipation instead of becoming, at its worst, a score.

## A British afterlife, and an American shrug

Don Bannister and Fay Fransella made personal construct psychology a living British conversation in the 1960s and 1970s. The repertory grid found users in occupational psychology and in clinics that never called themselves cognitive-behavioral. That afterlife is a reminder that "what counted as CBT" is a later American-British administrative category, not a natural kind. IAPT, decades on, did not staff Kelly. It staffed a grandchild of the Penn manuals and a set of NICE-recommended protocols. The grid stayed in a different building.

American clinical psychology, choosing its ancestors after Division 12's lists, wanted names that already had trials. Kelly had students and a theory. He did not have the 1979 object. Ancestry is written backward from the object that can be granted. This pack writes forward from 1955 so the backward writing does not look like fate.

## Columbus, and a book that needed two volumes

Ohio State in the 1950s was not Penn and not East 65th Street. Kelly's students remember a man who assigned a psychology that would not fit a pamphlet. Two volumes was a dare to a field that already liked tests more than theories. The repertory grid found users; the theory found a smaller room. That split — instrument yes, book maybe — is how American clinical psychology often treats its philosophers.

A 1955 reviewer who wanted a trial could not have one. A 1995 list-maker who wanted a manual could not photocopy one that looked like Guilford 1979. Kelly's afterlife therefore ran through societies and through British classrooms rather than through IAPT. Filing him as "precursor to CBT" is a later courtesy. He did not ask for the courtesy. He asked for a psychology of anticipation. This pack files the year so the courtesy does not erase the dare.

If a later editor finds a clean PD scan of a 1955 title *page*, it may sit as a period object. A generated "scientist at a blackboard" may not. The two-volume spine is the image. Type can carry it.
