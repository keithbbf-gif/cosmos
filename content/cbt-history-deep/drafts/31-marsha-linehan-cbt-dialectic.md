**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Marsha M. Linehan was born in 1943 and is living (verified 2026). Public books, the 1991 paper, and the 23 June 2011 *New York Times* interview she chose. No health speculation beyond what she put on that record. No UW scrape. No skills list.

She trained as a behaviorist. She had been a chronically suicidal patient. She said so, on the record, to Benedict Carey. The 1991 *Archives of General Psychiatry* paper and the 1993 Guilford book — *Cognitive-Behavioral Treatment of Borderline Personality Disorder* — made DBT a thing a hospital could staff: skills groups, individual sessions, a consultation team for the therapists, a phone-coaching idea administrators would fund or gut.

The survey pack already has a Linehan figure. This deepen stays inside the CBT family argument: a third-wave object that is also a first-wave staffing pattern and a second-wave grandchild.

## 1991–1993, a trial and a book

The dialectic in the title is not a philosophy seminar. It is a clinical stance: accept the person as they are *and* insist that they change, because either half alone had been failing. Zen practice entered her thinking as a discipline of attention, not as décor. This essay will not list modules. Those manuals are copyrighted, trained, and easy to parody. A history site that reprints them would be a legal mistake and a clinical one.

Hospitals that say they "do DBT" without a team are speaking a dialect she would not have recognized as the thing she built. Managed care found the team expensive and the skills cheaper. Markets like cheaper cousins. The 1993 book is the object that still judges the cousins.

Hayes's 2004 nickname put DBT in a wave. The 1991 paper did not ask for a geology. It asked whether a particular treatment, in a particular sample, moved particular outcomes. Those are different asks. This pack prints both.

## What she gave the wider CBT house

A reminder that some patients tear up the thought record. A reminder that a therapist needs a team. A reminder that acceptance without change can be abandonment, and change without acceptance can be another demand. The second-wave shop needed those reminders even when it did not adopt her initials.

Later adaptations — adolescents, other diagnoses — are second chapters. The historical core remains 1991–1993 and the 2011 public disclosure. Living people who choose a newspaper as the venue may be quoted as having chosen it. They may not be embroidered.
