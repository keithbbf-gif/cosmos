**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

David H. Barlow is living (verified 2026). Public books only. No health speculation. No Boston University scrape. This page will not teach an anxiety protocol or a unified protocol hour.

*Anxiety and Its Disorders* (Guilford, 1988; second edition 2002) is the big book. It treated anxiety as a house with rooms a researcher could name. Later, with colleagues, he issued Unified Protocol volumes (2011 onward): a transdiagnostic bet that emotional disorders might share processes a single sequence could address. The bet is a 2010s object. The 1988 book is an 1980s object. This pack keeps the interval.

## 1988, a house, not a pamphlet

American anxiety work in the 1980s sat beside Clark's 1986 panic paper and beside exposure traditions that already knew Wolpe. Barlow's book is a synthesis a department could assign. Synthesis is not a miracle. It is a career of labs, students, and a willingness to write long. Long books lose to lists. Lists still needed the long book in the background, the way Division 12 needed manuals it could point at.

He worked for years at Boston University; the Center for Anxiety and Related Disorders is the institutional name students remember. Institutions are not portraits. They are addresses. Addresses train workforces. Workforces make "anxiety CBT" sound like a single product. It was a literature. The 1988 book is how a literature became a shelf.

## The unified bet

By the 2000s the initials had multiplied. A transdiagnostic protocol is one answer to multiplication: stop writing a new church for every code. Process-based talk (Hayes and Hofmann 2018) is a cousin answer. Barlow's UP is a package answer. Packages can be randomized. Processes can be named. This pack files both without printing either as homework.

Critics said transdiagnostic work might sand off what a specific fear needs. Defenders said comorbidity was already the actual waiting room. Both sentences can be true in different hours. A rural hour that has never heard "UP" may already be treating more than one code in one person, because the person did not read DSM-III before they sat down. Resemblance is not a license to claim the brand.
