Zone 7a is not a fig climate that failed. It is a fig climate that uses a different verb. You do not “preserve the framework.” You *see what lived*.

The annual extreme sits around 0 to 5 °F. Fully dormant fig wood, on a good year with a good variety, can take the high teens. It does not reliably take a week of single digits, and it does not take a sudden drop when January thawed it first. So the January prune you copied from a zone 9 video is not early. It is fiction. You would be cutting a story.

## What winter does for you

A lot of years, winter is the pruner. Tips die. Sometimes the whole top dies to the mulch line and the crown sends up a thicket in May. Chicago Hardy, Brown Turkey, Celeste, and a few Mt. Etna types are in 7a yards because they will make a main crop on that new wood if the season is long enough. The breba — those embryonic figs on last year’s tips — is a rumor you stop counting on.

If you cut in February because you are tidy, you will cut live wood that still looks dead, and you will leave dead wood that still looks live. Fig bark stays gray-brown either way. The only honest test in 7a is green under the bark *and* a bud that moves.

## The actual calendar

**November–February.** Wrap what you intend to keep above ground. A tomato cage, leaves or straw, burlap, maybe a Christmas-light trickle of heat if you are that person. Do not prune through the wrap. Do not open it on a 40 °F afternoon in January to “let it breathe” and then forget it for a 4 °F night.

**March.** Still wait, in most 7a counties. Last frost can be mid-April. The bush may sit there looking like kindling while the forsythia screams. That is normal.

**April, often late April.** Green shows. Not on every cane. You scratch, you look, you follow the live tissue down. Cut the dead back to a live bud or a live crotch. If the cane is dead to the ground, cut it to the crown without leaving a stub that rots into the living stool.

Then stop. That *is* the prune. You do not also apply a one-third heading to “encourage branching” on wood that just survived a 2 °F night. The tree is already going to throw laterals like it has something to prove.

**May–June.** Thin the thicket. You want a number of shoots you can reach and that can thicken — think three to eight, same as the Georgia bush-form advice, except your “leaders” may be this year’s canes. Stake the ones that will carry fruit. They will be soft and heavy in August.

**July–September.** Main crop if the summer is long. In the colder 7a pockets, a hard spring cut plus a cool August is how you eat green figs in the first frost. If your season is short, you needed those surviving old laterals more than you needed a sculpted shape. That is next year’s argument, and only if next winter is kind.

## The mistake 7a copies from 8a

People move south in the literature without moving south in the driveway. They read “late winter prune in zone 8” and they do it on March 1 in 7a because they are tired of looking at sticks. Then April 20 a hard freeze burns the cuts and the new buds behind them.

The other copy-paste is the opposite: they treat 7a like zone 5 and cut everything to the ground every April “to be safe,” including live two-year wood that would have carried an earlier main crop. Dieback is not a command to start over. It is a map. Read it.

## If you want a breba in 7a

Put the fig in a pot and take it into an unheated garage when the real cold comes. Or put it on a south wall and wrap the whole fan. In-ground, unwrapped, hoping — that is a hobby, not a plan. Pruning will not create a breba that winter already cooked.

## Center note, for people who live in 8a and think in 7a

Some 8a winters *are* 7a winters. When that happens, steal this draft. Wait for green. The 8a calendar in draft 01 is the average year, not a promise.
