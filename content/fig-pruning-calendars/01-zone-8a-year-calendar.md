Zone 8a is the dishonest middle. The card says the average annual extreme is 10 to 15 °F. That is cold enough to kill a fig that still has green in the tips, and it is cold enough to kill a fully brown bush if the cold sits two nights and the wind has a lane at the crown. It is also warm enough, most winters, that last year’s wood lives, and then people start talking about breba as if they lived in Pasadena.

So this calendar is not a California calendar. It is not a “wait until May and cut the dead sticks” calendar either. It is a year you can actually walk.

## January

Leave the pruners in the house. Walk the bush. Look at the tips. If last August’s wood still has those little overwintering figs — the size of a pea, dull green or bronze, sitting at the nodes near the ends — write that down. That is breba wood. A January hard heading throws it in the bucket.

Check the wrap if you used one. Check the mulch. Do not fertilize. Do not water on a freeze night just because a blog said moisture protects roots. Wet crowns in 8a ice.

If an ice storm splits a leader, take the hanging piece so it does not peel more bark. That is repair, not pruning season.

## February

This is the start of the argument. In a quiet year, the last ten days of February are legal for dormant work in much of 8a. In a year that still has a 12 °F night on the forecast, you wait.

What “dormant work” means here, if you do it:

- Dead, snapped, and rubbing wood. Always.
- Extra suckers you do not need as replacement leaders. Usually.
- A heading of one-third to one-half of last year’s growth on the leaders you intend to keep — **only if** you have already decided the breba is not this year’s job.

UGA’s home-garden fig bulletins describe that heading: after frost danger, before growth starts, if you want more branching. They are writing Georgia, which is a lot of 7b and 8a. They are not writing a Mission orchard in the Central Valley.

If you want the early figs, February is a thinning month, not a shortening month. Take the crossing stuff. Leave the tips.

## March

Bud swell is the clock, not the calendar app. When the buds fatten and show green at the edges, structural work is over. You can still take a dead stub once you are sure it is dead. You cannot still take four feet off a leader and pretend you did not just delay the main crop.

Zone 8a last-frost dates wander. Some counties call it March 20. Some call it April 10. Your fig does not care what the county average is. It cares about the night that actually arrives.

If you wrapped the bush, unwrap on a stretch of nights that stay above the mid-20s, then be ready to throw a blanket back on if a late shot of cold is coming. Do not unwrap and immediately make a heading cut in the same afternoon. Let the wood speak for a week.

## April

Breba, if you have it, is swelling. Leaves are out. This is a watching month. A late freeze here does more damage than a tidy January prune, because the tissue is soft.

If a freeze burns the breba, do not “make up for it” by heading the new shoots in May. You already lost the early crop. Let the new wood run. That is now your main crop.

Pinch only if a first-year shoot is going straight up with no laterals and you already know you want a fork. One pinch. Not a haircut.

## May

Suckers you missed in February are obvious now. Take the ones that crowd the three to eight leaders you kept. Leave a candidate if a leader split or looks weak.

Air. That is the May job in the humid South. Crossed laterals in the middle of the bush are rust weather. Thin a few. Do not strip the interior bald. Fig bark sunburns.

## June

If your variety carries a breba, this is often the start of it in 8a — later than the Gulf, earlier than the mountains. Eat them. Do not prune while you are standing there with a bowl unless a shoot is broken.

Pinch the tips of long, whippy current-season shoots if you want them to stop racing and push laterals. This is the honest summer prune: a thumbnail, not a saw.

## July

Breba is ending or gone. Main-crop figs are pea-to-marble on this year’s wood. You can still take a water sprout that is shading fruit. You cannot still reshape the bush. A July heading on a late variety is how you meet October with hard green fruit.

## August

Main crop for Celeste and the early LSU set is on. Brown Turkey is coming. Alma is still thinking about it.

Stop pinching. Stop heading. If you live where UF/IFAS is the local voice, you will hear that a prune after harvest, in late summer, can be the window so next year’s buds can set. That is a Florida sentence. In 8a, late August is still a heat engine, and a hard cut now often pushes soft wood into the first 25 °F night. If you take anything after harvest here, take a broken branch and go inside.

## September

Harvest. Prop a limb if the fruit is pulling it over. Do not fertilize. Do not water into lushness unless the bush is in actual drought and fruit is shriveling. Soft September wood is January’s kill.

## October

Last fruit on the late ones. First frost in the colder 8a pockets. When the leaves yellow and drop, you may *look*. You may tag a leader you already know you will remove in late winter. You do not cut it now. A tidy October fig is a vain fig.

## November

Mulch the root zone after the soil cools. Wrap if your winters have a habit of 12 °F. Build the wrap so you can still get at a split later. The shears stay hung.

## December

Quiet. If you need a job, sharpen the bypass pruners and find the gloves you will actually wear. Fig latex is not a personality test. It is ficin. You will meet it in February.

## The one-line version

In 8a, the year has one real structural window: late February into the first swell of March, after the worst cold you are likely to get, before the buds commit. Everything else is repair, pinching, or leaving it alone. If a calendar sells you four pruning seasons, it is selling you a different plant.
