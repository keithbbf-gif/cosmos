USDA zones are a map of average annual extreme minimums. They are not a last-frost date, not a first-frost date, and not a count of nights below 20 °F. Two 8a towns can have a month of difference in when you may safely unwrap a fig, and a week of difference in when a main crop has to be finished.

If you prune by zone alone, you will be early in the cold town and late in the warm one, and you will think the drafts contradicted themselves.

## The dates you actually need

Write them for *your* address, not the airport if you can help it:

- Average last spring frost
- Late spring frost you have actually seen in the last five years
- Average first fall frost
- Early fall frost you have actually seen
- Coldest night last winter, from a thermometer you own

The prune window in 8a sits after the coldest nights you still believe in and before bud swell. Those are not the same as “zone 8a means February.”

The main-crop deadline is the fall frost. If your first frost is October 12 most years, a hard February heading that delays ripeness three weeks is a different decision than it is for a yard that runs to November 10.

## 7a

Last frost can be mid-April. First frost can be mid-October. That is a short conversation. Wait for green. Do not buy delay.

## 9a

Last frost may be February. First frost may be December or a rumor. You can spend delay. You still should not spend the breba unless you mean to.

## Microclimates inside one zone

A south wall, a hilltop, a river bottom, a parking-lot of a city. Drafts 36 and 37 go into this. Here I only need one rule: if your frost dates do not match the 8a paragraph in draft 01, obey your dates.

## How this changes a Saturday

You do not start cutting because a group chat said “we pruned today.” You start cutting because your worst cold is likely behind you, your buds are not open, and you know whether you have breba wood. That sentence has no zone number in it. The zone number got you to this folder. The frost dates get you to the wood.
