Every fig draft pretends the only risk is a bad crop. The other risk is your skin.

Fig latex carries ficin. It is a protein-dissolving enzyme. On skin, especially skin that then goes into sun, it can raise a burn that looks like you dragged a jellyfish up your forearm. UF mentions the irritant and the enzyme. Gardeners mention it after the fact, in July, with a photograph.

## Pruning makes more of it

A dormant cut still weeps. A June pinch weeps more. A saw cut on a warm March afternoon will paint your wrist if the glove slipped.

## What to do that is not precious

- Gloves you do not care about. The sap ruins them.
- Long sleeves. Not because you are formal. Because cuffs catch drips.
- Do not wipe your face. You will. Everyone does. Don’t.
- Wash with soap if you get it on you. Then stay out of the bright noon if you can.
- Kids and the white drops: same rules. The beads of sap on a fresh cut are interesting. They are not interesting.

## What not to do

Do not use the latex as a folk wart remover while you prune. I am not going to decorate that sentence.

Do not assume winter sap is harmless because it is thinner or slower. It is enough.

## Why this belongs in a calendar set

Because the people who prune badly often prune fast, in a T-shirt, on the first warm Saturday. That Saturday is also when you make the heading mistakes in drafts 17–23. Slow is a safety practice and a horticultural practice. The gloves make you slow.

February in 8a can be 70 °F by noon. That is the dangerous day: sap up, sleeves off, sun hard. If you can prune in the morning of a cloudy day, do. If you cannot, keep the cuffs anyway. The crop you save by a correct cut is not worth a streak on your wrist that lasts through June.

If you only take one habit from this file, take this: the pruners and the gloves live on the same nail. If the gloves are in the other shed, you are not pruning today.
