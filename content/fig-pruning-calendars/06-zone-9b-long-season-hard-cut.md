9b is the long room. Twenty-five to thirty. Figs do not die here unless you drown them, starve the roots in beach sand, or freeze them with a once-in-twenty-year night after a warm spell. You can cut hard in January and still pick a main crop that runs into October. That fact has created a style of pruning that looks like citrus: short, stout, and a little brutal.

It works. It also throws away a crop you could have eaten in June, and it can push late varieties into a souring season if you live where August is a thunderstorm, not a drought.

## What “hard” should mean

Hard is not “cut to the ground.” That is a 7a emergency or a renovation. Hard in 9b is:

- Fewer leaders than the romantic orchard picture — three to five is plenty in a small yard.
- Last year’s growth taken back by a third to a half, sometimes more on a giant.
- A real hole in the center so the sun does not only hit the roof of the bush.

You still cut to a bud or a crotch. You still refuse stubs. Fig wood in the humid 9b South rots like it is paid to.

## The calendar

**December–January.** This is your month. The bush is asleep enough. Do the structure before it decides February is spring.

**February.** Finish anything you left. By the end of the month you should be in cleanup only.

**March–May.** Leaves, breba if you left any, and the first pinches on runaway shoots.

**June–July.** Eat the early figs or stare at the empty laterals you cut off. Either way, do not start a second structural prune because you are bored.

**August–October.** Main crop. UF/IFAS, writing from Florida, will tell you that if you prune at all, late summer after harvest can be the moment that lets next year’s buds initiate, and that heavy winter pruning can erase a crop. Listen to that if you are actually in their peninsula and your “winter” is a rumor. In a 9b that still sees a real leaf-drop, January structure plus a light post-harvest tidy is a compromise: do the bones in winter, and if you take anything in August, take a crossing arm, not a third of the canopy.

## Why 8a should not copy the hard cut

Because 8a does not have this room. A hard January heading in 8a can mean main-crop figs that are still firm when the first 28 °F night arrives. 9b can spend that delay. 8a eats it.

If you are an 8a grower visiting a 9b yard, the lesson is not “cut more.” The lesson is “their season is paying for that cut.”

## Stupid, specifically

- Painting every wound, because someone did it to an oak.
- Fertilizing the week you cut hard, so you get a jungle and no fruit.
- Training a single trunk “like a real tree” next to a driveway, then watching a truck take the only leader.
- Buying Kadota or Magnolia, opening the canopy not at all, and blaming the rain for the sour fruit.

9b gives you time. Spend it on air and on a decision about the breba, not on turning the fig into a stump because you saw a video from a commercial lot.
