There is a kind of person who cannot stand to let a natural process be the last word. Winter kills three feet of every wand. They come out in March and they kill two more feet so it looks even.

That is a second prune the plant did not need.

## Catching up, not improving

When dieback is the event:

1. Wait until you can see it (drafts 02, 19).
2. Cut to live. Collar or bud. No stub.
3. Thin the crowd of replacement shoots later, not the same day if you are tired and angry.
4. Stop.

You do not owe the bush a vase. You do not owe the neighborhood a smooth outline. You owe the crown a clean edge and a counted set of canes.

## 8a, the year this happens

Not every 8a year. The year it does, steal the 7a draft and ignore anyone who says 8a figs do not die back. The card was an average. Your night was not.

A typical 8a dieback year looks like this: the lower two-thirds of last season’s wood is tan and live. The outer foot is pith. The beads are gone. If you “even” that to the lowest dead line on the worst arm, you have just headed the whole plant to its most injured member. Cut each wand to *its* live line. The bush will be ragged. Ragged is accurate.

## What you may still decide

If the live line is high and you have a forest of laterals below it, you can thin. Thinning is not heading. If the live line is the mulch, you are in cane culture (draft 11). Both are catch-up. Neither is a redesign.

If only one leader died and the others merely tipped, replace the dead leader with a sucker and leave the tipped ones. That is a surgical year, not a stool year. People jump to stool because it is a simpler story.

## Fertilizer, again

A dieback year is not a feed-heavy year. The plant is about to throw more shoots than you want. Nitrogen is how you lose the argument.

## Emotionally

It looks like failure. It is a climate tax. Pay it with a clean cut and a June pinch. Collect the main crop if the summer pays you back. Write the coldest night on the shed wall so February-you remembers.
