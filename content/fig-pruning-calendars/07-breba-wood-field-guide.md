People say “last year’s wood” as if it were a stain color. It is a location.

A breba fig is a fig that began as a tiny syconium on a shoot that grew last summer. It sat there through leaf drop. It spent the winter on a bare wand. In spring it swells with the first sap and tries to become breakfast in early summer. It does not grow from the brand-new green shoot you are watching in May. That new shoot is writing a different crop.

If you cannot point at breba wood with a glove on, you will prune it by accident and then write a review that the variety “doesn’t do breba here.”

## How to find it

Stand in front of a dormant Brown Turkey in late February, zone 8a, no leaves.

The fat, gray, barky arms are older. They are the leaders. They are not, by themselves, the breba crop. The breba is on the *younger* length: last season’s extension. That wood is smoother, often lighter, and it still has the internodes of a plant that was in a hurry. Near the ends, at some of the nodes, you may see the overwintering figs. They look like dull beads. They are not leaf buds. Leaf buds are tighter and sit differently. Once you have seen both on the same wand, you stop mixing them up.

No beads? You may still have breba-capable wood. Some varieties are shy about showing the embryos until the days lengthen. The wood is still last year’s extension. If you take that extension off at the old collar, you took the breba room.

## What winter does to those beads

In 9a they sit there looking bored and then swell.

In 8a they sit there looking bored until a 12 °F night, and then some of them turn to pith and drop when you touch them in March. The wood behind them may still be alive. A dead bead is not always a dead wand.

In 7b and 7a the beads are often the first thing to go, even when a chunk of last year’s wood lives. That is why 7a is a main-crop culture. The breba is a specialist project: wrap, wall, or pot.

## The prune that targets breba without saying so

Any heading that removes the outer third of last year’s growth is a breba prune. You can call it “size control.” The plant calls it eviction.

Thinning cuts are kinder to breba. A thinning cut takes a whole wand back to the leader and leaves the neighboring wand’s tips alone. You lose the fruit on the wand you removed. You keep the fruit on the ones you did not. Heading every wand “evenly” is how you lose the crop evenly.

## San Pedro types, said once, clearly

San Pedro figs (Desert King is the one people chase) make a breba without a wasp and need pollination for the main crop. In most of the Southeast there is no *Blastophaga* working your yard. If you prune off the breba wood of a San Pedro type, you have pruned off the crop. There is no main-crop consolation prize coming. This is not a vibe. It is the flower type.

Common figs — Celeste, Eastern Brown Turkey, Chicago Hardy, LSU Purple — do not need the wasp for either crop. You can choose. That choice is the next draft.

## A February test that does not waste wood

Pick one representative wand. Do not cut the bush first. Ask: if this wand is gone, do I still have other last-year extensions with nodes near the sun? If the answer is no, you are about to turn a two-crop plant into a one-crop plant. Write that down before the blade closes.
