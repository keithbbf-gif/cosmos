November is when I want people in this climate to be busy around the fig and idle with the blade.

## Do

- Pick the last fruit that will actually sweeten. Hard green Alma can become preserves if you know what you are doing; it cannot become a June fig if you leave it and hope.
- Stop feeding.
- Ease water once the rains return. Do not keep a summer schedule on a plant you want to harden.
- After a real cool-down, mulch the root zone. Leave the crown so it is not a wet hat.
- Build the wrap on the plants that need it, on a dry day (drafts 30, 42).
- Coil a bit of twine and a stake in the shed for May.
- Put the gloves on the same nail as the pruners, and then do not take the pruners off the nail.

## Do not

- Shape.
- “Just take the tall ones.”
- Fertilize because a lawn ad said fall feed.
- Rake the mulch off to make it look clean.
- Leave a torn storm branch flapping. That one cut is allowed. It is a tear-off, not a haircut.

## Why November feels like a prune month

The leaves drop. The skeleton appears. Your hands itch. That itch is how October/November mistakes are born. Walk around the plant with your hands in your pockets. Name the leaders out loud. Decide the February vote. Write it on a card in the shed: *Olympian — tips. Turkey — split. Hardy — wait for green.*

The card is the November prune.

## 9a/9b

Your November may still have leaves and fruit. Even more reason not to cut: you are still in harvest. When the leaves finally go, you are close to the January window. Wait for it. Do not invent a November window because the Piedmont drafts said the plant is asleep. Your plant may not be.

## 8a center

If you do nothing else from this whole folder, do November with empty hands. February is coming, and it is enough.
