**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Frantz Fanon was a psychiatrist at Blida-Joinville in colonial Algeria before he was the author undergraduates meet in a week on decolonization. *Peau noire, masques blancs* (1952) is a psychology book. It is about the white gaze, about the Antillean who has to be a person in a language that was built to unperson him, about the bodily experience of being hailed as a thing. *Les Damnés de la terre* (1961) is more famous and more explosive. The clinical fact sits in both: a hospital that does not understand the war outside its walls will call the war a symptom.

On 16 November 1989, Salvadoran soldiers murdered six Jesuit priests at the Universidad Centroamericana, their housekeeper, and her daughter. One of the priests was Ignacio Martín-Baró, a social psychologist who had been writing that psychology in Latin America was answering questions the United States had asked, while the questions of war, hunger, and *pueblo* went unasked. His English readers mostly met him after the murder, in *Writings for a Liberation Psychology* (1994).

This essay is not a tour of "diverse contributors." It is about traditions that treated the clinic as a political location because the patients already knew it was.

## The Clarks, Harlem, and a footnote in the Supreme Court

Mamie Phipps Clark, born in Hot Springs, Arkansas, earned a Columbia doctorate in 1943 — the first Black woman to do so in psychology. With Kenneth Clark she published the doll studies (1939, 1950) that asked children which doll was nice, which was them. The work entered *Brown v. Board of Education* (1954) as social-science citation. That is the famous sentence.

The less famous sentence is that Mamie Clark ran the Northside Center for Child Development in Harlem from 1946, because testing homeless Black girls at Riverdale had shown her a city that had no services and a profession that had no curiosity. Guthrie's *Even the Rat Was White* is the book that made this kind of omission a curriculum.

If a WOW Therapies reader is in Arkansas, the Hot Springs origin is not trivia. The clinical heritage of the state is not only asylums and visiting reformers. It includes a woman who left, built a center, and changed a constitutional case.

## Competencies, and the risk of a checklist

In 1992 Derald Wing Sue, Patricia Arredondo, and Roderick McDavis published "Multicultural Counseling Competencies and Standards" in the *Journal of Counseling & Development*. The paper gave programs a list: awareness, knowledge, skills. APA and counseling accreditors built careers on versions of that list.

Lists have a life. They can make a white clinician slightly less dangerous. They can also become a certificate that replaces the work: the hour in which you find out you do not understand this family's church, this person's migration, this language's words for shame. "Microaggression," a later keyword associated with Sue, became a culture-war object. The historical point is smaller. By the 1990s American counseling had admitted, on paper, that the generic patient had been white.

Lillian Comas-Díaz, Melba Vasquez, Beverly Greene, Nancy Boyd-Franklin, and others wrote the books that made that admission into techniques of care rather than into guilt. This essay cannot summarize them. The bibliography names doors.

Comas-Díaz's later *Multicultural Care* (2012) is a usable door for a reader who wants the American counseling version of this argument without a war next to the hospital. Boyd-Franklin's work on Black families refused both the deficit story and the polite silence. Greene wrote as a Black lesbian psychologist when that sentence still had to be a career decision. These are not garnish names. They are why a 1992 competencies paper did not remain a paper.

## Africa, Japan, India — not as spice

T. Adeoye Lambo, at Aro in Abeokuta in the 1950s and 1960s, built a village system in which patients lived with relatives and the hospital was not the only building that counted. He published in *The Lancet* (including "The Village of Aro," 1964). He later worked at WHO. The model is not a folk souvenir. It is a mid-century experiment in what a psychiatric institution is *for*. It is also, like every famous African clinical experiment written up in a London journal, at risk of being quoted as a parable and never staffed as a policy.

Takeo Doi's *Amae no kōzō* (1971; English *The Anatomy of Dependence*, 1973) is not a liberation text. It is a Japanese psychiatrist's argument that a certain permitted dependence is a psychological fact, not a Western developmental failure. This pack names it here so that "multicultural" does not mean only American competencies plus Caribbean and Latin American wars. Sometimes the argument is with the developmental chart itself.

Shōma Morita in Tokyo (1919) and Girindrasekhar Bose in Calcutta (1922) have their own figure essays. They belong in this era piece as proof that "the West invented psychotherapy, then exported it" is a bad map. Sometimes Europe was the late letter.

## Decolonial, as a word that can get lazy

A 2020s literature uses "decolonize therapy" as a title. Some of it is serious (who owns the diagnosis, whose suffering is billed, which languages get to be technical). Some of it is a brand. Fanon and Martín-Baró are not brands. They wrote from hospitals next to wars. If a clinic in a peaceful county uses their names, the honest use is: notice when your theory was written for a different state's questions.

Do not use this history to skip a suicide assessment. Do not use it to tell a client their only problem is America. Liberation psychology's own warning, in Martín-Baró, is that a psychology can serve the occupier *or* the *pueblo*. It does not become virtuous by quotation.
