**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Judith Lewis Herman was born on 31 March 1942, trained as a psychiatrist, and taught at Harvard while seeing patients whose stories official psychiatry had a habit of treating as fantasy, exaggeration, or a different diagnosis. She is living as of last verification (2026-09-14). This essay uses the books.

## 1981, and the unfashionable crime

*Father–Daughter Incest* (Harvard, 1981) is a clinical and sociological study of a crime that mid-century theory had a talent for dissolving into "seduction" and inner wish. Herman wrote as a physician who believed her patients and then went looking for a pattern. The book is not a memoir. It is a hinge between the feminist clinic and the trauma field that still thought of itself as a veterans' science.

## 1992, the public book

*Trauma and Recovery* put combat, political terror, and domestic captivity in one argument. Prolonged, repeated trauma, she said, produces a syndrome that single-incident PTSD does not exhaust. She called it complex PTSD. She described a social course of recovery — safety, remembrance, reconnection — that later trainings turned into posters. The posters are not the book. This series will not reprint the stages as a plan.

The *New York Times* treated the book as a major psychiatric event. DSM did not add her diagnosis. ICD-11, much later, did add complex PTSD. That lag is part of the history: who is allowed to name an injury, and how long the name takes to become billable.

## 2023

*Truth and Repair* returned to justice as a clinical topic: what survivors say they need from a community, not only from an hour. A counseling site can note the book without becoming a court.

## Living, and the rules

No health speculation. No lecture-circuit photography. No "Herman says you should…" protocol. The inheritance is a frame: trauma is political as well as private, and a field that remembers soldiers and forgets children in houses is not done remembering.

She belongs next to Janet and Myers in the trauma-era essay, and next to the feminist-relational essay, because she refused to let those be two shelves.
