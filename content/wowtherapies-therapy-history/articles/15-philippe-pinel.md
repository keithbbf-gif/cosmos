**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Philippe Pinel was born in 1745 at Jonquières, in the south of France, the son of a surgeon. He studied at Toulouse and Montpellier, reached Paris as a medical journalist and a cautious clinician, and was not, in 1789, a revolutionary saint. The Revolution made him a public physician. In 1793 he received a post at Bicêtre, the hospice on the southern edge of Paris that held, among others, men judged insane. In 1795 he moved to the Salpêtrière, the vast women's hospital. He died in 1826, famous, painted, and already on his way to becoming a legend that would annoy the archivists.

## The treatise, not the gesture

Pinel's *Traité médico-philosophique sur l'aliénation mentale* (1801; English 1806) is the book that earned the fame. He argued that *aliénation* could be studied as a medical object, classified, and treated by a discipline of the passions — *traitement moral* — rather than by irons as a first resort. He wrote case histories. He wrote as an Enlightenment physician who believed in order, observation, and the possibility of recovery.

He did not, as a lone figure in a storm, strike the chains from a kneeling woman. Dora Weiner's work (1979, 1994) is the correction that should travel with every reproduction of Tony Robert-Fleury's painting. Jean-Baptiste Pussin, governor of the insane at Bicêtre, and Marguerite Pussin, who worked those wards with him, practiced a psychological management Pinel learned from. Pussin replaced chains with straitjackets in 1797. Pinel later arranged the couple's transfer to the Salpêtrière. The physician's gift was to theorize, to publish, and to make a hospital policy out of what a former patient-turned-governor already knew how to do.

If that demotion feels unkind to Pinel, it is kinder to the truth, and kinder to the Pussins.

## Classification and the medical gaze

Pinel divided mental alienation into types (mania, melancholy, dementia, idiotism — the period words). The scheme looks crude now. It was an attempt to stop treating "the mad" as a single waste category. He watched the course of an illness. He asked about precipitation by grief, by revolution, by a blow to pride. He was not a psychotherapist in the later sense. There is no fifty-minute hour in the *Traité*. There is a superintendent's faith that a human being in a well-run ward might return to himself.

He also believed in authority. Moral treatment is not a conversation among equals. It is a structured kindness that can become, in other hands, a structured humiliation. Pinel is not innocent of the later asylum's pride.

## Paris, 1793: a physician who was not yet a founder

Pinel arrived at Bicêtre as a man who had failed, by Paris standards, at a fashionable practice and succeeded as a writer of medical journalism. He had translated and reviewed. He knew Cullen and the nosologists. He did not yet know the wards. The Revolution's committees moved physicians like furniture. A post at a hospice that held "the insane" was not, in 1793, a prize the Academy would envy.

What he found was a city of men in rooms that were also cells. Bicêtre was poorhouse, prison, and hospital in one administrative lump. The governor of the insane wards, Pussin, already kept notes and already believed that some of the men could be reached by a look, a routine, a withheld privilege, a walk. Pinel's notebooks from those years — the ones Weiner and others have used — show a student as much as a master.

The *Traité* of 1801 is written after Bicêtre and after the first Salpêtrière years. It is a book of classification and of hope that now reads as both humane and managerial. He wanted statistics. He wanted a language that a later superintendent could argue with. That is a different ambition from a painting of a single afternoon.

The English translation of 1806 (*A Treatise on Insanity*, D. D. Davis) carried him into British and American asylums that wanted a French republican founder to quote. Superintendents cited Pinel the way later clinicians cite Rogers: as a permission to be kinder than the last building. They did not always cite Pussin. The translation history is part of the myth history.

## Straitjackets are not freedom

When chains came off, jackets often went on. Pinel and Pussin both knew this. The moral of the reform is not "restraint ended." It is "restraint was made into a medical decision instead of a default." A later century would invent seclusion rooms, chemical restraint, and the quiet of a back ward that needed no iron because the door locked. Pinel's name gets borrowed for all of those, unfairly. It also gets borrowed for a kindness he did not invent alone.

Readers who want a hero of abolition should sit with that sentence. Readers who want to discard Pinel because the painting is a lie should sit with the *Traité* instead of the canvas.

## Why his name lasted

Nineteenth-century French psychiatry needed a republican founder to match Britain's Tukes and Italy's Chiarugi. The painting, the statue, the textbook paragraph — they did institutional work. They also erased the staff. A contemporary counselor who uses "Pinel" as shorthand for "we became humane" is repeating the erasure.

The usable inheritance is smaller and better: write down what you see; do not assume a chain is treatment; name the people who actually hold the keys; publish so that a hospital can be argued with.
