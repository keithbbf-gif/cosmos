**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Insoo Kim was born on 25 July 1934 in Korea, studied pharmacy at Ewha Womans University in Seoul — a family path toward a medicine business — and then remade her life in the United States as a social worker and family therapist. She died on 10 January 2007 in Milwaukee. With Steve de Shazer, her second husband, she co-founded the Brief Family Therapy Center in 1978.

## Milwaukee, not a footnote to Palo Alto

Berg had spent time around the Mental Research Institute's brief-therapy world. BFTC was not a branch office. It was a Midwestern clinic that asked a different first question. Not: what is the hidden structure? Not: what is the childhood scene? What is already working, even a little, and how could there be more of that?

De Shazer's *Keys to Solution in Brief Therapy* (1985) is the book people assign. Berg's own clinical writing and teaching — including *Working with the Problem Drinker* (1992, with Scott Miller) — are the reason the school had a voice that was not only his. She worked in child protection, substance settings, shelters, schools. The "miracle question" became the parody. The discipline was smaller: listen for exceptions, do not fall in love with the problem's story, end the hour with a next step the person can actually take.

She arrived in American social work without the Ivy pedigree the family-therapy histories prefer. Pharmacy, then Milwaukee Family Services, then consultation to systems that do not look like a private analytic practice: child protection, addictions, shelters. Solution-focused work makes more sense from that caseload than from a seminar on epistemology. If the hour is short and the client may not return, you had better notice what already works.

The "miracle question" and the scaling questions became training-room objects that this page will not reprint as a script. What belongs in history is the decision to treat a brief hour as a moral form: do not spend it proving the problem is deep if the person has to go back to a shelter tonight. Depth is not always a kindness. Sometimes it is a luxury of a long analysis. Berg's caseload did not have that luxury.

De Shazer wrote more of the theory books. Berg did more of the traveling teaching in later years, especially internationally. A history that lists only his titles is the old habit. Korean-language and other translations carried her voice into rooms this pack cannot survey. The fact of the carrying is enough: SFBT is not a Milwaukee curiosity.

## What the parody gets wrong

Solution-focused work, done badly, is cheerleading. It can miss depression that needs a hospital, violence that needs a plan, a psychosis that needs medicine. Berg's caseloads suggest she knew the difference between a brief therapy and a wish. A history series that only quotes the miracle question is doing the parody's work.

Done well, the school is a cousin of Adler's encouragement and of the MRI habit of interrupting failed solutions. It is also a Korean-American woman's career in a field that still tells the family-therapy story as a line of men from Bateson to Minuchin.

She had a daughter, Sarah, from her first marriage. The biographical pages that mention only de Shazer miss a life that was already a migration before Milwaukee. Pharmacy at Ewha is not a cute origin. It is a reminder that clinical talent in this field has often come through a side door the profession did not design.

Solution-focused questions spread into coaching and then into corporate trainings that have no confidentiality and no duty. That spread is not her fault and not her method. A counseling history names the clinic, 1978, Milwaukee, and stops before the keynote.

## Inheritance

School counselors and community agencies still use exception questions without naming her. Coaching borrowed the vocabulary and sometimes the ethics. This pack names her so the last essay in the figure line is not another Viennese ghost. It is a woman who changed the first question in an American city that is not New York or Palo Alto.

The Solution-Focused Brief Therapy Association and later trainings carried questions into schools and child-protection offices that had never heard of Milwaukee. Coaching and corporate rooms then carried the vocabulary out of confidentiality. That second carrying is not her method. The historical object remains BFTC, 1978, a Korean-American social worker and a Midwestern clinic that asked what was already working.
