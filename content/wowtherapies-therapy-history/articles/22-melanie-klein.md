**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Melanie Klein was born Melanie Reizes in 1882 in Vienna, married young, had three children, and came to psychoanalysis through Ferenczi in Budapest and then Abraham in Berlin. She began analyzing children, including, in a decision later readers still flinch at, her own. She moved to London in 1926 at the invitation of Ernest Jones. She died there in 1960, the origin of a school that still trains, still infuriates, and still describes some clinical hours better than anyone else.

## Play as association

Klein treated the child's play as the adult's free association: the toys were sentences. *The Psycho-Analysis of Children* (1932) made that technical claim in public. She did not wait for a verbal ego to arrive. She spoke to infants in the mind of a two-year-old about envy, greed, and the breast as a first object that is loved and attacked.

If that sounds lurid, it sounded lurid in 1932. It also gave clinicians a way to think about a rage that has no adult plot yet. Object-relations theory — the mind as a theater of inner figures — runs through her, through her students (Hanna Segal, Herbert Rosenfeld, Wilfred Bion in his Kleinian years), and then out into therapies that never say her name.

She analyzed children with toys as the medium because small children do not free-associate in sentences. That technical decision is her most durable gift and her most durable risk. A toy can be a sentence. A toy can also be a toy. The Kleinian ear that hears only inner war will miss a hungry house. The ear that refuses inner war will miss a child who is terrified of their own hate. Training, in her tradition, is an attempt to hold both — and the Controversial Discussions show how hard that holding was even among professionals who shared a language.

Her own children, including Melitta Schmideberg, who later opposed her in London, are part of the human cost of a method that did not yet have today's ethics about analyzing family. A history that skips that cost is a brochure for an institute.

## Positions, not stages

The paranoid-schizoid and depressive *positions* are not ages on a chart. They are organizations of anxiety: splitting the world into ideal and persecutory pieces, or bearing the grief that the loved person and the hated person are one. A later therapist who says a patient is "splitting" owes that verb, usually without a royalty, to this line.

Bion would take the apparatus toward groups and toward thinking itself. That is another essay. Klein's own essays on envy (1957) remain among the hardest pages in the literature: envy as an attack on the good because it is good. The idea has been abused to pathologize reasonable anger. It has also named something that appears in rooms where praise cannot be taken.

## The war in the Society

The Freud–Klein controversies were about children, about technique, about who owned Sigmund Freud's ghost, and about a refugee community cramped into London. Anna Freud's developmental caution faced Klein's early, dark infant. The minutes (King and Steiner, 1991) should be read by anyone who thinks "professional disagreement" is a calm phrase.

Klein did not win a crown. She won a stream. The Independent or Middle Group (Winnicott among them) grew in the space neither woman would cede.

## Caution for a general reader

Klein is easy to parody and easy to use as a hammer on mothers. She wrote about the infant's inner world, not about a verdict on your parenting. A website that turned her into child-rearing advice would be a betrayal of both the children and the theory.

She belongs in a counseling heritage series because adult psychotherapy — especially work with personality disorder, with envy in the transference, with the patient who cannot keep a good thing good — still speaks a dialect she coined. It does not belong as a protocol on a family blog.
