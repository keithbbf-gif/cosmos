**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

On Tuesdays in the 1880s, Jean-Martin Charcot taught at the Salpêtrière with patients in the room. Students, journalists, and visiting doctors watched women — the most photographed was Blanche Wittmann — produce the postures Charcot had classified as stages of *grande hystérie*. André Brouillet painted the scene in 1887: the neurologist at the front, the arc of male coats, a woman fainting into a colleague's arms. The painting is public domain. The power arrangement is not a secret in it.

Charcot thought he was doing neurology. He used hypnosis as a laboratory tool, not as a séance. He also built a culture in which a diagnosis and a performance taught each other. Later historians, including feminists who reread the Salpêtrière, have asked how much of the "classic" attack was trained. That question does not make Charcot a cartoon. It makes the Tuesday lesson a warning about what a famous clinic can teach a body to do.

## Mesmer's barrel and the word that survived him

Franz Anton Mesmer, in 1770s Paris, gathered patients around a *baquet* of iron rods and "animal magnetism." A royal commission that included Benjamin Franklin and Antoine Lavoisier concluded in 1784 that the effects were imagination, not a magnetic fluid. Mesmer left as a disgraced fashion. The commission's report is one of the first modern documents in which a state says: the cure is real as experience and false as physics.

What survived was a technique of attention and command. The Marquis de Puységur described "artificial somnambulism." Nineteenth-century physicians in Manchester, Nancy, and Paris argued over whether the trance was a special state or ordinary suggestion. Hippolyte Bernheim in Nancy said: it is suggestion, and you do not need a Salpêtrière celebrity to produce it. That quarrel — spectacle versus ordinary talk — is still inside psychotherapy, whether anyone says "hypnosis" or not.

## Vienna, a friend, and a house that talked

Josef Breuer, a respected Viennese internist, treated a woman the published case called "Anna O." (Bertha Pappenheim, who later became a social worker and feminist organizer, and who did not remain a case). In the early 1880s she invented, with him, a ritual she named in English "the talking cure" and, jokingly, "chimney-sweeping." They used hypnosis; they also used recollection. The published *Studien über Hysterie* (1895), which Breuer and a younger colleague named Sigmund Freud signed together, turned those afternoons into a method: symptoms as residues of scenes that had not been spoken through.

The book is not a miracle story. Pappenheim's later life was larger than the case: she organized Jewish women's social work, translated, and did not spend her remaining decades as "Anna O." Freud and Breuer soon disagreed about sexuality as the preferred plot. Freud dropped hypnosis because, he said, not everyone could be hypnotized, and because he wanted the patient awake enough to resist. Free association — say what comes, even if it is stupid — is a technical answer to a failed trance.

Freud visited Nancy as well as Paris. The talking cure is, among other things, what happened when a Viennese neurologist decided that suggestion was not enough of an explanation and not enough of a method. Whether he was right is a century of argument. That he made the argument in a private study rather than a Tuesday amphitheater is the social fact this essay is after.

## Janet's other unconscious

Pierre Janet, Charcot's student, published *L'Automatisme psychologique* in 1889, before the *Studien*. He wrote of the *subconscient*, of dissociation, of ideas split off from the main stream of personality. He treated hysteria as a narrowing of the field of consciousness, often after exhaustion or shock. He also kept a hospital job and a philosopher's vocabulary.

Freud and Janet spent years in a sour priority fight. Ellenberger, who admired both, refused to let the fight erase Janet. A trauma clinician who has never heard of Freud is rare. A trauma clinician who has never heard of Janet is still too common. The essay on trauma lineages in this series picks him up again. The point here is simpler: the talking cure had more than one inventor, and one of them did not think the Oedipus story was the skeleton key.

## What "hysteria" was doing as a word

*Hystera* is Greek for womb. The diagnosis carried that etymology like a stain. Men received related labels (neurasthenia, traumatic neurosis) with more dignity. Women received a theater. That is not the whole truth — male hysterics appear in the Salpêtrière notes — but it is enough of the truth that a contemporary counselor should flinch when an old book says "hysteric" and means "difficult woman."

The word has been retired from official manuals. The pattern it named — a body that speaks when speech is costly — has not retired. Conversion, dissociation, and panic still arrive in primary-care offices before they arrive in therapy. History does not tell you what to do on Thursday. It tells you why the file you are about to open was once a Tuesday demonstration.

## From public lesson to private hour

The decisive social change is not a theory. It is architecture. Charcot taught in an amphitheater. Freud, after the 1890s, taught in a waiting room and a study at Berggasse 19, then in lectures, then in a movement. The patient was no longer a lesson for forty men. She was a paying interlocutor whose name, in print, became a letter.

That privacy made a new cruelty possible (the analyst who is never contradicted by a ward round) and a new kindness (the person who is not asked to perform her worst day for students). Counseling as a profession later borrowed the private hour and tried, with ethics codes, to keep the amphitheater from coming back as a reality show.

This series will not teach hypnosis. It will not offer a script for "getting the story out." The historical fact is narrower and stranger: for a few decades, European medicine believed that a symptom could be talked into movement, and then the believers split over what the talk was moving.

Charcot's photographers — Albert Londe at the Salpêtrière — made hysteria into a visual science. The plates taught later students what an attack "looked like." A body that has been photographed for a Tuesday lesson is not a free narrator. The talking cure's privacy was, among other things, a refusal of that camera. Counseling inherited the refusal and then, in the age of the training tape, invited a quieter camera back in. Rogers's recordings and Minuchin's mirror are descendants. They are not Charcot's amphitheater. They still need a consent that Tuesday did not ask.
