**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Dorothea Lynde Dix was born in Hampden, Maine, in 1802, and raised, unhappily, in Massachusetts. She taught school. She wrote children's books. She had collapses of her own health that sent her, at times, to rest. In 1841 she agreed to lead a Sunday-school class in the East Cambridge jail. She found people with mental illness housed with criminals, in cold, in filth. She began to count.

The *Memorial to the Legislature of Massachusetts* (1843) is not a gentle document. She lists towns. She lists cages. She names the weather. She tells a legislature that a republic that prints sermons about charity has left living people in cellars. Other memorials followed, state after state, and into the British North American provinces. She helped force the founding and enlargement of hospitals. St. Elizabeths in Washington — the federal hospital for the army, the navy, and the District — is part of that ledger. In 1868 the hospital commissioned the oil portrait by Samuel B. Waugh that now sits in the National Portrait Gallery under a CC0 release (NPG.97.38). That is the image this series may use.

## The Civil War superintendent

When the war came she was appointed superintendent of women nurses for the Union, unpaid, for years. She wanted her nurses plain, Protestant, and older than thirty. Surgeons found her impossible. Soldiers called her kind. The same will that walked almshouses tried to command a medical bureau. It is of a piece. Dix did not believe in waiting for a gentleman to notice.

## What she got right, and what she could not see

She was right that jails are not hospitals. She was right that "out of sight" was an American method. She was right that a teacher with a notebook could move money.

She could not see, or would not accept, that the hospital she wanted would become, in the next century, another warehouse. Moral treatment needed staff and space. State budgets provided architecture and a census. Dix's faith was in the building. A later generation's faith — Clifford Beers, then the community-mental-health movement, then the mixed disaster of deinstitutionalization — had to argue with that building.

She was also a woman of her class and century: sure of her moral taste, not curious enough about the people she rescued as people with politics of their own. A hagiography helps no one. Neither does a sneer. She did the walking.

## Why a counseling site still says her name

Most clients will never sleep in a state hospital. The system that decides who does still sits behind the outpatient hour: commitment law, the ER, the jail that has become, again, a ward. Dix's method — go look, write it down, take the writing to the people who vote the funds — is not outdated. It is unfashionable.

She is not a psychotherapist. She belongs in this pack because the talking cure grew up in the shadow of the institutions she built and the institutions she emptied of their worst excuses.
