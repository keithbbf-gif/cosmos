**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Ignacio Martín-Baró was born in 1942 in Valladolid, Spain, entered the Society of Jesus, and made his working life at the Universidad Centroamericana in San Salvador. He was a social psychologist who ran surveys the government did not want run — about fear, about the war, about what *el pueblo* believed. In the early hours of 16 November 1989, members of the Salvadoran military murdered him, five other Jesuit priests, their housekeeper Elba Ramos, and her daughter Celina, at the UCA.

English-language clinicians mostly met him afterward, in *Writings for a Liberation Psychology* (1994), edited by Adrianne Aron and Shawn Corne.

## Psychology that had been answering the wrong country

Martín-Baró argued that Latin American psychology was imported: it asked North American questions with North American instruments and then declared the poor deficient. Liberation psychology, in his pages, changes the question. What does war do to a people's ability to think? What is "mental health" in a country where the healthy adaptation might be silence? Who is the psychologist's client — the occupier's order, or the people who have to live under it?

He did not write a treatment manual for a private office in a peaceful county. He wrote from a campus that was already a target.

## The fatal poll

His Instituto Universitario de Opinión Pública asked Salvadorans what they thought. In a dirty war, a poll is not a classroom exercise. Teresa Whitfield's *Paying the Price* places the UCA murders in the political sequence. This essay will not reconstruct the atrocity as a scene. The date is enough. The names of Elba and Celina Ramos belong next to the priests' names, because a history that mourns only the professors has learned nothing from the work.

## How a quiet clinic might read him without theft

You do not become a liberation psychologist by quoting him in a bio. You become slightly more dangerous to your own comfort: you notice when a diagnosis is a way to end a political sentence; you notice whose suffering is "trauma" and whose is "noncompliance"; you notice when your theory was funded to keep a workplace calm.

You also do not tell a client that their panic is only imperialism. Martín-Baró was a careful empiricist. He wanted better questions, not slogans.

No photographs of the murders as featured images. Type only. The UCA and Jesuit archives are not a stock library.
