**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 1908 Frank Parsons opened a Vocation Bureau in Boston. He was a lawyer and reformer, not a physician. *Choosing a Vocation* (1909) argued that a person should know themselves, know the work, and reason about the match. The book is earnest and of its time. It is also a founding document for a profession that would later be called counseling — a profession that did not begin as the treatment of madness and still, in some of its rooms, is not.

This matters on a site named WOW Therapies. The word *therapy* in American English now covers speech, occupational, physical, and mental-health work. The SLPWOW pack owns the speech lineage. This essay owns the mental-health counseling lineage: how a guidance job became a licensed clinical job, and how it still is not psychiatry.

## Three rooms that were not the same

**Psychiatry** is a medical specialty. It inherited the asylum, the prescription pad, and, for a mid-century interval, the couch. Its guild is the AMA and the APA (the psychiatric one — the acronym collision with the psychological APA is a student's rite of passage).

**Clinical psychology** dates a founding clinic to Lightner Witmer at the University of Pennsylvania in 1896; he used the phrase "clinical psychology" in 1907. Psychologists fought, for decades, for the right to test, then to treat, then to prescribe in a few states. Their doctorate culture is not identical with a counselor's master's culture.

**Counseling** grew from vocational guidance, school guidance, college personnel work, and the veterans' programs after 1945. The American Personnel and Guidance Association formed in 1952 from a merger of existing guidance groups and became, after several renamings, the American Counseling Association. CACREP, founded in 1981, standardized the master's. Virginia passed the first general counselor-licensure statute in 1976. Other states followed slowly; some still argue about title and scope. The letters — LPC, LCPC, LMHC, LPCC — are a map of those arguments, not a secret ranking. The Counseling Compact, with legislation spreading in the early 2020s, is an attempt to let a license travel the way a nurse's sometimes can. It does not make the fifty ethics codes into one.

A client does not need this org chart. A history series does, because "I saw a therapist" erases who was allowed to see them, under what law, and with what training in diagnosis versus in guidance.

## What counseling kept from guidance

A bias toward development rather than toward deficit. A workplace in schools. A comfort with psychoeducation. A habit of asking about career, family, and church in the same hour. At its best this is whole-person work. At its worst it is advice with a diploma.

Clinical mental-health counseling, as a later specialization, moved toward the DSM, toward insurance panels, and toward the same empirical arguments that reshaped psychology. The master's clinician in a community agency and the psychologist in a medical center now often run the same CBT hour and fill out the same form. The difference that remains is institutional: title, test, and who can sign which document.

## Ethics as the thing that makes it a profession

Confidentiality, competence, dual relationships, duty to warn (the Tarasoff line of cases, 1970s California, then a fifty-state patchwork), and the slow, incomplete reckoning with conversion practices — these are how a job becomes something you can lose.

Bayer (1981) on the 1973 diagnosis fight is psychiatric history that counseling inherited. The multicultural competencies paper of 1992 is counseling history that psychiatry sometimes ignored. Both belong in a training program that does not want to repeat the last century's official cruelties.

## Arkansas, without inventing a myth

Southeast Arkansas, like most rural American places, met mental-health care later and thinner than it met the state hospital. The state hospital at Little Rock (later the Arkansas State Hospital) is the older address. Outpatient counseling as a licensed job is the newer one. Travel, stigma, and the shortage of licensed people are part of the professional history, not a footnote. Telehealth and compact legislation are attempts to change the geography. They do not change the older fact: the profession was built in cities and universities and then asked to work everywhere.

School counseling is the sibling that still employs more people than the private-practice myth admits. The American School Counselor Association's later identity fight — guidance versus clinical mental health in a building full of children — is a professional history of its own. This essay will not annex it. It will say: a great many Americans first met a "counselor" as a person with a filing cabinet and a bell schedule, not as a fifty-minute outpatient.

WOW Therapies' live clinical identity, as of the site this pack was written against, is speech-language pathology. These essays do not pretend otherwise. They build an educational lane for readers who want the mental-health heritage that shares the word *therapy* and not the same license.

## Why the distinction is a kindness

People deserve to know whether they are seeing someone who can prescribe, someone who can test for a learning disability, someone who can treat a swallow, or someone who can counsel a grief. The words have been allowed to blur because markets like blur. History is one way to un-blur them without a sneer at any of the crafts.

The figure essays that follow are people. The profession essay is the building they did not all agree to share.
