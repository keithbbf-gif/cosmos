**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Edward John Mostyn Bowlby was born in 1907 into a London household that farmed children out to nannies and, in his case, to boarding school at a young age. Suzan van Dijken's account of the early life is the place to watch a theorist of separation being assembled. He trained in medicine, psychiatry, and psychoanalysis, worked with delinquent boys, and never became the kind of analyst his institute wanted. He died in 1990 on the Isle of Skye.

## The 1951 document

*Maternal Care and Mental Health*, written for the World Health Organization, surveyed what happened to children who lost a continuous caregiver — institutions, evacuations, long hospital stays. The science was uneven. The moral was loud. Newspapers and ministries heard: mothers must stay home. Bowlby meant, increasingly, that a child needs a reliable figure, not that a woman may not earn a wage. He spent the next thirty years trying to get the second sentence to catch up with the first.

The misreading still walks into parenting arguments. A historically literate clinician should not help it.

The Tavistock Clinic, where he worked after the war, was a place where child guidance, psychoanalysis, and later family work shared a building and not always a theory. Bowlby used that building to run a research unit that looked at separations as they happened — hospital visiting rules, the two-year-old left on a ward. James and Joyce Robertson's films of children in hospital sit in the same argument: look at the child, then change the visiting hours. Attachment theory's first policy victory was furniture and a cot for a parent, not a quiz.

He had worked, before the WHO assignment, with "delinquent" boys and had already decided that a broken affectional bond was a better first hypothesis than a broken character. The 44 juvenile thieves paper (1944) is an ancestor of the 1951 monograph. Institutes that wanted a drive story heard a social worker. He kept writing as if the institute were wrong.

## Ethology and the three volumes

Bowlby read Lorenz and Hinde and decided that proximity-seeking was a primary motivational system. *Attachment* (1969), *Separation* (1973), and *Loss* (1980) are the trilogy. Protest, despair, detachment — the sequence after a break — gave adult grief a developmental cousin. Psychoanalytic colleagues often treated him as a biologist who had wandered in. Developmental psychologists treated him as a resource.

Mary Ainsworth's observations (the next essay) gave his theory eyes. Without Uganda and Baltimore, Bowlby is an argument. With them, he is a research program.

He was analyzed, and he analyzed, and he still wrote as if the infant's need for a person were not a derivative of feeding. That sentence made him unwelcome. It also made Ainsworth possible. The trilogy is long on purpose: he would not leave grief as a footnote to anger.

A later popularization — "attachment parenting" as a consumer identity — is not his book. Neither is the adult-style quiz. Both used his prestige. A historically literate clinician can say so without sneering at parents who are trying to stay near a child in a hospital. That nearness was his actual policy target.

## In the adult hour

Bowlby did not write a couple's manual. Adult clinicians borrowed the idea that a patient is trying to keep a base, test a base, or live as if no base will hold. That borrowing can be precise. It can also become the dating-quiz industry this pack has already refused.

He was capable of a cold prose. He was also, in the trilogy, a writer of grief. The two tones are the man: a British official scientist who had been a lonely boy.

The WHO assignment came because postwar Europe was full of children who had been moved on purpose. He was not the only physician to notice. He was the one who kept writing after the emergency posters came down, until the notice became a theory with volumes. That stubbornness is the reason Ainsworth had something to test, and the reason a hospital cot for a parent can still be called, without much stretch, a Bowlby object.

The Robertsons' hospital films and Bowlby's volumes belong on the same shelf. Policy changed because someone showed a child's face after a parent left the ward, not because a theory was elegant. Elegance came after, in three books, for readers who needed a science as well as a film.
