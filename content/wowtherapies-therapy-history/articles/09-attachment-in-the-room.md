**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

During the Second World War, British children were moved out of cities, parked in nurseries, and filmed. Dorothy Burlingham and Anna Freud wrote from the Hampstead nurseries. René Spitz filmed infants in institutions and used the word *anaclitic depression*. John Bowlby, a psychiatrist and psychoanalyst who had already been arguing with his own institute, took a WHO assignment and in 1951 published *Maternal Care and Mental Health*.

The monograph was read, in newspapers and in parliaments, as: mothers must stay home or children will be ruined. Bowlby spent the rest of his life trying to refine "maternal deprivation" into something more precise — a need for a continuous, responsive figure, not a commandment against women's work. The misreading had already gone to work in policy.

James and Joyce Robertson's films of children in hospital — *A Two-Year-Old Goes to Hospital* (1952) is the one people still name — belong in the same file. They are not Bowlby's books. They are the pictures that made visiting-hour rules look like a theory you could no longer afford. Attachment research's first public victory was a cot in a ward, not a category on a dating site.

This essay is about that refinement, and about Mary Ainsworth's stubborn gift for watching actual babies in Uganda and Baltimore, and about the later industry that turned a research program into a quiz.

## Ethology in a consulting profession

Bowlby read Konrad Lorenz and Robert Hinde. He wanted psychoanalysis to survive contact with evolutionary biology and with children who were not on a couch. *Attachment and Loss* (three volumes, 1969, 1973, 1980) argued that proximity-seeking is a primary system, like feeding, not a secondary result of oral pleasure. Separation protest is not a tantrum to be interpreted away. Grief has a sequence that can go wrong.

The British Psychoanalytical Society did not throw a party. Bowlby was, for years, an insider who ate at the side table. That exile is part of why attachment theory later found its home in developmental psychology departments and then migrated back into clinics as a borrowed science.

## Uganda first, then a room with eight episodes

Mary Ainsworth, Canadian-born, worked with Bowlby in London, then studied twenty-eight Ganda infants in Uganda in 1954–55. *Infancy in Uganda* (1967) is still unread by people who think attachment research began in a laboratory with a one-way mirror. She watched how babies used a mother as a base for exploration in a world that was not Baltimore.

The Strange Situation — a twenty-minute sequence of comings and goings, stranger included — was built later, in the 1960s, with families in Baltimore. *Patterns of Attachment* (1978), with Mary Blehar, Everett Waters, and Sally Wall, gave the field its famous letters: secure, anxious-avoidant, anxious-resistant. Mary Main and colleagues later added disorganized/disoriented behavior and, in adults, the Adult Attachment Interview.

Those letters are research categories. They are not personalities. They are not a brand of husband. A clinician who says "she's an avoidant" after fifteen minutes has left Ainsworth's notebook and entered a magazine.

## Adult rooms, couple rooms

By the 1980s and 1990s, clinicians were asking whether the infant categories helped with adults who could not use another person as a base. Some of the best work stayed close to observation: how a patient greets, how they leave, how they treat the therapist as dangerous or as furniture.

Hospital visiting hours changed in living memory because films and monographs made the unvisited child impossible to unsee. A parent who now sleeps in a pediatric room is standing in a policy Bowlby and the Robertsons won. A therapy office that treats a missed session only as resistance, never as protest or as a test of the base, has not collected that win. The two rooms — ward and consulting room — are the same argument at different scales.

Sue Johnson's emotionally focused therapy for couples — a later, manualized descendant — put attachment protest in the center of the couple hour. Johnson died in 2024; her books are widely taught. This pack does not give her a full figure essay because the research spine is still Bowlby and Ainsworth. The couple application is an inheritance, not the origin.

Peter Fonagy and colleagues' work on mentalization, and the later "attachment-based" brand of therapies for adolescents, are further descendants. Some of that work is careful. Some of it is a certificate. The historical test is the same as Ainsworth's: did anyone watch, or did they only name a style?

## Pop attachment and the clinic's job

The internet sold "attachment style" as a dating label. The sale flattened a strange, ethical research program into a personality horoscope. It also revived the old misreading of 1951: if your child is distressed, you have failed the category.

A historically literate counselor can do three modest things:

- Speak of attachment as a *relationship pattern*, revisable, not a tattoo.
- Remember Uganda when someone talks as if the science were only American.
- Refuse to use the theory to send a woman home from work.

None of that is a protocol for "earning secure attachment in six sessions." Those products exist. They are not this series.

## Why the war nurseries still matter

The origin is not a lab. It is a century that separated children from caregivers on purpose — evacuation, hospital rules that banned parents, institutions that called themselves care. Attachment theory is, among other things, a long argument with those rules. Policy won in furniture when hospitals let parents stay; policy can still lose in a consulting room that hears only resistance. The ward and the fifty-minute room are the same argument at different scales. History does not finish the training; it names the scale.

John Bowlby's 1958 paper "The Nature of the Child's Tie to His Mother" is the theoretical hinge between the WHO survey and the trilogy: proximity-seeking as a primary motivational system, not a sequel to feeding or to oral pleasure. Analysts heard a biologist. Ethologists heard a clinician. The paper is still assignable. It is not a parenting blog, and it is not a law against a mother who earns a wage.

Mary Main, Carol George, and Nancy Kaplan's Adult Attachment Interview (the mid-1980s papers) moved the research from a twenty-minute room with a stranger to an hour of adult speech about early care. The AAI is a research instrument with a training that takes weeks. It is not a quiz you download. Clinicians who say "her AAI would be dismissing" after one intake have borrowed a prestige they have not earned. The historical gift of that work is smaller and better: adult narrative can carry infant strategy, and the carrying is researchable if anyone bothers to train. Cite the paper, not a podcast.
