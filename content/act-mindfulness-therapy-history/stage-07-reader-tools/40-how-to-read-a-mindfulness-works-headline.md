**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

A good headline names an object. A bad headline names a noun. "Mindfulness works" is a bad headline. It does not tell you whose mindfulness, in whom, against what, for how long, paid by whom.

Draft 02 gave a kit for therapy-history claims. This page is the kit applied to the specific sludge this pack's readers will meet.

**Whose?** Langer 1989, Benson 1975, MBSR 1979/1990, MBCT 2000/2002, ACT 1999, DBT 1993, a two-minute app, a corporate hour. If the paper cannot say, the headline is already lying.

**In whom?** Undergraduates, chronic-pain referrals, recovered people with three-plus depressive episodes, veterans, analogue pain-task volunteers, employees who could not refuse a workplace study. Teasdale 2000's split is the model: the same program did not show the same thing in two prior-episode groups. A headline that erases a split is a treatment decision.

**Against what?** Wait-list, treatment as usual, a pill, CBT, a sham timer, nothing. Öst 2008's complaint about methods is partly a complaint about weak comparisons. A large effect against nothing is a different animal than a small effect against a decent treatment.

**How long?** Eight weeks, 60 weeks, six months, a one-shot workshop. Relapse prevention lives or dies in the follow-up. A post-test glow is not a year.

**Who paid?** University intramural funds, NCCIH, a drug company (rare here, not zero in the wider mood literature), an app company, a hospital marketing budget. Payment is not automatic taint. Hidden payment is.

**What did they actually measure?** A self-report scale, a diagnostic interview, a workday, a blood number, a cold-pressor second. "Works" hides the verb. Works at what.

When you have those six, you still do not have a prescription. You have a *description of a study*. Description is this folder's job. Prescription is a clinician's job.

A few headline species, named so you can swat them:

- **"Ancient practice, modern science."** Category collapse. Drafts 05–06.
- **"Better than CBT."** Needs a named trial, a dated review, and limits. Usually missing all three.
- **"No side effects."** Sitting can harm people. Retreats can harm people. Avoidance of medical care can harm people. A history page will not list a risk table as if it were a consent form; it will refuse the fairy tale.
- **"Harvard says."** Langer and Benson both sat near that river. So did a lot of press offices.
- **"Recommended for depression."** Ask if they mean MBCT's recovered, three-plus census and a 2009 British guideline. If they mean a first episode on a Tuesday in Pine Bluff, they have jumped a continent.

For WOW Therapies staff who may one day post on social media: if you cannot fit the object, the census, and the year, do not post the finding. Silence is allowed. Hype is a claims-guardrail failure.

This page will not teach you to be a statistician. It will teach you to be a reader who does not confuse a noun with a table.

If a headline makes you want to enroll or to sneer, that is the flattening working. The antidote is dull: open the paper, or open this folder's dated essays, or walk away and call a licensed person if you are in trouble. Dull is a virtue on this desk.

A last species: **"meta-analysis proves."** Meta-analyses inherit every inclusion mistake of their children. Öst 2008 and Powers 2009 already disagree in tone. A later paper will disagree with both. "Proves" is a religious verb. Reviews estimate, under rules.

Walking away is a success. This series does not need you to become a methods nerd. It needs you not to be recruited. Recruitment is what a bad headline is *for*. A good headline is for a table.
