In May 1991, at the Association for Behavior Analysis in Atlanta, Wilson, Khorakiwala, and Hayes presented "Change in Acceptance and Commitment Therapy." Zettle's 2005 history and the ACBS "How old is ACT?" page both treat that title as the first documented use of the name in a talk. In 1994, Hayes and Wilson published "Acceptance and Commitment Therapy: Altering the Verbal Support for Experiential Avoidance" in *The Behavior Analyst*. The paper says, in so many words, that the approach had been known as comprehensive distancing and was renamed to avoid dissociative connotations. It also says the acronym is pronounced "act," not "A-C-T."

Pronunciation is branding. So is the pair of nouns.

**Acceptance**, in this paperwork, is not approval and not resignation as a poster uses those words. It is a wager that struggling to delete private experience often feeds the struggle. I am describing a definition, not assigning the wager.

**Commitment**, in this paperwork, is not a wedding. It is movement toward verbalized values even when the private experience stays loud. Again: a definition in a 1994 article and a 1999 book. Not a card sort.

The two nouns together answered a problem the old nickname created. "Distancing" sounded like leaving. The new name sounded like staying — staying with experience, staying with a life. Whether the work does that is a clinical question. Whether the name was chosen to say that is a historical one. The 1994 paper is explicit enough.

"Experiential avoidance" is the third term in the 1994 title. It named a target: the attempt to avoid unwanted thoughts, feelings, memories, as a *pattern* that can organize a life. The term traveled. It also got sloppy. Avoiding a burning building is not a pathology. A school that forgets the difference starts preaching. This pack will keep the 1994 sense: a verbal-behavioral pattern, hypothesized, researched, argued with.

Why not "contextual therapy," which Zettle says was already in circulation? I do not have a memo. I can see what "ACT" does that "contextual therapy" does not. It is pronounceable. It contains a moral-sounding noun (commitment) and a gentle-sounding noun (acceptance). It can be printed on a workshop banner. "Contextual" is a philosopher's adjective. The 1990s were a managed-care decade. Names that could travel, traveled.

Hayes, Strosahl, and Wilson's 1999 book locked the name into library catalogs. After that, "comprehensive distancing" became a historian's phrase. Students who met the school in 2005's *Get Out of Your Mind* never needed Big D.

Critics heard the name as a promise. Acceptance sounds kind. Commitment sounds strong. Kind-and-strong is a sales pitch even when the authors are sincere. Sincerity and sales can share a title page. A WOW Therapies history series can print the 1991 and 1994 objects without adopting the pitch.

There is a small, useful pedantry: ACT is a therapy name, not a claim that one "acts" instead of feeling. Popular summaries sometimes say that. The 1994 subtitle is about altering verbal support for avoidance, not about becoming a stoic mascot.

Living people: public papers only.

If a later editor wants a one-line caption for a timeline graphic: **1981 protocol (lab name) → 1991 talk title → 1994 journal title → 1999 book.** Four steps. No halo. No "and then the world was healed."

November 1991, Zettle notes, saw an AABT paper with "ACT" in the title (Wilson & Taylor). I have not held that handout. I print the month because the rename was a season, not a lightning bolt: late 1990 still saying distancing, 1991 saying ACT, 1994 locking the journal, 1999 locking the catalog.

The sister pack's third-wave essay already said the 1999 book is a landmark. This essay is the naming of the landmark. Names are not decorations. They are how a lab becomes a workforce.
