**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

The lab nickname, ACBS's own page says, was "Big D." The written name was comprehensive distancing. Steven Hayes, on that page, dates the first protocol to 1981. Robert Zettle, in a 2005 history essay, traces how the name sat beside synonyms — "a contextual approach to therapeutic change" (Hayes, 1987), "contextual therapy" (Zettle & Rains, 1989) — until late 1990 and early 1991, when "acceptance and commitment therapy" began to win titles.

I trust those two public accounts for the naming story. I have not held the 1981 protocol. The ACBS site claims it lives in a "History of ACT" area. A protocol I have not read is a rumor with a year. The year still matters. "ACT began in 1999" is a bookstore sentence. 1981 is a lab sentence.

Why "distancing"? In behavior-therapy English of the period, distancing named a move already adjacent to cognitive therapy: getting some space from a thought so it does not run the body. "Comprehensive" was the ambition — not a single technique but a stance toward verbal control. The trouble with the word, Hayes and Wilson wrote in 1994, was dissociative connotations. They did not want to sound as if they were teaching people to leave their lives. They renamed.

Zettle's 2005 essay is the best single secondary narrative I know in English for this stretch: student, colleague, historian of his own school. That last clause is a bias warning. He is not a hostile witness. He is also not a press release. He bothers with months: AABT 1990 still saying "comprehensive distancing"; ABA May 1991 saying "acceptance and commitment therapy"; a journal title in 1994.

The 1986 Zettle and Hayes paper remains the early published window. It describes a strategy aimed at the verbal-social contingencies that support a controlling relationship between reason-giving and dysfunction. If that sentence is heavy, good. The early work is heavy. Later popular books (*Get Out of Your Mind and Into Your Life*, 2005; Harris's *The Happiness Trap*, 2007) made the school sound like a friendly uncle. Uncles sell. Labs do not.

I will not reconstruct Big D as a how-to. Reconstruction would be a guess and a protocol. What a history page can say: a group of behavior analysts in the early 1980s thought the clinical problem was less the *content* of a thought than the *job* the thought was doing, and they tried to build a therapy that changed the job. They tested bits of it in analogue tasks and in Zettle's dissertation work. They argued with CBT in the same hotels. They waited — Hayes says — to publish some of the outcome work.

Waiting is itself historical. A school that delays RCTs can look principled (get the model right) or evasive (stay unfalsifiable). Corrigan, in 2001, accused some new behavior therapies of getting ahead of the data. Hayes answered. Öst, in 2008, graded the data that had arrived. This pack will not declare a winner. It will keep 1981–1991 from disappearing into a 1999 launch party.

For WOW Therapies: if a later service page ever says "we have been influenced by ACT since the beginning," ask which beginning. The bookstore beginning is 1999. The name-in-a-title beginning is 1991. The protocol-nickname beginning is 1981, according to the people who were there. "The beginning" is a marketing hour. History prefers three dates and a shrug.

Living people: Hayes and Zettle — public documents only. Zettle's 2005 essay is one of those documents.

Zettle and Rains (1989) used "contextual therapy" in a depression-treatment paper that later ACT bibliographies still cite. I have not re-held that PDF this pass; I mark it as a named synonym on Zettle's 2005 timeline. Synonyms matter because they show a school shopping for English before it found a banner.

The nickname "Big D" is almost too charming to print. I print it so the later brand name does not look inevitable. Names are chosen. This one was chosen because another name sounded like leaving.
