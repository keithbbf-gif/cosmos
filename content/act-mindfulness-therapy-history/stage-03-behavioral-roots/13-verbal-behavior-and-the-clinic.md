**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Appleton-Century-Crofts published B. F. Skinner's *Verbal Behavior* in 1957. It is a book clinicians in training are still told to fear or skip: dense, technical, famous for a review (Chomsky, 1959) that many people cite without reading either text. The clinic of the 1960s and 1970s, when it wanted language, mostly wanted something else — Ellis's sentences you could argue with, Beck's thoughts you could test, psychoanalytic talk you could interpret. Skinner's book sat on a shelf while CBT wrote thought records.

ACT's later claim — and it is a claim, not a miracle — is that the shelf was the wrong place for that book. If human suffering is tangled in how words work, then a clinical psychology that treats thoughts as things to be swapped for better thoughts may be obeying the same verbal machinery that got the person stuck. That sentence is a school's theory. It is not this page telling you to stop thinking.

What Skinner actually offered, in brutal miniature: verbal behavior is behavior, reinforced by a verbal community. A "tact," a "mand," an "intraverbal" — the jargon is a way of asking *what the talk is doing*, not what inner object it names. Most psychotherapists found this chilly. They were in the business of inner objects. The chill is part of the history. So is the fact that applied behavior analysis took the book into autism education and language training, a corridor this pack will not tour and must not flatten into ACT.

Hayes and colleagues in the 1980s were among the people who tried to bring a post-Skinner analysis *into* adult outpatient problems: depression, anxiety, the reasons people give for why they cannot move. They did not need Chomsky to be wrong about everything. They needed a way to talk about rule-following that was not "the patient has a cognitive distortion." Whether they succeeded is a later-stage fight (Öst, Hofmann, the meta-analyses). The 1957 book is the furniture they rearranged.

I will not teach verbal operants. I will not pretend I have a view about innateness. I will say: the intellectual parentage ACT claims is not the Buddha and not Rogers. It is a behaviorist who wrote a hard book about talking, plus a later research program (relational frame theory) that said the 1957 book was not enough. Claiming parentage is a political act inside a discipline. Historians should report the claim and the dissent.

The clinic that walked past *Verbal Behavior* had reasons. Skinnerian talk sounded like it left out meaning, love, and the unconscious. Some of it did. Some of the leaving-out was a refusal to reify. Patients still needed meaning, love, and a place for what they could not say. Third-wave rooms later smuggled those needs back in as "values" and "acceptance," which is why a person-centered clinician can hear a rhyme and a behaviorist can hear a betrayal.

For a WOW Therapies reader who will never open the 1957 book, the usable fact is smaller. When an ACT workshop says "the problem is language," it is not saying "stop talking." It is pointing at a 1957 bet that talk is something people *do* to each other. That bet can be fertile. It can also become a slogan that blames a patient's vocabulary for a landlord, a diagnosis, or a war. Slogans are what happen when a hard book becomes a weekend.

Chomsky's review is a historical object too. It helped make generative grammar a prestige science and behaviorism a punchline in many psychology departments. ACT grew anyway, partly in departments that never accepted the punchline, partly by speaking enough CBT English to get into hospitals. The bilingualism is part of the school's success and part of the family quarrel.

This series will not adjudicate Skinner versus Chomsky. It will keep 1957 on the table so 1999 does not look like it fell from a cushion.
