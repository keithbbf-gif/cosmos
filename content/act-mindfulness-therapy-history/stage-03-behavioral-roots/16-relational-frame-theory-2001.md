**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Kluwer Academic / Plenum published *Relational Frame Theory: A Post-Skinnerian Account of Human Language and Cognition* in 2001, edited by Steven C. Hayes, Dermot Barnes-Holmes, and Bryan Roche. It is a technical book. It is not a personality quiz, not a therapy brand, and not something a history page should paraphrase into a party trick.

RFT's public bet, in the school's own English, is that humans learn to relate stimuli in arbitrarily applicable ways — same, opposite, better, before — and that this relating can spread. A word can hurt like a thing. A future can govern a Tuesday. Once you can frame, you can suffer in ways a nonverbal animal does not. That last sentence is the school's poetry. The book's chapters are experiments, definitions, and arguments with other learning theories.

I will not teach frames. Teaching frames on a clinic website would be a parody of a research program and a temptation to diagnose your relatives. What I will say is why the 2001 object sits in this pack.

ACT writers have long insisted that the therapy is not a bag of metaphors. It is supposed to be tied to a basic-science account of language. The 2004 *Behavior Therapy* essay puts ACT and RFT in the same title on purpose. If you cut RFT out of the history, ACT becomes a vibe with a Guilford spine. If you leave RFT in, you have to admit that a large part of the school's prestige claim is *this hard book*, which most clinicians have not finished.

Reception was mixed, as hard books are. Some behavior analysts welcomed a way past the 1957 *Verbal Behavior* stalemate. Some found RFT jargon a church. Cognitive scientists often declined to convert. None of that settles whether a particular ACT trial "worked." It settles that the school wanted a laboratory parent, not only a cushion.

Dermot and Yvonne Barnes-Holmes, Irish laboratories, and later the implicit relational assessment procedure (IRAP) are part of this research culture. I name the culture; I will not sell a measure. Carmen Luciano's Almería group, Ian Stewart in Galway, and others sat on the 2005 ACBS planning committee. RFT was already international before the therapy's popular books were.

A danger for this series: explaining RFT badly. Bad explanations sound like "everything is connected" or "words create reality." The first is a poster. The second is a half-overheard philosophy course. RFT is a learning-theory wager with a specific experimental literature. If I cannot be specific, I should be brief. I am being brief.

Another danger: using RFT to intimidate. "You wouldn't understand, it's relational frames" is priest-talk. A WOW Therapies history page has no priests. A curious reader is allowed to know that a 2001 book exists, that ACT cites it, and that critics have asked whether the therapy needs the book or only likes having it.

Hofmann's 2008 "old hat" question can be asked here too. Does a clinician need RFT to sit with a person who is stuck in a sentence? Many good clinicians do not. The school's answer is that *understanding* the stuckness as verbal relating changes what you stop doing (arguing with the sentence as if it were a fact to beat). Whether that answer is true is an empirical and a clinical question this page will not close.

Living people: Hayes, Barnes-Holmes, Roche — public books only.

Put the 2001 spine next to the 1999 Guilford ACT spine. Two years, two jobs: a therapy you could train, a theory you could cite. The workshop economy later sold the first harder than the second. That imbalance is historical. It is how a science-facing school becomes a certificate.

A brief note on "post-Skinnerian" in the subtitle: it is a fight inside a family, not a press release that Skinner is obsolete. The 2001 book wanted a way past a 1957 stalemate. Whether it succeeded is a literature, not a weekend. Clinicians who have never opened the book can still be good clinicians. The school's prestige claim is that *someone* should keep opening it.
