**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In November 1982, at the Association for Advancement of Behavior Therapy in Los Angeles, Hayes, Korn, Zettle, Rosenfarb, and Cooper presented a paper whose title already contained the bet: "Rule-governed behavior and cognitive behavior therapy: The effects of comprehensive cognitive distancing on pain tolerance." I take the citation from the ACBS "How old is ACT?" page, which is the association's own memory. I have not held the 1982 handout. A conference title is still a dated object.

"Rule-governed behavior" is behavior-analytic English for a familiar misery: we follow sentences. Some sentences are useful ("look both ways"). Some were useful in a house that is gone and now wreck a life that remains ("if I feel it, I must fix it"; "if I think it, it is me"). The 1980s papers did not need a Buddha to see this. They needed schedules of reinforcement, and they needed a suspicion that *telling* a person to think differently might be one more rule.

Zettle and Hayes, in 1986, published "Dysfunctional control by client verbal behavior: The context of reason-giving" in *The Analysis of Verbal Behavior*. The paper is readable, which is a kindness. It treats reasons as things that can control behavior too well. Comprehensive distancing — the lab's then-name for the approach — is described as an attempt to change the *context* of that verbal control rather than to swap the reasons for prettier ones. I will not reprint the exercises. The historical point is the target: reason-giving, not "distorted cognitions" as Beck's manuals named them.

This is the fork the family still argues about. CBT, in its classical public form, treats certain thoughts as hypotheses to test. The 1980s contextual papers treat the *testing* as possibly more of the same verbal trap. Both can be caricatured. Beck was never only a thought-record machine; Hayes's group still used words in a room. Caricature is how schools recruit.

Why pain-tolerance studies? Because analogue tasks are what a lab can run while a theory is young. A cold pressor or a shock protocol is not depression. Hayes later said, on the ACBS history page, that he held back randomized outcome publication because the implications seemed large and the model was not ready — and that a 1980s pain-rationale study did not appear until *The Psychological Record* in 1999. That is a founder's memory of delay. Readers may find it principled or convenient. Historians should print the dates: 1982 talk, 1986 Zettle paper, 1999 analogue write-up, 1999 Guilford book.

Rule-following also has a politics. A woman whose "rules" were written by a church, a husband, or a clinic that called her borderline is not suffering only from language. If ACT's later popularizers made "defusion" sound like a way to tolerate oppression, they earned the critique Stage 6 will name. The 1980s papers are not that popularization. They are dryer, smaller, and easier to miss.

For a WOW Therapies reader: when someone says ACT is "just CBT with mindfulness," they are skipping this fork. When someone says ACT is "ancient wisdom," they are skipping it from the other side. The 1980s object is a behavior-therapy argument about instructions. You can reject the argument. You cannot honestly skip it and still tell the school's history.

Hayes and colleagues were not the only 1980s people writing about instructions. The wider behavior-analytic literature on rule-governance (Zettle's circle, but also work descending from Skinner's distinction between contingency-shaped and rule-governed behavior) is a hallway this pack will not tour room by room. The hallway existed. ACT grew in it. CBT grew next door and used a different floor plan.

This page will not teach you to spot your rules. Spotting your rules is a session. I will say: a Los Angeles conference in 1982 already paired "rule-governed behavior" with "comprehensive distancing" and "cognitive behavior therapy" in one title. The family fight is that old. The nickname "third wave" is younger.
