**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

The August 2000 issue of the *Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology* carried "Prevention of Relapse/Recurrence in Major Depression by Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy," by John D. Teasdale, Zindel V. Segal, J. Mark G. Williams, Valerie A. Ridgeway, Judith M. Soulsby, and Mark A. Lau. Recovered recurrently depressed patients (n = 145) were randomized to treatment as usual or to MBCT plus treatment as usual. Relapse or recurrence was watched for 60 weeks.

The authors reported that for patients with three or more previous episodes (77 percent of the sample), MBCT was associated with a lower risk of relapse. For patients with only two previous episodes, it was not. They described the result as a promising, possibly cost-efficient psychological approach to preventing relapse in a defined group.

That is the table. That is the split. Everything else is weather.

I will not convert hazard ratios into a promise. I will not hide the comparison (usual care, not a pill-for-pill fight with maintenance antidepressant in this particular design — readers who need that later literature should go to later papers, not to this paragraph). I will not pretend 145 people, in this design, settle a decade.

What the 2000 paper did historically was give MBCT a *journal object* a guideline committee could later name. NICE CG90 (2009) did name MBCT for people currently well after three or more episodes. The lineage from table to committee is not magic. It is how British guideline culture works: a trial, then more trials, then a sentence in a PDF. The 2009 sentence is not this website's advice. It is a 2009 committee's advice to the NHS.

Limits a popular article usually skips:

- The sample was recovered, not acutely ill.
- The split by prior episodes was crucial and easy to flatten.
- "Treatment as usual" is a messy comparison, as it always is.
- The program was manualized and group-based; a YouTube video is not the 2000 object.
- Authors have careers. Careers are not a reason to discard a table. They are a reason not to treat a table as scripture.

Replication and extension happened (later trials, a 2013 second edition of the book). This essay stays with 2000 so a reader can see what "MBCT works" first pointed at.

For ACT comparisons: this is a different design, different census, different manual. Meta-analyses that dump MBCT and ACT into a "mindfulness" bin are doing the Langer/Kabat-Zinn/Hayes noun problem at scale. Öst 2008, for what it is worth, was grading third-wave *behavior therapies*, with MBCT not the star of that particular guest list. Different reviews, different parties.

For WOW Therapies: you may cite this paper as a historical event on an educational page. You may not turn it into "we offer MBCT" or "mindfulness prevents depression." The first would be a service claim. The second would erase the split.

Living authors: public paper only. Do not scrape conference badges from 2000 to illustrate the table. The table does not need a face.

Ma and Teasdale later published a 2004 replication-ish study that the MBCT literature treats as a second look at the three-or-more finding. I have not rebuilt that table here. I name 2004 so 2000 does not look like a lone shot. Lone shots still matter. They are not a decade.

The sites were in the United Kingdom and Canada — Cambridge, Toronto, and the network those labs could reach. Geography is a limit. A Pine Bluff waiting room is not those sites. Importing a table across an ocean is allowed as education. It is not allowed as a silent protocol.

If you remember one number from this pack, remember two numbers: **145** and **the split**. A single success sentence without the split is how a journal becomes a lid.
