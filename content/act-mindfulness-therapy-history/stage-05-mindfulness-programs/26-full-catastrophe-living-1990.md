**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Delacorte published Jon Kabat-Zinn's *Full Catastrophe Living* in 1990. The title borrowed a line from *Zorba the Greek* — the full catastrophe of a life — and put it on a trade book about a hospital class. Eleven years after the 1979 program, the public had an object it could buy without a referral slip.

Trade books change a clinic's census. People who would never be sent to a Worcester basement could now meet "MBSR" in a bookstore. That is a gift and a leak. The gift: chronic pain and stress became speakable without a guru's robe. The leak: an eight-week, teacher-led, medically situated class became a paperback a person might try alone. This series will not help with the alone.

The book did the secularizing work in long form. It explained sitting and yoga in American medical English. It cited, as such books do, a mix of science and story. I will not audit every citation. I will say: 1990 is the year the hospital object became a mass-culture object. *Wherever You Go, There You Are* (Hyperion, 1994) then made the voice even more portable — shorter, more gift-book, easier to put in a stocking. Two doors, same author: the 1990 door still smells like a clinic; the 1994 door smells like a bedside table.

Reception among Buddhist teachers split, as draft 06 already said. Some were glad. Some saw ethics fall off the truck. Kabat-Zinn's 2011 essay is, in part, his answer about skillful means and maps. Readers can hold the 1990 book and the 2011 essay together and still not have to enroll.

For the ACT timeline, 1990 is weather. Hayes's group was still in the distancing-to-ACT rename. The 1999 Guilford ACT book would arrive in a country that had already learned to say "mindfulness" at a party. That country is Kabat-Zinn's as much as anyone's. ACT did not have to invent the noun. It had to decide what job the noun would do inside a behavior-analytic hour.

I will not excerpt practices. The book is still in print in later editions (Bantam and others). Editions accrete prefaces. Historians should name the 1990 first trade object and not pretend a 2013 printing is the same paper.

For WOW Therapies: a speech or OT clinic that keeps *Full Catastrophe Living* in a waiting room has made a cultural choice, not a service claim — unless someone presents it as treatment. Waiting-room books teach. They also imply. If you would not put a Guilford ACT manual out front, ask why the 1990 trade book feels safer. Safety may be real (it is not a psychotherapy protocol). Implication may still be a problem (it looks like a prescription).

The title's "catastrophe" is the part I still respect. It does not promise a hack. It promises that a life is large and that attention might be one way to stay in it. Promise is not proof. The 1990 object remains a book. Books are allowed to be moving. They are not allowed, on this site, to become classes.

Living author: public books only. Type, not a cover scan unless you have rights.

Later editions added prefaces that answer the McMindfulness years before the coinage. Prefaces are reception. A historian should still cite 1990 as the first trade object, the way we cite 1999 for ACT even after the 2012 cover changed its clothes.

Put 1990 on the timeline between Benson 1975 and Teasdale 2000. A cardiologist's paperback, a hospital teacher's paperback, a psychiatry journal's table. Three secular attention objects. None of them is a sutta. None of them is ACT. All of them taught American English what to say next.
