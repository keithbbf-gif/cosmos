**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Guilford Press published *Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy for Depression: A New Approach to Preventing Relapse* in 2002, by Zindel V. Segal, J. Mark G. Williams, and John D. Teasdale. The 2000 trial's manual now had a hardcover the NHS, a university clinic, or a training program could order. Two years from table to book is fast, and also normal: the 2000 paper had already called the program manualized and "in press."

The subtitle is the census again. Preventing relapse. Not "the new depression cure." A history page that drops the subtitle has started marketing.

I will not outline the eight weeks. Outlining would be a protocol. What I will say about form, as history: MBCT borrowed MBSR's class shape — a weekly group, homework, a day of practice in many deliveries — and bolted cognitive-therapy moves aimed at the relapse loop. Teachers needed training. Groups needed people who were well enough to attend. Those constraints are the opposite of an app.

A second edition arrived in 2013, after more trials and after NICE had named the approach. Second editions are how manuals admit the world moved. They are also how a 2002 object gets quietly replaced on syllabi. Historians should keep the first edition's year on the timeline even when the shelf holds the later one.

Reception: cognitive therapists could hear a sequel to Beck. Buddhist teachers could hear a sequel to Kabat-Zinn. ACBS people could hear a cousin who did not need RFT. All three hearings are partly right. The book is a hybrid object. Hybrids offend purists and help hospitals.

Compared with the 1999 ACT book, the 2002 MBCT book is more of a *course*. ACT's founding hardcover is a therapy to do in an hour, extensible, individual. MBCT's founding hardcover is a group class with a week-by-week spine. Administrators who say "we do mindfulness" may mean either, or neither. Ask which spine.

For WOW Therapies: this is the object to name when a reader asks what MBCT is — a 2002 Guilford manual for a defined relapse-prevention class — and then to refuse to teach. If a later clinical page ever offers the class, that page will need trained teachers, a census that matches the literature, and consent. This folder will still not be that page.

Training pathways grew after 2002: Oxford, Toronto, Bangor, and a diaspora of teacher-training shops that a later editor can list with current URLs if anyone ever staffs a class. This pack will not list them as a directory. Directories become ads. The 2002 book is enough to say *teachers needed training*.

The sister pack's third-wave essay already pointed at 2002. This essay is the book as a thing with a Library of Congress number. Paper again. I keep choosing paper because the internet wants a vibe.

Guilford, again. The same press as Linehan 1993 and Hayes 1999. A historian of American clinical publishing could write a different pack about Guilford's 1990s–2000s bet on manuals a hospital could order. We will only say: three spines, one house, three jobs. Three jobs are enough.

Living authors: public book only. The 2002 jacket art is almost certainly still in copyright. Type the title. Do not "recreate" a cover in a generator.

A note on "in press": the 2000 paper already treated the manual as forthcoming. The 2002 book is that forthcoming object arriving. Arrival is a year. Years keep a syllabus honest.

Put 1999, 2000, and 2002 in a row. An ACT professional book, an MBCT trial, an MBCT professional book. Three years, two schools, one fashionable noun. The noun does not make them one treatment. The paper trail is how you keep them from collapsing in a staff meeting.
