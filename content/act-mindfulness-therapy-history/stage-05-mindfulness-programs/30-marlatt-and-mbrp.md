**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Guilford published G. Alan Marlatt and Judith R. Gordon's edited *Relapse Prevention* in 1985. It is a cognitive-behavioral object about the return of addictive behavior, high-risk situations, and what a person does after a lapse. It is not a cushion book. It is the older spine that mindfulness-based relapse prevention later bolted a class onto.

Marlatt (1941–2011) spent decades at the University of Washington in the psychology of alcohol and other drugs. After his death, Sarah Bowen, Neha Chawla, and colleagues carried a manual into print: *Mindfulness-Based Relapse Prevention for Addictive Behaviors* (Guilford, 2011). The 2011 book is the orderable MBRP object most English-speaking trainers mean. Earlier papers and a research clinic sit behind it; I will not pretend I have listed every trial.

The historical shape should look familiar if you have read essays 25–29. Take an existing behavioral idea (relapse as a process, not a moral verdict). Borrow an eight-week-ish group form from the MBSR/MBCT family. Study it. Argue about effects. Watch the word *mindfulness* do too much work in headlines ("mindfulness treats addiction").

This page will not teach urge surfing, a phrase that has already escaped into popular recovery talk. It will not teach a lapse-and-relapse distinction as homework. Recovery is a place where DIY protocols do extra damage. If you want help with alcohol or other drugs, you need a setting that is actually staffed for that work — not a history blog.

Why include MBRP in an ACT pack at all?

Because popular "third wave" maps sometimes swallow it, and because ACT itself developed substance-use protocols (Hayes's group and others) that are *not* MBRP. Two schools can share a census and a fashionable noun and still be different manuals. A clinic that says "we do mindfulness for addiction" has not told you which spine, which trial, or which staffing.

Marlatt's older 1985 book also matters as a reminder that relapse prevention did not wait for a zafu. The cushion arrived later, as a hypothesized way to meet urges without the old automatic sequence. Hypothesis, then a 2011 manual. The sequence is the MBCT sequence with a different census.

I have not held every MBRP RCT. Later reviews exist and disagree in the ordinary way. This essay's job is the objects and the refusal: 1985, 2011, a University of Washington corridor, a noun that leaks.

Living coauthors of the 2011 manual: public book only. Marlatt's death in 2011 is a date on a title page, not a license to invent a deathbed scene. The book is the object.

For WOW Therapies: the live site's adult page, when last fetched, mentioned swallowing, voice, and cognitive-communication after neurological change — not addiction treatment. Do not let this educational essay become a quiet service expansion. If someone lands here from a search for "mindfulness relapse," the honest sentence is: a 2011 Guilford manual exists; this practice is not, by writing about it, offering it.

Marlatt's harm-reduction public voice — the idea that a lapse is information, not a moral collapse — predates the cushion and still offends abstinence-only rooms. That fight is older than MBRP. This page will not referee it. It will say: when "mindfulness" entered relapse prevention, it entered a field that already had a politics.

The sister pack's trauma and behaviorism essays already warned that a despised census (the "difficult" patient; the person who uses) attracts both neglect and faddish rescue. MBRP can be serious work in a serious clinic. On a blog it becomes a slogan. We will keep the book year and drop the slogan. Years are heavier than slogans. They sit still. 1985 and 2011 are enough furniture for this hallway.
