**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 1995, *Behaviour Research and Therapy* published Teasdale, Segal, and Williams on a dry question: how does cognitive therapy prevent depressive relapse, and why might attentional control — they put "mindfulness" in parentheses — help? The paper is a theory object, not a trial. It sits five years before their 2000 JCCP study and seven before the 2002 Guilford book.

Zindel Segal, J. Mark G. Williams, and John D. Teasdale were cognitive therapists. They had worked in the Beck tradition of thoughts, schemas, and relapse. They were not opening a zendo. They were looking at a clinical nuisance: people who had already learned the thought record, got well, and then got sick again when a sad mood returned and old loops started with less effort. Kindling, episode sensitization — they and others wrote in that register in the mid-1990s.

The 1995 wager, in my paraphrase of a paper you can still read, was that what protects after cognitive therapy might be a *relationship to* thoughts, not only a new set of contents, and that training attention might stabilize that relationship when mood dipped. I am describing their question. I am not assigning a practice.

This is the bridge the sister pack's third-wave essay named in a paragraph. This pack can afford the slower version. MBCT is not "Buddhism plus Beck." It is a relapse-prevention idea that borrowed MBSR's classroom form and a convert-Buddhist vocabulary of attending, because those were the tools on the English-speaking table in the 1990s. Kabat-Zinn's class already existed. IMS already existed. Cognitive therapy already existed. The three men tried to bolt the first to the third for a specific census: people *between* episodes.

That last clause is the one popular summaries drop. MBCT, in its founding documents, is not a treatment for someone in a raging first depression. It is a program for people who have been depressed more than once and are currently well enough to take a class. The 2000 trial's split — help reported for three-or-more prior episodes, not for two — made the census even more specific. Specificity is the opposite of a yogurt lid.

Why a cushion rather than another thought record? Because they thought the record was not what failed at 2 a.m. What failed, in their model, was the automaticity of the loop once mood provided a cue. Attention training was a hypothesized way to notice the cue without boarding the train. Hypothesis. Later trials and NICE's 2009 mention are later objects. This essay is the 1995 question.

Williams later wrote, with Teasdale, Segal, and Kabat-Zinn, *The Mindful Way Through Depression* (2007), a trade door. Same caution as other trade doors: not a trial, not a class, not this site's homework.

Living people: public papers only.

For WOW Therapies: if someone says "mindfulness for depression," ask which object. Langer 1989? Benson 1975? MBSR 1979/1990? MBCT 1995/2000/2002? ACT 1999? Those are five jobs. A speech-language clinic that works on cognitive-communication after stroke is in a sixth job and should not steal any of the five as décor.

Their mid-1990s papers on kindling and episode sensitization (including work with Michael Gemar) are the cognitive-science hallway behind the 1995 question. I name the hallway. I will not teach a model of recurrence. The hallway is why they did not need a conversion story to want a cushion.

The three therapists wanted a cushion because they wanted to stop a relapse they had already explained in cognitive terms. That is a modest, almost conservative origin. It is more interesting than a conversion story. It also explains why Buddhist teachers could feel both used and correctly cited. The citation was instrumental. The instrument was a class. The class had a census. The census was not a sangha.
