Marsha M. Linehan's 1991 *Archives of General Psychiatry* trial and 1993 Guilford book are already told, as a life and as a staffing pattern, in the sister pack's figure essay. Read that first if you want Tulsa, the 2011 *New York Times* interview she chose, and the consultation team. This page will not steal those paragraphs.

The angle that belongs here is narrower: she put a Zen-inflected attention discipline inside a behavior-therapy hospital object, and she did it before "third wave" was a 2004 title. Mindfulness in DBT is not MBSR's eight-week medical class and not ACT's hexaflex list. It is one piece of a four-part structure (individual therapy, skills group, team, between-session contact) aimed at a census other rooms had filed under impossible.

Zen, in her public telling, was a discipline of attention, not décor. Behaviorism was a question about what an act *does*. The combination offended people who wanted only a warm bath or only a token economy. It also produced a generation of clinicians who could stay in a room with chronic suicidality without becoming the rescuer or the punisher — some of the time. "Some of the time" is the honest tense.

This series will not list modules. TikTok already leaked the vocabulary. A clinic website that reprints "wise mind" homework would be a copyright problem and a safety problem. The sister pack said so. We say so again because this pack's readers will come looking for exactly that leak.

Öst 2008 graded DBT trials alongside ACT trials and found moderate effects and methods he judged less stringent than a CBT comparison set. Gaudiano 2009 argued Öst's comparison was apples and oranges, at least for ACT. The DBT half of Öst's paper is its own argument. Linehan's 1991 object remains a small, specific RCT, later extended and adapted. "DBT works" without a team is the brand problem the sister essay named.

Why keep her in an ACT/mindfulness pack if the life lives next door?

Because popular maps treat DBT, ACT, and MBCT as three flavors of one ice cream. They share a decade, a Guilford habit, and a word. They do not share a census, a staffing pattern, or a theory of language. Linehan's mindfulness is in the service of staying alive and staying in treatment. ACT's present-moment talk is in the service of a values-and-verbal-control model. MBCT's attending is in the service of not boarding a relapse train. Three jobs. One English noun.

Living person: public science and the 2011 interview only. No embroidery. No workshop photograph. No generated likeness. The sister portrait row already said type. We say type again.

For WOW Therapies: do not put Linehan on a speech-therapy page as if chronic suicidality were in the appointment book. Do not put a hexagon next to her name. If you mention her here, mention the 1991–1993 objects and the refusal to reprint skills.

Behavioral Tech and later training companies made DBT a workforce the way ACBS made ACT one. The workforce problem is shared: "informed" without a team. This pack's job is not to retell that company history. It is to keep the attention piece from floating free of the 1993 structure.

The hinge, as the sister third-wave essay said, is still hers: a despised diagnosis, a researchable treatment, a reminder that "evidence-based" can come from someone the old wards had given up on. This pack adds only: the attention piece was not a spa, and it does not license a blog to teach it. A spa sells calm. A 1993 book sold a team. Buy the right object or buy none. This folder buys neither. It names both.
