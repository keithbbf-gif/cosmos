**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 2004 and 2005, ACT Summer Institutes met in Reno and Philadelphia. ACBS's organizational history names those meetings as part of the de facto organization that then needed bylaws. A summer institute is a workshop with a nicer noun. By the 2010s the nicer nouns had multiplied: boot camps, advanced intensives, online cohorts, peer-reviewed trainer lists, books that taught the books.

A school becomes a workforce when it can be taught in a weekend and billed to an employer. That is not a slur. Medicine does this. DBT did this through Behavioral Tech and later companies. MBSR did this through the Center for Mindfulness and a diaspora of teacher-training pathways. ACT did this through ACBS, New Harbinger trade books, Guilford professional books, and a long tail of independent trainers.

Workshops are how ideas leave a lab. They are also how ideas thin. The 1999 book is slower than a Friday. RFT is slower than a Friday. Supervision is slower than a Friday. A clinician who "does ACT" after one Friday is speaking a dialect the 2004 *Behavior Therapy* essay would have called a hope, not a generation.

Money sits on the table. I do not have ACBS's ledgers. I can say the obvious: dues, conference fees, hotel blocks, trainer day-rates, and a popular book market (*Get Out of Your Mind*, 2005; Harris, 2007) built a livelihood structure. Livelihoods are allowed. Hidden livelihoods that pretend to be only a calling are how cults talk. This school is not a cult in the technical sense; it is a professional community with the ordinary vanities. Calling it a cult is a different family's insult, and this pack will not use it as a sneer. Calling it immune to money would be a different lie.

Peer-reviewed trainer status, as ACBS developed it, is an attempt to put a fence inside the workforce: some people are recognized to teach. Fences help. They also create insiders. A small-city clinician in Arkansas will meet the fence as a website and a fee. That is information, not a command to join.

Russ Harris's *The Happiness Trap* (2007) deserves a careful sentence. It is a popularizer, widely translated, often the first object a civilian holds. It is not a trial. It is not the 1999 book. A history series may name it as the airport door. It may not assign the exercises. Popularizers keep schools alive. They also set the tone the lab then has to live with — friendlier, simpler, easier to parody.

For WOW Therapies, the workshop economy is the most likely way this vocabulary arrives in Southeast Arkansas: a continuing-education Friday, a book, a supervisor who once sat in Philadelphia. That can be a good Friday. It does not create a service line. If the practice ever advertises ACT, the honest page will name training, supervision, and what it will *not* treat. This folder is not that page.

The sister pack said hospitals that buy "DBT" and cut the team have bought a word. The ACT version: clinics that buy a hexagon poster and skip supervision have bought a word.

I will not list current trainers. Lists go stale and become ads. The 2004–2005 institutes and the 2005 membership opening are enough dated furniture.

New Harbinger, Guilford, and a long tail of self-published workbooks made a print economy beside the hotel economy. Print is slower and still thins. A workbook that fits an airplane tray is a historical object. It is not the 1999 professional book. Keep the doors labeled.

If you are a clinician reading this for CE nostalgia, you already know the coffee and the badges. If you are a civilian, know that the badge is not a license and the coffee is not a trial. The workforce is real. The thinning is real. Both can be true in the same hotel.
