Sometime in the 2000s, trainers began drawing a six-part picture — later nicknamed the hexaflex — to show processes the 1999 book had discussed in chapters: acceptance, defusion, present-moment contact, self-as-context, values, committed action. I am not going to draw it. I am not going to define each point as if this were a slide deck. The picture is the historical object. The object escaped.

Diagrams do a job in a hotel ballroom. They give a tired audience a shape. They also become tattoos, Instagram cogs, and "which piece are you stuck on?" quizzes. A teaching picture is not a personality test. Once it behaves like one, the school has a merchandising problem.

I do not have a first-use citation I trust for the word *hexaflex*. Secondary workshop lore treats it as mid-2000s training slang. If a later editor finds a first printed figure with a date, upgrade this paragraph. Until then, the honest sentence is: the six-process list is in the professional literature; the cute name is oral culture that hardened.

Why refuse to reprint the figure?

Because a figure on a consumer website *functions* as a worksheet. People score themselves. People skip a clinician. People think they have "done ACT." The sister pack refused Linehan's modules for the same reason. Copyright is a second reason; Guilford and training companies do not exist to decorate our blog.

What a history page *can* say about the picture:

It is a descendant of the 1999 book's organization, not a seventh sacrament. It is a trainer's convenience, not a finding. It can make a rich, messy therapy look like a dashboard. Dashboards please administrators. They please people who like to be complete. They betray anyone whose life does not have six compartments.

Other schools have the same problem. CBT has the triangle. Psychodynamics has the triangle of a different kind. DBT has a hierarchy of targets that TikTok turned into a personality. Pictures travel faster than supervision.

For WOW Therapies, the temptation will be graphic. A designer will want a hexagon. The answer is no, unless the page is behind a training login this practice does not currently claim. Type is enough. A timeline of *books* is enough. A six-part cog would turn this educational series into a knockoff manual.

There is a kinder reading. Some clinicians use the picture the way a musician uses a circle of fifths: a reminder, not a cage. Those clinicians are not the problem. The problem is the public page that cannot tell a reminder from a protocol.

I will also say the quiet thing about process lists. Once you have six processes, you can write six research questionnaires, six chapters, six workshop hours. The list starts to govern the science. That can be fertile (you measure what you named). It can be circular (you find what you listed). Methodologists argue this in every school. ACT is not special. ACT is visible because the picture is so drawable.

Living trainers: no headshots, no "official hexaflex colorways." If ACBS or a publisher has a licensed figure, that is their property.

The sister pack allowed original diagrams of *schools* and *societies*, not of patient homework. Same rule. A map of Worcester–Barre–Reno–London–Almería is history. A cog that asks you to rate your defusion is a session.

If a later training company has trademarked a colorway, that is their business. It is not a reason for wowtherapies.com to borrow the cog "for education." Education about a picture is done with words: *there is a six-part teaching figure; we will not show it here.* That sentence is enough.

If this essay feels like it is wagging a finger at a shape, it is. Shapes have done a lot of damage in popular psychology. We can live without this one on the public site.
