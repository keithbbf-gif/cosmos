**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Steven C. Hayes is living as of last verification (2026-09-14). This essay uses only what he and his coauthors have put in public: papers, books, the ACBS pages that tell the lab's age. No health speculation. No origin-story cartoon.

The 1999 Guilford volume made him, with Strosahl and Wilson, a library heading. The work before that is how you avoid the cartoon of a man who invented a therapy in one hardcover.

Hayes trained as a behavior analyst and spent the 1980s writing about rule-governed behavior, stimulus equivalence, and a clinical method then called comprehensive distancing. The 1987 chapter "A contextual approach to therapeutic change," in Neil Jacobson's *Psychotherapists in Clinical Practice*, is one of the early book-form statements. The 1994 *Behavior Analyst* paper with Wilson is the name-change document. Between them sit conference titles, a delayed analogue study, and Zettle's 1986 window on reason-giving.

He also worked in a discipline that was losing prestige in many psychology departments. Behavior analysis kept a hold in applied developmental work and in a few universities (Nevada, among others, later became a visible ACT address). A school that grows from a minority corridor inside clinical psychology will sound, to CBT majorities, like a sect, and to behavior-analytic purists, like a compromise with feelings. Hayes's public writing has spent decades talking to both rooms. That bilingualism is not a personality note. It is an institutional fact.

The ACBS "How old is ACT?" page, in his voice as the site presents it, says he wrote a first protocol in 1981 and held back some outcome publication because the model was not ready. Readers may believe him. Readers may hear a man burnishing delay. A history series prints the claim and the later 1999 *Psychological Record* pain-rationale paper as the object he says was late. It does not psychoanalyze the author.

September 11, 2001, appears on the ACBS organizational-history page as the "spark" he later told as a story about listservs and a first world conference. I will not embroider grief. I will say: the association that formed in 2005 explains itself, in part, as a response to that week. Institutions choose their founding traumas. This one is on their website.

*Get Out of Your Mind and Into Your Life* (New Harbinger, 2005) is a different object from the 1999 professional book: a trade workbook voice, widely sold. This series will not assign it. Its existence is how a lab school met airport bookstores. Popularization is a historical event. It is also how careful process language becomes slogans.

Living-person rule, again, because founder essays leak: no gossip about family, health, or private practice. If a later journalist writes a profile, cite the profile as a profile. Do not upgrade it to a file.

For WOW Therapies, the usable caution is ordinary. A clinic that says "we follow Hayes" is saying something as vague as "we follow Freud." Which decade? Which book? Which training? The man is a bibliography, not a mascot.

The sister pack did not give him a figure essay. This pack will not invent a full biography either. One essay on the pre-1999 paper trail, one on the 1999 book, one on the society — that is enough founder. The school is larger than a surname. Strosahl, Wilson, Zettle, Walser, Luciano, the Barnes-Holmes lab, Twohig, and a lot of unnamed clinicians did the carrying. Surname history is a bad habit we inherited from psychoanalysis. We can do less of it and still be accurate.

If you want a method, you will not find it under his name here. If you want the paper trail before the bookstore, you have it: 1981 (claimed protocol), 1982 (conference title), 1986 (Zettle & Hayes), 1987 (chapter), 1991 (name in a talk), 1994 (name in a journal), 1999 (book and late analogue paper). That is a career, not a launch.
