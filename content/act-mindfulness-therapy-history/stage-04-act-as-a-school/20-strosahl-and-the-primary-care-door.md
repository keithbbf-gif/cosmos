**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Kirk Strosahl is the second name on the 1999 title page and a member of the August 2005 ACBS planning committee, listed then with Mountainview Consulting Group. He is living as of last verification. Public books and the association's own committee list only.

If Hayes is the lab-and-theory surname, Strosahl is the surname that kept ACT from remaining a university dialect. His public work has long sat at the meeting of behavior therapy and *primary care* — the fifteen-minute door, the family-medicine clinic, the problem of people who will never attend a twelve-session specialty hour. Focused acceptance and commitment therapy (FACT), later popularized in trainings and books with Patricia Robinson and others, is a child of that door. This page will not teach a brief protocol. It will say: the school grew a short form because the building it wanted to enter already had a clock.

Primary care is not a romance. It is where most distress shows up and where most psychotherapy never happens. A therapy school that cannot speak to a medical director in a community health center will remain a workshop. Strosahl's career, as the publication list and trainings show it, was in part the work of translation: contextual behavioral talk into something a physician could refer to on a Tuesday.

Translation has costs. Brevity can become a gimmick. "ACT in ten minutes" is a phrase that should make a historian wince even when the authors are trying to be useful. Usefulness and thinning share a hallway. This series will not decide how thin is too thin. It will note that the thinning is a historical response to American clinic time, not a discovery about the soul.

Strosahl's presence on the 1999 book also means ACT's founding object was never only Reno. It was already a collaboration between a university theorist, a values-minded academic (Wilson), and a man who worked the medical door. Schools that later look like one guru usually look, in their first hardcover, like a small firm.

I will not invent a biography. I have not written a life. Mountainview Consulting Group is a business name on a committee list. Businesses sell training. That is allowed. A history page should not become a vendor page. If you want his books, a library catalog exists.

For WOW Therapies — a small-city practice brand, not a medical-school department — the Strosahl door is the relevant temptation. It is easy to hear "primary care ACT" as "we can add this without staffing anything." Primary care behavioral health, when it is real, is a job with supervision, a clinic flow, and a medical home. When it is fake, it is a poster in a waiting room. This pack will not help a poster.

The sister pack's counseling-profession essay already warned that a title on a door is not a workforce. Same warning, different school.

Living-person rule: no gossip. No "he is the practical one" psychology of surnames beyond the public division of labor on a title page and a committee list. The title page is already a division of labor. We do not need a parlor game.

Robinson, Reiter, and others in the primary-care behavioral-health literature — a neighboring stream — already argued that the medical door needs its own job description. FACT sits near that stream. Near is not identical. This pack will not merge them into "brief therapy."

If Stage 4 has a moral that is not a protocol, it is this: a therapy becomes a school when it can live in more than one building. Strosahl named one of the buildings. The building has fluorescent lights. It is not a zendo. It is not a Guilford preface. It is the place most people actually go when they are not sleeping.
