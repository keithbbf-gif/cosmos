**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Kelly G. Wilson is the third name on the 1999 title page, a coauthor of the 1991 Atlanta talk that first put "acceptance and commitment therapy" in a heading, and a 2005 ACBS planning-committee member listed with the University of Mississippi. He is living as of last verification. Public books, talks, and that committee list only.

If the school had shipped without "values," it would have sounded like a clever way to stop fighting thoughts. Values are how it claimed a life. Wilson's public teaching — interviews, conference hours, later books — has often been the place English-speaking students heard that ACT was not about feeling better as a trophy. It was about a direction. I am reporting a school's sentence, not handing you a compass.

Values language is older than 1991. Existential therapists (the sister pack's Frankl and Yalom essays) already treated meaning as clinical. Humanistic work already treated a person's choosing. Religious traditions already had vocations. ACT's bet was to put values *inside* a behavior-analytic account: verbally constructed consequences that can organize action when immediate consequences punish it. That is a technical sentence. It is also how a lab school borrowed a humanistic noun without joining a humanistic guild.

The danger is obvious and has already happened. Values become a card sort, a corporate poster, a way to tell a woman her "value" is endurance. The 1999 book is not a poster. The weekend that follows the book can be. This page will not print a sort. It will say: once you put "values" on a workshop banner, you have hired a word that oppressors also use.

Wilson has been, in public, more willing than some colleagues to talk about suffering without a grin. I will not turn that tone into a biography. Tone is not a file. I mention it because reception history includes *how a school sounded* in the room, and he was one of the people who set the sound.

The 1991 talk title, "Change in Acceptance and Commitment Therapy," already pairs his name with the brand. Naming is not a footnote. Students who think Hayes invented the title alone are doing surname history again.

For WOW Therapies: "we help clients clarify values" is a sentence that can appear on a speech-language or counseling page with no ACT training behind it. The sentence is not owned by this school. If you use it, you are in a crowded corridor with existentialists, clergy, and guidance counselors. History's job is to keep the corridor crowded so a brand cannot rope it off.

This series will not ask you what you value. That question, asked by a stranger in a blog, is either therapy or advertising. We are not licensed for the first and we have refused the second.

Living-person rule: no health speculation. Wilson has spoken, in some public forums, about his own history; I will not embroider or even summarize those stories here unless a later editor decides a specific, dated, chosen disclosure belongs — and then only as he chose to tell it, the way the sister pack treated Linehan's 2011 *Times* interview. Default is silence.

Wilson later wrote, with others, books that put values in the subtitle hard enough for a catalog to notice. I will not list them as a shop. The 1999 collaboration remains the object this pack treats as founding. Later books are weather.

The values sentence remains the part of ACT that non-behaviorists hear as human. That is why it travels. That is why it gets cheap. Essay 38 (*When Acceptance Sounds Like a Muzzle*) returns to the cheap version. This essay only needs the 1991–1999 fact: a third author, a noun, a school that did not want to be only about not-struggling.
