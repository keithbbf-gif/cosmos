**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In August 2005 a planning committee formed to turn a de facto ACT/RFT community into an association. Membership opened in October 2005. The organization incorporated in 2006. An election in April 2006 produced a first board, due to meet at the Second World Conference in London in July 2006. I am quoting the timeline the Association for Contextual Behavioral Science publishes about itself.

Before the bylaws there were listservs (the site says they began in 2003), a World Conference in Sweden in 2003, and ACT Summer Institutes in Reno (2004) and Philadelphia (2005). The site says the spark for the listservs and first world conference was September 11, 2001, and that Steven Hayes tells that story elsewhere on the site. I will not retell a trauma as a mascot. I will say: this is the origin the society chose to print.

The August 2005 committee list is a map of the school at the moment it became a membership organization:

Dermot Barnes-Holmes and Yvonne Barnes-Holmes (Maynooth); Sonja V. Batten (VA Maryland / Maryland); Anthony Biglan (Oregon Research Institute); Frank W. Bond (Goldsmiths); Joseph Ciarrochi (Wollongong); JoAnne Dahl (Uppsala); Eric J. Fox (Western Michigan); Steven C. Hayes (Nevada); Carmen Luciano (Almería); Ian Stewart (Galway); Kirk Strosahl (Mountainview); Niklas Törneke (Swedish psychiatrist in private practice); Robyn D. Walser (National Center for PTSD, VA Palo Alto); Kelly G. Wilson (Mississippi).

Read the list as geography and as institutions. Ireland, Spain, Sweden, the United Kingdom, Australia, U.S. universities, a VA PTSD center, a consulting group, an Oregon prevention-science shop. This was not a single-campus sect on the day it wrote bylaws. It was already a small international firm.

Names matter for another reason. Walser's VA affiliation tells you the school wanted trauma-adjacent rooms without being "a trauma therapy." Biglan tells you some of the energy was prevention and cultural design, a stream this pack will not follow into policy. Törneke tells you psychiatrists, not only psychologists, were in the room. Luciano tells you a Spanish-language research culture was not an afterthought.

The name they chose — contextual behavioral science, not "ACT Society" — was a bet that the research program would outlive a therapy brand. Bets fail sometimes. As of the 2010s the public still said ACT more often than CBS. The *Journal of Contextual Behavioral Science* (2012 launch, as the field generally dates it) was another brick in the bet. I have not audited every issue. I name the journal as an organ.

A society is a workforce. Workforces collect dues, hold conferences, argue about who is in, and dilute. "ACT-informed" is the dilution phrase, the cousin of "DBT-informed." This series will keep saying that a weekend is not a consultation team and not a research clinic.

For WOW Therapies: ACBS is not an accrediting board for Arkansas licenses. Membership is not a credential you should put next to ASHA or a state counseling board. If a later staff page ever lists "ACBS member," treat it as a society membership, like belonging to a historical association. The public deserves that smallness.

Living people on the 2005 list: public roles only. No gossip.

The site's 2005 list is also a gender and role snapshot: Batten, Dahl, Luciano, Walser, Yvonne Barnes-Holmes on a committee that could have been only surnames from Nevada. It was not. A later editor who wants a "diversity paragraph" should look at this list instead of writing one. The list is better than a paragraph.

If you want a one-line caption: **2003 listservs and Sweden → 2004–05 institutes → August 2005 committee → October 2005 members → 2006 incorporation and London board.** A school became a 501-shaped thing (the site lists a Michigan P.O. box in the page footer I fetched). Things with P.O. boxes can be studied. They should not be worshipped.
