**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

The *Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta* (Majjhima Nikāya 10; a longer cousin is Dīgha Nikāya 22) is a Pali canon discourse on the establishments of mindfulness. It is a text. It has reciters, commentators, modern translators, and arguments about what a word meant in a monastic culture that is not Worcester and not Reno.

I put that dull sentence first because English-language therapy culture likes to treat "the Buddha said" as a footnote that blesses an eight-week class. Blessing is not a historical method. A sutta is not a syllabus.

What a general reader can usefully know, without a crash course I am not qualified to give:

The discourse describes attending to body, feeling-tone, mind, and mental objects, in a frame of being "ardent, clearly knowing, and mindful," putting away longing and discontent. That paraphrase is already an interpretation. Bhikkhu Ñāṇamoli, Bhikkhu Bodhi, Anālayo, and a century of earlier European philologists do not agree on every gloss. Anālayo's early-2000s monograph *Satipaṭṭhāna: The Direct Path to Realization* is one widely cited modern study; it is a scholarly object, not a clinic manual, and this page will not extract exercises from it.

Theravāda commentarial tradition (Buddhaghosa's *Visuddhimagga*, fifth century CE in the usual dating) organized meditation into a huge technical literature: forty subjects, stages, dangers, the difference between calm and insight. Convert American teachers in the 1970s inherited a *simplified* slice of that literature, often through Burmese and Thai lineages (Mahasi Sayadaw; Ajahn Chah) that had themselves been modernizing and exporting. "Insight meditation" as taught at IMS is a twentieth-century pedagogical form with Asian teachers and American organizers. It is downstream of texts. It is not the text.

Why does a WOW Therapies history pack mention a Pali discourse at all?

Because Kabat-Zinn, Goldstein, Salzberg, Kornfield, and a generation of clinicians cited "mindfulness" as if the English word were a transparent pane. The pane is stained. *Sati* in Pali is related to memory, recollection, keeping-in-mind — not to a spa adjective. Scholars have spent careers on that. A therapy blog that says "mindfulness just means paying attention on purpose" has performed a translation and hidden the labor.

Kabat-Zinn has been more candid than the blogs. In his 2011 *Contemporary Buddhism* essay he wrote about skillful means and "the trouble with maps": how to offer practices in a hospital without requiring Buddhist identity, and what is lost when the ethical frame drops away. That essay is one of the public documents this pack trusts. It is also a primary source by an interested party. Read it as a founder explaining himself, not as the last word on Buddhism.

Buddhist teachers have had opinions about the borrowing. Some are grateful that dukkha is discussable in a medical center. Some see a technique stripped of *sīla* (ethics), of refuge, of a view of liberation. Both opinions can be true about different rooms. A counseling history can admit the debt without pretending a county-clinic group is a sangha, and without scolding every nurse who ever used the word.

This page will not:
- teach a satipaṭṭhāna sequence;
- decide which translation is "the real" one;
- declare that MBSR is or is not Buddhism;
- declare that ACT is crypto-Buddhism (it is a behavior-analytic school that later used some of the same English).

This page will say: when a 1999 or 2002 Guilford book says "mindfulness," a literate reader is allowed to ask *which archive that word passed through*. The archive includes MN 10. It also includes 1970s American convert Buddhism, 1950s–70s Burmese and Thai export lineages, and a hospital's need for a secular billable noun. Those are different shelves.

If you want to read the sutta, read a named translation and a named commentary, knowing you are reading a translation. If you want a clinician, see a clinician. If you want this series to become a dharma school, it will refuse. The refusal is the point of Stage 2: keep the textual object textual so later essays can talk about clinics without stealing a canon.

I have not read the Pali. I am saying that out loud. Secondary English sources and the public statements of modern teachers are what I have. A pack that pretends otherwise would be doing the thing it accuses the yogurt lid of doing: wearing a borrowed antiquity.
