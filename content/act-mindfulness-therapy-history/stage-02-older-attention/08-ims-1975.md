**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 1975, Joseph Goldstein, Sharon Salzberg, and Jack Kornfield — young teachers who had been sitting and teaching after time in Asia — decided to found a retreat center. On 14 February 1976 they opened the Insight Meditation Society in a former Catholic novitiate at 1230 Pleasant Street, Barre, Massachusetts. The town motto on the common, the society's own history page says, read "Tranquil and Alert." They took it as a sign. Historians are allowed to smile and still keep the deed.

The purchase price circulating in that institutional history is $150,000, raised from friends and supporters. I have not audited the closing documents. I am reporting the story IMS tells about itself. A self-telling is a source. It is not a notary.

Why Barre matters to a therapy-history pack is painfully concrete. In the spring of 1979, Jon Kabat-Zinn sat a two-week vipassanā retreat there. About day ten, in a dormitory room he later identified with the Catskills / Karuna House wing, he had what he called a "vision" lasting perhaps ten seconds: a hospital program, a long tail of research, livelihoods for teachers. He wrote that account in 2011 in *Contemporary Buddhism*. He also wrote that he had hardly mentioned the experience because it was weird. Founders' weird hours become origin myths. This one at least has a date, a building, and a later essay.

IMS was not a clinic. It was a convert Buddhist retreat center in a Christian building, teaching a Burmese- and Thai-inflected insight style to people who could take two weeks off. That last clause is already class history. Retreat culture selects for people with time, money, or a desperation that looks like time. The patients who later filled Kabat-Zinn's referral stream were a different census: chronic pain, hospital politics, people who would never sew a robe.

Goldstein's *The Experience of Insight* (1976) and later *Insight Meditation*; Salzberg's *Lovingkindness*; Kornfield's *A Path with Heart* (1993) and the Spirit Rock project in California — these are public objects that carried the Barre style into bookstores. Spirit Rock (planning through the 1980s, land and buildings later) is the West Coast sibling, not a copy, and not this page's job to tour. The point is a workforce of English-speaking lay teachers who made "insight" a weekend vocabulary before MBCT existed.

The Dalai Lama visited IMS in 1979, the society says, and rolled a ball down the old bowling alley left from the novitiate years. I like the image and I do not need it to prove anything about therapy. It proves that a Tibetan head of a school and a New England convert center could share a building for an afternoon. Sharing a building is not sharing a doctrine.

Ethics: American insight communities, like zendos, have had teacher misconduct and public reckonings. A history that only prints the motto on the common is a brochure. This page will not catalog grievances I have not sourced. It will not pretend the rooms were only tranquil and alert.

For ACT, IMS is an upstream weather system, not a parent. Hayes's school grew from rule-governed behavior and a 1981 protocol nickname, not from Barre. The weather matters because by the 1990s a behavior therapist could say "mindfulness" and be understood in a hospital without saying "vipassanā." IMS and its sibling centers are a large part of why. So is Kabat-Zinn. So is a century of Orientalist paperback Zen. Weather has more than one front.

This page will not describe a sitting day. It will not tell you how to go on retreat. Retreats harm some people, help some people, and bore some people. None of that is a finding I am licensed to apply to you.

What I want a WOW Therapies reader to leave with is a map pin. Barre, 1976. A deed. A motto. A 1979 retreat that a founder later wrote down. The rest of Stage 5 happens in Worcester. The rest of Stage 4 happens in behavior-analysis conference hotels. The pin keeps those later rooms from claiming they invented the cushion.

Living teachers: Goldstein, Salzberg, Kornfield were alive at last verification. Public books and the IMS history page only. No gossip. Type, not portraits.
