**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In September 1979, a molecular biologist turned hospital teacher opened an outpatient program in Worcester, Massachusetts, and eventually taught the English-speaking medical world to say *mindfulness* as if the word had been waiting in the corridor. The program was new. The sitting was not.

I start here because the clinic origin story is a magnet. It pulls every older practice into a preface. Monks become "early MBSR." A sutta becomes "the first protocol." A Japanese psychiatrist becomes "ACT before ACT." That flattening is the aisle's trick in clerical clothing. This pack will name debts. It will not let a 1979 basement own the history of attention.

Human beings have trained attention for reasons that do not need a DSM code: liturgy, craft, hunting, military drill, grief, scholarship, love. A Benedictine office, a Qur'anic recitation school, a tea procedure, a piano étude — each is a technology of showing up. A historian who only notices the Buddhist ones is already doing the late-century American story, in which "mindfulness" became the export brand for a much older set of disciplines.

Even inside Buddhist Asia the practices are not one thing. Theravāda commentarial traditions, Chan/Zen monastic codes, Pure Land recitation, Tibetan ngöndro — these are different rooms with different teachers, vows, and ideas about what a mind is *for*. A county-clinic group that borrows a raisin exercise has not inherited them. Borrowing can be honest. Inheritance is a legal word.

The Western medical story has its own pre-1979 objects that are not Buddhism and not ACT. Mesmerism and later hypnosis made attention a clinical instrument and then a scandal; the sister pack's talking-cure essay owns that corridor. Edmund Jacobson's progressive relaxation (book 1929) taught physicians to treat muscle as something a person could learn. Autogenic training (Schultz) did adjacent work in German-speaking medicine. Herbert Benson, in 1975, named a "relaxation response" in a cardiology frame after studying practitioners of Transcendental Meditation. Those are medical-behavioral objects. They are not a sangha, and they are not a hexaflex.

Why does the distinction matter on a WOW Therapies history page?

Because a reader who is in pain will hear "older than a clinic" as "therefore natural, therefore safe, therefore yours." Age is not a safety certificate. Fasting is old. So is flagellation. So is a retreat that breaks a person who needed a hospital instead. This series will not romanticize premodern discipline to sell a modern hour. It will also not sneer at premodern discipline to sell a modern brand.

The honest sentence is duller. By the time American hospitals wanted a secular, billable, eight-week attention class, several streams were already in the building: Catholic and Protestant contemplative revivals; Jewish musar and later mindfulness-adjacent teachers; immigrant Buddhist communities; convert zendos; TM's medical papers; the human-potential circuit. Kabat-Zinn's originality — and it is real — was to build a *hospital object* that could be referred to, studied, and copied without requiring a conversion. Originality is not invention from nothing.

ACT's originality is a different originality. It came out of behavior analysis and a quarrel about language, not out of a Worcester referral pad. When ACT writers later said "mindfulness," they were often pointing at a functional idea — contact with the present, defusion from literal talk — that could be trained many ways. Some of those ways looked like meditation. Some looked like talking. A history that makes Hayes a secret Buddhist, or Kabat-Zinn a secret behaviorist, is writing fan fiction.

I am not competent to write the history of attention in India, China, Korea, Japan, Tibet, or Southeast Asia as if I had the languages. I will point at public English-language objects and mark the thinness. That is better than a paragraph titled "Eastern wisdom" with a lotus emoji behind it.

What I can say without leaving this desk: when a twenty-first-century clinic says "we use mindfulness," the word is doing at least four jobs — a Buddhist technical term in translation; a 1979 medical brand; a 1989 social-psychology construct (Langer); and a 2000s therapy-school flavor. Those jobs fight. Stage 2 of this pack pulls them apart far enough that later stages can talk about ACT and MBCT without stealing a monastery.

If you came here for a lineage chart that makes your favorite teacher the ancestor of your favorite manual, you will be disappointed. Lineage charts are how schools keep property. History is how property gets argued. Attention is older than a clinic. A clinic is allowed to be new anyway.
