Shōma Morita (1874–1938) published *Shinkeishitsu no hontai to ryōhō* in 1921, after clinical work often dated from 1919. He described a treatment for a cluster of anxious, hypochondriacal, socially frightened presentations then gathered under *shinkeishitsu*: rest, then work, then a stance later summarized as *arugamama* — as-it-is-ness — rather than a war on symptoms. The sister pack already holds a figure essay. This page will not steal it.

I reopen the door because Stefan Hofmann, in 2008, titled a commentary "Acceptance and Commitment Therapy: New Wave or Morita Therapy?" The barb has a real question inside it. A reader who meets ACT's acceptance talk and then meets Morita's refusal to wrestle a symptom can hear a family resemblance. Family resemblance is not identity. It is not a theft charge, and it is not a baptism.

Morita worked in Taishō and early Shōwa Japan, in a medical culture that had already imported German psychiatry and was arguing with it. His hospital was not Guilford Press. His patients were not undergraduates in an analogue pain study. A 1999 American book can *rhyme* with a 1921 Japanese book without being its translation. Translation is exactly where this pack's fence usually breaks.

Naikan, associated with Ishin Yoshimoto (1916–1988) in Yamato Kōriyama after the mid-century, is a different cousin: a structured self-examination with roots in Shin Buddhist confession practice, later used in correctional and clinical settings in Japan. I have not held Yoshimoto's Japanese texts. I am marking a trail that English secondary sources name. A trail is not a protocol, and this page will not list Naikan's questions. Those questions, asked as homework by a stranger on the internet, would be a cruelty dressed as heritage.

Why do cousin rooms matter to WOW Therapies?

Because "third wave" talk can sound like America invented acceptance in 1999. It did not. Because "mindfulness-based" talk can sound like only Buddhist convert lineages count. Morita and Naikan are Japanese clinical and religious-clinical objects with their own ethics, class assumptions, and afterlives. Because Hofmann's 2008 title is now part of the ACT reception history: critics used Asia as a mirror, sometimes to humble a brand, sometimes to imply "nothing new." Humbling a brand is fair. Erasing a Japanese psychiatrist into a gotcha is not.

Hayes and colleagues have, in various public talks, acknowledged older acceptance traditions without making ACT a branch office. I will not manufacture a quotation I have not got on the desk. The historical stance this pack can defend is: ACT's paperwork is behavior-analytic and late-century American (and then international). Morita's paperwork is early-twentieth-century Japanese psychiatry. Naikan's paperwork is mid-century Japanese religious practice moving into institutions. Readers may hear rhymes. This series will not flatten the rhymes into a single method.

Girindrasekhar Bose and other non-European clinicians in the sister pack already warned against a map that only has Vienna and Boston. The same warning applies when the map only has Reno and Worcester.

This page will not:
- teach rest-stage Morita treatment;
- teach Naikan questions;
- decide who invented acceptance;
- use "the East" as a puddle.

It will say: when you read "acceptance" in a 1999 Guilford book, you are allowed to remember that other hospitals, in other decades, already refused to treat a symptom as an enemy. You are not allowed to treat that memory as a license to skip the 1999 book, or as a license to skip Japan.
