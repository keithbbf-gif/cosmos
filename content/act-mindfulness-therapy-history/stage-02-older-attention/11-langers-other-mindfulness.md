**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Addison-Wesley published Ellen J. Langer's *Mindfulness* in 1989. The book gathered a social-psychology research program she had been running at Harvard: mindlessness as the premature cognitive commitment to categories, mindfulness as the opposite habit of mind — noticing novelty, drawing distinctions, staying in contact with context. Nursing-home studies, "counterclockwise" vacation experiments that later became famous and contested, the small tyrannies of a label: that is her archive, not a zafu.

Langer (b. 1947; living at last verification) has said, in interviews, that she was not writing a meditation book. Readers still filed her on the same shelf as Kabat-Zinn's 1990 *Full Catastrophe Living* because English has one fashionable noun. A historian who lets the noun win has already lost the plot.

Why keep her in an ACT pack?

Because later clinicians would say "mindfulness" and mean at least three incompatible jobs:

1. **Langer:** a cognitive style, often induced by instructions to notice new things, studied with social-psych methods.
2. **Kabat-Zinn / MBSR:** an eight-week medical-behavioral class with sitting and yoga, studied with health outcomes.
3. **ACT / DBT / MBCT:** therapy-school uses, each with its own manual and its own idea of what a thought is.

If you cite "mindfulness research" without saying which job, you can launder a nursing-home finding into a depression-relapse claim, or a 2000 JCCP table into a theory of creativity. That laundering is how popular science works. It is not how this folder works.

Langer's program also shows that secular American psychology did not need Buddhism to want a word for "not sleepwalking through a category." William James had already written about habit and attention; the sister pack allowed him only in a psychotherapy frame. Langer is James's late-century social-psych cousin, with IRB numbers. ACT's "present moment" talk sometimes sounds like her, sometimes like a meditation teacher, sometimes like a behavior analyst describing stimulus control. The sameness is English. The procedures differ.

I will not adjudicate the later controversies around the counterclockwise study's publicity. I have not held every raw file. I will say: a trade book in 1989 made *mindfulness* a Harvard-adjacent psychology word the same decade a Worcester clinic and a Guilford ACT manuscript were making it a clinical word. Three presses, three jobs.

Living person: public books and lectures only. No health speculation. Type, not a headshot. A later editor who wants a Harvard file-page photograph will almost certainly fail the license test. Keep the 1989 spine.

For a WOW Therapies reader, the practical yield is small and useful. When a headline says "mindfulness improves X," ask whose mindfulness. If the paper is Langer-style, you are in social psychology. If it is MBSR, you are in an eight-week class census. If it is ACT, you are in a psychotherapy trial with a different comparison class. Mixing the bibliographies is how a yogurt lid gets a footnote.

This series will not teach you to "notice novelty." It will not tell you that categories are bad. Categories keep a clinic from killing people. Langer's point, as I read her, was that *premature* categories make people smaller. A history pack has the same problem: "third wave" is a premature category if it stops you from opening 2008.

A later popularization of Langer's work — the "counterclockwise" hotel study in which elderly men lived as if it were 1959 — became a TED-adjacent story that this pack will not treat as a clinical finding. Reception is a historical event. A reception that turns a social-psych manipulation into a fountain-of-youth headline is the same flattening we refused for Teasdale's split.

Put *Mindfulness* (1989) on the table next to *Full Catastrophe Living* (1990) and *Acceptance and Commitment Therapy* (1999). The spines match. The insides do not. That is the whole essay.
