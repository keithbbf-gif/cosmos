**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Beacon Press published an English *The Miracle of Mindfulness* in 1975 (some catalogs, 1976), a slim book Thích Nhất Hạnh had written as a manual for social workers in wartime Vietnam and that a Western readership received as a gentle introduction to attention. The title did enormous later work. "Miracle" and "mindfulness" in the same breath is already a marketing problem a history series has to handle without sneering at a monk who was trying to keep people alive.

Nhất Hạnh (1926–2022) was a Vietnamese Thiền teacher, a poet, and an antiwar exile. Martin Luther King Jr. nominated him for the Nobel Peace Prize in 1967 — a nomination, not a prize; the Nobel was not awarded that year. He founded Plum Village in France in 1982. He taught "Engaged Buddhism," a phrase that meant ethics in a burning country, and that later American posters sometimes reduced to smiling while you wash dishes.

This pack includes him because English-language clinicians and hospital teachers cited him, assigned him, and borrowed his tone. He is not an ACT founder. He is not an MBSR employee. He is part of the weather that made "mindful" a word your intern already knew.

The wartime origin of *The Miracle of Mindfulness* matters. A book written to help workers stay human under bombs is not a wellness product. When the same pages are later sold next to a bath bomb, the pages have been relocated. Relocation is a historical event. It does not make the original cynical, and it does not make the bath bomb deep.

Plum Village and the Order of Interbeing built a transnational lay community with retreats, monastic training, and a distinctive language ("dear friends," walking meditation as a public image). Those communities have their own conflicts and governance, like every order. This page will not audit them. It will say: by the 1990s a person could meet "Thầy" in a paperback without meeting Vietnam.

Clinicians who quote him on a therapy website are often quoting a tone: slow, kind, anti-heroic. Tone is not a protocol, which is why this series can mention it. The danger is the reverse — treating his tone as if it *were* a protocol, a way to "do mindfulness" without a teacher, a community, or a view of suffering that includes history. Nhất Hạnh did not hide history. Exile is a historical condition. A clinic that borrows his gentleness and drops the war has performed a surgery.

He died in Từ Hiếu Temple in Huế on 22 January 2022. Living-person rules loosen after death; estate and photograph rights do not. Most widely circulated images remain copyrighted. Type, or a title-page object if you have a clean scan. No generated monk.

Comparison, briefly, so the later ACT essays do not fake a genealogy: Hayes's 1994 paper is about verbal support for experiential avoidance. Nhất Hạnh's 1975 book is about keeping a heart in a ruined country. A 2008 critic (Hofmann) could ask whether ACT was a "new wave or Morita therapy." Almost no serious person asked whether ACT was Plum Village. The English word *mindfulness* is the bridge, and bridges are where category errors happen.

If you want his life, read a biography that uses Vietnamese sources and does not wash the war out. If you want a dish-washing instruction set, this series will not provide one. If you want to know why a Southeast Arkansas educational page mentions a Vietnamese exile, the answer is: because the word on the yogurt lid passed through his English titles on the way to the hospital, and the hospital did not always say so.
