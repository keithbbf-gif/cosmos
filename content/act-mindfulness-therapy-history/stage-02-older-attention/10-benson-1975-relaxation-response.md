William Morrow published Herbert Benson's *The Relaxation Response* in 1975, written with Miriam Z. Klipper. A Harvard-affiliated cardiologist put a name on a pattern he and colleagues had been measuring in people who practiced Transcendental Meditation and then in simpler, stripped techniques: decreased metabolism, blood pressure changes, a physiological counter to a "fight-or-flight" story Americans already knew from mid-century stress talk.

Benson (1935–2022) did not found ACT. He did not found MBSR. He founded a *medical object*: a short, secular, repeatable practice that could be written up in *The New England Journal of Medicine* and *Psychiatry Clinics* and then in a trade book a family physician could recommend without saying "mantra" too loudly. The TM movement's own medical papers (Wallace and others around 1970–1972) are part of the same corridor. Benson's move was to genericize. Genericizing is how hospitals adopt things.

This matters to a mindfulness-history pack because "relaxation" and "mindfulness" would later be stacked as synonyms on intake forms. They are not synonyms. Benson's object was physiological and brief. Kabat-Zinn's 1979 object was an eight-week class with yoga, sitting, and a hospital census of chronic pain. Langer's 1989 object was social-psychological, about mindless categories. ACT's later use of "present-moment" language was functional and behavioral. Four objects, one sloppy English.

Benson also shows the pattern this pack keeps watching: a practice with a religious or movement home (TM, a Hindu-derived export with a celebrity phase) is measured, renamed, and offered as hygiene. Some scientists called that demystification. Some teachers called it theft. Both can be describing the same paperwork.

I will not teach Benson's steps. The book is still in print in various editions; that is a fact about publishing, not a referral. I will not adjudicate TM's internal controversies or the later marketing of "relaxation" audio. I will say: by 1975 an American reader could buy a cardiologist's paperback that treated attention-and-repetition as something a heart might care about. Four years later a Worcester clinic would treat attention as something a whole life of pain might care about. The paperbacks sat on similar shelves. The programs were not the same.

Critics of medical mindfulness sometimes write as if Kabat-Zinn invented secularization. Benson is a reminder that the hospital had already hired the move. Jacobson 1929 is a reminder that the hospital had hired it before TM. The late-century "mindfulness boom" is a rebranding as much as a discovery.

For ACT, Benson is weather, not parentage. A values-and-language therapy does not need a relaxation paperback. But the patients who arrived in the 1990s often arrived *already trained* by magazines to think that sitting quietly was a treatment-shaped object. That training is historical. It changes what a clinician can say without being heard as a guru or a nurse.

Benson later helped found what became the Benson-Henry Institute for Mind Body Medicine at Massachusetts General Hospital — an institutional afterlife that, like the Center for Mindfulness, is not the 1975 paperback. Org charts accrete. Historians should keep the Morrow object on the table so a 2020s institute page cannot backdate itself into a discovery myth.

Benson died in 2022. Photographs: confirm or use a 1975 title page. No generated white coat.

A small-city clinic in Arkansas that never bought the Morrow book still lives in a country the Morrow book helped make — a country where "just relax" is both a medical sentence and a dismissal. History's job is to keep those two uses from pretending they are one. This series will not tell you to relax. It will tell you when a cardiologist published a noun.
