In 1962, Shunryu Suzuki and students established the San Francisco Zen Center. In 1970, Weatherhill published *Zen Mind, Beginner's Mind*, talks compiled with the help of Trudy Dixon and others. Those two dates are enough to locate a room that later clinicians walked through, sat in, or read on a couch and then cited as if a paperback were a transmission.

The American zendo after 1950 was not a single room. D. T. Suzuki's English essays had already made "Zen" a modernist literary object in the first half of the century. Alan Watts sold a breezier version to people who would never sew a robe. Japanese-American and other Asian Buddhist communities had been practicing without waiting for Beat poetry. The convert boom — Philip Kapleau's Rochester Zen Center (1966), Maezumi's Los Angeles work, the San Francisco constellation, later scandal and reform — is the boom most often footnoted in therapy books. Footnotes are not lineage.

Why does this belong in an ACT/mindfulness history?

Because Marsha Linehan has said, in public, that Zen training shaped her idea of attention as a discipline, not as décor. Because Kabat-Zinn sat in Zen and vipassanā worlds before he opened a hospital class. Because a generation of psychologists in the 1970s–90s could drive to a zendo on a Saturday and back to a behavior-therapy lab on a Monday, and the two buildings did not have to agree about the self.

The zendo and the lab also failed in public. Sexual misconduct and authoritarian abuse in American Zen communities are part of the record, not a smear a history series invented. A pack that borrows Zen's prestige for a therapy school and hides the scandals is doing public relations. This page will not litigate every case. It will say: transmission is a human institution. Institutions harm people. A clinician who "got mindfulness from Zen" inherited both the sitting and the unreliability of teachers.

*Zen Mind, Beginner's Mind* remains the object a curious American is most likely to have held. It is a set of talks, edited, in English, aimed at students in a particular California decade. It is not a twelfth-century Chinese kōan collection, and it is not a DBT manual. People who quote "beginner's mind" on a clinic website are quoting a brand as much as a doctrine.

Sōtō Zen's emphasis on *shikantaza* — just sitting — appealed to clinicians who were tired of striving. That appeal is historically understandable. It is also how a religious discipline gets hired as an antidote to American self-improvement and then becomes a new self-improvement. The irony wrote itself by the 2010s. Stage 6 will deal with the yogurt. Here I only need the mid-century furniture: zafus in rented halls, a Japanese priest with imperfect English, students who would later become the people who trained the people who trained your intern.

Chinese Chan, Korean Seon, and Vietnamese Thiền have their own American stories (the last includes Thích Nhất Hạnh, a later essay). Lumping them as "Zen" is the same flattening as lumping ACT and MBSR as "mindfulness-based therapy." I will keep the names apart when I know enough to. When I do not, I will say so.

Living teachers and recent estates: most photographs are copyrighted. Type only. No generated robes.

This page will not teach posture. It will not tell you which center is authentic. Authenticity fights are intramural and often cruel. It will say: after 1950, an English-speaking person in a coastal city could sit in a converted building and hear that thoughts were not orders. Some of those people became therapists. Some of those therapists later wrote manuals that cited "Eastern practice" in a paragraph. The manuals are not the zendo. The zendo is not the manual. The citation is a historical event.

If you want Suzuki's life, read a biography that uses Japanese sources. If you want Linehan's life, read the sister-pack figure essay and the 2011 *Times* interview she chose. If you want a method, leave this folder.
