**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 2004, *Behavior Therapy* printed Steven C. Hayes's essay "Acceptance and Commitment Therapy, Relational Frame Theory, and the Third Wave of Behavioral and Cognitive Therapies." The title did two jobs. It placed ACT next to a basic-science program (relational frame theory). It also baptized a generation with a beach metaphor.

Waves are flattering. They imply sequence, progress, and a drowning of whatever came before. Behavior therapy already had a creation myth that ran that way: first, stimulus and response (Wolpe, Skinner in the clinic's doorway); second, cognition (Beck, Ellis); third, the *relationship to* thoughts and feelings. The myth is tidy enough to teach in an hour. Tidy is a warning.

Hayes's own 2004 characterization was more careful than the nickname that escaped it. He wrote of treatments "particularly sensitive to the context and functions of psychological phenomena, not just their form," of "contextual and experiential change strategies in addition to more direct and didactic ones," of broad repertoires rather than an "eliminative approach to narrowly defined problems." That is a family resemblance, not a geology. He listed neighbors: ACT, DBT, FAP, integrative behavioral couple therapy, and others that editors would shuffle. Öst's 2008 review used a similar guest list and then graded the parties.

The nickname was already in circulation among people who liked it and people who did not. Patrick Corrigan, in 2001, had warned about "getting ahead of the data." Hofmann and Asmundson, in 2008, asked "new wave or old hat?" Arch and Craske, the same year, asked whether ACT and CBT for anxiety were different treatments or similar mechanisms in different clothes. Hofmann separately wondered whether ACT was a new wave or a cousin of Morita therapy — a barb with a real historical question inside it, which this pack takes up later without collapsing the two rooms.

A practice site does not need to pick a champion. It needs to know the fight is about how much "acceptance" is a new school and how much it is exposure with better manners. Take what you need from that sentence. The historical fact is that a set of behaviorally trained clinicians put acceptance, mindfulness, values, and dialectics on the same page as an empirical tradition, and then argued in public about whether the page was new.

Nicknames do institutional work. They gather workshops, journals, and listservs. They also gather skeptics. Once "third wave" existed as a search term, meta-analyses could treat it as a class — Öst did — even as the people inside the class insisted they were not a class. ACBS, founded as an organization in 2005 and incorporated in 2006, named itself for *contextual behavioral science*, not for a wave. That was a bet that the research program would outlive the metaphor.

The sister pack's era essay on third-wave therapies (`13-third-wave.md`) already told the short public version: Linehan, Hayes, Segal–Williams–Teasdale, the boom, the flattening. This pack is the long version of that one room. It will not improve the nickname. It will keep saying *nickname* so a reader does not hear *law*.

There is a pedagogical cost. Students like waves. They can draw three boxes. The boxes hide the people who never left the first box: behavioral activation in the 1990s–2000s, exposure therapists who never needed a cushion, applied behavior analysts who thought "cognition" was a category error. The third wave did not abolish the first. It argued with it in journals, then on apps that had never read the journals.

There is a marketing cost. A nickname that sounds like progress sells weekends. A clinician who says "I do third-wave work" after three hours of training is speaking a dialect the 2004 essay would not have recognized as a school. The 2004 essay wanted principles and data. A weekend wants a certificate.

There is a justice cost, which later essays take slowly. If "third" means "after the old, sad therapies," it can smear whole traditions — psychoanalysis, humanistic work, family systems — as prehistory. Those traditions have their own scars and their own documents. They are not a beach the wave erased. This pack will not put Freud on trial again. It will say: a behavior-therapy family used a beach metaphor, and the metaphor leaked.

When I write "third wave" from here on, I mean a **historiographical convenience** that Hayes and others used in the mid-2000s, that critics used as a target, and that journalists used as a headline. I do not mean that acceptance was invented in 2004, or that CBT ended, or that a small-city clinic that never bought a Guilford book is behind the times.

The times, in this story, are made of paper: 1991, 1993, 1994, 1999, 2000, 2002, 2004, 2005, 2008, 2009. Paper is slower than a nickname and more useful.
