**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

I am going to waste the first page on what we will not do. If that sounds like a lawyer got to the manuscript first, good. The words *mindfulness* and *acceptance* have spent a generation sliding off research clinics and onto apps, and the slide is almost always greased with a symptom name.

This folder is a stack of historical essays for WOW Therapies. It does not diagnose, treat, or prevent any condition. It is not medical advice. It is not a silent conversion of a Southeast Arkansas therapy site into a mental-health menu.

That is not a humility ritual. It is the only way to talk about these rooms without lying in a soft voice.

Here is the usual lie, in its polite form: a writer tells you that the Buddha, a Japanese psychiatrist, a Worcester basement clinic, and a Reno workshop were all, without knowing it, doing the same thing. Then the writer names a modern distress — rumination that sounds like a diagnosis, a panic that sounds like a chart — and lets a cushion sit next to the condition until your eye does the rest. No one typed the forbidden verb. The sentence still did the work.

I refuse that sentence, including the version that swaps "treats" for "can help with" and hopes a history page will hold.

What I will do instead is slower, and less flattering to anyone who wants a hero therapy.

I will keep **contemplative categories** in their own languages. Satipaṭṭhāna is a textual object with translators and arguments. Zazen is a discipline with teachers and a schedule. Vipassanā as taught at the Insight Meditation Society in Barre after 1976 is not a 2002 Guilford manual waiting to happen. A monastery that sat because that is what the rule required was not waiting for Steven Hayes to baptize the hour.

I will keep **ACT** in the late twentieth century, where the name was built. A behavior-analytic lab used "comprehensive distancing" in the 1980s. A May 1991 ABA talk put "acceptance and commitment therapy" in a title. A 1994 paper in *The Behavior Analyst* put the name in a journal. A 1999 Guilford book made the school orderable. That is a research culture with conferences, listservs, and gaps. It is not a lost chapter of the Pali canon.

I will keep **MBSR and MBCT** in their buildings. Jon Kabat-Zinn opened a Stress Reduction and Relaxation Program at the University of Massachusetts Medical Center in September 1979; he later wrote that a ten-second "vision" on a spring 1979 retreat at IMS had shown him the assignment. Segal, Williams, and Teasdale published a 2000 trial in the *Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology* and a 2002 book. Those are dated objects. They are not a license to reprint a body scan.

I will keep **regulators and guideline committees** in their buildings. When NICE's 2009 depression guideline (CG90) named MBCT for a defined group of people who were currently well after three or more episodes, that was a committee speaking to the National Health Service. It is a historical fact about a guideline. It is not this site enrolling you.

I will keep **uncertainty** visible. Secondary literature does not agree on how much "third wave" is geology and how much it is marketing. Hayes's 2004 *Behavior Therapy* essay used the nickname in public. Hofmann and Asmundson, in 2008, asked whether the wave was old hat. Öst, the same year, said the early trials were not yet stringent enough for a certain kind of badge. If I flatten that into "science has spoken," I am already doing the app's job.

I will not invent a notebook I have not held. I have not sat in Hayes's 1981 lab reading the first protocol. I have not graded Öst's 13 ACT trials. When I mention a workshop economy, I am describing a public workforce — ACBS membership opened in October 2005 — not a count I performed at a hotel ballroom.

I will not give you a script. The minute a history essay hands over a breathing count, a values card, or a "notice that thought" exercise, it has changed jobs. Form matters — group, individual, telephone coaching, an eight-week medical class — and I will talk about form as history. I will not talk about form as a recipe for your Tuesday.

I will not do the "balanced" trick where the last paragraph says "see a professional" after the first twelve paragraphs have already sold the method. If a draft cannot live without a symptom payoff, the draft should die.

Why bother, then?

Because the documents are real, the rooms are real, and the borrowed word *mindfulness* is real, and they are not the same object. A person who wants to understand why a yogurt lid and a VA clinic now share a vocabulary is entitled to a history that does not recruit their diagnosis. A person who loves dharma history is entitled to hear a sutta as a sutta, not as a hack. A person who is tired of both wellness copy and sneering debunking is entitled to a third tone: specific, a little dry, unwilling to convert a teacher's cushion into a press release.

There is a fourth reader I am writing against: the one who thinks a claims fence is cowardice. I do not think it is cowardice to refuse a sentence that would be reckless on a service page and still tempting in an essay. Essays have more room than service pages. That is exactly why they leak.

If you want a clinician, see a clinician who knows you. If you want a named third-wave service, that belongs on a clinical page with training, supervision, and consent — and WOW Therapies does not, by hosting this history, claim to be that page. If you want the story, stay here. The story is long. It has a Catholic novitiate in Barre, a basement in Worcester, a 1991 Atlanta talk, a Swedish conference after September 11, and a 2008 review that refused the victory lap.

None of that is a treatment plan. I am repeating myself on purpose. Repetition is how a fence stays up when the next sentence wants a worksheet.
