In August 2008, *Behaviour Research and Therapy* printed Lars-Göran Öst's review of "third wave" randomized trials. He counted thirteen ACT studies and thirteen DBT studies, called the average effects moderate, and said the methods were less stringent than a comparison set of CBT trials. That sentence is a claim about a review. It is not a homework assignment, and it is not a tombstone on a school.

A therapy-history claim is a sentence that looks like news and behaves like a prescription if you let it. "MBCT prevents depression." "ACT is evidence-based." "Mindfulness reduces stress." Each of those can be made true enough to print and false enough to harm, depending on what you hide: who sat in the chairs, what they were compared with, how long anyone watched, who paid, and what the authors refused to claim.

This folder uses a small kit. I will name it once so I do not have to moralize in every later essay.

**A trial is an event.** Teasdale, Segal, Williams, Ridgeway, Soulsby, and Lau reported, in the August 2000 *Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology*, a randomized study of 145 recovered patients with recurrent depression, followed for 60 weeks. For people with three or more prior episodes, the authors reported a lower relapse risk in the MBCT-plus-usual-care arm than in usual care alone. For people with only two prior episodes, they did not. If a headline swallows the split, the headline has already started treatment without a license.

**A book is an object.** Hayes, Strosahl, and Wilson's 1999 Guilford volume can be ordered, cited, and argued with. It is not a hospital's staffing pattern. Linehan's 1993 Guilford volume *was* a staffing pattern — individual sessions, a skills group, a consultation team — and even that book is not permission to reprint the modules. A history site that lists the exercises has changed jobs.

**A guideline is a committee.** NICE CG90 (2009) named MBCT for a defined group of adults who were currently well after three or more depressive episodes. Committees update, narrow, and retract. A 2009 sentence is a 2009 sentence. It is not this practice telling a reader in Arkansas what to book.

**A society is a workforce.** ACBS opened membership in October 2005 after a planning committee formed that August. Workforces drift. "ACT-informed" on a clinic website often means a weekend workshop and a poster. The historical question is what the word named before it named a vibe.

**A nickname is not a stratigraphy.** "Third wave" is useful the way "Impressionism" is useful: it gathers a quarrel. Hayes used it in *Behavior Therapy* in 2004. Critics used it as a dare. If I treat the nickname as a law of nature, I have joined the marketing department.

When you meet a claim in this series, ask four dull questions.

1. **Dated where?** A year, a journal, a page. "Researchers have found" is not a date.
2. **Compared with what?** Wait-list, treatment-as-usual, a pill, another psychotherapy, a charismatic teacher? Effect sizes change jobs when the comparison changes.
3. **Whom did they enroll?** Recovered or in episode; veteran or undergraduate; English-speaking clinic or analogue pain task. Zettle's 1986 dissertation work is not the 1999 book, and neither is a county-clinic caseload.
4. **What did they refuse to say?** Good papers are full of limits. Marketing copy edits them out.

There is a fifth question I use on myself: **could a sad person do this after lunch?** If the answer is yes — if the paragraph has become a script — the paragraph does not belong here. History may describe that a group met for eight weeks. History may not meet with you.

People who write about psychotherapy inherit a temptation the furniture historians do not: the reader is sometimes in pain *now*. Kindness then sounds like a protocol. I would rather sound slightly cold and stay in my lane than sound kind and practice without a license. The educational note at the top of every file is not décor. It is the same fence as this paragraph, repeated because repetition is how fences stay up.

WOW Therapies is a practice brand. The live site, when last fetched, spoke of occupational, physical, and speech services in Southeast Arkansas. These essays do not quietly add "and ACT" to that sentence. If the practice ever offers a named third-wave service, that will be a different file, with training, supervision, and consent on it. Heritage is allowed to be wider than the appointment book. Heritage is not allowed to fake the appointment book.

You will also meet **borrowed prestige**. A sutta, a Zen teacher, a Nobel-adjacent laboratory, a VA hospital — each can be stacked next to a method until the method looks older or holier or more official than its paperwork. I will name the debt when the debt is real. I will not let the debt do the claiming.

You will meet **living people**. Hayes, Kabat-Zinn, Segal, Williams, Teasdale, Strosahl, Wilson, Linehan were alive at last verification (2026-09-14). Public books and public interviews only. No health speculation. No origin-story cartoon that makes a 2011 newspaper interview a destiny. The sister pack already holds Linehan's figure essay; this pack uses her only where mindfulness and staffing meet.

If a later editor finds a sentence that would be illegal or reckless on a service page, cut the sentence. Do not "soften" it into a how-to. Softening is how the fence fails.

A reader who finishes this file and wants a method should leave the folder. That is success. The success of a history pack is that it does not become a clinic.
