*Behavior Therapy* 35, no. 4 (2004) printed Steven C. Hayes, "Acceptance and Commitment Therapy, Relational Frame Theory, and the Third Wave of Behavioral and Cognitive Therapies," pages 639–665. Draft 03 already called the nickname a nickname. This essay is the paper as a dated object: what it tried to do in a flagship behavior-therapy journal, and what escaped.

Hayes needed, in public, to place ACT next to a basic-science program (RFT) and next to a generation (DBT, FAP, IBCT, and others). He needed to answer the "getting ahead of the data" smell Corrigan had named in 2001. He wrote that the new treatments had kept empirical roots, not only at the level of outcome but at the level of process. He also wrote, in the characterization I already quoted, about context and function, experiential strategies, broad repertoires, and issues that mattered for clinicians as well as clients.

A journal essay is a bid for prestige. *Behavior Therapy* is not a trade magazine. Getting the wave metaphor into that journal meant later meta-analyses could treat "third wave" as a searchable class. Öst 2008 is unthinkable without some such class. Hayes helped build the class even as ACBS, a year later, named itself for science rather than for a beach.

I have not reproduced the article. Readers with library access can read it. What I want a general reader to know is the *job* of the piece: intra-family diplomacy. It says to CBT majorities: we are not abandoning data. It says to behavior-analytic minorities: we are not only a bag of metaphors. It says to journal editors: here is a generation you should review as a generation. Diplomacy is not a crime. It is how schools survive. It is also how nicknames harden.

The same volume year included other "how empirical are the new technologies?" conversations (Hayes, Masuda, Bissett, Luoma, and Guerrero, 2004, in the same journal). A school that writes about its own empiricism is both doing science and doing PR. Historians should hold both verbs.

For the timeline: 2004 is the year the nickname went from hallway to pagination. 2005 is the year the society opened membership. 2008 is the year the nickname got a skeptical meta-analysis. 2009 is the year MBCT, a cousin with a different parentage, got a NICE sentence. Those four years are the institutional weather of this pack's second half.

Living author: the paper is public. No biography. No slide deck ripped from a 2004 talk and posted as if it were the article. The article has page numbers. Use them.

For WOW Therapies: do not cite "third wave" on a service page as if it were a credential. It is a 2004 journal metaphor. Credentials are licenses and training. Metaphors are for seminars.

If a later editor wants an epigraph that is not a protocol, use the year and the page range, not a slogan from a slide. 35(4): 639–665. That is a grown-up citation. It will not fit on a yogurt lid. That is why I like it.

The PDF I opened this pass is the version ACBS hosts. Pagination 639–665 is the journal's. If a later open-access copy differs in typesetting, keep the citation to *Behavior Therapy* 35(4). Hosted PDFs are a convenience. They are not the volume on the shelf.

The sister pack summarized this essay in a paragraph. This pack can afford to say: a nickname that wanted to be a generation succeeded, and then had to live with reviewers who took the generation as a hypothesis and tried to falsify it. Success and scrutiny arrived together. That is healthier than a brand that never met Öst. Meeting a reviewer is part of becoming a generation. Avoiding one is part of becoming a banner.
