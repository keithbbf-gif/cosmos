**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

The sister pack's third-wave essay already said the quiet part: acceptance, taught badly, can sound like "tolerate your oppression." Linehan's and Hayes's better students treat acceptance as a way to *move*, not as a muzzle. The critique still has to be answered in rooms where a woman's "value" has been defined by someone else.

This essay is that paragraph, slowed down, because this pack's subject matter is the muzzle's favorite vocabulary.

Feminist therapy, liberation psychology, and the multicultural essays in the sister pack (Chesler, Fanon, Martín-Baró) are the older furniture. A 1999 behavior-therapy book does not replace them. If ACT popularizers wrote as if politics were a fusion to defuse, they earned a political critique. I will not invent a quotation. I will say: values language without a critique of who installed the values is a known failure mode. Essay 21 (*Kelly Wilson and the Values Sentence*) named the card-sort cheapening. Here the cheapening is crueler.

Examples I will not fictionalize as composite patients — only as public patterns:

A workplace mindfulness hour that asks employees to accept a speedup.
A religious household that hears "acceptance" as "submit."
A clinic that treats a traumatized person's anger as a thought to defuse rather than as information about a crime.

Those patterns are in the culture. They are not "what ACT is." They are what the *words* do when they leave a supervised room. History of a school includes the words' afterlives.

Linehan's dialectic — accept and insist on change — was one technical answer to the muzzle problem, aimed at a specific census. ACT's pairing of acceptance with committed action was another. Neither pairing survives a weekend that only teaches the first noun. Workshops that sell calm sell the muzzle. Workshops that sell movement without asking *whose* movement sell a different muzzle.

This page will not teach a feminist session. It will not teach a liberation practice. It will say: if you meet "acceptance" on a poster, ask who benefits if you get quieter. If the beneficiary is you (less war with a ringing ear, say — a census a speech-language clinic actually meets), that is one story. If the beneficiary is a supervisor, a state, or an abuser, that is another. The word does not tell you which.

For WOW Therapies: the practice's public work, as fetched, is communication and access. Do not import "radical acceptance" onto a pediatric articulation page. Children are not a third-wave census. Parents who are already told to accept a waitlist do not need a hexagon.

Living theorists: public books only.

I am not saying ACT is reactionary. I am saying any therapy that names acceptance will be hired by reactionaries. CBT was hired to adjust people to bad jobs. Psychoanalysis was hired to adjust women to bad marriages. Hiring is a historical pattern, not a unique sin. The refusal is to pretend your favorite school is unhirable.

If this essay sounds angry, it is because the yogurt years were not only silly. They were a way to move the cost of a cruel economy into a worker's nervous system and call it wisdom. Purser named the corporate form. This page names the gendered and political form. Both are flattening. Both use our noun.

Martín-Baró's liberation psychology, in the sister pack, already treated mental life as something a war and a class system write on. Fanon already treated colonial psychiatry as a weapon. Those essays are the furniture. This pack does not restage them. It refuses to let a 1999 noun float as if that furniture were not in the house.

The school's better documents still exist. They can be read. They cannot be this blog's homework. The muzzle test is a reader's test: does this sentence make it harder to leave, to testify, to unionize, to say no? If yes, the sentence has left therapy and entered management. We will not print that sentence as heritage.
