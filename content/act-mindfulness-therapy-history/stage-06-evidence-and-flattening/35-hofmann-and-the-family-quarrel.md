**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 2008, *Clinical Psychology Review* published Stefan G. Hofmann and Gordon J. G. Asmundson, "Acceptance and Mindfulness-Based Therapy: New Wave or Old Hat?" The same year, *Clinical Psychology: Science and Practice* printed Hofmann, "Acceptance and Commitment Therapy: New Wave or Morita Therapy?" and Arch and Craske on whether ACT and CBT for anxiety were different treatments or similar mechanisms.

2008 was a quarrel year. Öst counted. Hofmann asked if the clothes were new. Arch and Craske asked if the engine was new. A school that had just named itself a wave met a family that did not want to be the beach.

Hofmann's "old hat" question is the one a historically literate reader should keep. Exposure, behavioral activation, and a refusal to argue endlessly with thoughts are not 1999 inventions. Cognitive therapy already had decentering. Humanistic work already had acceptance-as-warmth. Morita already had *arugamama*. If ACT's contribution is a *package* and a research program, say package. If the claim is a new mechanism, show the mechanism studies. Hayes's group has tried to show processes (defusion, experiential avoidance). Critics have said the processes overlap CBT's. This pack will not close a process debate with a slogan.

The Morita title is a sharper barb. Draft 12 already refused the collapse. Hofmann was not writing Japanese history; he was needling a brand. Needling can be fair. It can also flatten a 1921 Tokyo hospital into a punchline. Both happened in the reception. Our job is to keep Morita's paperwork Japanese and ACT's paperwork behavior-analytic, and then to allow a reader to hear a rhyme.

Arch and Craske's 2008 essay is the one I would hand a graduate student. It is less of a nickname fight and more of a "what, exactly, do you think is different in the hour?" fight. Difference can be real at the level of therapist talk and still small at the level of outcome. That possibility is not a gotcha. It is how many psychotherapy comparisons end.

Leahy and others wrote in a similar season. I will not catalog every commentary. 2008 is a cluster, not a single man.

For WOW Therapies: family quarrels are not service differentiators. "We are third wave, not old CBT" is a workshop sentence. A small-city clinic that respects both exposure and a careful acceptance stance does not need a beach metaphor to treat a person with dignity. Heritage education can name the quarrel. A homepage should not pick a champion.

Living people: public 2008 papers only. Do not turn Hofmann into a mascot of "the other side." He wrote specific essays. Specific is the point of this pack.

The healthy inheritance of 2008 is a habit: when someone says "new," ask "compared with which manual, which year, which outcome." When someone says "nothing new," ask "then why the 1994 rename and the 2001 RFT book?" Both questions keep a school from becoming a church.

The sister pack's CBT-era essay already told the second-wave story. This essay is the family refusing to be replaced by a nickname. Replacement is a marketing wish. Families stay. They argue. The argument is the history.

Hofmann's own later work on emotion and CBT is a different bibliography. I will not drag it in to settle 2008. People write more than one paper. 2008 is a cluster of papers, not a man.

I will not tell you who won. Winning is the wrong verb for 2008. The verb is *published*. They published. We can still read them without enrolling. A small-city journal club that reads Hayes 2004, Öst 2008, and Hofmann 2008 in one month has done more than a weekend that only sells a banner. The journal club does not need a certificate. It needs the PDFs.
