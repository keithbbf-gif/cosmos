**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

*Behaviour Research and Therapy* 46, no. 3 (2008) published Lars-Göran Öst, "Efficacy of the Third Wave of Behavioral Therapies: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis," pages 296–321. He gathered RCTs of ACT, DBT, CBASP, IBCT, and FAP. He found thirteen ACT trials and thirteen DBT trials, one CBASP, two IBCT, no FAP. He reported moderate mean effect sizes (about 0.68 for ACT, 0.58 for DBT, in the summaries that circulate) and said the third-wave RCTs used methods significantly less stringent than a comparison set of CBT studies. He said none of the third-wave therapies, on his reading, yet fulfilled criteria for empirically supported treatments as he applied them.

That is a 2008 reviewer's verdict, not a tombstone, not a victory, and not this site's advice about what you should book.

I have not re-run his statistics. Secondary discussions (including a CRD abstract) note limited search, possible single-reviewer bias, heterogeneity, small samples. Gaudiano, in 2009 in the same journal, argued that the ACT-versus-CBT methodological comparison mismatched populations and resources — apples and oranges. Later meta-analyses (Powers et al. 2009; others after) added trials and arguments. The literature did not freeze in March 2008.

Why give Öst his own essay, then?

Because popular ACT history likes to skip him. Because popular CBT history likes to stop with him. Both skips are propaganda. A school that asked to be treated as an empirical generation (Hayes 2004) earned a skeptical review. A review that used a nickname as a class earned replies about class membership. This is how adult disciplines behave.

"Empirically supported treatment" is itself a historical object (Chambless and colleagues, Society of Clinical Psychology lists). Lists change. Criteria arguments are intramural. A consumer website that says "ACT is an EST" or "ACT is not evidence-based" without a year, a list, and a link is doing a vibe. This pack will not update a Division 12 table in real time. It will say: in 2008, Öst said not yet, on his criteria, with the trials he had.

For WOW Therapies, this is the most important evidence essay in the folder. It models the voice we want on every later mention of "research shows." Name the paper. Name the year. Name the limit. Do not turn a moderate effect size into a promise. Do not turn a methodological complaint into a sneer that people should not study acceptance.

Öst also did a quiet service: he treated FAP's empty cell as a fact. He treated CBASP and IBCT as cousins with almost no RCT volume. The wave was not a wave of equal water.

Living author: public review only. A later Öst paper (he has a long list) is not this object. Keep 46(3): 296–321 on the table.

If you are a student writing a paper, read Öst and Gaudiano both, then a later review, then the primary trials you actually care about. If you are a civilian, you now know that 2008 exists. You do not need to adjudicate it in a waiting room.

The sister pack refused "studies prove school A beats school B" without a named trial, a dated review, *and* a sentence about limits. This essay is that sentence, in the shape of a man in a 2008 issue.

Öst is a Swedish behavior-therapy researcher with a long exposure-and-anxiety bibliography. He was not a random internet skeptic. That does not make him infallible. It makes the 2008 paper an intramural event, which is more interesting than a culture-war event.

I will not tell you ACT "works" or "doesn't." I will tell you a Swede counted thirteen trials and was not satisfied with the methods. Satisfaction is a reviewer's job. Your care is a clinician's job. This folder is neither.
