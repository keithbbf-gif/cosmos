# Claims Guardrails

This pack is **historical and educational**. It is not labeling, not advertising, and not a substitute for a clinician or a lawyer. Editors and importers must keep that frame when headlines, excerpts, and social copy are written. A careful essay can be wrecked by a reckless dek.

## Hard bans

1. **No disease-cure marketing claims.** Do not say a plant, isolate, or supplement "treats," "cures," "prevents," or "reverses" a disease as a present-tense product claim. Historical actors said those things; quote or paraphrase them as *their* claims.
2. **No dosing as medical instruction.** You may report that Withering titrated foxglove, or that a pharmacopeia listed a preparation, without converting that into how a reader should take anything. No milligram schedules, no "start with," no "ask your doctor" stacked next to a DIY amount (that pairing still reads as a protocol).
3. **No invented trials.** If you do not have a named study, regulator monograph, or systematic review in `BIBLIOGRAPHY.md` or a flagged follow-up, write "the clinical record is thin / mixed / later." Do not fabricate endpoints, p-values, or sample sizes.
4. **No synthesis, cultivation-for-diversion, or extraction how-to** for controlled or obviously abusable substances (opium alkaloids, cocaine, cannabis concentrates, lysergamides, etc.). History of isolation is allowed at the level of *who, when, where, what was named*. Not reagents, not yields, not kitchen methods.
5. **No sourcing advice** ("where to buy," "how to import," "how to grow for resin"). Botanical identity in a historical garden is fine.
6. **No "ancient secret the industry hid"** framing. Secrecy claims need a dated archive. Most of these plants were loudly sold.

## Required distinctions

| Phrase pattern | Use when | Avoid when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "was prepared for…" / "appears in the *X* as…" | attributing a historical indication | implying current efficacy |
| "folk use holds that…" | oral or ethnographic report | laundering it as evidence |
| "a randomized trial later found…" | you can name the trial and its limit | you only remember a headline |
| "regulators warned / banned / approved…" | you have the instrument (FR notice, act, EMA) | vibe |
| "dietary supplement under U.S. law" | post-DSHEA products, structure/function talk | calling an 1890 bitters a "supplement" without note |

Folklore and clinical evidence can live in the same essay. They cannot share a verb.

## Modern supplements and U.S. law

When the subject is a product sold today as a dietary supplement in the United States, the essay must not outrun **DSHEA (Pub. L. 103-417, 1994)** and the FDA's dietary-supplement authorities as they existed at the time of writing:

- Supplements are a **defined legal category**, not a scientific one. A capsule of powdered leaf and a capsule of an isolated constituent can sit in the same aisle for reasons of statute, not pharmacology.
- Manufacturers are responsible for safety and for not claiming to treat disease. FDA does not approve supplements the way it approves new drugs. Say that. Do not imply a secret NDA.
- **Structure/function** claims ("supports…") are a regulatory artifact. If you quote one, label it as marketing language under DSHEA, not as a finding.
- Serious adulteration, drug-spike, and ephedra-style stimulant stories belong in the historical file with their **Federal Register** or court dates. Do not turn them into brand smash pieces unless the public record is named.
- Other countries (Australia TGA, EU Traditional Herbal Medicinal Products Directive, Japan Kampo reimbursement, India AYUSH) have different boxes. Do not export DSHEA vocabulary onto them without saying so.

## Controlled substances

History of opium, coca, cannabis, peyote, ayahuasca, ergot alkaloids, and similar is in scope. Diversion, clandestine chemistry, and "how it feels / how to dose a trip" are out of scope. Religious and legal history (Native American Church, *Gonzales v. O Centro*, scheduling) is in scope when sourced.

## Colonial, Indigenous, and sacred knowledge

Do not present ceremonial plants as lifestyle products. Do not publish ritual instructions. Name Indigenous nations and authors where the record allows; do not write "the Indians used." When a manuscript is colonial (Badianus), say who wrote, who painted, who bound, and who later owned it.

Bioprospecting essays (vinca, taxol, qinghao, cinchona) should state who collected, who isolated, who patented or published, and what — if anything — returned to the place of collection. Uncertainty is better than a tidy "benefit-sharing" sentence that did not happen.

## Safety language that is allowed

- Historical poisonings, ergotism outbreaks, elixir sulfanilamide (1937), thalidomide as contrast, ephedra AE reports as *regulatory history*.
- "This plant is toxic" as a property known to the period or to modern toxicology, without a home-use workaround.
- Drug–herb interaction as a *documented* clinical and regulatory story (St. John's wort / CYP3A4 / ciclosporin and oral contraceptives are the teaching case). No interaction charts for readers to self-manage.

## Headlines, excerpts, SEO, and social

The same bans apply to:

- `<title>` and `<meta name="description">`
- WordPress excerpts and "more" tags
- Pinterest and Instagram captions
- newsletter subject lines

Forbidden excerpt pattern: "Discover the ancient herb that fights [disease]."  
Allowed: "How a 1552 college manuscript preserved Nahuatl plant names — and Spanish diagnoses."

## Review checklist before publish

- [ ] No present-tense cure/prevent/treat for a commercial product
- [ ] No reader dosing
- [ ] Folklore vs trial vs regulator are separable
- [ ] Controlled-substance passages have no method
- [ ] Modern aisle talk mentions DSHEA or the local equivalent
- [ ] Citations exist or are flagged soft
- [ ] Photo is schematic/historical, not a fake clinical scene
- [ ] Hed would still be honest if a plaintiff's lawyer read it

## If legal or medical review flags a line

Prefer deletion or conversion to a dated quotation over a fog of "may help support." Fog is how these essays become the thing they are written against.
