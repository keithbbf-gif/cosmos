# An Aztec Herbal in a Colonial Hand

The *Libellus de medicinalibus Indorum herbis* is small enough to hold in two hands. It was made in 1552 at the Franciscan college of Santa Cruz de Tlatelolco, in the city that had been Tenochtitlan. Martín de la Cruz, a physician working in Nahuatl knowledge, composed it. Juan Badiano, a teacher at the college, turned it into Latin. The paintings — plants with their roots and their Mexican soil still clinging, some of them among the most exact botanical portraits of the sixteenth century — were done by artists who knew the look of the Valley of Mexico better than any European woodcutter did.

The book was a gift, intended for the royal court. It left. For a long time Europe barely knew it. The Vatican library held it. Mexico got a famous facsimile in the twentieth century. The plants stayed where they were. The college sat on the site of Tlatelolco's market, a few years after the siege. A pharmacopeia made in a school for conquered nobles is not a neutral scientific instrument. It is still, as paint, one of the best plant books of its century.

## A college at the wound

The Colegio de Santa Cruz opened in the 1530s under Franciscan supervision, with Indian scholars who could already write. It trained sons of the Nahua nobility in Latin and theology. It was also, for a few decades, a place where Indigenous intellectual labor could still be written down in a form the empire might file. Fray Jacobo de Grado is sometimes named as the man who asked for the little book as a gift for the royal court; the dedication politics are a specialist file. The *Libellus* is not a free ethnography. It is a presentation object. Diagnoses are recast in a language Philip II's physicians could, in theory, read. The Nahuatl names remain, like stones in the Latin stream.

Bernardino de Sahagún's *Florentine Codex*, book 11, is the larger warehouse of "earthly things" from the same colonial generation: animals, plants, minerals, with Nahuatl text and paintings by the *colegio*'s artists and informants. Sahagún's project is a missionary encyclopedia. Martín de la Cruz's is a pharmacopeia. Francisco Hernández's later royal expedition (1570s) would try to do the same job with a Spaniard's authority and a larger team. Read them together and you get a city that still knew its hills, written by people who had watched the temples fall.

## What the pictures know

European herbals of 1552 were still arguing with Dioscorides about whether a German plant was the plant in the book. The Tlatelolco painters often skipped that argument. They painted the thing in front of them: the tuber, the flower, the way a leaf sits. Modern botanists have spent careers trying to lock those portraits to species. Some locks hold. Many remain **soft**. The honest caption says "this painting records a plant called X in Nahuatl, used in the text for Y complaint, possibly Z species."

"Used for" is their claim. It is not a trial. It is not a product.

## Conquest on the page

The *Libellus* exists because a colonial college existed. It also exists because Nahua medicine was not a rumor; it was a profession with plants, specialists, and a vocabulary for the body that did not need Galen (though the Latin may lean on European disease words). To call the book "the first American herbal" is a European filing habit. The first American herbals were oral, painted in other media, or already ash.

Do not turn Martín de la Cruz into a mascot for a supplement line. His name on a bottle would be the last twist of the gift that went to court.

## After the Vatican

Twentieth-century Mexican nationalism embraced the manuscript as proof of a scientific past. That embrace saved it from being only a curiosity. It also sometimes flattened it into a monument. A monument does not argue. The *Libellus* argues on every page: this plant, this name, this use, in a city under a new god and a new king. The argument is the reason to publish the pictures with care, and the reason not to invent the species we wish we could see.
