# Kava: Ceremony, Export, and the Liver File

In Vanuatu, Fiji, Tonga, and Samoa, kava is a drink of *Piper methysticum* root, prepared in a bowl, served in a sequence that is politics as much as pharmacology. Vincent Lebot, Mark Merlin, and Lamont Lindstrom's *Kava: The Pacific Drug* is still the book that treats the plant as a cultivated Pacific technology — many cultivars, many names, a drunkenness that is not alcohol's. Missionaries hated it. Planters sometimes taxed it. The bowl outlasted both.

In late-twentieth-century Germany and neighboring markets, kava became a tablet and a dropper for anxiety, standardized to kavalactones, sold in the European herbal-medicine style (closer to a traditional-use drug than to a U.S. supplement). Then the liver file opened. Case reports of hepatotoxicity accumulated. In 2002 Germany's BfArM and other European authorities suspended or tightly restricted the medicines. The UK followed. The FDA issued a consumer advisory. Pacific governments and some ethnopharmacologists argued that the wrong plant parts, the wrong solvents (acetone, ethanol versus water), and a non-ceremonial pattern of use had been blamed on a noble bowl.

The argument is not settled at the level of mechanism. Acetone extracts, peel and stem peelings (the "adventitious roots" and bark that some cheap exporters included), pipermethystine stories, kava lactone profiles of "two-day" cultivars versus "noble" ones: the toxicology papers disagree, and this pack will not pretend to referee them. That uncertainty is **soft**. The regulatory actions are not. A ministry can withdraw a license while a mechanism is still in committee.

## A cultivated psychoactive

Kava is not a wild weed someone tripped over. It is a sterile or near-sterile cultigen, moved by cuttings, bred for effects Pacific drinkers can name in local languages — *nakamal* talk in Vanuatu, *sevusevu* protocol in Fiji, the Tongan and Samoan chiefly sequences that a tourist bar collapses into a punchline. That is agriculture plus law. A history that starts at the German Commission E monograph (a late-century expert opinion, not a trial) has already missed the plant. Lebot's cultivar work is the scientific companion to the ethnography: chemotypes, island-by-island names, a warning that "kava" on a German label was never one thing.

Captain Cook's officers wrote down the drink with the usual imperial mixture of disgust and curiosity. Missionaries in the nineteenth century tried to break the bowl as they tried to break the Sabbath-breaking. They failed in different degrees by island. The bowl is older than their failure.

This essay will not describe how to make the drink. Ceremony is not content. Export powder is a different object and still not a recipe here.

## 2002 as a historical date

Like the 2004 U.S. ephedra rule, 2002 is a moment when a plant-with-a-culture hit a pharmacovigilance system built for medicines. Europe had given kava a medical box. The box came with a duty to act on signals. Acting on signals will sometimes punish a traditional use that is not the signal's true cause. It will sometimes save livers. You cannot know which from a blog. You can know that **once a plant is a licensed medicine, it can be un-licensed**.

Pacific exporters lost a market. Some later recovered under tighter specs (peeled noble cultivars, water extracts). The phrase "noble cultivar" became a quality-and-politics word. Quality words are better than romance. They are still marketing.

## Folklore, trial, regulator

- Folklore / ethnography: the bowl, the social calm, the numb mouth, the next-day body.
- Clinical: some German-era trials on anxiety endpoints; quality mixed; do not invent a meta-analysis here.
- Regulator: withdrawals, advisories, later cautious returns in some jurisdictions.

Keep the three on three lines. Do not let a travel writer merge them.

## Against the spa

U.S. "kava bars" and supplement capsules are a fourth object, usually under DSHEA or in a gray hospitality file, not under a Vanuatu village's rules and not under BfArM. They like the word *ceremony*. They do not hold the ceremony. If a magazine photographs a stainless-steel bar in Brooklyn, caption it as Brooklyn.

The liver does not care about the caption. The historian should.
