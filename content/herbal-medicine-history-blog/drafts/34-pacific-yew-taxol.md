# The Pacific Yew and a Forest Argument

The Pacific yew, *Taxus brevifolia*, is a dark understory tree of the Northwest, slow, untheatrical, used by some Indigenous nations for tools and, in limited ways, for medicine. In the 1960s the National Cancer Institute's plant-screening program — a vast, unromantic trawl run with USDA collectors — sent bark to the Research Triangle, where Monroe Wall and Mansukh Wani's group isolated a compound later named paclitaxel. It was hard to get. It was hard to make. It worked, in later trials, on cancers that had few options.

By the early 1990s the tree, the spotted owl's forest, and Bristol-Myers Squibb's CRADA were in the same newspaper paragraph. Arthur Barclay of USDA had collected the original bark in Washington state in 1962; the NCI number was a filing, not a legend. Wall and Wani published the structure in 1971. Susan Horwitz's lab later showed that paclitaxel stabilized microtubules — a mechanism that made the molecule intellectually fashionable as well as clinically stubborn. Fashion does not harvest a tree. Shortage does.

Goodman and Walsh's *The Story of Taxol* is the book that keeps those paragraphs from dissolving into a hero molecule. Read it for the contracts. The molecule will get enough press without you.

## Screening as a historical form

The NCI program vacuumed plants the way a later biotech would vacuum genomes. Most samples did nothing in the assays. The yew did something. That "something" is not a folk validation story. It is a bureaucratic lottery with chemists at the far end. Wall and Wani also isolated camptothecin from a Chinese tree in the same era; the method was the program, not a whisper from a healer, though collectors always stand on someone's land.

## Bark arithmetic

Early paclitaxel came from bark, and the arithmetic was ugly: thousands of trees for a few kilograms, figures the newspapers rounded and the Forest Service argued. Pacific yew had been a "trash tree" in timber language — too small, too slow, in the way of Douglas fir. Overnight it was a national-cancer resource. Environmentalists and some foresters saw a second harvest of old growth just when the Northwest was already in a war over the northern spotted owl and the 1990s timber recession. The Sierra Club and local groups asked why a public forest should be stripped for a private drug. The company and the agency asked why a woman in a ward should wait for a better synthesis.

Chemists hunted needles, Himalayan and European yew relatives, semisynthesis from 10-deacetylbaccatin III in *Taxus baccata*, then plant-cell culture and other routes. Robert Holton's Florida State chemistry and the later Bristol deal are a patent story. The historical point for this series is not the route (and we will not give one). It is that **a "natural product drug" can threaten the natural product**.

Indigenous Northwest use of yew — Haida, Tlingit, and other nations' tool and medicine files — is a separate archive and should not be drafted as the NCI's prologue. If a nation had a bark use, that is theirs to tell. The oncology object is a 1960s–90s industrial-scientific object that happened to need a tree that was already on someone else's land.

## The company and the agency

Bristol-Myers Squibb's deal with the government, the name Taxol as a trademark versus paclitaxel as a generic, the price, the shortage years: this is modern pharmacy, not a woodland fable. Patients waited. Other patients lived. Both are true. A magazine that only prints the survival curves is doing the company's work. A magazine that only prints the stumps is doing a different simplification.

## After the understory

Paclitaxel became a workhorse and then a generic. The Pacific yew is still a tree, less hunted for bark. The screening program's ethics — who consents, who is paid, what is left in the plot — are now written into later protocols that did not govern 1962. When you see a "from the forest" caption on a cancer drug, look for the CRADA and the cubic meters. The molecule is real. So is the cubic meter.
