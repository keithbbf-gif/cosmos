# Withering, the Foxglove, and a Shropshire Secret

William Withering was a Birmingham physician, a Linnaean botanist, and a man who did not like to be gulled. In the 1770s he heard of a Shropshire woman — sometimes called a wise woman, sometimes left unnamed — whose dropsy brew included twenty herbs. He decided the only one that mattered was foxglove, *Digitalis purpurea*, a plant every farmer already knew could kill a sheep and a child. In 1785 he published *An Account of the Foxglove, and Some of Its Medical Uses: with Practical Remarks on Dropsy, and Other Diseases*.

The book is famous because it is careful. It is careful because the plant is not. Withering had already published *A Botanical Arrangement* (1776), a Linnaean flora of Britain that made him a botanist in the eyes of men who did not care about his Birmingham patients. He belonged to the Lunar Society's orbit — Boulton, Watt, Priestley, the kind of Midlands network that treated nature as something you could take apart. Foxglove was not a moonlit mystery in that company. It was a vegetable with a reputation that needed cases.

## Dropsy as a body full of water

"Dropsy" was swelling, breathlessness, the failing pump we would later file under heart failure and renal disease. Physicians tapped, purged, blistered, gave squill, gave mercury. Digitalis, in Withering's hands, seemed to move urine and ease some of the drowned. He numbered cases. He reported the dead without a rhetorical veil. He tried, in a pre-statistical way, to find a form (powdered dried leaf, infusion) and a stopping rule (sickness, vision going green-yellow, the pulse changing). He preferred the leaf gathered at a stated season and dried with a stated care because he had already seen what a sloppy sample did. Those pages are historical documents about **titration as an ethic**. They are not a protocol for a reader.

Foxglove remains a plant that can stop a heart as well as steady one. The glycosides later named (digitoxin, digoxin from *D. lanata* in the twentieth-century industrial shift) are prescription objects with narrow windows. This magazine will not convert 1785 into a tea. A garden foxglove is a pretty poison. The 1785 book is a plea to treat it that way.

## What he stole and what he improved

Taking a folk brew and subtracting nineteen herbs is a kind of theft and a kind of science. The woman had a working, dangerous combination. Withering had case notes, a reputation, and a printer. Historians have worried the ethics in both directions: he erased a woman; he also stopped some of the random killing that comes with an unstandardized poison. Both worries can sit in the same paragraph.

He was already a botanist (*A Botanical Arrangement of All the Vegetables Naturally Growing in Great Britain*, 1776). He could tell *Digitalis* from the neighbors. That skill is the unsexy core of the story. Identification before enthusiasm.

## After the Account

Nineteenth-century medicine made digitalis a standard and then a mess — too many preparations, too much faith, too little assay. The twentieth century isolated cardiac glycosides and then, in many clinics, narrowed the indications. The plant remained in gardens, photogenic and still capable of a 999 call. Cardiology textbooks still nod at 1785 the way pharmacy texts nod at 1804. Nodding is not the same as repeating his infusions.

## The green vision

Withering recorded xanthopsia — yellowed vision — among other toxic signs. Painters and novelists later loved that detail (the van Gogh digitalis rumor is a separate, shaky file). The useful historical image is plainer: a physician at a bedside in the Midlands, watching a pulse, holding a leaf he does not romanticize. He calls the plant a vegetable, not a miracle. That tone is why the book lasted.

If you grow foxglove because it is pretty, good. If you grow it because a blog mentioned Withering and your ankles, stop. The Shropshire secret was never a secret from the plant. The plant was always honest about what it could do.
