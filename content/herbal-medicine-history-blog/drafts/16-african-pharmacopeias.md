# African Pharmacopeias Without the Postcard

The postcard is always the same: a man in profile, a bundle of roots, a caption that says "healer" and names no language. It is a lie of scale. The continent has written medical books, Islamic and Christian, in Ethiopia and along the Swahili coast; it has lineage knowledges that are not books; it has Cape Khoekhoe and San plant skill that Dutch and British collectors filed as "simples"; it has twentieth-century universities that isolated alkaloids from plants their neighbors already used. Abena Dove Osseo-Asare's *Bitter Roots* is the English book that follows four of those plants into labs and courtrooms.

This essay will not survey fifty-four countries. It will refuse the postcard. It will also refuse the opposite laziness: treating "Africa" as a single WHO program. Ethiopia is not Yorubaland. The Cape is not the Swahili coast. The diaspora is not a return ticket.

> **Photo:** A plate of a *named* species from a *named* flora (for example *Cryptolepis sanguinolenta*, *Hoodia gordonii*, or *Catharanthus roseus*) plus a sentence on where the knowledge sat.  
> **Caption:** One plant, one country, one argument. Not "Africa."  
> **License note:** BHL / national floras. No uncredited portraits of living healers.

## Books that are not European

Ethiopia kept medical and magical-medical manuscripts in Ge'ez and later in Amharic: the *Mashafa mawar'et* and related recipe-prayer books, prescriptions, plant names that specialists still argue over. Stefan Strelcyn and later Ethiopianists catalogued more than a tourist wants to admit exists. That is a written pharmacopeia. Treating "African medicine" as purely oral is a way of not learning a script.

Along the Sahel and the Swahili coast, Arabic formularies and local plants met the same way Unani met the monsoon: trade plus a literate pharmacy. Timbuktu's medical manuscripts are not a TED-talk punchline; they are books with owners. In the Gold Coast and the Slave Coast, the literate layer was thinner and the market herbalist thicker; both are histories. None of these is a primitive Dioscorides. They are themselves.

## Lineage knowledge and the problem of the collector

Yoruba plant knowledge sits in a world of *orisa*, markets, and specialists; some of it is public as market herbalism, some of it is not for a magazine. This pack will not print ritual. It will say that colonial ethnographers and later biochemists both treated the public part as a free input. The input had teachers. The papers often listed a species and a solvent, not a teacher.

At the Cape, European botanists (Thunberg, later the Kew networks) named and shipped. Khoekhoe names sometimes stuck as synonyms, more often did not. The later *Hoodia* appetite-suppressant story — San knowledge, a CSIR patent, a benefit-sharing agreement that had to be fought for — is a teaching case Osseo-Asare and journalists have already laid out. The lesson is not "plants work." The lesson is **who gets the contract**.

## Madagascar's flower, Ghana's roots

*Catharanthus roseus*, the Madagascar periwinkle, will have its own essay as an isolate story (vinblastine, vincristine). Put it here first as a reminder: a pretty garden plant with a Malagasy and wider Indian Ocean life was not "discovered" in Ontario. *Cryptolepis* and other West African febrifuges entered colonial and then nationalist laboratories as malaria and infection problems. Results were mixed, politics were not. A newly independent health ministry wanted a plant that was theirs. A company wanted a molecule that was anyone's.

## Diaspora

The Caribbean and the American South hold African diasporic herbalisms that are not "African medicine" in the postcard sense. They are creole systems: West and Central African knowledge under new forests, Christian and Islamic overlays, the brutal laboratory of the plantation. Londa Schiebinger's *Secret Cures of Slaves* is one entry. The entry does not license a tourist to collect.

## The university decade

Independence-era institutes — Mampong in Ghana, later Ibadan and Ife pharmacognosy labs, Madagascar's own ORSTOM and Malagasy teams — tried to turn market plants into papers. That is not "validation of tradition" as a slogan. It is a job, with solvents and arguments about who is first author. Osseo-Asare follows *Cryptolepis*, *Hoodia*, *Aloe*, *Aspergillus* stories (and periwinkle at the edge) into those labs. Read her before you write a caption that says "Africa gave the world."

## How to write this without a safari

Name the language. Name the century. Name the plant. Name the paper or the manuscript. If you cannot name those, you are not ready to caption the photo. The postcard will always be cheaper. It will also always be wrong.
