# Recipes Baked in Clay

The tablet later numbered among the Kuyunjik medical texts is not a hymn, though it sounds like one if you only hear the recitation. It lists plants, woods, fats, and beer. It tells the reader — a trained one, not a passerby — what to crush and what to say. The clay was written in Nineveh's libraries and baked by accident when the city burned. Fire, which ruins paper, made these prescriptions durable.

We do not have the shops. We have the lists. Ashurbanipal's seventh-century libraries at Nineveh, excavated by Layard and Rassam and then catalogued in the British Museum's K series, are why we have so many of them. A royal library is not a village healer's bag. It is the bag as a palace wanted it copied.

## Sumerian names, Akkadian jobs

By the second millennium BCE, Mesopotamian scribes were already living in a bilingual technical world. Sumerian logograms carried prestige and brevity; Akkadian spelled out how a thing was used. The diagnostic series *Sakikkū* (SA.GIG), the therapeutic series that modern editors have tried to reconstruct, the *šammu šikinšu* plant-description texts: these are named scholarly objects, not a pile of "ancient wisdom." A "drug" in these texts is not our over-the-counter category. It is a material with a direction: to bind, to fumigate, to plaster, to drink, to hang at the neck. The last of those bothers modern readers who want pharmacy and religion in separate drawers. The tablets do not keep those drawers.

JoAnn Scurlock, Mark Geller, and Barbara Böck have spent careers showing that some of these recipes are empirically fussy — quantities, substitutions, timing with the moon or with a symptom's hour — while the diagnosis may name a ghost or a god's hand. The ghost is not a decoration. It is part of the causal system. Drop it out to make the tablet look like a proto-PDR and you have edited the document. R. Campbell Thompson's 1924 *Assyrian Herbal* remains a monument of confidence and a minefield of identifications. Use it as a history of 1924.

## What they reached for

Dates, barley, onion-kin, mustard-kin, resins, cedar, myrrh when the trade allowed, mineral bitumen, river clay, animal fats. Thompson's old *Assyrian Herbal* mapped many of these onto Linnaean names with the confidence of 1924. Some mappings still stand. Many do not. When an essay in this pack says "*kasû* has been read as a mustard," that is a scholarly habit, not a field identification you could take to a market in Baghdad and get right every time.

The interesting historical fact does not require the binomial. The interesting fact is that a palace culture already kept **series**: long, copyable medical compilations with incipits, catch-lines, and the same anxiety later pharmacists would know — the anxiety that the next copyist will drop a plant.

## Beer as menstruum

A recurring instruction is to steep or wash a drug in beer or wine. That is chemistry as well as taste. Alcohol and weak acids pull different things from a root than water does. The scribes did not speak of alkaloids. They spoke of strength, of "cutting" a disease, of the drug being "good" when the patient sweated or slept. Outcome language in these texts is brief. They were not writing for a journal. They were writing for the next night shift in a house that smelled of smoke and dates.

## Against the cartoon of superstition

Nineteenth-century translators liked to present Mesopotamian medicine as magic with a few lucky plants attached. The tablets are ruder than that story. They include prognosis that looks like surrender, and they include wound care that looks like attention. They also include materia medica that later Greek and Syriac writers would recognize because the same resins moved west. Influence is not a relay race. It is a market and a translation problem.

If you want a clean origin for "Western pharmacy," you will not find it at Nineveh. You will find a habit: write the recipe down, name the plant as best the script allows, and accept that the god and the poultice are both on the payroll.

The clay outlasted the palace. The identifications are still being argued in journals with very small circulations. That argument is the live part of this history. A confident list of "Sumerian antibiotics" on a wellness blog is the dead part.
