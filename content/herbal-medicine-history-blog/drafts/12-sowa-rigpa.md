# Sowa Rigpa: Medicine on the High Road

Sowa Rigpa means the science of healing in Tibetan. The root text most students still circle is the *Rgyud bzhi* — the *Four Tantras* — a work that presents itself as Buddhist teaching and reads, in long stretches, like a physician's manual: pulse, urine, diet, a materia medica adapted to a world of barley, butter, juniper, and plants that live above the treeline. Indian Ayurvedic categories are audible. So are Chinese pulse habits. So is the plateau.

A medicine of the high road does not need to win a purity contest. It needed to work at altitude, in monasteries and in tents, with a pharmacy that could be dried and carried. Yuthog Yonten Gonpo the Younger is the traditional compiler-name attached to the *Four Tantras* as we have them; historians treat the attribution the way they treat Charaka — a lineage label, not a birth certificate. The Men-Tsee-Khang in Lhasa, and later the exile institute in Dharamsala, are the modern schools that kept the label employed.

## Compilation, not a single founder

Traditional histories name the Medicine Buddha and human translators (Vairocana in the origin stories). Historians argue about the date and the amount of Sanskrit original versus Tibetan composition (twelfth century is a common scholarly neighborhood for the text as we have it; some material is older talk). The *Ashtanga* of Vāgbhaṭa is audible. So is a highland dietetics that Indian plains medicine did not need. The argument matters less for this series than the fact of a **stable teaching picture**: three nyes-pa (rlung, mkhris-pa, bad-kan — wind, bile, phlegm, in the usual rough English), urine analysis as a public art, a pulse that is not exactly Chinese and not exactly Galenic, and a pharmacy of decoctions, powders, and pills.

"Pills" here are not DSHEA capsules. They are compounded, rolled, sometimes blessed, sometimes containing minerals and metals that modern toxicology wants to assay. This essay will not describe how to make a precious pill. It will say that the prestige of *rinchen rilbu* is part of the system's interior hierarchy — and that export versions have raised lead and mercury questions which are laboratory questions, not insults. A laboratory question can coexist with a lineage. It does not dissolve it. It also does not exempt a shop in New York that wants the prestige without the assay.

## What the plateau adds

Juniper smoke, pika and other animal goods in older lists, plants whose Tibetan names still fight for Linnaean pairs, a dietetics obsessed with cold and with barley. Chinese materia entered when politics and trade said so; Indian tropes entered with Buddhism and with scholars. Vincanne Adams and Sienna Craig, among others, have written the contemporary scene without romance: clinics in Lhasa and in exile, NGOs, the Indian government's recognition of Sowa Rigpa, the PRC's institutional TCM-style colleges for Tibetan medicine. Recognition is a double gift. It funds. It also standardizes.

A standardized Tibetan pharmacy is easier to regulate and easier to sell. It may be less like the teacher who knew one valley's gentian.

## Folklore, lineage, clinic

A village remedy, a tantra commentary, and a 21st-century outpatient clinic in Dharamsala are three different evidence claims. The first is ethnographic. The second is scholastic. The third can, in principle, be studied with the tools of outcomes research — and some studies exist, usually small, often on complex formulas, rarely the kind of trial that settles a ministry. Do not inflate them. Do not dismiss the whole craft because the trial is small. Those are two different mistakes, popular in two different magazines.

## Sacred plants, ordinary plants

Some materia sit near ritual. This pack does not publish ritual instructions. Ordinary plants — a cough decoction, a bitter powder — are the larger daily pharmacy and the better subject for a history that does not want to strip-mine a religion for color.

The high road is a real road: Leh to Lhasa, Kathmandu to exile settlements. Medicine moved on it with monks, merchants, and armies. If you need a closing image, use a dried plant in a cloth bundle, labeled in umê script, sitting in a metal box. That object has already traveled farther than a slogan about Shangri-la.
