# WHO, Traditional Medicine, and the Evidence Fight

In September 1978, at Alma-Ata (now Almaty), WHO and UNICEF declared that primary health care included, among other things, the need to work with what people already used. Traditional birth attendants, local plants, local practitioners: the declaration did not romanticize them and did not abolish them. It made them a planning problem. The later *Traditional Medicine Strategy* documents (2002–2005, then 2014–2023) are the bureaucratic grandchildren: words like *integration*, *regulation*, *access*, *rational use*.

Those words are fighting words if you live in a ministry. They are not a verdict on *Hypericum* or *má huáng*. Halfdan Mahler, WHO's director-general in the Alma-Ata years, liked to say that health systems had been built upside down — cathedrals of specialty care, empty dispensaries. Traditional practitioners were already in the empty places. The question was whether Geneva would pretend otherwise.

## Why Geneva cared

Decolonization left states with hospitals they could not staff and a countryside that had never been emptied of *hakims*, herbalists, bonesetters, and midwives. China had already, for national and Mao-era reasons, built "TCM" as a parallel official system (the 1950s colleges, the barefoot-doctor manuals, the English phrase *Traditional Chinese Medicine* as an export). India had the Board of Indian Medicine and later the AYUSH acronym's ancestors — Unani and Ayurveda as gazette objects. African states had a patchwork: Ghana's Centre for Scientific Research into Plant Medicine at Mampong, Nigeria's later councils, a WHO African Regional Office that would spend decades on "TM" programs and model laws.

Alma-Ata gave this a global sentence. The 1970s also had a Western countercultural hunger for the same sentence, which confused the file: a California seeker and a Malian ministry are not one "movement." Critics later said WHO was laundering unproven drugs. Supporters said WHO was refusing to pretend a nurse in a four-room clinic could ignore the market next door. Both can be true in the same district.

Margaret Chan's later WHO, and then Tedros's, kept TM on the program in different weather: intellectual-property fights, the 2019 global report on traditional medicine, a Beijing office flavor that made some Western member states twitch. The twitch is part of the history. So is the fact that a large share of the world's first contacts with care are still not an MD.

## Monographs as a genre

WHO's *monographs on selected medicinal plants* are expert reviews: botany, chemistry, uses, a cautious clinical paragraph, a regulatory paragraph. They are **soft** as proof. They are useful as a map of what an international committee was willing to type. Treating "WHO monograph" as "WHO endorsement of a cure" is a marketing move. Treating them as invisible is a different provincialism.

The evidence fight inside WHO — between people who want RCTs as the only door and people who want "traditional use" as a door — is the same fight as Commission E versus the FDA, in a building with flags.

## Integration's price

When a state "integrates" a tradition, it usually standardizes formulas, licenses schools, and leaves some old practitioners outside the fence (see Kampo insurance, see Sowa Rigpa colleges, see China's TCM hospitals). Integration is not a hug. It is a hiring and a forgetting. WHO documents tend to sound like hugs. Historians should not.

## What this series takes from Geneva

A reminder that herbal history is now, in part, **international administrative history**. A plant can be a village object, a DSHEA object, an EMA traditional-herbal-medicinal-product object, and a WHO-monograph object in the same decade. The objects have different burdens. This pack's job is to name the burden, not to pick a ministry's side.

Alma-Ata's most famous line is about health as a human right. The herbal footnote to that line is less famous: rights do not tell you whether a root works. They tell you the root will still be there when the truck of amoxicillin is late. Policy has to live in that lateness without lying about the root. Lying about the root is the one thing a history series can refuse to help with.
