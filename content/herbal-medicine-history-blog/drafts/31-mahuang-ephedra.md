# Má huáng, Ephedrine, and a Federal Ban

*Má huáng* is a broom-like *Ephedra*, a plant of dry Chinese north, listed in the old *bencao* as a pungent, warming, releasing drug — the sort of simple a classical physician used in a formula for a cold pattern, not as a daily powder in a gym bag. In 1885 the Japanese chemist Nagai Nagayoshi isolated the alkaloid ephedrine from the plant. In the 1920s K. K. Chen and Carl F. Schmidt made the pharmacology audible in English. Asthma and blood-pressure uses entered hospital medicine. The molecule became a drug.

A century after Nagai, U.S. supplement shelves sold "ephedra" for weight loss and sport. Metabolife, Xenadrine, and a cloud of smaller brands put ephedrine alkaloids next to caffeine in bottles that talked like locker-room samizdat. Deaths and strokes entered the FDA's adverse-event file; a 2003 RAND evidence report, commissioned in the argument, did not give the industry the safety story it wanted. Steve Bechler's death in 2003 — a young pitcher, heat, ephedra — was the public name that Congress already had in its hearings. On February 11, 2004, the Food and Drug Administration published a final rule declaring dietary supplements containing ephedrine alkaloids adulterated (69 Fed. Reg. 6788). The gym bag and the *bencao* had parted ways in law.

## Classical use is not a fat-burner

In formula medicine, *má huáng* is a sharp tool, often paired, often time-limited in the logic of the school. That is a historical and ethnographic fact about practice, not a dosing guide. Translating it into a 1990s "thermogenic stack" skipped the school, the pair, and the stopping rules. It kept the alkaloid's kick. DSHEA (1994) had created a lane in which a plant powder could be sold without the drug-approval file ephedrine as a drug already lived in. Marketers drove down the lane.

This is the teaching case for the whole pack's legal section. **A traditional name does not determine a modern category.** Category is a statute.

## What the 2004 rule did and did not do

The rule targeted ephedrine alkaloids in **dietary supplements**. It did not erase *Ephedra* from the biosphere. It did not convert classical Chinese practice into a felony. It did not regulate ephedrine-containing drugs that already sat in a different box (those have their own tight rules, including later combat-methamphetamine statutes that this essay will not map as a how-to). It said: this use, in this legal category, is not acceptable given the risk-benefit the agency applied to supplements.

Industry sued. The Tenth Circuit eventually let the rule stand. The legal remainder is that DSHEA's "significant or unreasonable risk" clause (21 U.S.C. § 342(f)) is not a dead letter if the agency is willing to build a file. Most botanicals never get that file. Ephedra got one because it behaved like a street stimulant with a plant name.

Chinese classical formulas that include *má huáng* continue in licensed practice in other countries under other boxes (Kampo *maoto*, TCM hospital decoctions). Those boxes have their own cautions. They are not the 1990s American bottle. Flattening them into one "ephedra ban" is how a gym scandal colonizes a literature.

Traditional herbalists in the U.S. argued that the agency had smashed a classical simple to punish a stimulant industry. The agency argued that the market in front of it was the stimulant industry. Both sentences describe a real collision. The Federal Register file, the RAND review, and the GAO paper trail are the evidence. Internet lore is not.

## Chen and Schmidt's hospital

The 1920s papers treated ephedrine as a possible oral relative of adrenaline's world — asthma, a pressor when needed. That is drug history, with the usual later narrowing as better agents arrived. It is the missing middle in stories that jump from the Han to the FDA. Molecules spend decades as ordinary medicines before they spend a decade as a scandal.

## Write it without a sneer at either room

Do not sneer at the *bencao*. Do not sneer at the agency for reading death and stroke reports. Sneer, if you must, at the label that put a classical name on a product whose actual community was a message-board stack. The plant is dry, old, and indifferent. The paperwork is where the history is.
