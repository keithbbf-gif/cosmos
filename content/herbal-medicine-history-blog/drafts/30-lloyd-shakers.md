# Shaker Farms and Lloyd's Crude Drugs

The Shakers — the United Society of Believers in Christ's Second Appearing — sold more than chairs. In New Lebanon, New York, and in other villages, they ran a medicinal-herb industry: gathered and cultivated plants, dried them in dedicated houses, packed them in tidy papers and tins with a reputation for not cheating the weight. Physicians in the mid-nineteenth century could order a Shaker package and expect the label to match the leaf. That reputation was the product.

In Cincinnati, John Uri Lloyd (1849–1936), pharmacist, novelist, and stubborn chemist of hydro-alcoholic extracts, supplied the Eclectic physicians with Specific Medicines and filled a library that still sits on Plum Street as a research collection. He argued with the German-style alkaloid habit when he thought the whole plant extract was the drug. Sometimes he was romantic. Sometimes he was right that the assay of a single marker misses the preparation physicians were actually using.

## Purity as a theology and a brand

Shaker herb circulars — New Lebanon, Hancock, Canterbury, Union Village — are beautiful in the way a well-made crate is beautiful. The 1850s–80s catalogs list hundreds of dried plants, extracts, and the famous Shaker garden seeds that funded other work. The theology of the villages — celibacy, confession, dance, separation from "the world" — is not a pharmacy topic except in this: a community that already lived by exactness applied exactness to peppermint and lettuce opium and dandelion. They were not "closer to nature" in the advertising sense. They were closer to a **standard**. Physicians wrote to the villages because the regular wholesale houses cheated. When the villages aged out and the industry shrank, the standard went with the people.

Collectors now pay for the tins. The tins were meant to be empty at the end of winter. A museum tin that is still full is a failure of the original job.

## Lloyd's quarrel with the crystal

Lloyd could isolate. He chose, often, not to reduce the drug to the isolate. The Eclectic "specific" idea was: a preparation trusted to a clinical picture, made the same way next year. That is closer to Kampo's insured extract than to a 1990s single-herb capsule, and closer to both than to a Galenic gravy of seventy names. Lloyd's *Stringtown on the Pike* is a novel; do not file it as a manual. His scientific papers and the *Lloyd Brothers* catalogs are the file.

Echinacea's American career runs through this world. Plains Indigenous use (various nations, various species) is the older file and must be named as such, not as a prologue to a Cincinnati product. The Eclectics then made *Echinacea* a house drug for "bad blood" and infection in their idiom. The late-twentieth-century cold-capsule is a third object, with trial results that are mixed and should be cited, not hummed.

## Crude drugs as a vanished trade

A "crude drug" is a dried plant or animal or mineral part still recognizable. Pharmacy schools once taught them in jars. The jars went to storage when the prescription became a manufactured tablet. Something was gained (reproducibility). Something was lost (the habit of looking). The Lloyd Library kept the looking. So did a few economic-botany museums. This series is on their side, without pretending a jar of senna is a modern standard of care.

## Two Americas

The Shaker village and Lloyd's Cincinnati were not the same America. One was a celibate sect on good farmland. One was a river-city German-professional pharmacy with a taste for argument. They meet in the package: a plant, a label, a promise that the inside matches the ink. 1906 would make that promise a statute. They had already been practicing it as pride.

No tin in this essay is a recommendation to self-treat. The recommendation is to visit the library if you are in Cincinnati, and to look at a Shaker label if a museum in the Berkshires will show you one. Pride in a label is a historical feeling we could use more of, especially in aisles that now promise "ancient wisdom" without a village or a chemist.
