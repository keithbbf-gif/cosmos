# 1906: When the Label Became a Law

On June 30, 1906, Theodore Roosevelt signed the Pure Food and Drug Act — the Wiley Act, in the habit of the Bureau of Chemistry. The statute did not say that patent medicines were finished. It said that if you put a drug or a food into interstate commerce, the label could not be false or misleading on certain particulars, and that a short list of dangerous ingredients (alcohol, morphine, opium, cocaine, heroin, chloroform, cannabis indica, chloral hydrate, acetanilide) had to be named.

The revolution was bibliographic. The bottle had to start telling a kind of truth. Samuel Hopkins Adams's *Collier's* series, "The Great American Fraud" (1905–06), had already named the alcohol-and-opium math for a mass readership. The American Medical Association kept a propaganda bureau that regulars called educational and nostrum men called a trust. Wiley had the Bureau of Chemistry. Sinclair had the stockyards. The statute is what you get when those files hit the same month.

## Wiley, Sinclair, and the two stomachs

Harvey Washington Wiley, chief chemist of the Department of Agriculture, Indiana-born, a chemist who treated borax as a moral problem, had spent years bullying Congress with data and with theater. His "hygienic table trials" — young clerks eating meals laced with preservatives — became the Poison Squad in the papers. The squad's menus are in the FDA's own history office: breakfast, then a measured adulterant, then notes on whether a man could still work. It is not a modern IRB protocol. It is how a bureau got a cartoon and a law.

Upton Sinclair's *The Jungle* (1906) was a socialist novel about labor that the country read as a book about sausage. Sinclair said he had aimed at the public's heart and hit its stomach. Roosevelt, who could live without socialism, could not live with the mail about meat. The Meat Inspection Act and the Wiley Act passed in the same political weather. Food and drugs rode through the same door because they shared a problem: interstate industrial stuff no local inspector could smell in time.

The American Medical Association and the muckrakers had already named the nostrum trade. 1906 gave them a federal hook. It did not give them a monopoly on virtue. Wiley later broke with successors who seemed to him too friendly to industry. Agencies have afterlives.

## What the Act could not do

It could not require that a drug work. Efficacy as a legal demand arrives later, in pieces, and with a bang after 1962 (Kefauver–Harris). It could not, by itself, create a modern FDA; the name and the power grow through the 1938 Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act, written in the shadow of Massengill's elixir sulfanilamide, which killed because the solvent was diethylene glycol and no safety file existed. 1938 is a different essay's center. Mention it so 1906 does not look like the end of history.

1906 also could not make states identical. Pharmacies and doctors still lived in state law. Narcotics would get their own federal tax-and-control logic in 1914 (next essay).

## Cannabis on the list

The 1906 list of discloseable ingredients includes *cannabis indica*. That is not the 1937 Marijuana Tax Act and not the 1970 schedule. It is a recognition that cannabis was an ordinary American pharmaceutical (tinctures, corn plasters, cough goods) that the buyer had a right to see named. The later criminal story (essay 37) is a change of political weather, not a botanical surprise.

## Why herbal history needs the statute

Because after 1906 a "vegetable compound" is a legal utterance. You can still lie in a newspaper ad for a while. You have a new problem on the bottle. The supplement aisle of the 1990s would invent yet another utterance ("structure/function"). The habit — the state grabbing the label as the place of truth — starts here in federal form.

Wiley is not a saint. He was a chemist with a moral style and enemies in industry. The Act is not a complete food system. It is the moment the United States decided that a national market needed a national sentence on the package. Herbal bottles were in that market. They still are.
