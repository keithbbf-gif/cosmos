# Peyote, Ayahuasca, and the Scheduled Sacrament

*Lophophora williamsii*, peyote, is a small cactus of the Rio Grande borderlands with a pre-Columbian and a long Indigenous ceremonial life. The Native American Church, coalescing in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, made the cactus a sacrament in a Christian-Indigenous form that federal and state law spent decades trying to police. Weston La Barre's *The Peyote Cult* (1938) is an early anthropological book and a dated one; pair it with nation authors and with Stacy Schaefer and Peter Furst's edited volume. The 1994 amendments to the American Indian Religious Freedom Act finally put a specific federal protection on the books for NAC peyote use.

*Banisteriopsis caapi* and *Psychotria viridis* (and related pairs), the ayahuasca of western Amazonian peoples, became, in the twentieth century, a church object as well — Santo Daime, União do Vegetal — and then an export spiritual commodity. In 2006 the U.S. Supreme Court, in *Gonzales v. O Centro Espírita Beneficente União do Vegetal*, held that RFRA required the government to allow the UDV's sacramental hoasca. The opinion, written by Chief Justice Roberts, is a legal text about burden and sincerity under the Religious Freedom Restoration Act. It is not a field guide. It does not bless a retreat center. It says the government had not shown a compelling interest that could not be served by something narrower than a total ban on the UDV's tea.

## Two plants, two legal stories

Peyote's U.S. story is bound to Native sovereignty, to the reservation system, to Comanche, Kiowa, Navajo, and other nations' different relationships with the cactus, and to a church that had to explain itself to white courts. Quanah Parker is the name popular histories attach to the early NAC; the church is larger than one man. *Employment Division v. Smith* (1990) made the legal weather worse; the 1994 AIRFA amendments were Congress's specific repair for peyote. Conservation is now part of the file: *Lophophora* is slow, the Texas and Mexican populations have been poached, and the NAC itself has had to talk about shortage. Non-Native "seeking" is a pressure on a finite population. This magazine will not help the pressure.

Ayahuasca's U.S. story is bound to Brazilian churches (UDV founded 1961; Santo Daime older in Acre), to RFRA after *Smith*, to a later wellness tourism that the 2006 opinion did not bless. Richard Evans Schultes's mid-century Amazon collecting put the vine into North American botany; the churches put it into North American law. Labate and Cavnar's volumes map the boom; read them as a warning as much as a guide. Amazonian nations are not a backdrop. Shipibo, Asháninka, and many others have their own names and rules, which a Brooklyn flyer does not inherit by buying a plane ticket.

## Scheduling as a blunt instrument

The Controlled Substances Act and the earlier treaties sort molecules and plants into lists. Sacrament does not appear as a botanical rank. Courts then spend years carving holes: NAC, UDV, sometimes others. The hole is a legal category ("sincere religious exercise"). It is not an herbal monograph. Confusing the two is how a sacrament becomes a product.

Mescaline and DMT as named compounds are twentieth-century laboratory objects (Heffter; later chemists this essay will not follow). The plants are older. The isolate path here is the same path as morphine, with a different church at the end instead of a different hospital.

## What this pack will not do

- No ritual steps, songs, or "set and setting" tutorials.
- No doses, no brew ratios, no "how to tell a good shaman."
- No maps to harvesting sites.
- No joke about "the real ancient psychedelics."

History is allowed: dates, statutes, case names, the fact of a church, the fact of a schedule, the fact of a conservation problem. That is already a long essay. The rest is someone else's business.

## A closing that is not a blessing

The older pharmacopeias in this series put sacred and ordinary plants on the same shelf and let the priest and the compounder fight. Modern U.S. law put some sacred plants on a police shelf and then, under pressure, wrote exceptions. The exceptions are narrow on purpose. A history series that treats them as a trend piece about "the psychedelic renaissance" has walked out of the archive and into a marketing calendar.

If you need a last object, use the U.S. Reports page or a NAC legal brief, not a cactus in a pot. The brief is the thing this country actually made.
