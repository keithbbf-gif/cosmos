# Coca Before the Isolate

In the Andes, a handful of dried *Erythroxylum* leaves with a pinch of alkaline ash is a work tool, a ritual object, and a courtesy. Miners chew. Travelers chew. Offerings include the leaf. The Inca state, the Spanish colony, and the republican governments all tried, in different centuries, to tax, bless, or restrict it. None of them were talking about a white powder. The powder is a German laboratory event of 1860.

Confusing the leaf with the isolate is the central trick of a hundred years of policy prose. This essay will not do the trick. *Erythroxylum coca* and *E. novogranatense* are cultivated plants with regional varieties (Bolivian *E. coca* var. *coca*, Amazonian *ipadu*, Colombian and Trujillo types). The taxonomy is a specialist fight. The historical claim is simpler: people of the Andes domesticated a leaf that could be dried, carried, and shared, and they did it long before a Göttingen dissertation.

## A plant with a state

Pre-Hispanic polities used coca as a prestige good and as a ration. The Inca state's control of coca lands on the *yungas* slopes is a standard ethnohistoric file — tribute, storage, gifts to officials, offerings to *huacas*. Spanish chroniclers — Cieza de León, Garcilaso de la Vega, José de Acosta, later Bernabé Cobo — could not leave it alone. They moralized (idolatry, the Devil's plant). They also noticed that a man with leaves could work a shift at altitude that a man without them hated. The Third Council of Lima and various viceroys tried restriction; the mines at Potosí made restriction look expensive. Colonial *obrajes* and the *mita* labor draft turned a courtesy leaf into a ration. The sacred and the extractive sat in the same basket. They still do, in different legal languages.

Paul Gootenberg's *Andean Cocaine* is the book that follows the later commodity without pretending the leaf was waiting to become a drug problem. The leaf had its own economy — *cocales*, taxes, the republican *estanco*. The isolate created another economy that would, by the late twentieth century, try to burn the first.

## What "use" meant

Chewing coca with an alkali (llipta, quinoa ash, burnt shells, depending on the valley) is a cultural technique. It is not a recipe this magazine will improve. Historically, users described less hunger, less fatigue, a sweeter mouth, a way to share time. Those are first-person reports, not FDA endpoints. Priests and *curanderos* used the leaf in contexts this essay will not instruct. Folklore and daily labor are both real. They are not a clinical trial of cocaine.

The Spanish sometimes filed coca under idolatry, sometimes under pharmacy. Jesuit and royal pharmacies in Lima and farther afield experimented with wines and tinctures. That is the beginning of a European medical fashion that would, in the 1860s–80s, become Vin Mariani and then something much harder.

## 1860, Göttingen

Albert Niemann, working from coca sent to Friedrich Wöhler's orbit, isolated the alkaloid he named cocaine. That sentence is the hinge of the later essays on regulation and on patent drinks. It is not a how-to. The isolation made it possible to dose a single compound, to inject it, to turn it into a patent and a vice. The leaf remained in the Andes, still chewed, still offered, increasingly criminalized by treaties written in other cities.

A history that starts in 1860 is a history of the isolate. A history that starts with the leaf is a history of Andean religion, labor, and botany. This pack needs both, in separate tones.

## Policy prose

The Single Convention and later U.S. scheduling flattened a lot of plants into "narcotics." Bolivia and Peru have spent decades trying to get the leaf recognized as a cultural good distinct from cocaine. Whether a given statute manages that distinction is a legal question. The botanical and historical distinction is not subtle. One is a leaf with a set of alkaloids in a matrix people chew. The other is a refined product with a different speed, a different market, and a different body count.

Write the leaf as a leaf. Leave the clandestine chemistry to the police files, where it belongs.
