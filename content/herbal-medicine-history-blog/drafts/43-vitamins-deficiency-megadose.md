# Vitamins: From Deficiency Diseases to Megadose Politics

James Lind's *A Treatise of the Scurvy* (Edinburgh, 1753) reports a shipboard comparison of cider, elixir of vitriol, vinegar, seawater, oranges and lemons, and a medicinal paste. Twelve sailors, six treatments, a result that later textbooks flattened into "Lind invented the clinical trial and discovered vitamin C." He did something rarer and smaller: he wrote down a comparison on a Royal Navy problem and preferred the citrus. He did not know ascorbic acid. He did not end scurvy in the Navy overnight. The Navy's later issue of lemon (then lime) juice is a long administrative story of its own.

In 1912 Casimir Funk, working on rice-bran fractions and beriberi, proposed *vitamines* — amines essential to life. The *e* later dropped. The class held: A, B-complex, C, D, E, K, the deficiency diseases that had been moralized as dirt or sin (pellagra in the American South; Goldberger's work is the brutal, necessary file).

This is not, strictly, herbal history. It is the neighboring category that taught twentieth-century publics to swallow a capsule "for health" when they were not ill in the old way. Cod-liver oil, rose hips, yeast cakes, the wartime orange-juice poster: plants and animals as vehicles, then the vehicle dropped. DSHEA's aisle is unthinkable without that lesson. A reader who thinks "supplements" began with echinacea in 1996 has missed the deficiency century.

## Deficiency as a scientific object

Christiaan Eijkman's chickens in the Dutch East Indies, fed polished rice and then bran, gave beriberi a laboratory. Gowland Hopkins wrote of "accessory factors." Elmer McCollum's rats, at Wisconsin and then Johns Hopkins, sorted fat-soluble A from water-soluble B. Joseph Goldberger, in U.S. Public Health Service work that still reads like a moral novel, showed that pellagra in Southern mill towns was a diet disease, not a contagion of the "poor stock" — and paid for the claim in professional insult. The isolation decades followed: C from paprika and adrenals (Szent-Györgyi and others), D from sterols and sunlight, B12 from liver and then bacteria.

This is textbook biochemistry and it earned its pride. Rickets and cod-liver oil, night blindness and liver, beriberi and bran — plants and animals as carriers of a molecule no one could yet name. Once the molecule was named, the carrier became optional. That is the same move as Sertürner's crystal, applied to food.

The pride goes wrong when it forgets that a deficiency disease is a specific, ugly, named thing. "Support" is not scurvy. A sailor's gums and a catalog's "immune blend" should not share a sentence.

## Pauling and the megadose

Linus Pauling, double Nobel, spent his later public life arguing for gram-level vitamin C against colds and then cancer. The argument is a historical event. The trial record did not give him the victory he wanted. This essay will not relitigate every study. It will say that **a famous scientist can become a supplement movement** and that movements do not dissolve when the *NEJM* is unkind. The 1970s vitamin counter was already political before Pauling; he gave it a halo.

## Proxmire's statute

Senator William Proxmire's 1976 amendment (essay 40's prequel) was the moment Congress told FDA that high-potency vitamins were not, by potency alone, drugs. The hearing record is a carnival of American beliefs about the body. It is also a preview of 1994. Once the vitamin is a political constituent, the botanical can ride behind it wearing the same "don't touch my bottle" T-shirt.

## Why the herbal series keeps this essay

Because readers will file "herbs and supplements" as one cultural object. History should unfile them: plants with old texts, isolates with benches, vitamins with deficiency diseases, DSHEA with a Senate. The capsule is the false friend that makes them look alike. Lind's sailors were not taking a multi. They were dying of a diet. The difference is the whole moral of the deficiency era.

No doses here. If you have a named deficiency, you have a clinician and a lab value. If you have a catalog, you have a politics. Lind, at least, counted twelve men. Count that high before you trust a catalog.
