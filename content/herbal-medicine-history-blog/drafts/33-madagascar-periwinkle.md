# A Garden Flower and Two Cancer Alkaloids

*Catharanthus roseus* (older tags say *Vinca rosea*) is a small, stubborn, pink or white flower that escaped gardens and went roadside in the tropics. Madagascar is the evolutionary home. The plant's folk uses — in various countries, for various complaints, including a reputation around blood sugar — are a scattered ethnographic file, not a single "Malagasy secret." In the 1950s, at the University of Western Ontario, Robert Noble, Charles Beer, and J. H. Cutts were looking at the plant for endocrine effects and found instead a collapse of white cells in rats. Eli Lilly's industrial development turned two alkaloids, vinblastine and vincristine, into chemotherapy.

Childhood leukemia survival numbers later printed in textbooks have this flower in their ancestry. Madagascar's treasury does not. The plant had already traveled as an ornamental — French colonial gardens, British glasshouses, Jamaican yards, Indian roadsides — before anyone in London, Ontario, ordered a sack. Cosmopolitan beauty is how a source country gets written out of the methods section: the flower was "available." Available is a political word.

## What they were looking for, what they found

The Ontario group was not hunting a cure in the marketing sense. They were chasing a plant rumor about glucose — a Caribbean and Philippine folk use had reached Western journals as a hypoglycemic hint — and a scientific question about hormones. Noble had been a colleague of Collip, of insulin fame; the lab knew how to think about extracts and blood sugar. The bone-marrow effect in rats was the surprise. White cells fell. A plant that was supposed to be a metabolic rumor became a hematology problem.

Surprise is how a lot of cytotoxic drugs enter the world. It is not a method you can industrialize by wandering a garden. Charles Beer did the chemistry that turned a biological observation into named alkaloids (vinblastine first, vincristine in the Lilly collaboration). Irving Johnson and the Indianapolis group ran the development that made Oncovin and Velban hospital words. The papers sit in *Canadian Cancer Conference* volumes and in *Cancer Chemotherapy Reports*, not in airport bookshops.

This essay will not sketch extractions. The historical claim: **a whole-plant folk reputation and a laboratory accident are not the same event as a drug**, and the drug is not the same event as justice.

## Two alkaloids, two careers

Vinblastine and vincristine are neighbors on a molecule and not twins in the ward. One became a Hodgkin and testicular-regimen workhorse; the other, in combination, changed acute lymphoblastic leukemia in children from a short sentence to a long protocol. Those protocol histories belong to oncology, with toxicities (neuropathy, marrow) that any honest hospital story has to name. They do not belong to a garden-center caption. The flower on a balcony in Antananarivo is not a chemo bag. Saying so is not poetry. It is category hygiene.

## The missing contract

Unlike the later (still imperfect) conversations around *Hoodia* or some Nagoya-protocol cases, the 1950s vinca story has almost no benefit-sharing with Madagascar. The plant was already cosmopolitan. The law was not yet designed for a source country. Those two facts get used as a shrug. A shrug is not an analysis. A cosmopolitan plant can still have a center of diversity and a national claim. The claim was not heard.

Osseo-Asare's *Bitter Roots* is about other species, but her question travels: who is listed as author, who as supplier of "botanical material."

## Folklore versus oncology

A grandmother's periwinkle tea for a metabolic complaint and a vincristine infusion in a children's ward share a species and share almost nothing else — dose, purity, intent, risk, evidence. Collapsing them into "the natives knew it treated cancer" is a lie that flatters everyone except the patient. The folk file, as far as the mid-century papers show, was not an oncology file. The Ontario rats were the hinge.

## After the flower

The drugs remain specialist, toxic, prescription, hospital. The flower remains a garden annual. The ethical remainder remains unpaid. If a magazine wants a useful ending, it is not a sunset over Antananarivo. It is a sentence in a methods section: *plant material obtained from…* and then the silence after the ellipsis. Learn to read the silence.
