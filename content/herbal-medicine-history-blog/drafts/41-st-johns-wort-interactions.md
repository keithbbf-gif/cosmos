# St. John's Wort and the Interaction Problem

*Hypericum perforatum* is a yellow-flowered plant of roadsides, named in English for a saint and a season, used for a very long time as a wound herb and, in various European folk and physician files, as a plant for "melancholy." German physicians and the Commission E monographs of the late twentieth century gave it a modern medical-herbal life. In the 1990s the United States met it as a dietary supplement for mood. Magazines (not this one, not in this voice) printed sunshine.

Then transplant pharmacists started finding ciclosporin levels on the floor. Women found breakthrough bleeding on pills that had been adequate. The mechanism, worked out in a run of papers around 1999–2000, was induction of CYP3A4 and P-glycoprotein — the plant was speeding the disposal of other drugs. *Lancet* and *Clinical Pharmacology & Therapeutics* are the places to start, not a forum post.

The plant did not become "bad." It became **a perpetrator of interactions**, which is a more adult category. Hypericin and hyperforin are the constituents later reviews argue over; this essay will not pick a favorite molecule for a reader to chase. The historical event is the clinic noticing a hole in a trough, then the kineticists naming an enzyme.

## Old wound, new indication

Dioscorides and the Latin herbals know a *Hypericum* for burns and bites; the perforate leaf held to the light is an old identification trick. Paracelsus and the German *Johanniskraut* file give it a midsummer and a saint. The melancholy career is real and later, more northern, more nineteenth- and twentieth-century official — a plant for "nerves" in the same cupboard as valerian. Commission E, in the late twentieth century, is an expert-monograph system, **soft** if you treat it as a set of trials. Some randomized studies on mild depression exist (the Linde reviews are the usual entry); they are mixed in quality and in result, and they are not a license to dump a transplant drug. A magazine should not pick a favorite study and call it settled.

The historical event of the 1990s is the scale: a European herbal became an American mass-market capsule under DSHEA, sold at checkout next to aspirin, without the German habit of treating it as a medicine with a professional insert. *Newsweek* sunshine and *Lancet* letters can occupy the same twelve months.

## The transplant clinic as a truth machine

A ciclosporin trough is a number a pharmacist believes. When the number falls after a patient starts a "natural mood" capsule, the argument ends. Oral contraceptives, some antiretrovirals, some cardiac drugs: the list grew as people looked. This essay will not print a personal management chart. Interaction lists go stale and they belong in a professional monograph. The historical claim is that **herb–drug interaction left the folklore file and entered the kinetic file**.

If a reader is on a narrow-therapeutic-index drug, the adult sentence is: tell the clinician about any botanical product. That is not a protocol for the botanical. It is a description of how modern pharmacy has to work after 1999.

## What the case did to the aisle

It gave FDA and the journals a teaching story that was not ephedra's cardiac story. It made "natural" look, to clinical pharmacologists, like "unlabeled inducer." Industry learned to put a caution on some Hypericum labels. Many did not. DSHEA does not require a full interaction insert.

Europe, having already treated kava as a medicine that can be withdrawn, treated St. John's wort as a medicine that needs warnings. The two systems (U.S. supplement, EU herbal medicine) produced different paper around the same plant. The plant is the constant. The paper is the history.

## No sunshine, no panic

Panic copy ("never touch this plant") is as stupid as sunshine copy ("nature's Prozac"). *Hypericum* remains a botanical with a real pharmacology. The 1990s mistake was selling that pharmacology as if it were a feeling and not a set of enzymes. Enzymes are not a vibe. They are why a yellow flower can empty a transplant drug out of a blood test.

Gerard's woodcut is still pretty. File it next to the 1999 letter, not next to a sunset.
