# Paracelsus and the Argument About Dose

Theophrastus Bombastus von Hohenheim (c. 1493–1541), who called himself Paracelsus, burned a canon — or had students burn one — at Basel and made a career of insulting dead physicians and living apothecaries. He wanted chemistry in the clinic: metals, mineral acids, distilled oils, antimony, mercury, opium in his own laudanums. He wanted the "inner alchemist" of the body. He wanted, loudly, to be right.

He was born in Einsiedeln, wandered as a military surgeon, quarreled in Basel in 1527–28, and died in Salzburg in 1541. The name Paracelsus was a dare at Celsus. Later toxicology printed him on posters: *Sola dosis facit venenum* — the dose makes the poison. The sentence is a fair compression of things he wrote in the *Sieben Defensiones* and related tracts. It is not a license to take a poison carefully at home. It is not, by itself, a modern dose-response curve. It is a sixteenth-century demolition of the idea that a substance has a moral essence independent of quantity and use.

## Against the gravy of names

Paracelsus looked at theriac and saw a coward's pantry. Why seventy ingredients if one *arcanum* will do? The attack on Galenic compounding is the part of him that still feels modern. It is also unfair. A compound can be a delivery system. He preferred a philosophy in which a disease had a specific chemical enemy, often a mineral one: mercury for syphilis in his idiom, antimony, salts, the *tria prima* of sulfur, mercury, and salt as a way to talk about bodies. Specificity is a powerful idea. It would later feed the isolate era (morphine, quinine, salicylic acid). He did not live to weigh those crystals. He lived in a world of impure salts and grand claims.

His mercury and antimony cures killed and saved in ratios we cannot audit. Patients with the great pox were already dying; a metal course could hurry or delay the dying. Iatrochemistry after him — Jan Baptist van Helmont, the Danish and German chymists, later the London pharmacopeia's uneasy mineral pages — kept the argument going until something like chemistry arrived to make the argument testable. The 1618 *London Pharmacopoeia* already had to decide which of his heirs to officialize. That decision is a committee's Paracelsus, not his.

## What "dose" meant in his mouth

It meant that a substance is not morally a poison or a remedy in the abstract. Context and quantity decide. That is a useful demolition of magical thinking about "natural = safe" and "mineral = death." It is also, in his practice, mixed up with signatures, stars, and a cosmology that a chemistry department would not fund. Quoting only the dose line is how you turn a sixteenth-century prophet into a safety officer.

When a supplement advert says "it's the dose that matters" next to a picture of a leaf, it is borrowing his prestige to dodge a different question: whether the leaf does anything at the dose in the capsule, and whether the capsule is a drug or a DSHEA object. Paracelsus cannot answer DSHEA. He is dead, and he was not in the Senate.

## Opium and the chemical shop

He used opium. So did everyone with a night practice. His contribution was to keep it inside a chemical rhetoric rather than only a Galenic one. The later history of laudanum (Sydenham's, then the patent centuries) owes more to English empirics than to Basel theatrics, but the theatrics made chemistry sound like a moral reform of the shop.

## How to keep him without the cosplay

No hooded "alchemist" stock photo. No "father of toxicology" without a wince — the fathers are many, and the children are inspectors with statutes. Keep the fight with Galen. Keep the stubborn idea that a single active might beat a paste of prestige. Then walk, in this pack, into Withering and Sertürner, who had the bad manners to measure.

The dose makes the poison. The archive makes the historian. Neither sentence tells you how much of anything to swallow.
