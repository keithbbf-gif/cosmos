# Galen's Compounds and the Humoral Shop

Galen of Pergamon liked to win. He won in the arena of demonstration, in the anatomy of animals he cut in public, and in the long run of the curriculum. For pharmacy he left a machine: every simple drug has a temperament — a degree of heat, cold, moisture, dryness — and the compounder's job is to match that temperament to a patient's imbalance. The machine is elegant. It is also a way to be wrong with perfect consistency.

He practiced in Rome in the second century CE, treated Marcus Aurelius's circle (and boasted about it), and wrote enough that later centuries could not lift the pile. Born in Pergamon in 129, trained in Smyrna and Alexandria, resident in the capital with a gap for a plague year, dead around 216. The *Method of Medicine*, the books on simples (*De simplicium medicamentorum temperamentis*), the books on antidotes: these became the furniture of the shop. A man who writes that much forces the shop to take a position.

## Simples and the degrees

A "simple" is a single substance. Galen graded many of them in degrees (a first-degree heater, a third-degree dryer). The numbers feel quantitative. They were not thermometer readings. They were a trained consensus, argued from taste, from what a drug seemed to do to urine or pulse, from older authority. Once the number existed, a compounder could do arithmetic: this cold plant offsets that hot disease. The arithmetic traveled better than Galen's actual bedside notes. It could be taught in Toledo and in Paris without a Mediterranean garden.

That is why humoral pharmacy lasted. It was portable. It was also why a northern student could assign a degree to a plant he had never smelled. Portability is a virtue in a curriculum and a vice in a field. Vivian Nutton's *Ancient Medicine* is the book that keeps Galen a physician in a city, not a floating system. The system still floated. That is the joke history played on him.

## The compound as an institution

Galen did not invent compounding. He professionalized a taste for it. Electuaries, plasters, oils, the great antidotes: the shop became a place where twenty ingredients could be virtuous because the theory said they covered the aspects of a disease. Later critics — Paracelsus among the loudest — would call this a gravy of names. They were not always fair. A compound can be a way to make a bitter root takeable, or a way to hide that you do not know which ingredient matters. Both things can be true in the same pot.

The medieval and early modern *officina* that still looks charming in photographs — rows of jars, Latin abbreviations — is a Galenic room even when the owner has never read Galen in Greek. The abbreviations are the residue of his victory.

## What he got from the east, and what he standardized

Galen used the materia of a wide empire: balsams, minerals, Egyptian and Syrian goods. He also used a Greek intellectual habit of total explanation. Dioscorides could leave a use hanging as "they say." Galen preferred to know why. The preference produced brilliance and forced errors. When the plant in a northern forest was not the plant in the text, the degrees were still applied. Students learned to trust the number.

Islamic physicians would keep the machine and rebuild the garden (Ibn Sīnā's *Canon* is unthinkable without Galenic architecture). Latin scholastics would do the same. The Renaissance would publish Galen in Greek and then begin, slowly, to walk away from the humors without walking away from the jars.

## A theory that outlived its anatomy

Galen's anatomy fell in the sixteenth century under Vesalius's corrections. His pharmacy took longer to fall, because a theory of qualities does not need a correct liver. It needs a classroom and a clientele that wants a reason. Even now, when a wellness writer says a food is "heating" or "cooling," a distant cousin of that classroom is speaking, usually without a degree chart and without Galen's ferocious demand for training.

This series will not revive humoral prescribing. It will say that for a very long time, the shop was a philosophical instrument. The plants were real. The arithmetic was a story about them. Stories that last a thousand years are not trivial. They are also not evidence in the sense a modern regulator means. Keep both facts in the same paragraph.
