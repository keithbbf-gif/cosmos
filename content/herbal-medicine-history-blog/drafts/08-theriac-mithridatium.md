# Theriac, or How to Outlive a King

Mithridates VI of Pontus spent his life trying not to be poisoned. The later story says he took a daily mash of antidotes so mixed that when he wanted to die, ordinary poison would not do the work. The mash, or a descendant of it, became *mithridatium*. A related imperial compound, *theriac* — the name points at wild beasts and at vipers — became something stranger: a public, regulated, expensive paste that cities used as a proof they had a real pharmacy.

You could buy a jar and feel yourself in the line of a king. That was the point as much as the viper. Mithridates VI Eupator ruled Pontus in the first century BCE, fought Rome, and lost to Pompey. The later pharmacists needed him more than he needed them. A king who tried to make himself unpoisonable is a perfect founder for a shop that sells fear in honey.

## Andromachus and the viper

Nero's physician Andromachus the Elder is credited with revising the great antidote, adding viper flesh, tightening the poem of ingredients. Galen wrote about these pastes as if they were state machinery. The ingredient lists that survive in later pharmacopeias run to fifty, seventy, more than a hundred names: balsams, myrrh, opium, squill, cinnamon when the ship came in, honey to bind. This essay will not reconstruct a working formula. The historical fact is the length. Length was authority.

Opium in the mix is the part a modern reader should not skip. Theriac was, among other things, a way to sell a small, chronic opiate inside a prestige brand. People felt better. They would.

## Venice, and the theater of mixing

By the later Middle Ages and the Renaissance, "Venetian theriac" — *teriaca andromachi* — was a trademark in all but law. The *Collegio degli Speziali* mixed it in public on a set day in the piazza, sometimes in front of the ducal officials, with the ingredients laid out like a proof. Jars were sealed. The *protomedico* system in Italian and Spanish cities made the paste an object of inspection. Counterfeits were a business, because anything famous is counterfeited; a "Venetian" jar packed in a lesser port is a running joke in the correspondence.

Cairo's *tiryaq*, Ottoman palace theriacs, Montpellier's and Paris's *thériaque*: each city treated the paste as civic furniture. The French Academy and the Paris faculty were still arguing about it in the eighteenth century, when chemists (and the occasional angry physician) had begun to ask which ingredient did anything. William Heberden's 1745 essay against mithridatium and theriac is the English demolition many later writers lean on: a prestige compound as an insult to the sick.

A public mixing is a beautiful answer to a trust problem. It is a poor answer to a pharmacology problem. You cannot see a molecule from a balcony. You can see a government.

## What it was asked to do

Bite, plague, fever, the unnamed dread. Theriac absorbed indications the way a famous hospital absorbs patients. When plague years came, the paste sold. When they receded, the paste still sat in the cupboard as a household god. Physicians who doubted the viper kept the opium and the aromatics. Physicians who doubted everything still had to compete with a name patients already trusted.

The decline was not a single experiment. It was the slow rise of simples that could be identified, then isolates that could be weighed (later essays). A paste of seventy names looks like wisdom until you have morphine in grains and quinine in ounces. Then it looks like a committee.

## After the king

Mithridates lost to Rome and the myth won. The word *mithridatism* still wanders through toxicology for acquired tolerance. The jars sit in museums, handsome, slightly ridiculous, historically exact. If you want a moral, do not take the cheap one ("look at those fools"). Take this: **prestige compounding is a recurring form.** The names change. The wish to outlive a king does not.

No one should mix viper and opium in a kitchen because a Renaissance city once did it on the square. The square was a government. Your kitchen is not.
