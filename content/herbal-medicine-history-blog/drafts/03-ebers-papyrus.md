# A Dining-Table Scroll from Luxor

Georg Ebers was a novelist as well as an Egyptologist, which is worth remembering when the scroll he bought took his name. In the winter of 1872–73, in Luxor, a dealer sold him a hieratic papyrus about twenty meters long, dated by its calendar to the ninth year of Amenhotep I — roughly the mid-sixteenth century BCE — and stuffed with remedies that were already old-fashioned when the scribe copied them. Ebers published it in facsimile in 1875. The book is still what most general readers mean by "Egyptian medicine."

It is a recipe book with theology in the margins. It is not a hospital chart. Leipzig University Library still holds the roll. The calendar date on it (year 9 of Amenhotep I) is a copyist's date, not the birthday of every recipe inside. Older material sits in a newer hand, the usual fate of a useful book.

## Length, and what length buys

Eight hundred or so prescriptions, depending on how you split them. Head, belly, skin, urinary trouble, the house of a woman, the house of a worm. Honey, beer, milk, dates, acacia gum, sycamore, castor (*kaka*, one of the less controversial IDs), frankincense, "bitter apple," minerals, dung — the last of which later European readers used as proof of absurdity, as if their own pharmacopeias had not listed the same. The Egyptian word often translated "drug" (*peqer* and related terms, depending on the passage) sits next to measures: ro, hin, handfuls. Someone was compounding, not only praying.

Prayer is there anyway. A recitation may bind the drug. Isis and Ra appear because a recipe without a story was, for this scribe, an incomplete technology. The Hearst Papyrus and the Berlin medical papyrus are shorter cousins. The Edwin Smith Papyrus, bought through another channel and quieter in the popular mind, shows a different Egyptian register: wounds, "an ailment I will handle," examination language that later surgeons recognized with a jolt. Ebers is the other drawer — the drawer of the house that boils and wraps. A civilization that can write both drawers is not waiting for Greece.

## The identification problem

Lise Manniche and others have tried to walk these names into gardens. Some are easy enough: *ricinus*, dates, barley. Many are not. The papyrus will give a word; a later Greek writer will give another; a Victorian will decide they are the same shrub. That chain is how "the Egyptians used aloe for X" gets printed on a bottle. Sometimes the chain holds. Often it is a wish.

When this series needs Egyptian matter, the safer sentence is: **the Ebers scribe assigned this plant-word to this complaint, in this vehicle (honey, beer, oil), with this measure.** The modern binomial can wait until a specialist will sign it.

## Castor, beer, and the body as a Nile

Castor oil's purgative reputation is one of the less romantic survivals. Beer as a vehicle is another (Mesopotamia used it too; see the clay essay). Egyptian medical thought also ran on vessels and floods — metaphors that are not cute if you live on a river that can fail. A drug that "opens" or "cools" is doing work inside an anatomy that is not Galen's and not ours. Translating every verb into a PubMed endpoint is how you get fake trials.

Ebbell's 1937 English version is still on free-book sites. It reads smoothly and is wrong often enough that it should be treated as a period piece. Use it to see how 1930s medicine wanted Egypt to look. Then go back to the facsimile.

## After Luxor

The papyrus made a career for Ebers and a genre for museums: the long medical book as national treasure. It also set a trap for herbal histories that want a single "first." Ebers is not first. It is the longest we have from that tradition, bought from a dealer, copied from older copy. The missing papyri — the ones that rotted — are the larger library.

If you stand in Leipzig and the librarians will show you the roll, you are not looking at a cure. You are looking at a bureaucracy of the body, written in a beautiful hand, for complaints that did not wait for Greek names. That is enough.
