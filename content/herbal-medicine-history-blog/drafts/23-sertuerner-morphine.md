# Sertürner Isolates a Sleep

Friedrich Wilhelm Sertürner was a young apothecary in Paderborn, then Einbeck, working with the sticky, variable, official drug called opium — the dried latex of *Papaver somniferum*. Pharmacopeias already told him how to make tinctures. They did not tell him which part of the tar put a patient under. In work he dated to 1804 and published more fully later (the 1817 paper is the one that traveled), he separated a crystalline substance he named after Morpheus. Morphium. Morphine.

He tried it on himself and on dogs. That sentence is not a dare. It is a record of how isolation ethics looked before institutional review boards. He was born in Neuhaus in 1783, lost his father early, apprenticed in a Paderborn shop, and did the kind of after-hours chemistry that official professors later claimed as their century. The poppy cake on his bench was the same official opium the Prussian and Westphalian pharmacopeias already recognized. He was not inventing a plant. He was taking a plant apart.

## Why a crystal changed the shop

Opium is a mixture. A tincture made in January is not a tincture made in July if the raw cake differs. A crystal with a name can be weighed. Weighing is the beginning of modern dose, of overdose as a number, of industrial production, of the later hypodermic century. Charles Gabriel Pravaz and Alexander Wood's mid-century syringes would give the crystal a new speed; the American Civil War and the Franco-Prussian War would give it a census.

Pelletier and Caventou took the same habit to cinchona (quinine, 1820) and to nux vomica (strychnine, 1818). Runge isolated caffeine from coffee in 1819. Mein and others worked atropine from belladonna in the 1830s. Niemann's cocaine (1860) is later in this pack. The 1800s become, in pharmacy histories, the alkaloid age — a German word (*Alkaloid*, Meissner 1819) for a family of plant bases that could be salted, sold, and eventually scheduled.

This essay will not describe how to separate anything. The historical claim is enough: **a plant drug became a chemical individual.** Law and medicine are still living with that move. So is the romantic who wants the "whole plant" back without giving up the numbered tablet.

## The dating squabble

Popular timelines say 1804. Historians point at publication lags and at what "isolation" meant in a shop without our analytics. Huxtable and others have walked the papers. For this pack, write: Sertürner isolated morphine in the first years of the nineteenth century and convinced the wider chemical world around 1817. If a caption needs one year, 1817 is the year the news behaved like news.

Gay-Lussac's circle paying attention mattered more than a hometown demonstration. Science, then as now, is a publicity system as well as a bench.

## What the sleep cost

Morphine did not abolish opium. It created a second, stronger object that could be injected when the hollow needle and the syringe matured (mid-century). The American Civil War and later European wars taught a generation the alkaloid's mercy and its hook. Regulation would follow (Harrison Act essay). Sertürner wanted a better, cleaner drug. He got one. Clean is not the same as kind in the long run.

He also got a template. Once you believe the useful part of a plant is a molecule you can name, the rest of the plant becomes "impurity" or, a century later, "entourage" depending on the marketing decade. Both words are arguments, not facts of nature.

## The shop after him

A provincial German apothecary changed what a Paris professor thought a drug was. That direction of travel — from the shop to the academy — is worth keeping. We tell too many stories in which universities invent and pharmacies only dispense. Sertürner dispensed, then invented, then published, then watched the world sharpen his crystal into an industry.

No part of this history is a method. If you need morphine, you need a hospital and a law. If you need the history, you need the 1817 paper and the poppy's older, messier life (essay 25).
