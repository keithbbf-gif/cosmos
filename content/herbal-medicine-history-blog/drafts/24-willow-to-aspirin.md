# From Willow Bark to a Trademarked Powder

In 1763 the Reverend Edward Stone of Chipping Norton wrote to the Royal Society about the bark of the white willow. He had dried it, powdered it, and given it to people with agues, on a hunch that a bitter bark from damp ground might stand in for the more expensive Peruvian bark. The letter is modest and specific. It is not a molecule. It is a country experiment in the century's style.

A hundred years of chemistry later, salicin and salicylic acid were named objects. Henri Leroux isolated salicin from willow in 1829; Raffaele Piria pushed the chemistry toward salicylic acid; Hermann Kolbe, in the 1850s–60s, gave industry a synthetic route from coal-tar phenol that made the tree optional. In 1897 Felix Hoffmann, at Bayer in Elberfeld, acetylated salicylic acid; in 1899 the firm was selling "Aspirin." Whether Hoffmann or Arthur Eichengrün deserves the credit is a fight historians still run — Eichengrün's later account, written after Nazi-era erasure, is the document that reopened it. The trademark is not in doubt. The stomach is not in doubt either: salicylic acid already worked and already burned; acetylation was a commercial answer to a known complaint.

## Not a straight line

The parable goes: ancients used willow → Stone → Bayer → your bathroom. The parable drops Greek and Latin uses that may not be the same species (a *itea* in a Greek list is not automatically *Salix alba*). It drops meadowsweet (*Spiraea* / *Filipendula ulmaria*), which gave the *spir-* in the trade name and had its own folk file as a fever tea. It drops Johann Buchner's earlier salicin work and the French and Italian chemists. It drops Kolbe, who made salicylic acid from coal-tar routes — which is how industry leaves the tree. It drops the fact that physicians were already using salicylates for rheumatic fever in the 1870s (MacLagan's salicin; Stricker and others on salicylic acid) before Bayer's tin.

Dioscorides and Hippocrates get dragged into aspirin ads the way they get dragged into wine ads. Check the passage. Often you are looking at a general astringent bark, not a prophecy of cyclooxygenase. John Vane's 1971 prostaglandin paper is a different century's explanation of a powder already on the market. Mechanism is not origin.

## Stone's hunch

Stone's "doctrine of signatures" flavor — a willow of wet places for a shivering wet disease — is of its century. His attempt to record amounts and cases is also of its century, and closer to Withering than to a brand. He thought he might spare people the cost of cinchona. That is an economic sentence. Herbal history that ignores price is church.

## The factory

Bayer's powder was a coal-tar-era product with a plant story in the advertising department. That dual citizenship is the modern condition of many "natural" drugs. The isolate or the analogue does the work; the leaf does the myth. Aspirin later became generic, then a cardiology object, then an object of debate about prevention — none of which belongs here as advice. The historical point is the **name**: *a-* from acetyl, *-spir-* from *Spiraea*, a meadow plant, printed on a German industrial package.

Jeffreys's book is a usable narrative if you keep an eye on the company's own shine. The Eichengrün claim (that a Jewish chemist was written out after 1933) is **soft** in the sense that it is contested; do not settle it in a caption. Say the credit is disputed.

## What the parable is for

It is for showing that folk use, clerical experiment, academic chemistry, and a dye company can all touch the same bitter idea without being the same act. It is not for selling willow bark as "natural aspirin." Willow bark is a variable plant material. Aspirin is a defined compound. Confusing them is how people get a gastritis and a story.

Edward Stone asked the Royal Society to look. Bayer asked pharmacists to stock a tin. This series asks the reader to keep the letter, the crystal, and the tin in separate rooms.
