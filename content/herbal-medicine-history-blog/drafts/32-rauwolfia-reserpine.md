# Rauwolfia and the Blood-Pressure Alkaloid

*Rauvolfia serpentina* is a shrub with a red root and a long Indian name-file: *sarpagandha*, a plant of Sanskrit and vernacular pharmacy, used for a cluster of complaints that later translators too quickly collapse into "insanity and snakebite and heart." In 1949 Rustom Vakil published a paper in the *British Heart Journal* on the plant's use in hypertension, bringing a Bombay bedside into a metropolitan journal. In the early 1950s, Ciba chemists isolated reserpine. The alkaloid lowered blood pressure. It also lowered mood in a way psychiatry had to notice.

The shrub did not move. The category did: from a classical *dravya* to a WHO-era essential-drug story to a mostly superseded antihypertensive with an important afterlife in the history of depression as a side effect. Leonhard Rauwolf, the sixteenth-century Augsburg physician whose name Linnaeus later locked to the genus, never saw this Indian species on his Syrian journey; the naming is European filing. The plant's working names were already in Sanskrit, Hindi, Tamil, and a dozen bazaar lists. A Latin commemorative is not a discovery.

## Softness in the ancient claim

Popular articles say "Ayurveda used rauwolfia for blood pressure for centuries." Blood pressure as a numbered clinic object is a nineteenth-century instrument story (the sphygmomanometer's career). Classical texts can speak of the head, the heart, the restlessness of a mind. They cannot speak of mm Hg. The honest lineage is: **a plant with a reputation in Indian practice → modern Indian clinicians measuring pressure → an isolate that made the reputation travel.** That is enough glory without the anachronism.

## Vakil's bedside and Ciba's bench

Vakil's 1949 paper is the kind of mid-century clinical report that looks modest now and was a bridge then: a Bombay physician, a series of hypertensive patients, a plant the Indian market already sold, a journal in London. He was not the first Indian doctor to take *sarpagandha* seriously in a modern ward; he was the one who got the metropolitan citation. Sen and Bose's earlier Calcutta work and the Chopra school's pharmacognosy sit in the same decade's Indian literature. Nationalist science wanted an Indian plant that could talk to mm Hg. It found one.

Ciba's isolation of reserpine (Müller, Schlittler, Bein, the early 1950s papers) and the brand Serpasil are the kind of mid-century pharmaceutical story that looks inevitable now and was a choice then. India supplied the plant and the first modern clinical push. Switzerland supplied the factory and the crystal's passport. Benefit-sharing in the 1950s was not a phrase with a protocol. Osseo-Asare's questions about African plants apply here with different names. So does the older question from cinchona: who owns a root that a village already priced?

## Before the sphygmomanometer

Colonial and Ayurvedic print of the late nineteenth century already pushed *Rauvolfia* as a sedative and an antidote plant. The "snake" in *sarpagandha* invited a signature reading that serious vaidyas did not need. What they needed was a root that quieted a violent ward. That psychiatric folk-clinical use is why reserpine later made sense to asylum doctors as well as to cardiologists — and why the mood cost should not have been a surprise. A plant known to still a mind will, when purified and given daily for pressure, still a mind.

## Psychiatry's shadow

Reserpine's depletion of monoamines became a pillar in the stories neuroscientists told about depression. Those stories later grew more complicated. The historical fact is simpler: a blood-pressure drug changed the emotional room, and physicians had to learn to see that as more than "sedation." Patients paid for the lesson. Newer antihypertensives took the market. The alkaloid remains a teaching molecule.

## Why it belongs in an herbal series

Because it is the cleanest South Asian example of the isolate path after opium and before artemisinin: a named classical plant, a nationalist and internationalist medical moment, a company, a side effect, a fade. Because it warns against the two stupid sentences — "the ancients already knew" and "nothing happened until Basel." The middle is Bombay, 1949, a journal, a sphygmomanometer, a root.

No one should boil *sarpagandha* from a website recipe because Vakil once wrote a paper. The paper is why hospitals, for a while, had a tablet. The tablet is why the shrub's Latin name still rings in pharmacology lectures. The lectures should also ring the Sanskrit, without pretending the Sanskrit is a protocol.
