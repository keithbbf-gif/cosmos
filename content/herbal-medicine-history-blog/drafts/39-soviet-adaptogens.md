# How "Adaptogen" Left the Soviet Lab

In 1947, in a Soviet scientific culture that liked a unifying word, N. V. Lazarev proposed *adaptogen* for substances that seemed to raise resistance to a variety of stressors without being a stimulant in the usual sense. The definition wandered — nonspecific, normalizing, relatively harmless — as definitions do when they are trying to birth a field. I. I. Brekhman, working in the Far East, made *Eleutherococcus senticosus* (Siberian ginseng, a marketing name that annoys botanists) the mascot. Military and sports institutes ran studies that are hard to read now: different endpoints, different controls, a political reward for a patriotic plant.

The word survived the USSR. It did better, commercially, than the footnotes. Brekhman published in Russian and, later, in English-enough venues to be quoted by California companies that had never seen Vladivostok. *Eleutherococcus* became "Siberian ginseng" on labels, a name botanists hate because the plant is not *Panax*. Hate does not move a box off a shelf. A name that contains "ginseng" does.

## A category, not a clade

Adaptogens are not a botanical family. They are a hope with a list: eleuthero, Asian ginseng, American ginseng, later *Rhodiola rosea* (the Soviet and Scandinavian file), later ashwagandha (an Ayurvedic *dravya* drafted into the word), later schisandra, later anything a brand manager can spell. The Soviet list had a logic — tonic plants of the official herbal, often Araliaceae, tested in cold, fatigue, and radiation-adjacent military protocols that are hard to reconstruct from a PDF. The American list has a season and a podcast.

Under DSHEA, "adaptogen" on a label is flavoring for a structure/function claim ("supports the body's response to stress") unless a company is foolish enough to claim to treat a disease. The FDA has not, by defining the word, made it a clinical diagnosis. The word does not appear in the statute. It appears in marketing departments that needed a noun more serious than "tonic" and less regulated than "antidepressant."

If you cannot name a trial you have read, do not write "research shows." A lot of the English "research shows" on this topic is a press release citing a Soviet paper no one in the room has seen, or a small modern study on a different extract. That is a **soft** zone. This pack will stay in the historical claim: **the word was coined, the institutes existed, the export happened.**

## Ginseng's older file

*Panax ginseng* does not need Lazarev. It has the *bencao*, the tribute trade, the later Korean and Chinese industrial farms, the Wisconsin *P. quinquefolius* boom. Brekhman's move was to offer a cheaper, Soviet, non-*Panax* tonic with a modern word. The Cold War liked a parallel tree. So did Western companies once the word was free to use.

## Sport, space, and the wish

Stories about cosmonauts and Olympic teams are not all false and not all checkable from an American desk. Treat them as institutional folklore unless a named study is in the bibliography. The wish is clear: a plant that is not a banned stimulant and still feels like an edge. That wish is older than 1947 (see coca, see *má huáng*). The Soviet contribution was a scientific-sounding noun.

## After the emigration

Walk a U.S. aisle now and "adaptogen" is on gummies. The gummy is a DSHEA object with a candy habit. Lazarev is not the author of the gummy. If an editor wants a moral, here is one you can defend: **categories travel lighter than methods.** The double-blind, the dose diary, the Russian-language negative result — those stay home. The noun gets a passport.

This essay is not a stack. It is a brief history of a noun. The plants on the list each deserve the older kind of history: a name, a place, a text, a trial if there is one. Do that work or leave the jar on the shelf.
