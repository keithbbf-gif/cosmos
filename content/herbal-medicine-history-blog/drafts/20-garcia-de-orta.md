# Garcia de Orta's Goa Conversations

Garcia de Orta printed his book in Goa in 1563, on a colonial press, in Portuguese, as a series of colloquies. A character named Ruano asks the questions a European physician would ask. Orta answers with the arrogance of a man who has lived in India for decades and is tired of Lisbon repeating Dioscorides about plants Dioscorides never saw. Areca, pepper, cinnamon, camphor, opium, the mango, the jackfruit, the drugs of the bazaar: each dialogue is a small fight about names and properties.

The book is charming if you like a sharp voice. It is also a book written in a conquered port, by a New Christian whose family would not be safe, about goods that moved because of guns as well as because of gardens. Goa in the 1560s was a Estado da Índia capital: hospitals, an Inquisition office already in the wings (1560), a harbor that mixed Gujarati, Arab, Malay, and Portuguese money. Orta's garden on the island of Bombay — leased, cultivated, later absorbed by the city that took the name — was a private physic garden at the edge of that harbor. The *Colóquios* are what a man writes when the harbor has contradicted his student notes.

## A physician in a warehouse

Orta served governors (Martim Afonso, later others) and kept that Bombay garden. He bought from Gujarati and Malabar merchants. He listened, sometimes, to Konkani and Arabic names. He argues, in the mango and the jackfruit chapters, that a European who has only read Pliny should shut up and taste. Harold Cook and Richard Grove have put him in the story of how the Portuguese empire became a machine for moving biological goods. He is not an ethnographer of village medicine. He is a court doctor in a factory-state, and he has the factory's confidence.

Cristóvão da Costa (Cristóbal Acosta) would plunder and illustrate him. Carolus Clusius would Latinize and rearrange him for Antwerp and Leiden. The rearrangement is how a Goa colloquy became a northern botanical standard. Something always changes in that kind of shipping: the dialogue's rudeness thins, the pictures multiply, the Portuguese Jews recede.

When he says a European book is wrong about cinnamon, he is often right about the botany and silent about the coercion that made cinnamon a Portuguese problem in the first place.

## Opium, pepper, and the ordinary bazaar

The *Colóquios* treat opium as a known Asian and European drug, pepper as a diet and a medicine, areca as a social habit the Portuguese had to learn not to moralize too quickly if they wanted to understand the room. Orta's tone is empirical in the Dioscorides way: look, taste, ask the merchant, compare the leaf. That tone later pleased historians who wanted a "scientific" Iberia. Fine. Also notice who gets to speak. Indian physicians appear, but the dialogue form gives Orta the last word.

Clusius (Charles de l'Écluse) would Latinize and rearrange Orta for a northern public. The rearrangement is how Goa reached Leiden. Something always changes in that kind of shipping.

## The Inquisition's shadow

Orta died in 1568. In 1580 the Goa Inquisition exhumed and burned his remains after proceedings against judaizing in his family. The book stayed. The body did not. A history of pharmacy that wants only plants will skip this. A history of the people who wrote the plants cannot. The colonial press that made the *Colóquios* possible was the same world that made a Jewish physician's grave unsafe.

## Why the conversations still matter

Because they are an early printed refusal of a Mediterranean monopoly on names. Because they show a tropical pharmacy as a port conversation, not as a temple secret. Because every later "spice trade" chapter that lists commodities without a voice should have to sit with Orta's sentences — and then sit with the silent ones.

No recipe in the *Colóquios* is an instruction for a modern reader. The instruction is editorial: when a European book and an Indian bazaar disagree, ask who has the leaf in hand. Then ask how the leaf got to that hand.
