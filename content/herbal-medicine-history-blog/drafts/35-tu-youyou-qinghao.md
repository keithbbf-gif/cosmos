# Tu Youyou, Qinghao, and a Cold Extraction

In the late 1960s the North Vietnamese and Chinese states had a malaria problem that was also a war problem. Project 523 — named for a May 23, 1967, meeting — put military need behind a screen of plants and synthetics. Tu Youyou, trained in both pharmaceutical science and the old literature, worked at the Academy of Traditional Chinese Medicine in Beijing. She and her group looked at *qinghao*, the Artemisia of the *bencao*. A line in Ge Hong's fourth-century *Zhouhou beiji fang* (the *Emergency Formulas to Keep Up One's Sleeve*) mentions a juice or soak that is not a long boil.

They tried milder heat. The extract, in their animal models, behaved. The crystalline sesquiterpene lactone later called artemisinin (*qinghaosu*) became the parent of a class that now sits, in combination, at the center of falciparum treatment guidelines. In 2015 Tu Youyou received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, shared with William C. Campbell and Satoshi Ōmura (avermectin / ivermectin — another soil-and-parasite story). She was the first Chinese woman to sit in that particular chair, and the first PRC scientist to take a science Nobel without a foreign lab as the main stage. The state newspapers knew what to do with that. Her own interviews stayed thinner: a group, a hint in Ge Hong, a lot of extracts that failed.

## What Ge Hong did not do

He did not run a randomized trial. He did not isolate a lactone. He recorded an emergency practice. Elisabeth Hsu and others have warned against reading the *bencao* and the formula books as if they were lab notebooks waiting for a Nobel. Tu's own lecture is careful enough if you listen: the old text **suggested a method of not destroying a heat-labile something**. The suggestion still needed a modern assay, a group, a wartime state, and a great deal of failure with other plants.

Saying "TCM cured malaria in the Jin dynasty" is false. Saying "a modern team used a classical hint" is true.

## Project 523 as a historical form

This was not a start-up. It was a command project, numbered for a date, with secrecy, duplication across institutes, and a Cultural Revolution weather that could ruin a career for a wrong slogan as easily as for a wrong assay. Zhou Enlai's name sits on the usual origin stories; the PLA's malaria problem in the south and in Vietnam sits on the less ceremonial ones. Tu's group at the Academy of Traditional Chinese Medicine was one node. Other nodes tried other plants and synthetics. Most nodes got nothing anyone would later put on a stamp.

The later telling sometimes makes Tu a lone grandmother of the nation. She has been clear about the group — Luo Zeyuan, Ye Zhuju, and the chemists who did the unphotogenic work. The Nobel Committee's habit of naming at most three people meets a Chinese habit of naming a collective. The tension is itself historical. So is the fact that a military project can produce a civilian essential drug and still leave the first papers in restricted files.

## After the crystal

Artemisinin derivatives plus a partner drug became the WHO-recommended therapy for uncomplicated falciparum in most places; resistance is now a field story on the Thai-Cambodian border and beyond. That is clinical and epidemiological present tense, not a home-use hint. *Artemisia* teas sold as malaria prevention are a public-health problem, not a heritage product. This magazine will not blur them.

## The cold part

The phrase "cold extraction" in popular retellings is already a simplification of a laboratory program. We keep it in the title as a pointer, not as a protocol. The pointer says: **reading old books can be a scientific act if you also have a bench and the willingness to be wrong.** It does not say: boil your backyard mugwort. *A. annua* is not a kitchen cure, and qinghao in the texts is a naming thicket (*A. annua*, *A. apiacea*, and others have been argued).

Tu Youyou's public style is dry. Match it. The romance is in the parasite, the war, the fourth-century sleeve, and the prize — enough romance without a glowing herb.
