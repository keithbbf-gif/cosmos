# A Surgeon's Book for the March

Pedanius Dioscorides of Anazarbus served, the later biographies say, with Roman armies. Whether or not he was a formal military physician, *De materia medica* reads like a book written by someone who had to recognize a plant in a market he did not grow up in. He groups substances not by the alphabet — later scribes would ruin that convenience — but by affinity: aromatics with aromatics, metals with metals, the look and the smell and the cheat.

He is impatient with books that only copy. He says so. Anazarbus is in Cilicia; the eastern Mediterranean of the first century was a Roman warehouse of resins, oils, and roots. A man who had walked with soldiers had seen the same plant under two market names. That irritation is the book's engine.

## Five books, and the problem of order

The Greek text that Lily Beck translated for modern readers is five books: aromatics and oils; animals and animal parts; grains, herbs, roots; other roots and herbs; vines and wines. It is a tour of the eastern Mediterranean's stuff: myrrh, opium poppy, squill, birthwort, the many honeys, the mineral bitumens, the things later squeamishness would drop. Dioscorides will tell you how a resin should snap, how a root is adulterated with cheaper weight, which city sends the better grade of a gum. That is pharmacognosy before the word.

John Riddle has argued, not gently, that this is a more empirical book than Galen's theoretical pharmacy, and that medieval Latin readers who preferred Galen paid for it. The opinion is earned if you have sat with both. Galen will explain why a drug *must* cool. Dioscorides will tell you what the farmers in a named place do with it, then what he thinks of their story. He will also be wrong, because a first-century field note is not a voucher specimen. Being wrong in a checkable way is still a better habit than being right by temperament.

## Mandrake, poppy, and the sensational pages

Every illustrated Dioscorides show ends at the mandrake. The Vienna manuscript's painter knew the folklore — the scream, the dog — and still had to draw a root. Opium is less theatrical on the page and more important in the history: juice of *Papaver somniferum*, already a known sleep and a known danger. Dioscorides is not innocent about harm. He records it. He does not write a clandestine manual, and neither will this essay.

The sensational pages are how museums get visitors. The working pages are how a Syrian or Italian doctor, a thousand years later, decided what to buy.

## The copies that became the book

Arabic translators took Dioscorides into a language that already had its own garden words; the result was argument as much as transfer (see Ibn al-Bayṭār). Latin Europe received him in pieces, then in fuller versions, then as a printed humanist trophy. The *Vienna Dioscorides* — made in Constantinople for the imperial princess Anicia Juliana around 512 — is the celebrity object: almost a thousand pages, painted plants, a dedication picture. It is also a warning. Luxury can freeze a text. Field notes want cheaper copies that wear out.

When later botanists began to sneer at "the ancients," they were often sneering at the gap between a Mediterranean name and a northern plant. Fuchs and the Renaissance herbalists tried to re-identify. Linnaeus later nailed names to specimens. The chain from Dioscorides to a modern binomial is a series of those repairs, not a straight road.

## Why he still sits at the front of the shop

Because he wrote a book you can use with your hands. Because he named cheats. Because the Mediterranean trade he assumed — resins from the south, roots from the hills, mineral salts from specific shores — is the same trade that would later feed Venice and then the colonial gardens. Because every subsequent "complete herbal" is, in some posture, answering him.

Galen would become the theory. Dioscorides remained the shelf. This pack follows the shelf.
