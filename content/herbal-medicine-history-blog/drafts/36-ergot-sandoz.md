# Saint Anthony's Fire to a Basel Alkaloid

Ergot is a dark spur on an ear of rye, the sclerotium of *Claviceps purpurea*. Eaten in enough bread, it closes vessels and burns limbs; the medieval name, Saint Anthony's fire, attached to a hospital order that tried to care for the gangrenous and the convulsed. Outbreak numbers in the older literature are **soft** — chroniclers loved a round figure — but the disease is not a myth. Whole villages could be marked.

The Antonines — the Hospitallers of St. Anthony — spread from a tenth-century house in the Dauphiné and became, for a few centuries, a specialized order for the burning disease. They cut, they housed, they collected rents on the promise of care. A hospital dedicated to a plant-fungus illness is as close as medieval Europe came to a public-health brand. When later physicians split ergotism into "gangrenous" and "convulsive" pictures, they were naming two ways the same contaminated staple could finish a body. Rye was the poor grain of damp northern fields. The fungus likes a wet flower. A bad August could write itself into a parish register.

Midwives in early modern Europe also knew a different, smaller, terrible use: a bit of ergot to force a uterus. The window between a living birth and a catastrophe was narrow. Adam Lonicer mentioned the midwife's spur in a sixteenth-century herbal; German *Hebammen* kept the knowledge when Latin doctors called it peasant cruelty. Official medicine spent a long time being disgusted and then, in the nineteenth century, adopting "ergot of rye" as an obstetric drug with the same narrow window. John Stearns's 1808 letter on *pulvis parturiens* is the usual American citation: a country doctor admitting he took a midwife's powder. The admission is Withering's foxglove story in a darker key.

## Pont-Saint-Esprit and the problem of a date

The 1951 outbreak in Pont-Saint-Esprit, France — bread, hallucinations, hospitalizations, deaths — was filed by some doctors as ergotism and by later writers as almost anything else (mercury, nitrogen trichloride, a secret test). The file is a warning about popular history: a dramatic bread poisoning attracts theories the way a spur attracts a story. For this pack, the medieval and early modern ergotism record is enough. The 1951 town is a footnote that has eaten more pages than it has earned. If you mention it, mention the argument.

## From famine fungus to assay

Nineteenth-century chemists poked at the spur and got messes. Charles Tanret crystallized ergotinine in the 1870s; George Barger's circle, in the 1900s–10s, began to sort a family of bases. In the 1920s Arthur Stoll at Sandoz in Basel isolated ergotamine, a defined alkaloid for a defined, later, narrow set of uses (migraine in the textbook career; postpartum bleeding in another). The company built an ergot franchise the way other firms built digitalis and opium franchises: assay, brand, insert. Albert Hofmann, looking at lysergic acid derivatives in 1938 and again in 1943, found LSD-25 — a story he told in *LSD: My Problem Child*. That book is a memoir. It is not a methods appendix, and this essay will not supply one.

The cultural afterlife of 1943 is enormous and mostly off our beat. Hofmann's bicycle ride has been printed more times than Stoll's isolation paper. The historical beat is: **a fungal contaminant of bread became a line of named compounds.** Medicine kept some (ergometrine / ergonovine in obstetrics; ergotamine in neurology, later narrowed). Control statutes took others. The fungus remained a field problem for agronomists, who now treat *Claviceps* as a crop-loss and a toxin-limit question, not a saint.

Sandoz's Basel is not a village oven. It is a dye-and-alkaloid city — Stoll's ergot line, Hofmann's lysergic acid work, the same Basel that aspirin stories wrongly borrow from Elberfeld. Ergot is how a medieval hospital and a dye company share a plant pathology.

## Folklore, fire, and witch files

A secondary literature tries to read European witchcraft and the Salem court through ergot. Some of that is suggestive. A lot of it is a hammer looking for a spur. Flag it **soft**. Ergotism is a sufficient horror without conscripting every convulsion in the archive.

## Why it sits with foxglove and poppy

Because it is a poison that became a drug by way of dose and isolation, and because the first experts were not professors. They were bakers who failed, priests who housed the dying, and women at the bedside of a bleed. Sandoz was late. Sandoz was also the reason a physician could write a milligram instead of a crumb of spur. Milligrams are progress. They are not innocence.

No agricultural advice. No "how Hofmann did it." If the magazine needs a last line, use the Antonines: a religious order as a public-health response to a plant disease. That is herbal history in a minor key — the plant as a plague, not as a gift.
