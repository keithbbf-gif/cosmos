# The Fever Tree and the Counting House

The story everyone likes is this: the Countess of Chinchón, wife of a viceroy in Lima, is dying of fever; a bark from the high woods saves her; she brings it to Spain; Linnaeus, grateful, names the genus *Cinchona* (and misspells the title). It is a neat story. It is also, in the form usually told, late and doubtful. A. W. Haggis and later historians have chased the countess through archives and come back with almost no contemporary body.

What we do have is bark, Jesuits, a Roman distribution network in the 1630s–40s, Protestant suspicion of a "Popish" powder, and — by the nineteenth century — a global commodity measured in ounces against a parasite that was eating armies. The genus name *Cinchona* is Linnaeus's 1742 honor to a countess whose fever story Haggis could not find in Lima's records. The name stuck. The woman, if she ever took the bark, did not need Linnaeus.

## Jesuits, Protestants, and a bitter taste

By the mid-seventeenth century, "Jesuit's bark" was a known fever drug in Rome — Cardinal Juan de Lugo is the name the older Catholic histories like — and then in northern Europe. It tasted brutal. It also did something the humoral syrups did not reliably do to intermittent fevers. Thomas Sydenham and later English physicians argued about when to give it (not at the wrong humoral moment, they worried). Robert Talbor (or Tabor) made a secret English remedy that Charles II and then the French court paid for; underneath the wine and the secrecy it was bark. Secrecy and bark went together because the bark was a business.

Indigenous Andean knowledge of the tree — Quechua *quina-quina* and related names, the *cascarilleros* who later climbed for the traders — is the least documented part of the official European file, which is a kind of documentation. Londa Schiebinger and others have written about how women's and Indigenous expertise get stripped when a remedy becomes a title. The strip is the historical event we can name even when the first teacher is anonymous.

## 1820: a name you can weigh

Pierre-Joseph Pelletier and Joseph Bienaimé Caventou isolated quinine in Paris in 1820. The isolate did not invent the bark's use. It made a dose reproducible and a patent path imaginable. It also raised the stakes of the tree. If the active was a crystal, then the right species and the right bark mattered in a new way. Collectors went into the Andes with more hunger than manners.

## Java, Kew, and the theft that was called science

Nineteenth-century European states wanted their own supply. Seeds and seedlings moved by hook, by paid collectors (Hasskarl, Ledger, Markham's projects), and by the ordinary imperial habit of treating another country's forest as a depot. Dutch plantations on Java became the machine that actually produced. Kew's economic botany sat in the middle as expert and as warehouse. Richard Drayton's *Nature's Government* is the book for that garden's conscience.

Charles Ledger's *calisaya* seed, grown on in the East Indies, is one of those stories in which a named Bolivian or mestizo assistant (Manuel Incra Mamani) does the lethal work and the European gets the memorial. Tell it that way or do not tell it.

## Malaria as an accounting problem

Armies, canals, colonies: malaria was a labor and logistics problem. Quinine was issued. It failed when the parasite or the logistics failed. It poisoned when the dose was clumsy. None of that is a home protocol. The historical point is that a highland tree became a condition of lowland empire. When synthetic antimalarials and then artemisinin (Tu Youyou's later essay) arrived, they were entering a market the bark had already made.

The countess, if she ever took the bark, is allowed to rest. The counting house is the truer monument: ledgers of ounces, fevers, and ships. A history of herbal medicine that skips the ledger is a fairy tale about trees.
