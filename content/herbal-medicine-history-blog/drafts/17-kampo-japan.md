# Kampo: From Tang Formulas to Health Insurance

Kampo means, roughly, the Han method — Chinese medicine as Japan received and reworked it. The first imperial imports sit in the Nara period, when continental formulas, pulses, and books arrived with monks and envoys. The *Ishinpō* (984), compiled by Tamba Yasuyori, is an early Japanese monument: a medical encyclopedia that preserved Chinese passages later lost on the mainland. That fact alone should complicate any story in which Japan is only a student.

Edo-period schools then fought, as schools do, about how much to trust the Song and Ming elaborations versus a return to older formula texts, especially Zhang Zhongjing's *Shanghan lun*. The *koho* (ancient-formula) physicians around Yoshimasu Todo wanted abdominal palpatation (*fukushin*) and blunt, older compounds. The *gosei* (later-generation) physicians kept more of the Jin-Yuan theory and a wider cabinet. The fight is technical. It produced a Japanese habit of **formulas as units** — *keishito*, *kakkonto*, *rikkunshito*, *shoseiryuto* — rather than a loose handful of "herbs." A unit can be printed, insured, and later granulated. A handful cannot.

## Not TCM, not DSHEA

Twentieth-century "Traditional Chinese Medicine" is a PRC institutional package. Kampo is a Japanese institutional package. They share ancestors and they do not share a ministry. After the Meiji Restoration, the 1874 medical regulations and the prestige of German *Staatsexamen* medicine pushed Kampo out of the official schools. A physician who wanted a state license studied virchowian pathology, not the *Shanghan lun*. Kampo nearly died as official training. It survived in families, in a few stubborn physicians (Yumoto Kyushin and others who kept teaching in the early twentieth century), in the 1930s academic revival around Otsuka Keisetsu, and then — this is the twist a U.S. reader needs — in the postwar National Health Insurance list. From the 1960s–70s onward, a finite set of Kampo formulas, manufactured to official specs by firms such as Tsumura, became billable.

The 1976 restoration of a larger insured list is a date Japanese pharmacists know and American herbalists usually do not. It is more important to this history than any cherry blossom.

Margaret Lock's ethnography of East Asian medicine in urban Japan, and later health-policy papers, describe what that billability does. It standardizes extracts. It makes a family doctor who never thought of herself as "traditional" able to write *shoseiryuto* for a complaint the textbook maps to that formula. It also leaves out the living, tweaking, one-off decoction a classical scholar-physician would have called the actual art.

A billable extract is not a dietary supplement. Japan's legal box is a medical one. If the same formula's plants appear in a U.S. capsule, that capsule is usually a DSHEA object with a different burden of proof and a different label. Do not translate *hoken* as "the supplement aisle."

## The *Shanghan lun* as a Japanese book

Zhang Zhongjing's cold-damage treatise (late Han, reconstructed) became, in Japan, a kind of constitution for the "ancient formula" (*koho*) school. Yoshimasu Todo and others in the Edo period stripped theory they thought was fluff and kept abdominal diagnosis and blunt formulas. You can dislike their bedside manner and still see the historical move: **reduce the pharmacy to a set of named compounds with named signs.** That move is why Kampo could later be computerized and insured.

## What the insurance list cannot hold

Folk *kampo-yaku* in the home, Chinese imports sold in Yokohama, Okinawan plants that never made the list, Ainu materia that the Yamato state did not file as Kampo. A national insurance schedule is a powerful historian. It is also a forgetter.

If this essay has an opinion, it is that Kampo's most interesting modern fact is bureaucratic. Not a cherry blossom. A code. The code is how a Tang-period formula idea still reaches a clinic in Sapporo on a Tuesday. Whether that formula does what the indication hopes is a clinical question for other rooms. The historical question is how it got a code at all.
