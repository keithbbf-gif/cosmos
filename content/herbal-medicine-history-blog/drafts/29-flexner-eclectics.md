# Flexner, the Eclectics, and a Vanishing Curriculum

In 1910 the Carnegie Foundation published *Medical Education in the United States and Canada*, written by Abraham Flexner, a schoolmaster who was not a physician and who had a very clear idea of what a scientific school looked like: Johns Hopkins, laboratories, full-time faculty, a hospital the school actually controlled. He visited, he graded, he was rude in print. Proprietary schools that lived on student fees and a few microscopes took damage they did not recover from.

Among the damaged were the Eclectic medical colleges — Cincinnati's Eclectic Medical Institute the most famous, with others in New York, Georgia, and the Midwest — which had spent the nineteenth century trying to be a botanic, less depleting alternative to "regular" medicine's mercury and bloodletting. John Haller's *Medical Protestants* is the sympathetic, unsparing history. They were not wizards. They were a curriculum with journals (*Eclectic Medical Journal*), a materia medica, and a chip on the shoulder. Flexner called the Cincinnati school better than some and still not Johns Hopkins. That sentence was enough, in the philanthropic weather of 1910, to start a closing.

## Thomson, Beach, and a people's pharmacy

Samuel Thomson, a New Hampshire farmer-physician of the early nineteenth century, sold a system of numbered botanic courses — lobelia, cayenne, steam, composition powder — and a franchise of "Thomsonian" rights that any family could buy. It was populist, anti-licensing, sometimes lethal (lobelia is not a toy), and wildly American. The regulars called it steam-and-puke. A lot of the country preferred it to calomel.

Wooster Beach and the later Eclectics tried to take the botanic impulse to school: to teach anatomy, to print journals, to professionalize without becoming regulars. Physiomedicalists (Alva Curtis and heirs) ran a parallel, more Thomsonian line. Homeopaths had their own colleges and their own later collapse or absorption into "regular" schools that kept a chair for a while.

The plants in the Eclectic *Materia Medica* (John King, later Felter and Lloyd) are a real American herbal: echinacea (the Eclectics loved *E. angustifolia* before the supplement aisle did), black haw, hamamelis, cactus, hydrastis, a hundred bitter barks. John Uri Lloyd's pharmacy (next essay) made the crude drugs respectable in the only way that counted in Cincinnati: they could be assayed, extracted, and shipped.

## What Flexner actually said

He said many of these schools were commercial and thin. He was often right about the thinness. He also assumed that the Johns Hopkins model was the only adult model, and that a botanic emphasis was, by itself, a mark of the second-rate. State boards and philanthropy read him as a shopping list. Schools closed. Surviving medicine became more hospital-centered, more laboratory-centered, and — this is the herbal point — **less interested in teaching a student to recognize a plant**.

The loss is not that "natural medicine was suppressed by a conspiracy." The loss is that a messy, sometimes useful, sometimes dangerous vernacular pharmacy lost its accredited hallway. What remained went into folk practice, into pharmacy's crude-drug counters (also fading), and, much later, into DSHEA's aisle, which has money and almost no curriculum.

## Regulars were not only villains

Regular medicine in 1910 was dropping some of its own worst habits. Antitoxin, surgery under asepsis, the first real laboratory diagnoses: Flexner was not inventing a cult. He was late to a shift. Eclectics who wanted to survive had to teach that shift too. Some did. The market for a third path still shrank.

Whorton's *Nature Cures* follows the longer American oscillation: botanic, water-cure, osteopathy, chiropractic, the 1970s renaissance. Flexner is a hinge, not the whole door.

## The empty lecture hall

If you walk through a surviving building in Cincinnati or look at a class photo from 1905, you see men (almost all men) who thought *Echinacea angustifolia* was worth a quiz. Today's oncology floor may use a vinca alkaloid without a single lecture on Madagascar. That is not hypocrisy. It is what a curriculum change looks like. The plant did not vanish. The requirement to know its face did.

This pack is, among other things, a replacement hallway. It does not restore a license. It restores the fact that the United States once taught botanical medicine as medicine, and then decided not to.
