# DSHEA, 1994: How the Aisle Got Its Law

On October 25, 1994, President Clinton signed the Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act, Public Law 103-417. The statute defined *dietary supplement* (vitamin, mineral, herb or other botanical, amino acid, a concentrate or metabolite of those, a combination), limited what FDA could demand before sale, allowed "statements of nutritional support" with a disclaimer, and required that disease-treatment claims stay on the drug side of the line. Senator Orrin Hatch of Utah and Senator Tom Harkin of Iowa were the political parents. Utah had industry. Iowa had Harkin's own story about a supplement and a friend's health — a story that belongs in the legislative history, not in a clinical textbook.

The aisle you walk in an American pharmacy is not a scientific classification. It is this statute, plus later cGMP rules (21 C.F.R. Part 111, fully effective in the late 2000s), plus a long argument about enforcement. GNC, the old health-food jobbers, the Utah manufacturers Hatch knew by name, the Prevention-magazine reader: these are the constituencies. A botanist is optional.

## Proxmire's earlier fence

In 1976 the Proxmire Amendment (Health Research and Health Services Amendments, Title V) limited FDA's power to treat vitamins and minerals as drugs merely because the dose was high. It was a response to an agency impulse, after the 1973 proposed rules, to cap potencies and to drag megavitamins toward the drug file. Rima Apple's *Vitamania* is the cultural book; the *Congressional Record* is the dry one. DSHEA eighteen years later widened the fence from vitamins to botanicals, amino acids, and the rest of the health-food store. The 1990s had ginseng, garlic, St. John's wort, melatonin, and a new kind of voter who had read that the government wanted the bottle.

FDA's 1993 advance notices and the Nutritional Labeling and Education Act fights of those years looked, to the industry, like a second Proxmire moment. Industry and consumer groups (an alliance that should always make a historian sit up) ran a campaign that included blacking out bottle labels in ads: this is what you will lose. Congress listened. The compromise is DSHEA. Harkin's personal story and Hatch's Utah manufacturers are not rumors; they are in the hearing record.

## What the law actually does

- **No pre-market approval** for most supplements, unlike new drugs.
- **Manufacturer responsibility** for safety and for the truth of whatever is said.
- **Structure/function claims** ("supports joint health") with a mandatory disclaimer that FDA has not evaluated the claim and the product is not intended to treat disease.
- **Disease claims** ("treats arthritis") make the product an unapproved drug.
- **New dietary ingredients** have a notification path that is still a fight.
- **Adulteration and misbranding** remain FDA's hooks, including the 2004 ephedra rule (essay 31).

This is not "unregulated." It is **differently regulated**, with the burden shifted. Critics in *NEJM* and *JAMA* at the time said Congress had legalized a fantasy. Supporters said Congress had stopped an agency from treating niacin like morphine. Both were talking about power.

## Boundary objects

Red yeast rice (Cholestin) contained a lovastatin-like statin. FDA said drug. The company said supplement. Courts and the agency spent years on the boundary. The teaching point: **chemistry does not decide; the statute's definitions and the product's history of use as a food or supplement decide, until they don't.** A capsule of powdered leaf and a capsule of an isolated constituent can share a shelf for reasons that would have bored Dioscorides and interested a Senate lawyer.

## The aisle as a historical category

Patent medicines were branded secrets. USP drugs were monographed. DSHEA supplements are a third American species: a food-like object with a drug-like hope and a disclaimer. This pack exists, in part, to keep that species from stealing the older stories. A *bencao* simple is not a DSHEA supplement until someone puts it in interstate commerce with a Supplement Facts box. The box is the citizenship paper.

If counsel wants a dull sentence on every modern product mention in this series, use this one: *In the United States, dietary supplements are regulated under DSHEA and are not approved by FDA as drugs to treat disease.* Then stop. Do not add a wink.
