# The Farmer Who Tasted Poisons

Shennong, the Divine Farmer, is said to have struck plants with a whip, tasted them, and mapped the harmless from the killing, his body a kind of mobile assay. He is also said to have invented agriculture. Both stories are doing the same job: they put the origin of knowledge in a body that works. The *Shen Nong Bencao Jing* — the *Divine Farmer's Materia Medica* — is the book that later scholars reconstructed from quotations, as if a Han library had once held the farmer's notes.

There was no farmer with a publication date. There was a genre. Han bibliographies already treat a *bencao* as a kind of book a library should hold. Tao Hongjing in the sixth century is the named editor who tried to sort the Divine Farmer's material from later accretions. The culture hero stayed on the title page because a title page wants a father.

## Three grades

The reconstructed classic sorts drugs into upper, middle, and lower grades (*shang, zhong, xia*). Upper drugs — ginseng is the celebrity — were said to nourish life and to be taken at length, "without harm." Lower drugs expelled illness and were not for lingering. That ranking is a philosophy of the body, not a modern safety study. It did, however, leave a long mark: the idea that some substances are food-like and some are weapons. Dietary-supplement marketing still lives off a vulgar version of that split, usually without the rest of the system (qi, flavor, channel entry) that made the split make sense.

Paul Unschuld's history of Chinese pharmaceutics is the book that stops you from treating this as a quaint list. The *bencao* is a literature: Han reconstructions, Tao Hongjing's *Bencao jing jizhu*, the Tang *Xinxiu bencao* (an official revision, 659, one of the first state herbals), Song enlargements, the absorption of foreign drugs (clove, frankincense, later New World goods), the fight over whether a name in an old book still points to the plant in the market. A state herbal is a taxonomical and a political act. The Tang revision was both.

## Li Shizhen's stubbornness

In 1596, after decades of travel, correspondence, and argument with printed predecessors, Li Shizhen's *Bencao Gangmu* went to woodblock. It is not a cute herbal. It is an encyclopedia that wants to settle synonyms, puncture obvious lies, and still leave room for wonders (the dragon bones, the odd animals). Carla Nappi's *The Monkey and the Inkpot* is the English book that treats Li as a writer, not as a statue in a TCM college lobby.

Li will tell you when he has only heard of a drug. That sentence — I have not seen this myself — is rarer in modern supplement copy than in a Ming pharmacologist. If you need a hero for this series, take the man who admits an unseen plant.

## Ginseng, ephedra, artemisia — names that will return

*Rénshēn* (*Panax* species; the naming is its own later fight) sits in the upper grade and in every later export story. *Má huáng* (*Ephedra*) sits in the classical lists as a releasing drug; the alkaloid ephedrine is a Meiji-era isolation, not a Han tablet (see the later essay). *Qīnghāo* (*Artemisia annua* and related names) sits in emergency formulas; Tu Youyou's cold extraction in the 1970s is a modern laboratory reading of an old instruction, not "the ancients ran HPLC."

Each of those plants will get its own trouble in this pack. The *bencao* essay's job is to show the shelf they sat on: a written, ranked, argued shelf, copied in provinces, sold in shops, and — after the twentieth century — drafted into nation-building as "Traditional Chinese Medicine," a phrase that is younger than Li Shizhen by a good margin.

## What not to do with a culture hero

Do not date Shennong. Do not put him in a lab coat in an illustration. Do not say he "discovered" a molecule. The farmer who tasted poisons is a way a civilization told itself that plant knowledge costs the body. Li Shizhen paid in time and in ink. The market that now sells "Shennong" teas pays in advertising. Keep the receipts separate.
