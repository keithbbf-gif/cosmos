# Bottles, Almanacs, and the American Cure

"Patent medicine" in the United States usually meant a secret-formula nostrum with a trademark, not a patent. The point of secrecy was the point of the business. If you knew it was 40 percent alcohol and a bitter, you might make it yourself. If you thought it was Lydia E. Pinkham's unique vegetable wisdom, you wrote to Lynn, Massachusetts, and you bought the bottle.

The nineteenth-century American landscape (the actual one: rail sidings, post offices, county fairs) was thick with bitters, sarsaparillas, female remedies, snake oil, worm candy, and something for the kidneys. James Harvey Young named the sellers toadstool millionaires. He was not being kind. He was being accurate about the margin. The post-Civil War cheap paper, the rural free delivery later, the lithographed trade card: these are the technical conditions. A man in a county without a regular physician could still have a picture of a kidney and a hope.

## What was in the glass

Alcohol, often — Hostetter's Stomach Bitters ran like a whiskey with a medical alibi. Opium, often, especially in soothing syrups and cough mixtures (Mrs. Winslow's, Godfrey's, paregoric by the pint). Strychnine and other "tonics," sometimes. Cannabis in some corn plasters and cough goods. Coca in some wines. Vegetable infusions that were not nothing and not enough. Chemical dyes. The better historians have assayed surviving bottles. The assays ruin some legends and confirm others.

Pinkham's Vegetable Compound, from Lynn, Massachusetts, was a mix of herbs (unicorn root, black cohosh, pleurisy root, and others in the published tradition) in alcohol. Lydia Pinkham wrote, or her family wrote in her name, and women wrote back about menstruation, childbirth, melancholy. The company printed the letters. A community of patients and a brand can occupy the same mailbox. She died in 1883; the face on the label did not.

Clark Stanley's Snake Oil Liniment is the joke that became an idiom. At the 1893 Chicago world's fair he killed rattlesnakes for the crowd. Later analysis of his oil (the 1910s, under the new law) found mineral oil, a bit of fat, capsaicin, and the usual theater. The real "snake oil" of Chinese railroad workers — a traditional liniment associated with the Chinese water snake — is a different object that American English stole and ruined. Say so, briefly. Hadacol, in the mid-twentieth century, proved the form could survive radio.

## The almanac as a distribution system

Hostetter's, Ayer's, and a dozen others printed almanacs that hung on a nail next to the calendar. Weather, jokes, testimonials, a picture of a man who was dying and is now plowing. This was not a sideshow at the edge of medicine. In many counties it *was* the printed medical culture. Regular physicians fumed. They also lost patients to a dollar bottle that did not require a visit.

The 1906 Act and the later advertising cases (essay 27) did not instantly empty the shelf. They changed what you could say on the label. Young's second volume, *The Medical Messiahs*, is the twentieth-century half: radio, then the FDA's long guerrilla war.

## Why this is herbal history

Because the plants were sometimes real (sassafras, yellow dock, gentian, cannabis in some cough goods, coca in some tonics) and because the **category** — a branded botanical drink that claims to fix a life — is the ancestor of the supplement aisle. DSHEA would later create a legal box that is not "patent medicine." The emotional box is related: a story, a bottle, a hope that the regular doctor is too proud or too costly to offer.

## Voice warning for editors

It is easy to get funny on this material. A little dryness is earned. Mocking the buyers is not. They had pain, shame, no antibiotics, and a postal service. The sellers who knew they were selling whiskey as a female tonic are fair game. The women who wrote to Mrs. Pinkham as if she were still alive (she died in 1883; the letters kept coming) are not a joke. They are the archive of a need.
