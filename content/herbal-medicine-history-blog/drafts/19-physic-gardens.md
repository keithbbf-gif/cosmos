# Padua, Chelsea, Kew: Gardens That Filled Bottles

In 1545 the Venetian Republic and the University of Padua planted an *orto dei semplici* — a garden of simples — in a circle inside a square, a geometry that still photographs well. The point was not geometry. Medical students had been learning plants from books that disagreed with the weeds of the Veneto. The garden put a labeled living body next to the name in Dioscorides and the *Canon*. If the labels were wrong, at least they were wrong in public.

Leiden followed in 1590, under Clusius's later shadow; Montpellier's older medical garden habit hardened into an institution; Oxford's physic garden (1621, later the Botanic Garden) put a yew and a catalog on the Cherwell; Paris's Jardin du Roi (1635) served a court as well as a faculty. A map of sixteenth- and seventeenth-century physic gardens is a map of who had a medical faculty and a fear of being cheated in the market. Pisa and Florence had planted a few years around Padua; the Veneto still gets the postcard because the circle is so photogenic.

## Padua's argument

Paula Findlen and others have written the Renaissance garden as a collection: a theater of nature, a princely display. True, and incomplete. The simples garden is also a warehouse for the hospital and the teaching clinic. A professor could walk a row of solanaceous plants and talk about madness and pupils without relying on a dried, swapped root from a merchant in Venice. The merchant still mattered. The garden was a check.

Padua's circle became a pilgrimage for later botanists. Linnaeus's generation still lived in this institution even when they wanted to rename everything.

## Chelsea's guild

The Worshipful Society of Apothecaries founded the Chelsea Physic Garden in 1673, on the Thames, in a warm pocket of London soil that Isaac Rand and later Philip Miller would make famous. The job was to train apprentices in the plants they would later buy as crude drugs. Sir Hans Sloane's patronage — a rent of £5 a year and a requirement to deliver well-prepared specimens to the Royal Society — endowed it. Sloane's own fortune sat on Jamaican plantation marriage and on a collector's appetite; the garden's warmth does not cancel that ledger. The garden still exists, still small, still stubborn, a fact that should shame larger institutions that turned into wedding venues.

A guild garden is a different political animal from a university *orto*. It answers to a trade. The labels serve a shop. Miller's *Gardeners Dictionary* then served the empire's other gardens. Labels travel.

## Kew's warehouse

Kew began as a royal pleasure and became, in the nineteenth century, the brain of British economic botany. Cinchona, rubber, tea, teak: the transfers that made plantations possible were planned with dried specimens, correspondence, and Wardian cases. Richard Drayton and Lucile Brockway have written this without the old imperial self-congratulation. A garden that fills bottles at home and fills ships abroad is not a neutral park.

When this pack talks about colonial extraction (essay 44), Kew is one of the addresses on the envelope. When it talks about honest identification, Kew's herbarium is one of the tools. Both sentences can be true. The garden does not get to pick which one we print.

## What a label does

A painted pot with a Latin name is a small machine for reducing a living, variable plant to a filing word. Pharmacy needs that reduction. Ecology hates it. The physic garden is the place they shake hands and then wipe their hands on their coats. Students left these paths and went into practice still capable of buying the wrong bark. The garden was never a complete fix. It was a better habit than the unlabelled sack.

If you visit Chelsea on a weekday, skip the gift lavender and stand in front of a poisonous bed — aconite, henbane, foxglove. Those plants were not there for atmosphere. They were there because the shop sold them and the apprentice had to know the leaf from the neighbor's leaf. That is the whole idea, and it is still a good one.
