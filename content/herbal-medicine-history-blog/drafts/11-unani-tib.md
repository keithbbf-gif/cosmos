# Unani Across the Monsoon

*Unani* means Ionian — Greek — in the mouths of the people who kept the word. The medicine it names in India and Pakistan is Greco-Arabic: humors, faculties, the *Canon*, the habit of the compound. It is also Indian in the only way that matters for a shop: the plants on the mat are often the plants of the subcontinent, and the patients bring complaints that never passed through Galen’s Rome.

A *hakim* in nineteenth-century Lucknow or Hyderabad was not doing "alternative medicine." He was doing medicine. The Nawabs' and Nizams' courts paid him that way. The alternative arrived later, when the colonial college and then the postcolonial ministry drew the lines. Delhi's Sharifi family, Lucknow's Azizi line, Hyderabad's unani dawakhana culture: these are proper names, not a mist called "the East."

## How a Greek word went east

The transmission is usually told as a relay: Greek to Syriac to Arabic to Persian to Urdu. Relays hide ships. The Indian Ocean carried not only texts but *materia* — myrrh and frankincense one way, spices, indigo, and Indian simples the other. Unani in the Deccan and the north absorbed *attar* and *majun*, opium as a known tool, cannabis in some compounds, metals in some lines, and a huge vernacular synonymy. Persian remained a learned language of the clinic into the colonial period; Urdu print then made the *Qānūn*'s grandchildren cheap.

Guy Attewell's *Refiguring Unani Tibb* is the book that treats this as social history rather than a decline-from-Avicenna story. Seema Alavi's *Islam and Healing* follows families through the Company and the Raj. Decline narratives are a colonial hobby. They need a golden past and a dirty present. The lithographed manuals of the late nineteenth century — Munshi Naval Kishore's presses in Lucknow among them — look, to a romantic, like a fall from manuscript gold. To a patient they looked like a shop that still opened. A lithograph is how a humoral pharmacy enters a railway century.

## Compounds, not capsules

Classical Unani pharmacy loves the compound: *majun* (electuary), *khamira*, *sharbat*, *raughan*. The logic is Galenic and practical — palatability, shelf, multiple qualities. The modern capsule of a single "Unani herb" is a different object, born of factories and export. If that capsule is sold in the United States as a dietary supplement, it lives under DSHEA, not under the *Canon*. The label's "ancient Unani" is a marketing tense.

In India the state category is AYUSH — Ayurveda, Yoga & Naturopathy, Unani, Siddha, Homoeopathy — a bureaucratic bouquet that would have puzzled Ibn Sīnā and Charaka equally. The ministry is a historical fact. It is not a proof of efficacy. It is a proof that these pharmacies became nations' business.

## What a monsoon pharmacy knows

It knows damp. It knows a fever season. It knows that a Persian name in a book may be a Tamil plant in the market (the old identification problem, never solved by patriotism). It knows the reputation of a house: which *hakim*'s *majun* is trusted. Reputation is data of a kind. It is not a trial.

Seema Alavi and others have shown how Unani families navigated colonial licensing, print, and the new hospitals. The story is not purity. It is negotiation. That is less pretty than a courtyard myth and more useful if you want to understand why Unani shops still exist on streets where a chain pharmacy also exists.

## After the gazette

The Indian Medicine Central Council Act (1970) and the later AYUSH ministry did not invent Unani. They licensed a version of it. A licensed *hakim* and a family dawakhana that never sought a number are not the same profession, even when they share a *majun*. Pakistan and Bangladesh kept their own boards. The diaspora shops in Bradford and in Jackson Heights sell a third version, often in DSHEA-shaped bottles if the destination is the United States.

## Write it without the postcard

No hookah-and-moonlight paragraph. No "East meets West." The monsoon is a climate and a shipping calendar. Unani is a profession that kept Greek words and Indian plants in the same jar. The jar is the subject. The rest is tourism.
