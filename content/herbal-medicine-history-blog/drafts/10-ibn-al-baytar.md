# Ibn al-Baytar's Walking Inventory

Diyāʾ al-Dīn Abū Muḥammad ʿAbd Allāh ibn Aḥmad al-Mālaqī — Ibn al-Bayṭār, "son of the veterinarian" — left Málaga and did not treat that as a tragedy. In the first half of the thirteenth century he moved through the Maghrib, Egypt, and the Levant, collecting names. The book that came out of the walking, *al-Jāmiʿ li-mufradāt al-adwiya wa-l-aghdhiya* (the *Comprehensive Book of Simple Drugs and Foods*), runs to well over a thousand entries. It is arranged as a working alphabet of stuff: plants, stones, foods that are also drugs.

He cites Dioscorides, Galen, al-Ghāfiqī, al-Idrīsī's plant notes, and men he met who had no books. He also contradicts them when a Syrian market or a Maghribi hill said otherwise. The *Jāmiʿ* is alphabetized in the Arabic manner, which is a working choice: a compiler who expects to be looked up, not read through as a romance.

## After the lectern

By Ibn al-Bayṭār's generation, Dioscorides had been Arabic for centuries — Istifan ibn Basil and Hunayn's circle, then the revisions that tried to match Greek names to Syrian and Egyptian plants. That translation was a beginning, not an end. Names slipped. A Greek plant with a dry-country cousin in Iberia invited confident error. Al-Ghāfiqī's earlier Andalusian herbal was one of his sources; he is not a lone genius walking out of a mosque. The *Jāmiʿ* is, among other things, a correction file. He will line up the Greek, the Arabic synonym, the Berber or Romance word he heard, and the look of the thing. This is not "Islamic Golden Age" as a poster. It is a man doing the unglamorous work of not repeating a dead traveler.

He served the Ayyubid court in Cairo under al-Kāmil as chief herbalist (*raʾīs al-ʿashshābīn*). Courts like inventories. They also like the prestige of a scholar who has been on the road. Both motives can fund good books. Cairo in the 1230s–40s was a city that could still buy Indian and East African goods; his entries know that. A walking herbalist who never left a valley would have written a shorter book.

## Food and drug in one alphabet

The title pairs *adwiya* and *aghdhiya* — drugs and foods. That pairing is older than him and younger than our supermarket aisles. A food can be a temperament. A drug can be a soup. The later European habit of putting "nutrition" in one faculty and "pharmacy" in another would have seemed, to this compiler, like splitting a plant down the middle for the convenience of two guilds.

When modern supplement law (DSHEA and its cousins) wrestles with whether a powdered leaf is a food or a drug, it is having a thirteenth-century argument with worse prose.

## What walking knows

A lectern botanist knows the best manuscript. A walking botanist knows that the same Arabic name is two plants fifty miles apart, and that a merchant will sell you the cheaper one. Ibn al-Bayṭār's authority is the sentence that says, in effect, *I saw this in the land of the Nubians; the Egyptians call it X.* Even when we cannot recover the exact species, we can recover the method. The method is the ancestor of pharmacognosy and of honest travel writing.

He died in Damascus in 1248. The *Jāmiʿ* went on being copied. Later European botanists would "discover" plants he had already tried to pin. Some of that is independent work. Some of it is the ordinary amnesia of people who do not read Arabic.

## Against the poster

Do not illustrate this essay with a stock courtyard and a lute. Illustrate it with a list. The romance of al-Andalus has its own files. This file is a man who thought a simple drug was worth a journey and a synonym. That is a professional ethic. It is also, if you need an opinion, a better ethic than the one that writes "ancient Arab secret" on a bottle in a mall in California.
