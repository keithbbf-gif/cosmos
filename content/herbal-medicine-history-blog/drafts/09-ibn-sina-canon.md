# The Canon in a Thousand Rooms

Ibn Sīnā (Avicenna to the Latins) finished the world in prose. The *Kitāb al-Shifāʾ* took philosophy and the sciences. The *Qānūn fī al-ṭibb* — the *Canon of Medicine* — took the body. He was born near Bukhara in 980, served courts, died at Hamadan in 1037, and wrote as if a complete book could reduce the chaos of practice to something a student in a strange city could open.

The *Canon* is five books (*kutub*). Book 1: general principles and anatomy. Book 2: simples. Book 3: diseases from head to foot. Book 4: diseases that are not local (fevers, and the rest). Book 5: the formulary, the *aqrābādhīn*. The simples and the formulary are why this series cares. They are a warehouse with an index. A student in Montpellier or in Isfahan could, in theory, find a drug without rereading the philosophy. That is a design decision, and it won.

## A Persian physician, a Greek machine, a larger market

Ibn Sīnā's architecture is Galenic: faculties, humors, pulses, the need for a cause. His market is wider. The simples include goods that had moved along the silk and incense routes for centuries — cassia, camphor, musk, Indian aromatics — sitting beside Mediterranean staples Dioscorides would have recognized. He will give temperament, degree, and indications in a compressed, teachable form. That compression is why the book won. A working doctor can hate a long story and still love a table.

He was not a field botanist in the Dioscorides manner. He was a systematizer who assumed the names could be made to behave. When they could not — when a Persian name and an Arabic name and a Greek name pointed at three shrubs — later commentators fought in the margins. Those fights are the live science.

## Latin rooms

Gerard of Cremona's Toledo generation put the *Canon* into Latin in the twelfth century. Andrea Alpago and the Renaissance correctors later tried to fix the Latin against Arabic copies. For four hundred years it was the long book in European medical faculties — Bologna, Padua, Paris, Montpellier, later the German universities. You lectured on it. You disputed it. You kept Galen and Hippocrates in the same breath because the *Canon* had already braided them. Gentile da Foligno and Jacques Despars wrote the kind of commentary that makes a modern reader grateful for indexes.

When Renaissance humanists cried for Greek purity, they were often crying against this Arabic-Latin Avicenna who had become the furniture. The cry missed what the *Canon* had done for pharmacy: it had made the compound and the simple equally respectable objects of study, and it had assumed that a drug from far away could be discussed with the same tools as a drug from the hill behind the hospital. Camphor and rhubarb sit in that assumption. So does the later European habit of wanting a "simple" from India to behave like a simple from Campania.

## What he does not give you

He does not give you a randomized trial. He does not give you a safe modern dose. He does not give you permission to sell "Avicenna's cure" in a dropper bottle. He gives you a picture of elite medicine at the moment when Bukhara and Isfahan could still think of themselves as the center of the useful world.

There is a later Iranian and Unani life of the *Canon* that never passed through Paris (see the Unani essay). That life is not a footnote to Europe. It is another set of rooms. The title of this piece is not a joke. The book was copied until the copies had their own weather.

If you only meet Ibn Sīnā as a name on a postage stamp or a "father of" list, you meet a statue. Open the simples. Watch a man try to make a continent of plants sit still long enough to be taught. That effort — not a miracle cure — is the historical event.
