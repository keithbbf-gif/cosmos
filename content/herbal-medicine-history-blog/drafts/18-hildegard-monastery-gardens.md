# Hildegard, Physica, and the Cloister Plot

Hildegard of Bingen (1098–1179) saw visions, bullied abbots, wrote music that still fills churches, and compiled a book of things — *Physica* — in which plants, elements, trees, stones, fish, and animals each get a short, forceful paragraph. The plant chapters are why the wellness industry learned her name. She will say that a herb is hot and dry, that it is good against a named sadness or a fever, that it should be taken in beer or as a mash. She writes like a person who has watched a garden and a sick dormitory in the same week.

She does not write like a 1980s naturopath. That voice was added later, mostly in German popular medicine after Gottfried Hertzka, and then in American translation. The Rupertsberg woman who lectured abbots and wrote the *Scivias* would have recognized the plants. She would not have recognized the trademark.

## The garden as a department

A Benedictine house needed vegetables, flavor, dyes, and a chest of simples. The infirmarian and the cellarer were not always friends. The *Capitulare de villis*, associated with Charlemagne's court, already lists medicinal and kitchen plants a royal estate should keep — a ninth-century shopping list, not a vision. Walahfrid Strabo's *Hortulus*, written at Reichenau two centuries before Hildegard, is a poem that is also a plant list: sage, rue, fennel, mint, the melon he baby-sat. The famous St. Gall plan (c. 820s) draws an ideal infirmary garden and a kitchen garden as separate rectangles. Real plots were messier. The cloister plot is still one of the West's durable pharmacies — not because monks were magicians, but because they were institutional. Institutions write things down and replant in the spring.

Hildegard's Rhine houses (Disibodenberg, then Rupertsberg, then Eibingen) sat in a country already full of Roman leftovers and German names. *Physica* mixes those names. A plant may carry a Latin label and a local use. Spelt (*Dinkel*), which the modern brand treats as a sacrament, is in her dietetic world as a grain among grains. That mix is the historical texture. A "Hildegard original" biscuit with a barcode is a different texture.

## *Viriditas* is not a supplement

Hildegard's theology of *viriditas* — greenness as a name for living vigor — is a mystical and ecological idea. It is easy to print on a tea tin. The tin is doing brand work. The visions are doing visionary work. Keep them in different sentences. *Causae et curae*, the other medical compilation associated with her, is messier in transmission; some of what circulates in German popular medicine as "Hildegard-Medizin" (Gottfried Hertzka and later) is a twentieth-century clinical folklore attached to her prestige. Flag it. She cannot consent.

## What she shares with the Salernitan world

The twelfth century was a century of translations (Constantine the African at Monte Cassino; later Toledo) and of Salerno's teaching. Hildegard is not a Salernitan. She is a German abbess with access to a Latin toolkit and a garden. She will sound, at moments, like the *Circa instans* — a simple, a quality, a use — because that was the international style. The style is not evidence that she secretly attended a college in Italy. It is evidence that the cloister was not sealed.

## Against the candlelit brand

Do not photograph this essay with a woman in a wimple holding lavender at golden hour. Use a plan, a parsley bed, a page. Hildegard was a politician of the spirit and a writer. The plants in *Physica* belong to a Rhine ecology and a humoral vocabulary. They are not waiting to complete anyone's protocol. If a magazine wants to honor her, it could start by quoting a plant paragraph in full — heat, use, vehicle — and then saying, plainly, that a twelfth-century indication is a historical sentence, not a license to treat a disease.
