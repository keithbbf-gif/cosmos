# Cannabis In and Out of the Pharmacopeia

In 1850 the *United States Pharmacopeia* recognized *Cannabis indica*. Physicians wrote tinctures for a grab bag of nineteenth-century complaints — spasms, sleeplessness, the always-useful "nerves." O'Shaughnessy had already reported on Indian use in the 1830s to a British medical public. The plant was not a counterculture. It was a crude drug with a smell and a variable punch, sitting near opium and valerian in the same wooden drawers.

In 1937 the Marijuana Tax Act made ordinary medical commerce a federal paper nightmare. In 1942 the USP dropped the monograph. In 1970 the Controlled Substances Act put cannabis in Schedule I. In 2018 the Agriculture Improvement Act — the Farm Bill — defined hemp by delta-9-THC percentage and reopened a legal field for *Cannabis sativa* as an agricultural good. CBD then flooded the U.S. supplement-adjacent market, dragging FDA into a series of warning letters: this is not an approved drug, this is not a legal dietary-supplement ingredient in the agency's current view, this claim is a disease claim.

The plant kept making flowers. The filing cabinets changed religion. William Brooke O'Shaughnessy's 1830s papers from Calcutta — *On the Preparations of the Indian Hemp, or Gunjah* — are the usual British doorway; they are also Company medicine. The USP's 1850 recognition was American filing of a colonial report plus a shop reality already on the Atlantic.

## What the old monograph knew

It knew a tincture, an extract, a variability problem. Nineteenth-century physicians already complained that one batch was noise and one was a sledgehammer. That complaint is why later isolates (THC, then CBD) looked like civilization to some pharmacologists and like a loss of "entourage" to later marketers. Both are arguments in a long file. Neither is a recipe.

W. B. O'Shaughnessy's Calcutta papers are colonial documents: an Irish physician in Company India, reporting on a plant Indians already knew. Read him for the transmission into British journals, not as an origin.

## Anslinger and the weather

Harry Anslinger's Federal Bureau of Narcotics campaign, the Hearst press, racialized stories about Mexican and Black users, the 1936 picture *Reefer Madness* as a later joke that was not a joke in its first rooms: Musto and a generation of historians have written this. The 1937 Act is a tax act in the Harrison tradition, used as a ban — *United States v. Sanchez* and later cases would show how a tax stamp nobody could get is a prohibition. The AMA's legislative counsel, William C. Woodward, testified that the medical evidence for a federal ban was thin. He lost. The medical lobby was weaker than it had been for opium; cannabis had fewer hospital friends by the 1930s, and the word *marijuana* in the hearings was doing ethnic work the word *cannabis* in the USP had not done.

The USP drop in 1942 is a quiet, important date. When a pharmacopeia forgets a plant, the next generation of pharmacists is not taught its face. The 1970 schedule then taught them a police face instead.

## Hemp, CBD, and the aisle

"Hemp" is an industrial and now a legal word, not a separate species in the way farm catalogs implied. The 2018 threshold (0.3 percent delta-9-THC on a dry-weight basis) is a political number. CBD products then occupied a space that looks like DSHEA and often fails DSHEA: disease claims on the internet, oily droppers, a consumer wish to be both legal and medical. FDA approved one purified CBD drug (Epidiolex) for rare seizures — a drug, with a file. That approval did not bless the gas-station dropper.

State-legal cannabis medical and adult-use systems are a third filing system, not a pharmacopeia and not DSHEA. This essay will not map them as a shopper's guide. It will say that the United States now runs at least three overlapping cannabis bureaucracies: controlled substance, farm commodity, and (in some states) licensed intoxicant. A 1890s tincture bottle would not know where to sit.

## No romance, no manual

Do not print a grow-room. Do not print a "then and now" that implies the tincture was harmless and the schedule was only madness. Cannabis is a psychoactive plant with a real medical research file and a real dependence and psychosis file. History can hold both. What it cannot hold is a magazine tutorial. The interesting remaining fact is bureaucratic: **a USP drug became a Schedule I substance became, in part, a Farm Bill crop.** Few plants have so many passports.
