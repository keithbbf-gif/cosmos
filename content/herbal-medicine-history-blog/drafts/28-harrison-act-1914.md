# The Harrison Act and the Locked Cabinet

The Harrison Narcotics Tax Act of December 17, 1914, pretended, in its bones, to be a revenue statute. Physicians, dentists, veterinarians, and certain dealers would register, pay a tax, and use order forms. Opium and coca leaves, and their compounds, salts, and preparations, would move on paper. The Supreme Court, in a series of cases, then told the country what Congress had also meant: a doctor could not, as a business, maintain an addict. The clinic became a crime scene if the prescription looked like a habit.

Francis Burton Harrison, a New York Democrat who later became Governor-General of the Philippines, gave the bill his name; the Treasury's Bureau of Internal Revenue gave it teeth. David Musto's *The American Disease* is still the book. The disease in his title is the policy as much as the dependence. The Philippines matter: American opium policy in a colony was a rehearsal for American opium policy at home, a fact the 1914 debates only half admitted.

## Before the lock

A respectable 1890s American pharmacy sold opium preparations as ordinary stock. Coca wines and tonics sat in the same culture as Vin Mariani and the early Coca-Cola formula (the later drink is a different, heavily processed object). Physicians injected morphine because they could and because pain is loud. None of this was hidden. It was a medical and commercial commonplace that the 1906 Act had only begun to label.

What changed in the 1910s was international (Hague Opium Convention) plus domestic race-and-vice politics that Musto and others have documented without prettying it. Enforcement fantasies attached to Chinese opium and to Black cocaine use in the Southern press. The plant was not the only thing being administered.

## Tax as a police power

Congress in 1914 was less sure it could just "ban" a medicine than later Congresses would be. The commerce clause and the police power of the states were a constitutional thicket; a tax was a familiar federal tool (see also the later Marijuana Tax Act). The tax-and-form machine let the Treasury's agents walk into a shop. *United States v. Doremus* (1919) and *Webb v. United States* (1919) told physicians that a prescription written to feed a habit was not a prescription the Act would recognize. *Jin Fuey Moy* (1916) had gone the other way on a technical point; the Court then hardened. Thousands of physicians found that their ordinary compassion, or their ordinary sloppiness, or their ordinary business, had a new name.

Some patients went to the remaining municipal clinics (New York, New Orleans, a short list); those were shut by the early 1920s. Some went to the illicit market that the law, like any prohibition, helped professionalize. The 1925 Geneva opium agreements and the later Federal Bureau of Narcotics (1930, Anslinger) are the administrative sequel.

This is not an argument for a kitchen poppy. It is an argument that **category changes are historical events**. The same tablet can be a grocery, a prescription, and a felony in three decades.

## What the cabinet did to herbal remnants

Paregoric, Dover's powder, cannabis tinctures (still legal, still official, not yet the 1937 target), cocaine in dental and ocular use: the shop's botanical-chemical mix was sorted into "ordinary" and "narcotic." The sorting taught Americans to see some plants as inherently police matters. Coca leaf and opium cake, which have long medical and cultural lives outside the U.S., entered U.S. law as a pair. The pairing still structures treaties.

## After Harrison

1920s enforcement, the Federal Bureau of Narcotics, Harry Anslinger's later cannabis campaign, 1970's Controlled Substances Act: a lineage of paper. The locked cabinet in a 1920s drugstore is the ancestor of the steel box in a modern pharmacy and of the database that now watches the box. If you want a closing image, use the register: lined columns, names, dates, quantities. It is a literary form. It is also a trap. This pack will not teach anyone how to slip it. It will say that the slip and the lock were born together, in 1914, under the polite name of a tax.
