*Atropa belladonna* is a Solanaceae shrub with a reputation that outran the shop: dilating pupils in the early modern cosmetic story, flying ointments in the folklore file, a standard official leaf in nineteenth-century pharmacopeias. The cosmetic story is half proverb. The official leaf is paper. The plant can kill a child who eats the berries. That sentence is enough warning. This essay will not improve anyone's ointment.

Geiger, Hesse, and Mein sit in the 1830s isolation file; 1833 is the date this pack's graphics locked. `[VERIFY]` the first clean atropine paper against Sneader or a history-of-chemistry handbook before a print timeline treats 1833 as sacred. Hyoscyamine and scopolamine are neighbor alkaloids from neighbor plants (*Hyoscyamus*, *Datura*, *Mandragora*). The family is a nightshade argument, not a single hero molecule.

## From materia medica to named principle

Dioscorides and the Latin herbals already treat mandrake and henbane as dangerous. Belladonna's vernacular names (deadly nightshade, dwale) are English warnings. Linnaeus’s *Atropa* remembers Atropos, the Fate who cuts the thread. Figure 1 is leaf to named base. Not a method. The pharmacopeia kept the leaf and the tincture after the isolate existed because shops are conservative and because a whole-leaf preparation is a different, dirtier object. Hyoscyamine can racemize toward atropine in some old preparations; scopolamine (hyoscine) is the neighbor alkaloid. Three tropanes. One family argument. A leaf infusion and a weighed crystal must not share a recipe card.

Ophthalmology and anesthesia later used atropine as a tool among tools. Those are hospital stories. They are not folk-use validations. A "traditional belladonna" capsule on a U.S. shelf, if it exists, is a DSHEA or a drug-misbranding problem depending on the claim — not a Renaissance cosmetic.

## Mein, a Fate, a tincture

Philipp Lorenz Geiger and Hesse, and the pharmacist Mein, sit in the 1831–1833 isolation file. `[VERIFY]` the first clean atropine paper against Sneader. The name *Atropa* is Linnaeus remembering Atropos, the Fate who cuts the thread. English *dwale* is an older warning. Nineteenth-century ophthalmology used atropine to dilate for the mirror; later anesthesia used it to dry secretions. Those are hospital tools. They do not validate a Renaissance cosmetic proverb and they do not instruct a folklore ointment.
