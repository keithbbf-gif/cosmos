The Advance Beneficiary Notice of Noncoverage is a government form with a bureaucratic name and a human job. It is how fee-for-service Medicare wants a person to hear, *before* the service, that Medicare is likely not to pay, and to choose anyway, or not.

CMS keeps the form (CMS-R-131), instructions, and a “Beneficiary Notices Initiative” page in public. The news is not that the form exists. The news is that clinics still treat it as optional stationery, or as a blanket people sign on admission “just in case.” Both habits miss the point.

## What the form is for

A specific service, on a specific date or short span, that you have a *reason* to think Medicare will deny — not medically reasonable and necessary as they mean it, or not a covered benefit, or frequency they will not buy. The person gets to say: bill Medicare anyway, I’ll pay if they refuse; don’t bill Medicare, I’ll pay; or never mind, I don’t want it.

That is a choice. A choice needs a brain that is not being rushed through a clipboard at check-in for an unrelated visit.

## What the form is not

It is not a general waiver of all future speech therapy. It is not a substitute for an LCD you have not read (essay 22). It is not a way to keep billing after you have decided the care is no longer skilled and just hope. It is not an ABN-shaped object for Medicare Advantage — Advantage is a plan with its own notices. Mixing them is how people sign the wrong religion.

It is not, by itself, a clinical discharge. A person can decline to sign, decline the service, or ask for a last visit to wrap goals. Those are clinical conversations that happen to have a form in the room.

## Why SLPs meet it

Maintenance that someone in the building thinks is “unskilled.” A second eval too soon. A treatment the LCD in your state is allergic to. A family who wants to continue after you have said, carefully, that Medicare’s idea of necessity and their idea of hope have diverged. Telehealth on a day when the authority is wobbly (essay 23) has also put ABNs in the conversation; ASHA has mentioned them in telehealth write-ups. The form follows the uncertainty. It does not create the clinical opinion.

If you do not have an opinion about coverage, you are not ready to present the form. “Our biller said to” is not an opinion.

## What this page will not do

It will not script the conversation. It will not tell you which box to tick. It will not tell you how to “ABN every 92507 after visit six.” Frequency recipes are how news becomes a liability.

It will tell you the form is public, current, and meant to be given *before*, not after a denial when everyone is already angry.

## A hallway version

“If we think Medicare won’t pay this particular thing, we have a government form that lets the person choose with their eyes open. It’s not a blanket. It’s not Advantage. It’s not a vibe.” If your compliance office has a policy that is stricter, their policy wins. If they have no policy, the CMS page is a better teacher than a Facebook example from 2014.

A last, small honesty: some buildings use the ABN as a way to keep a family paying after the clinician has already decided the care is not skilled. That is not what the form was built for. If the care is not skilled, the adult sentence is discharge or a different payer, not a clipboard. If the care might be skilled and Medicare might still say no, the form has a job. Mixing those two stories is how people lose trust in both.

Essay 22 is the document that often sits under the ABN: a local coverage determination that is law in one contractor’s neighborhood and a rumor in another.
