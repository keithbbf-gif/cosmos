For a long stretch of the 2000s, hoarseness could earn a proton-pump inhibitor the way a sore throat earned an antibiotic: automatically, from a story about reflux, before anyone looked at the folds. Primary care did it. Some ENTs did it. Patients arrived at voice therapy already on a purple pill and a diet they hated, still hoarse.

In 2018 the American Academy of Otolaryngology—Head and Neck Surgery Foundation updated its hoarseness (dysphonia) clinical practice guideline. The public fact sheet and the PubMed record are the objects to hold. Among the recommendations *against*: do not prescribe antireflux medicine for isolated dysphonia based on symptoms alone blamed on GERD or LPR, without visualizing the larynx. Also against: routine antibiotics for dysphonia; routine steroids before a look; imaging before a look. For: identify dysphonia, look (or refer to someone who can) if it lasts past about four weeks or sooner if something scary is in the story, and know that voice therapy is a thing you document after you have seen the folds.

That is ENT’s document, not ASHA’s. SLPs still live in its weather. We are often the people who notice the person has been on a PPI for eight months and has never met a laryngoscope.

## Why the guideline used “against”

The update group said the evidence for empiric PPI in isolated hoarseness was thin, the delay was real (you can sit on a pill while a lesion grows), and the harms of long-term PPI were no longer a shrug — infection, bone, kidney, the usual list that made internists nervous in the same decade. Stachler, Francis, and colleagues put consumer harm in the same sentence as cost. That pairing is the news. It is not a dare to rip a pill out of a person who has confirmed erosive esophagitis and a gastroenterologist.

Isolated is the word. A person with heartburn, a known reflux diagnosis, and a hoarse voice is a different story than a singer who was handed omeprazole because someone heard “morning voice.” Guidelines live in the adjectives (essay 08). Threads drop the adjectives.

## What an SLP does with a 2018 document in 2026

You do not prescribe. You do not discontinue. You ask whether anyone has seen the larynx, and you write that question where a physician will find it. You do not turn a guideline into a shame script for a family doctor who was doing what their 2012 in-service said.

You also do not start a voice exercise list from this URL. ASHA’s public voice page is for families. Your license is for the plan. The guideline’s own disclaimer says it is not a protocol for every person. Ours says the same louder.

## What has not settled

LPR as a culture war. Some laryngologists still treat reflux as a leading lady. Some voice SLPs roll their eyes so hard they miss a real GI story. The adult posture is the look first, the GI story when the look or the history asks for it, the pill when a physician owns the pill. 2018 did not end the war. It moved the burden.

New pills and new reflux tests will get their own papers. Read them with essays 01–05. Do not let a vendor of a reflux gadget promote itself into a replacement look.

## A hallway version

“ENT’s 2018 hoarseness guideline said not to start reflux medicine for isolated hoarseness from symptoms alone, without seeing the larynx. I’m going to ask about the look. I’m not going to prescribe, and I’m not going to run a therapy protocol in a news brief.” If a resident wants the executive summary, it is in *Otolaryngology–Head and Neck Surgery*. If a patient wants to stop a pill tonight, they want their physician.
