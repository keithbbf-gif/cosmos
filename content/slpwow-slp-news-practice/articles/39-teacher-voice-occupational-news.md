Verify the current CMS, MAC, state, or ASHA page before you act. This series does not evaluate patients and does not publish worksheets.

A teacher’s voice is equipment. Schools rarely budget it that way.

Occupational voice research — Roy and colleagues, and the papers that followed — has said for years that teachers report hoarseness, missed days, and career worry at rates that would shut down a factory line. NIDCD’s public voice pages and ASHA’s consumer voice page sit in the same neighborhood without always naming the classroom. The news is not a new muscle. The news is that amplification, room acoustics, class size, and sick-leave culture are voice interventions a building can buy, and often does not.

## Why this is a labor story

If the only answer to a hoarse third-grade teacher is “see an SLP after work and do your exercises,” the district has privatized a workplace hazard. Voice therapy can be skilled and useful. It cannot dry a gym they keep assigning as a classroom. It cannot shrink 32 five-year-olds. It cannot put a microphone in a budget that prefers a new scoreboard.

Unions have started to say “occupational” out loud in some districts. Workers’ compensation for voice is still a local maze. Some teachers are told hoarseness is personal wellness. “Wellness” is a word that moves money off the building and onto the person. Be suspicious of it here.

## What the 2018 ENT guideline adds, even for teachers

Look at the larynx if the hoarseness sticks, or sooner if the story is scary (essay 38). Do not start with a PPI and a hope. Teachers get both delays: they wait until break, and someone hands them a pill. A school nurse who can ask “has anyone seen your folds” is doing occupational health. A school SLP who can make the referral path less mystical is doing a colleague a favor. Neither is the teacher’s treating therapist unless the dual-relationship is clean and wanted — it often is not.

## Amplification is news because it is boring

Classroom amplification and personal mics show up in education-research abstracts the way fluoride shows up in dental ones: not glamorous, hard to get a press cycle. When a district pilots mics in K–2 and the nurse’s hoarseness log drops, that is a local story worth repeating as *news*, not as a brand. This pack will not rank devices. Rankings become ads.

## What this is not

A resonant-voice protocol. A “vocal hygiene” handout. Those objects exist in our literature and in ASHA’s professional pages. Dumping them here would be a worksheet. A teacher who needs therapy needs a licensed clinician and, often, an ENT look. A teacher who needs a mic needs a principal.

## A hallway version

“Teacher hoarseness is a workforce issue with data behind it. Therapy can be part of the answer. So can the room. I’m not going to homework a colleague from a blog.” If a school board wants citations, start with the public ASHA/NIDCD pages and the occupational papers you actually hold. If they want a hygiene flyer, they can write their own and date it.

Substitute teachers and coaches live in the same hazard and get even less of the story. A Friday football gym is a voice environment. So is a cafeteria duty. If the only “voice plan” in the building is a flyer on the SLP’s door, the building has not made a plan. Occupational news is allowed to be this boring: hours, rooms, mics, leave. Therapy is what you add when those are not enough, or when the folds need a look. Start with the room. Then open the referral path. That order is the headline.
