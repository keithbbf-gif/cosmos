Auditors do not collect adjectives at random. They collect *skilled*, because the benefit was built on it.

Medicare outpatient therapy, SNF therapy, and home-health therapy all, in their different manuals, want care that needs a therapist’s judgment — not a routine a family could run from a photocopied sheet. “Patient tolerated treatment well. Continue plan” became a meme because it does not answer that want. The meme is funny. The denial is not.

## Jimmo is older than your EMR and still news

The Jimmo settlement told CMS to remember that improvement is not the only door. Skilled maintenance — a program that needs a therapist so the person does not deteriorate unsafely — can be a covered idea when the facts fit. CMS keeps a Jimmo fact-sheet page because the field keeps forgetting. Home-health PDGM materials (SE20005) repeat the same moral: no improvement standard as a wall.

Forgetting Jimmo looks like a discharge the day the score plateaus, even though the swallow plan still needs a clinician to adjust and the family still cannot run it safely. Remembering Jimmo badly looks like “maintenance” as a slogan over a note that describes a chat and a smile.

This page will not write your maintenance note. It will say the public door exists, and that “plateau” is not a synonym for “unskilled.”

## What *skilled* is pointing at

A decision that could have gone the other way. An assessment inside the session, not only at eval. A change to the plan because the person in front of you was not the person in the eval. Teaching that is not a handout alone. A risk you managed. A collaboration with a nurse or a dietitian that changed the next hour.

None of those bullets is a template. Templates are how skilled becomes a word you sprinkle like pepper. Auditors can taste pepper.

School documentation has a cousin problem with different words: *specialized instruction*, *related service*, *progress toward the goal*. The auditor, if there is one, may be a Medicaid reviewer, not a MAC. The religion is still “why you, why this, why today.” Essay 26. Do not paste Medicare adjectives into an IEP to make it look medical. Do not paste IEP adjectives into a Part B note to make it look educational. Two clocks (essay 30).

## Why this sits in the school-vs-medical wave

Because traveling clinicians bring the wrong spice. Because families hear “not skilled” as “not worth it.” Because a SNF that under-documents SLP after PDPM (essay 19) can look, on paper, like a building that never needed speech — until the surveyor or the family asks about the choking event.

The news is not a new definition. The news is that the old definition is still the thing that gets a chart pulled at $3,000 (essay 16) and at $0, if the pattern looks odd.

## A hallway version

“Skilled is a coverage word. It means a therapist’s judgment was the point of the visit. Jimmo says the judgment can be about keeping someone safe, not only about a score going up. I’m not going to type the word as decoration.” If a vendor sells a ‘Jimmo-proof’ phrase bank, they are selling pepper.

A note that can take a question usually answers four ordinary things: what you assessed today, what you did that a family could not have done from a sheet, what changed because of what you saw, and what the next visit is for. You can write those in your own verbs. You do not need a purchased phrase. You do need to have been in the room. Auditors are not brilliant. They can tell when the room was empty.

School Medicaid reviewers, when they exist, ask a cousin of the same four things in education clothes. If your district’s claiming vendor sells a “skilled” dropdown for an IEP service log, treat the dropdown as a reminder, not as the sentence. The sentence is still yours.
