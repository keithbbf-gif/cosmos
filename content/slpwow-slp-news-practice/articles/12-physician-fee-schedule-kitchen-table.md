Here is the paragraph.

Medicare Part B pays most outpatient speech-language pathology the way it pays a lot of other clinic work: take the relative value of the code (work, practice expense, malpractice), adjust it a little for geography, and multiply by a conversion factor Congress and CMS fight about every fall. The product is a fee. The fee is not a salary. It is not what a family owes. It is not what an Advantage plan will do. It is the official arithmetic for fee-for-service Medicare, published in the Physician Fee Schedule, even when the person doing the work is not a physician.

That is the whole machine. The rest of this page is why the machine feels personal.

## Why it is called a physician schedule when you are not one

History. The schedule is the spine of Part B pricing. Therapy was hung on it. CMS’s Annual Therapy Update still says outpatient PT, OT, and SLP are paid at the MPFS *nonfacility* rate, and ASHA repeats a legal quirk that matters: speech-language pathology is paid at that nonfacility rate even when the walls are a facility. If you want the statute-level sentence, take it from CMS and ASHA, not from a Facebook group.

The quirk is why a 2026 change that *hurts* facility practice-expense values and *helps* nonfacility ones can still be a net plus for SLP codes. ASHA said so in the November 2025 analysis. Your locality may disagree in the second decimal. Locality is a later sentence (essay 24).

## The three pieces inside a code

**Work** is the time and intensity CMS thinks a typical clinician spends. **Practice expense** is the rent, the software, the gloves, the person who schedules. **Malpractice** is the insurance sliver. Add them (after geography) and you have RVUs. Multiply by the conversion factor and you have dollars.

When someone says “they cut our codes,” they might mean work went down, practice expense went down, the conversion factor went down, or a new efficiency adjustment shaved the work (essay 24). Those are four different cuts. Lumping them into “Medicare cut speech” is how rumors outrun transmittals.

## What the annual fight is about

MACRA froze the annual update at zero for years, then, starting 2026, split the world into two conversion factors: a slightly larger bump for clinicians in qualifying advanced alternative payment models, a smaller bump for everyone else. Congress also, in the 2026 cycle, layered on a one-time 2.5 percent. CMS’s fact sheet printed the results: $33.57 and $33.40, up from $32.35. Essay 13 stays there.

Then other policies climb back on the table: sequestration, GPCI, the efficiency adjustment, MPPR (essay 14). ASHA’s PDF warned that a clinician who only reads the conversion-factor smile will miss a possible cut in the four-percent neighborhood once the other knives are counted. That sentence is advocacy-adjacent and dated. Treat it as a weather report, not a paycheck.

## What this page will not give you

A locality rate. A CPT list. A “how to calculate your fee” worksheet. ASHA already publishes those objects for members. CMS already publishes the files. Copying them here would be a stale spreadsheet and a copyright problem. If you need a number for a contract, get it from the current CMS lookup or your MAC, on the day you need it.

This page will also not tell you that the schedule is fair. Fair is a political word. The schedule is a formula. You can dislike the formula and still describe it.

## How to hear the next final rule

When the July proposed rule and the November final rule land, ignore the first adjective in the subject line (*historic*, *devastating*, *win*). Find three facts: the conversion factor(s), whether MPPR moved, whether therapy thresholds moved. Then find one sentence about telehealth authority — which is often a *Congress* sentence, not a fee-schedule sentence (essay 23).

If you can hold those four facts, you can sit in a staff meeting without becoming the person who only heard a podcast.

The kitchen-table paragraph is still true if you forget the rest. RVUs times a factor, adjusted for place. Everything else is weather on top of arithmetic.
