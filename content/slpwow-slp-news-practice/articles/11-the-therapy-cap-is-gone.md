Verify the current CMS, MAC, state, or ASHA page before you act. This series does not evaluate patients and does not publish worksheets.

People still say “we’re near the cap.” They have been saying it since a law that no longer exists. On February 9, 2018, the Bipartisan Budget Act (Pub. L. 115-123) repealed the Medicare outpatient therapy caps. CMS’s own Therapy Services page still leads with that fact, because the field has not emotionally caught up.

The cap was a hard dollar stop, born in the Balanced Budget Act of 1997, later patched with exceptions that felt like a second job. When it died, it did not leave an empty desk. It left a threshold and a review process that *look* like a cap if you only hear the number.

That confusion is the news. The number in 2026 is $2,480 for physical therapy and speech-language pathology combined, and a separate $2,480 for occupational therapy. Those are KX modifier thresholds. They are not a law that says the next visit is forbidden. Essay 15 stays with the modifier. Essay 16 stays with the $3,000 medical-review line. This page is about the ghost.

## What “repealed” meant in a booth

A beneficiary can still receive medically necessary outpatient therapy after the old dollar. CMS says so. ASHA’s 2026 fee-schedule PDF says so. The sentence clinicians needed in 2018 — *you are not automatically done* — is still the sentence some families have never heard.

What changed for the claim is paperwork and risk, not a cliff. Above the threshold, the claim needs a KX that says: we looked, this is still necessary, the record can take a question. Without the modifier, CMS says the claim is denied. That is a denial for a missing flag. It is not a clinical discharge.

If your department still writes “maxed out Medicare therapy” in a chart, the chart is speaking 2017. Update the sentence. Do not replace it with a billing script from a blog.

## Why the ghost is so loud

The number is still printed every year. It still moves with the Medicare Economic Index. It still sits on the same web page that once explained a cap. Office software still has a field labeled “cap” because vendors do not rename fields for free. Older colleagues trained their hands on a cliff and never untrained them.

Patients learned the cliff from us. They budgeted visits. They postponed the swallow eval until January. Some still do. A plain-language correction — the hard stop is gone; we still have to show our work — is a clinical courtesy. It is not a promise that every visit will be paid.

## What did not come back

Congress did not restore an exceptions process as a separate application you mail. It did not restore a world without documentation. It did not make medical necessity a vibe. The Benefit Policy Manual still wants skilled, necessary care. Auditors still collect the word “skilled” (essay 32). Jimmo still sits in the background for maintenance that is skilled. None of that is a cap. All of it is easier to confuse with a cap when you are tired.

Section 53107 of the same 2018 law, while we are in the building, is about payment for therapy *assistants* in PT and OT. SLPs do not live in that sentence the same way. Do not borrow it.

## A way to talk without becoming the coder

You can say: “Medicare used to have a hard dollar cap. That law is gone. There is still a threshold where we have to attest, and a higher line where a record might be reviewed.” You can point to CMS’s page. You cannot, from this URL, tell someone how to complete a claim.

If a family asks “so we can come forever,” the answer is the old answer: as long as the care is skilled and necessary, and as long as their plan is Medicare the way we think it is. Advantage plans are not the fee-for-service page. Employer plans never were. The ghost of the cap has no jurisdiction there, and neither does this paragraph.

## Why reprint this in a 2026 pack

Because the CY 2026 transmittals are still spending ink on a 2018 repeal. Because ASHA is still spending a heading on it. Because hallway English has not caught up. News is sometimes the fact that an old fact has not arrived.

The next pages are the machinery that replaced the cliff: the fee schedule in one paragraph, two conversion factors, same-day reductions, the modifier, the review line. None of them is a protocol. All of them are easier if you stop calling them a cap.
