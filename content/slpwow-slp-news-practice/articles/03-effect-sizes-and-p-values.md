A star showed up in a table and someone in the break room said the treatment works. That sentence has wrecked more journal clubs than bad coffee.

In 2016 the American Statistical Association published a statement that should have ended the superstition. A p-value is the probability, under a specific null model, of seeing a result at least as extreme as the one you got. It is not the probability that the hypothesis is true. It is not the size of the difference. It is not whether the difference would matter to a person who is trying to say their grandchild’s name. Wasserstein and Lazar wrote it in public, in *The American Statistician*, in language a non-statistician can finish.

Communication sciences kept the star anyway. We are not unique. Medicine did too. The news is that you do not have to.

## What the star is actually pointing at

A small p-value means: if there were really nothing going on, and if the model is right, this pattern would be uncommon. That is a useful sentence. It is also a narrow one.

It does not tell you the model is right. SLPs work with messy scores, small n, and outcomes that are not independent (ten swallows from one person are not ten people). A p-value that pretends they are independent is a polite fiction.

It does not tell you the result will happen again. A one-shot clinic sample with a cute p can fail to replicate for the most boring reason in the world: the next twenty people are not the first twenty.

It does not tell you the result is big. With enough people, a two-point change on a 100-point scale will light up. With too few people, a change that would matter in a booth can look like noise. Both facts can live in the same literature. Pretending only one of them exists is how departments buy programs.

## Ask for the size, in the units of the booth

Effect size is not a magic Cohen number you memorize for a quiz. It is the authors telling you *how much*, in a unit you can picture.

A mean difference in words produced in a ten-minute sample. A change in a penetration-aspiration rating, with the number of swallows scored. A shift in a voice handicap index. A percentage of sessions in which a behavior occurred. Those are sizes. “d = 0.4” is a translation. Translations are fine if you can still see the original.

Intervals matter more than stars. A difference of 8 points with an interval that runs from −1 to 17 is a different story from 8 points that run from 6 to 10. The first story is “we are not sure.” The second is “we are surer, and you can still argue about whether 8 points matter.” Both are more adult than a single asterisk.

CONSORT wanted this honesty in trial reports years ago: primary outcome, effect size, precision. When a trial paper in our field skips it, that is not a style choice. It is a refusal.

## Clinical significance is not a synonym for p < .05

A family can feel a change that a test calls noise. A test can move while a person’s life does not. Those are not failures of science. They are two different questions.

Some papers try to name a minimal important difference. Some borrow one from another field. Some skip the question and let the p-value do the moral work. You are allowed to put the paper down and ask, in ordinary English: if this were my caseload, would I notice. That question is not anti-data. It is the reason the data were collected.

Do not turn the answer into a protocol on this site. Notice is not a recipe.

## What journal policy is doing, slowly

ASHA journals have been asking authors for more transparency — data statements, badges, registered reports that accept a paper before the stars are known. Registered reports are a structural answer to p-hacking: you agree on the analysis while you still cannot see the prize. *JSLHR* said so in 2021 and was still inviting them in 2026, including for work on clinical tools that use AI.

That is news about incentives. It will not clean the back catalog. The 2014 paper in your file cabinet still has whatever stars it has. Read it with 2016 eyes.

## A way to talk in the hallway without becoming a scold

You do not need to deliver a lecture on Neyman and Pearson. You can say: “What’s the actual difference, and in whom.” You can say: “Is that the primary outcome they said they would look at.” You can say: “I’m going to wait for the interval.”

If the person who forwarded the abstract looks disappointed, that is all right. Disappointment is cheaper than a department-wide habit built on a star.
