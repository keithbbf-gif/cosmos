**Disclaimer.** Educational news briefing for SLPWOW. Not billing advice, not legal advice, not a treatment protocol, and not medical advice. Rules change by contractor, state, and calendar year. Verify the current CMS, MAC, state, or ASHA page before you act. This series does not evaluate patients and does not publish worksheets.

Most SLP days are not an 8-minute-rule day. A lot of our codes are untimed: one eval, one treatment, one swallow look, regardless of whether the hour was kind. Then a timed code shows up — a 15-minute cognitive or language procedure, a shared therapy minute in a building that bills like PT — and suddenly someone prints a chart with eight rows and a yellow highlight.

The chart is the problem this page will not reproduce.

CMS’s math lives in the Claims Processing Manual, Publication 100-04, Chapter 5, section 20.2. Medicare Administrative Contractors quote it. First Coast’s public page is a clean example: timed codes are 15-minute units; a single timed service under eight minutes is not a unit; when more than one timed code happens on a day, you add the minutes *first*, then see how many units the day’s total will support. The familiar bands (8–22 minutes for one unit, 23–37 for two, and so on) come from that manual, not from a consultant’s refrigerator magnet.

## Why this is news and not a workshop

Because two clocks exist, and people mash them.

**Medicare’s total-then-assign clock.** Add timed minutes. Read the band. Then assign units to the services that actually ate the time, without inventing a fourth unit the total will not carry.

**CPT’s midpoint clock.** The CPT book has its own “you must pass the middle of the 15 minutes” sentence. APTA has been public for years that those two clocks are not the same, and that the payer gets to say which one it is using.

If your commercial plan is on midpoint and your Medicare book is on 8-minute totals, a habit that is honest in one place is a denial in the other. That is the headline. The worksheet is a derivative.

## What SLPs should hear without becoming billers

Untimed codes do not enter the timed pot. Extra time on an untimed 92507 does not become a second 92507. ASHA has said versions of that for years. Treating an untimed code as if it were a minute bank is how notes and claims divorce.

Timed codes, when you use them, want the minutes that were *treatment*, not the minutes you spent hunting a charger. Contractors say pre- and post-delivery do not count. That sentence is about the claim. It is not a permission slip to stop being a human in the doorway.

Group vs individual is a different fight. Do not drag it into the 8-minute paragraph.

## What this page will not print

The minute-to-unit table. The four classic CMS examples (33 and 7, 18/13/10/8, and friends). Those examples are in the manual and on MAC pages. Photocopying them here would be the worksheet dump the pack forbids, and they would be stale the day a contractor changes a footnote.

If you need the table, open the IOM or your MAC. If you need a department in-service, have a coder teach the current file. If you need a clinical hour, put the table away.

## A hallway version

“Medicare timed codes aren’t ‘every 15 minutes, bill one,’ and they aren’t ‘seven and a half and you’re in.’ They add the day’s timed minutes, then hand out a limited number of units. Most of my codes aren’t in that pot.” If someone looks disappointed that you will not draw the grid on a napkin, they can have the First Coast URL. Napkins become policy. Policy should come from the manual.

Essay 19 leaves the outpatient minute pot entirely. In a SNF, PDPM does not pay you for the minutes. It pays a component that was supposed to follow the person, not the clock. That was the whole point of the remodel. Whether the remodel felt like a raise is a different, local story.
