Verify the current CMS, MAC, state, or ASHA page before you act. This series does not evaluate patients and does not publish worksheets.

Every few months a post asks, *what is ASHA’s caseload number.* The answer has been the same since the 2002 position statement: there isn’t one. ASHA will not give you a national maximum, because a maximum becomes a minimum, and because sixty preschoolers with complex needs are not sixty articulation-only consults.

What they will give you is a word: **workload**. The portal page and the 2002 statement are public. Direct service is one slice. Evaluations, meetings, IEPs, Medicaid notes, teacher consults, make-up sessions, hallway emergencies — those are the rest of the pie. Adding a student adds pie, not just a name.

## What the 2024 survey actually printed

ASHA’s Schools Survey is not a rule. It is a photograph. For 2024, full-time school clinical service providers reported a median *actual* monthly caseload of **50** (range that runs from tiny to absurd; n in the thousands). The median they called *manageable* was **40**. Those two numbers, next to each other, are the news. The field is living ten names above what the field itself calls livable.

The photograph has other details worth not flattening. Day and residential settings sat lower (median actual 25). Telepractice offices sat a little higher (52). State medians ran from New York in the low thirties to Indiana in the high seventies. A national “50” that ignores Indiana and New York is a national shrug.

About a quarter of respondents said there was no barrier to a manageable size. About a quarter named a shortage of SLPs as the single greatest barrier. Shortage and caseload are a loop, not two problems.

Clinical service providers, in the same survey, averaged 23 hours a week in direct intervention and 6 on documentation. Forty-two percent had to make up missed sessions in some circumstances; 37 percent always or almost always. Those hours are workload. They do not appear in a headcount.

## Why ASHA will not give you the number you want

The 2002 statement is blunt. An arbitrary maximum is inconsistent with workload analysis. It can be used as a floor. It fails the student who needs intensive, indirect, collaborative service. *Endrew* later raised the stakes on “enough.” The portal cites it. A number that ignores the work is how you get a legal FAPE problem and a tired clinician in the same semester.

States that *do* print a number (some do; ASHA keeps a chart) are making a state-policy choice. Quote the state. Do not launder it as ASHA.

## What this is not

It is not a calculator for your district. ASHA has a workload calculator for members. This pack will not reproduce it. It is not a union slogan, though unions have every right to use the photograph. It is not a reason to refuse a student you have already agreed to serve. It is not a protocol for service delivery (push-in, pull-out, 3:1). Delivery models are a different argument that belongs in an IEP, not in a news brief.

## A hallway version

“ASHA won’t give a national cap on purpose. Their last survey photo is 50 actual, 40 ‘manageable,’ and six hours of paper around 23 hours of kids. If we only argue the headcount, we’re arguing the wrong object.” If an administrator wants the PDF, it is on asha.org. If they want you to stop documenting so the pie looks smaller, they want a fiction.

The other misuse of the photograph is to treat “40 manageable” as a new national cap ASHA “really” wants. They said they would not give you one. A median of what respondents called livable is a survey answer, not a position statement. Use it as a conversation starter. Do not launder it as a rule and then blame Rockville when a board attorney asks where the number lives.
