For a long time, a child who could not eat enough, or could not eat without fear, lived in three clinics that did not share a noun. GI said failure to thrive. Psychology said picky. Speech said swallow. Then the pediatric feeding disorder (PFD) language — Goday and colleagues, Feeding Matters, and the papers that followed — tried to give pediatrics a shared umbrella: medical, nutrition, feeding skill, and psychosocial, in some combination, for long enough to matter.

ASHA’s pediatric feeding and swallowing professional pages and AAP’s journals started using overlapping words in the same decade. That overlap is the headline. It is not a peace treaty. It is a chance to stop sending a family to three evaluations that each deny ownership.

## What the shared language is for

A referral path that does not begin with blame. A billing conversation that is not “is this even speech.” A research conversation that can compare apples. CDC’s milestone work does not try to be a feeding screen; ASHA’s public communication milestones are not a chew chart. The PFD conversation sits beside those objects, not on top of them. If a press cycle treats a new AAP article as if it replaced CDC checklists, the press cycle is confused. Surveillance is still surveillance (the pediatric pack). Feeding skill is a different look.

## What SLPs should hear without becoming the whole team

Skill — suck, swallow, breathe in an infant; chew, sip, self-feed in a toddler — is our traditional door. Medical safety is a physician door. Nutrition is a dietitian door. Force and fear at the table are a mental-health door. The news is that serious papers now say the doors should share a hallway. The old news is that many towns have one door.

School SLPs inherit the hallway at lunch (essay 31). A PFD label from a clinic is not an IEP category. It may still be a cafeteria safety story and a 504 story (essay 33). Translate. Do not swallow the label whole.

## What this page will not do

It will not give you a food-before-food hierarchy. It will not give you a “happy mealtime” script. It will not settle the oral-motor wars. Those wars have copyrights and feelings. A news site that dumps another clinic’s protocol is how we get lawsuit-shaped handouts.

If a 2024 or 2025 AAP clinical report is the object your department is arguing about, read *that* report, check the date, and mark `[VERIFY]` on any number you would print. This brief is about the *shape* of the headline — shared language, four domains, no single-owner myth — not a substitute for the current AAP PDF.

## A hallway version

“Pediatrics and SLP have been trying to share a name for kids who cannot eat enough or cannot eat without fear. The name is not a protocol, and it is not an IEP. I’m not going to run a meal from a blog.” If a pediatrician wants a partner, that is a referral, not a recipe. If a parent wants a diagnosis from this URL, they will be disappointed on purpose.

Insurance nouns lag the research nouns. A clinic may need a swallow code, a GI code, and a behavioral code to describe one dinner. Families hear that lag as “nobody agrees what is wrong.” You can say: the shared name is new; the payers are old; the child is still hungry. That sentence is news. It is not a prior-auth script. If your state Medicaid still has no pediatric feeding benefit language, that is a state headline worth taking to the association, not a reason to invent a home program on a blog.
