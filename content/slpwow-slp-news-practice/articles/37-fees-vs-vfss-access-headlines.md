Verify the current CMS, MAC, state, or ASHA page before you act. This series does not evaluate patients and does not publish worksheets.

FEES is a scope. VFSS is a movie with barium and a radiation budget. Both can answer swallow questions. Neither is a personality. The news in the last several years has not been a secret third instrument. It has been *access*: who owns the scope, who owns the fluoro time, who is allowed to bill, and who waits two weeks NPO for a slot that moves.

## Two access stories

**VFSS** needs a radiology partnership, a schedule, a body that can transfer, a dose of radiation you can justify, and a building that still has the room. Rural hospitals lose the room. Children’s hospitals ration the room. Outpatient wait lists become informal diet policies. A school child (essay 31) can age a whole IEP cycle waiting for a look that was “pending” in March.

**FEES** needs a trained SLP (or physician), a cleaning protocol, a privilege pathway, and a state that does not treat the scope as a forbidden object. It can go to the bedside. It cannot see the oral phase the way fluoro can. It can see secretions and a larynx that fluoro only glances. People who say “FEES is better” or “VFSS is better” as a global are telling you about their building, not about physics.

The headline is the building.

## Privileges, not protocols

State practice acts and hospital bylaws decide whether an SLP can pass a scope. Those documents changed in some states over the last decade and did not in others. A traveling CF who “did FEES every day” in one city can be a spectator in the next. That is news you tell a recruit before they sign. It is not a how-to.

Radiation credentialing, pregnancy policies, and pediatric fluoro culture are the VFSS twin. Also local. Also not a blog SOP.

## Payment sits in the attic

Which code, which setting, which LCD (essay 22), which same-day NCCI mood (essay 17). Instrumental exams are exactly the kind of object that picks up efficiency-adjustment weather (essay 24) and MPPR weather if something else happened that day (essay 14). Telehealth is not a VFSS. Anyone selling “remote fluoro” as ordinary Medicare telehealth is selling a story you should read against the current list, not against a 2020 waiver memory (essay 23).

This page will not pick a code. It will say the instrument you cannot get is not a clinical preference. It is a coverage and capital problem.

## What the argument in the literature is not asking you to do

There are papers that compare detection rates, comfort, cost, and aspiration yield. Read them with essays 01–05. Do not turn a detection-rate paper into a house rule that bans one instrument. A house rule that *requires* one instrument for every cough is the other fossil.

ASHA’s swallow pages describe both tools as part of a comprehensive look. Comprehensive is the word. Comprehensive is also expensive. The gap between the word and the slot is the news.

## A hallway version

“I want the look that answers the question we have, in the building we have, on the day we have. I’m not going to fight a holy war about scopes versus fluoro, and I’m not going to write a protocol on a news site.” If a CFO asks why the wait list is a safety issue, that is a conversation worth having in English. If a vendor asks you to demo a scope on a mannequin for a reel, that is a different conversation.
