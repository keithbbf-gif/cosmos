“Systematic review” is a compliment people give their own bibliographies. The words have a public meaning. PRISMA printed it. A review that skips the meaning and keeps the compliment is a literature list in a nicer font.

You will see both in our field this year, next year, and the year after. The news is not that reviews exist. The news is that you can tell them apart without a methods doctorate.

## What PRISMA actually asked for

A protocol, or at least a registered plan, so the question cannot wander after the papers arrive. A stated question. A stated search — databases, dates, terms — that another tired person could repeat. Two humans screening, or a reason why not. A flow diagram that admits how many records died. A table of what came in. Some attempt to judge the risk that the included studies are kidding themselves. A synthesis that matches the mess: meta-analysis when the outcomes can be combined, a narrative that does not pretend they can when they cannot.

That is a lot. It is also not optional if you want the adjective.

A paper that says “we searched PubMed” and then discusses the six PDFs the first author already had on the desktop has performed a search of a drawer. A paper that combines four different language tests into one forest plot without saying they are different tests has performed a collage.

## Certainty is a separate sentence

GRADE and its cousins try to say how sure we should be, given bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and the chance that unpublished disappointments are sitting in a file. You do not have to speak GRADE at the copy machine. You do have to notice when a review’s abstract says “effective” and the certainty table, if it exists, says “low” or “very low.”

Those two sentences can both be true. The second one should have been in the first paragraph. When it is not, the review has chosen a mood.

The thickened-liquid literature is a standing example, and essay 36 will stay there as a headline. Instrumental studies often show less penetration on thicker boluses. Outcome trials have not handed us a clean pneumonia story. A review that reports only the first fact is not systematic. It is selective. Selectivity is allowed in a commentary. It is a problem in a paper that borrowed the word “systematic.”

## Meta-analysis is not a blender

Putting numbers in a forest plot does not make them the same fruit. Different ages. Different dose. Different “usual care.” Different drop-out. A diamond at the bottom of a plot is a weighted average of whatever was poured in. If what was poured in was four small clinic samples and one large trial that asked a different question, the diamond is a decoration.

You are allowed to ignore the diamond and read the rows. Sometimes the honest review does that for you and says: we cannot pool this. Reward that sentence with your trust. It is rarer than it should be.

## Rapid reviews, scoping reviews, and other honest cousins

Not every useful paper is a systematic review. A scoping review maps what exists. A rapid review trades some rigor for speed in a public-health hurry. An expert narrative review can be the best thing you read all month if it *admits* it is a narrative. The failure mode is identity theft: a narrative that wears systematic clothing on the title page.

ASHA journals, like everyone else, publish all of these types. Their transparency push (data, badges, registered reports) helps the primary studies more than the reviews, but a registered review protocol is also a kind of news. If the authors posted the protocol and then changed the outcome, they should say so. If they did not post anything, you are trusting their memory.

## How this shows up in a staff meeting

Someone prints a review, highlights the abstract, and says we should change the diet order set. You can ask four questions without being the villain:

1. Can I see the search.
2. How many studies, and in whom.
3. Did they judge study quality, or only count titles.
4. Does the certainty match the verb in the first sentence.

If the answers are thin, the diet order set can wait. Waiting is not cruelty. Changing a house-wide habit on a list of titles can be.

This page will not tell you which swallow paper to believe. It will tell you that the word on the masthead is not a finding. Essay 06 is about the journals that hope you never ask.
