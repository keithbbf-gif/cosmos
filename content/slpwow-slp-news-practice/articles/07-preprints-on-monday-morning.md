**Disclaimer.** Educational news briefing for SLPWOW. Not billing advice, not legal advice, not a treatment protocol, and not medical advice. Rules change by contractor, state, and calendar year. Verify the current CMS, MAC, state, or ASHA page before you act. This series does not evaluate patients and does not publish worksheets.

A colleague texts a link at 7:12. The PDF looks like a journal article. It has a DOI. It has figures. It does not have a volume number, because it has not been through the argument yet.

That object is a preprint. During the first COVID years the public learned the word the hard way, watching drafts about masks and drugs move faster than reviewers. Communication sciences learned it more quietly. The servers (medRxiv, bioRxiv, OSF, PsyArXiv, and others) are still there. So is the temptation to treat a timestamp as a stamp of truth.

## What a preprint is for

Speed, for one. A finding can sit in review for a year. A preprint lets other labs see the method before the literature forgets the question. Credit, for another. A graduate student can point to a public draft when a search committee asks what they have done. Error-checking, if you are lucky: comments, replications, a reviewer who found you before the journal did.

None of those jobs is “tell a school district to change its eligibility.” None of them is “change the diet order set.” Those jobs need a process that can say no.

Peer review is a messy, biased, slow process. It still catches the figure that used the wrong denominator. It still asks for the table the authors hoped you would not need. A preprint has not had that argument in public, or has had only the argument the authors invited.

## How to read one without pretending it is finished

Use the same five questions as essay 01. Methods first. Who, what, how long, what was measured, what else could have done it. The preprint format does not excuse a missing n.

Look at the version. Servers label v1, v2, v3. If the tweet is quoting v1 and the PDF is now v3 with a different conclusion, you are not late. The authors changed their mind in public. That is a feature. Quote the version.

Look at whether a journal has since taken it. Many preprints become papers. Some die. Some split into two papers. Citing the preprint after the article exists is like citing the rehearsal after opening night, unless you are writing a history of the claim.

Look at the data. Open-science culture (Center for Open Science, TOP, ASHA’s badges) makes it more normal to park a draft next to a repository. A parked draft with no data and a huge claim is just a blog with better typesetting.

## What you can say on Monday

You can say: “There’s a draft that suggests X in this sample. It hasn’t been reviewed. I’m not changing the plan of care on it.” That sentence is adult. It keeps you in the conversation without turning your license into a megaphone.

You can send the link to a journal club with the word PREPRINT in the subject line. You cannot send it to a family as if it were NIDCD.

You can let it change what you *watch for*. Hypotheses are allowed to move faster than protocols. If a draft says a particular patient-reported voice item moved after infection, you can start listening for that item. Listening is not a treatment.

## What the pandemic taught badly

It taught some readers that preprints are lies. They are not. It taught other readers that journals are theater and only the server is real. That is also false. The honest lesson is smaller: the timestamp is not the verdict, and the press release will be worse than the draft.

CSD was never the center of that storm. We still inherited the habit of forwarding PDFs with the seriousness of a statute. Break the habit. Keep the curiosity.

ASHA journals now live in a world where a paper might have been public for months before they see it. Their registered-report path is almost the opposite object: review *before* the data. Both objects can be honest. They answer different fears. Preprints fear delay. Registered reports fear the star that arrives on purpose.

## A closer

If the text from your colleague is still sitting on your phone, you do not owe them a protocol by third period. You owe them a reading, or a “not today.” Either one is more scientific than a thumbs-up emoji under a claim that has not met a reviewer.
