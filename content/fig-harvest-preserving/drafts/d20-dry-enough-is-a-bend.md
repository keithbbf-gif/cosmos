I do not pull a load because the card said twelve hours. I pull a fig when it acts like leather and not like jam.

Cool one. Warm fruit lies. A hot half can feel wet and still be close, or feel dry and still hide a bead. I take a tray off, wait ten or fifteen minutes, then I bend. It should flex and not snap if I want a pantry fig. It should not smear. If I pinch and a bead of moisture shows at the seed, it goes back.

A snap-dry fig is a chip. Chips have a use in a bag with nuts. They are not what I want in a jar for Christmas. I pull earlier for those on purpose, not by accident.

Whole fruit gets cut. I sacrifice one. If the center is dark and jammy-wet, the tray is not done. If the center is tacky like a raisin, we are close. If the center is wet enough to shine, we are not close.

I have called a load done because I was tired. Tired is not a test. The jar told on me two weeks later — beads on the glass, a smell I did not invite. That is the conditioning draft and the mold draft. The start of both is a wet center I was too proud to recut.

8a kitchen air will put a little tack back on a fig that was perfect at 10 p.m. if you leave the trays out. I move finished fruit to a cooling rack and then into the conditioning jar the same night or the next morning. I do not leave leather on the counter for three days as décor.

Zone 7 dry houses can overdry without noticing. Their air finishes the job. Our air undoes it. I err toward a little more machine time here, then I condition.

A scale is a toy I have used. Weight stops moving when water is mostly gone. I still cut one. Numbers do not replace a knife.

If half the tray is done and half is not, I do not punish the done half. I sort. Sorting is the whole craft.

The clock gets a vote. The bend gets the gavel.

I keep a “witness fig” on the top tray, a fat one I know will be last. When the witness bends right, I still check a small one so I did not burn the Celestes. Two witnesses, two stories.

If I am unsure I run thirty more minutes and I check again. Thirty minutes is cheaper than a moldy quart. Pride is expensive.

A fig that is dry at the rim and wet at the stem end needed to be a half. I do not “fix” it with more heat until the rim is glass. I cut it open and I finish the pieces.

Kids will eat the warm ones. Warm ones are not the test. I hide the witness if I have to.

When the load is truly done the house smells like toasted sugar, not like soup. Soup smell means water is still leaving. I wait until the smell changes before I even start bending. Then I still wait for them to cool. The nose is a start. The knife is the end.

I do not trust a “crisp” setting on a cheap machine as a doneness cue. Crisp is a chip. I want leather unless I said chip out loud.

If two trays disagree I believe the wetter one. The dry tray can wait in a bowl while the wet tray goes back. Combining them in a jar is how conditioning fails on day two.

A fig that stains the paper towel after a cool rest is not done. Stain is water. I have ignored a stain because the clock was ugly. The jar remembered.
