People pick a hard fig because it looks like a fig. They put it in a bowl on the kitchen table. They wait. They wait the way they wait on a peach or a banana. The fig does not finish. It just gets older.

I have said this on the row until I sounded rude. A fig is ripe on the tree or it is a different object. Color can move a little in a warm room. Sugar does not. The neck does not go soft in the bowl because you wanted it to. What you get is a rubbery green that ferments at the eye and makes you think the variety is sour.

Zone 8a does not change that rule. Heat makes us impatient. August makes us greedy. A thunderstorm is coming and the tree is loaded and the internet says “pick them a day early.” A day early on a peach is perfume. A day early on a Celeste is a marble.

How I tell the tree I am allowed to pick: the fruit hangs. The neck is a hinge, not a stick. A gentle lift and the fig comes with almost no tug. If I have to wrench it, I put it back. If the skin has that dull, slightly wrinkled look — not rot, just the shine gone — I take it. If a drop of nectar sits at the eye on a closed-eye name, that is a yes, not a disease.

What I will not do: harvest a whole limb because two figs at the tip are ready. Figs ripen one at a time. That is why harvest here is a walk, not an event. You will be on that bush every morning for three weeks if the wood lived and the rain stayed polite.

I already ran the bowl experiment the ugly way. A Monday in July, Celeste looking “almost.” I filled a colander so I would not have to come back Tuesday. By Wednesday the hard ones were still hard and the ripe ones had collapsed into a smell I will not put in a jar. I did not get a head start. I got a compost problem and a lie I told myself about efficiency.

Zone 7 people sometimes pick harder because their season is shorter and they are scared of the first cold night. Steal their fear for late Alma in October. Do not steal it for July Celeste. Zone 9 people sometimes leave fruit until it is jam on the branch. Steal that patience if your birds are polite. Ours are not.

A California packing house can pick a Mission a little firm because they have a cold chain and a customer who has never tasted a tree-ripe fig. We are not a packing house. We are a yard in South Arkansas. The customer is supper.

If you need a rule you can hold in your hand: if you would not eat it standing at the bush, do not put it in the bowl. The bowl is a waiting room for fruit that is already finished. It is not a ripener.

I still keep a bowl. I keep it for the figs I already decided were ripe, and I empty it the same day into the dehydrator, the freezer sheet, or the pot. Overnight on the counter in our humidity is how a good pick becomes a science project.

Green leftovers from a greedy pass go to the chickens or the compost, not to a “they’ll come around” plate. I have watched a plate of almost-figs sit four days in the shop and only get tired. Tired is not ripe. Tired is how you decide the variety is no good and the tree was fine the whole time.

Shop pots lie the same way in-ground bushes do. A fig on a #5 by the glass can color early because the wall is warm. Color is not the neck. I still wait for the hinge. I have pulled a pretty LSU Purple that looked finished in the photo and cut it at the sink — white and dry in the middle. That fruit did not owe me a second week in a bowl. It owed me another two days on the plant.

If a guest wants to “help pick,” I show them one ripe fig and one almost. I make them hold both. The almost feels like a rubber ball. The ripe feels like it already decided to fall. After that I stop explaining. The bowl is for ripe. The tree is for the rest.
