A first-year fig that sets fruit is a compliment, not a crop.

PapaFig already said it on the introduction page: you may get fruit the first year, you will likely get it by the second, and year three is when an in-ground plant starts acting like a tree. I am not going to invent a pint count on top of that. I am going to tell you not to build August around a stick we potted in March.

Those early figs are often on a plant that still has a small root system and a large opinion of itself. If I take every one, the plant still lives. If I also let the pot dry out in July because I am “harvesting,” I cooked a baby for two figs.

What I do: eat the ripe ones. Leave the late green ones if the plant is struggling. Do not strip a one-gallon to fill a jam recipe. That recipe wants five pounds. The plant does not have five pounds. The plant has a story you can tell at supper.

A cutting we stuck this spring and potted before June may show fruitlets. Cute. Not a harvest. A #3 that overwintered in the shop and went out after frost is a better bet for a handful. An in-ground year-one in clay is lucky to keep leaves through August. If it also ripens two Celestes I will take a picture and I will not write a yield post.

Zone 8a heat is the bully. Young plants in full west sun with a harvest fantasy are how we get yellow leaves and hard peas. Afternoon shade for a first summer is not spoiling the tree. It is how the tree still exists in September.

I already made a “first fruit” jam with four varieties and a lot of sugar because I wanted the jar to exist. The jar existed. It tasted like sugar and a lecture. I do not do that anymore. Fresh, or wait.

Zone 7 first-year fruit is even more of a souvenir because winter is coming for the wood. Zone 9 can push a young plant harder. We are not Zone 9.

If a seller promises “heavy bearing the year you plant,” they are selling a mature pot or a sentence. Ask which. A mature pot can fruit. You paid for a plant that already did the waiting. That is honest. A rooted cutting is not a mature pot.

Harvest timing on a young plant is the same neck test. Expectations are not. Write “souvenir” on the label if you have to. The third summer is when I start talking like this tree owes the kitchen.

I will pull a fruitlet if it is sitting on a whip that cannot hold it and the pot is dry. That is mercy, not harvest. The plant can try again when the roots catch up.

Guests who see two figs on a baby want to pick both for the photo. I pick one if it is ripe. I leave one if the plant looks tired. The photo can be of the tree.

A first-year in-ground that fruits in a drought week will drop. I do not fertilize to “save the crop.” I water the pot or the hole and I let the drop happen. Pushing a baby with nitrogen in August is how you get soft wood for January.

When the third summer comes I stop calling it a souvenir. That is when the morning walk starts to matter and the freezer sheet comes out. Until then, eat what shrugs and take care of the plant. The kitchen can wait. The plant cannot grow a second first year.
