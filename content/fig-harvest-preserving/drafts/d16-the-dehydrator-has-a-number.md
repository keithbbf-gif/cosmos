I run fruit in a dehydrator at a number I can say out loud. For figs I start at **135–140°F**. That is the neighborhood NCHFP and the fruit-drying bulletins live in when they say 140°F. I am not going to pretend I invented a secret 118° setting that “preserves enzymes.” This is not a clinic. This is leather.

The machine lies if you only trust the dial. I have a cheap unit whose “140” is a wish. A probe on a tray in the middle is how I know. If I do not have a probe, I rotate trays and I accept that the back left is a different county than the front right.

Time is not a recipe. NCHFP’s fickle-fig note says **6–12 hours** as a drying window after you check whole fruit. Halves in our humidity often run longer because the air in the kitchen is already wet and the fruit came in wet. I have had Celeste halves done in an afternoon and Turkey halves still wet at the seed the next morning. I do not walk away for “24 hours” without a look. I also do not pull them at six because a blog had a clock.

Load: skin-side down or flesh-up, one layer, not touching if I can help it. Touching makes a glue spot that stays wet. Crowding is how you get a wet center and a hard rim.

I start with fruit that was ripe this morning, not fruit that sat. Tired fruit dries into a sour raisin. Ripe fruit dries into something I will eat in January.

Fan on. Door or vent as the manual says. If I seal a box in our August kitchen with no exhaust I am making a fig sauna. Sauna is the mold draft.

I already ran a load at “high” because I wanted them done before church. The edges turned to chips and the middles were jam. I finished them on a lower tray the next day and I called it a lesson. High heat is not speed. High heat is a crust.

Zone 7 winter drying in a basement can go faster because the room is dry. Our shop in August is the opposite. Steal their patience, not their clock.

When a tray is close, I pull the dry ones and leave the fat ones. One more hour for the whole load is how you burn the small Celestes to save a Turkey.

The number on the dial is the start. The bend test is the finish. The clock is a guest.

I do not dry on “herb” or “jerky” because the pictogram looked close. Herbs want a lower, slower air. Jerky wants a different story. Figs want fruit heat and time. If the manual has a fruit setting, that is the setting I try first, then I check with a probe.

Power blips here take a machine down in a storm. I do not leave a half-wet load sitting in a closed box overnight without heat. That is a mold box. If the power is out, I spread the trays in front of a fan or I freeze the halves and I start over later. Pride is how you keep a tray you should have thrown.

A stack of four trays is not the same as a stack of eight. I would rather run two honest loads than one stuffed tower. The middle of a stuffed tower is always the wet county.

I write the start time on a piece of tape on the lid. I write the temp I believe, not the temp I wish. Tomorrow-me is the person who has to decide if this load is food.
