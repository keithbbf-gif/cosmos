August rain on a loaded fig is not a surprise in South Arkansas. It is the week.

A fig that is almost ripe takes up water fast. The skin does not. You get a split at the eye or a crack down the side. That fruit is still food if you get it the same day and it smells like fig. Tomorrow it smells like a winery that failed.

I walk after the storm as soon as the lightning is done, not the next morning because I wanted dry socks. Same-day is the window. Not “this weekend.”

Splits go in the cook bowl. I do not put them on a guest plate. I do not dry them. A split in a dehydrator is a sugar fly invitation and a wet center. Freeze them if the pot is already full. Jam them if the pot is free. Compost them if the ants got there first or the eye is fizzy.

Closed-eye names split less at the ostiole and still crack if they are overfull. Celeste is not immune. It is just ruder to souring than Magnolia. Open-eye names in our humidity are a race even without rain. Rain just starts the gun.

What I will not do: blame the variety for a week of two inches and a tree I did not pick. The tree did what trees do. I skipped a morning.

Irrigation can split fruit too if I drought them and then drown them. Deep, even water through the swell is less drama than a panic hose the day the forecast turns green. I still get splits. I get fewer.

Zone 7 dry summers make people smug about sun-drying and “no split.” Then they move a cutting here and write me that the variety “soured.” The variety met 8a August. Steal their even watering. Do not steal their climate.

Zone 9 Gulf people already know this paragraph. They pick like it is a job. Steal that.

I already left a pretty split “for later” because supper was on. Later was a cloud of fruit flies and a sticky leaf. I do not leave later anymore.

If the split is brown, dry, and old, it is not a harvest. It is a mummy. Pull it so the next fruit does not sit against rot. Harvest is cleanup as much as it is a bowl.

A whole bush of splits after a historic rain is a kitchen day, not a photo day. We cancel the pretty plans. We run the sheet pan and the pot. That is how you keep a tree from being a waste story.

Birds and wasps read splits like a menu. If I am slow, I am the line cook for them. I do not throw a net over a storm-split bush at dusk and call it management. I pick.

The fruit that did not split still needs the regular ripe test. Rain does not ripen a hard fig. It only ruins the ready ones. Leave the salutes. Take the shrugs and the honest cracks.

I keep a towel in the truck of the golf cart for wet fruit. Wet figs in a deep bucket become jam before they reach the porch. Shallow tray, towel, shade, then the sink. I do not leave a wet tray in the sun “to dry the skins.” That cooks the splits.

If the forecast is two days of rain and the necks are already bending, I pick the almost-ready ones that would rather split than wait. That is the one time I lean early. I still will not take rocks. I take the ones with give. They go straight to the pot. They do not go to a guest plate and they do not go to the dehydrator.

A split I would eat over the sink is a cook fig. A split I would not put near my mouth is a rake fig. The difference is smell and time, not a variety name.
