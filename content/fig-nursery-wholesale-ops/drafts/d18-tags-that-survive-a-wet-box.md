A paper tag that turns to pulp in a wet box is not a label. It
is compost. I have fished a gray wad out of a bag and tried to
read it like a prophecy. The prophecy said I was cheap in
December.

The box will get wet. Not always from rain. From wood that was
a little too alive, from a warm pack hitting a cold truck, from
a heat pack I should not have used, from a snow that melted on
the porch. Plan for wet. Then if it stays dry you look careful
instead of lucky.

What survives here:

- Pencil on a cardstock strip, under the band, not on the outside
  of a soaked paper bag
- A laser-printed slip in a plastic sleeve
- Nursery tape that actually sticks to itself, written after the
  sap is off my fingers
- An aluminum or plastic tag if the lot is going onward into
  pots at their end and they asked for it

What does not survive: ballpoint on copy paper; a sticky note;
a name on the outside tape of the box as the only copy (tape
gets shredded); a marker that runs into a blue cloud.

I like the bundle card to be small enough not to act like a
sponge the size of a slice of bread. Giant paper in a bag will
hold a surprising amount of water and then lean on the bark.
That is a moisture source I did not intend.

Outdoor-proof is a summer problem for pots. In the box I want
*wet-box-proof*. Different test. I dunk a sample tag in the
sink, smear it with a wet thumb, and see if I can still read
the lot code. If I cannot, the tag does not ship. This is a
two-minute test I skipped for a year because I felt busy. I
was busy reprinting apologies.

Do not write the only name on the outside of the carton in a
place the carrier’s sticker will eat. Carriers eat labels. Put
the legal-ish marks they need — from, to, “live plants / dormant
cuttings” — and put the variety names inside. The inside is the
nursery. The outside is the street.

Keith will mention destination rules that want contents and
origin on the package (California’s marking statute is the
example people quote) **[VERIFY]** for the state you are
actually entering. That is a *package* mark, not a substitute
for the bundle card. Do both when the destination asks. Do the
bundle card even when nobody asks.

If the tag cannot survive the sink, it cannot survive Ohio.
I do not need a new brand of tag. I need the sink test.

I keep a “sink jar” on the pack table — a cup of water and
last week’s leftover cards. If a new card stock arrives, it
goes in the jar before it goes in a fifty. I have bought
pretty waterproof tags that smeared anyway. Pretty is not
the sink. The sink is the sink.

I write the lot code twice on the bundle card, once each
side, because one side will rub the bark. Redundant words
are ugly. Missing words are a March.
