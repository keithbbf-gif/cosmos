The product is dormant wood. Everything else is a delay or a problem.
People talk like the product is the variety name. The name is a promise
about a tree. The thing in the bag is a stick that either went to sleep
or did not.

Dormant, here, is not a calendar page. It is sap down and leaves gone.
Jack already wrote the test on FigRoots: a new cut should not milk, and
the bag should not be painted with dried sap. Lignified is the other
half — brown, gray, blackish bark, not a green herbaceous wand. You can
have lignified wood that is still moving. You can have a green stick
that looks “fresh” in a listing photo and is a mushroom farm by Friday.

In South Arkansas the window we trust is after a real defoliation and
before the buds swell like they mean March. That is usually late
November through February, with January as the honest middle. A warm
December can lie. A freeze in early November can strip leaves and still
leave wood that wants to bleed. I do not cut a mother because the shop
is open. I cut it because the wood passed the cut test that morning.

Wholesale makes the window stricter, not looser. A hobbyist can stick a
semi-awake cutting the day it arrives. A nursery receiving fifty cannot
baby each one. They will bag them, maybe fridge them, maybe wait for
bench space. If I ship wood that was never asleep, I am asking their
cooler to do a job the tree refused.

Texas A&M’s figs notes sit in the same camp on the farm side: hardwood
cuttings taken when plants are fully dormant are the ones they call the
common method. That is not a ship recipe. It is the same wood I am
willing to put in a trade carton. Softwood is a now problem. Green is a
now problem. I will root those here or I will not cut them for a box.

The window also has an end. When buds are fat and the bark is waking, I
stop selling “dormant cuttings” even if the listing is still up. I can
offer a rooted liner later. I can say sold out. I cannot keep printing
a winter word on a spring stick. That is how you earn a reputation for
arriving cooked.

I keep a notebook on the cooler door: first hard frost, first clean
cut with no sap, last week I will take wood for mail. The dates move.
The notebook does not. Next October I will remember the figs and forget
the week the sap ran. The page remembers.

Last December we had a week at 70°F and the list was live. I cut a
test stick on a Chicago Hardy that still wanted to bleed. We did not
pack that variety that week. We packed the ones that cut dry. The
shop looked idle. The cooler looked honest. I would rather look idle
than mail a warm stick into a bag and call it winter.

If you are planning a wholesale list in August, you are planning a
window, not a catalog. How many mothers will actually be asleep. How
many sticks of the length band you printed. How many weeks of weather
you will refuse. The name on the website is the easy part. The product
is the week the wood goes quiet.
