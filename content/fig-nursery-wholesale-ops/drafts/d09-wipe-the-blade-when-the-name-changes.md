I wipe the blade when the name changes. Not when I remember. Not
when the sap gets embarrassing. When the card in my pocket changes,
the knife gets a rag.

This is not a hospital speech. We are not sterilizing a person. We
are trying not to paint every mother with the same juice and the
same hitchhikers. Scale, mites, a dirty film, a canker I did not
see until the second plant. A cheap habit beats a paragraph about
pathogens I cannot see.

I carry two rags. One is dry, for sap, so the next label does not
turn into a brown smear. One is damp with the same kind of dilute
wash we already published on the Fig Pop page — the 10:1 water to
bleach mix, then we are done talking about bleach — or soap and
water if that is what I have. I dry the blade. A wet rusty hinge
is not sanitation. It is a worse tool.

I do not dip a foam tote of mixed varieties and call it a protocol.
I cut one name into one tote. I close that tote. I wipe. I cut the
next name. If I am being sloppy I will “just do these two together
because they are both dark.” That is how a lot card becomes fiction.

Pruners that chew get retired from the wholesale row. Crush is a
cull. A dull blade is how you manufacture culls and then feel sad
about waste. Sharpen before the window, not during it.

Sap on my hands is how labels migrate. I write the card before I
cut, then I cut, then I wash, then I touch the next card. If I
write after I am sticky, I will drag LSU Purple onto a Celeste tag
and I will not know until May. I have done the sticky version. I
did not like the May.

Keith will stop a row if a plant looks wrong — scale I can scrape
in sheets, a dieback that is not winter, a pot that smells like a
swamp. Those plants do not get a wipe and a pass. They get skipped.
A clean blade on a sick mother is a clean blade on a sick mother.

Buyers ask if we “treat” cuttings. I wash dirty wood. I dry it. I
do not sell a dip as a miracle and I do not mail a chemistry kit. If
a stick is slimy, it is trash. If you want a lab certificate, that
is a different business than this bench.

The habit takes ten seconds. The mix-up takes a year. I know which
one I am buying when I skip the rag.

A cheap kit lives in the same bucket as the cards: two rags,
a small bottle of the dilute wash we already published, a
dry cloth, a stone for the blade. If the kit is not in the
row, I go get it. I do not “finish this plant first.” Finishing
first is how the next name inherits the last juice. I have
finished first. The May fruit did not care that I was in a
hurry. The kit is lighter than a May.

I also wipe the tote rim. Sap on a rim transfers to the next
card I clip there. Cards are cheaper than rims, but I still
wipe the rim. The row is a chain. The blade is only the loud
link.
