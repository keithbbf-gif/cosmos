They offered a rush fee like it was a key. The oak was still at the yard. The hinge was still a SKU. The finish still had a window (piece 21). The fee would have bought a tired Saturday and a top that cupped in October. The shop said no (piece 42). That no was the honest product.

Rush is not a moral failing. Weddings, moves, a facility opening (piece 46) — dates are real. Money can buy some hours. It cannot buy physics. If you do not say which hours, the fee is a souvenir.

## What a fee can buy

Overtime on a known made-to-order object (piece 04) when the lumber is in the rack (piece 20) and the hardware is in the bin (piece 22). A jumped queue if the shop is willing to tell another customer the truth. A second shift on sanding. A freight upgrade from a slow truck to a faster one (piece 34).

Those are line items. Write them. "Rush" as a single integer is how shops take money for a wish.

## What a fee cannot buy

Kiln time at a yard you do not own. A hinge on the water. A recoat window. A stone template before the base exists. A stair that is too tight (piece 14). A cure (piece 21). Another job's already-glued clamps.

A shop that sells those things as rush is selling a remake. The remake will land after the wedding anyway, plus a scar.

## Queue ethics

Jumping a queue is a conversation with the people already on the whiteboard (piece 19). Some shops will not. Some will, and will call the jumped job. If you are the jumper, you are why someone else gets a delay notice. If you are the jumped, you deserve the call. 16 CFR Part 435 may require more than manners on remote orders (piece 25). Manners still apply on the showroom ack.

Do not be the dealer who rushes every special. You will train the mill to ignore you.

## When to refuse the date

If the only way to hit it is to skip rest (piece 20) or skip cure. If the path is unmeasured. If the drawing is not approved (piece 08). If two people in the house still disagree on stain (piece 06). Refusal is cheaper than a lawsuit and cheaper than a bad dinner (piece 42).

## How to ask without the script

"Here is the hard date. Here is what we can drop (leaf, special pull, white glove). What in the stack is already too long?" That is a job conversation. "We need it in three weeks, make it happen" is a script. This pack does not do scripts.

If the shop offers a smaller object that can exist by the date — a made-to-order instead of a custom, a shop stain instead of a hunt — listen. That is not upselling down. That is a calendar.

## Money

`[VERIFY]` any house rush percent before anyone prints one. If there is no policy, there is no percent. A one-off Saturday is a quoted labor line, not a vibe.

Refunds on a failed rush should be in the ack: the fee comes back if the shop missed; the fee stays if the customer changed the drawing. Write it. Do not improvise at the wedding.

## A rush that was real

Lumber in the rack. Made-to-order apron. House pulls. Household dropped the leaf and accepted threshold instead of room. Shop ran a Saturday. Date held. The fee was a Saturday line, not a magic percent.

A rush that was not: custom first-time built-in, stone not templated, Thanksgiving, customer still arguing stain. Fee offered. Shop took it. January delivery plus a strip. The fee did not come back because the ack was silent. Write the refund rule when you write the fee, or do not take the fee.

## Dealers who rush every special train the mill to ignore them

If every PO says RUSH, none do. Save the word for a wedding or an opening (piece 46) where you also dropped scope. A mill that has learned your store cries wolf will put your honest emergency in the same pile as your anniversary sale. Your household will still call you.

## The RFQ lines

Hard date or not. What may be dropped. Buyouts already in hand. Willingness to take a smaller object. Rush: not requested / requested, pending shop yes.

The key you were offered does not open a kiln. Ask what it opens. If the answer is "we will try," keep your money and keep the old table for the dinner.
