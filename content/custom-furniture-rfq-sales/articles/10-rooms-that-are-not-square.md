**Disclaimer.** Educational shop talk. Not a quote, not a contract, and not legal advice. Deposit terms, lead times, and cancellation rules live on the acknowledgment you actually sign. A shop in another state may run a different clock.

The 3-4-5 triangle on the floor showed it before the case did: 3 feet, 4 feet, and the hypotenuse that was not 5. The corner was 91 and change. A sideboard built as a rectangle would have left a wedge of shadow along the wall that every guest would call a gap.

Old rooms are not square. New rooms are not as square as the drywaller promised. Custom that dies into a wall has to admit this on the RFQ, or the shop will build a perfect object for a room that does not exist.

## How to catch it

Measure both diagonals of the floor plan. If they disagree, the rectangle is a parallelogram. Measure wall lengths at the floor and at the height of the piece. If they disagree, the walls lean.

A 3-4-5 check in the corner is older than any app. Twelve inches and sixteen, twenty and change on the diagonal if you are working small. Scale it up. Write the error as an inch of run over a foot of wall, not as a feeling.

Laser squares are fine. So is a long straightedge against the wall. The high spots are where a built-in will rock. The low spots are where you will scribe or leave a reveal on purpose.

## What the RFQ must say

"Walls out — diagonal A 14'2", diagonal B 14'6". Case will need scribe / reveal / shim plan." That sentence is the difference between a shop error and a room error.

If the piece is a freestanding table, out-of-square matters less for the object and more for clearance (piece 11). If the piece is a built-in, a window seat, an island against a jog, or a bookcase that must kiss the chimney breast, out-of-square *is* the object.

AWI tolerances assume a world with shop drawings and field verification. A house shop that skips the field number is not being rustic. It is being optimistic.

## Scribe, reveal, or fight

Scribe: the back or the side is made oversize and cut to the wall. Honest. Needs a site visit or a template (piece 16). Adds labor. Looks like the building and the furniture agreed.

Reveal: a shadow line you chose, even, so the eye does not see the wall's drunk. Honest if you name it. Looks like a designer. Looks like a mistake if you did not warn the customer.

Fight: build the rectangle and demand the wall behave. The wall will not. The customer will send a photo of the wedge. You will eat a trip.

Dealers selling built-ins into old houses should price scribe as a line, not as a surprise. Homeowners should not treat scribe as the shop covering a sin. Scribe is the sin of the building, paid for like any other labor.

## Floors

A case with four feet on a floor that drops will rock. A table will get a matchbook. A tall bookcase will look drunk. Measure the floor with a long level or a taut string. Write the drop. The shop can add a scribe rail, an adjustable foot, or a refusal (piece 42).

Do not hide a drop because you are embarrassed by the house. The house is not reading the email. The case is.

## Openings

Door and window openings rack. A piece that must center on a window should be centered on the *glass*, the *trim*, or the *opening* — pick one and write it. Those three centers are not the same in a plaster wall.

## Templates

When the wall is a curve, a jog, or a fight you cannot describe in inches, template (piece 16). Cardboard is cheap. Remakes are not.

## The photo that proves it

Shoot the tape on both diagonals. Shoot the 3-4-5. Put those photos in the RFQ (piece 07). A shop that ignores them is a shop that wants a fight. A customer who will not take them is a customer who wants a perfect rectangle in a 91-degree corner.

South Arkansas plaster will not become a cabinet shop because you asked nicely. The drawing can be square. The room is not. Write the room.
