**Disclaimer.** Educational shop talk. Not a quote, not a contract, and not legal advice. Deposit terms, lead times, and cancellation rules live on the acknowledgment you actually sign. A shop in another state may run a different clock.

They wanted eight. The room wanted six and a winter leaf. The catalog wanted 96 inches because 96 looks like a sentence. The tape wanted 84 and a little air to the hutch.

"A table for eight" is a social wish. A quote needs a length, a width, a height, a leaf story, and a pass behind the chairs. If you skip the pass, you will own a table that turns the dining room into a crawl.

## Seats are widths

A place setting wants width. Common planning books put a person in the 24-inch neighborhood; 30 is generous; 21 is a banquet squeeze. `[VERIFY]` the edition you are holding before you treat those as law. Elbows and armchairs change the number. A Windsor with elbows is not a side chair.

Ends take a person too, or they take a walkway. A trestle that blocks the end knees is a four-side table that seats six in comfort and eight in a story.

Write chair count *and* chair type. Then write the length that actually holds them. Then see if the room holds the length.

## The pass

Behind a seated person you want a zone to get up and a zone to walk. Planning references often treat about 36 inches from table edge to wall as a minimum to rise, and 44 to 48 as a place two people can pass. `[CITE NEEDED]` if you print a house standard. The room may give you 32. Then you choose: smaller top, fewer chairs, a bench on the wall side, or a leaf that lives in the closet except at holidays.

Do not steal the pass to win the chair count. The first dinner will teach you. The shop will not be there.

## Width

A 36-inch top is a narrow farm table: platters fight the glasses. 42 is a common comfortable. 48 is a hall table in a small room. Leaves that widen are rare; leaves that lengthen are the ordinary story. Measure the room the short way. That is often the width you actually get.

Overhangs are part of the width. A 2-inch overhang on each side is 4 inches of top that is not apron. Knees care about apron height and setback more than Instagram does.

## Leaves

How many. How wide each. Self-storing or not — self-storing changes the apron and the mechanism and the price. Where they live if they do not live in the table. A leaf in a damp garage will not match in December (piece 32, piece 36).

A table that is "for eight with the leaf" should be quoted as two objects: daily length and holiday length, each with a pass. If the holiday length kills the back door, the leaf is a fiction.

## Height and the chairs you already own

Standard dining height clusters around 29 to 30 inches to the top. Apron clearance over the chair arm is a fight you can measure today with the chairs you will keep. If the chairs are not bought, say so. A table built to a phantom chair will lose.

Counter-height and pub-height are different objects. Do not mix them in an RFQ without writing the chair height.

## The hutch, the swing, the vent

A table that fits the empty rectangle may still block the hutch drawer, the kitchen swing, or the only vent in the room (piece 18). Measure the live room (piece 09). Put the obstacles on the sketch.

## Dealers

Your catalog 96-inch is a hero for a showroom with a 16-foot wall. The customer's 11-foot room is a different SKU even if the joinery is the same (piece 04). Sell the length the pass allows. A smaller table that can be walked around is not a downgrade. It is a room that still works on a Tuesday.

## A worked length

Room 13'2" × 11'4". Hutch on the long wall, 18 inches deep. Kitchen swing on the short wall. Tape says 11'4" is the short way — that is your width budget. 42-inch top + 36-inch pass each side wants 9'6" plus the hutch if the hutch is on the long side. Do the arithmetic on the sketch. If the hutch eats the pass, the top goes to 36 or the chair count drops.

Eight people at 24 inches is 192 inches of edge if you seat all around — that is a 96 × 48 class object, which this room will not hold with a pass. So you are not buying eight. You are buying six daily and a leaf if the holiday pass still exists (piece 24). Write that on the RFQ so the shop does not price a 96-inch hero you cannot walk around.

## Chairs you already own are a spec

Bring the chair to the tape. Seat height, arm height, width at the elbows. A table priced without those numbers is priced for a catalog chair. If the chairs are not bought, write "chairs TBD" and accept that apron and height may need a hold until they exist. A shop that guesses a 30-inch height into an armchair house will eat a remake or a household that never sits back.

## The RFQ lines that price

L × W × thickness. Apron. Base type. Leaf count and storage. Chair count and type. Pass you will accept. Path for the top (piece 14). Finish (piece 31). That is a dining RFQ. "Something unique for eight" is not (piece 06).

The wall will not move. The leaf can. Start with the wall.
