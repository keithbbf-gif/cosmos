The top was 42 inches. The crate was 46 and a little more, plywood and a cleat, a corner that would not clear the rail. The path measure had been of the furniture (piece 14). The landing vetoed the box. The box was the job.

Crating is not overhead you hide. It is girth, weight, class, and a chance to arrive as one piece. Blanket-wrap is lighter and smaller and a different gamble. Write which. Measure the one you will actually ship.

## What a crate buys

A rigid skin. Fork pockets or skids. A place to write the job name. A chance the carrier treats it as freight instead of a sofa. It also buys inches you must add to every door and turn.

Ask the shop for crate-out dimensions on anything tight. A shop that will not guess a crate is a shop that has been surprised. Guess high.

## Blanket-wrap

Common on white glove and on short hops. The piece is the girth. The risk is the world. A good crew pads. A bad terminal does not. If the path is tight and the value is high, do not save the crate to look rustic.

Uncartoned furniture on a common carrier is how you fund a claim. Claims are slow. Dinners are not (piece 24).

## Split to fit

Tops off bases, aprons knocked down — those splits exist so the *boxes* turn (piece 14). Each split is a joint or a hardware set. Price them as part of crating, not as a surprise seam.

A path template of the crate (piece 16) is not cute. It is cheaper than a refusal.

## Weight and stairs

A crew of two has a limit. A turn stair multiplies the limit. If the piece cannot split and cannot be carried, you are in equipment — or a no (piece 42). Write the weight class on the RFQ if you know it. If you do not, ask.

## Dealers

Your routing guide may forbid certain crates or require a specific pallet. Attach it (piece 33). A mill's house crate may fail your PRO. Failing your PRO is not a finish defect. It is a paperwork defect you could have sent in week one.

## Inspection

Photograph the crate on the truck, all four corners, before you sign the BOL (piece 34, piece 37). If a corner is stove-in, write it. "Subject to inspection" scribbled after a clean sign-off is a weaker story than a note at the dock.

Open soon. Concealed-damage clocks are the carrier's. This pack will not invent the hours. It will say: do not let a crate sit in a garage for a month and then call the mill.

## Add the girth before you approve the length

Ask for a crate-out guess: +2 to +4 inches on a side is a common story, not a law. Add it to the smallest door. If the door loses, split or shrink the object now (piece 08), not at the landing. A household that approves 42 inches "because the door is 42" has approved a failure.

## Open soon

A crate in a garage for a month is a concealed-damage story the carrier will not love. Open, photograph, punch-list (piece 37). The mill can cure a miss they hear this week. They cannot cure a miss you discovered after the film had a new climate (piece 36).

## The RFQ lines

Crate / blanket / mixed. Crate-out estimate. Split plan. Path measured to the box. Photos on arrival: who.

The pretty top is what you wanted. The plywood is what the landing will judge. Quote the plywood.
