**Disclaimer.** Educational shop talk. Not a quote, not a contract, and not legal advice. Deposit terms, lead times, and cancellation rules live on the acknowledgment you actually sign. A shop in another state may run a different clock.

The elevation was taped to the shop door at eye height, pencil grease on the revision block, a coffee ring on the title. Someone had written REV C and a date. That sheet is the table. The email that said "looks great!!" is a feeling. Feelings do not go through the table saw.

Custom work needs a drawing the floor can build. Made-to-order work needs at least a marked-up catalog page with inches. Stock needs a SKU. If you skip the drawing on custom, you are buying a story. Stories remake badly.

## What belongs on the sheet

Plan and elevation, or a shop standard that is equivalent. Overall inches. Overhangs. Apron height. Leaf. Hardware locations. Finish system, not "oak-ish." Who supplies stone or glass. A note on the path if the piece splits (piece 14). A revision number and a date. A line for a signature — dealer, designer, or homeowner.

AWI shops already live this way. Case-goods shops that pretend they are too small for a revision block still need the same information. A napkin is a start. A napkin is not REV C.

If the shop draws and you approve, the shop owns the lines you signed. If you sent a designer PDF, say whose errors are whose. Silent PDFs are how a dimension dies at the wall and everyone points.

## Spec freeze

There is a day after which a change is a new job. Call it spec freeze. It should be on the ack: after approval, after lumber pull, after the first machine pass — pick the house gate and write it. `[VERIFY]` the house gate before anyone prints a week as policy.

Before freeze, changes are conversation and a revised quote. After freeze, changes are a change order: money, calendar, and sometimes a piece that is already the wrong piece (piece 30). People who "just want the overhang an inch longer" after the tenons are cut are asking for a different apron.

Dealers: do not promise your customer a freeze-free shop. You are selling a factory rule. Homeowners: freeze is not the shop being mean. It is the shop refusing to throw away a day of labor and a board that no longer fits the wall.

## "Looks great" is not a signature

Texts are easy. Approvals should be boring. "Approved: RFQ-104, REV C, 96 × 42, oil, no leaf, split base for stair — [name], [date]." That sentence can live in email. It should be copied onto the drawing.

A heart emoji under a photo of a sketch is how two people remember two different overhangs. The floor will build one.

If two people must approve — spouses, dealer and designer — the packet waits. Building on one "yes" and one "still thinking" is how you fund a remake out of the deposit argument (piece 29).

## When the drawing is wrong

It will be, sometimes. The wall was not square (piece 10). The tape was a customer tape (piece 17). The pendant was not in the photo (piece 15). The repair is a new REV and a new freeze, not a sullen sawyer "making it work." Making it work is how a reveal looks like a scar.

If the shop measured, the shop owns the inch. If you measured, you own the inch. Write that on the drawing (piece 17). Warranty language that says "customer measurements" is empty if the shop also refused to visit and did not warn you (see `CLAIMS_GUARDRAILS.md`).

## Commercial packets

Healthcare and hospitality RFQs already have finish schedules and typicals (piece 46). The approval drawing still needs a revision block that matches *this* room, not last year's typical. A typical is a starting point. A room is a room.

Do not let a chain's typical override a field measure without initials. The typical will win on paper. The wall will win on install day.

## What the saw reads

The saw reads the sheet on the door. It does not read your thread with the salesperson. If the thread and the sheet disagree, the sheet should win, and the thread should be closed with a REV D. If the thread wins, you do not have a drawing system. You have a chat.

## How a REV letter dies

REV A was the sketch. REV B added the leaf. REV C killed the leaf and added the split. The household approved REV B in a text and REV C in a voicemail. The floor built B. The stair needed C. That is not a mill error. That is three documents and no single signature.

Close the old REV when you issue a new one. Write SUPERSEDED on B. Keep B in the jacket so you can prove what died. If you shop three mills (piece 40), send the current REV only. A mill pricing B while you live on C is how you collect costumes (piece 41).

Commercial typicals need the same hygiene: typical-2024-03 is not room 214 until someone initials the field exceptions (piece 46).

Piece 30 is the change-order clock. Piece 25 is the date on the ack. This piece is only the object: the approved elevation is the order. Sign it like money, because it is.
