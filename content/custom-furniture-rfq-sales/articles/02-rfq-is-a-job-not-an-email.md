**Disclaimer.** Educational shop talk. Not a quote, not a contract, and not legal advice. Deposit terms, lead times, and cancellation rules live on the acknowledgment you actually sign. A shop in another state may run a different clock.

The inbox said "table??" and a photo of someone else's Pinterest. No inches. No doorway. No stain direction. No ship-to. That is not a request for quote. That is a wish that wants a price so it can feel like a plan.

An RFQ is a job packet. The shop can price what it can build. It cannot price a mood. Dealers who send one-pagers already know this. Homeowners who have only ever bought SKUs have to learn it once, in writing, before anyone cuts a cut list.

## The object

Name the thing as if a stranger will mill it. Dining table, 96 × 42, 1¾ top, breadboard or not, trestle or four-leg, apron height, overhang, leaves (how many, how stored), chair count you *think* you want — and then the room measure that will argue with you (piece 11).

"Like the one in the photo but longer" is a start. It is not an object. The photo has a thickness, a corner radius, a stretcher, a finish sheen. Say which of those you are buying and which you are not. If you do not know the words, say that. The shop can translate. It cannot invent your taste and then eat the remake.

Commercial jobs get a spec section: species, grade, finish system, edge, substrate, hardware series, AWI grade if it is millwork. A house job can be shorter. It cannot be emptier.

## The inches

Who measured, with what, on what date (piece 09, piece 17). Room, walls, ceiling, baseboard, the not-square corner (piece 10). Delivery path: door, hall, stair, landing, soffit, turn (piece 14). Allowances you already know — radiator, return, outlet, pendant (piece 15, piece 18).

If the shop will site-measure, say so and wait. If you are sending inches, own them. "About eight feet" is how a table arrives that blocks the back door.

Dealers: do not paste the catalog length and hope the customer's alcove is a catalog. The special is the inch.

## The path

A piece that cannot enter the house is not a piece. It is a refund argument on a porch. Width of the tightest door, height of the lowest soffit, whether the rail comes off, whether the top ships separate from the base. Crating adds girth (piece 35). White glove does not invent a wider stair.

If you have not walked the path with a tape, you do not have an RFQ. You have a hope.

## The finish and the hardware

Species and cut. Stain or oil or paint. Sheen. A physical sample in the room light, not a phone (piece 31). Hardware: finish, size, lead time if it is a buyout (piece 22). Customer-supplied stone, fabric, or a can of stain from the garage — say it now (piece 39). Those things gate the calendar more often than the mill does.

"Match my floors" is a sentence that has ruined more tops than a bad jointer. Floors are older, dirtier, and under a different lamp. Bring a sample board. Leave it on the table overnight.

## The commercial terms you think are boring

Ship-to. Dock, threshold, or room of choice (piece 34). Liftgate. Residential. A contact who will be home. Requested date — labeled as a *request* (piece 25). Who pays freight. Taxability if you know it; if you do not, do not guess on the RFQ and then fight the invoice (piece 45 lives in the checklist, piece 44).

Dealers: your PO terms are not automatically the shop's terms. UCC § 2-207 is the battle of the forms. A shop that quotes "subject to our acknowledgment" is not being cute. It is saying whose paper wins when your backside indemnity and their lead-time clause collide. Read both. Do not "per my PO" a custom top into a 10-day dock date the mill never saw.

## What does not belong

A novel. The shop does not need the divorce, the in-laws, or the Pinterest board of forty rooms. Two or three photos that show the room and the path, plus the inches, beat a memoir.

A threat. "If you want the job you'll beat this number" without a scope is how you get a cheap quote for a different piece (piece 41). Send the other quote's *scope* if you want a comparison. Hide the other shop's unit price if you must. Do not hide the leaf count.

A deposit. The RFQ is the ask. Money comes after a quote you accept and an ack you sign (piece 26). Shops that ask for a card to "hold the quote" are selling a slot before they have a number. Ask why.

## How long a real RFQ takes

A dealer one-pager: twenty minutes if the floor already sold the object. A homeowner first letter: an hour with a tape and a phone camera, longer if the stair is a problem. A commercial packet: days, because someone has to pull the finish schedule and the dock rule.

The shop's pricing time is separate. A clean packet can be quoted in a sitting. A "table??" email costs both of you a week of clarification that feels like neglect and is actually translation.

## The one-page test

Print what you sent. Could a millworker who has never met you build a takeoff from it? If the answer is no, you sent an email. Send the job.
