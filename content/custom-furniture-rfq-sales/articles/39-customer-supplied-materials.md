**Disclaimer.** Educational shop talk. Not a quote, not a contract, and not legal advice. Deposit terms, lead times, and cancellation rules live on the acknowledgment you actually sign. A shop in another state may run a different clock.

The can said "oak" in a handwriting that was not the shop's. It had sat in a garage through two summers. They wanted it on a new top so it would "match." It would not match. It might not even film. That can is a customer-supplied material. It is a gift that can stall a queue (piece 22) and a finish (piece 21).

COM, COL, customer stone, customer glass, a can, a slab they found at a yard — all of these can be good jobs. They are never casual jobs. Write who buys, who inspects, who owns a flaw, and when the object has to arrive.

## Finish they bring

Old cans change. Pigments settle. The system under the new top may not like the chemistry. A shop can refuse (piece 42) or can run a sample and say no after the sample. Budget the sample. Do not pour a mystery on a glued top.

If they insist, write "customer finish, no color warranty, shop labor only." As-specified still applies to the wood (piece 38). It does not apply to a can you did not specify.

## Stone and tops they buy

Sequence is the job (piece 16, piece 22). If their fabricator is late, the wood shop is in storage (piece 27). If their hole is wrong, the box is wrong. Who templates whom. Who files the miss.

A shop that cuts a wood hole to a verbal "standard sink" is gambling. Get the spec sheet.

## COM / COL

Yards, repeats, flaws, direction, fire codes on commercial (piece 46). Upholstery people already have this religion. Wood shops that take a chair to finish and a COM to a sewer still need the religion. Late fabric is a late chair. Flawed fabric found at sew is a fight about who looked at the roll.

Photograph the roll at receiving. Write the yardage received. Do not start a frame that needs 9 yards when 7 arrived.

## Slabs they found

Beautiful. Also: bugs, checks, a moisture that is not your kiln (piece 20). The shop should meter and reserve the right to refuse. A slab that is "already paid for" is not a reason to build a failing top. It is a reason to talk.

## Legal neighborhood

If the shop has made procurement commitments *or* has begun work around their material, cancellation gets stickier (piece 29). Their late stone can become your stuck top. Write a date after which the shop may invoice storage or unwind.

## Dealers

Your "customer will send hardware" is a buyout you do not control. Put a latest-arrival date on the PO. After that date the mill uses house hardware or parks the job. Silent waiting is how 12 weeks become 20 (piece 19).

## A latest-arrival date that means something

"COM / stone / can / hardware must be on our floor by [date] or the job parks, storage starts, and the ack date dies." The household can hate the sentence. They cannot say they were not told. A parked job is kinder than a half-finished top waiting on a sink that is still at a fabricator in another state.

## Photograph the roll at the door

COM that arrives short or flawed is a fight about who looked. Photograph, write yardage, date. Do not start a frame on a hope. The same habit applies to a slab they found and a can they love. Receiving is a measure (piece 09) for stuff that is not inches.

## The RFQ lines

Customer-supplied: list. Latest arrival. Inspected by. Warranty exclusions. Sample required: yes/no. Refuse right: yes.

The garage can keep its can. The job can keep a system that will still be a film in year five. If they want both, they can want — and the shop can still say no.
