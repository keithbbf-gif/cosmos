**Disclaimer.** Educational shop talk. Not a quote, not a contract, and not legal advice. Deposit terms, lead times, and cancellation rules live on the acknowledgment you actually sign. A shop in another state may run a different clock.

He said "probably around six" in the driveway, meaning thousands, meaning a table he had not measured, meaning oak he had not seen. She told her husband six. The shop later wrote eighty-four hundred on the quote because the top was two-inch and the stair needed a split base. Nobody was trying to cheat. Three different numbers had been allowed to wear the same name.

Call the first a ballpark. Call the second a verbal. Call the third a quote — and only when it is written, scoped, and dated. The ack is a fourth object (piece 08, piece 25). People who collapse all four into "the price" will spend the job unhappy.

## The ballpark

A ballpark is a shop saying: work like this, lately, has landed in a range. It is a kindness so you do not RFQ a dining room you cannot fund. It is also a trap if you take it to the bank.

A decent ballpark names what it assumed. "A plain 84-inch four-leg oak table, oil, local delivery, no leaves, no site-built base, no white glove up a turn stair — we have been around X to Y this year." The assumptions are the product. The numbers are a shadow.

A bad ballpark is a single integer with no object. That integer will travel farther than the table. It will be repeated to a spouse, a designer, a dealer principal. Then the real quote arrives and someone uses the word bait.

Write "BALLPARK — not a quote" in the subject line. Say what would move it: thickness, species, leaves, freight class, a site measure that finds a 92-inch wall. If you will not write those words, do not give the number.

## The verbal

A verbal is a ballpark with eye contact. It feels more true because a person said it in the room. UCC § 2-204 will form a contract from conduct. A verbal *price* is not automatically a contract. A verbal plus a deposit plus "start the oak" can become one, especially once specially manufactured goods are underway (piece 29).

The shop's job is to refuse to take money on a verbal. The dealer's job is to refuse to write a PO that says "as discussed." The homeowner's job is to ask for the email. None of that is cold. It is how you still like each other in month four.

If you must talk numbers on a walkthrough, follow with the same number in writing the same day, labeled. Memory is a bad ledger.

## The catalog "from"

A *from* price is a cart costume (piece 01). It is the smallest object in the photo, in the cheapest finish, on a dock you will not use. It is useful as a floor. It is fraud if the only number on the page is the floor and the asterisk is a whisper.

Dealers: your web tile that says "tables from $1,799" is your problem, not the shop's, if the special you send is $4,200. Do not yell at the mill for your marketing. Homeowners: if the tile got you in the door, fine. The RFQ is where you find out whether you are still in that photo.

## The written quote

A quote names the object, the inches, the finish, the path assumption, the freight assumption, the validity window, and the next document (ack + deposit). It can expire. Lumber and freight move. A quote that is "good forever" is a shop that has not been paying invoices.

Validity is not a sales trick. It is a statement about the world. Thirty days is common because mills and hardware houses will not hold a takeoff for free. `[VERIFY]` the house window before anyone prints a number as policy. If the window lapses, you re-RFQ or you pay a refresh. You do not get last month's oak at last month's diesel.

## The acknowledgment

The ack is the shop saying: we will build *this*, for *this* money, on *this* calendar, under *these* terms. Your PO may say something else. The signed ack is what the floor will read. If you want a fight about whose paper wins, that is UCC § 2-207 and counsel — not a blog (piece 02).

A quote you "accepted" in a text is still waiting on the ack if the shop's process says so. Do not cut lumber on a heart emoji.

## How the three false prices get into fights

The homeowner heard six, budgeted six, and the quote is eight. The dealer heard a dock date in a hallway and wrote it on the PO. The designer sent the *from* tile to the client as if it were the bid. In each case someone used a short name for a long object.

Repair is ugly but possible: write the three numbers down, write what each assumed, and decide whether the job is still the job. Sometimes it is. Sometimes the honest end is a smaller table or a later year (piece 43).

## A shop habit that prevents the driveway

Keep a line on the measure form: "No price spoken on site except a labeled ballpark, followed by email." It feels stiff the first month. It feels like mercy the first time a couple remembers two different integers.

Remote channels have their own mouth. If you put a number on a website next to a custom object, 16 CFR Part 435 may care about the ship story you told to get the click (piece 25). A ballpark on a product page is still a representation. If you cannot ship that object at that story, do not post it.

The driveway number can stay in the driveway. The job lives on paper.
