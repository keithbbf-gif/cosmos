**Disclaimer.** Educational shop talk. Not a quote, not a contract, and not legal advice. Deposit terms, lead times, and cancellation rules live on the acknowledgment you actually sign. A shop in another state may run a different clock.

The price list on the corkboard is last season's oak, last season's pull, last season's freight. Someone circled a number in pencil and wrote "maybe." That sheet is why this shop does not put a Buy button on a dining table.

A quote-only floor is easy to insult. The dealer wants a line for the PO. The homeowner wants the honesty of a tag. Both have been trained by sites that will sell a sofa at 2 a.m. with a credit card and a 30-day clock. Custom work is a different object. The wood is not on a shelf with a barcode. The room is not a stock photo. The number you need is the number that can sit on an acknowledgment without the shop lying or the customer feeling jumped.

## What the cart is actually selling

A cart price is a promise that this SKU, this finish, this freight, this week, is the same object for every buyer. That is a fair promise for a boxed chair from a warehouse. It is a costume for a table whose length is the room, whose oak is this kiln charge, whose stain is a board you have not seen in the north window.

When a shop posts $4,200 under a farmhouse table, it is doing one of three things. It is selling a stock size in a stock finish — made-to-order, not custom (piece 04). It is posting a *from* price and hoping you will not notice the asterisk. Or it is guessing. Guessing is how shops end up eating freight, eating a leaf that was not in the photo, or eating a dealer who thought the tag was a bid.

The corkboard is useful inside the shop. It is a memory of last year's math. It is not an offer. UCC § 2-204 will form a contract from conduct that is enough to show agreement. A public number next to a "buy" control is conduct. A shop that does not want that fight keeps the number in the quote.

## What you get instead of a button

You get a written quote after the shop has enough of a spec to price: species, size, joinery grade, finish system, hardware, who measures, who delivers, and where the truck stops (piece 02, piece 34). You get a date that is a queue, not a holiday wish (piece 19, piece 25). You get a deposit sentence that is about lumber and a slot, not a souvenir (piece 26).

That is slower than a cart. It is also how you avoid paying for a table that will not turn the stair (piece 14) or staining a top to a phone picture that was taken under a warm filter (piece 31, piece 32).

Dealers already know this in their bones when the line is a special. They still forget when the website looks like a catalog. Homeowners forget because every other object in the house was a SKU. Neither forgetting is a moral failure. It is a channel mismatch.

## Quote-only is not "call us so we can sell you"

A bad shop uses quote-only as a funnel: hide the number, book a consult, wear you down. A decent shop uses it as a filter. If you will not send a room measure, a path, and a finish direction, you are not ready to buy a piece that will be wrong in three dimensions.

The test is simple. After the RFQ, do you get a number you can put next to another shop's number, with the same scope written down (piece 40, piece 41)? Or do you get a vibe and a follow-up email? The first is quote-only done honestly. The second is a script.

This pack will not teach you how to close. It will teach you how to ask, how to measure, how to read a lead time, and how to treat a deposit like the money it is. If that sounds like less fun than a flash sale, you are in the right folder.

## Two readers, one corkboard

A dealer RFQ is a job packet: model or sketch, finish, qty, ship-to, requested dock date, house vs floor vs special (piece 05). The shop prices it like a plant: repeating a known object is cheaper than inventing one. The dealer still does not get a cart price for a 96-inch top when the catalog showed 84.

A homeowner RFQ is the same job with fewer part numbers and more room photos (piece 06, piece 07). The shop still needs inches, not "a table for eight that feels generous." Generous is not a dimension.

Neither reader is a lesser customer. The shop that talks down to the house account and up to the designer is the shop that will mis-measure both.

## What a quote is, legally, without the costume

A quote can be an invitation to deal or an offer. The difference is in the writing: quantity, price, description, and whether the shop meant to be bound. This folder will not decide your fact pattern. It will say: do not treat a pencil circle on the corkboard as an offer, and do not treat a detailed quote you asked the shop to hold for thirty days as "just a conversation" when you later want the lumber reserved.

UCC § 2-201 wants a writing for goods over the statute-of-frauds amount. Custom work also lives in § 2-201(3)(a) once the shop has made a substantial beginning (piece 29). That is why the ack exists. That is why the deposit exists. Quote-only is the front door to those documents, not a way around them.

Remote orders — mail, internet, phone — can drag in 16 CFR Part 435 (piece 25). A showroom handshake with a signed ack is a different clock. A website that says "ships in two weeks" under a custom table is borrowing the cart's mouth. Do not.

## How to tell a real quote-only shop

They will ask for the path through the house before they ask for a card. They will say when they do not know the freight. They will refuse a finish from a screenshot without a board in the room. They will write "not a quote" on a ballpark (piece 03). They will let a job go when the calendar is a lie (piece 42).

They will not apologize for the missing button. The button is for a different product.

The corkboard stays. Someone will still circle a number. The honest sentence is: that number was true for a different top, a different week, a different truck. If you want this room's number, send the tape.
