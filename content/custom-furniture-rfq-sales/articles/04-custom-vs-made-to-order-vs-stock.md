The dealer wrote "custom" on a PO for an 84-inch table the shop has built forty times. The homeowner wrote "in stock" on an email for a sideboard that exists as a drawing. Both used a word that felt true. Both were wrong. The floor will build what the drawing says. The calendar and the deposit will follow the *kind* of work, not the adjective.

Three products share a catalog page and they should not. Stock. Made-to-order. Custom. If you mix them, you will fight about weeks, about cancellation, and about why the shop will not "just swap the stain" after the top is sealed.

## Stock

Stock is a finished object, or a finished object plus a short pick-and-ship. It has a SKU. It has a location. Someone can walk to it. The quote is a price and an availability. The lead time is freight and the dock, not a kiln.

Cancellation on stock is a restocking conversation, a restocking fee if the ack says so, sometimes a full refund if the piece never left the warehouse. It is not specially manufactured goods. UCC § 2-201(3)(a) is not the sentence you need.

A floor sample is stock with miles on it. Price it like stock with a scar, not like custom with a discount.

## Made-to-order

Made-to-order is a known object the shop has already solved: same joinery, same hardware map, same finish system, in a size and a stain you pick from a list. The cut list is in a drawer. The jigs exist. The first one taught the shop. Yours is number forty-one.

This is most of what people mean when they say custom and should not. A 96-inch version of an 84-inch catalog table is made-to-order if the shop already knows the apron, the stretcher, and the crate. It is custom if the 96-inch length forces a new joint, a new leaf, or a new path through the house.

Lead time on made-to-order is queue + lumber + mill + finish + buyouts + freight (piece 19). It is shorter than a first-time object because engineering is done. It is not "two weeks" unless the shop is empty and the oak is in the rack. Empty shops are a rumor.

Deposits on made-to-order still make sense. The oak will be yours. The slot will be yours. The piece may still be saleable to someone else if you walk before the stain — sometimes. A 96-inch top in a rare green is not a floor leftover. Do not assume resale. Do not assume forfeit. Read the ack (piece 26, piece 29).

## Custom

Custom is a new object. New joinery, new proportion, a site-built fit, a finish the shop has not run, a stone or fabric that gates the wood, a commercial spec that is not the house line. The first drawing is the product. The second drawing is the product. The cut list comes after someone thinks.

Lead time includes thinking. People hate paying for thinking. They still pay for the remake when thinking is skipped. AWI-grade millwork, a built-in that hits three out-of-square walls, a table that splits for a stair — those are custom even if the species is ordinary oak.

UCC § 2-201(3)(a) cares about custom because the goods are not suitable for sale to others in the ordinary course once the shop has made a substantial beginning. Your name carved in the stretcher is the textbook. Your 41-inch height to clear a specific radiator is the same idea with less romance.

Identification of goods (UCC § 2-501) starts to matter when lumber is pulled and marked for this job. That mark is why the shop wants money in the drawer before the mark.

## Why dealers blur the words

"Custom" sells. It makes a special feel like a story. It also makes a customer think they can redesign after week six. If the object is made-to-order, say made-to-order on the RFQ and on the ack. Save "custom" for the job that needed a pencil before it needed a saw.

Dealers also blur "stock" to promise a date. If it is not in the building, it is not stock. A factory that can ship in ten days is a factory with inventory, not a custom shop that loves you.

## Why homeowners blur the words

The internet uses "custom" for monogrammed stock. The internet uses "handmade" for a factory with a hand-sanding station. You are allowed to want a known table in your stain. You are not required to call it an heirloom commission.

Ask one question: has this shop built this object before, in this structure, so that my choices are size and color? If yes, you are in made-to-order. If the shop has to invent the joint, you are in custom. If they can walk you to the piece, you are in stock.

## How the kind changes the RFQ

Stock: SKU, qty, ship-to, condition of sample if used.
Made-to-order: model or drawing number, size, finish, hardware, path, requested week.
Custom: all of the above plus the problem the piece is solving (the wall, the stair, the stone, the code). Attach the problem. Do not attach a novel (piece 02).

Mixing kinds on one PO is how a sideboard (made-to-order) and a built-in (custom) share a dock date that only one of them can keep. Split the lines. Split the deposits if the calendars split.

## The sentence on the ack

A clean ack says which kind each line is. Not as marketing. As a calendar and a cancellation rule. If your paper does not have that field, `[VERIFY]` whether it should. This pack will not invent the house wording.

The saw does not care what you called it on Instagram. The queue does.
