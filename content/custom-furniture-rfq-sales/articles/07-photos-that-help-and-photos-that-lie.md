The photo was beautiful. Warm lamp, wide lens, a table that looked ten feet long in a room that was nine. The shop priced ten. The room was nine. The fight was about a lens.

Pictures are part of an RFQ (piece 02). They are also how people launder a wish into a spec. The shop needs pictures that measure. It does not need pictures that sell the job back to you.

## What to shoot

The room from two corners, standing, not from the floor. The ceiling if the piece is tall. The baseboard and the outlet you will fight (piece 18). The tight spot on the path: door jamb, stair turn, soffit (piece 14). A newspaper or a tape in the frame so a stranger can guess scale.

If you are matching something, shoot that thing in the same light you will live with the new piece — morning and evening if the room turns yellow at 5 p.m. (piece 31). Include a known white (a piece of printer paper) so the shop can see what your camera did to the color.

Dealers: shoot the floor spot if the special has to die into a rug line or a window mullion. Do not send only the catalog hero. The catalog hero is a different building.

## Lenses and lies

A wide phone lens makes a small room look generous and a table look longer than the wall. Say "wide lens" in the email if you used one. Better: step back and use a longer crop. The shop is not pricing your square footage. It is pricing a piece that has to sit in it.

Night shots with warm bulbs make oak look like cherry and cherry look like a mistake. Overhead LEDs make everything green. The shop will not match a lie. If you only have night, say night.

Filters are a veto. If the inspiration photo is from a store, it already has a filter, a stylist, and a budget for flowers. Steal a proportion, not a color grade.

## The inspiration picture

One or two, not forty. Write the sentence: we want the thickness, or the trestle, or the rounded corner — not the carved apron, not the two-tone leg. If you cannot write that sentence, you are not ready to RFQ a copy, and the shop should not pretend you are.

Do not send a watermarked catalog page and ask for "the same for less." That is a different shop's drawing. Honest shops will not steal a line-for-line copy. They will build a cousin. If you own the rights or you are the designer of record, say so.

## What not to send

Children's faces. A safe in the closet. A pile of mail with your address. Unredacted other quotes with a competitor's customer list in the header. Forty nearly identical shots from a walkaround video.

A picture of a check or a card. Money comes later (piece 26).

A picture of a damaged piece you expect to be a warranty if you have not also written the ack number and the date. Photos without a claim are just photos.

## Shop photos going the other way

When the shop sends process photos — slabs, dry-fit, finish sample — those are information, not Instagram. Ask for a tape in the frame if scale matters. Ask what light the finish board was under. Do not approve a top from a dusty iPhone in the spray room and then reject it in your north window. Approve in the *room* or approve a board that has sat in the room (piece 31).

If the shop's photo claims "solid oak" and you can see plywood edge, ask. Photo claims are representations. FTC care about origin and composition lives next door to this. A pretty lie in a process shot is still a lie.

## A dealer walkthrough that wasted a week

The salesperson shot the catalog hero, the customer's smile, and a chandelier. The mill asked for the tight turn. Nobody had it. A week later the household sent a video of a landing that vetoed the crate (piece 14). The quote had to be redone with a split. The integer moved. The household thought the shop was baiting. The shop was missing a picture that should have been in the first packet.

Take the ugly picture first. The hero can wait. If you only have time for three frames, make them: tape on the long wall, the soffit, the outlet you will cover. Beauty is a later email.

If a designer sends renders, treat them as inspiration plus a warning: renders are square and empty. Photograph the real corner. Write "render is not field."

## A short shot list

1. Room, corner A.
2. Room, corner B.
3. Path tight spot with tape.
4. Ceiling height at the hang-up (fan, soffit, stair).
5. The thing you are matching, with printer paper.
6. Inspiration, if any, plus one sentence of theft.

That is enough. More than that is a folder the shop will not open twice.

The camera is a tool like the tape. Use it to tell the truth about a room. If the picture is trying to sell you the room, it is the wrong picture for a quote.
