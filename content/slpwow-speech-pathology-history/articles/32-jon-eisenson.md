# Jon Eisenson

> **Portrait placeholder.** Stanford and *The ASHA Leader* (11 September 2001) are the first places to ask. See `PORTRAIT_SOURCES.md`. Do not invent or generate a substitute likeness.

Jon Eisenson died on 27 July 2001 of prostate cancer, ninety-three, a longtime Stanford faculty member and, the Palo Alto paper said, an expert in aphasia and speech and hearing sciences. He had been ASHA president in 1958. He had also been a major in the Second World War who built a language-rehabilitation program for the Surgeon General.

## New York training

B.A., City College of New York, 1928. M.A., Teachers College, Columbia, 1930. Ph.D., Teachers College, 1935. Brooklyn College faculty, 1935–1942. After the war: professor of speech at Queens College, CUNY, and director of its speech and hearing center, 1946–1962. He also taught at Columbia’s College of Physicians and Surgeons and at NYU. The New York years are the ones that made him a textbook name in adult aphasia before the Boston battery existed.

## Halloran and the surgeon general

The war vita, repeated in the Duchan notes and the 2001 obituaries: assistant chief clinical psychologist for the War [Department, in the usual expansion]; chief clinical psychologist for language rehabilitation at Halloran General Hospital; the language-rehabilitation program under the Surgeon General. Rank: major. This is one of the clearest American links between a 1930s academic and a named military aphasia service.

## California, childhood aphasia, a second career

In 1962 he joined the Stanford medical faculty and became director of the Institute for Childhood Aphasia. The institute’s name is a period photograph: childhood language failure still wearing an adult neurology noun. Distinguished professor of special education at San Francisco State, 1973–1984. Extended studies at Stanford into the early 1990s. Visiting posts at Northwestern, Wisconsin, Colorado, Utah, Tel Aviv, Queensland. President of the Speech-Language-Hearing Foundation, 1965–1968. The California years are a second vita stacked on the New York one. Students who only met the 1973 textbook missed the army hospital; students who only met the obituaries missed the institute’s speculative geology.

*Adult Aphasia: Assessment and Treatment* (Appleton-Century-Crofts, 1973) is the book a lot of 1970s students owned. Earlier he had already been publishing aphasia notes out of the war hospitals; Duchan’s bibliography lists a 1947 *JSHD* piece, “Aphasics: Observations and tentative conclusions.” He wrote on “developmental aphasia” in a 1968 *JSHD* paper that later readers will find speculative — he said so in the title. Childhood language disorders have changed clothes several times since (childhood aphasia, SLI, DLD). Eisenson is a layer in that geology, not the present map. A later book, *Adult Aphasia* from Prentice-Hall (1984), and a piece written with his son Arthur, kept his name on the aphasia shelf into the 1990s.

Obituaries: *ASHA Leader*, 6 (11 September 2001); *New York Times*, 3 August 2001; Palo Alto Online, 8 August 2001.

## Place in the series

He is the New York–to–Stanford bridge, the war administrator, the 1958 president, the man who tried to make “childhood aphasia” a clinic with a sign. Goodglass was the Boston psychologist. Schuell was the Minneapolis empiricist. Eisenson was the textbook generalist who had seen the army hospital. The field needed that person when graduate programs were multiplying faster than specialists.
