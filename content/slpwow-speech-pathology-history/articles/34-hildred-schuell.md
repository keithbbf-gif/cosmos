# Hildred Schuell

> **Portrait placeholder.** University of Minnesota and the Minneapolis VA are the first asks. See `PORTRAIT_SOURCES.md`. Do not invent or generate a substitute likeness.

Hildred Schuell was born in South Bend, Indiana, on 15 July 1906 and was buried there, at Highland Cemetery, after her death on 17 February 1970. She had been, to the end, director of the aphasia section on the neurology service at the Minneapolis Veterans Administration Hospital and a professor at the University of Minnesota. ASHA made her a Fellow and then, nine months after she died of cancer, gave her the Honors of the Association. Tesak and Code record the delay. It is not a charming detail.

## The degrees

B.A., Milwaukee-Downer College, 1928. M.A., Middlebury, 1940. Ph.D., University of Iowa, 1946. She arrived at the Minneapolis VA in 1948, later saying she knew little about aphasia. She stayed more than twenty years. A fifteen-year stretch of work (1948–1963) ran on Public Health Service grants from the National Institute of Neurological Diseases and Blindness. The book that came out of it, with James J. Jenkins and Edward Jiménez-Pabón, was *Aphasia in Adults: Diagnosis, Prognosis, and Treatment* (Hoeber, 1964). The test was the Minnesota Test for Differential Diagnosis of Aphasia (University of Minnesota Press, 1965). In Madison people called it “the Schuell.” In Minnesota they called it “the Minnesota.” Tesak notes the nickname split.

## What she thought she was seeing

Schuell argued that aphasia was, at bottom, one disorder with a prominent auditory-processing problem, not a zoo of unrelated syndromes. That claim put her at an angle to Boston. Later writers have been kinder to her *treatment* instincts than to the single-disorder theory. Intensity, salience, controlled auditory stimulation — the things she cared about — now have a neuroplasticity vocabulary she did not live to use. Papers in the 2020s still test “Schuell’s stimulation approach” for severe chronic aphasia. This article will not write the approach as a home program. It will say she believed the damaged language system needed carefully dosed input, not a scavenger hunt for a lost center.

Her 1955 papers in the neurology journals and in *JSHD* (“Clinical Treatment of Aphasia,” with Virginia Carroll and Barbara Stansell Street) are the working prose: operational definitions, repeated observation, a refusal to invent a classification that the recovery curve would laugh at. “Diagnosis and Prognosis in Aphasia,” in the *A.M.A. Archives of Neurology and Psychiatry* that same year, is the physician-facing version: 203 patients in two series, patterns that stayed put except for gradual improvement. She had been writing “Aphasic Difficulties Understanding Spoken Language” in *Neurology* as early as 1953. The Minnesota Test did not come out of a sudden idea in 1965. It came out of a decade of repeating the same observations until they bored her into a battery.

## Place

She is the Minneapolis answer to Goodglass’s Boston and Darley’s Rochester. VA hospital, midwestern university, a test you could buy from a university press. James J. Jenkins, the psychologist on the 1964 book, and Edward Jiménez-Pabón, the neurologist, are not ornaments. The Minnesota Test is a three-trade object: speech pathology, psychology, neurology. She did not become a magazine name. She became a drawer in the test cabinet. For a clinician, that is the more serious afterlife.

Brookshire later sat on the same Minneapolis neurology service and counted connected speech instead of arguing about a single core. The city kept both habits. A student who only learns Boston syndromes misses the drawer and the counts.
