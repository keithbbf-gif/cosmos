# Women Who Built the Profession

The 1925 academy had a woman taking the minutes. Sara Stinchfield was not an ornament. She already held the first American Ph.D. in the work. She would be secretary for five years, then the first woman president (1939–1940), then an Honors recipient in 1953. If you need a single fact to kill the idea that “the founders were men and the women came later,” that is the fact.

The deeper story is less neat. Women did a huge share of the school and hospital labor, wrote some of the books that stayed in print, and still watched the named “schools” — Iowa, Wisconsin, Mayo, Boston — get told as men’s stories.

## The first generation, by name

**Sara Stinchfield Hawk** (1885–1977). Curry School of Expression, 1909; Pittsburgh B.A., 1914; Iowa M.A., 1920; Wisconsin Ph.D., 1922. Mount Holyoke, USC, the Orthopedic Hospital of Los Angeles, the John Tracy Clinic. Books on classification and, with Edna Hill Young, on a moto-kinesthetic method for children. ASHA’s membership gatekeeper when membership still meant something intimate.

**Margaret Gray Blanton** (1887–1973). Charter member. Co-author of *Speech Training for Children* (1919) and *For Stutterers* (1936). Less formal schooling than her husband, more titles on a shelf than many of the men who voted.

**Hildred Schuell** (1906–1970). Iowa Ph.D., 1946. Minneapolis VA. The Minnesota Test. *Aphasia in Adults* (1964). ASHA Honors, awarded nine months after she died of cancer — a detail that tells you how the association timed its gratitude.

**Mildred Templin** (1913–2008). Wisconsin trained, Minnesota career. *Certain Language Skills in Children* (1957) gave clinicians numbers for typical development instead of folklore. ASHA Fellow; Honors of the Association, 1981.

**Muriel Morley** (1899–1993). Newcastle. *Cleft Palate and Speech* (1945). Built a hospital service, then a university course (1959), then served as president of the British college (1971–1973). OBE, 1980.

**Margaret Greene** (1913–2007). *The Voice and Its Disorders* (1957), still in print in later revisions. Founded AFASIC in 1968. OBE, 1987.

**Elsie Fogerty** (1865–1945). A generation older. Central School, 1906; St Thomas’ clinic, 1913. Britain’s speech therapy begins, in several timelines, with her afternoon hours.

Add, without pretending this list is closed: Ollie Backus, Letitia Raubicheck, Mabel Farrington Gifford in California’s public-school work, Charlotte Wells, Ruth Beckey Irwin, Dorothy Sherman, Elise Hahn, Margaret Hall Powers (ASHA president, 1954), Edith Kaplan in Boston, Linda Nicholas in Minneapolis, Nancy Helm-Estabrooks later. A roster is not a census. It is a refusal to let the presidential photographs stand in for the labor.

## Why the labor was female

Public-school “speech correction” paid like women’s work because it was staffed as women’s work. Hospital posts after the war were often listed under neurology or ENT, with a woman in the therapy chair and a man’s name on the grant. University doctorates opened slowly. Stinchfield’s 1922 degree is famous because it was first, not because a stream followed at once.

The British story is even clearer. The College of Speech Therapists (1945) grew out of societies in which women were the profession, not a caucus. Morley’s fight in the late 1950s against being folded into the Professions Supplementary to Medicine as a doctors’ helper is a jurisdictional fight and a gender fight in one bill.

## What “first woman president” hides

ASHA’s 1954 president, Margaret Hall Powers, and 1939’s Stinchfield Hawk did not make the association a women’s association. Presidential lists through the 1950s and 1960s are still mostly men’s names: West, Johnson, Steer, Westlake, Carhart, Eisenson. The school clinicians, the ones who saw the caseload that justified the dues, do not appear on the gavel.

A responsible history does not invert the error and claim women “really” founded everything. Travis, West, Van Riper, Darley, Goodglass, Luria — the work is real. The correction is proportional: print the women who held the first doctorate, the first British hospital services, the aphasia test that wards actually used, the language norms that still sit in assessment chapters, and the voice book that outlived its first publisher.

## How to write them without a halo

Stinchfield’s classifications are dated. Schuell’s stimulation approach was built for a different evidence culture. Fogerty’s oral-English campaign belongs to empire as well as to clinic. Morley worked inside surgical teams whose aesthetics were not the child’s. None of that removes them. It keeps them in the same moral weather as the men.
