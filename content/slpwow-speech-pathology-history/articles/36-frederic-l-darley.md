# Frederic L. Darley

> **Portrait placeholder.** Mayo Clinic Archives and the 2001 *Aphasiology* memorial issue are the first asks. See `PORTRAIT_SOURCES.md`. Do not invent or generate a substitute likeness.

Frederic L. Darley (1918–1999) is the name on the 1969 dysarthria papers and on *Motor Speech Disorders* (1975). The life dates come from a 2000 field dedication and from Robert Marshall’s 2001 memorial introduction, not from a newspaper obituary in this pack’s hands. The work does not need the newspaper.

## Iowa first

Ph.D., State University of Iowa, 1950: *The Relationship of Parental Attitudes and Adjustments to the Development of Stuttering.* He is, at the start, a Johnson-era Iowa product. *Diagnostic Methods in Speech Pathology* (1963), with Wendell Johnson and D. C. Spriestersbach, is that product between hard covers. Then Mayo hired him into neurology.

Mayo’s institutional history puts Josephine Simonson, Darley, and Arnold Aronson into the Department of Neurology about six years after LeRoy Hedgecock’s 1949 audiology appointment — call it the mid-1950s. The spelling “Fredric” appears in one Mayo PDF. His own papers use Frederic.

## The 1969 ears

With Aronson and the neurologist Joe R. Brown he listened to seven neurologic groups, thirty-plus patients each, thirty-eight speech-and-voice dimensions, three judges, a seven-point scale. Flaccid, spastic, ataxic, hypokinetic, hyperkinetic, mixed. The claim: the ear can localize. Students still memorize the list. Duffy and others later said where the list holds and where it does not. Darley, the memorialists say, liked students who argued.

He also named apraxia of speech as a thing apart (Johns and Darley, 1970) and carried the argument into childhood with Yoss (1974). He wrote on the efficacy of language rehabilitation in aphasia (*JSHD*, 1972) when that question was still being treated as rude. Other Mayo papers from the same desk took the ear to multiple sclerosis (1972, with Brown and Norman Goldstein) and to Wilson’s disease (1974, with William Berry, Aronson, and Goldstein). The method was portable: if the disease was clean, the speech should cluster.

Arnold Aronson’s later voice work and Joseph Duffy’s later textbook are the afterlife. Darley is the person who made “motor speech” a course title instead of a paragraph under “dysarthria, see also.”

## The person in the memorials

The 2001 special issue of *Aphasiology* — Duffy, Wertz, and others — is affectionate without being empty. They call him a clinical scientist. They say he cared about nosology, the names of things. They say he shaped careers at Mayo and then kept showing up in other people’s reference lists after he was gone. That is a better portrait than a generated face.

## 1999

Bloomer died that year too. A cleft-palate review in 2000 named them in one dedication, with Starr and Zemlin. Motor speech and palatal speech lost their elders together. The classification survived. The argument about childhood apraxia survived. The habit of listening with a list survived. Those are the correct monuments.
