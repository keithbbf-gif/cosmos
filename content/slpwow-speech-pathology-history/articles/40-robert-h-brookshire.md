# Robert H. Brookshire

> **Portrait placeholder.** University of Minnesota, the Minneapolis VA, and ANCDS are the first asks. See `PORTRAIT_SOURCES.md`. Do not invent or generate a substitute likeness.

Robert H. Brookshire, Ph.D., CCC-SLP, ASHA Fellow, directed the Speech Pathology Section at the Minneapolis VA Medical Center and taught in Communication Disorders at the University of Minnesota. He edited the *Clinical Aphasiology Conference Proceedings* from 1975 to 1987. He received a lifetime-achievement award from the Academy of Neurologic Communication Disorders and Sciences in 1993. He and Linda E. Nicholas ran grant-supported aphasia research for twenty years and retired in 1997.

His birth date and death date were not found in a citable obituary by 14 September 2026. Plural Publishing’s author page, written in the past tense, is the fullest public vita. This pack will not invent a childhood or a gravesite. It will talk about the books and the measures.

## The textbooks, distinguished

*An Introduction to Aphasia* is first cataloged in 1973. *Introduction to Neurogenic Communication Disorders* is the later, wider book. Plural’s biography says that textbook line began in 1970 and dominated classrooms for decades; library records show 1991 and 1997 titles in the same family. Treat “1970” as the publisher’s memory of a first edition and check a physical title page before you print a year in a caption. What is not in dispute: for a long time, if an American undergraduate learned “neurogenic,” the prose sounded like Brookshire.

He also cared about how scientists write. *Writing Scientific Research in Communication Sciences and Disorders*, with Shelley B. Brundage, is the late book. Students who never saw the VA still met his hatred of fog.

## Nicholas and the connected-speech numbers

The paper most people now cite is Linda Nicholas and Robert Brookshire, 1993, *JSHR*: a system for quantifying how informative and efficient the connected speech of adults with aphasia is. Follow-ups in 1994 and 1995 asked how long a sample must be, whether main concepts are present, how right-hemisphere injury and TBI differ. Correct Information Units entered the language of the clinic. If you have ever counted CIUs, you have been in their method.

Brookshire’s earlier 1970s papers — pause time, token tests, naming under time pressure, clinician consequences in a session — are the behavior-of-the-hour literature. He watched therapy the way Darley watched dysarthria: with a list, and without romance.

## Minneapolis after Schuell

Schuell built the aphasia section. Brookshire, a generation later, ran speech pathology on the same neurology service and taught across the river at the university. The city had two famous aphasia habits: Schuell’s auditory stimulation and Brookshire’s discourse counts. They are not opposites. One is about input. One is about what comes out in connected speech. A student who only learns Boston syndromes misses both.
