# The Berlin School: Phoniatrics as a Medical Trade

Hermann Gutzmann’s 1905 inaugural lecture in Berlin had a dry title and a territorial claim: speech disorders as an object of clinical teaching. Phoniatricians in Europe still treat 1905 as a founding year. Not because no one had helped a stammerer before that afternoon, but because a medical faculty had agreed, in public, that the voice and the articulation of words belonged on the lecture list with the rest of the body.

## A teacher’s son, a dissertation on stuttering

Gutzmann was born 29 January 1865 in Bütow, Pomerania. His father, Albert, taught deaf children and wrote about speech errors as a school problem. The son took a medical degree in Berlin in 1887. The dissertation was *Über das Stottern*. He was twenty-two and already inside the family subject.

In 1890 father and son started a journal whose title is a program: *Medizinisch-pädagogische Monatsschrift für die gesamte Sprachheilkunde* — a monthly for the whole of speech therapeutics, medical and pedagogical at once. In 1891 Hermann opened a private outpatient clinic for the speech-impaired in Berlin. In 1896 he added a sanatorium in Zehlendorf. In 1907 the city clinic moved into the medical polyclinic; in 1912 Gustav Killian brought it under the Charité’s throat-and-nose service. The private phonetic laboratory followed.

This is not yet “speech-language pathology.” It is a physician’s specialty with teachers in the waiting room. Gutzmann trained both. Students came from across Europe: Max Nadoleczny, Rudolf Schilling, Hugo Stern, Miloslav Seeman, Giulio Panconcelli-Calzia, Gutzmann’s own son Hermann Jr. The Union of European Phoniatricians still calls the elder Gutzmann the father of the field and notes more than three hundred publications and thirteen books, among them *Des Kindes Sprache und Sprachfehler* (1894) and *Physiologie der Stimme und Sprache* (1909).

## What “phoniatrics” meant

The German division of labor hardened as the century went on. **Phoniatrics** stayed a medical specialty: larynx, resonance, neurologic voice, the prescription, the endoscope. **Logopedics** (the word Fröschels would popularize from Vienna in 1924) became the therapy profession — the person who sat with the child. The two shared patients and fought over status. They still do, under different national laws.

Gutzmann’s own writing does not respect the later fence. He measured breathing in stuttering for the habilitation. He wrote histories of speech therapeutics. He ran a school-facing journal. He wanted the university and the classroom in one sentence. American visitors who later called him a “speech doctor” were not wrong. They were translating a job that their own universities had assigned to psychologists and debate coaches instead.

## Berlin is not the only city

Leopold Treitel and Albert Liebmann were already publishing methods in German when Gutzmann was a student. Vienna would take the international association. Prague, Hamburg, and Munich had phonetic laboratories. The “Berlin school of the organists,” as one later phoniatric memoir called Gutzmann’s circle, was a density of people, not a monopoly.

The First World War filled the clinics with head wounds and voiceless soldiers. Gutzmann died on 4 November 1922 in Berlin-Lichterfelde, four years before the American academy named itself, two years before Fröschels called a meeting in Vienna. The German medical line did not stop. It passed to the son, to Nadoleczny in Munich, to the postwar university departments that still train physicians in *Phoniatrie und Pädaudiologie*.

## Why an American reader needs this page

ASHA’s origin story starts in Iowa City dining rooms and a New York hotel. That story is true and incomplete. Scripture took a Leipzig doctorate under Wundt and a Munich M.D. before he opened a New York clinic. Fröschels fled to St. Louis and New York. Stinchfield Hawk spent time in Vienna and London. The American field was not a prairie invention that later discovered Europe. It was a provincial chapter of a conversation that already had a Berlin address, a journal, and a lecture title from 1905.

When a modern clinician says “voice clinic” and means a joint session with an otolaryngologist, she is closer to Gutzmann’s hallway than to Van Riper’s undergraduate textbook. The names changed. The hallway is old.
