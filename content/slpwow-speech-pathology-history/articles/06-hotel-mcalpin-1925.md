# Thirteen People in the Hotel McAlpin

The National Association of Teachers of Speech was meeting in New York at the end of December 1925. Rhetoric, debate, theatre. In a room at the Hotel McAlpin, Robert West and Sara Stinchfield pulled aside the people who had come for a different reason. Stinchfield later said they “only succeeded in finding about a dozen people… who were really doing something which we then called speech correction.” The minutes of 29 December 1925 are in the ASHA Archives. Temporary officers: West, president; Stinchfield, secretary. The name they chose was the American Academy of Speech Correction. The purpose, as they wrote it, was “the promotion of scientific, organized work in the field of speech correction.”

There is a second origin scene, six months earlier. In the spring of 1925, after a weekend conference on speech disorders at the University of Iowa, Lee Edward Travis had some of the younger academics to dinner in Iowa City. The talk turned to an academy. December made the talk into officers.

Twenty-five people are counted as founders. The association’s own histories name the McAlpin meeting as the birth and the Iowa dinner as the conception. Both rooms were full of teachers who wanted out of the elocutionist’s shadow.

## Who was in the room, in spirit

West was thirty-three, a Wisconsin Ph.D. that same year — the second American doctorate in the new emphasis, after Stinchfield’s 1922 degree. Stinchfield was forty, already at Mount Holyoke, already writing tests. Travis was twenty-nine, Iowa-trained, already the person other people came to Iowa to study under. Smiley Blanton, who had opened Wisconsin’s Speech and Mental Hygiene Clinic in 1914, was the older medical man in the background; Margaret Gray Blanton was among the charter twenty-five.

They were not a hospital staff. They were a splinter of a speech-teachers’ convention. That fact decided a great deal. The American field would live in universities and public schools longer than it lived in medical colleges. Physicians joined later, or in parallel, or not at all.

## The first name did not last

1925: American Academy of Speech Correction.
1927: American Society for the Study of Disorders of Speech.
1934: American Speech Correction Association.
1947: American Speech and Hearing Association — the acronym ASHA starts here, when audiology has become impossible to treat as a hobby.
1978: American Speech-Language-Hearing Association.

Four legal names after the first. Each rename is a fight about who counts. “Academy” sounded exclusive and a little European. “Correction” began to embarrass people who had read more psychology than elocution. “Hearing” was a wartime and postwar fact. “Language” was the 1970s admitting that children who did not talk, and adults who had lost sentences, were not a side clinic.

The 1925 purpose clause — scientific, organized work — is the sentence that did not change. Everything else was renegotiation.

## What “scientific” meant in that hotel

It meant: we are not recitation teachers. It meant Iowa’s laboratories, Wisconsin’s clinic notes, Scripture’s leftover prestige, Travis’s electrophysiology. It also meant a membership committee that could keep the obvious quacks out. Stinchfield chaired membership later and sat on nomenclature. The early association was small enough to know everyone’s dissertation.

Elaine Pagel Paden’s *History of the American Speech and Hearing Association, 1925–1958* is still the book a later editor reaches for when the minute-books get dull. Charles Van Riper, who was not in the McAlpin room (he was twenty, still climbing out of a Michigan boyhood), wrote in 1981 that if the field wanted to know where it was going it should know where it began. He was talking about this hotel.

## What the minutes do not say

They do not say “language.” They do not say “swallowing.” They do not say “Augmentative.” They barely say “hearing.” They do not mention IALP, founded the year before in Vienna. They do not mention the deaf associations already fighting over oral methods. The American academy began as a professionalizing move inside white, English-speaking campus speech departments. That is not a smear. It is the starting census.

A later association would add audiology, language, multicultural statements, a Rockville office, a certification exam. The McAlpin room is not that building. It is thirteen people who were tired of being the odd table at a teachers’ convention.

West served as president through 1928. Stinchfield ran the paper. Travis went back to Iowa and manufactured a generation. The hotel is gone as a name. The habit — a December meeting, a gavel, a journal — is the oldest American habit the field has.
