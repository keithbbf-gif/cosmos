# Rochester Listens: Mayo and Motor Speech

In 1969 the *Journal of Speech and Hearing Research* printed two papers that still organize a Friday morning at the hospital. Frederic L. Darley, Arnold E. Aronson, and Joe R. Brown had listened to thirty-second speech samples from patients in seven neurologic groups — at least thirty people in each group, each diagnosis as clean as Mayo could make it. Three judges rated thirty-eight dimensions of speech and voice on a seven-point scale. The computer, such as it was, clustered the ratings.

They came out with names a student can still recite: flaccid, spastic, ataxic, hypokinetic, hyperkinetic, and a mixed flaccid-spastic picture in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis. The first paper was “Differential Diagnostic Patterns of Dysarthria.” The second, “Clusters of Deviant Speech Dimensions in the Dysarthrias.” The book, *Motor Speech Disorders*, arrived in 1975 from W. B. Saunders.

## How speech pathology got into Mayo

Mayo’s own otologic history records LeRoy Hedgecock, Ph.D., hired in 1949 as the first consulting audiologist. Hedgecock had been a speech pathologist at Indiana and Minnesota; he brought speech work with him until the neurology department appointed Josephine Simonson, M.A., Fredric L. Darley, Ph.D., and Arnold E. Aronson, Ph.D., about six years later. The spelling “Fredric” in that institutional PDF is a clerk’s variant; the papers and the 2001 memorial issue use Frederic.

Darley had taken an Iowa Ph.D. in 1950 on parental attitudes and stuttering, then helped Johnson and D. C. Spriestersbach write *Diagnostic Methods in Speech Pathology* (1963). Mayo redirected him. The clinic’s wealth was not a new theory of stuttering. It was a hallway in which a speech person could see thirty people with Parkinson’s, thirty with cerebellar disease, thirty with bulbar palsy, and ask whether the mouths sounded different.

They did, the judges said. “Speech indeed follows neuroanatomy and neurophysiology” is the line from the 1969 abstract that later writers never get over.

## Apraxia, and a fight about children

Darley also pushed *apraxia of speech* as a name that was not aphasia and not dysarthria. The 1970 paper with Donnell Johns on phonemic variability became a citation machine. With Kathe Allan Yoss, in 1974, he carried the argument into childhood — “developmental apraxia of speech.” That last phrase is still a courtroom. Darley did not end the argument. He made clinicians write down why they were using the word.

Joseph R. Duffy, who took the Mayo listening tradition into the next generation, wrote the 2001 *Aphasiology* paper on what Darley did and did not settle. The memorial issue is the right tone: love, then disagreement, which Darley had apparently trained people to do.

## Why “Mayo” became an adjective

Other centers had ears. What Rochester had was volume, a neurology department willing to share diagnostic credit, and a method you could teach: listen with a list, do not leap to a single impression, let the cluster point at a system. Students who never interned at Mayo still say “Mayo classification” the way they say “Boston classification” for aphasia.

The method has limits the 1969 papers could not see. Perceptual ratings drift. Diseases do not stay in seven bins. Instrumentation later found what ears missed. None of that makes the papers museum pieces. They are the reason a speech-language pathologist is in the room when the neurologist is unsure whether the problem is cerebellum or basal ganglia. The ear became a localizing instrument. That was the claim. It was a large claim to print in a speech journal.

## Darley’s year

A 2000 cleft-palate state-of-the-art paper dedicated its pages to four men who had died close together: H. Harlan Bloomer (1908–1999), Frederic L. Darley (1918–1999), Clark Starr, Willard Zemlin. The dates for Darley in that dedication and in the 2001 memorial are the dates this pack uses. A newspaper obituary was not in hand when this pack was staged. The field’s own memorials will have to stand until one is.
