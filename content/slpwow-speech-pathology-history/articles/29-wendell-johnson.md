# Wendell Johnson

> **Portrait placeholder.** ASHA and the University of Iowa hold photographs. Rights not cleared for this repo. See `PORTRAIT_SOURCES.md`. Do not invent or generate a substitute likeness.

Wendell Johnson arrived in Iowa City in 1926, twenty years old, valedictorian from Roxbury, Kansas, a stutter so severe the *Times Magazine* later called it grotesque. He had come to study English. He left a psychologist and speech pathologist, and he never really left. He died in Iowa City on 29 August 1965, fifty-nine, after a heart attack in 1955 had already taken most of his titles.

## The vita, without the myth

B.A. 1928, M.A. 1929, Ph.D. 1931, all Iowa — *The Influence of Stuttering on the Personality*, Travis advising. Research assistant, then the ranks, full professor 1943, director of the speech clinic the same year, later chief of the program and chair of the council that became the department. Editor, *Journal of Speech Disorders*, 1943–1948. ASHA president, 1950. Honors of the Association, 1946. A founder of the American Speech and Hearing Foundation, 1956. Louis W. Hill Research Professor at the end, when the administration had been set down.

He read Alfred Korzybski’s *Science and Sanity* while recovering from an appendectomy in the late 1930s and built one of the first university courses in general semantics. *People in Quandaries* (1946) is that book. He was elected president of the International Society for General Semantics in 1945. The semantics and the stuttering were, for him, one subject: what words do to people.

## “Not in the child’s mouth”

His most famous clinical sentence — stuttering often begins not in the child’s mouth but in the parent’s ear — is the diagnosogenic theory in a proverb. It moved a generation away from hunting a bad gene and toward hunting a bad conversation. It also licensed an experiment.

## Davenport, 1939

Mary Tudor, his graduate student, went to the Iowa Soldiers’ Orphans’ Home with a design: tell some fluent children they stuttered; tell some children who stuttered that they did not. Twenty-two orphans. No consent worth the name. The study was not published. People at Iowa called it the Monster Study. Johnson died in 1965. The university later apologized. Lawsuits came in the 2000s. Gretchen Reynolds’s 16 March 2003 *New York Times Magazine* piece is the public reconstruction.

This series will not reconstruct the protocol as a curiosity. It will say: the same man who wrote humanely about the damage of labels used labels as an instrument on children who could not leave. The theory and the orphanage are one career. Anyone who quotes the proverb in a parent conference is standing near that career. They should know the address.

## The building

The Wendell Johnson Speech and Hearing Center is still on the Iowa campus. His son Nicholas Johnson became an FCC commissioner — a different public life. The stuttering research program at Iowa remained a world center long after 1965. None of that cancels Davenport. The honest tour is: look at the building, then read the 2003 magazine piece, then decide what you will still use. Some of the later Iowa work on fluency is usable. The 1939 method is not.
