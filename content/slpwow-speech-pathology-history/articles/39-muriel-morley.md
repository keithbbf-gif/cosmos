# Muriel E. Morley

> **Portrait placeholder.** RCSLT and Newcastle University archives are the first asks. The *Oxford Dictionary of National Biography* entry exists behind a paywall. See `PORTRAIT_SOURCES.md`. Do not invent or generate a substitute likeness.

Muriel Elizabeth Morley (1899–1993) came into speech therapy through a want ad. In 1932 William Wardill, a Newcastle plastic surgeon, needed someone to score children’s speech before and after a new pharyngoplasty. He asked for an “educated woman.” Morley had been working as a secretary after a forced career change. She had not heard of the job he was describing. She took it.

## Diploma, thesis, three hospitals

She read, she learned to photograph, she trained with colleagues in Liverpool and London because she could not afford the London part-time courses up front. Diploma of the British Society of Speech Therapists, 1938. A thesis on cleft palate, passed April 1939, became *Cleft Palate and Speech* (E. & S. Livingstone, 1945): anatomy, surgery, speech, and what to do on a Tuesday, written so surgeons and therapists could share a book. Later editions kept the shared-book promise while surgery changed around it. This series will not lift home advice from those pages. It will say the 1945 object was already a team manual, not a finishing-school pamphlet.

By 1945 she had built Newcastle’s hospital speech-therapy service and was full-time across three hospitals, including sessions at the Royal Victoria Infirmary. The caseload included aphasic ex-servicemen with head wounds. She pushed parental involvement in an era that still liked to treat the child as a lone pupil. She helped install a university course in 1959. She edited the college’s academic journal (1966–1971) and served as president of the College of Speech Therapists (1971–1973). OBE, 1980.

The surgeon’s name in the 1932 advertisement is William Wardill in the plastic-surgery literature. Some speech-therapy retellings print Wardell. This pack uses Wardill and records the variant. The operation she was hired to score was a pharyngoplasty; her job, before she had a diploma, was to make the speech result visible enough that a surgeon could tell whether the new throat worked.

## The 1960 escape

When the Professions Supplementary to Medicine Bill threatened to list speech therapists as doctors’ auxiliaries, Morley wrote *A profession concerned with disorders of communication* and the college circulated it. The therapists came out of the bill. The 1960 Act passed without them. British SLT’s later independence has that memorandum in its attic.

## What the ODNB is for

Jane H. Schofield’s *Oxford DNB* life (2004) is the scholarly British biography. This pack used open histories (Wikipedia’s sourced page, RCSLT’s 2005 jubilee bulletin, the Strathclyde timeline) and flags the ODNB as the next paid read for a later editor. Birth year 1899 and death year 1993 are not in dispute.

## Beside McDonald, not under him

American cleft literature in the same decades runs through McDonald, Bloomer, Bzoch, McWilliams. Morley’s 1945 book is the British spine. A U.S. site that only prints Penn State is doing a national catalog. Newcastle had a surgeon who wanted speech numbers and a woman who made a profession out of the request. That is a founding as real as a hotel meeting.

The OBE in 1980 and the college presidency in the early 1970s are late medals for an early accident: a want ad, a diploma earned after the work had already started, a book surgeons could stand to read. British speech therapy’s hospital habit has that accident in it.
