# The Iowa School

Carl Seashore, dean of Iowa’s graduate college and a psychologist of music and hearing, wanted a scientific attack on speech disorders. He designed a course of study for a young man from a Nebraska sod house, Lee Edward Travis, and pushed him through a B.A. (1922), an M.A. (1923), and a Ph.D. in psychology (1924) in three consecutive years. Iowa’s own departmental history now says Travis was among the first people in the world to take a doctorate grounded in this new work. Wisconsin had already granted Stinchfield a Ph.D. in Speech in 1922. The two claims can both sit on the table: first in a speech department, first as a psychologist built on purpose for the disorders.

Travis stayed. A National Research Council fellowship, then associate professor (1927), professor and director of the speech clinic (1928). For a decade Iowa City was the place you went if you stuttered and wanted a laboratory, or if you wanted a job teaching people who stuttered.

## What they thought they were measuring

Samuel Orton, the Iowa neurologist, had a theory of mixed cerebral dominance and reading. Travis applied a cousin of that idea to stuttering. If the two hemispheres failed to agree on who drove the mouth, the result might be a block. He was among the first Americans to put electrical measures on that guess. The theory did not last in its first form. The habit of wiring the problem did.

Graduate students arrived with their own stutters and left with degrees. Wendell Johnson came from Roxbury, Kansas, in 1926 to study English, switched to psychology, and took a Ph.D. in 1931 with Travis — *The Influence of Stuttering on the Personality*. Charles Van Riper came from Michigan in 1930, worked as Travis’s assistant, and took a clinical-psychology Ph.D. in 1934. Frederic Darley finished a dissertation on parental attitudes and stuttering in 1950. A later Iowa bibliography would show that a startling share of the field’s early theses were Iowa theses.

## Johnson’s Iowa

Travis left for the University of Southern California in 1938. Johnson stayed, became director of the speech clinic in 1943, later chief of the program, and gave the university the building name it still uses: the Wendell Johnson Speech and Hearing Center. He also gave the field a sentence that traveled farther than most data: stuttering often begins, he said, not in the child’s mouth but in the parent’s ear.

That sentence has a body. In 1939 Johnson’s graduate student Mary Tudor ran an experiment at the Iowa Soldiers’ Orphans’ Home in Davenport. Twenty-two children. Some who stuttered were told they did not. Some who did not were told they did, and were corrected as if the fluent speech were error. The study was not published. People inside the program called it the Monster Study. The university later apologized. A *New York Times Magazine* reconstruction ran in 2003. Johnson’s diagnosogenic theory and his orphanage method are one career. A history that quotes *People in Quandaries* (1946) and skips Davenport is a eulogy, not a record.

## What Iowa exported

Laboratories. A respect for graduate students who were also patients. A conviction that stuttering was the problem on which a new science could prove itself. A later generation — Dean Williams, Oliver Bloodstein (who took the Iowa habit east), Joseph Sheehan on the West Coast arguing with it — spent careers answering Iowa even when they no longer lived there.

Iowa also exported people who left stuttering. Darley went to Mayo and renamed motor speech. Schuell had taken an Iowa Ph.D. in 1946 and went to the Minneapolis VA. The “Iowa school” is not a stuttering cult. It is a factory that happened to start on stuttering because the director stuttered, the dean could fund a lab, and the state hospital system would let a graduate student in the door.

## The building and the apology

You can still walk into the Wendell Johnson Speech and Hearing Center. The name is a fact of architecture. The 1939 study is a fact of ethics. Iowa’s present training program lives with both, which is the correct order: keep the clinic, keep the archive, do not let the brick do the apologizing.
