# Medicine Finds the Lesion

In April 1861, at the Bicêtre hospital outside Paris, a man who had been nearly speechless for years died. Pierre Paul Broca opened the brain — twenty-four hours after death, by his own account — and found a softened patch in the third frontal convolution of the left hemisphere. The patient had been known in the ward as “Tan,” for the one syllable he could still say. Broca showed the specimen to the Société d’Anthropologie within hours of the autopsy; the long paper ran in the *Bulletin de la Société Anatomique* that year. He argued that articulated speech had a seat.

The argument was not born in 1861. Franz Joseph Gall had already assigned “language” a bump on the skull. Jean-Baptiste Bouillaud had already bet that the frontal lobes mattered. Broca had a lesion he could point to in a wet room, and a patient whose life had been written down. That combination traveled.

## Two maps, then a fight

Carl Wernicke was twenty-six and in Breslau when he published *Der aphasische Symptomencomplex* (1874). He described a different failure: speech that flowed and missed its nouns, with poor understanding of what other people said. He put that failure farther back, in the superior temporal lobe, and he drew a connection between the two regions. Damage the connection, he said, and you get a third picture — speech that cannot find the words it can still understand.

The names stuck. Broca’s aphasia. Wernicke’s aphasia. Conduction aphasia. Medical students still meet them in that order, as if the brain had sat for a group portrait in the 1870s and never moved.

It moved. John Hughlings Jackson in London refused the idea that a “center” held a little man who spoke. He watched patients who could swear and could not ask for water. Henry Head, after the First World War, sat with wounded men and wrote *Aphasia and Kindred Disorders of Speech* (1926) in a prose that still feels like a person in a chair, not a diagram. Kurt Goldstein, who had run a Frankfurt institute for brain-injured soldiers, fled the Nazis and kept arguing that the whole organism reorganizes after injury. The localizationists and the holists spent fifty years talking past one another. Speech therapy inherited both stacks of paper.

## What the ward did not hire

None of these men was a speech therapist. The job did not exist in their hospitals under that name. Nurses, family, and time did the daily work. A few physicians — in Germany the “speech doctors” around Albert Liebmann and later Hermann Gutzmann — tried exercises. In France, some neurologists prescribed rest and a change of scene. The scientific prize was the lesion, not the afternoon hour.

That prize had a cost. Broca’s anthropology, his measurements of skulls, and the racial ranking he built from them are part of the same career as “Tan.” A history that quotes the 1861 paper and skips the craniometer is a brochure. The localization of speech and the ranking of skulls came out of one laboratory culture. Later clinicians kept the first and had to disown the second.

## How the map reached the clinic

When American speech correction organized itself in 1925, aphasia was still a physician’s word. The new academy worried more about stammering and the school lisp. The Second World War changed the caseload. Young men arrived at Halloran General Hospital, at the Minneapolis VA, at Framingham, at Boston, with holes in the left hemisphere and years of life ahead. Physicians needed people who would sit with those men for an hour and write down what came back.

Hildred Schuell learned aphasia on the job at the Minneapolis VA after 1948. Harold Goodglass took the psychologist’s post at the National Veterans Aphasia Center in Framingham in 1951. A. R. Luria had already spent the Soviet war years on the same injuries and published *Traumatic Aphasia* in 1947. The Boston and Minnesota tests of the 1960s and 1970s — Schuell’s Minnesota Test, Goodglass and Kaplan’s Boston Diagnostic Aphasia Examination — are late children of Broca’s autopsy and Wernicke’s pamphlet. They are also a refusal: a claim that the *pattern of behavior* can be described without waiting for the next post-mortem.

## What to do with the old names

Clinicians still say “Broca’s” and “Wernicke’s,” then apologize. The syndromes do not sit still under a scanner. A person with a frontal lesion may understand less than the textbook promised. A person with a temporal lesion may be more silent than fluent. The value of the 1861 and 1874 papers is not that they finished the map. It is that they made speech failure a fact a hospital had to write down.

The therapy profession did not descend from Broca the way a daughter descends from a father. It borrowed a vocabulary, a pair of famous lesions, and a permission to take language seriously as a brain event. The hour-by-hour work — the picture card, the pause, the family in the hallway — came from somewhere else: from elocution’s drills, from school clinics, from the VA’s afternoon light. The lesion told you where to look. It did not tell you what to say on Tuesday.
