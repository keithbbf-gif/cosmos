# Elsie Fogerty

*The Sketch* published her in September 1898: Anne Elizabeth Fogerty, thirty-two, already a voice teacher, not yet the founder of a school that would outlive the century. The Lafayette studio made her look like the West End. The work that mattered was ruder — Saturday classes, then a hospital almoner’s corridor, then children in districts she refused to call quaint.

![Elsie Fogerty](../assets/portraits/elsie-fogerty.png)

*Elsie Fogerty. The Sketch, 21 September 1898. Lafayette studio. Public domain.*

## From Crystal Palace to the Albert Hall

She was born 16 December 1865 in Sydenham, London. She trained under Hermann Vezin and at the Paris Conservatoire. In 1889 she was teaching elocution at the Crystal Palace School of Art and Literature, later at Frank Benson’s school of acting. In the 1890s she ran Saturday speech classes at the Royal Albert Hall. In 1906 she founded the Central School of Speech Training and Dramatic Art there. By 1908 there was a three-year course. In 1923 London University accepted Central as one of three houses that could grant a diploma in dramatic art — the fruit of her long campaign to make theatre a university subject.

She directed the school until 1942 in Central’s own later history; some obituaries let her keep the title until her death. The school’s version is the one this pack uses, with the disagreement flagged. She died 4 July 1945 in Leamington Spa. CBE.

The acting alumni are famous. The speech-therapy department is the reason she is in this series.

## St Thomas’, 1913

A British professional timeline dates her speech clinic to the Almoner’s Department at St Thomas’ Hospital in 1913. Wikipedia’s shorter life says 1912; Palmes’s 1991 dissertation calls her superintendent of “England’s first remedial speech clinic” and gives the work three decades. St Bartholomew’s had appointed Cortland MacMahon in 1911. First is a fight for plaques. Early is enough. Fogerty took stammering and other disorders out of the elocution studio and sat them down in a teaching hospital.

She also argued that voice was not a mouth trick. Posture, breath, the rest of the body — “body and voice,” later teachers called it. That is theatre pedagogy. It became, in British training, part of the clinician’s ear.

## Empire English

Fogerty cared about the “mother tongue” in a way a 2026 reader will not always like. Standard spoken English, verse speaking, the slum child who must be improved — the program has class and empire in it. Palmes’s dissertation is the place to go for the full argument. This profile will not wash it. The clinic and the prejudice grew in the same school.

## What Britain kept

A training house that could produce actors and therapists without thinking those were opposite careers.

A hospital session as a normal place to work.

A professional stubbornness: she wanted drama as a university subject and speech work as more than finishing-school diction. The Royal title Central received in 2012, and the Royal title the therapists’ college received, are late ceremonies for an early campaign.
