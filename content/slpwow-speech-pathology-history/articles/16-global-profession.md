# Names That Do Not Match

A parent who saw a logoped in Kraków and an SLP in Ohio is not confused. She has met one profession with two passports.

Ask for a speech-language pathologist in Vienna and you may be sent to a *Logopädin*. Ask in Berlin for the physician who scopes the larynx and teaches phonation and you want a *Phoniater*. Ask in London for the NHS clinician and the badge says speech and language therapist. Ask in São Paulo for *fonoaudiologia*. Ask in Tokyo and the postwar profession has its own association history, its own characters, its own fights with ENT. The patient has a stroke in every city. The door sign changes.

## Three international rooms

IALP, 1924, Vienna: Fröschels’s bet that logopedics and phoniatrics could share a congress.

The American academy, 1925, New York: teachers of speech who wanted a science.

The British college, 1945: hospital and school therapists who wanted a name the Ministry could see.

None of these rooms was the capital of the others. CPLOL (later a European logopedics standing body) and the Union of European Phoniatricians added more letterheads. WHO documents that try to count “speech therapists” per capita run into the same translation problem this essay is about.

## What travels, what does not

Tests travel. The BDAE has translations. Schuell’s ideas about auditory stimulation show up in papers that never use her name. Van Riper’s stuttering modification was taught in places that never saw Kalamazoo. Luria’s books, once they existed in English (1970 for *Traumatic Aphasia*), were read in clinics that had no time for Soviet psychology as a system.

Training hours do not travel. A European logoped’s education may sit in a polytechnic, a medical faculty, or a pedagogical college. An American SLP’s education sits, by now, in a graduate program with ASHA’s stamp. A British SLT’s education sits in a university that answers to HCPC registration. Equivalence is a lawyer’s job, not a historian’s.

## Colonial leftovers

English-language therapy arrived in some countries as a British or American export, attached to missionary schools, army hospitals, or aid programs. Oral methods for deaf children traveled with the Bells’ reputation and with Milan 1880. A global history that only celebrates “the spread of the profession” is doing public relations for the export. Local signed languages, local multilingual realities, local healers — they were already there. Sometimes the imported clinic was a gain. Sometimes it was a replacement.

This pack cannot write the Brazilian, Japanese, Indian, or African histories at the density they deserve; those need writers with the languages and the archives. What it can do is refuse the map that has Iowa in the center and everyone else as a later chapter.

## A practical table for a later editor

| Place / tradition | Common title | Older title | Medical partner |
|-------------------|--------------|-------------|-----------------|
| United States | Speech-language pathologist | Speech correctionist; speech pathologist | ENT, neurology (variable) |
| United Kingdom | Speech and language therapist | Speech therapist | ENT, plastic surgery, neurology |
| German-speaking Europe | Logopäde/in | Sprachheilpädagoge | Phoniater |
| France | Orthophoniste | — | ORL |
| Much of Latin America | Fonoaudiólogo/a | — | ORL |
| International congress | Logopedics & phoniatrics | — | Both in one meeting |

The table is a sketch. National laws move.

## Why SLPWOW’s readers get this essay

A U.S. private-practice site will mostly see American credentials. Families move. Clinicians train abroad. A parent who saw a logoped in Kraków and an SLP in Ohio is not confused. She has met one profession with two passports. The decent historical move is to say that out loud, and to name Vienna 1924 beside Hotel McAlpin 1925 without making either of them a mascot.
