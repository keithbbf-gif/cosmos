# Jamaica Plain: The Boston VA and Modern Aphasia

Harold Goodglass arrived at the National Veterans Aphasia Center in Framingham in 1951, first psychologist on the service. He had a City College B.A. in French (1939), a war as an Army Air Forces captain, an M.A. from NYU (1948), and a Cincinnati Ph.D. in clinical psychology (1951). He did not yet have the test that would outlive him.

By the mid-1950s the work had shifted toward the Boston VA Hospital in Jamaica Plain and, later, a Boston University affiliation. Edith Kaplan, Norman Geschwind, then a long corridor of names — Jean Berko Gleason, Martin Albert, Nancy Helm-Estabrooks, Sheila Blumstein, Edgar Zurif — made the place, for a while, the loudest room in English-language aphasiology.

## A test grown on the ward

Around 1960 Goodglass began standardizing the battery that Lea & Febiger published in 1972 as *The Assessment of Aphasia and Related Disorders*, with the Boston Diagnostic Aphasia Examination inside it. Kaplan’s name is on the book. A third edition, with Barbara Barresi, appeared in 2001. Translations followed. The cookie-theft picture became, absurdly, a piece of world culture: a kitchen, a stool, a sink overflowing, a child up to no good. Clinicians in other languages still point at that American kitchen.

The BDAE did not “prove” Broca and Wernicke. It gave people a shared way to disagree. Fluency, repetition, comprehension, naming — scored, not just narrated. The Boston Classification system that students still sketch on scrap paper is a descendant of 1874 and a product of 1960s lunch.

## Localization comes back wearing a lab coat

Mid-century American psychology had been wary of brain maps. Geschwind, reading the German and British literature that behaviorism had parked, argued for disconnection syndromes in modern dress. Goodglass’s earlier paper with Fred Quadfasel (1954) had already complicated laterality: most left-handed people with aphasia still looked left-hemisphere dominant. The Boston VA became the place where a modular brain was allowed back into the conversation, with patients down the hall who could test the modules.

This was not a speech-pathology department that invited neurologists in. It was a VA service in which psychologists, neurologists, and speech people shared charts. American SLP’s later comfort with “the neurogenic exam” owes more to that hallway than to 1925.

## Kaplan’s other invention

Edith Kaplan (1924–2009) is easy to reduce to “Goodglass’s co-author.” The Boston process approach to assessment — how the person gets the answer, not only whether the answer is right — is hers in the textbooks. The Boston Naming Test sat beside the BDAE. A history of speech-language pathology that omits her because she was a neuropsychologist is doing guild history. The patients did not care who signed the paycheck.

## The name on the door

Goodglass directed the Aphasia Research Center from 1969 to 1996. After his death on 18 March 2002 — complications of a fall, the university paper said — the center carried his name. The Gold Medal Award for Life Achievement in the Application of Psychology had come in 1997. He was still on an NIH grant at the end. Eighty-two years old and not done with anomia.

The Harold Goodglass Aphasia Research Center would later face the ordinary fate of named centers: funding weather, VA reorganization, the diaspora of students. The BDAE remains the portable version of the building.

## What Boston did not settle

Minnesota had Schuell. Rochester had Darley. Moscow had already had Luria. Boston’s fame in the English-speaking world sometimes flattened those rooms. A fair account is narrower: Boston made a *style* — the classified syndrome, the published battery, the joint rounds — that graduate programs could copy. Copying is how a local hallway becomes a profession’s common sense.
