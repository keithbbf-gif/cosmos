# Sara Stinchfield Hawk

> **Portrait placeholder.** ASHA Archives hold a 1939 president’s photograph; the University at Buffalo history site holds family pictures. Neither is a redistribution license for this repo. See `PORTRAIT_SOURCES.md`. Do not invent or generate a substitute likeness.

In 1922, at the University of Wisconsin, Sara Mae Stinchfield took a Ph.D. in Speech with a dissertation on graded speech tests. Smiley Blanton signed it. No one in America had taken that degree before. The *Journal of Speech and Hearing Disorders*, in March 1954, said that to hundreds of people she “represents what is speech correction in its finest and deepest sense.” The sentence is period velvet. The vita is steel.

## Auburn to Madison

Born 1885, Auburn, Maine. Diploma, Curry School of Expression, Boston, 1909. B.A., University of Pittsburgh, 1914. M.A., University of Iowa, 1920 — *A Preliminary Study in Corrective Speech* went into Iowa’s *Studies in Child Welfare*. Ph.D., Wisconsin, 1922. She also studied in Vienna and London; some later sketches say she studied with Freud. Treat that as reported, not as a dated training record.

Mount Holyoke, associate professor of psychology, speech correction, 1922–1932. Then Los Angeles: lecturer at USC (into 1944), work at Scripps, Berkeley, UCLA in various later notes, director of the speech clinic at the Orthopedic Hospital of Los Angeles, time at the John Tracy Clinic. She married Charles Lye Hawk at fifty. They had no children of their own; they took in nieces and nephews. She died in 1977. (UW–Madison’s staff page prints 1978. Duchan, the Buffalo site, and Wikipedia print 1977. This pack uses 1977 and records the variant.)

## The academy’s paper and gavel

One of twenty-five founders, December 1925. Secretary, 1925–1930. Membership chair from 1932. Nomenclature committee, 1939–1940. President, 1939–1940 — ASHA’s first woman in the chair. Fellow from the first year. Honorary life member, 1950. Honors of the Association, 1953.

Wikipedia, at one point, gave her presidency as 1930–1940. ASHA Archives and Wisconsin both say 1939–1940. Use the association’s dates.

## The books

*Speech Pathology with Methods in Speech Correction* (Boston: Expression Company, 1928) is the title that tells you the decade. She wrote on diagnostic categories. The nomenclature committee she chaired at the end of the 1930s is the same instinct in committee form: if the academy could not name a disorder the same way twice, it did not yet have a science. With Edna Hill Young she published on a moto-kinesthetic approach for children — hands on the face, a method later clinicians argue with and still teach in some rooms. This article will not write the method as a how-to. It will say she believed children’s speech could be taught through movement and touch, and that she put that belief between covers.

The Curry School diploma of 1909 is easy to treat as a picturesque start. It is also the older trade still showing: expression teachers who decided the work needed a university degree. She took that decision further than most of her classmates. Iowa’s child-welfare series printed her 1920 study; Wisconsin printed the doctorate. The Los Angeles orthopedic hospital and the John Tracy Clinic are the third act — the field leaving the Midwestern seminar for a West Coast hospital corridor.

## Why she is the lead American woman in this series

Because “first Ph.D.” is not a trivia question. It means a university agreed the work was a doctoral subject, and a woman finished it first. Because she ran membership when the academy was still small enough for membership to be policy. Because the Los Angeles years show the field leaving the Midwest campus and entering a hospital and a clinic for deaf children.

She was not a laboratory scientist in the Fairbanks sense. She was an organizer, a classifier, a teacher of teachers. The profession needed that person at least as much as it needed a servosystem paper.
