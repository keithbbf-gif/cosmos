# Carl Wernicke

The NLM print is a young man’s photograph, published in Munich by J. F. Lehmann: high collar, pale eyes, the face that later textbooks crop beside a temporal lobe. Carl Wernicke (1848–1905) wrote his important book at twenty-six and spent the rest of his life as a psychiatrist who also thought about language.

![Carl Wernicke](../assets/portraits/carl-wernicke.jpg)

*Carl Wernicke. Photographer unknown; published by J. F. Lehmann. NLM Images from the History of Medicine. Public domain.*

## Breslau, 1874

He was born 15 May 1848 in Tarnowitz, Upper Silesia. He studied medicine in Breslau, trained with Heinrich Neumann and, briefly, with Theodor Meynert in Vienna. *Der aphasische Symptomencomplex* (Breslau, 1874) described what English later called sensory aphasia: fluent, error-filled speech and a failure to understand. He placed the lesion in the posterior superior temporal gyrus of the left hemisphere and drew a fiber connection to Broca’s region. Damage the connection, he argued, and you get a third picture — conduction aphasia.

The drawing is the most successful diagram in the field’s prehistory. It is also a bet: that language is a set of centers plus the wires between them. Ludwig Lichtheim’s 1885 essay “Über Aphasie” expanded the scheme into the house diagram later called Wernicke–Lichtheim. Hughlings Jackson and Goldstein spent careers saying the bet was too simple. Geschwind, in Boston, spent a career saying the wires were due for a revival. Both arguments need Wernicke’s pamphlet, and then Lichtheim’s furniture.

Wernicke did not work from a single celebrity patient the way Broca’s “Tan” story travels. He worked from a *complex* — a pattern of symptoms that should travel together if the anatomy was right. That is why later test batteries feel like his grandchildren: they still ask whether fluency, comprehension, and repetition fall as a set. When they do not, the diagram has failed in a useful way.

## The rest of the career

He held posts in Berlin and then a chair in psychiatry at Breslau (from 1885), later Halle (from 1904). He wrote a textbook of psychiatry, described other neurologic syndromes (the encephalopathy that still carries his name is a different, later story, tied to thiamine and to work with his student Karl Bonhoeffer), and trained a generation of German psychiatrists. He was not running a speech clinic. He was running a university service in which aphasia was one chapter.

He died 15 June 1905 after an accident in the Thuringian Forest — a bicycle, in the usual telling. Gutzmann’s inaugural lecture on speech as a clinical subject was the same year, in another city, on another payroll.

## Why the name stuck in therapy rooms

Because the contrast was teachable. Nonfluent versus fluent. Frontal versus temporal. Production versus comprehension. Every later test battery, including Schuell’s and the BDAE, is in some sense a conversation with that contrast. The conversation now includes the complaint that real patients do not sit still in 1874 boxes. That complaint is also part of Wernicke’s inheritance: he made a box precise enough to fail in public.

He left no school of therapists. He left a word, a gyrus, and a diagram. For a medical ancestor, that is a lot. For a clinician looking for a Tuesday method, it is nothing, which is the correct division of labor.
