Virginia Tech put a June beetle on a Brown Turkey in their backyard guide and I felt seen. We have loud green fliers in fig week. People call all of them fig beetles and then import a California orchard PDF onto a South Arkansas stool.

Names matter because the **control stories are not the same**, and because one of these insects may not even live at your house.

## The one I meet in 8a East / South

**Green June beetle**, *Cotinis nitida*. NC State’s landscape note: about an inch, velvety green, bronze-yellow margins, sometimes reddish wing covers. Adults chew fruit. They will also mess with foliage. In some regions people call them fig-eaters. Grubs live in soil with organic matter, crawl on their backs, push little turrets in turf. Adults get loud around midsummer fruit — apples, peaches, figs.

That is my working animal for a humid 8a yard east of the dry line. I still look at the beetle before I write the name. I am not a museum.

## The one California and the Southwest wrote down

**Figeater / fig beetle**, *Cotinis mutabilis*, also handled as *C. texana* in UC IPM’s fig chapter. Velvet green, brown-yellow band, metallic green belly, a little horn on the face. Adults scrape into fruit. Excrement stains. UC said larvae eat organic matter on the soil surface; dry and harden the surface, starve the larvae, flood talk in orchard language. **No chemical controls recommended** in that guideline.

If you live in a true Southwestern fig belt, read UC. If you live in South Arkansas, do not run a Fresno flood recipe on a clay hole and call it IPM. The cousin is real. Your dirt is not their dirt.

Wikipedia’s range talk puts *C. mutabilis* in wetter Southwest and spreading with compost piles. **[VERIFY]** whether your county has that species before you wear their advice.

## The one that skeletonizes a rose and then visits

**Japanese beetle**, *Popillia japonica*. Smaller. Copper wings. Green head. White tufts. They skeletonize leaves. They will eat fruit. Traps baited with lure **pull more beetles into a yard** than they remove if the neighbors still have roses. I will not sell you a trap as fig control. That is a later draft.

Range is still moving. Some 8a counties have them thick. Some do not. A chewed leaf is not an ID. The beetle is the ID.

## What they all want

Ripe and fermenting fruit. A smell. A hole a bird already started. Green June and figeaters are clumsy big mouths. Japanese beetles are a committee.

None of them require a fog on fruit you will eat tonight. Handpick when you can stand it. Morning, while they are slower. A bucket of soapy water is a farm-stand cliché because it works on a small tree. A twenty-foot turkey will laugh at your bucket. Then you pick the fruit earlier and you net.

Sanitation is the adult chapter UC wrote for souring beetles and it helps these chafers too: do not leave a fermenting necklace. Do not run a compost heap of rotting fruit under the tree and act surprised.

Grub talk is a lawn talk. Green June grubs like moist organic soil. I will not treat a fig orchard with a lawn grub product as a fruit program. I will not write milky spore as a fig fix. Slow, regional, incomplete, and not aimed at your jam.

## What I do in the week they arrive

I pick. I pick again. I smash what I can reach without making a show of it.

I do not spray a ripe canopy.

I do not blame mosaic.

I do not write one Latin name for the whole country in a caption.

If the tree is a keeper and the beetles are a crowd, netting on a frame does double duty with birds. Mesh that stops a June beetle is not the same mesh that stops a driedfruit beetle. I still like a frame.

## The country is allowed to have three beetles

Write the one you hold in your hand. If you cannot name it, photograph the top and the belly and ask a person who owns a key, not a group that owns a jug. Then pick the fruit. The Latin will not sweeten a hole.
