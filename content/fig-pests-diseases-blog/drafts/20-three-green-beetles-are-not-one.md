Virginia Tech put a June beetle on a Brown Turkey in their backyard guide and I felt seen. We have loud green fliers in fig week. People call all of them fig beetles and then import a California orchard PDF onto a South Arkansas stool.

Names matter because the **control stories are not the same**, and because one of these insects may not even live at your house.

I have stood under a ripe tree with a neighbor who said “figeater” the way people say “blight.” He meant a loud green walnut with a clumsy landing. I meant *Cotinis nitida* if I was going to write it down. His cousin in Arizona meant a different *Cotinis*. A woman on a forum meant Japanese beetles because her roses were lace. We were having three conversations. The fruit was having one.

## Hold the beetle before you write the name

I photograph the top and the belly before I smash it, if I can do that without making a speech. Size. Color of the wing covers. Whether there is a horn on the face. Whether there are white tufts along the abdomen. A chewed leaf is not an ID. A hole in a fig is not an ID. The beetle is the ID.

If I cannot name it, I ask a person who owns a key, not a group that owns a jug. County office. A real entomology page. Not a marketplace listing for “fig beetle killer.”

Range is not a feeling. **[VERIFY]** your county before you wear somebody else’s advice.

## The one I meet in 8a East / South

**Green June beetle**, *Cotinis nitida*. NC State’s green June note: about an inch, velvety green, bronze-yellow margins, sometimes reddish wing covers. Adults chew fruit. They will also mess with foliage. In some regions people call them fig-eaters. That common name is how the country got sloppy.

Grubs live in soil with organic matter, crawl on their backs, push little turrets in turf. Adults get loud around midsummer fruit — apples, peaches, figs. They fly like they are late for a meeting they will ruin.

That is my working animal for a humid 8a yard east of the dry line. South Arkansas sticky August. I still look at the beetle before I write the name. I am not a museum.

Morning they are slower. I walk then anyway because the fruit is cooler. A beetle in the hand goes in soapy water. I do not fog the ripe canopy. I do not write carbaryl as a jam-crop dare.

## The one California and the Southwest wrote down

**Figeater / fig beetle**, *Cotinis mutabilis*, also handled as *C. texana* in UC IPM’s fig chapter. Velvet green, brown-yellow band, metallic green belly, a little horn on the face. Adults scrape into fruit. Excrement stains. UC said larvae eat organic matter on the soil surface; dry and harden the surface, starve the larvae, flood talk in orchard language. **No chemical controls recommended** in that guideline.

If you live in a true Southwestern fig belt, read UC. If you live in South Arkansas, do not run a Fresno flood recipe on a clay hole and call it IPM. The cousin is real. Your dirt is not their dirt. Clay that already holds a lake does not need a flood story from a dry orchard.

Wikipedia’s range talk puts *C. mutabilis* in wetter Southwest and spreading with compost piles. **[VERIFY]** whether your county has that species before you wear their advice. I will not pretend I trapped one here so I could sound complete.

I will not write one *Cotinis* for the whole country in a caption. That is how a useful page becomes a lie at the county line.

## The one that skeletonizes a rose and then visits

**Japanese beetle**, *Popillia japonica*. Smaller. Copper wings. Metallic green head and thorax. White tufts along the abdomen. They skeletonize leaves. They will eat fruit. They come in committees.

Traps baited with floral scent plus a sex lure **pull more beetles into a yard** than they remove if the neighbors still have roses. I will not sell you a trap as fig control. That is the Japanese-beetle-trap page.

Range is still moving. Some 8a counties have them thick. Some do not. A chewed leaf is not an ID. The beetle is the ID. If you have not held one, do not hang a lure “just in case.” You are mailing an invitation.

Milky spore is a slow, incomplete, regional grub story aimed at Japanese beetle larvae in some soils over years. It is not a July fruit rescue. It does not impress green June the same way people hope. I will not sell it as a fig-orchard fix.

## What they all want

Ripe and fermenting fruit. A smell. A hole a bird already started. Green June and figeaters are clumsy big mouths. Japanese beetles are a committee. Sap beetles are a later, smaller chapter — nitidulids in the eye, not a green walnut on a shoulder.

None of them require a fog on fruit you will eat tonight. Handpick when you can stand it. Morning, while they are slower. A bucket of soapy water is a farm-stand cliché because it works on a small tree. A twenty-foot turkey will laugh at your bucket. Then you pick the fruit earlier and you net.

Sanitation is the adult chapter UC wrote for souring beetles and it helps these chafers too: do not leave a fermenting necklace. Do not run a compost heap of rotting fruit under the tree and act surprised.

Grub talk is a lawn talk. Green June grubs like moist organic soil. I will not treat a fig row with a lawn grub product as a fruit program. Lawn insecticides are a turf argument with water and kids and labels. They are not a drip-line fig spray.

## What I do in the week they arrive

I pick. I pick again. I smash what I can reach without making a show of it.

I walk the ground for windfall. A peach dump next to the fig is a beetle farm for more than one species.

I do not spray a ripe canopy.

I do not blame mosaic. A painted leaf did not chew that shoulder.

I do not write one Latin name for the whole country in a caption.

If the tree is a keeper and the beetles are a crowd, netting on a frame does double duty with birds. LSU’s Mary Helen Ferguson said netting is the reliable bird tool. Mesh that stops a June beetle is not the same mesh that stops a driedfruit beetle. I still like a frame. I stake it. I check it. Netting that pools on fruit traps wildlife.

A pot I can reach is a bucket job. A tall turkey is a ladder I will actually use, or bird rent on the top third, or a years-long lowering prune. That is also beetle control. It is not a jug.

## Compost, turf, and the other life of the same insect

Green June grubs in a compost-rich bed are a reason I do not pile rotting fruit and call it feeding. UC’s fig-beetle note for the Southwestern cousin is an organic-matter larval story. NC State’s *nitida* note is a turf-and-organic-matter story. Different papers. Same habit: do not build a hotel and act shocked at check-in.

I will not write a grub drench onto a fig I eat from. I will not copy an older TAMU chemical aside onto a living tree. If a commercial planting needs a labeled insecticide, that is a licensed conversation with an agent and a label that says fig. **[VERIFY]** if you are that farm. This folder is a backyard 8a week.

## The country is allowed to have three beetles

Write the one you hold in your hand. If you cannot name it, photograph the top and the belly and ask a person who owns a key, not a group that owns a jug. Then pick the fruit. The Latin will not sweeten a hole.

I would rather miss a few green Junes with my hands than import a California flood, a Japanese-beetle bag, and a fruit-tree cocktail onto one South Arkansas stool. The fruit wants a pick. The names want a little respect. Both are cheaper than a fog.
