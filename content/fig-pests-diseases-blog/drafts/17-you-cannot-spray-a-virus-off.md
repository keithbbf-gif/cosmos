I need this in one hard page because the algorithm will offer you a protocol.

There is no backyard spray that removes fig mosaic disease from a tree. Not neem. Not oil. Not a compost tea. Not a peroxide fog. Not a “systemic” jug from the fruit-tree shelf. Not a heat lamp over a pot. UC IPM wrote the residential rule: no practical control. Replace the tree if it will not perform.

Florida’s older fig notes said there are no chemical recommendations for the virus. Oils may be talked about for mites. That is a different sentence.

If you take nothing else from this pack, take that split.

I have watched the protocol arrive in a group chat at 9 p.m. Three sprays. A tea. A full moon if the poster is feeling poetic. By 9:12 somebody has named a miticide. By 9:15 somebody has promised their tree “cleared.” I close the phone and go look at a painted stool that has fed me since before that thread existed.

## Why people think they cured it

A cool, easy May. The paint fades. They sprayed something in April. They write a testimonial.

Heat was the loudspeaker. Weather turned it down. The wood still has the file.

A hard prune. All new leaves for a while look “cleaner.” Then July arrives.

A foliar feed. The leaf greens around the pattern. The pattern is still there if you look.

A move from a west gravel pot to morning sun and a bigger tub. The plant grows. The owner credits the oil. The plant credits the roots.

I have done the prune. I have done the easy May. I have moved the pot. I have not done a cure.

The testimonial is a calendar wearing a costume. I will not argue with a stranger’s May. I will ask for their July photo, underside included. I usually do not get one.

## Heat therapy is a lab story

There is a real literature on thermotherapy and meristems and clean-up of woody plants in facilities that keep temperatures and test the result. Your driveway is not that facility. A heat mat on a cutting is for rooting, on a **thermostat**, in the range we already published for Fig Pops. It is not a virus protocol. Crank it and you cook the stick.

I will not write a home oven schedule. I will not write a black-pot-in-the-sun “solarize the plant” joke. You will kill the plant and keep the story.

I have cooked cuttings by accident when a mat stuck on. That was a thermostat I should have checked. It was not a clean-up. The ones that lived still painted in the first heat week, because the wood I took was already a file.

Do not treat a kitchen as a tissue-culture lab. I will say that once and I will not decorate it.

## What mite sprays do and do not do

If a labeled oil knocks back bud mites, you may reduce **new** infections on plants that are still clean. You do not uninfect the mother tree. UC’s agriculture note said controlling mites may help reduce incidence. Incidence is a population word. It is not an exorcism word.

A collection in a greenhouse can justify a grown-up conversation with an agent. A painted Celeste in the yard that still makes jam does not.

I will not name a miticide. Labels move. Fig as a crop is a paperwork orphan. **[VERIFY]** with the county if you are actually running a clean-stock bench. Do not copy a forum interval onto a tree you eat from.

Diatomaceous earth, neem, horticultural oil, bud-scale carving — none of those *clear* the virus. Mite pressure can be reduced. The virus in the tree stays. That is the guardrail I will not step over for a catchy title.

## Copper will not help you here either

People mash rust and mosaic because both make a yellow leaf. Copper talk belongs to a fungus chapter, and even there the states disagree.

LSU Pub. 1802 (2025): no fungicides currently labeled for figs in Louisiana. UAEX historic copper timing for rust when first leaves are full size. UF older fig notes: no EPA-approved fungicides for edible figs in Florida in that document. None of those sentences is a mosaic cure. Copper on a paint job is a miss at best and a burn at worst.

If the underside is grit, you are in rust. If the underside is clean and the blade is a map, put the jug down.

## What I will do instead

I will not propagate a disaster and call the babies clean.

I will not park a seedling I believe is clean in the pocket of a painted giant if I can help it.

I will not sell “virus-free” unless a lab file exists. It will not exist for a Facebook cutting.

I will keep a working painted tree watered and I will rake the rust so I do not stack problems.

I will cull a plant that never grows up and I will not hold a funeral for a virus I could not have sprayed off anyway.

I will photograph May and July on the same branch so I do not write a testimonial for a weather change.

I will take cuttings from flavor I want and assume the paint job comes along. Jack’s cutting specs — length, nodes, lignified wood — already live on FigRoots. This page is the medical history, not the tape measure.

I will mail the county a leaf if I cannot put the scene in a bucket. I am not a clinic. A cropped story is not an ID.

## A walk I actually run when somebody wants a protocol

We start at the whole tree, not the prettiest leaf.

We flip ten backs. Grit or no grit.

We smell fruit. Vinegar is a different folder.

We look at dirt. A puddle after a two-inch rain is not a virus.

We ask whether the tree grew and finished fruit last year. If yes, the protocol is a bowl. If no, the protocol is a replacement and a better hole, not a jug.

We do not walk to the farm store on that same trip if the only problem is paint. The aisle wants us confused. Confusion sells an “immune booster,” a fruit-tree systemic, and a bottle that says organic like that is a lab. I will not write those onto a painted stool.

If the plant is a first-year pot on a greenhouse bench, we still do not run a heat-lamp protocol. We check the thermostat on the mat if we are rooting sticks. We do not solarize a living tree in a black pot on gravel and call it clean-up. I have cooked sticks. I have not cleaned wood.

If the person brought a printed forum card, we read it for the word *cure*. If that word is there, the card goes in the same pile as the tea. If the card says *reduce mites on clean stock* and names a label, that is a county conversation, not a driveway mix. **[VERIFY]**.

## The protocol I will not run

Three sprays ten days apart. A tea. A full moon. A YouTube pair of gloves.

A peroxide fog “to oxygenate.” A garlic drench. A kitchen heat lamp overnight. A black trash bag in August.

If a sentence promises to *clear*, *eradicate*, or *cure* mosaic, it is a lie. If a sentence promises to *reduce mites*, it might be a label. Bring the label. Leave the cure on the internet.

I have a painted tree that still works. I pick it. That is the only protocol I will put my name on.
