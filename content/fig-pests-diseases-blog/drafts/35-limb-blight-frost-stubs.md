I have stood under a fig in March with a neighbor pointing at brown tips like they were a crime. He wanted a bottle. He wanted the wood green by Easter. The wood was dead. Dead wood does not drink copper.

Zone **8a** South Arkansas will write this scene most years. A night in the teens. A late freeze after the buds already moved. A rust year that flushed in October and then met January like a fool. Cold killed the tip. Then a fungus used the porch. The saw is the tool for both. The jug is a third chapter I will not write.

TAMU listed dieback as physiological cold injury. Young twigs die. They do not have to steal the crop. They do give fungi a porch. The handbook says prune the dead.

LSU’s 2025 PDF talks *Botrytis* limb blight: frost-damaged fruit, fungus into the shoot, spring wilt, buff spores, cut below the canker, clean the pruners.

TAMU’s limb blight (*Corticum* in their older name) is a rapid wilt beyond an entry at a dead fruiting scar. Remove the dead so it is not an infection site.

I am not keying your canker from here. I am telling you the 8a habit: **cold writes the first draft. The saw writes the second. A jug does not write a third.**

## The driveway sort before you name a fungus

I walk the whole tree. I do not start at the ugliest twig.

**Cold stub.** Brown from the tip back. Dry. A line if you scrape. Last year’s growth took the hit. Older wood may still be green under the bark. Mosaic did not draw that line. Winter did.

**Limb blight / canker talk.** A limb wilts from a *point*. Beyond that point the leaves hang. At the point: a dead fruit, a wound, a sunken patch, buff dust if the week is wet. LSU starts with frost-hurt fruit. TAMU starts with a dead fruiting scar. Same habit: the entry is dead business you left in the canopy.

**Rust hangover.** A tree that stripped in August and flushed in October will hand you extra brown in March. UAEX already warned that sequence. Winter wood, not a new disease I need to fog.

**Mosaic paint on live wood.** Oak-leaf yellow on a working leaf is not a frost line. Do not saw a virus.

If I cannot tell live from dead in January, I wait. January mood pruning is how you cut sleeping wood, or how you leave a wet stub in a week that will freeze again.

## Wait for green

I wait until I can see what pushed.

A fig in this climate will lie to you in February. Buds sit. A stool you wrote off will throw a shoot from the base. A tip you thought was only “late” will stay brown when the rest greens. Spring is the referee. I am not.

When green shows, I cut to it. Not a hat-rack stub “to be safe.” A clean cut into wood that looks wet and pale when I nick it, not brown and dry all the way through.

I do not sculpt a silhouette for a Christmas card. I take dead. I keep live stools. Virginia Tech said Southeast figs often live as bushes because trunks get hurt. That is 8a insurance. One trunk is a single bet. Five stools are a rebuild. The freeze-recovery talk in the other FigRoots pack is the long version. Here: dead wood off, live stools kept, no funeral for a virus, no bottle for a stub.

## Cut below the ugly

If a limb wilts from a point and stays wilted, I look at that point before I wave a saw.

Dead fruit still hanging. A fruiting scar that never cleaned up. A wound. A canker you can feel with a thumb. I cut below it into wood that looks clean. If I hit brown in the pith, I take another bite.

I do not leave a comb of stubs because I was tired. Stubs are hotels.

LSU said sanitize pruners with an EPA-registered disinfectant between cuts on blighted wood. I will not write a kitchen bleach novel. Do not ride a dirty blade from a canker tree to a clean rare name. Wipe. A product that is actually for tools. **[VERIFY]** the bottle in your own shed.

I take the wood off the place. I do not chip cankered limbs back into the drip line and call it mulch the same afternoon.

## Mummies are porches

People keep last year’s dried figs in the canopy because they stopped seeing them. Those mummies are how TAMU’s limb blight gets a door. They are also how sap beetles find a winter snack and how souring keeps a seat. They are not decorations.

I pick what I can reach in fall. What I miss, a freeze will sometimes drop. What still hangs in March comes off with the dead wood. One walk. Two jobs.

Frost-damaged fruit in spring is the LSU Botrytis porch. A late freeze after fruit has already set — we get those — leaves a crop that will not finish and a shoot that can take the fungus. I take those wrecks off. I do not spray them into health.

## What I will not do

I will not paint every cut with a mystery tar because a video said so. Wound dressings have their own arguments in orchard books. On a fig I usually let a clean cut dry. I am not sealing a peach canker program onto a Celeste stool.

I will not copper a dead tip. UAEX’s historic copper sentence is a May rust chapter, not a March funeral for brown wood. LSU Pub. 1802 in 2025 said no fungicides labeled for figs in Louisiana. I will not flatten those into a stub spray. **[VERIFY]** your own paper if you even have a legal use. The stub still has to come off.

I will not blame mosaic for a frost line. I will not fertilize a rust hangover into a tender October flush so I can have more dead in March. I will not keep a hat rack because I am sentimental about height. Height that is already dead is just a pole.

I will not write a weekly fungicide card so this page looks complete. Dieback is physiology. Limb blight, in the handbooks I will stand on, is sanitation plus a cut. This folder is a house tree.

## Bush form is a cold tool

I keep multiple stools on in-ground trees I care about in 8a. A single trunk after a bad January looks like a stick. A bush can lose a stool and still leaf. I will grow a pot collection as pots. I will grow an in-ground eater as a plant that can rebuild from the ground.

When a stool is dead all the way down, I cut it to the live crown or to the dirt. Young wood that dies back a foot and then pushes from below is ordinary. I cut the dead. I do not declare the variety a failure on the first brown tip. Celeste and the old yard names in this county have been writing that sentence longer than I have.

## The 8a year that makes extra dead

Rust strips in August. Warm October feels like a gift. The tree throws lime-green leaves. January collects. The saw in March is longer that year. Same wait-for-green. Same refusal to fog the brown.

A late freeze after budbreak is the cruel one. The tissue that moved is the tissue that dies. The wood that had not moved may still be fine. Wait. Then cut. Do not take the whole bush because the first leaves fried.

Clay that holds a lake at the collar is a different funeral. Sand that never drinks is a nematode wilt if the roots are already knotted. Those are not limb blight. Scrape the collar if the whole tree failed in heat. That is the Armillaria draft.

## The saw is sanitation

People want a spray that makes dead wood alive. That product is not in the aisle. It is a spring walk with a tool you wipe.

I take that walk before May rust talk, before June beetles, before I forget which limb was already gone.

If a limb wilts in May from a point, I do not wait for August to call it rust. I look for the entry. I cut. I bag a piece if I am about to do something expensive — the county clinic can still use a canker. A cropped photo of a brown tip is a rumor.

[Pick The Right Fig](https://figroots.com/2025/07/02/pick-the-right-fig-for-you/) already told you pots if the hole is a rumor, tight eyes if August stays wet, green fruit if birds write the calendar. This page is the wood chapter those sentences sit on. A pot can still freeze a tip. The saw still wins.

8a will give you this walk most years. Take it. Leave the bottle for a problem that still has a leaf.
