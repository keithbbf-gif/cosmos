Zone **8a** is a winter number. It is not a soil map. In South Arkansas I can walk from a clay puddle that holds a lake after a two-inch rain into a sand ridge that drinks a hose and forgets it. Root-knot nematodes like the second place. They will live in the first. They *win* in the second.

Texas A&M’s fig notes, the ones we have been using on FigRoots, are blunt that **sandy ground** is where root-knot gets ugly. UF/IFAS says the same family of worms is severe on figs in Florida sand. NC State talks nematodes as a limit next to cold. None of those sentences need a new trial from me. They need a grower who stops planting a mailbox name in the old melon row because the spot “drains real good.”

Drains real good is how you describe sand to a neighbor. It is also how you describe a hotel for *Meloidogyne*.

## What sand does that clay does not

Sand warms early. It dries fast. A fig in sand in July is already working for a drink. Knotted roots make that job worse. The plant hits noon like a drought even when you watered at breakfast. In clay, the same worms can sit under a tree that looks merely “average” until a wet week adds rot to the injury.

I am not giving clay a medal. Wet feet kill more baby figs in this county than people want to admit. A fig wants a hole that drains *and* a root that can still drink. Sand without nematodes is a fine fig soil if you will water. Sand with a vegetable history is a bet.

LSU’s 2025 disease PDF tells you not to use ground that just grew tomato, okra, or tobacco. Those crops build the same pest. I have watched people rotate tomatoes around a yard for a decade and then “retire” the corner to a fig because figs are easy. The corner is not retired. The tenant is still on the lease.

## South Arkansas is not one hole

People hear 8a and picture a map color. I picture the ridge behind a house, the old hog lot, the raised bed that was a garden when the kids were home, the low clay by the ditch. A county agent soil test will talk pH and fertilizer. It may or may not talk nematodes unless you ask. **[VERIFY]** what your parish or county actually runs. I will not pretend every 8a bag of results includes a root-knot count.

If I am planting a *tool* tree — Celeste, a Brown Turkey type we already know, something I can replace — I might use a hole I do not love, on a mound, with water and mulch, and I will not cry if it is average. If I am planting a name I waited a year to find, that name goes in a pot or in dirt I trust. [Pick The Right Fig](https://figroots.com/2025/07/02/pick-the-right-fig-for-you/) already said pots when you have nematodes. I am saying: assume sand-plus-garden-history is guilty until a root or a sample talks.

## Mounds, raised beds, and the lie of “I fixed the drainage”

A 12-inch mound in clay is a wet-feet fix. It is not a nematode eraser. You can mound infested sand and still hand the fig a knotted future. You can build a raised bed out of the same garden dirt and congratulate yourself for the lumber.

If I raise a bed for figs on a site I do not trust, the dirt in the box is mix I brought, or soil from a place that was grass for years, not the tomato horizon I scraped up because it was handy. Even then, I treat a rare name as a pot plant for a season so I can see the roots at pot-up.

Drainage and nematodes are two votes. Win both or admit which one you lost.

## What I do on sand I already own

I do not fumigate a backyard. I do not write you a Vapam story. Older TAMU handbook language about pre-plant treatment is exactly the kind of sentence that goes stale and was never a pot recipe. Rules change. **[VERIFY]** with a licensed applicator and your county if you are a commercial hole. This site will not cook a fumigant.

On sand I already own:

I keep the in-ground tool trees watered deeply enough that a knotted root still finds a drink. Shallow sipping grows a mat at the surface. Nematodes already own the surface in a lot of sand.

I mulch to cool the top of the hole. Georgia grower writeups and ordinary Southern practice agree on that much: a bare sand bowl in August is a stress machine. Stress is when nematode injury shows.

I do not run the collection across that sand as a “nursery row.” Overflow cuttings go in mix. [Outdoor](https://figroots.com/outdoor/) is pots and beds we manage. It is not a permission slip to use the yard as cheap medium.

I do not chase a miracle cover crop as if Tagetes is a fig nematicide. Marigold literature is a different crop conversation. I will not upgrade it.

If a young tree declines fast in sand, I lift it and look. If the roots are a necklace, I do not replant that name in the same spoonful. I may pot what is left, or I may admit the tree lost and save the label for a clean start.

## The first vote

In 8a sand, root-knot gets the first vote. Heat is second. Your hose is third. A fertilizer analysis is a distant fourth. If you want a rare fig to finish fruit in July, do not let the first vote be cast in last year’s okra dirt.
