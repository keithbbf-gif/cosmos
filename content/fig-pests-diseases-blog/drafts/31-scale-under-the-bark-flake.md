I flipped a pot in a 70-degree shop in January and the rim was sticky. Ants had a commute on the bench. The leaf looked dirty. The neighbor, looking at a phone photo in July, said mosaic. Mosaic does not smear off on a thumb. Sugar does. Then a fungus that eats sugar paints it black.

The cuttings magazine pack already has a line I like: a 70-degree shop grows scale. It does not grow winter. I am writing the pest page under that line.

Scale insects are bumps. They do not fly away when you blow. They sit on wood and on ribs of leaves and they leak sugar. The sugar grows sooty mold. The tree looks dirty and “diseased.” The disease is a film. The pest is a bump.

UC’s fig-scale chapter exists for California orchards. We have soft scales and the occasional armored surprise on pots that winter warm. I will not pretend I keyed every species on this place. I will tell you how I sort it from mosaic and rust.

## What I look for on a shop walk

Honeydew that is sticky on a leaf or a pot rim. You feel it before you name it.

Black film that smears. Sooty mold. Wipe it. The leaf underneath may be fine. The film will return if the leak remains.

Bumps that scrape. Armored scale looks like a little lid. Soft scale looks like a flattened drop. I use a fingernail on wood I can spare. A 10× loupe if I have it in the pocket. I am not a museum.

Ants commuting. Ants farm the leak. They are a clue, not the root. The ants page is next door. This page is the bump.

A shop plant that never got cold. Outdoor 8a wood with real winter — we do get nights that remind you zone 8a is a winter number, 10–15°F average annual extreme, not a beach — often comes cleaner than a pot that lived next to a water heater.

I walk the wood, not only the prettiest leaf. Mosaic likes to show on the new flush as paint. Scale likes a rib, a twig, the shady side of a pot you stopped turning.

## What it is not

Mosaic paint does not smear off. Rings. Oak-leaf yellow. Louder after a 98° week. No grit. No honeydew from the virus itself. UC said there is no practical residential cure for the virus. Oils may bother mites. They do not evict mosaic. Do not treat a painted leaf for scale because it is yellow. Flip. Wipe. Look at wood.

Rust grit is orange and underside-blistered. Thumb stain. Angular map. Fruit is not the rust host. Soot is a film. I have watched a person copper a sooty leaf because they were tired and the leaf was dark. The black smeared. Rust does not smear like that.

Spider-mite stipple is fine pepper on the top in a dry 8a week. Web if you let a greenhouse go. Not sticky. Not orange dust.

A tired top from nematodes does not come with a sticky rim unless scale is also invited. Knock a tool plant if you need the root story. Do not diagnose galls from soot.

A bird peck, a mammal grab, and a sap-beetle eye are fruit doors. Scale is a wood and leaf leak. Ants on ripe fruit in July are often a jam meeting, not a January shop meeting. Do not mash them.

## What I do

I scrape what I can reach on a pot I can handle. A toothbrush is a tool. It is not a personality. Alcohol on a cotton swab is a pot trick I have used on a few bumps I could see. It is not a twenty-foot turkey program.

I wash the sooty leaf if I cannot stand it. The film will return if the leak remains. I do not copper the soot. LSU Pub. 1802 (2025) already said no fungicides labeled for figs in Louisiana in that document. Sooty mold is not rust. **[VERIFY]** your own state’s label if someone sold you a jug for “black fungus on figs.”

I talk oil only as a **dormant or labeled** conversation on a plant I can move, not as a July fog on an in-ground jam tree. **[VERIFY]** the label for fig and for the temperature you will spray. Oil plus 8a heat cooks leaves. I have seen a shop plant burn because somebody treated January like July, and I have seen a July leaf cook because somebody treated a fig like an apple.

I do not use a random “systemic” from the ornamental aisle on fruit I will eat. Shop plants that are not a food crop this year are still going to be a food crop later. I keep that in my head.

I do not treat a painted mosaic tree for scale because the leaf is yellow.

I keep the pot block from becoming a jungle. Light. Air. Not a gift to scale. The winter-shop culture chapter is a different file. This is the bump chapter.

## Prevention is winter honesty

If you winter pots in a warm shop, you owe them a look in January. Walk the rims. Flip ten leaves. Check the wood under a flake of bark you can lift without stripping the plant.

Outdoor wood that froze may still carry a few. I do not oil a whole in-ground turkey as a rite of March unless I have a reason I can point at. A clean cold snap is not a spray date.

Bring a dirty shop plant outside in spring and you donate bumps to the yard. I look before a pot graduates. I do not put a crusted pot next to a clean cutting I care about.

## The ants

If I see ants and soot, I do not start an ant war as the main play. I start at the bump. Ant baits are a house conversation. They will not evict scale. They may reduce the farmhands.

I do not flood a nest in the root zone and then wonder why the fig sulked. I do not paint a trunk insecticide as a backyard dare.

A dirty leaf is a clue. The virus did not leak sugar. The bump did. Scrape first. Name second. Jug last, if the label even lets you.

## 8a shops and 8a yards

Warm winter pots leak more. A heater room in a wet January is scale weather even when the county is “humid South” on paper. Outdoor wood with a real cold night often resets the pretty insects better than a shop.

In July the same yard can have rust on an in-ground turkey, mites on a pot on hot gravel, and scale on the one pot that never left the bench. One word — blight — is useless. Name the room. Name the leak.

I would scrape a pot I can hold. I would not fog a jam tree because a leaf was black. I would leave mild mosaic on a tree that still works. I would not call sooty mold a virus. The bump is a bump. The paint is paint. Buy the name first.
