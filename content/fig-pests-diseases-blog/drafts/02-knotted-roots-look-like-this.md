You do not meet a root-knot nematode. You meet a fig that drinks like it is working and still folds at noon. You meet a fertilizer bag you did not need. You meet roots that look like they swallowed birdshot.

The worm itself is a thread you will not see without a microscope and a reason. *Meloidogyne* species live in the dirt and in the root. The female sets up a feeding site. The root swells around her. That swelling is the gall. Eggs wait in the soil for the next season. LSU AgCenter’s 2025 fig disease note (Pub. 1802) says the same thing in clinic English: poor growth, yellowing, low yield, poor fruit, **small galls on the roots**. Texas A&M’s *Texas Plant Disease Handbook* calls root-knot one of the common fig problems. UF/IFAS — J. W. Noling, *Nematodes of Backyard Deciduous Fruit and Nut Crops in Florida* (ENY-055) — puts figs on the list with peach and pecan for severe root-knot pressure in sand. NC State’s Dave Ritchie named nematodes next to cold as a limit on figs in North Carolina.

I am not writing a parasitology lecture. I am writing what I want you to see before you decide the tree is “just hungry.”

## A February tub, not a July autopsy

Late February I knock a spare pot I already owed a bigger can. The shop is cold enough that the mix comes out in a slab. I sit on a bucket and wash the roots in a plastic tub I will dump on the knotted side of the yard, not on the clean bench. The water goes the color of weak coffee. My fingers find the feeders.

That is when you meet them, if they are there. Not on a Sunday in July when the air is a wet wool blanket and a rare name is already stressed. Heat plus a torn root plus a collection you care about is how you turn a suspicion into a corpse.

I have done the July version once. The plant lived. I still felt stupid. Curiosity is not a reason to strip a mailbox name in 8a summer.

## The top lies. The root does not.

From the porch, nematode injury is a bad impression of drought, wet feet, and a cheap potting mix. The leaf may yellow without a mosaic pattern. The internodes stay short. New growth looks like it gave up in June. Fruit sets and then decides not to finish. You water. The plant wilts anyway. Or you do not water, because the pot is heavy, and it still looks thirsty.

That heavy-pot wilt is the one that fools people. The medium has water. The pipes in the root are the problem. Galls break the ordinary business of a feeder root. Fungi and bacteria get a door. The top writes a sad letter and you answer it with 10-10-10.

I have done that. It is a reasonable letter to answer if you have not looked downstairs.

South Arkansas will hand you the lie in two dialects. After a wet May the clay hole holds a lake and the tree looks tired for a water reason. Then June turns off the rain for ten days, the sand ridge goes to dust, and the same tired look shows up on a plant that has been watered. One story is wet feet. One story is plumbing. The leaf does not tell you which. The feeder roots do.

Virginia Cooperative Extension’s backyard fig guide (Burnett and Samtani, SPES-760P) puts nematodes on the same short list as rust and mosaic. They did not tell you to diagnose them from a porch photo. Neither will I.

## What galls look like in a hand

Knock a plant you can spare. Not the mailbox name. A spare Celeste, a seedling, a pot you were going to cull.

Wash the mix off in a tub you will not pour onto a clean bed. Look at the feeder roots, the thin ones, not just the woody shank.

What you want: a fibrous mat. Pale, branched, nothing that looks like a necklace of beads.

What you get with root-knot: swellings. Some are pinhead. Some are peas. They are not the even thickening of a healthy storage root. They sit on the young roots like knots in cheap twine. Severe cases look like the root tried to grow a second set of knuckles.

People send pictures of a thick, healthy shank and ask if that is a gall. Usually it is just a fig root being a fig root. Galls are on the feeders. Once you have seen a bad one, you will not unsee it. That is useful. It stops the third bag of fertilizer.

Do not diagnose a tree from one swollen nodule on an old woody root. Do not diagnose from a leaf. A rust-stripped canopy in August will fake “the tree is starving” from the driveway. Mosaic will paint a yellow that has nothing to do with *Meloidogyne*. If you need a name for a whole property, the county can talk soil samples. **[VERIFY]** with your extension office what they actually run for nematodes this year. I will not invent a lab code or a bag color.

Noling’s Florida note is the sentence I want on a nursery receipt: inspect the roots before the hole is open. Galled stock goes back or goes in the trash, not into the only clean bed you have.

## When I look, and when I do not

I do not rip a rare pot out of mix in July because I am curious.

I look at pot-up. I look at dormancy. I look when a tool plant is already coming out of a container for a good reason. If an in-ground tree is dying and I am going to move it anyway, I look then.

A sad top in August might also be rust stripping the factory, or a lake in clay, or a trunk that froze back and you have not admitted it. Dig for knots only when the story downstairs would change what you do. If you already decided that name lives in a pot of mix you trust, you do not need a July autopsy.

I will also not learn galls on the one plant I cannot replace. I keep a couple of tool plants for that. Ugly work. Honest work.

## How they move when you are not looking

They move in dirt. They move on a shovel. They move on a boot that walked the tomato row and then the pot bench. They move on a plant you bought because the leaves were pretty.

UF/IFAS is plain: sometimes the nursery plant is already occupied. Look at the roots before the hole is open.

They also live on weeds and on last year’s vegetables. LSU Pub. 1802 says do not pick a hole that just grew tomato, okra, or tobacco. I would add “and do not pretend a winter cover made the sand forget.” It may lower numbers. It does not issue a certificate.

A bucket of yard soil used as “cheap potting mix” is how a collection inherits a farm problem. [An Introduction to Figs](https://figroots.com/an-introduction-to-figs/) already said do not root in mystery local dirt. I am saying it again because people still do it for the overflow cuttings.

I have watched a tidy grower keep clean mix on the bench and then set the pot on a saucer of yard dirt “so it does not blow over.” The roots found the saucer. The fence had a hole.

## What I will not tell you I see

I will not tell you I can see “beneficial nematodes” winning in a teaspoon of Arkansas sand. I will not tell you a gall count from a photo. I will not tell you a variety is immune because a listing said “nematode resistant.” We do not print that phrase as a fact unless a named trial said so. LSU Purple’s foliage notes are not a nematode passport.

I will not name a species from your phone. *Meloidogyne* is the working name. A lab can split *incognita* from *arenaria* from something else. Your next hour does not need that split to decide the mix on that plant does not go on the clean bench.

I will not write you a drench that erases the worm. NC State’s fig-disease talk, as we have been using it, is that established plants do not get a homeowner chemical reset. Pots. Clean stock. Do not donate a rare cutting to that hole.

I will tell you the top is a rumor and the root is a witness. If the witness is knotted, treat that plant as occupied. Do not spread that soil. Do not pot the next rare stick in the same spoonful. Keep the tool tree alive if it is still earning its keep — water, mulch, no extra drought. Put the collection somewhere else.

The worm stays invisible. The plumbing does not.
