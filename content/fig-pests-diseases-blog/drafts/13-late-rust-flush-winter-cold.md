Arkansas’s rust note has a sentence people treat as decoration. Defoliation may make the tree put on foliage late, and that growth goes into winter soft. Then cold has an easier job.

Zone **8a** is not a tropical fig joke. Average annual extreme in the teens. We still get nights that settle an argument with a variety that was bred for a milder parish. A rust year that strips in August and a warm October that feels like a gift can stack into a March dieback people will blame on “the virus” or “the beetles.”

The virus did not do that. The beetles did not do that. The tree believed a second spring.

I learned that on a stool I liked too much. Mid-September, arms like broomsticks. Mid-October, a lime flush that made a neighbor honk from the road. I waved like a fool. January gave us a night that felt personal. March I scraped bark with a thumbnail and found brown where I wanted green. I wanted a villain with a Latin name. I had a calendar.

## What I see after a strip

Bare arms in September. A flush of lime-green leaves in a warm spell. People cheer. The tree is “coming back.” The tree is spending sugar it should be putting in wood.

Walk it before you cheer. The late leaves are thinner. They sit on wood that never had time to brown. Fruit still hanging when the canopy walked off is not a second crop. It is a leftover.

UAEX’s clinic note — Smith and Pavel, still using the older *Physopella fici* hat — did not invent that sequence to scare you off figs. They wrote it so you would not treat rust as only a cosmetic. Defoliation in two to three ugly weeks is a factory walking off. A factory that tries to rebuild in October forgets winter is still on the payroll.

The orange-dust chapter lives next door. This page is the bill after the dust.

## A Saturday walk in a rust year

I take a paper sack and I start at the drip line, not the trunk. Leaves that rust-stripped in August are still a spore hotel if you left them as a blanket. I rake away from the wood. I bag. I do not compost that pile against the trunk and call it feeding the soil.

Then I look up. If the tree already flushed, I do not strip those baby leaves to “stop the second spring.” They are late. They are still a factory if a freeze holds off. Cutting them for neatness is a hobby. Leaving them and refusing to feed them is a program.

I check pots I can actually move. A rust-stripped pot that flushes in a warm week can ride a wagon into a shed when the forecast turns mean. An in-ground turkey cannot. I will not pretend a sheet of plastic and hope is the same as a wagon.

I write the date on a card in the greenhouse: *stripped / flushed / first hard night.* I used to keep that in my head. My head lies in March.

## What I will not do in October

I will not hit a rust-stripped tree with a nitrogen bump “to help it recover.” Recovery, in this climate, is lignified wood and a quiet top. Lawn fertilizer in October is how you buy a tender tip. The bag does not know it is October. You do.

I will not water like July if the dirt is already wet from fall rain. I will not drought-stress a tree that has no leaves, either. Roots still live. They still rot in a clay saucer. They still go thirsty in a sand pocket that never held a drink. Mulch still helps. The hose gets boring and correct. I water the dirt, not a midnight leaf I do not have.

I will not copper the new baby leaves as a personality. If you even have a legal copper use, the clinic window UAEX wrote was when first leaves are full size, then three or four weeks later. That is a spring sentence, and it is a historic one. October copper on a panic flush is how you burn the only green the tree has left.

LSU AgCenter Pub. 1802 (2025) said no fungicides currently labeled for figs in Louisiana. UF’s older edible-fig note said no EPA-approved fungicides in that Florida document. Those sentences can sit next to UAEX’s historic copper timing without me flattening them into a hose recipe. **[VERIFY]** your own state’s label before you buy a jug because a blog — including this one — mentioned copper.

I will not prune a second time “to shape the new growth.” Leave the leaves it made.

I will not wrap a sweating plastic bag around a flush and call it a greenhouse. Breathable cover, if I cover at all.

## What I will do

Rake the first drop so the fungus does not keep splashing a flush you did not want. Ferrin and Overstreet’s LSU rust note (*Cerotelium fici*, Pub. 3159) is a litter-and-spore story. Litter you leave is next year’s start, and it is also this October’s splash onto baby leaves.

Let the tree harden. In our other FigRoots winter pieces we talk about not pushing a fig in September if you still want the wood in March. Rust years need that sentence more, not less. No late nitrogen. No “one more gallon of fish emulsion because it looks sad.” Sad is allowed. Soft is expensive.

Watch the forecast like a person who has lost wood. If an arctic shot is real, the in-ground tool trees get whatever wrap or mulch pile I already believe in for 8a — breathable, not a plastic sweat lodge. Pots move. I will not write a full winter-wrap manual here. That file already exists in the cuttings magazine pack. I am only saying: **a rust year is a winter year**.

If the late flush dies, cut the dead after you can see living green in spring. Not in a January mood. Cold-injured wood and limb blight like a wet stub. Clean cuts later. Disinfectant on tools between a canker and a clean tree is ordinary hygiene. I will not name a household bleach ratio as if it were a pesticide label.

I walk again after the first real leaf-out. I scrape. I look for a live cambium line. I count trunks that still earn the hole. A short live stool is still a fig. A tall brown flagpole is firewood you have not admitted yet.

## Variety talk without a vaccine

Some LSU releases were bred with leaf disease in the conversation. LSU Gold has a **moderate** rust-resistance note in the breeding history page. Moderate is not a winter waiver. A resistant leaf that still drops in a monstrous year can still flush late if October is foolish.

LSU Purple’s pages talk foliage-disease resistance and also cold sensitivity — better thought of as a Gulf plant than a northern-Louisiana bet. Stack that with a rust strip and a second spring and you have written a short trunk in 8a without meaning to. I like Purple fruit. I do not like Purple as a north-wall houseplant after a defoliation year.

Celeste holds a lot of 8a yards because the wood and the eye make sense here. It still rusts some years. I do not upgrade any name to immune. Resistance is relative. “Holds leaves longer some years” is not “immune,” and it is not “skips winter.”

A dry August will make a proud owner. A steam-bath August plus a warm October plus a teen night will make a quiet one. Believe the second year more than the first tag.

## Pots, clay, and the lie of “I’ll just fertilize”

Pots I can walk to a wall. That is the only rust-year luxury I will brag on. In-ground clay that holds a lake in November is a different winter than a sand ridge that drains and then freezes dry. Neither one wants October nitrogen.

I have watched a person dump a “winterizer” lawn bag under a stripped fig because the bag said winter. Winterizer is a turf story. A fig that already spent its sugar on a panic flush does not need a second assignment.

If the tree is a pot and the mix is already loud with slow-release beads from July, I leave it. I do not top-dress a crown in a wet fall.

## The March argument I will not have with a virus

People want mosaic to take the blame for dead tips. Mosaic is a paint job. It does not freeze a late flush. People want beetles to take the blame because they remember the necklace. Beetles eat fruit. They do not write brown cambium.

If March looks rough, you are not starting a spray. You are counting live buds and deciding which trunks still earn a hole. You are not coppering a corpse. You are not oiling a virus. You are sawing what is dead after you can see what is not.

Rust is a leaf tax. 8a winter is a wood tax. Pay the leaf tax with a rake and do not volunteer for the wood tax with October feed. The second spring feels like hope. March is the receipt.
