Our own SEO pillar list asked for this page: black fig fly, what it is, what we do from Arkansas. Not a Facebook rumor. Not a claim that it is in the county.

I will say the hard sentence first, because search traffic likes a scare and I will not feed it.

**I will not say Black fig fly (*Silba adipata*) is established in South Arkansas.** I will not say it is in my parish. I will not say it is “probably here” because a photo in a group looked dark and winged. If that status changes, a dated agency page should say so. **[VERIFY]** APHIS, your state department of agriculture, and the county before you tattoo a Latin name on a neighbor.

*Silba adipata* is a fly that attacks figs in parts of the world and has shown up in US regulatory talk around California plantings. Larvae in fruit. Drop. A biosecurity object. I will not write a field key from memory as if I have it in a trap behind the shop. I will not draw a wing vein and pretend I keyed it. I will write the 8a reader version.

This page exists so a scared search lands on an honest first paragraph instead of a panic caption.

## What we already do that is the same advice

Do not import mystery California pots and dump the soil in the yard. We said that for nematodes. UF said inspect nursery roots and do not plant galled stock. [An Introduction to Figs](https://figroots.com/an-introduction-to-figs/) already said do not root in mystery local soil. Soil and fruit move more than stories. A marketplace listing with a pretty fruit photo is not a phytosanitary certificate.

Do not import mystery pots from anywhere you cannot name. California is the sentence people want because the press release was there. Arkansas is not a free-import zone for dirt, larvae, scale, or a virus a tag called “clean.” Buy wood from people who will still answer a message next year. Ask where the plant sat. Ask what is in the pot. If the answer is a shrug, the plant can stay on their bench.

Do not leave a rotting necklace. That is sap beetles today. Driedfruit beetles and vinegar flies we already have. They carry souring yeasts. UC IPM said sanitation, not a yeasticide. A rotting mattress under the tree is also how you fail to notice an *unusual* drop. If every soft fig hits the dirt, you will not see the firm one that should not have.

Pick. Bag wrecks. Keep the ground readable.

Do not mash every fly on a fig into a federal name. Green June is a chafer on a ripe shoulder. Vinegar flies are the sour-necklace crowd. Sap beetles go in the ostiole. Those we pick. Those we already wrote. A black fly in a group thread is not a diagnosis.

## What unusual drop means from this driveway

Ordinary 8a drop: birds at dawn on a dark ripe fig. Split after a rain week. Souring that smells like a brewery, pink syrup at the eye, fruit that went to vinegar on the tree. A drought then a flood. A name that always drops if you wait a day too long.

Unusual, for the purpose of this page: fruit coming off while it is still firm, inside mined or tunneled, and it does not match a peck, a split, or a sour necklace you can smell. More than a one-off. A pattern you cannot put in a bucket you already own.

I do not need you to be a taxonomist. I need you to stop guessing in public.

Photograph it. Whole tree, fruit on the ground, fruit opened, a coin for scale. The photo draft is the shot list. Do not filter it. Do not crop out the ground.

Bag fruit the clinic will accept. **[VERIFY]** their intake this week. Heat cooks a sample. Follow their shipping note.

Call a desk that can call a federal desk — county extension, the state department of agriculture, APHIS through the channel they publish. I will not print a phone number that will be stale next year. I will print the habit: **unusual drop goes to a weekday desk, not a group chat titled “it’s here.”**

Do not mail a pot of dirt across the country “for ID” to a hobbyist. Do not drive a tray of fruit to a swap. Do not pin a fly on a cork and declare a county record.

I will not tell you I have seen this fly in my trees. I have not made that claim. I will not tell you I have not, as if a personal absence were a survey. Absence in my pot block is not a state map.

## What I will not do

I will not write a backyard bait as a federal program. Lures, traps, and destruction orders belong to agencies when they belong to anyone. A blog recipe is how you attract a mess or waste a week.

I will not tell you to destroy the county’s figs. I will not tell you to cut your neighbor’s tree. I will not tell you to fog the parish.

I will not mash this fly with driedfruit beetle or vinegar fly. Those we have. Those we pick. Those are souring.

I will not mash it with fig endosepsis. Endosepsis is a caprifig / *Blastophaga* problem. Common figs in Arkansas are not waiting on a wasp. Do not import California orchard panic into a Celeste row.

I will not use this page to sell a trap.

I will not write “established in Arkansas” in a caption, a title, or a whisper. I will not write “not yet but soon” as if I had a model. I will not write “watch for it this August” as a countdown.

I will not ID a dark fly from your phone. I will tell you to bag the fruit and call a desk.

I will not pretend a tight-eye fig is a force field against a regulatory pest. Tight eye is a rain and souring sentence. It is not a biosecurity certificate.

## Why mystery pots are the sentence I will repeat

Root-knot already taught this county the expensive version. You bring a plant. You dump the mix. You plant the hole. Three years later the baby is galled and you want a drench. NC State’s honest line, as we use it on FigRoots, is that established infested figs do not get a homeowner chemical reset.

Scale, mites, and mosaic ride wood. We already said symptom-free is not virus-free. We already said do not write “virus-free guaranteed.”

A fly that lives in fruit rides fruit and, if someone is careless, the pot and the trash around the pot. I do not need a detection in Arkansas to tell you not to mail yourself a problem. That sentence was true before this page had a Latin name on it.

If you trade cuttings, trade cuttings. Clean wood. No mystery soil. No leftover fruit in the box. No “bonus pot” from a parking-lot meetup you cannot map.

If you buy a plant, buy a source. Keep the label. The photo of a tag is part of biosecurity when something goes wrong.

## What I do in 8a that is not a fly program

I pick the necklace I already have. I rake. I net the tree I want to eat from. I pot rare names. I do not fog ripe fruit. I do not write a statewide spray card.

I watch fruit the way this calendar already asked: June and July are beetle and bird weeks. A rain week is split and sour. Firm drop that does not fit those buckets gets a bag.

I do not add a new bottle because I learned a new Latin name on a Tuesday.

Rust will still be the loud August. Mosaic will still paint a leaf after a 98° week. Root-knot will still sit in old tomato sand. Those are the animals I live with. A federal name, if it ever becomes a dated local sentence, will not be managed from a Facebook ID or a homemade bait I wrote on a fig blog.

## Why the page exists anyway

Because a scared search will land here and I want the first sentence to be honest. A real pest. A real agency. A not-proven yard. The habit that already protects you from nematodes and scale — clean stock, dirty soil stays dirty, unusual things get a bag — is the habit that protects you from the next insect with a press release.

If you want drama, the rust will provide it in August. If you want a federal name, wait for a federal sentence. I will update this draft when a dated page makes me. Until then, pick the necklace you already have and do not mail a pot of dirt across a desert for fun.

I will not close this with a checklist that looks like a detection program. I will close it with the only 8a move I trust: **name the bucket you actually have. If you cannot, mail the county. Do not invent an establishment story so the search engine feels current.**
