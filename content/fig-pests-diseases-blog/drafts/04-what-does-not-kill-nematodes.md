Every few months somebody wants a natural eradicate. They have a knotted fig or a sand hole that used to be tomatoes, and they have a video. The video has cinnamon. Or molasses. Or a bleach math problem. Or a jug that says “nematode” the way a vitamin bottle says “immune.”

I watched one of those videos on a tailgate in August. The tree behind us had already folded at noon. The pot was still heavy. The man had a spice jar and a look that said he would rather buy a feeling than knock a spare plant. I get that look. I have worn it. I still will not write him a yes.

I am going to waste a whole draft on **no**, because the yes list is short and the no list is where people lose a year.

NC State’s fig-disease stance — Dave Ritchie, as we have been using it — is the spine: there is **no chemical control for an established infested fig** that belongs in a homeowner blog. LSU AgCenter’s 2025 note (Pub. 1802) says nematodes are hard to control and easier to prevent. UF/IFAS (Noling, ENY-055) says inspect roots and do not plant galls. Texas A&M’s older handbook mentioned pre-plant fumigation around living plants as a way to kill the fig. That sentence alone should retire the backyard chemistry kit.

## The spice rack

Cinnamon is a fungus rumor that wandered into nematode forums. I bake with it. I do not dose a 15-gallon pot with it and call the galls evicted. There is no extension fig SOP that starts with a spice jar.

Garlic, clove oil, thyme oil, “essential oil blend” — same aisle, same wish. A lab can kill a juvenile in a dish with all sorts of things. Your yard is not a dish. The eggs are in the root and the soil. A smell is not a program.

Molasses as a soil drench is a story about feeding microbes so they outcompete the bad worms. Maybe a living soil is a better place for a tree to *tolerate* pressure. That is not the same sentence as “molasses killed my root-knot.” I will not write the second sentence.

I have had people add molasses, then add more, then decide the tree “looked greener.” August rain will green a lot of things for a week. Greener is not a count.

## The laundry room

Bleach in a hole. Hydrogen peroxide “to oxygenate and kill.” Dish soap “to suffocate them.” These are how you injure the fig and the things in the soil you actually wanted, while the population that lives *inside* the root waits you out.

I wash a pot or a pair of pruners. I do not irrigate a living tree with a disinfectant fantasy.

Ammonia, used motor oil, salt — I have heard all three on this county’s porches. Salt will help you finish the tree. Oil is a pollution story. Neither is a fig practice.

A neighbor once asked if a hot-water dump from a kettle would “cook the worms” the way a kitchen cooks a pot of beans. 8a August will heat the top inch of sand on its own. The female is inside the root. You will cook the feeder before you evict her.

## The garden-center jug

There are real nematicides in the world. They live behind licenses, setbacks, and labels that change. They are not a weekend figroots.com recipe. I will not name a product as if I am your applicator. I will not copy Vapam out of a handbook that also says do not use it around living plants.

“Beneficial nematodes” in a refrigerator box are a different animal — insect-attacking species sold for grub stories. People hear “nematode” and pour them on a fig as if they eat *Meloidogyne*. That is a language trick. **[VERIFY]** the species on the box if you buy them for turf grubs. Do not buy them as a fig root-knot cure. I have not seen an extension fig chapter that says they will.

Neem drenches show up in papers, including a seedling nano-emulsion study that looked tidy in a greenhouse. A greenhouse seedling trial is not your 8a sand under a ten-year Celeste. I will not turn that PDF into a hose schedule.

Castor oil, “organic nematicide” teas, a weekend bio-drench with a before-and-after that is two photographs in different light — same wish in a nicer bottle. If the only evidence is a marketplace listing or an unsourced thread, it does not get a sentence that sounds like a program.

## The cover-crop fairy tale

Marigolds (*Tagetes*) have a real literature on some *Meloidogyne* populations in some rotations. Mustard and sorghum-sudangrass have solarization-adjacent stories. None of that is “plant a ring of marigolds around the fig and the galls leave.” A fig is a long-lived woody plant. A six-week cover in a vegetable bed is a different clock.

If you want to rest a future fig hole, talk to the county about a real rotation and a sample. Do not decorate a dying tree with French marigolds and call it IPM.

Soil solarization — clear plastic, midsummer, moist soil, weeks of heat — can reduce numbers in a *bare* bed in a hot climate. It is a pre-plant tactic, not something you do over a living fig. It is also uneven, and 8a rain will laugh at a loose tarp. I have seen a sheet balloon in a Thursday storm and then lie in a puddle that cooled the dirt it was supposed to cook. I would rather pot the rare names than bet a collection on a plastic sheet I did not babysit.

I will not write a solarization recipe with hours and soil temperatures as if I ran a trial. I did not.

## What prevention actually is

Prevention is boring, which is why the aisle hates it.

Plant clean roots. If the nursery plant is beaded, it does not get a hole. Noling said that in Florida English. I am saying it in South Arkansas English.

Do not use last year’s tomato sand. LSU listed tomato, okra, and tobacco for a reason.

Do not move dirty soil on a shovel from a knotted plant to a clean pot.

Do not root cuttings in a bucket of yard dirt. [An Introduction to Figs](https://figroots.com/an-introduction-to-figs/) already spent that sentence. People still fill the overflow tray from the garden because mix costs money. The stick costs more.

Keep a living in-ground tree from stacking drought on top of galls. Water. Mulch. Do not cultivate a dust bowl under it.

Put names you cannot replace in mix you mixed. [Pick The Right Fig](https://figroots.com/2025/07/02/pick-the-right-fig-for-you/) said pots. I am not going to invent a more exciting sentence.

High organic matter, in LSU’s wording, can reduce root-knot *pressure*. Compost is not a fumigant. It is a better room to live in. I will add it to a bed I am building. I will not promise a count went to zero.

## What I will not write even if you ask twice

I will not give you a bleach dilution. I will not give you a cinnamon cup-per-gallon. I will not name a licensed nematicide as “what we use.” I will not tell you a variety is resistant because a listing needed a word. I will not use *eradicate* as a promise. The only honest place for that word is the refusal.

8a heat makes people desperate in the same week the tree is already telling the truth. Desperate is when the aisle does its best work. Walk past it. Knock a tool plant in the cool months. Ask the county if they still run an assay. **[VERIFY]** that desk this year. Then decide who gets the hole.

## The sentence I want on the shed wall

**You will not eradicate root-knot from a Southern fig hole with a weekend product.** You will decide which plants live in dirt you do not trust, and which plants do not.

If someone sells you eradicate, they are selling you a feeling. Keep the money for pots and mix. The worms will still be in the sand. Your rare stick does not have to join them.
