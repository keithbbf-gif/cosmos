I marked two baselines on the same end. That is the whole trick, and it is also the whole way to ruin a thick corner. The outer line was the usual tail length. The inner line was a second, shorter set of pins sitting in the meat you usually leave as a single socket. Houndstooth. Double dovetail. The names wander. The work does not: you are cutting two rows of teeth in one corner because the stock is thick enough to hold them and the load is rude enough to ask.

This is not a blanket-chest through-dovetail with extra pins for prettiness. Extra pins on a 3/4-inch board are just more sawing. Houndstooth wants 4/4 and change, or a laminated corner, or a tool-chest wall that is really a small beam. If you cut it in thin stock you have made lace. Lace does not carry a chest full of iron.

## Why a second row exists

A single row of through-dovetails resists being pulled apart along the tail axis. It is a famous joint because it is also a complete joint for most furniture. A second row, offset, adds glue surface and a second set of ramps deeper in the corner. On a heavy carcase — a tool chest, a blanket chest in thick oak, a chest-on-chest corner that will be racked when two people lift it — the inner teeth take some of the peel that the outer row would otherwise take alone.

There is also the look. I will not pretend the look is accidental. Houndstooth photographs. People who know, know. People who do not know still see that the corner is not a factory pin. Vanity is allowed if the thickness was already there. Vanity is not a reason to glue two boards into a fake thickness so you can cut a party joint.

## Layout without getting lost

I draw the outer tails first, the way I would on any chest. Then I mark the inner pins so they sit in the *spaces* of the outer row — the houndstooth stagger — not stacked like a ladder. Stacked is a different joint and a weaker picture. Staggered is the tooth.

The inner baseline is shorter. If you make it the same length as the outer, you have cut through-dovetails twice and you have no wall left at the inside arris. Leave meat. The inside of a chest still gets a finger.

Transfer carefully. A mistake on the inner row is a hole in a pin you cannot hide. I use a knife, not a pencil, on the inner work. Pencil is a blur at that scale.

Pins first or tails first: I still prefer tails first on the outer row, then I use the outer tails as a story for the inner pins. Some people cut the whole pin board as a stack of pins at two depths. If that keeps you from getting lost, do it. Getting lost is the failure mode. I have chopped the wrong pocket and turned a houndstooth into a gap with a name.

## Chopping two depths

You will want to rush the inner waste because it is small and dark. Do not. The inner sockets are stopped or they are through at a different length; either way the chisel is working in a hole that already has an outer wall you like. A slip here blows the outer pin from behind. Support the outer teeth. Chop down, not out.

A coping saw is less useful on the inner row. There is not always a path. I drill a small stopped hole when the geometry allows, then pare to the line. The drill is not elegant. The blown corner is less elegant.

## Dry-fit is two conversations

The outer row will seat and look like a finished chest corner while the inner row is still fat. You will be tempted to call it good. Tap the inside. If the inner pins are proud, the outer shoulders will open later when humidity moves and the inner row finally seats. Pare the inner cheeks. Do not sand them. Sanding the inner row is how you get a sloppy tooth that only shows when the lid is up and a flashlight is involved.

Glue: I still use hide when I can, because a houndstooth with a stuck inner row is a box you will not get apart without damage. Size the end grain. Work one corner at a time if the chest is large. Four houndstooth corners in one PVA open time is a sport I do not play in July in Arkansas.

## Where I will cut it

A tool chest in thick pine or oak, if the wall is really thick. A document chest that wants to look like it came from a shop that had time. A sample corner I keep on the shelf to show why I usually say no.

I will not cut it on a drawer. Drawers are half-blind for a reason; the front is a show face and the side is a runner. Two rows in a drawer side is jewelry on a work boot.

I will not cut it as a “brand signature.” Signatures that take three extra hours per corner belong on pieces that pay for three extra hours. The rest of the time a clean single row, well chopped, is the more serious flex.

## Failures

I staggered wrong on one practice corner and the inner pins lined up under the outer pins. It looked like a thick through-dovetail with extra saw marks. I kept it as a warning. The stagger is the word *houndstooth*. Without it you just have two depths and no pattern.

I also once left the inner baseline too close to the outer and the wall between rows became a veneer. It cracked on the dry-fit. Thickness is not a mood. If the board is 7/8, you may not have two honest rows. Say so and cut one.

## Thickness I will not fake

If the board is 7/8, I measure the two baselines on a scrap of the same thickness before I commit a chest corner. If the wall between rows is paper, I cut one row. A fake second row — a shallow nick that photographs as houndstooth — is a caption I will not write.

Clamp time on four thick corners in July is a relay. I still glue two, then two. PVA is a sprint I will not run around a 4-foot box with inner teeth I cannot see seating.

## Inner row, last look

I dry-fit until the outer teeth look finished, then I tap the inside. If the inner row is proud, I pare those cheeks and I do not call the corner done from the sofa view. The sofa view is how inner rows get left fat.

<!-- PACK-W2L -->

## Shop morning, thick corner

Scrap of the same thickness. Two baselines. Stagger, not a ladder. Wall between rows is meat, not paper. Outer tails, then inner. Chop the inner like veneer work. Glue two corners, then two, in July. Tap the inside before I like the sofa view.

## The judgment

Two rows of teeth are a thick-corner joint. They are not a way to make thin work look expensive. If the stock can hold a second baseline without turning the wall into paper, and the piece will be lifted, racked, or shown with the lid open, cut them. If you are cutting them so the photograph has more triangles, put the saw down and go flatten a top. The photograph will still be there when the corner is actually a corner.
