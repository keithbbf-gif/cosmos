The stool was four legs, two stretchers, a seat, and a handful of pins, and it fit in a canvas bag I would not be ashamed to put in a truck. Assembled, it had to take a hip. That is the test. A knockdown stool that only holds a coat is a coat rack. I sat on it in the shop before I liked it. The first version racked. The pins were pretty brass and they bent. I replaced them with steel that looked less like jewelry and more like a chair.

This is travel furniture without the chest. Same culture: nothing proud that will catch, joints you can do again, parts you can replace.

## Pins that are pins

Through-pins, tusked stretchers at this small scale, or a bolt with a captured nut. I like a through-pin with a little wedge or a cotter you can pull. Brass is fine if the section is serious. Brass that is a nail is a bent nail.

The seat can be a slab with four housings, or a piece of canvas, or leather. A slab seat wants to locate on the legs so it cannot skate. A canvas seat is a sling; the frame then is the whole chair and must not rack. Sling stools fail at the frame, not at the cloth.

## The pairing jig that makes holes agree

I built a scrap fence for the drill press — two V-blocks screwed to a plywood saddle, a stop at the far end, a clamp that holds a pair of legs as if they were one round. The first stool I drilled freehand on the second pair after the first pair had lived on the press. Those four holes did not live in one world. Two pins drove like furniture. Two rattled, then ovaled the first time I twisted on the seat. The pairing jig is not pretty. It lives under the bench. It is the joint.

I bore through both legs of a pair in one setup, then I flip the saddle and I do the other pair. Stretchers get their holes from the same bit, same depth stop, with the stretcher clamped in a wooden handscrew so the bit cannot walk off a round. A handscrew is slower than a quick-grip and it does not chew the arris I will later ease.

The pins themselves I size against a scrap I bored with that same bit — a pin-gauge block with two holes labeled fat and finish. If a pin will not enter the finish hole with a tap, it is not a pin yet. I do not take a caliper to a driveway. I take the block, or I leave the pins already fitted in a cloth roll.

## Mark the parts

I letter the joints. A knockdown you cannot reassemble without a drawing is a puzzle, not a stool. People will lose the drawing. The parts should only go together one way, or they should be identical in a way that any way is the way. I prefer lettering. I have also made four identical stretchers and been grateful.

Pencil on oil finish rubs off in a bag. I stamp a letter on the inside of each housing with a small set, or I cut a chisel nick that only fits one orientation. The nick is uglier than a stamp and it survives a truck.

## Failures

Pretty bent brass. Steel. Hip test.

I also made a stool whose stretchers were all different lengths by a 16th because I cut them “to the drawing” after the legs had already been shaved. Cut stretchers to the legs in front of you. Sequence.

A bag of parts with no spare pin: the pin that falls in the gravel is the pin you needed. Pack a spare. That is part of the joint.

The sling failure was quieter. I stapled a canvas seat too short, thinking a tight sling was a taut chair. The rails pulled into a diamond the first time a hip went in the middle. The pins were innocent. The cloth was a clamp I had not admitted. I recut the canvas with slack enough that the frame stayed a rectangle and the sit still had a belly. A sling is not a drum.

## A hip test that is not a joke

I sit on the stool in the shop, both sit-bones, then I lean to one corner, then I twist. A chair that only holds a centered polite sit is a photo stool. The pins will tell you first. If a pin bends, the stretcher will oval. If a stretcher loosens, the seat will rack. I mark the pin that complained and I change the section, not the wood species, unless the species was the problem.

A canvas sling wants the rails to stay a rectangle. I treat those rails like a chair’s side: real tenons or real pins, not a screw in the end of a dowel. A slab seat wants housings that locate the legs so the slab cannot walk. I cut the housings after the legs are the legs I will ship, not after the drawing of the legs I hoped to have.

I weigh the bag after the hip test, not before. A travel stool that is a travel anvil is a design I will change — thinner slab, or canvas, or fewer show pins. Light is not an excuse for a bent pin. I have taken a sixteenth off a slab underside with a jack plane and kept the housings. I have also switched a slab to canvas because the bag argued louder than the photograph.

## Packing is a joint

The bag or the trunk is part of the design. Pins in a small cloth roll, stretchers nested against the seat, legs in a pair. If a pin can fall into a seam of the bag, it will. I sew a pocket — a real stitched pocket on the seat underside or on the bag wall — or I am lying about the spare. Tape and a note are a spare that becomes litter. People lose notes.

I shake the bag before I close it. A rattle that is not the spare in its pocket is a pin that left. I fix the pocket. I do not trust a zipper to be a pocket.

I do not pack a stool with a wet finish. Knockdown parts finish-rub each other in a bag. I let the finish go quiet, then I pad the show faces with a scrap of the same canvas. A campaign stool with a rubbed-raw arris is a stool that traveled without a pad. South Arkansas heat in a closed truck will finish the rub for you if you packed impatient.

## Species and pins

Hickory pins in a softer seat housing will oval the housing. Oak pins in oak are the usual. Steel pins in a painted stool are honest and they will stain a wet rag if you leave them wet. I oil steel or I use a pin that was meant to live outside, and I still wipe it.

Brass of a serious diameter is fine on a show pin. Brass of a nail diameter is a souvenir of a hip. I have a bent one in a jar. It is not a mascot. It is a measurement I failed.

Pecan stretchers I have used because the rack had them. They take a pin cleanly. They also dent if the bag is the only pad. I wrap pecan show faces. Oak will forgive a neighbor stretcher. Pecan remembers.

## June swell and a hot truck

A bag in a hot truck will cook hide if you used hide on a tenon you also pinned. I keep knockdown stool joints dry-pinned or bolted so the heat is not a glue test. If I glue a tenon on a “travel” stool, I have made a travel stool that does not travel at the joint, only at the bag. Be honest which parts open.

June in this shop is a different pin story. Oak stretchers that took a steel pin with two fingers in January can want a tap in a wet week. I ease the holes a hair on a travel stool I know will see a porch in Bradley County humidity, and I keep the pins a hair under the winter fit. A bind in June is a pin you will beat with a rock in a driveway. I would rather hear a tiny rattle in January that oil will quiet than oval a hole on a landing.

The hallway changes this joint when you assemble the stool *before* the next flight, because you needed a place to sit a box. If the pins only go in on the bench, with a dead-blow and a good light, they will not go in on a stair with a bag in your teeth. I assemble once on the concrete outside the glass wall, standing, no vise. If I need the vise, I recut the pins.

## What I want from D:\BBF

The pile, lettered, on a blanket — legs paired, stretchers nested, pins in the cloth roll, the spare pocket turned out so you can see it is a pocket. The assembled stool with a person actually sitting — Jack in a build photo is allowed if he sat; a posed empty stool is a catalog. The bent brass pin next to the steel that replaced it, if that frame exists. The pairing jig on the press with two legs in the V-blocks, if anyone shot the boring. Do not invent the frame. Do not invent a filename.

## The judgment

A stool that fits in a trunk is a chair you can do twice, standing up, in a driveway, with a tap. Pins that do not bend, holes that were bored as a pair, parts that know their letters, a hip test before the bag. Campaign is not a finish color. It is a pile that becomes furniture without a second shop.
