The rail was longer than the shop was honest about. I could have joined two boards with a butt and a pair of dowels and called it a day. I cut a scarf instead — a long slope, two faces that wanted to be one beam — and I dry-fit it on the bench until the line was a hair I could live with. A scarf is how you make length without pretending a butt joint is a beam. It can also be how you pack a beam in two pieces if you key it.

I will not put on a kanawa-tsugi costume and pretend this shop is a temple carpenter. Those joints are real and they are a life. This is a furniture scarf: a slope, a key or a pair of bolts if it must travel, glue if it must stay.

## Slope is structure

A short, steep scarf is a butt with a bevel. I want length in the glue face — several times the thickness, not a polite 45. The faces have to be flat to each other. I plane them as a pair, like a sprung joint that learned trigonometry. Light under a straightedge. No hollow in the middle that the clamp will “take care of.” The clamp will take care of making a bent rail.

Grain: both halves should agree. A scarf between a wild board and a quiet board is a hinge at the line.

## Knockdown or beam

**Glued beam.** Clamp, cauls, time. This is restoration, or a long apron you could not buy in one length, or a repair in a house that will not move the rail again.

**Keyed scarf.** A sliding key or a pair of wedges across the slope. You can drive them out. This is closer to the temple family and also closer to a tusk. Fitting is everything. A loose key is a rattle. A fat key is a split.

**Bolted scarf.** Two faces, two bolts, captured nuts, maybe a plate. Honest. Ugly if you leave it on the show. I hide it on the back of a rail or under a top. This is the one I will actually ship.

## Failures

Steep scarf, failed as a butt. Recut longer.

I also glued a scarf and then put a mortise through the glue line. The mortise walls were half-and-half. They crumbled. Keep joinery out of the splice, or splice where the mortise is not.

A bolted scarf with round holes and no slots in a rail that still moved across its width: the bolts became oval makers. If the rail is wide, think like a breadboard.

## A long apron that was two boards

I needed an apron longer than a honest board I had in the rack. I scarfed two pieces of the same oak, same ripping, grain agreeing, slope about 8:1, [VERIFY] before anyone prints the ratio as law. I planed the faces as a pair, glued, cauls, overnight. Then I cut the tenons on the ends as if it had always been one rail. The scarf sits under the top, in the dark. That is a beam scarf. It does not travel.

If that apron had to ship in a short box, I would have bolted the scarf with two captured nuts and a backer plate, and I would have put the bolts where a hand can reach after the top is off. I would not have glued it and then pretended it was KD.

## Restoration

A broken rail in a house: a scarf is how you keep the old wood and add a length. The old wood may be a different moisture than the new. I meter both. I do not scarf a wet antique to a kiln-dried stick and hope. The line will open. Wait, or pick a mechanical scarf.

## What I want from D:\BBF

The dry-fit slope, light under a straightedge or the absence of light. A bolted scarf from the back. Do not photograph a 45 and call it a scarf.

## Keep mortises off the line

I already split a mortise that crossed a scarf. I will write it again. The splice is a beam. The mortise is a hole. Holes in splices are committee walls. Committee walls crumble. Move the joint or move the mortise.

## Plane the pair

I plane the two scarf faces as a pair, like a sprung joint that learned a slope. Light under a straightedge. No hollow the clamp will “take care of.” The clamp will take care of making a bent rail. Keep mortises off the line.

## Shop morning, long apron

I pick two pieces from the same ripping. I mark the slope with a stick, not a degree I liked on the internet. I saw fat. I plane as a pair until the dry-fit is a hair. I glue with cauls that keep the rail straight. I leave it alone longer than I want. Then I treat it as one board: face marks, tenons, the rest of the table. If this rail has to ship short, I stop before the glue and I bolt the scarf with nuts I can reach after the top is off. I do not do both and then forget which religion I chose.

## Slope as meat

A 45 is a butt in a costume. I want length in the glue face. I plane the pair. I keep mortises off the line. If the rail ships short, I bolt and I can reach the nuts. I do not glue and bolt and then forget which one is the religion.

I will not scarf a wet antique to a kiln-dried stick because the house wanted the rail back this week. Meter both. Wait, or go mechanical.

<!-- PACK-W4 -->

## Cauls that stay straight

I clamp a scarf with cauls that are straighter than my hope. A bowed caul makes a bowed rail. I check the rail on the bench before I cut tenons. A banana apron is a tenon that points at two different legs.

<!-- PACK-W5 -->

I mark the scarf “BEAM” or “BOLT” on the back so a future me does not mortise through a glue line I forgot. Future me is the person who will ruin a good splice. The mark is ugly and it is in the dark. The mortise stays off the line.

<!-- PACK-W6 -->
I keep the slope stick with the rail until the tenons are cut so I do not flatten a face I already trusted. A second planing “to clean it up” is how a scarf becomes a butt. The stick is the slope. The plane stops when the stick says so.

<!-- PACK-W7 -->
I do not put a finish on the scarf faces before I decide beam or bolt. Finish on a glue face is a butt joint with oil in it. Oil is not glue.

## The judgment

A scarf that carries a rail is a long-grain splice with a slope you can defend. Glue it to stay. Key or bolt it to travel. Do not dress a butt joint in a 45 and call it a scarf. The name is for a beam. The hallway does not care about the name. It cares whether the rail is one piece or two when it has to go up the stair.
