The rail was longer than the shop was honest about. I could have joined two boards with a butt and a pair of dowels and called it a day. I cut a scarf instead — a long slope, two faces that wanted to be one beam — and I dry-fit it on the bench until the line was a hair I could live with. A scarf is how you make length without pretending a butt joint is a beam. It can also be how you pack a beam in two pieces if you key it.

I will not put on a kanawa-tsugi costume and pretend this shop is a temple carpenter. Those joints are real and they are a life. This is a furniture scarf: a slope, a key or a pair of bolts if it must travel, glue if it must stay.

## Slope is structure

A short, steep scarf is a butt with a bevel. I want length in the glue face — several times the thickness, not a polite 45. The faces have to be flat to each other. I plane them as a pair, like a sprung joint that learned trigonometry. Light under a straightedge. No hollow in the middle that the clamp will “take care of.” The clamp will take care of making a bent rail.

Grain: both halves should agree. A scarf between a wild board and a quiet board is a hinge at the line.

## The slope stick and the pair of cauls

I mark the slope with a stick, not a degree I liked on a screen. For an apron I have used about 8:1. [VERIFY] before anyone prints the ratio as law. I saw fat. I plane the two faces as a pair — both halves in the vise as one thick board if I can, or one face then the mate registered to a fence I already trusted. The slope stick stays with the rail until the tenons are cut. A second planing “to clean it up” is how a scarf becomes a butt. The plane stops when the stick says so.

Cauls I keep for scarfs are straighter than my hope: two hardwood battens I checked on the jointer table, papered so they do not glue themselves to the rail. A bowed caul makes a bowed rail. I check the rail on the bench before I cut tenons. A banana apron is a tenon that points at two different legs.

I clamp in a sequence from the middle of the slope out so I do not trap a hollow. Hide or PVA is a religion I pick before the faces see finish. Finish on a glue face is a butt joint with oil in it. Oil is not glue.

I mark the scarf BEAM or BOLT on the back so a future me does not mortise through a glue line I forgot. Future me is the person who will ruin a good splice. The mark is ugly and it is in the dark.

## Knockdown or beam

**Glued beam.** Clamp, cauls, time. This is restoration, or a long apron you could not buy in one length, or a repair in a house that will not move the rail again.

**Keyed scarf.** A sliding key or a pair of wedges across the slope. You can drive them out. This is closer to the temple family and also closer to a tusk. Fitting is everything. A loose key is a rattle. A fat key is a split.

**Bolted scarf.** Two faces, two bolts, captured nuts, maybe a plate. Honest. Ugly if you leave it on the show. I hide it on the back of a rail or under a top. This is the one I will actually ship. I put the bolts where a hand can reach after the top is off. I do not glue it and then pretend it was KD.

One religion. I do not glue and bolt and then forget which one I chose.

## Failures

Steep scarf, failed as a butt. Recut longer.

I also glued a scarf and then put a mortise through the glue line. The mortise walls were half-and-half. They crumbled. Keep joinery out of the splice, or splice where the mortise is not.

A bolted scarf with round holes and no slots in a rail that still moved across its width: the bolts became oval makers. If the rail is wide, think like a breadboard.

The 45 I cut because I was in a hurry is the one that opened in the first season. It photographed as a scarf. It behaved as a butt. I recut the same two boards longer and I lost length I had already promised a tenon. Sequence: slope first, then the ends, then the joinery that is not the splice.

## A long apron that was two boards

I needed an apron longer than an honest board I had in the rack. I scarfed two pieces of the same oak, same ripping, grain agreeing. I planed the faces as a pair, glued, cauls, overnight. Then I cut the tenons on the ends as if it had always been one rail. The scarf sits under the top, in the dark. That is a beam scarf. It does not travel.

If that apron had to ship in a short box, I would have bolted the scarf with two captured nuts and a backer plate. I would not have glued it and then pretended it was KD.

## Restoration

A broken rail in a house: a scarf is how you keep the old wood and add a length. The old wood may be a different moisture than the new. I meter both. I cut the antique back to sound wood — past the split, past the oxidized end — and I plane that old face on a shooting board with a tall fence so I am not asking a warped rail to register on the bench. Then I wait until the two meters agree, or I go mechanical. A house that wants the rail back this week gets bolts, not a hungry glue line.

## Humidity, a short box, and a stair

A glued beam scarf that lives in South Arkansas will still move across its width. If the rail is wide enough to fight, I treat the bolts — when I have them — like fasteners in slots, not like pins in round holes. A narrow apron is less of a fight. A wide glue-up pretending to be a breadboard is a fight I already lost once.

The hallway changes this joint when the rail is longer than the turn. A beam scarf is one piece on the landing. A bolted scarf is two pieces and a plate you can start on a stair. I have carried a long oak apron up a farmhouse stair that had a wind at the top. The rail that was one piece scored the plaster. The next long rail I built as a bolted scarf I could assemble in the room. The house did not care about the name. It cared whether the rail was one piece or two when it had to go up the stair.

June heat under the glass wall will flash hide if I rush a beam scarf. I leave it longer than I want. A hungry clamp-up is a line that opens in August.

## What I want from D:\BBF

The dry-fit slope, light under a straightedge or the absence of light. A bolted scarf from the back, nuts you can reach. The slope stick lying on the rail. The pair of cauls, if anyone shot the glue-up. Do not photograph a 45 and call it a scarf. Do not invent the frame.

## The judgment

A scarf that carries a rail is a long-grain splice with a slope you can defend. Glue it to stay. Key or bolt it to travel. Do not dress a butt joint in a 45 and call it a scarf. The name is for a beam. Keep the mortise off the line. The hallway does not care about the name. It cares whether the rail is one piece or two when it has to go up the stair.
