The wedge disappeared. That is the point and the danger. I had a stopped mortise in a chair leg, a tenon with a kerf, a little wedge standing in the kerf, and one chance to drive the rail home so the wedge hit the bottom of the mortise and spread the tenon like a fist. If the wedge was long, the shoulder would not shut. If the wedge was short, the fist would not clench. I would not know which until the glue had grabbed. That is why this joint is not a knockdown. It is the opposite. I put it in this pack because it shows up on splayed chairs next to joints that *do* travel, and people confuse the two.

The craft pack showed a wedge you can see. This is the cousin you bury. Fox is stay. Tusk is go. Mixing the names is how a chair arrives in a crate that will not open without a saw.

## Measure the mortise like it is a well

Depth of the mortise, length of the tenon, length of the wedge, depth of the kerf. I cut the tenon shy of the mortise bottom by the amount the wedge needs to do work, and I cut the wedge so it will hit the bottom *before* the tenon does. The kerf stops before the shoulder so I do not split the rail back into the show.

Dry-fit without the wedge until the shoulder shuts. Then I mark, then I cut the fox, then I glue and I go. Hide is kind if I have to get back in, and getting back in is a saw through a chair rail. Plan the first time.

## The depth stick that hangs on the chisel

I mark a stick from the mortise depth and I hang that stick on the mortise chisel with a bit of string. A remembered depth is a long fox. A long fox is an open shoulder. I do not remember wells.

I plane the fox on a small shooting board — the same low-angle habit I use on a drawbore pin, a different taper. The fox wants a short, honest wedge, same species as the tenon unless I want a tell I will never see. A hard fox in a soft tenon splits the tenon. A soft fox in a hard tenon folds. Pair them. Then bury them on purpose.

The kerf I cut with a thin saw against a stop-block clamped to the tenon, so the kerf dies before the shoulder by a mark I made with a gauge. Freehand kerfs wander into the show. I have a rail with a hairline that started as a kerf I “knew” was short enough. I did not know. The block knows.

I keep the dry-fit rail on the bench until the fox is cut so I cannot lose the shut shoulder in my head. Head-shut is how moons are born in stopped holes.

A clamp across the chair’s posts is not optional at glue time. The fox will try to push the rail back out as it hits. I have a pair of handscrews that only do this job on chair glue-ups — they live on the chair bench, not in the general clamp pile, because I will “forget” them when the hide is already open. The handscrews hold the shoulder where the dry-fit left it while the fist clenches inside.

## Splay makes it meaner

On a splayed leg the tenon is already a compound. The fox does not cancel that. It adds a one-way clamp inside a hole that is not square to the room. I still scribe the shoulder. I still dry-fit without the wedge. The fox is the last act on a joint that was already honest.

The tenon on a splay is already a parallelogram in the mortise if you bored square to the floor. I bore square to the shoulder’s world, or I accept the parallelogram and I still fox along the tenon’s axis, not along the floor’s. A fox driven to the floor’s idea of down is a fox that will split a cheek.

I will not fox-wedge a knockdown. People ask. The answer is no. Use a tusk, a bolt, a pin you can drive back.

## Failures

Long wedge, open shoulder, pretty chair with a moon. I cut the rail out and I started over. There is no sneaking a buried wedge.

I also kerfed through to the shoulder. The rail split back to the show when the fox did its job. Stop the kerf.

A double fox (two wedges) in a wide tenon: possible, and a good way to split a tenon into three. I have. I now use one fox, or I use a visible pair of wedges on a through-tenon instead.

The skinny-rail fox is the one I should not have tried. The tenon had enough width to look like a tenon and not enough to take a kerf and still be a beam. It clenched and it split along the cheek into the show. I sawed the rail out. I loose-tenoned a new one and I left the fox in the jar.

## A chair I would still fox

A side chair with a stopped front-rail tenon into a square post, people who will lean back, a shop that will not see the chair again. I will fox that rail if the tenon is wide enough to take a kerf and still be a tenon. I will not fox a skinny rail. I will not fox a rail I might have to shorten later.

The dry-fit without the wedge is the whole rehearsal. Shoulder shut, cheeks a tap, mortise deep enough. Then I cut the fox to the stick. Then glue, then home, then I do not unclasp my jaw until the shoulder is where I left it on the dry-fit.

I say out loud, before I glue, that this rail will not come apart. If I cannot say that, I pick a tusk. The sentence in the air is cheaper than a saw through a chair.

## Hide versus a buried mistake

Hide gives you a theoretical second chance. The second chance is still a saw through a rail. I talk about hide as kindness, not as a time machine. PVA on a fox-wedge is a one-way door with a shorter hallway. I use hide when I can because I am superstitious about buried wedges, and superstition here is just memory.

South Arkansas June will grab hide faster than a winter morning under the same glass wall. I do not fox a chair when the hide is already skinning in the pot. I glue when I can still sneak a wipe, knowing sneak does not include pulling the rail.

## The hallway test

If the piece has to go up a stair in parts, this is the wrong wedge. A fox-wedged chair is a chair you carry as a chair. A tusked trestle is a trestle you carry as sticks. I have watched a crate get opened on a landing with a hope that the front rail would “just knock down.” It would not. The saw that would have opened it was in the shop. Fox is stay. Tusk is go. I will keep the names apart.

A humid landing does not change a buried fox. That is the point. The joint does not care about the stair. The stair cares about the joint you picked. Pick the one that matches the carry.

I do not need a cutaway of a finished fox unless we actually cut one for teaching. Do not invent a cutaway. Do not invent the frame.

## The judgment

A wedge you cannot see is a one-way swell in a stopped hole. Measure the well, dry-fit without the fox, then commit. It is not travel joinery. It is the opposite of travel. Fox is stay. Tusk is go. If the piece has to come apart, pick a tusk. If the piece has to stay a chair when the glue is tired, the fox is a fist you buried on purpose.
