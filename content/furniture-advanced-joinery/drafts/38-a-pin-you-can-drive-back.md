The pin moved. I had a punch on the exit side and a backer on the show, and the oak peg came out in two pieces because I had glued it “for insurance” five years earlier. Insurance is how a knockdown becomes a demolition. A drawbore that is supposed to travel is a dry pin in an offset hole, proud enough to hit, ugly enough to admit it might leave.

The craft pack already taught drawbore-then-drive for joints that stay. This is the reversible cousin: trestle stretchers, a bed rail you do not want to bolt, a knock-down bench. The offset still cams the shoulder home. The glue stays in the tenon cheeks if you want it — or it stays out if the whole frame must open. The pin itself stays dry. That is the sentence this joint will not survive if you break it.

## Offset without romance

I bore the mortise walls first, assemble dry, mark the tenon through those holes, then bore the tenon a little closer to the shoulder. How little: enough to pull, not enough to split. I sneak on a scrap. [VERIFY] any printed offset.

The pin is straight-grained, a little tapered if I am kind, or a steel pin if the piece will be taken apart often. Oak pins in oak are traditional and they can weld themselves with time and humidity even without glue. A little wax helps them remain pins.

## The taper board and the punch that lives with the spare

I taper oak pins on a shooting board I keep only for this — a low-angle block plane, a stop, a shallow cradle so the pin cannot roll while I take a whisper off the leading third. A pencil-sharpener pin is a toy. A pin that is tapered the whole length will wedge and then refuse the punch. I want a short lead and a straight body that still fills the hole the second time.

Steel pins I buy as rod and I file a tiny chamfer. I do not taper steel the way I taper oak. Steel will oval if the hole wandered; oak will sneak. Bore true for steel.

The punch is a real pin punch, not a nail, and it lives in a cloth roll with a hardwood backer I drilled a clearance hole through. The hole in the backer is the point. A scrap you hold “somewhere near” the exit is how you blow a show face when the punch walks. I have a dented rail that teaches this. I used a finishing nail because the punch was on the other bench. The nail skated. The face took a crescent I still see in raking light from the glass wall.

I hang that roll with the spare pin. If I cannot find the punch I will invent a worse one. The kit is part of the joint.

## Access is a design

I leave the exit proud, or I leave a hole I can get a punch into. A pin planed flush on both faces is a pretty permanent joint. Do not call it knockdown. If the show face must be flush, put the proud on the inside and tell the next person which face to punch. I write PUNCH THIS FACE on the inside of the rail in pencil. Future me on a porch will not remember. The pencil will.

A legend in a delivery note is better than nothing and worse than the pencil on the rail. Notes leave. Rails stay.

## Failures

Glued insurance. Split pin. Recut.

I also over-offset and split the tenon when I drove. The drawbore is a cam, not a clamp you invent with violence. If the shoulder will not shut, the tenon is fat or the mortise is wrong. Do not ask the pin to fix a moon.

A steel pin in a hole that wandered: the steel will still go, and it will oval the wood. Bore true. Steel does not sneak the way oak does.

The two-pin cock is the failure I had not named until a bench racked in the shop. I drove the near pin home first, hard, then the far pin had to fight a rail that was already cammed crooked. I now start both pins, seat them together, and I watch the shoulder from the end. Two drawbores on one rail are a pair. A pair driven as two stories is a twist.

## A bench that comes off a pair of posts

I built a knockdown bench with two posts, a stretcher, and a slab seat. The stretcher was drawbored with oak pins, proud on the inside, flush-enough on the show with a little nib I could still find. Delivery was a hallway and a turn. I drove the pins out with a punch and a backer, stacked the posts, and I did not split a rail. That is the only story this joint needs to earn.

The offset was modest. The tenons were dry in the cheeks except a swipe of hide I am still not sure I needed. The pins were waxed. The next assembly, the shoulders still shut. If they had not, I would have sneaked the tenon, not driven a fatter pin.

I drive one pin out and back in as a rehearsal before the piece leaves. If the rehearsal splits a pin, the customer was not going to do better. I recut from the same stick as the first so the diameter matches. A cousin stick is a cousin fit. Cousin fits oval the hole. The hole is the joint the second time.

## Punch, backer, the face you love

I hold a backer on the show face so the pin does not blow the exit. I punch from the proud side. I do not punch from the pretty side “because it is closer.” Closer is how you blow a show face. If both faces are show, I left a proud on the underside of the rail and I wrote it on the rail.

A pin that was painted in place is a pin you will wreck. If the piece is painted, either leave a proud you can cut and recut, or use steel and a finish that can be touched.

## When I will not reverse a drawbore

A chair that sees rack every day: I glue the cheeks and I treat the pin as permanent unless the customer paid for travel. A bed I already bolted: I do not also drawbore the same rail as KD. One system. Two systems in one rail is a rail that does not know how it comes apart.

I will not glue a pin for insurance on a joint I called knockdown. I already have the two-piece pin. That is the whole proof.

## Humidity, a landing, and no room for a punch

Oak pins in oak, in a wet June, can swell into a kind of weld even without glue. Wax is not a superstition. I also keep a spare pin of the same diameter in the delivery bag. The pin you split on the porch is the pin you needed.

The hallway changes this joint when the exit face is against a wall. A punch needs a path. I have stood on a stair landing with the proud side an inch from plaster and no way to swing. I now design the proud toward the open side of the assembled piece, or I leave enough rail length that you can rotate the frame a quarter turn on the landing. If the punch cannot arrive, the joint is the staying kind no matter what I called it in the shop.

South Arkansas heat in a closed truck will cook hide on a tenon I also pinned. On a travel stretcher I keep the cheeks dry if the whole frame must open. A swipe of hide I cannot reverse is a second insurance policy. I already know about insurance.

## What I want from D:\BBF

A proud pin, a punch, a backer with its hole — the three objects in one frame if we have it. The offset hole in a disassembled tenon, chalk on the original mark. That chalk is the whole lesson. The taper board with a pin in the cradle, if anyone shot the bench. The two-piece glued pin next to a whole spare, if that jar still exists. Do not invent the frame.

## The judgment

A pin you can drive back is a drawbore that kept its exit and never saw glue in the pin. Offset, dry pin, wax, a punch path you can still reach on a landing. The craft-pack pin stays. This one travels. If you cannot get it out without splitting the rail, you built the staying kind and called it the traveling kind. The names matter when someone is standing in a hallway with a punch.
