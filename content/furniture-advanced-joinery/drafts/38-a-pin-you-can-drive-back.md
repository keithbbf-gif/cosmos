The pin moved. I had a punch on the exit side and a backer on the show, and the oak peg came out in two pieces because I had glued it “for insurance” five years earlier. Insurance is how a knockdown becomes a demolition. A drawbore that is supposed to travel is a dry pin in an offset hole, proud enough to hit, ugly enough to admit it might leave.

The craft pack already taught drawbore-then-drive for joints that stay. This is the reversible cousin: trestle stretchers, a bed rail you do not want to bolt, a knock-down bench. The offset still cams the shoulder home. The glue stays in the tenon cheeks if you want it — or it stays out if the whole frame must open.

## Offset without romance

I bore the mortise walls first, assemble dry, mark the tenon through those holes, then bore the tenon a little closer to the shoulder. How little: enough to pull, not enough to split. I sneak on a scrap. [VERIFY] any printed offset.

The pin is straight-grained, a little tapered if I am kind, or a steel pin if the piece will be taken apart often. Oak pins in oak are traditional and they can weld themselves with time and humidity even without glue. A little wax helps them remain pins.

## Access is a design

I leave the exit proud, or I leave a hole I can get a punch into. A pin planed flush on both faces is a pretty permanent joint. Do not call it knockdown. If the show face must be flush, put the proud on the inside and tell the next person which face to punch. A pencil mark inside a rail is allowed. A legend in a delivery note is better.

## Failures

Glued insurance. Split pin. Recut.

I also over-offset and split the tenon when I drove. The drawbore is a cam, not a clamp you invent with violence. If the shoulder will not shut, the tenon is fat or the mortise is wrong. Do not ask the pin to fix a moon.

A steel pin in a hole that wandered: the steel will still go, and it will oval the wood. Bore true. Steel does not sneak the way oak does.

## A bench that comes off a pair of posts

I built a knockdown bench with two posts, a stretcher, and a slab seat. The stretcher was drawbored with oak pins, proud on the inside, flush-enough on the show with a little nib I could still find. Delivery was a hallway and a turn. I drove the pins out with a punch and a backer, stacked the posts, and I did not split a rail. That is the only story this joint needs to earn.

The offset was modest. The tenons were dry in the cheeks except a swipe of hide I am still not sure I needed. The pins were waxed. The next assembly, the shoulders still shut. If they had not, I would have sneaked the tenon, not driven a fatter pin.

## Punch, backer, the face you love

I hold a backer on the show face so the pin does not blow the exit. I punch from the proud side. I do not punch from the pretty side “because it is closer.” Closer is how you blow a show face. If both faces are show, I left a proud on the underside of the rail and I wrote it on the rail.

A pin that was painted in place is a pin you will wreck. If the piece is painted, either leave a proud you can cut and recut, or use steel and a finish that can be touched.

## When I will not reverse a drawbore

A chair that sees rack every day: I glue the cheeks and I treat the pin as permanent unless the customer paid for travel. A bed I already bolted: I do not also drawbore the same rail as KD. One system. Two systems in one rail is a rail that does not know how it comes apart.

## What I want from D:\BBF

A proud pin, a punch, a backer — the three objects in one frame if we have it. The offset hole in a disassembled tenon, chalk on the original mark. That chalk is the whole lesson.

## Humidity and oak pins

Oak pins in oak, in a wet June, can swell into a kind of weld. Wax is not a superstition. I also keep a spare pin of the same diameter in the delivery bag. The pin you split on the porch is the pin you needed. The spare is part of the joint, the same as the campaign stool.

## Punch from the proud

I punch from the proud side with a backer on the show. I do not punch from the pretty side because it is closer. Closer is how you blow a show face. Wax the pin. Oak in June can weld.

## Shop morning, reversible pin

Bore the walls. Mark the tenon. Offset a little, from a scrap. Wax the pin. Drive. Leave proud on the exit I will punch. Write on the rail which face. Spare pin in the bag. If I glue the pin for insurance I have made a demolition. I already split that pin.

<!-- PACK-W3 -->

## Weld in June

Oak pins in oak in a wet June can swell into a weld even without glue. Wax. A spare in the bag. Punch from the proud, backer on the show.

I will not glue a pin for insurance on a joint I called knockdown. Insurance is demolition. I already have the two-piece pin.

<!-- PACK-W4 -->

## Punch and backer on a nail

I hang a punch and a scrap backer with the spare pin. If I cannot find the punch I will use a nail and I will blow a face. The kit is part of the joint. The proud is marked with a pencil inside the rail.

<!-- PACK-W5 -->

I drive one pin out and back in as a rehearsal before the piece leaves. If the rehearsal splits a pin, the customer was not going to do better. I recut the pin, I wax, I try again. The rehearsal is cheaper than a porch demolition.

<!-- PACK-W6 -->
I cut the next pin from the same stick as the first so the diameter matches. A cousin stick is a cousin fit. Cousin fits oval the hole. The hole is the joint the second time.

<!-- PACK-W7 -->
I write PUNCH THIS FACE on the inside of the rail in pencil. Future me on a porch will not remember which face I left proud. The pencil will.

The spare pin in the bag is the same diameter as the pin in the rail. A cousin stick from the rack ovals the hole.

## The judgment

A pin you can drive back is a drawbore that kept its exit. Offset, dry pin, wax, a punch path. The craft-pack pin stays. This one travels. If you cannot get it out without splitting the rail, you built the staying kind and called it the traveling kind. The names matter when someone is standing in a hallway with a punch.
