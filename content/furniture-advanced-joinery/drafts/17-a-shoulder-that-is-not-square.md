Light showed under the shoulder in a thin moon. The rail was fair. The post was turned and honest. The tenon went home. The shoulder, cut square on the table saw because I was in a hurry, did not know the post was a cylinder. A square shoulder on a round post is a pair of tangent points and a lot of air. Glue will fill the air for a month. The month after that, the moon comes back.

This is the quiet curved joint. No steam in the sentence, no stave. The rail may be sawn from wide stock, or laminated, or even straight in plan and only curved in elevation — a bowed stretcher, an arched apron, a chair rail that lifts. The tenon can be as ordinary as the craft pack. The shoulder cannot.

## Scribe, do not guess a radius

I fit the tenon first, a little tight, so the rail sits where it will live. Then I set a compass to the largest gap I can find between shoulder and post, and I walk the scribe around, knife if I can, pencil if I must and then knife. The line is the new shoulder. Everything proud of that line is waste.

<!-- PHOTO: fig-02 D:\BBF\BBF Photos — compass and knife scribing a rail to a turned leg (filename pending shop pull) -->

A chisel, a shoulder plane, a scraper. I do not sand a scribe. Sanding rounds the only line that has to be sharp against a cylinder.

If the post is square, the scribe may still not be a straight shoulder: an arched rail meets a square post in a pair of curves in elevation. Same process. Fit, mark, pare. The table saw’s square cut is a first approximation, not a finished shoulder.

## Compound shoulders, a short cousin

A splayed leg plus a curved rail is two problems. I still tenon first to the drawing, then I scribe. Trying to calculate a double compound on paper for a one-off chair is how you cut a pretty wrong shoulder. The post in front of you is the paper.

Production shops have jigs that present the rail to a saw at the finished compound. If you have that jig and it is tuned to this chair, use it. Then sneak. Jigs get you close. Light gets you honest.

## Haunches and the open top

An arched apron that meets the top of a leg still wants a haunch so the mortise is not a slot to the sky. The haunch is scribed too, or it is buried under a top and you can be a little uglier. I am uglier under a top. I am not ugly on a chair rail a hand will find.

## Glue does not scribe

People butter a moon and call it a fit. Hide will even fill it, briefly. The rail then has a hinge of glue. Rack finds hinges. Pare until the moon is gone, then glue. If you cannot get the moon gone without loosening the tenon, the tenon was the wrong size or the rail is in the wrong place. Recut the tenon or recut the rail. Do not grout a chair.

## Failures

I table-sawed a square shoulder on a turned leg and filled the moon with a sliver. The sliver was pretty in the shop. After finish it was a stripe. Scribe the rail; do not inlay a confession.

I also scribed with a pencil only and then lost the line in oak pores. Knife. Oak will hide a pencil like it hides a scratch you wanted to keep.

A router with a bearing that follows the post can sneak a shoulder if you invent a jig that holds the rail. I have seen it work. I have also seen it chew the show arris. Hand tools at the end either way.

## The dry-fit light

I put the rail home and I look from the side the room will look from. A moon that only shows from the underside of a table can sometimes live if the top hides it. A moon on a chair rail cannot live. I mark the moon with chalk, I pull the rail, I pare, I put it back. I do this until the chalk has nowhere to go.

A flashlight is ruder than daylight and useful. The glass wall at late day is ruder still. I like the glass wall for this. It is unkind and it is honest.

## Compass setting

I set the compass to the largest gap, not the average. Averaging a scribe leaves the large gap. The large gap is the one the photograph finds. I walk the scribe, I check that the line does not fall off the tenon onto a place I cannot pare without loosening the fit.

If the gap is so large the scribe would eat the tenon, the rail is in the wrong place or the tenon is too short. Recut. Do not scribe a disaster into a smaller disaster.

## Turned legs

A cylinder is a kind scribe — a constant radius if the turn is fair. A turned taper is a changing radius. I scribe after the rail is at the height it will live, because a taper means the radius at the shoulder changes if the rail slides up a 16th. Dry-fit at the finished height. Then scribe.

## What I want from D:\BBF

A moon of light under a square-cut shoulder, then a shut scribe, if we have both. The compass on the rail. A shoulder plane shaving.

## Glue

I already said glue does not scribe. I will say it in the expansion because people butter moons. Butter is a hinge. Rack finds hinges. Pare until the moon is gone.

## Under the top

I allow an ugly haunch under a tabletop. I do not allow an ugly chair-rail scribe. The hand finds the chair. The tabletop hides the haunch. Spend the time where the hand is.

<!-- PACK-W2L -->

## Shop morning, scribe

Tenon home. Largest moon sets the compass. Knife. Pare. Glass-wall light. Again until chalk has nowhere to go. If the scribe would eat the tenon I recut the rail. I do not butter the moon. Butter is a hinge.

<!-- PACK-W3 -->

## Oak pores and a pencil

Oak will hide a pencil scribe in the pores. I knife. I have lost a line and then I have pared a shoulder that was a guess. A guess on a turned taper is a moon that comes back after finish.

I will not table-saw a square shoulder on a cylinder because the saw is already set up for something else. Setup time is not a reason to invent a moon.

<!-- PACK-W4 -->

## Shoulder plane last

The shoulder plane is the last tool, not the first. Compass, knife, chisel, then the plane for the last hair. Sandpaper is how I lose the arris that has to meet a cylinder. I keep the plane sharp enough that oak does not tear at the scribe.

<!-- PACK-TINY -->
The flashlight is ruder than I am, and I use it.

## The judgment

A shoulder that is not square is a scribe. The tenon can be ordinary. The light under the rail cannot. Fit, mark, pare, then glue. The curve you worked for is wasted if the meeting is two tangent points and a moon.
