The wedge came out with two fingers and a tap. That is the compliment. A tusk tenon that needs a dead-blow and a prayer is not a knockdown joint. It is a through-tenon that forgot the point. I had the trestle in two piles on the porch: a pair of uprights, a stretcher, two tusks, and a top that would ride on cleats. A hallway will not take a six-foot assembled base. The joint exists because houses have doors.

The craft pack already argued a wedge you can see on a through-tenon you do not plan to take apart. This is the cousin you *do* take apart. The slot is longer. The wedge is a tool. The shoulder still has to shut, or the trestle walks. A fox wedge is a stay: you drive it and you mean to lose it in the tenon. A tusk is a go: you drive it and you mean to find it again on a porch.

## What has to be true

The stretcher’s tenon goes through the upright. The tenon has a slot. The tusk (the wedge) goes through the slot, *outside* the upright, and as you drive it, it draws the shoulder of the stretcher against the upright. Drawbore logic, removable.

If the slot is too close to the upright, the wedge jams before the shoulder shuts. If the slot is too far, the wedge bottoms and the shoulder is still a moon. I cut the slot so a partly driven wedge already kisses the upright, and a fully driven wedge shuts the shoulder without splitting the tenon. That is a fitting job. Dry-fit. Chalk. Sneak the slot, not the shoulder.

I chalk the outside face of the upright onto the tenon while the shoulder is home. That chalk line is the slot’s near edge. I cut from the chalk, not from a number I liked on a story stick in the other room. A number I liked is how I once bottomed a wedge and left a moon I then had to plug.

The wedge has a slope. I like a shallow slope so it holds by friction and so a bump does not launch it. A steep wedge holds like a nail and comes out like a nail — which is to say, badly. A little soap or wax on the unused faces keeps the tusk from becoming furniture.

## Wood that can be a tusk

The tusk takes abuse. I cut it from something at least as hard as the tenon, grain running the length, no runout. A pretty contrasting wedge is allowed. A pretty wedge with wild grain is a tusk that will snap the first time someone uses a hammer because they did not know about the two-finger rule. Write a note in the delivery. People will still use a hammer. Leave enough proud that a hammer has a face to hit that is not the tenon.

A second, opposite wedge — a pair — is a lock some trestles use. I have. It is more to lose. A single tusk with a small pin or a leather thong through a hole in the fat end is a tusk you will find under the sofa. I bore the thong hole after the slope is fitted, not before. A hole bored on a blank that later gets recut for slope is a hole that lands in the thin end and tears.

I wax the unused faces of the tusk so it remains a tool. A dry oak tusk in an oak slot in a June shop on the glass wall can weld like a pin. Wax is not pretty. Two fingers after a summer move is pretty.

## The upright is a mortise that sees weather

The through-mortise in the upright has to be clean, walls parallel, because this joint will be assembled more than once. A ragged mortise gets worse every move. Chop it as if it were a show joint, because it is: people look at trestles. They sit on the floor at parties and they stare.

I clamp the upright to the bench edge with a holdfast and a caul so the waste falls into a bucket, not onto the iron. The far wall of a through-mortise is where a chisel goes greedy. I sneak that wall from the exit face after I have chopped most of the waste from the entry. A blowout on a trestle upright is a photograph you did not want.

The shoulder of the stretcher should be square to the stretcher and scribed to the upright if the upright is not flat. A trestle upright often has a little life. Scribe. The knockdown does not cancel the moon.

## Top, rack, the rest of the table

A tusk stretcher keeps the two uprights from walking apart. It does not keep the top from racking by itself if the top is only sitting there. Cleats, sliding dovetails, buttons, a breadboard — the top still needs a way to be a top. I like cleats that nest over the uprights so the top cannot skate, and I like the trestle to take the rack. A dining trestle that relies on the top for all the racking stiffness is a dining trestle that loosens when someone sits on a corner.

I dry-lift the trestle without the top. If the pair of uprights walks, the tusk is shy or a shoulder is still a moon. The top is not allowed to hide that walk.

## The shop door is the first hallway

A six-foot trestle does not love a turn in a 1920s house. I tape the shop door opening on the floor in chalk and I walk the assembled base through that rectangle before I like the finish. If the base will not make the shop door, it will not make the house. I then take the tusks out and I time myself. If the teardown needs a dead-blow, I recut the slot in the shop, not on a landing with a mattress watching.

I pack the tusks in a cloth roll taped to a stretcher, not loose in a box of clamps. A tusk that finds the gravel is a tusk you will replace with a scrap that does not match, and then someone will ask why one wedge is prettier.

## June weld, January rattle

South Arkansas will do both to the same oak. In a wet week the tusk swells in the slot and the two-finger compliment becomes a tap with a block. That is still a knockdown if the tap is a tap. If you have to beat it, you fitted the slot for January and you delivered in June. I leave a whisper of daylight at the fat end when I drive the tusk home in a dry shop in January, because I have watched a “perfect” winter fit become a welded pin by August.

The opposite failure is a January rattle after a June fit. The tusk that was kissed tight in humid air goes light when the heat runs. A thong keeps it from walking out. A shallow slope keeps it from launching. I do not solve a seasonal rattle by cutting a steeper wedge. Steeper is how a kid’s foot becomes a launch.

## Failures I will own

I cut the slot too far out. The wedge bottomed. The shoulder grinned. I plugged the slot and recut, which is a scar I can still read. Sneak the slot from a dry-fit with the shoulder already chalked.

I also used a steep decorative wedge because it photographed. It launched when a kid kicked the stretcher. Shallow slope. A thong.

A tenon that was too thin for the slot: the remaining meat split. The slot needs a tenon that is a small beam. Drawer-scale tenons do not get tusks.

I once bandsawed the slot “close” and finished with a chisel I was already tired of. The slot wandered toward the tenon end. The remaining meat looked like enough until the first hard drive, and then it opened along the grain like a book. I cut a new stretcher. A tusk slot is a mortise in a beam. Treat it like one: chop or rout with a fence, then pare. The bandsaw is for the waste outside the tenon, not for the wall that has to live.

## What I want from D:\BBF

Wedge driven, shoulder shut, proud enough to hit. The same joint apart, tusk in two fingers, slot and upright witness mark readable. If a plugged-and-recut slot still exists on a shop trestle, that scar is the better teacher. No filename until the pull. No invented photographer.

## The judgment

A tusk you can pull is a through-tenon designed for a hallway. Shoulder shuts, wedge comes out with a tap, slot fitted from the life of the dry-fit. The craft-pack wedge, and the fox you never see again, are for joints that stay. This one is for joints that travel. If it does not travel, you cut the wrong wedge.
