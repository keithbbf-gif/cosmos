The cam turned with that cheap, bright click everyone knows from a box that came in a flat pack. I was using one in a shop cabinet of ply, a jig closet, and I was not ashamed. I would have been ashamed if I had put the same cam in a walnut case I had already dovetailed on three corners and then “simplified” the fourth. A cam lock is a clamp you leave behind. It is not a pin, not a tail, not a shoulder. If you need a clamp in the piece, say so. If you need a joint, cut one.

This is the honest-knockdown essay the brief asked for. Not every knockdown is a tusk tenon. Some of it is hardware that a customer can turn with a screwdriver on a Sunday. The advanced part is knowing when that is enough.

## What a cam actually does

A steel or zinc cam in a hole grabs the head of a bolt or a dowel that has a groove. When you turn, you draw. Alignment is usually a separate dowel or two. The locating dowels are the closest thing to joinery. The cam is the clamp. In particleboard, the cam’s hole is also a crater waiting to egg-shape. In good ply, it can be serviced. In solid wood, I would rather have an insert and a bolt, or a barrel, or a tusk.

I will use cams in:

- Shop fixtures.
- A ply cabinet that has to live in a rental and leave no scars.
- The back of a case that already has a wooden front joint.

I will not use cams as the four corners of a piece I called a chest.

## Failures that look like design

A cam in a thin wall: the wall blows. Minimum meat is not a vibe; it is the diameter plus a life.

A cam without a locating dowel: the panel slides while you turn and then the cam grabs at an angle. The next turn chews. Always locate, then clamp.

A cam as a hinge: people do this. It is a hinge until it is a crater.

## A jig closet that earned a cam

I built a ply closet in the shop for jigs, 16 inches deep, doors I did not care about. Cams at the four corners, two dowels per corner, a back that is a sheet of 1/4 I could unscrew. It has come apart twice when I needed the space for a table glue-up. The cams still click. The dowel holes are a little oval. I will live with oval in a jig closet. I will not live with oval in a walnut bookcase I invoiced as furniture.

That is the standard I will use in a caption: *what is the piece for?* If the piece is a closet in a shop, the cam is a citizen. If the piece is a chest in a sitting room, the cam is a shortcut I should have to defend.

## How to make a cam last as long as it can

Locate first. Two dowels or a rabbet. Then the cam bolt goes into a hole that was bored from a fence, not from a guess. The cam sits in a hole that has meat around it — the manufacturer’s minimum is a starting point, not a dare. I do not put a cam in 1/2-inch ply and hope. I put it in 3/4 and I still wince if the case will be racked.

Turn until it seats, not until the zinc screams. A screamed cam is a cam that has already started to chew. If it will not seat, the bolt is the wrong length or the panel is not home on the dowels. Take it apart. Do not keep turning.

## Particleboard is a different weather

I will use cams in particleboard if the piece is already particleboard and the job is to make it come apart without making it worse. I will not specify particleboard as a furniture material in this pack. If a customer brings a flat-pack and asks me to “make it better,” I can move the cams to ply panels. I cannot make particleboard into oak by turning a cam harder.

## The sentence I will not write

I will not write that cams are “just as strong as dovetails if installed correctly.” They are not the same sentence. They can be strong enough for a ply box. Enough is a word. As-strong-as is a caption that is doing sales. This pack does not do that caption.

## Shop morning, jig closet

Ply parts. Dowels first. Cams second. Back that unscrews. I will use this religion in a shop closet. I will not use it as the fourth corner of a walnut chest I already dovetailed on three sides. Tired is not a system.

## Click and crater

Particleboard craters. Good ply can be serviced. Solid walnut gets a different clamp. I turn until it seats. A screamed cam is already chewing.

I will not write that a cam is as strong as a dovetail if installed correctly. Enough for a ply box is a word. As-strong-as is sales. This pack does not do that sentence.

<!-- PACK-W4 -->

## Dowel first

I set the locating dowels and I close the corner with a clamp before the cam turns. The cam then draws a panel that is already home. A cam that has to drag a panel home is a cam that will chew.

<!-- PACK-W5 -->

I keep cams in a drawer labeled FIXTURES, not FURNITURE. The label is a joke until it is not. If I reach into FURNITURE and I find a cam, I put it back. The jig closet can have as many clicks as it wants. The walnut case cannot.

<!-- PACK-W6 -->
I write the panel thickness on the cam drawer so I do not put a cam in a wall that cannot hold it. Minimum meat is a number on the packet. I do not dare the number in 1/2-inch ply and hope the click will save me.

<!-- PACK-W7 -->
I stop turning when the panel is home on the dowels. Extra turns after home are how zinc becomes a crater. Home is enough.

## The judgment

A cam that is not a joint can still be the right clamp. Use it where the piece is a box of sheet goods that must come apart. Do not use it where you already knew how to cut a tail and got tired. Tired is not a joinery system. The click is not a dovetail. The click can still be honest if the caption is honest.
