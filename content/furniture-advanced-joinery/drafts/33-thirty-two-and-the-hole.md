I drilled a test row in a scrap of maple ply, 32 millimeters on center, and the third hole was already a hair off because I had moved the fence to “improve” it. The fourth hole made a shelf pin lean. The 32mm system is not a vibe. It is a grid. If you will not honor the grid, do not use the language. Use a sliding dovetail and a face frame and be a different shop.

This essay exists in an advanced-joinery pack because knockdown is the brief, and because a one-man shop in South Arkansas will still be asked for a run of cabinets that have to ship and hang. I will not pretend the grid is craft in the dovetail sense. I will pretend it is a joint, because it is: a coordinated set of holes that locates hinges, pins, and fasteners.

A ply pantry that comes off a truck in parts and hangs square is a legitimate knockdown. The honesty is in the caption. Well-bored is a compliment. Dovetailed is a different compliment. This pack will not launder a grid into a tail.

## What the number means

Thirty-two millimeters is the spacing. The system assumes a line of holes, inset a standard distance from the front and sometimes the back, so that hinges, drawer runners, and shelf pins share a map. Euro hinges want a cup at a location the map already knows. If you invent a pretty inset that is not the map, you will buy a different hinge or you will bore a second lie.

Plywood and sheet goods are the native food. Solid wood moves and the grid becomes oval. I will use the grid in ply. I will not use it as the only structure in a solid-wood case that also wants to be a chest.

## Accuracy is respect

A line-boring machine is the tool that makes this not a religion of error. A shop without one can still do a small run with a good jig and a plunge router or a drill press and a fence you do not “improve” mid-row. The first hole is not the boss. The fence is the boss. A story stick with 32mm centers is a cheap boss.

I clamp the jig. I do not hold it. I drill every hole in the row before I move the fence. I test the first shelf pin in the first and last hole. If the pin leans, I stop the row. A leaning pin at hole twelve is a row I will plug.

I write the inset on the jig in ink, not in a number I keep in my head. Head numbers drift toward the hinge I used last year. The map does not drift. If the hinge I stock changes, I change the jig and I throw the old number away. Two maps in one shop is a leaning door.

Dowel construction in the same family — holes in edges, dowels or confirmat, a case that clamps up like a box — is knockdown if you can get back in, and it is permanent if you glue. Be honest which one you sold.

## A line I would bore in this shop

A ply pantry, 24 inches deep, doors with Euro cups, three adjustable shelves, one fixed. I would bore two rows, 37mm from the front if that is the hinge line I stock, and a back row for the shelf pins if the shelves are deep enough to want it. I would not bore a 32mm row in a solid-oak case side and then also dovetail the case as if the row were a decoration. The row is a system. The dovetail is a system. They can live in one shop. They should not live in one side without a drawing that knows both.

I hang the first door before I bore the last row of shelf pins, so I know the hinge map is the map I think it is. A door that fights the cup is a door I finish again, not a row I invent to please the door.

I keep a spare shelf pin in the hinge box so a leaning pin is a pin I replace, not a hole I force. Force ovals the map.

## Doors, backs, weather

Euro cups in a ply door are native. In a solid door they are possible and the door will still cup. I finish both faces. I hang the door after the case is square, not before. A grid does not square a case. A back and a clamp square a case.

Ply still shrugs a little when the shop weather swings. The glass wall will cook a stack of panels in August and the first hole of the afternoon will not be the first hole of the morning if you moved the fence to chase the shrug. I let the panels sit, I bore from a fence that stayed, and I accept that a pin will be a little proud in January and a little snug in June. That is ply weather. Solid oak shrugs more and ovals a grid. I keep the 32mm sentence in sheet goods. I keep tails in solids.

A case that has to ship is a case I assemble once in the shop, mark the sides, and take apart. The hallway at the house is not the place to discover that hole twelve leans.

I clamp the panel to the drill-press table, or to the bench under the jig, with the fence already locked. A panel I hold with a palm will walk toward the bit on hole eight. Maple ply and Baltic birch do not shrug the same amount in a wet week; I do not mix leftover sides from two sheets in one case and then wonder why one row feels snug. One sheet when I can. One fence. One map.

The sides travel as sheets in a van, not as a hung kitchen. I pad the rows so a pin hole does not arrive as a crater from a clamp. The 32mm system earns its keep when those sheets become a square box in a hallway that will not take a glued carcase. That is legitimacy. It is not a tail.

## Failures I will own

I moved the fence. The shelf leaned. I plugged the row, which in maple ply is a confession in dots. Leave the fence.

I also hung Euro hinges on a solid door that cupped. The grid was fine. The door was wood. The system does not cancel seasons. Finish both sides. Keep doors in the same country as the case.

Selling “handmade” on a 32mm kitchen without a single wooden joint is a caption problem. The kitchen can still be well made. The caption should not lie. This pack is not the place for that lie.

I once bored a row from a story stick I had copied in a hurry, and the stick was 32 millimeters on the first six holes and then a little long because I had ticked the last marks from the last tick, not from the first. Compound error. The last pin leaned like a man on a porch. Now I tick every mark from the same end, or I use a manufactured spacing bar, and I test first and last before I call the row done.

## The judgment

Thirty-two and the hole is a map. Honor the map or leave it. It will knock down. It will not be a dovetail. A shop can own both religions if it keeps the vestments in the right closet. A ply cabinet that ships and hangs is allowed to be proud of a true row. It is not allowed to call the row a tail.
