I set a combination square on the first corner and the blade hung in the air like it had been insulted. One hundred twenty degrees. The hex does not care about your square. It cares about six boards that each think they are the important one, and about a bottom that will only drop in if the drum closed true. People spline these. People biscuit these. I have. A jewelry drum that gets lifted by the lid ring will still ask the corners a ruder question than a spline likes.

This is not a hopper. The sides can be plumb. The compound lives in the *plan*: each joint is 120, so the end of each board is not a 90 meeting. If you cut 90-degree dovetails and then force the drum together, you have built a spring. Springs open. If you miter only, you have six pretty lines and six end-grain glue joints in a circle. Circles rack into ovals when you pick them up.

## The geometry you cannot fake

Interior angle of a regular hexagon is 120. Each board’s end, if the sides are plumb, is 30 off of a square end — or you can think of the meeting as two 60s, depending on how you hold your head. I lock a bevel to a story board I made once and kept: a scrap hex I trust more than a detent on a miter saw. Detents lie when the saw is tired. The story board is the drum I already got right, or a piece of plywood I cut carefully and checked with a stick across the flats.

Octagons are the same family, meaner by two corners. I will say hex and you can add sides. The more sides, the closer you are to a cylinder, and the less meat each joint has for tails. A 12-sided drum is a coopered thing pretending to be dovetailed. Stop. Cooper it.

## Tails on a 120

You can through-dovetail a hex. The tails are sawn at the plan angle so the cheeks land on the adjoining inside face. The baseline is still parallel to the rim if the sides are plumb. That part is kind. The unkind part is the acute and obtuse corners on each pin: one side of the pin is a knife, the other is a wide hall. You will overchop the knife. Everyone does. Pare it last, with the board held so the knife is supported.

I cut tails on every other board and pins on the others, like a square box, so I can sneak. If you cut all tails, you are a hero in a video and a coward at the dry-fit.

A sliding bevel set to the tail slope (the usual 1:6 or 1:8 in hardwood) is *in addition* to the plan angle. Two angles in the saw. This is why people jig the work so one of those angles becomes plumb. A simple cradle that holds the side at 30 degrees lets you saw the tail the way you always do. I built that cradle out of scrap after the second hex. I should have built it after the first.

## The dry-fit that tells you about wind

Six corners. If one is fat, the drum will not close and you will not know which one until you mark them. I letter the inside of every side. I dry-fit in pairs, then threes, then the whole. A band clamp is the only clamp that understands a hex. Bar clamps make ovals.

If the drum closes and sits flat on the bench, check the flats with a straightedge across opposite sides. If two flats are closer than the others, you have two joints that are thin or two sides that were not the same width. Width errors compound around a ring. I cut all six from the same ripped strip when I can.

## Bottoms and lids

A hex bottom is not a rectangle you can sneak. I scribe it from the inside after the drum is glued, or I cut it to the drawing and I leave a rebate that can take a little shame. A solid hex panel glued all around is a split in August. A plywood panel in a rebate is honest. A floating solid panel in a groove is prettier and wants six grooves that stop being cute when one of them runs out on a pin.

Lids: a hex lid with a lip that drops over the drum is furniture. A lid that is just another hex sitting on the rim will skate. I like a dust lip. The lip is a lining, slightly proud, and it is also why the drum had to be round-true. A lip on an oval drum is a lid that only fits in one orientation, which is a kind of joke if you did not mean it.

## When a spline is the adult

I will spline a hex if it is a lamp drum that never gets lifted by the corner, or a planter that will hold a pot and stay on the floor. I will dovetail a hex if it is a case someone will carry, or a tea-chest cousin that gets opened like a book. The joint should match the abuse.

Biscuits in a 120 miter: alignment, not structure. I have used them to keep the drum from slipping while the band clamp came home. I do not call that joinery in front of people I respect.

## Failures

I trusted a miter-saw 30-degree stop on all six ends and then wondered why the last joint was a gap you could post a letter through. Cumulative error. Now I sneak the last pair of ends as a pair, from the dry-fit, like a stave.

I also chopped a knife-edge pin from the wrong side and the corner crumbled. Support the acute. Or leave the acute pin a little fat and sneak it. Pride is a pile of walnut dust.

A router dovetail jig that only knows 90 will not save you. There are commercial jigs for angled boxes. If you own one and it is tuned, use it. If you do not, the cradle and the saw are the shop. [VERIFY] any jig name before it appears in a caption as if we own it.

## The last pair, again

I leave the last two ends fat on purpose. I dry-fit the four that are already joints, I mark the last meeting, I sneak. A hex cut from six identical settings is a hex that posts a letter through the last joint. Cumulative error is the only math I trust here.

A lid lip on an accidental oval is a lid that only fits in one orientation. I check flats with a stick before I glue, and I check again before I cut a lip.

## The 30-degree cradle

After the second hex I built a scrap cradle that holds a side at 30 degrees so the tail saw can stand up the way it likes. I should have built it after the first. The cradle is two wedges and a fence, lettered, and it is the only reason I still cut these by hand when a 90-degree jig is hanging on the wall doing nothing useful.

I cut all six sides from one ripped strip when the board will allow it. Width errors walk around a ring and become an oval you will blame on the band clamp. The clamp did not make the oval. The strip did.

## The judgment

A hexagonal case is a ring of joints, not a box with extra sides. Your square is a guest. The bevel and the story board are the bosses. Cut tails that live at 120, or admit you are building a splined drum and build a good one. Do not split the difference and hope the band clamp is a personality.
