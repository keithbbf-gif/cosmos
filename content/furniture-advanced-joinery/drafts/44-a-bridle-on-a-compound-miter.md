The corner was a miter you could see from the sofa and a bridle you could see if you got under the rim. I like that split. The craft pack already argued an open mortise on a square frame. This is the same mouth on an end that has two angles: a picture-frame table with a sloped rim, a hopper that wanted more meat than a dovetail in thin stock, an elliptical segment too thick for a spline and too proud for a secret miter.

<!-- PHOTO: fig-01 D:\BBF\BBF Photos — compound-mitered bridle dry-fit, cheeks and slot (filename pending shop pull) -->

A bridle is two cheeks and a slot. On a square, you saw the slot in one piece and the tenon in the other, shoulders square. On a compound, the shoulders are miters (or a miter plus a slope), and the slot walls have to follow. If you saw the slot plumb to the bench, you have built a spring again.

## Layout from the rim’s life

I lock the bevels to the actual rim — plan angle and elevation — the way I did on the hopper and the hex. I mark the miter around the show. I mark the bridle *inside* that fence if the miter is the show, or I let the bridle show on the underside if the underside is allowed to be teeth.

<!-- PHOTO: fig-02 D:\BBF\BBF Photos — layout of bridle on a compound end, bevel gauge (filename pending shop pull) -->

A cradle that holds the work so one cut becomes plumb is worth the hour. I built one after the second compound bridle. The first one I sawed freehand and I chased a wall for a day.

## Cheeks that can take a clamp

A bridle has a lot of glue surface if the cheeks are long. On a thin rim they are not long. Then the bridle is a spline with shoulders. I still prefer it to a bare miter. I do not prefer it to a tusk if the rim has to travel as a ring. A compound bridle is a pain to take apart. Call it permanent unless you pin it with a pin you can drive back.

The open slot will try to spring. A clamp across the cheeks while the glue sets is not optional. A bridle that springs is a miter that opens.

## Failures

Plumb slot on a sloped rim. Spring. Recut.

I also let the bridle eat the miter line on the show. A dark triangle. Fence. The secret-miter essay’s fence still applies when the bridle is hiding behind a bevel.

A router jig that only knew 90: I used it “close enough.” It was not close enough. The cheeks were fat on one side and starved on the other. Close enough is a gap.

## A picture-frame table that asked for this

A small table, the top a piece of stone or glass, the rim sloped so the stone sits in a rebate and the show is a mitered bevel. The underside can show a bridle. I cut the bridle in a cradle, I dry-fit the ring, I sneak the last corner, I glue with a strap and a clamp across each pair of cheeks so the mouths do not spring.

The rebate for the stone comes after the ring is true. I have routed a rebate in a ring that was still open and then the stone sat on a lie. Stone does not sneak.

## Tools that help, tools that lie

A bandsaw will get you close to a compound cheek if you have a fence that understands the bevel. A table saw with a jig is cleaner. A router jig that only knows 90 is a liar in this sentence. I will use a router to waste the slot if the jig presents the work so the cut is the cut I marked.

A shoulder plane still finishes the job. Compound shoulders are where machines get you near and hands get you quiet.

## Pinning for travel

If the rim must travel, I can pin the bridle with a pin I can drive back — the reversible drawbore essay. Then the bridle is a locate-and-glue-surface that can also open. I still clamp the spring at glue time. A pin will not close a sprung cheek that already dried open.

## What I want from D:\BBF

The dry-fit corner, miter shut, slot visible from below. The cradle. A strap on a four-corner rim.

## Hopper versus rim

A hopper box can use this bridle if the stock is thin for dovetails and you still want more than a spline. I would rather dovetail a hopper in 3/4. I will bridle a sloped rim that is 5/8 and proud of its bevel. The joint follows the stock, not the other way around.

## Clamp the mouth

A bridle will spring. I clamp across the cheeks while the glue sets. A strap on the ring is not enough if the mouths open. A sprung cheek is a miter that opens. The cradle that makes one cut plumb is worth the hour.

<!-- PACK-W2L -->

## Shop morning, sloped rim

Bevels locked to the rim. Cradle. Slot and tenon following the marks, not the bench. Dry-fit the ring. Sneak the last corner. Strap plus cheek clamps. Rebate after the ring is true. Stone or glass last. If the bridle ate the miter line I recut. A dark triangle on a show bevel is not a design.

<!-- PACK-W3 -->

## Spring and stone

Cheeks want to open. I clamp them. I rebate after the ring is true. Stone does not sneak. A router jig that only knows 90 is a liar on a sloped rim.

I will not call this knockdown unless I pinned it to come apart. A compound bridle is a pain to open. Permanent unless I designed the pin path.

<!-- PACK-W4 -->

## Cheek clamp pair

I keep a pair of clamps that only do bridle cheeks so I do not “forget” them because the strap is already on. The strap closes the ring. The cheek clamps close the mouths. Both. Stone after both have done their job.

<!-- PACK-W5 -->

I dry-fit the ring on the stone or a ply stand-in of the stone before I glue. A ring that is true on the bench and drunk on the stone is a rebate I cut to a lie. The stone is the other half of the joint. I let it speak before the glue.

<!-- PACK-W6 -->
I mark the inside of each corner with a letter after the dry-fit on the stone. A ring that only goes together one way should say so. Glue does not like a hunt. The letters take ten seconds. The hunt takes a ring.

<!-- PACK-W7 -->
I check each mouth with a feeler after the cheek clamps are on and before I walk away. A mouth that opened a hair will be a miter that opens a line. I retighten now.

The cradle that makes one cut plumb is still cheaper than a sprung cheek I have to recut.

## The judgment

A bridle on a compound miter is an open mouth that learned the rim’s angles. Cradle the work, keep the show fence, clamp the spring, and do not call it knockdown unless you pinned it to be. The square bridle in the craft pack is the alphabet. This is the word you spell when the frame left ninety behind.
