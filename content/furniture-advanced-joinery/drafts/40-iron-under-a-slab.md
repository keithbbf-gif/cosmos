The slab had already moved. I could see it in the finish — a dull line where a fastener had held a winter width against a summer want. The steel base was still a perfect rectangle. Steel does not care. Wood cares. I pulled every screw that had been driven into a round hole in a strap, I slotted the strap, I put the screws back in the middle of the slots, and I waited to see if the line would be the last one.

This pack is joinery, not a slab-fashion catalog. The knockdown is the base coming off so the slab can go up a stair. The joint is the allowance. Fasteners live in slots. A plate that wants to be glued to a face is a prison I will not build.

## Three honest attachments

**Figure-eight fasteners.** They rotate as the slab moves. I set them in shallow mortises, screw to the base in one hole and to the slab in the other. They are small. They are enough for many tops. They are not enough if the base is a skinny rectangle and the slab is a sail.

**Slotted C-channel or a wooden cleat with slots.** The slot is the breadboard idea in iron. Fix the center. Let the ends travel. I wax the slot. I do not crank the screw into a lock.

**Sliding dovetail battens**, glued at the center, free at the ends — the craft-pack cousin, still good under a slab if you do not also steel it into a prison.

## The Forstner jig and the slotting file

Figure-eight recesses I cut with a Forstner in a plywood fence that locates the fastener from the base’s hole, not from a tape I re-read on the underside of a tree. The fence has a pin that finds the already-drilled iron. I drop the Forstner, I chisel the waste to a flat, I set the eight flush. A proud figure-eight is a lump you feel through a tablecloth. A deep one is a fastener that rocks.

C-channel I slot on the bench with a file and a drill, or I have the shop mill the slots if the run is long. I start the screw in the middle of the slot in the weather I am in. If I start it at the end, August has nowhere to go. I wax the slot with the same paraffin I use on wooden slides, and I keep that wax off the finish. A locked screw is a round hole I dressed up.

I photograph the underside before the table leaves so a person who sees a screw that has walked will not tighten it to the end of the slot. That tightening is a split. The picture is part of the joint.

## The base should come off

Bolts into inserts in a wooden sub-frame, or machine screws into tapped steel, or a nest the slab sits in with locating pins. I letter the orientation. Slabs are not rectangles. They will only sit one way if the live edge is a conversation.

A trestle in wood with tusks is also a slab base. The tusk essay still applies. Steel is not a moral upgrade. It is a different movement story.

I check balance with the base off the slab, then on. A heavy live edge wants the base shifted, not a longer screw. A longer screw is not a counterweight. A shifted base is.

Locating pins — two short dowels or steel pins in the sub-frame — keep a live edge from landing a half-inch off the conversation it was built for. I bore those from a story stick I tape to the underside once, in the shop, and I letter the pins so they cannot swap. Without them, a slab that only sits one way will sit the other way on a stair when two people are tired.

I fasten from below with the slab on horses and a bar clamp standing in as a third hand, pad on the show so the bar does not print. I do not flip a heavy slab to drive screws from above unless I have help. A flip is how you bruise an arris you already eased.

## Failures

Round holes in a strap. Seasonal split. Slot the strap.

I also centered a heavy base under a slab that was all mass on one side (a big live edge, a hollow on the other). The table stood up. Then it did not. Balance is joinery when the floor is the other half of the joint.

Epoxy as a movement solution: it is a fill. It is not a clamp that cancels July. I will fill a check if the customer wants a fill. I will not tell them the fill made the slab a panel.

The wrong-axis slot is the one I cut along the length because the channel already ran that way. The slab’s width is the movement. I had given August a slot it did not need and I had pinned the width like a fool. People slot the wrong axis. I have. I recut. I filled the useless slots with epoxy I then sanded flush on the iron, which is a confession the next person can still read.

## A stair I already lost once

I built a slab table as a statue — base welded, top screwed through round holes, the whole thing a single object. The door at the house was a 30-inch truth. I took the top off in the yard with a driver I borrowed, and I slotted the strap with a grinder I also borrowed. That is not a method I will recommend. That is a scar. The next slab leaves the shop in two piles.

A 65-foot shop will let you assemble a statue and forget the stair. The glass wall makes the assembled table look finished. The stair does not care about the glass wall.

## Finish both faces

A slab finished only on the show will cup toward the weather it did not get. I oil the underside. I do not have to make the underside pretty. I have to make it even. A cupping slab will spit out figure-eights or it will lift a screw to the end of a slot and then split.

I check cup with a pair of winding sticks across the width before I set fasteners, and again after the first week in the shop weather. A slab that sat by the glass wall on a South Arkansas afternoon will tell you a different width than the same slab on the shady end of the bench. I fasten in the weather I expect the table to live in, screws in the middle of the slots, and I do not chase a morning cup with a tighter screw.

## Steel that is too stiff

A steel rectangle that cannot rack is good for the base and hard on the slab if you attach the long edges dead-hard. Slot the long direction that is actually the width fight. Fix the center. The length is less of a fight.

I tell the customer the top will move and the fasteners are supposed to look like they have traveled in their slots by August. A screw that stays in the exact middle of a slot all year is a screw in a slab that was already dead, or a screw that is too tight. I would rather see travel in the slot than a new check in the face.

A glued prison is a split with a modern look. I have been asked to glue a slab to a plate. I have said no. Once is enough to say it.

## The judgment

Iron under a slab is a knockdown base and a set of fasteners that live in slots. The tree is still a tree. The stair is still a stair. If you cannot take the base off, you built a statue. If the screws cannot walk, you built a split. Do neither and call it a table.
