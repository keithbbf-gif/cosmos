The pin was an inch and a quarter at the baseline. I had been cutting drawer pins all week and my hands still wanted that little jewelry spacing. A blanket chest in 4/4 oak is not a drawer. Two people will lift it by the ends. The bottom will be full of wool or tools or the stuff a family calls “the chest.” The corner has to be a beam. If you cut pretty little tails here, you have made a lace beam. Lace beams open.

The craft pack already taught waste between the pins and why a shoulder is a shoulder. This is the scale-up, and the scale-up is where shops get cute. Cute is a gap after the first move across a porch.

I am not adding a slope or a bow. The advanced part is *mass*, *rack*, and a *lid* that is itself a frame. If that sounds basic, watch a chest lofted by the rim.

## Spacing that matches the load

I space pins so a hand can still read them from the sofa, but I will not go below a pin width that looks like it could be a small tenon. On thick oak I like fewer, fatter pins rather than a piano. A piano on a chest is a lot of end grain drinking glue and a lot of acute corners to blow.

Tails through, pins through, no secret unless the lid asked for a rim miter (that essay is in this pack). The chest wants to be seen as built.

Chopping waste at this scale is a different body. A big chisel, a mallet you are willing to hear, and a coping saw that can still get through. I do not router these for fun on a one-off; a router in thick oak is a burn and a smell. A jig for a run of chests is another shop.

## The bottom is a racking rail

A chest without a proper bottom is a picture frame you store blankets in. The bottom board (or a framed panel) ties the four sides. If you only nail a plywood afterthought into a rebate, the corners will still loft into a diamond when two people lift the ends. I let the bottom into a groove or a rebate that has glue on the long grain and room on the cross, or I frame a panel and I treat the frame as the rail the dovetails were waiting for.

Dry-lift the empty carcase. If the rim lozenges, fix it now. A lid will hide the lozenge until the hinges bind.

## The lid is a racking machine

A solid slab lid, hinged on one long side, is a lever. Every open is a twist on the back corners. Those corners need pins that are not shy, and a hinge rail that is not a 1/2-inch afterthought. I like a lid that is a frame-and-panel, or a slab with breadboard ends (the craft pack already argued breadboards). What I do not like is a heavy slab on tiny hinges into a thin back board that was also your pin board. The pin board is doing two jobs. Give it thickness.

Stay chains or a lid stay are not decoration. They keep the slab from going over-center and yanking the back pins. I have seen a chest that lost its back row because someone opened it against a wall and the lid became a pry bar. A stay is cheaper than a new back.

## Glue at this scale

Hide is still kind. Open time on four big corners in a warm shop is not kind. I glue two corners, let them sit, then the other two, if the chest is large. PVA in July is a sprint I will not run around a 4-foot box. Clamp with cauls. Check for wind. A winding chest becomes a lid problem you will chase for a day.

## Failures

I cut drawer-scale pins on a thick chest because I liked the look on the bench. After a move, the rim had a little lozenge you could see in the lid gap. I added a bottom frame I should have built first. The pins were not the only sinner, but they were part of the choir.

I also hinged into the top half-pin. The screw split the pin. Move the hinge down, or add a hinge rail. Pins are not screw blocks.

## Cauls, winding sticks, the bench rack

I keep two pairs of sloped cauls for chest work — not hopper sloped, just thick pads with a leather face — so a bar clamp on 4/4 oak does not print a dent I will sand into a dish. The clamps sit on the sides, not on the rim. Rim pressure on a freshly glued mitered cousin (or even a square through-dovetail) can creep a half-pin. Side pressure lets the tails do the lateral work they were cut for.

Before I call a glue-up done I lay winding sticks on the top edges, glass-wall light raking. A twist now is a lid that will never sit. I can still strap a diagonal and wait. After the lid is hinged I am chasing a bind with a plane I should have used on the carcase.

If I am alone I rack the empty carcase on the bench: one corner down, a shove on the opposite. Rude. Useful. A polite two-person lift can hide a soft diamond because both people square it with their hands. The bench shove does not do you that favor. I watch the rim. A little diamond is a bottom problem or a pin problem. I fix it before any hinge mortise.

## The lift I failed, then rebuilt

I had a practice oak chest in the shop — 4/4, about four feet, no named job — with drawer-scale pins I cut because my hands were still in jewelry mode. Empty, two people, off the bench toward the porch step. The rim lozenged enough that a straightedge clicked on one long edge and sat off on the other. I should have seen it in the bench shove. I added a framed panel in the bottom: rails grooved for a floating panel, the rails glued to the long grain of the sides and left free on the ends. The diamond calmed. The pins were still lace. I did not recut the corners on that sample. I recut the *next* carcase with fewer, fatter pins, and I built the bottom frame before I called the dry-fit done.

That next dry-lift was boring. Boring is the grade.

I also learned to finish the bottom rails. Chests leave this shop across a porch. Two people, a step, a turn. Hands find the bottom. A sliver of a half-pin on the underside is a cut. Ease it. The load is the porch, not the catalog pose.

## Lid iron that is not a pin

I add a hinge rail — a separate strip, same species, glued to the inside of the back board below the rim — when the back is already a pin board. Screws live in that rail. I pre-drill. A split in a back board that is also a pin board is a split that opens a corner. The chest takes a hit; the hinge should not be the hit.

The stay gets a block of its own, not a screw into a tail. I have used a simple chain and I have used a hinged stay. [VERIFY] what the shop actually hangs before a caption names a brand. The geometry matters more than the catalog: the stay must catch before the lid goes over-center into a wall. I mock the arc with the lid on two clamps before I drill.

A slab lid in January will cup toward the fire if you finish only the top. I finish both faces. A cupping lid is a racking machine with a mood. August will flatten it and then the stay looks long. Finish both sides the week you hang it, not the week you deliver.

## Glue in two bites

Four corners of 4/4 oak in a July shop is more glue than I can spread before the first corner skins. I glue a pair of ends to one long side, I check for square and wind, I wait. Then I bring the second long side. Hide gives me the minutes. PVA does not, not around a box I cannot reach across without walking. I would rather two honest sessions than one heroic failure.

A bottom frame before the lid exists. Winding sticks on the top edges. Filename pending shop pull. I do not need a busier tail pattern for the camera. Busy tails on a beam are lace.

## Next time

I space for a lift, I tie the bottom before I hang a lever, and I keep screws out of half-pins. The porch is the load. Indoor captions do not cancel the step. A chest that only looks right on the bench is a box. A chest that stays a rectangle when two people turn on a stair is the joint I was cutting — mass, not jewelry, and a lid that is not allowed to become a pry.
