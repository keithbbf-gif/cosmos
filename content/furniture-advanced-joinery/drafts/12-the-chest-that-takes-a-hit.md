The pin was an inch and a quarter at the baseline. I had been cutting drawer pins all week and my hands still wanted that little jewelry spacing. A blanket chest in 4/4 oak is not a drawer. Two people will lift it by the ends. The bottom will be full of wool or tools or the stuff a family calls “the chest.” The corner has to be a beam. If you cut pretty little tails here, you have made a lace beam. Lace beams open.

The craft pack already taught waste between the pins and why a shoulder is a shoulder. This is the scale-up, and the scale-up is where shops get cute. Cute is a gap after the first move across a porch.

I am not adding a slope or a bow. The advanced part is *mass*, *rack*, and a *lid* that is itself a frame. If that sounds basic, watch a chest lofted by the rim.

## Spacing that matches the load

I space pins so a hand can still read them from the sofa, but I will not go below a pin width that looks like it could be a small tenon. On thick oak I like fewer, fatter pins rather than a piano. A piano on a chest is a lot of end grain drinking glue and a lot of acute corners to blow.

Tails through, pins through, no secret unless the lid asked for a rim miter (that essay is in this pack). The chest wants to be seen as built.

Chopping waste at this scale is a different body. A big chisel, a mallet you are willing to hear, and a coping saw that can still get through. I do not router these for fun on a one-off; a router in thick oak is a burn and a smell. A jig for a run of chests is another shop.

## The bottom is a racking rail

A chest without a proper bottom is a picture frame you store blankets in. The bottom board (or a framed panel) ties the four sides. If you only nail a plywood afterthought into a rebate, the corners will still loft into a diamond when two people lift the ends. I let the bottom into a groove or a rebate that has glue on the long grain and room on the cross, or I frame a panel and I treat the frame as the rail the dovetails were waiting for.

Dry-lift the empty carcase. If the rim lozenges, fix it now. A lid will hide the lozenge until the hinges bind.

## The lid is a racking machine

A solid slab lid, hinged on one long side, is a lever. Every open is a twist on the back corners. Those corners need pins that are not shy, and a hinge rail that is not a 1/2-inch afterthought. I like a lid that is a frame-and-panel, or a slab with breadboard ends (the craft pack already argued breadboards). What I do not like is a heavy slab on tiny hinges into a thin back board that was also your pin board. The pin board is doing two jobs. Give it thickness.

Stay chains or a lid stay are not decoration. They keep the slab from going over-center and yanking the back pins. I have seen a chest that lost its back row because someone opened it against a wall and the lid became a pry bar. A stay is cheaper than a new back.

## Glue at this scale

Hide is still kind. Open time on four big corners in a warm shop is not kind. I glue two corners, let them sit, then the other two, if the chest is large. PVA in July is a sprint I will not run around a 4-foot box. Clamp with cauls. Check for wind. A winding chest becomes a lid problem you will chase for a day.

## Failures

I cut drawer-scale pins on a thick chest because I liked the look on the bench. After a move, the rim had a little lozenge you could see in the lid gap. I added a bottom frame I should have built first. The pins were not the only sinner, but they were part of the choir.

I also hinged into the top half-pin. The screw split the pin. Move the hinge down, or add a hinge rail. Pins are not screw blocks.

## Two people, an empty carcase

I ask for a second pair of hands and I lift the empty chest by the ends. I watch the rim. A little diamond is a bottom problem or a pin problem. I fix it before the lid. A lid on a diamond is a hinge bind and a gap that walks.

If I am alone I can still rack the carcase on the bench by pushing a corner. The bench test is ruder than a polite lift. I like rude tests.

## Lid stays

A chain, a hinged stay, a torque hinge — something that keeps the slab from going over-center into a wall. I have seen the back pins pay for a missing stay. The stay is cheaper.

Hinge rail: a separate piece if the back board is also a pin board and is already doing enough. Screws into a half-pin are a split I already owned.

## Bottom as a frame

A framed panel in the bottom is a rail around a panel. The rail ties the sides. The panel can float. A single plywood slab in a rebate can also tie if it is glued on the long grain and free on the cross, or if it is screwed in a way I can defend. A nailed-only bottom is a picture frame.

I wrote it in the body. I will write it here. Do not bring drawer spacing to a chest out of habit. The habit is for drawers. The chest will be lifted.

## Hinge screws

I pre-drill the hinge rail. A split in a back board that is also a pin board is a split that opens a corner. The chest takes a hit; the hinge should not be the hit.

<!-- PACK-W2L -->

## Shop morning, thick chest

Fat pins. Bottom that ties. Dry-lift. Lid that is a frame or a slab with a stay. Hinge rail with pre-drilled screws, not a half-pin. If the rim diamonds I fix it before the lid. A lid on a diamond is a bind I will chase for a day.

<!-- PACK-W3 -->

## Porch lift

Chests leave this shop across a porch. Two people, a step, a turn. That is the load. Indoor-only captions do not cancel the porch. I finish the bottom rails because they will be grabbed. I do not leave a sliver of a half-pin for a hinge screw to find.

I will not cut piano tails on 4/4 oak to make a photograph busier. Busy tails on a beam are lace. Lace opens.

## The judgment

A thick-corner chest is joinery at beam scale. Fat pins, a bottom that ties, a lid that cannot pry the back apart. Do not bring your drawer spacing to this job out of habit. The habit is for drawers. The chest will be lifted. Build for the lift.
