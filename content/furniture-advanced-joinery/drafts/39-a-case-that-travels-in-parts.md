The shelf came out the back talking, the same rising friction as always, and then it was in my hands and the case was two sides and a top. I had not glued the tails. I had waxed them. The customer wanted a bookcase that could leave an apartment without leaving the apartment in the stairwell. A sliding dovetail can be that joint if you design it to be done twice. Most of them are designed to be done once, which is why they seize.

The craft pack taught the glued third. This is the unglued, or lightly glued, modular cousin. The structural idea is the same. The politics are not. On a travel case I glue a third — one shelf, or a top rail that is allowed to marry — or I glue nothing and I stay out of the can. An inch of “just because” on every tail is how a knockdown becomes a house case in June.

## Fit for a second slide

I cut the tail a hair easier than a permanent joint. I ease the arrises. I wax. I do not taper so hard that the shelf only seats in one humidity. A one-way taper is a one-way joint. Knockdown wants a tail that can come out without taking the socket’s show corner with it.

A stopped socket still keeps the show edge clean. The stop takes a beating on every teardown. I leave a little extra meat at the stop, or I put a small hardwood shoe in the stop if the case will travel often. Soft stop, blown corner, dark hole from the sofa.

## The sled that keeps the bit honest

I cut sockets and tails on a router sled I built for this — a plywood carriage, a fence I can reset, the same bit in the collet for both halves of one case. The bit that cut the socket cuts the tail. I do not switch bits because one is already in the collet for another job. Collet convenience is a stairwell seizure.

I ease the tail tips with one pass of a block plane, not with sandpaper that rounds the cheeks. Rounded cheeks are a sloppy second slide. A sloppy second slide is a shelf that tips.

The stop-shoe is a scrap of maple or hickory, inlet at the socket end, lettered to that socket with a chisel nick. I chop the inlet after the socket is routed, so the shoe’s shoulder is the stop the tail will hit, not a second guess in front of a routed wall. I glue the shoe with hide and a single pin from the inside face, proud enough to pull if a future teardown wrecks it. I send a spare shoe if the case will travel more than once. A blown stop is a show-edge hole. The shoe is cheaper than a plug I will always see.

I time the first teardown with a kitchen driver. If the back screws need a tool I will not send, I change the heads. Knockdown that needs a shop is a house case with optimism. Optimism seizes at the stop.

## What keeps it from being a house of cards

Unglued sliding tails will hold a shelf against tip-out. They will not keep a tall case from racking into a parallelogram if there is no back, no top that is a real rail, no diagonal. I use a back panel that screws off, or a top that is itself a sliding dovetail plus a couple of accessible screws, or a removable back frame. Something. A knockdown case with no back is a ladder.

The climbing and bowed sliding-dovetail essays in this pack still apply if the case is canted or the shelf is a smile. For travel, I keep the geometry square unless the piece paid for the circus.

A long unglued shelf will still sag. The tail is not a beam. I keep travel shelves shorter than my pride, or I add a center stile that is itself a sliding piece I can pull. A sagging shelf in a KD case is a shelf I made too long and then blamed on wax.

## Failures

I glued “just an inch” and then the inch was the inch that seized in June. For travel, I now glue nothing on the tails, or I glue a block that is not the tail — or I pick one third and I marry it on purpose. Be decisive.

I also blew a stop on the third teardown. Shoe. Meat. Do not treat a stopped socket like a disposable.

A case I lettered on the inside with pencil that wore off: the sides were not a pair anymore in my head. Stamp, or a chisel mark, or a story that only fits one way.

The bit-swap seizure was mine. I finished the sockets on a 14-degree bit, then I cut the tails the next morning with the 12-degree that was already warm. The tails entered like furniture and left like a wrecking story. I keep the case’s bit in a labeled bag clipped to the sled now. The bag is uglier than a tidy drawer. The tails still slide.

## A lettered teardown

I letter the inside of every side and the underside of every shelf. A, B, top, bottom. I do not rely on “the pretty face.” Pretty faces get finished the same. Letters live in a place a customer will not see and a future me will.

The first teardown in the shop is mandatory. If a tail seizes in the shop in June, it will seize in a stairwell in June. I wax, I try, I sneak. I do not force a seized tail toward a stopped show edge.

## The back that unscrews

A 1/4 or 1/2 back, rabbeted, screws on a regular grid. The back is the diagonal the sliding tails are not. I can pull the back in two minutes and the case becomes a set of boards. I cannot pull a nailed-and-glued back without a story. For travel, screws.

A removable back frame — a little door of a back — is nicer on a piece that will be seen from behind. Same idea. Something you can take off without a wrecking bar.

Assembly in a stairwell is back last, not back first. I have stood a pair of sides on a landing, slid the shelves from the back while a door was being held, and only then hung the back. If you hang the back in the shop and carry a case that is already a case, you built a house case and you brought it to a turn. The joint’s job is to let you carry boards around that turn.

## Humidity and wax

Wax in a hot shop can get onto a finish if you are sloppy. I wax the tail cheeks and I wipe the show. I rewax after a season if the case lives with me. A customer will not rewax. So the first fit has to be the fit that will still slide in year three, which means a little easier than my pride likes.

June under the glass wall is the rehearsal. I slide every shelf once in that heat before I like the case. A fit I am sweating on the last inch is too proud for a landing. I sneak now.

I will not sell a nailed-back oak case as modular. Modular is for parts. Parts that do not part are a house case. House cases are allowed. The word is not.

## What I want from D:\BBF

Parts on a blanket, tails visible, letters readable on the undersides. The assembled show edge. A hand sliding a shelf from the back — the talking friction. The sled with the labeled bit bag if that shop frame exists. A stop-shoe in its inlet, and the spare beside it, if anyone shot the teardown. Do not invent the frame.

## The judgment

A case that travels in parts is a sliding dovetail you did not marry — or you married one third and you can name it. Easy fit, wax, a stop that can take a beating, a back you can unscrew on a landing. The craft-pack tail stays in the house. This one goes down the stair. If it will not come out without a story, you built the staying kind again, and the glue can should have stayed shut or opened for a third you meant.
