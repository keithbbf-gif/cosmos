The shelf wanted to be level. The case side wanted to lean, a shallow cant I had already committed to on the drawing. A square sliding dovetail would have put the shelf on a slope or the socket through the show face at one end. I needed a socket that climbed — the same depth from the inside face, the same tail angle, and a path that was not square to the side’s edge. That is a compound sliding dovetail. It is also a good way to spend a morning on a jig you will use once.

The basic sliding dovetail is already in the craft pack: shelf from the back, glue the third, stop the show edge. This piece assumes you can cut that joint in your sleep and then someone asks for a magazine rack, a lectern, a sloped bookcase, a hopper cabinet with a level shelf. The shelf is still a beam. The side is still a board that moves. The meeting is no longer a right angle.

## What has to stay true

Three things, and only three:

1. The shelf’s top face is level in the room.
2. The tail’s cheeks are the dovetail angle you chose.
3. The socket’s depth does not break the show face.

Everything else — the path of the socket across the side, the shape of the shelf end — is slaved to those three. I draw the side in elevation, I draw the shelf as a level line, and I draw the socket as a band of constant depth behind the inside face. The band is a parallelogram. If you draw a rectangle, you have already failed the lean.

## Jig or saw

A router wants a fence. The fence for a climbing socket is a board clamped so its edge is parallel to the *shelf*, not to the case edge. I make a plywood saddle that references the inside face and the level line, and I run the bit in that saddle. The first pass is shallow. The last pass is the one that scares you at the thin end of a cant.

Hand-cut: you can saw the socket walls if you are patient and the shelf is not a production run. Two lines, two slopes. I have done one by hand for a lectern. I would not do a bookcase of them by hand unless the week had nowhere to be.

The tail on the shelf end is a compound cut. The end is not square to the shelf’s long edge; it is square to the *side’s inside face* in the installed position. That means you cut the shelf long, you scribe, or you trust the drawing and you sneak. I cut long and I scribe from a dry-fit of the case. The case has to be closed enough to tell the truth. A pair of sides in a rack is not a case.

## Glue, movement, the third that still matters

The shelf grain still runs into the side. The side still moves. Glue still belongs in a third — usually the front, so the reveal at the show edge stays shut. The cant does not cancel wood movement. It just makes the split look like a design feature until August.

On a plywood case the movement dies and you can glue more. Plywood cants are a different aesthetic. I will use them. I will not call them a solid hopper.

Wax the tail. The climb means more surface and more chances to stick at four inches. If it sticks, do not become a hammer. Back out. Sneak. The stopped show edge is still the place you will blow if you panic.

## When a dado is the adult

A sloped dado, screws from the outside, plugs or a face frame: that is most of the world’s sloped bookcases. Fine. The compound sliding tail is for the shelf that is also a structural rail, the case that will be dragged, the piece where you do not want a row of plugs on the cheek. If you do not need that, do not cut this joint to prove you can. The proof is a shelf that is level and a side that is still a side next year.

## Failures

I set the saddle to the case edge instead of the level line. The shelf sat on a slope you could set a pencil rolling on. I recut the tail and I lost the stop at the show edge. The dark socket winked from the sofa. I plugged it with a sliver of the same oak. I know which sliver.

I also glued a full-length tail on a solid canted side because the dry-slide felt “too loose” at the back. The side opened along the socket in the first summer. Loose at the back is often the movement allowance you were afraid of. A little shine on the tail is a passing grade. A split is not.

## Saddle from a level line

I clamp a straightedge to the inside face so its edge is the shelf’s world, not the case edge’s world. I run the bit in that saddle, shallow first. I check depth at both ends of the climb. The thin end of a cant is where show faces die.

The shelf is cut long. I scribe from a case that is already a case. Two sides in a rack are not a case.

## What I want from D:\BBF

The saddle on a leaning side. A level sitting on a dry-fit shelf. A show-edge stop that did not blow.

## Dado, again

If I do not need a clean cheek, I dado and I sleep. This joint is for the shelf that is also a rail. The craft-pack sliding tail remains the square default.

## Shelf as a beam

I check the shelf for cup before I cut the compound tail. A cupped shelf in a climbing socket is a jam at one arris and a gap at the other. Flatten first, then scribe, then slide.

## Shop morning, canted shelf

Case sides closed enough to be a case. Level line on the inside. Saddle to that line. Shallow pass, then depth from the thin end. Shelf long, scribed. Glue a third. If the shelf rolls a pencil I recut. I do not call a slope a design unless I drew it.

## Oak in a leaning case

White oak sides in this county will still move across their width when the glass wall cooks the bench and then a rain week walks in. A climbing socket is a long wound in that side. I leave the back of the tail able to shine. Shine is not a split. A split follows the socket like a creek. I would rather a customer ask why the back of the shelf looks burnished than why the case opened.

I will not cut this joint in a painted pine bookcase that is really a dado job. Paint does not cancel a compound tail. It hides the blowout until the first move.

## The judgment

A climbing socket is a level shelf in a leaning world. Parallel to the shelf, constant depth, glue in a third. The craft-pack sliding dovetail still applies; the cant just revoked your right to a square fence. If that sentence feels like too much work, dado it and go flatten a top. If the case has to carry itself without screws in the cheek, build the saddle and climb.
