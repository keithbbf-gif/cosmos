The rail came off the form with steam still in it, a little ghost of water on the oak, the strap mark a stripe I would plane later. It looked like the drawing. It would not look like the drawing in the morning. Springback is not a surprise. It is the wood remembering a straighter life. If I had cut tenons the night I bent it, I would have cut tenons for a chair that exists for one evening.

The craft pack already walked a spokeshave around a curve and a steam-bent slat in the materials pile. This is the joinery: a bent rail that has to enter two mortises in two posts, level, with shoulders that shut on a post that is not curved. The curve is in the rail. The posts are the square world. The tenon is the translator.

## Bend first, join second

I cut the rail blank long and a little wide. I bend. I let it dry on the form — hours to a day, depending on thickness and the shop. [VERIFY] any number before it becomes a caption. Then I let it sit off the form, stickered, until a moisture meter says it is in the same country as the posts. Then I mark tenons.

People cut tenons first because the blank is easier to hold when it is straight. Those tenons then point at a memory. After the bend, the ends have rotated a little in space. The mortises, if you already cut them, no longer agree. You can sneak a shoulder. You cannot sneak a tenon that is now entering the mortise on a diagonal the chisel did not plan.

If the design forces tenons-first — a production run with a dedicated tenoner — you overbend the form so the dried rail lands where the tenons need to be. That is a shop that has already wasted a pile of rails on test bends. A one-off shop bends, waits, then cuts.

## Mortises in the posts

The posts can be straight or they can have a little rake. The mortise is usually plumb to the post’s face or plumb to the floor. I pick the floor if the crest is a level line in the room, and I pick the face if the post is the only reference I trust. Write it down. A crest that is high on one side is a chair you will see from the sofa.

Depth: the rail is already a little angry. A shallow mortise that the tenon bottoms in will push the posts apart. I leave a gap at the bottom of the mortise, the usual furniture habit, more important here because the rail may still move a hair as it finishes drying.

## Shoulders on a curve

The shoulder of a crest-rail tenon is not a square doughnut. The rail’s face is curved. The post is flat (or turned). The shoulder has to be *scribed* to the post, or the post has to be notched to the rail. I scribe the shoulder to the post. A pair of compasses, a knife, a chisel. The show line is the scribe. Glue will not hide a gap that follows a radius.

A bare-faced tenon is common here — one cheek, one shoulder — if the rail is thin. Twin tenons if the rail is a beam. I will not print a thickness as law. I will say: if the tenon is a popsicle stick, the crest is a decoration and the first person who leans back will teach you about decoration.

## Species, grain, the ones that break

Oak bends if the grain is kind. Walnut can. Hickory will embarrass you with how well it bends and then it will try to spring home like a trap. Air-dried, straight grain, no runout on the tension side. A knot on the outside of the bend is a break you can schedule. I cut extras. The extra is part of the joint. [VERIFY] species actually used on the floor before a byline claims a house wood.

Green wood is a different shop — Windsor, later in this pack. Kiln-dried steam-bending is this shop’s more common fight: more breaks, more springback, still possible.

## Failures

I tenoned the night of the bend. In the morning the rail was a different smile and the tenons cocked. I recut the rail from a second blank. The first became a story stick for springback, which is a polite name for scrap.

I also steamed a rail with a small check I thought would stay a small check. The check became a split on the form. Checks grow in steam. Reject them before the box.

A metal strap that was too narrow left a dent I had to take out with a spokeshave, and I lost the thickness I wanted for the tenon. Wider strap, a leather pad, less heroics.

## Extras

I cut two blanks if the crest matters. Steam breaks rails. A knot on the tension face is a break I can schedule. I reject checks before the box. I wait on the form, then I wait off the form, then I meter, then I tenon.

A metal strap too narrow is a dent that costs tenon thickness. Wide strap, a pad, less heroics.

## Scribe the post

The crest’s shoulder is a scribe to a post that is flat or turned. I do not table-saw a square shoulder on a cylinder and butter the moon. The shoulder essay in this pack is the rest of that sentence.

## Tension face

I mark the outside of the bend before the box. A knot there is a no. A knot on the inside can sometimes live. I would rather cut a second blank than steam a no.

## Shop morning, crest

Two blanks if it matters. Reject the tension-face knot. Steam. Wide strap. Wait. Meter. Offer to posts. Tenon. Scribe. A rail I tenoned the night of the bend is a rail I recut. I have the story stick of springback. It is scrap.

## Steam and a meter

I do not guess “dry enough” because the rail feels like a rail. I meter it against the posts. A wet crest in a dry post will shrink and then the shoulders are a memory. A dry crest in a wetter post is a looser story. Same country. [VERIFY] the numbers before a caption prints them.

I will not steam kiln-dried hickory as my first experiment on a customer’s chair. Hickory will bend and then it will try to go home like a trap. Extras. Always extras.

## The judgment

Steam still in the rail means the joint is not ready. Wait until the rail and the posts are in the same moisture country, then cut tenons that enter mortises you already trust, and scribe the shoulders to the posts. The curve is a process. The mortise is a hole that does not care about your excitement. Let the rail finish becoming itself before you ask it to become a chair.
