The bevel gauge would not stay where I left it. The hopper side was already a parallelogram in elevation, and every time I set the stock on the bench the blade pointed at a different story. I locked it to the actual side — the board I had already planed to thickness — and I carried that angle to the end grain like it was a secret. A square dovetail on this box is a joint that only exists on paper. The sides lean. The tails have to lean with them.

I am not talking about a planter you hide behind the shop with a plastic liner. I am talking about a magazine box, a coal scuttle cousin, a wastebasket a customer will pick up by the rim. The moment you pick it up, the rim is a hoop in tension and the base is trying to stay a rectangle. If the joinery is square and the sides are not, the shoulders become wedges. Wedges open.

## What “compound” actually means here

A common through-dovetail is two boards meeting at ninety degrees, tails and pins sawn square to the face. The hopper is four boards that meet at ninety in plan and something else in elevation. The end of each board is a parallelogram, not a rectangle. If you saw the tails plumb to the bench, you have cut them square to a world the box does not live in.

The compound is the second angle. You keep the tail spacing you like — I still like pins that do not look like piano doves — and you tilt the saw so the kerf follows the slope of the adjoining side. The baseline is not parallel to the end. The baseline is parallel to the *rim* or to the *floor*, depending on which face you called the show. Pick one and write it on the board. I have mixed them. The dry-fit then is a lesson you do not need twice.

Some people call this a “hopper joint” and leave it. The name is not the work. The work is: every shoulder has to bear along a line that is not the line your square wants to give you.

## Layout that does not invent a degree

I do not start with “twelve degrees” unless the drawing already earned it. I start with the board width, the finished height, and how much the rim is larger than the base. A story stick is kinder than a calculator when the shop is loud. Mark the outside face of each side. Mark the top. If you flip a side and keep cutting, you will cut a left-hand slope on a right-hand board and the box will twist like a bad stair.

Transfer the slope to the end with the bevel. Then set the marking gauge from the *inside* face or the *outside* — again, pick one — and scribe the baseline so it is the same distance from the rim all the way around. On a hopper the “end” is already a slope, so a gauge that rides the end grain will walk. Ride the face. Pare the end square to the face first if the board came off the saw with a little roll. You cannot lay out a compound joint on a drunk end.

Pins or tails first is the same argument as a square box, only meaner. I cut tails first on hoppers because I can still sneak a pin. If you cut pins first on a slope, sneaking is a chisel in a corner that is already a knife.

## Sawing without chasing the line

The saw wants to stand up. Your wrist wants to stand up with it. I have a habit of setting a scrap with the same slope behind the work so the saw plate has a neighbor to look at. Some shops jig the piece in a vise so the cut is plumb and the *work* is tilted. That is the cleaner way if you have the vise real estate. In a 65-foot shop with a bench that already holds a glue-up, I often tilt the saw. Either method is fine. Mixing them on one box is how you get a pin that is fat at the top and starved at the baseline.

Chop the waste the way you always do, but watch the baseline. On a hopper the baseline is closer to the rim on one visual and farther on another. It is easy to overchop the acute corner. That corner is the one the customer’s thumb finds.

A coping saw through the waste still saves the chisel. Do not be proud.

## Dry-fit is a slope test

Square boxes tell you they are wrong with a gap. Hoppers tell you they are wrong by walking. Set the four sides on the bench without the bottom. If the rim is a trapezoid that will not sit, you have one side with a different slope, or one pair of tails sawn to the bench instead of the bevel. Chalk the high shoulders. A shoulder plane can still save you if the error is a hair. If the error is a degree, you are making a new side.

I dry-fit before I cut the bottom rebate or the liner. The bottom is a rectangle only if you measured the *inside* after the joinery closed. Measuring the drawing and cutting a bottom is how you get a panel that holds the box out of square forever.

Glue is ordinary. Clamping is not. Bar clamps on a hopper want to skate downhill. I use cauls with a matching slope, or I strap the rim and the base separately and let the tails do the lateral work they were cut for. Hide gives you a second chance if a side creeps. PVA gives you a box that is whatever it was at minute eight.

## The bottom, the liner, the thing people grab

A hopper that is only four dovetailed sides is a pretty accident. The bottom has to float or it has to be a frame. A glued-in plywood panel is fine if you admit the sides will still move across their width and you left a little in the rebate. A solid bottom glued all around is a split waiting for August in South Arkansas.

If the piece is a wastebasket or a kindling box, people will grab the rim. The rim is end grain and dovetail end grain in the same handful. Ease it. A sharp arris on a hopper rim is a cut you will hear about.

I have lined one with copper and one with a simple oilcloth. The liner is not joinery. The liner is why the joinery has to stay tight when the interior gets wet. Compound tails do not like a swollen liner shoving the acute corners. Leave room or the pretty joint becomes a sprung joint.

## Failures I will own

I once laid out from the long rim and then cut the short base as if it were a square box “just for the pins.” The pins were square. The tails were compound. The dry-fit was a staircase. I kept the sides for shop scrap and I cut four new ones. That is cheaper than filling a compound gap with epoxy and hoping raking light never visits.

I once trusted a miter saw’s detent for the side slope and then wondered why two opposite sides did not match. Detents are for casing. Hoppers want the bevel you locked to the board.

A router jig for compound tails exists in catalogs. I have used a simple sloping cradle and a guide bushing for a run of four identical boxes. For one box, the saw is faster than the jig you will tune for an afternoon. [VERIFY] before anyone prints a house jig as standard.

## What this is not

This is not a secret miter. This is not a bow-front drawer. Those are other arguments in this pack. The hopper is the first compound most shops actually meet, because planters and magazine boxes show up in the same year you think you have dovetails “handled.”

The judgment is small. Tilt the saw to the life the board is going to live, or the shoulder will live a different life than the side. The bevel gauge is the joint. The tails are just the part you can photograph.
