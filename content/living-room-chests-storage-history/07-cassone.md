# Cassone — a chest that had to walk through the street

The Italian marriage chest is the one museums taught us to want. Gilded gesso eagles. A painted triumph. Arms of two families on a box long enough to be a coffin, and sometimes shaped like one. The Met will show you a Tuscan cassone from about 1425–50 in pine and poplar, the decoration molded and gilt, and another from about 1480–95 that is more than six feet long. You can believe, looking at those, that a chest is a painting that happens to open.

That is a rich person’s truth. It is not the only one.

Fifteenth-century inventories often called the wedding pair *forzieri da sposa* — betrothal chests. *Cassone* in the documents can mean the sarcophagus-shaped kind, the antique quotation. We flattened the words. We also flattened the job. The chest held the bride’s textiles and the metal that counted. It was commissioned, often by the groom’s side, in the months between the public agreement and the day she changed houses. A good painted front took a workshop about a month. Two chests, two months, plus the carpenter, the gilder, the lock. Apollonio di Giovanni’s shop book, known from a later copy, is why we can say “pairs” as a practice and not as a romance.

Then the chests had to walk. The procession from her house to his was a civic event and a nervous one. Florentine statute in 1415 forbade throwing stones or garbage at the couple’s house, which is a law you write after the garbage. The cassoni went in the street with the dowry on them. They were furniture as announcement. When they arrived they lived in the bedchamber with the lettiera and the spalliere, not in a room we would call a living room. There was no such room to call.

Boccaccio had already used a cassone as a place to hide a lover, which is the other truth about a big lid: it is a room. Museums prefer the triumph of Scipio. Households knew the lid also closed on secrets and on laundry.

By the sixteenth century the painted front is less the fashion; carving and the architectural chest take more of the money. By the nineteenth century dealers are cutting fronts out of wrecked boxes and selling the paintings as panels. A lot of “cassone panels” in collections never had a floor. That is worth remembering when a living room puts a “Florentine chest” under a mirror. Ask whether the box was born a box.

Could you put a real cassone in a living room? You could. People do, if they have the floor and the insurance. It will look like a tomb that learned to gild. It will not look like storage. That is the inheritance: the Western living room still wants a chest to be a little ceremonial. Lane understood that with cedar and a lock. The Florentines understood it with a parade.
