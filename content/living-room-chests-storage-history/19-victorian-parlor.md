# The parlor that would not store anything

A middle-class American parlor before the Civil War, and for a while after, was a room with a job that excluded the chest. Company. Courtship. The piano if you had one. The dead, laid out, in the houses that still did that at home. Sundays. The room was the household’s public face. Faces do not have drawers if they can help it.

What the parlor had instead was display storage — whatnots, étagères, glass cabinets for the figurine and the shell. That is storage that denies it is storage. The object is on view. The extra blankets are upstairs. The extra life is elsewhere. When Gül Kaleş tracks the American parlor from 1850 to 1890, the useful observation is not a furniture plate. It is that children and comfort get in. The room begins to be used. The number of objects, later, has to fall, because you cannot live in a shop window.

Bok, in 1900, calls useless bric-a-brac the enemy. He thinks the Queen Anne clutter of the urban middle class is making women sick with attendance. He is conservative about gender and sharp about junk. The living room he wants is a clearance. Clearance needs a box. The chest, which the parlor had snubbed, becomes respectable the moment the room is for Tuesdays.

So the Victorian parlor is in this history as a negative space. It is why so many living-room chests still look a little ashamed — skirted, lamped, runnered, styled. They are entering a room that spent fifty years learning to hide work. A lidded chest in a parlor would have looked like a bedroom error. A lidded chest in a 1915 living room looks like simplicity. Same box. New permission.

If you inherit a parlor set and a cedar chest, the set will try to reinstall the Sunday rules. The chest will sit there like a person who brought a suitcase to dinner. I know which one I would keep.
