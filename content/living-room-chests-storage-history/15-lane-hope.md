# Lane, cedar, and the gift that starts the home

Red cedar — *Juniperus virginiana*, the American one — keeps moths honest. That is a chemistry you can smell. A lidded chest lined with it will hold wool. That is the material fact. Everything else in the twentieth-century “hope chest” is a script.

Altavista, Virginia, 1912. A small box company in trouble. John Edward Lane buys it at auction for five hundred dollars and puts his son Edward Hudson, twenty-one, in charge. They do not, at first, put the family name on the door. Standard Red Cedar Chest Company. Seven employees. A cabinet-room man who had been John’s professor at VPI. Rail junction: Virginian and Southern. The chests can leave town.

The First World War teaches the plant to make pine ammunition boxes at speed. After the war the speed stays and the cedar comes back. In the 1920s they advertise nationally. The chest is not sold as a box. It is sold as a beginning. The line in the Virginia Museum record: “The gift that starts the home.” A young woman stores linens and the idea of a kitchen she does not yet have. The lock is part of the story. So is the expectation that a dealer, a father, or a man in uniform will buy it.

J. Arthur Krauss, sales manager, later told Helen Hughes Lane that the Girl Graduate Plan was the biggest promotion he ever thought of. Officially 1930. Spade work in the twenties. The shop-floor story — the one former employees repeat — is that men made small chests from cedar cut-offs, jewelry-box size, and the women liked them. Krauss wanted them in the plant. A vice president did not. The miniatures happened anyway, some early ones bought from Pilliod in Ohio and Peterson Brothers in Chicago, then a miniature department in Altavista after the 1930 fire. The plan: a dealer gives a graduating girl a small chest and a discount on a large one. Parents walk into the furniture store. Lane meets the household before the household exists.

Lane’s own later numbers: between half and two-thirds of American girl graduates offered a box at the height, 1950s–60s; on the order of twenty-seven million miniatures all told. Those are company numbers. I will not swear to the census. I will swear to the shape of the funnel.

World War II ads put the same box next to a uniform. Buy it before you ship. The chest waits. The woman waits. Lane waits to sell the bedroom suite. By the 1960s the company is saying the quiet part: the cedar chest is a gateway product. Living-room furniture, dining-room furniture, recliners. The hope chest is not the end of the purchase. It is the first login.

The Altavista plant closed in 2001. The chests are still in attics and in living rooms, often under a window, often with a tapestry runner so nobody remembers they were supposed to be for a wedding. I have seen them used for photographs, for tax returns, for the extra blankets that Bok’s living room finally allowed in the front of the house. The moths still respect the lining if the lining is real.

This is the American living-room chest in industrial form. Not a Hadley. Not a cassone. A factory in a rail town that learned, from ammunition and from graduation lists, that a lid is a place you can store a future. The future they stored was narrow. The box outlived the script. That is the kindest thing I can say for it, and also the least.
