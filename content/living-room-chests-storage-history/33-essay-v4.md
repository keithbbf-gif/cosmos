# Draft 33 — tighter

The living room is younger than the box it keeps trying to hide.

A chest is a lid on a well. Six boards if the shop is ordinary: ends that become feet, sides nailed on, a bottom, a cover. The corners want to tear. Iron straps tell that truth. You sit on the lid. You keep in it what a house cannot leave out. In a poor room the chest is bench, table, and cupboard. Europe ran this object for centuries without a special name for the room. It sat where the life was.

Money made chests that had to walk. Fifteenth-century Tuscan marriage pairs — *forzieri* in the inventories, *cassoni* in our museums — took a workshop about a month per painted front and went through the street with the dowry on them. Florence had already forbidden throwing garbage at the couple’s house. Then the boxes lived in the bedchamber. Not in a living room. There was none.

Around 1600 an English joiner puts a sliding box in the bottom of a chest. Till, drawing box, drawer. You can reach a stocking without unstacking the well. The hybrid with drawers under a lid is seventeenth-century; “mule chest” is a name from about 1911. By the Restoration a city case can be all drawers. You stop sitting on storage and start facing it.

The French call the convenient case *commode*. Cressent’s, delivered August 1738 to Louis XV’s bedroom at La Muette, is a dated royal box in a sleeping room. Tallboys and highboys take the stack up like a closet that has not been framed. They are chamber furniture. A bonnet top in a television room has lost a fight with scale.

Connecticut Valley joiners, in the same long century, carved tulip-and-stem oak we file as Hadley and rosettes we still call sunflowers. More than a hundred of the first group; dozens of the second. Peter Blin’s 1681 credit is a document, not a signature on every front. The initials usually belong to the woman whose cloth went in. The houses had no living rooms.

The living room is an American winning name. The 1890s decorating books already use it. Edward Bok did not invent it. He sold plans, from 1895, that left the parlor off the paper, and he treated bric-a-brac as a nervous disease. He could not see what a drawing room drew except too much money and no taste. By about 1910 the magazine house prefers the new words. Permission, not coinage, is the event. A room used on Tuesday needs a place to put Tuesday. The parlor had refused that for decades: Sundays, company, display that denied it was storage. When the front room has to live, the chest comes in the wrong door. That relocation is the subject. There is no period type called living-room chest.

Lane made the inheritance a factory. Altavista, 1912, rails, cedar for moths. Ammunition boxes in the First World War; then the speed applied to a trousseau. “The gift that starts the home.” 1930: the Girl Graduate Plan, a miniature into a girl’s hands, parents into the store. Later company counts talk about tens of millions of miniatures. The same lid sits next to a uniform in the 1940s ads. By the 1960s the chest is a gateway to the rest of the furniture line. The plant closes in 2001. The boxes stay, often under a window, holding less than a wedding.

Radio put on a cabinet so the old parlor would take a machine. A 1928 set is even named Credenza. Television keeps the long face. The Eames Storage Unit, 1950–55, says “living room” in the copy and means it: steel, Masonite, five years of product. Storage becomes a horizon. The lid survives as an ottoman, which is a six-board in upholstery, and as a trunk used for a table.

Tansu belong in a different manners book — carried, stored out of sight, classed by job, recorded as a type in the Genroku era, wheeled already in the 1657 fire. Bandaji open by the half-front. If you put them in an American sitting room, keep the names. “Asian chest” is a salad.

What the box holds now is leftover narrative: blankets, cables, a year of paper, the game that does not match the idea of the household. A lid postpones. A drawer files. The historically responsible act in a real room is small. Right word. Room for the handles. A top you can clear with one hand. Tuesday, not a shrine.
