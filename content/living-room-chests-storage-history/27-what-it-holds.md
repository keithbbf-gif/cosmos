# What a living-room chest actually holds

Not dowries, mostly.

Blankets, because the room is allowed to get cold on purpose. The extra throw that the sofa is already wearing one of. Board games that do not fit the idea of yourself. Cables. The manual for the thing you will not repair. Photographs in a tin. Tax years. A dead laptop. Children’s drawings you cannot throw away and cannot look at every day. Whiskey, if the chest is a sideboard in denial. Vinyl, if you still have the argument. Linens, if the Lane script stuck in your family and someone still irons.

The medieval chest held the household’s movable wealth because the house might fail. The living-room chest holds the household’s leftover narrative because the house did not fail, not this year, and the leftover still has to go somewhere.

That is why the lid matters. A drawer is a file. A lid is a decision you postpone. You can drop a life in a lid-chest and close it. A drawer asks you to sort. People who say they are “bad at storage” often just need a lid.

I would rather a living-room chest hold ugly useful things than a curated stack of books with the titles facing the camera. The room is for living. The box is for the part of living that does not need an audience. If you style the interior of a chest for a photograph, you have rebuilt a parlor inside the one piece of furniture that was supposed to end the parlor.
