Wood lies with stain. Hardware has a harder time.

**Medieval and country chests.** Strap hinges. A hasp. Nails with heads that were not trying to disappear. If the iron is thin and even and “hand-hammered” in a pattern that repeats every three inches, it came from a catalog that also sells pirate hooks.

**Drawers.** Early English: no proud brass, just a pull that is a hole or a wooden knob or a simple drop. Restoration and after: brass, then more brass. Willow brasses, batwings, bail pulls, rosettes. Hepplewhite loves an oval. Chippendale a plate with a drawing-book ancestry. Victorian a preference for showing you paid for metal. Campaign: flush, because a protruding handle is a snag and a bruise.

**Tansu.** Iron as a corner and a carry point. *Mochiokuri* handles. Lock bars. After the 1880s, pressed decoration that can look older than it is. Sendai work can be very fine without being courtly. If someone calls every iron blossom “samurai,” they are selling.

**Bandaji.** The metal *is* the face. Thick plates, cutouts, hinges you can read from across the room. Regional. Do not mix a Jeolla face with a Gyeonggi story because both are yellow brass in a photograph.

**Lane.** Cheap enough locks and lid stays. The hardware is not the point; the smell and the ad are the point. A replaced stay is normal. A replaced lining is a small death.

**Living-room now.** Soft-close, push-to-open, a finger-pull routed in the edge so the chest can pretend it has no hardware. That is the parlor coming back as minimalism. I would rather see a knob. A knob admits that a person with a hand is invited.

The test I use on a used chest: unscrew one pull if I am allowed. Is the wood under it pale in the shape of the plate? Good. Has the plate been moved an inch to hide a break? Say so. Are there extra holes? A life. Hardware is the diary the lid does not keep.
