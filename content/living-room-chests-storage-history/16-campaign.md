British officers in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries needed furniture that could survive a ship and a mule and a damp station. The campaign chest is the type that still looks like that job after a century in a sitting room: two cases that stack, flush brass pulls that will not snag a lashing, corner guards, side handles, a height you can crate. Lost Art Press will give you a box to imagine — about forty by forty by twenty-two. Mahogany if you are looking at the British archetype. Teak turns up where the shop and the insects demanded it.

Earlier chests: fewer guards, pulls that still remember a swan’s neck, a little moulding. Later Victorian: more brass, rectangular flush plates, a standardized face you can date from across the room. Some have a secretaire drawer — leather, pigeonholes — because an officer is also a clerk. Bramah locks show up when the owner could pay for a lock that had a reputation.

India-made and China-made campaign is a real trade, not a footnote. I am not collapsing it into “British” because the brass matches. Different shops, different woods, different lies in the catalog.

The living-room afterlife is almost comic. A thing designed to be knocked down and lashed is prized because it looks like it has been somewhere. It has. Then it sits. The flush handles, which exist so a crate can close, become the reason the piece can live in a tight hall or behind a sofa without catching a coat. Function survives as manners.

I like campaign furniture in a living room more than I like most revival chests, because it does not pretend it was carved for a conversation. It pretends it might leave. Even if it never will. That is a more honest theater than “ Tuscan accent chest.”

If you buy one: look at the split. The two cases should meet like they have met a hundred times. Look at the screw slots and the dovetails. Look at whether the brass sits in a recess or floats on a repair. And do not let anyone tell you the bun feet are original without turning the chest over. Feet are the first thing a traveling box loses, and the first thing a dealer replaces.
