# Highboy, tallboy, and the chest that left the floor

Once the drawers had won, someone stacked a second case on the first, or put the whole stack on a stand with legs. England said tallboy. America said highboy. The dates you can defend without a fight are rough: the fashion is an eighteenth-century one, strongest before 1775, lingering after because a tall chest is a useful wall.

The point of the height is not grandeur, though grandeur came free. The point is the same as the first drawer: get the linen off the floor and into a place your back can stand. A lidded chest teaches you to kneel. A tallboy teaches you to reach. In a chamber with one window and a bed, the high chest is the closet that has not been framed yet.

Living rooms almost never wanted this object. It is too bedroom. It is too tall for the picture rail. It makes a sofa look like it failed a growth exam. When a highboy stands in a living room now, the house is usually announcing a collection, or the room is a colonial revival that never got the magazine memo about informal life. Bok’s living room is horizontal. The highboy is a steeple.

The lowboy is the highboy’s shorter companion — a dressing table with drawers, often the same leg and the same brass. Listings will call any low chest a lowboy. That is laziness. A lowboy has a job at a mirror. A living-room chest has a job at a lamp. They can look like siblings. They do not share a birth certificate.

What the living room kept from this family is the *chest-on-chest* idea at half height: two small over two long, or three over two, a credenza’s worth of stack. Federal American work, Hepplewhite inlay, Sheraton turned legs — those low chests migrate. They are the right height to hold a drink and a lie about how little you own.

If you meet a bonnet-top highboy in a room with a television, someone has lost a fight with scale. The bonnet will win. The television will look like a guest who sat in the wrong chair.
