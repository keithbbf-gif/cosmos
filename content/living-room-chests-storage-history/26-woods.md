**Oak.** The European default for joined and boarded chests until walnut becomes a city fashion. American joined chests too — Hadley, sunflower — oak faces, pine or oak elsewhere as the shop had it. Quartered oak later, Stickley, the living-room sideboard that wants to look like a principle.

**Walnut.** Restoration England, Queen Anne, a veneer over a carcass that insects also like. Bun feet in walnut are a bet against rot that often loses. Provincial commodes in walnut or cherry.

**Mahogany.** Eighteenth-century money. Tallboys, campaign chests, Federal American cases. The living room inherits mahogany as “traditional,” which means “we can still stain it that color.”

**Pine.** The cheap chest, the painted chest, the Shaker case, the country six-board. Paint is not a sin. Paint is how pine enters the good room.

**Cedar.** The American hope chest’s religion. Lining, sometimes the whole box. Moths. August closets. A smell that listings will mention even when the lining is plywood and a spray.

**Teak, rosewood, walnut again.** Mid-century imports and Danish shops. Teak oil as a personality.

**Keyaki, kiri, sugi, hinoki.** Tansu woods with jobs: zelkova for a face you can show, paulownia for a blade or a summer drawer that should stay light and kind to cloth, cedar and cypress for the case. Do not call them all “elm.”

**Camphor.** Travel and insects.

**Masonite and plywood.** ESU. The living room, 1950, admitting that a tree is not the only sheet.

A living-room chest made of “engineered wood” with an oak photograph on it is not a moral crisis. It is a six-board idea at a price. Say the material. The lie is calling it solid when it is a photograph.
