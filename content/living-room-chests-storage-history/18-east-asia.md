# Tansu, bandaji, camphor — do not say “Asian chest”

A living-room catalog will sell you an “Asian chest” the way a restaurant sells “Asian salad.” The words mean the buyer does not have to learn a name. Learn the names.

**Tansu** are Japanese mobile cabinets. The class is in the written record in the Genroku era, 1688–1704. Older wheeled coffers — *nagamochi kuruma-dansu* — are in the street during the Meireki fire of 1657; Dutch diaries mention chests on four wheels that blocked flight. In Edo houses the tansu is not a Western commode. It lives in the storehouse (*kura*), the storage room (*nando*), the closet alcove (*oshiire*), the shop platform (*choba*). It comes out. It goes back. Handles, rails, sometimes wheels. Interior partitions move. The chest is a piece of a house that can leave the house.

Types are jobs, not moods. *Isho-dansu* for clothing, kimono flat in long drawers. *Choba-dansu* for the merchant’s books, a face for the customer if the wood is good. *Kaidan-dansu* as stairs with drawers. *Funa-dansu* on the coastal rice ships, carried ashore so the captain looked like a man you should bargain with. Sendai clothing chests with keyaki faces and iron that remembers sword fittings. Yonezawa chest-on-chests with a cherry-blossom lock plate and the small hinged door up top. Sado work that still thinks like a ship. After 1868, when class law thins out, the regional faces bloom. Before that, the law is in the finish.

Hardware is structural. The case is joined to flex. Iron sits at the points that travel. After the 1880s English plate-pressing makes the iron easier to decorate. Do not read every blossom as “ornament from the start.”

**Bandaji** is Korean, Joseon and after. The name is half-closing. The upper front drops. You do not pull a stack of long drawers. You open a mouth and put the bedding in. Metal fittings — iron or brass, regional as a dialect — are the face. Gyeonggi, Ganghwa, Jeolla, Pyongyang: different weights, different cutouts. It is a blanket chest in Western mouths. Say bandaji anyway.

**Camphor** chests, Chinese and the export trade, use a wood moths also hate. They traveled. They ended up in Western halls and then in living rooms because they were already packed for movement and because the smell did a Lane job without Lane. I will not give you a cute dynasty-to-sofa story. Export furniture is its own industry, with its own fakes.

If you put any of these in an American living room, you are relocating a job. That is allowed. What is not allowed is sanding the region off so the sofa feels worldly. Keep the handles. Keep the word. Keep the fact that a tansu was often *not on display*. A living room that treats a choba-dansu as a trophy has reversed the original manners: the merchant showed the chest to a customer on purpose; you are showing it because you have no *kura* and you want the room to look like a shop you admire.

Reproduction tansu from outside Japan, elm veneer, reduced stair chests — they exist because the antiques got expensive and the houses got small. Say that too.
