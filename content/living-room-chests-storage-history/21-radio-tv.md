Radio could have sat on a table as a set of tubes and a speaker. For a while it did. Then it put on a cabinet, because the room it wanted was the room that had just been renamed for living, and that room still remembered being a parlor. A parlor would not take a machine. A parlor would take a piece of furniture.

So the 1920s and 1930s build consoles: lowboy legs, tapestry grilles, doors that close on the dial. Ozarka sells a “Credenza” model in 1928 — the Italian word now meaning a radio you can stand in the good room. High-end shops do the same joke in lacquer; Jean Dunand’s circa-1930 radio cabinet is a Paris object about jazz and antennas, not about oak. Most houses got walnut stain and a cloth mouth.

The cabinet does three jobs. It hides the works. It announces that the household owns the new sound. It gives the living room a shrine again, which is not what Bok asked for, but rooms are disobedient. Families stand around the grille the way they once stood around a piano. The chest has become a machine case.

Television inherits the job and makes it heavier. The console TV of the 1950s and 1960s is a credenza with a face. Record storage, later tape, later a cable box: the long low cabinet under the screen is the sideboard’s afterlife. When the screen goes flat and hangs on the wall, the cabinet stays, confused, holding remotes and the router and a row of books that are not quite books. We still want wooden clothes.

A living-room chest that is “for media” is this history, not the Hadley history. It is closer to 1928 than to 1675. If you take the electronics out, you still have a cupboard at serving height. That is why the empty media console is a decent liquor cabinet and a bad blanket chest. The depth is wrong. The doors are wrong. The ghost of a speaker grille is in the proportions.

I do not miss the console television. I miss the honesty of a grille. At least that box admitted it had a mouth.
