# Sources — what I used, what I trust, what I will not pretend

A draft that will not show its floor is a brochure. This is the floor.

I did not stand in the Met’s American Wing for this pass. I did not open a Hadley chest and run a fingernail along the tulip. I read museum records, dealer-adjacent histories that I treated as suspects, a couple of theses, and the usual encyclopedia pages that everyone copies from each other. Where those disagree, I keep the disagreement. Where a date is a dealer’s habit, I say so.

## Room

- *Ladies’ Home Journal* / Edward Bok. Bok did not invent the words “living room.” Decorating literature in the 1890s already used them. He pushed the name against parlor and drawing room, and he published house plans (from 1895) that dropped the formal parlor. Stanford White later said Bok had influenced American domestic architecture more than any man of that generation — praise from a man who had first told him to stop. American Heritage, Dec. 1984, “Edward Bok & The Simple Life,” is the piece I used for the house-plan crusade and the bric-a-brac sermon.
- Wikipedia “Living room” — useful for the Bok attribution and the warning that he is *erroneously* credited with inventing the term. I kept the warning.
- Gül Kaleş, *Parlors and Parler* (Univ. of Cincinnati thesis, 2002). The useful spine: parlor stays formal to about 1850; 1850–1890 the room loosens; 1890–1910 furniture count and room size drop; by about 1910 “living room” is the preferred American name for a room that is both family and public. A thesis is not a primary source. It is a careful secondary one.

## Chest, coffer, six-board

- “The Medieval Chest,” greydragon.org/library/chests.html — a working summary of six-board, hutch, and panel chests. Six-board from roughly the 9th century through the sixteenth and later; nailed laps; weak corners; iron straps when the maker could afford the honesty of metal. I treat the date range as a craftsman’s range, not a museum accession.
- Lost Art Press / Christopher Schwarz on mule chests (PDF excerpt of *The Anarchist’s Design Book*). The form is seventeenth-century. The *name* “mule chest” is late: OED about 1911. That one fact kills a lot of cute copy. I kept it.

## Drawers

- furniturestyles.net Restoration / Jacobean chest pages — dealer-adjacent. Used for the *sequence* (lidded chest → hybrid with tills → chest of drawers), not for prices or “95% of bun feet are replacements,” which I will not repeat as fact.
- antique67.com article on chests of drawers — same sequence; drawers as “tills” or “drawing boxes” from about 1600; runners as grooves, not later side-hung smoothness. I trust the inconvenience story (you cannot get the bottom shirt without unstacking the chest) more than I trust any one year.
- Penderyn Antiques, “The History of the Chest of Drawer” — trade blog. Used only where it agrees with the above: mid-1600s drawers under the box; later a full stack; Georgian oak linings and hand-cut dovetails as a quality mark, not a birth certificate.

## Cassone

- Metropolitan Museum: cassone records (Lehman 1975.1.1938, ca. 1425–50; marriage chest 16.155, ca. 1480–95) and the Heilbrunn essays “Weddings in the Italian Renaissance” and “Nuptial Furnishings in the Italian Renaissance.”
- The nuptial essay is the one that matters: fifteenth-century inventories often say *forzieri da sposa*; *cassone* may name the sarcophagus-shaped subset. Workshops of Apollonio di Giovanni painted fronts in about a month each, pairs the rule. Vasari on Dello Delli is later gossip with a job description inside it.
- Frist Art Museum, “Life, Love & Marriage Chests” — good on Tuscan production and the procession. I do not treat a cassone as a living-room chest. It is a bedchamber and street object that later rooms inherited as a type.

## American joined chests

- Patricia E. Kane, “The seventeenth-century furniture of the Connecticut Valley; the Hadley chest reappraised,” Winterthur Conference, 1974/75. About 125 chests in her count; tulip-and-stem carving; at least seven shop groups. Later writers push the surviving count higher (one thesis I read said ~175). I will not pick a winner. “More than a hundred” is honest.
- Martha H. Willoughby, “From Carved to Painted: chests of central and coastal Connecticut, c. 1675–1725” (Univ. of Delaware M.A., 1994). Sunflower group: 85+ survivors, second to Hadley. Peter Blin of Wethersfield: one 1681 account-book credit (Gershom Bulkeley) plus a family chest. Attribution to a single shop is the mid-century habit. Schoelwer (Yale University Art Gallery Bulletin, 1989) and later cataloguers treat multiple shops. Christie's notes that the “sunflower” is often a marigold or stylized rosette.
- I will not write “Peter Blin made the sunflower chests.” I will write “the Wethersfield / Peter Blin shop tradition,” and I will say the tradition is an argument.

## Lane, hope, cedar

- Virginia Museum of History & Culture, “Lane Cedar Chest.” E.H. Lane, Altavista, 1912, junction of the Virginian and Southern. WWI pine ammunition boxes; mass methods come home to cedar. Tag line in the record: “The gift that starts the home.” Altavista plant closed 2001 (company-history sites; I mark it secondary).
- Helen Hughes Lane, *A History of the Lane Company, The First Fifty Years* (1963), as quoted by lanecedarbox.com: J. Arthur Krauss on the Girl Graduate Plan. Official introduction 1930; spade work in the 1920s. Miniatures first as shop-floor / home hobby from cedar cut-offs, then a national dealer program. Lane’s own later estimate: on the order of 27 million miniatures. I treat 27 million as a company number, not a census.
- Campbell County Public Library / Staunton River notes: local start with Altavista High School graduates; then dealers nationwide.

## Commode, tallboy, credenza

- Wikipedia “Louis XV furniture” (and the usual French decorative-arts spine): commode = convenient; invented under Louis XIV to replace the *coffre*; Cressent gives the Régence/early Louis XV its curve. I used the 1738 Cressent commode for Louis XV’s bedroom at La Muette only as a dated royal example — bedroom, not salon, which is the point.
- English tallboy: trade sources put the high chest-on-chest / chest-on-stand fashion roughly 1700–1775. American highboy is the cousin with a different name and often a bonnet top. I will not give you a first-year for “the” tallboy.
- Etymonline / OED path for credenza: Italian *credenza* = belief, credit; English *credence* once meant the tasting of food against poison and the side-table where that happened. Modern domestic “credenza” as a named interior object is about 1920 in English, a prettier word than sideboard. Dining room first. Living room and office later, by drift.

## Tansu, bandaji

- Heineken, Ty and Kiyoko, *Tansu: Traditional Japanese Cabinetry* (Weatherhill, 1981) — via the Wikipedia synthesis I could check. First recorded Genroku (1688–1704). *Nagamochi kuruma-dansu* (chests on wheels) older in the record: Dutch East India diaries, Great Fire of Meireki, 1657. Class-determined under Edo sumptuary law; regional styles after Meiji 1868. *Isho-dansu* clothing; *choba-dansu* merchant; *kaidan-dansu* stair; *funa-dansu* sea. Kept in *kura*, *nando*, *oshiire* — not standing in the middle of the room like a Western commode. That last fact is the one living-room copy always skips.
- koreanantiquefurniture.com on *bandaji* (반닫이): Joseon clothing/blanket chest; name from *ban* (half) + *daji* (closing). Front drop, metal fittings, regional types (Gyeonggi, Ganghwa, Jeolla, etc.). Not a tansu. Do not write “Korean tansu.”

## Campaign

- Lost Art Press, “Campaign Chests” (2017) and Schwarz’s campaign-furniture notes. British officer furniture, eighteenth–nineteenth centuries. Archetype: mahogany, two-part, about 40 × 40 × 22 in., flush brass pulls, corner guards. Earlier: fewer guards, skeletonized / swan-neck pulls. Later Victorian: more brass, rectangular flush plates. India- and China-made campaign is a different shop tradition; I do not collapse them.

## Twentieth-century living-room storage

- Eames Foundation; Denver Art Museum; High Museum; Cooper Hewitt on the Eames Storage Unit. Herman Miller, 1950; knockdown first, then factory-assembled ~1952 with stronger legs; out of production 1955; some models reissued 1998. Warehouse vocabulary on purpose. Living room named in the original copy.
- Radio: the machine entered the parlor by pretending to be a cabinet. Ozarka “Credenza” model 89AC, 1928, is one labeled example of the word moving onto a radio. Jean Dunand’s lacquered radio cabinet, ca. 1930, is the luxury end. I do not have a single “first radio console” year and I will not mint one.
- Gustav Stickley Craftsman catalogs: No. 802 and No. 814½ sideboards as typed oak storage that wanted to be honest about being a box. Dining room by catalog. Living room by later use.

## What I will not use as proof

- Auction adjectives (“the finest,” “important,” “rare opportunity”).
- Any sentence that begins “chests have been used since ancient times” without a culture, a date range, and an object.
- Pinterest / Houzz “history of the accent chest.”
- A conversion of a museum object into a shopping category.

If a later draft states a year that is not in this file or in `40-fact-check.md`, the year is a mistake.
