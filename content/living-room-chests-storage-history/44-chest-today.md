# The living-room chest now

The factory towns have closed or moved. The catalog has not.

What a person can buy this year for the wall opposite the sofa is usually a long box with doors, a short stack of drawers, or a lid that wants to be a table. The woods are often photographs. The hardware is often missing on purpose. The copy says accent, media, storage solution. The job is still Tuesday.

IKEA’s long cabinets — the family that replaced a dozen named series in people’s mouths — are the democratic credenza. They are not a disgrace. They are a six-board idea at a price, with a door instead of a lid, in a room that still does not want to look like it stores anything. The shame is the same parlor shame in a thinner veneer.

The antiques are in a different economy. A Hadley-group chest is a museum object or a private one with a burglar alarm. A real campaign chest is a specialist’s listing. A tansu that has not been cut down is rarer than the internet suggests. What circulates in ordinary living rooms is Lane, reproduction oak, mid-century teak that may be Danish and may be a mood, trunks from attics, and the ottoman.

The honest contemporary piece would admit the relocation. It would have handles if it is meant to move, a lid if it is meant to postpone, drawers if it is meant to file, a depth that fits blankets *or* a depth that fits a receiver, not a compromise that fits neither. It would not call itself Tuscan. It would not smell like a spray. It would open with one hand.

I do not think the market will make that piece as a type. The market will keep sanding jobs off. The history is still useful: you can walk into a room and say, that is a lid pretending to be a table, that is a radio cabinet that lost its radio, that is a hope chest that outlived a script, that is a stair tansu in a house with no storehouse. Naming is the last cheap quality left.

If this folder does one thing besides the essay, let it be that. A living room that can name its box is already less of a parlor.
