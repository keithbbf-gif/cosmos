# Draft 35 — human-voice candidate

The living room is younger than the box it keeps trying to hide.

A chest is a lid on a well. If the shop is ordinary the count is six: ends that run down and become feet, sides nailed on, a bottom, a cover. The corners want to tear when you rack the box on a cart. Iron straps are not a look. They are the joint admitting what it is. You sit on the lid to pull your shoes on. You set a bowl there. You put inside it what a house cannot leave in the open. In a room with almost no other furniture the chest is cupboard, bench, and table. Northern Europe ran that object for centuries. Nobody needed a special word for the room. The chest sat where the life was.

Money made chests that had to walk. In fifteenth-century Tuscany a marriage pair could take a workshop a month per painted front. The inventories often say *forzieri da sposa*; our museums say *cassoni*. The boxes went through the street with the dowry on them. Florence, in 1415, forbade throwing stones or garbage at the couple’s house, which is a statute you write after the garbage. Then the chests lived in the bedchamber, next to the bed, not in a room for Tuesday talk. There was no living room to put them in.

The drawer is the well losing. Around 1600, in England, a sliding box shows up in the bottom of a chest — till, drawing box — so you can reach a small thing without unstacking the weather. The hybrid, lid above and drawers below, is a seventeenth-century object. We call it a mule chest. The joiners did not. The name is about 1911. By the Restoration a city case can be all drawers, walnut on a cheaper carcass, bun feet that wet floors and insects will eat and a later shop will replace. You stop sitting on your storage. You start looking at it. That is a real change in a room. A lid is a piece of floor. A drawer front is a face.

The French word for the next convenience is commode. The old coffer was in the way of smaller rooms. In August 1738 a Cressent commode is delivered to Louis XV’s bedroom at La Muette; the marble matches the chimney; the inventory number is on the back. Bedroom. The type walks into salons because the furniture of that reign was meant to be moved, not because someone invented leisure storage. English tallboys and American highboys take the drawer stack and stretch it into a closet that has not been framed. They are chamber furniture. Put a bonnet-top highboy in a room with a television and the bonnet will win. The television will look like it sat down in the wrong chair.

In the same long century, on the Connecticut River, joiners built oak chests we file as Hadley — tulip and stem, more than a hundred survivors, several shops — and Wethersfield-area chests with rosettes we still call sunflowers, though a catalog will sometimes admit marigold. In 1681 Gershom Bulkeley’s account book credits Peter Blin for a chest, a table leaf, and trenchers. A family piece got tied to that line. That is a document. It is not a signature on every rosette. The initials on the fronts are usually the woman whose cloth went in. Those houses had no living rooms.

The living room is an American winning name for a front room you use on a weekday. Decorating books in the 1890s already use the words for a room that looks like a person instead of a convention. Edward Bok, at the *Ladies’ Home Journal*, did not invent them. He did something more bossy. From 1895 he sold house plans that left the parlor off the paper. He thought bric-a-brac was making women sick with attendance. He could not see what a drawing room drew “unless it draws attention to too much money and no taste.” Stanford White told him to stop, then said Bok had done more for American domestic architecture than any man of the generation. By about 1910 the magazine house prefers living room to parlor. Streets are slower. Permission is the event. A room used on Tuesday needs a place to put Tuesday.

The parlor had refused that job. Company. Sundays. Sometimes the dead. Whatnots and glass — storage that denies it is storage. The blankets stayed upstairs. When the front room has to live, the chest comes through a door it was not supposed to use. That relocation is the whole subject. There is no period type called living-room chest. There is a young room inheriting old boxes, and a catalog sanding the jobs off until the leftover word is accent.

Lane did not sand. Altavista, Virginia, 1912, a rail junction, a failing box shop, a twenty-one-year-old named Edward Hudson Lane put in charge. Cedar because moths respect it. In the First World War the plant makes pine ammunition boxes and learns speed. The speed comes home to the trousseau. The line on the record: “The gift that starts the home.” In 1930 the Girl Graduate Plan is official — the spade work was the twenties — a miniature chest into a graduating girl’s hands, a discount on the large one, parents into the furniture store. Lane’s later memory talks about tens of millions of miniatures and, at the height, half to two-thirds of American girl graduates offered a box. Those are company numbers. The shape of the funnel is not. World War II ads put the same lid next to a uniform. By the 1960s the chest is a gateway to the rest of the line. The Altavista plant closes in 2001. The boxes are still under windows, holding photographs and extra blankets and the paper a household cannot quite throw away. The moths still respect a real lining. The script did not last as well as the lid.

The machine used the same door. Radio put on a cabinet because the room still had parlor manners and would not take a naked set of tubes. In 1928 a Chicago maker names a radio Credenza — the Italian sideboard word, already a little fancy in English, now a grille with legs. Television keeps the long face. When the screen goes flat and hangs, the cabinet stays, confused, with a router in its mouth. Mid-century catalogs, for once, say living room out loud. The Eames Storage Unit, Herman Miller, 1950 to 1955, steel and Masonite and plywood, sold for that room among others, knockdown until the first legs buckled in the crate. Five years of product. A long afterlife in photographs. Storage becomes a horizon of sliding doors. The lid survives as a trunk used for a table and as a storage ottoman, which is a six-board chest that put on sweatpants.

Other manners books exist. Japanese tansu — a class in the Genroku record (1688–1704), wheeled chests already blocking the road in the Meireki fire of 1657 — live in storehouses and closets and come out when needed. Handles first, display sometimes, law in the finish under Edo. Korean bandaji open by the half-front; the metal is the face; the name is half-closing. Camphor travels because insects hate the wood. If you put any of these in an American sitting room, keep the names. “Asian chest” is how a menu avoids a language.

What the box in the living room actually holds is leftover narrative. Blankets. Cables. A game that does not match the idea of the household. A year of paper. The drawing a child made that you cannot look at every day. A lid postpones a decision. A drawer files. People who think they are bad at storage often only need a lid.

The responsible act is small. Use the word that belongs to the object. Leave the handles room to be handles. Do not set a wet glass on carved oak. Do not build a parlor inside the one piece that was supposed to end the parlor — no styled interior for a camera, no books that have never been opened in that county. Clear the top with one hand. Open it on a Tuesday.

The room is about a hundred and thirty years old in the name that stuck. The box does not care.
