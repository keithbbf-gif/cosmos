**Disclaimer.** Educational furniture history for the BBF channel. Staged draft. Not a catalog, not a floor-plan service, and not a claim that this shop invented the living room.

I am not going to start with a sofa.

If I start with a sofa, you will look at the cushions, and that is a pleasant way to waste an afternoon and a bad way to understand why a box with a lid is still sitting against the long wall. The sofa is the loud object. The chest is the object that does the work the room was invented to hide.

The living room is a young room. I do not mean the drywall. I mean the name. Until the last years of the nineteenth century, the front sitting room in an American house that had such a room was a parlor, or a drawing room if the household wanted the English word. It was the buffer between the street and the rest of the house. You received in it. You kept the piano in it if you had a piano. In a great many families you also laid out the dead in it, because a parlor was the room you could afford to make formal and the room you could afford to close. That last use is real. The internet story that the influenza of 1918 invented a “death room” and that *Ladies’ Home Journal* then rebranded it as a living room is not. The name was already in the decorating literature of the 1890s. Edward Bok, who edited the *Journal*, pushed it hard. He is sometimes given credit for inventing the term. He did not. He did something more useful and more bossy: he stopped printing parlors on the cheap house plans the magazine sold, and he said out loud that a room furnished for Sunday and funerals was a waste of rent.

Bok’s sentence about the drawing room is still the right kind of rude. He could not see what it drew, he wrote, unless it drew attention to too much money and no taste. He wanted a room a family would actually sit in. Factory catalogs followed the language the way factory catalogs always follow language. Parlor suites — the matched set of sofa, reception chairs, and a lady’s chair that Anna Tobin D’Ambrosio and the Delaware thesis writers have tracked through the 1870s and 1880s — begin to be sold as living-room suites before the century is out. The frames did not change as fast as the word. A suite is a suite. What changed was the permission to live in the front room on a Tuesday.

That is the room this series is about. Not the great hall. Not the solar. Not the bedroom, which has its own chests and will keep them. The room Americans learned to call the living room: the everyday sitting room, the place the television later colonized, the place a credenza now pretends to be architecture.

Here is the part a furniture catalog will not tell you. The storage in that room is older than the room.

A medieval hall — Eames’s hall, oak, a board on trestles, benches along the wall — kept its wealth in chests because a cupboard that stood was still a luxury and a drawer that slid was barely an idea. The chest sat on the floor. It traveled. It was sometimes a seat. When the board came down, the hall was a different room. The box stayed. That logic does not die because someone in Philadelphia starts saying “living room” in 1898. It just changes clothes. The Lane cedar chest of the 1920s is a hope chest sold into a bungalow. The mid-century credenza is a long low box that learned to hold a hi-fi. The entertainment center of 1981 is a chest that swallowed a television and then was embarrassed about it. The storage ottoman is a padded lid. We keep inventing new names so we do not have to say we still live with a trunk.

I work in a custom shop that opened in 1999, in a nineteenth-century factory building on Main Street in Ferdinand, Indiana. That year is not magic. It sits on the far side of the entertainment-center boom and on the near side of the flat television that made half those cabinets look foolish. We build casework for rooms people sit in. We also refuse, more often than a catalog would like, to turn a linen press into a television box just because a client saw a photograph. The photograph is not a room. The room has a lid or it has drawers. Those are different machines.

If you take nothing else from this first essay, take the sequence. The chest is a type. The parlor is a social performance. The living room is a reform of that performance — Bok’s reform, and the reform of a hundred architects who were tired of a front room that could not be used. Storage did not wait for the reform. Storage was already in the house, along the wall, under the window, at the foot of nothing, holding the textiles and the plate and the papers you could not leave on a chair.

I will walk the type. Egyptian boxes, because dry air kept the wood. Medieval boarded and clamped chests, because the lock and the strap are the furniture. Cassone, because a marriage needed a street-facing box. Hadley and sunflower chests, because the Connecticut River Valley carved a tulip and called it a household. The French commode, because a drawer case learned to stand under a pier glass and borrowed a word that English later spent on a chamber pot. The credenza, because trust used to mean tasting the food. Tansu and bandaji, because a living room is not a European patent. Lane, because American industry figured out how to sell a smell. The entertainment center, because a cathode-ray tube needed a piece of furniture more than it needed a philosophy.

I will use ordinary words. A lid is a door that is also a seat if you are not careful. A till is a small box inside a larger box, usually at the right-hand end, for the things you would miss first. A mule chest is a lid with drawers underneath, a compromise that tells you the drawer was winning. A high chest is not a highboy until an American dealer needs a prettier word. A commode in Paris in 1730 is not a commode in a Midwestern bathroom in 1950. If you do not keep those apart, you will buy the wrong thing, or you will restore the wrong thing, or you will write a caption that makes a curator wince.

This shop is not going to sell you a living room at the end of a paragraph. We are going to name the boxes so the room can be argued with. The living room is younger than the box. The box is why the room still works on a Tuesday.

## Notes

Bok did not invent “living room.” He pushed it and kept parlors off *Journal* plans. Do not repeat the 1918 “death room” etymology. Grier for parlor culture; Delaware parlor-suite thesis for factory language. Eames for the hall-and-chest logic. Shop facts limited to 1999 / Ferdinand / Main Street.
