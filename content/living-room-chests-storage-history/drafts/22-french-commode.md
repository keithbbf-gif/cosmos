**Disclaimer.** Educational furniture history for the BBF channel. Staged draft. Not an appraisal of gilt-bronze, not a marble-matching service, and not a translation of a Paris sale catalog.

The duc d’Antin wrote Louis XIV in 1708 that he had been to Trianon to see the second bureau by Boulle, and that it was as beautiful as the other and suited the room. They were calling it a *bureau*. It was already a commode.

André-Charles Boulle delivered a pair in 1708 and 1709 for the king’s rooms at the Grand Trianon: two-drawer cases, marble tops, gilt-bronze enough that extra spiral supports had to help the cabriole legs carry the weight. Later inventories would know them as the *commodes Mazarines*. The Met’s related workshop example (Linsky collection; catalogued around 1710–20) is not that royal pair. The pair went to Versailles in 1932. What matters for this essay is the hesitation in the name. A new machine — low, wide, drawers instead of a lid, veneered in the expensive foreign woods that made you an *ébéniste* rather than a *menuisier* — did not yet have a settled noun in the household that commissioned it. *Commode* is the adjective that won: convenient. Latin *commodus*. Convenient compared with a coffer you had to empty from the top. Convenient compared with an armoire that took a whole wall. Convenient because you could pull a drawer without getting on your knees.

By about 1700 in Paris the type is a cabinet or chest of drawers low enough to sit at dado height — *à hauteur d’appui* — much wider than it is high, on high legs or low ones, a marble slab chosen to agree with the chimneypiece. That last matching is not decoration-as-afterthought. The commode stood under a pier glass or against the boiserie and was supposed to belong to the architecture of the room, which is why a later living room that drops a marble top onto a plywood box is stealing a sentence it cannot finish.

The maker is an *ébéniste*. The word comes from ebony, which is to say from tropical hardwood and from the prestige of working it as veneer. A *menuisier* made solid-wood seat furniture and paneled oak. An *ébéniste* made case pieces in veneer, marquetry, lacquer, and the gilt-bronze the French call ormolu. Guild lines were real. Charles Cressent (1685–1768) spent his career crossing them. Trained as a sculptor, *ébéniste* to the duc d’Orléans, he had mounts cast and chased in his own shop, which the *fondeurs-ciseleurs* and *ciseleurs-doreurs* did not appreciate. The Met’s Cressent commode 1982.60.56, about 1745–49, is the object I would walk a reader to. Pine and oak carcass, amaranth and *bois satiné* parquetry, portoro marble, two drawers in front, a door on each side, and gilt-bronze that is not jewelry on a box: Zephyr heads, acanthus, two boys swinging a monkey on a knotted rope. The mounts carry the crowned-C tax mark used on copper alloys from February 1745 to February 1749, which is as close to a date as most unsigned furniture gets. Cressent’s 1749 sale catalog, written when money was tight, describes a commode of this model. He did not sign. Attribution here is a body of mounts, a shape, and a paper trail, not a branded stamp.

Rococo in Cressent is a sculptor’s idea of a drawer front: the bronze draws the outline, the wood fills it. *Commode en tombeau* is a different silhouette — low, three ranks of drawers, short legs, the carcass swelling like a sarcophagus, sometimes called *à la Régence* in retrospect. Two-drawer commodes on taller legs are the other family. Dealers collapse both into “Louis XV.” The shop does not. A bombé carcass with a marble top that follows the plan is a bending-and-veneer problem. A tombeau with three drawers and a secret in the upper rank is a lock-and-runner problem. They are both commodes. They are not the same drawing.

Jean-François Oeben is the mechanical chapter. German-born, *ébéniste du roi*, dead in 1763, workshop inherited by Jean-Henri Riesener, who finished what Oeben had started, including the *bureau du Roi* that every decorative-arts survey wants to stop at. I will not stop there long. Oeben’s tables and commodes hide springs, slides, and writing surfaces that appear when you turn a key. The Met’s mechanical table (Linsky, 1982.60.61) is a table, not a commode, and I mention it only to say the mind is the same: a case that does more than open. If a listing says “Oeben mechanical commode,” ask for the stamp, the inventory, and de Bellaigue. `[VERIFY. Mechanical fittings traveled. A later shop can look clever.]`

Riesener is the Louis XVI sentence: rectilinear, mahogany or Japanese lacquer, mounts that think they are architecture instead of foliage. The Met’s 1783 commode for Marie-Antoinette, 20.155.12, oak veneered with ebony and seventeenth-century Japanese lacquer, gilt-bronze, marble, a companion drop-front secretary (20.155.11). She had them moved from Versailles to Saint-Cloud in 1787. They left the royal collections as payment to a contractor in the Revolution, went through British collections, and were bought at the Hamilton Palace sale of 1882 for Alva Vanderbilt’s Fifth Avenue house. That is not gossip. That is how a Paris drawer case becomes an American living-room ancestor: by being expensive enough to survive, and then by being photographed in a room that is no longer a *cabinet*.

London wanted the type before it wanted the politics. Chippendale’s *Gentleman and Cabinet-Maker’s Director*, 1754, puts “French Commode Tables” on a run of plates, XLIII through XLVIII in the first edition. Plate XLVII is the one I quote when a client says they want something French and then describes a bedroom dresser: doors or drawers in front, drawers at each end, “the middle part may be made with sliding shelves to hold cloaths.” Chippendale promises that a skillful workman in fine wood will give great satisfaction. He is selling a London shop the right to make a Paris idea in mahogany. Ince and Mayhew’s *Universal System* (1759–62) does the same work for a slightly different mailing list. English *commode* in that decade is a pattern-book word for a low decorative case. It is not yet a chamber pot. The pot is the next essay. Keep the city in the sentence.

What we actually build, in a custom shop that opened in 1999 in a nineteenth-century factory building on Main Street in Ferdinand, is not a Cressent. We build drawer cases. The French lesson that still pays is practical. A marble top is a lid that does not hinge; it is also a water-and-heat plane, which is why dining rooms liked it and why a living-room commode with a marble top will still take a wet glass better than a varnished lid. Ormolu is hardware that wants to be sculpture; cheap stamped brass is hardware that wants to be ormolu. Veneer is a skin. If the carcass underneath is not the right species and the right season, the skin will check. Paris knew that in 1710. High Point sometimes forgets it in a hurry.

A commode is not a chest. A chest has a lid. A commode is not a high chest. A high chest has legs and a second story. A commode is not, in this essay, a toilet. The French object is a low, wide, convenient drawer case that learned to stand in a paneled room and then taught London how to want it. Chippendale put it in a book so a provincial cabinetmaker could trace the plan. We are still tracing it, whether we say so or not, every time a living room asks for something long, low, and not a lid.
