**Disclaimer.** Educational furniture history for the BBF channel. Staged draft. Not a wiring diagram, not a vintage-electronics manual, and not a price list for 1981 case goods.

Marilyn Hoffman, writing from New York in the *Christian Science Monitor* on Christmas Eve 1981, had to explain that “entertainers” were no longer only people.

They were a breed of furniture. Large, handsome, single pieces, she wrote, evolved from a need to house audiovisual components out of sight. Leonard Eisen at Pulaski called them audiovideo furniture, style and status as well as function, contemporary and traditional and country oak, a thousand to twelve hundred dollars then, “do it all” pieces that hid the set and still had a shelf for crafts. Gary Hokanson, design director at American of Martinsville, called entertainers “the next generation of vertical storage.” He had looked at the market and found plenty of low and medium wall systems. Few were deep enough for color television sets, including the yoke that fits around the back of the mechanism, with removable backs and ventilation. People thought televisions were ugly. So he put doors on, doors that folded back, sets on swivels and slide-out shelves, outlets in the box. American’s pieces would retail between twelve hundred and thirty-five hundred dollars. Hoffman names the rest of the class as it walked into stores: Trend Line, Lane, Hammary, Stanley. Some looked like armoires. Some looked like sturdy chests, drawers above, cabinets below. Some had tambour doors that came down over the apparatus.

That paragraph is the object. I do not need a myth of a designer who invented leisure. I need a yoke.

A cathode-ray tube is a glass bottle. The screen is the wide end. The neck is behind, wrapped in the deflection yoke, and the box of the set is as deep as that physics. Living-room storage before 1981 had not been asked, as a factory problem, to be twenty-eight or thirty inches deep in the middle of a wall. Sideboards are shallower. Bookcases are shallower. A kas is tall. A Lane hope chest is a lid. Hokanson’s complaint is a joiner’s complaint: the wall systems on the floor would not swallow the bottle. So the bottle got a wardrobe. The linen press, which had already had a tired century, lost its trays and gained a hole. The armoire, which had held clothes, held a Sony. Hoffman says some of the new pieces resembled armoires on the outside. The Tribune will say it again in 1988 without shame: door chests and linen presses as alternatives to the wall system, TV and VCR behind doors, drawers beneath for clothing or tapes, selling best for bedrooms but suitable for any room.

I opened this shop in 1999. We were already on the late side of that boom. Clients still arrived with a measurement that was really a tube. They wanted a “built-in look” in a piece they could take if they moved, which is a contradiction the factories had already productized as a wall system. They wanted doors, because the set was still ugly, or they thought it was. They wanted the depth, because physics had not yet gone flat. We built casework to rooms. We also refused, more often than a catalog likes, to bore a nineteenth-century reproduction for a yoke. A Chippendale secretary is a fall front and a nest of drawers. It is not a twenty-nine-inch cavern. Hoffman’s factories were honest in one way the reproductions were not: they admitted they were engineering around a device.

Seven years after Hoffman, the *Chicago Tribune* runs “Systematic,” a Scripps Howard piece, first of May 1988. Home entertainment centers now range from compact chest-like units for a TV and VCR to floor-to-ceiling wall systems. Thomasville spans a wall, turns a corner, nine units, crown molding. Ethan Allen’s Circa 1776 wall system in maple is moving for young families, a retailer in Evansville says. Cherry and brass for traditional. Doors hide electronics; informal centers sometimes do not. Henredon French, Ethan Allen Canova, Thomasville contemporary door chests and triple cabinets, an armoire with black lacquer outline. This is High Point talking, not MoMA. The living room has become a case-goods opportunity. Storage that used to be a shame in the parlor is now a suite.

Christmas Day 1988, the Tribune again: “Media Room.” The space has a new appellation. Principal activities: television, stereo, reading. Southern Furniture Market has wall systems and upholstery for it. A source named High says entertainment units have soared in five years, one of the most rapidly growing items in case goods. Vladimir Kagan splits the customers: some want a recording studio, gear on view; others want electronics hidden in cabinetry that looks like architecture on the wall. Custom systems by Directional or Design Institute of America can run to ten thousand dollars then. I am not going to pretend those numbers are our numbers. They are 1988 newspaper numbers. What I will take is Kagan’s fork, because it is still the fork. Display the machine, or hide the machine. The entertainment center is the hide. The later media console, with the flat screen on top, is a half-hide: the screen is a picture, the box is a box.

Making, from a bench that has had to follow a factory thought without being a factory: the carcass is deep, so it is heavy, so it wants a back that can come off because the set wants air and a cable wants a hole. Tambour is a shop headache and a pretty lie; slats in a track, dust, a drop that binds in August. Pocket doors and bifolds were the “easy to fold back” Hoffman heard about. A swivel shelf is a lazy Susan for fifty pounds of hot glass. Ventilation is not a flourish. A CRT is a heater. If you box a heater, you get a failure. I have seen the failure as a melted back panel on a piece we did not build. I will not give you a ventilation recipe. I will say Hoffman already knew to ask for openings, and that the later Audioholics complaint about speakers in boxes is the same physics in another decade.

Lane is in Hoffman’s list. The cedar-chest company had already made television cabinets for GE; now it made entertainers. The hope chest and the entertainer share a factory and do not share a lid. One hides linens for a marriage. One hides a tube for a Tuesday. Both are about not looking at the contents. The living room, which Bok wanted lived in, now lives around a closed door the size of a wardrobe. That is not a tragedy. It is a dimension.

Use, in the houses that bought them, was exactly the catalog: the set, the VCR, the Beta if you had lost that argument, the records or the tapes, the figurines Eisen thought you would still display. When the tube died, the cabinet remained, too deep, a cave with a small new screen floating in it like a postage stamp. That embarrassment is a later essay. In 1981 it was not an embarrassment. It was vertical storage, Hokanson’s phrase, the press and the kas and the armoire conscripted because a bottle of glass needed a room of its own and the living room was the room that had the sofa.

I live above a shop at 1440 Main Street. We still get asked for “an entertainment center.” I ask how deep, and why. If the answer is a soundbar and a panel an inch thick, we are not in Hoffman’s story. If the answer is an inherited Sony that someone cannot let go, we are, and the honest drawing still needs a removable back and a door that will not rack. I will not put a CRT in a Lane hope chest. The lid is the wrong door. Hoffman’s people already knew that. They built a wardrobe and called it an entertainer, which is a word that has the grace to look a little foolish in the morning.
