**Disclaimer.** Educational furniture history for the BBF channel. Staged draft. Not a Danish-modern shopping guide, not a Herman Miller authentication, and not a revival of the Renaissance credenza.

Finn Juhl’s Sideboard of 1955, made for Bovirke, is a long low box on steel legs, sliding doors, a veneered carcass in teak or oak or walnut, optional tray drawers in colors he took from Goethe’s wheel.

That is a named object. I want named objects because “Danish credenza” is a mood board and mood boards are how a Vodder cabinet becomes a Juhl and a Juhl becomes a Target. House of Finn Juhl still publishes the piece as 1955, relaunched 2012, model FJ 5500 with trays, FJ 5507 without. Sliding doors. A frame that reads as furniture, not as a wall. If you put it in a living room it will hold records, or linen, or the objects a sideboard held when the dining room and the sitting room were still arguing about who owned the long wall. Juhl had taken criticism for sculptural chairs. The sideboard is him answering with a straight line and a colored interior. Storage can be a straight line.

Hans Wegner’s RY-26 for Ry Møbler is the other named low box I will stand behind as a type: teak, sliding doors, round recessed pulls, brass washers, oak interior, trays, a length around two meters, height under eighty centimeters. `[VERIFY: 1956 as a design year is dealer-common; a Ry catalog would be better.]` The RY-20 is the taller cousin, drawers below, sliding doors above, dates stamped on the back of surviving examples in the mid-1950s. Wegner is famous for chairs. The living room still needed a place to put the things the chairs are not for. A sliding door on a long box is a quiet machine. It does not swing into the walk. It does not pretend to be a fall-front *bandaji*. It is a cupboard on its side.

Do not hand Niels Vodder’s NV54 to Juhl because the fins look like Juhl. Vodder was Juhl’s cabinetmaker on documented pieces. Dealers still argue NV54. Arguments belong in the verify pile. The point of saying Vodder at all is that Danish “credenzas” were often cabinetmaker productions with model numbers, not anonymous teak. If you cannot name the shop, you are buying a silhouette.

Call it a credenza if you must. Then remember the other credenza. Essay 29 walked the word from tasting-table to 1920s long box. The Cloisters’ 53.95 is walnut and intarsia, 1440–50, Canozi da Lendinara. One sentence here: do not hang that intarsia on teak. English borrowed *credenza* late, then the twentieth century spent it on any long low office or living-room cabinet with doors. The poison-taster does not live in the Juhl sliding door. The word is a suitcase. If a salesperson uses it, ask whether they mean a lid, a drawer, a slide, or a wall. Those are four drawings. Teak oil is not a drawing.

The Eames Storage Unit is not a credenza and I will not stretch it. Charles and Ray Eames, 1949, shown in Alexander Girard’s *Exhibition for Modern Living* at the Detroit Institute of Arts, sold by Herman Miller from 1950, original run through 1955. LACMA’s 400-series example is M.86.105: enameled steel, birch plywood, Masonite, the house on Washington Boulevard turned into a cabinet — cross-bracing, off-the-shelf parts, modular bays. The Eames Foundation’s notes are the ones I trust: as openly engineered as a bridge, introduced in the Eames room at Detroit, related to the house they had just moved into. A living room can use an ESU as a room divider, a bookcase, a place for objects that can stand color. It will not hide a twenty-five-inch television behind oak doors, and it was not asked to. Depth is honest. The later entertainment center will lie about depth.

George Nelson’s Comprehensive Storage System, Herman Miller, 1959, is the wall version of the long box. Cooper Hewitt’s 2013-41-1 is teak, steel, a lamp, about eight feet by five-eight. The Met’s 1985.427.1–17 is the kit of parts: drawers, shelves, a cabinet, braces, ninety-six-inch uprights. Herman Miller produced CSS until 1973. Cooper Hewitt’s essay on it is right about the postwar apartment: go up the wall so the floor can stay for people; use it as a divider if the plan is open. I will not follow that essay into a drinks cabinet. Another pack owns liquor storage. CSS can hold a hi-fi, books, a desk surface, the Tuesday clutter. It is Shaker’s wall with aluminum poles. It is String’s thought with American corporate production. It is not a cassone.

What the mid-century living room did, in the houses that bought these things, was admit storage without admitting a trunk. A lid is a peasant memory. A sliding door is modern. A pole-and-shelf system is architecture. The shame Grier’s parlor taught — do not stack, display — gets inverted. Now you display the teak and hide the stuff. Or, with Eames and Nelson, you display the stuff in colored cubbies and call it design. Either way the chest is still there. It lost its lid. It gained a Danish name or a Zeeland label.

In this shop I hear “credenza” every month. Ferdinand, 1999, made-to-order. The drawing that follows the word is almost never Cloisters 53.95. It is a long low box, thirty-two to thirty-six inches high, doors or drawers, a finished back if it floats, cable holes if a screen will sit on it. I ask about the screen last. I ask about the door first. Sliding doors need a good track and a carcass that will not rack. Hinged doors need space to swing and cup hinges that will still close in August. Drawers need runners that can take the weight of the object you actually own, which is rarely the object in the photograph. I will not invent a Juhl we built. We have built long low living-room casework. We have refused to call it Renaissance. We have refused to mill a CRT niche into a reproduction of a 1955 sliding-door box just because a client still had a deep set in 2004. The set was the problem. The box was innocent.

Use now: records if you still have records, board games, the extra blankets that did not go in the Lane, the router that blinks. A Juhl-colored tray drawer is a till that came out of the chest and learned paint. A Wegner sliding door is a cupboard that learned to live under a painting. An ESU is a climbing wall for objects. CSS is the built-in you can unscrew. None of them is a hope chest. All of them are what happens when the sitting room agrees to hold things and refuses to look like a trunk.

The residual split is the word. Credenza 1450 is trust and plate. Credenza 1955 is a long low box. If you keep the Cloisters object in the other essay, you can use the modern word in this room without poisoning the teak. If you fuse them, you will hang intarsia on a sliding door in your head, and a shop drawing cannot use a head. Ask for the door. Measure the wall. Leave the taster in the fifteenth century.
