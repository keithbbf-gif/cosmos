**Disclaimer.** Educational furniture history for the BBF channel. Staged draft. Not the liquor-cabinet series, not a bar design, and not a wine-storage guide.

The Metropolitan’s cellaret 1970.271 is mahogany, white pine, and tulip poplar, New York, 1810–20, twenty-four and a half inches high, and the museum puts it in the dining room: a case for bottles, essential beside the sideboard, often standing in the sideboard’s central opening and wheeled to the table.

That is the proper room. This pack is allowed a light touch because the same small chest also sat, and sits, in rooms people sit in when they are not dining — a drinks box by the fire, a lidded cube that holds the bottles you do not want on a tray all afternoon. Another writer owns liquor cabinets, built-ins, the whole wet wall. I am not that writer. I will describe the object, name the Federal habit, and stop before I become a bar catalog.

A cellaret (museums often drop an *e*; American trade likes *cellarette*; I will take the Met’s spelling and mention the other) is a small case, usually with a lid, fitted inside for bottles, sometimes lead-lined or zinc-lined, sometimes with a drawer, often on a stand with legs in the Hepplewhite taste — tapered, inlaid, a little urn or shell if the shop is showing off. English examples of the 1780s–90s sit in the same family as the sideboard that Shearer and Hepplewhite were drawing: dining storage, lockable, movable. Southern American examples, Baltimore among them, can be quite proud of the inlay and quite serious about the lock. Phyfe’s New York shop, or a shop close enough that the Met will say “attributed,” made 11.60.146, 1800–1830, mahogany, another lidded case on a stand. Montgomery’s Winterthur Federal catalog is the American shelf. I am not going to recatalog Winterthur from Ferdinand.

Construction, briefly. A lid, so we are closer to a chest than to a commode. Partitions for bottles, so the interior is a till that multiplied. A lock, because alcohol is inventory. Handles on the ends if it is meant to be carried; casters if it is meant to roll from sideboard to table. Lead lining is a period answer to ice and to spill; it is also a conservation headache. I will not specify a liner as if this were a shop drawing for a wet bar. I will say: if you open a cellaret and find a raw interior with bottle rings, you are looking at a fitted chest. If you find a later refrigerator thermostat, you have left this essay.

Hepplewhite’s *Guide* does not need to invent the drinks box. Bottle cases and the sideboard’s deep ends are already in the pattern-book air of 1788. The cellaret is the version that can leave the sideboard: a separate chest, often octagonal or sarcophagus-shaped in the grander English examples, simpler and rectangular in a lot of American Federal work. Gloag will file the noun under dining furniture. The OED will give you *cellaret* as a case for bottles in a dining room. I am not fighting those rooms. I am noting that a small locked chest is easy to carry into the next room, and households did.

Montgomery’s Winterthur plates are where I would send a reader who wants more Federal examples than two Met accessions. Inlay on the lid, a stand that is really a small table, partitions that can be lifted out. Southern cellarets sometimes look like miniature chests-on-stands. That stand is not a high chest’s stand. It is a way to get bottles off a damp floor and up to a hand. If the stand is later, the lid-box may still be period. Ask. A marriage of box and stand is as common here as on a dressing table.

The living-room afterlife is small and I will keep it small. Not every house had a dining room. Not every bottle waited for dinner. A lidded mahogany box on stand is a convenient place to put drink in a parlor that has no sideboard, or in a living room whose sideboard already wandered in from essay 28 and is full of other things. Colonial Revival liked the silhouette. Later interior photography likes a cellaret used as an end table, which is a lid used as a top, which will mark. If you set a sweating glass on a cellaret lid, you are using a chest as a table. Fair. Wipe it.

I have not built a Phyfe. I have built lidded boxes. The difference between a drinks chest and a blanket chest is the partitions and the lock, not a philosophy. If the partitions come out, you have a small chest. If they stay, you have a cellaret even when the room is a sitting room. That is as far as the type needs to go for this pack.

What this essay will not do: Prohibition hiding places, Murphy-bar bookcases, 1950s blonde cocktail cabinets, the 1980s “entertainer” that hid a television and a bottle in the same cavity, built-in wet bars, wine-fridges in credenzas. Those are liquor-cabinet problems. They are real. They are not mine. A cellarette is a small, usually lidded, often Federal, bottle chest. The living room may host it. The living room does not get to inflate it into a typology.

On this floor I will build a lidded box with partitions if someone asks for a drinks chest for a sitting room. I will not call it a liquor cabinet on an invoice to make it sound like a renovation. I will not design a lighted glass interior. I will ask how many bottles, whether the lid must take a glass, and whether the piece needs to roll. Rolling is dining-room memory. A stationary box beside a sofa is living-room use. Both are storage. Only one of them wants casters.

A last boundary, because the other pack will need it: a cellaret is not a sideboard with a bottle drawer. It is not a secretary whose fall has been lined with mirror. It is not a kas with a shelf of decanters. Those conversions exist. They are liquor furniture by use. The type I am allowed to name is the small chest, lid, partitions, lock, Federal or Hepplewhite in grammar, sitting now — if it sits here — in the room with the sofa. The lid is the tell. When the lid goes and doors arrive, you have left the chest and entered the cabinet another writer owns.

Gloag will give you the noun. The OED will fight you on the spelling. The Met will show you a New York case that belonged next to a sideboard. I will leave you there, with the lid closed. If you want the history of how Americans hid drink in furniture, you are in the wrong pack. If you want to know why a small chest with a lock is sitting beside a living-room chair, you have it: bottles, a lid, a Federal habit that did not always stay where dinner was served.
