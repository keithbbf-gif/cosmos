**Disclaimer.** Educational furniture history for the BBF channel. Staged draft. Not an attribution of a carved chest to Peter Blin from a photograph and not a Hadley, Massachusetts, chamber of commerce.

The Metropolitan’s chest-with-drawer 10.125.682 is oak and pine, 1690–1710, made in the Connecticut River Valley in Massachusetts. 33 by 46¾ by 20 inches. Gift of Mrs. Russell Sage, 1909. The “Hadley” motif — a tulip and leaf, laid out with a template — covers the facade. There is a drawer under the box. The lid is the part that usually lies. Snipe hinges, tang hinges, gimmals: two pieces of iron hooked together, driven through a pine top and clinched. They hold until someone sits, or until the pine splits along the clinch, and then the lid is later. I will not say this particular lid is original without Safford in my hand. I will say the type is famous for replaced tops, and that a museum chest with a quiet lid is not a miracle. It is a hinge.

Henry Wood Erving (1851–1941), a Hartford collector, bought a chest in an old house in Hadley, Massachusetts, in 1883 — Historic Deerfield’s account of the Hepzibah Dickinson chest still tells it this way — and described it to his friends as his “Hadley chest.” The initials on that found object were RD. The name stuck to a whole group: joined chests, often with one or two drawers, made along the river from Springfield toward Deerfield, about 1680–1730, carved with double-petaled tulips and tilting oak leaves on a punched ground, often with a young woman’s initials. Hadley is a town. It is not the factory. The type is a valley.

This essay has two objects and one problem. The problem is naming. Erving named Hadley from a purchase. Collectors named sunflower from a carved marigold that looks, to later eyes, like a sunflower. Peter Blin’s name then swallowed a Wethersfield-area tradition that Schoelwer, Sotheby’s, the Wadsworth, and Kevin Sweeney have all, in different tones, refused to leave as one man’s work. If you take nothing else, take the refusal. Types are shops and neighbors. They are not signatures until a label says so.

Walk 10.125.682 as construction before poetry. Joined oak, pine lid and parts, a chest cavity, a drawer, the mule of the last essays wearing a Connecticut River face. The carving is a vocabulary you can stencil: tulip, leaf, scroll, a ground that is not smooth. Clair Franklin Luther published *The Hadley Chest* in 1935 and started matching initials to girls. Some of those matches are documentary. Some are hopeful. Yale’s Mary Root chest, the one Luther tied to Mary (Russell) Root of Sunderland, came through Garvan. Historic Deerfield HD 96.036, 1715–1720, Hatfield or Hadley area, is initialed HD for Hepzibah Dickinson of Hatfield, who married Jonathan Belding in 1720. The chest kept a family line to the donor. Most Hadley chests, Deerfield notes, post-date 1700 even when the joinery looks seventeenth-century. That is useful. The style is conservative. The dates on lives are better than the dates on vibes.

Lids. Again. Pine wants to cup. Snipe hinges tear. Battens on the underside try to keep the board flat and become the first things a restorer replaces. If you are looking at a Hadley in a living room, look at the top last if you want to be kind, and first if you want to be accurate. The carving on the front is what Erving loved. The lid is what the household used.

Now the other Met chest. 66.190.1, chest with drawers, 1675–1700, Wethersfield, Connecticut. White oak, yellow pine, white cedar. 39⅞ by 48 by 21¼ inches. Gift of Mrs. J. Woodhull Overton, 1966. The Museum’s heading is Workshop of Peter Blin. Typical of the so-called sunflower chests: shallow-relief floral carving and applied ornament, split spindles, bosses. The center panel on this one has the owner’s initials in a wreath of tulips, a variant of the three-sunflower standard. The museum notes that the carving resembles sixteenth-century embroidery patterns. That is a joiner looking at a textile, or a shop that knew what women valued. It is not a sunflower from Kansas.

Peter Blin (the man: French-speaking, the literature puts him in Wethersfield, death 1725; birth dates wander) was a joiner in a town that had several woodworkers. Walter Dyer in 1935 tied the group to Wethersfield through the Rowlandson cupboard. Newton Case Brainard counted provenances. Schoelwer, *Yale University Art Gallery Bulletin*, Spring 1989, looked at eleven chests and four cupboards and would not allow a common attribution. Variations in the carved panels were conceptual, not just careless. Sotheby’s, selling the Brewster-Newell chest, said the number of survivors is too large for one pair of hands, and that Blin more plausibly started a style that apprentices and neighbors kept cutting. The Wadsworth’s Nutting-collection sunflower chest, 1926.314, once Erving’s, later Nutting’s, is the object that essay by Brandy Culp uses to say the same: many hands, central Connecticut, a turner like Nathaniel Foote in the picture as well as a joiner. Philip Zea’s *Great River* catalog is the valley book that keeps Hadley and sunflower in one watershed without making them one shop.

Making is joined oak with applied maple or other turned ornament that falls off. Glue and nails release. Sotheby’s noted how often the bosses and half-columns are later casualties. A sunflower chest without its applied work looks half-dressed. A Hadley with a replaced lid looks complete if you like tulips. Both types are chests with drawers. Both are the mule admitting the drawer is winning, carved until the facade is a textile. We do not cut Hadley tulips in Ferdinand as a signature. If a designer brought Safford’s plate and asked for a study piece, that would be a study piece. It would not be 1690.

Use is household storage for people who could pay for carving: textiles, the small goods in the drawers, a lid that was still a seat. Initials on the front are often a woman’s, a movable-goods claim, the hope-and-dower essay in oak. Not every chest can be walked to a wedding. The raid on Deerfield in 1704 swallowed lives that had owned furniture; the Dietrich Foundation’s Thankful Smead chest is that dark cousin, and I will not turn it into a tear for a living-room caption. The object in the Met is Sage’s 1909 gift, a collector’s chest, already a type name, already on the way to a gallery.

The residual is Erving in Hadley in 1883, inventing a type from one house, and Blin’s name still printed on a Met label that also says workshop. Walk the river, not the nickname. The tulip is a template. The sunflower is a marigold. The lid, if it is pine, is probably the newest part of the oldest idea in the room.

## Notes

Met 10.125.682 (Sage 1909; Safford vol. I) and 66.190.1 (Overton 1966; workshop of Peter Blin) are the two objects. Erving 1883: Historic Deerfield / Zea accounts of the RD chest bought in Hadley. Luther 1935 is the initial-matching book, to be used with doubt. Schoelwer, *Yale University Art Gallery Bulletin*, Spring 1989; Zea, *The Great River*, 1985; Wadsworth 1926.314 (Nutting; once Erving’s). Sotheby’s Brewster-Newell note and Wadsworth/Culp on many hands. Do not claim 10.125.682’s lid is original without Safford. Do not make Blin a one-man factory.
