**Disclaimer.** Educational furniture history for the BBF channel. Staged draft. Not an attribution of an unsigned Newport case to John Goddard and not a price guide for a bonnet top.

The Metropolitan’s Philadelphia high chest of drawers 18.110.4 is mahogany, mahogany veneer, yellow pine, yellow poplar, northern white cedar, and brass. 91¾ by 44⅝ by 24⅝ inches. 1762–65. John Stewart Kennedy Fund, 1918. On view, when it is on view, in Gallery 752. The scrolled pediment with a figural finial quotes plates in Chippendale’s *Director* (1754). The serpent-and-swan carving on the bottom drawer looks to Thomas Johnson’s *New Book of Ornaments* (1762). The Met’s current text allows an attribution to Benjamin Randolph, with carving possibly by Hercules Courtenay. I will leave the names as the museum leaves them. What I will not leave is the word *highboy*. The object is a high chest of drawers. *Highboy* is the American dealer’s prettier noun, a salesman’s lift, the same instinct that turned a kas into an armoire and a chest of drawers into a bureau. Heckscher’s Met volume II is the catalog that keeps the period language on the page.

This essay has one problem: a high chest is not a chest. Not in the sense this pack started with. There is no lid. There is no till. There is a stack of drawers in two stages — a lower case on cabriole legs, an upper case, a bonnet or a scroll pediment — and the primary door is a pull. The mule chest was the treaty. The high chest is the drawer after it learned architecture. If you sit on it you are standing on a chair. If you pile, you are using the top as a shelf under a broken-arch pediment that was never a seat.

Newport is the other city the brief named, and the Met is again the honest shelf. 2015.50: high chest, Christopher Townsend, 1748, mahogany, chestnut, and white pine, 70¼ by 39 by 21⅜ inches, gift of Gerald and Kathleen Peters in honor of Morrison H. Heckscher, 2015. A documented Townsend, dated, the kind of object that keeps the Goddard-Townsend shop from being a fog. 1980.139: high chest of drawers, Newport, 1750–90, mahogany and chestnut, 84⅛ by 40 by 21⅜ inches, gift of Mrs. E. P. Moore in memory of Rear Admiral E. P. Moore. The museum’s note is the Newport flex: no other Newport high chest with four claw-and-ball feet with open talons and carving on all four knees; a refinement of the Boston Queen Anne high chest; plum-pudding mahogany on the drawer fronts; raised panels in the hood answering the skirt. John Goddard (1723/24–1785) and John Townsend (1732–1809) are in the related objects, block-and-shell men as much as high-chest men. The block-and-shell chest of drawers is a cousin, not a lid. Thomas Townsend’s chest-on-chest, 2005.52, about 1772, is the stacked-drawer solution without cabriole legs, a different silhouette solving the same problem: how to get more drawers into a chamber without a lid.

Philadelphia Chippendale is the louder cousin. 18.110.6, also Kennedy Fund 1918: high chest of drawers, Philadelphia, 1755–90, mahogany, tulip poplar, yellow pine, 99 by 45½ by 25 inches. The Met calls the city’s immigrant craftsmen the makers of the most elaborate carved colonial American furniture. Bonnet top, cabriole, shell carving, fluted colonnettes at the corners of the upper case on the examples that have them. Heckscher’s volume is where the construction notes live: how the two stages meet, how the dustboards run, which woods are secondary. I will not fake a Heckscher page number I do not have open. I will say the book exists so that a living-room “highboy” can be walked back to a city.

Making is cabinetmaking, not chest-making in the boarded sense. Dovetailed drawers, often cedar or poplar secondary, cock-beaded edges, brass that was the expensive glitter. The upper case is a box that must lift off for the stair, which is why so many high chests survive as two objects in two houses. Cabriole legs are the failure point: ankles snap, knees lose their carved leaf, glue blocks let go. Bonnet tops lose their cartouche. Fluted quarter-columns take the hit from chairs. The bonnet itself is a small roof: shaped boards, a pitch, sometimes a closed tympanum, sometimes an open scroll with a central finial on a plinth. Fluted colonnettes, when Philadelphia or Newport wants them, are quarter-columns glued into the front corners of the upper case, reeds catching light so the tall box does not read as a crate. You can build all of that without a lid. That is the point. In this shop we build tall casework. We do not stamp Townsend on it. We do know what a two-part carcase does in a farmhouse stair in Dubois County. The colonial high chest already knew the stair. Campaign furniture learned a different split. Do not fuse them because both come apart. One is for a mule. One is for a Philadelphia stair hall.

Use is the chamber, then the collector’s parlor, then the museum. Clothing, household linen, the same textiles the lidded chest held, now sorted by drawer. The high chest faces the bed, or the dressing table (the lowboy, another dealer word for a dressing table). When American living rooms later take a high chest, they are taking a bedroom monument and asking it to be a display of mahogany. It will not hold a television kindly. It will not hold a blanket as a pile. It will sort. That is the lid’s defeat, carved and gilded in the pediment. A bonnet that loses its cartouche still sorts. A cabriole that has been glued at the ankle still sorts. What it will not do is become a hope chest by standing in the front room. Height is not a lid.

The living room is younger than the box, and the high chest is the object that tells you the box stood up and dropped the lid. *Highboy* will be on the tag in a shop in 2026. Ask for the city. Ask whether the feet are open-talon Newport or Philadelphia ball-and-claw. Ask whether the upper lifts off. Ask whether anyone has called it a chest because they missed the drawers. The word can stay if the drawers stay. The lid is not in this object, and it has not been for a long time.

The residual is Christopher Townsend, 1748, given in Heckscher’s honor. The cataloger got a high chest with a date. The dealer got a highboy. The lid, by then, had been gone so long that American English had to invent a boy to keep the word chest in the room.

## Notes

Heckscher, Met American furniture vol. II (1985), is the catalog. Objects: 18.110.4 (Philadelphia, 1762–65; Randolph / Courtenay as the Met currently allows); 18.110.6 (Philadelphia, 1755–90); 1980.139 (Newport, 1750–90); 2015.50 (Christopher Townsend, 1748, gift in Heckscher’s honor); 2005.52 (Thomas Townsend chest-on-chest, ca. 1772) as the stacked cousin without cabriole legs. *Highboy* stays a later popular word. Moses and the Newport literature for Goddard-Townsend. Do not put *highboy* on a 1760 bill of sale unless a document does.
