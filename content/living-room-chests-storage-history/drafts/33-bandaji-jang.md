**Disclaimer.** Educational furniture history for the BBF channel. Staged draft. Not a catalog of Korean antiques, not an appraisal, and not a claim that this shop builds Joseon reproductions.

The Philadelphia Museum of Art’s Chest (*Bandaji*), 1945-57-158, is linden and iron, late nineteenth or early twentieth century, thirty-three and a half inches high, and the iron is the furniture.

I start there because a photograph of Korean chests in a Western living room will try to sell you atmosphere — dark wood, a brass lock, something “Asian” against a sofa — and atmosphere is how a *bandaji* becomes a tansu and a tansu becomes a coffee table. The PMA object is plainer than that mood. Linden does not throw a loud grain. Northern shops used it anyway, the museum says, possibly in the Pyongyang country, because a quiet board lets openwork iron do the talking. Pine, paulownia, and zelkova are the more common bodies farther south. The peninsula is not one factory. The fittings tell you that before a dealer’s caption does.

*Ban* is half. *Daji* is the opening and closing. A *bandaji* is a chest whose primary door is the upper half of the front, hinged at that half’s lower edge so the panel drops toward you. It is not a six-board English chest. The lid is not on top. You do not sit on it unless you are careless and the flap is shut and you treat the top like a bench, which people did, and which is why tops get scarred. You drop the face, you reach in for bedding and clothes, you lift the face, you lock it. In a house without a closet, that motion is the closet. The Asian Art Museum in San Francisco, on F2013.57.1, is blunt about the contents: clothing and blankets inside, heavy blankets sometimes piled on top, and in other households grain, dishes, money, books. A living-room chest in Indiana that holds throws and board games is doing a late, smaller version of the same job. The room name is American. The job is not.

The hinge line is the argument. A top lid is a horizontal door. A *bandaji* flap is a vertical door that learned to fall. If you have ever hung a fall-front desk, you already know the fear: the panel wants to rip the screws out of the stile when it goes past forty-five degrees with a child’s weight on it, or it wants to slam when the hand lets go. Joseon ironworkers answered that with long hinges, lock plates, corner braces, and the decorative openwork that looks like jewelry until you count how much of the front it covers. On a northern chest the metal can blanket the face. The Asian Art Museum calls one Bakcheon type *sungsung-i* — an onomatopoeia for wind through holes — because the plates are pierced until they are almost lace. That is still structure. Thin wood, a wide flap, a lock that has to catch every night. The piercings are a regional grammar, not a gift-shop motif.

I will not pretend this shop has a Joseon bench practice. We opened in 1999, at 1440 Main Street in Ferdinand. We build made-to-order casework and we haul it on our own trucks. What we have is the same physics. A drop front is a lever. A lock plate is a keeper. A chest that sits on short legs — *jokdae* in the Korean literature — is a chest that was designed to live off a heated floor, not to sweat on stone the way an English parish box does. If you copy the silhouette and leave the flap underset, or you hang it on cheap knife hinges, you have made a picture of a *bandaji* and not a *bandaji*. Clients bring photographs. I ask where the door is. If the door is the upper half of the front, we are in this essay. If the door is a pair of leaves stacked in stories, we have moved to *jang*.

*Jang* is the standing cabinet. Two levels, three levels — *ich’ung jang*, *samch’ung jang* — doors that swing, sometimes a small drawer in the waist, a cornice that makes the stack read as architecture. The National Museum of Korea keeps both the common *bandaji* and the taller *jang*; the *jang* was the more expensive household object, the one modest houses could skip because a *bandaji* already held the bedding. I am not going to invent a Seoul accession I have not stood in front of. `[VERIFY: pull a current NMK relic number and the gallery’s own romanization before speaking a specific chest.]` The type is what matters here. A *jang* is a cupboard. A *bandaji* is a chest that opens in the face. When the two thoughts marry, you get a hybrid like the Met’s 63.121.2a–i: lacquered wood, mother-of-pearl, tortoiseshell, ray skin, brass wire, early twentieth century, forty-two and three-quarter inches high, cataloged in English as a chest and in Korean as a two-level *bandaji-jang*. The Met’s label points at *taegeuk* roundels and trigrams that suggest the Korean Empire years, 1897–1910. That object is not the iron northern workhorse. It is court-adjacent dress on a stacked body. If you use it as the only picture of “the Korean chest,” you have done what export catalogs do to China: one photograph, one country, one lie.

The Met also shows, on loan, a twentieth-century chest with a flap door, wood and metallic fittings, about thirty-three inches high — closer to the PMA proportions, closer to the thing a household actually used. Stand the two next to each other in your head. One is inlay and empire symbols. One is a face that drops. Both are storage. Only one is the ordinary machine.

A *bandaji* is not a tansu. Essay 32 already took the Japanese mobile cabinet — Heineken’s types, Morse in the closet, wheels and stairs. I have to repeat the refusal because American living rooms collapse East Asian cabinetry into a single low box with dark metal. Korean work likes the drop front and the stacked door cabinet. Japanese merchant furniture likes the slide and the portable stack. You can put both against a sofa. You cannot share a shop drawing. When a client says they want “a tansu for the living room” and the picture is a *bandaji*, I do not correct their pronunciation as a sport. I correct the hinge. The hinge is the history.

Where did these objects live before they learned our room name? In a Joseon house the bedding chest belongs with sleeping and with women’s inner rooms more than with a parlor performance. I am not going to tour a reconstructed hanok from a tourist brochure, and I am not going to invent a temple story because a dealer needs reverence. Textiles take volume. A heated floor wants the box up on feet. The chest is low because the room is lived on the floor. That is enough. The living-room afterlife is the part this series owns. After the mid-twentieth century, Korean chests travel into American sitting rooms the way cassoni and kas and Lane hope chests travel: as a box that will hold the extra blankets and look like it came from somewhere. `[VERIFY: do not pin that traffic to a single importer or a single decade without a dated catalog.]` The *bandaji* was not born to hide a television. It was born to hide bedding. We are the ones who put it opposite the couch and called it décor.

On the bench, the questions are ordinary. Does the flap clear the drawers or shelves inside, if the chest has them? Some interiors are empty. Some have a shelf. Some have a drawer at the bottom, which is already the mule-chest compromise in another language. Does the lock still meet? Northern iron can look intact and still be tired at the pin. Do we leave the legs? A chest designed for an ondol floor, set on a Western carpet without feet, will look dumped. A chest given a later stand it never had will look like a museum object with the wrong plinth. I would rather see the original feet and a pad than a Queen Anne bracket glued on because someone thought the living room required furniture. That glue is a caption.

Use is still textile, even now. Blankets. The winter duvet. The tablecloths that do not belong in the dining-room sideboard because the dining room lost its sideboard. Kids’ sleeping bags. The shame, in an American living room, is to have a box that looks like storage. The *bandaji* absorbs the shame because the ironwork photographs as ornament. That is not a moral failure of the owner. That is what iron always did. English parish straps kept oak from opening. Korean plates kept a flap from racking and told you which valley made it. Decoration is clothes. Construction is the argument. We already said that about medieval chests. It is still true when the clothes are pierced bats and swallowtails.

If you take one distinction from this essay, take the door. Front-drop is *bandaji*. Stacked swinging doors are *jang*. Sliding doors and a Japanese name are someone else’s chapter. A living room that uses any of them as a blanket box is not wrong. It is late. The room is younger than the box. The box was already doing Tuesday work in a house that did not call the sitting room a living room, and did not need to.

I have no Joseon commission to confess, and I am not going to invent one. What I have is a flap I can hang, a lock I can refuse to fake, and a client who thought East Asia was one silhouette. The PMA linden chest is thirty-seven inches wide. That is a living-room dimension. It was a bedding dimension first.
