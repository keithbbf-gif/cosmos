**Disclaimer.** Educational furniture history for the BBF channel. Staged draft. Not a reproduction plan and not a naming certificate for a blanket chest with a fake drawer front.

The word is late. The object is not. Shop literature, following the OED, puts *mule chest* in English around 1911. `[VERIFY the dictionary date before a camera.]` The form it names — a lift-lid box with one or more working drawers in the base — is a seventeenth-century English habit that runs into the eighteenth, in oak, in the country long after London wanted a full chest of drawers. A mule is a hybrid. The later word is an admission, not a period invoice. Inventories will say chest, coffer, chest with drawers, a drawing box in the bottom. They will not say mule. If a dealer tells you the joiner called it that in 1660, the dealer is selling color.

This essay has one problem: the hybrid is not a missing link in a schoolbook. It is a household that wanted the pile and the sort at the same time. The last essay was the fork. This one is the household that refused to choose. Lid on top: blankets, the bulky cloth, the things you can stand to unstack twice a year. Drawers below: small linen, the things you want on a Tuesday, the objects that used to live in the till and outgrew it. The till often remains, a little lidded box at the right-hand end inside the chest cavity, sometimes called a candle box in the trade when it held tapers. Two systems, one oak frame.

English examples are joined as often as they are boarded, and the difference still matters. A boarded mule is a six-board thought with a drawer punched into the lower front, runners inside, nails doing too much work. A joined mule is stiles and rails, panels, the drawer hanging in a frame that already knew how to be a cupboard. Yorkshire and the North like a long drawer under three panels, a carved top rail, a dated or initialed facade. Bonhams sold a Charles II oak coffer with drawer, possibly Halifax, about 1670, with an internal lidded till and a regional lock-plate, from the Olive collection. I will not pretend I have that chest on a bench. I will pretend I have seen the type: lid, three panels, a drawer that is trying to look like two, extended stiles as feet. Shibden Hall at Halifax still shows related Yorkshire oak. Chinnery is where you go when the dealer says Jacobean and means 1911.

American versions are everywhere once you have the eye, and they must not steal the Hadley essay. The Metropolitan’s 10.125.682 is a chest-with-drawer, 1690–1710, Connecticut River Valley, oak and pine, the Hadley tulip on the facade, a single drawer below the box. That is a mule in construction and a Hadley in decoration. Peter Blin’s Wethersfield sunflower group, Met 66.190.1, is a chest with drawers, two of them, still a lid. New England inventories fill with chests with one drawer, chests with two. The drawer is winning from the bottom up. By the time you have three drawers and no cavity, you have a chest of drawers and the lid has lost. The mule is the document of the fight.

False drawers are the type’s bad conscience. A panel scribed to look like a drawer, with dummy pulls, tells you the look had already beaten the runner. Some “mule chests” on the market have a later drawer inserted into a plain coffer. Some have a later lid clapped onto a chest of drawers. Ask whether the drawer sides are as old as the panels, whether the runner is a groove in original oak, whether the lid’s hinges agree with the back rail. Snipe hinges, Follansbee’s gimmals, tear lids. Replaced lids are common. A replaced lid on a mule is not a moral failure. It is the seat problem from essay 16, still unpaid.

Making one now, on purpose, is a drawing we do. A joined carcase, a lid with battens, a stay, one or two drawers below with stops so they cannot dump. The temptation is to make the drawer too deep and the chest cavity too shallow, which is a chest of drawers with a toy lid. The type wants a real cavity. If your hand cannot put a folded blanket in from the top, you have not made a mule. You have made a dresser with a secret. Hardware: a lock on the lid, maybe a lock on the drawer, pulls that do not fight the carved rail. In August the lid still binds if the battens were pinned through in the wrong month. The drawers still swell. Hybrids fail twice.

The drawer in a mule is the part a boarded carpenter had to invent on the fly. Early English drawers in this position are side-hung: a groove in the drawer side, a runner in the case, a bottom that may be a single oak board sliding in from the back. There is often no dustboard worth the name. Dust from the chest cavity sifts down onto the shirts. A joined mule can house the drawer in rails the way a cupboard houses a shelf. American pine mules of the eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries — Pennsylvania German chests over drawers are cousins, not twins — often keep the lid as the main volume and treat the two short drawers as the till’s children. A till and a drawer in the same object is not redundancy. The till is reached from above, in the dark, for the small thing you do not want to dump the blankets to find. The drawer is reached from the front, in company. Two audiences.

Use is exactly the compromise. Farmhouses and parish vestries keep the form because it still piles. Town houses keep it because the drawer is fashion. American households put the chest-with-drawer in a chamber, then, much later, in a living room as a “blanket chest” with a useful little drawer for remotes. That last sentence is our floor. I do not hate it. I hate a fake drawer. A living-room mule with a working drawer is an honest descendant. A living-room mule with a drawer front that does not open is a piece of scenery.

Do not confuse the mule with campaign furniture. A campaign chest is already a chest of drawers that knocks down; the mule in that story is the animal that carried the halves. The words met in English by accident. Do not confuse it with a hope chest, though many hope chests are mules in all but name — lid, a drawer or two, a till. Do not confuse it with a high chest. A high chest has given up the lid and climbed.

The residual is the 1911 word stuck on a 1670 box. We needed a name that confessed the mixed parentage. The joiner already knew. He left a lid because people still sat and still piled, and he cut a drawer because the till was tired. The living room keeps buying that confession whenever it wants a bench that sorts. The drawer won. The lid, on a mule, is a treaty, not a defeat.

## Notes

OED *mule chest* ca. 1911 is repeated in Lost Art Press and shop literature; confirm the entry before a year on camera. Chinnery for English lid-plus-drawer oak. Met 10.125.682 and 66.190.1 as American cousins whose type-names belong to essay 19. Bonhams Olive Collection Yorkshire coffer with drawer, ca. 1670, as a regional English example (sale object, not a museum accession). Follansbee on gimmals/snipe hinges and replaced lids. Do not derive the name from army mules.
