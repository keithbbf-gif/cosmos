**Disclaimer.** Educational furniture history for the BBF channel. Staged draft. Not an AV specification, not a speaker-placement guide, and not a demolition order for a 1980s wall system.

The yoke went away, and the wardrobe looked foolish.

That is the whole plot, and then it is not. A flat panel hangs, or it stands on a heel an inch thick. The twenty-eight-inch-deep cave that Hokanson needed in 1981 for the neck of the tube is now a dark garage for a screen the size of a window pane. People tried, for a while, to keep the armoire. They centered the little rectangle in the big hole and called it custom. It looked like a postage stamp in a doorway. The living room laughed, quietly, and bought a low box.

The low box is a credenza that admits the television. Dealers call it a media console. I will call it a chest that lost its lid and then lost its depth. Thirty-six inches high was tall when the set was a cube you hid. Eighteen to twenty-four inches high is enough when the set is a picture on top. Doors still hide the blinking lights. Open cubbies still display the objects a parlor would have called clutter. The back is still a mess. The mess moved. It did not leave.

Cables are the residual object. Hoffman’s entertainers already had outlets in the carcass. That was honesty: the furniture is part of a circuit. Flat panels, soundbars, streaming bricks, power bricks, a router that wants air, a game console that wants more air, a subwoofer that wants a corner the architect did not draw — the living room is a nest of cords that the photograph crops. A built-in can swallow them if the electrician and the millworker speak. A bought console swallows them if you cut a hole and accept dust. I have cut the hole. I have also refused to pretend a wire channel is “wireless.” Wireless is a radio. The power cord is still a cord. Tuesday clutter now includes a black spaghetti the nineteenth century could not have named.

Soundbars are the other new parasite. A strip of speakers that wants to sit under the picture, or hang, or hide in the console until someone like James Larson writes that hiding speakers in furniture is how you spend money to make sound worse. Larson’s Audioholics piece of 31 March 2021 — “Entertainment Centers: Destroyer of Good Sound Systems” — is not 1981 evidence. It is a later dissenting voice, which is how this pack was told to use it. His complaint, and the forum complaint around it, is old physics: a speaker in a cubby is a speaker with a new, worse cabinet; ports need air; the entertainment center becomes a panel radiator; ventilation for amplifiers is not a lifestyle word, it is heat. Hoffman already wanted vents for a CRT. Larson wants you not to jail the loudspeakers. I am not going to write a placement diagram. I am going to say the shop gets both requests in the same week: hide everything; make it sound like a theater. Those requests fight. A door that closes is a door that reflects. An open bay that lets a speaker breathe is an open bay that shows the speaker. Kagan’s 1988 fork never left. Some people want the recording studio. Some people want architecture on the wall.

Built-ins took a lot of the work the entertainment center used to do. A wall of doors, scribed, painted with the room, a panel on a lift or on a fixed cleat, is the Shaker thought with a coaxial cable. It is also the thought you cannot take when you move. Renters and the rest of us keep buying the low box. IKEA’s grids — BESTÅ among them, year still on the verify pile — made the low box a default. Custom shops make a heavier default. The drawing is the same height problem: do not build a cave for a ghost tube; do not float a ninety-inch screen on a jewelry box. Measure the panel you have, and the panel you might have in five years, and then admit you do not know the second number.

I opened in 1999. Plasma was already in the magazines. Clients were still measuring depth for sets their uncles owned. We built some deep cabinets because the set in the room was deep. We built some shallow ones because the client had jumped. We learned to ask which year of physics we were serving. A shop that ignores the device will mill a beautiful wrong thing. A shop that worships the device will mill a cabinet that is ugly in eight years. The compromise is a carcass that can lose a shelf, a back that can come off, a top that can take a panel now and not look like a missing tooth later. I will not invent a named media-wall. I will say we have built living-room casework after the tube, and the honest ones look more like Juhl’s long box than like Pulaski’s entertainer.

Making, after the yoke, is mostly the back and the heat. A removable back panel is not a confession of cheapness; it is how you reach a brick that died. A grommet is a hole you meant. A shelf that is only fifteen inches deep will take a streaming box and will not take a 1981 set, which is the point. If someone wants a soundbar shelf, I put a shallow ledge that does not wrap the bar in three sides of oak. Three sides of oak is a new speaker cabinet you did not tune. If they want the bar hidden, I tell them Larson’s complaint and I still hide it if the household wins, because the household lives here and the review does not. Vent slots in a kick or a back rail are ugly in a drawing and correct in July. We paint them when we can. We do not pretend they are ornament.

What died is the linen-press silhouette as a television strategy. The armoire can go back to coats, or to the guest-room clutter, or to the antique mall. What did not die is the need to put the machine somewhere. A panel still has a power supply. A soundbar still has a brick. A living room without a place for those objects is a parlor again: performance, no Tuesday. Bok would not have loved the cables. He would have recognized the problem. Sit in the front room and the front room fills.

Use, now, is smaller inside the box and larger on the wall. The console holds the bricks and the games and the photo albums that did not go digital. The wall holds the picture. The speakers, if you listen to Larson, hold themselves, on stands, out of the carcass. If you listen to the rest of the household, the speakers go in the cubby so a shin does not find them. I live with both kinds of rooms. I know which one photographs. I know which one gets vacuumed.

The residual tension is heat and pride. A 1981 entertainer was proud of hiding an ugly heater. A 2021 console is proud of showing a beautiful picture and is still hiding a heater, just a smaller one, in a shallower box, with a soundbar that wants to be seen and not seen. We can mill the box. We cannot mill the argument. Take the doors off and you have Nelson’s open bays. Put them on and you have Hoffman without the yoke. Either way you still have a living-room chest. It just got short.
