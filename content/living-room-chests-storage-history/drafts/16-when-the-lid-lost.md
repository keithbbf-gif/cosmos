**Disclaimer.** Educational furniture history for the BBF channel. Staged draft. Not a claim that this shop invented the drawer and not a measured drawing for a geometric chest.

The Victoria and Albert Museum’s W.28-1918 is a chest of two drawers on a stand, English, about 1680, oak with a pine front, walnut veneer, herringbone banding, brass drops, bun feet, from Boughton House, gift of the Duke of Buccleuch. Late, pretty, already a different religion from a boarded coffer. The drawers are the door. There is no lid to sit on. If you came through the first fifteen essays of this pack, this is the hinge. I mean that as a piece of hardware and as a piece of history. The lid lost.

This essay has one problem: convenience versus volume, and the room’s habit of sitting on the box. A lidded chest is the bigger cube. You open the sky of it and you pile. Linens, meal, the parish register, the things that are large or that do not need to be found in a hurry. The till is the only sort, a small box at the right-hand end for the things you would miss in a fire. A drawer is a lesser cube you can pull without unstacking. You sort. You lose total volume to runners, dustboards, and the spaces between. You gain Tuesday. England in the seventeenth century is where the household, or the part of it that could pay a joiner, starts to buy that Tuesday.

Chinnery is the oak book. Eames is the medieval lid. Between them the sequence is not a cartoon of progress. Dug-outs, boarded chests, and clamp-fronts keep being made while London learns the nest of drawers. The Wadsworth, writing about sunflower chests, uses a formulation I will borrow as a museum sentence, not as a patent office: the chest of drawers was not introduced into wealthy urban English homes until about the 1630s. `[VERIFY against Chinnery’s inventory citations before making 1630 a hard year on camera.]` Early drawers are side-hung: a groove in the drawer side riding a runner in the case. They are called tills or drawing boxes when they first sit in the base of a chest that still has a lid. That hybrid is the next essay’s mule. This essay is the moment the lid is no longer the primary door.

Why the drawer wins is not a mystery on a bench. I have packed a blanket chest. The blankets you want are at the bottom. You lift the ones you do not want. You set them on the lid, or on the floor, or on a child. You close. A week later you do it again. A drawer lets you keep the winter wool in the bottom box and the tablecloths in the top without a weekly excavation. Hardware gets more expensive — more lock plates, more pulls — and the joiner earns a longer job: runners, kicker, a carcase that cannot rack or the drawers bind. The Restoration and later seventeenth century love geometric mouldings on drawer fronts, cushion panels, split bobbins, oak that has been asked to look architectural. Those clothes are not the argument. The runner is the argument.

Volume is what you give away. A boarded chest is almost all interior. A chest of drawers of the same external cube is a set of smaller boxes plus wood. For textiles that is often a fair trade. For grain it is not, which is why farmhouses keep lids longer. For documents a till was enough. For a growing urban wardrobe — shirts, bands, the small linen that creases — the drawer is the better servant. Theft is the other pressure. A chest of drawers with a pair of doors in front of the drawers, an early form the literature still argues about, lets you lock once. Forman’s joined thinking, even when he is writing chairs, is the same shop: mortise and tenon, a frame that can take a sliding box. The drawer is a joiner’s object. Nailed boarded chests take a drawer later and worse.

The lid as a seat is the cost the furniture catalogs forget. Medieval halls used the chest as a bench. Cassoni were sat on in the *camera*. English oak coffers still live in halls as window seats. A lid that is also a seat wants thickness, battens, a hinge that does not tear out, and a stay if you have any sense. When the primary door becomes a drawer, the top can become a surface: a place for a candlestick, a dressing cushion, later a marble on a commode. You stop sitting, or you sit on a chair that was always the marked object. Storage stands up. The high chest in a later essay is this thought taken to the ceiling. The commode in Paris is this thought veneered. I will not steal those chapters. I need only the fork: lid equals pile plus seat; drawer equals sort minus cube.

In this shop, opened 1999, 1440 Main Street, we still lose the argument both ways. A client wants a “chest” and points at a row of drawers. A client wants a “credenza” and means a lid. I ask where the hand goes. If the hand lifts, we are in essays 1 through 15. If the hand pulls, we are here. A living room after Bok wants both: a low lid to put feet-adjacent objects inside, and drawers for the remote controls and the extra leaves. The entertainment center was a chest of drawers that swallowed a cathode ray tube. The storage ottoman is a padded lid, a relapse. We have not settled the fork. We have only renamed it.

Making a drawer in oak, seventeenth-century style, is a groove and a runner and a bottom that slides in from the back. Dovetails, when they come, are a later neatness on this form. Side-hung drawers gum up with dust and with a case that has gone out of square on a damp floor. You feel that on any old chest of drawers you have to hip-check shut. A lid never asks the carcase to stay rectangular in the same way. It asks the hinge. Different failures. I prefer a lid that binds in August to a drawer that has lost its runner, but that is a shop prejudice, not a history. History voted for the drawer in town and kept the lid in the country for another hundred years.

Use in a living room now is the same fork wearing nicer names. Blanket chest, toy chest, window seat: lids. Media consoles, dressers in the sitting room, campaign chests: drawers. If you take nothing else from this hinge essay, take the test. Can I get the thing I want without emptying the box? If yes, a drawer has been by. If you sit, a lid is still in the room. When both are true, you have a mule, and that is the next door down the corridor.

The residual is W.28-1918’s missing lid. Boughton could afford the pretty drawers. Somewhere in the same century a parish chest still opened to the sky. The living room inherited both habits and then pretended they were decorating choices. They are machines. Pick the machine.

## Notes

V&A W.28-1918 (Boughton House, ca. 1680, two drawers on a stand) is the lidless opening. Chinnery for oak and inventories; Eames for the lid world; Forman for joined thinking. Wadsworth’s “about the 1630s” for urban English chests of drawers is a museum sentence, not a patent. Side-hung drawers and the mule hybrid belong here as the fork, and in essay 17 as the treaty. Do not let a living-room “chest” mean drawers without saying the lid lost.
