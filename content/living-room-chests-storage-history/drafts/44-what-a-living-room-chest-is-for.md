**Disclaimer.** Educational furniture history for the BBF channel. Staged draft. Not a catalog, not a quote, not a finish schedule, and not a coupon. The shop is talking about use.

I am standing in the shop.

The building is a nineteenth-century factory on Main Street in Ferdinand. We opened in 1999. The address is 1440. I live above the work. We build made-to-order. We run our own trucks. The work goes to designer showrooms; I will not read you a city list, because the list changes and a list is a brochure. Public facts stop there. Everything else about a job is the client’s, or it is moisture, or it is a hinge.

A living-room chest is for the things that are not for sitting.

That is the whole definition if you are willing to let it be homely. The sofa is for bodies. The box is for the rest. Blankets. Games. The extra leaf. The router. The wool that is too hot for the closet in June and too useful to put in the attic. The papers that have not earned a file. The remote controls that breed. Bok wanted this room used on a Tuesday. Tuesdays produce volume. A parlor could hide volume because a parlor was closed. A living room that cannot close has to store in public. The chest is how public storage keeps a face.

I ask four questions, and they are construction questions, not a personality quiz.

Do you want a lid? Then you have a chest in this pack’s strict sense: a box whose primary door is on top. You can sit on it if we stay the lid and you are not foolish. You can pad it and call it an ottoman. You can leave it oak and call it a blanket chest. You will lift, not swing. You will not put a coffee cup on an open chest and then close it with the cup in your other hand without learning. Lane sold this machine with a smell. A six-board carpenter sold it with nails. A *yixiang* sold it with camphor. The living room still buys it when the household wants volume in one motion.

Do you want drawers? Then the lid has already lost, or is losing. A chest of drawers, a mule chest, a low dresser that wandered in from the bedroom — the hand pulls, the volume comes out in slices. Drawers are kinder to knees and crueler to a house that is not square. They also let you classify. The till was a small lid inside a big lid. A drawer is a till that learned to travel the length of the case. Stickley’s sideboard mixed doors and a drawer because households mix objects. We still mix them.

Do you want doors? Hinged, sliding, tambour, a drop-front like a *bandaji*, a pair of leaves like a *jang* or a kas. A door is a cupboard thought. You get a face, a plane, a place to hide the ugly or to show the grain. Hoffman’s entertainer was a door around a tube. Juhl’s sideboard was a slide around Tuesday. A door wants space to move, or a track that will not fill with grit. August will tell you if you lied about the gap.

Do you want a wall? Built-in, String, Haller, CSS, a painted run of carcasses that unscrew, a plaster run that will not. The wall is the Shaker answer and the rental answer, depending on the fasteners. It is the most architectural and the most likely to be photographed as if storage had disappeared. Storage has not disappeared. It has gone vertical. Pegs, poles, bays. The floor stays for feet. The ottoman then puts a chest back in the middle of the floor because the wall filled up.

Those four are the history. Egyptian boxes, parish lids, cassoni, Hadley, commodes, kasten, tansu, *bandaji*, Lane, ESU, the entertainer, the media console — they are lid, drawer, door, or wall, sometimes two at once. If a conversation in this shop cannot pick one, the drawing will pick one anyway, and the room will live with the pick. I would rather the room pick.

I am not going to close with a finish. I am not going to tell you which wood is correct for a life. Moisture is local. Ferdinand in August is not Altavista in January and not Kyoto in the rainy month. A lid that bound last summer will bind next summer if we ignored the grain. That is not brand language. That is August.

I am not going to invent a commission to prove we belong in the story. The public anecdote is that the first piece was a Bombay Chippendale secretary at sixteen. It is a secretary, which is a desk pack’s object, and I will not drag it into the living room except to say: a teenager can learn a fall front, and a fall front is a door, and doors are one of the four. The living-room work since 1999 has been the four, in rooms people sit in, sometimes with a screen, sometimes with only a lamp and a pile of books that will not fit the bookcase because the bookcase was styled empty.

What the chest is *for*, if I have to say for, is not marriage, not moths, not status, not Italy. Those have been advertisements. Marriage put textiles in a box; the box still works if nobody marries. Moths were a way to sell cedar and camphor; a tight lid is the older method. Status is how a credenza borrowed a tasting word. Italy is a suitcase. Use is Tuesday. If the box holds what the household actually owns, it is doing the first job of furniture. If it holds a photograph of what the household aspires to own, it is a parlor again.

I will refuse a few things, because refusal is part of use. We will not mill a CRT cave for a panel an inch thick unless you still own the cave’s ghost and say so. We will not call cedar medicine. We will not fake a through tenon with a glued block and call it Craftsman. We will not baptize a trunk Mughal for luck. We will not turn a liquor-cabinet request into this conversation; that is another pack. We will not print a coupon at the end of a history. If you want a piece of furniture, that is a different document, with measurements, and it will still start with lid, drawer, door, or wall.

The residual fact is the one this series started with. The living room is younger than the box. The box is why the room still works when the guests have gone and the throw is on the floor and the game has lost a piece and the cable has come out of the back of the shallow cabinet. Pick a door. Hang it so it stays. Put the Tuesday in. That is what a living-room chest is for. The rest is names, and we have spent forty-three essays stealing the names back. The shop will keep stealing them. The hinge will not care what we called it, as long as the hinge holds.
