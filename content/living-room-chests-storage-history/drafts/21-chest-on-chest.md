**Disclaimer.** Educational furniture history for the BBF channel. Staged draft. Not a measured shop drawing, not a period reproduction specification, and not a claim that this shop has restored a Goddard-Townsend case.

The Metropolitan’s Thomas Townsend chest-on-chest (2005.52) is mahogany, chestnut, and tulip poplar, Newport, about 1772, and it does not have a lid.

I start there because the trade still wants a lid in the word *chest*. A lid is a door that lies down. This object is two drawer cases stacked, the upper one sitting in the waist of the lower, a scroll pediment tying the architecture together, raised panels over the top drawers that echo the curves of the bonnet. Townsend was a member of the Goddard-Townsend shops that made Newport’s reputation. This is the only documented piece of his. Heckscher bought it for the American Wing in 2005 with the Friends of the American Wing and a handful of other funds, and the catalog will tell you more about the Gardiner family descent than I will. What I need from it is the machine: drawers, two carcases, a joint at the waist, no hinge on top.

English cabinetmakers had already named a cousin. The tallboy — late seventeenth century in walnut, then mahogany through the eighteenth — is a double chest of drawers, the upper section usually a shade narrower than the lower, the whole thing standing on bracket feet or, later, ogee brackets, sometimes with a secretaire drawer pretending the lower case is also a desk. You reached the top drawers with bed steps. When high beds went out, the steps went out, and the tallboy began to look like a piece that asked too much of a short person. That is a use history, not a sermon. The English word is still used, lazily, for anything tall with drawers. It is not a synonym that erases construction.

The American fork is the one a living-room chest pack cannot blur. A high chest — dealers say highboy; museums, if they are being careful, say high chest of drawers — is a chest of drawers on a *stand*. The lower section has legs. William and Mary examples stand on turned legs with stretchers. Queen Anne and Chippendale examples stand on cabriole legs, often with claw-and-ball feet, the skirt carved, the upper case a separate drawer box dropped onto the stand. There is air under the piece. You can see the floor. The Philadelphia Museum of Art, on a high chest and dressing-table pair, calls that raised space a North American confection, and notes that the British had largely given up the chest-on-stand by the 1730s. `[VERIFY: PMA gallery text, not a Heckscher page. Speak it as a museum’s claim until you have Kirk or Heckscher under it.]` A chest-on-chest does not have that air. Two cases. Short feet. The lower case is itself a chest of drawers, not a table that learned to hold drawers.

Put the two Philadelphia objects in the same room in your head. The Met’s high chest 18.110.4, 1762–65, mahogany and the usual secondary woods, scrolled pediment with a figural finial, the serpent-and-swan on the bottom drawer taken from Thomas Johnson’s *A New Book of Ornaments* (1762), the pediment nodding at Chippendale’s 1754 *Director*. Legs. Stand. Linens in the drawers, the catalog says, which is honest: this was bedroom storage that later generations dragged into halls and sitting rooms because it photographed as “colonial.” Then look at 1975.91, the chest-on-chest attributed to Thomas Affleck and the carver James Reynolds, 1770–75, also Philadelphia, also mahogany, ninety-seven and a half inches of architectural case. A 1772 entry in William Logan’s ledger is the documentary hook. Scroll pediment again, but the lower section is a chest, not a stand. Heckscher’s catalog treats the two types as cousins who stopped sharing a floor plan. Townsend 2005.52 and the Philadelphia chest-on-chest 1975.91 are, the Met itself says, two elegant answers to the same problem: how to put a scroll pediment on an architectural case without the pediment looking glued to a box. Neither answer is a lid.

Newport and Philadelphia are not interchangeable veneers. Newport likes blockfronts and shells, raised panels that repeat the pediment, a certain tightness in the bonnet. Philadelphia likes carving that thinks it is sculpture: rococo ornament, applied leaf, a finial that wants to be a bust. Both cities made both types. Both cities made dressing tables to sit under the high chest as a matched lower register — the object the nineteenth century would start calling a lowboy. That pairing is a bedroom suite. The living room gets it later, when Colonial Revival and then the apartment hallway need a tall thing that holds tablecloths and tax records and the extra blankets you will not admit you own.

Construction is the argument I will actually build. Two cases means two backs, two bottoms, a waist molding that has to register, and a set of drawer runners that cannot rack when the upper case is lifted. Period pieces were made to come apart. You do not carry a seven-foot mahogany cliff up a Newport stair as one object if you can help it. The upper case sits in a rebate or on a molded lip. If the lip is worn, the upper case walks forward a sixteenth every decade until a restorer “fixes” it with a screw that does not belong. Dustboards between drawers are not a moral improvement. They keep wool from dropping into the drawer below and they stiffen the case. Cockbeading protects veneer edges. The top board of a chest-on-chest is not a lid. It is a roof. If someone has cut a till into it, they have confused this machine with the boarded chest in essay 02, and the confusion will show at the first humid August.

I work in a custom shop that opened in 1999, at 1440 Main Street in Ferdinand, Indiana. We have built stacked drawer cases. We have not built a secret Townsend. What the floor teaches is ordinary and sufficient: an upper case is a box that wants to rack; a lower case is a box that wants to splay; the waist is where those two wishes meet. If you glue them into one carcass because a trucking company charged for two pieces, you have made a wardrobe that cannot turn a stair. If you leave them separate and do not pin the location, the upper case will tell you, in winter, that wood moves across the grain and you should have left a little.

The lid question is not pedantry. A mule chest is a lid with drawers underneath — hybrid, the drawer winning. A chest-on-chest is the drawer’s victory complete, then stacked because a house without closets still needed volume and a single case seven feet tall is an ugly way to get it. Stacking lets you graduate drawers, put small things up high, put weight down low, and take the thing apart when you move. A high chest does the same work with a different politics of the floor: the legs say table, the drawers say chest, the combination says the English shop of 1690 had not yet decided which word would win, and the American shop of 1760 decided to keep the indecision because it looked expensive.

I am not going to pretend the attributions are settled. They do not, not on every attribution, not on whether a particular bonnet was recut, not on how much of a Philadelphia carving is Reynolds and how much is a shop hand who had looked at Reynolds. Heckscher is the catalog I keep nearest. Kirk is the book that will not let American work pretend it had no English uncle. Moses is the Newport monograph when the shells start arguing. A dealer’s “highboy” on a bracket-foot chest-on-chest is a stolen word of the ordinary kind: height sold as romance. Ask whether there are legs. Ask whether the lower section is a stand or a second case. Ask whether the top opens. If the top opens, you are not in this essay.

The living room did not invent this object. The living room inherited it, shortened the name, and sometimes cut the legs down so a television could sit on the lower case. That last injury is common enough that I look at the feet first. A high chest with later bun feet under sawn-off cabriole knees is a bedroom machine that was punished for being tall. A chest-on-chest with its waist screwed shut is a two-piece object pretending to be architecture. Both still hold sheets. Both will still be called chests. Neither is a box with a lid. The lid lost. The stack is what we live with, in the room that is younger than either case.
