**Disclaimer.** Educational language notes for the BBF channel. Staged draft. Not a plumbing history, not a medical text, and not a joke at the expense of anyone who grew up calling the toilet a commode.

I have watched a grown person flinch at a word on a drawing.

The drawing said *commode*. I meant a low drawer case, marble top, the French machine from the last essay. They heard a toilet. We were standing in a shop in Ferdinand, Indiana, that opened in 1999, and the air went careful in the way it does when two dialects occupy one noun. I did not laugh. The flinch is the history. This essay is the map of how one French adjective meaning *convenient* ended up as a headdress, a chest, and a pot, and why an American living-room conversation still trips on the third.

English borrows *commode* from French *commode*, the noun use of an adjective, from Latin *commodus*: proper, fit, convenient. The OED’s earliest evidence for the English noun is late 1600s, before 1687, in George Villiers, second duke of Buckingham. Etymonline dates the headdress sense to the 1680s. Wordorigins prints the scene from “The Militant Couple,” published in a 1704 miscellany: Sir John, provoked, strikes off my lady’s commode; she throws his periwig on the fire. `[VERIFY: OED’s “before 1687” is Villiers’s lifetime; the printed text many of us can actually read is 1704. Do not flatten the gap.]` Fleetwood Sheppard, in the same volume, loads a court lady’s “bald Buff-forehead with a high Commode.” A 1719 song in *Wit and Mirth* puts “a huge commode sat cocking” on a woman’s head so she showed “as Tall agen.” The thing is a fontange: a tall lace-and-wire headdress fashionable at Louis XIV’s court, a scaffold you wore. Convenient, if you wanted to be seen. Inconvenient if anyone swung at it. That sense dies. It does not die all at once. It dies the way fashions die, which is to say it becomes a joke and then a footnote.

The furniture sense is a reborrowing, not a costume that grew drawers. Paris had already made the low veneered case. Chippendale, in the 1754 *Director*, plate XLVII, describes “a French Commode Table, with doors or drawers in front, and drawers at each end,” the middle “with sliding shelves to hold cloaths.” That is English print using the Paris noun for a London shop drawing. Etymonline dates the English furniture sense “decorative chest of drawers” to 1786, which is later than Chippendale, and that lag is worth leaving visible. Pattern books are not the same corpus as the OED’s quotations. A cabinetmaker can live with a word for thirty years before a lexicographer’s slip of paper catches it. I will not pick a fight with 1786. I will say the *Director* is already selling the object under the French name, and English households that could afford the object learned the name from the plate.

Then the pot.

A close-stool is the older English object: a boxed seat that hides a chamber pot, attested from 1410 in the OED’s *close-stool* entry. Convenient furniture. Of course someone, eventually, called it a commode. Here is the part this pack will not smash into a single year. Etymonline says the meaning “chair housing a chamber pot, usually kept in a bedroom” is first attested 1851, from the notion of convenience. Wordorigins, working the same files, prints William Dyott’s diary for 21 April 1802, Cadiz: no garden or backyard to any house, “the commode is always at the top of the house.” Dyott is in Jeffrey’s 1907 edition, volume 1, page 198. A *Times* advertisement of 1 April 1851 offers “Inodorous chamber commodes affording great comfort to invalids.” Night commode is its own OED lemma. I am not going to tell you which year “wins.” I am going to tell you they are different kinds of evidence. A soldier’s diary in Spain is a man using a household word he expects a later English reader to understand. A newspaper advertisement is a trade using the word in public. A dictionary’s first attestation is the earliest quotation that dictionary has filed. Collapsing 1802 into 1851 is how a caption lies. Keep both. Mark the contest. If you speak this on camera, say the names of the sources.

American English then did what American English does with a polite noun: it moved the word from the cabinet that hid the pot to the porcelain that replaced the pot. A Dallas Morning News classified of 14 February 1926 sells a Munger home with “Lavatory and commode downstairs, four good size bedrooms and bath upstairs.” That is not a French drawer case in the hall. That is a toilet. Midwestern usage — the usage I grew up around, the usage that walks into this shop — still hears *commode* and sees a bathroom. Some families never said toilet. Some still do not. There is nothing ignorant about that. It is a successful euphemism. The Paris *ébéniste* lost the American living room because a different convenience got there first.

This is why a client flinches. They are not uneducated. They are correctly hearing the dominant American sense. I am correctly using a museum sense. Both of us are speaking English. The repair is not to sneer. The repair is to put a city in the sentence. A commode in Paris in 1730 is Cressent’s monkey-swinging boys on gilt-bronze, a marble top, drawers. A commode in a Cadiz lodging in 1802 is, in Dyott’s mouth, the house’s necessary, on the roof because the plot has no yard. A commode in Dallas in 1926 is downstairs next to the lavatory. A commode in a Ferdinand bathroom in my lifetime is the toilet. If I write the word on a shop drawing without a qualifier, I have chosen to confuse a person I am supposed to be helping.

I do not use *commode* on a drawing unless the client has already used it for furniture, or unless we have had this conversation. I say low chest, or drawer case, or I say the French piece and I say French. If they want the Paris object, we talk marble, veneer, mounts, height of the dado. If they want something to hide a wastebasket, that is a different drawing, and I will not dress it in ormolu language. Gloag’s dictionary will give you the furniture sense in a paragraph. Gloag will not stand in the room when the flinch happens. The flinch is the American chapter.

A headdress, a chest, a close-stool. Three machines. One adjective: convenient. The convenience of being taller than the other women at court. The convenience of a drawer instead of a lid. The convenience of a pot you do not have to carry through a dining room. English kept all three until two of them became antique and the third became plumbing. The living-room pack has to live with the survivor. I will keep stealing the furniture sense back, one qualified sentence at a time. If I slip and say commode to a person who grew up with the bathroom word, catch me. That slip is how a sale hides a hinge, and also how a shop talks past the person paying for the box.
