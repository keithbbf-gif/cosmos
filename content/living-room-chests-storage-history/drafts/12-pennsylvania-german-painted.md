**Disclaimer.** Educational furniture history for the BBF channel. Staged draft. Not an appraisal of a painted blanket chest and not a county-of-origin certificate for a unicorn.

The Philadelphia Museum of Art’s 1803 chest for Margretha Bladt is tulip poplar and white pine, painted, with brass and iron, 30¾ by 54¼ by 22⅜ inches. Gift of Arthur Sussel, 1945. Accession 1945-12-1. Bern Township, Berks County. The front is arranged in tablets. A large tulip, rearing unicorns, zigzag borders, men on horseback, crowned men growing out of vines. The lid and the ends carry the same grammar. Inside, a household could glue fraktur — birth, baptism — to the lid. The museum says chests like this were given to young men and women coming of age, and that a bride traditionally filled one with tablecloths, clothing, blankets. Two sentences, both true, not the same sentence. I want them both in the room before anyone says “dower” as if it were a wedding license.

Monroe H. Fabian’s *The Pennsylvania-German Decorated Chest* (Universe / PMA, 1978) is the book this essay stands on. Beatrice Garvan’s PMA collection catalog is the other spine. Between them they took a type that collectors had been calling “dower chests” and made it a study of paint, construction, and county habits without turning every heart into a symbol dictionary. I will not improve Fabian by inventing a township for a bird. That is the one rule. Motifs travel. Shops share templates. A unicorn on a chest in a living room in 2026 does not make the chest Berks unless the wood, the paint, the drawer arrangement, and the comparison set say so.

The people are Swiss, Palatine, Upper Rhine. They settled the southeastern counties — Berks, Lebanon, Lancaster, Lehigh, Montgomery, others — and they kept a chest-making thought that Europe would have recognized: a lidded box, often with drawers below, painted rather than left in oak. The Met’s American Wing holds a row of them given or bought in the years when “Pennsylvania German folk art” was being named for modern eyes. 23.16, Rogers Fund 1923: Berks County, about 1780, yellow pine and tulip poplar, 28⅝ by 52½ by 23 inches. The Met’s own note puts unicorns and men-on-horseback among motifs often seen on Berks marriage chests, and mentions the Index of American Design as the later popularizer. 44.109.1: Lebanon County, 1786, poplar, Rogers Fund 1944. 34.100.7: Lehigh, 1800, yellow poplar. 34.100.8: Lancaster, about 1780, yellow pine. 34.100.6: Montgomery, about 1800. 34.100.2: Berks, yellow pine, birds and horses and flowers. Those are catalog geographies. They are not a folk map I drew on a napkin.

Keller’s work on the “black-unicorn” chests of Berks is the kind of local study that keeps me honest. Cabinet shops she lettered A through F, a decorator she could tell from the enamel-like paint and the spacing, templates pounced through pin-pricks so the tulips repeat. Dates on related chests from the 1770s into the early nineteenth century. That is a workshop argument. It is the opposite of “Pennsylvania Dutch magic.” If I cannot point to Fabian’s figure or Keller’s plate, I will say *painted chest, southeastern Pennsylvania*, and stop. A living-room owner who was told their box is “Berks County, 1780” by a man at a fair has a story, not a dendro date.

Construction is plainer than the paint. These are not Italian cassoni and they are not joined oak Hadley chests, though they share the hybrid already winning in the period: a lid on top, drawers in the base, bracket or ogee feet. Poplar and pine, because the paint is the face and the hardwoods were not the point. Dovetailed or nailed cases depending on the shop. A till sometimes. A lock. The paint is casein or oil on a ground, in tablets that mimic panelled architecture — arches, columns, checks — around the unicorns and the tulips and the riders. Original surfaces survive when a later owner did not “clean” them. Victorian overpaint has protected some of the first layer, which is a joke the Hadley people also know. Do not strip a Berks chest because a magazine told you to find the wood. The wood was never the show.

How the paint got there is a shop problem, not a folk miracle. Keller’s decorator “3” looks, on the black-unicorn group, like a man with templates: pin-prick the outline, pounce powder through, then paint. The dotted surrounds on stems may be the pounce marks kept as a border. I have not pounced a unicorn on this floor. I have laid out a repeating ornament with a template, and I know the difference between a hand that copies a hole-pattern and a hand that invents a bird. If a living-room chest has three identical tulips, look for the template before you look for a township genius. If the paint sits in tablets that ignore the drawers’ edges, ask whether the drawers are later. Making and use meet there: a chest filled with towels will wear the lid at the front edge; the facade, facing the room, keeps its enamel.

Making, on this shop floor, is a different humility. We can build a poplar chest with a lid and two drawers. We can even paint tablets if a designer brings a documented face. We cannot fake 1803. The thing that dies in a reproduction is not the unicorn. It is the hand that had a template and a parish and a German dialect on the next bench. I will not offer a “Pennsylvania German finish” as a menu item. I will say: if you want this type in a sitting room, you are asking for a painted volume with a lid, and you should decide whether the paint is a copy of a named object or a new thing that should not wear someone else’s initials.

Use is the coming-of-age pile. Blankets, socks, embroidered towels, the textiles a new household runs through in a year. For a woman, often the trousseau. For a man, clothes and the goods that were not tools and land. The chest sat in the bedroom or the *Stube*, not in a parlor that had not yet been invented in those houses. When those objects later walk into an American living room, they are doing Grier’s later work: display that used to be storage. The unicorns face the sofa. The lid still opens. If you put drinks on the lid you are in the afterlife essay. Lightly: a painted dower chest is a bad coffee table. The paint is not a finish for condensation.

I am careful with *dower*. Essay 11 already stole the word back toward law. In this literature, “dower chest” is the collectors’ English for the decorated Pennsylvania-German box, initialed and dated, associated with a young owner. Fabian did not need the chest to have gone to a church. Coming of age is enough. A chest dated 1786 with a girl’s name is a document. A chest with no name and a dealer’s “Berks unicorns” is a guess. Keep the guess in the guess pile.

The living room is younger than these boxes, as the first essay said. What the living room does to them is put them on axis with a fireplace and ask them to be folk art. They were household machines with a public face, like the cassone, without Florence. The street here is a township road. The civic display is a Sunday, a chest in a bedroom with the lid up, the linens counted. Paint made the counting visible to anyone who entered.

If you remember one object, remember Bladt’s 1803 chest and Fabian’s book together. The girl or woman named on a Berks box had a volume. The county is a research result. The unicorn is a template. None of those three facts should be asked to stand for the other two.

## Notes

Fabian 1978 is canon for this type; Garvan 1982 for the PMA collection. PMA 1945-12-1 (object 198734) for Bladt, Bern Township, Berks, 1803. Met 23.16 and the de Forest / Rogers Fund dower chests (44.109.1 Lebanon 1786; 34.100.7 Lehigh 1800; 34.100.8 Lancaster ca. 1780; 34.100.6 Montgomery ca. 1800; 34.100.2 Berks) are catalog geographies, not a motif map I drew. Keller 1984/1991 for black-unicorn shops in Berks. Do not assign a bird or a heart to a county without a figure number.
