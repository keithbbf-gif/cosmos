**Disclaimer.** Educational furniture history for the BBF channel. Staged draft. Not a timber guide, not a moth-proofing claim, and not an appraisal of classical Chinese hardwood.

LACMA’s Camphorwood Trunk, M.2015.69.5, is leather, camphor, brass, and paint, first half of the nineteenth century, made in China for people who were not going to live in China.

That is the sentence I want in the room before anyone says “the Chinese chest.” The object is a travel box. Red leather, floral borders, brass mounts, about forty-one inches long. The museum’s note — JoAnna Reyes Walton, 2015 — puts it in the Canton export trade that also fed Latin America: nesting sizes, two to five feet, the smallest sometimes packed with tea, wool uniforms in mind because wool feeds moths and a long sea road is a moth’s favorite weather. The smell is real. The sales pitch is older than this particular trunk. Camphorwood (*Cinnamomum camphora*, and the trade is sloppier than the Latin) has been used inside China for cabinets that keep insects off documents and clothes. Export workshops learned that Western buyers would pay for the smell as a guarantee. A guarantee is a commercial sentence. It is not a medical sentence. This shop will not call a chest a treatment. Cedar and camphor are woods with a smell. The insects mind. Your lungs are not a catalog.

Set that trunk next to a different camphor object, one that never needed leather. The Victoria and Albert Museum’s clothes-storage chest FE.77:1-1983 is the domestic cousin Craig Clunas put on the page in *Chinese Furniture* (1988, p. 90, fig. 78): a deep rectangular *yixiang*, camphor, a round brass lock plate, a stand. Christie’s, selling a related eighteenth-century Met deaccession, quoted Wang Shixiang on why the box is long — robes and dresses, not a tourist’s blankets — and pointed at the carpenter’s manual tradition that names deep clothes trunks and medium ones (*yilong*, *yixiang*). `[VERIFY: if you speak the Lu Ban jing passages, read Ruitenbeek, not a dealer paragraph.]` Same genus of wood. Different job. One is a sea trunk that learned Spanish as *baúl de alcánfor*. One is a household clothes chest that stands in a Chinese interior and does not care what a living room is. If you veneer the difference with the word “camphor,” you have done the moth’s advertising for it.

Then leave camphor entirely.

The Met’s Ming wardrobe 1976.193.7a–o is huanghuali, sixteenth century, more than eight feet tall, metal fittings, bought with Vincent Astor money in 1976. The museum’s own sentence is the one I keep: the closet as we know it did not exist in China; capacious wardrobes stored clothing and bedding below, hats above. That is a standing cupboard, a round-corner or square-corner cabinet depending on the family, joined in the classical hardwood manner Wang spent a career describing — mitered, mortised, no glue as a religion, baitong or huangtong hardware. Hongmu is not a synonym. Hongmu is a later, broader trade name for a cluster of dense dark woods that filled in when huanghuali became rare and expensive. A hongmu cabinet of 1890 and a huanghuali cabinet of 1590 may share a silhouette and share nothing else that a bench would call species. Dealers who say “rosewood” in English are often hiding that fork. I will not hide it. The living room that wants “a Chinese chest” is usually asking for height under five feet and a lid or a pair of doors. The Met wardrobe is a wall. Put it in a sitting room and you have imported a bedroom’s closet, not a camphor trunk.

Compound cabinets are another machine again. The type has many names in the trade — traveling cabinet, treasure cabinet, apothecary cabinet, *yaogui* when someone is sure about medicine. Small carcass, paneled doors flush with the frame, then a surprise of little drawers and a central compartment when you open it. Gustav Ecke published interiors of this kind. Auction catalogs still argue whether a given example held drugs, seals, writing tools, or games. `[VERIFY: do not call a specific cabinet a pharmacy without the catalog’s own hedge.]` What the living room wants from that type is pigeonholes. What the type wants from you is not to be scaled up into an entertainment center. The drawers are small because the objects were small. A remote control will fit. A cathode-ray tube will not. We will get to the tube. It does not belong in huanghuali.

Clunas is useful here because he refuses the romance I am refusing. *Chinese Furniture* is a V&A book about a collection, which means it has to live with lacquer court furniture, Ming hardwood, and later export in the same building without pretending they are one style. *Chinese Export Art and Design* (1987) is the other half of his warning: from the sixteenth century on, Chinese workshops made things for Portuguese, Dutch, English, and later American orders, often from a drawing the foreigner supplied. Canton camphor trunks in Western trunk shape are that trade. A huanghuali sloping-stile cabinet is not. If your living room contains a brass-bound camphor box with a shipping label ghost on the leather, you own export. If it contains a honey-colored hardwood cabinet with baitong face plates and a floating panel, you own — or you own a reproduction of — a different workshop and a different century. I have seen both called “the Chinese chest” on the same afternoon. That afternoon was a failure of nouns.

In the shop the failures get practical. Camphor is oily and aromatic. It machines. It also laughs at a water-based finish that was mixed for maple. Export trunks are often a camphor carcase with a leather skin; the leather is the part that tears at the corner, and the camphor is the part that still smells when you open a box that has been in an attic since someone’s grandfather came home. I will not give you a restoration sequence. I will say the smell is not a warranty. It fades. It was never penicillin. Clients ask whether a camphor chest will “protect the wool.” I say it will discourage some insects some of the time, which is what a tight lid and a clean closet also do, and that we do not sell medicine. The Lane essay will say the same thing about American cedar. Two woods, two countries, one dishonest adjective.

Compound cabinets tempt a different mistake: we get asked to “do something like that” as a living-room bar. Another pack owns liquor cabinets. I will not follow the drinks. What I will follow is the drawer. A wall of tiny drawers in a sitting room is a classification system. It wants objects that have names. If you fill it with the plastic that accumulates under a television, you have used a scholar’s cabinet as a junk till. That is allowed. It is also a tell. The till in a medieval chest was for the things you would miss in a fire. The tiny drawer in a *yaogui* was for the things you would miss in a prescription, or a game, or a desk. Tuesday clutter is not a prescription.

Hardwood cabinets tempt the third mistake: we get asked to drop a television into a reproduction round-corner cabinet because the doors already hide things. The yoke of a CRT made that request for thirty years. A flat panel makes it look foolish, which is a later essay. The older foolishness is botanical. You cannot get huanghuali at the lumberyard. You can get a reddish hardwood and a story. This shop will tell you the species we can actually buy, and we will not stamp a Ming date on a door because the hardware is yellow.

What a living room can honestly take from this pile is modest. A low camphor clothes chest as a blanket box — the *yixiang* thought, not the sea-trunk thought — is a chest with a lid, and we already know what a chest is. An export trunk as a coffee table is a travel box pretending it never left the hold; that pretence has its own chapter. A pair of doors on a low cabinet is a credenza problem in Italian, a sideboard problem in English, and a *gui* problem in Chinese. None of those sentences requires moths, emperors, or a single national silhouette.

I work in Ferdinand, not in Suzhou. The useful import is the split. Smell is a market. Hardwood is a cabinetmaking tradition. Compound drawers are a small-object architecture. Leather over camphor is a ship. If you keep those apart, you can put any one of them against the long wall and know what you are living with. If you fuse them, you will buy a trunk and call it a Ming wardrobe, or you will buy a wardrobe and wonder why it will not go up the stairs.

There is no single Chinese chest. There are chests China made, and chests China made for people who would open them somewhere else. The living room, being young, takes both and asks for a smell. The smell was never the joinery.
