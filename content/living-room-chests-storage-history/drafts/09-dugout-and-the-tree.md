**Disclaimer.** Educational furniture history for the BBF channel. Staged draft. Not a dating service for a log on a porch and not a repair specification for a parish chest.

The dug-out at St Michael and All Angels, Kingstone, Herefordshire, was a tree before it was a box. Andy Moir’s 2021 tree-ring work, published in Rachel Sycamore’s Woolhope gazetteer, puts the felling and conversion of that oak between 1243 and 1273. The lid is a slice of the same thought. The cavity still shows the adze. Iron has been asked to keep a hollow log from becoming two halves of firewood. That is a thirteenth-century object that a nineteenth-century antiquarian would have called primitive, and therefore first. The rings do not agree.

This essay has one problem: a hollowed log is not a date. It is a way of making a box. Folk museums still stand dug-outs in the oldest corner of the room. Dealers still write “early” because the tool marks look rude. Some of those chests are early. Some of them are rural and late and honest about it. Sycamore and Jodie Lewis, in *Church Archaeology* in 2022, said the quiet part with numbers. Dug-outs were not confined to the dawn of chest-making. They run, in the two western counties they tested, from the thirteenth century into the sixteenth, and the documentary cousins run later still. The simplest ironwork in their sample belongs to the youngest chest, not the oldest. Crude is not a chronology.

Eames sorted medieval chests by how they were built. That order still saves you from romance. A dug-out is a log with a lid. You choose a trunk — oak if you have it, elm if the parish has elm — you open a cavity with an adze and a gouge, you leave walls thick enough that the box does not split into kindling, and you cut a lid from the same tree or from a plank. The radial cracks will run where the medullary rays told them to run. That is not a fault in the design. That is the design admitting it is a tree. Chinnery’s English oak tradition keeps the same warning: the three early construction families — dug-out, boarded, clamp-fronted — coexist. Pickvance’s Westminster dates sit on the clamped side of that family. They do not make a dug-out older by looking at it.

I want the contrast with the six-board in the same breath, because the two are always being lined up as ancestors and descendants, and they are not. A six-board chest is six planks: front, back, bottom, two ends, a lid. The carpenter already had a saw and nails or pegs. The ends often drop as feet. The box wants to burst when the front and back shrink across the grain and the ends do not. Iron straps are not jewelry first. They are the thing that keeps the oak from opening at breakfast. A dug-out wants to split along the rays, from the inside out, because you have emptied the heart and left a hoop of sapwood and heartwood arguing with the weather. Different failure. Different shop. A man with an adze and a man with a saw can live in the same century and they did.

Sycamore’s dated sample is worth walking because it is a workshop argument, not a mood. Kingstone, 1243–1273, has the most intricate ironwork of the four that returned dates: the oldest, in this group, is the one someone spent money on. Orleton II, at St George, Orleton, 1347–1377. Munsley, St Bartholomew, 1354–1388. Feckenham, St John the Baptist, Worcestershire, the base felled 1435–1465. Lindridge, St Lawrence, 1519–1549, the simplest iron, the latest tree. `[VERIFY: speak those ranges from Sycamore’s table after Moir, not from an RFS newsletter that OCR’d the parish names.]` Kirkby-in-Furness, Cumbria, has a pair of dug-outs whose published rings sit in the 1610–1645 range. A Kent note on iron-bound chests points to a 1691 churchwardens’ chest at St Michael, Hernhill, as a seventeenth-century dug-out still in the building. `[VERIFY Hernhill before a camera.]` The type did not retire when the joiner learned to mortise a stile.

How you make one is not a mystery and it is not a weekend. You need a log of a diameter that will still be a box after you have stolen the middle. Too thin and the walls check through. Too green and the whole thing moves like a boat. The lid, if it is taken from the same trunk, will cup the way that tree cupped. Some lids sit in a rebate. Some sit flush, as Sycamore describes on a small elm example at Eckington, where the lid is a slice of the same log and the cavity still carries the adze. Hinges on a dug-out are often straps, because there is no stile to house a pin. Three locks on a parish box are not a flourish. They are a committee. The priest keeps a key, the churchwardens keep the others, and the deed does not travel without a meeting. That is furniture as law. The living room inherited the shape and forgot the meeting.

In a folk museum the dug-out is asked to do a different job. It stands for “before joinery,” which is a story the open-air gallery likes because a log photographs as origin. I have no quarrel with a log. I quarrel with the caption. A Swedish or Norwegian farm chest hollowed from a trunk in the eighteenth century is not a cousin of Kingstone because it looks rude. It is a cousin of whoever still had a tree and not a joiner, or who wanted the strength of a hoop instead of the convenience of boards. American “log chests” in mountain collections get the same lazy early. Some of them are nineteenth-century and local. `[VERIFY any named American dug-out against its holding museum; do not invent a county or a pioneer.]` Existence is not identity. A hollow log in 1850 is still a hollow log.

The shop I work in opened in 1999, at 1440 Main Street, Ferdinand, Indiana. We do not hollow logs. We mill, we join, we hang lids on frames. I still look at a dug-out the way I look at a wide pine lid in August: the tree is in the room. A joined chest lets the panel shrink inside a groove. A six-board argues with its nails. A dug-out has nowhere to go except crack. If you fill those cracks with filler and call it restored, you have taught the box to be a reproduction of itself. Better to let the rays show and to keep the iron honest.

Use is the other half of the argument. A parish dug-out held vestments, plate, the register, the money that was not supposed to sit in a house. It sat on flags, and the flags ate the bottom, which is why so many bottoms are later boards or a ruin. People sat on the lid because a chest is a seat if you are not careful, and the hinge paid for that habit. In a house the same form held meal, seed, cloth, the things that rodents and damp wanted. Volume is the point. You cannot sort a dug-out. You pile. The till, if there is a till, is a later courtesy, a small box dropped into a hollow that was not designed for compartments. When a client now wants “a primitive chest” for a living room, they are often asking for the photograph of a tree and the function of a blanket box. Those are not the same drawing. A six-board will take a till and a pair of battens and a stay. A dug-out will take a stay if you are kind to the iron. Neither one is a coffee table until someone puts a lamp on it, and that is a different essay.

I keep Kingstone in my head when a caption says early. 1243–1273 is early enough, and it is also a date, which is more than a feeling. The Tindridge-that-was-Lindridge chest, 1519–1549, is the one that should hang in every dealer’s office: simplest iron, later tree. The type persisted because it worked, because a parish could pay a local man with an adze, because a farm could spare a trunk. It did not persist because history had not yet invented the plank.

A living room that puts a dug-out against the long wall is putting a tree in the sitting room and asking it to hold throws. That can be an honest object if you let the cracks be cracks. It is a dishonest object if you hang a “medieval” tag on a log your grandfather hollowed for grain. The joiner’s chest has stiles. The dug-out has memory. Learn to see which one you are looking at before you tell the century.

The next chests in this pack are boards and frames and marriages. They are not later because they are prettier. They are later, or not, because someone dated them. The log was never a prologue. It was a choice.

## Notes

Sycamore’s Woolhope gazetteer (2022) and the dendro table after Andy Moir (2021) are the dated spine. The RFS newsletter summary OCR’d parish names (Ringstone, Beckenham, Tindridge); the gazetteer and the *Church Archaeology* paper are the ones to speak from: Kingstone, Orleton II, Munsley, Feckenham, Lindridge. Eames remains the construction taxonomy; Chinnery the English oak context; Pickvance the clamped-and-dendro cousin, not a dug-out catalog. Folk-museum “early” captions are the error this piece is for. No American log chest is named without a holding record.
