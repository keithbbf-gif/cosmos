**Disclaimer.** Educational furniture history for the BBF channel. Staged draft. Not a guide to South Asian marriage custom, not an appraisal of brass-clad trunks, and not a claim that this shop has a documented Indian commission.

The V&A’s chest IS.15-1982 is wood under steel and brass, nineteenth century, from Kathiawad, the peninsula that is now part of Gujarat, seventy-three centimeters high, eighty wide, fifty-eight deep.

The museum calls the type *patara*. Amin Jaffer put it on pages 116–117 of *Luxury Goods from India*. The catalog paragraph is the one I want in a living-room series, because it refuses the two lies this subject attracts. The first lie is that a dowry chest is a temple object. The second is that it belongs only in a bedroom photograph. The V&A says metal-mounted storage chests of this type are standard household furniture in Kathiawad, used across castes, especially associated with the Kathi, and that by Kathi custom a *patara* goes with a bride as a receptacle for textiles and other valuables. Then the sentence that belongs in this pack: in Kathiawadi houses, *pataras* are typically placed against one wall of the family sitting-room, most often with other trunks, boxes, and containers used for storage.

A sitting-room wall. Other trunks. That is not a parlor suite and it is not a cathedral. It is a room people sit in, with the household’s boxes in a line, which is the oldest living-room diagram I know.

I am going to stay inside what a catalog will stand behind. I will not invent a wedding procession, a locked jewel, or a priest. Marriage chests are a real class — cassone in Florence, hope chests in the American mail-order century, *patara* here — and the class is civic and domestic, not mystical. A box that travels with a bride is a box that has to survive a cart and then sit still for decades. Steel and brass over wood is how you make a carpenter’s chest look like it can take a knock. The cut-out floral plates on IS.15-1982 are not a screen. They are cladding. Underneath, a joinery carcase still has to keep its corners. If the metal is later and the wood is tired, you can see the tiredness at the lid. I have not had this object on the bench. I have had the American cousins: tin-covered trunks, sheet-brass campaign boxes, the hope chest that grew a veneer. Cladding is honest when it is doing work. Cladding is a problem when it is the only reason someone said the word *Mughal*.

*Peti* is a word you will hear for a box or chest in Hindi and Urdu and in a dozen shop conversations that are not trying to be catalogs. *Pithara*, *pitara*, *damchiya* — Rajasthan and neighboring usage for a heavy dowry chest, often teak or another dense hardwood, often iron-banded, sometimes with a drawer in the base. I am not going to flatten those nouns into one silhouette. A Gujarati *patara* clad in sheet steel is not automatically a Rajasthani iron-banded chest, even if both held a bride’s textiles. Regional work is the point, as it was with Korean *bandaji*. If a listing says “Indian dowry chest” and shows a carved slant-lid box the size of a radio, ask for the place. If it says “Mughal” and the hinges are twentieth-century hardware-store steel, you are looking at a dynasty used as a stain.

Metal-clad trunks from courtly North India — Mughal and Rajput households, later merchant copies — are a real type: wood, sheet metal or pierced overlay, big hasps, sometimes a hidden drawer. They photograph like armor. Armor is the wrong metaphor and I am not going to dress it up. They are traveling and storing boxes for textiles, papers, and the household goods that could not be left on a *charpoy*. Which brings me to the object I will not steal.

A *charpoy* is a bed. Four legs, a turned or simple frame, webbing. People sit on it. People sleep on it. It is not a chest. Shelter magazines will put a *charpoy* in a living room and call it a daybed, which is fair, and then a stylist will park a brass box on it and the caption will imply the bed is storage. The bed is seating. The box is the chest. Keep them apart the way we keep a chair apart from a coffer in an English hall. This pack is about the box.

Sandalwood boxes and ivory-inlaid caskets are not living-room chests either. They are small. They live on a table. They held jewelry, documents, betel, the things a till would hold inside a larger chest. Museums are full of them because they survive in cabinets. A living room that needs somewhere to put blankets cannot use a sandalwood writing box except as a wish. I have been asked to “scale up” that wish. Scaling up a casket is how you get a coffee table with too many little legs and a lid that will not take a cup. We can build a chest. We cannot honestly call it ivory.

What was the making? On a metal-clad *patara* the wood is the box and the metal is the skin and the lock. The lid is still a lid. Weight is the first shop fact. Brass and steel add pounds before you put textiles in. A lid without a stay will teach a wrist. The V&A object is not huge by cassone standards, but it is dense. In a sitting room it sits against the wall because that is where a heavy box goes when you still need the middle of the room for people. The American entertainment center will later pretend a wall of doors is architecture. The *patara* line is older and ruder: the wall is for the boxes, the floor is for sitting, storage is not ashamed of being storage.

I work in a custom shop in Ferdinand, opened 1999. We do not have a Gujarati metal shop in the back. We have hardwood, hinges, and clients who have traveled, inherited, or bought a trunk and now want it to live with a sofa. The questions are the same questions as the *bandaji*. Is the door a lid or a face? Is the metal original to the carcase? Has someone already turned it into a table by parking a sheet of glass on it? I will not invent a named job. I will say we have been asked to put a glass top on a trunk, which is the 1960s–70s habit this pack will get to, and that a *patara* with a glass top is a sitting-room chest wearing a coffee table. The cladding still tells you it was built to be knocked. The glass tells you we got polite.

Use, now, is the V&A’s use with the labels changed. Textiles. Papers. The objects you do not want on a chair. In a Midwestern living room the brass floral plates read as ornament and that reading pays for the storage, emotionally, the way pierced Korean iron pays for a blanket drop. I do not sneer at that. I sneer at the caption that needs a temple to justify a box. A family sitting room in Kathiawad already justified it. Bok wanted Americans to sit in the front room on a Tuesday. Other houses were already sitting, and the chests were already on the wall.

If a South Asian chest lands in our shop, I want the regional name if we have it, the hinge type, and a refusal to baptize it Mughal for luck. If we are building a new chest for a sitting room that wants that weight and that metal, we are building an American box with a cladding problem, and we should say so. Honesty here is the same honesty as the stolen-words essay. *Peti* is not cassone. *Patara* is not Lane. A charpoy is not a trunk. Ivory is not a living-room volume.

The residual fact is the V&A’s, not mine. The chest sat in the sitting room with the other boxes. The living room did not have to be invented for storage to be visible. Storage was the furniture of the wall. We are the ones who got embarrassed and padded the lid.
