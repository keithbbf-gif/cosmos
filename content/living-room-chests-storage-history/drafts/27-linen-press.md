**Disclaimer.** Educational furniture history for the BBF channel. Staged draft. Not a laundry manual, not a television-cabinet design guide, and not essay 40.

A linen press, in the older English, is a screw.

The V&A’s small oak example — bought in 1892 as English, now read as Dutch — has sled feet, fluted uprights, ebonised jewels, and a central screw that lowers a plate onto a stack of folded cloth. Britannica in 1911 still knows this machine: a contrivance, usually oak, for pressing sheets and napkins, resembling an office copying-press. Brooklyn’s larger Dutch press, 12.877, stands in a period room so you can see the other half of the job: after you press, you store. The kas in the next gallery, if the museum has hung the story that way, is where the crisp cloth lives. Two objects, one household. Textiles were expensive. You did not leave them in a heap.

That screw is not the object most people mean when they say linen press in an American living room. They mean a tall cupboard: cornice, two doors above, drawers below, and inside the doors a stack of sliding trays. Georgian mahogany, Federal mahogany, later walnut, later factory oak. The trays are the argument. Drawers are expensive. A tray is a shelf with a lip that runs on bearers, oak-lined if the shop is being decent, sometimes with a remnant of baize or a later brass rail. You fold a sheet, you slide the tray, you do not rummage. The doors keep the dust off. The lower drawers take the heavier cloth or the things you want without opening the press. Two sections, because a seven-foot cupboard that does not come apart is a curse on a stair.

Chinnery’s *press* — the doored cupboard in the inventories — is the ancestor of this English sense. Cloth press, linen press, the 1552 “presse in yt.” By the eighteenth century the British trade has specialized the word toward textiles and toward this silhouette: a chest of drawers carrying a wardrobe that is not quite a wardrobe, because the inside is trays, not a hanging rod. Hanging is a later habit. Rods appear when clothes want to hang and when someone has lost patience with folding. A linen press with an original rod is unusual. A linen press with a later rod is a house deciding it needed a closet. I note the rod. I do not moralize it.

American shops made the form. Federal inlaid examples follow Hepplewhite’s oval panels and tear-drop cornices; plainer country presses are walnut or cherry with bracket feet and no inlay worth a catalog. They stood in chambers and in passages, in houses that still did not have enough closets, which is most houses before the twentieth century decided a bedroom without a closet was a failure. The living room gets them the way the living room gets everything it did not order: inheritance, apartment walls without closets, a piece that looks architectural against a long wall. Sheets in a sitting room are not a scandal. They are a household that put the cloth where the room was.

The trays themselves are joinery you can read with a hand. Oak sides, a thin bottom, a front lip high enough that a folded sheet does not walk off when you pull. Bearers in the carcase, sometimes just strips, sometimes grooved. If the trays are missing, the bearer scars remain: a line of nail holes or a clean rabbet, a ghost of width. That ghost is how you know a later owner gutted the piece. A cupboard that never had trays is a clothes press of another kind, or a wardrobe, or an armoire whose interior was always hanging space. I will not call those linen presses to be polite. The name is the interior.

Irish and British households also kept the literal screw in mind longer than American ones did: a hot press, an airing cupboard, a place where sheets go to dry against a cylinder. That is architecture, not a free-standing case, and I mention it only so a reader who grew up with a hot press does not think I have ignored the laundry. The free-standing mahogany press is the object that can walk into a living room. The airing cupboard cannot, unless you move house.

Then the tube.

I am going to touch this and then take my hand off. Marilyn Hoffman, *Christian Science Monitor*, 24 December 1981: “entertainers,” entertainment centers, American of Martinsville, Gary Hokanson thinking about Beta systems and video recorders and people who hated looking at a television. Pulaski, Lane, Hammary, Stanley. Some of the new pieces resemble armoires on the outside. Doors that fold back. Swivels. Slide-out shelves. Electrical outlets. The 1980s factory did not need a genuine Georgian press. It needed the silhouette: tall, doored, a cavity. Antique dealers and kitchen-table restorers did the other half of the work — pulling trays out of actual linen presses so a cathode-ray tube could sit in the stack. Ventilation was an afterthought. Speakers were an afterthought. James Larson’s later Audioholics complaint about entertainment centers as acoustic and thermal mistakes is a different decade’s dissent, not 1981 evidence. Essay 40 owns that furniture. This essay only needs the scar: a tray-bearing cupboard is easy to hollow because the trays were always a removable interior. The 1980s noticed.

On this floor, in a shop that opened in 1999, after that boom and before the flat panel made half those cavities look foolish, we still get asked to “make a linen press that will take a screen.” I will build a tall cupboard. I will not call a ventilated media box a linen press. The trays are the name. If you do not want trays, you want a press in Chinnery’s old sense — doors — or you want an armoire, or you want the entertainment piece another writer is paid to worry about. Moisture still applies. A hollow case with a heat source is a different drawing from a tray stack that holds cotton. August will tell you which drawing you actually built.

Hoffman named companies. I will not turn those names into a product timeline. Lane appears in her sentence; Lane also appears later in this pack as a cedar chest, which is a lid, not a press. Do not fuse them. Pulaski and Stanley were hunting a silhouette. The silhouette they hunted was already tired as a linen cupboard. That is the whole touch this essay is allowed.

Two machines, then. A screw that makes cloth flat. A cupboard whose interior is a chest of trays. The first belongs to the laundry and to Dutch genre painting. The second belongs to the chamber and then to the living room, and then, for a bad twenty years, to the television. I will restore trays when the object is a linen press. I will not put a sheet on a slide-out swivel and call it history. The word was already tired. The tube finished it. The trays, if they survive, are still the right way to hold a blanket without pretending the cabinet is architecture.
