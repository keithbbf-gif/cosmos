**Disclaimer.** Educational furniture history for the BBF channel. Staged draft. Not a wedding planner and not an attribution of a painted panel to a Florentine shop from a photograph.

The Metropolitan’s cassone 14.39 is poplar, linen, polychromed and gilded gesso, with a front painted in tempera and gold. Height 39½ inches, width 77, depth nearly 33. The label still says what the street was supposed to hear: *Cassone with painted front panel depicting the Conquest of Trebizond*, after about 1461, attributed to the workshop of Apollonio di Giovanni di Tomaso and the workshop of Marco del Buono Giamberti. John Stewart Kennedy Fund, 1914. Bought in 1913 from Stefano Bardini in Florence, who sold the Renaissance the way a later dealer sells a hope chest: complete, named, and ready to mean more than the joints can support.

The museum now says the quiet part. The chest is not intact. Parts are not original to each other. The painted front may come from another box. The Strozzi emblems on the ends — a falcon on a caltrop — belong to this carcase. Weisbach’s 1913 claim that the whole object came out of Palazzo Strozzi does not, therefore, interpret the battle on the front. That is the right way to start a cassone. On a document, a date, and a joint that lied.

A *cassone* is a large Italian chest. In the fifteenth century the nuptial version was a civic object. It was commissioned in the months between the *giuramento* and the day the couple went to live together. It was often one of a pair. It carried the bride’s *forzieri* goods — the word the inventories actually use, *forzieri da sposa*, betrothal chests — through the street from her father’s house to the groom’s. The street was the point. Gold leaf, pastiglia, a narrative everyone in the parish could read at a walking pace: Trebizond falling to Mehmed II in 1461, a tournament, Scipio, the kind of Roman triumph a Florentine banker wanted painted on the box that held the linen. The Met’s nuptial-furnishings essay is blunt about the vocabulary. *Cassone* in later writing covers the type. In the documents, *cassone* may be the sarcophagus-shaped subset of *forzieri*. I will keep saying cassone because that is the word the museum hung on 14.39. I will not let it mean a pine blanket box in Ohio.

This is not a hope chest. An American hope chest is a later, Protestant, mail-order mood: a girl, a pile of towels, a smell of cedar, a future that is private. A Florentine cassone is public. It is family heraldry on a cart. It is a month of a painter’s time on a front — the Met essay gives that workshop pace — and two months for the pair. Apollonio and Marco del Buono ran a shop that specialized in this work. Vasari, writing later, puts furniture painting in the lives of men we now pretend only made altarpieces. The account book of their shop, known from a 1670 copy, is the reason we can say the chests were ordered in pairs without inventing a diary. `[VERIFY the 1670 copy’s exact commission tally against Callmann before saying a number on camera.]` Callmann’s 1974 monograph is still the painter’s book. The carpenter, the gilder, and the locksmith do not get a monograph. They are in the object.

How you make one is a small factory. Poplar for the box, because Tuscany had poplar and because a painted chest is a ground, not a grain. Linen over the wood so the gesso will not crack off at the first cart rut. Gesso, then pastiglia relief, then gold, then tempera. The lid is a lid. It is also, once the procession is over, a bench in the *camera*. People sat. The fronts took the photograph. The ends took the arms. The lock took the textiles that were the actual dowry. A cassone without a lock is a picture frame that happens to be deep. In this shop we still think that way about a painted chest: the paint is the clothes, the hinge is the machine. I have never been asked to gild a caltrop. I have been asked to hang a lid that someone will sit on. The Florentines already knew that problem.

The *spalliera* problem is how the cassone left the house and became a painting. *Spalla* is a shoulder. A *spalliera* is a horizontal panel meant to sit at shoulder height, in the wainscot, above the chests, the daybed, the bed. Anne Barriault’s distinction, as the Met essay carries it, is size and viewpoint as well as vocabulary. Cassone fronts are long and low, built to be read from a street and then from a bench. *Spalliere* are larger, proportionally higher, drawn for a standing eye. Biagio d’Antonio’s *Story of Joseph* at the Met, 32.100.69, 27 by 59 inches, tempera on wood, Friedsam Collection, is the kind of domestic narrative that museums now hang at picture height. It was not a chest lid. National Gallery NG4906, *Cassone with a Tournament Scene*, is a rare survival of a box, and even that one was heavily restored in the nineteenth century: side panels from another chest, lion’s feet added to make it grander than it was. Very few painted chests remain intact. Usually the paintings have been detached. Dealers in the nineteenth century understood that a Botticelli-adjacent rectangle sells harder on a wall than on a hinge.

That theft is not only a market story. It is a change in what a room thought a picture was for. By the later fifteenth century, a panel with perspective wanted a viewer standing in a *camera*, not a crowd in a street. The chest did not stop being a chest. The best painting left it. What remains in the furniture galleries — 14.39 with a front that may be a stranger, 16.155, the Met’s other painted marriage chest, poplar, painted and gilded gesso, about 1480–95, Rogers Fund 1916, 38½ by 81 by 32 inches — are objects that still have depth. Depth is the thing a living-room chest still has to offer. A panel on a wall has stopped being storage.

I am not going to baptize an American hope chest with this word. Salesmen have tried. A “cassone” in walnut from a 1920s factory, or a “cassone” in pine from a midwestern antique mall, wants Florence and has Ohio. The civic part will not travel. What will travel is the habit of putting a family’s story on the front of a box and walking it through a town. Pennsylvania-German dower chests paint unicorns for a different street. Norwegian immigrant *kister* carry rosemaling across an ocean. Those are later essays. This one stays with the cart.

Use, after the street, is the *camera*. The pair of chests sat against the wall. Linens, clothing, the portable wealth of a marriage that the law treated as movable. The lid was a seat for visitors and for the people who lived there. The narratives — chastity, triumph, the schoolmaster of Falerii, Trebizond — were lessons hung at shin height. I will not pretend I know which lesson a Strozzi bride was supposed to take from a battle whose panel may not belong to her husband’s box. I know what the lock was for.

In a living room now, a cassone-shaped object is usually a coffee table with a past, or a reproduction with gold paint that will not survive a damp June. We can build a long low chest with a lid. We cannot build 1461. If you want the type, ask for a lid, a lock, a pair if you are serious, and a front that can take a story without being stolen off the box in a hundred years. If you want a picture, buy a picture and stop calling it furniture.

The residual fact is the one the Met already printed. The most famous cassone in New York may be wearing another chest’s face. The street still happened. The joinery still has to be asked, one corner at a time, whether it was there.

## Notes

Met object page for 14.39 (object ID 192693) is the opening document: Bardini, Kennedy Fund 1914, after ca. 1461, Apollonio and Marco del Buono workshops, and the later demonstration that the painted front may not belong to the Strozzi carcase. The Met nuptial-furnishings essay supplies *forzieri da sposa*, the pair, the month-per-front workshop pace, and Barriault on *spalliere*. Callmann 1974 remains the painter monograph. NG4906 is the rare surviving box with honest nineteenth-century restoration. Do not call 14.39 intact. Do not call a cassone a hope chest.
