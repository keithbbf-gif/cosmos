**Disclaimer.** Educational furniture history for the BBF channel. Staged draft. Not a poison-tasting reconstruction, not a Danish-modern catalog, and not a claim that a teak cabinet inherits a Renaissance workshop.

The Cloisters credenza (53.95) is walnut with intarsia, fifty-eight inches high, one hundred twenty-five inches long, twenty-five inches deep, 1440–50, attributed to Lorenzo and Cristoforo Canozi da Lendinara, and it is not a mid-century Danish box.

I start with the inches because the object is a wall. Eight panels. The upper two-thirds of each panel a rose-window geometry, lancets under that, square intarsia fields in the bottom third. Six panels make three pairs of doors. Behind the doors, two shelves. Dishes. The Met’s text is calm about function: a sideboard for preparing and serving food, later a display for expensive plate, often draped. Most medieval furniture is wrecked by use and climate. This one is unusually well preserved. Attribution is attribution: the brothers from Lendinara, northern Italy, associated with their early style. Lorenzo (1425–1477), Cristoforo (ca. 1426–1491). I will not turn that into a signature I have not seen.

The word is the walk. Italian *credenza*: belief, trust, credit. Medieval Latin *credentia*. The servant tasted — *credenziare* — so the lord would not drink poison. The table that held the assay became the sideboard that held the plate. Trust, made furniture. English had the same idea in *credence*. Middle English used credence for the tasting and for the side-table the food sat on before it was served. Churches kept the credence table by the altar for the vessels of the mass. Gloag quotes John Britton, 1838: a shelf across a piscina, or a buffet for plate. Then English mostly forgot the household sense and kept the church sense. In 1883, etymonline and the OED agree, English borrows *credenza* back as “an Italian sideboard,” a prettier word than the homely one it already had. By about 1920 the modern piece — long, low, doors, sometimes a marble top, living room or office — is wearing the Italian because *sideboard* sounded like dinner and *cupboard* sounded like a kitchen. Etymonline says the modern furniture piece begins to be mentioned in domestic interiors from around 1920. That date is the living-room hinge.

The 1911 *Britannica* entry on credence is still the church table and the medieval buffet. Gloag will give you both, and then the later Italian noun. I keep them in that order so a living-room caption cannot skip the altar and land on teak. A credence table in a sanctuary is a small, honest shelf. The Cloisters object is a ten-foot cupboard. The 1920s living-room piece is a long low cabinet that wanted a word fancier than sideboard. Three heights, three jobs, one family of trust. Only one of them has rose-window intarsia. Only one of them is 53.95. The inches are the argument a photograph will try to hide.

Do not hang the Cloisters intarsia on Danish teak.

I have to say it twice because photography will do it anyway. A Finn Juhl or a Vodder or a well-made anonymous teak cabinet from a Copenhagen shop in 1958 is a long low case with doors, a credenza in the 1920s–60s English sense, and it has every right to that twentieth-century noun. It has no right to a caption about poison-tasters and rose windows. The Canozi brothers were intarsiatori, perspective in wood, the same trade that dressed choir stalls. Danish teak is oil, sliding doors or hinged, a different joinery, a different century’s idea of a sitting room. Both are storage in a room people sit in. Both are not chests with lids. The stolen part is the romance. A dealer who puts 53.95 in the first paragraph of a listing for a 1960s cabinet is selling trust he did not pay for.

Essay 39 in this pack will name Juhl and Wegner and the Eames unit and Nelson’s poles. I will not steal that chapter. I will only plant a fence: if the caption needs Lendinara to sell teak, the caption is wrong. If the living room needs a long low case, the living room can have one without a Renaissance godfather. The 1883 English borrow is already the pretty word. The 1920s room is already the use. That is enough ancestry for a hi-fi.

Office credenzas are the other 1920s child: desk height, often matching a desk, files, a back-work surface. That is not this pack’s deep subject — another writer can have the office — but the living room borrowed the same silhouette because apartments and then suburban houses wanted a long closed plane under a painting. Hi-fi equipment fit. Liquor fit, which I will not make a chapter. Linens fit. The television, later, sat on top and then wanted in, which is how a credenza gets confused with an entertainment center. Essay 40 can argue with that. Here the argument is etymology plus inches: a Renaissance serving cupboard four feet high and ten feet long is not a 1958 teak box, and a 1958 teak box is not a lie if you let it be 1958.

On the floor in Ferdinand I ask, when someone says credenza, whether they want a lid, a drawer, a door, or a place to hide a cable box. Essay 04 already used that sentence. I will use it until it sticks. Doors and a long top: we can build that. Intarsia rose windows: that is a different shop, a different year, a different kind of drawing, and I will not fake Lendinara in maple. Marble on a new long case is a French commode memory, not a Cloisters memory; 53.95 is walnut, not stone. If they want the poison story in the room, I will tell the poison story and then build a cabinet that does not pretend to have tasted anything.

The Cloisters piece is on view in Gallery 16 at this writing. Dimensions again, because captions shrink it: nearly ten and a half feet long. A 1920s living-room credenza is often six feet and proud of it. Scale is part of the walk. The Renaissance cupboard is a wall of doors. The modern one is a sofa-length box. If you photograph them in the same crop, you have lied about the room.

The long walk is the point. Trust becomes a table. The table becomes a cupboard for plate. English forgets, borrows the Italian back as a luxury noun, and then spends a century putting hi-fis behind the doors. The Cloisters object stays in Gallery 16, 1440–50, Canozi if you accept the attribution, dishes on two shelves. The living-room object is a descendant by nickname, not by workshop. Nickname is allowed. Workshop is not transferable. If I catch a caption nailing intarsia to teak, I will treat it the way I treat a lid called a commode: the word is trying to buy two centuries at once. Pay for one.
