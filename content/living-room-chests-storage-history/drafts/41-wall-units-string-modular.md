**Disclaimer.** Educational furniture history for the BBF channel. Staged draft. Not an IKEA catalogue, not a USM dealer sheet, and not a claim that this shop invented modular walls.

In 1949 the Bonnier publishing house ran a competition for a bookshelf that ordinary Swedish homes could afford, ship, and assemble. Nils “Nisse” Strinning and Kajsa Strinning won it.

The shelf is wires and boards. Ladder-like side panels in coated steel, shelves that drop in, cabinets if you want them later. String Furniture’s own history is the one I will use: 194 entries, a winner that became furniture in the 1950s, an order for the new UN headquarters in New York in 1952, a gold medal at Milan, a place at Helsingborg’s H55. The origin story they like — Nisse turning a dish rack vertical because Kajsa had asked him to dry plates and he did not trust the towel — is company lore. `[VERIFY: treat the dish-rack anecdote as lore unless you have Strinning’s own dated telling.]` The competition brief is not lore. Books needed shelves. Shelves needed to pack flat. The living room, in a small postwar apartment, needed the wall to work without a carpenter.

That is the modular sentence. Shaker stole the triangle in plaster. Nelson, ten years after String, stole the whole wall with aluminum poles. The Eameses stole the bay. String stole the least material that would still hold a book. I have hung a lot of shelves. Wire ladders are honest about what they are. They also rack if you load them like a kas. The living room that treats String as architecture is the living room that has agreed to see storage. The living room that treats it as a poster is going to put three paperbacks and a plant on it and call it done. Both happened.

USM Haller is the same sentence in chrome and sheet steel, with a different birth. Paul Schärer Jr. comes back to the family metalworks in Münsingen in 1961, asks Fritz Haller to design a factory that can change, then — 1962, in the company’s own timeline — they start making furniture to match the building: tubes, a ball with six threaded holes, powder-coated panels. Patent 1965. The factory’s own offices get the system. Series production 1969; a large early order from Rothschild in Paris for hundreds of workstations. Some design timelines say 1963. I will not fight a rounding in the street. I will not pretend 1963 is a baptismal certificate either. `[VERIFY if you need a single year.]` What I will not do is invent a Swiss living-room myth. Haller is office architecture that walked home. It looks at home in a sitting room because a sitting room in certain decades wanted to look like an office that had good taste. The ball joint is the butterfly key of 901 without the oak. You can see how it comes apart. You can add a bay. You can move house with it, which a Pleasant Hill cupboard cannot.

Nelson’s CSS, 1959, sits between them in this pack’s chronology and was already named in the credenza essay: Herman Miller, poles, bays, American corporate modular. I will not replay the accession numbers. I will say the 1960s–80s “wall unit” in an American furniture ad is often a poorer cousin of these three — particleboard towers with glass doors, lights, a place for the tube — and sometimes an actual String or CSS. Hoffman in 1981 was already complaining that many wall systems were not deep enough for a CRT. So the modular wall and the entertainment center are not synonyms. One is a kit for books and objects. One is a cave for a bottle of glass. High Point fused them because fusion sells. A shop drawing should unfuse them.

IKEA is the kit that reached the houses CSS did not. I will only use years the IKEA Museum will stand behind. A bookcase called TIGA appears in the 1967 catalogue as a “real bookcase.” BILLY is in the 1979 catalogue — developed on the back of earlier models, Gillis Lundgren in the usual telling, oak and pine at first. The IVAR family is older than its name: a surplus-ish untreated shelf that IKEA sold, BOSSE then INGO then UFFE then IVAR; the 1975 catalogue already admits people are staining INGO and putting it in the living room instead of the garage. The 1984 catalogue jokes that the shelf is so finished they can only change the name. Those are living-room walls that unscrew. They are not Stickley. They are not Haller. They are the democratic version of Bonnier’s 1949 problem.

BESTÅ is the IKEA sentence people want me to date because it became the default low modular cabinet under a television. IKEA’s own service pages say the family was updated in 2015 and that older cabinets from before 2015 still take new doors. That is a fact about a system already in the world. Tertiary pages say the line launched in 2007. I have not verified that on an IKEA Museum catalogue spread, so I will not. `[VERIFY: open the Museum’s 2007 and 2008 catalogues and find the first BESTÅ page before speaking a year.]` What I can say without a year is the type: a grid of carcasses, doors, drawers, legs, a low wall you assemble into a sideboard or a media box. String’s ladders for a generation that wanted closed doors. Hoffman’s entertainer without the yoke depth. After the tube went flat, BESTÅ-type grids inherited the living room because they were the right height for a panel and the right width for Tuesday clutter. The year can wait for a catalog.

In Ferdinand we build made-to-order. We are not a flat-pack house. The modular lesson still lands on the bench. A wall of units is a series of carcasses that must line up, share a plane, and hide a floor that is not level. String forgives with space. Haller forgives with the joint. Particleboard towers forgive nothing; they telegraph every dip. Clients ask for “a wall of built-ins that we can take with us.” That is CSS/Haller/String talking through an American mouth. I tell them what unscrews and what is scribed to plaster. I will not invent a Haller we installed. I will say we have built bayed living-room storage that can be unbolted, and we have built plaster-to-plaster work that will die with the house, and those are different estimates even when the drawing looks related.

Use is books, objects, the extra blankets in a closed bay, the router in a closed bay, the display of the things a parlor would have put on an étagère. Open bays collect dust and photographs. Closed bays collect life. String’s original brief was books. A living room that uses String only for ceramics has not sinned; it has changed the brief. USM in a sitting room often holds the same mix an office would: paper, objects, a place to set a drink I am not going to turn into a bar essay. IKEA grids hold whatever will go through the door of a hatchback.

The residual fact is Bonnier’s. A publisher needed households to own shelves so the publisher could sell books. The furniture is a distribution problem that learned to look like design. Every modular wall since is a distribution problem: factory to truck to apartment, add a bay when the child learns to read, take a bay when you move. The Shakers built the wall into the limestone. String built the wall so it would fit in a carton. The living room, being young and often rented, prefers the carton. I prefer a door that still closes in August. Sometimes that door is on a wire ladder. Sometimes it is on a ball joint. Sometimes it is scribed to Main Street plaster and does not unscrew. Ask which kind of wall you are buying. The kit and the house are not the same machine.
