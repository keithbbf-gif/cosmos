The parish kept a box because the parish was a person in law.

That sentence is why we still have English church chests when we do not have the beds, the benches, or the trestles. You can burn a bench. You cannot, easily, throw away the coffer that holds the charter, the plate, the register. Eames knew this from the documents. Pickvance knows it from the cores. I know it the way a shop knows a job that cannot be declined: the contents make the carcase sacred, and then the carcase outlives the contents’ first lock.

Four clamped chests at Westminster Abbey now have tree-ring dates. The Long Chest: 1170–1202. The Hutch-type Chest: 1237–1269. The Lesser Treaty Chest: 1271–1287. The Deep Chest: 1275–1290. Those ranges are Pickvance’s published numbers as this draft is staged. `[VERIFY against the latest paper before you say them on camera.]` They are not “Norman.” They are not “the oldest coffers in England” as a dealer’s superlative. They are oak that grew, was felled, and was joined in a century we can write.

A second group — pin-hinged, clamped, arcaded Gothic fronts in East Kent, St John’s Hospital at Canterbury, Graveney, and cousins — carries the earliest dendro-dated Gothic arcading on English clamped chests. Pickvance argues for a Canterbury workshop that was not simply the abbey’s extra bench. I like the argument because it is a shop argument. Templates move. Men move. A town that can cut arcade on a chest front can cut arcade on a stall. The Icklingham and Buxted chests have been called the oldest carved coffers in England; the dates have wandered from the late twelfth century to the late thirteenth as Pevsner, Cautley, and the parish changed their minds. `[CITE the current dendro, if any, for Buxted before you pick a side.]` Method is the point. Iron style and a romantic label are not a date. A core is.

Three locks appear on some parish chests because three men were supposed to hold three keys. Churchwardens, a priest, a split authority. I have not counted every surviving staple. I have read the habit in the literature and seen the extra hasps. A living-room chest with three locks is theater. A parish chest with three locks is a committee. Do not confuse them in a caption.

The Coronation Chair in the same abbey is the opposite kind of object: named maker, Walter of Durham, years around 1297–1301, a stolen stone, a chair that became a coronation seat by use. I mention it so the chests are not lonely. Westminster could name a painter-carpenter for a king and still leave the deed-boxes anonymous. Anonymity is not a lack of skill. It is a lack of the later habit of signing a carcase.

In a living room, the parish chest shows up as a look: iron, oak, a lock plate too serious for the remote controls inside. I have been asked to make “a church chest, but nicer.” Nicer means the iron does not scratch the floor and the lid has a stay. I can do the stay. I will not put fake rivets on a pine box and call it Westminster. The parish chest earned its iron by holding a legal life. Your throw blanket is not a legal life. It can have a chest. It cannot have that caption.

What to take from the church is the conservation of purpose. A box that must outlast a vestry meeting is built like it. A box that must outlast a fashion season is built like that. Pick the purpose before you pick the arcade. The arcade is clothes. The deed is the job. The living room’s deed is smaller — a passport, a photograph, the papers that are not in the cloud because someone in the house does not trust the cloud. That till still wants a lid. It does not need a fake faculty roll.

I close on the core, not the romance. If you cannot date it, say so. If you can date it, name the lab and the range. “Norman chest” is a story told to a tourist. A range of years with a hyphen is a shop note. This series prefers the hyphen.

A parish chest that still sits in an aisle has usually been emptied and refilled and emptied again. Registers to the archive. Plate to a safe. Odd keys to a vestry drawer. The carcase remains because moving it is a faculty and a fight. Furniture that is hard to throw away is furniture that teaches. The living-room analog is the inherited Lane that no one loves and no one will carry to the curb. Institutional inertia is a conservation strategy. It is also how a bad box stays in the good room.

Fabric rolls that pay for a lock and a pair of hinges are furniture documents even when they never name the box. A cathedral that can tell you what it spent on iron in 1280 is closer to a shop ticket than a guidebook caption. I would rather read a roll than a romance. The roll will lie about prices in the way accounts lie. It will not tell you the chest is Norman if the oak was felled after Becket.

The living-room lesson is institutional. A parish chest is shared property. A household chest is not, even when three people have a key to the same till. Shared property wants witnesses. Private property wants a hinge that does not squeak. If you copy the iron and not the institution, you have copied a look.

Parish registers moved, in later centuries, from the chest to a safe to an archive. The chest sometimes stayed in the aisle as furniture of memory. That afterlife is why tourists photograph iron. It is also why a living-room reproduction with three fake locks is a costume. Three locks meant three offices. One living room does not have three offices. It has a remote control and a story.

If you are asked to “do a church chest” for a sitting room, ask what is being stored. If the answer is extra throws, build a blanket chest and put honest straps on it if straps are wanted. If the answer is papers, build a till and a lock that a locksmith will service. If the answer is a look, show the client Pickvance’s dates and ask whether they want a date or a mood. Moods are cheap. Dates cost a core.
