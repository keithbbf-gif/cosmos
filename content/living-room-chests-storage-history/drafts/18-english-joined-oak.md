**Disclaimer.** Educational furniture history for the BBF channel. Staged draft. Not a joint specification and not a certificate that a coffer is “joined” from a photograph of three panels.

The Victoria and Albert Museum shows an English chest, W.5-1938, dated 1600–25, in the furniture galleries. Another, W.6:1, 2-1918, about 1650. A third, W.15-1933, 1630–1700. CIRC.540-1919, mid-seventeenth century, from Somerset. I am not going to pretend I have had those four on a bench in Ferdinand. I am going to use them the way Chinnery would want: as a date range in which English oak furniture is a joined thought, not a nailed one, and the chest is the object that still has a lid while the cupboard and the chest of drawers are already thinking in frames.

This essay has one problem: joined is not a synonym for old, and boarded is not a synonym for poor. They are two constructions. A boarded chest is boards. Front, back, bottom, ends, lid; nails or pegs; iron straps; ends often continuing as feet. Eames’s medieval six-board is that family. It does not die in 1600. West Country nailed coffers keep being made because a carpenter can make them. A joined chest is a frame. Vertical stiles, horizontal rails, mortise and tenon pinned with oak pegs, panels sitting in grooves so they can shrink without splitting the carcase. The lid may still be boarded. The till is at home here, a small lidded box housed against an end, usually the right, for the things you would lift the lid to find first. Once you have a frame, a drawer is not a conversion. It is another opening in a language you already speak.

Chinnery’s *Oak Furniture: The British Tradition* is the book I keep naming because it is the British tradition in one set of covers: boarded, joined, inlaid, inlaid pretending to be joined, the regional habits, the inventory words. He will not let *cupboard* mean a doored box before it does. He will not let a panelled facade on a boarded chest pass without a look at the ends. That look is the whole craft of this essay. Three panels on the front can be planted mouldings on a single board. They can also be true panels in a frame. From a photograph in a living room, you often cannot tell. You tell from the top of the stile, from the back, from whether the panel rattles in winter.

How you make a joined chest is the reason this shop still cares. You plane stiles thicker than the panels. You cut mortises. You cut tenons. You plough a groove. You bevel the panel edges until they enter the groove and still have room to move. You assemble with hide glue if you are later, with a dry pin if you are earlier and stubborn. You peg. You hang the lid on snipe hinges or on straps. You fit a lock in the top rail. You slide a till into the interior. The carving — lunettes on the top rail, lozenges in the panels, a date and initials — is clothes. I will carve if the drawing asks. I will not call the carving the joinery.

The till is the interior sentence. Essay 2 already defined it. Here it belongs to the joined chest as a standard, not a luxury. Documents, coins, a letter, later a pair of spectacles. You can reach it without emptying the blankets if it is at the end. People still ask, in this shop, what the little box is for. I still say: the things you would miss. A boarded chest can have a till nailed in. A joined chest can house one as if the frame had expected it. That difference is small and I like it.

Fit the till and you will feel why joinery is not a decoration. The till sits on the chest bottom, or on a rail, against an end stile. Its own lid is pin-hinged or leather-hinged, small enough that a hand finds it in the dark. If you nail a till into a boarded box, the nails telegraph when the end board moves. If you house it in a joined chest, the stile does not move in that direction. Same little box, different argument with February. Panel shrinkage is the other winter lesson. A panel left free in its groove will show a line of unfinished wood in a dry room. That line is not a defect. It is the chest working. Glue the panel around the perimeter and the stile splits instead, which is how a living-room “restoration” becomes a crack you cannot hide with wax. Boarded ends do not offer you that choice. They split along the grain and you strap them. Two traditions, two repairs.

Use is household linen, clothing, plate if the lock is serious, the same jobs the boarded chest had, with a better chance of surviving central heating. Panels move. Boards split. A living room with a joined oak coffer as a window seat is using the lid as a bench, which is period and also how lids die. A stay is kindness. A cushion is a later kindness that hides the wear. Regional England kept making these into the eighteenth century, lunette-carved, dated 1690, 1703, while walnut veneer and bun feet took the towns. The V&A’s Bateman Chest, W.33-1948, London, 1720, James Moore the elder, is already another world: gilt gesso, not the parish oak of CIRC.540-1919. I mention it so the century is not flattened. Joined oak is a tradition, not a year.

American joiners brought the thought. Ipswich, Massachusetts, the Searle-Dennis shop tradition, Met 10.125.23, 1685–1700, red oak, white oak, hard maple, white pine, is not English, and it is the same grammar. Forman taught us to see New England joined work as a trade, not a folk miracle. The Hadley and sunflower essays will spend that grammar on particular carvings. This essay should stay on the English bench long enough for the reader to feel a mortise.

In Ferdinand we join carcases every week. We do not often join them in riven oak with a till at the right. When we do a blanket chest, we are usually in milled hardwood, a lid, maybe a drawer — a polite mule. The joined oak chest in a living room now is almost always an antique, or a reproduction that wants to be sat on. I will ask whether the panels are free. If they are glued around the edge “so they don’t rattle,” someone has stopped the chest from being joined. They have made a picture of a chest.

The residual is the groove. A boarded box has no place for the wood to go except into a crack. A joined chest has a groove. The living-room chest of drawers you already own is speaking that language, even if the lid is gone. The frame taught the drawer. The drawer paid the rent. The oak, if it is English and seventeenth-century and true, still has a till in the dark.

## Notes

Chinnery, *Oak Furniture: The British Tradition*, is the book. Eames for boarded and clamped predecessors. V&A W.5-1938, W.6:1, 2-1918, W.15-1933, CIRC.540-1919 (Somerset), and W.33-1948 (Bateman, 1720, another world) are the London shelf. Met 10.125.23 (Ipswich, Searle-Dennis) is the grammar after it crossed, not an English chest. Confirm on the V&A page that W.5-1938 is joined before describing its panels in a close-up. Till at the right-hand end is common, not a law.
