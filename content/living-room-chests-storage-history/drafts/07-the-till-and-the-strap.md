**Disclaimer.** Educational shop and history notes for the BBF channel. Staged draft. Not a locksmith’s manual and not a rubbing template for Gothic iron.

Open a decent old chest and look at the right-hand end before you look at the carving.

There should be, if the box was a household box and not a later empty, a small lidded compartment fixed against the end — the till. Sometimes it has its own lock. Sometimes the till lid is missing and you can see the groove. Eames finds tills in the documents because the documents care about the things that fit in tills: coin, a deed, a ring, the letter. The chest holds volume. The till holds priority. You can empty a chest of blankets and still not have the paper. You cannot empty a till without meaning to.

I still build tills. Clients ask if the little box is “necessary.” Necessity is the wrong word. A till is a decision about what you would lift the lid to save. If the answer is “nothing,” you do not need a chest. You need a donation pile. If the answer is “the passports and the extra house key,” you need a till, and you need it at the end you can reach without taking out the winter.

The strap is the other fitting that is not decoration first. Met 13.205.2, Westphalia, fifteenth century, oak, iron banding with fleur-de-lis terminals: the museum will tell you the terminals keep decorative forms from earlier Gothic. The straps belt a box whose job is older than the terminals. They keep the boarded or lightly framed carcase from bursting. They give a thief something to waste time on. They give a cart something to bang that is not the oak’s arris. I have seen straps added in the eighteenth century to a sixteenth-century box, and straps added last year with black paint and a story. New iron on old oak is a confession. It may be a necessary confession.

Pin hinges belong to the clamped family and to a literature that once dated them “thirteenth century” as if a hinge were a coin. Dendrochronology has confirmed and stretched that habit. A pin hinge is a wooden or metal pin through stile and lid. It is quiet. It is also a tear waiting for a lid opened too far. Strap hinges are louder on the front and kinder to the wood if they are long enough. Butt hinges are later and they let a living-room chest pretend the lid is a tabletop. I will not put butt hinges on a box I have called medieval in a caption. I will put them on a blanket chest a child will sit on, because I like children with teeth.

Locks migrate. A chest may have had a sliding bolt, a hasp, a plate lock, a later padlock through a staple that is the only original iron left. Parish chests were picked. Household chests were picked. The lock you see is often the lock that survived the last thief or the last lost key. Date the box by the carcase if you can. Date the lock as a lock.

In a living room, the till and the strap become psychology. We hide the till as a “secret drawer” because Americans like secrets that are not actually secret. We hide the strap as a “decorative band” because we are ashamed of iron. I would rather show the strap. A chest that admits it is clamped is a chest that will not surprise you in a dry January when the humidity drops. A till that is obviously a till will get used. A secret compartment that needs a ten-minute speech will hold a dead battery and a takeout menu.

Westphalia’s fleur-de-lis terminals are pretty. I am not against pretty. I am against pretty that forgets the belt. The living-room chest that has no till and no strap and no lock is a coffee table that has not told you yet. We will get to those. This essay stays with the fittings that made a box a household.

Eames’s accounts pay for locks the way a shop ticket pays for locks: as a line, not as a poem. A pair of hinges. A plate. A hasp. Sometimes a till lock listed separately, which is how you know the till was not an afterthought. When the till lock is later than the carcase lock, someone decided the small box had become more valuable than the blankets. That is a household history you can read without a diary.

I have opened chests in houses where the till was stuffed with instructions for a television that no longer exists, and chests where the till held a marriage license and a key to a box at a bank that closed. The till does not editorialize. It holds what the last hand put there. That is why a till in a new living-room chest still makes sense. You are building a place for the next last hand.

On the bench, a strap is a length and a thickness before it is a lily. Too thin and it is jewelry that bends. Too thick and it fights the lid. The fleur-de-lis terminal is a way to end a strap without a sharp corner and with a little court left in the iron. Westphalia liked that ending. England liked a simpler fishtail or a leaf. I will not invent a national character out of a terminal. I will say the terminal is where the smith signed without writing a name down.

A till lid has its own grain problem. It is a small board. It cups. The hinges, if they are tiny butts, tear. Period tills are often pin-hinged or sit in a groove and lift out. A lift-out till is a tray. A hinged till is a box. I prefer the hinge if the contents are papers you do not want to dump. I prefer the tray if the contents are jewelry you want to carry to another room. The documents do not care. The hand does.

Straps that wrap the lid as well as the carcase are traveling straps. They say the box left the house. Campaign chests and trunks will take that story. A household strap that stops at the cornice says the box stays. Read the iron to the end of the strap. If it hooks a hasp on the lid, the chest was meant to be closed in a cart. If it only belts the front, the chest was meant to sit and not burst.
