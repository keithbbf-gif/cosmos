**Disclaimer.** Educational furniture history for the BBF channel. Staged draft. Not a dining-room package, not a silver inventory, and not the dining-table pack another writer owns.

The Metropolitan’s sideboard 45.77 is mahogany and satinwood, silver-plated copper, verre églomisé, about 1795–1805, eighty-nine inches long, and the museum’s first sentence is the one I need: unknown in American homes prior to the 1790s, then quickly essential to the fashionable dining room.

Storage and display of silver, porcelain, glass. A knife box riding along. Marquetry, inlay, a lunette, reverse painting on glass. It is a tour of Federal showing-off, and it is a board at the side of a dining room. That is the job. This essay exists because the job did not stay in that room.

English shops assembled the type out of a table. Robert Adam liked a serving table flanked by urns on pedestals — cutlery in the urns, a heater in a pedestal, a gallery at the back. Hepplewhite’s *Guide* (1788) and Sheraton’s later plates get the credit in conversation. The trade argument, which I will not pretend to settle, gives Thomas Shearer the serpentine inlaid sideboard in the 1788 London book of prices, and then gives Hepplewhite the popularity because his name stuck to a shop that outlived him. Sheraton, in the *Cabinet Dictionary* (1803) and the drawings people still photocopy, prefers a convex swelling between square ends, often reeded legs, sometimes a brass gallery. Hepplewhite’s name got glued to serpentine fronts with concave corners and tapered legs. Those are pattern-book dialects, not blood tests. An American shop in Baltimore or New York could mix them by Thursday.

What the new piece did was gather the pedestals, the bottle drawers, the silver drawers, and the serving top into one carcass on six legs, or four, long enough to hold a course and low enough to be a table you stood at. Cellarettes sometimes tucked under the center and rolled to the diners; that object gets a light essay of its own, and I will not steal it. Bottle drawers in the sideboard are enough here: dining storage, lockable, shaped for glass. A server, in later American retail, is often the same idea with more drawers and fewer cupboards. A buffet is a word that wanders from French *buffet* — a side for food — onto anything with a top you can plate from. None of these is a chest with a lid. All of them are cousins of the old cup-board, doors included, because by 1790 *cupboard* has already completed Chinnery’s walk.

The wander into the living room is a housing story, not a style story. Dining rooms shrink. Apartments lose them. The parlor reform — Bok’s living room, the bungalow’s one good room — asks a single space to host sitting and sometimes eating and always the objects that used to have a dedicated wall. A sideboard is already a long, low, closed case with a usable top. That is, by accident, the brief for a living-room storage piece. You take the knife boxes off. You stop keeping the gravy boat in the right-hand cupboard. You put linens, or the good glasses you still own, or the board games, or the hi-fi, or the router in a basket. The top takes a lamp and a stack of books. Dealers say buffet. Interior photography says credenza, which is the next essay’s stolen Italian. The object, if it still has bottle drawers and a serpentine front, is a sideboard in a room that no longer dines.

Montgomery’s Federal catalog will show you the American shop’s version of the same gathering: inlay, tapered legs, a center drawer, deep ends for bottles. Southern examples can be long and proud. New England examples can be shy and still have the bottle wells. The knife boxes that sit on top are their own small architecture; most of the ones on American sideboards were English, and I will not start a Baltimore knife-box industry from a wish. What travels into the sitting room is usually the long case, not the urns. Adam’s pedestals stay in the dining-room photographs. The carcass walks.

A buffet, in restaurant and later household English, is a place you stand at to take food. A server, in twentieth-century American retail, is often shorter than a sideboard, more drawers, fewer doors, a piece a waitress could work. Those retail distinctions are real enough to ask about and not real enough to treat as eighteenth-century law. Shearer did not draw a “server.” He drew a sideboard. If a living-room client wants a server, I ask whether they mean height, or drawers, or the absence of a dining room. Height we can measure. Dining rooms we cannot invent.

I have seen Federal sideboards in sitting rooms with televisions on them, which is a load the original legs were not asked about. I have seen them painted because someone was brave and the mahogany had already been stripped by a grandmother. I have seen apartment living rooms where the only closed storage is a sideboard under the window because the closet is in the hall and the hall is a lie. That use is legitimate. It is also how a dining word becomes a living-room word without anyone circulating a memo.

This shop opened in 1999 in Ferdinand, on Main Street, in a building that has seen more than one fashion in case goods. We build long low cabinets for rooms people sit in. When a designer says sideboard and the floor plan has no dining room, I ask what will live inside and whether anyone will plate food on the top. If the answers are blankets and no, we are building a living-room case that has borrowed a dining silhouette. I will not call it a sideboard on the invoice just to romance the inlay. I will call it what it does. If they are going to serve from it at Thanksgiving in the same room they watch in, then the old name is still earned. Mixed rooms earn mixed nouns.

Do not let me become the dining-table series. Pedestal tables, banquet ends, the height of a dining plane — another pack. Arts and Crafts sideboards with copper hardware — another pack, or a touch if Stickley must appear, not a chapter. What belongs here is the storage argument: a long closed case invented to hold the kit of a meal, later holding the kit of a sitting room because American houses stopped giving meals their own architecture. Shearer’s serpentine and Sheraton’s swell are how it looked when it was young. The apartment wall is how it looks when it is old and still working.

A sideboard that never left the dining room is complete in that room. A sideboard that wandered is this pack’s object: a board at the side of a life that no longer sets a separate table. The drawers still fit silver. They will also fit the other things. Ask which, before you cut bottle partitions out as if they were a defect.
