**Disclaimer.** Educational furniture history for the BBF channel. Staged draft. Not the desk pack, not a library-fitter’s manual, and not a catalog of secretaries for sale.

The Metropolitan’s secretary and bookcase 46.67.89 is mahogany, satinwood, and ebony, 1795–1810, possibly Philadelphia or possibly Baltimore, eighty-nine and a half inches tall, and the museum puts it where I need it: parlors and entry halls, on display, because a combined desk and bookcase was a symbol of literacy as much as a tool.

A fitted secretary drawer, the front of which drops down to make a writing surface. Books above. Correspondence and household accounts in the cubbies. Visitors could see that you had paper and spines. That is parlor storage. It is not, in this pack, an invitation to write the history of the desk.

I have to keep saying that because the object wants to wander. A fall-front is a lid that became a door that became a writing board. The desk pack owns the slope, the kneehole, the bureau, the partner’s desk, the office. We own the wall: a bookcase that holds a household’s bound things in the room people sit in, and the secretary only insofar as it is a cupboard that can shut the paper away when company comes. Chippendale’s *Director* already knows the library bookcase as a piece of room architecture — glazed doors, a pediment, a base — and the Met holds a 1753 drawing (20.40.2(15)) that was published as plate LXIX in 1754 and later renumbered. Those plates are for a gentleman’s library. American parlors stole a smaller version of the thought: books as furniture you live with, not only as tools you work at.

Glass is part of the storage. A glazed door is a cupboard that agrees to show. Dust still gets in, but slower, and the spines stay in the room’s performance of literacy. Solid doors make a press that happens to hold books. I have built both. A living room with solid-doored “bookcases” is often a living room that wants to hide paperbacks and a router. Fair. Call it a press if the doors are blind. Call it a bookcase if you can read the titles without opening. The breakfront’s projecting center is where a shop sometimes puts a solid fall or a pair of paneled doors, with glass in the wings. That mix is not indecision. It is a plan that wants books on the sides and a cupboard in the middle.

Breakfront is a shape, not a religion. The center section projects. The wings recede. In plan, a shallow T, or a push in the middle of a long glazed case. The projection does two decent things: it breaks a wall of glass so the object does not read as a shop window, and it gives you a deeper cupboard in the middle for taller books or a fall-front. English shops loved it for library bookcases. American Federal shops made secretary-bookcases that are not always breakfronts; 46.67.89 is a stacked case, not a three-bay wall. I will not call every glazed bookcase a breakfront. If the center does not step forward, it is a bookcase. If it does, say so. The word is a plan.

The secretary is the fall-front. French *secrétaire à abattant* is a drop front in a vertical case; English secretary often means the drawer that hinges down, with pigeonholes behind, under a bookcase. Newport’s great desk-and-bookcases — the Met’s 15.21.2, blocked drawers, shell-carved fall, a pediment that wants a church — are among the most serious pieces of American cabinetmaking, and they are also, the museum now says in so many words, workstations tied to the trade that included enslaved people, sugar, and the rest of the triangle. I will name that and I will not linger. Lingering is the desk pack’s job, and the moral inventory’s job. In this essay the Newport machine is a boundary: writing and books in one tower, too much desk to live here as a chapter, too much parlor storage to pretend we did not see it.

A public anecdote about this shop, and only the public one: the first piece at sixteen was a Bombay Chippendale secretary. That sentence lives on the about page and in profiles. It is not a product, not a client, not an hour count. I mention it because a secretary is how a lot of us learned casework — drawers, a fall, a bookcase on top, a pediment that will forgive you or will not. Living above the shop on Main Street in Ferdinand does not make that teenage box a Townsend. It makes it a reason I still look at a fall-front and think about hinges that have to hold a writing plane full of elbows.

The fall is hardware before it is romance. Quadrant stays, chains, later brass arms: the same family of problems as a chest lid, except the plane comes toward your ribs and then has to sit level with a teacup on it. If the stay fails, the fall becomes a trap. If the hinges are light, the screws walk out of the rail. Period secretaries that still write did not get that way by accident. Someone sized the hardware to the board. I will not specify a hinge here as a product. I will say the living-room secretary that is never opened still needs a stay that would work, because someone will open it, once, to see.

Parlor, not office. Grier’s parlor is a performance; Bok’s living room is a reform of that performance. Books in that room are part of the performance even when they are also read. Glass-front cases keep the dust off and keep the spines on view. Solid doors make a press. A secretary lets you shut the unpaid bills when the minister comes, or when the minister does not come and you would rather the bills not sit on the sofa. Twentieth-century living rooms put breakfronts opposite the fireplace and filled them with a mix of Dickens, Hummel, and, later, a stack of videotapes that did not belong. The videotapes are not a moral failure of the breakfront. They are the living room doing what it always does: stuffing new objects into old architecture.

What I will not do is specify pigeonhole layouts as if we were selling a writing life. Pigeonholes are interior joinery. They can hold stamps or they can hold nothing. The living-room question is volume and glass and whether the fall-front will be used as a bar, which is a conversion I have seen and will not romanticize. A fall-front used as a bar is a desk that lost its paper. Another pack owns liquor. Another pack owns desks. We own the fact that a sitting room needed a place for books and a place to hide paper, and that British and American shops answered with glazed cases, projecting centers, and a hinge in a drawer.

If a client says they want a secretary for the living room, I ask whether they write there. If they write there, we are on the border of the desk pack and I will say so. If they want the silhouette — books above, a flap, drawers below — for storage and for the look of literacy, we are in this essay. The flap still has to stay. A secretary whose fall is glued shut is a bookcase with a scar. Leave the hinge. Even if no one writes, the machine should still be able to.
