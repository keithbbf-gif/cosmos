**Disclaimer.** Educational furniture history for the BBF channel. Staged draft. Not a glossary that will win an auction argument by itself, and not a restoration guide for a joined oak cupboard.

Chinnery prints the line I keep on the bench: 1552, “my new cubbarde with ye presse in yt.”

A cupboard with a press in it. Two nouns, one object, and the second noun is doing the work the first cannot yet do. If you hear *cupboard* in a Tudor inventory and picture a tall box with doors, you have arrived too late. A cup-board is a board for cups. A shelf. A side table. An open frame that holds plate and drinking vessels where people can see them, because seeing them is part of the wealth. *Court* in this compound is not the king’s household. It is the French *court*: short, low, spare. Peter Thornton argued that reading in *Furniture History* in 1971. A court cupboard is a low, open display — three tiers is a common memory of the type — bulbous supports, a place to show silver. A livery cupboard, from *livrer*, to deliver, is in the same family of open or pierced serving furniture, food for the night, a household ration. I am not going to pretend those two words never overlap in the documents. They overlap constantly. What they share, early, is air. You can see through them.

Doors are a different machine. *Press*, *aumbry*, *locker*: those are the words that already mean a closed compartment. An aumbry is a doored recess or a small cupboard, church and house both, a lock if you have anything worth locking. A press is a cupboard with doors — cloth press, livery that has been enclosed, the thing you put textiles into so they do not take dust. Chinnery’s point, and the reason this essay exists, is that the open cup-board begins to grow enclosed compartments without immediately changing its name. The inventories say so if you let them.

1527: “A waynescott cupborde wt. two aumbries.” A wainscot cupboard with two doored boxes in it. The cupboard is still the board; the aumbries are the news.

1552: “my new cubbarde with ye presse in yt.” Same thought, different noun for the enclosure.

1565: “one littell cobord with a loker.” A little cupboard with a locker.

1600: “A ioyned livrie cupbourde with two close cupbourdes in it.” By 1600, Chinnery says, *cupboard* is beginning to mean the closed compartment itself. The old sense — a board for cups — is dying in favor of a doored box. *Press* keeps meaning cupboard for another century, which is how English gets itself confused, and which is how a later American dealer gets to say “court cupboard” while pointing at a press.

I like that ladder because it is a workshop ladder. Someone had an open frame. Someone fitted two small doors. Someone’s appraiser had to decide which word to write down. The object in the room was mixed before the dictionary was mixed. That is the usual order.

Look at the oak. The Met’s British court cupboard 1974.28.76, about 1600, walnut in this example, fifty and a half inches high — already not a side table you put your shins under, already on its way to being a piece of architecture against a wall. Titles lie in the same way inventories lie: they freeze a type while the type is still moving. Read the middle. If the middle is a canted cupboard with doors, you are looking at what some texts call a court cupboard with a middle, which is to say a press pretending to remember it was a board. The V&A’s later oak buffets, with columnar supports and a closed center, sit in that same argument. After the Restoration the fashionable house moves on to walnut cabinets and side tables. The oak cupboard becomes “early oak,” which is a market, not a date.

New England makes the American chapter, and it is mostly a press chapter. Large two-stage oak cupboards are the show pieces of a seventeenth-century household that has anything to show: textiles, silver, the things you do not leave in a boarded chest if you can help it. The Met’s 1683 cupboard from northern Essex County, 2010.467, oak and maple and yellow poplar, ebonized turnings that interpret classical columns without having seen Rome, channel-molded drawers with applied bosses. The museum calls it a cupboard, which is already the modern sense — doors, storage, status. American collectors, for a long time, said “court cupboard” for almost any joined oak press with a recessed upper stage and a shelf in front of the doors. Sometimes the upper stage is open. Often it is not. Chinnery’s warning travels the Atlantic whether or not the dealer has read Chinnery. If the thing has doors on the part that does the holding, it is a press. If you want the old adjective *court*, show me the air.

On the floor, this distinction still earns its keep. A client says cupboard and means four doors and a cornice. A client says cupboard and means open shelves for cups, which is historically the better sentence and commercially the rarer object. I ask whether they want to display or to hide. Display is a board, a plate rail, a court in the old sense, a dresser in the Welsh sense, a sideboard that has not yet grown doors. Hide is a press, a kas, a linen cupboard, a credenza, a wall of panels. Mixing them is allowed — 1552 already mixed them — but mixing them without knowing you mixed them is how you get a glass-front bookcase called a court cupboard because someone liked the word *court*.

Construction follows the nouns if you let it. An open cup-board is a frame and shelves and turned supports; the load is vertical; the show is the plate. A press is panels, hinges, a lock, interior shelves or drawers, a cornice that throws a shadow so the piece reads as architecture. Joined oak does both, sometimes in the same object, which is why the inventory needed two words. When we build a doored case in Ferdinand, we are building a press even if the purchase order says cupboard. I will not correct a client’s English in the room. I will correct the drawing. Doors get hinges and a stop and a lock if they asked for a lock. Open shelves get a plate groove if they are actually going to hold plates, and they get dust, which is the old cup-board’s tax.

Forman and the Winterthur literature on New England press cupboards are the American shelf beside Chinnery. They will not let a Connecticut sunflower shop’s cupboard be called court just because an oak dealer likes the word. Recessed upper stage, applied ornament, doors that close on cloth: press. Open lower shelves with turned supports and plate on view: closer to court, if you must use the adjective. Most survivors that Americans grew up calling court cupboards are presses with a memory of a shelf. Memory is not air.

By 1600 the language is already walking toward the closet. The living room, much later, inherits both habits: the urge to show, the urge to shut. A glass-front cabinet is a court cupboard that admitted weather. A blank-doored credenza is a press that learned Italian. Neither is a medieval hall’s board for cups, but both are still answering the 1552 sentence: a place to put the vessels, and a place to put the things you do not want handled. If a caption says court and the doors are closed, believe the doors.

## Notes
