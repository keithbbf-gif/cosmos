**Disclaimer.** Educational history for the BBF channel. Staged draft. Not an excavation report and not a claim that this shop has handled royal wood.

I am going to start with a number, because the numbers are how this material gets lied about.

Carter 021 is a painted wooden box from the antechamber of KV62. Cairo JE 61467. Burton photographed it. Carter published it in 1923 with plates that made every later postcard possible: the king in a chariot on the lid, hunting on one face, war on the long sides, the king as a sphinx on the ends trampling the enemies of the North and the South. When they opened it, the box held sandals, cult robes, necklaces, a headrest, and a belt. That is a chest doing chest work. It is not a throne.

JE 62028 is the golden throne. Carter numbered that one 91. If you have read a furniture series that hung 91 on a chest, the series swapped the objects. I am putting the swap on the table in the first page because this pack will not repeat it. A throne is a marked seat. A box is a volume with a lid. Egypt had both. The magazines prefer the seat.

Dry air is why we have the wood. Most of the world ate its furniture. Egypt, in the right tombs, kept gesso, linen, ivory stringing, and the cedar and native woods the joiners could get. Geoffrey Killen’s catalogs are the shop reading: legs, rails, the way a box is framed, the way a lid sits. I am not an Egyptologist. I am a person who hangs lids. When I look at a New Kingdom box I look at the runners first. Hatnefer’s canopic box in the Met (36.3.53a, b) is cypress under linen and gesso, painted white, shaped as a shrine, the base on two runners that imitate a sledge. Sledges moved weight over sand and mud brick. The box that holds the four compartments of a woman’s internal organs still admits it had to be dragged. That is furniture being honest about freight.

Hatnefer died in her seventies, early in the joint reign of Hatshepsut and Thutmose III. Senenmut, her son, could by then afford a burial that included the box, a gilded mask, a heart scarab, jewelry. The Met’s 1935–36 excavation below TT 71 is the find-spot. The canopic box is not a living-room object. It is a storage object whose job was theological and wet. I mention it because the living-room chest inherits the same grammar: a lid, a carcase, a reason the contents cannot sit on the floor. The reason changes. The grammar does not.

Ordinary Egyptian boxes — cosmetic chests, linen chests, the house boxes that never saw a royal valley — are the majority and the minority in museums. The majority in life. The minority in vitrines. Painted plaster over wood, ivory knobs, a sliding lid on a small casket, a gable lid on a shrine-shaped box. Killen will give you the joints. A sliding lid is a different hinge philosophy. It does not become a seat. It does not knock a child. It also does not open with an armload of blankets. Form follows the hand that used it, not a theory of “the chest.”

Tutankhamun’s painted box is a propaganda object that also held shoes. That double job is the Egyptian specialty. The sides teach you who the king is. The interior held what a body needed in a tomb that was also a house. I do not need to believe the tomb was a bungalow to see the storage. A headrest in a box is a pillow in a drawer. A belt in a box is a till without the till.

In this shop we do not build shrine-lids for canopic jars. We do build boxes whose outside is asked to speak — a carved front, a veneer that faces the sofa — while the inside holds the unspeakable: chargers, board games, the extra throw, the papers that are not for the coffee table. Clients want the outside to be Tutankhamun and the inside to be a closet. Egypt already ran that experiment. The painted box is beautiful because the painting is good, not because painting a chest makes it royal. A badly painted chest is still a chest. A golden throne is never a chest, no matter what a caption says.

Killen’s volumes are the closest thing this series has to a shop manual for the Nile. He draws the joints. He names woods when the wood can be named. He does not need the chariot to justify a box. If you only know Egyptian furniture from the throne and the gold, you will think furniture is treasure. Treasure is what survived in a king’s valley. The house used painted wood that did not get a JE number.

The joinery, when Killen can see it, is not primitive. Mortise-and-tenon, dowels, a shoulder that would still be a shoulder in Ferdinand. Ivory stringing is a veneer argument with teeth. Gesso is a ground for paint, and paint is how a box becomes a wall you can carry. I have gessoed nothing royal. I have watched a painted finish on a lid take a fingernail in week two because someone wanted the picture and not the film build. Egypt’s painted boxes lasted because the tomb was dry and because the gesso was a system, not a weekend. That is not mysticism. That is climate plus craft.

I want one more object in the room before I leave Thebes. Cosmetic boxes — small, sliding lids, ivory, a duck, a girl swimming — are not living-room chests. They are the till’s cousins: priority, not volume. Linen chests are volume. The painted box of Carter 021 sits between those scales. It is large enough to hold robes and small enough to be carried into an antechamber and set down as a picture. That in-between size is the living-room size. Too big for a drawer. Too small to be architecture. A box you walk around.

What to carry into the living room from Dynasty 18 is not the chariot. It is the runners, the lid, the refusal to leave volume on the floor, and the habit of putting a picture on the thing that holds your sandals. We are still doing that. We call the picture a finish. We call the sandals seasonal décor. The box has not noticed.

Carter 021 / JE 61467 is the chest. Carter 91 / JE 62028 is the throne. If a caption swaps them, the caption is furniture history done from a postcard. We will not do that here.
