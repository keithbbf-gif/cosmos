**Disclaimer.** Educational furniture history for the BBF channel. Staged draft. Not a coffee-table catalog, not a vanity specification, and not a claim that this shop has a matching high-chest suite on the floor.

The Metropolitan’s Newport dressing table (1994.449) is mahogany, 1740–50, thirty and three-eighths inches high, cabriole legs ending in slipper feet, a carved shell that belongs to the skirt rather than sitting on it like a badge. The museum calls it a dressing table. Almost everyone else, if they like the object, will call it a lowboy.

That second word is the late one. Gloag treats *lowboy* as a collectors’ term for an eighteenth-century dressing table, often made to stand with a matching high chest. Etymonline dates *lowboy* to 1891, a hybrid of English *low* and a supposed French *bois*, built to pair with *highboy*. Shop literature likes to say the word appears in *House Beautiful* in August 1899. `[VERIFY: I have not had that issue on the bench. 1891 and 1899 are both late. The object is not.]` The eighteenth century did not need the joke. It had *dressing table*, and in England sometimes *low chest* or a side table with drawers. Britannica in 1911, which is already speaking collector, says the lowboy is a small table with one or two rows of drawers, named in contradistinction to the tallboy, used most often as a dressing table, sometimes as a side table, oak or walnut or mahogany, brass pulls, and in the better Chippendale examples cabriole legs, claw-and-ball feet, carved knees, a shell under the center drawer.

So the machine is older than the nickname. A case of drawers, table height, on legs, a top you can use as a board. The back edge of the top is often left unmolded because the piece stood against a wall. The backboard is secondary wood — pine, chestnut — because nobody was supposed to see it. Norman Vandal, writing for people who would actually cut the legs, notes that last fact as construction, not as insult. If you find a “lowboy” with four molded edges and a show wood back, you are looking at a piece that expected to be walked around, which is a different social life, or a later restorer who thought symmetry was kindness.

English and American shops both made the type through Queen Anne and Chippendale. English walnut examples of the 1710s sit on cabriole legs with pad feet; mahogany takes over as the century warms up. American work follows the high chest: New England slipper feet and a restrained shell; Philadelphia a livelier skirt, ball-and-claw, carving that wants to match the high chest it was sold with. Met 32.93 is the Philadelphia dressing table made en suite with high chest 18.110.4. Same shop, the catalog thinks. Different carvers, the catalog also thinks, because the dressing table’s ornament has more depth and kick than the tall one. That is a useful sentence for anyone who still believes a suite means one hand. A suite means a contract. Hands vary.

The bedroom is the first room. Cosmetics, combs, a basin, the small drawers for the things you did not want a servant fishing out of a lid-chest. The high chest held linens. The dressing table held the morning. Britannica’s “sometimes as a side-table” is the hinge this pack cares about. A table-height drawer case with a usable top is already halfway to a parlor. You can set a candlestick on it. You can set a decanter on it. You can set, later, a lamp, a stack of books, a bowl that is doing nothing. Once the living-room reform tells an American family to sit in the front room on a Tuesday, a spare dressing table is a convenient immigrant. It does not look like a bedroom if you take the looking glass off. A lot of looking glasses were taken off.

What it is not is a coffee table.

A coffee table is a low horizontal plane in front of a sofa, a late type, a cousin of the cocktail table, a place for shins and magazines. A dressing table is thirty to thirty-two inches high, which is writing height, dining-side height, the height of a hand that is not reaching to the floor. If you cut the cabriole legs to make a coffee table, you have destroyed the drawing. I have seen it done. I will not pretend it is witty. The knee carving dies in the saw cut. The drawer bottoms end up closer to mop water than a Newport shop ever intended. The object becomes a trunk without a lid and without the honesty of a trunk. Clients ask, sometimes, whether a lowboy would “work” in front of a sofa. The answer is: it will work as a bruised antique. It will not work as the object it is. If you need a low box in front of the sofa, that is a different brief, and this series has other chapters for lids you can put your feet on.

Heckscher’s Met catalog splits the type the way a shop splits it: New England high chests and dressing tables in one group, Pennsylvania in another. That is not regional boosterism. Legs, skirts, and the way a shell is allowed to eat the rail change by river. A Connecticut Queen Anne dressing table can look almost shy next to a Philadelphia Chippendale one. Both are table-height drawer cases. Both were bedroom furniture first. Both are the object the 1890s needed a cute name for once Colonial Revival put a “highboy” in the hall and wanted a matching short sentence.

In this shop we still get the word as a mood. Someone wants a lowboy and means a credenza. Someone wants a lowboy and means a coffee table with drawers. Someone wants a lowboy and means the Newport object, shell and slipper feet, and then asks whether we can make the top marble because they liked a commode in a photograph. Those are four drawings. I ask which room, which height, whether the back will show, and whether there is a high chest in the story or only a nickname. Matching a high chest is a proportion problem: the dressing table repeats the lower case of the high chest at a scale you can use as a table. If you blow it up to sofa length you have a sideboard that learned the wrong dialect.

The living-room afterlife is not a crime. Occasional use is in the type from the beginning — Britannica already says side table. An apartment that has no bedroom suite can still use a table-height drawer case against the long wall, under a window, behind a sofa if the sofa is pulled forward. Drawers for the remote controls and the extra chargers and the cloths for the glass. A top for the lamp. That is storage in the room people sit in, which is this pack’s job. What I will not do is let the nickname erase the height. Lowboy means low relative to the highboy, not low relative to the floor. It left the bedroom. It did not kneel.

If a listing says lowboy and shows a lid, it is a chest. If it shows no drawers, it is a table. If the legs have been cut, it is a salvage. If it matches a high chest, say so: that pairing is the eighteenth-century sentence. The nineteenth century only gave us a cute word. The cute word is how the object walked into the parlor without having to admit it used to hold a basin.
