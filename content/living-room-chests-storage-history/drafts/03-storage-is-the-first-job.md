**Disclaimer.** Educational history for the BBF channel. Staged draft. Not a theory of all furniture and not a claim that this shop has excavated Orkney.

Furniture histories like a chair.

I understand why. A chair photographs. A chair has a sitter, and a sitter has rank. The klismos, the throne, the Coronation Chair at Westminster — Walter of Durham, oak, a stolen stone underneath, a named maker around 1297–1301 — those objects let a writer sound like a court. A box with a lid lets a writer sound like a house. This series is about the house.

House One at Skara Brae still has its dresser: two stone shelves on slab uprights, set on the wall opposite the door. A hearth in the center. Flagstone boxes at the sides that Childe called beds and that later fieldworkers have been less sure about. The furniture is still in the room because the furniture is the room. Orkney did not have timber enough for a framed seat, and it did not need one to make a rear wall do more than hold up a roof. Historic Environment Scotland dates the late houses roughly 2900–2500 BCE. Childe published a Pictish village in 1931 and then had to swallow the radiocarbon. The labels — dresser, box-bed, seat of honor — are inherited from a nineteenth-century croft. They are also the only nouns we have that still fit the masonry. I am not going to call that dresser the first living-room cabinet. I am going to say the first job of furniture, in a house that wanted to keep things off the floor, was storage facing the door.

Egypt kept wood because Egypt was dry. Hatnefer’s chair gets the magazine cover. The boxes and chests in the same tombs do the work: cosmetics, linen, the tools of a life that had to travel into a grave. A chair is a sentence about who sits. A chest is a sentence about what cannot be left in a bag. Most people, in most centuries, had more of the second problem than the first.

Medieval halls make the same point with oak. The marked seat is rare. The chest is common. Eames built a book from wills that leave a “great chest,” from accounts that pay for a board and a pair of trestles, from parish boxes that held charters because the parish was a legal body and you do not throw away the box that holds the deed. When the trestles are stacked, the hall is free for a court or for sleep. The chests stay along the wall. They are the movable closets. They are sometimes the seats. They are why a fifteenth-century room is still full of boxes if you have not yet met the standing cupboard.

The American parlor of 1880 inverted the ratio, or tried to. Grier’s parlor is a performance of comfort: matching suite, piano, the etagere with the objects that proved you had been somewhere. Storage in that room was supposed to be invisible or ornamental. You displayed. You did not stack. The living-room reform — Bok’s reform, the bungalow’s reform — said the family should sit in the front room on a Tuesday. Sitting on a Tuesday creates clutter on a Wednesday. Magazines, sewing, the children’s things, the father’s papers, later the records, later the tapes, later the remote controls that breed in the dark. The living room inherited the parlor’s shame about storage and the household’s need for it. That is why the twentieth century invented so many polite boxes: the cedar chest that smelled like a future marriage, the credenza that sounded Italian, the wall unit that sounded like architecture, the ottoman that you could put your feet on so you would not have to admit it was a chest.

In this shop we still lose arguments to that shame. A client wants “a clean look.” A clean look is often a photograph in which someone else’s life has been put in a closet down the hall. The hall closet is not in the picture. We can build a wall of doors that disappear. We can also build a chest that looks like a chest, which is an older honesty. I do not romanticize either. A disappearing door that binds in August is not clean. A chest used as a dumping ground for things that should have been thrown away is not honest. It is a lid over a decision you did not make.

Storage is the first job because life is material. Textiles take volume. Papers take a till. Plate takes a lock. A violin takes a shape that will not fit a cube. A television, for thirty years, took a depth that wrecked a lot of eighteenth-century reproductions. The chair can be borrowed. The box has to be sized. That is why chests are conservative. A lock plate can be up to date. The box is a box. Met 13.205.2, the Westphalian oak chest with iron banding, wears early Gothic fleur-de-lis terminals on a fifteenth-century body. The straps are fashionable. The job is older than Gothic.

I want the reader of this series to walk into a living room and see the storage before the upholstery. Where does this room put the things that are not for sitting? In a lid? In a drawer? In a wall? On an open shelf that will be photographed once and regretted weekly? Those four answers are the history. The chair can wait. The chair has had enough books.

If you make furniture, this is a relief. You do not have to invent a new sitting theory. You have to fit a volume to a wall and a hinge to a hand. If you buy furniture, it is a test. A living room with nowhere to put a blanket is not a finished room. It is a parlor that forgot it was reformed.

Inventories make the same argument without a photograph. A seventeenth-century New England probate that lists one joined chair and three chests is not a house that failed to buy seating. It is a house that had more textile than rank. Chinnery’s English inventories do the same math with different spelling: cubbord, presse, great chest. The appraiser counted what could be sold. Chests sold. Chairs, if they existed in number, would have been counted too. Their absence is data.

The living room of 1955, if you could walk into it without nostalgia, would confirm the first job in plastic and veneer. A coffee table with a drawer. A hi-fi cabinet. A cedar chest under the window because the hall closet was already full of coats the bungalow did not plan for. Open shelves with a few books and a lot of objects that were supposed to look like a personality. Grier’s parlor at least admitted it was a performance. The living room pretends the performance is casual and then buys another box.

I have measured rooms where the sofa was specified to the inch and the storage was “we’ll see.” We saw. The remote controls went in a bowl. The bowl went on a stack of magazines. The stack went on a trunk that someone had already asked us to make into a table. That trunk was doing the first job badly because we had asked it to do the second job — holding drinks — at the same time. A chest can be a table. A table is a poor chest. Decide.

The next essays stay with the box. We will name construction, then locks, then the rooms and the marriages and the ships that carried chests into other climates. Sitting will appear when sitting is the point. Most of the time, the point is what you hid.
