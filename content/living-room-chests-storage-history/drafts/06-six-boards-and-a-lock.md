**Disclaimer.** Educational shop and history notes for the BBF channel. Staged draft. Not a cutting list and not a license to “restore” a parish chest with construction adhesive.

Count the boards.

Front. Back. Bottom. Two ends. A lid. That is six. If you have a seventh, you have a dustboard or a till lid or a repair. If you have four, you have a box that is cheating or a photograph that hid the ends. The six-board chest is the carpenter’s chest. It does not wait for a joiner’s frame. It uses the planks a sawyer already made and the nails or pegs a carpenter already owned.

Eames put boarded chests in a family next to dug-outs and clamped hutches. The boarded box is the one people still make without calling a cabinetmaker. I have seen them in barns that no one would let a curator into, pine, the lid a single cupping board, the lock a hasp that last closed in a year no one wrote down. Those are not “medieval survivals.” They are the construction staying cheap because it works until the grain wins.

The grain will win if you let it. The front and back are wide boards. They shrink across their width. The ends are usually set with their grain running vertically, or they sit in grooves, or they are nailed across the front’s end grain in a way that will split someone a generation later. When the front wants to get narrower and the ends will not let it, the box opens at the corner or the front splits down a summer check. Iron straps — not the pretty Gothic ones first, the ugly ones — are clamps you do not take off. A lock plate that wraps a corner is doing two jobs. The key is the second job.

A lock on a chest is not jewelry. It is the reason the box is furniture instead of a crate. Parish chests held charters. Household chests held plate and linen. A crate is open to the room. A chest is a room that locks. I have fitted locks that were heavier than the lid. That is not a mistake if the contents are the point. It is a mistake if the lid is pine and the lock is a statement.

The lid of a six-board chest is the weak famous part. One wide board, or two, hinged with pins or straps. If you open it past vertical, the purchase on a pin hinge will tear the top off the back. Museum chests have replaced lids the way old cars have replaced floors. A snipe hinge or a tang hinge has almost no wood to hold. The Hadley chests will teach that again in a later essay. The six-board teaches it first, because there is no frame to hide the failure.

On the bench, I still build the type when a client wants a blanket box and does not want to pay for a clamped reconstruction they will not see. I say the word *movement* until they are tired of it. I will not glue the ends to the front as if oak were MDF. I will let the front and back run in a groove, or I will strap it, or I will talk them into a joined chest. A six-board that is finished like a piano is a lie about how it was born. A six-board that is oiled and strapped is a sentence I can stand behind.

Dating these boxes is where dealers get religious. Six boards do not make a date. A boarded chest can be 1300 or 1890. The iron, the lock, the saw marks, the species, the find-spot, and — if anyone paid — a core, those make a date. Pickvance’s dendro work is mostly on clamped church chests, not on every boarded box in a pub. If someone tells you a six-board is Norman because it is crude, they are dating their taste.

What the living room inherits from this construction is the honesty of volume. A six-board chest does not pretend to be a wall. It sits on the floor. It takes a blanket. It takes a lock if you have something worth locking. It will split if you put a humidifier on one side and a radiator on the other. That is not a defect in the Middle Ages. That is a defect in the room.

Species matters more than romance. English boarded chests are often oak because oak was the hall’s wood. American six-boards are often pine because pine was what the sawmill had. Pine moves more, dents more, and takes paint better. Oak splits with a sound you remember. If a listing says “medieval oak” and the end grain looks like eastern white pine, the listing is selling a century.

Hardware schedules on a six-board are short and they should stay short. A pair of hinges, a hasp or a plate lock, maybe a pair of handles if the box is meant to move. Handles on the ends tell you the chest left the room. Handles on the front tell you someone wanted the look of travel. I have put false handles on a box once, at a client’s insistence, and I still dislike the sentence those handles write. A chest that does not travel should not wear luggage.

The bottom board is the board nobody photographs and the board that fails on a wet floor. Medieval chests sat on stone. American blanket chests sat on pine floors that were not always dry. A bottom that is nailed up into the ends will drop its nails when the ends shrink. A bottom set in a groove has a chance. If you “restore” a six-board by screwing a new plywood bottom up from below, you have made a crate with old sides. Say so on the ticket.

Chinnery’s oak tradition keeps boarded chests in the vernacular long after the joined chest has taken the parlor. That is not a failure to modernize. That is a carpenter still having six boards and a lock and a floor that is damp. American six-board blanket chests of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries — pine, often painted, often with a till — are the same sentence in another forest. The paint is not a date. The cut nails might be. The till usually is original if it is still there, because no one adds a till for fun in a restoration that is trying to look expensive.

I close on the lock because the lock is the furniture. Without it, you have boards. With it, you have a household that decided some things were not for the hall. The living room still makes that decision. It just hides the hasp behind a soft-close lid and hopes you will not ask what the key was for.

## Notes

Eames / Pickvance / Chinnery. Six boards ≠ a date. Movement across the front is the failure mode. American painted blanket chests are cousins, not medieval survivals.
