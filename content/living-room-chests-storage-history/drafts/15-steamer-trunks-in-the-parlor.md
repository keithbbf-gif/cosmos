**Disclaimer.** Educational furniture history for the BBF channel. Staged draft. Not a luggage appraisal and not a coffee-table conversion guide.

Louis Vuitton opened a shop in 1854 at 4 Rue Neuve-des-Capucines. The house’s own history is allowed to say what the object changed: trunks had been leather, and the leather smelled; lids had been domed to shed rain, and the dome would not stack in a railway van or a steamship hold. Vuitton glued on a canvas, first the ordinary grey later called Trianon grey, and he built a flat lid rimmed in iron, with nailed beech slats, poplar for lightness. In 1867 he patented “a packing and travel trunk” for fifteen years. Slat, canvas, iron corners, rivets. *Malle à lattes.* That is a trunk. It is not a chest.

This essay has one problem: after the voyage, the luggage sat in the parlor as if it were furniture, and American rooms learned to confuse a steamer with a chest. A chest, in this pack, is a household box whose door is a lid. A trunk, in the later commercial sense, is a traveling box: canvas, slats, a lock that has to survive a hold, trays, maybe a printed paper lining. It may live in a sitting room afterward. That afterlife does not make it a cassone, a hope chest, or a campaign chest of drawers. The campaign essay already took the drawers that went to war. This one takes the box that went in the cabin.

Period catalogs are snobs about height. A steamer, in the narrow trade sense, is a low flat-top trunk, often about twelve to fourteen inches high, meant to slide under a berth. `[VERIFY the inch range against a named catalog page if speaking it as a rule.]` Wardrobe trunks that stand on end, hanging clothes in a closet that travels, are a later, taller cousin — Vuitton added a wardrobe line in 1875, and narrower clothes in the 1910s made that form more useful. Smithsonian NMAH 2022.0058.01.01, Anastacio Atig Omandam’s canvas wardrobe steamer from the Daguhoy Lodge basement in Stockton, is the immigrant labor version of the standing trunk, 42 by 24 by 23 inches, canvas, 1880–1920 type. It is a steamer in the broad American mouth and a wardrobe trunk in the catalog. I will keep the distinction. Under the bunk versus at the foot of the bunk.

Cheap slat trunks are the majority. Vuitton is the name that photographs. Hartmann, Oshkosh, Belber, the unmarked canvas boxes with basswood slats and japanned hardware — I will not rank them by a price I do not have. The construction is shared: a light wooden crate, a covering, slats to take the hits, metal corners, a lock, a tray. Labels on the outside are the Grand Tour. Blithewold’s Van Wickle trunk still wears Hamburg-Amerika, Hotel d’Angleterre, Hotel Russie, Hotel Santa Lucia from a 1903 trip. That is Grier’s parlor logic stuck to canvas. You had been somewhere. The etagere had souvenirs. The trunk was the souvenir that could still hold a blanket.

Vuitton’s later canvases are anti-theft as much as fashion. Stripes in the 1870s, Damier check 1888, the monogram patented 1896 under Georges Vuitton after Louis’s death in 1892. I will not do the brand’s century. I need only this: the recognizable covering made a trunk into a heraldry, like Strozzi’s falcon, for a class that traveled by steam. The cheaper trunk used the same slat idea without the heraldry. Both could sit in a front room.

Making, from a cabinet shop’s side of the street, is crate-making more than joinery. Poplar or basswood, nails, a molded edge, slats let into the lid so the stack above you does not punch through. Canvas glued and tacked. The interior: paper or cloth, a tray with canvas loops, a lid pocket for papers. The lock is the expensive bit on a cheap trunk and the religious bit on a Vuitton. A factory slat trunk is a production drawing: same crate, three canvas colors, hardware from a barrel. The slats are beech or oak on a better box, basswood on a worse one, nailed through the covering so the rivet line is the decoration. Open a cheap steamer and you will find a paper lining printed in stripes, a tray that sags, a tray loop torn by a hand that was in a hurry on a dock. Open a Vuitton of the same decade and you will find the same jobs done with a tighter tolerance and a label that lists prizes. The job does not change. We do not build steamers at 1440 Main Street. We have been asked to stand one as a table. Essay 42 owns that afterlife. Here I will only say: a trunk whose lid becomes a coffee table has stopped traveling and started taking rings from glasses. The slats were not made for that. If you do it, you are not restoring a chest. You are retiring luggage.

Parlor use is display that still has a hinge. After a crossing, the trunk that had lived under a berth was sometimes too labeled to hide. It sat in a hall because a hall is where luggage pauses, and then it stayed because the stickers did the work an etagere did with shells. Grier’s parlor is a performance of having been somewhere. A canvas box with Hotel Russie on the lid is cheaper than marble and louder than a stereoscope. Families who never crossed still bought slat trunks for the same silhouette: low, flat, a lock, a look of travel. Mail-order houses sold them as steamer trunks for attics. The living room inherited the ones with the best paper still stuck on. When the television arrived, some of those trunks were dumped; some were asked to be tables. The dumped ones were honest. The tabled ones are a later chapter.

Use on the ship is packing and stacking. Flat lids made cities of trunks in a hold. Domed lids, the older rain logic, wasted air. Use in the cabin is the low steamer under the berth, the thing you could reach without a porter. Use in the house, afterward, splits. Servants’ attics. Storage rooms. And the parlor, when the stickers were the point. Edwardian and American Gilded Age interiors photograph trunks in halls. Later, the same objects wait in barns until someone says “steamer trunk coffee table.” Lightly, because that essay is spoken for: the living room keeps eating travel goods. Campaign chests, immigrant *kister*, Vuitton slats. The sitting room is a dock.

Do not mix the Swedish immigrant trunk at the Smithsonian, 2002.0362.01, dated 1867, painted tulips, initials ABAD, found in a Jamestown attic, with a canvas steamer. That painted box is a household chest that traveled. It belongs next to Vesterheim, not next to Rue Neuve-des-Capucines. The confusion is exactly this essay’s enemy. Paint and a date do not make a steamer. Slat and canvas and a berth height do not make a dower chest.

I have lifted a canvas trunk with a broken handle and felt the crate flex. That flex is why the slats exist. A joined oak chest does not flex that way. If a salesperson calls a low trunk a chest, they are selling the living room a word this series stole back in essay 4. Ask: does it have slats? Was it meant to stack? Does the lock look like luggage? Then it is a trunk. It may still be the most honest object in the room. It does not become a cassone by sitting under a lamp.

The residual is the flat lid. Rain wanted a dome. The train wanted a stack. The parlor wanted the stickers. The living room still wants a low box against the wall. Call it luggage if it traveled as luggage. The hinge on a trunk and the hinge on a chest are both hinges. Only one of them was designed for a hold.
