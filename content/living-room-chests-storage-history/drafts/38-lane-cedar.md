**Disclaimer.** Educational furniture history for the BBF channel. Staged draft. Not a product-safety manual, not an appraisal of a serial number, and not a medical claim about cedar. For latch hazards see the U.S. CPSC, not this shop.

In 1912 John Edward Lane paid five hundred dollars at a bankruptcy auction for a packing-box plant in Altavista, Virginia, and put his twenty-one-year-old son, Edward Hudson Lane, in charge of making chests.

They did not, at first, put the family name on the door. The venture was incorporated as the Standard Red Cedar Chest Company: John president, Edward vice president and general manager, a corrugated-iron building at a junction of the Virginian and Southern railways, machinery ordered in a hurry. `[VERIFY: the $50,000 machinery figure lives in company-history retellings; do not treat it as a ledger scan.]` The product was a hope chest — a lidded box in aromatic red cedar, or cedar-lined, sold to households that were storing linens and clothes toward a marriage. Essay 11 already separated hope from dower; this chapter is the factory that sold hope as a smell. There were already dozens of American cedar-chest shops. Altavista’s advantage was location, a father with contracting capital, and a son who would learn, during the next war, what a factory could do.

World War I interrupted the romance and improved the line. The plant made pine ammunition boxes for the federal government. The Virginia Museum of History & Culture’s Lane page is dry about the gift: an efficient assembly system, then, after the Armistice, those methods poured back into chests. In 1922 the firm took the family name and went national with advertising. The tag line the VMHC still quotes is the one that matters to a living-room series: “The gift that starts the home.” Not the gift that finishes a parlor. The gift that starts. A bungalow needs a box. A marriage, in that advertising, needs a smell.

I will stop at the smell. Eastern red cedar (*Juniperus virginiana*, and the trade is sloppy) is a wood with a familiar odor. Moths dislike it some of the time. Lane’s copy leaned on moths the way Canton export copy leaned on camphor. This shop will not call either wood a medicine. We will not talk about airways, cures, or wellness. A tight lid and clean wool do more than a faded lining. If a chest still smells in 2026, that is a residual fact about heartwood. It is not a prescription.

The Girl Graduate Plan is the other residual fact, and it is a sales machine, not a folkway. In the mid-1920s — Lane’s sales manager J. Arthur Kraus in the Campbell County notes; some papers spell Krauss — `[VERIFY spelling against the VMHC Lane collection]` the company and its dealers gave high-school girls a miniature cedar chest at graduation, often with a path toward a full-size chest. Miniatures had already been shop-floor end-of-day toys. Kraus made them a national invitation into a furniture store. Edward Lane later called the plan one of the great promotions in the industry. Academic work on the Lane papers at the VMHC treats it as a case in youth marketing: get the future household’s buyer while she is still in a cap and gown. I will not sneer at the girls. I will sneer at the sentence that pretends a free box is not an advertisement. It was. It worked for decades. It withered when the hope-chest assumption about women’s lives withered. That withering is history, not a moral I need to embroider.

If you have a Lane in the living room now, you can often date it without a family story. The VMHC’s public instruction is the one I use: read the serial number backwards. Their example is printed so you cannot fudge it — 553170 means 07-13-1955, the thirteenth of July, 1955. Not every chest is that tidy, and later corporate chapters scramble habits, but for a long middle stretch the reverse serial is a factory clock. I like a factory clock. It is ruder than “circa mid-century” and kinder than a guess from a veneer.

What was the making? A lid, a carcase, a cedar interior or a cedar body, a lock, a till or a tray if the model ran to that, a base that might be bun feet or a scalloped apron depending on the year and the suite. During World War II and after, Lane was not only chests; the VMHC notes television cabinets for General Electric, tables, then bedrooms and recliners. The chest remained the brand’s childhood. In a living room the Lane is a blanket box that photographs as a marriage. In a bedroom it is the same box. Bok’s Tuesday sitting room takes it because a hope chest is prettier than a plastic bin and because the smell, while it lasts, does the talking the ironwork does on a *bandaji*.

Hardware is where I will be careful. Older toy chests and storage chests can have lid supports that do not hold a lid in every position; the CPSC has warned about lids that come down on a child’s head or neck. Lane’s documented, named hazard is different and specific: *Lane* and *Virginia Maid* chests manufactured from 1912 through 1987 with latches that automatically lock when the lid closes. The company recalled on the order of twelve million chests in 1996. Children died in them. CPSC later fined Lane for delay in reporting. Reannouncements followed, including 2014 and 2018. Replacement latches have not always been available; the Commission’s later public line has been to remove the lock. I am not going to tell you which screws. I am not going to draw a latch. If you own one, you go to the CPSC, not to a nostalgic paragraph and not to a shop-floor workaround in an educational essay. A lid stay that we hang in this shop on a new chest is a different conversation, and even there I will not publish a how-to. The history is that a marriage box can be a trap. That fact sits beside the miniature graduate chest and will not be sanded off.

Corporate end: Interco bought Lane in a deal approved in 1987. The VMHC calls it a hostile takeover. Company-history writeups give stock terms I will not pretend I audited. `[VERIFY: Interco dollar figures.]` Furniture Brands later. The last Lane cedar chest made in the United States, the VMHC says, rolled off in the summer of 2001 and the Altavista plant closed. The living rooms kept the boxes. Serial numbers still read backwards. The smell still gets called medicine at estate sales. It is not.

I opened a custom shop in Ferdinand in 1999, two years before that last American Lane. We do not manufacture hope chests for graduates. We build made-to-order casework. People still ask us to match a Lane they inherited, or to put a glass top on one, or to “make it a coffee table.” That request belongs with the padded lid and the trunk-as-table. What I will do here is refuse the romance. Altavista was a packing-box plant for five hundred dollars. The ammo boxes taught the line. The advertising taught the country that a lid and a smell could start a home. A living room that still uses that lid for blankets is using a factory correctly. A living room that uses it as a story about wellness is using a factory as a pharmacist.

The residual fact is the serial. 553170 is a Wednesday in July 1955 if you read it the way the Virginia museum tells you to. The marriage the ads promised may or may not have happened. The box is still a box with a lid. Check the latch with the people whose job is latches. Then put the wool in, or don’t. Cedar is a wood. It is not a vow.
