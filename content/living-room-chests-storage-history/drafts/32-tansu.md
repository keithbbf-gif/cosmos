**Disclaimer.** Educational furniture history for the BBF channel. Staged draft. Not a tansu authentication service, not a legal history of Edo sumptuary law, and not a claim that this shop builds Sendai ironwork.

Edward Morse, in *Japanese Homes and Their Surroundings* (Boston, 1886), puts the tansu in the closet.

“The tansu—a chest of drawers not unlike our bureau—is often placed within the closet, which is also a receptacle for chests and trunks.” Bedding lives in the large closets closed by fusuma. Bed-chambers, he says, rarely have the ordinary high closet an American expects. The cabinet stands inside the architecture; it is not the architecture. That sentence is why this essay exists. A later Western living room will treat a tansu as a credenza, a stair, a wall. Morse, looking with 1886 eyes, still sees a movable chest of drawers parked in a cupboard.

The word is later than some of the objects. Heineken — Ty and Kiyoko, *Tansu: Traditional Japanese Cabinetry*, Weatherhill, 1981 — is the English monograph this pack will not skip. *Tansu* is first recorded in the Genroku era, 1688–1704, two characters that seem at first to talk about food storage and firewood, both written with the bamboo radical, which is a hint that early containers were not all wood. Do not turn that etymology into a just-so story about bamboo chests. It is a lexical clue. The objects we care about are wood, joinery, iron.

Wheels are documented earlier than the Genroku word. *Musashi Abumi*, Asai Ryōi’s 1661 account of the Meireki fire, shows Edo trying to flee with chests on wheels. The fire started in the third year of Meireki, 1657, and killed on the order of a hundred thousand people; I will not pretend to a precise body count from a furniture essay. Dutch observers in the city — Zacharias Wagenaer is the name the tansu literature likes — recorded big chests on four wheels blocking the roads. `[VERIFY: quote Wagenaer from Heineken’s citation or from the diary, not from a dealer page.]` *Nagamochi kuruma*, coffers on wheels, are the oldest documented category of this mobile cabinetry. *Kuruma-dansu* is the later household name.

Then the ban. Tansu writers, Heineken among them, and later summaries such as the Dentsdelion note, say wheeled trunks were outlawed in Edo, Osaka, and Kyoto after the congestion of 1657: people could not run, firefighters could not get through. `[VERIFY: this is the standard furniture-history claim. It is not a statute I have on the bench. Mark it. Do not invent an edict number.]` In other provinces, wheels stayed. In Meiji, wheels came back even in the big cities because the law had become a memory. Brooklyn’s kuruma-dansu 75.126 is late nineteenth or early twentieth century, Yamagata, keyaki and sugi, *kijiro* lacquer, merchant quality, wheels as convenience, not as a 1657 street problem. Do not hang a Meireki moral on a Meiji chest.

Types, because a living room that says “we want a tansu” usually means one photograph.

*Isho-dansu*: clothing. Kimono, folded, in chests that are often stacked, two sections, sometimes with a long top drawer and a serious lock. Sendai *isho-dansu* are the ones people mean when they mean iron: open-grain keyaki faces, sugi carcasses, hardware from smiths who knew sword fittings, a vertical locking bar or a double-action lock. Kiri (paulownia) is the other clothing wood — light, stable, kind to silk. Sugi (cryptomeria) is the quiet boxwood. Keyaki (zelkova) is the show. If a listing says tansu and the iron is stamped foliage on thin sheet, look twice. Sendai iron is the argument, not a mood.

*Kaidan-dansu*: step chests. Drawers and sliding doors built into a stair. Brooklyn 77.142, nineteenth century, Kawagoe, seventy-eight inches of ascent that is also storage. Heineken is careful: often modular, movable if necessary, used in the Tōhoku as attic access and, in farmhouses, in the seasonal work of silkworms under thatch. Morse’s fig. 177 is the Western drawing of the cousin: “Kitchen closet, drawers, cupboard, and stairs combined,” boxes piled into a compact stair, some steps closed, a cupboard with a swinging door — a hinge Morse notes as rare in the house proper — bedding and lamps inside. He does not call that tansu. He calls it a closet in the kitchen. Heineken’s point, which I will keep, is that tansu are not furniture-as-architecture until later. A kaidan-dansu is the type that wants to be a house. Even then, the better ones can be taken apart. Mobility is the tradition. A stair that cannot move is a stair.

Kansei, 1789. Matsudaira Sadanobu’s reforms reached merchant display. The tansu literature says a two-section, double-door clothing chest for merchant-class women — *ryōbiraki kasane-dansu* — was an accepted form, sometimes limited in finish, unlacquered when lacquer would have been too much. `[VERIFY: Heineken’s page, not a blog’s moral.]` Sumptuary law is a real Edo tool. It is also a real way for a later writer to sound sure. I would rather be accurate and a little unsure than sure and wrong.

Koizumi’s *Traditional Japanese Furniture* is the other monograph when Heineken’s English needs a Japanese-language backbone. I am not going to fake a Koizumi page I am not looking at. I will say the two books disagree in emphasis the way good books do, and that a Sendai chest is still a Sendai chest.

On this floor we understand mobility. A case that ships in two sections, hardware that is the lock and the handle and the decoration at once, wood chosen for the job (light for clothing, hard for the face): that is shop thinking. We do not build tansu as a line. We do not put fake Sendai iron on maple and call it Genroku. A living room that wants a tansu can have a clothing chest against a wall, which is Morse’s bureau taken out of the closet, or it can have a step chest, which is architecture and should be admitted as such. What it should not have is a caption that says “Japanese furniture was always built-in” or “tansu were banned after a fire.” Always is a lie. Banned is a legal claim. The fire is 1657. The word is Genroku. The wheels are in *Musashi Abumi*. The closet is in Morse. Keep those four dates in four sentences and the living room will not be able to turn a merchant’s clothing chest into a philosophy of the East.

Morse, again, because his eyes are period and limited and useful. He thinks Japanese houses lack the clutter that fills American attics. He finds chests in the kura, the fireproof storehouse, with the dried herbs and the spinning wheel. He draws a kitchen stair that is also drawers. He does not write a theory of tansu. He writes a house. Heineken writes the cabinets that lived in that house and could leave it. A Western living room that lines three *isho-dansu* under a painting is taking Morse’s closet and turning it inside out: the bureau is now the architecture. Fair as use. Wrong as origin story.

A tansu is a cabinet that expected to move. When it stops moving, it is still a cabinet. When a Western room uses it as a wall, Morse’s closet has been turned inside out. That inversion is the modern living room’s habit. It is not the Edo definition.
