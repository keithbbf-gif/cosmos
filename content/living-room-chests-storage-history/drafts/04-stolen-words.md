I am going to steal the words back.

A showroom, a listing, and a museum caption all use furniture words the way a magician uses a sleeve. The object stays the same. The noun changes, and with it the price, the room, and the century you think you are standing in. This essay is a map of eight nouns this series cannot afford to blur. If you already know them, read it anyway. The damage is done in the blur.

**Chest.** Old English *cest*, from Latin *cista*, from Greek *kistē*: a box. In this pack a chest is a box whose primary door is a lid. When drawers appear under the lid we will say mule chest, or chest-with-drawer, and we will mean a hybrid. When the lid disappears we will stop saying chest unless we are talking to a salesperson who is talking about height. “Chest of drawers” is English living with a dead metaphor. The drawers won. The word did not get the news.

**Coffer.** A chest with a treasury smell. The word comes into English from French *coffre*, and it likes iron, money, and churches. A coffer may be a chest. It may also be a ceiling recess, which is how architects steal from furniture and never give the word back. In a parish, the coffer and the chest are often the same oak. In a catalog, *coffer* means “pay more, it sounds Norman.” Ask for the construction. If it is six boards and a lock, it is a chest that learned French.

**Cassone.** Italian for a large chest, usually a marriage chest, usually fifteenth- or sixteenth-century when a museum bothers to hang a panel from one. The *cassone* faced the street on a wedding day. It was painted or pastiglia’d or intarsia’d because the street was the point. A *cassone* is not a hope chest, though American salesmen have tried that kindness. A hope chest is a later, Protestant, mail-order mood. A *cassone* is civic. If you see a “cassone” in pine from Ohio, you are looking at a word that flew without the city.

**Commode.** French adjective, “convenient,” from Latin *commodus*. In Paris from about 1700 it is a low, wide, veneered drawer case, often with a marble top, made by an *ébéniste* — a worker in ebony, which is to say a worker in expensive foreign wood and in the gilt-bronze that made the drawers worth walking toward. Cressent, Oeben, Riesener. Chippendale, in the 1754 *Director*, puts “French Commode Tables” on a plate so London can want them. English then spends the same word on a headdress (late seventeenth century) and, later, on a chair that hides a chamber pot. Etymonline dates the pot-furniture sense to 1851; a Dyott diary of 1802 is in the word-origin literature. `[VERIFY before saying a year on camera.]` In a Midwestern bathroom, *commode* means toilet. In a Paris catalog of 1760, it means the most expensive box in the room. If you write the word without a city, you will embarrass someone who knows and confuse someone who does not.

**Bureau.** French for office, desk, the baize-covered table where writing happens. In British English a bureau is often a fall-front desk with drawers below. In American retail, “bureau” wandered toward the bedroom chest of drawers and stayed there, overlapping *dresser*. A dresser, older, was a sideboard for dressing food — *dressoir* — before it became a mirror with drawers. None of these is a living-room chest until an American apartment puts a bureau behind the sofa and hopes. We will let the secretary and the breakfront have their own essay. We will not let *bureau* mean “any drawers.”

**Credenza.** Italian *credenza*, belief, trust. The servant tasted the food (*credenziare*) so the lord would not drink poison. The table that held the assay became the sideboard that held the plate. English *credence* did the same work and then forgot it; the church kept the credence table by the altar. In 1883 English borrows *credenza* back as an Italian sideboard, a prettier word than the homely one. By the 1920s the modern living-room and office piece — long, low, doors, sometimes a marble top — is wearing the Italian because “sideboard” sounded like dinner. A credenza in a living room is a chest that lost its lid and gained a diplomatic past. The poison story is real. The walnut Cloisters credenza (53.95, 1440–50, Canozi da Lendinara) is a museum object, not a mid-century Danish box. Do not hang the intarsia on a teak door.

**Kas.** Dutch *kast*, the big cupboard of New Netherland and the later Hudson Valley. Kenny, Safford, and Vincent’s Met catalog, *American Kasten*, is the book. A kas is not a wardrobe in the English sense and not an armoire in the French sense, though all three are standing cupboards for textiles. A kas is architectural: cornice, paneled doors, a lock you can hear. It came into American houses that did not yet have closets. If a listing says *kas* for a 1970s pine cupboard, the listing wants New Amsterdam and has Ohio.

**Press.** In English inventories, a press is a cupboard with doors — cloth press, linen press, “my new cubbarde with ye presse in yt” (1552, in Chinnery’s citations). It is not a printing press unless you are in a different shop. A linen press in later British and American use is a tall cupboard, sometimes with sliding trays, for sheets. In the 1980s an American factory will take that silhouette, remove the trays, and sell it as an entertainment center. The word was already tired. The television finished it.

Chinnery’s warning is the one I keep on the bench. Before 1600, *cupboard* is still often a board for cups — an open side table, a court cupboard if you want the old adjective *court* (short, low). By the seventeenth century the word is moving toward a doored compartment. *Press*, *aumbry*, *locker* are the words that already mean doors. If you read a will aloud and hear “cupboard,” do not picture IKEA. Picture a board, then ask whether the appraiser said *close*.

I use these words the way I use species names. Oak is not “wood.” A commode is not a chest. A credenza is not a kas. When a client says they want a credenza, I ask whether they want a lid, a drawer, a door, or a place to hide a cable box. Those are four different shop drawings. The stolen word is trying to buy all four at once.

The rest of the series will keep stealing the words back, one object at a time. If I slip and say chest when I mean drawer case, catch me. That slip is how the history gets sold.
