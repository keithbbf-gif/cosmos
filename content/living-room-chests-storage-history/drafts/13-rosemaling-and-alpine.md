**Disclaimer.** Educational furniture history for the BBF channel. Staged draft. Not a valuation of a painted trunk and not a travel guide to Tyrol.

Vesterheim’s Vest-Agder trunk 1973.008.062 is dated 1837. Place of origin: Ryggesaas, Flekkefjord. Made by Vorm Mjaavatn, who carved with a hunting knife. Painted by Ivar or Kjetil Urdal — the museum leaves the painter as a pair of famous names. Initials for Peder Tollisen Ryggsaas. Later initials added in 1881 by Lars Bertesen Roiseland. The trunk held money and medicine. When Pedersen emigrated in 1890 he brought it. Family history then puts it among Ozette people near Ozette Lake, Washington, about 1900, and finds it again thirteen years later in an abandoned fishing hut. Gift of Augsburg College. Wood and metal, 28 by 45½ by 22½ inches. That is a lot of story for one box. The part I trust on the museum page is the making, the date, the style name (Vest-Agder rosemaling), and the immigration. The Ozette chapter is family history on a label. I will not improve it.

This essay has one problem: painted chests from Norway and painted chests from the Alps get stacked in American living rooms as if “folk flowers” were a country. They are not. Rosemaling is a Norwegian decorative painting with regional hands — Vest-Agder, Os, Telemark, Hallingdal, others — on chests, trunks, cupboards, bowls. A *kiste* is a chest. An immigrant trunk is often that chest after a ship. An Alpine *cassapanca* is an Italian word for a chest-bench: *cassone* plus *panca*. Tyrol painted furniture in fir, casein, faux marble, a different joinery and a different sitting habit. A dealer tag that says “Alpine” on a bench in Indiana is often a climate, not a catalog.

Start in Decorah, because that is where the American evidence is willing to name itself. Vesterheim Norwegian-American Museum, 520 West Water Street, keeps the trunks that came with people. 1998.020.001: small trunk, Os-style rosemaling, about 1850, Sogn, name Erich Pladsen Gjelle inside the lid. Lena Ellingson Lund’s parents emigrated from Sogn, north of Bergen, in 1850. Gift of Rosemarie Klein Moore. Another object, 1977.039.001, is a small rectangular box, dovetailed, secret drawer in the base, Os-style painting including a portrait on the back, inscribed Martha Karina Hans Datter and 1864, attributed by Nils Ellingsgard in 1979 to Nils Midthus or Ola Lien Midtbø. Those are chests as documents. Dates and names are on the paint. The ocean is in the provenance, not in the scrolls.

Rosemaling is not a peasant accident. It is a trade. Regional schools differ in palette and in how the C-scrolls and flowers sit on the ground. A Vest-Agder trunk does not look like an Os trunk except to someone who wants “Norwegian” the way a showroom wants “Alpine.” I am not a rosemaling scholar. I am a person who hangs lids. What I can see, and what the museum pages keep repeating, is that the painting wraps a boarded or dovetailed box with iron handles, made to travel as well as to store. When the box becomes an immigrant chest, the interior holds the portable household: clothes, papers, medicine, the things you would not trust to a sack. Scrapes on the slats and the iron are the voyage. Do not sand them into a coffee table without admitting what you erased.

The American sequel is revival, which is where tourist attribution gets a second chance to lie. Per Lysne, an immigrant from Sogn, painted in Stoughton, Wisconsin. Vesterheim’s “Earthquake Trunk” — Herborg Tvedt’s old trunk, later painted by Lysne in 1941 as a birthday gift for her daughter Ruth — is a revival object sitting on an immigrant object. Lysne is called, in that literature, the father of rosemaling in America. `[VERIFY: keep that as Vesterheim’s formula for a revival painter, not as the origin of rosemaling.]` A trunk painted in 1941 for an American birthday is not 1837 Vest-Agder. It is a living-room ancestor of the painted “Old World” chests sold to people who wanted flowers and a country. The shop-floor version of this error is a client with a photograph of red scrolls who says Alpine. I ask: Norway, Sweden, Tyrol, or a furniture market in the 1970s? Those are four drawings.

Now the Alps, and the word that does too much. *Cassapanca*: a bench whose seat is a lid, or a chest that learned to be sat on as a profession rather than an accident. Italian, and then the word travels into dealer English for any painted Alpine seating-chest. Tyrol — north and south, Innsbruck to the Dolomites — painted fir furniture for *Stuben*: greens, reds, faux marble panels, dates and initials, iron locks. The Tiroler Volkskunstmuseum in Innsbruck keeps rooms of it, farmhouse interiors moved indoors, painted chests among the household goods. I will not invent an accession for a 1791 fir chest in a private shop window. Dated Alpine chests exist. Tourist copies exist. The museum in Innsbruck is the place to start if you need the type to have a valley.

Making differs in ways a living-room buyer never asks. A Norwegian *kiste* for travel wants iron handles, a lock, a lid that can take a stack of other trunks, paint that can live in a hold. An Alpine *cassapanca* wants a seat height, a back if it is a bench-form, a lid you will sit on every day, paint on a wall-facing front. Casein on fir is common in the Alpine literature; it dries matte and it chips in rectangles. Rosemaling on pine or mixed woods can be oilier, brighter, more scroll than panel. I have not painted either in this shop as a historical copy, and I will not claim a technique I have not run. What we do know from any lid is this: if people sit, the hinges fail, and the front paint at the knees wears first. Museum chests that look mint on the facade and tired on the lid are telling the truth.

Use, in Norway, is household and dowry and then emigration. Not every rosemaled chest was made for America. Many were twenty years old when they were packed. Some were dowry chests whose marriage-job was over, reused as trunks. Some were made for the journey. Dates on the front can be a wedding, a birth, an emigration, or a painter’s advertisement. `[VERIFY a specific date’s meaning from the object’s inscription, not from a mood.]` Use, in Tyrol, is the *Stube*: sitting, storing, showing. Use, in an American parlor after 1890, is display. The immigrant trunk in the sitting room is a flag. The “Alpine bench” from a later importer is a different flag, often without a family.

I work in Ferdinand, Indiana. We see both objects come through designer photographs. I will not fuse them. If the box has Vest-Agder scrolls and a Vesterheim cousin, it is a Norwegian chest that traveled. If it is a painted bench with a dealer’s “Tyrolean” and no valley, it is a sitting-room idea. The living room will take either. History will not.

The residual is the 1837 trunk that still had to be a box in 1890. Paint is a region. Travel is a provenance. “Alpine” is a word that wants both and earns neither until you name the valley, the museum, or the painter. If you cannot, say painted chest, and sit down.
