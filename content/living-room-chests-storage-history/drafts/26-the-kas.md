**Disclaimer.** Educational furniture history for the BBF channel. Staged draft. Not a Hudson Valley attribution service and not a claim that every tall pine cupboard in a listing is a kas.

The Metropolitan’s kast 09.175 is painted yellow poplar, red oak, and white pine, probably New York, 1690–1720, sixty-one and a half inches high and almost as wide, and the doors wear fruit in grisaille.

Pomegranate and quince, blue-gray and black and white, painted to imitate the carved festoons a richer Dutch cupboard would have had in oak. The Met’s object page, and Kenny, Safford, and Vincent behind it, will tell you kasten stored linens, and that those fruits are marriage and fertility talk, and that this may have been a dowry piece. I will leave the *may*. Paint is not a contract. What I will not leave is the type. This is a kas. A kas is not a wardrobe in the English sense, not an armoire in the French sense, not a linen press in the later British sense, though all of them are standing cupboards for cloth. A kas is a Dutch idea that took American wood in New Netherland and then in the English colony that still spoke Dutch in the kitchen: New York, New Jersey, a bit of coastal Connecticut, from the late seventeenth century into the early nineteenth, which is longer than a fashion is supposed to last.

The book is *American Kasten*, Metropolitan Museum of Art, 1991, Peter M. Kenny, Frances Gruber Safford, Gilbert T. Vincent, published with the exhibition that ran that winter. If you are going to say *kas* on this channel, that catalog is the shelf. Dutch *kast* — cupboard, case, the big one. American usage keeps *kas* for the singular and *kasten* for the plural, or it mixes them, and I will not police a Dutch noun in an Indiana shop beyond asking which object we mean. The form, at its most typical, is a large free-standing cupboard, two paneled doors, an over-scaled cornice, a base with a drawer or drawers, ball feet or, in country work, cut-out feet. Some are in two stages and come apart for stairs and for the narrow doors of a stone house. The cornice is not a molding you tack on at the end. It is the architecture. Albany work, the Chipstone essay on Mohawk woodwork will show you, sometimes pitches a relatively narrow board at an angle to fake mass — a plank cornice that reads as a full entablature until you look at the end grain. Restorers cut these cornices down when a ceiling came in or a doorway would not take the width. A kas without its cornice is a kas with its hat in the barn. Believe the scars.

Houses without closets are the use. A Hudson Valley house of 1740 does not have a walk-in. It has rooms, and it has this cupboard, and the sheets and tablecloths and the better clothes live behind the lock you can hear. Dutch New York did not invent textile storage. It invented, or rather it imported and then grew, a particular box for it: wide, architectural, meant to stand in a room you also sat in, not necessarily in a chamber you only slept in. Historic Huguenot Street still shows kasten in the Hasbrouck houses because that is where they stood — in the house, as furniture that was also a closet. Built-in kasten exist. The Glen-Sanders house has architectural ones the Chipstone authors worry over, cut down, plastered, later opened. Built-in is not the enemy of the type. Built-in is the type admitting it always wanted to be a wall.

Not every tall cupboard is a kas.

That sentence is the whole essay if I am not careful, so I will unpack it. A New England oak press with bosses and ebonized columns is a press. A Pennsylvania-German schrank is a schrank, with its own cornice habits and its own painted grammar, and folding it into *kas* because both are tall and Teutonic is how a listing gets drunk. A nineteenth-century pine kitchen cupboard from Ohio is a pine kitchen cupboard from Ohio. A 1970s “kas” in distressed blue is a word that flew without the river. Kenny’s catalog has a geography: New York, New Jersey, the Dutch-speaking corridor. Outside that corridor you need a story — a migrant joiner, a family that moved, a form copied from a print. Without the story you have a cupboard. Cupboards are allowed. They do not get to wear New Amsterdam for free.

Wood tells some of the truth. Gumwood in Albany. Yellow poplar that takes paint. Red oak. White pine as secondary, as back, as the quiet wood. Kas doors are panels. If someone has hung flush plywood and called it a kas, they have called a refrigerator a kas too, in their hearts. Hardware is iron, often, and the lock is not ashamed. Interiors are shelves, sometimes drawers in the base, sometimes a later rod for hanging which is a confession that the twentieth century wanted a wardrobe. I do not scold a rod if the family needed a rod. I note it. The original job is folded cloth, stacked, the Dutch equivalent of what a linen press does with trays and what a chest does with a lid. Different doors, same textile.

Staten Island Historical Society’s four-door kas, oak and yellow pine, 1650–1700, descended in a family that petitioned to settle the island in 1661, is the kind of documented object the 1991 catalog exists to keep us honest. Early, joined, locks you can name, not a mood. Kings County, Albany, the mid-Hudson: Kenny’s exhibition sorted shops and habits so a gumwood Albany cornice would not be sold as a New York City painted kas. I am not going to re-sort them from Ferdinand. I am going to insist that *kas* is a regional word with a book, and that the book is not optional.

On this floor we have built tall doored cases. We have not built a 09.175, and I will not claim a Hudson Valley commission this shop does not have. What we have built is the problem: a cornice that has to turn a corner without looking applied, doors that have to stay flat through an Indiana August, a base that has to carry weight without the feet looking like afterthoughts. A kas is heavy in the drawing even when the wood is poplar. That heaviness is the point. It is a closet you can see. American houses later hid the closet in a wall and then felt virtuous about the “clean look.” The kas is the opposite virtue. It says, in a room, that cloth lives here.

If a living room today wants a kas, I ask whether it wants the cornice, the lock, the width, and the honesty of a closet in the sitting room. A lot of people want the word and a slim media cabinet. That is essay 40’s problem, not this one. This one is a Dutch cupboard that kept being made because closets had not yet won, and because a painted quince on a door is a cheaper festoon than a carved one, and because linens still had to go somewhere. Say kas only when the object can stand it. The rest of the tall boxes can keep their own names. They have earned them by not being this.
