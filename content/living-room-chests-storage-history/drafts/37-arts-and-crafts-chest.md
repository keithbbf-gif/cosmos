**Disclaimer.** Educational furniture history for the BBF channel. Staged draft. Not a survey of the Arts and Crafts movement, not a Stickley authentication service, and not a catalog of Mission reproductions.

The Met’s Sideboard Table 1981.440.2 is white oak, poplar, and cast iron, about 1901, thirty-nine and a half inches high, forty-eight wide, and the tenons come through the ends and get wedged with keys so you cannot pretend the joint is a secret.

Gustav Stickley catalogued the form in 1901, in *Chips from the Workshops of Gustave Stickley*, as Side Board No. 901. The museum’s example wears a decal the firm used only in 1902 and 1903, plus a retailer’s label, and the square faceted iron pulls that cluster on pieces from those years. I am not going to do a fumed-oak sermon. I am going to stay with a living-room box. A sideboard is storage at serving height. In an American bungalow and in the Craftsman interiors Stickley published, that box stands in the dining room if you are strict and in the living room if the house is small, which bungalows are. It holds the linens, the flatware, the things you do not want on the table between meals. Doors with butterfly keys. A drawer. A top you can park a lamp on. The living-room credenza of 1955 is this thought in teak. The entertainment center of 1981 is this thought with a hole for a tube. No. 901 is the oak version that still wants you to see how it was made.

The joints are the clothes that are also the body. Through tenon and key at the ends: the stretcher cannot slip and you are invited to look. Butterfly keys on the doors: a dovetail-shaped inlay that keeps a split from running, or pretends to, and in Stickley’s early mood is allowed to be as loud as a strap hinge on a medieval chest. Iron hardware, square, faceted, not trying to be brass fruit. Cathers will give you the factory at Eastwood, the United Crafts name before 1904, the magazine. Another pack can have the movement. This pack needs the case. When a client says “Mission” and means a coffee table with slats, I point at a sideboard. Storage is the first job. A slatted table is a later, louder object.

Chests of drawers sit in the same shop and often in the same house. Stickley made them for bedrooms. American living rooms steal bedroom chests constantly — we have already watched *bureau* wander. A Craftsman chest of drawers in a sitting room is not a scandal. It is a drawer case that lost its dresser mirror and gained a lamp. The honest-joint rule still holds. If the sides are plywood pretending to be quartersawn oak, the butterfly is a sticker. I have no patience for a sticker butterfly. Either the panel can move and the key is doing work, or you have printed a joint on a slab. Stickley’s own factory, being a factory, walked that line for a living. Modestly priced, the Met says, for the average American. Average Americans still needed somewhere to put the extra blankets. A lid-chest in oak, a settle with a hinge, a sideboard with doors: three living-room answers from one mill.

Harvey Ellis is the fork, and I will keep him in a year. He is at Stickley’s from March 1903 until he dies on 2 January 1904. The Met’s Music Cabinet 1981.292 is mahogany, about 1903, inlay of grape clusters and vines in pendant panels, bracket feet, a form that does not look like No. 901. *The Craftsman* in January 1904 explains the inlay as emphasizing structural, especially vertical, lines. That explanation is Stickley talking himself into decoration. After Ellis dies, the shop keeps some forms and often drops the inlay because inlay argued with the “honest” line Stickley had already sold. The music cabinet is living-room storage if you had sheet music. Most of us do not. What we still have is the lesson of the interval: even a factory that advertised construction as morality could not stop a talented architect from putting vines on a door for ten months. I like the vines. I like the keys on 901 more, for this series, because a key is a shop sentence. A vine is a drawing.

I am not going to Red House. William Morris’s house is a pilgrimage for another essay in another pack. If you need an English ghost in this room, let it be the idea Stickley already advertised: furniture whose structure is the ornament, at a price a household might pay. Voysey and Mackintosh haunt Ellis’s overhangs; the Met says so on the ladies’ desk. They do not need a tour here. The living-room object is the sideboard, the chest, the settle that lifts. Settles with lids are chests that learned a back. People sit, then they stand up and take out the blankets. That hybrid will return as the storage ottoman, padded. Oak and a hinge are the unpadded version.

On our floor — Ferdinand, 1999, made-to-order — “Arts and Crafts” arrives as a photograph of a sideboard someone saw in a magazine that was itself quoting Stickley. The questions I actually ask: do you want through tenons, or do you want the look of through tenons? Do you want iron, or a bronze that photographs warmer? Do you want doors, drawers, or a lid? A settle-lid is a different stay than a sideboard door. A chest of drawers in quartersawn white oak will show ray fleck that a paint-grade built-in will not. I will not invent a named Craftsman commission. I will say we have built casework that lets a mortise read, and we have refused to fake a through tenon with an applied block, because an applied block is a cartoon of 901 and the Met already has the real cartoon, which is not a cartoon.

Use in a living room now is unheroic. The sideboard holds the things the credenza would hold: serving pieces if you still serve, board games if you do not, the cable box if you have given up. The chest of drawers holds maps, batteries, the wool throw. The settle holds the same throw and a person. None of that requires a movement. It requires a joint that will still be a joint in August. Stickley’s oak moves. Everyone’s oak moves. The keyed tenon is a permission for movement that a glued miter does not give. That is the Craftsman storage fact I will keep when the other pack takes Morris and the rest.

The residual tension is Ellis’s ten months. A factory can believe in exposed joinery and still want a vine. A living room can believe in a clean wall and still need a place for the extra blankets. No. 901 solves the blankets without the vine. The music cabinet solves a narrower need with a drawing. Both are storage. Only one is the ordinary living-room machine. I know which one I would hang a door on without a speech.
