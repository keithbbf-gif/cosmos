# The mule chest and the name that showed up in 1911

There is a chest with a lid, and under the lid there is still a chest, and under that there are one or two drawers. You lift for the blankets. You pull for the things you need without unfolding the blankets. English joiners were making that hybrid in the seventeenth century. Oak. Sometimes a till inside the lid-space — a little long box along the end, the medieval leftover. The drawers were called tills too, or drawing boxes. They ran in grooves. They were not pretty. They were the first honest admission that a pile is a stupid way to file a shirt.

We call this a mule chest. The joiners did not.

Christopher Schwarz, who has the habit of checking the dictionary when the furniture world gets poetic, put the OED date at about 1911. Three centuries of objects; a little more than a century of the cute name. “Mule” because it is a hybrid, says the usual account — chest plus chest of drawers, like a horse plus a donkey. There are worse etymologies in the same file: women’s backless slippers (also mules); a peddler’s pack on a live mule; secret compartments for “muling.” The slipper is a real word. The pack-animal theory wants an engraving nobody has. Hybrid is the one that does not need a novel.

Why does the late name matter in a living-room history? Because living-room copy loves to sound old. “A Jacobean mule chest brings warmth to the seating group.” No one in Jacobean England said that sentence. They said chest, or they said the drawers, or they said nothing and pointed. When we use the dealer’s word we are using an Edwardian or Georgian-revival habit — the same decades that invented the living room as a magazine project. The object is seventeenth-century. The vocabulary is cousin to Bok, not to James I.

The form itself is the right ancestor for a lot of living-room storage. It is low enough for a window. It has a lid you can set a lamp on if you are willing to move the lamp. It has a drawer so the remote and the extra plugs do not live in the blankets. It is already a compromise. The living room is a compromise. They recognize each other.

I would rather say “lidded chest with drawers in the base” in a caption and let mule out at night, with its date, like a dog that will steal food if you pretend it is a wolf.
