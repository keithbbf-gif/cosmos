# Drawers — the day the pile lost

A chest is a well. You take out the top to get the bottom. That is fine when you own two shirts and a cloak. It is a tax when you own a dozen shifts, or when the thing you need is the pair of stockings you put in first because you were tired.

Sometime around 1600, in England, a joiner puts a sliding box in the bottom of a chest. Groove in the case, tongue or runner on the box. Pull. The small thing comes out without the weather of the lid. They called it a till or a drawing box. The word *drawer* is the gerund winning.

By the middle of the seventeenth century the hybrid is common enough that the next step is obvious: stop lifting. Fill the case with drawing boxes. Put doors over them for a while, because a wall of drawer fronts still looked improper to someone. Lose the doors. Let the fronts be the face. The Restoration chest of drawers — oak or pine carcass, walnut veneer if you are in town, bun feet, maybe Dutch flowers in marquetry — is the form most people can still name without help.

Dovetails make the drawer a grown object. A grooved box can be a tray. A dovetailed box can be emptied and slammed for a hundred years. Georgian work wants oak linings, thin sides, a bottom that can move with the weather. The living-room chest that is all drawers is this invention, lowered and stripped of its highboy ambition.

What the drawer did to rooms: it let storage stand up and still be reached. A lidded chest wants floor. A stack of drawers wants wall. The parlor, which did not want storage, could ignore both. The living room, which wanted Tuesday, needed the wall. That is why the credenza and the low chest of drawers and the media cabinet are all cousins of 1650, whether they admit the cousin or not.

The cost is dust and the loss of a seat. You do not sit on a stack of drawers the way you sit on a six-board. You put a lamp on it. You put framed people on it. The lid was a piece of floor at table height. The drawer front is a face. We started looking at our storage instead of living on it.

I like a drawer that smells like the inside of an old book — dry wood, a little wool. I distrust a drawer that closes on a soft-close slide as if shame were a hardware finish. The invention was access. Soft-close is manners for a room that is still a little afraid it is a parlor.
