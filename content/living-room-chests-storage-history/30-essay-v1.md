The history of storage furniture in the Western domestic interior begins with the chest, a lidded rectangular case whose ordinary boarded construction — conventionally six boards — is attested in northern Europe from the early medieval period through the early modern and, in inexpensive work, well beyond. The chest served as receptacle, seat, and, in furniture-poor households, table. Iron straps at the corners of boarded examples are structural acknowledgements of weak nailed laps. Parallel luxury traditions include the Italian marriage chest of the fourteenth through sixteenth centuries, documented in inventories as *forzieri da sposa* and in later literature and museum practice as *cassone*, often commissioned in pairs, painted or ornamented in gesso, and carried in nuptial processions before installation in the bedchamber.

The introduction of the drawer in England around 1600 — contemporary terms include till and drawing box — addressed the inconvenience of stacked storage. Hybrid forms with one or two basal drawers beneath a lidded compartment appear in the seventeenth century; the collector’s term “mule chest” is late (Oxford English Dictionary, c. 1911) and should not be retrojected. By the Restoration, the chest of drawers as a vertical stack of drawing boxes is established in urban work, frequently with walnut veneer over oak or pine, bun feet, and, in ambitious examples, marquetry of Dutch derivation. Eighteenth-century developments include the English tallboy and the American highboy; the French commode, whose name denotes convenience, replaces the *coffre* in elite interiors from the late Louis XIV period and attains a characteristic bombé, ormolu-mounted form under the Régence and Louis XV. A documented royal example is the Cressent commode delivered 4 August 1738 for Louis XV’s bedroom at the Château de La Muette.

American joined oak chests of the Connecticut River Valley — the Hadley group, with tulip-and-stem ornament, and the Wethersfield “sunflower” group, whose principal rosettes are often botanically ambiguous — belong to the same century as the English drawer revolution and stand in houses without living rooms. Attribution of the sunflower group to Peter Blin (a 1681 account-book credit) remains a shop-tradition hypothesis rather than a monographic certainty.

The living room, as an American preferred designation for the principal informal-and-public daytime room, is a late-nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century formation. Decorating literature of the 1890s already employs the term. Edward Bok of the *Ladies’ Home Journal* did not coin it; he promoted it against parlor and drawing room and, from 1895, published inexpensive house plans that omitted the formal parlor. Secondary work on the American parlor (Kaleş, 2002) describes a loosening of formality after 1850 and a preference for “living room” by about 1910. The chest’s relocation into this room follows the program: a room used on ordinary days requires ordinary storage.

Twentieth-century industrial and modern episodes include the Lane Company’s cedar “hope” chests (Altavista, Virginia, from 1912; Girl Graduate Plan officially 1930); radio and television consoles that admitted machines to the former parlor by means of wooden casings; and the Eames Storage Unit (Herman Miller, 1950–55), whose marketing named the living room explicitly. Japanese tansu (recorded as a class in the Genroku era, 1688–1704; older wheeled examples in 1657) and Korean bandaji are mobile or half-fronted storage traditions that enter Western living rooms by collecting and retail, not by internal evolution of the parlor.

The contemporary “living room chest” or “accent chest” is a retail category, not a period type. It inherits, selectively, the lid of the boarded chest, the frontality of the joined American chest, the height of the sideboard and commode, the sliding door of the cupboard, and the machine-hiding job of the radio cabinet. A historically responsible account keeps these inheritances separate at the level of names and dates even when they share a wall.
