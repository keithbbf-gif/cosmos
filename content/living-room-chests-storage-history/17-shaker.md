The United Society of Believers did not invent the cupboard. They invented, or rather they insisted on, a room in which the cupboard had already done its work. Peg rails. Built-in cases. Oval boxes in nests. A cupboard-chest from New Lebanon, the Met’s pine “weave-chest,” cupboard above and four drawers below, about 1800–1850: that is a living-room problem solved in a workroom. The floor stays clear. The extra life goes into the wall.

People who write living-room advice still steal this without the religion. “Built-ins add value.” “Clutter is stress.” Bok said bric-a-brac made women nervous. The Shakers said accumulation interfered with a life aimed at the second appearing of Christ. Different heavens. Same hostility to a pile on a chair.

The free-standing Shaker chest of drawers is a quiet stack: wooden knobs, legs that remember a turning lathe, no applied carving. Enfield cupboards, Hancock cases, Pleasant Hill’s yellow paint — community to community the accent changes and the refusal does not. The refusal is ornament that does not hold something.

In a contemporary living room a Shaker-style chest does well because the living room, after 1910, wanted to look used but not messy. A row of wooden knobs is the opposite of a cassone front. It is also the opposite of a Lane advertisement. Nobody is getting married on the lid. Somebody is putting away the extra throw.

I will not pretend every Shaker object in a catalog is communal, early, or honest. There is a reproduction industry with a better conscience than most, and a fake industry with none. The test I trust: do the drawers run well, is the back finished as if a person might see it, and does the piece make the room quieter. If it shouts “Shaker” in a typeface, it is not.

Kaidan-dansu — the Japanese stair chest — gets compared to Shaker built-ins because both put storage in the dead space of a house. The comparison is a rhyme, not a kinship. Leave it as a rhyme.
