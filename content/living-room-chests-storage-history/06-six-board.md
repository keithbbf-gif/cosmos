# Six boards and a pair of iron straps

If you ask someone to draw a chest, they draw a six-board chest. They do not know that is the name. They draw two ends that go to the floor, two sides nailed to those ends, a bottom dropped into a groove or a rebate, a lid. They draw a box a person could sit on. That drawing is more than a thousand years of ordinary European furniture.

The construction is almost rude. The side boards lap the ends and take nails. The corners have no interlocking pride. They have leverage, and leverage wants to tear the nails out when you pick the chest up by the lid or rack it on a cart. So the honest six-board chest wears iron. Straps at the corners. Hinges that are straps with a pin. A hasp. Sometimes so much iron the wood is the thing that holds the metal together.

Makers who had time and a different training built hutches and then panel chests — extended stiles, framed faces, lids that sat on a dustboard. Those are stronger. They are also more furniture. The six-board survives because it is cheap and because cheap is how most lives get stored. You see them in inventories as “a chest,” not as a type. Types are for us.

What did they hold? Linen if you had linen. A wool blanket. A will. A loaf, in some houses, which is why you still hear “bread chest” in odd corners of English. Tools. The extra of a life that did not have closets. In a furniture-poor room the chest is seat, table, and cupboard. You sit on the lid to pull your shoes on. You set the bowl on it. You sleep knowing the lid is the lock.

A living room that contains a six-board chest — real or reproduction — is doing something slightly medieval and not admitting it. It is putting the extra life in the middle of the talking. The difference is the floor. A medieval chest often stood on dirt or on cold flags; the slab ends kept the bottom off the damp. A living-room six-board stands on an oak finish and a rug. The damp is gone. The lid still wants to be sat on, and someone will put a tray on it and call it a coffee table.

I care about the straps. If the straps are thin stamped “antique” steel with fake clinch nails, you are looking at a mood. If the straps are thick and a little stupid, if they wrap the corner as if they remember a cart, you are closer to the job.

The six-board is not a style. Chippendale is a style. The six-board is a count.
