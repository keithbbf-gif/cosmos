For once the catalog says the room. The Eames Storage Unit, Herman Miller, 1950, is sold for the living room, the dining room, the bedroom, or as a divider. Steel extrusions, diagonal braces, plywood, Masonite, perforated metal. Warehouse vocabulary. Charles and Ray had already built a house out of industrial parts. The ESU is that house at furniture scale.

It lasts five years in the first life. Knockdown at first — you bolted your own living room together and swore. About 1952 the factory ships them assembled, with stronger legs, because the first legs buckled in the crate. 1955: out. 1998: back, as a classic. Five years of product, then a long afterlife of photographs. That is a very mid-century story.

Around it, the teak sideboard and the American credenza do the quieter work. Florence Knoll’s office cases leak into apartments. Danish shops send long boxes with sliding doors that run like they were taught manners. George Nelson’s Comprehensive Storage System is wall architecture. The living room of 1958 is a horizontal room with a horizontal box. Bok would recognize the clearance. He would not recognize the steel.

Why this moment matters to a chest history: the lid is almost gone. The living room has chosen drawers and sliding doors. The seat-on-the-lid medieval habit survives in the storage ottoman and the trunk coffee table, which are jokes the decade also tells. But the serious money is in the long face against the wall. Storage has become a horizon.

I will not call every tapered leg “iconic.” I will say the ESU is the rare living-room storage piece whose first advertisement already knew the argument of this set: the room is for living, the objects multiply after a war, and the box should be able to change its mind. Modular is just a later word for a chest that admits the week will not stay the same.
