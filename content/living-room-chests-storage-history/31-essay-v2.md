# Draft 31 — second full pass (too much company)

*This pass talks too much to the reader and winks. Kept to show the other ditch. Do not publish.*

Okay, so here’s the thing nobody putting an “accent chest” in a cart wants to hear: the living room is the new kid. The chest is the grandparent who still knows how to sit on a lid and call it a chair. If you’ve ever bought a low wooden box for the wall behind the sofa and felt, vaguely, like you were participating in History, you were — just not the history on the tag.

Let’s back up. (See? This is the voice going wrong already. “Let’s back up” is a podcast.)

For a very long time in Europe the chest is the storage. Six boards. Iron if you could. You put the good cloth in, you sat on top to get your shoes on, you hoped the nails held when the cart hit a rut. In Florence, if you had money in the 1400s, your marriage chests got painted and walked through the street so the neighbors could count your textiles. In Connecticut in the 1680s a joiner carved tulips or those flowers we keep calling sunflowers and put a girl’s initials on the front. In Edo a merchant’s books lived in a chest that could be carried or rolled, and the law had opinions about lacquer.

Drawers show up in England around 1600 because people got tired of archaeology-for-socks. Someone — a lot of someones — put a sliding box in the bottom of a chest. Then they filled the whole case with sliding boxes. Then they stacked cases. Then the French made the commode convenient and pretty enough to live in a small salon. Then the English made tallboys that belong in a bedroom no matter what your open-concept floor plan believes.

Meanwhile the parlor — and this is the American plot — spent the nineteenth century pretending nobody stored anything. Whatnots. Figurines. Sunday best. Sometimes a coffin. Storage was upstairs like a servant. Then the magazines, and Edward Bok, who is not a folk hero exactly, said the front room should be used. Living room. Tuesday. And Tuesday needs a box.

So Lane built cedar chests in a rail town in Virginia and gave little ones to graduating girls so the parents would walk into the store. Radios put on walnut suits. Eames built a steel shelf that lasted five years and then became a poster. Your storage ottoman is a medieval chest in sweatpants.

Look — I like all of this. I do. I like a campaign chest in a front room because it looks like it might leave. I like a bandaji if you call it a bandaji. I like a Lane if you admit it was an ad as much as a box. What I don’t like is the copy that says “this stunning piece brings old-world craftsmanship to today’s lifestyle.” That’s not history. That’s a narcotic.

If you take one thing from this draft, take the gap: the room is about a century and a bit, as a winning name. The box is a millennium. Your job, in a real room, is not to reconcile them with a runner and a bowl. It’s to open the lid on a Tuesday and know what century you borrowed.

*End of v2. Faults: “okay so here’s the thing”; fake intimacy; “see?”; a good ending trapped in a bad party.*
