# Glossary — names that arrived late, and names that did not

Furniture language is a junk shop. Dealers, museums, magazines, and factories use the same word for three objects and three words for one. This list is how the drafts use the words. If a later essay drifts, this file wins.

**Accent chest.** Retail, late. A low chest sold for the living room because “chest of drawers” still smells like a bedroom. Not a period type.

**Armoire.** Tall cupboard. French word, English shops. Clothes and linen. A vertical answer to the chest. Not a living-room native; people put them there anyway when the closet lost.

**Bandaji (반닫이).** Korean chest, Joseon and after. Half the front drops or swings. Blanket and clothing. Say bandaji. Do not say tansu.

**Blanket chest.** American / English usage for a lidded chest, often six-board or paneled, that held textiles. Foot of the bed is the cliché. Keeping room and later living room are real too.

**Cassone.** Museum Italian for a large decorated chest, fourteenth–sixteenth centuries. Inventories often said *forziere*. Sarcophagus shape is one subset. Marriage is the famous use, not the only use.

**Cedar chest / hope chest.** In twentieth-century America, usually a factory lidded chest lined in red cedar (*Juniperus virginiana*), sold to hold a future household. Lane made the type industrial. The older European marriage chest is an ancestor only in the way a church is an ancestor of a catalog.

**Chest.** A box with a lid. The oldest ordinary storage furniture in the European house. Also, loosely, any case that is not a cupboard. The loose use is how “chest of drawers” happened.

**Chest of drawers.** A stack of drawing boxes and no lift lid, or only a dummy. English, mid–late seventeenth century as a familiar form. Bedroom by habit. Living room when the stack is low and the copywriter is hungry.

**Choba-dansu.** Japanese merchant chest for account books and the face of the shop.

**Coffer.** Chest, often traveling, often with a rounded or trunk lid, often bound. The word English copy uses when it wants age.

**Commode.** French case, “convenient,” Louis XIV onward. Drawers, often a marble top, often a salon or chamber object. In later English, also a bedroom cupboard for a chamber pot. Say which one you mean. These drafts mean the French case unless they say otherwise.

**Console.** A table against a wall, or a low cabinet pretending to be one. Radio and television stole the word (*console radio*). Living-room storage hid behind it.

**Court cupboard.** English, sixteenth–seventeenth century. Open stages and a closed nest. Display of plate; some storage. Hall furniture.

**Credenza.** Italian sideboard / trust-table. In English interiors from about 1920, a genteel word for a low cabinet. Dining room, then office, then the wall under the television.

**Dower chest.** Chest associated with a woman’s goods at marriage. Overlap with hope chest, cassone, painted German and Alpine chests. Do not collapse the regions.

**Drawer / till / drawing box.** A sliding box. Early English drawers run in grooves. Dovetailed drawers are a later, better habit, not the birth of the idea.

**ESU.** Eames Storage Unit, 1950–55, Herman Miller. Modular steel-and-panel storage sold for living rooms among other rooms. Not a credenza, though it can act like one.

**Funa-dansu.** Japanese shipboard chest. Showpiece as well as lockbox.

**Hadley chest.** Collector/museum name for a group of joined, carved oak chests from the upper Connecticut River Valley, late seventeenth–early eighteenth century. Not all from Hadley. Tulip-and-stem is the usual tell.

**Highboy.** American name for a tall chest on a stand or a chest-on-chest of a certain ambition. English cousin: tallboy.

**Hutch.** In medieval chests: a boarded chest with extended stiles, stronger than six-board. In later American kitchens: a cupboard-on-sideboard. Do not mix the two in one sentence without a warning.

**Isho-dansu.** Japanese clothing tansu. Drawers for kimono laid flat.

**Kaidan-dansu.** Stair chest. Architecture you can drag if the house requires it.

**Living room.** American preferred name, by about 1910 in the magazine sense, for the main daytime room that is both family and company. Younger than parlor as a household institution; younger than the chest by a millennium.

**Lowboy.** Low dressing table, often paired with a highboy. Not a synonym for TV console, except on the internet.

**Mule chest.** Modern (ca. 1911) English name for a lidded chest with drawer(s) in the base. Hybrid. Do not put the name in a seventeenth-century mouth.

**Ottoman.** Padded stool or low seat, the name from Turkish via the eighteenth–nineteenth century West. Storage ottoman is a later convenience: a soft chest that wants to be sat on.

**Parlor.** From French *parler*, to speak. The formal front room. Company, Sundays, sometimes a laying-out. The living room is the argument against it.

**Press / linen press.** Tall cupboard for textiles, shelves or sliding trays. The chest stood up.

**Radio cabinet / television console.** Furniture whose job is to make a machine look like it always lived with the chairs.

**Sideboard.** Dining storage and serving. English late eighteenth century in its classic form (Shearer / Hepplewhite / Sheraton orbit). Living rooms borrow them because they are the right height for a lamp and a drink.

**Six-board chest.** Two ends, two sides, a bottom, a lid. The folk memory of “a chest.”

**Sunflower chest.** Collector name for a Wethersfield-area joined group, ca. 1675–1710, carved rosettes that may be marigolds. Peter Blin shop tradition is a hypothesis with one good document and many lookalikes.

**Tallboy.** English high chest-on-chest, eighteenth century especially.

**Tansu (箪笥).** Japanese mobile cabinetry, recorded as a class in the Genroku era, with older wheeled and carried boxes behind it. Not stationary Western case furniture that happens to be Asian.

**Trunk.** Traveling chest, often canvas- or leather-covered, often with a hump. Steamer trunks become coffee tables in the twentieth century because the living room needed a low box and the attic had one.

If a word is not here and a draft uses it like a type, that is a fault.
