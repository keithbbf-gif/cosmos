# Built-in versus the box you can take when you leave

There are two ways to solve a living room that has too much life in it.

You can build the storage into the wall — Shaker cupboards, inglenook benches with lids, postwar bookcases around the chimney, IKEA PAX behind a false door, a window seat that is a chest. The room gets quieter. The next owner inherits your idea of quiet. You cannot take the quiet with you except as a higher sale price.

Or you can keep a box that is not the house: a chest, a tansu with handles, a campaign case in two parts, a credenza on legs. Mobility is the oldest storage idea that is not a hole in the ground. Japanese tansu make that idea a culture. British campaign furniture makes it a career. American life mostly makes it a yard sale.

The living room, being young and often rented, should have preferred the mobile box. It often prefers the built-in, because magazines like a wall that looks decided. A decided wall is a parlor habit. It says the room is finished. A chest says the room might have to move.

I am biased toward the box you can take. Not because built-ins are immoral — they are how a small house breathes — but because this set is about chests, and a chest is a refusal to let the carpenter of the house own your blankets.

If you have both, you are rich in the only way a room can be rich without showing off: you can hide things, and you can leave.
