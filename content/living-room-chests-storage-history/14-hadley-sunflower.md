# Hadley, sunflower, and the initials that are not the maker

New England’s joined oak chests are the American objects that make people say “our chests” as if oak were a citizenship. Two groups get the magazine ink.

The Hadley group — the name is a collector’s convenience, not a town limit — comes out of the upper Connecticut River Valley in the late seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries. Patricia Kane, at Winterthur in the mid-1970s, worked through about a hundred and twenty-five of them and saw at least seven shop groups in the tulip-and-stem carving. Later counts run higher. I will not referee. There are a lot. They are joined, not nailed six-board. They have panels. They have a vocabulary of tulip, leaf, and vine that can be related to eastern Massachusetts carving (Searle–Dennis) without being a copy. Many carry initials. The initials are usually the woman whose textiles went in, or the household mark, not a signature on the stile.

The sunflower group is Wethersfield-area work, roughly 1675–1710, eighty-five or more survivors if you trust the thesis counts: chests with no drawer, one, or two, plus cupboards and boxes. Applied turnings. Carved rosettes that catalogs still call sunflowers and scholars will call marigolds or just rosettes. In 1958 Houghton Bulkeley found a 1681 credit in Gershom Bulkeley’s account book to Peter Blin of Wethersfield for a table leaf, a chest, and two dozen trenchers, and he tied that chest to a family sunflower chest without drawers. That is a real document. It is not a shop-wide signature. Susan Prendergast Schoelwer and the people who write Christie’s notes have been saying for decades that several shops, or a shop and its echoes, are in the group. Windsor, Connecticut, has related earlier chests. I will write “Peter Blin shop tradition” and I will not tattoo Blin on every rosette.

These pieces stood in houses that cooked in one room and slept in another and did not have a living room. When one of them stands in a living room now, it is usually the most expensive quiet in the house. You do not put the television on it. You do not — if you have any sense — set the wet glass on the lid. You let it do what it did: hold cloth and announce that someone in the valley could pay a joiner.

The living-room lesson is smaller than the scholarship. Frontality. These chests are faces. The carving is for the room, not for the contents. A modern “accent chest” with a stamped mandala is trying to buy that frontality by the square foot. It usually fails because the carving on a Hadley is part of the joinery’s rhythm, not a sticker.

If you are lucky enough to live with one, you live with a name that is wrong (Hadley), a flower that may be wrong (sunflower), and initials that are not the maker. That is a good education for anyone who writes furniture history in a hurry.
