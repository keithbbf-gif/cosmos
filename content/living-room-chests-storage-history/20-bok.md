# Edward Bok and the Tuesday room

Edward Bok edited the *Ladies’ Home Journal* and believed the American house had become a lie. Too many turrets. Too much machine ornament. A front room stuffed for a visitor who did not come. He said, of the drawing room, that he could never see what it drew “unless it draws attention to too much money and no taste.” He published plans, from 1895, five dollars for the blueprints, houses from fifteen hundred to five thousand dollars, and he took the parlor off the plan. Stanford White told him to stop, then later said Bok had done more for American domestic architecture than any man of the generation. Frank Lloyd Wright sent plans. Ralph Adams Cram sent plans. The bungalow got a shove. The kitchen shrank into built-in cupboards. The cupola lost.

Bok did not invent the living room. The phrase is already in 1890s decorating literature, where it means a room that looks like a person. What he did was make the name a program. Use the room. Stop keeping a shrine. Put the family in the front and let the front look like a family.

Once that program wins — and by about 1910 the magazine house prefers the new name — storage has to come in from the chambers. You cannot “live” in a room and also exile the extra blankets, the extra music, the sewing, the toys. The chest, the low cupboard, the bookcase with doors, the later radio cabinet: they are the furniture of Bok’s victory. He thought he was simplifying. He was also inviting the box back.

There is a conservatism in him I will not sand off. The living room he wanted still had a wife in it as the maker of the simple life, children near the soil, suburbs as a moral geography. A history of the room that quotes only his taste and not his household politics is a brochure. The chests that followed — especially Lane’s — fit his simple life and also fit a narrower script about who the room was for.

Still. Tuesday. That is the useful part. A chest in a living room is a Tuesday object. It is there because someone refused to keep a shrine.
