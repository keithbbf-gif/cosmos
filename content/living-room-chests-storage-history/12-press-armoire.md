A lid is a horizontal door. At some point the door stands on its side and the chest becomes a cupboard. English said press, linen press, clothes press. French said armoire. The trays slide, or the shelves sit, and the textiles do not live in a pile you have to archaeology.

This is not a living-room native either. Presses stood in chambers and passages. Shaker built-ins ate whole walls of dwelling rooms, which is closer to our problem: a room you live in still has to swallow the extra. The free-standing armoire in a living room is usually a closet apology — the 1920 bungalow or the 2004 loft that was sold as “open” and then asked where the coats go.

The court cupboard is the Renaissance English cousin that wanted to be seen: open shelves for plate, a closed nest below. Hall furniture. Display as a kind of storage, or storage as a kind of display. The living-room bookcase with a closed base is a court cupboard that learned to read.

I keep the press in this set because the living-room “cabinet” is often a press that lost its height. BESTÅ, the media credenza, the bar cabinet — sliding doors on a box. That is cupboard logic at chest height. The lid lost. The drawer lost. The door came back, wide and low, because a television wants a blank face when it is off and a mouth when it is on.

If you have a true linen press in a sitting room, with trays, you can put tablecloths in it or you can put the board games in it. The trays do not care. They were built for things that wrinkle. They will accept things that rattle. That is the unsentimental afterlife of good storage: the job stays, the contents get less dignified.
