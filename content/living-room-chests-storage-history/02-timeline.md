# Timeline — the box is old; the room is not

A year with no object is a rumor. This is a spine, not a chronicle of every shop.

## Before anyone said living room

- **9th–16th c. (and later, cheaply):** six-board chests in northern Europe. Five boards make the box; the sixth is the lid. Ends often drop past the floor and become slab feet. Corners are weak. Iron straps are not decoration. They are the joint admitting what it is.
- **14th–16th c., Tuscany especially:** large decorated marriage chests. Documents say *forzieri da sposa* more often than the museum word *cassone*. Painted or gilded fronts. Carried in the wedding procession. Lived in the bedchamber. Pair is the rule for the expensive ones.
- **15th–16th c., Italy:** *credenza* as a serving / tasting sideboard — trust in the food, then trust in the furniture name. Not a living-room piece. A dining and court piece.
- **16th–17th c., England:** court cupboards, presses. Display and linen. The hall and the chamber, not a “living room.”
- **ca. 1600, England:** drawers — tills, drawing boxes — appear in the base of lidded chests. You can reach a small thing without opening the whole weather.
- **17th c., England:** the hybrid we later called a mule chest. Lid on top, one or two drawers below. The *name* is about 1911. The object is not.
- **mid–late 17th c., England:** chest of drawers as a stack. Restoration walnut, Dutch-influenced marquetry, bun feet that rot and get replaced. Oak does not leave the countryside.
- **1657, Edo:** wheeled chests in the streets during the Meireki fire. Dutch diaries notice them because they blocked the road. Mobility as a civic fact, not a style note.
- **1675–1725, Connecticut River Valley:** Hadley-group joined oak chests (tulip-and-stem); Wethersfield “sunflower” / marigold group. Initials of a daughter more often than a maker’s signature. These stood in houses that did not have living rooms.
- **1688–1704, Genroku:** *tansu* in the written record as a named furniture class. Older mobile boxes already existed. Edo law decides who may own what finish.

## The polite century

- **Louis XIV–XV, France:** the commode replaces the *coffre* in rich interiors. “Convenient.” 1738: Cressent commode delivered for Louis XV’s bedroom at La Muette — a dated royal box in a sleeping room. Salons get their own commodes because the furniture moved with the new smaller rooms, not because someone invented leisure storage.
- **ca. 1700–1775, England:** tallboy / chest-on-chest. America says highboy, and often adds a bonnet. Lowboy is the dressing cousin, not a living-room TV stand, however many listings lie.
- **1754:** Chippendale, *The Gentleman and Cabinet-Maker’s Director*. Chests as plates you can order from.
- **1788:** Hepplewhite, *The Cabinet-Maker and Upholsterer’s Guide* (posthumous).
- **1791–94:** Sheraton, *The Cabinet-Maker and Upholsterer’s Drawing-Book*.
- **1789, Kansei Reforms:** *ryobiraki kasane-dansu* named as acceptable for merchant-class women — unlacquered. The state in the drawer.
- **1815–1848:** Biedermeier commodes — cherry, birch, quiet faces. A bourgeois box after Napoleon.
- **19th c., Shaker communities (New Lebanon, Hancock, Enfield, Pleasant Hill, etc.):** built-in cupboards, cupboard-chests, oval boxes. Storage as a moral clearance of the floor.
- **18th–19th c., British officers:** campaign chests in two parts, flush brass, meant to travel. Later they sit still in English and American sitting rooms and pretend they always did.
- **Joseon, Korea:** *bandaji* as a common clothing chest — half the front opens. Regional iron and brass as a language.

## The room gets a new name

- **to ~1850, U.S. middle class:** parlor is formal. Sunday, company, sometimes the dead.
- **1850–1890:** the parlor loosens. Children get in. Comfort argues with gentility.
- **1890s:** “living room” in decorating literature. A room that is supposed to show a person, not a convention.
- **1895– :** Bok’s *Ladies’ Home Journal* house plans. Parlor dropped. Kitchen compacted. Bric-a-brac called a nervous condition.
- **~1910:** “living room” preferred, in the American magazine sense, to “parlor.”
- **1912:** Standard Red Cedar Chest Company, Altavista, Virginia (Lane). Cedar as moth-war and as a script for a girl’s future house.
- **1914–18:** Lane builds pine ammunition boxes. The factory learns speed. The speed comes home.
- **1920s:** “credenza” circulating in English interiors as a smart word for a low sideboard. Radios buy wooden clothes so they can stand in the family room without looking like apparatus.
- **1930:** Lane Girl Graduate Plan, official. Miniatures as the hook; full-size hope chest as the sale.
- **1939–45:** Lane ads to GIs — buy the chest on the way to the war or the wedding. Same box, new uniform.

## After the parlor is gone

- **1950–55:** Eames Storage Unit, Herman Miller. Living room named in the copy. Steel, Masonite, plywood. Discontinued 1955. The living room keeps the problem the ESU was built for: too many objects, not enough architecture.
- **1950s–60s:** Danish teak sideboards and American “credenzas” colonize the wall opposite the sofa. Television consoles inherit the radio cabinet’s job: hide the machine, display the household.
- **1950s–60s:** Lane estimates half to two-thirds of U.S. girl graduates offered a miniature chest.
- **1970s:** larger “family rooms” and the football Sunday. The living room either becomes the nice front room again or it swallows the TV.
- **1998:** ESU reissue. Nostalgia for a five-year product.
- **2001:** Lane Altavista plant closes.
- **now:** retail “living room chest,” “accent chest,” “media credenza,” storage ottoman. Young names for an old job — keep the room livable by putting the extra life in a box.

If you need one sentence for the spine: **the chest is medieval and older; the drawer is seventeenth-century; the living room is a late-Victorian argument that won.**
