# Draft 32 — the room and the box

The living room is younger than the box it keeps trying to hide. That is the history, if you want it in one sentence. The rest is joinery, later names, and a magazine campaign that let Tuesday into the front of the house.

A chest is a lid on a well. Six boards if the maker is in a hurry, which most makers are: two ends that often run past the floor and become feet, two sides nailed on, a bottom, a cover. The corners are weak. Iron straps are not a style. They are the joint telling the truth. You sit on the lid. You set a bowl on it. You keep in it whatever a house cannot afford to leave in the open — cloth, tools, a paper that matters. In a room with little furniture the chest is cupboard, bench, and table. Northern Europe ran this object for centuries without needing a special word for the room it sat in. It sat where the life was.

Money made other chests. In fifteenth-century Tuscany a marriage pair — *forzieri da sposa* in the inventories, *cassoni* in our museums — could take a workshop a month per painted front. They walked through the street in the wedding procession, which is a polite way to say they were displayed in public with the dowry on them, and Florence had already found it necessary to forbid throwing garbage at the couple’s house. Then the boxes lived in the bedchamber. They were never living-room furniture. There was no such room.

England, around 1600, puts a sliding box in the bottom of a chest so you can reach a small thing without unstacking the well. Till. Drawing box. The word drawer is that convenience sticking. The hybrid — lid above, drawers below — is a seventeenth-century object. We call it a mule chest; the name is about 1911, a dealer’s hybrid for a joiner’s hybrid. By the Restoration a city chest can be all drawers, walnut on a cheaper carcass, bun feet that insects and wet floors will replace. The pile has lost. Access has won. You stop sitting on your storage and start looking at it.

The French name for the next convenience is commode. The coffer was in the way of smaller, movable rooms. Cressent’s commode for Louis XV, delivered in August 1738 to the bedroom at La Muette, is a dated royal box in a sleeping room, marble matched to the chimney. The type walks into salons because that reign’s furniture was built to be rearranged, not because someone invented leisure. English tallboys and American highboys take the drawer stack and make it a closet that has not been framed yet. They belong in chambers. A living room that takes a bonnet-top highboy has lost a fight with scale.

Across the Atlantic, in the same decades England is filling cases with drawers, Connecticut Valley joiners are carving tulip-and-stem panels on oak chests we file as Hadley — more than a hundred survivors, several shops — and Wethersfield-area shops are carving rosettes we still lazily call sunflowers. A 1681 account book credits Peter Blin for a chest; a family piece got tied to that line. The rest is tradition and argument. The initials on the fronts are not his. These chests stood in houses that did not have living rooms.

The living room is an American winning name. Decorating books in the 1890s already use it for a room that looks like a person instead of a convention. Edward Bok, at the *Ladies’ Home Journal*, did not coin it. He did something more bossy. From 1895 he sold house plans that left the parlor off the paper. He thought bric-a-brac was a nervous disease. He could not see what a drawing room drew “unless it draws attention to too much money and no taste.” Stanford White told him to stop, then praised him. By about 1910 the magazine house prefers living room to parlor. The real street is slower. The permission is the point. A room used on Tuesday needs a place to put Tuesday.

The parlor had spent a long time refusing that. Formal talking. Sundays. Sometimes the dead. Display storage that denied it was storage — whatnots, glass, the figurine. The blankets stayed upstairs. When the front room becomes a living room, the chest comes in through a door it was not supposed to use. That relocation is the subject. There is no period type called “living room chest.” There is a young room inheriting old boxes.

Lane understood the inheritance as a product. Altavista, 1912, a rail junction, a son put in charge of a failing box shop. Cedar because moths. War work in pine ammunition, then the speed applied to trousseau furniture. “The gift that starts the home.” In 1930 the Girl Graduate Plan goes national: a miniature chest into a girl’s hands, a discount on the full size, parents into the dealer’s door. Company memory later says tens of millions of miniatures. World War II ads put the same lid next to a uniform. By the 1960s Lane is saying the chest is a gateway to the rest of the house. The Altavista plant closes in 2001. The boxes remain, often under a window, often holding something less ceremonial than a wedding.

The machine learned the same door. Radio put on a cabinet so the former parlor would accept a set of tubes. In 1928 a Chicago maker even names a radio Credenza. Television inherits the long face. When the screen goes flat, the cabinet stays, holding the router. Mid-century catalogs, for once, say the room out loud. The Eames Storage Unit, 1950 to 1955, Herman Miller, steel and Masonite and plywood, sold for the living room among other rooms, knockdown, then factory-assembled because the first legs buckled. Five years of product. A long afterlife in photographs. The lid is almost gone. Storage has become a horizon of sliding doors.

Other rooms, other rules. Tansu in Japan — a class in the Genroku record, wheeled chests already in the 1657 fire — live in storehouses and closets and come out when needed. Handles are not decorative first. Bandaji in Korea open by the half-front. Camphor chests travel because insects hate the wood. A Western living room that calls all of this an Asian accent has failed the first job of a history, which is to keep the names.

What the living-room chest holds now is not a dowry. Blankets. Games. Cables. The year of taxes you will not need until you need it. A lid is a postponed decision. A drawer is a file. People who think they are bad at storage often only need a lid.

If you put one of these boxes in the room you sit in, the historically responsible act is small. Use the right word. Leave the handles room to be handles. Do not set a wet glass on a carved oak lid. Open it without moving a project off the top. Tuesday, not a shrine.
