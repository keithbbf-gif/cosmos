This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

The book is too thick for a clinic shelf and too strange for a peace conference. *Semantography (Blissymbolics)*, 1949, later 1965: Charles K. Bliss’s logical writing “for an illogical world.” He had been Karl Kasiel Blitz, born 1897, a Jewish engineer who survived Nazi camps, reached Shanghai, watched Chinese characters work across spoken dialects, and after the war tried to build a script that could not be bent into propaganda. Nations did not adopt it. In 1971, a Toronto teacher did.

That split — inventor’s purpose versus classroom purpose — is the vita. The University of Toronto fonds still hold correspondence and the later legal weather between Bliss and the Canadian Blissymbolics organizations. A 1982 agreement left an international body with authority to publish the marks for people with communication disabilities. Bliss died in 1985. The marks did not.

## What he built

Pictographs and ideographs that can be combined. A dream of unambiguous meaning. A personality that did not enjoy being edited by therapists. Later clinic overlays added English glosses because partners needed them. The glosses are a compromise he would have hated and children needed. Both sentences can sit in one paragraph.

He is not the founder of AAC. He is the author of a symbol system AAC borrowed. McNaughton’s team found him through Helfman, then through Laurentian’s copy of the book. The accidental path is the true path.

Shanghai is not a montage. It is the place a refugee engineer watched a writing system work across speech communities and decided the world needed a cleaner one. The cleaner one failed as diplomacy and succeeded as a clinic accident. Success as an accident is why the legal files exist: an inventor who thought he had built a United Nations tool watching therapists add English captions. Both parties were sure they were protecting meaning. Only one party had to live with a child who needed a word on Tuesday.

## What later people kept

A grammar of marks that is more than a noun sticker. A reminder that symbols are designed, not natural. A cautionary tale about an inventor who wants the last word on how disabled children may use his invention. The last word, in a clinic, belongs to the user. That ethic is later than Bliss. It is why he is a profile and not a saint.
