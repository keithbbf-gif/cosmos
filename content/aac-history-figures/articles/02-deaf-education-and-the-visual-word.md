This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Madrid, 1620. Juan Pablo Bonet puts a book into the world with a title that already picks a fight: *Reducción de las letras y arte para enseñar a hablar a los mudos* — a reduction of the letters, and an art for teaching the mute to speak. The book is about fingerspelling, articulation, and the hope that a visible alphabet can lead to an audible one. It is not a communication board. It is not cerebral palsy. It is not ISAAC. It is in this series because AAC’s later wars about “real language” are older than the plastic overlay.

The Deaf community has its own histories, its own schools, and its own reasons to be tired of speech-language pathology treating sign as a warmup for speech. This essay will not annex those histories. It will say, once, what AAC borrowed and what it had no right to claim.

## Two public rooms

Pedro Ponce de León, a sixteenth-century Benedictine, is often named as an earlier teacher of deaf students in Spain. The documentary trail is thinner than the later medals. Bonet’s 1620 book is the object a historian can hold. He describes a manual alphabet and a method aimed at speech. Later AAC lectures sometimes flatten him into “the first AAC author.” He was not. He was a priest-scholar in a country that had already begun to treat some deaf heirs as educable so they could inherit. The legal motive is part of the page.

Paris is the other public room. Charles-Michel de l’Épée (1712–1789) opened a school that used methodical signs — a hybrid of the signs deaf Parisians already used and a French grammatical overlay he trusted. The school became a European attraction. It also became a later battlefield. Oralist reformers in the nineteenth century, including people the profession pack treats at more length, would argue that sign kept deaf students from speech and from the nation. The 1880 Milan congress is the official bruise. AAC inherits the bruise every time a team says a child may have a board “only after speech fails.”

Samuel Heinicke’s oral school in Germany is the counter-room. The argument is not a personality contest. It is a theory of what a person is for. If the person is for the spoken nation, the hand is a threat. If the person is for saying what they know, the hand is a channel. AAC, when it is honest, takes the second job and then has to live next door to the first.

## What AAC actually inherited

A permission: a visual token can carry a sentence.

A warning: the people who already own that visual language may not want it redesigned by hearing teachers with a new acronym.

A habit of the method: fingerspelling, signed English systems, and later Paget-Gorman and Makaton sit on a line that runs through these schools. Some of those later systems were built for people who were not Deaf — children with intellectual disability, adults after illness. The borrow is real. The consent was not always asked.

Blissymbols, in 1971 Toronto, will look to some visitors like “Chinese writing for children who cannot speak.” Charles Bliss had watched characters in Shanghai and wanted a world script. Shirley McNaughton’s classroom wanted a way for children with cerebral palsy to say something other than *yes* and *no*. Neither project is de l’Épée. Both depend on the older public fact that a mark can be a word.

## Who is not in the official story

Deaf adults who already had a language before the abbé arrived. Deaf teachers who were later pushed out of classrooms. Children whose hands were tied to force oral drill. AAC users who were told that a sign or a board would “keep them from talking,” a sentence with a Milan grandfather and a 1980s IEP cousin.

A magazine that calls de l’Épée an early SLP is being kind to the credential and rude to the century. He was a cleric with a school. The school changed what Europe thought a deaf child could be. AAC’s job is to remember the school without stealing the language.
