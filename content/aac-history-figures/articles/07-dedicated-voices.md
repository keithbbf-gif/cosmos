This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

A board needs a partner. A typewriter needs a page. A voice needs a room. The 1970s are when some rooms first heard a machine say a sentence a disabled person had composed. The sentence was often robotic, expensive, and late. It was also public. A clerk could not pretend not to see a card if the card had learned to speak.

Alan Newell’s Talking Brooch, out of Dundee in the early 1970s, was a wearable display: the message on the chest, the partner’s eyes doing the work. It is easy to laugh at a brooch. It is harder to admit that a wearable public text is a theory of conversation. The later smartphone is a brooch that got a speaker.

The Canon Communicator — a small tape-print device of the later 1970s — let a person tap out a message and tear off a strip. HandiVoice, from HC Electronics / Phonic Ear about 1978, is the box Rick Creech later named as an early machine he actually used: phonemes, patience, a voice that was not his larynx and was still his sentence. Votrax chips sat in basement prototypes, including Bruce Baker and Kenneth Smith’s 1981 AIM 65 experiment. None of these is “the first SGD.” The first is a prize. The usable fact is a decade when voice output left the demo and entered a few school bags.

## The voice that was not built for AAC

Dennis Klatt’s work at MIT produced a synthesis that Digital Equipment Corporation shipped as DECtalk in the mid-1980s. The famous later user is Stephen Hawking, whose DECtalk voice became a public identity. Hawking is not an AAC clinician and is not a mascot for this brand. He is evidence that a synthesis designed for the telephone lab could become a person. Klatt died in 1988. The voice did not.

Dedicated devices — Liberator, earlier Express hardware, later Dynavox boxes — will spend the 1980s and 1990s being sold as medical equipment. They are heavy. They have warranty cards. They have a sales representative who knows the funding code. They also have a sound. For some users that sound is the first time a stranger has waited for the end of the sentence.

## What the dedicated years cost

Money. Time. A candidacy process that could refuse the box. A voice that made a child sound like a man, or a man sound like a cartoon. Repair. The shame of a battery funeral in a classroom. The later iPad will be accused of being “not a real device.” The accusation is a descendant of these years, when *real* meant *dedicated*, *funded*, and *ugly on purpose*.

Portability was already a fight. A rack at Stoke Mandeville is not a backpack. A Canon in a pocket is. AAC’s later argument about whether a system can survive a cafeteria begins as an argument about whether the voice leaves the ward.

Toby Churchill’s Lightwriter line, in Britain, is another dedicated cousin: a dual-display typewriter-talker that let the user and the partner see the same sentence growing. Zygo’s boards and later boxes sat on the Toronto exhibit floors Weiss had demanded. None of these names is a consumer recommendation. They are the 1970s–80s street map. A magazine that only says “SGDs arrived” is skipping the street.

## Residue

A 2026 app that speaks a sentence is standing on Klatt, on Votrax, on a 1978 HandiVoice, and on every user who was willing to let a room hear a machine and still call it *me*. The app is lighter. The identity problem is the same.
