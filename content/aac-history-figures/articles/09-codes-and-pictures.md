This is history for a general reader. It is not a treatment plan, not a diagnosis, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Three different rooms, three different bets.

Britain, the 1970s. Margaret Walker and colleagues begin what will be called Makaton: a small, taught vocabulary of signs and later graphic symbols, originally for people with hearing impairment and learning disability, meant to be used *with* speech, not instead of a life. The founding-year wording slips between sources. The usable fact is a structured, teachable core that spread through UK health and education long before most American SLPs could spell it.

Pittsburgh and Wooster, 1980–1983. Bruce Baker, a linguist listening to AAC users, decides that a keyboard of letters is the wrong miniature language for a device. He wants icons that mean more than one thing, sequenced — semantic compaction, trademarked Minspeak. Rick Creech’s enthusiasm, in Baker’s later telling, keeps the project alive. Baker and Kenneth Smith put a prototype on an AIM 65 with a Votrax chip in 1981. In December 1981 Baker signs with Barry Romich at Prentke Romich. The first commercial release is remembered for the 1983 ASHA Convention in Cincinnati, on Express III hardware. Vendor memory is not a sworn date. Independent secondary accounts repeat the Cincinnati release. Use both, and say so.

Delaware, 1985. Andy Bondy and Lori Frost, at the Delaware Autism Program, are tired of systems that wait for the adult to start. Picture exchange — the child gives a picture, the adult gives the thing — is a theory of initiation dressed as a card. The 1994 *Focus* paper is how most researchers met PECS. The classroom work is older than the paper. Pyramid Educational Consultants is the later company. This series will describe the historical bet, not teach the phases.

## What the three bets share

A small set. Teaching. A claim that pictures or signs can be language-like without being a natural sign language. A risk of becoming a brand.

What they do not share is the user they imagined. Makaton imagined a taught community — family and staff signing the same core. Minspeak imagined a generative device user who could learn icon sequences the way a person learns words. PECS imagined a child who would not initiate and an adult who had been doing too much of the pointing. Later clinics stacked all three in one backpack and then wondered why the child was confused. The confusion is not the child’s.

Picture Communication Symbols (Mayer-Johnson) and other single-meaning sets are the fourth, quieter bet: one picture, one gloss, a page that can be photocopied. They have no single hero in this pack. They have a generation of SLPs who laminated until the kitchen smelled like adhesive.

## Fights that were about language

Is a sequence of icons a word? Is an exchange a sentence? Does a taught sign vocabulary compete with a Deaf sign language in the same building? Does a picture system trap a child in requesting? Mirenda and others will write the autism-and-AAC papers that refuse the easy yes. Kangas and Lloyd will refuse the wait for “prerequisite” cognition. Those papers are the 1980s talking back to the methods in this essay.

Bliss remains in the room as the older, more grammatical cousin. Some teams kept Bliss. Many moved to PCS because the partners could guess a line drawing of a shoe faster than a Bliss mark. Guessability is not language. It is Tuesday.

## Residue

A 2026 “core board” with *I*, *want*, *go*, *like* is standing on all three rooms and on Bliss and on the orthopedic card. The core is not a product. It is an old argument about whether the tray can hold a life.
