This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

An alphabet is a communication board that schools already know how to teach — except when the student cannot hold a pencil or speak the sound. Then the alphabet becomes optional, and the optional thing disappears. AAC’s literacy history is mostly a history of disappearance: children who got symbols for *want* and never got letters for *when*.

David Yoder, in later interviews, kept pointing at reading. Shirley McNaughton has said the same from the Bliss side: the marks were supposed to be a bridge toward print, not a gated community. The orthopedic card already had letter boards for the children someone thought were “high.” The scandal is the children who got only nouns.

## Who wrote the refusal

Karen Erickson, David Koppenhaver, Janice Light, David McNaughton, and colleagues around the Center for Literacy and Disability Studies (UNC) and the AAC-RERC spent the 1990s and 2000s saying, in papers and in rooms, that conventional literacy instruction was being withheld on the basis of disability label. They were not the first to notice. They were the ones who made it citable in the AAC journal and in special-education programs that had been using picture schedules as a substitute for books.

The instruction they described was not a secret clinic method. It was shared reading, alphabet knowledge, phonological work adapted to a point or a gaze, writing with selection rather than handwriting. This series will not teach the sequence. It will say that the sequence existed, was published, and was ignored in many IEPs because a device vendor had sold a page of icons and called it language.

Janice Light and David McNaughton’s later papers on literacy and on meaningful adult roles belong on the same shelf. So does the quiet fact that many “AAC rooms” never had books in reach of the child. A symbol wall is not a library. A library that never lets the child pick the next letter is a display. The Center for Literacy and Disability Studies made that distinction assignable. Assignable distinctions change IEPs more slowly than apps change, which is why this essay is still a present-tense complaint.

## Devices that could spell and rooms that would not

Minspeak systems could produce words. So could old alphabet boards. So can any modern app with a QWERTY. The barrier was rarely the firmware. It was the adult who believed literacy was a reward for speech, or a reward for a cognitive score, or a luxury after requesting was “functional.” Functional, in that usage, meant *I can guess what you want*. Literacy means *you can tell me something I did not guess*.

Michael B. Williams, who writes as a user, has insisted on a full toolbox: speech remnants, low-tech, spelling, device. His “multi-modal man” essay is a literacy document as much as a device document. A person who can spell is harder to keep on a noun page.

## Residue

A 2026 core board that has no alphabet on the back is repeating 1950. A classroom that reads to a child who uses AAC and never lets the child pick the next letter is repeating 1950 with better furniture. Erickson and Koppenhaver’s public work is the refusal. The residue is a keyguard and a teacher who waits.

Alphabet boards in orthopedic wards already knew this. The later scandal is not that letters were invented in 1998. The scandal is that icons became a ceiling. A ceiling can look like kindness. It is still a ceiling.
