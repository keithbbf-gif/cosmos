This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

A speech-generating device can cost as much as a used car and then be stamped *school property*. June arrives. The device goes into a closet. The child goes home without a voice. That story is so common it has become a joke at ISAAC receptions. Jokes at receptions are data.

Funding is not a subplot. It is how AAC became a medical benefit, a special-education related service, a vendor territory, and a family credit-card problem at the same time. POSSUM was already a company in 1961. Prentke Romich dates to 1966. Dedicated devices of the 1980s and 1990s learned to speak the language of U.S. insurers and, later, Medicare SGD criteria. The criteria asked about diagnosis, prognosis, and whether the device was “speech.” Tablets later broke the sentence. If a device can also be a cartoon machine, is it durable medical equipment? The policy fights of the 2010s are that question in federal prose.

## Three owners

The insurer wants a code and a restriction. The school wants a tool for educational benefit and an inventory number. The family wants a voice in the grocery store. The user — if anyone asks — wants to keep the vocabulary they already built when they change schools. Those four wants do not fit on one purchase order.

Beukelman and Mirenda’s opportunity barriers were partly this. A policy that forbids a device on the bus is not a motor impairment. A practice that locks the volume because the classroom is loud is not an access assessment. Light’s interaction work assumed a partner who would wait. Funding assumes a partner who will file.

Internationally the furniture changes and the plot does not. National health services, charity loans, court cases, and a company demo on loan are all ways of answering who pays. Sudha Kaul’s later ISAAC presidency and Erna Alant’s Pretoria centre are reminders that the American Medicare story is not the world. This essay still uses the American objects because SLPWOW’s readers will meet them first. It should not pretend they are universal.

School-owned devices create a second plot at graduation. The vocabulary the teenager built in tenth grade may be a file the district considers surplus property. Adults who acquire AAC after a diagnosis meet a different office: vocational rehabilitation, insurance, a charity, a spouse’s credit card. The offices do not share software. The person does. A history that only narrates the pediatric IEP is a partial ledger.

## The vendor in the room

ISAAC invited distributors from the Toronto challenges onward. The exhibit hall pays for the coffee and shapes the imagination. A clinician who has only ever seen one company’s language system will assess toward that system. Lloyd and others tried to keep the journal from becoming a catalog. Users like Creech and Williams tried to keep the lecture from becoming a commercial. Neither project is finished.

## Residue

A device that cannot go home is a part-time mouth. A vocabulary file the user cannot export is a landlord. The 2009 pocket-computer price drop changed the landlord without abolishing rent. The ethical residue is simple and not a shopping list: the message belongs to the person. The metal and the software are temporary.

Medicare SGD criteria and school inventory tags are American furniture. A British loan closet and a South African NGO device pool are other furniture. The plot — someone other than the speaker holds the title — repeats. Title is not authorship. Authorship is the message.
