This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

The first communication board in most American memories is a rectangle of cardboard with a grid. A child with cerebral palsy looks at a square. An adult says the word. Or the child points with a headstick, a mouthstick, a fist, a gaze that a nurse has learned to trust. There is no voice chip. There is no scan beep. There is a card, and a person willing to treat the card as speech.

Those cards show up in orthopedic hospitals and “crippled children’s” clinics through the first half of the twentieth century — the letterheads are the ones we would not print now, and they are the ones in the filing cabinet. Eugene T. McDonald’s later writing on cerebral palsy and communication sits on that ward culture. So do unnamed nurses who ruled a piece of shirt cardboard and a grease pencil. Zangari, Lloyd, and Vicker, writing the field’s first long historic essay in 1994, already had to say that much of this work was local, perishable, and poorly dated. A magazine that invents a “first board in 1924” is doing the thing the 1994 paper refused.

## What the ward thought it was solving

The orthopedic hospital of 1930 was not trying to found a discipline. It was trying to get a child through a meal, a lesson, and a visiting hour without guessing. Polio, tuberculosis of the bone, birth injury, the long list of names that later became *cerebral palsy* — these were bodies that might not walk and might not speak and were still expected to be polite. A chart on the tray was politeness with a grid.

The grid was usually nouns. Cup. Toilet. Yes. No. Pain. The interesting boards grew verbs and people and a way to say *you are wrong*. Most did not. A noun board is a shopping list. A language is a shopping list that can argue.

Teachers in those wards were often the ones who refused the shopping list. They wanted letters. An alphabet board is slower than a noun chart and larger than a life. It is also the only cheap way, in 1948, to say a word that is not on the card. AAC will spend the next fifty years arguing about that trade: speed against generativity. The argument is already on the tray.

## Who wrote it down

Physicians wrote etiology. Therapists wrote motor goals. The communication card, if it appears, is an appendix. McDonald and a handful of later clinicians put the card in the middle of the page. That is why he is in this pack as well as in the profession series. The residue is not a test battery. It is the sentence: this child has something to say, and the limbs will not say it, and that is not a reason to wait.

Gaze was already a channel. So was a yes/no blink. So was a partner who scanned the board aloud — “Do you want the cup? the book? the bed?” — until the child signaled. Later engineers will call that *partner-assisted scanning* and put a beep on it. The beep is new. The patience is not.

## What the card could not do

It could not follow the child into a street that had no partner. It could not keep a private sentence. It could not speak to a clerk who would not look. Those three failures are why the 1960s typewriter and the 1970s voice box will look like miracles even when they are slow, expensive, and ugly. They are also why a laminated board is still in the bag in 2026. The battery dies. The card does not.

Institutions left people out. Children labeled uneducable did not get a board because a board implied a mind. Adults in back wards got a call bell, if that. The later Kangas and Lloyd paper — “What are we waiting for?” (1988) — is an argument with this decade as much as with the 1980s. The wait was never only about cognition. It was about who was worth the grease pencil.
