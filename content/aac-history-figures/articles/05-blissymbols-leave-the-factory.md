This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

September 1971, a Toronto library. Shirley McNaughton, teaching nonspeaking children with cerebral palsy at the Ontario Crippled Children’s Centre, finds Elizabeth Helfman’s *Signs and Symbols Around the World*. A chapter describes “the newest symbol language” — Blissymbolics — a pictographic-ideographic script Charles K. Bliss had published as *Semantography* in 1949. Bliss wanted nations to stop lying to each other. McNaughton wanted ten students to say something that was not a guess. The borrow is the founding accident of modern symbol-based AAC in North America. The accident has documents. Use them.

The source book itself was not on the library shelf. The team found *Semantography* at Laurentian University in Sudbury in February 1972. October 1971 is when the classroom work is usually dated. Two dates, one school year. Do not collapse them into a single “Bliss arrives in Canada” cartoon.

## A script that wanted to be a United Nations

Charles K. Bliss was born Karl Kasiel Blitz in 1897. He survived Nazi camps, reached Shanghai, watched Chinese characters work across spoken dialects, and after the war — in Australia — built a logical writing “for an illogical world.” The 1949 book is thick, eccentric, and almost unread. The 1965 edition is the one clinic histories usually cite. Bliss thought ambassadors would use his marks. Ambassadors did not.

What the marks could do, in a 1971 classroom, was sit still. A child with unreliable speech and a reliable eye could look at a symbol that meant *want* or *mother* or *pain* while the spoken languages in the room kept changing. Jim Grice drafted consistent forms so the children did not have to guess the teacher’s handwriting. Bliss himself later argued with the Canadians about what the symbols were *for*. The University of Toronto fonds still hold the legal weather of that argument. A 1982 agreement left Blissymbolics Communication International with authority to publish the marks for people with communication disabilities. The inventor and the clinic were not the same project. The series will not pretend they were.

## What Toronto actually started

A team, not a lone genius. Teachers, clinicians, a draftsman, children, families. A foundation in 1975 (Blissymbolics Communication Foundation, later Institute, later International). Shirley McNaughton as the public name because she would also become ISAAC’s first president. Visitors from Wisconsin and elsewhere who carried overlays home. David Yoder’s later welcome of Bliss talks in the United States is part of that traffic.

The children were not “nonverbal” in the casual later sense. Many vocalized. Few had functional speech. The old ward card had given them nouns. Bliss offered a grammar of marks: combine, invert, mark tense. Whether a given child used that grammar or treated the board as a better noun chart is an empirical question the early years did not always ask. Later AAC research will ask it until the room is tired. The tiredness is healthy.

## Who was left out

Children who could not reliably point or look. Adults. People whose languages were not the English and French of a Toronto hospital. Bliss’s own dream of a world script sat uneasily beside a clinic that needed English glosses under every mark. The glosses won, because the partners needed them. The marks remained, because the children needed something that was not the alphabet yet.

OCCC is now Holland Bloorview Kids Rehabilitation Hospital. The 1971 letterhead said *crippled*. Keep the letterhead in the essay. Change the sign on the door.
