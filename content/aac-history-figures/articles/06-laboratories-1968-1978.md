This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Madison, 1971. Gregg Vanderheiden, an electrical engineering undergraduate, is talked into visiting a school. He meets a boy the later Trace histories name Lydell: cerebral palsy, little or no functional speech, a wooden communication board for classwork. Vanderheiden pulls in classmates. The informal group becomes the Trace Research and Development Center. Fifty years later he will still tell the story as a benevolent deception. Use the published memoir. Do not invent the classroom.

The same decade has other rooms that did not know they were cousins. Shirley McNaughton’s Bliss classroom in Toronto, 1971. John Eulenberg’s Artificial Language Laboratory at Michigan State, from 1974, with *Communication Outlook* as a samizdat journal for people who were building voices in basements. Lyle Lloyd and Macalyne Fristoe arriving at Purdue in 1977 and putting a stand-alone course on the books while the thing still answered to *nonspeech communication*. David Yoder at Wisconsin, already in the work, learning from the students who would become Beukelman and Vanderheiden. These are not a school. They are a coincidence with letterhead.

## What the laboratories thought they were building

A device for one child. Then a device for the next child. Then a way to write about the device so the third child did not start at zero. Trace’s early years are full of auto-monitoring communication boards, scanning, and the slow realization that access — the switch — is a research problem, not a hardware footnote. MSU’s lab treated synthetic speech as a linguistic object: if the machine is going to talk, what should it be allowed to say, and who decides the phonemes? Purdue treated the problem as a curriculum: if this is going to be a profession, someone has to teach it before ASHA has a special-interest group.

Deborah Harris’s 1974 dissertation, in Yoder’s later telling, was about teaching children to use the device the engineers had just made. That sentence is the decade: engineering, special education, and communication disorders in one hallway, none of them sure whose grant this was.

## What they called it

*Nonspeech.* *Assisted communication.* *Augmented communication.* *Communication aids.* The words are not pedantry. *Nonspeech* names an absence. *Augmented* names an addition to speech that might still arrive. *Alternative* — the word ISAAC will keep — names a channel that does not apologize. The 1983 East Lansing meeting will spend hours on this. The 1970s already knew the hours were coming.

Visitors moved. Bliss overlays left Toronto for U.S. clinics because someone put them in a suitcase. Trace reports left Madison because Vanderheiden mailed them. *Communication Outlook* left East Lansing because Eulenberg printed it. A field that will later complain about paywalled PDFs began as a stack of stapled newsletters.

## Who was not in the lab

Most children. Most adults. Most countries. Most languages. The published 1970s map is Midwestern and Canadian with a British engineering cousin (POSSUM, Dundee). Scandinavia, Australia, and later South Africa and India will enter the official photograph in the 1980s through ISAAC. They were not empty before that. They were unpublished in English.

Users are in the labs as testers and, sometimes, as the reason the solder exists. They are rarely in the author line. Michael B. Williams and Rick Creech will later write themselves into the record. The 1970s laboratory photograph still has a child in the chair and a graduate student at the bench. Keep both people in the caption. Do not let the bench steal the chair.
