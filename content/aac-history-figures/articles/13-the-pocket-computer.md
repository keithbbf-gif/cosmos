This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

April 2009. AssistiveWare, a small Amsterdam company, releases Proloquo2Go for iPhone and iPod touch: a full symbol-based AAC application on a consumer pocket computer. Combined with a speaker case, the company’s later telling says, the kit could sit under five hundred U.S. dollars. Dedicated speech-generating devices that year still lived in the thousands, behind a sales representative and a funding packet. David Niemeijer’s public essays call this a democratization. The word is a company word. The price drop is a fact. The App Store as a distribution channel is a fact. Traditional manufacturers noticed.

April 2010, the iPad. The same application now had a surface a child could hold like a book. Adoption jumped. Parents bought tablets on a Saturday. School teams met the tablet on Monday, sometimes without a vocabulary design, a backup board, or a way to keep the device from becoming a cartoon machine. Saltillo, Tobii, PRC, and Dynavox released iOS apps over the next few years — Hershberger of PRC later said the App Store changed distribution as much as the hardware. The dedicated box did not vanish. It lost its monopoly on the idea of a voice.

## What the pocket years fixed

Weight. Stigma, sometimes: a tablet looks like everyone else’s tablet. Price, for families who could not wait for Medicaid. Time-to-first-message, for a parent who refused the two-year candidacy wait. Repair, in the ordinary sense that a smashed screen is a known consumer problem, not a six-week depot mystery.

Michael Williams, writing as a user who had already lived through phoneme devices and low-tech, welcomed the new tools and kept his old ones. That is the adult sentence. The field’s teenager sentence was “there’s an app for that,” which USSAAC later used as a title with the irony included.

## What they broke or revealed

Assessment as gatekeeping had hidden a truth: many people had been denied devices because of cost, not because of a careful motor profile. When cost fell, the gate looked less medical. That is uncomfortable for a profession that had built identity around the gate. It is also uncomfortable for users who then received a tablet with a default vocabulary in the wrong language and no instruction.

Dedicated-device advocates were not only defending a business. Some users need switch access, keyguards, speakers that work in a cafeteria, mounting on a wheelchair, a funding stream that replaces a broken unit. A consumer tablet is a poor wheelchair mount unless someone engineers it. The 2010s literature is full of both evangelism and hangover.

There is also the vocabulary-file problem. A dedicated system, at its best, let a user keep a language they had spent years learning. A cheap app, at its worst, offered a default page in one dialect of English and a reset button a sibling could find. Democratization without export is a new landlord. Williams’s toolbox sentence is the adult correction: keep the paper, keep the old file, do not confuse a store icon with a life.

Privacy is the quieter break. A medical device had a smaller data appetite. A consumer tablet wants accounts, updates, and sometimes a cloud. AAC messages are not playlists. The field is still late to that sentence.

## Residue

A 2026 clinic that treats “iPad AAC” as a single method is as sloppy as a 1996 clinic that treated “Dynavox” as a single method. The glass is a surface. The vocabulary, the access, the partner, and the backup card are still the work. POSSUM’s selector and McNaughton’s overlay did not retire. They got a backlight.
