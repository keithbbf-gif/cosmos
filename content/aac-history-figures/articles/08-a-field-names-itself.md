This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

East Lansing, May 1983. John Eulenberg hosts a gathering at Michigan State’s Artificial Language Laboratory. People who had met in Toronto in 1980 and 1982 at conferences still called *nonspeech* sit down to decide whether they are a field. They argue about *alternative*, *augmentative*, *augmented*. Gregg Vanderheiden and David Yoder, in Shirley McNaughton’s later memory, help the infant keep both *augmentative* and *alternative*. The society is named the International Society for Augmentative and Alternative Communication. ISAAC is the acronym. AAC is the later everyday word. Both are 1983 inventions in the official telling. The work is older. The letterhead is new.

*Tales of ISAAC*, compiled by Judy Seligman-Wine in 2018 with Penny Parnes and Melanie Fried-Oken, is the document to trust for the gossip that is also structure. McNaughton remembers Larry Weiss of Zygo challenging Toronto to hold a second conference and to treat distributors as part of the room. She remembers a 1982 planning meeting at Halton Hills in cold showers. She became the first president (1983–1986). Gunnar Fagerberg of Sweden came next. The presidency was international on purpose. The first biennial conference under the new name was Boston, 1984 — not Toronto, not East Lansing. The journal *Augmentative and Alternative Communication* began in 1985. Lyle Lloyd edited it from 1986 to 1993.

## Why the words took a weekend

*Nonspeech* had been accurate and grim. It named what was missing. Parents and users were already in the Toronto rooms; a society that wanted them in the constitution could not define itself as an absence. *Augmentative* kept faith with people who still had some speech. *Alternative* kept faith with people who did not. The slash in AAC is a peace treaty. Later clinicians who say “AAC is only for people with no speech” are violating the treaty.

Howard Shane, George Karlan, David Beukelman, and Lyle Lloyd appear in McNaughton’s recollection of the naming argument. Other memories in *Tales* shift who said what. That is what a founding weekend is. Do not flatten it into a single inventor of the acronym.

## What a society can do that a lab cannot

Meet every two years in a different country. Print a journal that is not a vendor newsletter. Admit users as members, at least on paper. Fight with itself about research versus practice versus sales. ISAAC did all of that, imperfectly, and still does. USSAAC and other national chapters are later furniture. The 1983 object is the international bet: this is not only a Midwestern engineering hobby.

The journal’s first years are where Light’s 1988 interaction paper, Romski and Sevcik on severe intellectual disability, and Kangas and Lloyd’s “What are we waiting for?” become citable. A field is a pile of citations that can argue.

Zangari, Lloyd, and Vicker’s 1994 historic perspective — ten years after Boston — is the first long attempt to write the social and technical plot from a North American desk while admitting that other countries’ files were thinner in their hands. Read that admission. A founding story that only lists East Lansing, Madison, and Toronto is already a first-world plot. ISAAC’s conference list after 1984 is the correction: Cardiff, Anaheim, Stockholm, then farther.

## Who was still outside

Most users. Most languages. Most school districts. ASHA had not yet made AAC one of the “Big 9” knowledge areas; Lloyd later helped push that change, and it is a 1990s–2000s residue, not a 1983 gift. Manufacturers had been invited and would sometimes seem to own the exhibit hall. The society’s job was to keep the exhibit hall from owning the science. It did not always succeed. The attempt is the history.
