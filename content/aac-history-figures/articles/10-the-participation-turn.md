This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Baltimore, 1992. Paul H. Brookes publishes *Augmentative and Alternative Communication: Management of Severe Communication Disorders in Children and Adults* by David R. Beukelman and Pat Mirenda. The subtitle still says *disorders*. Later editions will say *complex communication needs*. The shift in the subtitle is the decade. A disorder lives in a person. A need lives in a life.

Beukelman had already been a clinician in Seattle and then Barkley Professor at Nebraska (from 1985), with a long collaboration at Madonna Rehabilitation Hospital in Lincoln. Mirenda brought autism, inclusion, and a talent for writing sentences a teacher could use on a Thursday. Together they gave the field a book that could be assigned. Assigned books create a field faster than journals. The journal had existed since 1985. The book is how a master’s student in 1994 met AAC without moving to Purdue.

## Opportunity barriers, not just access

The Participation Model — associated with Beukelman and Mirenda and taught hard in the later editions — asked two kinds of questions. Opportunity barriers: policy, practice, attitude, knowledge. Access barriers: the person’s motor, sensory, linguistic, and literacy profile. A child who has a device in a closet has an opportunity problem. A child who cannot hit a switch has an access problem. Clinics that only measure access keep buying hardware. Clinics that only preach opportunity keep laminating slogans. The model’s usefulness was that it made both visible on one page.

Janice Light’s 1988 paper on interaction had already told the field to look at the partner, the pause, the who-starts. The Participation Model put that insight into a textbook diagram. Light would, much later, become Beukelman’s co-author on the fifth edition (2020), after Mirenda’s four. The baton is public. Do not turn it into a soap opera.

## Against the wait

Kangas and Lloyd’s 1988 “What are we waiting for?” is the other pole of this turn. Teams had been waiting for prerequisite cognitive scores before offering a board. The wait produced silence that looked like data. Romski and Sevcik’s work at Georgia State with people labeled severely intellectually disabled — System for Augmenting Language, published in the same late-1980s journal issues — was empirical rudeness to the wait. People learned symbols when someone talked to the symbols and expected an answer.

The World Health Organization’s later ICF language (2001) gave participation a UN accent. AAC clinicians who already had Beukelman and Mirenda on the shelf recognized the furniture.

## What the turn did not fix

Funding still wanted a diagnosis code. Schools still wanted a child to “show potential.” Vendors still wanted a feature list. Participation language can become a poster. Beukelman, who died on 5 February 2022, kept saying in late talks that his ideas came from people in his community, not from a diagram. Believe the late talks.

The 1992 book’s first subtitle also left people out by saying *severe*. Plenty of users have some speech and still need AAC. The later phrase *complex communication needs* is wider and still a clinic phrase, not a person’s name for themselves. Michael Williams preferred *multi-modal man*. He was right to prefer it.

Joe Reichle’s work on beginning communicators — later collected with Beukelman and Light — belongs in this turn even if this pack does not give him a solo profile. The participation question is not only for people who already spell. It is for people whose first symbol is still being argued about in a meeting that should have ended last year. A textbook that can hold both the ALS clinic and the preschool is why 1992 outsold a pile of good papers.
