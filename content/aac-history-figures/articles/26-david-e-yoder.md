This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

A USSAAC “Leaders & Legends” interview is not a sworn vita, but it is a public one. David E. Yoder describes working in what would later be called AAC from the 1950s, then learning as much from students at Wisconsin as they learned from him: David Beukelman, Deborah Harris, Gregg Vanderheiden. He says he had no senior mentors in the work. That sentence, from a man who later mentored half a field, is the profile.

He is living as of that interview. This pack will not invent a birth year. UNC Chapel Hill is the later institutional home most clinicians attach to his name. Wisconsin is the laboratory hallway.

## What the hallway produced

An engineer (Vanderheiden), a dissertation on teaching children to use a device (Harris, 1974, in Yoder’s telling), and a clinician-researcher who would write the textbook (Beukelman). McNaughton has said that Yoder’s welcome made U.S. Bliss talks possible in the 1970s. He was in East Lansing when the society was named; McNaughton puts him with Vanderheiden on the *augmentative and alternative* compromise.

He keeps returning, in late talks, to literacy and to presuming that learners can learn. Those are not slogans when they come from someone who watched the first devices fail in rooms that only wanted speech. They are a syllabus.

UNC’s later AAC and literacy reputation — Erickson’s centre sits in the same university system’s later story — is not a claim that Yoder owns every subsequent grant. It is a claim that a Wisconsin hallway professor who said yes to an engineer and then kept saying yes to reading is a specific kind of ancestor. ASHA presidencies and honors, if a later editor wants to add them from a necrology or a CV, can wait for a source this pack will not guess. The interview is enough for the hallway.

## Residue

A field that mythologizes lone engineers needs Yoder in the caption: the communication-disorders professor who let the engineer into the course and then let the work change the course. The residue is a permission structure. Labs die without one.

## On the record

A mentor who says the students taught him is either being polite or telling the only true origin story a new field has. In Wisconsin the engineer, the special educator, and the SLP were in one hallway because no guild yet owned the problem. Yoder’s gift was not to own it. It was to keep the hallway open long enough for ISAAC to need a name.
