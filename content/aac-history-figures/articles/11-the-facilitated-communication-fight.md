This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

A field that exists to give people a voice will be tempted by any method that looks like a voice. Facilitated communication (FC) is the temptation with a bibliography. Australia in the 1970s and after — Rosemary Crossley’s published work, the later public case of Anne McDonald — taught a method in which a facilitator supports a person’s hand or arm toward a keyboard or board. Douglas Biklen’s 1990 writing brought the method hard into North American special education. Classrooms adopted it because the sentences that appeared were fluent, adult, and heartbreaking. Families had been told their children had nothing to say. The page said otherwise.

Then the tests. When the facilitator could not hear or see the question, the fluent sentences failed. When the facilitator could, they returned. The authorship, in those designs, sat with the helper’s knowledge. That is not a rumor. It is why ASHA’s 1995 position statement — and the 2018 reaffirmation and update — told clinicians not to use FC as communication. Other associations wrote parallel sentences. Rapid Prompting Method and related later practices entered the same argument with their own brand names and their own thin evidence. This essay is not a catalog of brand names. It is a history of a fight AAC could not sit out.

## Why it belongs in an AAC series

Because teams used letter boards. Because ISAAC’s world includes people who were harmed by false authorship and people who still defend the method as a civil right. Because “presume competence” is a moral instruction that does not license a method that fails a message-passing test. Because AAC already had unaided and aided systems that did not require a facilitator to hold the hand: boards, signs, switches, devices. The existence of those systems is why FC was never the only door.

Howard Shane and others in the clinical AAC community wrote early against the method. They are not villains in a movie about open-mindedness. They are people who had spent the 1980s building candidacy criteria (Shane and Bashir, 1980) and then had to watch a technique skip assessment entirely.

## What the fight did to families

It split rooms. A parent who had finally heard “I love you” on a facilitator’s watch did not want a journal article. A clinician who had seen a false abuse allegation generated by a facilitator’s spelling did not want a workshop. Both rooms are in the history. Neither room gets a homemade protocol from this magazine.

Anne McDonald’s life and legal personhood are public in Australia. Name her as history, not as a mascot. Do not invent her inner life. Do not use schoolchildren’s names from any district that adopted FC.

## Residue

The later phrase *supported typing* sometimes tries to keep the furniture and change the sign. Read the method, not the sign. Independent pointing, independent gaze, independent switch use — the old access problem from essay 04 — remain the channels this series can describe without selling a how-to. The moral residue is double: do not treat silence as emptiness, and do not put words in a person’s hand and call it their voice.

A 2026 reader who meets a viral video of a supported hand should know that AAC already had this argument, in public, for thirty years. The video is not a new method. It is a new distribution system for an old claim.
