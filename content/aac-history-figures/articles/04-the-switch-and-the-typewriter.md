This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Stoke Mandeville, about 1960. A volunteer named Reginald Maling watches men with high spinal injuries call a nurse by blowing on a whistle hung near the mouth. The whistle is a channel. It is also an insult to the rest of the day. Maling begins to build a selector: suck and blow on a mouthpiece, a row of clicks, a grid that maps those clicks onto a typewriter key or a lamp or a wireless. He calls it a Patient Operated Selector Mechanism. The hospital paper will print P.O.S.M. The company founded in 1961 will lean on the Latin *possum* — I can. Both names are in the 1963 *Paraplegia* article by Maling and D. C. Clarkson. Use the paper, not the modern website, when you want the year.

Gregg Vanderheiden, writing his own early-device memoir decades later, called POSSUM perhaps the earliest electric communication device in this line: a sip-and-puff controller for a standard typewriter. He was not in Aylesbury in 1960. He was naming a ancestor from Wisconsin. The ancestor is real. The “earliest” prize is the kind of prize this series treats as a later slide. Other rooms were already wiring teletypes. The Stoke Mandeville room is the one with a published 1963 figure and a company that still exists.

## What the switch actually did

It turned a tiny, reliable movement into a choice. That sentence is the whole later field of *access*. Scanning, step-scanning, directed scanning, morse, eye-gaze — all of them are later costumes for the same bet: find a movement the person owns, and attach a vocabulary to it.

The 1963 demonstration culture matters. Maling and Clarkson showed the system at the Copenhagen congress of the International Society for the Rehabilitation of the Disabled. A former rugby player with a C5 injury ran the exhibit. JAMA’s London correspondent, that same year, told American doctors a person might type twenty-five words a minute by sucking and blowing through a grid. Twenty-five is a journalist’s number. The usable fact is smaller: a person who could not reach a key could still write a letter.

The system was environmental as much as linguistic. Lights, bell, wireless, page-turner, drill. Communication and control lived on one selector. Later AAC will split those jobs — a speech-generating device here, a smart-home switch there — and then spend the 2010s putting them back on one tablet. The 1963 rack already knew they were one problem.

## What it was not

It was not a symbol set. It was not a language intervention. It was not a children’s service. It was rehabilitation engineering in a spinal-injuries centre, aimed at men who had already had a life with speech. Children with cerebral palsy, and adults with intellectual disability, are mostly not in this photograph. They will have to wait for Trace, for Toronto, for Purdue. The wait is part of the history.

It was also not cheap, not portable, and not a secret the National Health Service could hand every ward. POSSUM became a company. Companies have salesmen. Salesmen have a habit of arriving before the assessment. AAC’s later fights about funding and gatekeeping start here, in a polite British form.

## Residue

A modern eye-gaze user who types on a grid is closer to Maling’s click-count than to a cartoon of 2010. The cartoon has a bright app. The inheritance is the selector. Find the movement. Map it to a token. Let the person finish the sentence.

Comhandi and other mid-1960s scanning teletypes sit in the same decade. Vanderheiden lists them as cousins, not children. Keep them as cousins. The family is engineering. The later surname is AAC.
