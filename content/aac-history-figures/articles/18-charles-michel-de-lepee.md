This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Paris, a school that tourists could visit. Charles-Michel de l’Épée (1712–1789), a cleric, built an education for deaf students around methodical signs: the signs already used by deaf Parisians, plus a French grammatical apparatus he trusted. The school made deaf children visible as pupils. It also made a later oralist backlash possible, because a public method can be publicly banned.

He is not an SLP. He is not an AAC inventor. He is in this pack because the later field’s fear of the hand — “if they sign or point they will not talk” — is a nineteenth-century argument wearing a 1980s IEP. Milan 1880 is the official bruise. de l’Épée is the earlier room the bruise tried to close.

## The man and the method

Born 24 November 1712 at Versailles by the usual reference works; died 23 December 1789 in Paris. Those calendar details are stable enough for a caption. The inner life is not our business. The method is. Methodical signs were a hearing teacher’s redesign of a living language. Deaf historians have said so for a long time. AAC clinicians who later built signed English systems and Makaton-like vocabularies for people who were not Deaf repeated the redesign with new motives and new risks.

The profession-history sibling pack has its own de l’Épée scene. This one stays with the visual channel as a civil fact: a person may speak with the hand, and the state may still punish them for it. AAC’s job is to remember the civil fact without claiming the language.

## Residue

Every unaided system in a modern AAC textbook — gesture, sign, glance — sits downstream of the public European argument this school forced. Every team that refuses a sign or a board until speech is “exhausted” sits downstream of the argument that closed the school’s descendants. Both residues are live.

He did not write a communication overlay. He wrote a school. Schools outlast overlays.

The successor who matters in Deaf history is often Roch-Ambroise Cucurron Sicard, and then the Deaf teachers the oralist years tried to fire. This AAC profile will not annex that succession. It will say that a hearing cleric’s school made the visual channel a European public fact, and that AAC later treated the fact as a toolbox item. Toolbox items can be useful. They can also be rude. The ruder later systems are the ones that rename a living sign language as a “strategy.”
