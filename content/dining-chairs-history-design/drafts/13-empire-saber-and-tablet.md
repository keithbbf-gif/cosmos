The saber is a kick. Rear legs sweep back; front legs may sweep forward; the back becomes a tablet or a curved bar; the old pierced splat loses the argument. Empire — French first, then English and American readings — wants antique power without Gothic lace. The dining chair that comes out of this is closer to the painted klismos than to Chippendale, and closer to a slight lounge than Hepplewhite’s hoop.

The 1802 *London Chair-Makers’ and Carvers’ Book of Prices* (and the 1808 supplement) prices curule and Grecian work as hours. That is the document I trust more than a decorator’s “Empire elegance.” Someone got paid to carve or to bend that kick.

The sibling pack owns Empire tables — pillar and claw — and Lannuier’s French New York. This hour is the chair’s kick at dinner.

## What happens to the back

A tablet is a horizontal rest for the shoulder blades. It can be reeded, carved, gilt, inlaid. It hits a band. If the band matches you, the chair is famous. If it hits your neck or the middle of nothing, you will pad it and spoil the line. Some Grecian chairs keep a more continuous curve, a klismos memory. Those are kinder to a range of spines and meaner to the shop, because curves cost.

Upholstery fattens. The nineteenth century is on its way to comfort as a project. A stuffed seat on saber legs is already a little at war with the drawing. The drawing wanted thin. The sitter wanted mercy. Dining rooms split the difference and then split the horsehair.

Curule chairs — X-frames — show up as a type in the price books and as a luxury in rooms. They are better as occasional seats than as a set of eight at a long table. The X fights knees and the person beside you.

## The kick as a dining problem

Saber rear legs occupy the aisle. Servants — and later, whoever is carrying the platter — meet them with shins and hems. Coats hang on them. Vacuum cleaners still hate them. A dining room with tight passage and eight sabers is a bruise map.

Front sabers can kick a table stretcher or a pedestal base. Pedestal tables and saber chairs are a pair in the catalog and a negotiation on the floor. Measure the spread.

Short grain in a cut saber is the shop’s fear. Laminated or steamed curves, where a shop could do them, are safer. A CNC saber in weak stock is a warranty claim. I have seen the break. It is always at the same place in the curve, and always when a guest leans to tell a story.

Stretchers, again, are the adult. Some Grecian chairs refuse them for line. I would not refuse them in a house that eats.

## American names

Phyfe’s Grecian chairs are the New York sentence. Lannuier brings a French accent and a shorter American career. Regional shops copy both. Painted fancy chairs — Baltimore, then Hitchcock — take the light sit and put bronze powder on it. Do not confuse a saber parlor chair with a Hitchcock mill chair. The sit can be cousins. The factory is not.

Haircloth, cane, rush: same menu, different decade. Leather appears when someone has had enough itch. Empire dining rooms that wanted display used more gilt than gravy can forgive. The chairs that survived in kitchens lost the gilt and kept the kick.

## The sit

A little more rake than Federal square-backs, often. You find the tablet and you stay. Getting up to carve is a slightly longer rise. Host chairs in this style can be throne-adjacent: heavier, more arms, more carving. They photograph as power. They sit as a warning to the rest of the table. That is not accidental.

Seat height still wants a table in the high 28s to 30s. Thick upholstery steals the gap. If you recover an Empire chair with modern foam, you may have built a child’s reach to the plate. Compress the foam with a fist and measure what a two-hour sit will actually be. Then decide.

I like these chairs when the saber has meat and the tablet is in the right band for the people who live there. I do not like them as a restaurant row. The kick is a per-chair cost. Multiply by sixty and you have a waiter's injury report.

## Hours, not ruins

The 1802 book and the 1808 supplement are not poetry. They are a shop’s argument about what a Grecian chair costs in time: extra for a curve, extra for a carved tablet, extra if the front legs kick as well as the rear. I will not print a shilling line from memory. `[CITE NEEDED]` for any priced hour you quote. The point that survives without a number is the one I trust: the kick was labor. Someone stood at a bench and made a saber that a later catalog would call antique.

French Empire and English Regency both wanted Rome and Greece in the dining room. The French chair can be heavier in gilt. The English and American Grecian side chair is often the one you can still pull to a table without sitting in a monument. Thomas Hope’s interiors are arguments about rooms. Kenny and Brown will keep Phyfe from becoming a mood. I stay under the sitter.

A New York Grecian side chair in mahogany, cane or haircloth, reeded or plain tablet, is a different object from a gilt parlor klismos that never saw gravy. Auction language flattens them. Your thighs will not. The parlor chair is often shallower and more raked. The dining chair still has to let you cut.

## The tablet as a band of luck

I have sat in tablets that found the blade of the shoulder as if the maker had met my family. I have sat in tablets that found the vertebra I use to nod, and I left the table early. There is no average spine in a set of eight. There is a band. If the household is one height, you can get lucky. If the household is a mix, the kinder Grecian chairs are the ones that keep a longer curve — a klismos memory — instead of a hard shelf.

Padding the tablet is the usual household edit. It works. It also turns the chair into a different silhouette and a different height at the back. A tied-on pad walks. A stitched pad stays and advertises itself. I would rather move the sitters than upholster a tablet that was meant to be wood.

Host chairs in the style add arms and often a thicker tablet, sometimes a carved crest that photographs as authority. Rising to carve from that chair is a longer motion than from a Federal square-back. The arms may clear a period table and fail a later one that grew a drawer apron. Measure the arm as you would measure a Phyfe host in any other decade: to the underside of the skirt, with the chair pulled in as if someone were cutting.

Seat depth on many Grecian sides is moderate to shallow. Shallow helps a short thigh under an apron. Shallow numbs a long femur by course three. People blame the tablet for a dead ham that is really a rail and a depth. Sit for the length of a real meal before you buy six. Twenty minutes in a dealer’s aisle is a greeting, not a dinner.

## Pairing the kick, not the pillar

The sibling pack can keep the pillar-and-claw table. I need to know where the front saber lands when the chair is in eating position. A pedestal foot that spreads is a cousin of the saber: both occupy a circle on the floor. The chair’s front kick can tap the platform or the carved paw. A four-leg table with a box stretcher is worse. The saber wants to live in that box.

I put the chair at the place setting and look down. If the front legs are already kissing the table’s architecture, the sitter will learn to sit short of the plate. That is a dinner tax. Pull the chair back until the saber clears and you have moved the person into a reach. Neither is comfort. The fix is a different pair, not a different opinion.

Rear sabers need an aisle. I walk the path a platter takes. Eight chairs with a generous kick in a room measured for Chippendale posts will write themselves on shins. Coats still hang on the rear curve. I have taken a wool sleeve off a saber used as a hook for a season; the finish had a shine that was not wax. Count the chairs in the used position, not in the catalog rendering. A leafed table that narrows the aisle when closed punishes the rear sweep twice.

## Who made the hour

Phyfe’s shop was a New York operation with a name later rooms could hang on. Lannuier’s shop was French in training and brief in American years. Most of the Grecian dining chairs in this country were not either man. They were regional copies, painted fancy cousins, later factory Empire with a tablet stamped into cheaper wood. Southern shops — including shops that used enslaved labor — made fashionable seating for rooms whose inventories said “mahogany chairs” and stopped. Name the shop when a mark or a document exists. Name the labor when the context is a plantation or a documented bound workshop. Do not print a romantic cabinetmaker onto an anonymous saber.

Gilt belongs to rooms that wanted display. The chairs that stayed in service lost the gold and kept the curve. I trust those more. A surviving gilt Grecian in a dining context is often a chair that was spared, not a chair that worked.

Haircloth on a saber frame is the formal American sit: hot, shiny, a split waiting at the front rail. Cane is cooler. Rush is the cheaper breath. Leather is the later mercy. Foam on a Grecian revival is a height lie. Compress it. Measure.

## Short grain, stretchers, oak

The shop fear is not a figure of speech. A saber cut from a straight board has grain that runs out of the curve. That is where the guest’s lean becomes a crack. Steam-bent or laminated sabers keep fiber around the kick. I look at end grain and at the inside of the curve. A CNC outline in weak stock is a drawing with a warranty date.

Stretchers under a Grecian dining chair offend some eyes. They save rooms that eat. A medial stretcher and side stretchers turn the kick into a box again. I would rather see a slightly less pure outline than a chair that racks when someone turns to the person on the left. Dinner is a lateral load. The klismos painting did not have to survive that. The dining chair does.

An Arkansas oak saber is a legitimate quote if the rear legs have meat at the short grain and the tablet is placed for the people who live there. It is a bad quote if the oak is stained to fake mahogany and the kick is a stencil of a museum photograph. Pair it to a table that can take the visual weight of a kick, and keep the gilt off the gravy path.

I buy these chairs when I can sit in the actual set at the actual table. I do not buy them from a photograph of a tablet. The tablet is a band of luck. The kick is a floor plan. The seat is a recover waiting to happen. If those three agree, the chair is a good long sit with a little lounge in it. If they do not, you have bought a coat hook and a bruise.
