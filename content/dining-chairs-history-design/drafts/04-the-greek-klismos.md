The klismos everyone knows is paint. A red-figure woman sits in a chair whose back is a curve and whose legs are sabers, light as a line. The seat is a thin plane. There is no fat splat, no ball-and-claw, no stretcher box you can lean a boot on. Gisela Richter spent a career turning those paintings and a handful of reliefs into a furniture history. The wood, for the classical type, is almost not there. That absence is the first fact. The second is that later Europe could not stop rebuilding the silhouette from the drawings.

If you sit in a museum’s “klismos reconstruction,” you are sitting in a hypothesis with a weight limit. If you sit in a Phyfe Grecian side chair, you are sitting in a New York shop’s idea of Greece, with mahogany and a rush or cane seat that Greece did not owe them. Both are useful. Neither is a fifth-century dinner.

## What the drawing claims

The type, as Richter and later handbooks fix it: four saber legs, the front pair kicking forward, the rear pair kicking back; a concave backrest on slim stiles; a seat that looks too thin for a modern Thanksgiving. The whole object reads as sprung. A Windsor is sprung in the sticks. A klismos is sprung in the outline. That is why furniture people still say the word when they mean elegance without upholstery.

Whether the legs were bent, cut from curved timber, or laminated in some lost way is an argument the paintings will not settle. Bentwood as Thonet later industrializes it is not a Greek factory. Cut sabers from solid are easy to imagine and easy to break at the short grain. A shop that has split a rear saber on a revival chair knows the cost of the drawing. `[CITE NEEDED]` before anyone prints a construction method as known.

The sit implied by the paintings is upright enough to talk and relaxed enough that the back is used. This is not a throne. It is not a stool. It is a backed chair that has already decided the back is a comfort, not only a rank. That decision is the Greek contribution I trust more than any reconstructed inch.

## Dinner is the wrong word, and still

Classical dining, for the men the texts care about, is often the couch: reclining, a symposium furniture, a different spine. Women and other households sit in chairs in the pictures. A klismos in a vase scene may be a domestic seat, a wedding seat, a mythological seat. I will not turn every painted chair into a dining chair. I will say this: when Western dining rooms later wanted a light, fine, unpadded side chair, they reached for this outline. The klismos became dining by adoption.

The saber leg at a table is a coat-catcher and a shin-finder. It kicks into the aisle. It also clears a round pedestal in a way a square Chippendale leg does not. Saarinen will later clear the floor by another religion. The klismos clears by thinning and kicking. If your dining room is a passage, the kick is a bruise. If your dining room is a picture, the kick is the point.

## Scarce wood, loud afterlife

A few late antique and provincial seats get dragged into the conversation. Treat them as cousins, labeled. Do not hang a whole classical type on a Roman survival. The honest chain is: paintings and reliefs → Renaissance and neoclassical reading of the antique → late eighteenth- and early nineteenth-century Grecian chairs in London price books and New York shops → every later “klismos” in a catalog that means saber legs and a concave tablet.

The 1802 *London Chair-Makers’ and Carvers’ Book of Prices* prices curule and Grecian work as labor. That is a document. Phyfe’s Grecian chairs are documents. Thomas Hope’s interiors are arguments. A 1990s reproduction sold as “authentic klismos” is a mood. I would rather sit the Phyfe and know what year it is.

Federal America loves this chair because it is antique without being Gothic, light without being cheap, and drawable in a room that also wants a sideboard. The dining-room pack owns Phyfe’s tables. Here the chair: a tablet or a curved back, saber legs, a seat that is often cane or rush because the frame is too refined to hide a fat pad and still look Greek. Haircloth appears when the room is formal and the sitter can itch.

## What the body gets

A concave back that hits you mid-blade can be a kindness for a short sit and a bar for a long one, depending on where the curve lands on your particular spine. There is no lumbar pillow in the type. There is a plane. People who like klismos chairs like being held without being stuffed. People who hate them want a pad and a higher stop.

Seat height in surviving revival examples often sits in the dining band because the shops that made them were making dining rooms. That is not evidence for Athens. It is evidence for 1810. Seat depth is frequently shallow. Shallow is good for short thighs and for getting in under an apron. Shallow is a numb ham for a long femur on course four.

The saber rear leg puts the back’s load on a curve that wants to split. Stretchers, when revival shops add them, are a confession. Some painted Greek chairs in pictures show no stretchers. A woodworker looks at that and thinks about rack. Dinner racks a chair. A symposium couch racks differently. The klismos as a dining chair in a house with children is a type that needs honest stretchers or honest lamination or an honest refusal to let twelve-year-olds lean.

## Do not reconstruct a restaurant

I have seen restaurants put “klismos” chairs at tables of 30 inches and call it Mediterranean. The sit is usually a reproduction with a foam pad that destroys the line and a finish that cannot take wine. If you want the drawing, keep the seat thin and the table apron shallow. If you want the dinner, measure the gap and stop caring whether the vase painters would recognize the stain.

A shop in Arkansas that cuts a saber dining chair is quoting Empire and Federal, not quoting a pot. That is a clean quote if the rear legs are thick enough at the short grain and the stretchers are real. It is a dirty quote if the saber is a CNC outline in weak stock. The drawing is cheap now. The short grain is not.

Richter is still the place to start, then the vase, then the price book, then the American chair with a provenance. Skip the paragraph that says the klismos is the most beautiful chair ever made. Beauty is not a dimension. The curve is.
