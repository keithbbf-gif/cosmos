The front rail is where dinner signs the chair. Haircloth splits there. Leather wears there. Rush abrades there. Your hamstring learns an arris there in the first twenty minutes if the shop left the edge square and the seat a little high.

I run a hand along the rail before I care about the splat. A quarter-round or a softened arris is a kindness that does not show in a hero shot. A sharp edge is a drawing that forgot a body.

## Height and edge are one hit

A seat that is too high puts more weight on the rail and less on the feet. The rail then becomes a bar. People call this “circulation” and buy a pad. The pad slides them forward and the bar moves with them. Fix the height or the edge. Do not stack sins.

A seat that is too deep puts the rail at the popliteal fold. Same bar, different cause. The depth essay and this one share a patient.

Waterfall upholstery can hide a sharp frame. Press through the cloth. If you find a knife, the recover should include a radius on the wood, not only a thicker foam.

## Historical rails

Compass fronts are already a curve in plan; they can still be sharp in section. Windsor saddles drop the front a little if the saddle is real. Federal squares are honest bars. Plastic stackers sometimes have the only decent radius in the room because the mold required it.

Carved front rails — beading, gadrooning — are pretty and can be mean. A bead is a small bar. Fingers love it. Thighs do not, if it is the contact.

## Shop practice

We radius the front rail as a default on a dining seat you will sit on in clothes, and more so if anyone sits in shorts. We do not radius a show rail on a museum copy until the client says they will eat. Then we radius it.

A customer who insists on a knife-edge “period profile” can have it on a chair that lives against a wall. At the table I will argue.

Finish on the rail should take abrasion. A soft wax on a front rail is a week of dinner. Oil and a later wax, or a film finish you can repair, is more honest.

I like to see a slightly proud cushion or cane wrap that takes the first hit. I do not like a cushion so proud it raises the sit into the apron.

The thigh is not a critic you can out-argue with a style name. It is a measurement with nerves in it. Soften the bar. Then see if the height was the rest of the problem.

## Two clocks, two sins

I sit. I wait. I do not decide in the first thirty seconds, when pride is still doing the work. At fifteen minutes a sharp arris announces itself as a line. At forty minutes a high seat announces itself as a tingle. Those are different clocks. The rail can be kind and the height cruel. The height can be right and the rail a knife. I write both on the ticket.

I have watched a household add a pad, then a second pad, then complain that the chairs were “cutting.” The chairs were high, the rail was square, and the pads had walked them onto the arris. Stephen Pheasant’s buttock–popliteal length is why the rail’s location matters. The nerve is in the fold. A dining chair does not have a slider to move the fold off the bar. You have a saw, a rasp, and a decision about the finished sit.

BIFMA’s cascade-front language on task chairs is a cousin. It is not a dining code. A wooden front rail can learn the same mercy with a plane. It does not need a mesh waterfall.

## Recover, recane, and the wood underneath

If we are recovering a balloon-back, I pull the old foundation and I look at the frame’s front member before I order foam. A knife under a century of horsehair is a common find. The recover includes a plane pass. If the client wants the knife “because it is original,” the chair can live against a wall.

Rush and cane take the first hit if they wrap the rail. When they sag, the wood comes back. A recane that is pulled too tight can lift the sit and still leave a sharp rail waiting underneath. Ask the recaner to ease the front. Ask to see the wood.

Vinyl shines and then cracks along the same line haircloth splits. I look at old seats for a polished stripe or a split that follows the rail. Conservation labs will have notes if an editor wants a cited study; `[CITE NEEDED]` before this wear is printed as a lab result. Trade observation is enough for a shop: the front rail is where dinner signs.

July dinners are a test the catalog does not run. A sharp rail in linen is a line you can feel through the cloth. A sharp rail on bare skin is a review. People who wax a front rail like a tabletop are treating a wear surface as a show surface. It will not stay show. Oil and a later wax, or a film you can repair, is more honest.

If we are copying a period profile, we copy the period’s kindness when the period had it — a rounded compass, a saddle drop — and we refuse the period’s knife when the client will eat every night. A wall chair can be a drawing. A dinner chair is a thigh. Arkansas oak dining seats get the radius as a default. We can leave a show bead on a chair that will not eat. The thigh writes either way. I would rather the writing be a polish than a split.

## Corners the tape misses

A tape across the front edge tells me radius only if I also use a finger. Numbers do not catch a leftover arris the sander missed at the corners. Compass seats fail at the corners more than in the middle. Square Federal rails fail as a straight bar. I check both places. A gadrooned rail fails as a row of small bars; I sit and I feel which bead is the contact.

The third hour will still find the rail first, even if the splat is as stern as it wants for forty minutes. Soften the bar. Then see if the height was the rest of the problem.

## Historical rails I sit on purpose

A Queen Anne compass that is kind in plan can still be a knife in section at the corners. I sit and I turn, because dinner turns. The corner is where a short femur ends up. A Windsor whose saddle is real drops the front a little and takes the first hit in the dish; a router dent called a saddle leaves the bar where it was. Federal squares are honest bars — easy to radius, easy to leave sharp. Plastic stackers sometimes have the only decent radius in the room because the mold required it. Carved beads and gadroons are pretty and can be mean. A bead is a small bar. Fingers love it. Thighs do not, if it is the contact.

I like a slightly proud cushion or a cane wrap that takes the first hit. I do not like a cushion so proud it raises the sit into the apron. One variable.

## Shop ticket for a rail

On a new oak dining seat the radius is a default. I write the edge as “eased” or “show bead” on the ticket so the last pass is not a guess at the sander. Show bead is for a chair that will live against a wall, or for a client who has heard the argument and still wants the knife at dinner. I will argue once. I will not hide a knife under foam.

If the seat is already too high, easing the rail will not invent a floor. If the seat is already too deep, easing the rail will make the fold’s meeting kinder and will not move the fold. I note height and depth on the same ticket as the edge. Three lines. One thigh.

A customer who insists on a knife-edge “period profile” can have it on a chair that lives against a wall. At the table I will still argue. The hamstring learns the shop’s last pass either way. I would rather it learn a quarter-round.

I also check the underside of the front rail, where a leftover arris from a tablesaw can hide. The thigh does not only meet the top of the edge. It wraps. A kind top and a sharp bottom is still a bar. One more pass with a block plane. Then I sit again before I call the ticket done.
