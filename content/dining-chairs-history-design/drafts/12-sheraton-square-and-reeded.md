Thomas Sheraton was a draftsman who published like a teacher. *The Cabinet-Maker and Upholsterer’s Drawing-Book* (1793) and later the *Cabinet Dictionary* (1803) square the fashionable chair: a rectangular back, verticals you could have inked, legs that taper and take reeding like a column that learned manners. If Hepplewhite is a hoop and a picture, Sheraton is a grid. Federal America sits in both, often in the same room, and then Phyfe will pull the grid toward Greece.

I start with the square because dinner is a rectangular problem — a table with sides, a plate in front of you, a neighbor’s elbow. A square-back chair agrees with that geometry. It also parks you. The compass seat’s little turn is less invited. You face the roast.

## Reeding is not structure

Reeding is a set of parallel beads cut or applied on a leg or a stile. It catches light. It also catches a rag and a fingernail. On a dining chair the lower reeds get kicked. On a cheap revival they are applied half-rounds that pop. On a good shop chair they are cut in the solid and the arrises still have a little softness so they do not slice.

Structurally the leg is a taper. The reed does not hold the joint. The mortise in the rail does. People stroke reeds and think they are buying joinery. They are buying a drawing. Buy the mortise.

Sheraton’s plates also like turned and fasced legs, sometimes a little colonnette. The dining risk is the same as any delicate lower leg: a sideways shove from a child, a vacuum cleaner, a dog. Tipped feet are common. A tipped reeded leg that does not match the reed is a confession.

## Square backs and the shoulder

A rectangular back can carry a cane panel, a splat in a frame, a pair of verticals, a painted tablet. The contact is often higher and wider than a vase splat and less of a hoop than a shield. Some sitters find this the most “neutral” Federal sit. Neutral is not empty. It is a plane that does not argue.

Cane in the back is cool and can print a pattern on a silk dress. Solid panels are hotter. A tablet with a painted scene is a parlor idea that dining rooms copied; gravy does not care about the scene.

Sheraton’s text distinguishes rooms more than Chippendale’s plates always did. I will not fake a long quote. `[CITE NEEDED]` if you print a *Dictionary* sentence about dining-room chairs. The useful idea is already in the furniture: drawing-room seating can lounge a little; dining seating stays lighter and more wipeable. Haircloth, cane, a slip seat you can recover after the holidays.

## America reads the plate

New York, Salem, Baltimore, Charleston shops make Sheraton-style chairs with local woods and local labor. Montgomery still applies. Southern shops — including shops that used enslaved labor — produced fashionable square-backs for dining rooms whose inventories later said “mahogany chairs” as if mahogany were a maker. Name the shop when you can. Name the labor when the shop is a plantation context.

Phyfe’s early work sits on this line before the Grecian turn. Kenny and Brown will sort the man. This hour only needs the type: square, reeded, a dining chair that wants to look like a drawing and sit like a tool.

## The sit at the grid

Seat is often closer to a rectangle. Front rail is straighter. Thighs meet a bar that is less compass-kind and more Federal-honest. Depth is moderate. Height pairs with the new sideboards and pedestal tables of the same years — sibling pack for those forms.

Because the back is square, stacking is still poor and hanging is still a maybe. These are not Windsors. They live in the room. A Federal dining room that “puts the chairs away” is usually putting them against the wall, which the square back likes. A shield can look wounded against a wall. A square looks like architecture.

Arms: Sheraton armchairs can be quite upright. Good for the host who must rise and carve. Bad if the arms hit a table that gained a skirt in a later marriage of furniture. Always the apron.

## Translation into oak

Reeding on quartersawn oak is a different light than reeding on mahogany. It can be handsome if you stop pretending it is mahogany. A southern shop that cuts a square-back dining chair in white oak, with honest mortises and a cane or leather seat, is making a Sheraton argument in another language. A shop that stains oak red and cuts reeds with a cheap cutter is making a mall argument.

I would rather eat in the oak grid than in a broken-point shield that is “more period.” Period is not a flavor. It is a set of joints and a date.

If the client wants “Sheraton dining chairs,” I ask to see the table legs. If the table is a farm trestle, the reeds may look lonely. If the table is a pedestal with fire, the reeds may look right. Pair first. Buy second.
