The ends of the table still remember rank. Two armchairs, often a little taller, often a little wider, sometimes carved when the sides are plain. Host and hostess is a furniture-store phrase for a leftover inscription. Egypt put a name on a back. A medieval hall put a wainscot at the high table. A Victorian cloth put arms at the ends and sides along the length. We still buy the set that way, even when no one carves and the door is behind everyone.

I do not need the pair. I need to know whether the arms clear and whether the household wants a head.

## What the end chair is for

Rising to serve. Talking down the length. Facing the door, in houses that cared who entered. A slightly more lounge-ish back for the person who stays when others clear. None of that requires a throne. It requires a chair you can get out of without walking on a child, and arms that do not fight the apron.

In some families the “host” chair is just the one that does not wobble. Honesty has a sense of humor.

Etiquette books made a diagram of who sits where. Kasson’s *Rudeness and Civility* is manners, not joinery. If you quote a manual, quote a named one. I will not invent Mrs. Somebody’s rule about the wife’s back to the pantry.

## When the hierarchy is a problem

A wheelchair at an end is often the best place — more room, a view of the table — unless the armchair is bolted to the hierarchy and cannot leave. The accessibility essay takes the rest. A left-handed carver at an end that was drawn for a right-handed show is a small household fact. Children in host chairs because the arms trap them is a common mistake; they need a side and a booster, not a throne.

A table that seats ten with two hosts and eight sides is a rhythm. A table that seats four with two hosts and two sides looks like a court. Scale the rank to the room.

## All sides, all arms, benches

Cafés and Thonet 14s gave everyone the same hoop. Shaker rooms mixed benches and slat-backs without a carved head. Banquettes refuse ends. Islands refuse the whole diagram. Those are later and earlier refusals. A modern oak set of six identical sides is a legitimate choice. You are not required to buy arms to make the table “complete.” Completeness is a catalog.

All arms is a restaurant and a cleaning problem. I have said this. It remains true.

## Shop practice

If we build hosts, they match the sides in seat height and front-rail kindness, and they differ in arms and maybe a little back height. We do not raise the host seat “for importance.” Importance does not help a femur.

A customer who wants the hosts in a different wood is telling a story. I will build it if the heights match. I will not build a lounge at one end and a Federal shield at the other and call it a set.

Bradley Brand does not need a “host collection.” The household needs two chairs that clear, or none.

The leftover inscription is not evil. It is leftover. Use it if you like a head of table. Drop it if you like a hoop for everyone. Measure either way.

## Clearance at the ends is still clearance

The ends of the table often have a different underside — a pedestal spread, a trestle foot, a drawer that only exists at one end, a skirt that returns. Measure the path at the end, not only along the side. A host armchair that clears a side apron can still kiss a pedestal that flares.

Seat height should match the sides. I do not raise the host seat “for importance.” Importance does not help a femur. Front-rail kindness should match. A knife-edge host and a radiused side is a set that punishes the person who stays longest.

Back height may differ. A little more back is a rest. A lot more back is a throne that photographs and then makes the sides look like children. Scale the rank to the room. A table that seats ten with two hosts and eight sides is a rhythm. A table that seats four with two hosts and two sides looks like a court.

Width: a wide host at a later leaf crowds the first side chair. Period ends were often for tables that did not grow a cheap leaf. If the table grew, the hosts may need to be the first things you retire, or the first things you replace with sides.

## Shop: match the sit, differ the job

If we build hosts, they match the sides in seat height and front-rail kindness, and they differ in arms and maybe a little back height. We build the arms to the end’s underside and to the scoot, or we refuse the arms. A customer who wants the hosts in a different wood is telling a story. I will build it if the heights match. I will not build a lounge at one end and a Federal shield at the other and call it a set.

Bradley Brand does not need a “host collection.” The household needs two chairs that clear, or none. If they want a head of table, we can turn a slightly taller back and a cleared arm. If they want a hoop for everyone, we stop at sides.

The leftover inscription is not evil. It is leftover. Use it if you like a head of table. Drop it if you like the same chair all the way around. Measure either way. The door, the platter, and the femur will tell you whether the title was worth the timber.

## Who stays, who serves, who faces the door

I have eaten in a house where the host chair faced the kitchen because the roast required a person who could stand without asking a neighbor to move. I have eaten in a house where the host chair faced the front door because the household still cared who entered. I have eaten in a house where the two arms sat at the ends because the catalog had shipped them that way and no one had ever asked why. Three briefs. One furniture-store name.

Rising to serve is the job that justifies a little extra back and a cleared arm: you push back, you stand, you walk. A throne that traps you is the wrong tool for that job. Talking down the length is a different job: you want a view, not a crest that puts your head in a photograph and your plate in the middle distance. Facing the door is manners and also a sight line; Kasson can own the manners. The chair only has to let you turn.

If no one carves and the door is behind everyone, the pair of arms is optional timber. Keep it if you like a head. Drop it if you like a hoop. Measure either way. A shop ticket that says “host” without an underside number is a title without a sit.
