Arne Jacobsen’s Series 7 — the 3107 in Fritz Hansen’s numbering — is a pressure-molded veneer shell on a chrome or later base, a waist in the middle, a stack when you need the floor. 1955. Thau and Vindum’s monograph and the maker’s records are the trail. The Ant is a cousin with a different cut. Do not mix the names if you are buying.

This is a dining chair that also became a meeting-room chair and a million photographs. I care about the waist against a table and the way the shell holds you without a splat, a hoop, or a Y.

## One piece, a waist

The shell is seat and back in one thought. The waist is where people say it looks like a person. It is also a flex point. You sit, the shell gives, the back comes to you a little. That give is the comfort. A dead copy in thick plastic without the flex is a tray with a pretty outline.

Edges can be sharp if the trim is cheap. A well-made Series 7 has an edge you can live with at the back of the knee. A bad one cuts. Run a hand before you buy six.

The tubular base is four legs, light, a cousin of the DCM stance. It scrapes. It stacks with the shells nested in a way wood posts never will. That stacking is a dining-room fact for small houses: the extra chairs become a column in the corner. The balloon-back never offered that mercy.

## The sit

You are on a curve. There is no pad unless you add one (people add one; it changes height). The back contact is a continuous rise. Some lumbar-minded sitters like the waist. Some find it a poke. There is no knob. There is a size.

Duration: good for a dinner, questionable as an all-day office chair, which did not stop offices. This series stays at the table. If your dinner is two hours and your back wants a tablet, sit in the chair at the table before you order a set.

Arms exist on some versions and change the slide-in. Side chairs are the stackers.

## Pairing and color

Jacobsen’s shells came in colors that dining rooms used as the bright thing. A row of white 7s at a dark oak table is a common truce. A row of colored 7s at a colored table is a cafeteria. Neither is wrong. The apron still matters. The shell’s front lip is the thigh meeting.

A Series 7 at a tulip table is a midcentury conversation. A Series 7 at a farmhouse table is a 2010s catalog. The sit does not care. The photograph does.

## Copies and the waist

The outline is everywhere. Fritz Hansen is one object. The unlicensed forest is another. Flex, edge, and base quality decide the sit. I will not moralize about licenses beyond: pay for the chair you are sitting in.

A wooden shop cannot press a 7 without becoming a plywood shop. What it can steal is the idea of a continuous back-and-seat and the idea of stacking as a dining kindness. A ladderback stacks in a different way. Both are answers to the spare chair.

I like a Series 7 for a weeknight and for the extra guest. I like it less as the only chair in a room that wants oak weight. Mix if the heights match. Mixing without measuring is how one guest sits in a hole.

## The Ant is a different cut

The Ant — Fritz Hansen’s 3100, 1952 in the usual dating — is the cousin people mash into the same listing. It is a narrower shell, a sharper waist, a different back cut. Early Ants stood on three tubes; later Ants grew a fourth. The Series 7 is the wider dining thought: more seat, more back, four legs as a habit. If a seller says “Jacobsen” and shows a skinny three-leg, you are not buying the chair this hour is about. Thau and Vindum keep the models apart. A check should too.

I sat both in the same afternoon at a thin-top oak table. The Ant was a perch with a pretty outline. The 7 was still a perch, but the shell gave me more thigh and a waist that actually found my back. Neither is a Mission slat. Both are one piece of veneer doing the work a splat and a seat rail used to split. The names are not interchangeable at the price of a set of six.

The 3207 and other armed versions change the slide-in. They are the same shell with a different argument under the apron. Side chairs remain the stackers. If you want arms, measure the path with a knife in your hand, the way the arms essay will ask. A 7 that cannot scoot is a meeting-room leftover parked at dinner.

## Allerød and the press

Fritz Hansen’s press is the factory fact. Veneers go into a mold; heat and pressure make a shell that is seat and back without a joint at the waist. Face veneers change the photograph — teak, oak, ash, walnut, a painted lacquer — but the bend is the same idea. I will not print a layer count from memory. `[VERIFY]` against a maker note if an editor wants a number in a caption. What I will say is that a cheap copy often looks like the outline and sits like a cafeteria tray because the laminate is thicker, or the mold is shallower, or the plastic stand-in has no spring.

The chrome four-leg base is a tube story, cousin to the DCM’s stance and cousin to every café that wanted light metal under a wooden room. Later powder-coat and later colors do not change the scrape. Felt glides help a finished floor and die on grit. Plastic glides click. A dining room that keeps the 7s on a pine board will write the tubes into the floor in a season if nobody watches the feet.

The tube meets the shell at a plate or a fitting you can see if you turn the chair over. That meeting is where copies save money. A loose plate ticks through a meal. A cracked fitting is retirement. I look there before I look at the lacquer.

Stacking is a kindness the balloon-back never offered. The shells nest. The bases interleave if you are patient. A column of six in a small apartment is why the type left the meeting room and came to dinner. A column of twelve in a damp garage is a creep test the veneer did not ask for. I would rather see four extras against a wall than a tower that teaches the bottom shell a new shape.

## A dinner in the waist

I ate a two-hour dinner in a white 7 at a dark oak table whose apron was honest and shallow. The shell gave when I sat. The chrome stayed out of my neighbor’s knee. The waist found me in a way a Federal square-back never does. I did not miss a splat. I missed a place to hang a coat; the saber and the balloon-back still win that small job.

The same chair at a tulip table is a midcentury conversation the sibling pack already owns from the table side. The sit does not care about the conversation. The camera does. A row of rust and mustard 7s at a farmhouse table with a thick top and a drawer apron is a catalog page that forgot the gap. The waist will not save a jammed thigh.

The front lip is the thigh meeting. A well-trimmed edge is a radius you can live with at the back of the knee. A badly sanded copy is a paper cut that lasts the roast. I run a finger under that lip the way I run a finger under a wooden front rail. Same nerve, different material.

Pads appear. A thin pad on a 7 is a household edit I have seen in good rooms. It changes the finished height. It also hides the lip. Measure after the pad. A fat pad turns the chair into a different product and then the apron becomes the review. Foam on a flexing shell also changes how the waist comes to you. One variable at a time if you are trying to learn anything.

Duration: a weeknight is this chair’s friend. A wedding supper is a maybe. People who love the 7 for dinner often stand, serve, and sit. People who stay planted through four courses sometimes want a pad or a different back. Offices kept the type for a workday. That is a different duration and a different reach. This series stays at the table.

Children like the light weight and the slide. That is also how a 7 meets a plaster wall. The Windsor was light and painted. The 7 is light and chrome. Same family of problem, different scuff.

## What the shell records

Veneer checks at the edge in a dry house. Lacquer chips at the front lip where plates and belt buckles work. The waist can lose its spring if someone stacked too many in a hot attic. None of that is a style failure. It is use. A shop that “refinishes” a 7 by filling the edge and burying the flex under a heavy film has made a tray with a famous outline.

A wooden shop in south Arkansas cannot press a Series 7 without becoming a plywood shop, and it should not CNC the waist into a slab of oak and call the result a 7. What it can take from Allerød is smaller: a continuous back-and-seat as an idea, a front edge that a thigh can live with, and the admission that spare chairs may live as a column. A ladderback answers storage another way. A rush Hitchcock hangs. The 7 stacks. Those are three answers to the same small-house brief.

If a client brings six 7s and asks for a table, I measure the compressed sit with the pad they actually use, and I get under their present table for the underside number. The brand is not the variable. The pair is. A custom oak table that ignores the shell’s lip and the chrome’s stance will make the famous waist look like a mistake. It will not be the chair’s mistake.

I will not run a corporate history of Fritz Hansen. The dining fact is a pressure-molded shell, a tube base, and a sit that is all curve. If that curve finds you at your table, the rest is provenance money. If it does not, no color will fix it.
