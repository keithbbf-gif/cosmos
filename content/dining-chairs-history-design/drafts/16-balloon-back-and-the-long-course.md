The balloon-back is an O you look through. A rounded open back, often walnut or mahogany, sometimes rosewood in richer rooms; a stuffed seat on coil or webbing; cabriole or turned legs that have learned a later century’s carving. It is the dining chair of a meal that has become a program. Soup, fish, a roast, a sweet, talk that is part of the service. John Kasson’s manners book is not a furniture catalog, but duration is a furniture problem. A balloon-back assumes you will stay.

I do not start with “Victorian excess.” Excess is a later sneer. The hoop is a sitting idea: a continuous curve for the shoulders, air in the middle, a seat that forgives the hours the hoop will not.

## The hoop and the stuffing

Some backs are laminated or built-up curves; some are cut and joined. Look at the break when there is one. Short grain in a cut balloon is the same fear as a saber. A split at four o’clock on the O is a common patient.

The stuffed seat is the nineteenth century’s mercy. Horsehair, then springs, a show cover that dining wrecks. Front rails take the thigh and the gravy. Recovering is expected. A museum-perfect cover on a chair with a wrecked frame is a lie about use. A wrecked cover on a sound frame is a dinner chair that worked.

Compared to a Federal slip seat, you sit higher on the stuffing until it compresses. Pair the recovered height to the table. Victorian tables are often in the same 28-to-30-inch world, but a fat reupholstery can turn a lady’s reach into a child’s. Compress, measure, then recover to a number, not to a plumpness.

## Long course, short lumbar

There is still no office lumbar. There is a hoop that catches you when you finish a sentence and a seat that lets you slump a degree. People who love balloon-backs love that combination. People who hate them feel uncontained — the O does not hold the low back, and the stuffing lets you sink into a C that the next morning will invoice.

A two-hour office sit and a two-hour dinner are not the same. Dinner has courses, standing, passing, wine, a different boredom. The balloon-back is built for the social sit, not the keyboard sit. If you work at a dining table in a balloon-back, you are using a historical chair as a bad task chair. That is your choice. It is not the type’s failure.

## Sets, ends, and the cloth

Victorian dining wants a set. Six, eight, a pair of arms. The cloth hides the table and makes the chairs the vertical show. Balloon-backs photograph as a rhythm of O’s. Servants — in houses that had them — moved behind that rhythm. The hoop is kinder to a sleeve than a pierced Gothic, worse than a solid splat for hanging a coat.

American factories — Grand Rapids later in the century — will press and carve cheaper versions. Belter’s laminated rococo is a cousin in method and a different silhouette; the sibling pack has Belter at the table. This hour stays with the balloon as a dining hoop, including the plain walnut ones that never saw a mansion.

Walnut from Midwestern forests, mahogany that traveled, oak in the later reform years. An Arkansas dining room in 1870 might have mail-order cousins of this chair. I will not invent the catalog page. I will say the type traveled.

## What I look for

A hoop without a glue-starved split. Legs that have not been recut into stilettos. Castors that still make sense on the floor you have (castors on a thick rug change height). A seat that has structure, not only foam. If the webbing is gone and the foam is a slab, you are sitting on a promise.

New “Victorian dining chairs” with a CNC O and a foam pad can be fine if the sit is numbered to the table. They are not 1860. Do not pay 1860 prices for the CNC.

I would eat a long meal in a sound balloon-back with a recovered seat at the right height before I would eat the same meal in a shield-back with a broken point. Mercy has a hoop. The drawing has a point. Dinner, on a long night, votes for mercy.

## How the O is made, and how it fails

I look at a balloon the way I look at a saber: I want to know whether the curve is fiber or a drawing cut from a plank. A built-up or laminated hoop can carry grain around the O. A cut hoop has short grain at the sides. The split at four o’clock — or eight — is the patient I see most. Glue-starved joints in a hoop that was made in pieces open as a hairline first. People stain over the hairline. The next guest leans to pass a dish and the hairline becomes a mouth.

A shop repair that sisters a spline into the O can be honest if you can still see the chair. A repair that fills the split with putty and a dark wipe is a year of dinners at most. I would rather see a clean break and a named mend than a hoop that photographs as “original condition.”

Legs tell the other half. Cabrioles that have been recut into stilettos after a foot rot are a height change and a stance change. Turned legs that have lost their lower beads to a vacuum or a dog are common and livable. Castors that still roll on a hard floor will roll you away from the plate while you cut; castors sunk into a thick rug steal height and then refuse to roll. I treat castors as a dimension, not as jewelry.

## Stuffing is a number

Horsehair over webbing is the older mercy: a firm crown that gives a little and then stops. Coil springs under a dining seat are the later mercy: a bounce, a noise when they fail, a sit that can be too lively if the upholsterer was thinking of a parlor. Foam is the recover default now. Foam is also a moving target. A fat modern slab on a balloon-back can sit you into a child’s reach at a table that was honest in 1870.

I compress the seat with a fist, then with my weight, then I measure floor to compressed sit. I measure again after twenty minutes if I can steal them. The number I want is the number the table will meet, not the plumpness in the photograph. Victorian tables often live in the same 28-to-30-inch world as Federal ones. The chair is what changed. Recover to a finished height. Do not recover to a mood.

Show covers on dining balloons are expected to die. Front rails take gravy and the thigh’s polish. A museum-perfect cover on a wrecked frame is a lie about use. A wrecked cover on a sound frame is a dinner chair that worked. I date the joints. I read the seat as a diary. Haircloth still appears on earlier cousins; floral needlework and later factory cloth are hospitality, not a year stamp.

## The social sit, not the keyboard sit

A long course is a series of small rises: pass, pour, stand for a toast, sit again, lean to hear the person two down. The balloon-back is built for that interrupted stay. The hoop catches you when you finish a sentence. The stuffing lets you slump a degree. The O does not hold the low back. People who love the type love that combination. People who hate it feel uncontained and then invent a lumbar complaint that is really a missing plane.

I will not print a national average of courses. Kasson’s manners book is about duration and civility, not about a count of plates. Duration is the furniture problem. A wedding supper and a Tuesday soup are different clocks in the same hoop. The chair is innocent of the menu. It is guilty of a hoop that does not contain a slump if you stay for the speeches.

Working at a dining table in a balloon-back is using a historical chair as a bad task chair. That is a household choice. It is not the type’s failure. The later essay on lumbar can take the office fight. Here I only need the dinner: you are meant to leave, eventually, for a parlor or a sofa. The specialized dining room and the unstackable chair arrive together. The balloon is a thick, padded version of that occupancy.

Arms on balloon dining chairs are often at a height that looks generous and then fails a later thick top. Measure. Side chairs along the length are the workhorses. The end chairs are rank if the household still wants a head.

## What I will eat in

A hoop without a glue-starved split. Legs that still have their feet. A seat with webbing or springs or a specified foam, not a slab on a rumor. Castors that match the floor you have, or feet that replaced them on purpose. Height paired after the recover.

New “Victorian dining chairs” with a CNC O and a foam pad can be fine if the sit is numbered to the table. They are not 1860. Do not pay 1860 prices for the CNC. A shop that wants the mercy of a hoop in oak can build an honest O with laminated stock and a leather or cloth seat at a stated height. That is a balloon argument in another wood. A shop that bandsaws the O and leaves the short grain for the holiday lean is making the saber’s mistake in a kinder outline.

I have sat a long night in a recovered walnut balloon at a table that fit and understood why the century kept the hoop. I have sat forty minutes in a plump revival at a thick farm apron and understood nothing except the gap. Mercy is a hoop plus a number. The drawing alone will not feed you.
