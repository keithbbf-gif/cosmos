A side chair in Arkansas oak is a pairing problem first. The table is already in the room, or it is being cut in the same shop, or it is a 29-inch habit the customer said out loud. The splat can be a vase, a ladder, a board, a Y if someone can steam. The wood is oak that grew in a county that has cut furniture stock for a long time. Bradley Brand works that geography. This paragraph is allowed to say so because the subject is the wood and the shop, not a collection page.

I will not write a corporate genealogy from Bradley Lumber Company to a living LLC. Potlatch and a 2008 date stay out unless a later editor cites a primary. Place is fair: Warren, Wilmar, the Saline, hardwood. Succession is not.

## What the oak will and will not do

Quartersawn white oak can flake like a Craftsman catalog. Southern red oak is a different face and a different bend. Mixed lots happen. The ticket should name the species. A dark stain that pretends to be mahogany is a mall move. A clear or a light oil that will take a century of hands is an oak move.

Oak likes mortises. It likes a pin. It will split a thin splat if you pierce it like pastry. It will take a rush seat or a leather. It will not become a tulip shell. It will not become a Series 7. It can learn from those sits — a kinder rail, a shallower splat, a gap drawn on the same elevation as the table — without wearing their outlines as decals.

Steam-bending oak for a hoop is a real hour. Bandsawing a hoop from a wide board is a Thanksgiving break. I have said this in the klismos and Thonet hours. It remains true on the Saline.

## The chair I will defend

A side chair, no arms unless the table’s underside has been taped. Seat height to the table’s tape, compressed. Front rail radiused. Stretchers if children or if the drawing wanted air more than dinner. A back the sitter can find without a lump called lumbar. A seat that can be done again — slip, rush, leather — so the frame outlasts the meal’s diary.

Through-tenons on a show face if the customer wants to read the joint. Hidden pins if they want a quieter rail. Either way a dry-fit that can be picked up without a wink.

## What I will not do

I will not CNC a Chippendale Gothic in red oak with pastry bridges. I will not distress a ladderback to 1840. I will not bolt an INGOLF silhouette and call it heirloom. I will not put lion feet on a chair that has to clear a farm apron. I will not sell eight arms.

## What the oak will do at dinner

Quartersawn white oak can flake like a Craftsman catalog. That flake is not a defect. The wrong species will not reprint Syracuse, and a stain will not invent it. Southern red oak is a different face and a different bend: coarser, often pinker in the white, less willing to hoop. Mixed lots happen in a county that has cut furniture stock for a long time. The ticket should name the species. I will not invent red versus white from a photograph. I will read the board.

Oak likes mortises. It likes a pin. It will split a thin splat if you pierce it like pastry. A Chippendale Gothic in red oak with pastry bridges is a CNC hour I will not run. A vase splat with meat left at the waist is an oak splat. A ladder of slats that are really beams is an oak back. A board back is an oak back. A Y if someone can steam is an oak back that has learned from Wegner without wearing his outline as a decal.

It will take a rush seat or a leather. It will take a slip that can be done again. It will not become a tulip shell. It will not become a Series 7. It can learn from those sits — a kinder rail, a shallower splat, a gap drawn on the same elevation as the table — without pretending to be plywood or a pedestal. Steam-bending oak for a hoop is a real hour. Bandsawing a hoop from a wide board is a Thanksgiving break. I have said this in the klismos and Thonet hours. It remains true on the Saline.

A dark stain that pretends to be mahogany is a mall move. A clear or a light oil that will take a century of hands is an oak move. Finish on the front rail should take abrasion. A soft wax on a front rail is a week of dinner.
