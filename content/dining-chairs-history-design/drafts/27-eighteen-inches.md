Eighteen inches is a camp, not a shrine. I start there because a lot of American tables are sold as 29 or 30 inches to the top, and a lot of chairs that work with those tables land a finished seat in the 17-to-19-inch band. The camp is a pairing habit. It is not BIFMA. It is not ISO 9241. It is not a 5th-percentile female popliteal height copied out of an office guide and nailed to a dining room.

Stephen Pheasant’s *Bodyspace* — the anthropometrist — treats popliteal height as the bone of seat height: the seat should let the feet meet the floor (or a footrest) without a dead-leg. That is true at dinner. It is also true that dinner seats a mixed table. You do not get a lever under each cousin. You get one height and a set of bodies. The camp is a compromise that fails softly for a lot of adults in shoes, at a table in that 29-to-30 band, for the length of a meal rather than a shift.

I measure from the finished floor to the compressed sit. Not to the rail. Not to the catalog.

## What steals the number

A pad that is two inches in the shop is not two inches after an hour. Foam dies. Down flattens. A “18-inch chair” with a new pillow is a 20-inch chair until Thanksgiving, then something else.

A rug is a height. A pad plus a rug is a stacking error. Castors on a hard floor versus a pile rug are a height. Recut feet on an antique — to match a later table, or to replace rot — are a height you will not find in the style book.

Upholstered dining chairs lie more than rush. Rush compresses less in the first sitting and more over years. Cane is a drum until it is a hammock. Leather on webbing has its own drop. Measure the sit you will sell, or the sit you will eat in, in the condition it will actually have.

Shoes matter less at dinner than in an office study that assumes a certain heel. Barefoot summer and winter boots still change a short person’s floor contact. I do not design a dining chair for a spike heel. I design it so a bare foot and a street shoe both have a chance.

## Tables that were not sold as thirty

Islands at 36 inches want a different seat — a stool hour, not this one. Coffee tables want a lounge. Antique tables wander. A drop-leaf that sits at 28 inches wants a lower chair or a thinner pad. People who say “old chairs are uncomfortable” are often in a correct old chair at a new thick-top table, or in a recut chair at an original table. Measure both.

European 75-centimeter tables and American inch tables are in the same neighborhood and not identical. If you import a chair, import the tape.

Children’s booster seats are a confession that the camp is for adults. The children essay takes the rest.

## Percentiles are not a guest list

Office furniture likes 5th-percentile female to 95th-percentile male, often from military or CAESAR data, for a workstation that one person owns. Dinner is a shared object. If you build for the tallest femur, short sitters dangle and go numb. If you build for the shortest popliteal, tall sitters fold and hit the apron. The camp in the high 17s to 18s with a 29-to-30 table is a historical average of shops that had to pick. It is not a kindness to every body. Footrests, a thinner pad for a short sitter, a higher chair for a long-femur host — those are household edits. A single SKU will not do them.

I will not paste Allsteel’s office tables into this paragraph as dining law. Openshaw and Taylor are clear they are talking about work. BIFMA X10.1-2024 is explicit about computer-use furniture. You may borrow the idea of fit. You may not borrow the numbers as if they were a dining code.

## Shop practice

Mock the height. Plywood on sawhorses for the table, a shop stool or a stack of plywood for the seat, a real person, a plate. If the shoulders climb, the seat is low or the table is high. If the thighs jam, the next essay — the gap — is the problem, not the 18. If the feet dangle and go tingling, the seat is high.

In a Wilmar shop I will not publish a house standard until someone writes it down. `[VERIFY]`. I will publish the method: finished, compressed, paired, with the floor covering named.

Arkansas oak chairs for a customer who already owns a table should be sold to that table’s tape, not to a catalog camp. That is the only “custom” that matters on height.

Eighteen inches is where I start when there is no table yet and the customer says “normal dining.” Then I say the sentence about pads and rugs, and I watch their face. If they already bought a 3-inch cushion online, we are not in the camp anymore. We are in arithmetic.

## How I put the tape down

The number is finished floor to compressed sit. I put a straightedge across the seat where the sit bones will be, not on a crowned front rail, not on a pillow’s peak. I sit, or I have the person who will eat sit, and I measure again after a minute. Foam dies in that minute. Down dies faster. Rush moves less at first and more over years.

I name the floor covering on the ticket. Bare oak is one height. A wool rug with a pad is another. Castors on a hard floor versus a pile rug are a third. If the chairs will live on a rug that is not in the shop, I add the rug’s thickness to the problem or I take a chair to the house. A number without a floor is a catalog.

I do not measure to the rail and then “add an inch for the cushion.” That inch is a wish. I measure the sit I will sell. If the cushion is not built yet, I mock it with the foam we will actually use and I sit it. Stephen Pheasant’s popliteal height is the bone of this measurement: the seat should let the feet meet the floor, or a footrest, without a dead-leg. That is true at dinner. It is also true that dinner seats a mixed table. One height. Several femurs. The tape does not vote. It reports.

A 5th-percentile female popliteal height copied out of an office guide is not a dining room. Openshaw and Taylor are talking about a workstation one person owns. BIFMA X10.1-2024 is talking about computer-use furniture. You may borrow the idea of fit. You may not nail a percentile to a Sunday roast.

## Older chairs that wander

Period seats wander. A Queen Anne compass with a thin slip seat can land under the camp. A stuffed balloon-back can land over it until the horsehair settles. Windsors vary with the saddle and with later recuts. Mission seats are often honest planks in the high 17s. Fancy chairs and Hitchcocks can be a little low by later thick-top tables. I will not print a style as a height. I will print: measure the object in front of you.

Recut feet are a height you will not find in the style book. Rot at the foot, a later floor, a desire to match a new table — shops have been sawing an inch off rear posts since there were posts. The rake changes when you cut the back legs and not the front. The sit height changes either way. A chair that “feels wrong” in a house museum is sometimes a chair that was tipped for a different floor.

European 75-centimeter tables and American inch tables are in the same neighborhood and not identical. Import the tape with the chair. A Danish shell that was happy at a thin-top table will dangle or jam at a 2-inch oak slab with a breadboard.

Islands at 36 inches are a stool hour. Coffee tables are a lounge. A drop-leaf that sits at 28 inches wants a lower chair or a thinner pad. People who say “old chairs are uncomfortable” are often in a correct old chair at a new thick-top table, or in a recut chair at an original table. Measure both.

Children’s boosters are a confession that the camp is for adults. The children essay takes the rest. A household that dines with a toddler and a long-femur guest is already running two heights at one cloth. The camp will not save that. A footrest, a thinner pad, a different chair at one place — those are household edits.

## When the camp fails

Short sitters dangle and go numb if you build for the tallest femur. Tall sitters fold and hit the apron if you build for the shortest popliteal. The camp in the high 17s to 18s with a 29-to-30 table is a historical average of shops that had to pick. It fails softly for a lot of adults in shoes. It fails hard for some bodies. Soft failure is tingling that arrives with dessert. Hard failure is a person who will not sit through the roast.

A seat that is high turns the front rail into a bar. The thigh essay and this one share that patient. A seat that is low makes the shoulders climb toward the plate. People call the low seat “childish” and add a cushion, then they steal the gap. Fix the pair. Do not stack sins.

Shoes matter less at dinner than in an office study that assumes a heel. Barefoot summer and winter boots still change a short person’s floor contact. I do not design a dining chair for a spike heel. I design it so a bare foot and a street shoe both have a chance.

I have sat a “18-inch” catalog chair that measured 19 and a quarter after a new pillow and a rug. The host said the chairs had always been fine. The table had always been 30. The rug was last winter’s. Arithmetic.

## The chair that went to the rug

A shop floor is a liar if the chairs will live on a wool rug with a pad. I have taken a finished oak side out of the shop and set it on the client’s floor covering and watched eighteen become seventeen and a half. The table had not moved. The camp had not moved. The pile had.

Recut antiques lie in the other direction. A set that was sawn down to meet a later thick-top table will measure “correct” in a catalog photograph and then dangle at the original table if the pair is ever reunited. I look at the feet for a later saw cut, a different finish at the tip, a caster that was not born with the chair. The rake essay owns the angle that cut also changed. This hour owns the height it stole or added.

If their face tells me they already bought a 3-inch cushion online, we are in arithmetic. We are not in a shrine. I start at eighteen only when there is no table yet and they say “normal dining.” Then I say the sentence about pads and rugs, and I watch whether they already spent the inch.
