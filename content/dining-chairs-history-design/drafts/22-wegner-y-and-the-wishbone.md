Hans Wegner said he was in search of one good chair, and then he made more than one. The Wishbone — CH24, 1949, the Y-back everyone can outline in the air — is the dining chair of that search that most people can name. Christian Holmsted Olesen’s Designmuseum book is the monograph trail. Carl Hansen’s production is the long factory afterlife. I want the sit: a steam-bent hoop, a forked splat that is actually a wishbone of wood, a paper-cord seat that will record every meal.

The sibling pack’s Danish modern essay owns the American import mood. This hour is the Y at the plate.

## The Y is a splat that learned steam

The back is a bent top rail and a forked member that meets it. You see through. You also get a contact that is a hoop plus a fork, not a tablet, not a shell. Some spines love the air. Some never find a place to rest and call the chair hard. A thin cushion on a Wishbone is a common household edit. It changes the height. Measure.

Steam-bent oak, beech, or the later species list — read the chair you have. The bend is the Thonet lesson in a Danish shop language. A crack at the bend is a specialist. Do not stain over a split and sell “character.”

The front legs and the back posts are a chair in the old sense: a box that can rack. Wegner’s joints are the pride. Copies hide them. Look under. Look at the shoulder.

## Paper cord is a rush cousin

Paper cord — twisted paper, woven — is the seat that made the type famous in a certain finish. It is not indestructible. It abrades where jeans and the front rail meet. It stains. It can be rewoven. A sagging cord is a sit that has gone hammock, like rush, like cane. People who buy a Wishbone for “the look” and then put a plywood pad on it have bought a sculpture.

The weave pattern is part of the sit: a slight give, a texture, a coolness compared with foam. I like it for dinner. I do not like it for a toddler with a fork and a berry.

Height of the cord seat is a dining band. Pair the table. A thick Danish tabletop with a deep apron will still find a thigh. The Y does not repeal the gap.

## Arms that are the hoop

The Wishbone’s “arms” are not host-chair arms. They are the ends of the bent rail, a place for hands, a little width. They can fight a crowded table and a thick skirt. They are also why people feel the chair is friendly: you have somewhere to put a hand when you talk. Side-chair versions and other Wegner dining chairs exist without that. Know the model.

I would not call this an armchair in the Phyfe sense. I would call it a side chair that grew handles.

## Copies, oak, and the one good joint

The silhouette is stolen more than almost any postwar chair except the 14 and the plastic stacker. Some copies sit decently. Many have a Y that is a flat cutout and a cord that is a nylon. The sit tells. So does the underside.

An Arkansas oak shop asked to “do a Wishbone” should ask whether they can steam a rail and weave a seat. If not, they should build a different good chair and stop chasing a Danish shadow. Quartersawn oak can make a Y. Steam and cord are the hours.

I have sat in a real CH24 at a too-thick table and hated the apron, and sat in one at a thin-top oak table and understood the fame. The chair is not a magic spine. It is a specific contact. Pair it.

Wegner’s other dining chairs — the many — deserve their own hours in a later pack if anyone wants them. This series only needs the Y as the type that taught a generation that a dining chair could be joinery you look at while you pass the salt.
