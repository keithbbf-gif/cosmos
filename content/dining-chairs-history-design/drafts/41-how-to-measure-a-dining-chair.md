Bring a tape. Bring the pad you will actually use. Get under the table. Sit. Pull in. None of this is advanced. Most of what goes wrong is a photograph and a number that meant the rail, not the sit.

I measure five things on a chair and two on a table, then I measure the pair.

## On the chair

**A. Finished seat height.** Floor to the sit after you compress the pad with a fist, or after you sit and have someone else read the tape. Bare floor or the rug that will live there. Say which.

**B. Seat depth.** Back contact to front edge, along the sit. Note a compass curve. Note a dish.

**C. Front edge.** Sharp or radiused. Write a word if you do not have a radius gauge.

**D. Arm height and width** if arms exist. Floor to top of arm, and the inside width that has to pass the table.

**E. Overall back height** if you care about the photograph or a window sill. It is the least important number for the meal.

Optional: rake, if you have a way to see it. A photo from the side against a plumb door jamb is enough.

## On the table

**F. Top height.** Finished floor to show surface.

**G. Underside height.** Finished floor to the lowest thing a thigh or a knee will meet: apron, drawer, slide, underside of a thick top.

Write G minus A. That remainder is the gap. If it is a smear of air, walk away or change a pad.

Also write the apron’s drop from the top (F minus G, roughly, if the top thickness is the only difference). That drop is the thief.

## The path

Sit. Pull in as if to cut. Watch the arm. Watch the cabriole knee. Watch the saber. Watch the pedestal foot. A parked measurement is a liar.

If a wheelchair is part of the meal, measure the cave with that chair, not with your shin.

## What catalogs give you

Overall height, sometimes a seat height that meant the rail, a width, a depth that meant the back of the rear leg to the front of the knee. Pretty. Insufficient.

Auction listings are worse. Museum labels are better when they give seat height; they still may not give you the table.

## Shop ticket

When we build, these numbers go on the ticket with the floor covering named. When we do not build the table, the customer’s underside photo with a tape is a required snapshot, not a niceness.

I do not need a laser. I need honesty about the pad and the rug.

If you only do this once, do it before you buy six of anything, including a box from a warehouse and including a custom oak. The wood does not forgive a bad pair. The foam will pretend to, then die.

## Finished means compressed, and the floor is a height

I have watched people measure a rail and call it a seat. The rail is a number. The sit is another number. Foam, down, a rush that has sagged, a cane that has hammocked, a leather on webbing — each of those is a different sit on the same rail. Compress the pad with a fist, or sit and have someone else read the tape. Write which method you used. Write whether the floor was bare or the rug that will live there. A rug is a height. A pad plus a rug is a stacking error. Castors on a hard floor versus a pile rug are a height. Recut feet on an antique are a height you will not find in the style book.

Eighteen inches is a camp, not a shrine. The height essay already took that fight. This hour only needs the tape on the object in the room. If the catalog said 18 and the compressed sit on your rug is 19 and a bit, you are not in the camp anymore. You are in arithmetic. The wood will not forgive the arithmetic. The foam will pretend to, then die.

Seat depth is back contact to front edge, along the sit. A compass curve is not a straight depth; note the curve. A Windsor saddle is a dish; note the dish. A flat INGOLF plank is a number you can trust until you add a pad. A balloon-back with a sprung seat is a number that will change as the springs tire. Write the number you have today and a word about what it will do.

Front edge: sharp or radiused. Write a word if you do not have a radius gauge. The thigh will write a longer word later if you skip this. Haircloth splits there. Leather wears there. A knife-edge “period profile” is a drawing that forgot a body.

## The table’s two numbers, and the remainder

Top height is finished floor to show surface. Underside height is finished floor to the lowest thing a thigh or a knee will meet: apron, drawer, slide, underside of a thick top. Write underside minus finished seat. That remainder is the gap. If it is a smear of air, walk away or change a pad. The gap essay owns the complaint. This hour owns the subtraction.

Also write the apron’s drop from the top — top height minus underside, roughly, if the top thickness is the only difference. That drop is the thief. A thick top with a shallow apron can still steal. A thin top with a deep drawer apron will steal more. I have measured farm tables whose show surface was 30 inches and whose underside was a drawer that left no thigh. The catalog would have sold you the 30.

If you do not own the table yet, measure a table of the same underside height. Stores hate this. Do it. A parked measurement of a chair in a showroom, with no table, is a liar.

## The path of the scoot

Sit. Pull in as if to cut. Watch the arm. Watch the cabriole knee. Watch the saber. Watch the pedestal foot. A measurement taken with the chair parked six inches out is a measurement of a photograph. Dinner happens at the plate.

Arm height and width if arms exist: floor to top of arm, and the inside width that has to pass the table. The arms essay already took clearance. This hour only needs those two numbers on the ticket. A host armchair whose arms clear a 28-inch apron will not clear a 30-inch table with a thick top and a drawer. Measure the table you will use.

If a wheelchair is part of the meal, measure the cave with that chair, not with your shin. Underside height, width, depth, nothing in the knee path. The access essay owns the requirement. This hour owns the tape on the underside.

Optional: rake, if you have a way to see it. A photo from the side against a plumb door jamb is enough. Overall back height if you care about a window sill or a photograph. It is the least important number for the meal. I still write it so no one is surprised when the splat hits a sill.

## What catalogs and listings give you

Overall height, sometimes a seat height that meant the rail, a width, a depth that meant the back of the rear leg to the front of the knee. Pretty. Insufficient. Auction listings are worse: a style name, a period guess, a photograph of the splat. Museum labels are better when they give seat height; they still may not give you the table. IKEA will give you a diagram that is closer to honest than a lot of auction houses, and still will not give you your apron.

A southern oak new chair should be sold to the table’s tape, not to a catalog camp. That is the only custom that matters on height. When we build, these numbers go on the ticket with the floor covering named. When we do not build the table, the customer’s underside photo with a tape is a required snapshot, not a niceness. I do not need a laser. I need honesty about the pad and the rug.

## Failure cases

Measuring the rail and ordering six. Measuring on a bare shop floor and delivering onto a thick rug. Measuring a new foam and forgetting it will die. Measuring a chair parked out from the table and missing the cabriole that hits the apron when you pull in. Trusting an auction “seat height” that meant overall minus a guess. Trusting a catalog depth that included the rear leg’s rake. Skipping the underside because the top was 30 inches and that sounded like dinner.

I have cut a chair to a number a customer read off a website. The website meant the rail. The pad was two inches. The rug was another. The apron was a drawer. The pair failed before the finish dried. Now I ask for the underside photo. If they cannot get under the table, I ask them to send someone who can. If they will not, I will not cut six.

If you only do this once, do it before you buy six of anything, including a box from a warehouse and including a custom oak. Five numbers on the chair, two on the table, the path of the scoot. That is enough to keep you from buying a drawing.
