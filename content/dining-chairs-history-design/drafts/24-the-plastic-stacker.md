The plastic stacker is a shell you can lift with two fingers and a column you can build in a closet. Robin Day’s Polypropylene chair for Hille is a named early type; there are others. `[CITE NEEDED]` before a “first” year in a caption. I care less about the patent race than about the sit: a slick seat, a back that flexes if the plastic is honest, four legs or a wire base, a chair that does not care about your mahogany story.

Cafeterias, churches, rentals, and then the home extra chair. The matched set of eight balloon-backs is a possession. The stacker is a capacity.

## What plastic does to dinner

It wipes. That is the gravy argument won. It also sweats in a humid room and cracks in a cold truck. UV eats outdoor cousins. Indoor polypropylene still fatigues at the boss where the leg meets the shell. A cracked boss is the retirement.

The sit is often shallow and slick. You slide forward unless the shell has a dish. Cheap stackers are trays. Better ones have a curve you can stay in. None of them are haircloth. None of them are rush. They are the opposite of a document. They are replaceable, which is a moral some houses want and some houses hate.

Stacking puts weight on the shell below. A stack of twelve in a garage in August is a creep test. I would not make that stack the heirloom plan.

## Dining rooms after the set

Open plans and small rooms needed chairs that leave. The stacker is the honest extra. Using stackers as the only dining chairs is a different aesthetic: cafeteria at home. That can be a choice. It is not a failure if the heights match the table. It is a failure if the table is a 30-inch oak and the stackers are a 16-inch seat from a kids’ set, or a 19-inch seat from a conference room.

Institutional grades and backyard grades get mixed in garage sales. Sit before you buy the set of six from the listing. Flex the back. Look at the boss.

## The body

Short duration is fine. Long duration depends on the dish of the shell. Arms are rare; when they exist they ruin the stack. Side chairs only, as a type.

Children like them because they are light and they slide. That is also how they crash into walls. The Windsor was light and painted. The stacker is light and anonymous. Same family of problem.

A wheelchair user at a table surrounded by stackers at least has a chance to move a chair with one hand. That is not accessibility as a program — later essay — but it is a real difference from a Mission post.

## What a wood shop should learn

Not how to inject polypropylene. How to admit that storage is part of dining. A wooden chair that hangs, or stacks in a limited way, or is light enough to carry four, is answering the stacker without becoming one. Thonet knew. Windsor knew. A heavy oak throne does not know.

I will not sneer at a decent stacker at a weeknight table. I will sneer at a stacker that pretends to be a Wishbone with a decal. The plastic should be plastic. The oak should be oak.

If the client wants “something we can put away,” I ask how many, how often, and whether they will actually stack them. Most people will not stack eight after every meal. They will leave them out and hate the look. Then they wanted a different chair.

## Named shells, not a first

Robin Day’s Polypropylene for Hille is the named early type I will put on a caption if a museum record is in hand. The usual dating is 1963. `[VERIFY]` against a Hille or museum object note before a “first” year. Injection-molded polypropylene, a steel frame, a shell that could be a product instead of a carver’s afternoon. That is the factory fact. The patent race is a different essay and I will not invent a winner.

Other shells arrived in the same decades. Magistretti’s Selene, Colombo’s Universale, later contract stacks with names that change by catalog — all-plastic, glass-filled, a sled base, a four-leg tube. The Panton cantilever is a cousin that stacks in a different geometry; do not file it here as a cafeteria four-leg. I care less about the family tree than about the sit: a slick seat, a back that flexes if the plastic is honest, a boss where metal meets polymer.

Institutional grades and backyard grades share a silhouette and not a life. A church basement chair and a “dining” stacker from a patio listing can look like siblings in a photograph. Sit. Flex the back. Look at the boss. The contract chair often has a thicker wall and a metal frame that was meant to be dragged. The backyard cousin often has UV chalk and a hairline at the screw.

Hille’s own later production and the unlicensed forest are two objects. I will not moralize about licenses beyond: pay for the chair you are sitting in, and do not call a brittle copy a Day.

## The boss is the joint

Wood chairs fail at mortises and at stretchers. Plastic stackers fail at the boss — the thickened well where a screw or a rivet ties the shell to the frame. That is the joint. Fatigue lives there. A cracked boss is the retirement, even if the outline still photographs.

I turn the chair over. I look for whitening in the plastic, a split that starts at a hole, a screw that has been replaced with a longer one that is eating a new path. People “fix” a wobbly stacker by overtightening. The polymer creeps. Then the hole is oval and the dinner ticks.

Stacking lugs and ribs are the other factory thought. They keep a column from welding itself into a single sculpture in August. Cheap molds skip them or put them where they print through to the sit. You feel a rib as a line under a thin dress. You feel a missing lug when the stack leans.

A gas-assist or a later thin-wall trick can make a shell that flexes nicely and also makes a shell that oil-cans. I do not diagnose a mold from a garage-sale chair. I sit, I flex, I look at the boss.

## A sit that wipes, and a sit that slides

The gravy argument is real. A polypropylene seat wipes. Haircloth does not. Rush does not. That is why cafeterias and then kitchens wanted the type. It is also why the sit is often slick. You slide forward unless the shell has a dish. Cheap stackers are trays. Better ones have a curve you can stay in. None of them are a document. They are replaceable, which is a moral some houses want and some houses hate.

Sweat in a humid room is the other plastic fact. A July dinner in a closed dining room will glue a bare thigh to the shell. A pad appears. The pad steals height and then slides off because nothing on the slick seat holds it. A tied-on cushion is household history. Measure after it.

The back flex, when the plastic is honest, is the comfort. You sit, the shell comes to you a little, you stay. A dead copy in thick plastic is a tray with a pretty outline — the same sentence I used for a bad Series 7, and it belongs here too. The material is different. The failure is the same: outline without spring.

Duration: short is fine. Two hours depends on the dish and on whether your feet reach. A 16-inch kids’ stacker at a 30-inch oak is a dangling confession. A 19-inch conference shell at the same table is a thigh tax. Institutional and home heights get mixed in the same listing. The tape is the grown-up.

Children like them because they are light and they slide. That is also how they crash into walls. A wheelchair user at a table of stackers can move a chair with one hand. That is not a program. It is a real difference from a Mission post.

## Columns, closets, and the dinner that stays out

Stacking puts weight on the shell below. A stack of twelve in a garage in August is a creep test. A stack of six in a coat closet is the honest extra. I would not make the tall stack the heirloom plan. I would also not sneer at a decent stacker at a weeknight table.

Open plans and small rooms needed chairs that leave. The matched set of eight balloon-backs is a possession. The stacker is a capacity. Using stackers as the only dining chairs is a cafeteria-at-home choice. That can be a choice. It is a failure if the heights do not match the table.

If you will not stack, you wanted a different chair — a Windsor that hangs, a ladderback that lines a wall, a Thonet that is light enough to carry four. Storage is part of dining. The stacker is only one answer.

A wood shop should not inject polypropylene. It should admit the brief. A wooden chair that hangs, or stacks in a limited way, or can be carried two in each hand, is answering the stacker without becoming one. Thonet knew. Windsor knew. A heavy oak throne does not know.

I will sneer at a stacker that pretends to be a Wishbone with a decal. The plastic should be plastic. The oak should be oak. If the outline is Day’s, say Day. If the outline is a cafeteria, say cafeteria. Dinner will find the boss either way.
