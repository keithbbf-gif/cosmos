“Does it have lumbar support?” is a sentence from a showroom that sells mesh. At a dining table it usually means: will my low back hurt after the roast. That is a fair fear. The word is still the wrong tool. Lumbar, in the office literature, is a shaped contact you can move, often a pad or a shell bulge with a height knob, designed for a person who will sit through a shift in one chair. Etienne Grandjean’s task-fitting book and ISO 9241-5 are that world. BIFMA’s computer-use standard is that world.

A dining chair offers a splat, a hoop, a row of slats, a tablet, a Y, a cane plane, a molded waist. You meet it where it is. You leave when the plates leave, or you should. If you do not — if the dining table is your desk — you have a furniture problem this series named in the opener and will not solve with a knob glued to a vase splat.

## What a dining back actually does

It keeps you from falling backward. It gives you a rest between bites. It sometimes hits a useful band of the pelvis or the mid-back. It is not a treatment plan.

People with a back that needs a specific contact should sit in the chair at the table for a long hour before they buy six. They should not trust a tag that says “ergonomic dining.” That tag is often a foam bump. Foam bumps move when they compress. Then they are a lump in the wrong place.

A firm slat at the right height can be better than a soft lump at the wrong one. A hoop that catches the shoulder blades can be enough if the seat height and the gap are right. A lot of “back pain at dinner” is feet dangling, thighs jammed, or a rake that makes you crunch forward — the last three essays — blamed on the absence of a knob.

## Historical backs, named as contacts

Vase splat: narrow band. Pierced splat: less wood, more snag. Shield: rim plus picture. Tablet: a horizontal bar. Windsor sticks: a grate. Rush/cane: little back, more seat. Balloon hoop: shoulders. Mission slats: a wall. Shell: a curve. Wishbone: air plus fork.

None of these is “lumbar support” in the catalog sense. All of them can be kind or cruel. Kindness is placement and duration, not a feature name.

## Duration again

A meal that lasts forty minutes forgives a stern splat. A wedding supper will not. People who love hard chairs for dinner often stand, serve, and sit. People who sit through four courses in a Federal shield without a break are the ones who write reviews.

If your household’s dinner is a two-hour talk, bias toward a back you can find without a crunch, a seat that does not tingle, and a gap that lets you change a thigh position. That is dining ergonomics. It is not a mesh chair in oak clothing.

I will not tell you to buy an office chair for Thanksgiving. I have seen it. The arms never clear. The wheels eat the rug. The look is a surrender. Fix the pair instead.

## Shop practice

We do not advertise lumbar. We ask how long they sit and whether anyone at the table has a specific medical need. A medical need is a conversation with a person, not a bump we CNC into every splat. We can place a slat. We can change a tablet height on a new chair. We cannot invent a knob that belongs on another product.

A pillow tied on is honest. A carved “ergonomic wave” on a Queen Anne copy is not.

## Chalk, a shirt, a splat

I sit in the chair at the table with a plate. After ten minutes I reach back and feel where the wood actually lands. Chalk on a shirt will print the band. A vase splat prints a narrow stripe. A shield often prints the rim and never the picture. A tablet prints a bar. A Mission wall prints a rectangle. A shell prints a comma. I write the height of that print from the compressed seat. That number is the contact. It is not a lumbar specification.

If the print is a poke, we can move a slat on a new chair. We cannot move a period vase without making a different object. A foam bump sold as “ergonomic dining” prints in one place on Tuesday and in another on Sunday once it has compressed. Then it is a lump. A pillow tied on is honest because you can move it or take it off.

Stephen Pheasant treats posture as a system with the table. That is the useful sentence from the anthropometry. The unuseful sentence is any catalog line that treats “lumbar” as a dining option package. Thomas Pheasant the furniture designer is a different person. Do not file the anthropometrist under Baker. Grandjean and ISO 9241-5 still belong at the workstation. BIFMA’s computer-use standard still belongs there.

## Shop: place a slat, refuse a wave

A CNC ripple on a new oak splat is a wave we will not cut unless a named sitter sat the mock and asked for that contact. Even then I would rather move a slat than carve a slogan into the wood. If we are copying a period back, we copy the contact the period back actually offers — a vase band, a tablet, a grate — and we let the sitter decide after an hour. We do not “improve” a shield with a foam kidney. The shield is a picture. The sit is the rim. Improving the picture does not move the rim.

If six sitters chalk six different heights, you are making a dining chair, not a task chair. Pick a band that fails softly. Spend the kindness on height and gap and rail. Let the back be a rest between bites. The knob can stay at the office.

## Failure that is not the splat

I keep being asked to “add lumbar” to chairs whose sitters cannot find the floor. Dangling feet make a person slump. The slump makes the vase splat a poke. A knob would not have found the floor. The height essay would have. I keep being asked the same of chairs whose thighs are jammed. The crunch forward is not a missing pad. It is a missing gap. Fix the pair first. Then see whether the back contact is the rest of the problem.

An office chair at Thanksgiving is a failure I have sat through. The arms never cleared. The wheels ate the rug. The recline made the gravy a reach. The look was a surrender. The household had bought a word. They had not bought a pair.

A medical need is still a conversation with a person. We can place a slat. We can change a tablet height on a new chair. We cannot invent a mechanism that belongs on another product and glue it to a vase. If they need that mechanism, they need a different chair for the hours that are not dinner — and a dining chair that does not fight them for the hours that are.

People who love hard chairs for dinner often stand, serve, and sit. People who stay planted through four courses in a Federal shield without a break are the ones who write reviews. If the household’s dinner is a two-hour talk, bias toward a back you can find without a crunch. That is sitting. It is not a mesh chair in oak clothing.

## An hour against named backs

I sat a pierced Chippendale splat that snagged a sweater and never found my low back. The contact was mid-spine, a pretty hole, a dinner that asked me to hold myself up. I sat a Mission slat wall that found me immediately and then refused to let me change my mind. I sat a Jacobsen waist that came to me when I sat down and left me alone when I leaned to pour. Those are three contacts. None of them is a knob. All of them lasted or failed as a duration, not as a feature.

A tablet back on a Grecian chair is a bar. If the bar is at your shoulder blades, you have a rest. If the bar is at the wrong band, you have a poke. Moving the tablet on a new chair is a shop hour. Moving the tablet on a period chair is a different object. I will not “improve” a museum copy with a second rail and call it kindness.

The long-dinner essay will take the wedding supper. This hour only needs the sentence: if you will sit two hours, sit the back at the table for one hour before you buy six. Bring the clothes you will wear. Bring the shoes. The mesh showroom will not run that test for you.
