The sella folds. That is the Roman sentence I trust. Crossed legs, a seat of leather or fabric or a slat, a magistrate’s sign that can travel and still be a seat. Ole Wanscher’s *Sella Curulis* treats the folding stool as a symbol of dignity with a long afterlife. I start with the hinge, not with a marble throne. Dinner in a Roman house of means is often a couch problem anyway. The backed chair is not the center of the meal. Office is. Teaching is. The *cathedra* is a chair with a back for a person who speaks. The dining room of later America will forget that split and then reenact it with a host chair.

Wood in Pompeii and Herculaneum is a carbon ghost when it is anything. Penelope Allison’s household work is rooms and finds, not a catalog of oak side chairs. Bronze and iron fittings, wall painting, a stone or masonry bench in a shop — those are the survivals. A reconstruction dining room with eight matching klismos-type chairs is usually a twentieth-century wish. Say so when you walk into one.

## Vocabulary, not a suite

*Sella* is seat. *Sella curulis* is the folding curule seat of office. *Cathedra* is a backed chair, later a bishop’s and a teacher’s word. *Solium* leans toward throne. *Lectus* is the couch, the dining and sleeping furniture that carries the meal in the houses the texts care about. If you flatten these into “Roman chairs,” you cannot see why a folding stool would be more important than a splat.

The curule sit is not a long dinner sit. It is appearance, judgment, a body held up in public. The crossed legs make a low, wide stance. You can rise. You can fold the object and the office moves. Later campaign furniture — the traveling desk, the folding stool of an officer — is in this family whether the maker read Livy or not. A dining chair that cannot travel does a different job. A dining chair that pretends to be a curule for the gram is quoting power at a pot roast.

The cathedra’s back is authority of another kind. Someone sits and others listen. A dining host chair is a faint echo: same end of the table, less doctrine. I would rather name the echo than claim a lineage of joinery. The joints are not the same. The social diagram is.

## How Romans ate, in a sentence you can sit with

In the dining rooms we have texts for, elite eaters recline. That posture changes the furniture: a couch with a rise, a table that is low or brought, service that moves around bodies that are not parked in side chairs. This is not a Victorian course in a backed balloon. It is a different machine. When popular history says “the Romans invented the dining room,” it is often talking about a space and a ritual, not about a set of chairs.

Ordinary people — shops, apartments, the unpainted majority — sat however the room allowed: stool, bench, step, a chair if they had one. I will not invent their percentage. Allison’s point, as I read it, is that household assemblages are mixed and that we over-trust atrium houses. For this series the useful caution is: do not draw a Roman dining chair type and then hang a restaurant on it.

Herculaneum’s carbonized wood, where it exists, is a miracle and a specialist literature. Cite the piece. Do not generalize a carbonized couch into a national seating style.

## Bronze, ivory, and the imported luxury

Rome’s expensive seats borrow. Hellenistic lines, Egyptian stone, Greek names. Ivory fittings and bronze legs show up in the luxury record because metal and tusk outlast ash. A bronze leg in a museum is not a chair you can sit in. It is a terminal. Shops that cast animal feet in later centuries are in a conversation with Rome’s bronze whether they know it or not. Empire furniture in France and America will make that conversation loud: sabers, wreaths, lion feet, a painted “antique” that is actually a Paris or New York product.

If you want a Roman sit that a modern diner would recognize, you are probably looking at a stool or a bench in a tavern painting — and even that is a genre. The backed dining chair as a matched set is not Rome’s gift. Rome’s gift to later dining rooms is the idea that a seat can be a badge, and the idea that a meal can be a performance in a dedicated space. The chairs themselves, when they reappear in Revival rooms, are translations.

## What a body does on a fold

A folding stool has a slack seat if the leather is old and a hard seat if the slats are new. The crossed frame can pinch. The sit is slightly sprung. You do not get a rake. You do not get arms unless the type has grown them. You get out quickly. That is office. At a long meal a folding stool is a guest seat, which is exactly how later houses use the camp stool when the eighth cousin arrives.

A cathedra with a solid back lets you stay and talk. That is closer to dinner. Without a surviving population of wooden cathedrae we should not publish a seat-height habit. We can say the back exists to keep you there.

Couches ask the hip and the elbow to do work a dining chair assigns to a splat. People who have eaten on a modern dining couch — a banquette — know a version of this: you slouch, you slide, you need a table that comes to you. The Roman lectus is not a banquette, but the refusal of the individual side chair is family. The last essay in this pack takes the banquette as a modern refusal. Rome is the ancient one, for the houses that could afford the performance.

## Afterlife without a fake pedigree

Medieval curule chairs, Renaissance X-chairs, Savonarola and Dante seats in the trade — later names, later joints, the same crossed idea. A 1960s chrome X-base dining chair is not a sella. It is a base that wanted less leg slum. Do not print a family tree that runs bronze Rome to Saarinen. Print a habit: when a seat wants to mean office, it often folds or crosses. When a seat wants to mean dinner in the nineteenth-century sense, it multiplies and matches.

A shop that is asked for “Roman dining chairs” should ask which movie. Then offer a stool, or a Grecian saber, or a honest refusal. The client usually wants Empire. Empire is a later hour. It has mahogany and a tablet. It has almost nothing of the sella except a memory of antique power.

Wanscher stays on the desk. The vase and the relief stay labeled as pictures. The carbonized wood stays rare. That is enough Rome for a dining-chair series. The meal was mostly not this object. The object was mostly not the meal. Both facts keep the later side chair from pretending it is inevitable.

## The fold as a joint, not a metaphor

A curule stool is a pair of frames that cross and a seat that spans them. Leather, fabric, or slats. The crossing is the joint you have to trust. If the hinge pins or the bindings are tired, the sit goes slack and the frames want to scissor. I have sat camp stools that pinched a calf on the way down. The Roman object is in that family even when the bronze fittings are finer than a surplus store. You get a wide, low stance. You can stand up without walking a four-leg box backward. You can fold the dignity and move it.

That is office. It is not a two-hour roast. At a long meal a folding stool is a guest seat, which is how later houses still use the extra stool when the eighth cousin arrives. Rank can travel. Dinner, in the nineteenth-century sense, wants to stay and multiply.

I will not print a standard seat height for a sella. Survivals in wood are not a population. Bronze and stone give you a type, not a popliteal habit. `[CITE NEEDED]` before anyone uses a single museum bronze as a dining-height rule.

## The lectus is the pairing

In the dining rooms the texts care about, the table comes to the couch. Low, brought, shifted. Service moves around bodies that are on an elbow and a hip. The *lectus* has a rise. When popular history says Rome invented the dining room, it is often talking about a dedicated space — the *triclinium* — and a ritual of three couches. It is not talking about a set of eight matching backs. I will not invent how many couches Allison found in a given house type. A masonry bench in a Pompeian shop is a survival you can still photograph. A carbonized couch fragment in Herculaneum is a miracle; cite the piece.

Banquettes in a later essay refuse the individual side chair. Rome, for the houses that could afford the performance, refused it first. The refusal is not a lineage of joinery. It is a habit of the meal.

A *cathedra* keeps you. The back is why. A dining host chair is a faint echo: same end of the table, less doctrine. A *solium* leans toward throne. You do not make eight. Egypt wrote a name on a back. Rome wrote office on a fold and teaching on a back. The Victorian cloth wrote arms at the ends. We still buy the set that way.

Labor inside a Roman house of means includes enslaved workers. I will not invent a furniture-shop headcount or a named joiner for a bronze leg without a source. Allison’s households are find-spots, not a payroll. `[CITE NEEDED]` before a draft prints a slave-made-chair sentence tied to a specific piece. A bronze animal foot in a case is a terminal, not a dinner. Empire shops later spoke to those terminals about antique power, not about how a Roman framed a seat rail.

## Shop: which movie

A client who asks for “Roman dining chairs” usually wants a folding stool that photographs as power, a Grecian saber that photographs as antique, or a leather-backed side chair that wipes. The first is a sella cousin. The second is Empire. The third is a dinner sit Rome would recognize only as a stool with ambitions. I ask which movie, then which table. A high oak rectangle with eight X-frame chairs is a shin fight. A modest stool at a tavern-height board is closer to ordinary Roman sitting than any gilt saber, and it will not make the client happy if what they wanted was Napoleon.
