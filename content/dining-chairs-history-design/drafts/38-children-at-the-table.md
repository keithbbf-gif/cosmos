A dining chair is sized for an adult camp. A child at that table is a person whose popliteal height is not 18 inches of sit plus a 30-inch top. They perch on the front rail. They slide forward. They kneel. They tilt. They grow. The household’s tools are a booster, a footrest, a rail they are allowed to use, and a refusal to pretend a side chair is a high chair.

I will not design a “youth dining chair” as a cute silhouette they will hate in two years. I will design the adult chair so it can take a booster without becoming a tray, and I will tell the truth about dangling feet.

## Dangling is a duration problem

A child whose feet do not meet a surface will slump, swing, and leave. A foot on a stretcher is an old answer. A footstool is a better one if the stretcher is too high or too thin. Books under the feet are a household classic. I care that the feet find something. I do not care that the photograph is clean.

Boosters raise the sit so the plate is not a shrug. They also steal the gap and change the rail the thigh meets. A thick booster at a table with a deep apron is a jammed thigh. Try it before a holiday.

Straps belong on objects designed for straps. ASTM high-chair rules are a different file. Do not hang a grocery-store strap on a Windsor splat and call it a system.

## Tilt and rack

Children are the dinner test. Rear legs, pierced splats, saber kicks, plastic bosses — they will find them. A stretchered oak side is more patient than a stretcherless saber. A stacker slides into a wall. A Wishbone cord takes a fork.

I would rather a child eat in a sound ladderback they cannot destroy in one lean than in a museum shield. The shield can wait.

## Growing

The same chair should work for a decade of a child’s body if the booster goes away in time and the feet keep finding a surface. A chair that only works with a branded toddler seat is a product ladder, not a dining chair.

Islands and counter stools are worse for children than a 30-inch table if the drop is a fall. That is not this hour’s full argument. It is a warning. Dining height is already a climb.

## Shop practice

We radius front rails. We put stretchers where a foot might go, and we do not pretend that is only “period.” We refuse to cut a child-sized dining set that will be trash in three years unless the client is asking for a play table. We can cut a bench that seats two small people and later seats one adult — a length that changes job.

No public photograph of a recognizable child without a release. The figure in this pack can be a booster and a tape.

The table is a social machine that includes people who cannot yet sit the camp. The chair should allow the edit. It should not pretend the edit is unnecessary.

## Two products, one table

A high chair is a strap object with a footrest and a restraint geometry that ASTM writes rules for. A dining side chair is an adult camp: about eighteen inches of sit, a 29-to-30-inch table, a gap under an apron, no harness. I will name the high chair as a cousin. I will not make the side chair do the strap job. Do not hang a grocery-store strap on a Windsor splat and call it a system. The splat was not drawn for that load, and the child was not asked whether a pierced Gothic was a good place to be tied.

Historical households improvised. A pillow on a joint stool. A pile of books under a small pair of feet. A bench that seated two children and later seated one adult. A Windsor that a child could drag. None of those were high chairs. They were edits of the adult furniture. The edit is still the honest tool. A branded toddler seat that only works with one catalog chair is a product ladder. When the child grows, the ladder wants another purchase. I would rather the adult chair take a booster that can leave, and a footstool that can leave, and a stretch of years in the same oak.

I will not design a youth dining chair as a cute silhouette they will hate in two years. The small chair that matches the adult set in miniature is a photograph. It becomes trash or a plant stand. A bench that changes job is furniture.

## Dangling is a duration problem, named again

A child whose feet do not meet a surface will slump, swing, and leave. That is not a manners lecture. It is popliteal height. Stephen Pheasant’s *Bodyspace* — the anthropometrist — treats foot contact as the bone of a seat. Dinner does not get a lever under each cousin. It gets a booster, a stool, a stretcher they are allowed to use, or a pair of books. I care that the feet find something. I do not care that the photograph is clean.

A stretcher as a footrest is an old answer and a mixed one. If the stretcher is at a height the child’s heel can find, it works. If it is a thin spindle too high or too far forward, it is a slip and a bruise. A footstool is better when the stretcher is wrong. I put stretchers where a foot might go on a dining side, and I do not pretend that is only period. Farm chairs knew this. Fancy chairs sometimes forgot it for the line.

Boosters raise the sit so the plate is not a shrug. They also steal the gap and change the rail the thigh meets. A thick booster at a table with a deep apron is a jammed thigh. Try it before a holiday. A thin, firm booster that can be wiped is a household tool. A plush booster that compresses an inch during gravy is a moving target. The measuring essay’s fist on the pad applies to children too.

## Tilt, rack, and the objects they find

Children are the dinner test. Rear legs, pierced splats, saber kicks, plastic bosses, a Wishbone cord, a Cesca tube — they will find them. A child tilting onto the back legs of a stretcherless saber is a parallelogram with a person on it. A child hanging from a pierced Chippendale splat is a cantilever the shoe was not priced for. A child who uses a Thonet 14 as a walker on casters it does not have will walk the screws loose. A stacker slides into a wall and takes a chip.

I would rather a child eat in a sound ladderback they cannot destroy in one lean than in a museum shield. The shield can wait. A rush seat will take crumbs and a wet wipe and a small pair of knees. A cane seat will take a fork through it if someone is bored. Leather wipes. Foam bottoms and then shows a crater where a child knelt to reach the butter. Vinyl splits. The seat essay already named the diary. Children write it faster.

Islands and counter stools are worse for children than a 30-inch table if the drop is a fall. That is not this hour’s full argument. It is a warning. Dining height is already a climb. A 36-inch island with a swivel stool and a child is a different product and a different injury. Keep dinner at a table if you can.

## Growing, and the chair that lasts a decade

The same adult chair should work for a decade of a child’s body if the booster goes away in time and the feet keep finding a surface. That means a front rail that is not a knife, a stretcher or a stool, a back that does not snag a shirt, and a weight the child can eventually pull in without a groan. A Mission post that takes two adults to move will not be a child’s chair even when the child is twelve. A Windsor, a 14, a Series 7, an INGOLF, a ladderback — light chairs are children’s furniture even when they were not sold as such.

A chair that only works with a branded toddler seat is a product ladder. A chair that works with a household booster and then works without one is a dining chair. I have watched a child go from books under the feet to a stretcher to the floor in the same oak side, over years, at the same table. That is the sit I will cut for.

## Failure cases

A grocery strap on a splat. A thick booster under a deep apron, tried for the first time when the relatives are already in the drive. A stretcherless saber sold as a family chair. A museum haircloth at a table where a child will kneel. A cane Cesca used as a climbing frame. A youth set in painted pine that will be trash in three years, unless the client asked for a play table and we said so.

Cutting a child-sized dining set that matches the adult splat in miniature, then storing it in an attic when the child turns eight. I have seen those sets. The joinery is often a dowel and a prayer. The sit is a perch. The adult chair with a booster would have been kinder.

No public photograph of a recognizable child without a release. The figure in this pack can be a booster and a tape. Faces are not required to show that height is a stack and that feet still need a floor.

We radius front rails because thighs of every age meet them. We put stretchers where a foot might go. We refuse the cute silhouette unless it is a play table. We can cut a bench that seats two small people and later seats one adult — a length that changes job. The chair should allow the edit. It should not pretend the edit is unnecessary.
