Every dining room has a lie about how many people it can seat. The table opens. The chairs do not multiply. The spare chair is the truth: a Windsor on a peg, a ladderback against a wall, a joint stool under the board, a Thonet from the extra crate, a Series 7 column, a stacker in the garage, a folding metal thing that bites a finger.

Claw-and-ball sets do not stack. Mission posts do not hang. Balloon-backs occupy even when no one is eating. The specialized dining room and the unstackable chair arrive together. If your room must become a floor, you want the other line.

## Older mercies

Stools go under. Benches slide. Windsors hang (the legend overstates nesting; hanging is real). Ladderbacks line a wall. Fancy chairs sometimes hang. None of this is cute until a magazine says so. It is capacity.

A hall bench that becomes seating for the overflow Thanksgiving is the medieval solution with a cushion.

## Modern mercies

Jacobsen’s nest, the plastic stack, the folding contract chair, the leftover INGOLF in the bedroom. Banquettes that seat three where two chairs would stand. IKEA taught the box. The cafeteria taught the column.

People who buy eight matching unstackable chairs for a table that seats six on Tuesday are buying a photograph. They then hate the occupancy. I would buy six that live at the table and two that stack or hang, if the heights match. Matching the sit matters more than matching the splat.

## Shop practice

If a client needs overflow, we talk hangers, a shallow stack, or a bench before we talk eight claws. A peg rail is not a Colonial fantasy if they will use it. If they will not, they want a stacker and a closet.

Do not design a finial that cannot hang if hanging is the brief. Do not design a splat that cannot take a neighbor’s back if stacking is the brief.

The spare chair is not an afterthought. It is the plan for the day the table is honest about how many people came.

## The lie about eight

A table sold as seating eight is a leaf and a hope. The chairs that live at that table on Tuesday are usually six, or four and a bench. The other two or four exist as a problem the room pretends not to have. Claw-and-ball sets do not stack. Mission posts do not hang. Balloon-backs occupy even when no one is eating. The specialized dining room and the unstackable chair arrive together. If your room must become a floor — a child’s birthday, a band practice, a bed for a guest — you want the other line.

People who buy eight matching unstackable chairs for a table that seats six on Tuesday are buying a photograph. They then hate the occupancy. The chairs live in a corner, or they live in a garage and come in scratched, or they live around the table all year and the room cannot be walked. I would buy six that live at the table and two that stack or hang, if the heights match. Matching the sit matters more than matching the splat. A Series 7 and a Wishbone at the same finished seat are a household. A pastry Gothic and a folding metal chair at two different heights are a shrug all night.

I have sat at a Thanksgiving where the two extras were folding chairs from a closet, two inches low, and the guests on those chairs spent the meal in a shrug that everyone blamed on the turkey. The turkey was fine. The spare chairs were a different camp.

## Older mercies that were capacity

Stools go under the board. That is the medieval and the farm answer, and it still works if the stool height matches the chair height. A joint stool that is two inches lower than the side chairs is a hole with a plate. Benches slide. A hall bench that becomes seating for the overflow is the same answer with a cushion. Windsors hang. The legend overstates nesting; they nest less well than the postcard. Hanging is real. A peg rail is not a Colonial fantasy if someone will use it. Nancy Goyne Evans’s Windsors were kitchen and tavern and porch chairs in part because you could carry four and clear a room.

Ladderbacks line a wall. Fancy chairs sometimes hang. Shaker rooms hung chairs as a habit of the floor, not as a magazine. None of this is cute until a photograph says so. It is capacity. A farmhouse that lined eight slat-backs along a plaster wall was storing seats, not decorating.

Do not invent a peg-rail as universal. Some rooms had hooks. Some had a loft. Some had a porch. Some had nothing and the extra stool lived under the table until someone kicked it. The type that can leave the floor is the type that can come back.

## Modern mercies and the column

Jacobsen’s nest, the plastic stack, the folding contract chair, the leftover INGOLF in the bedroom: the closet learned a column. IKEA taught the box. The cafeteria taught the count. A stack of 7s in a closet is a Danish answer that a lot of American houses used without knowing the number. A stack of polypropylene shells in a garage is the same answer in a cheaper material. Both scratch if you stack them dirty. Both live if you stack them clean and not too high.

Banquettes that seat three where two chairs would stand are a floor-plan mercy and a trapped-middle problem. The last essay takes that type. A bench that slides under a table is older and cleaner if the height matches.

Folding metal chairs that bite a finger are still spare chairs. Warn the guest or change the plate. A low fold at a 30-inch table is a duration problem, not a manners problem. I am not joking. I have watched a person of ordinary height spend two hours with shoulders up because the spare chair was a card-table leftover.

## Heights, again, as a household inventory

The spare chair from another decade is how one guest sits in a hole. Keep a tape. Write the finished seat of the chairs that live at the table. Write the finished seat of the extras. If they differ by more than a little, either change a pad or change which chair comes to dinner. A Windsor that is a true saddle and an INGOLF that is a flat plank can share a table if the compressed sits match. They cannot share a table if one is a porch height and the other is a dining camp.

I would rather mix a splat and keep a height than match a splat and mix a sit. Stores hate this. Households that eat do not.

If the spare is a host armchair that will not leave the end, it is not a spare. It is a throne that occupies. The host essay already took rank. This hour only needs the fact that an armchair is a poor overflow object: it does not stack, it does not hang, and it fights the apron when you try to park it at a side.

## Failure cases

Designing a finial that cannot hang when hanging is the brief. Designing a splat that cannot take a neighbor’s back when stacking is the brief. Buying eight claws and then asking where the extras go. Stacking chairs that were not drawn to stack, so the crest of one bites the seat of another and starts a rack from a dent. Hanging a rush-seat Hitchcock by the crest until the rush sags into a bag. Storing a cane Cesca in a garage stack where the cane takes a tube from the chair above.

A peg rail that was installed for the photograph and never used is not a mercy. It is a decoration. If they will not hang, they want a stacker and a closet. If they will not stack, they want a bench that slides or a hall that can take a line of chairs. I talk hangers, a shallow stack, or a bench before I talk eight claws.

Contract stackers that were specified for a cafeteria sometimes arrive in a dining room as “extra modern chairs.” Their sit is often a different camp — a little higher, a little harder, a shell that was meant for twenty minutes. They can work as overflow if you warn the guest and if the height is close. They cannot replace the six that live at the table unless the household wanted a cafeteria.

The spare chair is the plan for the day the table is honest about how many people came. Occupancy is a design choice. The room that pretends eight matching unstackables are a closet will spend the year walking around them.
