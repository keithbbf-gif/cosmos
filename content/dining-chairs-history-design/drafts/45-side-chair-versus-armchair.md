The side chair is the dining chair. Armless, it slides, it packs along a length, it lets a platter pass, it does not invent a collision with the apron. The armchair is a special: ends, a rest, a leftover inscription, a wider timber bill. Stores sell “two plus four” because the nineteenth century did and because the photograph looks complete. Completeness is not a law.

I have already written the arm’s geometry and the host’s rank. This hour is only the choice, as a buyer or a shop, without repeating those ledes at length.

## What you gain with arms

A place for a forearm during a long talk. A little help getting up, for some bodies. A visual head of table. A wider sit that can feel like a chair instead of a perch, if the seat grew with the arms.

## What you lose

Path. Server space. Stacking. Hanging. A wheelchair’s ability to take an end if the armchair will not leave. Money. Width at a leaf. A child trapped in a throne.

Farthingale history already showed that clothes and arms fight. Aprons and arms fight. The fight is old.

## Hybrids

Wishbone rails, Windsor continuous arms, tulip wraps, Cesca tubes — “arms” that are not host boxes. They still occupy a slice. Measure them as arms even if the catalog says side chair.

Benches and banquettes refuse the distinction. Stools never entered it.

## Shop practice

Default: sides. Arms only to a taped underside, and only as many as the table can walk around. Same seat height. Same front-rail kindness. No raised host sit.

A customer who wants all arms can have them after they sit a crowded mock and try to pass a dish. I have watched the order change in that minute.

If the room is a café of 14s, do not add two carved hosts “for balance.” Balance is a magazine. The hoop was the idea.

The type is the side chair. Everything else is an edit. Edit with a tape.

## What you gain, named as a sit

A place for a forearm during a long talk. That is real. I have sat a three-hour dinner in a host chair whose arms cleared, and the forearms were a mercy at hour two. A little help getting up, for some bodies. That is real too, if the arm is at a height the hand can use and if the chair itself will leave when you stand. A visual head of table. That is a leftover inscription. Useful if you want it. Not a law. A wider sit that can feel like a chair instead of a perch, if the seat grew with the arms. A lot of dining armchairs kept the side-chair seat and added timber at the sides. That is a wider bill, not a wider sit.

The arms essay already took geometry. The host essay already took rank. This hour is only the choice. I will not walk those streets again as if they were new.

## What you lose, named as a path

Path. A server with a platter along a length of sides can pass. A server along a length of arms cannot, or can only by lifting the platter over a rail that was meant to be a rest. I have watched a dish stop at the second armchair and then travel back the other way. Width at a leaf is the same problem in a different year: the leaf adds plates; the arms do not add aisle.

Stacking. Hanging. An armchair is a poor spare. It does not go on a peg. It does not nest. It occupies even when no one is eating. The stacking essay already took occupancy. This hour only needs the fact that arms are anti-spare.

A wheelchair’s ability to take an end if the armchair will not leave. The access essay already took the cave. This hour only needs the weight and the arms. Two carved hosts that complete a photograph are a barrier if they will not move.

A child trapped in a throne. Arms that were a rest for an adult are a fence for a small person who wants out. Farthingale history already showed that clothes and arms fight. Aprons and arms fight. Children and arms fight. The fight is old.

Money. Wider timber, more joints, more finish. Two plus four is a nineteenth-century set and a store’s completeness. Completeness is not a law.

## Hybrids that are still arms

Wishbone rails, Windsor continuous arms, tulip wraps, Cesca tubes — arms that are not host boxes. They still occupy a slice. Measure them as arms even if the catalog says side chair. A Wishbone’s Y is a contact and a rail; the rail will meet an apron if the table is thick. A Windsor continuous arm is a hoop that will not hang the way a bow-back side will hang. A tulip wrap is a pedestal’s cousin and still a width. A Cesca tube is a line you can hang a coat on and a line that will hit.

Benches and banquettes refuse the distinction. Stools never entered it. A joint stool has no side and no arm. A hall bench has a length. The last essay takes the built-in. This hour stays with the individual chair and the choice to add timber at the sides.

Stores sell two plus four because the nineteenth century did and because the photograph looks complete. Balloon-back sets came that way. Grand Rapids catalogs came that way. IKEA will sell you six sides and no hosts, or a pair of arms if you want them, and the honesty there is that the side is the default. I would start where IKEA started, even in oak: sides, then arms only to a taped underside.

## The crowded mock

A customer who wants all arms can have them after they sit a crowded mock and try to pass a dish. I have watched the order change in that minute. Six sides and two arms at the ends is a set I will cut if the underside has been taped and the arms clear. Eight arms is a fence. I do not sell eight arms.

Default: sides. Arms only as many as the table can walk around. Same seat height. Same front-rail kindness. No raised host sit. The host essay already refused the raised throne. This hour repeats the height because stores still raise it.

If the room is a café of 14s, do not add two carved hosts for balance. Balance is a magazine. The hoop was the idea. If the room is a Mission table with posts that already occupy, adding arms is a second occupation. If the room is a pedestal that already fills a cave, putting armchairs at the ends is how a wheelchair loses the honest place.

## Failure cases

Filling the table with arms because the photograph looked complete. Mixing a host sit that is higher than the sides, so two people shrug and four people do not. Adding carved hosts to a set of 14s. Specifying continuous-arm Windsors as overflow chairs. Buying two plus four without walking a platter around the mock. Treating a Wishbone as armless because the catalog said side.

The side chair is the dining chair. Armless, it slides, it packs along a length, it lets a platter pass, it does not invent a collision with the apron. The armchair is a special: ends, a rest, a leftover inscription, a wider timber bill. Mixing them is a set. Filling the table with arms is a fence.

## Historical two-plus-four, without a second lede

Hepplewhite and Sheraton priced sides and elbows as different labor. Chippendale’s plates are not always titled dining, but the side chair is the type that packed a table. Duncan Phyfe’s New York dining rooms kept the inscription at the ends and the working chairs along the length. Balloon-back catalogs later sold the same arithmetic as a photograph: two hosts, four or six sides, a completeness you could buy in a crate.

A farthingale chair was an answer to clothes: armless, so a skirt could sit. The Renaissance hour already took that fight. The dining fact that remains is older than foam: arms occupy a slice of air the body and the platter both want. Victorian host chairs made that slice a rank. Café 14s refused it. A Windsor continuous-arm is a tavern rest that will not pack like a bow-back side. I measure it as an arm.

In a shop I set a taped table, six sides, two arms at the ends, and a platter. Then I set eight arms. The second mock is a fence. People who eat can see it. People who are buying a photograph sometimes cannot until they sit. I wait for the sit.

The type is the side chair. Everything else is an edit. Edit with a tape.
