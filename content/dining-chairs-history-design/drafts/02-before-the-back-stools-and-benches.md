A backed chair in a medieval hall was a small political fact. Everyone else had a length of oak and a neighbor’s hip. We walk through house museums now and the chairs are what the camera wants. The meal was the board and the bench. If you start the history of the dining chair with Chippendale, you have started in the last fifth of the story and in the richest rooms of that fifth.

I want the older sit. Not as romance. As a reminder that a back is a decision, and that a lot of good dinners never made it.

## The ground, the mat, the low seat

Before joinery, people ate off the ground or off a low platform. That is not a preface for children. It is still how a large part of the world takes a meal, and it makes the Western dining chair look like what it is: a raised seat designed to meet a raised table. Change the table height and the chair’s reason changes. Floor sitting has its own ergonomics — hips, ankles, a table that comes to you — and I will not fake a cross-cultural survey I have not earned. I name it so the backed chair does not pretend to be human nature.

Egypt will get its own hour because the chair there is rank in cedar. Greece will get the klismos because the vase painters would not leave it alone. Both of those are already special. The ordinary ancient meal, in a lot of houses, was a stool or the couch of the symposium, which is a dining furniture of a different job: reclining, not sitting up to a plate the way a modern side chair does. Do not flatten those into “early chairs.”

## The stool is a chair that refused a back

A stool is not a failed chair. It is a seat whose job is to get a body to a height and then stay out of the way. Three legs find an uneven floor. Four legs and stretchers make a box that can take a sideways shove. A turned joint stool — English oak, a board seat, legs in splayed mortises, a box of stretchers — is dining furniture in every inventory that bothers to list the cheap seats. Victor Chinnery’s oak tradition is full of them. They stack in the mind even when they do not stack in the barn. They go under the table. They move to the hearth. They do not claim an end.

Sitting on a stool at dinner is a different back story. You hold your own spine. You can turn to the person behind you. You can leave without walking a chair into a wall. You also fatigue differently. There is no rail to slump into when the talk gets long. People who call stools primitive are often people whose dinners last two hours and whose backs have been trained by a splat.

The folding stool — the sella, the camp stool, the later campaign cousin — is dignity that packs. Rome made a language of it. Traveling officers and officials sat on something that could fold and still mean office. That is not a dining-room product in the 1890s sense. It is a reminder that “chair” and “authority” traveled together long before “dining chair” meant a matched set of eight.

## The bench is the table’s other half

A bench is honest about the meal. It says the table is a length, not a place setting. You slide. You squeeze. You stand up in a wave when someone in the middle needs out. Medieval halls and later farm kitchens ran this way because the architecture did: a board on trestles, a bench along it, maybe a backed chair for the person whose rank required a back. Penelope Eames’s furniture of the twelfth to fifteenth centuries is chests and benches more than chairs. The scarce backed seat is the point.

Ergonomically the bench is a compromise drawn as a line. One height for every femur. No back rake, or a wall that becomes the back. Seat depth is the plank. If the plank is a ten-inch oak, long thighs have no support and short legs dangle from the same edge. Cushions appear because the wood does not care. A bench against a wall can be a settle: now you have a back, and now you have a piece that does not pull up to a table as freely. Settles are room furniture that sometimes fed people. They are not side chairs.

Refectory sitting — monastery, school, later college — kept the bench because the table was a rule. You can still see the type in a dining hall that never got the memo about side chairs. The sit is social before it is personal. A dining chair individualizes the meal. That individualization is a history, not a default.

## When a back was expensive

A back is timber, time, and a joint that has to take a lean. A stool can be a board and four legs a turner already knows how to make. A backed chair asks for posts that run long, a crest or slats, and a decision about whether the back is structural or upholstered theater. In a timber economy that already spent its oak on the hall roof and the chest, the backed chair waits.

Wainscot chairs — paneled backs, often arms, often too heavy to be a casual dinner seat — sit at the end of a high table and look like architecture. They are closer to a settle that learned to be a person. Joint stools stay under the board. This split lasts a long time. You can still walk into a house where the “good chairs” came out for company and the everyday meal was a bench in the kitchen. Colonial Revival later sold the bench as charm. The original was capacity.

## What this does to the body

A bench teaches a vertical sit. You cannot recline without finding a wall or a neighbor. You share heat. You cannot easily sit at an angle to the table, so the plate stays in front of you. Children can be packed. So can laborers at a harvest table. The modern complaint about benches — “I can’t get out” — is the design working as designed. The modern praise — “it’s friendly” — is the same fact in a softer mouth.

A stool lets you pivot. It also lets you slump in ways a splat prevents. People with backs that need a stop will hate a long stool dinner. People who hate being trapped will hate a backed chair in a tight room. Neither preference is a moral. Both are why the backed dining chair, when it finally floods the market, still has to compete with banquettes, stools at islands, and the leftover kitchen bench.

## The leftover type

We still eat on stools. The kitchen island is a counter height, not a dining height, and the sit is a different number — a later essay. The tavern stool never died. The folding stool comes out for extra guests and then lives in the hall closet, which is the medieval overflow seat with better steel. Picnic tables are benches with a marketing department.

When a shop in south Arkansas cuts a set of dining chairs, it is cutting the late type. That is fine. The late type is what most buyers mean. I only want the buyer to know that the type is late, that the bench was the meal for longer, and that a backed chair has to earn the timber it uses. If the back is only a drawing, and the sit is worse than the joint stool it replaced, the shop has gone backward with more work.

Shaker communities kept benches and slat-backs in the same dining rooms for reasons that were not nostalgia. Capacity, stacking, a theology of the shared table. The country-revival stores of the twentieth century kept the bench because it photographed as “farm.” Same silhouette, different brief. I would rather sit a real joint stool at a harvest table than a distressed bench sold by the foot. The stool still tells the truth about height. The distressed bench often lies about history and about the thigh.
