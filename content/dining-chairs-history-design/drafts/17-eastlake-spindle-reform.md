Charles Eastlake’s *Hints on Household Taste* (1868, then American editions) is a reform argument: construction you can read, less naturalistic fruit, turning and geometry instead of a cabriole that pretends to be an animal. American factories heard “Eastlake” and made a style — incised lines, ebonized maple or walnut, galleries of spindles, a chair that looks like a small machine for sitting upright. The sibling pack has Eastlake in the dining room as a reform interior. This hour is the spindle against your back.

The sit is more vertical than a balloon-back. The stuffing, when it exists, is thinner. You are being asked to behave. That is not a metaphor I invented. Reform furniture often sits like advice.

## What the book wanted

Eastlake wanted furniture whose joints made sense and whose ornament was carved or turned rather than applied naturalism. He was not designing a Grand Rapids SKU. The distance between the book and the factory chair is the American story. Incised lines are easy for a machine. Honesty is not a groove.

A dining chair in this language: a rectangular or gently arched crest, a row of spindles or a splat that is a board with cutouts, turned front legs, sometimes castors, a seat that may be rush, cane, or a modest pad. Compared to rococo revival, you can clean it. Compared to a Hepplewhite shield, it is heavier and less pictorial.

I will not call it “masculine.” I will call it rectilinear and a little stern.

## The grate again

Spindles against the back are a Windsor idea in a darker suit. Air moves. Contact is a row. If the spindles are too far forward in the frame, they poke. If they are too far back, you never meet them and you slump toward a crest that hits the neck. The good ones place the row where a firm hand belongs.

Ebonizing hides mixed woods and fingerprints and also shows dust. Dining rooms that went Eastlake often went dark. A dark chair at a dark table is a cave unless the cloth or the china brings light. That is a room problem the chair cannot solve.

## Factory reform

Grand Rapids and other factory towns could turn spindles all day. The reform style was perfect for a lathe and a shaper. Pressed ornament still sneaks in; “Eastlake” in a dealer’s mouth can mean any chair with a groove. Look at the joints. Look at whether the spindles are through-tenoned or only parked in shallow holes. Look at the seat frame.

Labor is factory labor: not a London moralist, not a single cabinetmaker’s name. Women still did rush and cane in many towns. I will not invent a workforce number. I will not pretend the chair is Arts and Crafts yet. Stickley is a later sermon with thicker posts.

## The sit at dinner

You sit up. Courses still exist, but the chair is less of a lounge than the balloon. People who like a firm sit like these. People who want to melt after wine do not. A modest pad helps; a thick foam ruins the vertical and the table number.

Arms on Eastlake dining chairs can be high and boxed. Measure the apron. Castors on a period chair at a modern hard floor will roll you away from the plate while you cut. Chocks or replacement feet are a household decision, not a moral.

I have sat in a factory Eastlake that was more honest than a carved rococo that had lost its glue. The spindle chair told me what it was. The rococo told me a story about a palace it had never seen. Dinner went better with the spindles.

## Translation

A new oak chair with turned spindles and a leather seat can be an Eastlake argument without ebonizing. Do not incise fake “random” grooves. If you want geometry, draw it. If you want a farm chair, make a ladderback and stop borrowing a London reformer’s name.

Arkansas oak likes turning. A shop that already turns bed posts can turn a dining spindle. The sit still has to be placed. Reform is not a finish. It is a decision about what the back is allowed to do.

## The book, the plate, and the SKU

Eastlake’s English pages want furniture whose construction you can read and whose ornament is turned or carved rather than applied fruit. American editions carried that sermon across the water. Grand Rapids and other factory towns heard a style name. Incised lines are easy for a machine. A groove is not honesty. I look at whether the spindles are through-tenoned or only parked in shallow holes, whether the seat frame is a box or a veneer of a box, whether the turning is crisp because a lathe was sharp or sloppy because a catalog needed a silhouette.

I will not treat every incised-line chair as Eastlake. The word in a dealer’s mouth can mean any dark chair with a scratch. Date the hardware, the seat, the proportion. A 1910 factory chair with a groove is not 1868. A 1875 ebonized maple with a gallery of spindles and a cane seat is in the argument even if Eastlake never signed it.

The book is not a Grand Rapids SKU. The distance between them is the American story I actually care about: reform as a product, sold by the set, sat by people who had never read the plate. That is not a betrayal. It is how most dining chairs arrive.

## Where the grate meets the back

Spindles against the spine are a Windsor idea in a darker suit. Air moves. Contact is a row. If the row is too far forward in the frame, you get poked in the same place all night. If the row is too far back, you never meet it and you slump toward a crest that hits the neck. I sit and then I slide a hand between my back and the spindles. The good ones place the row where a firm palm belongs — mid-blade, not mid-nothing.

A splat that is a board with cutouts is the other Eastlake back. It reads as geometry. It sits as a plane with holes. Kinder than a poke if the plane is where you are. Meaner than a balloon hoop if you wanted to melt. Reform furniture often sits like advice. I mean that as a description of rake and stuffing, not as a joke about morals.

Ebonizing hides mixed woods and fingerprints. It also shows dust and every chip as a pale wound. Dining rooms that went Eastlake often went dark: dark chair, dark table, a cave unless the cloth or the china brought light. That is a room problem the chair cannot solve. A pale oak Eastlake argument — turned members, a leather seat, no fake ebony — can be easier to eat in and still be geometrically stern.

## Factory hours, women’s seats

Labor is factory labor: lathes, shapers, finishing rooms. Not a London moralist at a bench. Not a single cabinetmaker’s name on a rail. Women still did rush and cane in many towns. I will not invent a workforce number. I will not pretend the chair is Arts and Crafts yet. Stickley’s thicker posts and the magazine sermon are a later hour. This chair is a grate and a groove and a catalog.

Pressed ornament still sneaks in. Look at it. If the “carving” is a skin, you are in the factory’s cheaper mouth. If the turning is in the solid and the spindles pass through, you are closer to what the book asked for even if the finish is a spray. I pay for the joint I can see, not for the word Eastlake on a tag.

Rush, cane, a modest pad: the seat menu is the older one. A thick foam ruins the vertical and the table number. A slip seat you can recover after the holidays is the adult. Haircloth is less common on these than on Phyfe; the reform sit wanted wipeability and a little less itch. Vinyl arrived later and is a dinette wearing a groove.

## Dinner in a stern chair

You sit up. Courses still exist. The chair is less of a lounge than the balloon. People who like a firm sit like these. People who want to melt after wine do not. I have sat a factory Eastlake through a long Sunday and been grateful for the grate. I have sat a carved rococo that had lost its glue and been grateful to stand. The spindle chair told me what it was. The rococo told me a story about a palace it had never seen.

Arms can be high and boxed. Measure the apron. Castors on a period chair at a modern hard floor will roll you away from the plate while you cut. Chocks, replacement feet, or a rug that is a height: household decisions, not morals. A chair that rolls while you saw a roast is a chair that has not been paired to the floor.

At a crowded table the rectilinear back is easy to park against a wall between meals. The balloon’s O looks wounded against a wall. The Eastlake looks like a small machine at rest. These chairs occupy. They do not stack. They do not hang well if the crest is a gallery. They live in the room the way Mission will later live, only thinner.

## What I will turn

A new oak chair with turned spindles, through tenons, and a leather or rush seat can be an Eastlake argument without ebonizing and without a London name. Do not incise fake “random” grooves. If you want geometry, draw it and cut it on purpose. If you want a farm chair, make a ladderback and stop borrowing a reformer’s caption.

Arkansas oak likes the lathe. A shop that already turns bed posts can turn a dining spindle in a day and still fail the sit if the row is in the wrong place. Place the grate. Number the seat to the table. Leave the sermon in the book. I will eat in the oak grate. I will not pay extra for a groove that could sit on any other essay with the names swapped.
