The back does not arrive all at once. It arrives as a board nailed to a stool, as a paneled wainscot that weighs like furniture-as-wall, as a French chair named for chatter, as an Italian *sgabello* that is still half a stool with ambitions. Peter Thornton’s interiors are useful here: decoration and seating as a room language, not as a catalog of types you can order in six-packs. Dinner is still a board in a lot of houses. What changes is how many sitters get a stop for the spine, and how much that stop costs.

I care about the sit. A back lets the meal last. It also pins you in a direction. Benches kept you social and trapped. Stools kept you free and tired. The backed chair tries to sell both, and for a long time it only sells to the people whose names get inventories.

## Italy: the sgabello and the carved yoke

A sixteenth-century sgabello is often three-legged or trestle-legged, a seat board, a splat or a yoke of a back carved into grotesque or armorial theater. It stands away from the wall like a sculpture that can take a person. It does not look like a Chippendale side chair and it should not be forced to. The back is a field for carving. The sit can be surprisingly mean: a narrow seat, a hard edge, a back that hits too high or too low depending on the carver’s drawing.

These are hall and chamber chairs. They come out. They show. A dining use is possible and not required. Wealthy Italian rooms had many kinds of sit; the painted cassone and the tapestry get more pages than the cheap stool. Same bias as Egypt: we photograph the carved back.

When a later decorator says “sgabello dining chairs,” they usually mean a set of high-backed board chairs around a refectory table. That is a twentieth-century restaurant idea with a Renaissance noun. A real sgabello is often a singleton or a pair. Matching eight carved yokes is a palace or a lie.

## France: talking, not only eating

The *caquetoire* — the gossip chair, the conversation chair — is a wide-seated, often armed chair of the later sixteenth century, built for talk in a room that is not yet a dining room in the nineteenth-century sense. The seat is broad. The back can be open. You turn to the person beside you. That turning is the design. A dining side chair that cannot turn is a later, more axial religion: face the plate, face the host, keep your elbows.

I will not print a fake etymology parade. The name is the clue. Seating is being asked to support conversation as a pastime. Dinner will inherit that. A long meal is talk with food. A chair that only works if you sit square to a trencherman’s board is already slightly old-fashioned when talk becomes the point.

Upholstery — turkey-work, leather, velvet — starts to matter as the back becomes a cushioned plane. The joinery is still oak or walnut; the comfort is cloth. Cloth fails first. Museum chairs with original textile are scarce and often too fragile to sit. The sit you imagine is a sit in a reproduction cover.

## England: wainscot, turned chairs, and the leftover stool

English oak keeps the paneled back: a small wall behind you, sometimes a crest, sometimes arms. These chairs are heavy. They stay. They mark the end of the table or the hearth. Turned chairs — thrown, with triangular seats or square, with knobbed posts — are another shop, another price. Both can appear at meals. Neither floods the hall the way joint stools do.

The Farthingale chair, the wide-seated armless chair of the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries, is a clothing fact: skirts. Dining is not the first job. The lesson for later side chairs is the armless wide seat. Arms fight clothes and aprons both.

Thornton’s Dutch and French comparisons keep England from looking like the only story. Low Countries rooms, Spanish leather chairs, the trade in seats — a dining-chair series that stays in London will miss the leather back that wipes, which is a dinner fact. Leather is gravy-friendly. Turkey-work is not.

## What changes for the body

A back, even a bad one, reduces the muscular work of sitting. People stay. Courses can multiply. Conversation can outlast hunger. The chair begins to individualize: this is my place, my arms, my cushion. Place settings will later freeze that. The backed chair is already a place setting in timber.

Rack becomes a problem as soon as the back is a lever. A stool has little to lean on, so it has little to break by leaning. A backed chair invites the lean. Mortises in the rear posts take a beating. Stretchers matter more. The dining chair of the eighteenth century is, among other things, a rack solution. The Renaissance back invents the problem that Chippendale’s joints will have to solve at dinner.

Seat heights wander. Tables wander. There is no eighteen-inch habit yet as a printed law. There is a room and a board and a chair that may have been built for another room. Recut legs show up early. Museums recut too. Measure, then doubt.

## Do not skip to walnut

The next hours go Queen Anne and compass seats, then Chippendale splats. Those are not the “arrival” of the back. They are the arrival of a fashionable, lighter, more reproducible backed dining chair in a consumer sense. The Renaissance is the messy multiplication: some backs carved, some paneled, some upholstered, most people still on stools.

A shop asked for “Renaissance dining chairs” should ask whether the client wants a refectory photograph or a sit. A sit might be a leather-backed side chair with honest stretchers and a seat you can wipe. A photograph might be eight sgabelli and a candle. I know which one I would eat in.

If you only remember one motion from this hour, remember the moment a sitter leans and the rear posts take it. That lean is dinner deciding to stay. The rest of the series is how shops kept the lean from destroying the chair, and how some shops gave up and sold a drawing.

## The sgabello as an object, not a set

A sixteenth-century sgabello is often a singleton you can pick up and place. Three legs or a trestle, a seat board, a back that is a carved yoke or a splat turned into armorial theater. Grotesques, a crest, a family’s claim. It stands in a hall the way a candlestick stands: to be seen, then used. The sit can be mean. Narrow board. Hard front edge. A back that meets you too high, as if the carver were drawing a shield and forgot a spine.

Some sgabelli are closer to a stool with a billboard than to a dining side chair. Matching eight carved yokes around a refectory table is a palace project or a twentieth-century restaurant with a Renaissance noun. I have sat in the restaurant version. The backs were high, the seats were pads, the candles were doing the century work. The meal was fine. The name was rented.

Dinner, if it happened in the sgabello, happened as an event you staged, not as a nightly set of six. Trapezoidal *caquetoire* plans turn you to the person beside you; Thornton’s interiors are the room language — cite the plate if you name a room `[CITE NEEDED]`.

## Cloth, leather, and who keeps them

Once the back is a plane, someone will pad it. Turkey-work, velvet, leather. The joinery is still a frame. The comfort is cloth. Cloth fails first — light, moth, gravy, a damp winter. Museum chairs with original textile are scarce and often too fragile to sit. The sit you imagine is a sit in a reproduction cover.

Leather is the dinner friend. Spanish leather chairs, Low Countries rooms, a back you can wipe. Thornton’s comparisons keep England from looking like the only story. A dining-chair series that stays in London will miss the wipeable back. Turkey-work is a needle labor, often women’s labor in the household or specialist work you should not collapse into “upholstery” as if it were one man with a hammer. I will not invent a workshop’s gender roster. I will say the cover is a separate trade from the frame, and dinner attacks the cover.

The Farthingale chair — wide, often armless, late sixteenth and early seventeenth century — is a clothing fact. Skirts. Dining is not the first job. The lesson for later side chairs is the armless wide seat. Arms fight clothes. Arms also fight aprons. The two fights are cousins.

Recut legs show up early. A chair built for one board gets shortened for another. Museums recut too. Measure, then doubt. I have seen a paneled back loosen at the shoe after a holiday of tilting. The carving was still pretty. The chair was a parallelogram.

## Pairing and shop

A refectory table wants length, not a place setting. Benches still do that job. A backed chair at the end is rank. A row of leather sides along a shorter table is the beginning of the later set — individual places, wipeable seats, a back that lets the course multiply. Ask which pairing the client means when they say Renaissance.

If they want the photograph, they want high board-backs and candles. If they want the sit, I would rather build or find a leather-backed side chair with honest stretchers, a seat you can wipe, and a height that meets the actual apron. I know which one I would eat in. I know which one photographs.

Place settings will later freeze the individualization the backed chair already started. This is my cushion. This is my arms. The stool did not say mine. The bench said ours. The Renaissance back says stay, and then it has to survive the stay.
