A dining chair racks. That is the test the parlor chair can dodge and dinner cannot. Someone leans to tell a story. Someone drags the chair in with a foot. A child tilts onto the back legs. The rectangle of rails wants to become a parallelogram. If the joints are cylinders in end grain, the holes oval and the glue shines. If the joints are shoulders and cheeks — a tenon in a post, a stretcher that is a real beam — the box argues back.

The furniture-craft pack already wrote a chair that tries to walk as a joinery hour. I will not steal that lede. This hour is the meal: the lean, the scoot, the weekly abuse a dining chair takes that a hall chair does not.

## Where the lean goes

The rear posts and the side rails take the storyteller. The front rail takes the scoot-in. Stretchers take the foot and the rack. A stretcherless high-style chair is a line that dinner will invoice. I am not pious in a museum. I am pious in a kitchen.

Through-tenons and pins are readable. Dowels in a front rail are a short lever. Loose tenons with honest cheeks are a modern tool that can still be a shoulder. Biscuits are alignment. Confirmat screws in ply are a different product.

Hide glue in a hot kitchen is reversible and mortal. PVA creeps. Epoxy hides a gap you should have recut. None of them replace a closed shoulder. Dry-fit until the chair can be picked up by one rail without a wink.

## Dinner-specific abuse

Salt and a wet wipe at the joint line. A kick from a vacuum. Dragging across a grit floor. Hanging a purse on a pierced splat. Stacking chairs that were not drawn to stack. Flat-pack bolts that loosen every August.

A café 14 expects a screwdriver. An oak mortise chair expects a closed shoulder and a pin. Do not treat them as the same maintenance.

## Historical boxes

Windsors rack in the seat holes; wedges are the answer. Hitchcocks rack in repeating mortises. Chippendales rack in the rear post at the splat shoe. Mission chairs rack less in feeling because the section is huge; they still rack if the slat is only parked. Bentwood racks at the screws. Tubular steel racks at the welds and the mounts.

Each type has a tell. Learn the tell before you buy six.

## Shop practice

We test a prototype with a lean and a scoot, not with a brochure. We put a stretcher where the drawing wanted air if the chair is for a house with children. We do not dowel a front rail and then talk about generations.

A customer who wants a stretcherless saber can have it with a laminated curve and a warning, or they can have stretchers and a dinner. I know which I will sit in.

Arkansas oak likes a through-tenon you can see on a trestle. On a side chair the pin can be shy. Either way the shoulder closes. That is the dinner test. The rest is fashion above the rail.

## The lean I watch in a room

A twelve-year-old leans to finish a story. An adult does the same thing with a wine glass and a longer sentence. The rear posts take that load as a pair of columns that want to rotate in the side-rail mortises. If the shoulder is closed and the tenon has cheeks, the box argues back. If the joint is a dowel cluster in end grain, the holes start oval before the year is out. I have tipped chairs in dining rooms where the front rail was still pretty and the rear post already winked when you lifted by one side.

The scoot-in is a different force. People drag the chair with a foot, then pull themselves to the plate. The front rail takes a rack in plan. A stretcher under that rail is a beam. A stretcherless saber is a line. I am not pious in a museum. I am pious in a kitchen that eats four nights a week.

Hanging a purse on a pierced Chippendale splat is a third force, closer to a cantilever than a lean. The splat shoe at the rear rail is where that chair racks. I have seen the shoe split while the claw-and-ball feet still photographed as money. The furniture-craft pack already wrote a chair that tries to walk as a joinery hour. This hour is only the meal that walks it.

## What oval holes look like

Turn the chair over. A round mortise that has become a slot is dinner written in geometry. Glue shine in the slot is a later injection. A pin that has loosened and been replaced with a screw is a confession. Corner blocks added with drywall screws to a chair that never had them are a later shop trying to invent a shoulder after the fact. Corner blocks that were original — glued and screwed in a nineteenth-century factory seat frame — are a different document. Learn which.

Through-tenons you can see on the outside of a post are readable even when the finish is dark. Hidden pins want a raking light. Dowels want a magnet if someone used steel, and a guess if they used wood. I do not invent a house tenon thickness. I look at whether the shoulder is closed. Dry-fit until the chair can be picked up by one rail without a wink. That sentence is the shop’s whole test. Glue is a guest at that test, not the host.

Hide glue in a hot kitchen is reversible and mortal. A joint that has opened every August and been re-glued every September is a joint that never had a shoulder. PVA creeps under a lean. Epoxy hides a gap you should have recut. Polyurethane glue foams into a cavity and looks like work. None of them replace a closed cheek. I will recut a tenon before I will fill a gap and call it a repair.

## Type-by-type tells at the table

Windsors rack in the seat holes. The legs are round tenons in a plank. Wedges in the tops of the legs are the answer; a missing wedge is a chair that will walk when you drag it. Spindles that have loosened in the bow are a different rattle. Paint hides both until you sit.

Hitchcocks rack in repeating mill mortises. The stretchers are why those light painted boxes still exist. A loose front stretcher on a stenciled fancy is Thanksgiving waiting to happen. Knockdown ancestry means some joints were assembled by someone who was not the turner. A later glue-up that turned the mill product into a cabinetmaker’s object can hide that. When you repair, decide which honesty you are keeping.

Chippendales rack in the rear post at the splat shoe, and in the side rails if the tenons were thin for the fashion of the line. A pastry-thin Gothic piercing is a second weakness above the rail; people hang coats on it. Mission chairs rack less in feeling because the section is huge. They still rack if the slat is only parked in a groove and the shoulder never closed. A Stickley that has been “tightened” with a table key in the wrong place is a sermon that forgot its joint.

Bentwood racks at the screws. A Thonet 14 expects a screwdriver; that is maintenance, not a defect. Tubular steel racks at the welds and at the cane or wood mounts. A Cesca whose cane frame has loosened from the tube will rack in two materials at once. Plastic stackers rack at the bosses and at the metal frames if they have them. Flat-pack bolts loosen every August. Do not treat a café 14 and an oak mortise chair as the same product with different hats.

## Dinner-specific abuse I will name

Salt and a wet wipe at the joint line. A kick from a vacuum. Dragging across a grit floor — grit is a lapidary shop for tenons. Stacking chairs that were not drawn to stack, so the crest of one bites the seat rail of another and starts a rack from a dent. Flat-pack chairs whose confirmat screws in ply were never a shoulder. A child who tilts onto the back legs as a sport. An adult who does the same thing and calls it relaxing.

A wet kitchen that swells and shrinks the posts around a tight tenon will loosen a hide-glue joint on a seasonal clock. That is not a reason to switch to epoxy. It is a reason to keep the shoulder closed so the joint can work as wood, not as a gap filled with a polymer. I have taken chairs apart in September that looked sound in January. The wink was already there in the dry-fit if anyone had picked the chair up by one rail.

## Failure cases

The stretcherless saber at a table with children is the failure I refuse most often. The customer wants the line. The line wants a laminated curve and a warning, or it wants stretchers and a dinner. I know which I will sit in. A laminated saber can hold if the lamination is real and the side-rail shoulder is still a shoulder. A bandsawn saber from a wide board with a dowel into end grain is a Thanksgiving break.

Doweling a front rail and then talking about generations is the other failure. The front rail is a short lever. Cylinders in end grain oval. I have replaced those rails. The new rail gets a tenon.

A repair that adds a stretcher to a chair that never had one can be honest if you say so and if the mortises are cut as mortises. A stretcher screwed to the inside of the rails with corner irons is a confession you can see from the floor. I would rather cut the mortise.

Buying six chairs from a photograph of the splat, without a lean test, is how a dining room acquires a parallelogram. I test a prototype with a lean and a scoot, not with a brochure. I put a stretcher where the drawing wanted air if the chair is for a house with children. Arkansas oak will take that stretcher. The pin can be shy on a side chair. The shoulder still closes.
