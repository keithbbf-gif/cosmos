A ladderback is slats in a pair of posts, like rungs, a seat of rush or splint or Shaker tape, turned or shaved posts, often a finial that catches a dust rag. It is older than Mission and thinner. It stacks and hangs better than a claw. It sat at farm tables and then at 1930s “Early American” dinettes when factories needed a chair that read as ancestor.

The sibling country-revival essay owns the later room mood. This hour is the slat against your back, and the difference between a chair that was always this and a chair that was sold as memory.

## The ladder as a sit

Each slat is a bar. Three or four slats are a common count; more is a taller back. Placement is everything, as with Stickley, but the section is slighter. You get a grate. Air. A little give if the slats are thin and the posts can spring.

A slat that hits the lumbar as a ridge is a bad ladderback for you, not a bad type. Move to another chair. There is no knob.

Rush and tape are the seats I trust. A factory plywood seat in a “country” ladderback is a dinette. Splint can be handsome and hard on bare legs. A cushion tied on is household history; it changes height.

Finials are how you hang the chair on a peg in the story, and how you break a finial in the truck. I like a clean post more than a fancy finial on a chair that will be stacked.

## Shaker, farm, factory

Shaker slat-backs are a documented shop culture: tape seats, a theology of the shared table, benches in the same room. Museum objects at Hancock and Pleasant Hill are the trail. Do not call every tape seat Shaker.

Southern farm ladderbacks — including chairs made by enslaved and later Black turners whose names inventories swallowed — are a line that later rooms called “primitive.” Look at the turnings. Look at the rush. Name the labor when the context is a plantation or a named shop.

Factory Revival ladderbacks of the twentieth century can be decent sitters if the slats are placed and the rush is real. They can also be dowels and a decal. The 1930s dinette pair — enamel table, thin ladderbacks — is a depression and a style. The sibling dinette essay owns the table. Here: the chair is light, often too light, and the slats are close to a Hitchcock mill’s economy.

Nutting and the Colonial Revival taught a nation what “old” looked like. A ladderback in a 1925 catalog is not 1725. Date the catalog.

## Stacking, hanging, dinner

These chairs can stand in a row against a wall. They can hang. They can, in some patterns, nest a little. That is why kitchens kept them. A dining room that needs to become a floor for a fiddle or a visiting bed still understands this type.

At table the posts are thinner than Mission and kinder to the person walking behind with a platter. Arms are less common; when they exist they are low and rural and will fight an apron or not, depending.

I like a good ladderback at a harvest table more than at a pedestal mahogany. The pair should agree about work.

## New oak

An Arkansas shop can make this chair without lying. Turned posts, slats mortised through or into, a rush or leather seat, a height paired to the table you sell. That is a legitimate dining chair. Distressing it to look like 1840 is the lie. Let the oak be this year’s oak. Hands will do the rest.

Do not call it Chippendale. Do not call it Stickley. Call it a slat-back and sit in it for an hour. If the slats are wrong, move them on the next one. That is a shop luxury the factory Revival did not always take.

## How a slat is placed

A ladderback is a grate. Three slats are a common farm count. Four or five make a taller back and a different hit. Placement is the sit, as with Stickley, but the section is slighter and the posts can spring if the stock is honest. A slat that lands as a ridge across the low back is a bad chair for that spine, not a bad type. There is no knob. There is a height.

I sit, I find the contact, I mark it with a piece of tape on the post if we are making the next one. Factory Revival chairs cannot take that mark. Their slats were a mill’s increment. You live with the grate or you move to another chair.

Mortise through the post and you can see the slat’s end as a small honest rectangle. Stopped mortise and the post stays a turning. Both are legitimate. A dowel and glue pretending to be a slat is a dinette. Wiggle the slat. If it rotates, dinner will tick.

Posts are turned or shaved. Sausage turnings, ball turnings, a plain taper — the vocabulary is country and it is also catalog. Finials catch a dust rag and break in a truck. I like a clean post on a chair that will be stacked or hung. A ball finial is a story. It is also a handle for a child.

The front stretcher takes the feet and the rack. A missing front stretcher on a “cleaned-up” Revival chair is a chair that will walk. Look before you buy six.

## Rush, tape, splint, plywood

Rush is the seat I trust on this type when the underside still looks like rush — butts, color, a weave that was not printed. A too-even blond rush is a recent hand. That is not a sin. It is a date. Re-rushing is expected. Original rush with an oxidized underside is a prize. Plywood dropped into a rush frame is a lost diary.

Shaker tape — cotton or wool webbing, a checker, a seat you can retape — is a documented shop culture at Hancock and Pleasant Hill. Do not call every tape seat Shaker. The theology of the shared table is a museum sentence. The sit is a firm, slightly grippy plane that does not wipe like leather and does not sag like old rush on the same clock.

Splint can be handsome and hard on bare legs. It rasps. It also lasts. A cushion tied on is household history; it changes height and it hides the weave you paid for.

A factory plywood seat in a “country” ladderback is a 1930s or later dinette wearing slats. Fine if you know it. A lie if the listing says 1840. The sibling dinette essay owns the enamel table. Here the chair is light, often too light, and the slats are close to a Hitchcock mill’s economy.

## An hour against the slats

I sat an hour in a three-slat farm chair with a rush seat that had started to hammock. The first slat found my back. The second was air. The third was a shoulder I did not need. The hammock stole a little height and gave me a dish. Dinner was possible. A fourth slat on a Revival cousin in the same room hit me as a bar. I moved.

At a harvest table these chairs are in their language: thin posts, a platter can pass, the chair can live against a wall when the fiddle needs the floor. At a pedestal mahogany they look like visitors. The pair should agree about work.

They hang. They line a wall. Some patterns nest a little. That is why kitchens kept them. A dining room that must become a bed for a visitor still understands this type. Finials make hanging easy and make stacking mean. Choose the post for the storage you will actually do.

Arms, when they exist, are low and rural. They will fight an apron or they will sneak under a shallow skirt. Measure the path. A ladderback armchair is not a Phyfe host. It is a farm chair that grew a rest.

An Arkansas shop can make this chair without lying. Turned posts, slats mortised, a rush or leather seat, a height paired to the table you sell. Distressing it to look like 1840 is the lie. Let the oak be this year’s oak. If the slats are wrong on the first one, move them on the second. That luxury is the whole difference between a shop and a Revival mill that had already printed the grate.
