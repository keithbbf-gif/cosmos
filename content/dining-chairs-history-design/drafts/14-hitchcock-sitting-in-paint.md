The stencil on the seat rail is a factory’s mouth. The dining-room pack already walked Hitchcocksville: the brick mill, the bronze powder, the weekly counts in the Henry Ford account, the women who stenciled, the rush. I will not rebuild that floor. I want what happens when you sit in the chair at a table that is not a museum riser.

A Hitchcock at dinner is a light painted frame with a seat that breathes and sags, a tablet or slat that hits a narrow band of back, and stretchers you will find with your heels. It is closer to a fancy Windsor than to a Phyfe Grecian, even when the outline quotes a tablet. Paint is the wood. Rush is the cushion. Both fail in houses that eat.

## The sit is the rush

Rush is a plant seat. Tight, it is springy and a little raspy through a cloth. Loose, it is a hammock that drops you toward the rails. Original rush with an oxidized butt on the underside is a document. A later rush that is too even and too blond is a recane’s cousin. Plywood in a marked frame is a common repair and a lost sit. I would rather see honest new rush than a board.

Cane, when it appears, is cooler and more even. It prints less of a grid on the thighs than a bad rush sag. Both want a table that does not dump crumbs into the weave as a lifestyle. Both can be wiped less easily than leather and more honestly than foam.

Seat height on the Ford’s marked side chair is in the mid-thirties overall; the seat itself lands in a dining band `[VERIFY live label]`. Pair it with a table from the 1820s–40s world, not with a 36-inch island. People who call Hitchcocks “children’s chairs” are often at the wrong counter.

## Paint is structure you can ruin

The maple or birch under a Hitchcock was not the show. Grounds run black, dark green, simulated rosewood. Strip the paint and you have a pale turned frame that was never meant to be read as timber. You have also removed the brand. I will say this as often as the mill essay does, because sitters still ask to “show the wood.”

Bronze-powder fruit on the tablet is closer to your shoulder blades than you think. You do not lean into it the way you lean into a solid splat; you meet a slat or a pillow of a tablet. The decoration survives if people sit like adults. It dies if coats are hung on the crest.

A later Kenney revival chair can look like 1828 from across a room. The sit may be a twentieth-century rush and a heavier hand. Date the hardware, the stencil wording, the seat. Do not date the feeling.

## Lightness, tilt, and the mill joint

These chairs are easy to tilt. Rear posts take the storyteller. Stretchers — front, side, medial — are why the box still exists. Hide glue in a kitchen that goes to 90 in August is a guest. The mill knew that; the kiln on the second floor, in Braden’s account, is a process fact I will not re-narrate. The dining fact is: a loose stretcher is a chair that will walk at Thanksgiving.

Knockdown ancestry means some joints were designed to be assembled by someone who was not the turner. A tight later glue-up can hide that. When you repair, decide whether you are restoring a mill product or turning the chair into a cabinetmaker’s object. I prefer the mill’s honesty: repeating mortises, paint, a seat that can be done again.

## Dinner and the pretty rail

Gravy on bronze powder is a conservation problem. So is a wet glass on a stenciled tablet. These were middle-class dining chairs, not altar pieces, and they still do not want a soak. A tablecloth helps the table more than the chair. The chair wants sitters who do not hang purses on the crest and a floor that is not gravel.

At a crowded table the lightness is a gift. You can pull six in and out without a groan. Arms, when they exist, are fancy-chair arms and will fight a deep apron. Side chairs are the dinner type.

Southern shipments of parts and chairs — the mill essay has Charleston in the early parts trade — mean you will find the type far from the Farmington River. Sitting in one in a Southern dining room is not a New England costume if the chair actually traveled. It is a distribution fact.

## What I tell a buyer

If you want the look and a weekly roast, budget for re-rush in a decade or two. If you want to strip the paint, buy a different chair. If your table is a thick farm apron, sit in the Hitchcock at that table before you buy six. The tablet may be fine and the knee may not.

A new painted fancy in oak or maple, honestly turned, with a rush or tape seat, is a legitimate descendant. A dark spray stain and a decal is not. The sit will tell you in ten minutes.

I like these chairs at a breakfast table more than at a five-course performance, which is my prejudice about paint and rush, not a law. They were dinner chairs for a lot of Americans who never owned a claw. That is enough dignity.

## Heels, stretchers, and the light box

The first thing my feet find in a Hitchcock is a stretcher. Front, sides, often a medial bar — a box you can map with your heels without looking. That box is why a chair this light still exists after a century of scoots. A missing medial stretcher is a chair that will walk. A replaced stretcher in a paler wood, painted to match, is honest mill maintenance if the joints are tight. A stretcher glued into a blown hole is a chair that will walk again after August.

Because the frame is light, people tilt. The storyteller goes onto the rear posts and the rear stretchers take a load they were not drawn as a lounge to take. I have watched a guest do it through pie and then apologize to the chair. The apology does not reset hide glue. If the household tilts, I want to see the rear posts for checks and the seat for a racked rhombus. A mill chair can be re-squared. It cannot be wished back into square.

Children love the lightness. They can drag a Hitchcock the way they cannot drag a claw-and-ball. That is a household gift and a joint tax. Felt on the feet and a rule about not riding the tilt beat promoting the chair to the parlor. A racked chair puts one front rail high and the rush goes slack on the low side.

## Rush as a diary, not a cushion

A tight rush seat has a crown. You feel a slight rise under the sit bones and a rasp through a thin cloth. That crown is the mill’s mercy. When the rush dries and the meals accumulate, the crown becomes a valley toward the side rails. Then the rails themselves become the sit. People add a pad. The pad steals height and hides the diary. I would rather re-rush on the old schedule than sit a pad on a hammock and call the chair comfortable.

Look at the underside. Oxidized rush, irregular butts, a color that is not store-blond — that is a seat that has been a seat. A too-even weave with clean paper-colored twists is a recent hand, which is fine if you name it. Plywood dropped into the frame is a different chair. The sit becomes a plate. The brand on the rail then advertises a product the seat has resigned.

Cane in a fancy painted frame is the cooler cousin. It drums. It prints a grid. It fails at the holes if the rail splits. Both rush and cane want crumbs kept off as a habit, not as a religion. A tablecloth helps the table. A sitter who does not grind salt into the weave helps the chair. I have cleaned gravy out of rush with a patience I would not wish on a weeknight. Budget the re-rush. Do not budget a miracle.

## Paint, bronze, and the shoulder

The ground — black, dark green, a rosewood grain that is paint pretending to be a log — is the chair’s skin. Scratch it and you see pale maple or birch that was never the show. A stripper’s Hitchcock is a turned skeleton. Collectors still ask for “the wood.” I keep saying no. The stencil on the rail is a name. The bronze-powder fruit on the tablet is a surface you will lean near. Sit like a person who knows the tablet is painted, not like a person hanging a coat on a crest.

Bronze powder dulls. Gravy spots it. A wet glass on a stenciled tablet leaves a ring that conservation will not call charming. These were middle-class dining chairs. They still do not want a soak. I wipe them as I would wipe a painted door: damp, not wet, and I do not use the kitchen’s citrus oil as a personality.

A Kenney revival chair after 1946 can carry the name and a later rush. The sit may be heavier, the turning a little thicker, the stencil a sequel. Date the rail wording and the seat, not the feeling across a room. Nostalgia is not a construction date.

## Pair the light chair to a real table

A Hitchcock wants a table from its own height world: a drop-leaf, a breakfast table, a dining table in the high 20s to 30 inches, not a 36-inch island and not a thick farm apron that meets the knee before the rush meets you. I sit the actual chair at the actual table. The tablet can be kind and the knee still lose. Side chairs are the dinner type. Fancy arms fight a deep skirt the way any arm does.

At a crowded table the lightness is the gift I keep coming back to. You can pull six in and out without a groan. You can hang two if the household still hangs chairs, though I would rather not hang a stenciled tablet against a peg. The type is closer to a Windsor in that mercy than to a mahogany set that lives where it stands.

Southern rooms that received the type sit in it without costume if the chair actually traveled. I will not rebuild the mill’s shipping story. A painted light chair is easy to crate. You will find the sit far from the Farmington River. That does not make every black stencil a Hitchcock.

## What I tell the shop

A new painted fancy in maple or oak, honestly turned, with a rush or tape seat and tight stretchers, is a descendant I will eat in. A dark spray stain and a decal fruit is a Halloween chair. Ten minutes on the rush will sort them. If the client wants the look and a weekly roast, I write re-rush into the decade and I refuse the strip. If the table is a breadboard monster with a drawer apron, sit the pair before anyone writes a check for six.

Bradley County can turn the posts. It cannot reprint a mill stencil as a proof of year. Honest paint and a seat you can do again are enough dignity for dinner. The mill essay next door can keep the brick and the weekly counts. I keep the heels on the stretcher and the gravy off the bronze.
