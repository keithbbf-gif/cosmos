A Windsor is a plank that learned to hold sticks. The seat is the hub: saddle-shaped if the shop is proud, thick enough to take holes, often elm in English work, pine or poplar in American, painted so the mixed woods disappear. Legs and stretchers and spindles are turned hardwoods — maple, oak, hickory, ash — driven into the seat, wedged, sometimes glued. The back can be a bow, a comb, a fan, a sack-back. There is no splat in the Chippendale sense. There is a forest of sticks and a rim that takes your shoulder blades as a rake of small contacts.

Nancy Goyne Evans wrote the book. Wallace Nutting wrote the Revival. If you date a dining set from a 1917 handbook, you are dating a mood. If you sit in a Philadelphia bow-back at a drop-leaf, you are in the other dining-chair line: kitchen, tavern, porch, merchant breakfast, and also the parlor when the mahogany was for Sundays.

The sibling splat-to-ladder survey already refused to omit Windsors. This hour is the sit and the construction, not a second survey.

## The seat is the joint

Every stick is a round tenon in a hole. The saddle — dished, with a pommel that separates the thighs a little — is comfort carved into the hub. A flat plank is cheaper and meaner. I can feel a good saddle in the first ten seconds. I can feel a router-made “saddle” that is only a dent.

Wedges in the tops of the legs, sawn and glued, keep the legs from pulling out when you drag the chair. Spindles are often not wedged the same way; they rely on a dry, tight hole and paint. Paint is not a cover-up in the honest type. Paint is the finish that lets hickory and pine read as one object. Strip a Windsor and you have committed the Hitchcock crime with different species.

Hickory and ash in the bow want spring. A steam-bent bow that has lost its spring is a tired chair. A broken bow is a specialist repair. Do not sister a bow with a flatish oak from the hardware store and call it original spirit.

## Lightness is a dining feature

You can hang a Windsor. You can carry four. You can put them on a porch when the kitchen is hot. Mahogany claw-and-ball chairs do not hang. That is why farmhouses and later “Early American” dinettes kept the type when they needed to clear a room. Nesting is overstated; they nest less well than the legend. Hanging is real.

At table the sticks give you a back that is a grate. Air moves. A long back can be a comb that supports the shoulders; a low sack-back is a different sit. Neither is a sofa. People who call Windsors uncomfortable are sometimes sitting in a cheap Revival with dowels where through-tenons should be, and a seat that was never saddled.

## Kitchen, tavern, dining parlor

The Windsor is not “the poor man’s Chippendale.” It is a different brief: fast to make in a turning shop, repairable, light, painted, good enough for company if the paint is fresh. Boston and Philadelphia shops made them at a high level. Rhode Island and Connecticut filled the roads. They sat at formal tables and at tavern tables. A dining-room history that only photographs mahogany is a class history.

Fancy Windsors — bamboo turnings, painted decoration — sit next to Hitchcock in the later story. The mill will industrialize the stick chair. The handmade Windsor is already a product, just a shop product.

Nutting’s reproductions after 1917 taught a generation that the ancestral dining chair was a bow-back. Factories agreed and then cheapened the turning. A 1925 Windsor set around a gateleg is Revival dining. A 1765 Philadelphia Windsor at a drop-leaf is the type before it was a costume. Both can be honest if you name the year.

## The sit

Saddle height in good examples often lands in a dining band because the tables were dining tables. Again: pair the objects. A Windsor pulled to a kitchen island is a different number and usually a bad one; the island wants a stool.

The pommel can bother people who want their thighs together. It can help people who want a little separation and a place that does not slide them forward. Spindles hit as a row of fingers. If your back wants a single plane, you will hate this. If your back wants air and a little give, you will not go back to a solid splat on a summer night.

Arms on a sack-back or a continuous-arm Windsor are a dining problem when they meet an apron. Continuous-arm chairs are beautiful and wide. Measure. Side chairs without arms are the workhorses at a crowded table.

Stretchers — H, box, bulbous — keep the legs from walking. A Windsor with loose legs is a chair that was dragged across gravel or stored in a damp shed. Re-wedging is possible. Do not glue a loose leg into a rotten hole and hope.

## Arkansas and the leftover stick

Southern shops made stick chairs. Not every painted spindle chair is a Philadelphia bow. Some are later factory. Some are kitchen. An oak shop in Bradley County asked for “Windsor dining chairs” should decide whether the client wants through-tenons and a real saddle or a catalog silhouette. The silhouette is cheap. The saddle is the hour.

I like a Windsor at breakfast more than at a black-tie dinner, which is a prejudice about paint and sticks, not a law. I have seen a perfect bow-back hold a formal room because the table was simple and the plates were not competing. The chair is light. Let the table be quiet.

Evans for construction and shops. Nutting for the Revival document. A named museum chair for any public dimension. Paint analysis if you are about to strip. You are not about to strip.

The sit through a meal is a rake of small contacts, not a plane. Spindles give a little. A cheap Revival with dowels where through-tenons should be will not. I have sat a good saddle through breakfast and stood up without the numb ham a shallow Federal seat can leave. I have sat a flat factory plank for the same hour and wanted a pad I would not put on a real Windsor.

Paint hid the mix of woods and also took the gravy. Recoat is ordinary. Strip is not. Southern shops made stick chairs that are not Philadelphia bows; some are kitchen, some are later factory. Name the year if you can. Do not hang Nutting’s 1917 mood on a 1765 seat.

If the client wants eight matching Windsors around a carved pedestal, I ask whether they want the chairs to look like they lost a fight with the table. Pair first. Through-tenons second. Silhouette last. Stretchers that still lock matter more than bamboo turning that photographs as fancy. A loose H-stretcher on a gravel-worn chair is a sit I will not send to dinner until the wedges are honest again.
