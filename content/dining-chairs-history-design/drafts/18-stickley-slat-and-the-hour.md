Gustav Stickley’s dining chair is a set of posts and slats that refuse to disappear. Oak — often quartersawn, the flake showing — a leather or rush seat, a back that is a wall of horizontals or verticals depending on the model, corbels if the shop is speaking the full dialect. *The Craftsman* sold this as a way to live. The dining-room pack already put Stickley at the Mission table. I want the hour you spend in the slats.

The sit is upright, broad, a little unyielding. You do not forget you are in a chair. That is the point and the complaint.

## Slats as a plane

A slat-back is older than Stickley — ladderbacks, Shaker, country — but Mission slats are thicker and the posts are architecture. Contact is a set of bars. A thin pad or a leather sling changes the feel; a hard leather on a hard frame is a long two hours if the slat hits your lumbar as a ridge. Some models place a wider splat or a leather pad at the right height. Those are the ones I would eat in.

Prairie high-backs — Wright’s dining chairs that make a room inside the room — are a related occupation of air. The sibling Prairie essay owns that table. Here: a high slat-back at dinner blocks the person across from you and holds you in a stall. That can be intimacy. It can be a fence. Measure the conversation, not only the inches.

## Leather, rush, and the oak flake

Leather wipes. That is dinner. It also cold-cracks in a dry house and stains if someone believes in saddle soap as a personality. Rush is the older seat in the same moral universe. A reproduction “Mission” with a vinyl seat is a dinette in a costume.

Quartersawn white oak is the show. Flake is not a defect. A southern red oak will not flake the same way; do not promise a Craftsman catalog face from the wrong log. Bradley County can cut honest oak posts. It cannot reprint Syracuse. That is fine. Honest posts are enough.

Fumed finishes and dark stains hide the flake and also hide repairs. I would rather see a lighter oak that will take a century of hands than a black chair that photographs as “period” and shows every scratch as a white line.

## The chair that occupies

Claw-and-ball chairs occupy with carving. Mission chairs occupy with section. You feel the posts when you walk behind. You feel them when you vacuum. A dining room of eight wide posts is a forest. Stacking is a joke. Hanging is a fantasy. These chairs live where they stand. The specialized dining room and the unstackable chair, as the splat-to-ladder essay said, arrive together. Mission is a late, heavy version of that occupancy.

Arms on Mission dining chairs are often at a height that fights a thick tabletop more than a thin apron. The corbel under the arm is a place crumbs go. Wipe it.

## Factory and the sermon

Stickley is a factory with a magazine. Other firms — Limbert, the lesser marks, the later reproductions — flood the type. Shop marks matter if you are paying Stickley money. The sit does not always matter to the mark. I have sat in unmarked slat-backs that were kinder than a famous chair whose slats were placed for a drawing.

Labor is factory and finishing-room labor. The romance of the lone craftsman is part of what the magazine sold. Name the shop when you can. Do not name a monk.

## The hour

An hour in a good Mission chair is a firm, square sit that keeps you at the plate. A third hour depends on the slat placement and the leather. People who work at these chairs as desks often add a cushion and then a backache. People who eat at them and then leave for a sofa are using the type as designed.

I like a Mission side chair at a trestle or a square oak table that can take the visual weight. I do not like one at a glass-top pedestal. The posts look lonely and the glass shows the crumbs on the corbel.

If a client wants “Stickley dining chairs” in new work, I want to know whether they want the sermon or the sit. The sit is slat placement, seat height, leather thickness, and an apron gap. The sermon is a magazine. I can build the first. I will not reprint the second.

## Where the slat actually hits

I sit, then I slide a hand behind my back. A Mission slat that meets the blade as a wide, slightly eased bar can hold an hour without becoming a ridge. A slat that meets you as a square edge at the exact height of a belt will write itself into the evening. Some models place a wider splat or a leather pad in that band. Those are the chairs I would keep for a long Sunday. The thin verticals that photograph as a fence are a different sit: more air, more chance you never find a plane, more chance you slump and then blame the type.

Ladderbacks and Shaker slats are older and usually thinner. Mission slats are architecture. You feel thickness as soon as you lean. That thickness is the sermon made tactile. It is also a contact that does not forgive a bad height. There is no hoop to catch a range of spines. There is a set of bars. If the household is mixed in height, a pad that ties on and walks is the usual edit. The pad steals the finished seat number. Measure after it compresses.

Prairie high-backs — Wright’s dining chairs that make a room inside the room — occupy air in a related way. The sibling Prairie essay owns that table. Here I only need the stall: a high slat-back at dinner can block the face across from you and hold you in a bay. That can be intimacy. It can be a fence. I measure the conversation as well as the inches. A medium Stickley side chair is already a wall. A prairie-height back is a room. Do not buy the height from a photograph of a hall.

## Leather, rush, and the hour

Leather wipes. That is dinner. It also cold-cracks in a dry house and stains if someone believes in saddle soap as a personality. A good Mission leather is a firm skin over webbing or a board, not a squashy lounge. Too thin and you feel the frame as a tray. Too thick and you have stolen height and built a bounce the posts did not ask for. I press the seat, wait, measure floor to sit. The number is the pair with the table, not the catalog’s “deep seat.”

Rush is the older seat in the same moral universe: textured, a little raspy, a diary of sag. A reproduction “Mission” with a vinyl seat is a dinette in a costume. Vinyl wipes. Vinyl sweats. Vinyl is not the Craftsman page. I will sit it in a house that needs wipeability and I will not call it Stickley money.

## Posts in the aisle, crumbs on the corbel

Claw-and-ball chairs occupy with carving. Mission chairs occupy with section. You feel the posts when you walk behind with a platter. You feel them when you vacuum. A dining room of eight wide posts is a forest. Stacking is a joke. Hanging is a fantasy. These chairs live where they stand. The specialized dining room and the unstackable chair arrive together; Mission is a late, heavy version of that occupancy.

Arms on Mission dining chairs often sit at a height that fights a thick tabletop more than a thin apron. The corbel under the arm is a shelf for crumbs. Wipe it. I have run a finger along a corbel after a holiday and found a geology of gravy. The moral furniture still eats. Side chairs along the length are the workhorses. The armchair is the host if the household still wants a head. Measure the arm to the underside of the skirt with the chair pulled in as if someone were cutting.

A trestle or a square oak table can take the visual weight. A glass-top pedestal cannot. The posts look lonely and the glass shows the crumbs on the corbel. I have sat a good slat-back at a thin oak top and understood the fame. I have sat the same outline at a glass disc and felt as if the chair had been left in the wrong century on purpose.

## Factory, marks, and the magazine

Stickley is a factory with a magazine. *The Craftsman* sold a life. Other firms — Limbert, the lesser marks, the later reproductions — flood the type. Shop marks matter if you are paying Stickley money. The sit does not always follow the mark. I have sat in unmarked slat-backs that were kinder than a famous chair whose slats were placed for a drawing.

Labor is factory and finishing-room labor. The romance of the lone craftsman is part of what the magazine sold. Name the shop when you can. Do not name a monk. I will not invent a production total or a weekly count. I will not flatten the later Stickley revivals into the first decade of the century. Date the hardware, the seat, the proportion. A 1980s “Mission” with a foam pad is a sit, not a document.

Through-tenons that show on the posts are the honesty people stroke. They are also a place the finish fails and a place a sloppy copy fakes with a painted end grain. Look. A pinned tenon that is proud and clean is a joint. A decal of a tenon is a drawing.

## What I will build

New work in Arkansas oak can take the sit without reprinting the magazine: posts with meat, slats eased where a back will live, a leather or rush seat numbered to the table. I will not fume a pale oak into a photograph of 1905 if the household wants to see flake and hands.

An hour in a good Mission chair is a firm, square sit that keeps you at the plate. A third hour depends on the slat and the leather. People who work at these chairs as desks often add a cushion and then a backache. People who eat at them and then leave for a sofa are using the type as designed. I like that honesty. I do not like the chair as a workday. Dinner is the brief.
