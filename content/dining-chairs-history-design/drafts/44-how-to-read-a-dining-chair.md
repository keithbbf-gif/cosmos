Turn the chair over. The listing photograph will not. Corner blocks, screws that do not belong, a seat that is plywood in a rush frame, a stretcher that was added, a stretcher that was removed, a front rail that has been planed to take a thicker pad. The meal writes underneath.

Then sit, if you are allowed. Then look at the table you will actually use. Reading a dining chair without a table is reading half a verb.

## Frame

Species, if you can. Secondary woods on American chairs are a dating and a shop clue — pine, poplar, chestnut, a later replacement. Look at the mortises. Look at the pins. Look at dowel holes that ovaled. Look at the rear post where the lean lives.

A CNC Gothic with pastry bridges will show in the thickness of the piercing. A real shop splat will show in the meat they left.

Marks: Hitchcock stencils, Stickley shop marks, Thonet paper labels, IKEA codes, nothing. Nothing is common. Do not invent a maker from a vibe.

## Seat

Rush underside. Cane spline. Haircloth split. Foam slab. Springs. Slip seat. The diary. Date the frame anyway.

## Feet and tips

Recut ankles, new tips, castors that raised the sit, castors that were removed and left a hole. Rake changes here. Height changes here. A “short” antique is often a recut antique.

## Back

Where it hits you. A tablet, a hoop, a grate, a shell, a Y, a pastry piercing. Snags. Repairs at the narrow waist of a vase splat. A bow that has lost spring.

## The pair

Tape A and G from the measuring essay. If you cannot sit at the table, sit at a table of the same underside height. Stores hate this. Do it.

If the chair is a host, walk the arm in.

If you will have children, tilt it. If you will have a wheelchair at the meal, see whether the chair can leave.

## Money

Pay for closed shoulders and a seat you can do again. Do not pay 1810 prices for a 1998 foam and a decal. Do not pay Wishbone prices for a nylon weave and a flat Y. Provenance is extra after the sit is true.

A southern oak new chair should be priced as a new chair with named joints, not as a faded mill postcard.

## What I write down

Finished seat, underside of table, remainder, depth, edge, arms, one sentence about the back contact, one sentence about the underside of the chair. That is enough to keep you from buying a drawing.

This series is a set of those sentences with history attached. If you only keep one method, keep the underside of both objects and the lean on the rear post. Dinner will find both.

## Turn it over before you believe the splat

The listing photograph will not turn the chair over. I do. Corner blocks, screws that do not belong, a seat that is plywood in a rush frame, a stretcher that was added, a stretcher that was removed, a front rail that has been planed to take a thicker pad. The meal writes underneath.

Species, if you can. Secondary woods on American chairs are a dating and a shop clue — pine, poplar, chestnut, a later replacement. A mahogany show face with a pine seat frame is a common American fact, not a fraud. A later oak rail in a walnut chair is a repair. Look at the mortises. Look at the pins. Look at dowel holes that ovaled. Look at the rear post where the lean lives. A round mortise that has become a slot is dinner written in geometry.

A CNC Gothic with pastry bridges will show in the thickness of the piercing. A real shop splat will show in the meat they left. I do not need a laboratory for that. I need raking light and a willingness to look at the waist of a vase splat where repairs hide.

Marks: Hitchcock stencils, Stickley shop marks, Thonet paper labels, IKEA codes, nothing. Nothing is common. Do not invent a maker from a vibe. A bronze-powder fruit on a tablet is a factory’s mouth if the rail says so. A decal that says a name the mill never used is a sequel. Date the hardware, the stencil wording, the seat. Do not date the feeling.

## The seat is a diary; date the frame anyway

Rush underside: oxidized butt or blond even twist. Cane: hand holes or a spline, or a plastic sheet stapled to pine. Haircloth: shine and a split at the front rail. Foam: a slab, a smell, a yellow stain on the webbing. Springs: a voice, or a hole with a board over it. Slip seat: the honesty of a seat that was meant to be done again. The seat essay already took materials. This hour only needs the diary as a reading.

A 1790 shield with a 1950 foam pad is a dining chair that stayed in use. A 1790 shield with a perfect period-style haircloth and no wear is a chair that has been curated. Both can be honest if the listing is. Do not date the frame by the present seat alone. I will keep saying that until a listing stops saying original condition about a 1998 foam.

## Feet, tips, and the height that moved

Recut ankles, new tips, castors that raised the sit, castors that were removed and left a hole. Rake changes here. Height changes here. A short antique is often a recut antique. I have measured chairs whose style book wanted eighteen inches and whose feet had been cut to meet a later thick-top table, or cut to replace rot, and now sit at sixteen and a bit with a rake that dumps you into a lounge. The rake essay owns the lounge. This hour owns the saw cut.

Look at the grain at the ankle. A later tip that does not match the post is a sentence. A caster hole filled with a dowel is a sentence. A chair that rocks because one foot was recut more than the others is a sentence you will feel before you see.

## Back, pair, money

Where the back hits you: a tablet, a hoop, a grate, a shell, a Y, a pastry piercing. Snags. Repairs at the narrow waist of a vase splat. A bow that has lost spring. A Mission slat that hits you at the shoulder blades. A Series 7 that hits you almost nowhere and then lets you slouch. Write one sentence about the contact. That sentence is worth more than the style name.

## Failure cases

Buying the photograph of the splat. Trusting a style name without an underside. Paying period money for a Revival seat. Missing the recut foot and then blaming the type for a short sit. Missing the plywood in a rush frame and then blaming rush. Skipping the table because the chair was pretty in a warehouse. Skipping the lean because the listing said sturdy.

I write down finished seat, underside of table, remainder, depth, edge, arms, one sentence about the back contact, one sentence about the underside of the chair. That is enough to keep you from buying a drawing.

## Sit, if you are allowed

Then sit. A reading that never leaves the underside is a cabinetmaker’s reading. Dinner is a body. I sit before I write the sentence about the back contact. A shield point in the spine, a hoop that holds, a grate that prints a shirt, a Y that is a bar if you slouch, a Mission slat at the shoulder blades, a Series 7 that lets you disappear into a C — those are sits. The style name will not tell you which one you have.

I have sat in a labeled Phyfe-style saber that was a later factory echo and a good sit, and in a labeled “period” balloon-back whose springs had been boarded over and whose sit was a box. The label was not the chair. Winterthur and MESDA period rooms sometimes reseat; a museum chair can be a document of a later seat. Read the room’s note. Then sit if the room allows it. If it does not, sit a cousin at a table of the same underside height.

In a shop I do the lean after the sit. Pick the chair up by one rail. If it winks, the shoulder is open. If it does not, the box may still fail a twelve-year-old, but you have a starting honesty. Dinner will find the underside and the lean. Write both down.
