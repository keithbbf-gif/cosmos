I keep a list of sentences that wreck a purchase. They are not always wrong. They are underspecified. Dinner is a pair and a duration. A myth is a sentence that forgets one of those.

## “Old chairs are uncomfortable”

Sometimes the chair was recut. Sometimes the table is new and thick. Sometimes the pad is gone and the haircloth is honest. Sometimes the chair was a parlor seat. Sometimes you are sitting in a Revival that was always cheap. Measure. Then insult the right object.

A good 1810 seat at a 1810 table can be a better pair than a 2020 foam throne at a 36-inch island.

## “Soft means comfortable”

Soft bottoms. Soft raises you into the apron and then dies. Soft on a cane frame is a person who bought the wrong type. A firm sit that lasts two hours beats a heavenly ten minutes. The long-dinner essay is this myth’s opposite number.

## “I need lumbar support”

You need a contact you can live with for the length of your meal, and a height and a gap that do not make you crunch. You do not need a knob on a vase splat. If a medical need exists, sit the chair, do not buy a word.

## “Arms are more comfortable”

Arms are more comfortable when they clear and when you want a rest. They are a fence when they hit. Six arms are a server’s injury. Two arms at the ends are a leftover inscription that can be useful.

## “They have to match”

The sit has to match. The splat can be a conversation. Eight identical pastry Gothics at a wrong height are a matched failure. Six sides and two stackers with the same finished seat are a household.

## “Windsors / Hitchcocks / INGOLFs are kids’ chairs”

They are light chairs at a dining height. At an island they are the wrong number. At a 30-inch table they are a type. The insult is usually a counter.

## “Ergonomic dining chair”

That phrase is a foam bump or a task chair in costume until proven otherwise. Ask for numbers: finished seat, underside, depth, edge, duration. If the tag cannot answer, the tag is the product.

## “If it hurts, you have bad posture”

If it hurts, the pair may be wrong. Posture lectures are how stores keep a SKU. Change a height, a pad, an edge, a duration. Then talk about sitting up if you still want to.

## “A bench is always friendlier”

A bench is friendlier until you are in the middle and need out, or until your femur is not the femur the plank was cut for. It is a type, not a virtue.

## Shop practice

I do not argue myths with adjectives. I put a tape on the pair and a plate in front of the sitter. The myth either dies or it becomes a preference we can name.

## The pair is usually the other half of the sentence

I keep the list because stores keep the sentences. They are not always wrong. They are underspecified. Dinner is a pair and a duration. A myth is a sentence that forgets one of those.

“Old chairs are uncomfortable” sometimes means the chair was recut at the feet to match a later table, or recut to replace rot, and the rake and the height both moved. Sometimes the table is new and thick and the apron is a drawer. Sometimes the pad is gone and the haircloth is honest. Sometimes the chair was a parlor seat parked at dinner. Sometimes you are sitting in a Revival that was always cheap. Measure. Then insult the right object.

A good 1810 seat at a 1810 table can be a better pair than a 2020 foam throne at a 36-inch island. People who cannot sit in old chairs are often at the wrong counter. Windsors, Hitchcocks, INGOLFs are light chairs at a dining height. At an island they are the wrong number. At a 30-inch table they are a type. The insult is usually a counter.

“Soft means comfortable” is the myth that sells pads. Soft bottoms. Soft raises you into the apron and then dies. Soft on a cane frame is a person who bought the wrong type. A firm sit that lasts two hours beats a heavenly ten minutes. The long-dinner essay is this myth’s opposite number. I have sat in a chair that was kind at minute ten and gone at minute forty. I have sat in a chair that was a little hard at minute ten and still honest at minute one-twenty. I will cut the second chair.

## Words that are products

“I need lumbar support” is a workstation sentence. You need a contact you can live with for the length of your meal, and a height and a gap that do not make you crunch. You do not need a knob on a vase splat. If a medical need exists, sit the chair. Do not buy a word. The lumbar essay already refused the office doctrine. I will not bring it back as a comfort accessory.

“Ergonomic dining chair” is a foam bump or a task chair in costume until proven otherwise. Ask for numbers: finished seat, underside, depth, edge, duration. If the tag cannot answer, the tag is the product. BIFMA X5.1 and X10.1 and ISO 9241-5 are computer-use papers. They may be named as cousins. They are not a roast.

“Arms are more comfortable” when they clear and when you want a rest. They are a fence when they hit. Six arms are a server’s injury. Two arms at the ends are a leftover inscription that can be useful. The arms essay and the host essay already took geometry and rank. This hour only needs the myth: more timber is not more mercy if the timber hits the apron.

“They have to match” is a photograph. The sit has to match. The splat can be a conversation. Eight identical pastry Gothics at a wrong height are a matched failure. Six sides and two stackers with the same finished seat are a household. I would rather mix a back and keep a height than match a back and mix a sit. Stores hate this. Households that eat do not.

## Posture, benches, and the lecture that keeps a SKU

“If it hurts, you have bad posture” is how a store keeps a chair on the floor. If it hurts, the pair may be wrong. Change a height, a pad, an edge, a duration. Then talk about sitting up if you still want to. A front rail that is a bar will not be fixed by a lecture. A seat that is too high will not be fixed by shoulders back. A foam that has bottomed will not be fixed by a reminder to sit like an adult.

“A bench is always friendlier” until you are in the middle and need out, or until your femur is not the femur the plank was cut for. A hall bench, a refectory plank, a banquette that signed a lease with the wall — those are types, not virtues. The last essay takes the banquette. This hour only needs the myth: a plank is not automatically kind. It is a sit with a depth and a height and a trapped middle.

I do not argue myths with adjectives. I put a tape on the pair and a plate in front of the sitter. The myth either dies or it becomes a preference we can name. A preference is fine. A preference that forgets the apron is a purchase I will try to stop.

## Failure cases I will not invent quotes for

I will not invent a customer sentence. I will name the purchases. Six matched claws at a table whose underside was never taped. A memory-foam pad on a cane Cesca, then a complaint that the table felt high. A task chair with wheels parked at a farm table and called ergonomic dining. A “lumbar” pillow tied to a shield back. Eight arms. A bench ordered from a photograph of a breakfast nook, too deep, so everyone slouched, and then a complaint about old-fashioned posture.

A Revival Hitchcock stripped of paint because someone wanted to show the wood, then called uncomfortable because the rush was gone and a plywood insert was the sit. The mill essay owns the paint. This hour owns the sentence that blamed the type instead of the board.

If you arrived at this series from a search for comfort, start with the gap and the eighteen-inch camp, then the front rail, then duration. Leave the splat for later. Fashion is above the rail. The complaint is usually below it. The myth is a sentence that forgot to measure.
