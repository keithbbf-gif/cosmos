The joint stool is a small table that learned to be sat on. Four legs, often turned, splayed so the footprint is bigger than the seat; rails or stretchers that make a box; a board pegged or nailed or housed on top. English oak, sometimes elm in the seat, the finish worn to a polish where hose and hand hit. Victor Chinnery’s *Oak Furniture* is full of them because houses were full of them. They are not charming until a dealer says so. They are the seating that inventories could afford to list in quantity.

A hall bench is the same economy drawn long. You sit in a row. You face the board. You share the height. The backed chair, when it exists, is a wainscot or an armed seat at the end — architecture for one person. Penelope Eames’s medieval furniture already showed how scarce that back was. The seventeenth-century hall does not reverse the math. It multiplies stools.

## How a joint stool is joined

The name is the method. Joint, not nailed as the whole idea. Legs mortised into the seat rails, or rails mortised into the legs, depending on the shop and the decade — look at the piece, do not recite a single recipe. Stretchers low enough to rest a foot, high enough to let a broom pass if anyone bothered. The splay is not decoration. A vertical four-leg box with a small top tips. A splay turns a shove into a wider stance.

Turned legs are a lathe story. A village turner could make a set. The seat board is the part that fails in a house: split along the grain, worn dished, replaced with a board that does not match. I would rather see a replaced seat on original legs than a “perfect” stool with new everything and an old story.

This is dining height when the table is a board on trestles or a joined table in the same oak family. A joint stool pulled up to a 30-inch Victorian table is often a stretch. People do it anyway. Their shoulders tell.

## The bench as social joinery

A bench can be a plank and trestles, or a joined chest-bench, or a settle that grew a back and became room furniture. The dining bench I mean is the one that lives with the long table: moveable, or light enough to pull, without arms that trap the middle sitter. Getting out of a populated bench is a choreography. Medieval and early modern households accepted the choreography because the alternative was more backed chairs than the timber and the rank allowed.

Settles against a wall give you a back and take away the pull-up. They are winter furniture: a settle by a fire, a table brought or not. A dining series that treats every settle as a dining chair is confused. Sit in one and try to get your knees under a decent apron. The settle was not drawn for that meeting.

Wainscot chairs — paneled backs, often crested, often armed — sit at the high table and weigh like a small door. They are closer to the Egyptian rank problem than to a side chair. You do not make eight without being a college or a court. The joint stool is how the rest of the hall reaches the beef.

## What the sit feels like

No back. You hold yourself or you find a wall. Seat is hard. A cushion appears on Sunday if the house has cloth. Foot on the stretcher is a habit, then a polish, then a wear pattern a conservator can read. You can turn 90 degrees and talk to the person behind you. You can stand and move the stool with one hand. Children stack themselves in ways a splat forbids.

Fatigue is real. A two-hour meal on a joint stool is a core exercise. People who grew up with backed chairs call this uncomfortable. People who grew up with benches call the backed chair fussy and hard to store. Both are describing their training. Ergonomics as a modern science arrives late and then spends most of its language on offices. The joint stool never got a BIFMA test. It got a floor.

Thigh clearance is simple: there is no chair arm and often no chair back to hit the table. The stool goes under. That is a superpower the backed dining chair will spend three centuries trying to match with stacking, hanging, and the “please don’t leave them out” request.

## America, oak, and the leftover stool

New England and the South both know joined stools and benches in early rooms. Survivals are scarcer than English oak piles; climate and fashion took them. When they survive, they get called Pilgrim, which is a Colonial Revival word. Look at the joints and the turnings. Look at whether the seat is a replacement. Do not date a stool by a fireplace in a photograph.

Southern halls and later dogtrots used benches because the table was still a length. Enslaved labor made furniture that later rooms attributed to a family name. If a joint stool or a bench from a plantation context is the object, name the labor problem. Do not use “vernacular” as a bleach.

Arkansas mills in the later timber century cut oak that became other people’s colonial kits. A shop now that is asked for “period stools” should decide whether the client wants a sit or a prop. A prop can be distressed. A sit needs a splay that works and a board that will not fold you.

## Why the type never quite dies

Extra guests. Kitchens. Pubs. School refectories. Picnic tables. The island stool is a cousin at the wrong height. Every Thanksgiving produces a folding metal version of the overflow seat. The joint stool is the wooden ancestor of that overflow, except it was not overflow. It was the set.

When a dining-chair buyer says they need two armchairs and four sides, they are speaking the language that replaced this hour. That language is comfort, rank at the ends, and a photograph of a tablescape. The stool’s language is capacity and a floor that is not level. I would keep one good joint stool in a house that otherwise sits on backed chairs, if only as a measuring stick. If your fancy side chair cannot be more pleasant, for an hour, than a board on four splayed legs, the fancy chair is only a drawing.

Chinnery for the object. Eames for the scarcity of medieval backs. An inventory, named, if you want a count. The rest is the sit: hard, low or not, your own spine, the table an inch from your knee.
