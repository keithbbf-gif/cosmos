Thomas Chippendale’s *Director* (1754, then later editions) does not invent the dining chair. It opens the splat and sells the opening. Ribbon, tassel, Gothic tracery, Chinese fret — the back becomes a drawing you can see the window through, and a catch for a sleeve. A parlor can live with that catch. Dinner, with serving and coats and a reach across a corner, lives with it every course.

The sibling pack already sat in Philadelphia dining rooms. I will not rebuild the port. I want the hole in the wood, the haircloth, the claw, and the way a pierced splat fails when someone hangs a bag on it.

## What the plate is selling

The *Director* is a pattern book for gentlemen and the shops that work for them. Chair plates mix parlor and dining uses; the dining chair is not always labeled as such. What you get is a vocabulary: a yoke crest, a splat that is no longer a plain vase, seats that can be compass or square, cabriole legs that have learned ball-and-claw, or straight Marlborough legs when the room wants less knee.

Gothic tracery in a splat is a church window shrunk to a back. It is pretty and it is a collection of short-grain bridges. A shop that pierces honestly leaves meat. A shop that pierces to match a photograph leaves pastry. I have seen revival “Gothics” snap at the first move. The plate did not mention the fillet you must leave.

Ribbon-backs look softer and are not. The ribbon is still a splat with holes. The sit is a narrow contact unless there is a shoe and a crest that catch you higher and lower. People who love these chairs love the air. People who sit two hours in them often add a cushion and then complain the table feels high.

## Philadelphia, and not only

American Chippendale chairs — Philadelphia especially — take the book and spend labor on carving that London might have saved for a richer commission. Heckscher’s catalog is the object trail. The sit is still a side chair: armless at the sides of the table, armed at the ends if the household can pay. Haircloth is the formal American seat for a long time after. It shines. It itches. It splits at the front rail where the thigh works it. Replacement horsehair is a specialist job. A floral chintz on a Philadelphia claw-and-ball is a later hospitality.

Straight legs appear when the shop or the client wants less projection under the apron. That is a dining intelligence. Cabriole and claw are a parlor intelligence that dining rooms adopted because the look had won. If your table has a deep skirt, pray for Marlborough.

## Piercing as a dinner fact

A solid splat will snag less. A pierced splat is a rack of little mortises for cuffs, necklaces, the corner of a shawl. Serving behind a row of Gothic chairs is a textile sport. I do not treat that as a joke. Household labor — enslaved in the South, hired in the North — moved around these chairs with platters. The pretty hole is their snag as much as the sitter’s.

Children put fingers through piercing. That is charming until the sliver. It is also how a splat gets a break at the bridge.

Structurally the piercing removes wood from the very part that takes a lean. The crest and the shoe have to finish the job. A chair used only as a display in a period room can keep pastry bridges. A chair used at Thanksgiving cannot.

## Claw, pad, and the floor

Ball-and-claw on a dining chair is a rug-catcher and a pride. The carving is a Philadelphia and Newport brag. On a bare floor the claw is noisy. On a thick rug the chair sits higher and the table relation changes — another way “old chairs are uncomfortable” turns out to be a floor covering. Tip the rug, measure again.

Rear raking legs still take the lean. Stretchers, when present, are the adult in the room. Some high-style chairs omit stretchers for line. Dinner will test that omission. I am not pious about stretchers on a museum masterpiece. I am pious about stretchers on a chair I have to guarantee.

## The sit, without a moral

Seat heights in labeled American examples wander around the band later shops will call dining. Pads change them. Recut feet change them. A compass front still appears; so does a squarer seat as the century turns toward Federal. Depth is often moderate. The front rail can be a carved or molded edge that the thigh learns.

Arms on end chairs must clear the table or the armchair is a parlor chair in exile. Chippendale armchairs with knuckles and a wide stance are magnificent and often too wide for a leaf that was added later. Measure the leaf, then the arms.

I like these chairs best when the piercing is thick enough to trust and the table is honest about the knee. I like them least when a dining room is trying to be a gallery and the sitters are trying to be guests. The *Director* was always a little gallery. Dinner was always a little mess. The good shops knew both.

A new oak “Chippendale” with a CNC Gothic and a foam seat is a costume unless the bridges have meat and the seat height matches the table you actually own. The book is public domain. The short grain is not.
