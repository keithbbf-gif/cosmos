The seat is the meal’s furniture. The back is the photograph. I look at the seat first when I want to know whether a chair has eaten. Rush sags. Cane drums and then hammocks. Haircloth shines and splits at the front rail. Leather wipes and cracks. Foam feels kind on Tuesday and bottoms on a holiday. Vinyl is the dinette’s wipeable cousin and the 1970s captain’s-chair answer. None of these dates the frame by itself. A 1790 shield with a 1950 foam pad is a dining chair that stayed in use. A 1790 shield with a perfect period-style haircloth and no wear is a chair that has been curated.

Date the joints. Read the seat as a diary.

## Rush

A plant seat, woven around or through rails, tight as a drum when new, a sag when the rush dries and the meals accumulate. Farm, tavern, kitchen, Shaker, Hitchcock, Colonial Revival — the same material in different social rooms. Re-rushing is expected. Original rush with an oxidized underside is a prize. A too-even blond rush is a recent hand. Plywood in a rush frame is a lost diary.

The sit is textured, a little raspy, cooler than foam. Crumbs go into the weave. That is dinner.

## Cane

Lighter, cooler, a Federal and a fancy-chair and a Thonet and a Cesca breath. Woven through drilled rails or in a spline. Recaning is a trade. A museum cane can be too tight and too even. A sloppy recane cuts the thigh. Plastic cane-pattern is a tray.

Cane prints a hexagon on bare legs. People who hate that buy a pad and steal the height.

## Haircloth

Horsehair, the formal American seat from Chippendale through Phyfe and after. It itches. It shines. It is correct under a mahogany table and hostile in July. It splits where the thigh works the front rail. Replacement horsehair is a specialist job. A floral chintz over a Philadelphia frame is hospitality, not a dating tool.

## Leather

Wipes. Gravy is not theoretical — Herter’s Vanderbilt dining chairs wanted stamped leather for a reason the sibling pack can own. Cold houses crack it. Hot hands stain it. Saddle soap as a personality will do harm. A good leather dining seat is a wipe and a conditioner’s restraint.

Mission leather is a moral as well as a skin. Vinyl pretending to be that moral is a dinette.

## Foam and the long lie

Foam is mid-century mercy and a later catalog default. It compresses. It turns a numbered seat into a moving target. It can be the right recover on a balloon-back if you specify a firmness and a finished height. It is the wrong recover on a cane frame if you wanted the type.

A slab of foam on plywood in a “farmhouse” chair is a stacker that went to the paint booth.

Springs under a dining seat are a nineteenth-century comfort Grand Rapids kept. They fail as springs fail. Listen.

## Shop practice

We pick the seat for the meal, not for the mood board. A house that eats Sunday roast and has children: leather or a wipeable cloth over a firm pad, or a rush they will agree to redo. A house that wants the document: cane or rush and a tablecloth. A house that wants Thonet: cane, and a recane fund.

I will not warranty a seat as if it were a finish. Seats are consumable on a long enough clock. The frame should outlast them. That is the honesty of a slip seat, a rush, a cane — they were always meant to be done again.

Do not date a chair by its present seat alone. I will keep saying that until a listing stops saying “original condition” about a 1998 foam.

## The underside as a date

When I tip a chair I am looking for a diary the listing forgot. Rush that has been there a generation oxidizes on the butt — brown, dusty, a little hairy where the twist meets the rail. New rush is blond and even, sometimes still faintly green if the bundle went on wet. That is not a moral. It is a date. A rush seat with a plywood panel screwed to the rails from below is a chair that lost its weave and then lost the chance to tell you when. I have seen that sandwich sold as original condition. The frame can still be 1830. The sit is a 1990s board.

Cane tells a different underside. Hand-caned through drilled holes shows pegs or raw holes and a slightly irregular hexagon. Spline cane is a machine-cut sheet pressed into a groove; the spline is a reed or a plastic lookalike. Both can be honest. A plastic cane-pattern sheet stapled to a pine frame is a tray wearing a chair’s name. I will recover a spline if the groove is sound. I will not call it hand-woven.

Leather and haircloth hide their dates in the tack line and the webbing. Old webbing is linen or jute, tacked, sometimes with springs that still have a voice. New webbing is often nylon and a staple gun. Foam that has gone to dust leaves a yellow stain on the webbing and a smell. That smell is the long lie announcing itself.

A too-perfect museum recane can be too tight and too even; a sloppy kitchen recane cuts the thigh at the holes. The quality of the replacement is a sitting fact. The fact of replacement is a dating fact.

## Pads that steal the type

People buy a cane or rush chair because they liked the breath, then they buy a pad because they felt the hexagon on a bare thigh in July. The pad is not a crime. The pad that is two inches of memory foam on a cane drum is a theft of height and of the type. You are now sitting on a kitchen cushion at a Federal height, and the apron will tell you. A thin, flat pad — cotton, a firm felt, something that can be shaken out — keeps the sit and the wipe. Tie it or do not; if it slides you onto the front rail, it is worse than no pad.

I have watched a house put quilted pads on six Cesca chairs and then complain that the table felt high. The chairs were not high. The pads were a stack. The cane was doing nothing except looking expensive through a hole in the cloth.

Haircloth does not want a pad. Haircloth is the formal sit: itchy, shiny, correct under mahogany, hostile in July. It splits where the thigh works the front rail, which is how you know the chair ate. If the household cannot live with that, recover in a cloth that can be cleaned and keep the slip-seat frame. A floral chintz over a Philadelphia frame is hospitality. It is not a dating tool.

Leather wants a wipe and a conditioner’s restraint. Saddle soap as a personality will do harm. Cold houses crack the hide. Hot hands stain it. Herter’s Vanderbilt dining chairs wanted stamped leather for a reason the sibling pack can own: gravy is not theoretical. Mission leather is a moral as well as a skin. Vinyl pretending to be that moral is a dinette. I will recover a Mission seat in a real hide if the frame is sound. I will not stain vinyl to look earnest.

## Failure cases I keep seeing

A recane pulled too tight will crown and then split at the holes. A recane pulled too loose hammocks in a month. A rush woven wet and never allowed to take its shrink goes slack in a dry winter. A leather oiled like a baseball glove stays greasy and then cracks at the folds because the oil went rancid. Vinyl meant to look like Mission leather splits at the welts and shows a gray foam that was never a hide.

Springs under a dining seat — Grand Rapids kept them into the twentieth century — fail as a chorus. One spring lets go and the sit becomes a hole. People put a board over the hole. Then they put foam on the board. Then they wonder why the chair feels like a box. The repair is to re-tie or replace the springs, or to convert honestly to a firm pad and say so. A hidden board is a lie the thigh finds.

The worst failure is a cane frame with a plywood insert so the children will not put a foot through it. The children will put a foot through the plywood later, and you will have lost the cane holes or crushed them. If the household cannot live with cane, change the type. Do not convert a breath into a tray and keep the name.

A slab of foam on plywood in a farmhouse chair is a stacker that went to the paint booth. It can still sit if you specify a firmness and a finished height. It cannot be dated as 1840. I will recover it as a kitchen chair. I will not distress the foam to look like rush.

Paper fibre rush is not cattail. It is a later trade material that looks even and lasts if it is woven well. I will use it. I will not call it original plant. Cattail with an oxidized underside is a prize. A too-even blond rush on a Hitchcock that still has its stencil is a recent hand, which is fine if the listing says so.

## A kitchen that eats versus a room that displays

I ask what happens on the seat besides sitting. Sunday roast, children, a dog that believes chairs are furniture for dogs, a weekly wipe with something wet and lemon-scented — that house wants leather, a wipeable cloth over a firm pad, or a rush they have already agreed to redo. A room used four times a year and photographed once wants cane or haircloth and a tablecloth, and a climate that is not an attic in August.

Thonet 14s in a café expected cane and a screwdriver. They did not expect a velvet cushion tied on with ribbon. A Mission oak with a leather seat expected gravy and a wipe. A balloon-back with a sprung seat expected a long course and a housemaid’s week. Shaker tape and rush expected a meetinghouse and a kitchen, not a museum humidity. Match the meal to the consumable. The frame will outlast the seat if you let it.

## How I specify a recover

On a slip seat I write the species of the frame if I know it, the finished height after the pad, a firmness in a word I can defend — firm, not plush — and whether the show cloth is a wipe or a document. I do not write a warranty in years. I write that foam will change and rush will sag and leather will need a restraint about oil. Seats are consumable on a long enough clock. That is the honesty of a slip, a rush, a cane. They were always meant to be done again.

On a rush or cane I write the method — hand or spline, cattail or paper fibre if that is what the house can afford — and I photograph the underside before and after so the next person can date our work. A museum cane that is too tight is a different brief from a kitchen cane that has to take a Sunday. I pull for the meal.

If the customer wants the chair to look new and eat every night, I tell them the seat is a diary they will write again. Date the joints. Read the seat as a diary.
