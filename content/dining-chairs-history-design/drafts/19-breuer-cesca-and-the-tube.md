Marcel Breuer’s B32 — later called Cesca, after his daughter Francesca in the marketing afterlife — is a tubular steel sled with a cane seat and a cane back in wooden frames. Christopher Wilk’s MoMA furniture book is the place to start. I will not untangle every license and every Italian factory in this hour. I want the sit at a table: a chair that springs a little, prints a hexagon on you, and slides on a hard floor like a thought you have not finished.

Thonet had already made sitting a product in beech. Breuer puts the product in the plumbing of the century. The cane is the memory of the café. The tube is the claim that a dining chair does not need four carved posts.

## Sled, not four legs

A sled base is two runners. It wants a flat floor. On a rug it fights. On a grit floor it scratches and then the scratches collect dirt. On a good hard floor it lets you pull in without lifting. That is a dinner gift for people who hate the scrape of oak.

It also means the chair can skate away while you cut. Felt or a stop is a household decision. Castors would make it a different, worse object.

The tube flexes. A heavy sitter will feel a give that a Stickley post refuses. Some people call that comfort. Some call it cheap. It is the material. A cracked tube or a wrinkled bend is a retirement, not a glue job.

## Cane again, on a modern frame

The seat cane sags on the same schedule as a Thonet if the use is the same. The back cane is a plane you meet higher. Cheap copies use a cane-pattern plastic or a rush that is not rush. The sit becomes a tray. I would rather recane a real frame than sit a plastic hexagon that cannot tear and cannot breathe.

Wooden seat-and-back frames on the steel are the warmth. They also shrink and split if the house is a desert in winter. Pegs and screws loosen. Tightening is maintenance, like a 14.

Arms on the armchair version change the dinner: they can clear a thin table and fail a thick one. The side chair is the workhorse. The armchair is the host if the host wants to look modern instead of carved.

## Dining rooms that wanted to be new

These chairs entered apartments and institutional dining and every later restaurant that wanted “Bauhaus” without a lecture. They stack poorly compared with plastic. They hang if you are bold. They occupy less visually than Mission posts and more than a ghost of a shield.

## What the sled does to a floor, and to a cut

I pull a Cesca in without lifting. On a good hard floor that is a dinner gift for anyone who hates the scrape of oak posts. On a grit floor the runners scratch and then the scratches collect dirt. On a rug the sled fights: it either climbs the pile and sits high or it refuses to move and you lift the chair anyway, which is the job the sled was meant to spare you. I treat the floor as part of the pair. Felt on the runners is a household decision. Castors would make a different, worse object — a chair that skates while you saw a roast.

The same slide that is a gift is a tax. The chair can leave you while you cut. I have watched a guest chase a Cesca backward an inch at a time through the main course. A stop, a rug pad that is not a pile, a sitter who plants feet: those are the fixes. The tube will not grow a stretcher box.

The tube flexes. A heavy sitter feels a give that a Stickley post refuses. Some people call that comfort. Some call it cheap. It is the material. A cracked tube or a wrinkled bend is a retirement, not a glue job. I do not sister steel with a wooden dowel and call it a shop trick.

## Cane on wood, wood on steel

The seat cane sags on the same schedule as a Thonet if the use is the same. Tight cane is a drum. Soft cane is a hammock toward the wooden frame and a hexagon that prints harder because you have sunk. The back cane is a plane you meet higher — more tablet than hoop, more air than a slat wall. Cheap copies use a cane-pattern plastic or a rush that is not rush. The sit becomes a tray that cannot tear and cannot breathe. I recane a real frame before I sit a plastic hexagon.

The wooden seat-and-back frames are the warmth. They are also the part that shrinks and splits if the house is a desert in January. Pegs and screws loosen. Tightening is maintenance, like a 14. I check the screws before I blame the tube. A Cesca that rattles is often a wood-to-steel joint, not a broken idea.

Arms on the armchair version change the dinner. They can clear a thin table and fail a thick one. The side chair is the workhorse. The armchair is the host if the host wants to look modern instead of carved. Measure the arm to the underside of the skirt with the chair pulled in. A tube arm that looks light in a photograph still occupies a slice of apron.

Foam added on top of cane is a confession. If you need foam, buy a different chair. A pad on cane steals height and hides the diary of the weave. I would rather change types than dress this one as a balloon.

## Copies, licenses, and the name Cesca

I will not untangle every license and every Italian factory in this hour. Wilk for the furniture. Later Knoll literature for the name Cesca — a marketing afterlife, not a 1928 baptism. Do not flatten Gavina into Bauhaus. A documented Knoll or Gavina-era chair is one object. A no-name chrome 14-with-a-tube is another. Pay for the one you are buying. The sit can be close. The steel quality is not. I look at welds, at the evenness of the bend, at whether the runners sit flat. A twisted sled is a chair that will rock before it flexes.

Copies are the majority of what people sit in. That is not a moral. It is a census I will not invent a number for. The silhouette traveled. The cane quality did not always. I sit the chair I am offered. I do not sit a catalog page from 1928.

I will not run a Bauhaus campus tour. The dining fact is cane plus spring plus a sled: a specific fatigue, a specific floor, a specific recane fund. Institutional dining and later restaurants wanted the line without a lecture. Homes wanted the same. The oak, if you still want it, can stay in the table.

## Pairing without a museum

A Cesca at a thick farm table can look like a mistake or like a relief, depending on whether you wanted a period room or a meal. A Cesca at a glass or laminate table is the catalog pair. Apron clearance is usually easy because the sled is low and the seat frame is the only bar. Knees win more often than they do with cabrioles. I still mock the pair. A drawer apron can find a thigh even when the legs have disappeared into runners.

Seat height is a modern dining band. Pair it. A 14 and a Cesca can live in the same height world and still be different clocks: beech spring versus steel spring, hoop versus cane plane. I have eaten an hour in a tight Cesca at a thin top and understood why the tube entered the dining room. I have eaten forty minutes in a slack copy on a grit floor and understood why people go back to posts.

A shop in oak cannot become a tube mill by wishing. What it can learn is the sled’s lesson: a chair that pulls in without a scrape is a kindness, and a chair that flexes is a fatigue you must choose. Build the kindness in wood if you can — a light frame, honest cane or leather, stretchers that do not rack — and leave the chrome to the people who can weld a flat runner.
