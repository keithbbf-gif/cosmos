# Bibliography

Working list for the series. Object records beat general surveys. If a draft cites a fact not on this list, the draft should name the source in its own Sources section. Office-furniture standards are cousins, not dining law.

## Pattern books and price books

- Thomas Chippendale, *The Gentleman and Cabinet-Maker’s Director* (London, 1754; 1755; 1762). Chair plates: splat, Gothic, ribbon-back; the dining chair is not separately titled in every plate.
- George Hepplewhite, *The Cabinet-Maker and Upholsterer’s Guide* (London, 1788). Shield, oval, and heart backs; sideboard as a priced companion.
- Thomas Sheraton, *The Cabinet-Maker and Upholsterer’s Drawing-Book* (London, 1793) and *The Cabinet Dictionary* (1803). Square backs, reeded legs, drawing-room vs dining-room notes.
- *The London Chair-Makers’ and Carvers’ Book of Prices* (1802; 1808 supplement). Curule, Grecian, and carved seating as priced labor.
- *The Cabinet-Makers’ London Book of Prices* (1788) — Shearer circle; sideboard and dining furniture as a set.

## Ancient and early chairs

- Metropolitan Museum of Art, chair of Hatnefer (36.3.152), New Kingdom, cedar and ebony, object record. <https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/543868>
- Metropolitan Museum of Art, chair of Reniseneb (68.58), New Kingdom. <https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/544856>
- Gisela M. A. Richter, *The Furniture of the Greeks, Etruscans and Romans* (London: Phaidon, 1966). Klismos from vase-painting and relief; surviving wood is scarce.
- Ole Wanscher, *Sella Curulis: The Folding Stool, an Ancient Symbol of Dignity* (Copenhagen: Rosenkilde and Bagger, 1980).
- British Museum and Vatican collections: Roman *sella* and *cathedra* types; treat reconstructions as reconstructions.
- Penelope M. Allison, *Pompeian Households* (Los Angeles: Cotsen Institute, 2004) — household furniture in context; wood rarely survives.

## Medieval to Renaissance

- Penelope Eames, *Furniture in England, France and the Netherlands from the Twelfth to the Fifteenth Century* (London: Furniture History Society, 1977). Chests, benches, the scarce backed chair.
- Victor Chinnery, *Oak Furniture: The British Tradition* (Woodbridge: Antique Collectors’ Club, 1979, rev.). Joint stools, wainscot chairs, turned chairs.
- Peter Thornton, *Seventeenth-Century Interior Decoration in England, France and Holland* (New Haven: Yale, 1978).

## American and English high style

- Charles F. Montgomery, *American Furniture: The Federal Period* (Winterthur / Viking, 1966).
- Morrison H. Heckscher, *American Furniture in The Metropolitan Museum of Art, Late Colonial Period: The Queen Anne and Chippendale Styles* (New York: MMA, 1985).
- Peter M. Kenny and Michael K. Brown, *Duncan Phyfe: Master Cabinetmaker in New York* (New York: MMA, 2011).
- Nancy Goyne Evans, *American Windsor Chairs* (New York: Hudson Hills / Winterthur, 1996) and *Windsor-Chair Making in America* (2006).
- Wallace Nutting, *A Windsor Handbook* (Saugus, 1917) — Revival document, not a primary for 1760.
- Donna R. Braden, Henry Ford essays on Lambert Hitchcock and Hitchcocksville. Henry Ford object 00.3.4586.2 and related marked chairs.
- Patricia Phillips Marshall and Jo Ramsay Leimenstoll, *Thomas Day: Master Craftsman and Free Man of Color* (Chapel Hill: UNC Press, 2010).
- John Bivins, Jr., *The Furniture of Coastal North Carolina, 1700–1820* (Winston-Salem: Museum of Early Southern Decorative Arts, 1988).

## Bentwood, factory, reform

- Christopher Wilk, *Thonet: 150 Years of Furniture* (Woodbury, N.Y.: Barron’s, 1980) and later MAK / Vitra writing on No. 14 / Konsumstuhl Nr. 14.
- Alexander von Vegesack, *Thonet: Classic Furniture in Bent Wood and Tubular Steel* (New York: Rizzoli, 1997).
- Grand Rapids Public Museum and Grand Rapids Public Library trade catalogs — press-backs, dinette sets. Cite the catalog, not a mood.
- Charles L. Eastlake, *Hints on Household Taste* (London, 1868; American eds.). Reform language; American Eastlake is a factory reading of it.
- Gustav Stickley, *The Craftsman* (1901–1916) and the Stickley Museum / Craftsman Farms object records. Slats as structure and sermon.

## Modern sitting

- Christopher Wilk, *Marcel Breuer: Furniture and Interiors* (New York: MoMA, 1981). B32 / Cesca; tubular steel and cane.
- Martin Eidelberg et al., *The Eames Lounge Chair: An Icon of Modern Design* (Grand Rapids / Merrell, 2006) — lounge is not dining; use for plywood method. For dining: DCM / DCW object records at MoMA and Vitra.
- Knoll and Saarinen collection records for the Pedestal (Tulip) side chair, 1955–57.
- Christian Holmsted Olesen, *Wegner: Just One Good Chair* (Hatje Cantz / Design Museum Danmark, 2014). CH24 Wishbone, 1949.
- Carsten Thau and Kjeld Vindum, *Arne Jacobsen* (Copenhagen: Arkitektens Forlag, 2001). Series 7 / 3107, 1955.
- IKEA museum / IKEA catalogs for INGOLF and earlier flat-pack seating — cite year of the catalog page `[VERIFY]` before printing a first-year.

## Sitting science (use as cousins)

- Stephen Pheasant and Christine M. Haslegrave, *Bodyspace: Anthropometry, Ergonomics and the Design of Work*, 3rd ed. (Boca Raton: CRC / Taylor & Francis, 2006). Popliteal height, buttock–popliteal length, thigh clearance. Dining is a pair with the table.
- Niels Diffrient, Alvin R. Tilley, Joan C. Bardagjy, *Humanscale 1/2/3* (Cambridge, Mass.: MIT Press, 1974) and later *Humanscale* sheets.
- Henry Dreyfuss / Tilley, *The Measure of Man and Woman* (rev. eds.).
- Etienne Grandjean, *Fitting the Task to the Human*, 5th ed. (London: Taylor & Francis, 1997) — task sitting; do not paste office lumbar doctrine onto a two-hour dinner.
- Allsteel / Openshaw and Taylor, *Ergonomics and Design: A Reference Guide* (2006) — office fit; 5th-percentile female to 95th-percentile male language. Name it as office.
- ANSI/BIFMA X5.1 (general-purpose office chairs, safety/durability tests) and ANSI/BIFMA X10.1-2024 (ergonomics for computer-use furniture). **Not dining standards.**
- ISO 9241-5 (workstation posture and furniture layout) — computer work.
- ISO 5970 (chairs and tables for educational institutions) — school, not dinner, but the pair idea is the same.
- EN 1335 (office work chair) and EN 16139 (strength, durability, safety for non-domestic seating) — institutional cousins.
- CAESAR (Civilian American and European Surface Anthropometry Resource) and ANSUR / Natick — say which survey you mean. Military sitting height is not a dinner table.

## Seats, upholstery, cane, rush

- Frederick Litchfield and later trade manuals on recaning; museum conservation notes from Winterthur and the Met on rush and cane as expected replacements.
- Period haircloth / horsehair: Winterthur and MESDA conservation essays; replacement horsehair is a specialist job.
- Shaker Museum / Hancock and Pleasant Hill object records for slat-backs and tape or rush seats.

## Labor and the South

- Robert F. Trent and others on enslaved labor in Southern shops — name the monograph the draft uses.
- Arkansas mill geography: Bradley County, Warren, Wilmar, Saline River. Furniture *stock* is not the same as a named factory chair line. Do not invent Fullerton-to-BBF succession.

## Rooms and meals

- Leslie Susan Berman, *The Evolution of the Nineteenth-Century American Dining Room* (M.S. thesis, University of Pennsylvania, 1997).
- John F. Kasson, *Rudeness and Civility* (New York: Hill and Wang, 1990) — manners at table, not furniture, but duration of the meal matters to the chair.
- Museum period rooms: Met American Wing; Winterthur; MESDA. A chair in a period room may have been recut or reseated.
