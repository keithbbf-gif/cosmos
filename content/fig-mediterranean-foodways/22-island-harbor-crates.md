An island fig has a deadline with a horn.

The boat — ferry, caique, the tired line that still calls twice a week — is the market. Miss it and you have fruit for the house, which is not a tragedy and is also not rent. Catch it and you have cash for the house, or at least a favor owed in Piraeus or Split. This makes island drying and island packing a little faster, a little more superstitious, than the same work on a valley road. A valley can wait for a truck. An island waits for a schedule written by weather and a company that does not love figs.

On Naxos the flats of fresh figs go toward Athens if the week is right, stacked with a tenderness that looks like fear: leaves as padding, a crate that must not be the one at the bottom. On Samos the dried strings and bags remember a time when the island sold sweetness the way it sold wine, a memory the souvenir shops have already turned into a label. On Vis the *hib* and the dried fruit ride with the same people who ride, in a bag that will be opened as a gift on the mainland and as a snack on the deck when the crossing is longer than the pride of the person who packed it.

Hvar, Korčula, a dozen smaller piers: the same math, different paint. Someone always says the next island’s figs are sweeter. Someone is always a little right. The boat does not adjudicate. The boat takes what fits.

Crates are politics. Whose crate, whose name stenciled, whose cousin is working the hold. A small pier does not have a commodity exchange. It has a man who can say no to your box because the space went to tomatoes, or to a cousin’s furniture, or to nothing because the sea is in a mood. Figs lose that argument more often than they win it. Tomatoes shout louder. Figs sulk and leak and make a smell that will be remembered at the next loading.

I like the hour before the ramp. The fruit is already no longer orchard. It is not yet city. It is in between, in the shade of a tarp that has been patched with a bag, in the argument about whether the wind will be kind. Someone sits on a cooler and eats one, which is either quality control or hunger. Both are legitimate inspection regimes.

The foodway of islands is often told as isolation. It is the opposite. An island that grows figs is an island that intends to talk to a coast. The crate is the sentence. The boat is the mouth. If the sentence fails, the house eats the paragraph. That is also a foodway, just a quieter one.
