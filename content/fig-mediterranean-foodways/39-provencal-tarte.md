Recipes will give you pastry, almond cream, a spiral of halved figs, thyme, a glaze that shines like a brochure. The foodway is simpler: too many figs, a neighbor who said she might come, an oven that was already hot from a gratin or a loaf. You roll what you have. You cut the fruit that will not last the night. You do not wash the board as if a photographer were coming.

The fresh fig does not want a long bake. It wants to collapse a little and stay itself. Overbake and you have jam in a crust, which is a different honesty and a fine breakfast. Underbake and you have a soup that insults the pastry. The good tarts look slightly ruined. The ruined look is juice finding a low place. If you have arranged a perfect spiral, congratulations; now let the oven disorder it.

Goat cheese on the side is the cliché because it is the correct cliché. A *banon* wrapped in chestnut leaves, or an ordinary *chèvre* from the same wind, is a salt the fig already understood on the hill. Honey is optional and often a mistake; the fruit is already a honey and a second honey is a speech. Thyme is optional and often a success; it smells like the walk to the tree and not like a sachet in a tourist drawer.

There is a savory cousin that the bistro will price as a main: figs with a bird, figs with a vinegar, a pan sauce reduced until it is only shine. At home it is a chicken, a handful of the day’s split fruit, a splash of whatever wine was open, bread for the juice. That dish is older than the tart in the pastry-school sense. The tart is older than the bistro. Both are garden accounting.

Dried figs have a smaller role in this kitchen. They appear in winter, with a nut, with a cheese that has aged past politeness. The tart is a late-summer object. Do not make it in January with imported fruit and call it Provence. Call it a craving. Cravings are allowed. Geography is not a costume you can buy at the market on Rue d’Alger.

I do not need a famous chef in this paragraph. I need a tree against a south wall that holds heat after six, a cat who believes the basket is hers, a tart eaten warm because everyone is too greedy for the cool slice. That is Provence without lavender oil in the sentence. Wipe the tin with the last crust. That is the review.
