We are not supposed to be sure.

*Sykophantēs* — fig-revealer, fig-shower — is a word that later came to mean a professional accuser, a person who made a living by pointing. One old explanation, repeated until it became a fact in after-dinner speeches, is that Athens forbade the export of figs and paid attention to people who reported the smugglers. Another is ruder, and about gestures in the street that had nothing to do with fruit. A third is that the word is a joke we have lost the punchline to. Philology does not owe us a tidy orchard. A magazine that prints the export-ban story as settled law is doing the after-dinner speech.

What we can keep, without cheating, is this: Attica grew figs that people cared about enough to put them into law, comedy, and insult. Dried figs were food and freight, the kind of cargo a city notices when it is missing. Fresh figs were a calendar — a week, a glut, a price. The agora was a place where a fruit could become a case because the agora was already a place where speech could become a case. Markets and courts shared a weather.

Attic figs had a reputation later writers liked to borrow: the local good thing, the fruit a city is proud of and therefore nervous about. Pride and nervousness are market emotions. They still are, in Aydın when a grade is wrong and in Kymi when a pair is mismatched. The ancient word is not required to see that.

I like the uncertainty. It keeps us from turning a pun into a documentary with a costume budget. The useful image is not a policeman in a fig tree. It is a market that was also a court, a city that could imagine a person whose job was to show the fruit and ruin a neighbor — or to show something else and let the fruit take the blame in the etymology.

Aristophanes would have had a better time with this paragraph than I am having. He would have put the informer in a basket and let the basket talk. We will stay on the ground. If a vase painter left us a market scene, use it and do not invent a fig police.

If you use the word sycophant in a fig piece, use it once, then put it back. The living foodway does not need a Greek costume for every stall. The dead foodway, the one in the agora, deserves a sentence that admits what we do not know. The fruit was there. The pointing was there. The rest is a story the language kept after the law forgot, which is how a lot of food history survives: as a word that outlived the invoice.
