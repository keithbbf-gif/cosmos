The romantic sentence is that every fig is unique. The packing shed does not have time for that sentence.

Aydın İnciri, in the language that protects it, is not allowed to wander past ninety fruits per kilogram if it wants to keep its face. The prettier lots live around forty-five to sixty-five. Those numbers are not nutrition. They are a handshake. A buyer who cannot see the orchard can still see a count. Too many pieces and the fruit is small, or dry to a husk, or a mix that was bulked. Too few and someone is selling you a different dream.

Women at the sorting table — again, often women — do this math with their hands. Cups, eyes, a rhythm. The fruit is classed into sizes the way olives are, the way oranges are, the way any crop that wants a ship must be. A magazine that finds this unpoetic has not paid for freight.

Grades also hide a social map. The extra-large go to gift boxes, to the Gulf, to the shops that will nestle each fig in a paper frill as if it were a jewel that might bruise. The middle go to the ordinary bag that a student will eat on a train. The small go to paste, to industrial bakery, to the domestic market that does not need to be flattered. None of these destinies is a moral rank. They are a valley allocating sweetness.

İzmir’s commodity exchange has, for decades, been a place where a number for the season is spoken out loud. Eighty thousand tons, seventy-six thousand exported, a price that holds or does not. Those figures change. The gesture does not: someone counts, someone argues, someone writes a grade on a lot that a man who never saw a *kerevet* will trust.

In older ports the grades had other names — crowns, layers, pulled figs, layered figs, the vocabulary of a wooden box trade that California and İzmir both spoke. You can still find the words in yellowed catalogs. The fruit inside has not read them. The fruit only knows whether it was handled as if it might be worth a count.

I like standing at the end of a sorting line more than in the orchard. The orchard is pretty. The line is the foodway becoming money without quite becoming cynical. A woman tosses a fig one inch to the left and changes its life. That inch is history.
