The list is short and it does not care about your brunch.

Wheat, barley, grape, fig, pomegranate, olive, date — *shivat haminim*, the seven species of Deuteronomy 8, the foods whose first fruits could go to the Temple when there was a Temple. Figs are in the middle of the sentence, between the vine and the pomegranate, as if the writer knew a hillside. Honey in that verse is date honey for many readers. The fig does not need to be the honey. The fig is already named.

Tu Bishvat, the new year for trees, became, in exile, a way to eat the list when you could not stand in the orchard. Dried figs travel. That is not a metaphor. A community in Poland or Morocco or Brooklyn could put a fig on a plate in midwinter and be in conversation with a land it might not see. The fruit was often the Smyrna kind or the local kind or whatever the grocer had. The ritual was the name.

There are seder-like Tu Bishvat tables now, with four cups and a curriculum, and there are tables that are only a bowl of the seven and a blessing. I prefer the bowl. It is harder to turn into content. You eat a fig. You say the words. You do not have to claim the fig did anything to your blood.

The Mishnah’s fuss about first fruits is a fuss about quality and origin, which should sound familiar if you have been reading the PDO pieces. Sacred agriculture and protected agriculture are not the same. They share a habit of maps.

On a good Tu Bishvat I have seen a child take the fig first because it is sweet and leave the barley because barley looks like homework. That child is in the tradition. The tradition was never only for people who chew slowly.

If you stage this photograph, do not put the fig on a slab of marble with a dew drop. Put it on a plate that has held other dinners. The feast is a kitchen feast. The verse is old. The chewing is present tense.

A dried fig on this table is not a lesser offering than a fresh one. It is how the list learned to travel. That is not a consolation prize. That is the technology of a holiday that had to leave the orchard and still wanted the orchard’s names.
