On Vis they will tell you the fig is not done when it is dry. It is done when it can sit.

The first work is ordinary Mediterranean labor: fruit on *lijese*, slatted wooden frames, one layer, turned, taken under a roof when the night thinks about dew. Seven days, fifteen, depending on the wind off the water and whether August decided to be an adult. That part a grower from Aydın would understand without a translator.

Then a gesture the Anatolian yards do not always bother with. The dried figs, or the ones nearly there, take a quick bath in boiling seawater, or in water someone bothered to salt. Seconds, not a stew. The idea is a skin that will keep, a surface less interesting to the wrong kind of guest. People who write recipes for tourists skip this or call it optional. People who keep a cardboard box of figs above a cistern do not skip it.

After the dip, the fruit goes back to air and then into company: bay leaves, rosemary, sometimes sage. Not a garnish. A lining. The same shrubs that make the island smell like a roast when you walk uphill in July become a packing material that happens to be a flavor. A fig stored on bay is a fig that will taste like the hillside even in March, for better or worse. There are families who find rosemary in a sweet a moral error. There are families who would not eat a fig that had been stored naked.

The leaf trick migrates into the cake. *Smokvenjak*, *hib* on Vis, is dried figs ground with rakija and fennel, pressed, then dried again under the same greens. That is a later chapter. Here the leaves are still furniture. They keep the fruit from touching itself into a block. They keep the box from smelling like a cellar. They are free.

Vinodol, a step from the sea and two from snow, used to send whole columns of people inland with baskets of dried figs on their backs, trading them toward Gorski Kotar for things the coast did not grow. The dip and the leaf were what made that walk possible. A wet fig is a short conversation. A finished one is currency.

I like this method because it is slightly ugly. Salt water, a boil, a pile of leaves that look like floor sweepings. No one will put it on a wellness plate. It belongs to a kitchen that understands the Adriatic as a pantry and a threat. The sea preserves and the sea steals. You use a little of it on purpose, then you shut the lid.
