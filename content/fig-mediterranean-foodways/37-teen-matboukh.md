Someone’s father had a spoon like a flat oar.

That is how *teen matboukh* enters a family: not as a recipe card, as a tool. Dried figs cut into chunks that look too rough for a magazine, water, sugar, anise, mastic pounded with a little more sugar so it does not stick to the pestle and disappear into a complaint. A long, low cook until the pieces surrender and the pot starts to pull a skin that you will scrape and put back in, because that skin is the jam becoming itself. Sesame toasted in a dry pan and thrown on the surface so the jar looks like a field after harvest. Walnut on the plate, later, if the house is in a good mood and someone remembered to buy them.

The jam is a breakfast and a visit and a filling. Bread, a white cheese, the sesame that falls onto the cloth, a coffee that can stand the anise without becoming a fight. In a weak year the figs were the cheap bag from a shop that also sells batteries. In a proud year they were the ones the family dried on its own roof, turned, covered, argued over. You can taste the difference in the skin. You cannot taste the speech about the roof.

Beirut apartments that still bother will make a smaller pot and call it the same name. Mountain houses will make a pot that requires two people to move. Both are the dish. The apartment one will be eaten faster because there is less room to hide a jar.

Mastic is the note people forget to name. It makes the jam smell like a church gift shop in the best way, a pine-clean sweetness that is not vanilla and will not be vanilla no matter what a blog substitutes. Anise is the note people cannot forget. If you hate anise you will call this a mistake and you should make a different jam. If you do not, you will understand why the pot is made once and eaten for months, a little at a time, as if it were a season.

I have seen the paddle. It was worn on one side, the side that knew the bottom of the pot. That wear is the recipe. When a blog says “simmer thirty minutes,” it is talking about a different object, a small saucepan, a weekday, a person who has to be somewhere. The old pot is a weekend and a conversation that keeps getting interrupted by the need to scrape. If you leave it, you will have a brown that is not the good brown.

Put it on *maamoul* week if you want to be fancy. Put it on a Tuesday, on bread, with a walnut that is not rancid, if you want to be accurate. The oar stays in the drawer. The jar does the rest of the year.
