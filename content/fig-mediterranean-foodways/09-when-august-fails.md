Every drying culture has a sentence it says when the sky misbehaves. In Kymi the product file allows a special oven before the fruit is opened and paired. In Cosenza the “protected” method is a greenhouse that must not exceed fifty degrees. In Aydın the plastic tunnels go up along the same roads that used to trust the open *kerevet*. In the Algarve a grandmother will finish a tray in the residual heat after bread, as if the fig were a second loaf.

Purists hate this paragraph. Purists can eat a moldy tray.

Sun-drying is not a moral condition. It is a weather condition that people learned to use because fuel was dear and August was, for a long time, reliable. When August puts a week of humidity into the first harvest, as it did in the Menderes in documented seasons, the open yard becomes a casino. The fruit that was going to be a gift becomes animal feed. The feast table in December does not accept “we were authentic” as an ingredient.

So the oven enters as a cousin, not a replacement. Low heat, door cracked, a wooden spoon to keep the seal from closing, the same trick Croatian home cooks now use on *smokvenjak* when they cannot spare two weeks of terrace. The point is to walk water out without cooking the fruit into a candy that forgot it was a fig. If you can smell caramel, you have started a different dish. Fine dish. Different.

Glass houses in Calabria are a compromise with dignity: still the sun, minus the dew, minus the sudden rain that turns a *cannizza* into a complaint. Tunnels in Nazilli look worse and do the same math. A photographer who wants “tradition” will walk past them to find one photogenic wooden tray. A cook who wants figs in the house will not.

There is a class story here if you want it. Export lots face buyers who can write “sun-dried” into a spec and mean a lab number, not a postcard. Domestic lots face aunts who can taste a kiln. Between them sits the grower, who would like to be paid. The honest magazine line is not “never oven.” It is “oven as insurance, sun as first choice, heat as a tool with a temperature.”

I have stood in kitchens where the family’s entire winter sweet was a sheet pan of figs that had been rained on and rescued at night. They were darker. They tasted a little stewed. On the Christmas plate, next to the ones that had a perfect August, nobody gave a lecture. They ate from both tins. That is the foodway: not a dogma about light, a refusal to let a cloud cancel January.
