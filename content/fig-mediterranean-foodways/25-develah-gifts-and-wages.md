When Abigail goes to meet David in First Samuel, the list of what she takes includes *develah* — cakes of pressed figs — among the other portable arguments: bread, wine, sheep, grain. The text is not doing recipe writing. It is doing diplomacy with food that will last the ride.

*Develah* means something like pressed-together. Chopped dried figs, mashed, shaped. You can hold it. You can send it. You can give it to a person who has been running and needs a sweetness that is not a metaphor. Other verses hand the same cakes to the exhausted and the celebrating. The object sits between gift and ration.

This is the ancestor of every later fig cake that a magazine will call an energy bar, as if the twentieth century invented pockets. The Dalmatian *smokvenjak*, the Spanish *pan de higo*, the leftover paste in a Gaziantep room — they are not copies of Abigail’s bag. They are the same problem solved again: fruit that will not stay fresh, labor that must be fed, a visit that must not arrive empty-handed.

I do not want to baptize every modern cake as biblical. That is a kind of kitsch. I want the old word on the table so we remember that “pressed figs” is a technology. It precedes sugar. It precedes the tin. It precedes the idea that a sweet is a course rather than a tool.

A wage in fruit sounds picturesque until you have been paid in something you must eat before it teaches you a lesson. Dried fig cakes were useful because they were stable and because everyone agreed they were worth something. Agreement is the hard part of a currency. A king can declare a coin. A pressed fig needs a village that has already decided it is food and freight.

Do not hang a modern “biblical diet” sign on this. The verses are not a menu for your week. They are evidence that a mash of figs could do the work of a gift, a ration, and a kindness to someone who had been running. That is enough work for one object.

On a winter Levantine table you may still meet a mass of figs that has been shaped and sliced. Call it jam if it is wet, cake if it is firm, *develah* if you are reading aloud. The blessing, if one is said, is for the fruit of the tree. The history is for the press — the hands that made a fruit into a shape that could travel without a crate.
