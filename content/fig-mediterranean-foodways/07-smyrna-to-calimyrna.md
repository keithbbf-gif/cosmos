For a century of English-language grocers, the dried fig was Smyrna. The word meant a pale, seeded, honeyed disc that arrived in a wooden box with a paper doily and a lie of geography. Most of those figs had not seen Smyrna’s water. They had seen Aydın’s dust. İzmir was the mouth. The valley was the stomach.

Smyrna the city is İzmir now, and the old label is a ghost that still sells. Walk a British shop in the 1970s in your head and the cellophane still says it. Walk an Aydın packing shed now and the workers will tell you, politely, that the fruit is Aydın İnciri, Sarılop, a protected name, a map, a count per kilo. The harbor does not get the credit. The harbor got the steamers.

When American planters in the late nineteenth century wanted that fruit, they did not want İzmir. They wanted the invoice. Cuttings moved. Names moved faster. Calimyrna is California plus Smyrna, a wedding announcement printed on a crate. The San Joaquin yards learned the same sun math, the same wasp problem, the same grading by size. They also learned that a Mediterranean method in a continental valley is a translation, not a clone. Different wind. Different rain. A different idea of what a worker’s day is for.

The foodway is the method, not the trademark. Fruit that ripens toward dryness on the tree. Collection from the ground or the near-ground. Trays. Night covers. A shed where women — it is often women — sort with a speed that looks like boredom and is not. Boxes that once were wood and are now cardboard with a window. A buyer in Hamburg or Minneapolis who can tell, without poetry, if the lot is last year’s.

What changed is the mouth of the story. “Smyrna” flattered a Levantine port that Europeans already had in their novels. “Aydın” asks you to learn a province. “Calimyrna” asks you to believe a new world invented a fruit it imported. All three names can sit on one table and the eater will not settle the politics. The eater will only notice whether the skin is thin and whether the center is a jam or a sponge.

There is a tenderness in keeping the old word, and a theft. Descendants of the growers who dried for the İzmir merchants are still drying. The merchants’ grandchildren may be in a different trade. California’s fig towns have their own unions and their own silences. A magazine that reprints “Smyrna fig” as if it were a cultivar should at least add a footnote: the harbor was a door. The yard was the house.

If you write a caption, write the yard. A crate stencil is a costume. A *kerevet* in the Menderes and a wooden tray in Fresno are cousins who would recognize each other’s hands and argue about the weather. That argument is the history. The brand is just what got printed on the way out.
