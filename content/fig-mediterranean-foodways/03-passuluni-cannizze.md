In the Crati valley they wait for the stalk to bow.

That is the signal, not a calendar. The Dottato fig — Ottato if your mouth is older — starts the season green-straw and a little smug, a table fruit if you are greedy. Left alone, the skin shifts toward beige, the neck softens, and the fruit begins to make its own raisin. Locals call that stage *passuluni*. It is not yet a dried fig. It is a fig that has started to think about winter.

They pick by hand, August into October, because a pole would insult the skin. Three kilos of that wilted flesh will become one kilo of the thing the PDO is actually talking about: Fichi di Cosenza, dried, light amber, a seed so small you chew and wonder if the lawyers imagined it. The empty achene is the boast. Other Dottati grown outside the map taste like the same family at a louder table.

Then the reeds. *Cannizze* — bamboo or cane frames that hold the fruit off the dirt so air can be a worker. Three to seven days in the open, turned at least twice a day at the start, or a shorter stay in a glass house that must not pretend to be an oven. Fifty degrees is the ceiling in the protected method; five days is the courtesy. The point is the same as a *kerevet* in Aydın and not the same at all. Here the fruit already gave up water on the branch. The rack is a finishing school.

Cosenza has been writing this down longer than the DOP stamp. A 1587 note treats dried figs as food that keeps and as money that moves. Pagano in the 1850s and Casella in the 1930s are the names the dossier cites when it wants to look serious. The Murattiana statistics of 1812 already have the fruit leaving the Kingdom of Naples. None of that is a origin myth. It is a shipping history that got a logo in 2011.

Walk the Tyrrhenian side, Belmonte Calabro to Amantea, and the drying story turns into pastry before you are ready. That is another piece. This one stays on the *cannizza*, because the confectioners of Amantea are only as good as the wilt they were handed. A fig dried too far becomes a coin. A fig dried too little becomes a regret in a tin. The rack is where that decision is made, in public, in the yard, where a neighbor can see if you got lazy.

There is a ratio people repeat without writing it down: sun, turn, shade if the weather sulks, never the ground. The ground is for olives that fell and for arguments. Figs that touch soil pick up a flavor the Christmas box will not forgive.

When the fruit comes off the reeds it is not a dessert. It is a raw material with manners. Some will be eaten as they are, the way a person eats a prune when they are being honest. Most will be split, crossed, stuffed, baked, or walked through chocolate in a workshop that still thinks of itself as a house. The *passuluni* on the tree made that possible. The *cannizza* made it legal to call it Cosenza.

If you photograph the rack, photograph the shadow under it. That gap is the whole method. Air is the ingredient nobody lists.
