A fig does not swim. It takes a road, then a rail, then a gangplank.

For the long nineteenth century and a good piece of the twentieth, that gangplank was Smyrna’s. The city faced the water and the valley faced the city. Figs came down the Aydın line with cotton, raisins, carpets, tobacco — the ordinary mixed freight of a port that had learned to speak several ledgers at once. Greek, Armenian, Levantine, Turkish, European houses had their own ways of counting the same sweetness. The fruit did not learn the languages. The boxes did.

What a steamer wanted was a fig that would not become a problem in a hold. Dry enough, graded, layered or pulled into the shapes the catalog had already sold to a grocer in Liverpool. The doily and the wooden box were not nostalgia then. They were packaging. A stain on the paper was a claim.

The 1920s broke more than a municipal name. Populations moved; firms ended; the waterfront changed its accent. The hinterland did not stop drying. It found new offices, new stamps, a republic’s institutions, a commodity exchange that would eventually quote the season in tons and dollars. The word “Smyrna” stayed in English longer than it stayed on the map, the way “Turkey red” stayed on wheat and “Mocha” stayed on coffee. Markets are conservative about syllables.

If you walk the Kordon now you will not see a fig mountain. You will see the same lie all beautiful waterfronts tell: that the city is the product. The product is inland, on a tray, in a shed that does not have a sea view. The port is a verb. It moves. A café can sell you the view. It cannot sell you the grade.

The British railway that ran down to Aydın was part of that verb: cotton first, then everything the valley could crate. Figs rode the same rails as other people’s wars and other people’s thirst. A steamer at the end of the line did not care which empire stamped the paper that week. It cared whether the hold would be a problem in Gibraltar.

I like old bills of lading more than old travel writing. Travel writing will give you a camel and a sunset. A bill will give you a grade, a weight, a destination, a merchant who will be forgotten and a fruit that will be eaten by a person who never hears the word Menderes. That is a market: a successful disappearance of geography into breakfast.

When a modern shop still prints “Smyrna style” on a bag from a different continent, it is quoting the steamer. You can call that romance. You can call it a fossil. Either way, the harbor taught the world a taste and then the world kept the caption and lost the dock.
