Ask an old grower in Germencik what ruined a tray and you will not hear a speech about terroir. You will hear about a night.

Dew is a small word for a large theft. A fig that spent the day tightening its skin will drink the evening if you let it. The sugar that was concentrating loosens. The surface goes dull. In the morning you have fruit that looks finished and behaves wet. Pack that and you pack an argument with a buyer in İzmir who has seen this movie.

So the work has a second shift. Late afternoon, when the light turns yellow and the flies change their pitch, someone walks the yard and begins the pile. Not a heap like laundry. A careful gathering, fruit off the *kerevet*, into a mass that can take a cloth. The cover is ordinary — woven, plastic, whatever keeps the sky off — and the gesture is not. You are putting the harvest to bed.

The Aydın specification, the dry one that lawyers filed so Europe would stop calling these “Smyrna figs” as if a dead port still owned them, writes the same choreography in the tone of a manual. Control the trays in the early morning or late day. Pile and cover against insects and dew. Spread again in a single line at first light. It reads like bureaucracy. In the yard it reads like not wanting to cry in September.

Rain is the louder cousin. A few millimeters in the first week of August can cut a district’s drying crop by a fifth; Germencik, İncirliova, and Efeler have already lived that sentence. The fruit is half a food and half a weather report. People who do not grow figs think of the Mediterranean as a climate. People who dry them think of it as a mood that changes after lunch.

This is why the plastic tunnels exist, those low, netted rooms that look like someone tried to grow a greenhouse on a budget. They are ugly in photographs and beloved in wet years. They steal a little sun and give back a night that cannot reach the skin. Purists mutter. Purists also like to get paid.

Insects are the other night shift. A fig is an invitation. The ostiole — that small opening at the eye — is a door if you are a beetle and a scandal if you are a grader. Covers help. So do clean yards, dogs, and the habit of not leaving windfalls to ferment into a bar. None of this is picturesque. All of it is why a “sun-dried fig” is not a fruit that happened to sit outside.

There is a tenderness in the cover cloth that the export brochure will never print. Someone’s grandmother did this. Someone’s bored cousin is doing it now with headphones in. The fruit does not care. The fruit only notices water.

In the morning the reverse: uncover, spread, single layer, a glance for the ones that went wrong in the dark. Those go to the other basket. The good ones go back into the light as if the night had been a rumor. By the third day, if the valley has been kind, you can pinch a fig and feel the argument settle. It will travel. It will wait. It will not wake up wet in a crate on a ship.

That is the whole craft, minus the romance: a day of sun, a night of refusal, a morning of starting over. The Mediterranean is famous for light. The foodways are built by people who learned what the light forgets.
