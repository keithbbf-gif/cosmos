In the Archaeological Museum in Naples there is a small wall that understands lunch.

Two figs, a loaf, a sill. Fourth Style, Herculaneum, inventory 8625 if you are the kind of person who likes numbers and a guard who has heard the number before. The fruit is not a cornucopia. It is a portion. The bread is the other half of a sentence Pliny and the poets already knew: this is food that does not need a sauce to be food. A later caption on a tourist photograph calls them “the most modest and frugal of foods.” The caption is doing a little too much work, but the frugality is visible. Two. Not a pile.

Roman fig culture was not modest in the field. Pliny lists varieties the way a merchant lists invoices. Cato, in the farming book, had opinions about planting and about what a farm should hold. The Pompeian fig had a reputation for drying, which is how a fruit becomes freight. Gardens were a flex for people who could afford a wall. Banquets could be stupid with abundance — dormice, a mountain of something, a poem about the mountain. And still the painter, or the patron who paid the painter, chose two figs and a loaf. I trust the two more than I trust the banquet lists. Banquet lists are literature. A sill is a kitchen. Kitchens outlast menus.

We do not need to pretend Vesuvius froze a recipe or that we know whether the figs were fresh that morning. We can say a city ate figs and bread and thought that pair was worth making permanent in pigment on a wall that was not a temple. That is already a lot. It means the fruit was ordinary enough to recognize from across a room and special enough to depict when a house wanted to look like a house.

If you use this image — and you should; see IMAGE_SOURCES — do not crop out the bread. The bread is the point. A fig without starch is a tasting for a person with time. A fig with bread is a meal for a person with a day. The Mediterranean has been making that meal longer than it has been making essays about it. Credit the photographer if the file is a modern shot of an ancient wall. The wall is public. The photograph may not be.

I stood in front of a reproduction first, then the thing itself, and the thing itself is smaller than the idea. That is also accurate. Lunch is small. History inflates. The foodway is the smallness that survived the inflation, two fruits and a loaf still doing their job while we walk past looking for a bigger story.
