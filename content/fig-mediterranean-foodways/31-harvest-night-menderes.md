When the last good drop has been taken and the trays are either under cloth or already in the shed, a yard will sometimes become a table.

This is not the Gold Fig stage. There is no mayor, no banner, no child in a costume that will itch. There is a pot — meat if the week paid, beans if it did not — bread that has seen better mornings, a salad that is mostly onion and a tomato that was not pretty enough for town. And the figs that were always going to be eaten here: the split ones, the ones with a little dust, the ones that would have lowered a count and started an argument with a man in a shed. Someone pours rakı or something softer. Someone’s child falls asleep in a chair that is too big. A dog who has spent the week policing the cover cloth finally lies down as if the season were a job that clocked out.

The feast is the refusal to let the entire crop become an invoice. A valley that exports must keep a night. If it does not, the work becomes only work, and the children will leave faster, which is already happening, which is why the night matters more than the speech at the festival.

Neighbors come or they do not. If they come, they bring a plate that is not figs, because bringing figs to a fig yard is a kind of joke. Cheese, a green, a bottle. The conversation is weather, a price heard on the phone, a cousin in Germany, the tunnel someone does not want to buy because last year was dry enough and this year was not.

Songs happen or they do not. I have been at both kinds. The better night was the quiet one, mosquitoes, a fluorescent tube the color of weak tea, a man talking himself out of plastic. The figs on the table were darker than the ones in the morning crate. They tasted like the tree, not like the grade. Juice on a wrist that had been sorting since first light.

Call it thanksgiving if you need an English word. The local word is closer to “we are done for today.” That is a feast theology I can live with. It does not require a hymn. It requires a second pot of tea and the decision not to look at the trays again until morning.

If you are a guest, eat the ugly fruit. Leave the pretty ones if they are still in a box. The box has a destination. The ugly ones have you. That is the invitation. Say thank you to the yard, not to the crop. The crop does not care. The yard does.
