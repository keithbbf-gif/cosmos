Before the plastic tray and the nitrogen flush, a household had a rafter.

The figs that were not pretty enough for a buyer and not wet enough to throw away were split or pierced and run onto a string. Cotton, twine, a stripped rush. You see the same gesture with peppers in a Hungarian porch and with mushrooms in a Polish attic. In the fig latitudes the string is a winter necklace. Children pull one down as if it were jewelry and eat from the bottom, which is how you know a house is functioning.

On Samos and Chios the strings hang where air moves and smoke from the kitchen does not quite reach. Smoke is a flavor some families want and some call a mistake. The point of the necklace is not perfume. It is portioning. A crate asks you to commit. A string asks you to take three and leave the rest in the weather of the room.

Coastal Anatolia did the same work with less poetry. A *ip* of figs over a stove, a nail in a shed, a row above the animals if the winter was dry enough. The animals, it should be said, have opinions. So do ants. The necklace is a bet that sugar and air will outrun both.

There is a market version. You still meet it in late-summer stalls: figs sewn into a circle or a long rope, sold as a gift that looks like craft. Sometimes the fruit is the good fruit, glazed with a last sun. Sometimes it is the fruit that would not sit flat in a box. You can tell by the tightness of the skin and whether the seller wants you to taste or only to photograph.

The necklace is also a calendar. Hung in September, it is still a little giving. By Advent it has become a chew. By the week after Epiphany, if the room was too warm, it is a regret with white sugar bloom on the shoulders — not mold, not always, just the fruit exhaling its own sweetness. Old people will tell you that bloom is a compliment. Young people will throw the string away and buy a bag. Both are practicing foodways.

I have watched a woman on a Cycladic terrace thread figs while talking about a cousin’s wedding, which is the correct ratio of work to news. She did not look up. The needle knew the eye of the fruit. When the string was full she tied a loop that would fit a hook she could not see from where she sat, because the hook had been there since her mother.

This is not a lost art. It is an art that looks poor in a catalog and rich on a Tuesday in January when the shops are closed for a storm and the house still has a ceiling. The crate is for export. The necklace is for the people who dried the fruit and then refused to let the fruit leave.
