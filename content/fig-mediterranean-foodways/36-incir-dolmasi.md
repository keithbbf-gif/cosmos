Turks already knew how to stuff a leaf, a pepper, a mussel, a squash that had no other plans. Stuffing a dried fig is the same idea in a sweeter key: a hollow, a tenant, a lid that is the fruit itself.

You open the fruit at the eye or the neck — houses argue about which door is polite — you make a room with a finger, you put in walnut. Sometimes a crumb of hazelnut. Sometimes a scent of clove or mastic that you will only notice when you are tired of the walnut. You close the door. Some houses poach the figs first in a light syrup or in tea so the room is kind and the skin will not crack in front of a guest. Some serve them as they are, a chew and a crunch, the honest weeknight version. Some take the extra step of a grape pekmez glaze, which is the southeast talking, Gaziantep’s grammar leaking west.

This is a guest sweet and a tea sweet. It looks like more work than it is, which is why it survives when fancier pastries die with the person who had the time. A plate of ten looks like a pastry shop. A plate of ten is a bag of figs and a bag of walnuts and a person who can make a hole while the kettle boils. Children can learn it. That is a test of a living dish.

Kaymak, if you have it, is the luxury next door: a stuffed fig and a spoon of cream that makes the plate Ottoman in the postcard sense. I will not stop you. I will say the fig and the walnut were already a complete sentence, and the cream is a second paragraph for people who like second paragraphs.

There is a cousin in Calabria, the single stuffed Dottato with citrus peel, and a cousin in the Levant, the jam with sesame that forgot the silhouette. The Turkish one keeps the outline of the fruit. That outline is the pleasure. You are eating a room. You can hold it. You can count the rooms on a plate the way you count guests.

If the walnut is rancid, the room is a ruin and the guest will be polite and the house will know. Buy the nut as carefully as the fruit, and keep both away from a warm cupboard if you can. This is not a rule about virtue. It is a rule about a small house that deserves a tenant who does not smell of last year.
