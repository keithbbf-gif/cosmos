Watch a Sarılop orchard in the real harvest and you will understand why the work looks lazy to a person who picks apples.

The fruit is supposed to fall. Not rot-fall. A chosen drop: the neck has loosened, the moisture has already started its walk downward, the fig has become what the files call a sour fig because language is imperfect. Crews come through every day, or every other day, and take what the tree released. Leave it and the ground teaches a different lesson — dust, ants, a ferment that will not grade.

This is harvest as a conversation with gravity. The tree decides the hour. The person decides the interval. Miss a day in a hot week and you have made vinegar. Come too often and you have paid wages to stare at dirt.

Kymi does the opposite on purpose. The PDO voice is explicit: not the fallen ones. The skin is too fine; the dirt is a dishonor. They cut, they open, they pair. Two valleys, two religions of the ground. Both are correct inside their fruit.

The fall method offends people who want agriculture to look like care. It is care. A fig forced off the branch for drying can be a fig that still thinks it is a peach: too wet, too proud, a long stay on the tray and a short life in the box. The drop is the plant’s own dehydrator. The *kerevet* is only the second chapter.

There is a class of writing that calls this “traditional knowledge” and then explains it as if the growers had been waiting for a consultant. They were waiting for the next pass. They know which side of the tree drops first, which wind brings the Tuesday lot, which year the fruit clings because the wasp was late. That is not wisdom in the poster sense. It is a job.

I have picked up a fallen fig that was perfect and one that was a ruin, two meters apart. The perfect one had a little dust that a cloth would forgive. The ruin had a wet eye and a smell like a bar at closing. Same tree, same morning. The foodway is the ability to tell them apart at walking speed, without a meeting.

If you photograph a “fig harvest,” decide which theology you are in. Hands in the air with scissors is Kymi. Hands at the ankles is Aydın. A ladder is often a fresh-fig picture from somewhere else, Bursa or a Roman garden, and it will mis-caption a drying story if you let it.
