Most towns are named for a spring, a saint, a forgotten landlord. İncirliova is named for a crop. Fig-valley. The older name, Karapınar, dark spring, was a water name, which is also honest: this part of the Büyük Menderes is a wet idea in a dry season. In the 1930s the water name lost. Atatürk, the story goes, looked at the trees and the paperwork changed.

You can dislike foundation stories and still like this one, because the town then behaved as if the name were an assignment. A fig-growers’ union. A fruit market. A station on the İzmir–Aydın line, the British-built railway that had come for cotton during someone else’s civil war and stayed for everything the valley could crate. Electricity in 1930. A bank in 1927. The ordinary furniture of a place that intends to sell.

Stand near the highway now and İncirliova will not give you a postcard. It will give you trucks, a sky that is too white, the smell of packing when the season is on. The romantic visitor wants a man on a donkey. The market visitor wants a lot number. Both can be found if you are not picky about which decade.

The Gold Fig Festival — *Altın İncir* — is the town saying its name out loud to itself. Pageants are easy to mock. So is a place that refuses to pretend it grows something more fashionable. The festival is a market with costumes: growers, graders, the people who make the paste, the people who sell the fresh fruit that will never see a *kerevet*. A child in a fig costume is an easy photograph. The useful photograph is the stall where last year’s grades are still being argued.

Names do work. Incirliova on a bill of lading is clearer than a village number. Aydın on a PDO is clearer than “Turkish figs,” which is a supermarket shrug. The town’s name is a caption that got promoted to law.

I do not think Atatürk wandered around renaming every orchard. I do think a state that wanted a modern countryside was happy to let a valley advertise. The trees were already doing the advertising. The sign just caught up.

If you go, go in season and go to the place where money changes hands, not only to the statue. Buy something that will not photograph well: the small ones, the paste, the fruit that missed the extra class. That purchase is how a named valley stays a valley and not a logo.
