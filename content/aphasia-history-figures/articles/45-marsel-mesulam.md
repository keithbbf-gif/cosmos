M.-Marsel Mesulam is a living neurologist. This pack will not write a private biography, will not scrape a university headshot, and will not invent a birth year for a caption. The public object is “Slowly Progressive Aphasia without Generalized Dementia,” *Annals of Neurology* 11 (1982): 592–598. The paper did not invent the patients. It gave neurologists permission to see a language-led degeneration as its own pattern, not as a failed Alzheimer’s story.

Later consensus criteria split nonfluent/agrammatic, semantic, and logopenic variants, argued about proteins, and moved. A magazine essay in 2026 will not adjudicate the latest table. It will say what 1982 changed: the clock. Stroke is a date. Progressive aphasia is a calendar. Families who had been told for years that the missing nouns were stress now had a name a memory clinic could not talk past.

## What this profile will not do

It will not list signs. It will not offer a self-screen. It will not describe therapy as a protocol. Progressive language loss belongs with a physician and a licensed clinician. Historical naming is not a consumer test.

Pick’s older descriptions of language and personality change sit behind 1982. Mesulam’s gift was the phrase and the insistence on *primary* — language first, dementia not required at the door. Therapy literature caught up in pieces, because a downward slope does not fit a six-week episode of care. That mismatch is part of the history.
