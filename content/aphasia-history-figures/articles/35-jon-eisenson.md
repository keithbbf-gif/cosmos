This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Jon Eisenson was born in 1907 and died in 2001. He is one of the people who made aphasia a speech-pathology subject in the United States rather than only a neurologist’s afterthought. *Examining for Aphasia* gave clinics a binder they could carry before the BDAE was a brand. Wartime language rehabilitation gave him a caseload of young men. Queens College and later California gave him students. The Institute for Childhood Aphasia used the adult word on purpose.

The childhood choice is the argument. He wanted schools and physicians to take a child’s language failure as seriously as they took a veteran’s. The risk was the metaphor: a child is not a small Broca. The gain was the seriousness. Later consensus would prefer *developmental language disorder* for language that never arrived and keep *aphasia* for acquired loss. Eisenson’s institute sits on the earlier side of that rename. A profile that scolds him for the word without mentioning the seriousness is doing the later piety.

## The binder

*Examining for Aphasia* is easy to condescend to after the WAB. It was, for a generation, how a community clinician began. It asked questions the house diagram would have recognized and some the house would not. It is a historical object. Do not scan a still-restricted edition onto a blog.

He wrote other books — on stuttering, on language development — that this aphasia series will not annex. The residue here is the binder and the institute name.

## Binder and institute

*Examining for Aphasia* is easy to condescend to after the WAB. It was how a community clinician began. Do not scan a still-restricted edition onto a blog. The Institute for Childhood Aphasia used the adult word to force seriousness. Later consensus preferred *developmental language disorder* for language that never arrived. Both moves are historical. A profile that only scolds the word has not met the veteran caseload that trained his ear, or the school that would not take a child’s silence as a brain fact.

He also wrote on stuttering and development. This series will not annex those books. The residue here is the binder, the wartime hour, and the institute name that still startles people who think aphasia is only a stroke.


Queens College students carried the binder into schools and hospitals that had never heard Framingham’s weather. That is a different fame from Goodglass’s and a more common one. Childhood aphasia as a name was a wager on seriousness. The wager had costs this series already named. The veterans had already trained the ear that made the wager feel necessary. Both caseloads sit in one life. Do not pick one to make the other tidy.
