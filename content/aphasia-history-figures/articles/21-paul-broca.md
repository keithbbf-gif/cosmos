This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Pierre Petit photographed him in the studio style of the Second Empire: dark coat, watch chain, the face of a surgeon who also measured skulls. Pierre Paul Broca (1824–1880) is in this series because a ward name — “Tan” — and an 1861 autopsy still sit in every introductory lecture on aphasia. He is not in it because he was a speech therapist. He was not.

He was born 28 June 1824 at Sainte-Foy-la-Grande. He trained in Paris, became a surgeon, a professor, a politician late. He died 9 July 1880 in Paris. Wernicke’s pamphlet was six years old. The American academy was forty-five years away.

## The specimen and the meeting

Louis Victor Leborgne died at Bicêtre on 17 April 1861, at eleven in the morning. Broca opened the brain twenty-four hours later and showed it to the Société d’Anthropologie the same day. The long remarks ran in the *Bulletin de la Société Anatomique*. He argued a seat for articulated language in the third left frontal convolution. He wanted the word *aphemia*. He lost the word. The world said *aphasia* and then put his name on the nonfluent type.

Lelong’s case thickened the claim. Later scans of the preserved brains thickened the argument about how much cortex the 1861 paper actually saw. The public event remains the meeting. The jars remain the private argument. Do not collapse the ward, the anthropological society, and the anatomical bulletin into one cartoon slide.

He had predecessors. Gall, Bouillaud, Auburtin, Marc Dax. Broca had the specimen and the room. Medicine rewards the person who brings the brain to the meeting.

## The other laboratory

The same career includes the Société d’Anthropologie de Paris (he founded it in 1859), a flood of cranial measurements, and racial rankings that later science discarded and later ethics condemned. He argued with Pierre Gratiolet about whether the frontal lobes were the seat of higher function; Leborgne arrived in the middle of that argument. The man who gave the field a convolution also gave the nineteenth century a scientific vocabulary for hierarchy. A profile that prints only the convolution is doing public relations for a lecture slide.

## What speech-language pathology actually inherited

A permission: language failure can be a focal brain fact, not a moral one.

A pair of names that are too tidy for the scans and still useful as a first map.

A habit of the specimen: the brain in the jar, the photograph, the paper. Goodglass’s Boston and Schuell’s Minnesota are behavioral answers to that habit.

The line from Bicêtre to a modern therapy hour is real and thin. It runs through wars, VA wards, and a lot of people Broca would not have hired. Keep Leborgne’s name. Leave the racial anthropology in the same vita.

A sibling profession series has a Broca profile for the profession’s ancestry. This one is the aphasia profile: the year, the word fight, the jars. The sentences are not copied. The man is the same.

## Two societies, one week

The Société d’Anthropologie and the Société Anatomique were not the same room. Broca showed the brain to the first within hours; the long remarks ran in the second’s bulletin. Collapsing them is how a slide gets written. Keeping them apart is how a year stays a year.

He kept arguing through the 1860s. Laterality thickened. Critics arrived. More brains arrived. The man who is frozen at Leborgne is a mascot. The man who measured skulls in the same career is the one this series will not leave in the corridor. A permission to treat language as a brain fact and a vocabulary for ranking heads came from one laboratory culture. Later clinicians kept the first and had to disown the second. The disowning is part of the job, not a preface you can skip.


Lelong’s handful of words is the second case students skip. Without it, Leborgne is a curiosity. With it, the convolution becomes a claim. Later slides of the preserved brains are a third case: the object arguing with the paper. Keep Leborgne’s name in the lecture. Keep Lelong’s. Keep the craniometer in the same hour. A mascot cannot hold all three.
