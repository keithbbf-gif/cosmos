This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Joseph M. Wepman was born in Copemish, Michigan, on 24 December 1907 and died in Riverside, California, on 18 March 1982. Those dates come from later memorials, not from a newspaper this article held in its hand; if a better obituary disagrees, the article should be corrected, not defended. He took a B.A. at Western Michigan in 1931, a Ph.M. at Wisconsin in 1934, and a Ph.D. at the University of Chicago in 1948 — a timeline that already says “war in the middle.”

With Ward Halstead he published the Halstead–Wepman Aphasia Screening Test in the *Journal of Speech and Hearing Disorders* in 1949. The Language Modalities Test for Aphasia came later and tried to ask, systematically, which door the language was still using: speaking, hearing, reading, writing. Modality is a 1950s word that later cognitive work would split into finer representations. In a Chicago clinic it was already a way to stop saying “he has aphasia” as if that were a single lock.

## Recovery as a social fact

Wepman’s later essays, including a piece Martha Taylor Sarno put in *Aphasia: Selected Readings* (1972), talk about recovery and reintegration with a personal heat that the batteries do not. He was willing to sound prejudiced in the ordinary clinical sense: some methods he disliked, some hopes he thought were theater. A 2026 reader can take the heat and leave any dated certainty. What remains is the insistence that returning to a life is not the same as gaining a point on a screen.

He taught in Chicago’s world of psychology and speech. He is not a household name in master’s programs that skip from Schuell to the WAB. That skip is why he is here.

## Chicago, modalities, heat

The 1949 Halstead–Wepman screen and the later Language Modalities Test tried to stop “he has aphasia” from meaning a single lock. Speaking, hearing, reading, writing — four doors. Later cognitive work would split each door into representations. In a Chicago clinic the four doors were already a way to listen.

The 1972 essay in Sarno’s selected readings is willing to sound prejudiced in the ordinary clinical sense: some methods he thought were theater. Take the heat. Leave any dated certainty. Returning to a life is not the same as gaining a point. That sentence is why he is not a skip between Schuell and the WAB.


Copemish to Chicago to Riverside is a Midwestern vita with a war in the middle. The 1931 Western Michigan bachelor’s and the 1948 Chicago doctorate already narrate the interruption. Screening in 1949 was a way to talk to a service that had more men than hours. Modalities later were a way to talk to a language that had more than one door. Heat, in 1972, was a way to talk to colleagues he thought were performing recovery. All three talks are why he stays in the roster.
