Roman Jakobson was born in Moscow in 1896 and died in Cambridge, Massachusetts, in 1982. He is a linguist, a Prague Circle emigrant, a Harvard and MIT weather system, and — for this series — the author of a short wartime book with a long title: *Kindersprache, Aphasie und allgemeine Lautgesetze* (Uppsala, 1941). Child language, aphasia, and general sound laws. The claim is that the sounds a child acquires last are the sounds an aphasic speaker loses first, because both obey a structural order.

The claim is too neat. Later phonology and later aphasiology have spent decades saying so. It is also one of the first times a serious linguist treated the lesion as evidence about the language system rather than as a medical souvenir. That habit — the lesion as a window on structure — is the ancestor of a dozen 1970s dissociations, even when those papers never cite the 1941 booklet.

## Exile as method

Jakobson wrote the book as a refugee intellectual, not as a ward clinician. He had seen aphasia; he had not built a VA service. The danger of the booklet is a linguist’s certainty about a hospital he does not run. The value is the insistence that “he said the wrong sound” is a structured event. Goodglass’s later agrammatism papers are a different, more clinical linguistics. Jakobson is the earlier, more imperial one.

He also wrote about metaphor and metonymy, about the poetic function, about aphasia as a split between similarity and contiguity disorders — another neat pair that later people used and then outgrew. Keep the pair as history. Do not make it a diagnostic shortcut.

## Neat laws, real habit

The 1941 claim — last acquired, first lost — is too neat. Later phonology said so. The habit remains: a wrong sound is a structured event, and a lesion can be a window on the system. The 1970s dissociations are later, more clinical versions of that habit.

Moscow, Prague, Uppsala, Cambridge — exile is the method and the risk. The 1941 booklet treats the lesion as a window on structure. Windows cut both ways: you can see the system, and you can stop seeing the ward. Keep the window. Keep the ward. The neat law about last-in, first-out is a law to argue with, not a drill to hand a parent.
