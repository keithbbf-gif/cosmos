This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

By the 1950s the American VA had two loud rooms. In Minneapolis, Hildred Schuell treated aphasia as a reduction of language that could be stimulated back along a hierarchy of difficulty. In Boston, Harold Goodglass and Edith Kaplan treated aphasia as a set of syndromes you could hear if you knew what to ask. Both rooms were full of veterans. Both rooms wrote books that later graduate programs treated as rival churches.

They were not churches. They were two answers to the same administrative question: how do you describe a person so that another clinician, next year, knows what you heard?

## Minnesota

Schuell came to the Minneapolis VA in 1948. She was a speech pathologist, not a neurologist, and she learned the caseload by sitting with it. The Minnesota Test for Differential Diagnosis of Aphasia (MTDDA) and the 1964 book *Aphasia in Adults* (with James J. Jenkins and Edward Jiménez-Pabón) argued that most aphasia was a general language deficit of varying severity, with a few special complications. Therapy, in that frame, was controlled stimulation: not a parlor game, not a rest cure, and not — in this magazine — a recipe.

The reputation later hardened into “Schuell’s stimulation approach,” a phrase that can be printed on a slide and misunderstood as homework. What she actually built was a way to listen for what still went in, and to stop pretending that a silent man had a silent mind.

## Boston

Goodglass arrived at Framingham in 1951 and became the Boston VA’s language person in a city that also had Norman Geschwind’s neurology and, soon, a culture of disconnection syndromes. The Boston Diagnostic Aphasia Examination, with Kaplan, first appeared in 1972. It wanted profiles: fluency, naming, repetition, comprehension, the old Wernicke–Lichtheim questions asked with pictures and a scoring sheet. Kaplan’s process — how the person failed, not only that they failed — sat inside the battery and then outgrew it.

Boston sounded more like 1874 than Minneapolis did. That was a choice. It made the test readable to neurologists. It also made “Broca’s” and “Wernicke’s” American clinic words again, after decades of holist weather.

## The fight that was not quite a fight

Textbooks still set the two schools at odds: unidimensional severity versus multidimensional syndromes. Clinicians who used both knew the secret. A person can have a severity and a pattern. A stimulation hour can follow a BDAE. A BDAE can be given by someone who has read Schuell. The fight was louder in print than in the better rooms.

What the fight did produce was a generation of students who thought they had to pick a parent. Life-participation work in the 1990s had to tell those students that the parent was not the person in front of them.

## Who was left out

Women with stroke, again. People who did not sound like the standardized pictures. Spanish-speaking veterans in a battery written for English. The Boston and Minnesota books are English-language objects. Global aphasiology would spend the next fifty years saying so.

## Residue

Open a hospital closet. If there is a BDAE or a later Western Aphasia Battery, Boston won the form. If the clinician still talks about “what he can take in,” Minnesota won the hour. Most closets, if they are honest, have both dusts.


## Students and the parent problem

Graduate programs in the 1970s and 1980s sometimes made students pick a parent: stimulation or syndromes. The pick was a loyalty test, not a clinical one. A person can have a severity and a pattern. A stimulation hour can follow a BDAE. The fight was useful for writing exams. It was less useful for a Thursday at 2 p.m.

Spanish-speaking veterans in English batteries, women on civilian wards, people who could not sit for the full Minnesota or the full Boston — they are the ones the parent fight did not score. Later life-participation work had to say the parent was not the person. That sentence is this era’s unpaid bill.


The two rooms shared veterans and did not share a theology. Print made them churches. Practice, in the better hours, made them furniture in the same closet. A student who still thinks she must pick a parent can put this essay down and open both books. Severity and pattern are not a loyalty test. They are two ways to write Tuesday so November can read it.
