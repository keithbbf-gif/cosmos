This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Jules Déjerine was born in 1849 in Geneva and died in 1917 in Paris. He is the localizationist’s localizationist: the man who wanted the map finer, not looser, and who treated Marie’s 1906 title as an insult to a life’s work. With Augusta Déjerine-Klumpke he produced plates and books (*Anatomie des centres nerveux*) that made fiber and cortex look like something you could trust. The alexias — reading lost with writing kept, reading lost with writing lost — are among the aphasia-adjacent facts that still carry his name.

He had come up through the Paris hospitals the hard way, a provincial Protestant in a Catholic capital, and he did not take kindly to being told that Broca’s convolution was a superstition. The 1906–1908 exchange is therefore not only a data fight. It is a fight about whether the method of clinico-pathological correlation still deserved the room.

## Reading as a separate architecture

The house diagram is a model of spoken words. Déjerine’s alexia with agraphia and alexia without agraphia forced the map to admit print as its own roads. Later cognitive neuropsychology would rename the roads. It would not have had them to rename without this kind of stubborn anatomy. Geschwind’s 1965 splenium story is, in one mood, Déjerine updated for a Boston audience.

He was not a speech therapist. The plates are not a treatment. The residue for an SLP is the permission to treat reading and writing as language, not as school skills that happen to sit near a stroke.

## The chair, again

When he died in 1917, the institutional story went to Marie. Augusta’s later treatment by that story is a separate profile and not a sentimental footnote. Jules’s vita includes the plates she worked and the wards they shared. A “Déjerine” caption that means only the husband is a bad caption.

## Print as a road

Alexia with agraphia and alexia without agraphia forced the map to admit print as its own architecture. Geschwind’s 1965 splenium story is, in one mood, Déjerine updated for Boston. Cognitive neuropsychology renamed the roads. It would not have had them without this stubborn anatomy.

He came up the hard way, a Geneva-born Protestant in a Catholic capital, and he heard Marie’s title as an insult to a method, not only to a gyrus. The plates he made with Augusta are household labor as well as a husband’s reputation. A “Déjerine” caption that means only the husband is a bad caption. The 1917 chair going to Marie is the institutional last word. It is not the scientific one.


Fiber and cortex, drawn until they looked trustworthy — that is the plates’ achievement and their risk. Trustworthy drawings make students stop looking at the person. Déjerine wanted them to look at the person *and* the fiber. The 1906 fight is what happens when someone says the fiber you trusted is a superstition. He heard a method dying. The method did not die. It got ruder and finer at once.
