This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 1965 Norman Geschwind published “Disconnexion Syndromes in Animals and Man” in *Brain*, in two parts (88: 237–294 and 585–644). The papers are long, learned, and openly nostalgic for a nineteenth-century habit: if you know the tracts, you can predict the breaks. Wernicke had drawn a line between a sensory and a motor word-center. Geschwind drew a city.

Conduction aphasia became, again, a disconnection. Alexia without agraphia became a story about the splenium and the left occipital lobe. Apraxia, agnosia, the callosal syndromes — the 1965 papers made the white matter fashionable in a decade that had been listening to holists and behaviorists and needed a map it could teach.

## Boston as a weather system

Geschwind worked in a city that already had Goodglass and would have the BDAE. Neurology and speech pathology in Boston did not always share a paycheck, but they shared patients and a taste for syndromes. Students learned to hear a disconnection as if it were a sound. Sometimes it was. Sometimes it was a story laid over a messy stroke.

The return of the diagram was a relief to people who had found Goldstein un-scorable. It was a threat to people who had watched a syndrome label stop a clinician from listening. Both reactions were earned.

Geschwind also wrote about the anatomy of laterality, about handedness, about the larger left planum temporale in some brains — work that later imaging would complicate. He died in 1984, fifty-eight years old, with a generation of residents still talking in his sentences.

## What the return was not

It was not a simple restoration of 1885. The 1965 papers knew animal work, knew the callosum, knew that “centers” were at best useful fictions. It was not a therapy. Nothing in the *Brain* papers tells an SLP what to do on Tuesday. The leak into the clinic was cultural: permission to say “conduction,” permission to point at the arcuate fasciculus, permission to feel that the house had been rebuilt with better timber.

Later tractography would redraw the timber. The arcuate story is now a bundle of bundles. Dual-stream models (Hickok and Poeppel and their arguments) would split dorsal and ventral paths in ways Geschwind would have recognized as family and not as copies. The 1965 papers remain the American event that made disconnection a default explanation again.

## Who was left out

Patients whose lives were not a syndrome. Clinicians outside Boston who were still doing Schuell in a basement. European work that had never abandoned tracts — the diagrams had not died in every language. An American essay that starts in 1965 is starting at a fashion, not at a birth.

## Residue

When a student says “the arcuate” with too much confidence, Geschwind is in the room. When a clinician says “the scan does not match the sound,” Head is in the room answering him. The two voices together are the adult version of the map.


## Tractography’s later weather

The arcuate fasciculus of the lecture slide is now a set of tracts with arguments attached. Dual-stream models split dorsal and ventral paths in ways the 1965 papers would have recognized as family. None of that makes Geschwind a fool. It makes 1965 a fashion that became a default explanation. Defaults need dates.

Clinicians outside Boston kept doing Schuell in basements while residents talked disconnection. Both rooms were real. An American essay that starts in 1965 is starting at a journal event, not at a national practice.


Boston’s weather made disconnection sound like a dialect you could learn. Basement clinicians kept stimulation cards anyway. Both were answering a real caseload. The 1965 papers are a journal event with a long echo. The echo is not a national practice. If a student says “arcuate” as if it ended the visit, give her Head’s 1926 remark about agreeing on the picture first.
