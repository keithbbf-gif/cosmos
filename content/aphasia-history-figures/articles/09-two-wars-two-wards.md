This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Aphasia had always been a civilian fact — a merchant, a teacher, an old man in a parish bed. The two world wars made it a personnel fact. Young men arrived with metal in the left hemisphere and a life expectancy that embarrassed the old autopsy culture. Someone had to sit with them for an hour, write down what came back, and argue with a pension board.

The first war produced Head’s volumes, Goldstein’s institute, and a scatter of European “speech doctors.” It did not produce an American profession ready for the caseload. In 1918 the United States still barely had university speech clinics. Iowa and Wisconsin were experiments. ASHA’s ancestor association would not meet in the Hotel McAlpin until 1925, and even then it worried more about stammering than about shrapnel.

## The Soviet war

Aleksandr Luria spent the 1940s on traumatic aphasia in a state that wanted soldiers returned to work. *Traumatic Aphasia* appeared in Russian in 1947; the English translation waited until 1970. The book is full of men who can do one thing with a word and not another. Luria’s functional systems — not a single center, not a mush — let him talk about recovery as reorganization. The politics around him were not gentle. A history that prints only the handsome theory and skips the Soviet hospital as a state machine is doing public relations.

What traveled west was the permission to think of language as a set of collaborating systems and to take rehabilitation seriously as a science. What did not always travel was the time Luria claimed for observation.

## The American VA

After 1941 the United States built a different machine: Halloran, Framingham, Minneapolis, later the Boston VA in Jamaica Plain. Physicians needed people who were not physicians to sit with the living. Some of those people came from speech correction. Some came from psychology. Hildred Schuell learned aphasia on the job at the Minneapolis VA after 1948. Harold Goodglass took the psychologist’s post at the National Veterans Aphasia Center in Framingham in 1951 and then spent decades at Boston. Jon Eisenson had already been in the wartime language-rehab business.

The ward was male, military, and under-described on the women’s side. Wives sat in hallways. Civilian women with stroke waited in other buildings. “Aphasia” in the American 1940s literature often means “the GI.” That skew is part of why later life-participation work had to rediscover the kitchen and the bus.

The hour became a job. Tests became a way to talk to chiefs. Schuell’s Minnesota Test, Eisenson’s *Examining for Aphasia*, later the BDAE — these are late children of the war ward, not of Broca’s jar. They are also a refusal: a claim that the pattern of behavior can be described without waiting for the next post-mortem.

## Who was left out

Colonial soldiers in European armies whose injuries did not become famous series. German and Japanese civilians. The American Black veteran in a segregated hospital system. Anyone whose aphasia came from a stroke at forty-five in a town without a VA. War writes the textbooks. Stroke writes the caseload. The two facts have never been the same size.

## Residue

A modern hospital still runs on the war’s invention: a named therapist, a note, a score, a discharge goal. It also runs on the war’s limit: the goal is often “independent enough,” not “back to the person you were.” Luria and Schuell both knew that. The difference is the building that hired them.


## The civilian after the GI

When the veterans aged out of the textbooks, the caseload did not shrink. It changed sex and age and cause. Stroke, tumor, infection, later progressive disease. The American literature was slow to write the kitchen with the same seriousness it wrote the ward. Sarno’s Rusk hallway is a postwar correction. So is every later group that is not a VA group.

Colonial and segregated hospitals produced injuries that did not become famous series. That is not a diversity paragraph pasted on. It is why “the war taught us aphasia” is a half-truth. The war taught a particular army’s aphasia to a particular set of journals. The other injuries waited.


A discharge goal written for a GI is not a discharge goal written for a grandmother who wants the bus. The war invented the named therapist and the note. Stroke invented the caseload that outnumbered the war. When a modern hospital still sounds like a personnel office — “independent enough” — it is speaking the war’s language. When it asks about the bus, it is speaking the later correction.
