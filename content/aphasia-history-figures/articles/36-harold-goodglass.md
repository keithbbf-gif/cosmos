This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Harold Goodglass was born in 1920 and died in 2002. In 1951 he took the psychologist’s post at the National Veterans Aphasia Center in Framingham. He spent the rest of a long career making Boston a place where you could hear a syndrome if you knew what to ask. The Boston Diagnostic Aphasia Examination, with Edith Kaplan, first appeared in 1972 (Lea & Febiger). It wanted profiles: fluency, naming, repetition, comprehension — the old Wernicke–Lichtheim questions asked with pictures and a scoring sheet.

He had already, with Joseph Hunt and with others, written papers on agrammatism and grammatical complexity that made “Broca’s” a linguistic problem again, not only a mouth problem. The 1958 work is easy to skip if you only know the binder. The binder is the public object. The papers are why the binder had ears.

## Boston weather

Jamaica Plain, the VA, Geschwind’s neurology, Kaplan’s process — Goodglass lived in a weather system. Students learned to say “Broca’s” and “Wernicke’s” as American clinic words after decades of holist weather. Sometimes the words fit. Sometimes they were a story laid over a messy stroke. Goodglass knew both facts. The BDAE’s later editions and the Boston naming traditions tried to keep the hearing honest.

He wrote *Understanding Aphasia* (1993) as a late, readable book. It is a good door for a clinician who wants the man without only the score sheet. This series will not turn it into a protocol.

## Residue

Open a hospital closet. If there is a BDAE, Boston won the form. The better hour still begins after the binder closes. Goodglass, who was a psychologist among physicians, knew that the form was how you talked to the building. The person was why you stayed.
