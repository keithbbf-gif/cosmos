This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 1966 John Marshall and Freda Newcombe described a man whose reading errors were not a general ruin. Some words broke in ways that looked like a path to meaning without a path to sound; some errors looked like the opposite. “Deep dyslexia” and later “surface dyslexia” became the public names. The point was not a new lesion. The point was a model of *reading* with more than one route, which a stroke could cut selectively.

Cognitive neuropsychology — the ugly useful phrase — treated the damaged reader or speaker as evidence about the undamaged system. Max Coltheart drew box-and-arrow models that looked like Lichtheim and behaved like a computer scientist’s flowchart. Alfonso Caramazza argued that group studies of “Broca’s aphasia” hid the only facts that mattered: the single case, the dissociations, the theory that could be falsified by one person.

Elizabeth Warrington and colleagues at the National Hospital took semantic memory seriously as a system that could fail while other language held, or the reverse. Tim Shallice wrote the rules of the game for a generation: associations are cheap; dissociations, if you trust the tasks, are news.

## What this did to the clinic

It did not immediately change the VA binder. It changed the journals. Naming was no longer one score. It was semantics versus phonology versus a lemma nobody could see. Therapy research began to target a level — semantic feature analysis is a later cousin of this habit — which is why this essay must stop before it becomes a how-to. The historical fact is the targeting, not the worksheet.

It also produced a new arrogance. If the model is pretty, the person can become a demonstration. The best of the tradition refused that. The worst wrote papers in which a human being is a set of percentages with a two-letter name.

## Who was left out

People who could not give 400 naming trials. Clinics without a research psychologist. Languages whose orthography did not care about the dual-route fights of English. The cognitive turn was disproportionately English, Italian, and a few other tested orthographies. Global clinics kept doing severity and conversation because that was the caseload.

Bilingual aphasia — Michel Paradis and others — sat beside this turn and was not always invited in. A person who loses one language and keeps the other is a dissociation the box models should have loved. The literature loved it late.

## Residue

Every time a clinician says “it’s semantic” or “it’s phonological” instead of only “he can’t name,” the 1970s are in the room. Every time a student thinks a flowchart is a brain, Lichtheim has been reincarnated in a nicer font. The turn was a gain in precision and a risk of mistaking the font for the person.


## Single cases and the people who could not sit for them

Caramazza’s insistence on the single case was a methodological ethic: group studies of “Broca’s aphasia” hide the only facts that can kill a theory. The ethic has a cost. Four hundred naming trials are not a community clinic. Languages whose print does not care about English dual-route fights were late to the party. Bilingual dissociations — one language gone, one held — should have been the turn’s favorite food. The literature ate them late.

The better papers treated a person as a theory’s risk, not as a demonstration. The worse papers gave the person a two-letter name and a table. This series is on the side of the risk.


Box-and-arrow models look like Lichtheim with a better font. That is funny until a person becomes a percentage. The turn’s ethic — a dissociation can kill a theory — is worth keeping. The turn’s luxury — hundreds of trials, a research psychologist, an English orthography — is worth naming as a luxury. Community clinics did conversation because that was the hour they had. Both hours are history.
