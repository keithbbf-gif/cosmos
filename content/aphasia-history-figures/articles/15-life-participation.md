This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

For a century the scientific prize was the lesion, then the score. The person went home and tried to buy bread. Martha Taylor Sarno, working at NYU’s Rusk Institute, treated that fact as if it were data. The Functional Communication Profile asked what a person actually did with language in a life, not only what a picture card allowed. It is still a score. It is a score that remembers the hallway.

Audrey Holland’s work on conversational treatment and on Communicative Activities of Daily Living (CADL, 1980) made the same turn with different furniture: situations, humor, the right to be a speaker even when the nouns fail. Later, AphasiaBank would record discourse as a research object, not as a warm-up.

## A statement, not a brand

In 2000, Roberta Chapey and colleagues published the Life Participation Approach to Aphasia (LPAA) as a statement of values: the goal is participation, the person and the family are the experts on the life, reduction of impairment is welcome when it serves that life and is not the only god in the room. The statement had ancestors. It also had a politics. American reimbursement likes impairment scores. Participation is harder to bill. The LPAA did not dissolve that fact. It named it.

Nina Simmons-Mackie, Aura Kagan, and others made conversation itself a treated object — supported conversation, partner training — which, again, this magazine will describe as history and not as a protocol. The historical claim is simple: the partner is part of the impairment’s world. Ignoring the partner is a methodological choice, not a neutrality.

## Who was left out

People without a partner. People whose “participation” the clinic imagined as a middle-class hobby. People whose language was not the therapy language. The social turn can become a new piety — “we treat the whole person” — as empty as the old piety of the AQ. The useful version keeps asking: what did this person do on Tuesday, and who was in the room?

Global work on aphasia and community (connect groups, stroke clubs, later online rooms) is part of this era and is easy to Americanize. Britain’s Connect, Australia’s aphasia groups, and a dozen local circles did the work without waiting for a 2000 position paper.

## Residue

A modern clinic that writes a goal about ordering in a restaurant is living in this era whether it says LPAA or not. A modern clinic that writes only a naming percentage is living in 1972. Both dates can be in one note. The life-participation turn did not retire the BDAE. It told the BDAE it was not the last word.


## Billing and piety

American reimbursement likes impairment scores. Participation is harder to code. The LPAA did not dissolve that fact. It named it. The useful clinic writes a restaurant goal and still knows the AQ. The pious clinic says “whole person” and has no Tuesday.

Connect groups, stroke clubs, later online rooms — Britain, Australia, a dozen local circles — did the work without waiting for a 2000 position paper. An American series that starts the social turn in 2000 is starting at a statement, not at a kitchen table.


Partner training is a historical turn, not a worksheet in this article. The partner is part of the impairment’s world; ignoring the partner is a method, not a neutrality. People without a partner, and people whose “participation” the clinic imagined as a hobby, are the limit of the piety. Ask what happened on Tuesday. That question is older than 2000 and harder than a poster.
