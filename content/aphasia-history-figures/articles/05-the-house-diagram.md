This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 1885 Ludwig Lichtheim published “On Aphasia” in *Brain* (volume 7, pages 433–484). The German work sat behind it; the English text is how the Anglo-American classroom met the drawing. Centers for a motor image of words, a sensory image of words, and the concepts between them. Lines that can break. A little house, or a little stick figure, depending on the textbook artist. Later people said “Wernicke–Lichtheim model” as if the two men had sat for a group portrait. They had sat for a problem: if you believe in centers and connections, how many clinical pictures should you expect, and where should the lesion be?

The diagram is a teaching object. It is not a photograph of a brain. That sentence has to be repeated because the house is so good at pretending otherwise.

## What the house was for

Wernicke had given medicine a second failure and a connecting tract. Lichtheim, a physician in Bern and then elsewhere in the German-speaking world, wanted the scheme to generate types: the motor failure, the sensory failure, the conduction failure, the transcortical pictures in which repetition survives because the short circuit between the two word-centers is intact while the road to concepts is not. The power of the model was prediction. The danger of the model was the same word.

If a patient did not fit, you could move the break on the sketch instead of listening longer. Henry Head, forty years later, would accuse the “diagram-makers” of doing exactly that. He was not always fair. He was not always wrong.

Sigmund Freud’s 1891 *Zur Auffassung der Aphasien* belongs in this decade’s argument even if a counseling site will want him for other reasons. Freud the neurologist attacked the simple center. He argued for a more distributed, less house-like language field. The pamphlet is short, early, and usually skipped in SLP programs. It is part of why “the house” was never the only German-language story.

## Who lived outside the lines

The house is a model of *words* as associations. It is thin on sentences, on pragmatics, on the person who can swear and cannot ask for water, on the bilingual who loses one language and keeps the other, on the reader whose print dies while speech limps on. Jules Déjerine’s alexias will need another map. Jackson’s automatic speech will need a hierarchy, not a box. Goldstein will need an organism.

It is also thin on women as theorists. The 1885 paper is a man’s paper in a man’s journal. Katharine McBride is not born yet. When she and Weisenburg finally test 234 people with a psychological battery, they will be answering the house without drawing it.

## Residue

Medical students still meet Broca, Wernicke, conduction, transcortical motor, transcortical sensory, in that order, as if the brain had sat still. Some of them will later learn dual-stream models, dual-route reading, and the habit of saying “ Broca’s” and then apologizing. The apology is earned. The 1885 paper remains the reason the apology is necessary: it made a generation believe that a line on a page could be a lesion in a life.

A later graphics pass may redraw the 1885 plate for the web. Credit Lichtheim and *Brain*. Do not animate it into a “brain tour.” Do not put a smiling stock patient in the doorway of the house.


## How a drawing teaches badly

A good teaching object survives by being photocopied. Each copy loses a caution. By the time an American student meets “transcortical sensory” as a multiple-choice item, the 1885 paper’s hedging is gone. Lichtheim wanted prediction. The classroom wanted a key.

Freud’s 1891 pamphlet is the other German-language object of the decade and is usually skipped in SLP programs because the author’s later fame belongs to another website. In 1891 he is a neurologist attacking simple centers. Put him in this essay as that, not as a couch.

Women do not draw the house. They will, fifty years later, write the book that tests 234 people without drawing it. That delay is not an accident of talent. It is an accident of who was allowed to publish a scheme and who was allowed to hold a stopwatch.


If you photocopy a house often enough, you forget it was an argument. Lichtheim was arguing with a problem: how many pictures should a center-and-path theory predict? The classroom kept the pictures and lost the “should.” That loss is why Head could sound so angry in 1926 and still be answering 1885. A later editor who redraws the plate should print the 1885 citation in the caption, not only a cute roof.
