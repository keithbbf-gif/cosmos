This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

The oldest Western medical notes do not say *aphasia*. They say a man was struck, or fevered, or “without speech,” and they move on to the pulse. Hippocratic writers already knew that a sudden loss of words could travel with a weak side of the body. They did not need a convolution to notice that. What they did not have was a habit of sitting with the failure long enough to ask whether the mind was gone or only the names.

That question runs through the early history. For a long time the answer was mixed with theology, with the four humors, and with the idea that memory lived in a little room inside the head. A person who pointed at a cup and said the wrong word could be called forgetful, mad, or punished. The interesting writers are the ones who refused the easy insult.

## Three early modern beds

Arthur Benton and Robert Joynt, writing in 1963, named three seventeenth- and eighteenth-century physicians who left usable bedsides: Johann Schmidt (or Schmiedt) of Danzig, Peter Rommel of Ulm, and Johann August Philipp Gesner of Nördlingen and Roth. The dates are not a lineage. They are three people who wrote more than a sentence.

Schmidt, in the 1670s, described a woman who could not read after a stroke and then, later, a man whose speech failed in a way that looks to modern eyes like a fluent emptiness. Rommel, in 1683, described a woman who could not speak spontaneously and still could recite prayers — automatic speech surviving the ruin of conversation. That split, between what comes when you ask for it and what comes when the old ritual starts, will return in John Hughlings Jackson two centuries later. Rommel did not call it hierarchical dissolution. He wrote down a parish fact.

Gesner’s chapter “Die Sprachamnesie,” in the second volume of his *Samlung von Beobachtungen aus der Arzneygelahrtheit und Naturkunde* (Nördlingen: Beck, 1770), is the first long European essay that treats the loss of language as its own problem. Benton later called it the first major work devoted to the subject. “First” is a historian’s prize, not a laboratory one. What Gesner actually did was stay with a seventy-three-year-old man, K.D., who had been well and then spoke a fluent mess. The man could not read or write. He was not paralyzed. Other physicians had already seen him; Gesner quotes their letters. He argues that the trouble is not a general collapse of intelligence and not a general collapse of memory. It is a failure to join ideas to their verbal signs.

That last sentence is 1770 pretending to be 1970. It is also 1770 remaining 1770: Gesner still talks in the old faculty language, and he sometimes blames a “sluggishness” of the whole mind rather than a torn place in the cortex. Claudio Luzzatti, rereading the chapter on the bicentennial of Gesner’s death, was honest about both halves. The essay is not a proto-Broca. It is a proto-patience.

## What the words were

Before Trousseau helped make *aphasie* ordinary French in the 1860s, physicians used a junk drawer: *alalia*, *alalie*, *aphonia* (which is not the same thing), *speechlessness*, *loss of memory of words*. Jacques Lordat, after his own 1825 illness, would prefer *alalie* and *paralalie*. Broca would try *aphemia* and lose the naming fight. The vocabulary is not pedantry. Each word smuggles a theory. *Aphonia* points at the larynx. *Amnesia* points at a storehouse. *Alalia* points at a child who never started. A magazine that collapses them into “they didn’t have a word yet” is being kind to the present.

Caregivers in those centuries did the daily work without a profession attached. The physician visited, wrote, and left. The wife, the servant, the nurse, the parish — they lived with the pointing and the wrong noun. Early case reports almost never name those people. The absence is part of the record.

## What this era did not do

It did not localize. Gall had not yet sold the skull as a map. It did not test. There was no battery, no control group of eighty-five healthy adults, no Western Aphasia Battery score. It did not hire a speech correctionist, because that job was still elocution and the schoolroom.

It did, sometimes, refuse the moral story. A man who said neologisms was not automatically a fool. That refusal is the usable inheritance. The rest — the humors, the cell doctrine, the guess that the whole mind had slowed — later centuries had to put down.

A modern clinic that begins a first visit by asking what the person can still do, and whether the person knows the miss, is closer to Gesner’s better page than to a cartoon of 1861. The cartoon is useful. It is also late. The word that would not come was already a medical object in 1770. It just had not yet become a specimen in a jar.


## A note on the older beds

Johann Schmidt (Schmiedt) of Danzig, writing in the 1670s, left a woman who lost print after a stroke and a man whose speech emptied while the limbs still obeyed. Peter Rommel’s 1683 woman could not start a sentence and could still finish a prayer. Benton and Joynt put those beds beside Gesner because they are usable, not because they form a school. There was no school. There were parish physicians who wrote more than a line.

The temptation, in a 2026 magazine, is to call them “early SLPs.” They were not. They were doctors who happened to listen. The listening is the inheritance. The credential is a later American and British invention. Keep them apart.

What a caregiver in 1683 actually did — the pointing, the shame, the neighbors — almost never enters the Latin. That silence is why this series keeps repeating the hallway. The hallway is older than the jar.
