Macdonald Critchley was born in 1900 and died in 1997. He is Queen Square in a later key than Jackson: a neurologist of enormous reading, a writer of essays that wander into history, art, and the odd corners of language. *Aphasiology and Other Aspects of Language* (Edward Arnold, 1970) is the public object for this series. The word *aphasiology* in his mouth is a culture, not a billing code.

He wrote about the parietal lobes, about migraine, about the history of neurology, about people whose language or skill had been changed by disease. The risk of that range is souvenir-hunting. The gain is a reminder that aphasia sits in a life that also has handedness, occupation, and a first language the examiner may not share.

## English weather, late

By 1970 the American batteries were already filling closets. Critchley’s book is not a binder. It is a learned refusal to let the binder be the only English voice. Students who only know the WAB can still read him for the historical chapters and for the tone: a man who had seen enough to be unimpressed by a new acronym.

He is not a speech therapist. British SLT has other parents. He is the neurologist who kept writing after Head, after the NHS, after the tests, as if prose still mattered.

1900–1997: a century’s English neurologist who outlived the diagram fight and the first batteries and still chose prose. *Aphasiology* as a culture word is his residue. Billing codes later stole the word and made it small. Read him when the closet feels like the only England. It is not.

Queen Square trained him to treat a rare sign as if it were a culture’s clue. That habit can become souvenir-hunting; it can also keep a clinician from deciding that the WAB has finished England. He wrote about handedness, occupation, and the first language the examiner may not share because those facts change what a “score” means. A 1970 book cannot replace a 1982 battery. It can keep the battery from sounding like the only grown-up in the room.
