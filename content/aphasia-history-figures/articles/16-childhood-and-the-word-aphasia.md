This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Adult aphasia is a loss. Childhood language trouble is often a failure to arrive. The same English word has been asked to cover both, and the field has spent a century arguing about the cover.

Nineteenth-century physicians already described children who lost speech after fever or convulsion. They also described children who had never had the words. The second group collected names: congenital aphasia, developmental aphasia, dysphasia, specific language impairment, developmental language disorder. Each rename tried to stop a parent or a school from hearing “stroke.” Each rename also hid the children whose language *did* leave after a brain event.

## Eisenson’s institute

Jon Eisenson’s Institute for Childhood Aphasia at Stanford (later work also associated with other programs) used the adult word on purpose. He wanted physicians and schools to take children’s language failure as seriously as they took a veteran’s. The risk was the metaphor: a child is not a small Broca. The gain was the seriousness.

Other clinicians refused the word. They feared a neurology costume on a developmental difference. They were not always wrong. A school that says “aphasia” to get a service, or refuses “aphasia” to avoid a stigma, is not doing linguistics. It is doing bureaucracy.

## Landau–Kleffner

In 1957 William Landau and Frank Kleffner described children who lost language in the setting of an epileptic disturbance — acquired epileptic aphasia, later Landau–Kleffner syndrome. Here the adult word is less of a metaphor. Something arrived and then left. The syndrome is rare, contested at the edges, and not a template for late talking. It belongs in this series because it is one of the few childhood stories that is truly *acquired* in the adult sense, and because it has been used, badly, as a rumor diagnosis on the internet.

This essay will not list signs. A child who loses language needs a physician, not a magazine.

## Who was left out

Deaf children, whose oralist history is a different cruelty and a different literature. Bilingual children, whose “delay” is often an examiner’s monolingual clock. Girls, in some older series that preferred boys. The adult VA ward, which trained the profession’s ear, did not train it for a four-year-old.

## Residue

ASHA and international consensus have moved toward *developmental language disorder* for the children whose language never arrived on time and *aphasia* for acquired loss. The street has not always moved with them. Caregivers still search “childhood aphasia” and land on a mixture of history, hope, and junk. A profession site can do one honest thing: keep the words separate, name the fight, and refuse to sell a protocol in the gap.


## Bureaucracy is a theory

A school that says “aphasia” to get a service, or refuses “aphasia” to avoid a stigma, is not doing linguistics. It is doing a budget. Eisenson used the adult word to force seriousness. Later consensus used *developmental language disorder* to stop a parent from hearing “stroke.” Both moves are historical. Neither is a protocol for a late talker.

Landau–Kleffner is rare and has been used, badly, as a rumor diagnosis. This essay will not list signs. A child who loses language needs a physician. Deaf children’s oralist history is a different cruelty and a different literature; do not file it under this word to save a paragraph.


Parents search the adult word because the adult word is the one the internet knows. A profession site can keep the words separate without scolding the search. Acquired loss in a child is rare and urgent. Developmental language that never arrived on a monolingual clock is common and often misread. Landau–Kleffner is not a template for late talking. Eisenson’s seriousness is not a license to dress a four-year-old in a veteran’s metaphor.
