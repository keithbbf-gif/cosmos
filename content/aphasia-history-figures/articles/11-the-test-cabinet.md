This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Theodore Weisenburg died in 1934 with the manuscript almost out the door. Katharine McBride, who had written a 1932 Bryn Mawr dissertation on the first forty cases, saw *Aphasia: A Clinical and Psychological Study* into type at the Commonwealth Fund in 1935. They had examined 234 patients and, because nobody knew what “normal” adults did on the same tasks, 85 people without aphasia. The book is the moment American aphasiology decides that a psychologist’s stopwatch belongs next to a neurologist’s hammer.

Everything later is a descendant or a quarrel.

## A cabinet fills

Joseph Wepman and Ward Halstead published an aphasia screening test in 1949. Eisenson’s *Examining for Aphasia* gave speech clinics a binder they could carry. Schuell’s Minnesota Test tried to sort severity and type for a VA service. Goodglass and Kaplan’s BDAE (1972) made profiles that neurologists could read. Andrew Kertesz’s Western Aphasia Battery (1982) made a quotient — the AQ — that administrators could file. The Token Test (De Renzi and Vignolo, 1962) asked how much a person could take in when the sentences were only tokens and verbs. Porch’s PICA tried to make every gesture a number.

Each binder solved a real problem. How do you compare Tuesday to November? How do you talk to a physician who will not read a three-page narrative? How do you train a student who has never heard fluent emptiness?

Each binder created a new problem. The score became the person. Families asked for “his number.” Students learned the pictures and not the pause. English pictures stood in for lives lived in other languages. A man who could run a shop and failed the cookie-theft description became a lesser man on paper.

## What a test cannot do

It cannot hear the marriage. It cannot hear the joke that still works. It cannot hear the language that was never the test’s language. Martha Taylor Sarno’s Functional Communication Profile, and later the ASHA FACS, tried to drag the cabinet toward the hallway — what the person actually does, not what the picture card allows. Audrey Holland’s CADL put communication in situations. Those instruments are still tests. They are tests that remember the hallway.

A magazine essay will not rank the batteries. It will say this: the cabinet is a historical object. The 1935 book is public enough to discuss. Modern test forms are commercial. Do not scan a WAB booklet into a blog. Describe what the idea was.

## Who was left out

McBride left the field for a college presidency. The cabinet filled with men’s names and one woman’s vanished career. People who could not sit for two hours. People whose insurance paid for a screen and not a BDAE. Children, for whom “aphasia” would become a fight over a word (essay 16).

## Residue

Open the closet again. The AQ and the BDAE profile are still how many hospitals begin. The better hour begins after the binder closes. Weisenburg and McBride already knew they needed the 85 controls because the binder lies when it has no comparison. Later people forgot that the comparison is not the life.


## Norms, pictures, and money

Weisenburg and McBride already knew they needed 85 people without aphasia because a binder without a comparison is a story. Later commercial batteries sold the comparison. They also sold pictures that assumed a kitchen, a cookie, a certain English. A man who could run a shop and failed the cookie-theft became a lesser man on paper. That is not a reason to burn the closet. It is a reason to remember what the closet was built to do: talk to a chief, train a student, compare Tuesday to November.

Do not scan a still-restricted WAB or BDAE form onto a profession blog. Describe the idea. The 1935 book is the one you can still hold as history. The later forms are merchandise.


A chief wants a number. A family wants a future. A student wants a picture that will not embarrass her. The cabinet tried to serve all three and became a fourth thing: a way to mistake English clip art for a life. Weisenburg and McBride built the comparison group because they knew the first three hungers. Later merchandise forgot the 85 controls were an ethic, not a luxury.
