This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Franz Joseph Gall did not wait for a wet specimen. He waited for a bump. In Vienna and then in Paris he taught that the mind was not a single vapor in a ventricle. It was a set of organs in the cortex, each with a job, each able to enlarge the skull above it. Language — the memory of words, the faculty of speech — had a seat. He pointed, as he often did, near the eyes, at the orbital region of the frontal lobes, on the evidence of people he had known who talked easily and people whose speech had failed.

The method was a mixture of brilliance and circus. The brilliance: cortex as the organ of mind, faculties as separable, lesions as theoretically informative. The circus: reading character from the head in a rented hall, a traveling collection of skulls, a science that could be sold to anyone who wanted a bump for music or theft. Later textbooks kept the first half and used the second as a joke. The joke is earned. The first half is why Broca had a fight to walk into.

## Bouillaud’s bet

Jean-Baptiste Bouillaud was a Paris physician who took Gall seriously and wanted hospital proof. In the 1820s he argued that the frontal lobes were necessary for articulated speech. He collected cases. He argued with colleagues who preferred the whole brain, or the striatum, or nothing so neat. He is supposed to have offered money against anyone who could show a severe loss of speech without a frontal lesion. Whether the purse was theater or cash, the posture was the same: this is a claim you can lose.

The claim was too clean. Frontal lesions do not always steal speech. Speech can fail from damage farther back. Bouillaud’s frontal loyalty would later look both prophetic and stubborn. Prophetic, because Broca’s 1861 specimen sat in a third frontal convolution. Stubborn, because Pierre Marie in 1906 would walk into the same city and say that convolution was not the seat anyone had promised.

Ernest Auburtin, Bouillaud’s son-in-law, carried the argument into the Société d’Anthropologie in 1861, just before Leborgne died. He told the room that a man with a frontal lesion and lost speech was the kind of fact they needed. Broca, who had a patient in that condition at Bicêtre, heard a cue. The autopsy did not fall from the sky. It arrived in a week when a society was already taking sides.

## The other laterality

Marc Dax, a physician in Sommières, wrote in 1836 that loss of speech followed left-hemisphere disease. The paper was not a Paris event. His son Gustave Dax later fought to have the observation recognized, and the fight over priority — Dax versus Broca, province versus capital — still produces footnotes that sound like family law. Broca’s 1861 reports did not yet insist on the left side the way later lectures would. The left-hemisphere story thickened over the next few years, as more cases accumulated and as Broca’s own claims grew teeth.

A fair history keeps both men in the room without turning it into a theft detective story. Dax saw a pattern in the countryside. Broca had the specimen, the society, and the journal. Medicine rewards the meeting. It does not always reward the earlier letter.

## Who was not invited

None of this was speech therapy. The daily hour still belonged to whoever sat by the bed. Phrenology’s public face included racial and criminal rankings that later science discarded and later ethics condemned. Gall’s language organ and Gall’s hierarchy of types came from one culture of measurement. A series that prints only the organ is making a mascot.

Women appear in these decades mainly as patients. Augusta Déjerine-Klumpke is not born yet. The clinic that will hire Hildred Schuell does not exist. The argument is a men’s society argument about where a faculty lives.

What the era left to the next one was permission: speech can fail as a local brain fact, not as a moral weather. The autopsy of 1861 used that permission. It did not invent it.


## How a bump became a permission

Gall’s public lectures were a problem for Vienna and a career for Paris. The collection of skulls traveled. The language organ was one exhibit among dozens — theft, music, parental love — and the exhibit culture is why later scientists could laugh him out of the room. The laugh is cheap if it also throws away cortex-as-mind.

Bouillaud’s cases are hospital cases. They have dates and wards. They also have the usual nineteenth-century habit of treating a woman patient’s speech as a curiosity and a man’s as a faculty. Read them twice. The second reading is for who gets to be a theory.

Gustave Dax’s later campaign for his father’s 1836 observation is easy to enjoy as a priority soap opera. It is also a reminder that Paris is not the only France. Sommières saw a pattern. The capital saw a specimen. Both sentences can be true without a theft plot.
