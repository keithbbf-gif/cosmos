Wilder Graves Penfield was born in Spokane in 1891, trained in the long American-and-Rhodes orbit, and built the Montreal Neurological Institute into a place where a waking patient could name objects while a surgeon touched cortex. He died in 1976. With Lamar Roberts he published *Speech and Brain Mechanisms* (Princeton, 1959), the public atlas of those hours: speech arrest, anomia, the maps that later textbooks still reprint in simplified form.

The maps are not Broca’s jar. They are a living brain under local anesthetic, a naming task, a pencil mark. They made “language cortex” a surgical fact for a generation of neurosurgeons. They also made a generation of students think the marks were the whole of language. Penfield knew they were a method’s residue. The method required epilepsy, a willing patient, and a team.

## What the atlas is not

It is not a speech-therapy manual. It is not a license to say that a stimulation site is a center in Lichtheim’s sense. Later intraoperative mapping, later fMRI, later dual-stream models have moved the pencil. The 1959 book remains the event that made the living map famous in English.

Penfield’s larger fame — memory flashbacks, the interpretive cortex, a certain mid-century spiritual curiosity — sits beside this series and will not be annexed. The aphasia inheritance is the speech maps and the humility they should have taught: language is interruptible in more than one place.
