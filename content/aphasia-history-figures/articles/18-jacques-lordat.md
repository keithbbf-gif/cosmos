This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Jacques Lordat was born at Tournay, near Tarbes, on 11 February 1773, the son of a master surgeon. He took a medical doctorate at Montpellier in 1797, attached himself to the Barthez circle, and in 1813 took the chair of physiology he would hold for half a century. He died in Montpellier on 25 April 1870, at ninety-seven. The long life is not the story. The story is a year in the middle.

In 1825 Lordat lost his speech. He did not lose the wish to speak. He later used that distinction as data. The 1843 *Analyse de la parole pour servir à la théorie de divers cas d’alalie et de paralalie*, drawn from his 1842–43 physiology course and printed in the *Journal de la Société de Médecine Pratique de Montpellier* (volume 7, pages 333–353 and 417–433, continuing into volume 8), is one of the first careful auto-observations of acquired language failure. He preferred *alalie* and *paralalie* to the words that would win later. He was not naming Broca’s future. He was naming stages of an act.

## A physiology of the act

Lordat broke speaking into steps a later cognitive psychologist would recognize without loving the metaphysics: the idea, the inner word, the spoken word, the failures that can sit between them. He described, from memory, a bilingual complication — language leaving unevenly — that later papers would treat as news. He wrote as a Montpellier vitalist, a heir of Barthez, not as a cortical localizer. The 1843 text is not a missing Broca. It is a different science: the act analyzed from the inside of a man who had been unable to perform it.

There is a vanity risk in any self-report. There is also a kind of evidence no jar can give: what it is like when the inner word will not become the outer one, and the mind knows the miss. Later patients will try to write that and be called anecdotal. Lordat had a chair, so the anecdote became a lesson.

## What he was not

He was not a speech therapist. He was not kind by profession to every patient; we have his own case, not a clinic book. He did not win the naming fight. *Aphasia* would belong to Trousseau’s Paris, not to Montpellier’s *alalie*. A profile that calls him “the first aphasiologist” is doing a recruitment poster. He was a physiologist who used his own wreckage.

## Montpellier, not Paris

Lordat’s vitalism — Barthez’s heir, fifty years in a chair — is why the 1843 text does not sound like Broca. He is analyzing an act, not pinning a convolution. Later cognitive writers who recruit him as a founder are doing a compliment that moves him to a city he did not work in. Keep him in Montpellier. Keep the 1825 year separate from the 1843 publication. Web pages that collapse them are writing a caption, not a vita.

The vanity risk of self-report is real. So is the evidence no jar can give: the miss that the mind knows. Later patients who try to write that evidence get called anecdotal. Lordat had a chair, so the anecdote became a course.


He worked after the episode. The chair did not wait for 1843. That fact matters: the 1825 wreckage became a course because he survived into a long old age and kept the memory usable. Ninety-seven years is not a sentimental close. It is why a self-report could become a physiology lesson instead of a family story that died with the man.
