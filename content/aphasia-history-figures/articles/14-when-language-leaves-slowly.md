This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Stroke is a date. Progressive aphasia is a calendar. Families notice a pause that lengthens, a noun that will not arrive, a sentence that thins, and they are told, for years, that it is stress, or aging, or “just word-finding.” In 1982 M.-Marsel Mesulam published “Slowly Progressive Aphasia without Generalized Dementia” in *Annals of Neurology* (11: 592–598). The paper did not invent the patients. It gave neurologists permission to see a language-led degeneration as its own pattern, not as a failed Alzheimer’s story.

Later work split the pattern into nonfluent/agrammatic, semantic, and logopenic variants, argued about tau and TDP-43 and Alzheimer pathology, and built consensus criteria that still move. This essay will not adjudicate the latest criteria. It will say what the 1982 event changed: the clock.

## A different ward

VA aphasia was often a young or middle-aged man after a hemorrhage or an infarct, with a slope that, if it rose, rose in months. Progressive aphasia is often a person still going to work, still driving, still arguing about the name of the thing, while the nouns leave. The SLP’s job — historically — had been built on recovery curves. A degeneration curve asks for something the old binders were not written for: maintenance, communication aids, family education, and the honesty that “improvement” may mean “still telling the story.”

Pick’s disease and other frontal-temporal stories are older than 1982. Arnold Pick described language and personality change at the turn of the twentieth century. What Mesulam did was make *primary progressive aphasia* a phrase a memory clinic could not talk past.

## Who was left out

People whose insurance required a stroke code. People whose first language was not the clinic’s language, so that “word-finding” was misheard as a second-language pause. People whose families were told to wait until the dementia was “general” enough to count. The 1982 paper is a neurologist’s paper. The therapy literature caught up in pieces, and is still catching up, because a progressive caseload does not fit a six-week episode of care.

## Caution

This is not a place for a differential-diagnosis flowchart or a home program. Progressive language loss belongs with a physician and a licensed clinician. Historical naming is not a self-screen.

## Residue

The phrase PPA is now ordinary. The ordinary is the achievement. A person whose language is leaving slowly is no longer required to wait until everything else has left too. The older aphasia history — Broca’s date, Wernicke’s pamphlet, the VA hour — still sits on the shelf. It just is not the only clock in the building.


## What the old binders could not bill

A six-week episode of care assumes a slope that can rise. A degeneration curve asks for maintenance, communication aids, family education, and the honesty that “better” may mean “still telling the story.” Reimbursement likes impairment change. Progressive aphasia does not always offer it. The mismatch is why therapy papers lagged the 1982 neurology paper.

Pick’s older descriptions sit behind Mesulam. The 1982 gift was the phrase and the door: language first, dementia not required on the intake form. People whose first language was not the clinic’s language still get misheard as “second-language pause.” That mishearing is a current fact, not only a historical one.


A memory clinic that can say PPA at the door has already done the 1982 work. A therapy episode that only knows how to bill improvement has not. Families still collect years of “it’s stress” before a name arrives. This essay will not turn those years into a checklist. It will say the clock changed, and the payment system did not always change with it. That gap is the present tense of this history.
