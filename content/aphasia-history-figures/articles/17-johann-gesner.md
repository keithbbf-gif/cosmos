The second volume of Gesner’s *Samlung von Beobachtungen aus der Arzneygelahrtheit und Naturkunde* (Nördlingen: Beck, 1770) does not look like a classic. It looks like a working physician’s miscellany. Inside it sits a chapter, “Die Sprachamnesie,” that Arthur Benton later called the first major essay devoted to aphasia. Benton was a careful man. He meant: here, at last, someone stayed with the problem long enough to argue about what kind of memory had failed.

Johann August Philipp Gesner was born in 1738 and died in 1801. German biographical pages give the names Johann Augustin or Johann August Philipp; this pack uses the form on the 1770 title tradition and in Luzzatti’s 2002 review. He practiced and wrote in the world of small-city German medicine — Nördlingen appears on the imprint — not in a Paris society. The science is letters, not a jar.

## K.D.

The patient Benton and Luzzatti keep returning to is a seventy-three-year-old man, K.D., previously well, then suddenly fluent and wrong. He could not read or write. He was not paralyzed. Other physicians had already seen him. Gesner quotes them. That habit — letting the prior letters stand — is already more honest than a later case report that pretends the author arrived first.

Gesner’s conclusion, in modern dress: this is not a collapse of intelligence and not a collapse of all memory. It is a failure to join ideas to their verbal symbols. In 1770 dress: a language amnesia, a sluggishness that can still be distinguished from madness. Luzzatti, on the bicentennial of Gesner’s death, noted that the theoretical half sometimes slides back toward a general slowing of mind. The clinical half stays specific. Both halves are Gesner. A profile that prints only the proto-cognitive sentence is recruiting him.

## Why he is in a 2026 series

Because Broca’s year is a bad place to start if you care about listening. Because “fluent aphasia” has an eighteenth-century bedside, not only an 1874 pamphlet. Because the word *amnesia* on language is a theory, and theories should be seen as they were born — in a miscellany, next to other observations, without a brand.

He did not found a clinic. He did not train students in a method. He wrote a chapter. The residue is the chapter.

## What Benton was doing

Arthur Benton’s 1965 *Medical History* paper is not a fan letter. It is a neurologist-historian trying to give the eighteenth century a usable object. Luzzatti’s 2002 rereading is the corrective: Gesner’s theory sometimes slides toward a general sluggishness of mind. Both papers should sit on a later editor’s desk. This profile used them as warrants, not as scripture.

Gesner did not name K.D. for a magazine. He named him for other physicians. The initials are already a kindness and a distance. Do not invent the man’s trade or his wife. The chapter is enough.


Nördlingen on the imprint is not local color. It is the opposite of a Paris society. The miscellany next to the chapter — other observations, other organs — is how an eighteenth-century physician published. Aphasiology later extracted the chapter and left the miscellany. Extraction is how classics are made. It is also how a working doctor becomes a founder he did not apply to be.
