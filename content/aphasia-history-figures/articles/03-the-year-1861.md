This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Louis Victor Leborgne died at the Bicêtre hospital on 17 April 1861, at eleven in the morning. He had lived for years on a ward that knew him as “Tan,” for the syllable that stood in for almost every word. Pierre Paul Broca did the autopsy twenty-four hours later and, a few hours after that, showed the brain to the Société d’Anthropologie de Paris. The long paper — “Remarques sur le siège de la faculté du langage articulé, suivies d’une observation d’aphémie” — ran the same year in the *Bulletin de la Société Anatomique*. Two rooms. One specimen. Do not collapse them.

The anthropological society was already arguing about frontal lobes and higher function. Auburtin had spoken. Broca had a patient whose life had been written down: right hemiplegia, almost no speech, years in the hospital. The scientific fame is the softened patch in the third left frontal convolution. The human fact is a man who had been in the building longer than the theory.

## Aphemia, then the other word

Broca wanted *aphemia* for the loss of articulated speech. Armand Trousseau and others preferred *aphasie*, which was already wandering through French medical talk. Broca lost the naming fight. The world said aphasia and then, later, put his name on the nonfluent type. That is a classroom convenience. It is not what 1861 thought it was doing. Broca was arguing a seat for articulated language, not founding a therapy.

A second famous case, Lazare Lelong, an elderly man who could say a handful of words, let Broca argue that the convolution was not a curiosity. Later writers went back to the preserved brains — they still exist — and argued about how much cortex was actually destroyed, and whether the 1861 paper saw what later slides see. The public event remains the meeting. The jars remain the private argument.

## What the year was not

It was not the birth of speech-language pathology. The American academy is sixty-four years away. Gutzmann is not yet born. No one is hired to sit with Leborgne for an hour and write a progress note. Nurses and time do that work, unnamed.

It was not a clean left-hemisphere doctrine on day one. Broca’s laterality claims sharpen across the mid-1860s, as more cases arrive and as the Dax priority fight gets louder. A lecture slide that prints “1861: Broca discovers Broca’s area in the left frontal lobe” is doing three jobs with one year and getting at least one of them early.

It was not only a kindness to the patient. The same career includes the Société d’Anthropologie (Broca founded it in 1859), a flood of cranial measurements, and racial rankings that later science discarded. The man who gave the field a frontal convolution also gave the nineteenth century a scientific vocabulary for hierarchy. A year-essay that quotes the *Bulletin* and skips the craniometer is a brochure.

## Residue

Every introductory course still starts here, because a specimen you can point to travels better than Gesner’s letters. The permission is real: language failure can be a focal brain fact. The habit is also real: wait for the lesion, name the type, move on. Goodglass’s Boston and Schuell’s Minnesota are behavioral answers to that habit. They keep the respect for pattern and drop the wait for autopsy.

Keep the 1861 date. Keep Leborgne’s name. Keep the two societies in two buildings. Leave the racial anthropology in the same year as the paper. The jars still exist. So does the argument about how much cortex the remarks actually saw.


## After April

Broca did not stop in April. The mid-1860s papers thicken laterality, answer critics, and collect more brains. A lecture that freezes him at Leborgne is doing the same thing the cartoon slide does: one date, one gyrus, one saint. The man kept arguing. The jars kept arriving.

Trousseau’s preference for *aphasie* is sometimes told as a mere branding. It is a theory fight. *Aphemia* points at articulated speech as a faculty. *Aphasia* was already wandering toward a broader loss. The broader word won, and then the classroom put Broca’s name back onto a subtype. History is not tidy that way. Naming is a second autopsy.

The Bicêtre of 1861 is also a place that held people for years. Leborgne’s almost-speechless life was longer than Broca’s interest. A year-essay that loves the meeting and forgets the years in the building is writing from the society’s chair.


The Bicêtre specimen culture did not end in 1861. Later physicians reopened the jars, photographed the slices, and argued in print about the insula and the white matter Broca may have under-described. That later argument is not a gotcha. It is what happens when a public event is also a wet object that outlives the meeting. Keep both: the society room, and the jar that would not stay quiet.
