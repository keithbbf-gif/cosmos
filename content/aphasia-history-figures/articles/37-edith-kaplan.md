This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Edith Kaplan was born in 1924 and died in 2009. She is the name that should have been on more title pages and, when it was, was sometimes filed under “collaborator.” She co-authored the Boston Diagnostic Aphasia Examination with Harold Goodglass (1972). She also built, in neuropsychology more broadly, the Boston process approach: the error is information. How a person fails a block design or a naming item is not noise around a score. It is the thing you came to hear.

Aphasia inherits that habit even when the hour is not a full BDAE. A paraphasia is not only a mark in a box. It is a road. Semantic versus phonemic, a gesture that almost is the word, a refusal that is a defense and not a blank — Kaplan’s ear is in those distinctions.

## A woman in the weather

Boston’s mid-century rooms were full of men with titles. Kaplan had the title of the person who actually watched the behavior. Later obituaries and teaching lore are frank about the gap between influence and institutional credit. This profile will not invent office politics it cannot source. It will say the ordinary fact: the process approach is hers, and the BDAE is not only Goodglass.

She trained students who still talk in her sentences. That is a residue more durable than a quotient.
