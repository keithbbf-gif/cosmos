This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Ludwig Lichtheim was born in 1845 and died in 1928. He is the man whose name is glued to Wernicke’s with a hyphen on lecture slides, usually next to a stick-figure house. The English paper is “On Aphasia,” *Brain* 7 (1885): 433–484. The drawing is a set of centers and connections that should, if you believe in them, generate motor, sensory, conduction, and transcortical pictures when particular lines break.

He practiced in the German-speaking medical world — Bern is part of the usual itinerary — as an internist and neurologist, not as a professional diagram artist. The house made him immortal in a way that would have surprised a man who also wrote about other organs. Aphasiology is greedy that way. It keeps the sketch and mislays the rest of the vita.

## Prediction as a virtue and a vice

The power of the 1885 scheme is that it tells you what you have not yet seen. The vice is that it tells you what you must have seen. Head’s later insult — “diagram-makers” — is aimed at the vice. It is not always fair to Lichtheim the clinician. It is fair to Lichtheim the classroom object, which outlived him and began to answer for sins of people who never read page 433.

The house is a model of words as associations. It is thin on sentences, on automatic speech, on reading as its own architecture, on the bilingual split. Freud’s 1891 aphasia pamphlet, Déjerine’s alexias, Jackson’s hierarchy — all of them live in the same decades and refuse to move into the little rooms. A profile that treats 1885 as the end of theory is doing the house’s advertising.

## Residue

Medical students still meet the types in the house’s order. Some of them will later apologize. The apology is part of the inheritance. So is the habit of asking, when a person cannot repeat, whether the short circuit or the long road has failed. That question is Lichtheim’s even when the scan has stopped believing in his rooms.

## Internist, not artist

Lichtheim wrote about other organs. The house made him immortal in a way that would have surprised a working internist. Head’s insult to “diagram-makers” is aimed at the classroom object that outlived him. It is not always fair to the clinician.

The 1885 English text in *Brain* is how the Anglo-American closet met the drawing. Photocopy by photocopy, the hedging left. By the time “transcortical” is a multiple-choice item, prediction has become a key. A later graphics pass may redraw the plate. Credit the 1885 paper. Do not animate a smiling patient into the doorway.


Bern and the German-speaking internist’s life are easy to skip because the hyphenated name does the work. Un-hyphenate him. He is not Wernicke’s junior partner. He is the man who asked a theory to generate types and then watched the types escape into classrooms that did not read page 433. The escape is the fame. The unread page is the caution.
