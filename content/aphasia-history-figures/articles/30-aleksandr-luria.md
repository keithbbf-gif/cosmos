Aleksandr Romanovich Luria was born in Kazan in 1902 and died in Moscow in 1977. English readers meet him as Alexander; this pack uses Aleksandr on first mention and Luria after. He came up through Soviet psychology — Vygotsky’s orbit, the long political weather — and spent the 1940s on men whose language had been torn by the war. *Traumatic Aphasia* appeared in Russian in 1947. The English translation waited until 1970 (Mouton), which means a generation of Western clinicians met the book as a retroactive classic.

Functional systems: not a single center, not a mush. A function is a coalition of regions that can be rebuilt when one member fails. Recovery is reorganization, not a miracle in a gyrus. The idea traveled west as permission to take rehabilitation seriously as a science. The hospital that produced it was a Soviet machine that wanted soldiers returned to work. Both sentences belong in the vita.

## The man and the state

Luria’s later fame in the West (*The Mind of a Mnemonist*, *The Man with a Shattered World*) can turn him into a literary neurologist. The war book is less charming and more useful to this series. It is full of men who can do one thing with a word and not another. It is also a document of a state science. A profile that prints only the handsome theory is doing public relations.

He was not an SLP. Soviet defectology and Western speech pathology are cousin professions with different passports. The residue in an American clinic is the habit of asking which system failed, and whether another coalition can be taught to carry the load. That habit can become a protocol; this magazine will leave the protocol in the journals.

## 1947 and 1970

The Russian book and the English book are not the same event. A generation of Western clinicians met *Traumatic Aphasia* as a retroactive classic. What traveled was reorganization as science. What did not always travel was the time he claimed for observation, or the state that wanted soldiers returned to work.

*The Man with a Shattered World* made him literary. This series wants the war book. Functional systems — a coalition that can be rebuilt — leaked into Western SLP as a habit of asking which member failed. The habit can become a worksheet. Leave the worksheet in the journals. Keep the coalition.


Kazan, Moscow, the Vygotsky weather, the war hospitals, the 1970 English delay — the itinerary is a politics, not a postcard. Western clinics borrowed reorganization and sometimes left the state on the cutting-room floor. Put the state back. A science that wanted soldiers returned to work is still a science. It is not a neutral one. The coalition idea can live in an American note without pretending it was born in one.
