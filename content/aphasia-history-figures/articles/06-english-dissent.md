This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

John Hughlings Jackson did not draw a house. He watched people who could swear and could not ask for water. At the National Hospital for the Paralysed and Epileptic, Queen Square, and in the pages of *Brain* (a journal he helped make serious), he argued that the nervous system was a hierarchy. Higher, more voluntary, more propositional arrangements could dissolve and leave lower, more automatic ones running. A curse is not a sentence. A hymn is not a request. The difference is not a missing box. It is a level.

The prose is dated and the patience is not. Jackson would rather describe a patient for pages than name a center. Later localizationists found him unusable. Later holists found him a saint. He was a physician who hated being hurried by a sketch.

## Bastian and the other London

Henry Charlton Bastian, also London, also nineteenth century, tried to keep psychology and anatomy in one practice. He wrote about word-memory and about the difference between thinking a word and saying it. He is less famous than Jackson and less fun to quote. He matters because English neurology was not a single dissent. It was a set of people who had read Paris and Breslau and did not want to become them.

The National Hospital’s culture — long notes, famous names, a certain imperial confidence — is part of the story. So is the fact that speech therapy in Britain would grow from elocution and from Elsie Fogerty’s Central School, not from Queen Square’s lectures. The physicians wrote the disorders. Other people eventually sat for the hour.

## Head after the first war

Henry Head had already wrecked his own nerves in the name of sensation research — the famous collaboration with W. H. R. Rivers, the arm, the notes. After 1914 he sat with wounded men whose language had been torn by metal and infection. *Aphasia and Kindred Disorders of Speech* (Cambridge, 1926) is two volumes of a man in a chair, not a diagram on a wall. He sorted patients into verbal, syntactic, nominal, and semantic groups that later batteries did not keep. He attacked the “diagram-makers” by name and by temperament.

He was not always fair to Lichtheim. He was not always clear. He was almost always present. The 1926 book is one of the few classics a clinician can still read as if a person wrote it. That is its method and its limit. Presence does not scale to a VA caseload. Tests would come from people who had less time and more need to compare.

Head also wrote the 1908 Paris debate into English memory. He noted that Marie and Déjerine could not agree on the clinical features, and therefore could not agree on the anatomy. The sentence is dry. It is the whole problem of classification.

## Who was left out

Working-class patients at Queen Square enter the literature as cases. Their work, their accents, their return to a dock or a shop, rarely do. British speech therapy’s women — Fogerty, later Muriel Morley, later the NHS generation — are not in Jackson’s footnotes. This essay is about a dissent inside medicine. The profession that will sit with aphasia in Britain has another door.

## Residue

Clinicians still use Jackson without saying his name every time they distinguish automatic from propositional speech, or notice that a person can sing a lyric and cannot order lunch. They still use Head every time they refuse to let a test score finish the visit. They still need the house, too, because a ward cannot run on essays alone. The English dissent did not kill the diagram. It made the diagram answer to a person in a chair.


## Queen Square and the other door

The National Hospital produced famous notes and a certain imperial confidence. It did not produce the British speech therapist. Elsie Fogerty’s Central School (1906) and the St Thomas’ speech clinic (1913) are the other door — elocution meeting a hospital, women meeting a caseload. Jackson’s footnotes do not walk through that door. A series that only quotes Queen Square is quoting one England.

Bastian’s word-memory is easy to skip because he is less quotable than Jackson. Skip him and you lose the English attempt to keep psychology and anatomy in one practice without a house drawing. Head will later sound like the only dissenter. He was not. He was the loudest book.


Automatic speech is not a party trick. It is how a family first notices that “he can still…” and then learns the still does not include a request. Jackson wrote that split as hierarchy. Head wrote the wounded men as people in chairs. Between them, English neurology gave the later therapist a pair of ears and almost no credential. The credential came through another door, with other women’s names on it.
