## Georgian and Regency machines

- Cellaret / cellarette: "little cellar." Mid-18th-century dining-room case for bottles, often ensuite with a sideboard, often metal-lined. Open-topped ancestors are cisterns / wine coolers. Liners historically lead, zinc, copper, occasionally marble. Lead is a museum fact, not a shop specification. [Sydney Living Museums, Cook and the Curator](https://blogs.sydneylivingmuseums.com.au/cook/of-cellarets-sarcophagi-and-other-grave-matters/index.html); [Journal of Antiques, "Drinking to the Future"](https://journalofantiques.com/digital-publications/joac-magazine/features/drinking-to-the-future/)
- A. Hepplewhite & Co., *The Cabinet-Maker and Upholsterer's Guide*, London, I. & J. Taylor, 1788 (reissues 1789, "improved" 3rd ed. 1794). Sideboard drawers: bottle partitions (Guide plate 29 describes nine bottles on the right), lead-lined glass-washing well with a valve or plug, linen in the long center drawer. Separate "cellerets, called also gardes de vin": mahogany, brass hoops, partitioned, lead-lined, used where the sideboard has no drawers. Met Watson Library 161 H41. [Met catalog](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/700240)
- Thomas Sheraton, *The Cabinet-Maker and Upholsterer's Drawing-Book* (1791–94). "Cellaret amongst cabinet-makers denotes a convenience for wine, or a wine cistern." Sarcophagus form ("imitation of the figure of ancient stone coffins"), often under the sideboard, rings for servants to move it. Cellaret sideboard as a developed form; pedestals as plate-warmer / pot cupboard / wine; vases as butler's water or knife cases.
- Robert Adam circle, c. 1760s–70s: side-table flanked by pedestals and urns; one pedestal commonly a bottle store. Later condensed into the fitted sideboard because the three-piece set needed a large room.
- George Smith, *A Collection of Designs for Household Furniture*, 1808: Egyptian Revival cisterns / coolers among the pattern-book graves.
- Do not specify lead liners for new work. Say the history, then name stainless, copper, or a removable tub.

## American dining rooms and pictures

- Henry Sargent, *The Dinner Party*, about 1821, oil on canvas, Museum of Fine Arts, Boston, acc. 19.13. Gift of Mrs. Horatio Appleton Lamb in memory of Mr. and Mrs. Winthrop Sargent. Gentlemen's dinner; cellarette / wine cooler visible at the table. Historic New England used the picture when restoring the Otis House dining room. [Otis House / Historic New England notes](https://otis.house/location/dining-room/); MFA object record.
- MFA Boston cellaret / wine cooler, 1815–1825, Boston: rosewood veneer, white pine, brass stringing, mahogany veneer, lead lining. Acc. 1978.125. Lock marked "GR / PATENT."
- Metropolitan Museum cellaret, New York, 1810–20, mahogany / white pine / tulip poplar, acc. 1970.271. Met label: essential with the sideboard; often stood in the sideboard's center opening and was wheeled to the table. [Met 1970.271](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/1522)
- Regional American work (Boston, New York, Charleston) is documented in museum collections and in the Journal of Antiques survey. Do not invent a Charleston maker or a price.
- Duncan Phyfe / Honoré Lannuier as public Federal–Empire New York names for sideboards. Use as type, not as our lineage.
- Butler's pantry: service room between kitchen and dining in 19th- and early-20th-century houses — sink, china, glass, silver, sometimes a pass. A wet workroom. Not a cocktail stage. The 21st-century "butler's pantry" revival is often a second kitchen or a display corridor. Keep the two jobs separate.

## Concealment, repeal, cocktail cabinets

- Eighteenth Amendment proposed Dec. 18, 1917; ratified Jan. 16, 1919; effective Jan. 17, 1920. Volstead Act same effective date. [Constitution Annotated](https://constitution.congress.gov/browse/essay/amdt21-S1-2-1/ALDE_00013854/)
- Twenty-first Amendment: repeal certified Dec. 5, 1933. FDR Proclamation 2065 that day. [American Presidency Project](https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/proclamation-2065-date-repeal-the-eighteenth-amendment)
- Concealment furniture is a real period brief. Do not invent a secret-compartment patent number or claim every 1920s bookcase was a bar. Soft language: some cabinets hid bottles; many households hid nothing because they had nothing, or hid it in a closet.
- Art Deco cocktail cabinet: 1920s–30s fitted case for bottles, glass, tools; mahogany / walnut / exotic veneer, lacquer, chrome, plate glass, Bakelite. A form, not a brand. Print sources treat it as a defining Deco object. [Irish Times / trade writing on Deco cabinets](https://www.irishtimes.com/life-and-style/homes-and-property/fine-art-antiques/art-deco-everything-you-need-to-know-but-were-afraid-to-ask-1.3767931)
- Philco RadioBar, 1936 publicity: "The Radiobar is not a 'cellarette' to hide, but a magnificent piece…" Interior light, ice container, chrome racks, working radio. Useful as a named, checkable object — not as our product. [Period brochure language, widely quoted](https://artdecocollection.com/furniture/bars/spectacular-art-deco-philco-radiobar-1936/)
- Home cocktail culture after repeal is a hospitality history, not a furniture invention. The cabinet is the furniture answer to a new homework: mixing at home.

## Home bars and built-ins

- Bar cart / serving cart: older hotel and restaurant cousin; mid-century domestic popularity as a mobile hospitality piece. Treat as a type, not a single inventor.
- Mid-century credenza / sideboard as a dry bar: bottles behind doors, sometimes a drop front. Common in American interiors 1950s–60s.
- Rec room / rumpus room / basement wet bar: postwar suburban type, especially 1950s–70s. Plumbing and a counter, not only a cabinet. Tiki and paneled dens are period flavors, not requirements.
- Conversation-pit and sunken-bar rooms are late-1960s–70s interior fashions. Soft dating.
- Commercial bar language leaking into houses: brass footrail, mirrored back bar, speed rail. Those are restaurant parts. A house that wants them is asking for a different object than a cellarette.
- Wet bar: sink + supply + waste in a room that is not the kitchen. Dry bar: no water. The words get stolen. Keep the plumbing as the definition.
- Built-in: scribed to the building, unfinished backs common, site joints, often tied to MEP. Freestanding furniture: finished as an object, can leave on a truck. This shop's public delivery method is company trucks for furniture. A wet built-in is a construction sequence.

## Shop practice (industry, not a spec)

- A standard 750 ml wine or spirits bottle is roughly 11.5–13.5 in tall and about 3–3.5 in in diameter. Champagne and magnums are taller or fatter. Measure the client's actual bottles. Do not design from a mood board.
- Standing bottles want clear height plus fingers. Stemware hanging under a shelf steals 3–4 in and rattles if the case is thin.
- Typical kitchen base depth is 24 in. A bar that is only storage can be shallower. A bar that is a work counter wants landing space in front of the bottles.
- Undercounter refrigeration sold as "built-in" is usually front-venting. Typical 24 in units want a cutout on the order of 24 in wide × about 34–34¼ in high × 24 in deep — **read the sheet**. Freestanding / rear-vent units cannot be boxed like a drawer bank. Perlick and others make shallower (about 18 in deep) front-vent units; name the type, not a bid.
- Ice makers want water and a drain (gravity or pump). Condensation is a finish problem.
- Sinks near bars: local electrical code decides GFCI. Do not quote a code section on camera without the current book.
- Alcohol, citrus, and water attack many furniture films. Conversion varnish / catalyzed systems and stone are industry answers. Site stain on a wet top is how you get rings.
- Interior lights: heat and ultraviolet. LED is the current low-heat habit. Wine cares more about light than most spirits; still, do not cook a cabinet with halogen.
- Historic lead liners are toxic. New work uses a removable tub or a modern liner. Say that every time lead is mentioned.
- Soft-close and glass: useful. Locks: historic (wine was money) and sometimes still wanted. A lock is not a personality.
