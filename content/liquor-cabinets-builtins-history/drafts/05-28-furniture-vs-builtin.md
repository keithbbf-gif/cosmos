I can tell which object you want by asking who owns the gap.

If the gap between the case and the wall is allowed to exist — a shadow line, a plinth that stops short, a piece you can pull out to get a dropped earring — you are in furniture. We finish the sides. We finish the back if the back might be seen, and even if it might not, we treat the back like part of an object. We put it on a truck. We set it down. We go home.

If the gap is a moral failure — if the case must die into the wall, if the crown must return, if the top must scribe to a wavy plaster — you are in built-in. The back can be plywood because the back is the wall. The sides may be unfinished where they hit the drywall. The calendar includes a site day. The piece does not leave as a thought. It leaves as parts, or it is made by a millwork shop that lives on a jobsite.

This shop's public promise is the first object. Made to order in Ferdinand. Our trucks. Coast to coast. A liquor cabinet can be that object. A wet bar wall usually cannot.

People mash them because a rendering hides the gap. The rendering is always a built-in. Shadows are even. Walls are true. In the house the wall is a 1904 plaster wave, or a 2008 drywall butt that telegraphs a stud. Furniture forgives that. Built-in argues with it.

Cost. A built-in is not "the same cabinet, attached." It is scribe time, site finish touch-up, coordination with a painter, a floor that is not level, a fridge that arrived a different height than the drawing. Furniture has its own costs — finish all around, a structure that can rack on a truck — but they are shop costs. I can schedule shop costs. Site costs schedule me.

Movement. A house moves. A piece of furniture can be shoved. A built-in that is tight to a ceiling will tell you about seasonal gaps. We leave reveals on purpose. A reveal is not a mistake. It is a joint that can breathe. Clients who want "no reveal" want a painted room, not a wood object, or they want to be lied to until February.

Delivery. I mention the trucks again because they are a teacher. If the piece cannot fit through the door, furniture has a problem we can sometimes solve by splitting a top. A built-in was never going through the door as a whole. Different design. If you ask for a twelve-foot bar as "one piece," I will ask which door in your house is twelve feet.

I will make a cabinet that looks architectural. Quiet. Long. Almost wall-like. That is still furniture if the gap is honest. I will not call it a built-in to make it sound more custom. Custom is not the same as attached.

If you are in a rented house, furniture. If you are in a house you will sell in three years, furniture, unless the built-in is such a good wall that it is value. If you are making a room that will keep this wall for twenty years and the wall is already wet, built-in. Those are life sentences, not style sentences.

The cellarette was furniture. The Adam pedestal was furniture pretending to be architecture. The rec-room bar was architecture employing furniture. Know which direction you are going. Pretending is fine if we name it. Employing is fine if we hire the trades.

Next the wet bar, said as plumbing that hired a cabinetmaker, not as a wood species.
