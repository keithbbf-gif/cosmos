If you want the ancestor of the wet bar that is not a tavern, do not look at the rec room first. Look at the pantry between the kitchen and the dining room.

Butler's pantry. Service room. In a nineteenth-century or early-twentieth-century house it is where the work hid. A sink. Drainboards. Glass storage. China. Silver. Sometimes a pass window. Sometimes a locked closet. The dining room could look like nobody sweated. The pantry took the sweat.

This is a wet room. Not because someone wanted a cocktail story. Because glasses come back dirty and silver wants a towel and a roast wants a landing that is not the dining table. The plumbing is vocational.

I get drawings now that say butler's pantry and show a corridor of glass doors with LED strips and a wine fridge and a marble ledge for posing. That can be a beautiful corridor. It is not the old job. The old job is ugly in the best way: a sink you can scald, a drawer that can take weight, a door you can shut on a mess. If you want the old job, I will talk like a casework shop that has to live next to a plumber. If you want the corridor, say display. Say overflow kitchen. Say the word bar if you mean bar. Leave the butler out of it unless you are staffing one.

Why the theft happened.

Because the word sounds like staff, and staff sounds like money, and money photographs. A "butler's pantry" in a listing is a luxury caption. The caption does work. It also trains a buyer to expect pretty, not service. Then they put a delicate finish next to a sink and wonder why the door skins moved.

Shop practice, pantry version.

If there is a sink, the carcase next to it is a wet carcase. No particle board that will drink a slow leak. A finish that can take bleach if the household uses bleach. Handles you can work with wet hands. A toe that can take a drip. I am not writing a spec. I am telling you the pantry is closer to a kitchen than to a liquor cabinet.

Glass storage in a pantry is not a bar display. It is a lot of stemware that has to come out fast and go back wet. I want slides that do not rain crystal on a tile floor. I want a shelf edge. I want a light that does not heat the top row. The pretty glass-door bar wants you to look. The pantry wants you to finish.

Silver wants a cloth drawer and a lock if the silver is real. That lock is the Georgian inventory lock again. Different room. Same reason.

The pass — if the house has one — is a hole in a wall with a shelf. It is not a bar top. People will try to lean on it. Build it like they will lean, or put a rail that tells the body no.

When a designer puts a butler's pantry on an open plan, they are often trying to buy back the closed workroom the open plan destroyed. I respect the impulse. The dining room performance needed a backstage. Open plans fired the backstage and then wondered why the bottles and the blender live on the counter. A pantry is a legal backstage. A glass corridor is a backstage with the curtain stuck open.

This shop builds furniture. A true pantry is millwork plus MEP plus a floor that can take a sink. That is a built-in in our three-object language. We can make the cases. We do not pretend the drain is a hardware option.

If your house has an old pantry, do not rip the sink out to make a dry bar unless you have another sink that can do the work. I have seen that renovation. The dining room looks cleaner. The kitchen looks angrier. The bottles look nicer. The glasses get washed in a crowd.

Service is a dignity. It does not need a brass rail. It needs a drain that works.

We are leaving the dining-century now. Next the lock changes meaning. The law arrives in 1920, and the furniture is asked to look innocent.
