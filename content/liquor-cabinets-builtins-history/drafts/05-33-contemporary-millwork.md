The contemporary bar is a glow.

Glass doors. A stone slab. A strip of light that makes the bottles look like they are on television. A fridge with a glass face. Sometimes a sink so small it is a jewel. Sometimes no sink and a lot of confidence. This is a built-in most of the time. The wall is the piece.

I will not sneer at the glow. Light in a case is an old Deco habit. Stone is an old landing. Glass is an old way to show off. What is new is how cheap the glow got, and how often it is the only idea.

Shop fights I will have, politely.

LED is low heat compared with the old puck halogen that cooked a cabinet. Low is not none. A tape stuck to a wood shelf will still make a line of heat and a line of adhesive failure. Mount the light so it can be replaced. Do not bury a tape in a dado you cannot reach. Do not let the driver — the little brick — live in a wet well. Do not color-cycle unless the room is a game room and everyone admits it. White, decent, dimmable. The bottles have colors already.

Glass doors show everything. They also rattle if the carcase is thin. They need a catch that does not slam. They need a lock if the household needs a lock, and a lock on glass is a special hardware, not a closet lock we hope. Tempered. A way to replace a pane. A mullion that is a mullion, not a stripe of metal that cuts every label in half.

Stone. A good landing. A cold landing. A landing that will stain if it is marble and you are sloppy with red wine and lemon. I like a stone that can take the household, not a stone that can take a photograph. The edge is a hip edge. Ease it. A sharp stone edge at bar height is a medical story. The overhang if people sit — that is a steel conversation, not a wood conversation. Stone breaks.

Glass-door fridges are a second television. They hum. They sweat. They want a front vent if they are built-in. They want to be the size on the sheet, not the size in the rendering. We will do the machine. I mention it here because the contemporary bar treats the fridge as a window. A window that hums.

The contemporary bar also loves an open shelf of glass. Open shelves in a house with a dog and a vacuum are a music. Closed storage for the extra. Display for the few. The 1970s den displayed everything and then the dust won.

If this shop is in that room, we are making doors and cases that can stand next to stone and glass without looking like we wandered in from a dining table. The finish has to talk to the stone. The wood has to take the light without going orange in a way nobody intended. Samples in that room's light, not in the shop's fluorescent. I still like pen and paper. I also like a sample on the wall at six in the evening.

Do not let the glow replace the landing. I have seen bars that photograph like a jeweler and have no place to set two glasses and a bottle. The homework still exists. Repeal did not get repealed.

A contemporary millwork bar is not more serious than a dry cabinet. It is more attached. If the household will still be this household in ten years, attach it. If not, consider the honest piece.

We have enough types. The rest of the series is the bench: bottles, glass, light, hardware, finish, tops, cold, climate, trucks, codes, and then how to read a room.

Put the tape measure down on the actual bottle. That is the next episode, and it is the one I would play for a younger maker before any of the history.
