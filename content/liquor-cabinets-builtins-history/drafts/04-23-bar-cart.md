The cellarette rolled to the table. The bar cart rolls to the conversation.

That is not a new soul. It is a new silhouette. Two or three shelves. Handles. Wheels that may or may not be serious. Glass on the top if you are mid-century. Brass if you are a hotel. Wood if you are a shop like mine and you do not want the piece to sound like a tea trolley in a catalog.

Hotels and restaurants had serving carts before living rooms wanted them. The domestic cart is hospitality as a visit. You do not make the guest walk to the cabinet. You bring the cabinet's useful part into the circle. That is either generous or showy. It is usually both.

The job is mobility. I will be dull.

Wheels have to roll on the floor you have. A rug eats small casters. A board floor chatters if the casters are hard and the load is glass. A threshold is a cliff. If the cart cannot cross the room where the evening happens, it is a still life.

Wheels have to stop. A brake, or enough weight and honest casters that do not skate when the floor is a little off. A cart that walks away while you pour is a cartoon.

The handle is a structural part. People will pull the handle with a loaded top. If the handle is a decoration screwed into a thin rail, it will teach you about screws.

Weight. Bottles are heavy. A bottom shelf of bottles and a top shelf of glass is a stable idea. A top shelf of bottles and a bottom of napkins is a sail. I have watched a pretty cart go over. It is loud.

The top is a serving surface on the move. A rail helps. A rail also catches a sleeve. A glass top shows the shelf below and shows every water spot. A wood top can take a pad. I like a wood top with a removable glass or tray. The tray comes off and goes to the sink. The cart stays in the room. That is the cistern lesson: the wet part leaves.

Mid-century carts get photographed in front of a sofa like a pet. Fine. Pets have to be house-trained. If the cart lives in a hall, measure the hall. If it lives in a dining room, it is a cellarette and should be allowed to look like furniture, not like a hotel leftover.

This shop delivers on trucks. We know wheels. We also know that a piece with wheels is not therefore a casual piece. The joinery still has to take a racking load when the cart hits a rug edge. Corner blocks. A web. No skinny stretchers that are only lines in a rendering.

I will not build a cart as a substitute for a cabinet if the collection is a collection. A cart is a service for an evening, not a library. Ten bottles. The glasses you will actually use. The tools. If you need thirty bottles, you need a case, and the cart can ferry. Two objects. The cellarette and the sideboard already taught that.

There is a market for a cart that never moves. It sits against a wall and completes a photograph. I can make a pretty stand. I will not put casters on it for the story. Casters that never roll get flat spots and then they roll badly when someone finally tries.

If you want the old dining-room job in a living room, a cart is often better than a built-in. It does not ask the building for a drain. It does not ask me to scribe to a crooked wall. It asks you to push. That is a reasonable ask.

Next the case that does not roll and does not confess: the credenza that opens into a party. Mid-century closed furniture with a surprise interior.
