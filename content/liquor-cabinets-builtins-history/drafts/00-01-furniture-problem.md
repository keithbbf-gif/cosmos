I'm not going to start with a party.

If I start with a party, you will look at the bottles, and that is a pleasant way to waste an evening and a bad way to understand a piece of furniture. The bottles are the easy part. The hard part is the box. The box has to stand still, take weight, hide or show what you want, and not fall apart the first time someone sets a wet glass on it and walks away talking.

A liquor cabinet is a furniture problem. I mean that as a compliment. Furniture problems have measurements. They have grain. They have a door that either hangs or it doesn't. Lifestyle problems have adjectives. I cannot hinge an adjective.

This shop makes casegoods. Chests, cabinets, servers, desks. A bar is a cousin of those, not a different religion. The cousin has a few extra enemies. Alcohol. Citrus. Ice melt. Light that fades a label and heats a void. Glass that rings if the carcase is thin. A bottle that is taller than the pretty sketch. A stone top that someone wants to treat like a cutting board. A refrigerator that someone wants to treat like a drawer.

None of that is glamorous. All of it is the job.

I opened a custom shop in 1999. We are in Ferdinand, Indiana now, in an old factory, and the work still leaves on our own trucks. I mention the trucks because a liquor cabinet that cannot survive a ride is not a liquor cabinet. It is a stage set. We have been paid to make objects that get used. A dining table that cannot take an elbow is a sculpture with a dining-table complex. A bar that cannot take a wet night is the same disease in a different room.

People will tell you the liquor cabinet is about hospitality. Hospitality is a behavior. The cabinet is an object that either helps the behavior or fights it. A good cabinet makes the second pour quieter than the first. A bad cabinet makes a noise, or a stain, or a conversation about who left the door open and cooked the whiskey.

There is also history in the box, and we are going to walk it. Not because I want you to live in 1810. Because the old names are still hiding in the new drawings. A cellarette is a little cellar that came upstairs. A cistern is a tub that learned manners. A cocktail cabinet is a fitted machine from a decade that had just gotten its bottles back. A wet bar is plumbing that hired a cabinetmaker. If you do not know which of those you asked for, you will buy the language of one and the problems of another.

I have watched drawings arrive with the word *bar* and a picture of a hotel. I have watched drawings arrive with the word *cabinet* and a picture of a closet. I have watched drawings arrive with no word at all, just a Pinterest rectangle and a note that says "something like this." Something-like-this is how you get a piece that looks right in a photograph and wrong in a room.

So this series is going to be rude in a useful way. We will name the object. We will name the century when the object changed jobs. We will name the shop habits that keep a bottle from teaching you physics at midnight. We will not invent a famous bar this shop built. We will not sell you a finish. We will not end with a coupon.

If you make furniture, this is a map of a case that looks simple and is not. If you buy furniture, it is a map of why two cabinets that share a photograph do not share a life. If you just like history, it is a furniture history that never got a clean textbook because the people who lived it were either serving dinner or hiding the evidence.

I want you to leave the first episode able to say one sentence without blushing: this is a box for bottles, and the box has duties. After that we can talk about mahogany, and mirrors, and the 1970s, and why a built-in is not a piece we can put on a truck.

The bottles can wait. The box cannot.
