Innocence is a silhouette.

A bookcase is innocent. A radio is innocent. A sideboard is innocent if you do not open the pedestal. A window seat is innocent. A bar with a rail is not. If you are asked to hide bottles in a decade when bottles are a legal problem, you do not invent a new shape. You borrow a shape the room already forgave.

That is the whole lesson. I can teach it without a diagram of a trick latch.

Look at a parlor of the 1920s. The tall case that holds a radio is already the tallest new object in the room. It has a cloth grille, knobs, a finish that matches the rest of the suite. Behind that volume is air, tubes, a chassis. Air is tempting. A shop that wanted to be clever could split the volume: radio where the radio had to be, bottles where the chassis did not. I am describing a type that existed, not a plan. After repeal, the RadioBar will sell the split as luxury.

A bookcase is even easier to borrow, and easier to fake later. Every decade has bookcases. A dealer can call any deep bookcase a Prohibition piece. Ask: is the depth bottle depth or book depth. Books want eight to ten inches. Bottles want twelve to fourteen if they stand in two rows, or a single row and a door that does not kiss the corks. A bookcase that is fourteen inches deep may just have been a bad bookcase. Depth is a clue, not a verdict.

Sideboards we already know. They had bottle interiors before the amendment. In the dry years they could keep doing that job without changing clothes. The most effective concealment is often no concealment. A sideboard that always held wine still looks like a sideboard.

Window seats and benches with lids are cellarettes that sat down. A lid, a well, a cushion. That is a little cellar with upholstery. It is also a place people sit, which means the lid hardware has to take a body. I do not love a bottle bench unless the well is a removable tub and the cushion is honest about being a lid. Spills and sitting are a bad marriage.

What looking innocent does to the interior.

It starves the work. A radio silhouette may not give you a landing to cut a lemon. A bookcase silhouette may not give you a light. A sideboard silhouette may not give you standing room for magnums. If you borrow a shape, you inherit its limits. That is why a lot of concealment pieces are bad bars and good stories.

If a designer asks me now for "the innocent look" without the old legal weather, I translate: closed, quiet, no rail, no color-changing LEDs, no glass if glass would turn the piece into a store window. We can do that. We should call it manners, not Prohibition. Manners do not need a false spine.

False spines. I will say a little more. They photograph. They yellow. They make a joke that gets told every time a new guest finds the latch, which means the secret lasts one party. If you want books, put books. If you want bottles, put doors. A hybrid with a single real shelf of books at the top and doors below is a piece I will build. A wall of fake literature is a prop shop.

Radios that still work are a different problem. If a chassis is live, you have heat and voltage in a box with alcohol. I am not wiring 1930. If someone brings a period radio cabinet to convert, the first question is whether the electrical is staying. If it is staying, a licensed person looks at it. If it is leaving, we can talk about the case as a case.

I live above a shop. I have a lot of books. I have a lot of tools. I do not mix them in a cabinet that has to lie. The innocent silhouettes taught a useful restraint. They also taught a habit of lying that furniture should outgrow when the law outgrows it.

Next the piece that comes out of the decade proud: the Art Deco cocktail cabinet, fitted like a machine, shiny enough to be a little indecent, and finally allowed to say what it held.
