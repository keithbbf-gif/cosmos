A credenza is a long, low case. Doors. Sometimes drawers. A top that will collect mail if you let it. In the 1950s and 1960s it is how a living room stores the things it does not want to look at: records, linen, the good glasses, the bottles.

When the doors open and the inside is a bar, the piece is doing Adam's old trick without the urns. Architecture on the outside — or at least calm. Work on the inside. No rail. No neon. A suburban parlor can stay a parlor until someone decides the evening has started.

I like this object. I will say that as a shop. It matches how we already build. Casegoods. Made to order. A quiet outside. An interior that is fitted if the client will pay for fitted. It is a dry bar in the language we stole back. No plumber. A truck can take it.

The mid-century ones often have a teak or walnut face that is a single argument: grain running, doors almost invisible, a pull that is a lip. The interior is sometimes a disappointment — a single shelf, a too-short bottle height, a fake wood paper that was modern in a year that loved paper. If we build the cousin, we do not copy the paper. We copy the calm.

Door. That is the surprise. Not a book spine. Not a radio cloth. A door. Adults can operate a door. The 1920s hide is over. The 1950s have a different privacy: the bottles are not illegal. They are merely not the decoration. I respect that. A lot of good rooms still want it.

The long top is a temptation. Television. Plants. A record player that needs a lid. If the credenza is a bar, I want some of that top left clear as a landing. If the television owns the piece, the bottles have moved into a piece of media furniture, and media furniture has vents and wires and a different repair cycle. Say which owner is in charge.

Height. A credenza is low. Bottles standing on a low shelf are a bend. Bottles standing in a low interior may not stand at all if the case is too low. Measure. A low case with bottles on their sides is a wine trick and a whiskey mistake. Labels and corks have opinions.

Depth. The mid-century living room does not always have dining-room depth. A slim credenza looks right and then cannot take a bottle plus a door plus a hinge. I would rather make the piece two inches deeper than make a door that kisses every cork.

This is also the object that gets called a liquor cabinet in a will. "The cabinet in the living room." Nobody says credenza except a designer. Fine. The word cabinet is older. The long low case is the mid-century shape of it.

I opened in 1999, which is not mid-century, and I still live with the aftertaste of those rooms. They taught America that furniture could be quiet and still do a job. They also taught a furniture industry to make a lot of quiet boxes with bad interiors. Our job is the interior. The quiet we already know.

If you send me a credenza picture and a sink, we are not in this episode anymore. We are in wet country. If you send me a credenza picture and a 24-inch fridge, I will ask about the vent and the cutout and whether the long calm face can survive a grille. Often it cannot. Then we split the object: a case for bottles, a separate house for cold.

The credenza is a piece. It leaves on a truck. Remember that while the next rooms try to glue the bar to the house.

Next the rec room. Basement. Paneling. A sink that is proud of itself. The suburban wet bar as a room, not a cabinet.
