I like a pattern book because it is a shop drawing that survived.

A. Hepplewhite and Company published *The Cabinet-Maker and Upholsterer's Guide* in London in 1788. The Met has a copy. There was a 1789 reissue and a fatter 1794 edition that dropped some of the cabriole-legged fashion. The book is not a novel. It is plates and letterpress for people who had to build Monday. On the sideboard plates he does something I still respect. He shows the insides.

Right-hand drawer: partitions for nine bottles, the partition standing off the bottom, cloths behind. Left-hand drawer: a well lined with lead for water to wash glasses, and he wants a valve-cock or a plug. Also a place to change the water that is cooling the wine. Center drawer: linen. He even tells you the sideboard without those drawers is just the old side-table, and that cellarets — "called also gardes de vin" — are mahogany, brass-hooped, partitioned, lead-lined, and used where the sideboard has no belly.

That is a specification. Dated. Named. Wet. I do not have to romanticize it. I can argue with it.

Nine bottles is a number. I have built interiors for people who think they have nine bottles and have thirty. The *Guide* is a dining-room number, not a collector number. If you are a collector, you are not in Hepplewhite's drawer. You are in a different piece, or a different room. Do not make me deepen a 1788 drawer until it becomes a cellar. The carcase will tell on you.

The plug is a number too, in its way. It means someone expected dirty water. It means the dining room was allowed to rinse. It means the "we never had wet bars until the suburbs" story is lazy. They had wet drawers. They just did not give them stools.

Sheraton, a few years later in the *Drawing-Book*, keeps the cellaret sideboard and then talks like a cabinetmaker. Cellaret, he says, denotes a convenience for wine, or a wine cistern. Sarcophagus shape — we will get to the graves. Rings at the ends so servants can move it. In large circular sideboards he will even put a plate-warmer in a drawer, tin all around, a heater underneath, because heat and wine and linen were allowed to share a piece if the tin was honest.

He also describes the grand room that refuses the fitted belly: sideboard table, pedestals, vases. One pedestal a plate-warmer. One a pot cupboard or a wine store. The vases for water or knives. That is Adam's temple, still in print. The book is holding both answers — stuffed carcase and temple set — and telling you which room they belong to.

Why this matters on a shop floor in Indiana.

Because clients send "Hepplewhite sideboard" the way they send "mid-century." They mean a silhouette. They do not mean nine bottles and a plug. If I build the silhouette and skip the interior, I have built a costume. If I build the interior and skip the conversation about lead, I have built a hazard. The book is useful only if we read the inside of the drawer.

Brass hoops on a separate cellaret are not jewelry. They are barrels talking. They keep a lined box from blowing apart when wood moves and metal does not. A hoop is a clamp you leave on. I have used metal straps on wet boxes for the same physics. I do not have to flute them. I do have to admit the physics.

Stringing, inlay, a serpentine front — those are the parts that photograph. The partition an inch and a half off the bottom is the part that keeps a bottle from sitting in the last half inch of melt. That dimension is more important than the inlay. I will fight a designer for that inch. I will not fight them for a satinwood line they already love.

American shops had these books. Baltimore, Hartford, Salem, New York — the Met's notes put the *Guide* on this side of the water by the 1790s. So when we get to Sargent's Boston dining room, we are not looking at a sudden American invention. We are looking at a London interior that learned to speak Federal.

I will not build you a museum copy unless you are a museum. I will take the *Guide*'s honesty: draw the bottles, draw the water, draw the linen, and do not let the pretty front pretend those three jobs are one drawer.
