A commercial bar is a machine for selling drinks quickly to strangers. A house is a machine for living. When the house borrows the machine's parts, it borrows the machine's mess.

Brass footrail. In a tavern it tells the body where to stand and it takes mud. In a house it tells the body this room is pretending. I can like a pretend. I want the client to know they will polish it or they will live with spots. Brass is honest about neglect. It spots. It can be lacquered, and then the lacquer wears in the one place a shoe hits, and the rail looks worse than honest brass.

Speed rail. The bottles stand in a row at the bartender's thigh so the hand does not think. In a house a speed rail is often four bottles of things you use and eight bottles of things you used once. It also collects spill. It is a wet ledge at a height that coats a sleeve. If you want one, we put a drip edge and a way to take the rail off and wash it. If you do not want to wash it, you want a closed cabinet.

Mirrored back bar. Restaurants use it so the room looks bigger and so the bartender can see behind. Houses use it because restaurants did. It will show the cords, the ugly outlet, the top of a fridge, the dust on the bottles. A mirror does not hide. It doubles. If the back bar is messy, you get two messes.

Ice bins, soda guns, beer taps — I am stopping the list. Those are licensed, drained, cleaned objects. A furniture shop can wrap them. A furniture shop should not pretend they are hardware.

Why the leak happens.

Because a photograph of a hotel bar is a powerful photograph. Because a designer has been in a hotel more recently than in an 1821 dining room. Because "bar" in American English still means the tavern more than it means the cellarette. The word pulls the parts along with it.

I will build a house bar that has none of these parts and is still a bar. A counter. A sink if we are wet. A case for bottles. A landing. A light. That is enough. The Georgian dining room already proved it. The credenza proved it. You do not need a rail to be hospitable. You need a glass that arrives.

If you want the parts, we treat them as parts. Named. Cleanable. Not glued into a veneer in a way that makes the next repair a demolition. A rail that bolts through a plinth can be replaced. A rail that is buried in a shaped apron is a marriage.

There is also a body fact. A footrail assumes a bar height and a standing service. If your house bar is a sitting height — kitchen counter, 36 inches — the rail is in the wrong novel. If the bar is 42 and the stools are right, the rail has a chance. Heights first. Jewelry second.

I deliver furniture on trucks. I do not deliver a beer system. If a project becomes a beer system, the project has a different general. I can still make the wood. I will not be the last person to touch the glycol.

The hotel leak is the end of the home-bar history I wanted to tell as history. After this, the language splits into furniture versus built-in, wet versus dry, pantry versus living room, kitchen-adjacent bottles, contemporary millwork with LED and stone. That is typology and shop, which is where this channel earns its keep.

If you take one sentence from the hotel: do not let a photograph of strangers drinking hire your plumber and your brass polisher without a meeting.

Next we draw the joint that decides everything on our bench: is this a piece, or is this the wall.
