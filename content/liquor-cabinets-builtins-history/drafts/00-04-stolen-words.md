I am going to take four words away from the catalog and give them back their jobs. If that sounds unfriendly, good. Friendly words are how a sink disappears from a drawing.

*Wet bar.* Wet means water. Supply and waste. A basin you can rinse a glass in without walking to the kitchen. That is the whole definition. It is not a wood species. It is not a brass rail. It is not a television over a mirror. I have seen "wet bar" written on a plan that had no drain. That is not a wet bar. That is a dry cabinet with a wish.

*Dry bar.* Dry means no water. Bottles, glass, a landing, maybe a fridge that is its own closed system. A dry bar can still make a mess. Wine is wet. Ice is wet. The word is about the building, not about your habits. If you want a dry bar because you do not want a plumber in the living room, say that. It is an honest sentence. If you want a dry bar because a magazine used the phrase under a photograph of a sink, you have been sold a feeling.

*Cocktail cabinet.* That one has a decade. After repeal — December 5, 1933, if you want the civic date — mixing at home became a performance with tools. The cabinet is the fitted case for that performance: bottles, glasses, shaker, a drop leaf or a slide, sometimes a light, sometimes a mirror so you can watch yourself be generous. The Art Deco ones are the ones people collect. Victorian houses had lockable drink furniture too. The cocktail cabinet is the twentieth-century machine, not the eighteenth-century cellar. If you ask me for a cocktail cabinet, I hear hinges and interiors, not a plumber.

*Butler's pantry.* This is the one that got dressed up and taken to a party it was not invited to. In a nineteenth-century house the butler's pantry is a service room between the kitchen and the dining room. Sink. China. Glass. Silver. A pass, sometimes. It is a wet workroom for people whose job was to keep the dining room looking like nobody worked. It is not a place you pose with a coupe. The twenty-first-century revival often means a second kitchen, or a corridor of glass doors, or a place to hide the small appliances. Those can be good rooms. They are not the old job. If you want the old job, you want a workroom. If you want the new job, say display and overflow. Do not say butler unless you mean service.

Why do I care.

Because a shop prices water. A shop prices stone that will see citrus. A shop prices a fridge that needs to breathe. A shop prices a piece that has to leave on a truck versus a piece that has to be scribed to a crooked wall. If you use wet when you mean dry, the ticket is a lie. If you use pantry when you mean cocktail cabinet, the room will be built for the wrong hands.

I also care because the stolen words make buyers feel stupid. A person stands in a showroom and says they want a wet bar, and they mean they want the feeling of being able to pour without leaving their guests. That feeling can be a dry cabinet ten feet from the kitchen. It can be a cellarette that rolls. It can be a built-in with a basin. Three prices. Three calendars. One stolen adjective.

Here is the clean set I want this channel to use.

If there is a sink, say sink.
If there is a fridge, say fridge, and say whether it vents out the front.
If the piece can leave the house, say furniture.
If the piece is the wall, say built-in.
If the room is for service, say service.
If the room is for standing and talking, say that.

Bar can stay as a nickname after the jobs are named. Nicknames are fine. Nicknames as specifications are how you get a white ring in a walnut top and a compressor that dies in a box we built too well.

I opened on Capitol Hill selling furniture to designers because designers were already in the business of not letting a nickname write a purchase order. That is still the hope. If you do not have a designer, you can still refuse the nickname. Take a tape. Take a photograph of the wall. Write sink or no sink at the top of the page.

We are done with the frame. Next we go downstairs in the language and upstairs in the house: the little cellar that came into the dining room in the 1700s, lined with metal, and taught a generation that wine did not have to live in the ground.

The words can come with us. They have to walk behind the objects this time.
