Before I let the 1920s take over the story, I want to put a dull cabinet on the bench.

A lockable case for bottles is ordinary furniture in 1890. It is ordinary in 1910. Hotels have them. Houses have them. Clubs have them. The lock is the Georgian lock in a new skin: inventory, staff, children, the cost of the bottle. Nobody needs a constitutional crisis to order a lock from a hardware catalog.

If we skip this dull cabinet, Prohibition gets to pretend it invented hiding. It did not. It invented a reason to hide from the law, which is a different hide from hiding from the help. The wood does not know the difference. The mouth does.

I have been in houses where a "Prohibition cabinet" was just a late-Victorian bookcase with a later story. Stories are cheap. Construction dates are not. Look at the glass. Look at the hinge. Look at whether the "secret" compartment is a factory drawer with a spring that Sears would have sold to anyone. A secret is a mechanism. A story is a caption.

The dull cabinet also keeps us from treating every closed door as a romance. Most closed doors are dust control. Most locks are a key on a string. I would rather build you a dull excellent cabinet than a theatrical one that cannot take a bottle of Chartreuse that is taller than the sketch.

What did a pre-Prohibition liquor cabinet look like, if we are allowed to be boring.

A chest on a plinth, or a tall case, often in oak or mahogany, sometimes with a mirror inside the door because a mirror is cheap grandeur. Bottle racks. A drawer for tools. A lock. Maybe a fold-down shelf. It is closer to a secretary than to a tavern. The secretary I made at sixteen was a different brief, but the family is the same: a case that opens into a small room of ordered work. Bottles instead of paper.

English and American catalogs of the 1890s and 1900s will sell you "spirit cases" and "cellarettes" long after the dining-room cooler has gone out of fashion in the magazines. The word cellarette gets stretched. Dealers still stretch it. A cellarette is a little cellar. A spirit case is a locked cabinet. If you use the words as I use them, you can shop. If you use them as a listing uses them, you will buy a trunk.

Temperance is in the air in those years. The dull cabinet does not resolve it. Some households had no cabinet because they had no bottles. Some had a cabinet and a pledge on the wall. Furniture history is not a moral history. It is a record of what was built. I will not preach. I will measure.

The Eighteenth Amendment is proposed in December 1917, ratified in January 1919, and goes to work on January 17, 1920. That is a civic date. The dull cabinet does not vanish that morning. Some of them get emptied. Some of them get filled more carefully. Some of them get moved to a closet. The object is older than the law.

Why this matters in a custom shop.

Because a client will ask for "a Prohibition bar" when they want a lock and a dark wood and a feeling of being clever. I can do the lock and the wood. I will not do the clever as a fake brick wall. If they have a real reason to lock, we lock. If they want a story, I will give them this episode: the lock is older than the story they want.

A dull excellent cabinet is also the ancestor of the dry bar we will defend later. No sink. No rail. A case. A key if you need a key. That object never needed a constitutional repeal to be legal. It needed bottles and a room.

Hold the dull cabinet in your head. The next decade will try to make every bottle furniture a rebel. Some of it was. A lot of it was a closet. A lot of it was the same oak case with the same lock and a new fear.

I opened a shop in 1999. I have no Prohibition stories from this bench. I have lock stories. I have height stories. I have clients who wanted a secret drawer because a movie had one. I built a secret drawer once that was a shallow tray for passports. That is a different series. For bottles, I prefer a door that shuts and a lock that works. Mystery is not a finish.
