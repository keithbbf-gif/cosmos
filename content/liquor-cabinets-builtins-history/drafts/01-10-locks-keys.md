The MFA's Boston cooler has a brass lock marked "GR / PATENT." I like that stamp. It is not a novel. It is a hardware house telling you the lid was worth locking in a year when George was still on the coin.

Wine was money. Imported, often. Taxed. Counted. A staffed house is a house with many hands. Some of those hands were trusted. Some of those hands were hungry. A lock on a cellarette is not a joke about husbands. It is inventory control. The same dining room locked the plate. Hepplewhite lines a drawer with green cloth for silver under a cover. Silver and wine are the two things a dining room could not shrug off if they walked.

Decanters got their own wooden cases, sometimes with locks of their own. A decanter is a bottle that has already given up the customs seal. It is the wine after it has become the house's. A locked decanter case is the house saying: this is no longer the merchant's problem.

I keep that in mind when a drawing arrives with a lock "for the look." Looks are cheap. A lock is a small machine that has to meet a strike in a door that moves with the seasons. If you want the look, I can put on an escutcheon and no works. I would rather not, because the first guest who has a key on a ring will try it. If you want a lock, we buy a good one, we fit the strike, we keep a key in a place a cleaner can be told about, and we admit why.

Why, now.

Children. Not a moral lecture. A physical one. A low cabinet with glass and whiskey is a gravity experiment. A lock is one answer. A high shelf is another. A closed room is a third. Pick one. Do not pick a decorative keyhole.

Household help, or the person who stages the house for sale, or the teenager's friends. I am not going to narrate your family. I will ask who else has hours in the room. If the answer is "everyone," a lock is theater. If the answer is "a rotating cast," a lock is manners.

Collectors. A bottle can cost more than the cabinet. That sentence would have made sense in 1820 too, in a different currency. If the bottle is the treasure, the case is a safe that has to look like furniture. Safes have weight, and they have hardware that does not come from a hobby drawer.

Insurance. Sometimes a rider wants evidence of a lock. I am not your broker. If they want a lock, we fit a lock, and we do not laugh at the paper.

What a lock is not.

It is not Prohibition. We are not there yet in this series, and even there, most concealment was not a patent lock. It was a closet. A lock on a 2026 cocktail cabinet does not make the room a speakeasy. It makes the room a room with a lock. If you want a story, tell the story with your mouth. Do not make the hardware lie.

Shop notes, short.

A lid lock and a pair of door locks are different fittings. A cellarette lid wants a half-mortise or a rim lock that can take the slam of a heavy lid. Doors want a catch that does not rack the stile. Glass doors want a lock that does not point a key toward a pane. Soft-close and a lock can fight; the last half inch of magnet is not a deadbolt. Decide which job is in charge.

Keys get lost. I like a small extra key in the job folder, labeled, not taped inside the cabinet where the first curious person will find it. The old houses kept keys on a board in a pantry. We are not those houses. We are a shop that will get a call in two years.

Decanters on a modern interior need a pad. Glass on wood without a pad is a ring waiting for a warm day. Felt is cheap. A thin leather is better if someone will spill. The lock does not protect you from a ring.

I grew up in a carpenter's house. Tools got put away because tools walked. Bottles walk too. I do not moralize it. I hinge it.

When we get to Prohibition, the lock will try to become a personality. Hold this episode in your head. The Georgian lock is about money and staff. The 1920s hide is about the law. The suburban rec-room lock is about kids. Same hole in the wood. Three centuries. Three reasons.

Next we cross the water. The American dining room gets larger, gets prouder, and gets a painter in Boston who leaves us a picture with the cooler sitting right where the Met said it would sit — at the table, working.
