I need three objects on the bench before we go any further, because the English language has a habit of calling all of them a bar.

The first object is a *cellarette*. Little cellar. It is a lidded case, sometimes on wheels, sometimes made to sit in the mouth of a sideboard, built to hold bottles in the dining room so you did not send a servant down every time a glass went empty. The good ones were lined with metal so you could put cold water or ice against the glass. The Met has a New York example from about 1810 to 1820 that they will tell you was wheeled up to the table. That sentence is the whole form. It moves. It serves. It is furniture, not architecture.

The second object is a *liquor cabinet* — or a cocktail cabinet, if you are standing in 1934. It is a casegoods piece. Doors. Maybe a drop front. Maybe a light. Maybe a lock. It lives against a wall the way a chest lives against a wall. You can pick it up, if you brought enough people, and you can put it on a truck. We do that for a living. The cabinet does not own a drain. If you pour ice water into it, you have made a mistake, not a feature.

The third object is a *built-in bar*. Now you have hired the building. The piece is scribed to a wall, or it *is* the wall. The back may never be finished because nobody will ever see the back. There may be a sink. There may be a fridge that vents into the room through a grille you are not allowed to hide behind a basket of napkins. There may be a stone top that is also a counter and also a splash. This object does not leave on our truck, not as a single finished thought. It leaves as parts, or it is made by a millwork shop that lives on the jobsite calendar.

Those are not three sizes of the same thing. They are three contracts.

A cellarette assumes a dining room that still believes in courses and in someone who will roll the wine to the table. A liquor cabinet assumes a room that wants the bottles present without committing a plumber. A built-in assumes you have already decided the bottles live here, in this wall, with this water, for as long as the house keeps that wall.

People mash them because the photograph looks similar. Dark wood. Glass. A bottle with a label you recognize. The photograph is a liar. The liar is useful for selling fabric. It is useless for ordering wood.

I can tell you which one you asked for with three questions.

Does it move?
Does it have water?
Does it belong to the building or to a truck?

If it moves and has a liner, you are in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, or you are making a polite modern cousin of that century. If it does not move and has no water, you are in cabinet country. That is our ordinary country. If it has water, you have left ordinary country. You are in a wet trade. I will still talk about the wood. I will not pretend the wood is the boss.

There is a fourth word that tries to sit with these three: *wet bar*. I am saving the stolen words for their own episode. For now, just hear the adjective. Wet. That is plumbing. A wet cellarette is a historical liner. A wet cabinet is a leak. A wet built-in is a plan.

This shop will make a cabinet that holds bottles as honestly as it holds linens. We have made servers and chests and pieces that open like a small room. We have not, on the public record I am allowed to use, built you a speakeasy. I will not invent one for the sake of a story. The authority I have is casework and the long memory of dining rooms.

When a designer sends a sketch, I want those three objects separated on the page. Cellarette. Cabinet. Built-in. Pick one. If you want a hybrid, say the hybrid out loud: a freestanding cabinet with a stone top and no sink. A built-in dry case with a fridge. A cellarette that does not use lead. Hybrids are fine. Unnamed hybrids are how you get a fridge with no air and a finish with a white ring.

The rest of this series will walk the history so those three names get their jobs back. Then we will walk the shop so the jobs can be built. If you take nothing else from this episode, take the three questions. Move. Water. Building or truck.

The pretty bottle can stand on the bench while we answer them. It has always been better at waiting than the drawing is.
