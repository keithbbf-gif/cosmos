December 5, 1933. The Twenty-first Amendment has the states it needs. Roosevelt puts it in a proclamation. The Eighteenth is gone. I am not going to do the movie version with a street full of jazz. I was not there. Neither were you. What I can do is name the furniture problem that starts the next morning.

The bottle is allowed to be in the room again without a legal costume. The host is allowed to be seen mixing. Mixing is homework. It has tools. It has a sequence. It has an audience if the living room is the living room. The cocktail cabinet we just talked about is the furniture answer. So is a tray. So is a table you already owned. Not every house bought a Deco machine on December 6. A lot of houses bought ice.

The homework is the educational object.

You need a landing that is not the good mahogany dining table unless you have a pad. You need water, even if the water is a pitcher, because sugar and citrus and rinsing are wet. You need a place for the things that smell — citrus peel, a used shaker — that is not a cushion. You need glass storage that does not chip when you are talking. You need bottles where your hand expects them, because hospitality is timing.

A hide does not want any of that. A hide wants silence. Repeal changes the desire from silence to competence. The piece should help you be good at a small public task. That is closer to a secretary than to a safe.

Philco's RadioBar, 1936, is the loud commercial cousin. The brochure language that still gets quoted is the tell: it is not a cellarette to hide. It is a magnificent piece. Interior light. Ice container. Chrome racks. A working radio. Hosts proclaim it, guests thrill to it — that is their copy, not mine. I use it because a manufacturer said the quiet part. The hide is over. The performance is for sale.

Notice the ice container. Notice the light. Notice the radio. The piece is gathering the rec room twenty years early: sound, cold, pour. A rumpus room in a living-room finish. We will get to the basement. The 1930s already wanted the cluster.

What repeal does not do.

It does not make every household a cocktail household. Beer and whiskey neat do not need a fitted slide. It does not abolish dining-room sideboards. It does not make a wet bar. Plumbing is still a kitchen and a pantry in most houses. The living-room sink is still a later suburban luxury.

It does not abolish the lock. Children still exist. Theft still exists. The lock's story changes. It is no longer a conversation with a statute. It is a conversation with a household. We already had that episode.

For a shop, repeal is a permission to let the interior be proud. Fitted racks. A labeled place for the bitters. A leaf that is finished on the working face. No false spines unless someone is making a joke they will get tired of.

I will not claim Americans became sophisticated because a cabinet got a chrome rail. People had been mixing before. What changed is the right to let the furniture participate without a lie. That is enough. That is a lot.

If you are building a house now and you want "repeal energy," I translate: a dry cabinet that is good at the homework, in the room where people actually stand, with a landing, with a light that does not cook the whiskey, with no sink unless you are ready for a plumber. You do not need a radio in the door. You have a phone.

The next stage is the home bar as a piece of the American house: carts, credenzas, rec rooms, paneled dens, hotel brass. The cabinet steps out of the parlor and starts trying to be a room.

We can leave the hide on the hook. If you still want it, we will build manners, not a costume.
