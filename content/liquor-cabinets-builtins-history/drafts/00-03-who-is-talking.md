I should tell you who is talking, because the internet will put a face on a script and call it a maker, and then the script will start claiming bars it never built.

My name is on the door. Keith Fritz Fine Furniture. We started in 1999. The first shop was a storefront on Capitol Hill in Washington. I lived above it. I still live above the shop. The building changed. It is a nineteenth-century factory in Ferdinand, Indiana, about thirty-three thousand square feet, and the wood comes in the back and the finished work leaves the front on our own trucks. We make on the order of several hundred pieces a year. That number is a limit. It means we are not a factory that can eat a weekend of returns. It means a wet built-in that goes wrong is not a SKU. It is a week.

I am the son of a carpenter. I grew up in Siberia, Indiana, which is a real place. I made my first serious piece at sixteen, a Bombay Chippendale secretary that took something like twelve hundred hours. That is not a brand story. That is how long a complicated case takes when you do not yet know what you are doing and you refuse to stop. A liquor cabinet is a complicated case with extra weather inside it.

I used to cold-call designers. In 2001 we built a dining table for President Clinton. I mention that once so we can put it down. It is a true job. It is not a liquor cabinet. I will not slide from a public table to an invented bar because the topic of the week is bottles.

Here is what this shop is, for this series.

We are a custom casegoods shop. Chests, cabinets, servers, desks, dining furniture. Made to order. Sold, usually, through the designer-showroom door. Delivered by us. That is enough authority to talk about doors, finishes, glass, weight, and the difference between a piece and a wall.

Here is what this shop is not.

We are not a plumber. We are not an electrician. We are not a general contractor. When I talk about a wet bar, I am talking as the person who has to wrap wood around a sink and a cold machine. I am not the person who signs the permit. If your local book wants GFCI within a certain distance of a basin, that is a licensed conversation. I will name the conversation. I will not quote you a section from memory and call it law.

We are not a speakeasy historian with a private archive. I will use public objects. Hepplewhite's *Guide*, 1788. Sheraton's *Drawing-Book*. Henry Sargent's *Dinner Party* at the MFA, about 1821. A New York cellaret at the Met. The Eighteenth Amendment going to work on January 17, 1920, and going away on December 5, 1933. A Philco RadioBar in 1936 that bragged it was not a cellarette to hide. Those are checkable. A secret bookcase I invent in a client's grandfather's house is not.

We are not a catalog of hotel parts. Brass rails, speed rails, underbar ice bins — those are restaurant language. I can tell you what they do. I will not pretend they are how a dining room in Ferdinand thinks.

I still like pen and paper. The company runs on computers. Both of those can be true. I still think you should touch a door before you decide it is the door. That opinion will show up when we get to glass and lights. You can disagree after you understand the object. You should not disagree before, because then you are just defending a rendering.

If you came here for a tour of pretty bars, there will be pretty bars. They will show up as evidence. The product is the history and the shop practice. I want a younger maker to stop drawing a 24-inch fridge into an 18-inch hole. I want a buyer to stop calling a built-in a cabinet when what they want is a piece of the house. I want the channel to be a place where the words get their jobs back.

That is who is talking. A shop. A truck. A person who has been paid to make cases, and who is tired of watching a bottle photograph do the work of a drawing.

The next episode takes the words that got stolen — wet, dry, cocktail, pantry — and gives them back their plumbing.
