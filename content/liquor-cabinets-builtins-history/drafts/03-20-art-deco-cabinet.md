If the cellarette is a well, the cocktail cabinet is a toolkit.

You open it and the piece stops being a box. A slide comes out. A leaf drops. A mirror throws your hands back at you. Chrome stems wait in a rack. Bottles stand in a row like a small orchestra. There is a place for bitters that is the size of bitters. That fittedness is the form. Art Deco did not invent mixing. It invented a piece of furniture that assumed mixing was a domestic job with an audience.

Dates, loose on purpose. Collectors will tell you the decade they care about is the mid-1920s into the 1930s. In the United States those years include the dry law and the first wet years after. In France and England the cabinet does not need the amendment to exist. Do not make the form wait for 1933. Do not pretend every Deco cabinet is a speakeasy prop. Some are just rich.

Materials are the other definition. Mahogany. Walnut. Burrs. Exotic veneers that a later war will make look like a different century. Lacquer that reads as wet even when it is dry. Chrome. Plate glass. Bakelite knobs. The machine age wanted furniture to look like it had been designed by a person who had seen a train. Verticals. Steps. A skyscraper in two feet of width.

I have to be careful here. I love veneer. I also know veneer on a cabinet that lives next to ice is a bet. The old lacquer is hard and it chips in a way that looks like money when it is original and like damage when it is yours. If we build a cousin now, we talk about a film that can take a wet glass on the slide. A slide is a bar top that hides. Finish it like a bar top.

The mirror is not vanity only. It throws light into a deep case. It lets the host see the pour without turning toward the room. It also shows fingerprints and it breaks. Tempered if we can, and a way to replace it without dismantling the carcase. Period mirrors were just mirrors. We do not owe the period a trip to the hospital.

Chrome racks: measure the glass, not the drawing. Stemware has gotten taller. A 1930 rack will bite a 2020 wine glass. I would rather make fewer, taller bays than force a coupe from a catalog that does not exist anymore.

Drop fronts and slides need honest hardware. A drop that holds a bottle and an elbow is a structural leaf, not a jewelry fall-front. Stays. A rail so a glass does not walk off the edge. If the leaf is also the only landing, it is going to get used as a table. Build the table.

There is a cocaine joke that circulates around mirrored slides in dealer talk. I am not repeating it as history. Cocaine had its own legal weather. A mirror is a mirror. If a dealer needs a vice to price a cabinet, the cabinet should be priced as furniture first.

Shop, Ferdinand, now. I will build a fitted interior. I will not spray a two-tone sunburst on a walnut door because a movie did. If you want Deco, bring a room that can take a shiny vertical. A quiet farmhouse dining room will lose. A city apartment with a hard ceiling and a hard floor might win. The form is loud even when the wood is brown.

The cocktail cabinet is also the first ancestor that wants electricity as a habit. A little light when the door opens. That light, in period, is heat. Now it is an LED and a switch that should not live in a wet well. We will do lights as their own episode. For now: the Deco cabinet assumes you will look into it. The cellarette assumed you would look down. Different eyes. Different joinery.

When a client says "bar cabinet" and sends a Deco picture, I translate: fitted interior, a working leaf, doors that disappear the mess, maybe a light, no sink. That is a dry machine. It is a lot of shop hours in the interior. The exterior can be simple if the interior is the point. A lot of money in this form is spent where the camera never goes until the door opens. That is the right place to spend it.

Next the civic morning: December 5, 1933, and the new homework of mixing without hiding. The furniture is allowed to brag. Some of it will brag with a radio.
