The 1970s do not invent the home bar. They turn up the contrast.

Dark paneling. Shag if the house had shag. A mirror that makes a small den feel like it has a twin. A bar that is no longer a credenza you open. It is a wall you stand at. Sometimes the floor drops. Conversation pit. Sunken lounge. The bar sits on the rim like a lifeboat. I say that with affection and with a limp I do not actually have. Steps in the dark next to glass are a design decision.

I will keep the dating soft. Late sixties into the seventies. Some rooms earlier. Some rooms that never got the memo and still look like 1974. The type is the room that wants to be a club and a family room at once.

What that does to wood.

Paneling is millwork, not furniture. It belongs to the wall. If we build a bar in that room, we are in built-in country. Scribe. Unfinished backs. A top that dies into a panel. A fridge grille that has to live in the panel line. This is not a truck piece. This is a site piece.

Mirrors on the back bar: period correct and hard on a face at eight in the morning. Also hard on a shop, because a big mirror is a freight and a safety problem. If we do a mirror, it is in parts, it is replaceable, and it is not the only light. A dark den with one mirror and one yellow bulb is a cave. Caves are for weekends. Houses have Tuesdays.

Sunken floors are architecture. I do not pour them. I will say the furniture has to know the step. A cabinet that sits on the upper level and opens toward the pit has doors that swing into a drop. A cart that lives in a pit does not want to come upstairs with a load. If you love the pit, love it with lighting on the nosing. That is not a furniture joke.

The 1970s bar loves a pass-through to the kitchen. That is the butler's pantry idea after the butler has been fired. A hole. A ledge. A way to hand a plate without walking. It is a good idea when the ledge is not also the only bar top. Two landings. One for food. One for the pour. They can be the same height. They should not be the same two square feet.

Smoke. I will say it. Those rooms were built for cigarettes. Finishes yellowed on purpose and then on accident. If we refinish a 1970s bar, we are not restoring a nicotine glaze unless a conservator is in charge. We are putting a film that can take a wet glass and a cleaner that is not a cigarette.

I will build a quiet 1970s cousin: closed wood, a real top, a sink if the room is already wet, no disco. The decade had good carpenters. It also had a lot of paneling that was paper. We can tell them apart with a thumbnail.

If you ask for "vintage bar" and send a pit, I will ask whether the pit is staying. If the pit is going, do not keep the bar height that was designed for a floor that will not exist. Heights remember floors.

The den taught a generation that hospitality could be a destination inside the house. Not the dining room. Not the kitchen. A third place. That is valuable. It is also how you get a room nobody uses until December. If you are going to spend a built-in's money, spend it on a room that has a weekly life. A bar that is only for a party is a party hat. Party hats live in a box.

Next the parts that keep sneaking in from restaurants: brass rails, mirrored backs, the hotel lobby that wants a zip code in a house.
