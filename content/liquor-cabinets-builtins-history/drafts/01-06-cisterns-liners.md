If you only look at the mahogany, you will misunderstand the object.

A cistern, a wine cooler, a cellarette with a well — those are wood jackets. The weather lives in the metal. Cold water. Ice when the house could get ice. Snow in a climate that still had it. In New South Wales they will tell you they used well water because ice was a rumor. Same machine. Different refrigerant.

The liner was lead more often than a modern shop wants to say out loud. Zinc. Copper. Sometimes marble. The MFA has a Boston cooler, 1815 to 1825, rosewood on the outside, lead on the inside. That is not a scandal. That is 1820. Lead was the sheet a cabinetmaker could work, and it kept water away from the carcase. It also kept water in contact with a poison. We do not specify lead. I will say that every time the word comes up, because someone in a comments section will think authenticity means a toxic tub.

Hepplewhite, in the 1788 *Guide*, does not whisper about this. He puts a lead-lined well in a sideboard drawer for washing glasses, and another for keeping wine cool, and he wants a valve or a plug to let the dirty water out. He is describing a wet piece of dining-room furniture in the same breath as the linen drawer. The dining room was allowed to be a little scullery if the scullery was mahogany.

Hear that. The wetness is not a 1950s invention. The suburban wet bar did not invent water next to bottles. It moved the water to a rec room and gave it stools. The Georgian dining room already had a plug.

What does a shop do with that history now.

You do not line a new cabinet with lead. You do not "age" a well with a material that will leach. If a client wants the old job — bottles standing in cold — you give them a removable tub. Stainless. A copper insert they can take to a sink. A plastic tub if they are honest and the wood is too pretty to wet. The wood is the furniture. The wet is a part that can leave.

I have seen people pour ice into a raw oak interior because a movie did it. Oak will tell on you. The stain will bloom. The bottom will cup. The smell will change. That is not character. That is a finish failure with a story attached.

Copper is pretty and copper is a metal that wants a living finish or a lacquer you will have to renew. Zinc is the old American counter metal; it spots; it is honest. Stainless is the restaurant answer and it looks like a restaurant if you leave a polished 2-inch rim showing. All of those are choices. None of them is "lead, but safe."

Marble shows up in the museum lists as an occasional liner. Marble is also a stone that stains with red wine and does not love acid. If you want marble, you want it as a top you can wipe, not as a well you pretend is a cooler. We will get to tops later. The cistern is not a top. The cistern is a basin.

Open-topped coolers — the hip-bath shapes in George Smith's 1808 book, the Egyptian ones that look like they are waiting for a funeral — those are the ancestors that did not bother with a lid. You could see the ice. You could also see the ring of dirt that ice leaves. A lid is hygiene as much as it is lock and style.

When I draw a modern cousin, I draw the liner as a part with a part number in my head even if it is shop-made. How does it come out. How do you dry it. What happens if someone leaves two inches of limewater in it for a month. If I cannot answer those, I do not get to call the piece a cooler. I get to call it a box with a wish.

The wood around the liner has to expect a leak. Not hope for one. Expect. A bottom that can be replaced. A finish on the interior that is not the same polite oil you put on a jewelry drawer. Cross-grain battens if the bottom is wide. No MDF in a wet well. I should not have to say that. I have had to say that.

A last shop sentence, because it saves a fight. If the client wants the *look* of a cistern and the *life* of a dry cabinet, we do not put a fake drain plug on the outside for charm. Fake plumbing is how you get a guest who tries it. The old plugs were real. Hepplewhite's valve was real. We either do the wet job with a removable tub, or we do a dry case and keep our hands off the theater.
