Before the sideboard learned to hold bottles in its own belly, the fashionable dining room bought a little temple.

Picture a side-table. Long. Handsome. Not much inside it. Flanking it, a pair of pedestals, the kind that look like they are waiting for a bust. On the pedestals, urns. Robert Adam's world, 1760s and 1770s, and every provincial shop that could read a plate. One pedestal is a plate-warmer — tin lining, a heater, racks. The other is storage. Sometimes a pot cupboard, which is a polite English way to say the dining room still had bodily facts. Sometimes a cellarette. The urns hold water for the butler, or knives. Sheraton will still be describing that set a generation later, because the set worked in a large room and looked like architecture.

Hear the trick. The bottles are not a piece of furniture you recognize as a bar. The bottles are inside a column. The water is inside a vase. The heat is inside a box that you are supposed to read as stone. Neoclassical design is very good at hiding work in monuments.

I like this ancestor because it tells on every client who says they do not want the room to "look like a bar." Neither did Adam. He wanted the room to look like Rome had come to dinner. The wine still needed a home. So the home learned to wear a toga.

The set had a problem. It needed space. A table plus two pedestals plus urns plus the clearance to open them is a dining room with money and length. Smaller rooms — and most rooms are smaller rooms — could not afford the theater. So the cabinetmakers did what cabinetmakers do. They stuffed the jobs into one carcase.

That stuffing is the fitted sideboard. Hepplewhite's 1788 plates show it without blushing: bottle partitions on one side, a lead well on the other, linen in the long drawer. The pedestals did not die. They migrated. Regency dining rooms grow pedestal ends again, heavier, and one of them is still a bottle store. Victorian sideboards will turn the pedestal into a carved cliff with a cellarette in the basement of the cliff. The job keeps walking. The costume changes.

In the shop, a pedestal is a tall box with a door you cannot see until you know where to press. That is a hardware problem and a proportion problem. If I build you an "Adam" pedestal in 2026, I am not building Adam. I am building a tall cabinet that wants to look like it is not a cabinet. The door has to land on a reveal that does not shout. The interior has to take bottles without making the column bulge. Heat, if anyone still wants a plate-warmer, is a modern conversation with UL and a lawyer. We do not put a spirit lamp in a new pedestal because a pattern book did.

The urn is worse. An urn that holds water is a vessel. An urn that holds knives is a box. An urn that is only a shape is a souvenir. I will make a souvenir if you pay for a souvenir. I will not call it a butler's urn. The old urns earned their name by being used by a person whose job title we no longer staff.

What I will take from Adam, for a living room that does not want a tavern, is the permission to hide the work. A pair of tall cases flanking a serving table. Bottles in one. Glass in the other. No rail. No neon. No stools. The room stays a room. The bottles stay employed.

People will say that is "built-in thinking" in furniture. It is older than built-in thinking. It is furniture pretending to be architecture, which is the opposite of a built-in, which is architecture employing furniture. Keep those directions straight. Adam's pedestals can leave the house. A niche cannot.

If your dining room is short, do not force the temple. That is how you get urns that bruise hips. Take the fitted sideboard. It was invented for you. If your dining room is long and you want the temple, we can talk about two cases and a table that is not trying to be all three. I will still ask the three questions. Move. Water. Building or truck. Pedestals can move. Urns that hold water are wet. Niches are the building.

Next, the books that taught every shop how to stuff the temple into a drawer: Hepplewhite's *Guide* and Sheraton's *Drawing-Book*, and the phrase *cellaret sideboard* that is still hiding in antique-dealer English.
