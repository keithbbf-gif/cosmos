January 17, 1920. The Eighteenth Amendment is at work. The Volstead Act is at work. Manufacture, sale, transportation of intoxicating liquor for beverage purposes — I am not your lawyer, I am giving you the civic weather. December 5, 1933. The Twenty-first Amendment is certified. Roosevelt proclaims the repeal that day. Thirteen years, give or take the last morning.

What does that weather do to a cabinetmaker.

It does not invent a new joint. It invents a new brief for some clients: the piece should not look like a bar. It should look like a bookcase, a radio, a sideboard, a window seat. The bottles should not announce themselves to a person who did not need to know.

I said some clients. I will keep saying it. Most American households were not commissioning trick furniture. Most American households were dry, or quiet, or buying from a bootlegger and putting the bottle in a cupboard that already existed. The antique market later needed a story, and "Prohibition" is a story that raises a price. Soft language only. Some cabinets hid. Many did not.

The brief, where it existed, is still interesting as a shop problem.

If the piece must look like a bookcase, the door bars have to read as shelves from across a room. That means false spines, or real books on a shallow ledge, or a mullion pattern that does not shout "bar." False spines are a theater prop. They collect dust and jokes. A shallow ledge of real books is more honest and harder to live with, because someone will want to read the book.

If the piece must look like a radio cabinet, it is riding a different boom. Radios were furniture. A tall case with cloth and knobs was already in the parlor. Putting bottles behind that cloth is a pun. The Philco RadioBar of 1936 — after repeal — will make the pun official and then brag that it is not a cellarette to hide. We will get there. During the dry years the pun is quieter.

If the piece is just a closet, it is not furniture history. It is a closet. I will not dress a closet up as a design movement.

Enforcement was uneven. Cities were wetter than counties. A dining room in a wet city might not have bothered to hide. A dining room in a dry county might have emptied the sideboard and left the partitions as fossils. Region matters. I am not going to map every county. I will say: do not take a New York apartment story and apply it to a farmhouse as if the law felt the same.

What I will not do on this channel.

I will not give you a how-to for concealment that could be read as advice for hiding inventory from anyone. This is history and shop practice for legitimate households. A lock for children is one thing. A lecture on fooling an inspection is not a furniture essay I will write.

I will not invent a raid on a house I did not stand in. I will not name a client. I will not claim this shop has a Prohibition lineage. We opened in 1999.

The useful residue of the brief is aesthetic, and it is moral in a small way.

Aesthetic: we still get asked for bars that do not look like bars. The open-plan living room does not want a tavern. The old brief is useful. Closed doors. No rail. No neon. A case that could be a cabinet for dishes if you changed the interior. That is good taste and also a 1920s ghost.

Moral, small: a piece of furniture should not lie more than the client needs. If the law is gone, a fake bookcase is a costume. Costumes are allowed. They should be priced as costumes. A good cabinet that happens to be quiet is not a costume. It is manners.

Thirteen years is long enough for a style to grow up in the dark and come out blinking. The style that comes out blinking is the cocktail cabinet — fitted, mirrored, proud. Repeal does not only return the bottle to the table. It returns the right to let the furniture say so.

Next, the innocent objects: bookcases, radios, and the furniture of looking like you were only reading.
