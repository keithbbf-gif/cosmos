# Platform Shoes and Altitude Inflation

A platform shoe is a lie you can walk in. The sole adds a storey under the foot so the wearer gains height without a stiletto's pitch — or, in the loud decades, with the pitch as well. Fashion returns to that extra inch whenever it wants a silhouette to read from across a room.

The 1970s glam version is the one people still steal from. David Bowie in Kansai Yamamoto and in stacked boots that made him a cartoon of a man. Elton John in cherry-red platforms that were already a stage set before he sat at the piano. Terry de Havilland in London cut rainbow stacks in leather and glitter. On the high street, a cork or crepe platform under a sandal did a quieter job for people who were not on Top of the Pops. Disco kept the height: a strappy sandal with a chunky sole, a boot that turned a dance floor into a small mountain range. The materials were patent, metallic leather, sometimes a wood wedge painted to look like sculpture.

The 1990s brought Buffalo. Buffalo London's 1340-style boots — the ones the Spice Girls wore into every magazine of 1997 — put a fat, rounded platform under a sneaker-boot hybrid and made height look like a club, not a ballroom. Vivienne Westwood had already staged the joke in 1993: the Super Elevated Gillie, a mock-Scottish lace-up on a cartoon mountain, the pair Naomi Campbell fell in on the catwalk. That fall became a caption. The shoe survived it. Club kids and mall kids bought cheaper stacks from the same idea: more sole, more attitude, less apology.

Then the height went out of fashion for a stretch. Ballet flats, Converses, and a skinny heel took over the 2000s photographs. Platforms waited in the closet with the rest of the 1990s.

Cork, crepe rubber, EVA foam, and a stacked leather lift are different ways to buy the same inch. The 1970s liked cork and wood. Buffalo liked a rounded, almost cartoon foam. The 2010s brought them back as flatforms: a thick, level sole under a sandal or a brogue, sold by Jeffrey Campbell and then by everyone with a mall lease. The look was less glam, more "I want the height and I still have to walk to the train." Later in the decade and into the 2020s the stack got louder again. Alessandro Michele's Gucci put a platform under the Horsebit loafer and made a prep shoe into a parade float. Naked Wolfe, Steve Madden, and a dozen online labels sold mega-platforms that photographed well from a low camera angle. Runways at Versace and at Maison Margiela played with the same inflation. Height became a graphic again.

Beds run a parallel argument about altitude, and here the rhyme is earned because both are about how far a body sits off the floor. The 1970s platform bed — a mattress on a plinth, sometimes upholstered, sometimes lacquered — put the sleeper on a stage. Hollywood and the better suburban catalogs sold the idea as modern luxury: you climbed into sleep. A high sleigh bed, with a scrolled headboard and footboard, does the same work in a more traditional dialect. You are up. The room has a horizon line at mattress height.

The 2000s went the other way. The low Japanese-style platform — a slim wood frame just off the floor, popular in West Elm catalogs and in IKEA's MALM years — asked the room to feel calmer and closer to tatami logic, even when the rest of the apartment was a laptop and a laundry basket. Futon culture and a taste for "zen" marketing (the word did a lot of unearned work) made low look expensive. A person sitting on that bed had a different relationship to the window sill.

Shoes and beds do not copy each other on a calendar. They do answer the same question: how much air do you want under the body? When fashion wants a silhouette to shout, the sole thickens. When interiors want a room to hush, the mattress drops. When both want drama, you get the stacked boot and the sleigh. When both want a reset, you get a flat sneaker and a frame that almost disappears.

The platform will keep coming back because height is cheap theater. You do not need a new last or a new textile science. You need a thicker midsole and a photograph. The 1970s did it in glitter. The 1990s did it in Buffalo's gum-and-leather stack. The 2010s and 2020s did it in loafers that refuse to be quiet. The bed, slower and heavier, will keep choosing high or low on its own slower clock. The two only look related when you notice that both are arguments about the floor.
