# Logomania Waves and Quiet Backlashes

A logo is a signature that escaped the lining. Some decades want that signature on the outside of the bag, the belt, the button. Other decades want it hidden, as if wealth were a private joke. The swing between those moods is older than Instagram and younger than heraldry. Fashion just happens to run it on a short loop.

Damier checks, monogram flowers, a repeating GG: the print is the product and the receipt. Louis Vuitton's monogram canvas, drawn by Georges Vuitton in 1896, was already a brand before the word "logomania" existed. Gucci's GG, Chanel's interlocking Cs, Hermès's carriage — these marks sat on leather goods for most of the twentieth century as a kind of polite code. The 1980s and 1990s turned the code into a shout. A Speedy or a Keepall in monogram was not shy. A Gucci loafers pair with the Horsebit was a password. Chanel quilted bags with the CC clasp did the same work in a different accent. Status was something you could read from across a restaurant.

The early 2000s made the shout louder and cheaper to copy. John Galliano's Dior saddle bag, often in logo jacquard, hung on hips in every magazine. Takashi Murakami's 2003 collaboration with Louis Vuitton — the monogram in multicolor on white, then the cherry print — turned a trunk-maker's mark into a cartoon. Von Dutch trucker hats, Ed Hardy rhinestone skulls, and Juicy Couture's "JUIC" velour spelled the wearer's taste in letters an inch high. Counterfeits followed the real bags down Canal Street and into office parks. The logo had become the product.

Then the marks receded for a stretch. Phoebe Philo's Céline (the accent still on the E) sold a quiet, expensive blankness. The Row, founded by Mary-Kate and Ashley Olsen, treated a visible logo as a mistake. People who still bought Vuitton often chose Empreinte leather or a smaller, darker print. The pendulum had not broken. It had gone still.

Alessandro Michele's Gucci, from 2015 and peaking in public around 2017, put the logo back on everything: belts, slides, berets, a Dionysus bag that could not be mistaken for anyone else's. The GG became wallpaper. Balenciaga under Demna played a harsher version of the same game — the logo as joke and as threat. Vetements printed DHL on a T-shirt and asked whether a courier's mark was any less a brand than a house's. Streetwear had already trained a generation to want a Supreme box logo the way an earlier one wanted a LV. Michele did not invent logomania. He gave it a Renaissance costume and a garden print.

The backlash had a name by the early 2020s: quiet luxury, old money, stealth wealth. Succession's costumes, Loro Piana knitwear, The Row again, a beige baseball cap with no text. The joke was that the clothes still cost a fortune; they just refused to spell it. TikTok made a genre of guessing whether a coat was "quiet" or merely dull. Houses that had shouted in 2017 learned to whisper in 2023, at least in the lookbooks. The monogram never left the factory. It waited.

Furniture has the same argument, and here the comparison is not cute — it is about where a maker is allowed to sign. A dining table with the manufacturer's name burned into the apron, facing the room, is doing logomania. So is a chair with a metal badge on the crest rail, or a sofa with a script embroidered on a throw pillow that came in the box. The other tradition hides the signature. Thos. Moser stamps or labels the underside. Gustav Stickley's red mark and joinery sit where a person who turns the piece over can find them. George Nakashima signed boards; the live edge was the advertisement, not a decal. A person who cares can still know. A guest does not have to.

The quiet-luxury living room of the 2020s — oatmeal linen, a nameless lamp, a rug with no border logo — is the interior version of a Loro Piana baseball cap. The 1980s lacquer-and-brass room with a branded ice bucket is the interior version of a monogram keepall. Neither is more honest. Both are taste choosing how loud the receipt should be.

Logos will come back in a print you can see from the street, then go back under the apron. That is the wave. The only durable choice is whether you like the mark itself — the Vuitton flowers, the GG, the Moser stamp — or whether you are wearing someone else's volume knob. The furniture equivalent is simple: if you would be embarrassed to find the name on the underside, you probably do not want it on the pillow either.
