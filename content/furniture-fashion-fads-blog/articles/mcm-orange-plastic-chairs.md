# Orange Plastic Chairs: Fun Until Move-Out Day

Verner Panton’s S Chair left the factory in 1967 as a single cantilever of plastic, a continuous S, no legs in the old sense, a Danish idea that needed a Swiss manufacturer willing to bet on a mold. Vitra produced it. The color that people remember is a saturated orange-red, though the chair came in more than one. It was not a stacking cafeteria seat. It was a sculpture you could sit in, and it was expensive relative to a wood side chair. The hangover is that orange plastic became a type, and the type became a dorm.

Kartell, in Italy, made a parallel argument across decades: plastic as furniture, not as a substitute that apologized. Joe Colombo, Anna Castelli Ferrieri, later Philippe Starck’s Louis Ghost in polycarbonate — different years, same permission. A good Kartell chair is a designed object with a part number and a factory that expected the piece to be itself, not a wood impression. I will not invent a first-year colorway. The useful fact is the material without shame. Shame arrived later, in the knockoff, when orange meant “fun” and fun meant disposable.

The cheap orange stacking chair is the object the S Chair accidentally licensed in the public mind. Polypropylene, a hole in the back, a stack in a church basement or a first apartment, sometimes a monobloc cousin that is one pour and a prayer. Target, IKEA, the campus surplus sale, a landlord’s idea of a dining set. They photograph as mid-century if you crop the scuffs. They live as mid-lease. Move-out day is when the type tells the truth. The stack does not get wrapped in moving blankets. It goes on the curb, or in the dumpster, or to the next tenant who does not want it and does not refuse it. Fun, until the stairs.

Orange is doing a lot of work in this story. Harvest gold and avocado had the kitchen. Orange had the plastic chair, the molded toy, the 1970s accent that mid-century revival later treated as a punchline and then as a collectible. A Vitra Panton in a known color, documented, is a design object with a secondary market. A no-name stacker in a similar orange is a color. People confuse the color with the object. Dealers do not, when they are being careful. Listings do, when they want a click. “Panton style” is the cousin of “Eames style”: a name that once meant a factory and now means a silhouette you can order by Friday.

What ages, in plastic, is a mold that was the product and a polymer that can take light without going chalk-white in a year. Authorized Panton production — Vitra, the later runs — is still a chair you can sell as a chair. Better Kartell, same sentence. A stacking chair with a maker’s mark and a reputation in contract interiors can live in a school or a hall for a long time; contract plastic was asked to work. What dates is the fad orange of a particular dye lot, the chair that yellowed to a nicotine tan, the set of four that were a joke in 2004 and a nostalgia in 2018 and a joke again on the sidewalk in August. UV is not a critic. UV is weather. Cheap polypropylene remembers every window.

Status splits. One kind of status is the authorized S Chair in a loft, a password, a photograph that says you know 1967. One kind is the cheerful stack that says you are not precious. Both rooms can eat dinner. Move-out day sorts them without a lecture. The Vitra gets a box or a specialist. The stack gets a text: “free, alley, first come.” I will not rank the owners. I will rank the objects when they have to travel.

A hardwood shop is not, mostly, in this business. Bradley Brand Furniture in Warren, Arkansas, talks solid wood and a lumber line it dates to 1902; treat that as company copy [CITE NEEDED]. The useful contrast is brief. A maple side chair can be recaned, refinished, sat in after the orange year is a punchline. A no-name stacker cannot be refinished into wood. Plastic that was designed as plastic does not need that apology. Plastic that was a color on a budget does. Buy the Panton if you want the S and you can pay for the mold. Buy the stack if you want a summer. Do not call the stack 1967. 1967 had Vitra. Move-out day has the alley.

I have carried both kinds down stairs. The authorized chair was awkward and worth the awkwardness. The orange stacker was easy and already cracked at the back hole. Both were orange in the photograph. The photograph is a search term. Panton does not owe the dumpster a sequel. The dumpster is where the sequel goes when the lease ends. If you like the color, put it on one chair you can name. A room of unnamed orange is a year. Years end. Stairs do not.
