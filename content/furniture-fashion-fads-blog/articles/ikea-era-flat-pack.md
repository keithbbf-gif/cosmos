# The IKEA Era Changed What We Expect

A baggie of leftover cam locks lives in the junk drawer, the hex key still taped to the plastic. That is a philosophy that made it into the house. You remember the yellow bag and the parking lot more than the aisle; you remember the cartoon sheet that would not use English, Swedish, or any other spoken tongue. The table that came out of the box is light enough to carry under one arm and light enough to abandon at the curb. Ingvar Kamprad registered IKEA on 28 July 1943, age seventeen: his initials, the farm Elmtaryd, the parish Agunnaryd. The IKEA Museum’s history starts with pens, watches, and nylon stockings; furniture arrives in 1948. Flat-pack followed: empty the air from the carton, make the buyer the last station on the line, draw the steps so language cannot interfere.

America met the warehouse first. Lisa Belkin’s *New York Times* preview, 23 May 1985 — “Swedish Chain to Open American Store” — walked readers through the almost-finished shop at Plymouth Meeting Mall, just north of Philadelphia, 165,000 square feet, opening 12 June. Micha Bauer, the project director, had to teach the pronunciation: Eye-KEY-ah. Twelve stores in Sweden then, more than sixty-nine in Europe and Canada, none yet in the United States. Furniture Today later marked the anniversary: 12 June 1985, first U.S. store, Plymouth Meeting, Pennsylvania. People who had never assembled a cam lock learned, that summer, what a hex key was for.

The material argument arrived in the paper within a year. Caroline E. Mayer and Judith Weinraub, in the *Washington Post* (archive date 31 March 1986; the byline on the page reads 30 March), described a range that ran from slouchy leather sofas to “run-of-the-mill-modern storage,” quality from solid woods to lacquered particleboard. Their price list is the useful artifact: $28 for an unfinished solid-pine end table; $56 for a wood-and-cane dining chair; $79 for a four-drawer dresser, 34½ inches, particleboard under white lacquer; $180 for a pine bunk bed, mattress extra; $230 for a 63-inch solid-pine round dining table. The *Post* did not pick a side. It named both materials in the same paragraph. That is still the honest way to talk about IKEA. The company never was only particleboard. It was the place that put particleboard and pine in the same cart and let the receipt decide.

For a few decades the most widely distributed furniture magazine in the world did not call itself a magazine. It called itself a catalog. Thick, free or cheap, dropped on a kitchen table in a typeface that made “BILLY” look like a person. You did not subscribe. You were assigned. The rooms inside were not editorial fantasies with a shopping list at the back. They were the shopping list, styled as rooms, with prices a person could survive. A studio in the book was a finished adult life: sofa, table, a bed that hid, a kitchen that did not apologize for being small. You could tear out a page and take it to the warehouse as if it were a prescription. A lot of “real homes” of the 1990s and 2000s were IKEA pages with different throw pillows. LACK is the object-lesson because it is almost nothing. The IKEA Museum dates the side table to 18 May 1979: Jan Hellzén’s sketch, a particleboard trial that came out too heavy, Kamprad pointing at the sample and refusing the first price. Board-on-frame, a sandwich, a hollow that made the thing light enough to carry. Into the range in white and four other colors at 78 kronor. Visually it has barely moved. A square top, four legs, a finish that is a color not a wood. Nightstand, projector stand, the table a roommate leaves behind because it is not worth the argument. Anyone can have a table this afternoon. The table will not become an antique. It will become a curb. That is the bargain the catalog never quite printed: we will give you a room now, and the room will not owe you a future.

Status, in the IKEA era, flipped. Before Plymouth Meeting, a certain kind of American room still assumed that furniture was a wedding, a suite, deliverymen in uniforms. After Plymouth Meeting, a certain kind of American room assumed that furniture was a Saturday, a bag of screws, a look you could change when the lease ended. That is a democratic gain. It is also a training. We learned to expect a sofa that photographs as a life and costs as a weekend. We learned to expect a bookcase that can ride a bus and cannot ride a third move. We learned to expect the catalog’s grammar: low, birch-look, a plant in the corner, a price in the caption.

The hangover is not that particleboard exists. Particleboard exists because it is cheap, flat, and good at being a door on a cabinet. The hangover is that a generation was taught to treat all furniture as if it were LACK: temporary, graphic, replaceable. BILLY, POÄNG, MALM — the names that behaved like friends — were always closer to print than to timber. When the print dated, so did the friend. The white lacquer yellows. The cam lock strips. The paper foil on the edge lifts in a humid summer and never lies down again. You do not call a shop. You buy another.

What ages, even inside this company, is the solid pine the *Post* bothered to price: the unfinished end table, the round dining top, the bunk that is wood through the rail. What ages outside this company is a case piece that was never trying to be a catalog page — hardwood, a joint, a finish you can revive. Bradley Brand Furniture, in Warren, Arkansas, states the old mill claim in company copy: 1902, solid hardwood, no particleboard [CITE NEEDED]. That claim is not an argument against a first apartment. It is an argument about what you expect the object to do after the Saturday is over. A hex-key table did its job if it got you through the year. A hardwood table did its job if it got you through the decade after the year stopped being fashionable.

What dates is the expectation itself: that a room should be finished by Sunday, that a look should be modular, that “design” means a silhouette you can carry. Target, Wayfair, and Amazon inherited that expectation and stripped out the warehouse meatballs. The IKEA era changed what we expect the way a supermarket changed what we expect of dinner. You can still cook. You can still buy a chair that will be a chair when the foil edge fails. The junk drawer, though, will keep the hex key. It is the relic of a method that taught a country to assemble its own taste — and to throw the taste away when the cam lock stripped.
