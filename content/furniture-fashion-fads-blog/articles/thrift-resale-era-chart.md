# Thrift and Resale: The Anti-Fad Infrastructure

Buying used used to be the refusal. You did not take the new oak suite because the old one still stood, or because the new one was ugly, or because money was spoken for. The Goodwill back room and the church rummage sale were not a style. They were a habit that did not need a name.

Then used got a ticker.

1stDibs started in 2000, Michael Bruno listing Paris flea-market furniture after a walk through the Marché aux Puces. Dealers first, then a buy button in 2013, then a public company: Nasdaq: DIBS. That ticker is the joke and the mechanism. A Louis XVI commode and a signed Eames lounge stopped being "someone's old stuff" and became a quote. Mid-century modern turned into the index everyone could read without a decorative-arts degree. Search "Eames" the way you search a stock. Watch the price of a rosewood lounge the way a trader watches a chart. MCM became a ticker even on sites that were not public. The letters meant a look, a decade, and a bid.

thredUP opened in 2009 as a clothing consignment machine — send the bag, take the credit, let a warehouse photograph the sleeve. Depop followed in 2011 from H-Farm in Italy, Simon Beckerman's idea that a fashion magazine's pages should be shoppable; it grew into a social flea market, later sold to Etsy in 2021, later still under eBay. Chairish arrived in March 2013 from Anna Brockway and partners who had already built travel software: a consignment floor for other people's sofas, lamps, and art, no museum velvet rope, a seller cut the company itself put near 55 percent. Depop is the closet that wants an audience. thredUP is the industrial sorter. Together they made "I buy used" a storefront you could screenshot.

The anti-fad became a fad the usual way: it got good at talking about itself. Vintage stopped meaning your aunt's basement and started meaning a filter. A 1970s rosewood dresser that would have sat for months on a suburban curb in 1998 could, by the 2010s, be a "statement" with shipping and a return policy. Fast fashion taught the same shopper that new clothes were disposable. Resale taught her that last year's dress could be this year's personality. Furniture lagged, then copied. A cloud-shaped sectional that will not survive a move still gets listed as "barely used." A real teak sideboard from 1962 gets listed as an investment. Both listings use the same buttons.

MCM is the furniture version of the blue-chip ticker. The original objects — Nelson, Eames, Juhl, Wegner, the American case-goods copies that filled offices — had a second life in the 1990s when *Friends* and Design Within Reach and a thousand Brooklyn stoop sales agreed that tapered legs were the grown-up look. 1stDibs professionalized that hunger. Chairish widened it. By the time Instagram could tag a seller, "mid-century" described a new sofa from a container as often as it described a 1958 chair. The ticker detached from the object. You could buy the index without buying the history.

Depop and thredUP never pretended to be furniture houses. They still changed furniture because they changed the moral language. If the closet could be circular, the living room felt guilty for being linear. People began to perform the Goodwill run the way they had performed the Target drop: as content. A haul video is not a refusal of fad. It is fad with a different supply chain. The anti-fashion stance — I don't buy new — became a style you could date, like normcore, and then a style you could sell.

There is a real refusal underneath the performance. A chair that already exists does not need a new tree, a new foam pour, a new cardboard coffin. The Salvation Army sofa that lasts another decade is a better climate object than a "sustainable" sectional that lands in a dumpster after one lease. Resale infrastructure, when it actually moves objects into new houses, is the anti-fad machine. Resale-as-content is just another season.

Watch how the platforms sort. 1stDibs still wants provenance: dealer, decade, a photograph that looks like a museum loan. Chairish will take the unbranded walnut table if the photograph is clean. Depop will take the weird lamp if the seller has a face. thredUP will not take your credenza at all. That split is the chart. Clothing got industrial resale. Furniture got a luxury listing site, a mid-market consignment site, and a hope that Facebook Marketplace would do the rest. The hope is uneven. A marble table is heavy. Shipping eats the virtue.

A floor that sells pieces meant to be the next person's used furniture — bbfur is in that business even when the piece is new — is closer to the old refusal than a haul video is. Buy something that can appear on Chairish in twenty years without shame. That is the test. If the only afterlife is a curb, you did not beat the fad. You delayed the trash day.

The punchline of the resale era is not that everyone suddenly became frugal. It is that frugality got a brand, a ticker, and a feed. Used is now a department. MCM is now a ticker symbol you can feel in your wrist when an Eames listing refreshes. The anti-fad built warehouses, apps, and an IPO. It still works, when you use it as a warehouse. It fails when you use it as a personality. The old rummage sale did not need your identity. It needed your ten dollars and a way to get the dresser down the steps.
