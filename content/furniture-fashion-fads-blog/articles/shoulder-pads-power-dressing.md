# Shoulder Pads and the Power Silhouette

A shoulder pad is a wedge of foam, felt, or horsehair. Sewn into a jacket, it changes how a person occupies a doorway. That is the whole trick, and for most of the 1980s it did more social work than a business card. The silhouette said the wearer had a meeting, a salary, and a right to the width of the sidewalk.

The line did not start with Dynasty. Adrian built Joan Crawford's 1930s shoulders at MGM; Elsa Schiaparelli treated the same plane as sculpture. Christian Dior's 1947 New Look went the other way — a soft shoulder and a nipped waist — and the hangover from that hourglass lasted into the early 1960s. What the 1980s did was put the width back, then sell it to women walking into banks, law firms, and television studios that had only recently stopped treating them as guests.

Giorgio Armani's jackets for women in the late 1970s and early 1980s were the grown-up version. The construction was loose in the chest, the canvas light, the shoulder still broad. He was borrowing the male banker's outline without borrowing the starch. Claude Montana went harder: the shoulder as a cornice, wool so architectural it could have been drawn with a T-square. Thierry Mugler treated the same idea as science fiction — hips and shoulders as planets. On American television, Nolan Miller's costumes for Dynasty translated the runway into something a department store could ship by Friday. Joan Collins as Alexis Carrington, jeweled and padded, taught a country what a power suit looked like at dinner.

The working uniform that followed was a three-part argument. A jacket in wool crepe or tropical-weight wool, sometimes a polyester blend that held the pad's shape through a commute. A silk blouse with a pussy bow, a hard collar, or the sort of gold chain that sat on the sternum like punctuation. A skirt just below the knee, or a trouser with a crease. Donna Karan's "seven easy pieces," shown in 1985, tried to make the system portable: bodysuit, skirt, jacket, the pieces talking to each other. Anne Klein, Calvin Klein, and Ellen Tracy filled the racks at Bloomingdale's and Neiman Marcus. A woman could buy the silhouette without buying Montana.

Rooms did related work. The 1980s living room that still photographs as "the decade" is lacquer, brass, and smoked glass: a dining table with a piano finish, an étagère that reflects the lamp twice, a brass gallery rail on a sideboard. Hollywood Regency — Billy Baldwin's earlier language of lacquer, Greek-key tape, and a chair that looks expensive in a still — came back through shelter magazines that shot apartments as if they were boardrooms with sofas. Memphis, Ettore Sottsass's short, loud experiment out of Milan, put the squiggle and the laminate on a coffee table and dared the room to stay polite. An oversized sectional with a rolled arm and a high sheen was not trying to be a farmhouse. It was furniture in a suit.

The kinship is not a metaphor you have to force. A padded shoulder and a brass-trimmed cabinet both add a hard edge to the outline. Both catch light. Both announce that the occupant has arrived and would like the arrival noted. You can dislike the taste and still see the grammar.

The 1990s took the pads out. Calvin Klein's slip dresses, Helmut Lang's slim tailoring, and the Jil Sander jacket with almost no padding reset the line. A generation that had watched jackets steamed on a hanger decided the extra width looked like costume. Closets filled with bias-cut silk charmeuse and one narrow blazer. The office did not get less political; it got quieter about the armor.

The 2010s revival was smaller and more knowing. Balmain under Olivier Rousteing put the shoulder back on evening suiting around 2011 to 2014 — a glittering, Instagram-ready pad. Later in the decade, Khaite, The Row, and Toteme offered a quieter version: a structured knit or a wool blazer with a modest insert, enough to read as a meeting without reading as Alexis. Demna's Balenciaga played with volume again, but the coat was architecture more than office cosplay. The pad returned as a citation. You could wear it on a Tuesday if you wanted; you did not have to.

What rooms kept from the 1980s is the brass and the gloss, now often mixed with a mid-century chair so the lacquer does not have to carry the whole apartment. A black dining table still works. A brass lamp still works. The full Memphis living room is harder to live with than a single padded jacket, which is why the fashion revival stayed tiny and the furniture revival stayed selective. The power silhouette never needed a funeral. It needed a place to sit when the meeting ended, and a closet that could put the foam away until someone asked for width again.
