The child is lying on the living-room carpet with the Sears Wish Book open to a room that does not exist in the house. A sofa, a lamp, a tree in a tub, a father-shaped blur in a cardigan. The room is a set. The set is for sale in pieces. You could, if the adult cooperated, reconstruct a corner of it. You will remember the corner longer than you remember the chair. That is the catalog’s job: to get the want in before the sit.

*Photo cue: FFF-14a — Sears Wish Book interior page, mid-century or 1970s room setting; public-domain or licensed catalog spread, full room in frame.*

Sears did not invent the furnished page, but the Wish Book made the furnished page a household object, seasonal, thick enough to sit on. The rooms were typed: colonial, Mediterranean, “modern,” a den with knotty pine. Each type was a kit. The kit taught a family what a room was supposed to contain. Ethan Allen took the next step and put the kit in a gallery — a store that was already a house, so that you walked through the want instead of turning a page. The gallery salesperson could say, as a fact, that this was how the collection lived. You had not sat long. You had walked through a sentence.

Design Within Reach opened in 1999, Rob Forbes’s company, and made a different sentence. It was a catalog and then a shop for furniture that had been, for most Americans, a specifiers’ secret: Herman Miller and the European moderns, sold as if they were available to a person with a credit card and a loft. DWR was, in a real way, a Herman Miller retail catalog channel before it was a mall presence. Herman Miller acquired DWR in 2015. The acquisition is a fact about who owns the picture now. The earlier fact is that Forbes understood the picture as the product’s first life. An Eames lounge in a white room, correctly photographed, is a want that precedes the leather. The leather may disappoint. The photograph rarely does.

IKEA’s catalog — a paper brick that the company treated as a flag — taught a cheaper, more complete version of the same lesson. The rooms were small. The prices were on the page. You could have the whole sentence. The hangover is an apartment of particleboard that looked, in the photograph, like a finished thought. IKEA was honest about the thought being a system. The buyer who wanted the photograph without the allen key was not IKEA’s problem.

*Photo cue: FFF-14b — IKEA catalog room, small footprint, price tags visible; dated edition, paper stock obvious.*

Wayfair began in 2002 as CSN Stores, a bundle of niche sites, and became a single name later. The picture multiplied. Instead of one Wish Book room or one IKEA kitchen, a search returned a hundred sofas in a hundred identical white rooms, the same houseplant cloned across SKUs. The body still had not met the chair. The chair might arrive in three boxes. The want had already been satisfied and then immediately replaced by the next thumbnail. Instagram and TikTok finished the move from page to feed. A room is now a clip. The clip has a sound. The furniture is a prop that can be tagged. The tag is a link. The link is a cart.

High Point Market, twice a year in High Point, North Carolina, is where a lot of those pictures get their raw material. It is a wholesale taste factory. Buyers walk buildings. Reps stage rooms that will never be lived in. A finish that will be on a thousand retail floors is decided in a showroom that smells like carpet glue and coffee. The shelter magazines used to translate High Point for the public with a lag of months. The feed translated it in a week, then in a day, then in the same afternoon. I will not invent circulation figures for the Wish Book, or unique-visitor counts for Wayfair, or High Point attendance. Those numbers exist in trade reports and they change with the method. The structure does not need them. The picture still goes first.

Status rides the medium. A Wish Book room said: this is what a family owns. An Ethan Allen gallery said: this is what a family aspires to, with a payment plan. DWR said: this is what a person of a certain education sits in. IKEA said: this is what a person of a certain decade can finish in a weekend. Wayfair said: this is what a search returns. The feed says: this is what a stranger’s apartment looks like at 4 p.m. in good light. Each status claim is a picture claim. The chair is asked to live up to a frame.

*Photo cue: FFF-14c — High Point Market showroom corridor, staged room through an open door, buyer badge if visible; trade photograph, not a brand ad.*

The hangover is a house full of pieces that were bought to complete a picture and then outlived the picture. The Mediterranean suite after the Wish Book went colonial. The gallery collection after Ethan Allen’s next gallery looked different. Walk an older Ethan Allen store even now and you can see the lag: a room that was last year’s page, still fully dressed, waiting for a couple who will sit for thirty seconds and then photograph the fabric. The DWR chair after the loft became a two-bedroom and the dog chose the leather. The IKEA wall after the move, when the cam locks stripped. The Wayfair sectional after the thumbnail’s fabric turned out to be a pile that mats. The feed’s hangover is faster and more personal: a room you copied from a stranger, which dated when the stranger posted the next room. You are left with the objects. The objects were never the point. The point was the frame.

A Saturday in a regional gallery — not Ethan Allen, a cousin with the same script — still runs this way: the salesperson builds a whole room on a clipboard from a catalog spread taped to the podium. The couple nod at the spread, not at the sofa. They have already lived in the picture. The sofa is a delivery problem. That is the hangover in advance. The room arrives. It is smaller. The lamp from the page is back-ordered. The want has nowhere to sit except on what actually came.

What ages, in this system, is a piece that can leave the picture and still be a piece. A walnut table photographed in 1962 in a Wish Book and again in 2014 on a phone is the same table if the top is walnut. A sofa photographed in five catalogs is five sofas. Upholstery is a picture medium. Wood is less so, unless the wood is a print. The feed prefers the print because the print is consistent under a ring light. Consistency is not the same as quality. It is the same as a thumbnail that matches.

A craft shop is almost invisible in the feed unless someone films the shop. Bradley Brand Furniture, Warren, Arkansas, will not be a For You Page unless a customer makes it one. Its copy says solid hardwood, no particleboard or MDF, and dates Bradley Lumber Co. to 1902 as company history pending a primary record [CITE NEEDED]. The contrast is not that a Warren table is above pictures. Everything is pictured now. The contrast is that a table built to be used can survive the death of the picture it was first sold with. A table built to match a picture dies with the picture.

I have watched people sit in a showroom chair for four seconds and then photograph the chair instead. The sit was a formality. The photograph was the decision, to be compared later with other photographs. The body will meet the chair at delivery, when the height is wrong or the seat is deep or the arm hits a hip. By then the want has already been spent. Returns exist. They do not restore the hour you spent inside the picture.

Forbes, if he was honest in 1999, knew that DWR was selling access to a photograph that used to belong to architects. Sears, in any of the thick years, knew it was selling a Christmas morning that included a room. IKEA knew it was selling a plan. The feed does not know it is selling. It is a behavior. The cart is attached afterward, like a hinge on a door that is already swinging.

If you want a chair, sit in one that is not in a picture. If you cannot, sit longer than the clip. The body is still the last to know. That order — picture first, sit later — is the fad. Reverse it and you get a house. Keep it and you get another page.
