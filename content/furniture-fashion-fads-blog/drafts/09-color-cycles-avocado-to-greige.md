The refrigerator is avocado and the range is harvest gold, and they were sold as a pair. The laminate on the counters tries to split the difference and fails. Someone has left a teak cutting board on that laminate, and the teak is the only object in the room that does not look like a year. You can date the kitchen to a band of years in the 1970s without opening a cabinet. The wood does not help you. The appliances do.

Paint is the cheapest way a house changes its mind. A gallon of alkyd in 1973 cost less than a dinette and covered more surface. That is why color cycles run through kitchens first and furniture second. The wall can be wrong for a weekend. A sofa dyed to match the wall is wrong for the life of the fabric. The hangover is always the same: the paint gets rolled over, and the matching furniture sits in the garage because it still works and no longer belongs.

Harvest gold and avocado were not accidents of taste. They were appliance colors with names on chips, pushed through General Electric, Frigidaire, and Sears into rooms that had just finished with turquoise and pink. Gold read as sun and wheat; avocado read as the outdoors brought inside after a decade of white-and-chrome kitchens. Both colors photographed as warmth in the catalogs. In person, under a fluorescent shop light, they went muddy. That muddiness is what people remember now, and it is unfair to the better rooms — a walnut table under a gold range still looks like a table. The gold range looks like 1974.

By the early 1980s the gold had to go. Dusty rose and mauve arrived as a correction that was also a fashion. They sat on brass, on pickled oak, on the gray-mauve carpet that department stores rolled out by the acre. A mauve sofa in a 1986 Ethan Allen gallery was not a joke. It was a finished thought: softness after harvest, femininity after earth-tone masculinity, a room that wanted to look expensive without looking brown. The same sofa in 1994 looked like a bridesmaid. Color does that. It does not wait for the springs to fail.

*Photo cue: FFF-09b — mid-1980s dusty-rose living room, brass lamp, pickled-oak wall unit; department-store or shelter-magazine spread.*

Hunter green and burgundy took the 1990s by the collar. Ralph Lauren Home had already taught a generation that a room could dress like a study. Pottery Barn and the mall versions followed with forest green slipcovers, oxblood leather club chairs, and gold-on-green wallpaper borders that no one has forgiven. The signal was club, lodge, money that had read a book. A cherry entertainment center stained almost black sat under a hunter-green wall and called the whole thing traditional. Traditional, in that decade, meant a color story you could buy in one Saturday. The wood, if it was actually cherry, still had a chance. The painted pine “country” suite in the same green did not.

Then the 2000s wanted Tuscany. Not Tuscany the place — Tuscany the paint aisle. Faux-finished gold plaster, grape-and-vine iron, a dining set with a distressed ochre base and a faux-travertine top. Home Depot and Lowe’s sold the glaze kits; furniture floors sold the matching chairs. The status claim was travel and appetite. You had been somewhere warm, or you wished to look as if you had. A solid oak table with a clear finish could live through that wish. A dining set whose entire identity was the ochre glaze could not. When the glaze dated, the set dated. There was nothing underneath the costume except MDF and a hope.

Millennial pink is the first color in this list that arrived as a joke people meant. Around 2016 the phrase stuck to a dusty, slightly dirty rose that was not quite 1980s mauve and not quite ballet. Pantone’s Rose Quartz, paired with Serenity for 2016, gave the trade a chip to point at [CITE NEEDED for the exact pairing language on the Pantone release]. Instagram did the rest. Blush velvet chairs, rose-gold hardware, a sofa in a pink that photographers liked at 10 a.m. The furniture that took the year hardest was the cheap upholstery: a blush modular that existed to be photographed next to a monstera. Two lease cycles later it reads as a filter. A walnut credenza photographed in the same apartment does not.

Greige is the apology. Agreeable Gray, Sherwin-Williams SW 7029, became a default in the 2010s the way builder-beige had been a default in the 1990s. I will not rank it. Paint companies publish “most popular” lists that are marketing documents as much as sales ledgers, and a rank without the method is a story [CITE NEEDED before any claim that Agreeable Gray was the bestselling interior color in any year]. What can be said without a press release is that greige walls — gray with enough yellow or brown to keep a room from going cold — showed up in staging, in open houses, in the rental units that turned over every twelve months. The point of greige is that it refuses to be a year. That is also how it becomes one. A whole street of Agreeable Gray interiors is as dated as a street of hunter green, only quieter about it.

The 2020s reached for terracotta and sage the way the 1970s reached for gold and avocado: earth again, after a decade of gray. Sage cabinets, terracotta planters, a rust velvet chair that wants to be a desert. Sherwin-Williams, Benjamin Moore, and every private-label big-box chip rack offered a version. Furniture followed in bouclé the color of oatmeal and in “organic modern” oak that had been brushed and then stained just enough to look like a mood board. Some of that oak is oak. Some of it is a printed grain on a substrate that will not take a second finish. The color cycle does not care which. It will move on either way.

Here is the mechanic, and it is not mysterious. Paint is fashion you can reverse with a weekend and a drop cloth. Upholstery is fashion you reverse with a check. Case goods finished in the color of the year are fashion you reverse only if the substrate will accept a new coat — solid wood will; a vinyl wrap will not, not honestly. The objects that date fastest are the ones whose whole argument was the chip. A harvest-gold dinette whose plastic seats were molded in that gold has no second life except irony. A maple table that sat under harvest-gold walls can be wiped, oiled, and left alone.

Status works the same way as the chip, only slower to admit it. The 1970s gold kitchen said modern plenty: new appliances, a house that had been updated since the wedding. Mauve said softness and a kind of mall prosperity. Hunter green said you had taste that came from a catalog with a crest. Tuscan gold said you cooked. Millennial pink said you were online in the right year. Greige said you were not trying, which is the most expensive thing to try. Terracotta and sage say you have recovered from gray and would like to be well. None of these claims is wicked. They are just claims, and they expire.

The hangover is visible in any Midwestern basement and any Facebook Marketplace radius around a college town. Avocado refrigerators that still run. A mauve sleeper sofa with a broken pull-bar. A hunter-green wing chair whose velvet has polished to a slick at the headrest. A faux-finished Tuscan buffet that will not die because the particleboard is still structurally polite. A blush velvet chair listed as “mid-century” by a seller who means “I bought it in 2017.” Greige sectionals the size of a carport, too heavy to move, too recent to throw away, already the color of last decade’s open house.

What ages is not “neutral,” which is just a cycle that pretends to sit out the dance. What ages is a finish that belongs to the wood and a color that belongs to pigment, not to a campaign. An oil-rubbed walnut table from the 1960s still looks like walnut. A painted pine armoire in 1998 hunter green looks like 1998 unless you strip it, and stripping painted pine is a penance, not a project. A leather club chair in an actual brown — not oxblood, not cognac-as-a-hashtag — will scuff and then look used, which is different from looking finished.

A hardwood shop in a small town does not issue a color of the year. Bradley Brand Furniture, in Warren, Arkansas, sells solid hardwood and says so; the company’s own copy also places Bradley Lumber Co. at 1902, which should be treated as company copy until a primary record sits next to it [CITE NEEDED]. The useful contrast is not moral. It is mechanical. A table that is maple or oak through the top can be refinished when the walls change. A table whose top is a photograph of oak cannot. Paint will keep cycling. That is what paint is for. The mistake is asking the sofa to cycle with it, then being surprised when the sofa becomes the thing you cannot afford to be wrong about.

I have stood in kitchens where the cabinets were still the 1970s oak and the walls had been three colors since. The cabinets were fine. They had been fine under gold, under white, under greige. Someone had wanted to replace them because a television show had replaced cabinets. The oak did not care. The show will have a new color next season. The cabinets will still be oak.

If you are keeping a piece, keep the piece whose color is in the material. If you are indulging a year, indulge it in a gallon, a pillow, a lamp whose shade you can throw away. The harvest-gold range was honest about what it was: an appliance with a fashion skin, meant to be replaced when the compressor died. The harvest-gold sofa pretended to be furniture. That pretense is the whole cycle. It will run again in terracotta, and then in whatever name they give the next gray.
