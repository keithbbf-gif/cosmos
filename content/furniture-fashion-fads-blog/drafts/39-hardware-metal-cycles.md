Open a junk drawer in a house that has been owned twice and you will find the last decade in metal. A polished-brass rosette from 1987. A satin-nickel bar from 2001. An oil-rubbed bronze orb that still has the fake wear in the crevices, applied at the factory. A brushed-gold cup pull that matched a 2014 faucet. A matte-black hex knob, 2018, the screw still in it. Someone kept them because hardware feels like money even when it cost four dollars. Someone will put one of them back on a cabinet “temporarily.” Temporarily is how rooms get dated.

Kitchen and furniture hardware is the fastest metal cycle in the house. Cabinets are expensive to replace. Knobs are not. So the industry sells you a new year without selling you a new box. The wood — or the MDF that is pretending to be wood — stays. The jewelry changes. If you want to know what year a listing photograph was staged, do not look at the sofa first. Look at the pulls.

Polished brass owned the 1980s. Bright, yellow, lacquered so it would not spot, matching the faucet, the towel bar, the entry lamp, the chandelier that looked like a brass bouquet. The lacquer yellowed. The yellowing was blamed on the brass, which is a metal that would have been fine if anyone had been willing to polish it. Instead the 1990s called brass “dated” and ran to chrome and satin nickel. Nickel photographed as clean. Chrome photographed as a gym. Both said: we are done with the 1980s. Bathrooms went silver overnight. Kitchens followed. Furniture followed the kitchens because the same buyers walked both aisles.

Oil-rubbed bronze took the 2000s. A dark, almost black-brown finish with bronze showing on the edges, or supposed to. A lot of it was a baked coating on zinc, with the “wear” printed on. The finish matched the Tuscan wall, the dark espresso stain, the wrought-iron chandelier. It was Mediterranean without the boat fare. Builders put it on entire subdivisions. For a while you could not buy a towel ring in anything else at the national chains.

Brushed gold — champagne, “golden champagne,” the names multiplied — arrived as the polite 2010s correction. Not the yellow brass of 1987. A quieter, pinker, more Instagram metal. It went on faucets first, then on cabinet pulls, then on the light fixtures that had been nickel last season. Design accounts treated it as warmth. It was warmth you could SKU.

Matte black had a hard run from about 2015 to 2022. Hex knobs. Long bars. Black on white shaker. Black on walnut-look. Black on everything that wanted to look “modern farmhouse” or “industrial” without choosing. The finish hid fingerprints less well than the ads claimed, showed grease in a kitchen, and turned every room into the same room. When everyone has the same black bar pull, the pull stops being a choice and becomes a default. Defaults date faster than choices.

Then unlacquered brass came back. Living finish. The spots, the darkening, the argument that you should touch your hardware and let it record the touching. This is the old brass without the 1980s lacquer, sold as honesty. Some of it *is* honesty: solid brass that will take a polish or take a tarnish, your call. Some of it is a coating that imitates tarnish and then chips to a white metal. The word *unlacquered* is doing a lot of work. Ask what the knob is made of before you ask what year it is pretending to be.

Knobs are the cheapest way to re-date a cabinet. They are also the cheapest way to fake a renovation in a listing. Swap the oil-rubbed bronze orbs for black bars, paint the oak white, keep the box. The box is still a 1994 box. The hinges may still be the original overlay. A buyer who looks at the jewelry and not the carcass pays for a year that is only skin. A buyer who opens the door and looks at the hinge plate, the shelf pins, the side material — particle, plywood, hardwood — is buying a cabinet.

Zinc is the quiet material in this story. Most “brass” knobs at the big-box price are zinc alloy, plated. Most “oil-rubbed bronze” orbs are zinc. Most matte-black bars are zinc with a powder coat. Zinc casts well, machines cheaply, and takes a finish like a mannequin takes paint. It is not a sin. It is a category. The category fails when the plating wears at the finger point and the white-gray metal shows through, or when last year’s finish is so specific — that orb, that hex, that champagne — that the wear pattern is a timestamp.

What ages: simple unplated metal, or solid brass that can be polished, or a steel pull with a shape so dull it never had a year. A plain round brass knob from 1910 and a plain round brass knob from 2010 are cousins. You can shine them or let them go dark. They do not announce a cable-show season. What dates: the zinc orb in last year’s finish. The oversized hex. The cup pull that was specified to match a faucet that has already been replaced. The complete matching set of twenty-four pulls in a finish that had a three-year run.

Furniture hardware follows the same clock at a slight delay. Dresser knobs went from brass to pewter to oil-rubbed to black to “antique brass” again. A good case piece can wear three generations of pulls and still be the same dresser. That is one reason hardwood case goods outlast the metal cycle. You unscrew the year and put on a quieter one. I have pulled oil-rubbed bronze off a decent maple cabinet and found the 1990s satin nickel holes already there, filled with wood putty the color of chewing gum. Under the nickel, a single brass hole, off-center. The door was a calendar. Three owners had bought a new year for six dollars a knob and left the maple alone, which was the right instinct in the wrong order. Change the metal if the metal bothers you. Do not mistake the change for a kitchen.

The hangover is in the drawer I started with, and in the donation bin: bags of perfectly good pulls in a finish nobody will admit to wanting for another fifteen years. Brass will come back. It has. Black will come back after people get tired of the living finish they just installed. Nickel is already waiting. The cycle is not a secret. The industry prints it in the fixture catalogs a year before it hits the furniture floor.

Furniture pulls run a half-step behind the kitchen, which is useful if you watch the fixture aisle. When the faucet goes champagne, the dresser follows in a season. When the faucet goes black, the nightstand does too. A bedroom that matched a 2017 kitchen in matte black will look like a 2017 kitchen even after the kitchen has gone back to brass. That is how a house gets out of phase with itself: each room updated to a different metal year, none of them talking. The quietest fix is to pick one dull, refinishable metal and let the loud rooms wait.

If you want a knob that will not scold you in 2030, buy a simple shape in a metal you can refinish, or a metal that was never finished in the fashionable sense. If you want this year’s kitchen in an afternoon, buy the orb. Know which purchase you are making. One is hardware. The other is a subscription to the year, billed in bags you will not throw away.
