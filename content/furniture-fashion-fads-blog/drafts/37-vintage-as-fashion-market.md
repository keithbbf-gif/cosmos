The listing said “Eames lounge, Herman Miller, original,” and the photographs said otherwise. The rosewood was a print. The seat angle was a cousin, not a sibling. The ottoman sat a little too high. In the comments — there are always comments — someone typed the serial-number lecture and someone else typed “Eames style is fine if you’re honest.” The price sat between a real one and a joke. That interval is the market now.

Vintage furniture used to be how you avoided the catalog. Estate sales, the uncle’s basement, a church rummage, Craigslist when Craigslist was still a city wall. You bought a thing because it was there and it was cheaper than new, or because it was better than new and the person selling it did not know. The information was uneven. That unevenness was the last bargain in American furniture.

Then the information evened out. 1stDibs, founded in 2000 by Michael Bruno, turned the Paris flea-market walk into a remote catalog with dealer names and asking prices that assumed a shipping crate. Instagram added the handsome dealer who shoots a teak sideboard on terrazzo and will not tell you the reserve.

Midcentury modern became a ticker. Teak peaked in the public eye around 2014, when every design feed decided a Danish sideboard was the adult purchase and the word *Danish* started appearing on American walnut. Marble and brass had a loud year around 2018: the tulip table, the drinks cart, the lamp that looked like a hotel in 1972. Mediterranean oak — the heavier, browner, carved or simply thick European look — is the maybe-next, the one dealers are already testing in captions. I am dating those years the way a shop dates a finish: by what walked in the door and what the asking prices did, not by a study. If you have a better peak, write it in the margin.

The authentic Eames lounge — Charles and Ray Eames, Herman Miller, 1956 — is a known object with a known fake. Shock mounts, a shell, a contract with a factory. There are serials, replacement parts, and a service culture. “Eames style” is a legally careful phrase that means: we took the silhouette and none of the liability. Both objects sit on the same apps now. The apps are not a museum. They are a mall with better lighting. A new buyer learns the difference the expensive way, or learns it from a forum, or never learns it and lives with a chair that looks right in a photograph from six feet.

Auction houses still do the old work: catalogs with condition reports, specialists who will tell you the marquetry is later, a hammer price that is public. Sotheby’s, Christie’s, Wright, Rago, the regional rooms that still print an estimate and a provenance paragraph — they are not saints. They have sold copies. They have also published the copy as a copy, which is more than a caption usually does. The Instagram dealer does a different work: narrative, scarcity, a story about a Palm Springs estate that may or may not have a bill of sale behind it. Some dealers are straight. Some are inventing provenance the way a catalog invents lifestyle. The feed rewards the story. The auction house, at its best, rewards the object. At its worst it rewards the same story with a lot number.

The ticker has a few documented beats if you watched asking prices instead of mood boards. 2012 to 2015, teak and the broader “Danish” tag: sideboards, sewing tables pressed into consoles, chairs with paper-cord seats that were either original or a weekend re-wrap. 2016 to 2019, marble tops and brass stems, the tulip and the drinks cart, anything that could sit under a globe pendant and look like a hotel bar. 2020 to 2022, the pale wood and the blob chair, which is another essay, and a run on anything labeled “organic modern.” Dealers are already writing “European oak” under American pieces that are only oak. The caption is doing the fashion. The wood is doing what wood does.

Buying used was the anti-fad. You were supposed to be opting out of the circular, the package, the West Elm kit, the white slipcover. You were supposed to be taking the thing that had already survived a fashion and would survive yours. Then the fashion moved onto the used object. A teak dresser is not safer from dating than a new dresser if you bought it because 2014 told you teak was the year. You paid a fashion premium for an old thing. The old thing is still old. The premium is what dates you.

I have watched people flip a chair in eighteen months because the marble-brass year ended and the chair was chrome. The chair did not change. The caption did. That is a fashion market with inventory that happens to have been built in 1968. The build year is not a moral shield. It is a material fact. Material facts are why used furniture is still worth buying. Fashion is why it is suddenly expensive.

Estate sales remain the least fashionable door, which is why they still occasionally work. You walk a house in a morning, you look at the underside, you open a drawer, you smell whether the “teak” is teak. You pay the family or the company running the sale. You do not pay a dealer’s narrative. You also buy a lot of junk, and you need a truck, and you need to know what a dovetail looks like when it was cut by a machine in 1978 versus a bench in 1930. The knowledge is the discount. Platforms flattened the discount by teaching everyone the words. They did not flatten the ability to look at a joint.

Bradley Brand Furniture, in Warren, Arkansas, sells new hardwood and says so: company copy claims a 1902 lumber line and a refusal of particleboard. [CITE NEEDED.] That shop is not a vintage dealer. The useful contrast is still the same sentence. A solid case piece, new or old, has a next life: a refinish, a hardware change, a drawer repair. A fashion object — new printed grain or old plywood that was already tired — has a next listing. If you are buying used as an anti-fad, buy the next life. If you are buying used as a fashion year, admit the year. The admission is cheaper than the story.

What ages: a marked piece from a factory that still exists or is well documented; an unmarked piece whose wood and joints can take another century of ordinary use; a table whose top is thick enough to plane. What dates: the “Eames style” bought at peak caption; the Mediterranean-oak sideboard purchased because a reel said Europe was back; the entire matching vintage *kit* assembled from three apps in a weekend to look like an apartment that had been inherited. Inheritance takes longer than a weekend.

The hangover is already in storage units. Teak that missed the 2014 window and now looks like brown furniture again. Brass-and-marble carts that photograph as 2018. Blond wood that had a year and then lost it. None of that wood asked to be a ticker. People did that. People can stop.

Craigslist and the estate sale still punish haste and reward a truck. That is their remaining virtue. 1stDibs and Chairish punish ignorance and reward a shipping budget. That is their remaining vice, or their business, depending on whether you needed the crate. Both can get you a good object. Both can get you a silhouette with a story stapled to it. The anti-fad that became a fad is not “buying old.” Buying old is ordinary. The fad is buying old *on time*, in the year the caption named, at the price the caption taught. A teak dresser bought in 1998 because it was thirty dollars is not a fashion victim. A teak dresser bought in 2014 because a feed said Denmark is how you grow up — that dresser is a subscription. The wood cannot tell the difference. Your receipt can.

A used chair is still the right purchase when the chair is a chair. It becomes the wrong one when it is a share. The oldest furniture advice in this series is also the least fashionable: open the drawer. Look at the back. Sit in it. If the listing’s paragraph is longer than the time you spent looking, you are buying the paragraph.
