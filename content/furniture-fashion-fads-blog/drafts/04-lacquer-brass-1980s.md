The cocktail table is a rectangle of smoked glass on a brass X, and it still has a ring from a plant that was never emptied. Under the glass: a remote, a TV Guide from a year the magazine still existed, a book of matches from a restaurant that is now a parking lot. The dining room beyond it is black lacquer, or a black that wants to be lacquer — high shine, brass ferrules on the chair legs, a sideboard that reflects the chandelier as a smear. This is not Memphis. This is the American 1980s as a furniture floor sold them: power, gloss, metal that caught a downlight. Memphis was the argument in Milan. This table is what the argument looked like after it went through a catalog.

The Memphis group was constituted on December 11, 1980, in Ettore Sottsass’s apartment in Milan. The first collection showed at the Arc ’74 gallery during the Milan Furniture Fair in September 1981. Sources split on the opening day: the 18th or the 19th. Say both. Fifty-five pieces. Sottsass’s Carlton and Casablanca — the totem bookcases that look like a cartoon of a cabinet and a cabinet of a cartoon — and Martine Bedin’s Super Lamp, a snake of colored bulbs on wheels, were the images that left the room. Plastic laminate, cheap and shiny, used as if it were marble. Color that refused beige. Forms that refused the good taste the Italian factories had been exporting as modern.

Karl Lagerfeld furnished a Monte Carlo apartment in Memphis. Artnet, citing a 1983 German magazine interview, has him calling it “love at first sight” and “the Art Deco of the ’80s.” Quote it that way: through the citation, not as a line you overheard. Sottsass left the group in 1985. Memphis dissolved around 1988. Eight years, or a little less, depending on how you count the afterlife of the photographs. The photographs did better than the sofas. They still do.

American rooms did not, as a rule, buy a Carlton. They bought a parallel 1980s that shared a shine and refused the joke. Black lacquer dining suites. Brass-and-glass coffee tables from the mall and from the better galleries. Lane and Henredon in high gloss: dining and bedroom case goods that wanted to look wet. A Lane table from those years, if the finish is actually lacquer on a real top, still takes a light if you dust it. A mall cousin with a film on a hollow apron does not. Yuppie power rooms — the word is dated, the rooms were real — held a lacquer desk, a brass lamp, a leather chair that faced a city or a golf print, a blotter that never saw ink. The status claim was money that had somewhere to be by eight. You were not the Mediterranean-oak person. You were not waiting for Old World. You were already late.

The other 1980s sat in the next subdivision: Southwestern pastels, bleached pine, a howling coyote, terracotta that had never been clay. Same decade, opposite boast. One room said night and metal. One room said sun and dust. Both rooms were sold by the same kind of floor, sometimes on the same Saturday, to people who would not have recognized Sottsass’s name and did not need to. Fashion in furniture is allowed to fork. A buyer could walk out of a department store with a brass-and-glass table and, two weekends later, a washed-pine dinette for the sunroom, and feel consistent both times. The hangover is that both forks are now in the same thrift store, and only one of them still looks like a decision.

Memphis, for all its laminate, was an argument about taste. The American gloss suite was an argument about evening. The power room wanted to look like an office that had been invited home. High Point showed both the joke and the evening, and buyers wrote numbers on the evening. That is not a failure of Milan. Milan was never going to furnish Columbus. Columbus was going to furnish Columbus, with a shine it could finance.

Retail had a ladder here, as it always does. At the top: the actual Memphis objects, then the Italian and French names that used lacquer as a craft — a chest built to take a polish, edges that were edges. In the middle: Henredon, Lane, the department-store gloss suites, brass that was brass. At the bottom: the mall cocktail table, a hollow brass-colored tube, a glass that chipped at the corner, a dining set whose “lacquer” was a film on particleboard that telegraphed every hip check as a white moon. IKEA’s American life had started in 1985, in Plymouth Meeting, Pennsylvania, and it would eventually teach a different shine — birch, white, a price that did not require a power job. In the mid-1980s the mall table still felt like arrival.

What ages is a well-made lacquer chest. Lacquer, done as a finish and not as a sticker, can be cut back, built up, repaired by a finisher who still owns the word. The box underneath has to be a box: solid wood or a stable core with an honest skin, drawers that run, a top that is a plane. Black will go in and out of fashion the way black suits do. The chest does not have to apologize for 1986 if 1986 was a construction and not a costume. A brass pull that is brass can be cleaned. It can also stay dull. Dull brass on a good box is not a failure. It is a surface that has been touched.

What dates is the brass-and-glass cocktail table from the mall. The X-base, the glass that never sat quite level, the ferrules that loosened, the way every magazine and every remote lived in public because the table had no drawers and no shame. That table was a year even when it was new. It photographed as money. It lived as a dust farm. Southwestern pastel, in the other room, dated on a different clock — sun-struck pine going cheap, a coyote going joke — but the mall brass table dated as soon as the next catalog put a wood slab on hairpin legs and called it honest. Honesty is a fashion word. The table was never claiming honesty. It was claiming night.

The hangover is specific and still in storage units. A black dining set too formal for the way people eat now, which is at the island, standing. A mirrored dresser from a “contemporary” bedroom group, the mirrors foxed, the brass going the color of a forgotten hinge. A Memphis poster, or a Memphis-ish print, in a frame that was also brass. The real Carlton, when it appears at auction, is an artifact with a price that has nothing to do with a den in Columbus. The replica Carlton, sold later as a silhouette, is a password, the way the Eames knockoff is a password. Same problem, louder colors.

I will not sneer at the buyer who wanted the shine. A first good job, a first dining room that was not parents’ oak, a wish to look like the city even if the city was a forty-minute drive — those are ordinary wishes. Lane and Henredon were not swindles. They were factories answering a mood. The swindle, when there was one, was the photograph of lacquer on a board that could not take a second life. Gloss on paper is a year. Gloss on a chest you can refinish is a finish.

Bradley Brand Furniture, in Warren, Arkansas, does not do Memphis and should not be asked to. The company’s own copy still says hardwood, no particleboard, and dates Bradley Lumber Co. to 1902; that date is company copy until a primary record sits beside it [CITE NEEDED]. The useful contrast, in a 1980s essay, is not a moral about color. It is a mechanical one. A lacquered hardwood case can be ugly for a decade and handsome when black comes back, because the case is still a case. A brass-colored tube around a void cannot come back as anything but scrap. The shop that refuses the void is not above fashion. It is beside it, staining oak darker when the floor wants dark, and leaving you a board when the floor wants something else.

Memphis ended. The mall table did not get a dissolution date. It got a basement. If you are keeping one object from the glossy binge, keep the chest that can take a new polish, or the brass that is brass on a wood that is wood. Let the cocktail table go to the person who wants a prop. Props are allowed. They are not furniture. Furniture is what is left when the downlight is off and the plant ring is still there, and you can still set a drink on something that does not wobble.
