A bed is two fashions sharing a floor. One is the mattress: pillow-top, pillow-top-on-pillow-top, memory foam, hybrid, the boxed thing that expands in the guest room like a science trick. The other is the frame — canopy, sleigh, iron, platform, a winged headboard the size of a small wall. The mattress leaves every eight to twelve years if the household is lucky, sooner if they believed the foam advertisement. The frame, if it is a frame, stays. Most of the drama in American bedrooms is people throwing away the lasting half because the temporary half changed shape.

Canopies are the oldest of the loud forms. Four posts and a textile sky: warmth, insects, privacy, a room inside the room. Colonial and Federal American beds did this in maple and mahogany; Victorians piled on the walnut and the drapery. Then the twentieth century opened the windows and the canopy looked like a fever. It came back anyway, thinner, in the 1980s and 1990s as a “romantic” four-poster from the same factories that made the cherry dining sets, and it has come back again in linen — airy, pale, a hotel in Tuscany as imagined by a catalog. The linen canopy of the 2020s is not trying to keep out mosquitoes. It is trying to photograph as softness. Same posts. Different excuse.

The sleigh is an American Empire memory that retail never quite buried. Early-nineteenth-century cabinetmakers — the Duncan Phyfe orbit and its many followers — rolled the headboard and footboard like a runner. Mahogany, veneer, a scroll that belonged next to a saber-leg chair. A century and a half later, the 1990s wanted that roll in reddish cherry, high-gloss, a footboard you barked your shin on in the dark. Thomasville, Broyhill, and a dozen North Carolina names sold the sleigh as the grown-up bed, the one that matched the armoire and the two nightstands. It was heavy. It was formal. It made a modest room feel like a furniture store. I have helped move more cherry sleighs than I have seen anyone look happy to inherit. The form is not the problem. The finish cycle is. That particular cherry — orange, glassy, proud of itself — is a year, not a wood.

Box springs were the silent partner of those tall frames. A mattress sat on a boxed coil unit, eight or nine inches of extra height, so the sleeper got in and out at chair height. When platforms arrived, the box spring became the first thing to leave, and the mattress dropped a foot. People kept the old high nightstand and wondered why they were reaching down for the water glass. Fashion in beds is often a height change that nobody names. Pillow-tops added height back; foam thinned it; hybrids split the difference. A frame that can only look right at one stack will look wrong every time the mattress industry changes its mind.

Iron beds have a cleaner conscience. Nineteenth-century brass and iron were sold as sanitary: no bedbugs in the posts, a frame you could wipe. The 1990s and 2000s brought the iron bed back as cottage, as porch, as the white-painted frame from a catalog that also sold quilts. Some of those frames are honest steel with a powder coat. Some are thin tubes that rack if you sit to put on socks. A good iron bed is a drawing in space. It does not argue with the mattress. It also does not hide a bad one.

Then the room dropped. Platform beds of the 2000s went low, “Japanese-ish,” a mattress on a shallow deck, no box spring, sometimes no visible frame at all. The futon shops and the Design Within Reach knockoffs agreed for once: closer to the floor meant closer to a kind of seriousness. Hotel modernism liked the look. So did apartments with eight-foot ceilings, because a high sleigh in those rooms is a piece of furniture wearing a hat. The platform’s fad finish was the upholstered rail in microfiber, or the Zen-teak stain on rubberwood. The platform’s useful idea was simpler: a mattress does not need a box spring if the deck is solid and ventilated. Hardwood slats, a low rail, a headboard you could change. That idea outlasts the stain.

The 2010s and 2020s belonged to the winged upholstered headboard, sometimes the whole bed upholstered, a throne for sleeping. Restoration Hardware, Pottery Barn, and every regional mattress store sold a camelback or a nailhead wing in linen, velvet, performance fabric. King-wide, sixty inches tall, a piece of wall you could stain with hair oil. Hotels did this first; houses followed, because houses now wanted to look like hotels. The wing said: this is a destination, not just a place you lose consciousness. It also said the bed was a sofa that did not have to face anyone. Comfortable. Hard to move. Impossible to refinish. When the fabric dates — and that oatmeal linen will date — the whole bed is a reupholstery bill or a bulk-trash object.

Mattress fashion ran on a parallel track, louder, with more television. Pillow-tops of the 1990s and 2000s sold the idea that you should sink, then sink again. Memory foam, commercialized from NASA-adjacent material by Tempur-Pedic and then by everyone, sold the idea that you should not sink so much as be hugged by a chemical. Hybrids tried to be both. Boxed mattresses tried to be neither, just convenient. None of this is a frame problem until the mattress height changes. A pillow-top on a sleigh with a high footboard can look like a bread loaf in a too-small pan. A thin foam on a tall upholstered wing looks like a twin that wandered into a king. The frame that survives these changes has a little vertical mercy: slats you can reset, a headboard that does not assume twelve inches of quilted foam.

What a hardwood shop would do — and what Bradley Brand’s listings imply when they talk about solid wood, Warren, Arkansas, the 1902 lumber line, no particleboard — is build the part that should outlive the foam. Posts, rails, a headboard in oak or maple you can refinish when the cherry-orange craze (or the gray-wash craze, or the blond-ash craze) is over. Hook the mattress with slats, not a disposable box. Let the textile be textile: a canopy you can take down, a cover you can wash, a headboard panel you can re-cover without throwing away the posts. The company is not a bed specialist in the way a mattress chain is. The contrast is still the series question. Particleboard platforms in a linen slipcover are a mattress accessory. A frame is a piece of furniture.

Retail sold cycles as identities. The sleigh buyer was traditional. The platform buyer was modern. The iron buyer was romantic. The wing buyer was “custom.” The linen-canopy buyer is restful, which is the current word for rich. None of those identities survive a move as well as a simple hardwood bed that can take a new mattress size within reason. (A queen frame will not become a king without lying. Do not lie to a rail.)

The hangover in the bedroom is quieter than the wall unit at the curb, but it is there. Cherry sleighs on Marketplace, too big for the new apartment. Upholstered wings with a stain no dry cleaner will touch. Platforms whose slats have snapped because they were MDF. Canopies with the original cabbage-rose fabric still hung, a time capsule the owner is afraid to undress. The mattresses are already gone. They always go first. That is their job.

A bed-form cycle is not a failure. Rooms need to be born. The failure is treating the frame as if it were the mattress: disposable, branded, year-stamped. Buy the foam for the back you have this decade. Buy the frame for the room you will still be sleeping in when the foam has a new chemical name. Four posts in a hardwood, a decent rail, a headboard that can change its clothes — that is not a refusal of fashion. It is a place for fashion to sit without taking the house with it when it leaves.
