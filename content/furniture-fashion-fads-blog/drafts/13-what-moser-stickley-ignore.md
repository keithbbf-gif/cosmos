The arm is one piece of wood that runs from the front post, around your back, and down the other side without a break you can feel. Thos. Moser’s Continuous Arm Chair, designed in 1977, is that line. Tom and Mary Moser had founded the shop in 1972 in a grange in New Gloucester, Maine. The chair is still the piece people can name. The shop moved to Auburn in 1987. Company timeline says they have hand-signed pieces since 1977 [CITE NEEDED against the firm’s own chronology if you want a signature in the wild]. You pick the chair up by the arm and the arm is the structure, not a decoration applied to a structure.

Gustav Stickley would have recognized the instinct and argued about the style. He ran *The Craftsman* from October 1901 to 1916. United Crafts became Craftsman Workshops, Eastwood, Syracuse. He despised “Mission” as a name for the furniture — a sales word, a Spanish fantasy, a label that made the work sound like a revival of something it was not [CITE NEEDED for his exact wording; the dislike is consistent in the secondary literature]. He wanted oak, visible joinery, hardware that looked like it had a job. The magazine taught a reader how to live with that furniture and, not incidentally, how to want it.

He was not alone and he was not the only Stickley. L. & J.G. Stickley — Leopold and John George — set up in Fayetteville, New York, in 1905. Stickley Brothers was in Grand Rapids by 1891. The successor to L. & J.G. still operates in Manlius, New York, which is a continuity most furniture names cannot claim. The brothers disagreed, competed, and sold to overlapping customers. What they shared, when the work was good, was a refusal to hide how a piece went together. What they did not share was a single moral. This is not a sermon about purity. Gustav went bankrupt. Factories cut corners. “Craftsman” became a word other people owned. The useful remainder is a habit: make the joint, finish the wood, do not ask the object to be a year.

What Moser and the Stickley line ignore is not “fashion” as a human fact. People will always want a new chair. They ignore the apparatus that makes a chair a season. Color-of-the-year is the obvious one. A shop that builds in cherry and walnut does not wait for Pantone. The wood already has a color. You can stain it, and both traditions have stained, but the stain is in service of the board, not in service of a press release. Distressed-on-purpose fashion is the next. Beating a new piece so that it looks inherited is a kind of theater Stickley’s hardware did not need and Moser’s signed chairs do not bother with. The wear arrives. It arrives from use. Pre-distress is a finish schedule for a showroom that will not see use until the customer gets home, and then the fake wear and the real wear argue.

Barn-door hardware as jewelry is a smaller tell, and a louder one. A sliding door on black strap steel became, in the 2010s, a kitchen necklace. The hardware was the point. The door was a prop. A Craftsman hinge, when it was good, was iron because the door was heavy. Moser’s hardware disappears into the job. Logo status is the last ignore. A name burned on the underside is a record. A name on the apron, in brass, facing the dining room, is a handbag. Thos. Moser’s signature, if the timeline is right, is a maker’s mark for the person who turns the chair over. It is not a chest logo for the person across the table.

Retail taught the opposite lessons for a century. Mission became a mall category: dark oak, a clavos nail, a pew that had never been in a church. The 1990s Pottery Barn and the 2000s restoration catalogs sold “artisan” as a stain color. The 2010s sold farmhouse as a barn door and a clock face. None of that is Stickley’s fault, and it is only slightly Moser’s problem. Copies need a name to copy. The original shops keep making the chair. The chair does not have to answer the copy.

Status, in the Moser and Stickley worlds, is supposed to be quiet, and sometimes it is not. A signed Continuous Arm in a dining room of a certain kind of New England house is a known object. A Stickley settle in a study is a known object. Collectors pay for labels, for shop marks, for the right stretchers. That market can become as vain as any other. The difference, when there is one, is that the vanity is attached to a joint you can still read. A fake Mission chair with a burned-in name is vanity attached to a burn. Learn the joint. The burn will lie.

The hangover from the fashion versions is in every Habitat for Humanity ReStore: a “Mission” coffee table with a plywood shelf and oak-colored edges, a farmhouse bench whose X-brace is a stencil, a barn door in the garage because the next owner wanted it off the track. The hangover from the real shops is different. It is a chair that needs a new seat rush, a settle that needs a cleaner who understands oil, a table whose top has a cup and can be flattened. Repair is the point. A piece that cannot be repaired was never in this argument.

I am not going to pretend that ignoring a year is a personality. Shops ignore years because their jigs are set up for a chair, not for a collection launch. That is a production fact. It produces, as a side effect, furniture that does not look embarrassed in 1995 or 2015 or 2028. The Continuous Arm is dated 1977 if you know the date. If you do not, it is a chair with a long arm. A Gustav Stickley tabouret is dated if you know *The Craftsman*. If you do not, it is a small oak table with a visible joint. Dating requires a caption. Aging does not.

Bradley Brand Furniture, in Warren, Arkansas, is not Moser and is not Stickley, and should not be drafted into a lineage. Its product copy says solid hardwood, no particleboard, no MDF. It also claims Bradley Lumber Co. from 1902, which is company copy until a primary record says otherwise [CITE NEEDED]. The useful neighborliness is only this: a shop that builds in hardwood and does not print the grain is, by habit, ignoring the same apparatus. Color-of-the-year would require them to stain against the board. Distress-on-purpose would require them to damage a top they just flattened. Barn-door jewelry is not a dining table problem. A logo on the apron would be a different business. They can stay a hardwood shop. That is enough of a position.

Stand a Continuous Arm next to a 2016 color-of-the-year side chair from a mall brand. The mall chair will tell you the year even if you sand it. The pigment was the idea. Stand a Stickley settle next to a distressed dining set whose wear was applied with a chain in a factory. The settle’s dents, if it has them, will be in the places a body goes. The factory’s dents will be in the places a camera goes. That is the whole essay, and it is not a moral. It is a map of where the tool hit.

What they ignore, they ignore because looking up would cost them the chair. A year is a short customer. A joint is a long one. Gustav lost the factory anyway. The Mosers built a company that still answers the phone in Maine. L. & J.G.’s successors still build in Manlius. Continuity is not a halo. It is a shop that kept a jig. The jig does not know what Pantone named this year. Good. The year will name itself something else, and the arm will still be one piece.
