The sliding door is off the track. It is pine, or a pine veneer, black strap hardware, a handle that is a handle the way jewelry is a handle — meant to be seen, not particularly meant to be used twice a day by a person in a hurry. Behind it: a water heater, or a pantry, or a pair of coats. The house is a 2016 spec build in a county that grows more garages than hay. The walls in the living room are shiplap, painted white, running past a television the size of a small door. A Mason jar holds spoons. Another holds flowers that came from the supermarket in a rubber band. This is modern farmhouse as it arrived in the rooms that had never had a farm: a hardware store of signs, a softness of white, a black line of iron to keep the white from floating away.

*Fixer Upper* went out as a pilot on HGTV on May 23, 2013, and ran through April 3, 2018. Chip and Joanna Gaines, Waco, Texas. The show did not invent white paint or old wood. It gave a lot of people permission to put those two facts in the same sentence and call the sentence a house. Shiplap — boards with a rabbet, historically a way to weatherproof a wall or a hull — became a living-room skin. Sliding barn doors became interior architecture. Mason jars became lighting and storage and a kind of sincerity you could buy by the case. Black iron. White paint on old wood. Magnolia Market, in Waco, sold the objects that photographed like the show. The show sold the town. The town sold a weekend.

Restoration Hardware had been working a parallel industrial for a longer time. Stephen Gordon founded the company in Eureka, California, in 1979; later it became RH under Gary Friedman and a catalog that treated warehouse salvage as a luxury vocabulary: reclaimed wood tables, zinc, iron beds, a scale that implied you had a loft even if you had a great room. Those catalogs were heavier than the show. They sold a factory floor as a living room and charged for the square footage of the photograph. Pottery Barn and West Elm — Williams-Sonoma’s other rooms — sold a lighter industrial: metal bases, Edison bulbs, a “workshop” stool, tables that read as factory without requiring a factory. The 2010s put these cousins in the same house. Farmhouse was the walls and the jars. Industrial was the table and the lamp. Together they were a subdivision style with a name: modern farmhouse, which is to a farm what a food court is to a kitchen.

The style left the living room and went onto the elevation. Board-and-batten, black window frames, a porch that had never held a tractor, a metal roof on a house that was otherwise vinyl. Builders learned the word because buyers pointed at the show. A plan that had been “Craftsman” in 2006 became “farmhouse” in 2015 with a paint deck and a different door. That is not a crime against architecture. It is how production housing wears a year. The furniture had to keep up with the siding. Black iron inside, white oak or white paint, a table that looked as if it had been dragged from a barn that the lot had never included.

Status was authenticity you could schedule. You were not the Tuscan-granite person of 2004. You were not the black-lacquer person of 1987. You had discovered honesty: wood that looked used, metal that looked like work, a door that looked like it had closed on animals. The animals were metaphorical. The door was on a closet. That does not make the buyer a fool. Television is a more efficient catalog than a catalog, and *Fixer Upper* was a good catalog. People wanted a house that felt kinder than the granite blob and less temporary than a beige rental. White paint is a kindness. A heavy table is a kindness. The fashion was in the jewelry and in the claim that a ranch in a cul-de-sac had always been this rural.

Retail had layers. At the top: actual old wood with a job — a factory cart that had been a cart, a table whose top had been a floor, a zinc that had been a counter. RH, at its better moments, sold some of that, and sold a lot of new goods that impersonated it at a price that felt like salvage even when it was production. In the middle: Pottery Barn’s industrial dining, West Elm’s metal-and-wood, the Magnolia SKUs that a visitor could carry out. At the bottom: the barn-door kit from a big box, the foam “shiplap” or the MDF plank with a painted bevel, the Mason-jar pendant with a reproduction cage, the “reclaimed look” table whose grain was a print. IKEA and Wayfair and Amazon filled the bottom without needing Waco. They needed a filter named rustic.

The hangover is already built into houses that are not ten years old. Barn doors on interior closets in 2019 new-builds — off the track, as this one is, or never quite closing on a bathroom that needed a door that sealed. Shiplap in a ranch that never had a ship, or a barn, or a reason for a horizontal line except that the television show had one. Black interior doors in a house whose exterior is beige vinyl. A sliding door across a pantry that makes a noise at 6 a.m. Hardware as jewelry: straps, arrows, a cast “handmade” that came in a plastic bag. The jewelry dates. The drywall was always drywall.

What ages is actual old wood with a job. A table that was a table, thick enough to plane. A beam that holds something. A board that was outside and is now a mantel and still looks like it has weather in it, because it does. White paint on that board is a decision you can reverse, or live with, or refresh. Black iron that is iron, on a piece that needs iron, ages the way a hinge ages. What dates is the hardware as jewelry — the barn-door strap on a closet, the mason jar that is doing lamp work it was not blown for, the shiplap that is a wallpaper of wood, the word “farmhouse” on a sofa tag in a room that has underfloor heating and an HOA.

I have stood in kitchens where the island was a real hardwood top and the door to the pantry was a barn kit, and the top will outlast the kit by a generation if anyone bothers to oil it. That pairing is the 2010s in one glance. The wish was weight and story. The purchase was often story applied to a house that already had HVAC. Chip and Joanna painted a lot of real rooms in Waco. The copies painted a look onto a floor plan that had been drawn before the show existed. Both acts are allowed. Only one of them is a renovation. The other is a costume on a spec.

Bradley Brand Furniture, in Warren, Arkansas, does not sell a barn door and does not need a Magnolia adjective. The company’s own copy still says hardwood, no particleboard, and dates Bradley Lumber Co. to 1902; that date is company copy until a primary record sits beside it [CITE NEEDED]. The useful contrast in this decade is a top that can take the white paint off. Farmhouse fashion used wood as a feeling. A hardwood shop uses wood as a thickness. When the feeling moves — and it has already moved, toward quieter oak, toward a beige that is ashamed of the word farmhouse — the thickness is still there. The strap hardware is in a junk drawer with the oil-rubbed bronze grapes from the Tuscan year.

Modern farmhouse as a subdivision style will have a second life in the resale photos, the way Tuscan did: a little apologetic, a little stubborn, a sliding door that the next owner will take down and not know where to put. Take the door down if it does not close. Keep the table if the table is wood through the top. Leave the Mason jars to the pantry, where a jar is a jar. The show ended in 2018. The houses did not. That is the ordinary afterlife of a fashion that got into the walls. Walls are harder to return than a coffee table. That is why this fad will haunt longer than Memphis, and with less color.
