William Lawrence Murphy did not invent the wall bed in a New York walk-up, though New York is where the object became a rental feature and a punchline. He was working in San Francisco around 1900, in a one-room place on Bush Street, with a bed that folded into a closet so the room could pretend to be a parlor. Family stories later dressed that invention as courtship: a young woman of that decade was not supposed to enter a man’s bedroom, so the bedroom had to disappear. Treat the romance as legend. The hardware is the fact. In 1918 the Patent Office granted him a patent on a pivot bed; earlier filings sit around the first years of the century. In 1925 the Murphy Door Bed Company moved manufacturing to Suffolk County, New York — the court record in *Murphy Door Bed Co. v. Interior Sleep Systems*, 687 F. Supp. 754 (E.D.N.Y. 1988), is the cleanest public narrative of the move — and the name stuck to every bed that vanished into a wall, whether Murphy built it or not.

New York apartments did the rest. Hotels and efficiency suites of the 1920s advertised the Murphy as an amenity: pull the bed down and you had slept; put it up and you had a sitting room. Charlie Chaplin had already found the comic version in *One A.M.* (1916), a five-minute fight with a folding bed that taught the country what the mechanism could do to a man in a dinner jacket. The fashion was not the mattress. It was the claim that a small room could be two rooms if the furniture would cooperate.

Every housing squeeze since has brought the wall bed back as if it were news. Postwar studios. Recessions that sent adult children home. The 2008 crash and the decade of micro-apartments that followed. Tiny-house television in the 2010s, in which a couple smiled at a camera while a queen-size platform folded out of a wall that also held the pantry. IKEA daybeds — HEMNES, BRIMNES, the trundle that pretends to be a sofa — sold the same idea without the drama of a falling frame: sit now, sleep later, store the spare pillows in the drawers. Architects of accessory dwelling units and “flex rooms” still draw a wall bed first, because the drawing looks like extra square footage you did not build.

Hardware is the part that is not a fad. A proper wall-bed mechanism is steel, balanced, rated for a mattress weight, with a motion that does not require a second person or a prayer. Murphy’s own company still sells the floor-mounted steel beds the founder started with, or close descendants of them; competitors sell piston lifts, folding desks that become beds, and sofa-wall hybrids that try to be every room at once. The test is boring and physical. Does the bed stay up? Does it come down without slamming? Can you change the sheets without a degree in yoga? Is the cabinet anchored to studs, not to drywall hope? A folding frame that fails those tests is a hazard with a wood-look skin.

Suburban houses that were not small bought the same hardware for a different lie. The 1980s and 1990s guest room — a room that was also a sewing room, then an office, then a guest room again — got a wall bed so the treadmill could live on the floor. That is compact living as a schedule, not as a square footage. The mechanism still has to work. The cabinet, in those years, was often honey oak or a white raised panel, matching the rest of the house’s millwork so the bed would not admit it was a bed. When the house went gray in the 2010s, those oak cabinets looked suddenly like a den that time had left. People painted them. Paint does not fix a tired spring.

The fad is the skin. In one decade the cabinet is white Shaker with shaker knobs, so the room can call itself a farmhouse office. In the next it is walnut-look slab doors and LED strip lighting, so the room can call itself a studio. Gloss white lacquer after a boom in glossy lofts. Distressed gray after the reclaimed-wood catalogs. A mural on the underside of the bed so that, when it is down, you sleep under a forest. None of that changes the pivot. All of it dates. I have seen wall beds sold as “custom built-ins” whose entire custom work was a box around a kit from a hardware company, finished in the paint color of the year. That is legitimate carpentry. It is not a new invention. The kit is doing the cleverness. The paint is doing the fashion.

Retail split the idea into classes. At the top, a millwork shop builds a wall that is actually a wall: bookcases, a desk, the bed in the middle, everything scribed to the crown. In the middle, Resource Furniture and its peers sell Italian mechanisms and cabinets that cost as much as a used car, which is honest if the hardware is the Italian hardware they claim. Below that, Amazon and the ready-to-assemble aisle sell a folding frame and a headboard-shaped box, particleboard, cam locks, a warning card about tipping. IKEA’s daybeds sit in a different, more modest tradition: they do not hide. They look like beds that have agreed to be sofas until evening. That honesty is why they age better than a fake armoire whose doors never quite close over a queen.

Status attached itself in opposite ways. In a 1920s hotel, the Murphy said the management had thought about your parlor. In a 2010s tiny house, it said the owners were clever enough to live in 200 square feet on camera. In a pandemic guest room, it said the household could host without giving up the office. In a city studio, it still says the rent is the rent, and the furniture will have to do two jobs because the landlord will not. None of those claims is false. The false note is the listing that calls a folding bed a “lifestyle solution,” as if the squeeze were a taste.

What remains after the television crews leave is the same question Murphy asked a steel pivot: can the room change its job by night? If yes, the bed is a tool. If the cabinet doors are already peeling and the pistons sag, the tool was a finish. Bradley Brand’s public copy — hardwood, Warren, Arkansas, the 1902 lumber claim, no particleboard — belongs here only as a reminder that a wall of real wood around good hardware is a different object from a papered box around a folding frame. The company is not a Murphy maker. The contrast is material. A cabinet you can resand and rehinge will still be a cabinet when the next housing squeeze puts wall beds in the magazines again. A cam-lock cathedral will be in the alley.

There is also the daybed line, which never needed a wall. A wooden frame, a mattress, a trundle, bolsters. IKEA sold millions of the idea; so did every unfinished-furniture store in the 1980s. HEMNES in pine, a little formal; BRIMNES with the drawers that eat the spare sheets; the metal daybed that looks like a hospital until you put a quilt on it. The daybed is compact living without the theater. It does not convert the room so much as admit the room is small. That admission is why daybeds look less dated than wall beds dressed as libraries. They were never pretending to be architecture.

Hotels never fully dropped the type. Extended-stay chains and micro-hotels still spec wall beds because a sitting room at 4 p.m. and a bedroom at 10 p.m. is a rate they can charge. The hardware in those specs is usually the expensive kind: commercial rating, a closer, a lock. Home versions borrowed the photograph and not the spec. That gap — commercial pivot versus weekend kit — is the difference between clever and fad. Clever is a steel balance you can service. Fad is a finish that photographs as a built-in library and behaves as a folding cot in a box.

Fashion will keep returning to the disappearing bed, because housing keeps shrinking relative to what people think a grown life requires. The clever part is old. The finishes will keep lying about that. If you are buying one, buy the mechanism first — ratings, anchors, a mattress the frame can actually hold — and treat the cabinet doors as clothes. Clothes can be changed. A cheap pivot in a fashionable box is how a studio acquires a piece of furniture that is stuck halfway between floor and wall, which is not two rooms. It is a broken one.
