The chair is almost not there. That is the pitch. Four legs, a seat, a back, all of it clear, all of it catching a lamp in a green-tinged edge that is the only proof the object occupies space. In the listing photograph the room looks larger. In the room the seat is already scratched in a fan where someone sat down with a key in a back pocket. This is acrylic furniture’s promise and its hangover in one object: glamour as disappearance, then a cloud of scuffs that will not disappear.

Lucite is a trade name that got promoted to a material. DuPont’s acrylic, Plexiglas, Acrylite — polymethyl methacrylate, the clear plastic that could be thick, polished, and machined like a precious stone if you had the shop for it. In 1960s Los Angeles, Charles Hollis Jones had the shop, or found the people who did. He came out of the Hudson-Rissman showroom world, made a name in acrylic and metal, and built furniture for a Hollywood client list that later profiles repeat like a guest book: a vanity stool in the Lucille Ball line of stories (Los Angeles Times, 6 May 2001, on a Christie’s sale of Jones work), Wisteria chairs associated with Tennessee Williams, tissue boxes and wastebaskets ordered in quantity for Frank Sinatra’s Palm Springs house (same Times piece; Jones’s own later interviews). Arthur Elrod’s Palm Springs house, John Lautner in the background, a Post table that shows up in *Diamonds Are Forever* [CITE NEEDED on the exact on-screen piece if you are writing a shot list]. The point is not the famous names as decoration. The point is that see-through furniture, at that level, was custom, polished, and expensive — a stunt of engineering sold as ease.

Jones’s better pieces are not “invisible.” They are geometric. Nickel or steel hardware, thick acrylic slabs, edges that catch light like a cut. The Metric line, the Post tables, the Ziggurat experiments — these are objects. They happen to be clear. Clarity was the flex: I can afford a material that does nothing but be present. You were supposed to see the carpet, the architecture, the view. The furniture got out of the way and, by getting out of the way, took over the room.

Miami had a different temperature. Hivo and Estela Van Teal began Van Teal in 1973, first in a garage, then sculptures, then lamps (company history; High Point showroom 1976). Acrylic was the soul of the line: stacked clear and colored Lucite, geometric lamps, sculptures that sat on a credenza and announced that the room had been to South Florida. Saks and I. Magnin carried early pieces, if the later biographies are right [CITE NEEDED on the first Saks season]. Van Teal is not Jones. Van Teal is the popularization of the same see-through luxury — glamour you could buy as a pair of lamps if you could not commission a lounge. The 1970s and 1980s Miami version is chunkier, more colored, more willing to be a sculpture that also lights a corner. It yellows the same way. Sun is not a friend of old acrylic. Neither is smoke.

Then Philippe Starck, in 2002, made the ghost polite and multiple. The Louis Ghost armchair for Kartell is not acrylic. It is injection-molded polycarbonate, one piece, a Louis XV silhouette in ice (V&A; Starck and Kartell both date the design 2002). That distinction matters if you are a snob about polymers, and you should be a little bit of a snob, because the Ghost is a different contract. It is a factory chair, stackable in spirit if not always in practice, sold by the pair, indoor-outdoor in the marketing. It made “invisible historic” a dining-room default for people who had never heard of Charles Hollis Jones. In the first three years Kartell sold more than 100,000, per the V&A’s notes. The Ghost is a good industrial object. It is also the door through which every later clear chair walked — including the ones that were not Kartell, not polycarbonate of that grade, and not designed.

The 2010s filled dining rooms with acrylic chairs from every price rung. Some were honest thick-cast pieces. Many were the Amazon stool and its cousins: thin legs, a molded seat, a price that assumed you would throw it away when it clouded. They photographed like the Ghost. They lived like a CD case. Hosts loved them for small apartments and for the wedding-industrial tablescape. Guests loved them until they stood up and the chair stuck to a bare thigh, or until a chair leg hairline-cracked under a guest who had been told the chair was “surprisingly strong.” I will not invent a lawsuit. I have seen the crack.

Status, with see-through furniture, is a trick about space and money. Clear says you have nothing to hide and a lot to show: the floor, the art, the architecture. In a small room it says you refused visual weight. In a large room it says you could afford custom air. Hollywood Regency and Miami modern both used lucidity as jewelry. The 2010s apartment used it as a diet. Same material family, different hunger. What you were not supposed to mention was maintenance. Acrylic scratches if you look at it with a belt. It yellows in UV. It takes hairline cracks at bolt holes. The “invisible” chair becomes, in five years, a map of every dinner. Polycarbonate of Kartell’s grade behaves better; it is not immortal. Cheap acrylic behaves like the disposable it is.

Collectors now treat a documented Jones piece as furniture-as-object, which is correct. The hardware is real. The acrylic was worked, not just poured into a toy mold. A Van Teal lamp from the late 1970s, if the color hasn’t gone nicotine, still does the Miami job. A Louis Ghost still does the dining-room job if you accept plastic as plastic and stop asking it to be a ghost. The Amazon stool does a photograph. Then it does a closet.

What dates is the idea that clear is automatically modern. It is not. Clear is 1930s novelty, 1960s Hollywood, 1970s Miami, 2002 Kartell, 2014 apartment therapy. Each wave leaves a yellow. The 2010s wave is already in the thrift aisle, stacked, a little cloudy, priced as if transparency were a stain. What still works is the Jones object as an object — not as a set of eight around a glass table, unless you like living in a showroom, but as a table or a pair of chairs that hold a corner and a story. One piece of thick acrylic is jewelry. Eight matching thin ones are a rental.

A hardwood shop has almost nothing to say about Lucite except no. Bradley Brand Furniture in Warren, Arkansas, in its own copy, is Bradley Lumber 1902, solid hardwood, no particleboard. Acrylic is not particleboard; it is a different refusal. Wood scratches too, but a scratch in oak is a mark you can sand or leave as a year. A scratch in cheap acrylic is a fog that spreads. You do not recane Lucite. You do not reupholster it. You polish, if the piece was thick enough to polish, or you live with the cloud, or you replace. Jones-level work was built to be polished. The stool was not.

I have moved more clear chairs than I have enjoyed sitting in. They are light, which is a virtue in a fourth-floor walkup and a vice when a guest leans back. They announce taste and then announce every heel mark. The honest ones — Jones, a good Ghost, a Van Teal lamp that still throws a clean color — earn their keep as designed objects. The rest earn a listing title: *lucite glam stool, preowned, some wear.* Some wear is the whole story. See-through furniture promised a room without furniture. It delivered a room in which the furniture could never hide what you did to it.

If you want one piece, want a documented Jones or a Kartell Ghost you will not pretend is crystal. Leave the Amazon stool to the photograph it was born for. The status of invisibility was always a little cruel. Objects are not supposed to vanish. They are supposed to take a life and show it, or take a life and be repairable. Acrylic, at the high end, can show a life if you keep the sun off it and own a polish. At the low end it only shows the year you clicked Buy Now.
