The rosewood is almost certainly not rosewood. You can tell from the door, before you sit, because the grain repeats every eighteen inches the way a printed ribbon repeats. The leather is a leather-like. The ottoman is a cube of foam with button tufts that were glued, not pulled. The listing says “Eames style,” which is the most American phrase in furniture: a name that once meant a factory in Michigan and a pair of designers in California, now a silhouette you can order by Friday.

Charles and Ray Eames’s lounge chair and ottoman left Herman Miller in 1956 as a serious object — molded plywood shells, a metal spine, leather that was meant to take a life. It was never cheap. It was never supposed to be the whole room. The hangover is that it became the room’s password. If you had the chair, or something that read as the chair from across a loft, you had joined a church that started as a factory and ended as a feed.

Mid-century modern, as a living style, was not a single year. It was a twenty-year argument after the war: George Nelson at Herman Miller, Florence Knoll and Eero Saarinen at Knoll, the Danish export wave (Hans Wegner’s Round Chair, often called The Chair; Finn Juhl; Arne Jacobsen’s Series 7 for Fritz Hansen). American suburbs took a thinner version — blonde suites from Bassett and Lane, a kidney coffee table, a starburst clock — and called it modern because the legs were tapered. Both versions were sincere. The factory in Zeeland, Michigan, and the factory in Virginia were not doing the same work. They were answering the same wish: a house that did not look like grandmother’s mahogany.

The first hangover happened on schedule. By the mid-1970s, modern looked cold to a country that had just bought Mediterranean oak and harvest gold. The Eames chair did not disappear; it went into offices and the houses of people who had never believed the oak. Everyone else bought a carved suite and put a macramé owl on the wall. That is not a fall from grace. That is a cycle. The chair waited.

The boom that people mean when they say “mid-century is back” started as a retail education project. Rob Forbes founded Design Within Reach in 1999 (the idea, he has said, came in 1998 when he tried to furnish an apartment with the London modern he could not get through an American trade gate). The first catalogs put designer biographies next to SKUs. Herman Miller, which had not been a mall brand, sold through DWR’s paper and then its stores. The Washington *Post* noticed the catalog-to-store move in 2004. Herman Miller bought DWR in 2015, which is a tidy ending for a story about access: the factory absorbed the shop that had taught people to want the factory.

*Dwell* arrived on newsstands in October 2000, Lara Hedberg Deam’s magazine, Karrie Jacobs’s “Fruit Bowl Manifesto” insisting that modern houses were lived in, fruit on the table, not airless. The magazine did not invent the revival. It gave the revival a kitchen. Then *Mad Men* (AMC, 2007–2015) gave it a whiskey and a sofa people could point at without saying “Saarinen.” Television is a more efficient catalog than a catalog. You do not have to admit you are shopping.

What the boom sold, after a while, was not the chair. It was the password. West Elm, launched by Williams-Sonoma in 2002, put tapered legs on prices a first apartment could stand. Article, AllModern, Wayfair’s modern filters, the “mid-century walnut” finish that was a photograph of walnut — these made a silhouette into a default. The status claim changed. In 1962 the Eames lounge said you knew architects. In 2012 the knockoff said you watched the right show and you were not your parents’ Tuscan kitchen. Same silhouette, different boast.

I will not pretend authorized production is innocent. Herman Miller and Vitra have spent decades policing a shape. Licensed chairs are still expensive because the name is the product as much as the shell. A person who buys the licensed chair is buying a factory’s continuity and a lawyer’s file. A person who buys the $79 version is buying a picture. Both are allowed. Only one will still be a chair when the picture is tired.

The hangover now is not that modern looks dated. Parts of it refuse to date, which is the problem. A Wegner wishbone — the CH24, Carl Hansen & Søn, from 1949 — still looks like a chair because it was a chair: steam-bent back, paper cord, a joint you can read. The hangover is the apartment in which every object is a wishbone’s cousin, in a walnut print, under a replica Sputnik, above a rug that is a copy of a copy of a David Hicks. The room does not look like 1956. It looks like 2014, which is a year that already has a flea-market section.

Danish teak of the 1950s and 1960s is the other boom, and it has a cleaner hangover because the originals were so often better than the copies. Real teak oilers know the weight. The copies are rubberwood or something called “teak finish.” Estate-sale prices for a Finn Juhl-attributed piece and a dining set that is only brown have split so far apart that the word “Danish” in a listing is now a test of the seller, not the object. Chairish and 1stDibs made that split a market. Craigslist still hides the sleeper that a person who knows joints can find.

What ages, in this story, is almost insultingly simple. Molded plywood that is actually plywood. A metal base with a real weld. Leather that can be conditioned and, later, replaced. A teak table whose top is teak through the thickness. What dates is the password used as wallpaper: the word “Eames” on a box that contains none of his decisions, the hairpin legs on a particleboard desk, the sunburst clock that was already a joke in 1998 and became sincere again in 2011 and is a joke again if you wait.

Retail did one more thing to the boom: it taught a generation that “authentic” was a SKU. Design Within Reach sold authorized pieces and also sold the idea that you were now the kind of person who knew the difference. The stores — glass, pale oak floors, a lounge on a plinth — were classrooms with price tags. You left knowing the name of a designer you had not known at lunch. That is not a small public service. It is also how a silhouette becomes homework. Once the homework is widely assigned, the knockoff is the crib sheet.

Knoll and Herman Miller had always sold to architects first. The revival sold to people who wanted to live as if an architect had already been there. The difference shows up in the accessories. A 1961 office with a Saarinen pedestal table did not need a replica Noguchi lamp from a site that also sells phone cases. A 2016 apartment often did, because the password had become a set, and sets are how fashion closes. Florence Knoll’s own interiors were spare because the furniture was the decision. The hangover interiors are crowded because the furniture is the costume.

A hardwood shop watching this boom has a peculiar job. It cannot out-Eames Eames. It should not try. The useful thing it can do is refuse the printed grain that lets a “walnut credenza” exist at a price that walnut cannot. Bradley Brand’s own copy, out of Warren, Arkansas, insists on solid hardwood and no particleboard, and dates Bradley Lumber Co. to 1902 [CITE NEEDED — company copy]. That is not mid-century, and should not be sold as mid-century. It is a different bet: an object that does not need a designer biography because the board is the biography.

The Eameses were interested in how people sit and how factories might serve that sitting. The knockoff is interested in how people post. Both objects occupy the same corner of the room. Only one of them was designed for the corner. The other was designed for the frame. If you want the password, buy it cheap and admit the year. If you want the chair, buy the chair, or buy something else entirely that was also built to be sat in — a Windsor, a Continuous Arm, a kitchen chair with a seat you can recane. The hangover is full of passwords. It is short on chairs that were only chairs.
