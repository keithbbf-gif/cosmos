In the third week of March 2020 a lot of American dining tables became offices. The good ones — a solid oak top, a height meant for elbows and plates — did the job without complaint. The bad ones, glass and chrome from a 2004 package, trembled when anyone typed. Between those poles a new piece of furniture entered the house at speed: the standing desk, the converter, the folding banquet table from the garage, the chair that cost more than the table, the webcam ring that made a kitchen look like a broadcast. Nobody had time to be tasteful. The meeting was in ten minutes.

I will not tell you what share of the labor force worked from home. Pew and the Bureau of Labor Statistics published numbers through that year and after; if you need the figure, go to the page and quote it. The standing-desk boom had a head start. Uplift, out of Austin, and Fully (later brought into the Herman Miller world) had been selling steel frames and bamboo or laminate tops to people who already believed sitting was a moral problem. IKEA’s BEKANT — a plain, adjustable, office-looking desk with a cable tray that actually existed — was in the catalog for the rest of us. When offices closed, those names became hunt-and-peck purchases. Lead times stretched. People bought desk converters that sat on the dining table and turned dinner into a two-level shrine of monitors. Some of those converters were honest metal. Some were particleboard shelves on a spring, a fad chassis around a useful idea.

The dining table’s other new job was school. A household with children in 2020 did not have an office. It had a table that had to be a classroom at 10 a.m., a desk at 2 p.m., and dinner at 6. The furniture that survived that schedule was the furniture that could be cleared. A standing desk with two monitors bolted to an arm could not be cleared. A hardwood table could. That is not a design manifesto. It is a Tuesday. The houses that bought a second folding table for the kid, and kept the good table for dinner, made the only spatial decision that still looks sane.

The chair acquired a status it had previously reserved for actual offices. Herman Miller’s Aeron, designed by Bill Stumpf and Don Chadwick and introduced in 1994, left the cubicle and entered the Zoom frame. Mesh, a visible tilt mechanism, sizes A, B, and C: it photographed as competence. It also had twenty-five years of knockoffs behind it. By 2020 you could buy a mesh chair that gestured at an Aeron from a warehouse brand, or a used Aeron from a liquidated tech office, or the real thing at full price if the credit card still worked. The status was not comfort, though the real chair is comfortable. The status was the silhouette in the background of the call. A dining chair said you were making do. An Aeron said you had decided the house was now a workplace.

Converted dining tables were the honest middle. A household that already owned a hardwood table had, without planning, the best cheap desk of the pandemic: mass, a finish that can take a mat, room for two people if the day required it. When the workday ended, the table could be a table again. That conversion is older than Zoom. Writers have eaten on their desks for as long as desks have been tables. What was new was the duration, and the way a folding table from a church basement — white plastic, steel legs — became, for millions of people, the first dedicated work surface they had ever owned.

Retail behaved like a storm. Office-supply chains sold out of basic chairs. Amazon’s warehouse brands invented ergonomic names overnight. Used-office liquidators did the first honest business of the year, moving used Steelcase and Herman Miller into living rooms that had never wanted to look like cubicles. IKEA parking lots filled with people who had come for a BEKANT and left with a children’s stool because the desk was gone. Fully and Uplift’s sites showed wait times that would have been a joke in February. None of this requires a statistic. The objects are still in the houses.

What remains, a few years on, divides cleanly. The piece that earned its floor stayed: a desk with a top you can work on, a chair that does not hurt, a lamp, a place for the brick of a power supply. The folding table went back in the closet, or to the curb, or became the hobby bench. Standing frames that were never adjusted after the first week became expensive fixed desks, which is fine if the height is right and dishonest if the household is still telling itself they “stand now.” Converters that turned a good table into a bad one were the first to leave. The Aeron, if it was an Aeron, is still the best chair in the building. The mesh knockoff that peeled at the lumbar is in the same closet as the folding table.

Return-to-office orders, where they came, left a second hangover: a spare bedroom that is still half a workplace because nobody wants to admit the chair cost what it cost. Hybrid weeks produced hybrid rooms. Monday’s laptop on the dining table, Wednesday’s laptop at the office, Friday’s laptop on the sofa. The furniture that can do all three without looking like a command center is, again, a table and a chair you can put away. The furniture that cannot is a motorized frame in the only spare room, waiting for a job that now happens two days a week. That is a lot of steel for Tuesday and Thursday.

Bradley Brand’s own copy — Warren, Arkansas, Bradley Lumber 1902, no particleboard — belongs in this hangover as a type, not as a 2020 product story. A solid-hardwood table or desk does not care whether the laptop is a work laptop. It does not have a grommet shaped for last year’s monitor arm. It will still be a table when the employer wants people back, or does not. The fad was not wood. The fad was the belief that a particular mechanism (sit-stand, mesh, RGB lighting under the monitor) would make a kitchen into a career.

Status in that year had a strange smell. People who had never thought about chairs thought about chairs. People who had thought about chairs for a living watched their expertise become a consumer good. A standing desk in a one-bedroom apartment said: I am taking this seriously. An Aeron said it louder. A folding table said the truth, which was that the job had moved and the furniture had not caught up. I prefer the folding table’s honesty, and I prefer the hardwood dining table’s competence, to the standing desk that was bought as a personality.

Craft, here, is almost embarrassingly simple. A work surface needs stiffness, a height that matches the chair, and an edge that does not carve into a forearm. A chair needs to fit the person, not the catalog. Hardwood and decent joinery get you the first without a motor. A used Aeron or a well-made wooden chair gets you the second without a new brand. Motors fail. Apps that raise the desk on a schedule are a finish. The house does not need a finish to do work. It needs a table that does not rock and a place to put the work away when dinner starts.

What dates is the emergency aesthetic: cable raceways in neon, a “battlestation” of three monitors in a room that is also the bedroom, the converter still clamped to a table that would have been fine without it. What lasts is the older office idea, which is also the older dining idea: one good horizontal plane, one good chair, enough light, and the humility to fold the extra table and put it away. March 2020 was not a style. It was a scramble. The furniture that understood the difference is still in the room. The rest is in the closet, waiting for the next scramble to justify it, which is not the same thing as being needed.
