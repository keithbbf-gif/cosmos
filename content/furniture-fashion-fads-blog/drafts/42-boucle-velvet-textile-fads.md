The chair had a decent bone. You could feel it when you lifted one corner: a hardwood rail, a sprung seat, weight that was not all foam. Over the bone, a cream bouclé the color of a labradoodle. The loops had gone gray on the arms and shiny on the headrest. A single thread had pulled near the seam and started a run that no one would stop, because stopping it meant admitting the chair was already last year’s cloud. The frame could have taken another cloth. The cloth was the purchase people remembered.

Bouclé is not a 2020 invention. The word is French for buckled or looped; the cloth is a yarn with knots and loops that photograph as texture. Eileen Gray used it. So did the wider midcentury trade: the woolly, nubby upholstery on a club chair or a Bibendum that needed to read as modern without reading as slick. Le Corbusier, Pierre Jeanneret, and Charlotte Perriand’s Grand Confort has spent much of its later life in a pale bouclé that people now treat as original law. You bought the chair. The cloth was a coat.

Then 2020 through 2023 made the coat the chair. Every direct-to-consumer brand, and a few old ones that wanted the traffic, issued a cream blob: a rounded lounge, a “cloud,” a sheepish kidney of foam and wood-look legs, all in the same off-white loop. The silhouette was midcentury if you squinted and nursery if you did not. The cloth hid a lot of cheap foam. Texture is camouflage. On a feed, at three feet, the chair looked expensive and soft. In a room, on a July afternoon, it looked like a pet.

Velvet ran a parallel, older cycle. Crushed velvet in the 1990s — the sofa that looked wet, the jewel tones, the swirl that changed when you sat. It was prom and a casino and a suburban basement. People fled it for microfiber, then for the linen-look slipcover, then for velvet again. The 2020s version arrived as “performance velvet”: a polyester pile with a stain-resistant finish, sometimes a Crypton or Crypton-type treatment, sometimes an indoor-outdoor weave that had left the patio. Sunbrella, long a name for a deck chair that would not rot, came inside and sat on a sofa that would never see rain. The selling point was the child, the dog, the red wine. The effect was a velvet that did not behave like velvet. It behaved like a uniform.

Crypton and the other performance weaves are a real chemistry. I am not mocking a parent who wants a sofa that can take a spill. I am naming the fashion. Once “performance” became the default swatch, a whole run of sofas photographed as the same matte pile in the same ten colors. Navy. Emerald. Camel. The jewel tones of the 1990s without the crush. The cloth announced that you were a practical person with a design account. That announcement dates. Practical people will still need a washable sofa in 2032. They will not need this particular pile in this particular emerald.

Fabric is the fastest date stamp on a good frame because fabric is the cheapest thing to change in a factory photograph and the first thing a room sees. A frame — hardwood rails, proper joinery, a spring unit that can be re-tied — is slow money. A mill can change a SKU of cloth in a season. Retail follows the mill. You sit down in a showroom and think you are choosing a chair. You are choosing a year, in a fiber.

The cream bouclé year was cruel to pale loops. Dirt is not a moral failure. Dirt is physics. A looped yarn holds a crumb. A cream loop displays it. People covered the chairs with throws, which defeated the blob, or they lived with a gray that crept inward from the arms like a tide. Resale listings filled with “barely used” cream chairs that were used the ordinary amount. The next fashion will be a darker bouclé, or a velvet, or a leather-look that photographs as “quiet.” The frames, if they were frames, will still be there.

Recover versus replace is the only adult question. If the bone is good — hardwood, a spring deck, corners that do not rack — recovering is the older economy. You pay a shop. You pick a cloth that is not this year’s blob. You get the chair back as a chair. If the bone is foam on a plywood silhouette with legs that unscrew from inserts, recovering is a donation to a shape that was never meant to outlast the cloth. Replace that one, or live with it until the lease ends. Do not send it to an upholsterer and pretend you bought a Gray.

A solid case piece does not wear a date in a looped yarn. A chair with a real frame can change its date. A blob with a costume cannot. Buy the frame if you can find it. Buy the cloth knowing you will argue with it.

I have sat in a 1960s lounge with new wool and felt the original decision in the angle of the back. I have sat in a 2021 cream loop and felt only foam finding its sitting-shape. Both were called bouclé in the caption. The caption is a swatch name. It is not a provenance. Eileen Gray does not owe the blob anything. The blob owes Gray a footnote and a more honest hangtag.

What ages: a frame you can recover; a wool or a tight-weave linen that wears into itself; a leather that was leather. What dates: the cream loop of 2021, the crushed swirl of 1996, the performance emerald that matched a thousand rooms, the indoor-outdoor stripe that still looks like it is waiting for a deck. One pillow in a fashion cloth is a note. A whole sofa in a fashion cloth is the song.

The hangover is already at the curb on bulk-trash mornings: blob chairs that cannot stand a second cloth, velvets that went bald at the headrest, slipcovers in a bouclé-look knit that stretched. A few of those pieces had frames. Most did not. The ones that did will be recut. The ones that did not will become the next decade’s joke, the way crushed velvet became a joke and then a nostalgia and then a joke again.

Indoor-outdoor cloth coming off the patio is the latest version of an old wish: that a sitting room could take weather. Weather, indoors, is a child, a dog, a belt buckle, a Saturday. Sunbrella and its cousins will take some of that. They will also take the room toward a pool house, even if the pool is a hope. If that is the room you want, fine. If you wanted a club chair and got a deck chair in a nicer color, look at the selvage. The mill told you. The hangtag used the word *performance* the way a catalog used *lifestyle*. Both words mean: this cloth is the product. The chair is the delivery device.

If you like the loop, use it on something whose bones you have checked. If you like velvet, ask whether you want the crush, the performance, or the old cotton pile that marks when you sit. Marks are not the enemy. A mark means a person was in the room. The enemy is a cloth that cannot take a mark and cannot take a new cloth. That is a costume with legs.

Sunbrella can stay outside. It can also come in, if you want a sofa that behaves like a patio. Just do not call that hospitality, or midcentury, or a cloud. Call it a cover. Covers come off. That was always the point of a good chair.
