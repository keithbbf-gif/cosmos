The Chesterfield arrives in American rooms as a finished argument: you are a person with a study, even if the room is a laptop and a candle. Deep buttons, arms as high as the back, a roll that says club. Cognac leather — that amber-brown the catalogs treat as a personality — does the rest. The trouble is not the form. The form is a sofa. The trouble is the story the form is asked to tell in a house that never had a club, and the material that is asked to fake the years.

Start with the dispute, because the dispute is the honest part. Popular lore gives the sofa to Philip Stanhope, 4th Earl of Chesterfield (1694–1773), a commission for upright sitting that would not wreck a coat. Historians and makers have been looking for the paper trail for a long time. Paul Fleming of Fleming Howland, interviewed in *The Independent* (12 June 2011), said his shop had gone to museums and could not find a single document that explained the name. The word “chesterfield” as a sofa turns up in the nineteenth century and, in some dictionaries, around 1900; a James Roberts painting of Balmoral’s drawing room, 1857, shows buttoned sofas that later eyes call Chesterfields, in plaid rather than hide. Early examples were often velvet. The leather we now treat as inevitable is a later habit. In Canada the word became, for a long stretch, just the word for a sofa, any sofa, which is a useful reminder that names wander. Say the origin is disputed. Then sit down.

The club itself is easier to locate than the earl. Victorian and Edwardian London rooms — the Reform, the Garrick, the rooms men used as a second address — kept buttoned, upright seating because the point was talk, not a nap. American men’s clubs borrowed the look. American living rooms borrowed the clubs. By the time a Pottery Barn catalog could sell a “man cave,” the furniture had traveled so far from St. James’s that the only remaining signal was the color of the hide and a nailhead the factory applied with a gun.

Victorian London’s gentlemen’s clubs used the type, or something close to it, and that is the image American retail still sells: a room for men, a drink, a book not being read. Restoration Hardware and Timothy Oulton spent the 2000s and 2010s industrializing that image — distressed leather, nailheads, a finish that looked like a century of elbows. Pottery Barn’s “man cave” catalogs of the same years put a cognac club chair under a mounted something and called it a den. The chair did not need a den. It needed a caption.

What ages is a well-worn real hide. Leather that was leather all the way through — full-grain or at least top-grain, aniline or semi-aniline, a cow that had a life — takes the body. The seat goes concave. The arms go dark. The buttons pull little craters that are not printed on. A chair like that, fifty years in, looks like furniture. It does not look like a theme. You can condition it. You can live with the scratch. You cannot reverse the sitting, and that is the point. The sitting is the finish.

What dates is the “distressed” bonded leather that peels. Bonded leather is leather dust and scraps in a binder, with a polyurethane face that can be embossed to look like a hide. It photographs as cognac. After a few summers it maps, then cracks, then sheds a gray underlayer onto black trousers. I have seen club chairs, two years from the box, wearing their distress as a disease rather than a history. The catalog said vintage. The chair said landfill. Split leather and heavy pigment can fail in a related way: they look armored on day one and chalked by year five. None of this is a secret among upholsterers. It is a secret among the people who bought the gravitas.

Retail taught the country that a study could be ordered. Restoration Hardware’s galleries — those warehouse rooms of repeated sofas — made the Chesterfield a module. Timothy Oulton’s darker, more theatrical version added trunks and maps for people who wanted the British Empire without the history lesson. The distressing on those pieces was a factory process: sanding, wax, a stain in the creases, a story about a farmhouse that never existed. Pottery Barn and Crate & Barrel sold a cleaner club chair, often in a leather that was real enough and a silhouette that was almost shy. Ashley and the warehouse brands sold the peel. The price ladder was a material ladder, and the photographs were not.

A person could, and still can, buy a decent hide on a decent frame from a local upholsterer and skip the club caption entirely. That chair will not be in the catalog. It will also not peel in two summers. The gentleman’s-study fashion made that option look incomplete, because the fashion was a room — a globe, a decanter, a book with a handsome spine — not a place to sit. Rooms like that photograph. They do not always get used. I have sat in more unused cognac chairs than used ones. The unused ones are the dated ones, because nothing has happened to them except time and central air.

Status worked because cognac leather is instant. You do not need books. You need the color and the buttons. A room with a tufted sofa in that brown tells visitors the household has opinions about whiskey, even if the whiskey is a bottle for guests. The gentleman’s study, as a fashion, was never about study. It was about permission to be a little heavy, a little masculine, a little expensive-looking in a culture that had spent a decade on white linen and glass. The chair was a counter-fashion. Counter-fashions date when the next counter arrives.

There is a kinder reading. People wanted a chair that felt substantial after years of foam and metal. Leather, even imperfect leather, has a smell and a temperature fabric does not. A tufted sofa holds you up instead of swallowing you. Those are real pleasures. They do not require a fake club, a fake title, or a fake century of wear. A new full-grain chair will look new. That is allowed. New hide that is allowed to get old is how you get the object the catalog was imitating.

Bradley Brand Furniture, in Warren, Arkansas, is not a leather house. The company’s own copy talks about hardwood, the 1902 Bradley Lumber claim, and a refusal of particleboard. The useful contrast is still craft: a frame you can reupholster versus a frame that is a staple gun and a hope. Club chairs fail first at the hide, then at the foam, then at the joints. A hardwood frame with proper eight-way hand-tied springs, or at least a sprung unit you can get at, can take a new hide in twenty years. A cardboard-and-webbing frame inside a bonded skin cannot. When you buy gravitas, ask what you are buying under the color.

The hangover is already in the resale apps. Cognac Chesterfields from the peak years sit too large for the rooms they are leaving. The distressed ones look tired in a way that is not charming. The bonded ones are unsellable except to someone who has not yet learned the word. A mid-century leather lounge — a different religion, less club, more office — holds value better, because it was never pretending to be an earl. The club chair that survives is the one that was sat in by a person, not pre-sat by a factory sandpaper.

Fashion will keep using leather as shorthand for seriousness. Law offices, hotel lobbies, the home office that wants to look like both. The form can take it. The form has taken velvet, horsehair, and whatever the 1850s had on hand. What it cannot take, forever, is a printed face on dust. If you want the study, buy a chair whose hide can become one. If you want the photograph, buy the photograph. They are different prices, and only one of them will still be a chair when the cognac goes out of style, which it will, the way hunter green went out, the way oxblood went out, the way the man cave went out and left a lot of nailhead still trying to look like a decision.
