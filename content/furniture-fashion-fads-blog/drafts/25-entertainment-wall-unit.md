The opening is thirty-two inches wide, eighteen deep, and framed like a shrine. Oak-look laminate, a little gold on the pulls, a dusty ring where a VCR once sat on a shelf that no longer exists in any living room that still has a pulse. The television that was meant to live in that hole is gone. The hole remains. On bulk-trash morning the whole cathedral goes to the curb — piers, glass doors, the cubby that held the cable box as if cable were a mineral deposit — and the new set, a thin 65-inch rectangle, leans against a wall that was never designed to hold a picture that large.

That is the entertainment wall unit’s last public act: proving, in daylight, that furniture can be more specific than the object it was built to serve.

American living rooms did not always have a piece of furniture whose only job was to look like a small theater. Radios sat on tables. Early televisions were furniture themselves — blonde consoles with speakers in the knees, the screen a porthole. The cabinet was the set. When the tube grew and the box around it shrank, the room needed a second cabinet to hide the first. By the late 1970s and through the 1980s, retailers began selling “entertainment centers” and “wall units” as a category, not a custom built-in. The names promised a system. The object delivered a hole.

The hole had a number. A 19-inch or 25-inch CRT was a bedroom set. A 27-inch was a respectable living-room picture in the mid-1980s. Then 32-inch and 36-inch tubes arrived, fat as steamer trunks, and the catalogs widened the bay. Broyhill, working out of Lenoir, North Carolina, treated the category as seriously as a dining suite. A 1987 design patent filed by Gary A. Huffstetler and assigned to Broyhill Furniture Industries covers an “entertainment cabinet unit” with the stacked, pilastered face that still turns up at auction — drawers below, a void above, side towers for the things that were supposed to make watching a ritual (U.S. Patent D311830, granted 1990). In May 1988 the *Chicago Tribune* quoted Broyhill’s Larry Hazen on a market he called “explosive”: units that had started 30 to 32 inches wide were being offered up to 40 inches for wide-screen sets, and heights that had begun around 76 to 80 inches were being cut toward a “continental” 52 to 54. Lane answered with country-pine armoires that hid the electronics upstairs and kept drawers below, as if the television were a secret the room was still too polite to admit.

Sauder, in Archbold, Ohio, made the democratic version: ready-to-assemble particleboard, printed oak, cam locks, an afternoon on the carpet with an Allen wrench. The company still sells entertainment credenzas; the older ones were taller, deeper, and more certain about what a television was. A Sauder wall unit from the 1990s is a theology of veneer paper. It looks like furniture from eight feet away. From two feet it is a photograph of wood glued to a pressed panel. Bradley Brand Furniture, in Warren, Arkansas, prints the opposite claim in its own product copy — Bradley Lumber 1902, and no particleboard — which is useful here only as a material contrast, not as a sermon. The hangover of the wall unit is not that people wanted a place for the set. It is that the place was engineered for one width of glass, one depth of tube, and one decade of cables.

Then came the brief, heavy moment of rear-projection. Late 1990s into the early 2000s, the living room acquired a set the size of a dresser: 50, 55, 60 inches of plastic and mirrors, a picture that bloomed if you sat in the right cone and died if you stood up. Furniture followed, grudgingly. The bay got wider. The shelf got deeper. Some units sprouted a low, wide pedestal instead of a vertical shrine, because the new set would not fit in a closet for a 27-inch tube no matter how you angled it. Those pedestals had a short career. Flat panels arrived, first as luxury and then as the only thing in the big-box aisle, and the rear-projection monster became the second curb object of the decade — too heavy to donate, too ugly to keep, too recently purchased to feel like a joke.

Retail sold the wall unit as the living room’s face. Levitz and the regional warehouse stores stacked them in rows, each finish a weather report — honey oak, pickled oak, “washed” pine, cherry-look, black lacquer for the people who had seen a loft in a magazine. Sears and J.C. Penney put them in the same circulars as the sets they were sized for, which was honest merchandising: buy the hole and the thing that fills it on the same Saturday. Rooms To Go later packaged the wall unit with the sofa, the coffee table, and the lamps, so the room arrived as a single decision. The status claim was not subtle. A wall of cabinetry said the household had enough electronics to require architecture. Glass doors said the electronics were worth displaying. Pocket doors that folded back said you could hide the whole century if company came.

What the unit was meant to say about the owner: we are a family that watches together, and we have organized the watching. The VHS spines faced out. The CD changer had a slot. The remote had a tray. Some units included a light, as if the television were a relic. In finished basements the wall unit became a rec-room altar, flanked by bar stools and a neon beer sign. In apartments it ate the only blank wall and made the sofa face one direction for the next fifteen years.

The hangover is physical. A 65-inch flat panel is about 57 inches wide without the soundbar. The old bay is 32. You cannot “make it work.” The side piers that once held speakers now hold board games and tax files. People try. They set the new set on top of the old unit, where it looks like a billboard parked on a bungalow. They gut the center bay with a jigsaw and leave a ragged mouth. They list the piece online as “solid wood entertainment center,” which is a hope, not a species. The honest listings say “won’t fit modern TV” and still ask $150 because the thing is heavy and someone once paid $1,200.

I have seen more of these at the curb than I have seen in rooms that still use them as designed. The ones that survive do so by quitting the television. They become liquor cabinets, craft cupboards, the place the printer lives. That is a decent second life for a Broyhill that was actually casework. It is a sad one for a Sauder cathedral whose paper edges have already swollen from a single spilled soda.

The craft question is not whether a television deserves furniture. It is whether the furniture should guess the diagonal. A shop that works in solid hardwood — the kind of work Bradley Brand’s listings describe for credenzas and storage, and that a hundred unnamed cabinetmakers still do — can build a low, deep piece with a top that will take a set, or no set, or a set that has not been invented. Legs, a plinth, drawers that are drawers. No hole the width of a 27-inch tube. No pediment. No assumption that next year’s picture will be the same animal as this year’s. The television sits on it, or hangs above it, or leaves. The piece remains a table with storage. That is a credenza. It is an older idea than the entertainment center, and it will still be a credenza when the next display technology arrives as a sheet you clip to the paint.

What dates is the specificity. The wall unit that can only be a wall unit. The oak-look cathedral with the gold-tone gallery rail. The “home theater” package that includes fake columns and a place for a receiver the size of a microwave. What still works is a horizontal surface at the right height, a way to hide the mess of cords, and a refusal to carve a permanent aperture for a product with a two-year replacement cycle. The people who bought the cathedrals were not fools. They were given a number — 27, then 36, then the rear-projection boast — and they bought furniture that believed the number. The number changed. The furniture did not.

If you are standing in a room with one of the leftover colossi and a new set in a box, the kindest thing you can do for the room is admit the mismatch. Sell the shrine to someone who needs a pantry wall. Put the picture on a piece that does not care what size it is. The living room will look suddenly less like a closed showroom from 1994, and more like a place a person might read, talk, or, yes, watch something — without first walking through a particleboard nave to get there.
