The chair is iron painted white, the back a morning-glory scroll, and it is sitting on a department-store floor in Los Angeles in 1945 as if the war had not just ended. Brown Jordan — Robert Brown and Hubert Jordan, Pasadena — showed the Morning Glory collection at Bullock’s Wilshire that year. Iron, romantic, a little movie-set. Three years later the same company put Leisure on the floor: aluminum frames, vinyl lace, a lighter idea of sitting outside. The company would keep doing this, collection after collection. Tamiami, by Hall Bradley, came in fashion colors, as if a patio chair were a dress. Outdoor furniture is always a dress. That is why it spikes.

Indoor furniture can pretend it is permanent. Outdoor furniture has to admit weather. The admission is what makes it fashionable. A new finish, a new weave, a new claim that this time the stuff will winter over, and the patio is a room again. Then the weave chalks, the sling tears, the fire pit rusts at the seam, and the spike is over. What remains is a stack.

Telescope Casual, upstate New York, folding chairs and tables that knew how to go back in a shed. Lloyd Flanders, Menominee, Michigan, woven furniture that treated wicker as a year-round argument. Redwood decks in the 1970s, the lumber still cheap enough that a suburban lot could grow a platform and call it living. Teak from the contract world and the catalog world, oil-dark, heavy, the status wood of the boat and the club. All-weather wicker later, a resin weave over aluminum, sold as the end of rotting rattan. Each of these is a real product with a real shop behind it. Each of them also rode a spike when the culture decided the yard was a room.

The 1950s and 1960s sold the patio as a California export. Aluminum and vinyl lace photographed well. The drink cart came outside. Status was a pool and the chairs that proved you used it. The 1970s added the redwood deck and the hot-tub surround, cedar and redwood going silver, a wet bar with a pass-through. The 1990s and 2000s added the all-weather package from the warehouse club: a table, six chairs, a market umbrella, a cover that lasted one season. Frontgate, Restoration Hardware, and the big catalogs sold a more expensive version of the same diagram — chaise, fire table, teak that had never seen a shipyard.

Then 2020. People were home. The yard was the only restaurant. CNBC, 17 May 2020, reported the spike with names attached. Overstock’s Ron Hilton said outdoor demand “exploded.” Frontgate’s Lindsay Foster talked chaises and fire pits. Bed Bath & Beyond reported boosts in the same direction. Frontgate’s pool floats were “up over 100 percent,” which is a company figure given to a reporter, not a census, and should be read as such (CNBC, 17 May 2020). I will not invent the rest of the percentages. The qualitative fact is enough: the patio became the spare room, and the spare room got furnished in a hurry.

A spike has a hangover the way a wedding has a garage. The 2021 and 2022 patios of a certain kind of suburb still hold the evidence: a resin wicker set whose left-arm chair has a broken weave, a propane fire table that is now a table because the burner fouled, an umbrella base the size of a tire and twice as ugly, a stack of folding chairs no one folded. Cheap resin remembers every winter. It chalks. It goes chalk-white and then dirty-white. It is not the vinyl lace of 1948, which at least knew it was festive. It is furniture that was a click, delivered, assembled with a hex key, and then asked to be a room for two summers of a pandemic.

Teak is the counter-example people reach for, and teak can take it, if it is teak. Plantation teak, properly dried, oil or no oil, goes silver and stays structurally itself. A lot of what was sold as teak in the spike years was a thinner story: mixed hardwoods, a teak-colored stain, a tabletop that will cup. All-weather wicker is honest when the aluminum frame is decent and the weave is a replaceable sling idea. It is a costume when the weave is the entire identity and the frame is a tube that kinks. Brown Jordan’s better iron and aluminum, Telescope’s better folding stock, Lloyd Flanders when the weave is still tight — these are outdoor furniture that expected to be refinished or re-strapped. The warehouse set expected to be replaced.

Status outdoors is louder than status in the dining room because the neighbors can see it. A 1970s redwood deck said we built. A 1990s hunter-green resin set said we shopped the club. A 2000s teak-and-stainless package from a catalog said we winter in a place that needs a cover. A 2020 fire pit and a sectional that could have been indoors said we are not going inside, and we have the delivery slot to prove it. The signal is hospitality that faces the street or the fence. When the spike ends, the signal stays out there in the rain, which is why outdoor fashion is crueler than indoor fashion. You cannot hide a dead chaise behind a door.

Retailers love the outdoor spike because it is seasonal and therefore forgivable. Miss this spring and there is next spring. High Point and the casual-furniture shows in Chicago and the Carolinas have long treated outdoor as a line you refresh like ready-to-wear. Hall Bradley’s Tamiami colors were fashion colors. That was the point. You were not supposed to keep the same strap color for twenty years, though the frame might last if you let it. The contemporary equivalent is the powder-coat of the year — sage, terracotta, a black so matte it photographs as a hole — sold on aluminum that is the same extrusion as last year. Paint, again. Fashion, again.

What ages outdoors is mass, joinery, and a finish you can cut back. Wrought iron that can be ground and recoated. Teak that can be sanded. A sling chair whose cloth is a part number. What dates is the novelty of the season: the egg chair in the wrong resin, the faux-wood plastic Adirondack that fades to a bone color no tree ever was, the solar-lantern chandelier over a table that cannot take a real one. The 2020s also produced outdoor rugs in patterns that belong on a phone, and outdoor “sectionals” that brought the cloud problem into the damp. Foam that sits in weather is a faith-based material.

A hardwood shop is not, mostly, in this business. Bradley Brand Furniture in Warren, Arkansas, talks solid hardwood and no particleboard or MDF in its product copy; it also claims the Bradley Lumber line back to 1902, which remains company copy until a primary record is attached [CITE NEEDED]. The quiet contrast is still useful. A maple island in a kitchen will outlast three generations of patio sets bought for the same house. That is not because indoor wood is virtuous. It is because the patio set was never asked to last. It was asked to arrive in time for the first warm Saturday.

Behind a row of college-town rentals the remainder is already out by the bins: four identical resin tables, one missing a leg, two umbrellas with torn vents, a fire bowl filled with rainwater and a single sandal. That is the 2020 spike’s leftover, not the Frontgate chaise in a magazine. Hilton was not wrong that demand exploded. Foster was not wrong that chaises and fire pits moved. The hangover is not their problem. It is the alley’s.

If you are buying outdoor furniture as furniture, buy the frame you can repair and the surface you can refinish. If you are buying a summer, buy a summer and do not call it an investment. Morning Glory iron from 1945, kept painted, is still a chair. A resin stack from 2020 is already a disposal question. The patio will be fashionable again. It always is, the first week the heat breaks. The question is whether you want a dress or a bench. Brown and Jordan, in 1945, were willing to sell you either, and they were clear about the iron. Clarity is the rare outdoor finish.
