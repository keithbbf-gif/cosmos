The dresser lists to the left because the corner has gone soft. You can see the substrate where the paper tore: a pale crumb, not quite wood, not quite anything you would name at a lumberyard. A cam lock spins in its hole and does not catch. The piece has moved house once. The second move is the one it will not survive, and everyone in the room already knows it. On the other wall, an older chest — oak, heavy, a drawer that sticks in August — waits as if it had been waiting since a different administration. Two storage technologies. One of them was designed to be furniture for a while. The other was designed to be furniture.

Max Himmelheber, in Germany, developed particleboard in the 1930s and 1940s — a board made of chips and resin, waste wood given a job. The patent is often cited as 1932 or 1933. I do not have the number in this file. [CITE NEEDED] Postwar reconstruction needed houses and the furniture to put in them, and a board that did not require a clean log was a serious answer to a serious shortage. Particleboard is not a moral failure. It is an industrial success. The lie arrived later, when the board put on a photograph of oak and went to a store that sold “wood” to people who wanted wood.

IKEA made the board respectable as a philosophy, not only as a price. Furniture for the many, flat in a carton, cheap enough to leave. The 1985 American opening, Plymouth Meeting, did not invent the substrate. It taught a country that the substrate could be a life: student years, first jobs, rooms that would be abandoned on purpose. That teaching was generous. It still is, for the years it fits. The trouble is the years it does not fit — the dresser asked to be an heirloom, the cabinet asked to live beside a sink, the “oak” asked to take a refinish. Philosophy and joinery are not the same department.

Veneer, laminate, and paper are three different skins, and the trade has spent decades hoping you will not sort them. A real veneer is a thin slice of actual wood, glued to a core. It can be beautiful. It can be repaired by a person who still knows how. It is not solid, and it should not be sold as solid. A laminate is a plastic sheet, often with a photograph or a print of wood, bonded to a board; it laughs at a coffee cup and fails at a chip. Paper, in the cheap case goods, is a printed grain rolled onto particleboard or MDF and edged in a plastic strip the color of the print. You cannot sand a photograph. You can only live with it until the edge lifts.

California’s Air Resources Board wrote formaldehyde rules that actually changed what could be sold — a rare furniture fact with a regulator’s name on it. If you name a phase (CARB Phase 2, or the later federal alignment), attach a date from the rule text. [CITE NEEDED] I will not invent the year on this page. What can be said without a press release is that the board in the corner of the room is not only a style problem. It is a chemistry problem the state decided to treat as air. That is a better sentence than any brand claim about “clean.”

The joints are the other honesty test. A screw-cam lock — the little steel barrel, the bolt that draws two boards together — is a brilliant packing idea. It is a poor life idea. Every move loosens it. Every over-tightening strips it. A dresser built on cams is a dresser built for the first assembly, not for the third. Swelling at the sink is the domestic version of the same physics: particleboard drinks, and once it has drunk, it does not return to the dimension the cam was cut for. The corner goes pale, then furry, then soft. People keep using the drawers because the drawers still hold shirts. Holding shirts is not the same as being a case.

Solid hardwood is not a hymn. It is a set of operations you can still do. You can resand a top. You can re-glue a broken rail. You can replace a runner. You can hate the stain and change it. You can inherit the chest and not inherit the year it was stained. None of that makes the owner virtuous. It makes the object available to a second life. A lot of old solid pieces are ugly, under-scaled, or repaired badly. Keep them or don’t. The option is the point. The particleboard dresser in the listing does not offer the option. It offers a first impression and a weight that surprises you when you try to be careful with it. The weight is glue and crumb. It is not a promise.

Status has attached itself to both materials, in different decades, which is worth saying without a sneer. In 1974 a carved oak suite was status. Some of those suites were oak. Some were oak-colored board. In 2004 a granite kitchen with “cherry” cabinets was status, and a lot of the cherry was a print. In 2023 a quiet oak table is status, and the table may be oak or a photograph of oak. Buyers are not the villains of this essay. Buyers are busy. The villain, when there is one, is a caption that says wood and means paper. IKEA, at its clearer moments, has been more honest about the board than the mall brands that wrapped the same board in a heritage typeface. Honesty is a catalog choice. It is also a hinge.

The hangover is the trash-day object I keep seeing in the same hour as a 1970s table nobody wants to carry. Paper lifted at the drawer edge, white tooth of the core, a cam on the sidewalk. Next to it, sometimes, the oak table. Carry the table. Leave the tooth. The street is not sentimental. It is sentimental about the possibility of a second use. Particleboard that has swollen has used its life. Solid wood that is ugly has not.

Bradley Brand Furniture, in Warren, Arkansas, prints “no particleboard” on its own product copy. That is a contrast, not a hymn. The same family of copy dates Bradley Lumber Co. to 1902; treat the date as company copy until a primary record sits beside it [CITE NEEDED]. A shop that refuses the board is refusing a set of operations it cannot reverse later: the cam, the paper, the swell. It is not refusing cheap furniture as a class, and it should not be asked to sneer at the student who needs the carton. The student is right about the carton. The carton is wrong about the word oak.

What ages is the piece you can get back into: a solid top, a joint that is a joint, a skin that is wood if it claims to be wood. What dates is the photograph used as a species. Grain that repeats every eighteen inches. An edge band that peels. A finish that is the print. Fashion will keep needing that photograph, because the photograph is how a new year gets on a truck at a price a new year can stand. A hardwood shop will keep making a top. You can buy from both without lying to yourself if you know which purchase is a while and which is a chest. A while is allowed. A house that is only whiles is a sequence of cartons that you happen to sleep between.

Himmelheber’s board did a job. The job was reconstruction, then plenty, then a philosophy of leaving. Those are real jobs. The job it cannot do is pretend, after the paper tears, that it was a tree you could meet again with sandpaper. The oak chest on the other wall does not need that pretense. It needs wax, or a plane, or to be left alone. Leave it alone if you want. The option is the furniture.
