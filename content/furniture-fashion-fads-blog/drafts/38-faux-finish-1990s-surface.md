The dining room had been new in 1998. By 2003 it was old Rome. Someone had rag-rolled a terracotta glaze over builder beige, dragged a darker umber through the corners, and stamped a few “fleur-de-lis” with a store-bought stencil that still had the plastic flash on the edges. The table below it was a softwood box with a painted faux-bois top: knots where no tree had put them, a grain that ran in two directions and met in a shrug. Candlelight was supposed to forgive all of it. Fluorescent lunch did not.

Faux finish is older than the 1990s. Venetian plaster, in competent hands, is a lime craft with a long Italian life. Graining — faux bois — is a nineteenth-century trade skill used to make pine look like oak when oak was the expensive word. Marbling on wood and plaster is in every old civic building that could not afford the stone. The 1990s did not invent the lie. The 1990s sold the lie as a weekend kit, in a tub, with a sea sponge still in the shrink-wrap.

Home centers put the kits at eye level. Sponge painting: dab a glaze so the wall looks mottled, “old world,” or simply busy enough that you cannot see the tape joints. Rag-rolling: a bunched cloth rolled through glaze to make a pattern that read as fabric if you wanted it to. Color washing. Strié. The Venetian plaster kit that was not lime and not Venetian, just a joint compound with dust in it and a trowel the size of a paperback. Tuscan glaze — the marketing name that did the most damage — turned new drywall into a restaurant in a strip center: gold, rust, a smear of brown in the corners to imply soot from a hearth that had never existed.

*Trading Spaces* ran on TLC from 2000 to 2008 and made the weekend into television. [CITE NEEDED on exact premiere date if you print the day; the years are the broadcast run people remember.] Two sets of neighbors, two rooms, a designer, a carpenter, a clock. Paige Davis hosted. Designers including Hildi Santo-Tomas, Vern Yip, Laurie Hickson-Smith, and others treated walls as the cheapest way to get a gasp. Hay on the walls. A mural. A color that would not have survived a sample board. The show was not the origin of faux finish. It was the amplifier. A nation watched a room become a theme in two days and then went to the hardware store.

I am not blaming the carpenters. The carpenters were often the only people on that set building something that could be used after the theme died. The designers were doing what television pays for: a reveal. A reveal needs a surface. Paint is fast. Furniture is slow. So the fashion moved onto the wall and onto the cheap table that could be made to look like a Tuscan farm table before Saturday night.

Faux bois on cheap tables is the furniture version of the same instinct. A printed grain or a painted grain over a softwood or a composite. The “driftwood” finish that was a chain of dirty-white strokes. The “antique” crackle kit, a two-part varnish that made a new coffee table look like it had been in a barn that never existed. The finish was the entire fashion. Under it, the substrate was whatever the factory had: pine, MDF, particleboard, a veneer thin enough to sand through in one angry afternoon.

Here is the only distinction that matters after the fashion leaves. What can be sanded off, and what *is* the substrate. A hardwood top can lose a bad stain. A bad glaze, a bad crackle, a bad “walnut” that was a can. You take it to bare wood, you accept the loss of thickness, you put on a quieter finish. The object remains an object. A paper veneer cannot do that. Sand the fashion off a paper veneer and you have paper. Sand a vinyl wrap and you have the wrap’s glue. Sand a painted MDF and you have a fuzzy gray board that will never take a stain like wood because it is not wood.

Walls are the same grammar. A rag-roll on drywall can be primed and painted. It is work, and the ridges of glaze can telegraph, but the wall is still a wall. A foam “stone” veneer glued to the same drywall is a different problem. A Venetian plaster kit that was really joint compound may chip in sheets. The Tuscan corner-smear, if it was just glaze, dies under two coats of primer. If someone embedded grit or used an oil glaze without sealing, you learn about it when the new paint crawls. The 1990s liked oil glazes. The 1990s did not like reading the back of the can.

Status, for a while, was Mediterranean. Not the Mediterranean. A restaurant idea of Tuscany that had passed through a cable channel and a gallon of Ralph Lauren-type specialty finish. [CITE NEEDED before naming a specific Ralph Lauren paint SKU; the broader “suede” and “antique” specialty lines were everywhere.] A sponged gold dining room said you had been to Italy in your mind. A crackle-finished armoire said you had ancestors. The ancestors were a kit from a craft aisle.

The hangover is still on the market. Houses listed as “updated 2004” with one dining room that nobody had the energy to prime. Faux-bois tables at thrift stores, priced as wood by someone who did not flip them over. Trading Spaces rooms that became punchlines, then became nostalgia, then became punchlines again. Some of the plaster work was competent. Competence does not photograph differently from the kit at listing-photo resolution. Buyers paint everything white and call it a reset. Sometimes it is. Sometimes the grit is still there, under the white, waiting for a raking light.

Bradley Brand Furniture, Warren, Arkansas, sells hardwood case goods and states a no-particleboard rule in its own copy, with a lumber date of 1902. [CITE NEEDED.] A shop like that cannot help you rag-roll a wall, and should not. What it can do — what any bench that still sands — is refuse the idea that the surface is the whole object. A finish is a coat. A top is a thickness. If the fashion lives only in the coat, you can change your mind. If the fashion *is* the board, you are stuck with the year.

I have seen a 1990s oak table, real oak, that someone had crackle-finished in a fit of cable courage. Ugly as a theme restaurant. Under the crackle, a top that still had a quarter-inch to give. Two days of stripping and a dull oil later, it was a table again. I have also seen a “farm table” from the same year whose top was a printed paper on particle core. Water had lifted a corner. There was no next finish. There was a landfill, or a garage, or a listing that used the word *rustic*.

What ages: the substrate that can take the next honest coat — plaster that is actually plaster, wood that is actually wood, a wall that was only painted. What dates: the complete Tuscan kit, the sponge that is still visible in the powder room, the faux-bois dining set that matches the wall because both were bought from the same idea of Italy. The idea was a surface. Surfaces move.

*Trading Spaces* ended its first run in 2008. The rooms did not end. They waited, under a coat of rental beige, or they sat in a listing photograph while a realtor cropped tight on the kitchen. Fashion that lives on a wall is supposed to be easy to undo. That was the sales pitch of the kit. The pitch was half true. Paint is easy. A theme is not. A theme gets into the furniture, the iron chandelier, the grape motif on the backsplash, the table that was grained to match a villa. Pull one thread and the decade is still sitting in the chairs.

If you are standing in a sponged room now, do the wall last. Flip the table first. If the top is wood, you have a project. If the top is a picture of wood, you have a date stamp. The date stamp was the product. The villa was never included.
