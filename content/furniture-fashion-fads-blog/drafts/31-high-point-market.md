High Point, North Carolina, is a small city that becomes, for two weeks a year, the place a sofa arm is chosen for the rest of the country. April and October. Buyers from Rooms To Go and from a one-room boutique in a town you have not heard of walk the same downtown, sit on the same samples, and decide, in a kind of wholesale weather, whether next season’s arm is a track, a roll, a shelter, or a kidney. The public does not get in. The public gets the result six months later, in a showroom that will swear the arm was inevitable.

The town’s name is a railroad fact. High Point was the highest point on the North Carolina Railroad when the place was established in 1859, according to the city’s own furniture-history page. After the Civil War, southern factories grew on timber and on labor leaving the farms. High Point Furniture Manufacturing Company’s first factory is commonly dated to 1889 [CITE NEEDED — confirm against a primary business record]. Around it, a cluster: Thomasville, Lexington, Lenoir, the Broyhill country. The city now styles itself the Furniture Capital; the title is marketing that happens to sit on a real pile of mills. The same municipal page claims that after the Second World War, an estimated 60 percent of American furniture was made within a 150-mile radius of High Point — the city’s figure, not mine. Joe Exum Brown’s list of companies that operated in High Point and nearby, compiled through 2003 and posted by the city, is the sober version of the boast — page after page of names, many of them gone.

What the mills made, for a long time, was the middle of the American house: oak suites, cherry suites, the bedroom that matched, the dining room that matched. Market week was how those suites found a buyer in Ohio or Texas without the buyer touring every factory. A retailer walked a building, sat, wrote an order, and went home to wait for a truck. That is still the skeleton. The flesh changed when manufacturing moved and the showrooms stayed. High Point became less a factory town on those two weeks and more a stage set for factories that might be in Vietnam, Malaysia, or a remaining North Carolina plant with a smaller payroll.

The Market itself began as a local showing. NCpedia’s entry on the International Home Furnishings Market traces a 1909 exhibition by manufacturers in High Point and neighboring towns, two buildings, buyers from other states. In June 1921 the ten-story Southern Furniture Exposition Building opened for two shows a year. Grand Rapids, Chicago, and New York still held the larger markets then. As southern factories moved up in finish and as travel got easier, the buyers came south. Showrooms multiplied, first at factories, then in the old downtown, then in a complex that after 1988 went under the name International Home Furnishings Center and spilled into building after building. I will not put an attendance figure here. Organizers and encyclopedias publish them; they move. Temporary architecture is part of the taste. Permanent showrooms occupy old stores, purpose-built towers, and floors that exist to be Market. Between Markets some of those floors go dark. During Market they sprout carpets, vignettes, espresso, and a sofa no one will sit on except professionally. Other exhibitors take tents, parking structures, and pop-up rooms that will be plywood again in ten days. You can walk out of a marble lobby into a hallway of raw drywall and then into a perfect living room that will be gone before the month is. The temporary room is a sales argument. It has no obligation to a real window or a real child. That is why Market interiors look more like magazines than houses do. They are magazines you can sit in.

Thomasville, Lexington, Broyhill — the names a person of a certain age still uses as shorthand for “furniture” — were factory towns before they were logos. Thomasville Furniture’s story is a long North Carolina manufacturing line that later became a licensed brand after corporate weather I will not pretend to narrate without a filing [CITE NEEDED]. Lexington, out of Thomasville, spent a century learning to put a lifestyle name on a casegood. Broyhill, founded in 1926 in Lenoir, made the middle-class oak that still turns up in the entertainment-center essay and the sleigh-bed essay. Market is where those factories, and the Asian factories that replaced a lot of them, still go to be seen by the people who write the orders.

Buyers are the hidden authors of your living room. A Rooms To Go buyer who likes a track arm and a performance fabric will put that combination in a hundred stores. A boutique buyer who likes a skirted roll will put it in one store and a window. The consensus is not a conspiracy. It is a week of sore feet and a fear of being the shop that missed the arm. When enough orders land on a shape, the shape becomes “what people want,” which is a polite way of saying what buyers already bought. Fashion magazines pretend the desire starts in the house. Sometimes it starts in a High Point hallway with a badge on a lanyard.

Taste as wholesale consensus has a cost and a use. The use is that a small retailer in Arkansas or Oregon can see, in person, what the factories are making, instead of buying blind from a photo. The cost is sameness with a six-month delay. You can date a suburban living room by the arm the Market liked two seasons before the sofa was delivered. The kidney pillow. The tuxedo. The English roll. The wide track. The performance linen that is not linen. None of those is wicked. They are a weather system. If you want a room that does not look like a weather system, you buy an older piece, or you buy from a shop that does not change its arm because a hallway in North Carolina changed its mind.

Bradley Brand Furniture sits off that weather in a useful way. Warren, Arkansas, is not High Point. The company’s copy — Bradley Lumber 1902, hardwood, no particleboard — describes a factory that sells what it builds, not a Market storyboard. I am not putting their catalog on a Market aisle they may or may not walk. The contrast is the point of the series: a solid-oak object that does not need a new arm every April versus a sofa whose identity is the arm. Both kinds of furniture have buyers. Only one kind is trying to be next season before this season has been sat in.

The hangover of Market, for the rest of us, is the leftover consensus. Sofas that were last year’s certainty, already marked down, already slightly wrong in the arm. Dining chairs with the splat the hallways liked. A gray-wash casegood that every chain bought and every chain is now done with. High Point is not to blame for your room. High Point is where your room was previewed, professionally, by people whose job is to guess you. Sometimes they guess well. Sometimes they guess a waterfall island and a cognac Chesterfield in the same year and the houses of that year look like a committee.

Walk downtown on a non-Market week and the showroom buildings are just buildings. That is the tell. The architecture is waiting for a temporary use. So is a lot of the furniture it sells. The piece worth keeping is the one that would still make sense if the Market never opened again: a hardwood table, a chair with an arm you like because you like it, a sofa whose shape is a decision rather than a briefing. The rest can be fashion. Fashion is how rooms get born. Just know where the arm was born, and that it was born on a badge, in a town named for a railroad grade, twice a year, whether you were in the building or not.
