The coyote is howling on a kitchen wall in Ohio. He is black, cut from sheet metal or printed on a plaque, head tipped back, a little bandanna implied if not painted. Under him: a bleached-pine dinette, terracotta tile glued to a tabletop that is not stone, and a runner with a stepped diamond that wants you to think of a chief’s blanket. The house has vinyl siding. There is no mesa. This is the 1980s Southwestern boom as it actually arrived — not in Santa Fe, but in a subdivision that had never seen a viga until a decorator ordered one in foam and whitewash.

Santa Fe had a real boom. The city’s adobe and pueblo revival architecture, the galleries on Canyon Road, the tourist trade that thickened in the 1970s and 1980s — that is a place with a climate and a history. What furniture stores sold as “Southwestern” was a climate of the mind. Bleached pine. Washed finishes that made new wood look sunstruck. Terracotta, or a glaze that impersonated it, on cocktail tables and lamp bases. Pastel Navajo-ish patterns on upholstery and area rugs: pink, turquoise, sand, a geometric that had been through the mill and come out mall.

The last point needs a hard line. Diné weaving — Navajo weaving, if you use the older English name and mean the same people — is not a fad. It is a textile tradition with named regions, named weavers, wool, and a market that has been distorted by traders and tourists for more than a century. A Two Grey Hills rug is not a throw pillow from Pier 1. A Germantown eye-dazzler from the late nineteenth century is not a pastel diamond printed on olefin. This essay is about the printed cousin: the pattern as atmosphere, sold by the yard, unconnected to a loom in the Navajo Nation. Flattening Native work into a 1986 color story is how the fad thought. It is not how the work should be written.

Ralph Lauren understood the difference between a place and a collection, and sold the collection. In 1981 he put a Santa Fe line on the runway: Native-inspired prints, frontier clothes, prairie dust in a New York show (Vanity Fair’s Lauren chronology, 2012). In 1983 Ralph Lauren Home opened with Log Cabin, Thoroughbred, New England, and Jamaica — American rooms as wardrobes (company history; October 1983 home catalog). Southwestern and desert motifs kept returning in apparel and, later, in home lines that wore names like Desert Modern [CITE NEEDED on the first year a Desert-titled furniture suite shipped]. Canyon Road, much later, made the West a Milan installation. None of this is ethnography. It is fashion. Fashion is allowed to borrow a sky. It is not allowed to call the borrowing a people.

The mall did not bother with Lauren’s restraint. Pier 1 Imports, the furniture wings of department stores, the “Santa Fe” boutique in a strip center: cactus lamps with ceramic arms, howling coyotes in every register from cast iron to refrigerator magnet, whitewashed beams stuck to a flat ceiling in Plano or Indianapolis. Vigas — the round roof beams of adobe construction — are structural in New Mexico. In a 1988 great room in a non-desert suburb they were half-round foam or pine, painted chalk, nailed up as jewelry. The room said ranch. The HVAC said Ohio.

Kitsch has a face here, and the face is the coyote. The howling coyote silhouette became a Southwestern hieroglyph the way the flamingo became Florida: instantly readable, cheap to stamp, slightly comic even when the owner was earnest. I will not invent an artist of record for the first yard-art coyote; the motif spread faster than attribution [CITE NEEDED for a first commercial designer]. What matters is saturation. Once the coyote is on the mailbox, the switch plate, and the toilet-tank cover, the furniture has to keep up. Bleached pine armoires with punched-tin panels. Chairs with ladder backs and a “distressed” wash in peach or aqua. A sofa in a pastel blanket stripe that pills in a year. The terracotta-tile table — tiles set in a wood frame, grout that darkens, edges that chip — was the coffee-table version of a hacienda floor, scaled for a wall-to-wall carpet.

Status was vacation and earthiness at once. You were not the brass-and-glass person anymore. You were not the Mediterranean-oak person. You had discovered the West, or the catalog had discovered it for you, and the West in 1985 meant texture: rough wood, clay, a textile that looked handmade if you squinted. It was a moral upgrade that required no politics. Adobe-colored walls, a kiva fireplace that never saw a pueblo, a pot that looked like Acoma until you turned it over and found a sticker from a gift shop in Albuquerque Airport — or, more often, from a factory that had never been to New Mexico. The owner got to feel closer to land. The land was a finish.

Retail had a ladder. At the top: actual Navajo textiles, Spanish colonial furniture from New Mexican makers, the kind of carved pine chest that a Santa Fe dealer would stand behind. In the middle: Lauren, and the better department-store interpretations — solid pine, real leather, a blanket that at least was wool. At the bottom: the pastel suite, the foam viga, the cactus lamp whose “pottery” is slip-cast and whose shade is a parchment printed with Kokopelli. Kokopelli, the humpbacked flute player, got the same treatment as the coyote. A figure with a long ceremonial and commercial history became a drawer pull. That is not appreciation. That is clip art.

The 1990s did not kill the look so much as bleach it further, then walk away. By the time Tuscan kitchens and buttery yellow walls took the magazine covers, Southwestern pastel already looked like a time stamp. The pine went from sun-bleached to just cheap. The punched tin dented. The tile tables lost grout in the dishwasher era, when nobody wanted a table you could not wipe with one rag. Whitewashed beams, once a romance, read as a theme restaurant. People painted the pine dark or white, depending on which recovery they were in. Some of the better solid-pine pieces survived that paint and can be stripped. The pastel stain on a soft wood is harder to undo than the catalog promised. Bleach and pigment go deep when the mill wants them to.

The hangover is standing in a Habitat for Humanity ReStore, usually along the back wall with the other case goods that did not sell online. A pastel pine armoire. Peach or washed turquoise, maybe a sun motif routed into a door, maybe tin that has oxidized into a color the designer did not choose. It is too big to ignore and too dated to place. Someone will buy it for a cabin, or for a kid’s room, or to paint navy. The coyote plaques go in a different bin. They have become joke objects, which is a kind of honesty the armoire has not earned yet.

What dates is the costume applied to a room that is not in the desert: the fake viga, the pastel Navajo-ish print, the coyote as punctuation. What ages is more stubborn and less theatrical. A solid pine or hardwood chest that can take a new finish. A wool blanket that was woven as a blanket, not printed as a vibe — and if it is Diné work, it ages as art and as utility, not as a fad, and it should be named that way. A terracotta floor tile that is actually fired clay, set in a house that can carry the weight, will outlast the tile-top table. The table was always a souvenir.

Bradley Brand Furniture, in Warren, Arkansas, does not sell a Santa Fe collection. The company’s own copy still points at Bradley Lumber, 1902, and at solid hardwood without particleboard. That is useful here only as a contrast in material, not as a Western fantasy. Bleached pine from a 1987 suite and a hardwood case built to be refinished are both wood until you ask them to live a second life. One of them was stained to look like weather. The other can take weather, or a darker stain, or a repair at the corner where the kid hit it with a tricycle. A punched-tin panel on MDF is a picture of the Southwest. A hardwood door is a door.

I have stood under more fake vigas than real ones. The fake ones are lighter. That is the tell, and it is also the joke. You can have a desert in a climate-controlled mall, but you cannot have the weight. The 1980s Southwestern room wanted the weight of adobe and the ease of a credit application. It got a wash, a silhouette, and a pattern that had been through so many printers it no longer pointed at a weaver.

If you are keeping one object from that binge, keep the one that is wood all the way through and has no coyote on it. Let the Diné textile, if you are lucky enough to own one, sit outside this essay’s fad entirely. It was never asking to be a dinette. The pastel pine armoire can go to the Habitat store. It has already done the work of telling a decade. The decade is over. The howl, on the Ohio kitchen wall, can come down whenever you are ready to hear the quiet.
