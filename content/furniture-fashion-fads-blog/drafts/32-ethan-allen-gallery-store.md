Ethan Allen’s real product was never a single Windsor chair. It was a room that arrived already decided: the table, the sideboard, the two armchairs, the finish that matched, and a person who would come to your house and tell you the decision was yours. Period reproduction as a product line. The in-home designer. The gallery store. For a long middle of the twentieth century, that package was how a certain American household learned what “colonial” looked like, and later what “formal” looked like, and later still what the company thought modern looked like. Other factories sold furniture. Ethan Allen sold a finished sentence.

The founding is 1932, and the founders are two New Yorkers, not a Vermont patriot. Theodore Baumritter and his brother-in-law Nathan S. Ancell started a housewares sales agency — plaster gnomes, trellises, garden swings, other people’s goods. The *New York Times* obituary of Baumritter (9 August 1994) has them buying a bankrupt furniture factory in Beecher Falls, Vermont, three years later; the company’s own timeline says 1935 and a $25,000 sawmill; other accounts say 1936. [CITE NEEDED on the year — 1935 versus 1936.] Beecher Falls is a place. “Beecher Hall” is a slip that turns a mill town into a man. In 1939 they put a line of early American furniture under the name Ethan Allen, after the Green Mountain Boys’ leader, and they were particular about who could sell it. Encyclopedia.com, drawing on a 1965 *Home Furnishings Daily* piece, describes a group of twenty-eight pieces shown first to selected buyers who would display the line properly. Exclusive franchise, coordinated floor. The gallery idea is already there, before the word.

Vermont mattered as a story. Ancell, in the *Times* obit, is remembered saying they “fell hopelessly in love” with the state and with what New England represented. That love became a brand: maple, maple, and more maple; tavern tables; pencil-post beds; a finish that said museum without requiring a museum. Headquarters later moved to Danbury, Connecticut (1972, in the standard corporate histories), and the Baumritter Corporation took the furniture’s name. By the late 1970s the company *was* Ethan Allen. Farooq Kathwari’s later leadership pushed the brand through the decades when colonial stopped being the only American sentence. The through-line is not a wood. It is the idea that the store is a set of rooms, and that you leave with a plan.

The gallery store made that idea walkable. Not a warehouse of sofas. A sequence of staged interiors: dining, living, bedroom, each in a finish family, each implying that buying one piece without the others was a little incomplete. You did not browse a rack. You walked through someone else’s finished life, then another, then another, until your own house started to look underdressed. The in-home designer — a title that covered a range of training — brought fabric swatches and a tape measure into the actual house, which was both a service and a closing technique. People who would have been afraid of a decorator could say they had an Ethan Allen designer. The fear went down. The ticket went up. I have been in those rooms. The good designers listened and saved people from a suite they did not have space for. The lesser ones sold the suite anyway, because the suite was the product.

Franchise discipline made the rooms repeatable. A gallery in a mall suburb and a gallery on a commercial strip were supposed to teach the same period. That is how a brand becomes a house style for a class: not one masterpiece, but a hundred rooms that agree. Competitors noticed. The local furniture store that had sold mixed makers started grouping “collections.” The department store built a gallery inside itself and called it a store. Ethan Allen did not invent coordination. It industrialized the feeling that coordination was what grown people bought.

The 1970s through the 1990s were the colonial and formal high-water years for a lot of American houses, and Ethan Allen was one of the teachers. Cherry and maple dining rooms. Chippendale-ish chairs that were not Chippendale. A sofa with a skirt. A secretary desk. Brass. The look said: we are settled. It also said: we bought a period as a package, not as an attic. There is a difference between inheriting a real eighteenth-century form and buying a well-made twentieth-century memory of one. Ethan Allen specialized in the memory, at a quality that was often higher than the discount colonial coming out of the same southern factories. The furniture was not a joke. The coordination was the fashion.

Later the brand had to modernize or become a time capsule. The galleries took in cleaner lines, paler woods, upholstery that could sit next to a flat-screen. Kathwari’s Ethan Allen advertised a more current house while keeping the designer and the room-as-package. Some of that modernization was graceful. Some of it was a colonial company wearing a modern jacket, the way a person wears a jacket to a meeting. Customers who wanted the old maple could still find it, or something like it. Customers who wanted to not look like their parents could be walked toward a different vignette in the same building. The building was the constant.

What the brand taught, more lastingly than any splat or finial, is that rooms come in coordinated packages. That lesson escaped the gallery. Pottery Barn learned it. Restoration Hardware learned it. Rooms To Go industrialized it. IKEA, in a different class, taught it with a catalog (see the next essay). Once you have learned that a room is a set, it is hard to unlearn. You start to see a lone table as unfinished. You start to want the matching nightstands. Matching is a comfort. It is also how a house starts to look like a floor display, including the parts of the floor display that will date together — all of them, on the same morning.

Bradley Brand Furniture, Warren, Arkansas, sells a different sentence. Company copy points at Bradley Lumber 1902 and at hardwood without particleboard. A solid-oak table from a shop like that can sit with chairs that do not match it, in a room that was not designed on a gallery floor. That is not a moral victory. It is a different retail structure: object, not package. Ethan Allen’s structure was the package, and it gave a lot of households a coherent room they could not have designed alone. Credit that. Then notice the hangover. Whole dining rooms in the same fading cherry. Living rooms in which every piece is “Ethan Allen” the way a suburb is a developer’s name. When the period goes out, it goes out as a set.

Resale is full of the brand, which is a kind of respect. The older maple still looks like furniture. The formal cherry of the 1980s looks like the 1980s. The transitional modern of the 2000s looks like a company changing clothes. Buy the piece, not the room, unless you still want the room. A gallery can be a good teacher and a poor roommate. The teacher says: consider the whole. The poor roommate says: you may not have a chair from somewhere else. A grown house needs the first lesson and should refuse the second.

The patriot on the label never ran the mill. Two housewares men in the Depression did, then a Vermont factory, then a Connecticut headquarters, then a chain of rooms that taught the country to buy a period as if it were a product line. That is a real achievement in American retail. It is also why so many American dining rooms look like they were purchased in an afternoon, correctly, and then frozen. If you are in a gallery now, take the designer’s tape measure. Leave with one thing you will still want when the rest of the package has become someone else’s period.
