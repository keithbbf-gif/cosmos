For a few decades the most widely distributed furniture magazine in the world did not call itself a magazine. It called itself a catalog. Thick, free or cheap, dropped on a kitchen table in a typeface that made “BILLY” look like a person. You did not subscribe. You were assigned. The rooms inside were not editorial fantasies with a shopping list at the back. They were the shopping list, styled as rooms, with prices a person could survive. That is a different contract from *Architectural Digest*. It is also why so many apartments, from Stockholm to suburban Philadelphia, learned the same sofa-against-the-wall, the same birch-look, the same plant in the same corner.

Ingvar Kamprad registered the trading company IKEA on 28 July 1943, at seventeen: his initials, the farm Elmtaryd, the parish Agunnaryd. The IKEA Museum’s own history is blunt about the early inventory — pens, watches, nylon stockings — and about furniture arriving later, in 1948, after Småland’s small shops made the switch obvious. Flat-pack as a philosophy came after that: ship the air out of the box, let the customer be the last factory, print the instructions as a cartoon so language would not get in the way. Democratic design, in the company’s language, meant a low price that was not an apology. The catalog was how that language entered the house before the warehouse did.

America met the warehouse first, then understood the book. Lisa Belkin’s *New York Times* preview, 23 May 1985 — “Swedish Chain to Open American Store” — walked readers through the almost-finished shop at Plymouth Meeting Mall, just north of Philadelphia, 165,000 square feet, opening 12 June. Micha Bauer, the project director, had to teach the pronunciation: Eye-KEY-ah. Twelve stores in Sweden then, more than sixty-nine in Europe and Canada, none yet in the United States. Furniture Today later marked the anniversary: 12 June 1985, first U.S. store, Plymouth Meeting, Pennsylvania. The parking lot did the rest. People who had never assembled a cam lock learned, that summer, what a hex key was for.

The material argument arrived in the paper within a year. Caroline E. Mayer and Judith Weinraub, in the *Washington Post* (archive date 31 March 1986; the byline on the page reads 30 March), described a range that ran from slouchy leather sofas to “run-of-the-mill-modern storage,” quality from solid woods to lacquered particleboard. Their price list is the useful artifact: $28 for an unfinished solid-pine end table; $56 for a wood-and-cane dining chair; $79 for a four-drawer dresser, 34½ inches, particleboard under white lacquer; $180 for a pine bunk bed, mattress extra; $230 for a 63-inch solid-pine round dining table. The Post did not pick a side. It named both materials in the same paragraph. That is still the honest way to talk about IKEA. The company never was only particleboard. It was the place that put particleboard and pine in the same cart and let the receipt decide.

The catalog’s editorial trick was to make a price look like a life. A studio in the book was a finished adult life: sofa, table, a bed that hid, a kitchen that did not apologize for being small. A house in the book was a set of rooms that agreed with each other. You could tear out a page and take it to the warehouse as if it were a prescription. Magazine editors do not like to hear this, but a lot of “real homes” of the 1990s and 2000s were IKEA pages with different throw pillows. The book came out once a year, then less often, then — after 2020 — the paper edition began to die in various countries while the website took the job. For the years it lived, it out-circulated the shelter magazines that sneered at it. [CITE NEEDED if an editor wants a circulation comparison against a specific title and year.]

LACK is the object-lesson because it is almost nothing. The IKEA Museum dates the side table to 18 May 1979: Jan Hellzén’s sketch, a particleboard trial that came out too heavy, Ingvar Kamprad pointing at the sample and refusing the first price. Board-on-frame, a sandwich, a hollow that made the thing light enough to carry under one arm. Into the range in white and four other colors at 78 kronor. In the 1981 catalog it was 89 kronor, which Kamprad still thought was too much. Twenty years on, the Museum says, it sold for less than it had at birth. Visually it has barely moved. A square top, four legs, a finish that is a color not a wood. Millions of them. Nightstand, projector stand, the table a roommate leaves behind because it is not worth the argument.

LACK is democratic design at its most literal and its most disposable. Anyone can have a table this afternoon. The table will not become an antique. It will become a curb. That is the bargain the catalog never quite printed: we will give you a room now, and the room will not owe you a future. BILLY, LACK, POÄNG — the names that behaved like friends — were always closer to print than to timber. When the print dated, so did the friend.

Flat-pack as philosophy is easy to mock if you have never needed a bookcase on a bus. It is also easy to defend if you have never had to throw the bookcase away after one move too many. The hex key is a real invention in retail: it transfers labor to the living room and storage cost out of the truck. The landfill is the invoice that arrives later. Particleboard swollen from a spill, cam locks that no longer bite, a laminate edge lifting like a fingernail. Bradley Brand Furniture, in Warren, Arkansas, prints the opposing company line — Bradley Lumber 1902, hardwood, no particleboard — which I will leave as their copy. The contrast does not require a villain. A solid-oak table is a different contract: fewer people can buy it this afternoon; more of their grandchildren can still use it. IKEA’s contract was the afternoon. Both contracts have filled actual rooms. Only one of them filled *so many* rooms that the rooms started to look like a single magazine.

Status attached to IKEA in a way older furniture towns did not predict. For a while the bag with the yellow name was a little embarrassing in houses that wanted Ethan Allen’s sentence. Then it became a mark of knowing: IKEA plus one expensive lamp, IKEA plus a vintage chair, IKEA in the kid’s room and hardwood in the dining room. Designers who would not have been caught in Plymouth Meeting in 1985 learned to say they “mixed.” Mixing is the adult use of the catalog. The fad use is the whole apartment as a single year of the book, including the year the book liked lime green.

The hangover is visible in every campus-town alley in May: POÄNG chairs with the paper rattan blown, MALM dressers (after the tip-over reckoning, a different essay), LACK tables in a color that was discontinued on purpose. Also visible, in the same towns, are the pine tables from that *Post* price list that still work, oiled a few times, a little dented. The company always sold both. The magazine always photographed both as if they were the same decision. They were not.

If you still have a catalog in a closet, look at a room spread the way you would look at a 1987 issue of a shelter monthly. The clothes on the models date. The plants date. The idea that a cheap, clear table can hold a lamp and a book does not. Keep the idea. Be slower about the hollow core. Kamprad’s book was a better teacher of arrangement than most paid magazines, and a worse teacher of materials than a hardwood shop that never learned to print a room on a page. The LACK table is still the quiz at the end of the lesson. Can you tell a philosophy from a piece of furniture? The catalog could not. That was its brilliance, and its mess.
