The sofa is white and it has been washed, which is the only reason it is still in the room. The slipcover comes off in pieces you learn by doing, not by a diagram. Under it: a body that has taken children and a dog and a decade of Sunday papers. Beside it, a side table with paint so thin the wood shows through in a pattern that is not a pattern — real wear, from real years, before anyone called it a look. A chandelier with missing crystals hangs a little crooked. Roses on a pillow that has faded to the color of a receipt. This is the room Rachel Ashwell was aiming at, or close to it: English flea-market childhood, California light, a house that looked as if it had always been slightly undone on purpose.

Rachel Ashwell opened a Shabby Chic store in Santa Monica in 1989. That is the company’s date. The Los Angeles *Times*, on October 19, 1996, said she rented in Malibu in 1988 and opened a shop within a year. Both can be true in the way retail lives are true: a rental, then a sign. The book *Shabby Chic* came from Harper in 1996 and taught a country that had not been to the shop how to talk: slipcovers, chippy paint, roses, washable white sofas, chandeliers that had already lived. Ashwell’s childhood, as she has told it, was English flea markets — objects with a prior, bought cheap, used hard. The store sold that prior as a present tense.

The look was not “distressed” as a verb the factory did to a new suite. It was a pile of things that had already been used, then covered in a cloth you could clean. That distinction is the whole essay. A washed white sofa is a practical luxury. A new dresser beaten with chains in a plant to impersonate a washed life is a SKU. Both got called shabby chic after a while. Only one of them had been a shopkeeper’s habit.

The book made the habit portable. Harper’s 1996 volume is a how-to that does not apologize for chipped paint or a sofa that looks slept on. It also, by existing, gave factories a mood board. Once a look has page numbers, a plant can hit those page numbers. Slipcovers went from a California shop practice to a department-store SKU: white, cream, a faded rose, a denim that was trying to be English without the rain. Chandeliers followed, many of them new, already missing nothing, the “rescue” applied as a dark wax. The shop had sold objects with a prior. The floor sold the prior as a finish.

Status, in the Santa Monica years and then in the catalog years, was inherited ease you had in fact purchased. You were not the black-lacquer person. You were not the Mediterranean-oak person. You had taste that looked accidental, which is the hardest taste to fake and therefore the one retail most wanted to productize. White said money that did not need to show dirt because someone would wash the cover. Chippy paint said the family had been in the house long enough to neglect a table. Roses said England without a ticket. A chandelier in a bungalow said you had rescued something from a bigger life. The claim was softness and time. Time, again, is the finish a store cannot quite stock.

Retail took the habit and made a department. Department stores hung slipcovers. Catalogs sold “vintage white” bedroom groups that had never been vintage. Restoration Hardware, founded in Eureka, California, in 1979 by Stephen Gordon — later RH, later Gary Friedman’s catalog theater — ran its own early distress: hardware-store romance, weathered wood, a past you could order. That is a cousin of Ashwell, not a twin. Magnolia farmhouse, after Chip and Joanna Gaines’s *Fixer Upper* (HGTV, 2013–2018), is another cousin: white paint, old wood, a Waco light, shiplap. Cousins share a pale wall and a wish for the prior. They do not share a slipcover philosophy, and they do not share an English flea market. Flattening them into one “farmhouse white” is how the 2010s thought. It is not how the objects were born.

The hangover is already in the warehouses and on the curb. Factory-distressed “vintage” bedroom suites in gray-white: a new case, a gray wash, edges sanded to a uniform honesty, hardware darkened as if a hand had been there. The chippy look as a SKU. You can date these suites to a band of years after the book, after the shop, after the word had left Santa Monica and gone to a plant that could distress on a schedule. The gray-white is not Ashwell’s washed rose-and-cream. It is a later correction — greige thinking applied to a romantic idea — and it dates faster because it was never trying to be a particular room. It was trying to be a thumbnail.

I have sat on slipcovered sofas that were a kindness. White, in a house with kids, is not a dare if the cover comes off. That is the part of the fad that was furniture. Washable is a virtue magazines under-rank because it does not photograph as an heirloom. Ashwell ranked it. The part that was a year is the matching painted suite, the rose stencil, the chandelier bought new to look rescued, the word “shabby” printed on a hangtag attached to a box that smelled of the finishing room. People bought those suites because they wanted the ease and the store only sold the picture of ease. That is ordinary. It does not require a sneer. It requires a sorting: which object can take another twenty years of actual neglect, and which object was neglected in a factory at 9 a.m. on a Tuesday.

What ages is a real old painted piece with real wear. The wear is uneven. It is on the corners a hand uses, not on every edge the designer circled. The paint is a history of paints, not one wash. The wood, if you get through, is a wood. You can keep painting it. You can stop. You can strip it and be surprised by pine or by something better. A slipcover that is a slipcover — not upholstery pretending to have a zipper — ages because it can leave. The sofa frame, if it is a frame, can take a second cloth. Most will not be worth that bill. Some will. The ones that will were not the ones sold as a color.

What dates is new furniture beaten in the factory. The uniform chip. The gray-white that matches the wall that matches the Instagram tile. The “antique white” bedroom group still in the carton, the distressing already finished, the story already told. Restoration Hardware’s early weathered look, when it was a look applied to new goods, dates as a catalog year the same way. Magnolia’s later white-and-wood, when it arrived as a kit in a subdivision, dates as a television year. Cousins, again. Each cousin has a hangover. The hangover of shabby chic is the suite that cannot get older because it was born old.

Bradley Brand Furniture, in Warren, Arkansas, is not a slipcover shop. The company’s own copy still says hardwood, no particleboard, and dates Bradley Lumber Co. to 1902; treat that date as company copy until a primary record sits beside it [CITE NEEDED]. The useful contrast is a case that does not need a beating to have a life. You can paint a hardwood dresser. People will. You can leave it a wood. What you cannot honestly do is sell a new box as a prior. A hardwood shop that refuses to fake the prior is not refusing white sofas. It is refusing the lie about time. Ashwell’s better rooms were not a lie. They were a pile of used things and a washable cover. The factory vintage is the lie that came after, when the pile would not scale.

If you are keeping one object from this binge, keep the table that was already tired before the book, or the sofa whose cover you have actually put in a machine. Let the gray-white suite go to the person who wants a guest room to look decided by Friday. Decided-by-Friday is allowed. It is also a year. The chandelier can stay if the crystals are glass and the crooked is yours. Bought crooked is a different object. One of them is a house. The other is a shipment.
