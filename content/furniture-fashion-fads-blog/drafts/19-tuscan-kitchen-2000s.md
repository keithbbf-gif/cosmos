The island is a peninsula of granite with an ogee edge thick enough to look edible. Under the overhang, a carved corbel that never held an arch. The cabinets are “cherry,” a printed grain that repeats every eighteen inches if you know how to look. On the backsplash, tumbled travertine in a mosaic no farmhouse in Tuscany ordered from a big-box aisle. A grape cluster is cast into the oil-rubbed bronze pull. The year is about 2004. The subdivision is new. The kitchen is old-world, which in those years meant wine-country leisure in a house that still smelled of carpet adhesive.

Call the window 1998 to 2008 and you will not be far wrong. That is the decade when HGTV matured into a furniture-and-finish authority and *Trading Spaces* (TLC, premiered 2000) taught a generation that a weekend and a themed room could change a life. Tuscan was not the only theme. It was the one that sold the most granite. Faux plaster — or a sponge-and-glaze stand-in — on walls that were still gypsum. Grape motifs on tile murals, on iron pot racks, on the aforementioned pulls. Oil-rubbed bronze as the hardware religion, replacing the chrome and brass of the decade before. Travertine on the floor if the budget held, ceramic printed as travertine if it did not. Cabinets with a glaze in the corners, a “old world” distress that came from the finishing line, not from a century of hands.

The look was signaling something precise. Not Italy. Leisure. Specifically the leisure of wine country as Americans had learned it from Napa tourism, from *Under the Tuscan Sun* (the 2003 film, after Frances Mayes’s 1996 memoir), from food television that had already made olive oil a personality. A kitchen that looked as if you pressed your own oil was a kitchen that said you had time, even if you were heating a rotisserie chicken from the supermarket. The granite blob — those islands that grew like a geological event, seating four on one side, a sink on the other, a cooktop in the middle — was a banquet table for people who ate standing up. You were supposed to gather. The gathering was the point. The grapes on the hardware were a toast.

*Trading Spaces* and its cousins did not invent glazed cabinets. They democratized the permission to theme a room hard. A Tuscan kitchen in a 2002 episode might have been a weekend of plaster compound and a mural. A Tuscan kitchen in a 2004 spec house was a package: cabinet door style (raised panel, maybe an arch), finish name (*Tuscany*, *Sienna*, *Umber*), granite remnant or slab, bronze faucet with a shepherd’s-crook spout. The builder’s designer had a binder. The binder had a page. The page had a glass of red in a stem that would never live in that cabinet.

A real Tuscan farm table is a different object. Thick hardwood top — walnut, chestnut, oak, whatever the farm had — boards that can be planed when the wine soaks in. Stretchers you can kick. A base that has been knocked apart and reglued by someone who still owns clamps. It is repairable because it was built as a work surface, not as a print of a work surface. The American “Tuscan” dining suite that matched the kitchen was often a cherry-colored film over a veneer over a composite. The carved apron was a mold. The trestle was a silhouette of a trestle. You could not plane it. You could wipe it, and if you wiped too hard you polished a hole in the fiction.

Granite deserves a word that is not worship. It is stone. It is heavy. It does not care about your grapes. In the 2000s it became the proof of a kitchen renovation the way oak cabinetry had been the proof in 1988. Speckled slabs, busy slabs, gold-veined slabs that fought every dish you put on them. The edge profiles got thicker as the years went on, a lip of stone that said custom even when the cabinet box underneath was a standard 24-inch depth from a factory in a state that is not Tuscany. Travertine, softer, pitted, sealed or left to stain, was the floor and the backsplash of the same dream. Both materials can be honest. Both were used as costume jewelry on a cabinet run that would not outlive the mortgage.

Oil-rubbed bronze was the hardware of seriousness. It photographed as old. It hid fingerprints better than polished chrome. It also hid, for a few years, how many identical grape-and-leaf pulls a single manufacturer could ship to a hundred subdivisions. When the fashion moved to polished nickel and then to matte black, the bronze did not get more Tuscan. It got leftover. A lot of it is still leftover, because changing pulls is work and the cabinets are still there, glazed and patient.

The hangover is a kitchen that cannot be updated without a dumpster. Glazed cherry-print doors do not become Shaker by wishing. Granite with a double ogee does not become a soapstone slab without a fabricator and a week of dust. Faux plaster walls yellow in the corners and telegraph every picture hook. The grape mural is a time stamp as clear as a fax machine. People now paint the cabinets white or greige and keep the granite, which is how you get the hybrid rooms of the 2010s: wine-country stone, millennial paint, hardware in a third decade. The room never quite agrees with itself. That disagreement is the hangover.

What dated fastest was the motif. Grapes. Ivy. The word *Tuscany* in a script on a wall plaque. The pot rack as sculpture. What dated next was the glaze — that darker sludge in the panel corners that was supposed to be age and read, by 2014, as a factory effect you could spot from the street. What still works, if the bones are real, is the idea of a kitchen that can take a beating: a hardwood top, a stone that is actually stone, cabinets in solid wood or at least a plywood box with doors you can replace. The style was a signal. The signal faded. The work surface did not have to.

A butcher-block island that can be resanded is the quiet opposite of the printed-cherry suite. Maple, oak, walnut — a thick glued-up top, oil or a finish you can cut back, a few millimeters of wood to give the next owner. You will see knife marks. You should. A farm table and a butcher block share a theology: the top is sacrificial and renewable. Bradley Brand Furniture, Warren, Arkansas, puts that theology in company language that is older than HGTV — Bradley Lumber, 1902, in their copy; solid hardwood; no particleboard. I am not assigning them a Tuscan line they did not build. I am saying a shop that refuses a particle core would have sold you the block, not the blob’s matching cherry-print table. The granite can stay if you like stone. The grape pull can go in a jar.

I have cooked in kitchens that were trying to be Chianti and kitchens that were trying to be nothing but a place to cut an onion. The onion does not care. The person paying the fabricator cares a great deal, which is why the 2000s Tuscan kitchen felt like a promotion: you had arrived at a kind of adult plenty. Wine in the rack. Copper that was not used. A range with more burners than burners used. The room was a restaurant you owned. Then the restaurant style changed, and you still had the booth.

New subdivisions from that decade are now old enough to renovate. The first renovation is almost always the kitchen, and the first decision is whether to keep the granite. Keep it if it is a good slab and you can live with the edge. Replace the doors if the print has started to ghost. Do not keep the mural out of loyalty. Loyalty is for a table that can be repaired.

A real farm table from Tuscany, or from a hardwood shop that never claimed Tuscany, will take another generation of elbows. The printed suite will take another listing photograph and then a curb. That is the whole essay, in oak and in film. The 2000s kitchen wanted old-world. It bought a binder. The thing that ages is the surface you can sand. The thing that dates is the grape you cannot unscrew fast enough.
