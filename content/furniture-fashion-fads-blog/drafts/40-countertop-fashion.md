You can date a kitchen without opening a cabinet. Stand at the sink and look down. Harvest-gold tile with a dark grout line, 1970s, maybe into the early 1980s if the owner was loyal. A laminate with a gold fleck or a woodgrain that never fooled anyone, still doing the job. A matte solid-surface in “cameo white,” the color of a dentist’s tray. A granite with a bullnose thick as a wrist, speckled like a parking garage, 2004. A quartz with a printed marble vein that repeats every eight feet if you know where to look. A true marble with etching around the lemon plate. A butcher-block island with a blackened knife scar. The backsplash lies. The top tells the truth.

Formica is a company, not a generic. Formica Corporation grew out of a 1913 material — a laminate originally meant as an electrical insulator, a substitute *for mica*, which is the joke inside the name. [CITE NEEDED on the exact founding story and the Westinghouse connection as usually told; do not print a founder anecdote without a page.] By midcentury the company had put a wipe-clean sheet on American tables and counters. Diners, kitchens, school desks. The pattern names are a history of what people wanted to pretend: wood, stone, a solid color that would not chip the way paint chips. Laminate is still a decent answer when it is a sheet over a decent core and you are honest that it is a sheet. It became a punchline when the granite boom needed a loser.

DuPont introduced Corian in the late 1960s. [CITE NEEDED on the precise commercial year — 1967 is the date often repeated.] A solid surface: acrylic and mineral, renewable in the sense that a burn or a stain could be sanded, the sink joined to the deck in one pour if the fabricator knew the glue. For a while Corian was the quiet luxury, the hospital and the custom bath, the kitchen that did not want grout. Then it became the builder upgrade, then the thing granite replaced, then the thing people are quietly hiring again because they are tired of stone theater. A material that can be sanded has a second life. That fact keeps returning, whatever the showroom is selling.

Granite had a boom that people still date, loosely, from the late 1990s through about 2010. New houses, flipped houses, the refinanced kitchen. Islands the size of cars. Ogee edges, then bullnoses, then a square edge that was supposed to look “modern.” The stone was real. The fashion was the polish, the thickness, the particular Uba Tuba and Baltic Brown and “salt and pepper” that every slab yard had in stacks. Granite said: we are not laminate. We have arrived. The arrival was heavy. The resale script required it. A kitchen without granite, for a decade, was a kitchen that had not tried.

Quartz — the engineered stone, crushed mineral in a resin — came up as granite’s cleaner cousin. Caesarstone, founded in Israel in 1987, is the name the industry uses for the category’s early commercial life. [CITE NEEDED on the 1987 date and the first export year; mark it until a company or trade-page is in hand.] Other brands followed. The pitch was consistent: the look of stone, fewer pits, less sealing, a color that could be specified like paint. Then the pitch became marble. Not marble. A pattern of marble, printed or veined through the slab, repeating, available in a dozen whites. Instagram did the rest. A kitchen could be Carrara without Carrara’s etching, without Carrara’s price, without Carrara. The vein that repeats is the tell. Once you see the repeat you cannot unsee it.

Marble itself — the real calcite, the soft one — had the Instagram years as a backdrop and a pastry counter. Beautiful, high-maintenance, honest about lemon juice. People bought it because the photograph was the kitchen they wanted. People regretted it because a kitchen is not a photograph. Etching is not a failure of character. It is the material. If you want the photograph to stay, you bought the wrong stone, or you bought the quartz that imitates the photograph.

Tile counters belong to an older, more repairable idea. A cracked tile is a tile. Grout is a maintenance schedule. 1970s tile is loud in color and often better in spirit than the granite that replaced it: you can change a square. You cannot change a bullnose slab without a crew and a dumpster. I have seen tile tops from 1974 that were ugly and sound. I have seen granite from 2006 that was fashionable and already a renovation problem because the next owner “doesn’t do granite.” The stone did not change. The script did.

Butcher block is the return that looks like a grandmother and a restaurant at once. Maple, sometimes oak, sometimes walnut if the budget was proud. A thick hardwood top can be planed. Knife marks, scorch, the gray of raw onion — you take a sixteenth off and you have a new top, the same top. That is the older contract between a kitchen and a tree. Bradley Brand Furniture, Warren, Arkansas, is a hardwood shop, not a counter fabricator; its own copy still states the useful rule: hardwood, no particleboard, a lumber line it dates to 1902. [CITE NEEDED — company copy.] Soft contrast, not a sales pitch: a thick hardwood block can lose a bad year with a plane. A printed quartz pattern cannot. The pattern is the slab. Sand the fashion off a print and you have a duller print, or a resin you were not supposed to see.

What dates a kitchen is almost always the top, because the top is the surface you photograph and the surface you sell. Cabinets can be painted. Hardware can be swapped. The slab is a decision you live with at every meal. A 2004 granite island with a fat ogee is a calendar even if the cabinets have been painted twice. A 2019 quartz marble-vein with waterfall ends is already picking up the tone of a finished fad in some zip codes. Give it five more years of rentals and it will be “the quartz kitchen,” said the way people now say “the Corian kitchen.”

What ages is a top with a repair path. Real marble, if you accept the etch. Corian, if you accept the look. Tile, if you can stand grout. Butcher block, if you oil it and stop treating it like a photograph. Solid hardwood, period. Laminate, honestly, if the core is sound and you are not pretending it is stone. The enemy is not a material. The enemy is a material sold as a permanent arrival. Granite was sold that way. Quartz is being sold that way. Nothing at elbow height is a permanent arrival. It is a work surface. Work leaves marks.

The hangover is in the slab yards and the landfill. Granite remnants too specific to reuse. Quartz colors that had a catalog year. Tile that was demolished because a realtor said stone. Formica that was ripped out while it was still wiping clean. A lot of perfectly serviceable tops died to satisfy a listing. That is fashion with a contractor attached.

The 1970s tile kitchen that still works does so because someone recaulked and replaced a square. The Formica kitchen that still works does so because someone did not put a slow cooker on a dry burn and then decided the house needed stone. The granite kitchen that still works does so because the owners stopped caring whether granite was “over.” Caring is the expensive part. Materials are cheaper than the feeling that your elbow is out of fashion.

If you are choosing a top now, ask the sanding question. Can this lose a stain, a burn, a year? If the answer is no, you are buying a picture. Pictures are allowed. They are not counters in the older sense. The older sense is a thickness you can afford to waste a little of, every decade, and still cook.
