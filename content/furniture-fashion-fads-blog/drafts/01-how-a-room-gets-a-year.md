The dining chairs do not match the table, and that is the first honest thing in the house. The table is oak, heavy, a little thirsty on top where someone once used it as a desk. The chairs are metal, black, the kind a catalog called “industrial” in 2014 and a thrift store now calls “chairs.” Between them sits a decade. You can feel it in the way guests pick a seat: they trust the table and they test the chairs. Furniture fashion is that interval — the time between a purchase that was trying to say something and a leftover that only has to hold a body.

Taste cycles in rooms the way hemlines cycle in photographs, only slower and more expensive to undo. A sofa is not a dress. You cannot bag it up in May. You live with last year’s certainty while this year’s catalog trains your eye to see it as a mistake. That lag is the business. Retail needs the lag. If last year’s sectional still looked finished, this year’s sectional would be optional.

Status is the second clock. A room is a claim about the person who paid for it. Mid-century modern, when it was new, claimed the future: Charles and Ray Eames’s lounge for Herman Miller in 1956, Florence Knoll’s corporate calm, the idea that a house could look like an airport lounge and that this was a compliment. Mediterranean oak in 1972 claimed Old World substance — carved, dark, Spanish by way of North Carolina. Black lacquer and brass in 1986 claimed money that had somewhere to be by eight. Distressed white in 1997 claimed inherited ease you had in fact bought at a boutique in Santa Monica. A barn door in 2015 claimed rural authenticity in a house that had never seen a barn. Quiet oak and linen in 2023 claimed that you had stopped claiming things. Each claim was sincere in its year. Sincerity does not prevent a year from ending.

The American furniture trade has always needed a picture before it needed a chair. The 1923 Sears catalog sold rooms as finished thoughts; you could point at a page and become that page. Ethan Allen later sold the same idea with a designer who came to the house. Design Within Reach, which Rob Forbes started in 1999 after failing to furnish a San Francisco apartment without a trade handshake, sold designer biographies next to the objects and made modernism feel like literacy. IKEA’s catalog, arriving in American mailboxes after the Plymouth Meeting, Pennsylvania, store opened on June 12, 1985, sold a whole apartment as a readable story in flat color. Instagram sold the story without the paper. The object is late to its own fashion. The picture is early.

High Point, North Carolina, still gathers the wholesale version of this twice a year. Buyers walk temporary rooms and decide what a sofa arm will be in the stores that have not yet been told. Thomasville, Lexington, Broyhill — the old factory names — taught the country that a style could be a line, a line could be a suite, and a suite could be a truck. Fashion in furniture is not a lone genius in Milan, though Milan has had those (Ettore Sottsass’s Memphis group opened at Arc ’74 in September 1981, fifty-five objects, and the argument is still loud). Fashion in furniture is also a buyer from a regional chain writing a number on a pad in a showroom that will be plywood again on Monday.

What ages and what dates is not a mystery if you stay in the material. A finish that belongs to the wood — oil, shellac, a stain that does not pretend to be another species — can be ugly for a decade and handsome again when the ugly decade ends. A finish that *is* the decade (harvest gold plastic, printed oak on particleboard, a gray-wash that exists to match Agreeable Gray) has nowhere to go when the decade leaves. Upholstery is almost always fashion. That is not an insult. Fabric takes a body and a sunbeam and a spill; it is supposed to be replaceable. The crime is a frame that cannot take a second fabric, or a cushion that was never a cushion so much as a first impression.

Particleboard is the twentieth century’s honest cheapness and its most common lie. Max Himmelheber’s German work in the 1930s made a useful board from waste and necessity [CITE NEEDED: patent number and year, often given as 1932 or 1933]. IKEA made that board a philosophy: furniture for the many, flat in a box, cheap enough to leave behind. The lie is the paper photograph of oak glued to the board, edged in plastic the color of oak, sold as wood to a person who wanted wood. Solid hardwood is not a moral victory. It is a mechanical one. You can plane it. You can glue a broken leg. You can hate the stain and change it. Bradley Brand Furniture, in Warren, Arkansas, prints “no particleboard” on its own product copy and dates a lumber ancestor, Bradley Lumber Co., to 1902; treat that date as company copy until a primary record sits beside it [CITE NEEDED]. The useful contrast is not heritage theater. It is a top you can sand after the fashion color fails.

People buy fads because rooms have to be lived in now. A first apartment needs a table before it needs an heirloom. A young family needs a sofa that can take juice. A retired couple wants the living room to look decided before the grandchildren come. Fashion is how those needs get a shape in a given year. The Mediterranean dining set was a real table. The Cloud-style sectional, launched by RH in 2015 after Timothy Oulton’s long development with the company, is a real place to lie down. Rachel Ashwell’s slipcovered sofas, sold from Santa Monica after she opened Shabby Chic in 1989, were washable, which is a virtue. Chip and Joanna Gaines’s *Fixer Upper* (HGTV, 2013–2018) gave a lot of people permission to paint something white and feel they had begun. None of that deserves a sneer. What deserves a hard look is the object that was built to photograph and not to be repaired.

The hangover is the part magazines skip because it is not pretty. It is the entertainment wall unit sized for a 32-inch television, too good to trash, too ugly to keep, occupying a basement in Fort Wayne. It is the barn door on a pantry in a 2017 spec house, off the track, leaning. It is the blush velvet chair listed online as “mid-century” by a seller who means 2017. It is the waterbed frame, Charles Hall’s late-1960s idea gone retail, now a curious rectangle in a garage [CITE NEEDED on Hall’s thesis year]. Hangovers are proof the fashion worked. If nobody had believed it, there would be nothing to trip over.

A hardwood shop that does not issue a color of the year is not above this system. It is beside it. The shop still has to sell. It still stains cherry darker when the market wants dark, lighter when the market wants pale. The difference is that the board remains a board. When the stain year ends, a person with sandpaper and patience can recover a table. When the board is a photograph, patience has nothing to hold.

This series is a walk through the rooms America was sold, decade by decade and object by object: the mid-century boom and the copies that ate it; the dark oak Spanish of the 1970s; lacquer and brass; shabby white; farmhouse iron; the quiet-luxury voice that arrived to scold the cart; the sofa that became architecture; the patio that became a second living room; the word “biophilic” printed on a cane headboard. It is also a walk through the shops that declined to look up from the joint — Gustav Stickley’s *Craftsman* (1901–1916), Thos. Moser’s Continuous Arm of 1977, the quieter American hardwood floors that never learned the word “collection launch.”

You do not have to pick a tribe. You can own a fashionable lamp and a boring table and be right about both. The only test worth keeping is simple, and it is not a vibe. Will this object still be itself when the picture that sold it is embarrassing? If yes, it is furniture. If no, it was a year. Years are allowed. Just know which you are buying, and what it will cost to stop living in it.
