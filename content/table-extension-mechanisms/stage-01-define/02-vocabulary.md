# Leaf, flap, filling piece, draw

The language is a mess because the trade kept the pretty words and
threw out the verbs.

**Leaf.** A board added to enlarge a top. In English furniture talk
it usually means a loose or hinged addition, not the fixed center.
Americans will call any insert a leaf, including the one that never
leaves the undercarriage. Fine. Just do not let the noun do the
work of a mechanism.

**Flap.** A hinged leaf that hangs. Drop-leaf, Pembroke, sofa table,
gateleg. The flap has a down life. It sees the side of the apron.
It collects chair-back bruises. A flap is not stored; it is parked
vertical. That is a different contract than a board you carry to a
closet.

**Filling piece.** Jupe's word, 1835, patent 6788. The radial top
opens and leaves gaps. You fill the gaps. The pieces are not
"leaves" in the farmhouse sense. They are wedges. They have a
handedness. They live in a fitted case because they will not stack
like rectangles. If you call them leaves you will design the wrong
box.

**Draw.** Dutch *trektafel*, English draw-leaf, sometimes "draw
table." The verb is the point. You draw the extra tops out from
under. On the good old ones the fixed top then drops between them,
so the working plane is the leaves' plane, not the original top's
old height. That drop is not a defect. It is the kinematic joke
that makes the thing flush. Modern copies that pull out and stay
high have not understood the joke.

**Gate.** A leg frame that swings like a small door to catch a
flap. Stretchers make it a gate instead of a lonely leg. A spider
gate is the same idea starved for elegance. A flat gate, in the New
York oval tables Chipstone wrote up, is a different shop language
again.

**Butterfly.** Two animals share a name and they are not related.
In late-seventeenth-century New England, butterfly means the
wing-shaped bracket that swings out under a drop leaf. In late
twentieth-century catalogs, butterfly means a center leaf hinged
down its middle that folds and hides under the top. If you mix
those in one sentence you are already lying to the next carpenter.

**Slide.** The rails the top halves ride. Wood, steel, geared,
plain. An *equalizer* is a slide that forces both halves to move
together, usually with a rack and pinion. A slide that does not
equalize is a two-person job pretending to be furniture.

**Concertina.** Not a dining leaf. An apron that folds out like a
bellows so a card-table top can open and still have a leg at each
corner. I keep it in this vocabulary because it taught the skirt
to change length. Dining tables that telescope their aprons are
in that family whether they admit it or not.

**Self-storing.** Marketing. Means the extra board has a home
inside the table. Does not mean the plane is true. Does not mean
you can open it with one hand. Does not mean the stored board
will still match the finish in five years. See draft 03.

I use these words tightly in the rest of the series. If a source
uses them loosely I say so. The point of a vocabulary is not
pedantry. The point is to stop a flap and a filling piece from
borrowing each other's hardware.
