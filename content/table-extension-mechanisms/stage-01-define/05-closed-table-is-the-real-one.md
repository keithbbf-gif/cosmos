# The closed table is the real one

Most of the year the table is closed.

That is the fact I wish designers would tape above the bench.
Holiday photographs sell extensions. Tuesdays pay for them. If
the closed object is a stubby box with a seam down the middle
and an apron that looks interrupted, you have built a party
prop that loiters in the room like a folded banquet table that
never got put away.

I can forgive an open table that looks a little long. Length is
the point of the day. I have a harder time forgiving a closed
table that looks unfinished. The center seam on a split top is
the usual tell. Some makers hide it with a rule joint or a
sham drawer-front. Some just leave two sawn edges kissing and
hope the finish blends. In raking light it reads as a crack.
You will eat looking at a crack from September to November.

Proportion shifts when you subtract leaves. A pedestal that is
correct under 96 inches can look like a mushroom under 60. A
four-leg frame that is brave at eight feet becomes
short-coupled and restless at five. Trestles have the opposite
problem: they can look right closed and then, open, leave a
span of top that no trestle is actually supporting. The closed
table is where you learn whether the base was designed for a
size or merely centered under a maximum.

Drop-leaves have a closed life that is honest. The flaps hang.
The object becomes narrow. A gateleg with both leaves down is
almost a hall table. That skinny thing is not a failure of the
open form. It is the Tuesday form. New England houses were
small. The table had to earn the floor on ordinary days. I
trust that family of objects more than I trust a 96-inch
showroom slab with two leaves in a cardboard sleeve.

Draw-leaves are honest in a different way. Closed, they are
already their full visual length; the extra is hidden in
thickness and in the way the top sits a little high, waiting
to drop. The closed table is the one you see. The open table
is the same silhouette, longer. That is why a good trektafel
does not look like it is missing teeth.

If I am judging a design I look at the closed photographs
first. I look at the seam, the apron continuity, the
overhang, the way a chair tucks. I look at whether the
hardware reads through the top as a ridge. Then I look at
the open state as a proof, not as the product.

An extension that is only beautiful when extended is a
costume. Costumes are fine in a dining room that is a stage.
Most dining rooms are also the homework table, the bill
table, the place you set a grocery bag because you have
not reached the kitchen. Design the closed one. Let the
open one be a trick the closed one knows how to do.
