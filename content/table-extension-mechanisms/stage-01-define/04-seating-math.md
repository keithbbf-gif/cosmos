# Seating math before hardware

Hardware is a late decision. Sitters are an early one.

I start with a dull count. How many people eat here on a Tuesday.
How many on the day the table has to prove itself. Those two
numbers are the closed length and the open length. Everything else
— leaf width, slide length, pedestal split, whether you even
deserve a leaf — hangs off them.

The Tuesday number is the one catalogs skip. A table that seats
twelve and lives at twelve is a banquet slab. A table that seats
twelve and lives at six is an extension. If your Tuesday is four
and your holiday is ten, you need about 48 inches of honest extra
top, not "seats 10–12" printed under a styled photograph where
nobody has elbows.

Elbows are the unit, not chairs. Twenty-four inches per person is
the old generous rule. Twenty-two will do if the chairs are slim
and the people are related. Twenty is a diner counter. I have
watched eight people "seat" at a 72-inch table and spend the meal
performing a kind of seated apology. That table did not need a
better finish. It needed another leaf or fewer invitations.

Leaves come in habits. Twelve inches is a pair of sitters if you
are lucky and the seam does not land in a lap. Eighteen inches is
the American furniture-store default and it is not a coincidence:
it is one person plus slop, or two people squeezed. Twenty-four
is a real two. If you need four extra souls you are in two-leaf
country, or one very wide leaf that will sag unless you give it a
spine.

Round tables lie. Adding a ring of filling pieces grows
circumference, which feels like seats, but place settings are
wedges, not rectangles. A Jupe that goes from 160 cm to 229 cm
can be described as six-to-twelve. I believe the six. I want to
see the twelve with chargers, glasses, and a roast before I
believe the twelve. Circles also punish the person whose setting
lands on a radial seam. There are more seams than on a rectangle.
That is the tax for keeping the shape.

Ends versus sides. A rectangular leaf in the middle adds two
seats if the long sides are where people already sit, and zero
useful seats if you only added length for a centerpiece. Leaves
at the ends of a refectory add heads of table — status seats,
often the worst seats, because that is where the draw-leaf
overhangs the legs. Gatelegs add width, which can add sitters
along the long sides and then immediately take them away with a
gate in the knee.

So: write the two numbers on the wall of the shop. Closed sitters.
Open sitters. Convert to inches with elbows, not with optimism.
Then pick a motion that can produce that delta without stealing
the Tuesday table. If the delta is one sitter, you do not have an
extension problem. You have a chair problem. If the delta is
eight and the room is ten feet, you have a room problem, and no
slide from Rockler is going to fix the walls.
