# The leaf is not the mechanism

I keep meeting people who point at a board and call it the invention.

The board is a leaf. Sometimes it is a flap. Sometimes, if you are
reading Robert Jupe's 1835 patent, it is a filling piece. It is wood
cut to a width, finished on one face, bored for pins if anyone
bothered. It is cargo. You can lose it in a closet and the table
still exists. Ugly, short, but itself.

The invention is the part that keeps that cargo in the same plane as
the rest of the dinner.

Plane is the whole job. Not "bigger." Bigger is easy. Two sawhorses
and a door are bigger. An extension has to become bigger and remain
a table: one height, one line down the long edges, one place for a
glass that does not walk toward the crack. If the leaf sits two
millimeters high, every plate clicks. If it sits two millimeters
low, gravy finds the ditch. If the two halves of the top open on a
bias, the seam becomes a river.

That is why I refuse the catalog sentence. *Features a butterfly
leaf.* Features a board. Tell me how the board arrives, how it is
held, how the apron behaves, where a knee goes, and what happens in
a dry house in February. Then we can talk about whether anyone
invented anything.

A drop-leaf on a gateleg is a hinge plus a swinging frame. The leaf
is the cheap half. The gate is the argument: it has to swing clear
of stretchers, lock without racking, and still leave a place to sit.
A Dutch trektafel hides two leaves under a floating top. The leaves
are just extra oak. The argument is the rails and the moment the
center drops home between them. Jupe's circle is the same lesson
with more brass. The filling pieces live in a fitted case. The
invention is the capstan that turns rotation into radial travel.

I have sat at tables where the leaf was the prettiest board in the
room — bookmatched, quarter-sawn, the whole sermon — and the
mechanism was a pair of stamped slides installed out of parallel.
The pretty board announced itself by rocking. I have sat at a
scrubbed pine farm table whose leaf was a different species, a
little too yellow, and the plane was dead true because someone had
cambered the wood slides and then lived with them for twenty years.

Pretty is not the test.

If you are designing one, start with the motion, not the board. How
does a single person open it in a room that already has chairs?
Where does the extra length go — ends, center, or circumference?
What keeps the two halves square to each other? What keeps the
added surface from becoming a trampoline? Answer those and the leaf
is almost a leftover. Fail them and no amount of walnut will save
Sunday.

The rest of this series is that argument, staged. History is here
because the motions are old and we keep renaming them. Hardware is
here because the motions have parts. Scars are here because I am
tired of green-log furniture: a table that "extends" in the
photograph and refuses in the room.
