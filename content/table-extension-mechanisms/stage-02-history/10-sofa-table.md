# Dining that pretends it is only visiting

The sofa table is a Pembroke that got longer and learned to
stand in front of upholstery.

Late 1790s into the Regency. Flaps at the *ends*, not the
sides — that is the first thing people get wrong. You pull it
up to a sofa. Two people can sit side by side and write, or
take tea, or pretend they are not eating. The base is a pair
of trestles, or later a single pedestal on a platform with
out-curving feet and, if the carver was paid, brass lion
paws. Britannica's description is fine. The social use is
better: this is a dining table that refuses the dining room.

End flaps change the mechanics. A side flap on a Pembroke
adds width for two settings across. An end flap adds length
for the person at the arm of the sofa. The brackets are
still brackets. The honesty is different. Closed, the object
is already a long narrow desk. Open, it is a slightly longer
desk. Nobody is pretending this seats eight. I respect that.

It also shows how extension follows furniture fashion, not
just hunger. There was a moment when the sofa became the
center of a room and needed a table that could live with it
without looking like leftover dinner. The flaps are a
courtesy to that fashion. When the fashion died, the form
lingered in antique shops as "sofa table," which now means
any low long thing behind a couch. Most of those do not
extend. The word survived. The mechanism did not.

Regency examples on a pedestal are the ones that teach a
later lesson. A single stem under a long top is already a
cantilever problem. Add end flaps and you have two more
cantilevers. The lion paws look like they are doing work.
They are mostly doing theater. The real work is the
spread of the platform and the stiffness of the stem. A
lot of later split-pedestal dining tables are this object
after it admitted it was for dinner.

I would not build a sofa table to solve a dining problem
today. I would steal its end-flap restraint. If your
extension only needs two more heads of table, flaps at the
ends with brackets will do it without splitting the top,
without equalizer slides, without a leaf that has to live
somewhere. The closed object stays the object. That is the
Pembroke lesson stretched to sofa length, and it is still a
good lesson.
