# Two animals named butterfly

There are two butterflies in this story. They do not share a
grandfather. They share a catalog word, which is worse.

The older one is American and late seventeenth century. A
small drop-leaf table. Instead of a full gate, a wing-shaped
bracket swings out from the undercarriage and props the leaf.
The wing, seen from the end, is the butterfly. Nebraska
extension historians — the old university bulletins that
bothered with furniture — called it a New England product,
not an English import. I have no reason to fight them. The
form is compact, a little rustic, and completely obvious once
you have seen the bracket. No extra leg on the floor. No
stretcher to bark a shin. The leaf is still a flap. The
support is just less architecture.

The younger one is a folding center leaf. Two boards hinged
to each other, stored under the split top, pivoted so they
can swing up and unfold into the gap. Fine Woodworking has
drawn this more than once. Barrel hinges on the leaf.
Locating keys on the edges. A shelf or a pair of pins so the
folded pack does not fall into the stretchers. This butterfly
is a twentieth-century (and later) answer to the lost-leaf
problem. It is self-storing. It is also a small machine.

If you write "butterfly table" without saying which animal,
you have not specified a mechanism. A restorer will open the
wrong drawer of parts. A buyer will think they are getting
New England and receive a geared slide from a box store.

I have a soft spot for the old wing. It is a bracket with a
shape. You can carve it badly and it still works. You can
carve it well and it becomes the only decoration the table
needs. The failure mode is simple: the pivot wears, the wing
droops, the leaf follows. A competent person with a
straightedge and a new pin can bring it back.

The folding leaf I respect without loving. It solves a real
scar — leaves that do not survive a move — and it introduces
new ones. Hinge lines. Pivot clearance. Keys that must work
in two configurations, open and closed. A pack that grows
dust and then refuses to unfold in front of guests. It is
good engineering when it is good. It is a party trick when
it is not.

Names should cut. This one smears. In the rest of these
drafts I will say *wing bracket* or *folding center leaf*
unless I am quoting a source that already chose the pretty
word. The insect can keep the rest of its life.
