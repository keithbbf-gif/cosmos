# Victorian dinner as a machine

By the time the dining room was a dedicated room, the
table could stop pretending to vanish.

The split-pedestal extension is that admission in
mahogany. Two stems, each on a platform or a set of
claws. The top is divided. Slides — first wood, later
iron — let the halves part. Leaves drop into the wound.
You can add one, two, three. You can keep going until the
room or the slides give up. Closed, it is already a
serious table for a serious class. Open, it is a banquet
that still claims to be the same object.

This is the ancestor of the furniture-store dining table.
I do not say that as an insult. The form solved a
nineteenth-century problem: guests as a variable, display
as a constant. You did not want a gate in a waistcoat.
You did not want flaps hanging in a room meant to impress.
You wanted a plane that grew down the axis of the carpet.

The machine is the slides. Early wood slides are
finger-jointed and often cambered, a slight upward bow so
the open table does not smile at the floor. Later metal
slides carry more weight and announce themselves less
when they are good. Equalizers — rack and pinion, both
halves moving at once — are the civilized version of two
servants pulling in time.

The social tell is the leaf count. A table with four
leaves is not a family object. It is a house that expects
to perform. Those leaves lived in a cupboard, sometimes
in bags, sometimes in a press built for them. Victorian
self-storing is a room, not an undercarriage. We pretend
we invented convenience. We invented smaller rooms.

Pedestals help seating. No corner legs in the middle of
the long sides. They hurt support. As the halves part,
the stems go with them, and the leaves in the middle
become a bridge. That is why sag bars and extra legs
appear on the long nights. A fifth leg that drops from
the center leaf is not elegance. It is a confession that
the machine has a span limit.

I can like a split pedestal when the closed seam is
quiet and the slides do not rack. I dislike it when the
closed table is treated as an inconvenience on the way
to the party. See draft 05. The Victorians at least
built the closed one as if a butler would see it every
morning. That is more than I can say for some of their
great-grandchildren in the catalog.
