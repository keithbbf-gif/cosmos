# The top that drops

The Rijksmuseum keeps a draw-leaf table, anonymous, Netherlands,
about 1600–1625, object BK-NM-5850. Oak, some ebony, baluster
legs carved with festoons. Closed length 170 cm. Open, the
museum gives 320 cm. That is not a leaf added to a top. That is
a top that learned to become the middle.

You pull the two under-leaves out on their rails. When they are
home, the main top drops down between them. The working plane
is now the leaves' plane. The old top has taken a step down and
joined the party. Length roughly doubles. No closet. No extra
stand. The extra dinner was under your plates the whole time.

I like this more than almost any later cleverness, because the
cleverness is kinematic, not decorative. The rails have to
carry a cantilevered leaf without letting it dive. Old shops
did that with wood: tapered slats running through mortises in
the frame, sometimes through a mid-rail so the slat could not
pitch. Stops kept you from pulling the house down. The taper
is not folk wisdom I invented. Dutch woodworkers still describe
it when they repair these things: a slat a little thicker where
it must not droop, a little thinner where it has to enter.

The form is older than that particular museum oak. English
talks about sixteenth-century "draw tables." I am cautious
with the earliest dates. What I will stand on is the
seventeenth-century Dutch civic version: affluent houses, oak
and ebony, ball feet, Y-stretchers, a table that could be a
family board on Monday and a company board on Thursday without
bringing a second object into a tight room.

There is a later echo I like for a different reason. In 1902
H. P. Berlage had a trektafel made in Amsterdam by J. B.
Hillen — extra leaves still under the top, the old verb in a
new mouth. The idea had not needed the Industrial Revolution.
It needed rooms that were smaller than the guest list.

If you meet a modern "refectory with draw leaves" and the
center never drops, you are looking at a cousin who forgot the
joke. Those pull-out ends often sit on sliders and stay in
their own plane, which means the maker now owes you a way to
bring three thicknesses into one height. Some do it with
wedges. Some do it with hope. The 1600s version is ruder and
smarter: change which surface is the boss, and let gravity
finish the alignment.

I would rather repair one of those rails than debug a motor.
The scar, when it comes, is honest. A slat wears. A stop
splits. A top no longer drops true because someone refinished
it thicker. You can see the failure with a straightedge. That
is a history I trust.
