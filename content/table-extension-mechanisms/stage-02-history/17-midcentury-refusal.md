# The century that refused to extend

Mid-century modern made some of the best dining tables
of the last two hundred years and then declined to let
them grow.

I am generalizing, and I know it. There are exceptions:
flip-under leaves, hidden slides, a few honest
extensions from Denmark and Italy. The famous photographs
are still the refusal. Saarinen's pedestal is a single
stem and a continuous lip. You do not split that tulip
without ending the sculpture. Eames and Nelson and the
Knoll catalog loved a slab that was the size it was. The
room was supposed to be enough. Guests were supposed to
be the number that fit.

That is an ethic, not a failure of machining. After the
Victorian banquet machine, a fixed beautiful plane felt
like honesty. No seam. No leaf in a bag. No apron that
breaks. You bought the table for the life you claimed
to have, not the life you performed twice a year.

I have eaten at those tables. They are right until the
night they are wrong. Then you put a card table at the
end, or you eat in shifts, or you move to the kitchen
counter and call it casual. The refusal did not delete
the guest-list problem. It outsourced it.

When mid-century did extend, it often did it as a
slight: a thin leaf that did not match, a slide that
was an afterthought under a top that wanted to be
solid. The sculpture came first. The mechanism borrowed
clothes. You can feel it in the way some of those
leaves meet the edge profile — the lip dies, the
bevel does not turn the corner, the rim becomes a
flat. The open table is the closed idea with a patch.

I am not asking Saarinen for a Jupe. I am asking later
designers who cite that century to admit the trade.
If you want a continuous rim, you may not get a leaf.
If you want a leaf, you owe a rim that can survive
being cut. Most "mid-century style" extensions in
current catalogs want both and deliver a rim that
looks amputated when you add the 18-inch board from
the carton.

The refusal is useful as a negative law. Some objects
should not extend. Forcing them is how you get a
beautiful idea and a bad dinner. Better a second table
in the other room than a tulip with a scar down its
face. I would rather the designer say no. No is a
mechanism too.
