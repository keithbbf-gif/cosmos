# A door under the dinner

A gateleg is a drop-leaf that grew a pair of small doors.

Fixed center. One or two hinged flaps. Under each flap, a
leg-and-stretcher frame pivoted at the side of the table so it
can swing out and catch the leaf. The frame is the gate. When
it is closed, the leaf hangs. When it is open, you have more
table and less place to put a knee.

England had them by the later sixteenth century; the form is
common in oak by the seventeenth. Turned legs, box stretchers,
a center that is barely a board wide. Britannica will tell you
the spider-leg variant shows up in the third quarter of the
eighteenth — the same idea, legs drawn out to a nervous
thinness. I care less about the turning profile than about the
social fact: this is a table that admits the room is small.

American colonies took it and made it a dialect. New England
gatelegs are a study in how many people you can feed in a
house that also has to hold a bed and a chest. The table
against the wall is a console. The table in the room is a
dinner. Same object. The gates are the difference.

The cost is written in shinbones. A gate occupies the zone
where a sitter wants to live. Large flaps sometimes got two
gates, which helps the leaf and hurts the seating even more.
Cabinetmakers knew it. That is one reason later card tables
invented the concertina: keep a leg at each corner and stop
asking a guest to straddle a stretcher. Dining rooms were
slower to learn. Plenty of Victorian oak gatelegs still put a
bar across a lap and call it tradition.

I do not romanticize them. A worn gate sags. The leaf then
falls a degree and every pea rolls. The hinge stile loosens
in its socket. Someone drives a screw where a pivot should
have been bushed. The hanging flaps get hit by chairs until
the rule joint looks like a chewed lip. All of that is the
history too.

What I will defend is the honesty of the closed form. No
hidden leaf, no second object, no "seats twelve" fiction.
You can read the capacity from across the room. Leaves down:
one or two. Leaves up: four, maybe six if the flaps are
generous and the gates are placed with mercy. That
readability is a kind of design law. A lot of later
extensions spent two centuries trying to hide what the
gateleg was willing to say out loud.
