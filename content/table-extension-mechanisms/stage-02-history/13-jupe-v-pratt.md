# The copy that went to court

A year after the patent, Jupe was in court.

Jupe v. Pratt, December 6, 1836. The Furniture History
Society has the best narrative I have, in a 2018 newsletter:
Samuel Pratt's shop had begun advertising something Jupe
read as a pirate. Pratt was not a naïf. He and his father
had their own patents. They knew what a drawing on parchment
was worth. Jupe brought models and the two sheets of
official drawings. Sir Frederick Pollock argued for him.
Pratt's side called no witnesses and said the inventions
were unrelated. I was not in the room. I will not invent
the verdict as folklore.

What I will take is the speed. Patent sealed in March.
A rival on the market in 1836. Trial the same year. The
expanding circle was valuable enough to fight over before
the Great Exhibition made it a souvenir of empire. That
is how you know a mechanism has left the workshop and
entered money.

The drawings survive at Kew, cited as MFC 1/42. If you
care about enablement, that is the artifact — ink and
watercolor on parchment, 26 by 34¼ inches — not a brass
boss on a later dining table. The boss is a brand. The
parchment is the claim.

I keep this draft next to the pretty Sotheby's prose
because furniture history likes genius and hates the
week after genius, when someone else builds a version
with a different story. Pratt's argument, as reported,
was difference. Jupe's argument was identity. That is
every later lotus table in miniature. Is a motorized
radial top with resin filling pieces the same invention?
Is a wooden cam that opens eight slices? At what point
does "diverge from a common centre" stop being Jupe and
start being geometry?

I am not counsel. I am a person who has watched
designers treat 1835 as a mood board. Mood boards do not
go to the National Archives. If you are going to name a
table a Jupe, you owe the radial motion, the filling
pieces, and the honesty that the extra surface still
lives somewhere else. If you are going to name it a
tribute, say tribute. The 1836 fight is what happens
when the name and the motion come apart and both sides
have a shop to feed.

The scar for this series is simple. Mechanisms get
copied because they work. The first copy is a compliment
and a theft. History that only praises the patent date
is half a history. The other half is the week someone
else advertised.
