# Flat gates and draw-bars

New York's early Baroque oval tables with falling leaves are
not New England gatelegs in nicer clothes.

Chipstone's 1994 essay is the piece I trust here: *Flat
Gates, Draw-Bars, Twists, and Urns*. The tables have oval
tops, hanging leaves, and a support language that includes
flat gates, trestle bases, and draw-bars — sliding bars you
pull to catch a leaf, instead of swinging a turned gate
across the floor. Some of the cheap ones use red gum where
a New England shop would have used something else. The
turnings and the stock sizes belong to New York documented
work. You can argue taste. You cannot argue they are
copies of Boston.

Why it matters to this series: the drop-leaf problem was
solved more than once, in more than one shop dialect, in
the same century. A swinging gate is one dialect. A
draw-bar is another. A flat gate on a trestle is a third.
All of them are trying to get a hanging oval leaf up into
a plane and keep it there while someone cuts meat.

The small ones, Chipstone notes, can be under a foot wide
with the leaves down. Inventory language puts an "ovall
table" in a closet — meaning a small room, not a coat
cupboard — in Gertruy Van Cortlandt's 1724 house. That is
the closed-table law in a Dutch-style townhouse. The object
had to vanish between uses. Width was the enemy. The
extension was how it reentered the parlor.

I will not pretend I have handled the Johnson or Van
Cortlandt tables. I have read the measurements and looked
at the photographs like everyone else. Tops on the trestle
group run smaller than the four-leg draw-bar cousins. Some
of these were tea and service. Some could feed four and
might have been the dining table in a house that did not
own a bigger lie.

The scar I take from New York is this: if you only study
English oak gatelegs and then look at American work, you
will invent a single family tree and miss the shop. Draw-bars
are a different hand motion. You do not swing. You pull.
The leaf still falls. The support still has to be there
when a guest leans. Same dinner, different verb. History
that only follows the pretty turning is not history of
mechanisms.
