# The apron that learned to breathe

Card tables are not dining tables. I still need them here.

Early eighteenth-century England wanted a square of baize
that could vanish against a wall. The top folded in half.
Opened, it had to be a table again: four corners, four
legs, a surface that did not hinge under a stake. The
cheap way was a gateleg. A rear leg swings out and
catches the leaf of the top. You get your fourth corner.
You also get a gate in someone's knee and a base that
looks lopsided if you have eyes.

The expensive way was concertina. The apron folds out
like a bellows. The back legs travel with the expanding
skirt and land at the new corners. Closed, a leg at each
corner. Open, a leg at each corner. Symmetric. The hinges
in the frieze are the mechanism. The baize is just the
excuse.

Dealers will tell you this is why a concertina card table
costs more. They are not wrong. It is more work. It is
also a different seating law. You can sit a long rubber
of whist without straddling a stretcher. Susan Silver's
shop voice says the quiet part: a gateleg is uncomfortable
to sit at for any length of time if the game is the point.

I bring this into a leaf series because dining extensions
keep relearning the lesson. When a split-top dining table
opens, the apron is suddenly too short for the top, or
the top is suddenly a raft on a too-small frame. Some
makers telescope the skirt. Some add apron leaves. Some
leave the gap and let you see under the table into the
slides. That gap is a concertina problem that nobody
named.

The concertina also belongs with New York draw-bars and
Dutch rails as proof that the eighteenth century was
already thinking in verbs: swing, fold, draw, breathe.
Dining shops were conservative. Games shops, working for
people who lost money in public, spent more on the feel
of a corner. If your dinner guests stay two hours, you
are in the games business whether you like it or not.

A surviving concertina with its original frieze hinges
is a small education. Open it slowly. The skirt does not
drag if the hinges are right. The legs do not toe in. The
top lands on a frame that was always waiting for it. That
is the standard I want from a dining equalizer, and I did
not get it from a dining equalizer. I got it from a card
table that was tired of gates.
