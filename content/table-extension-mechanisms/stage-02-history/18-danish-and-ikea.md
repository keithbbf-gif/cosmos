# Flip-leaf honesty and the catalog cousin

Danish shops after the war did not refuse the guest.
They just refused the Victorian cupboard full of
leaves.

The good ones have a flip or a pull-out that lives
with the table. You slide the ends, a leaf comes up
from the middle, you rotate it into the plane, you
push home. Wooden slides. Dowels. A motion you can
learn in one evening and then do in the dark. I have
used a 1960s oak one that still sounded like a well
boat — wood on wood, a little drag, then a stop you
could feel in the handles. No branding. No motor.
No lie about seating twelve.

That object is closer to the trektafel than to Jupe.
The extra surface is already in the body. The closed
table is the real table. The open table is the same
citizen, longer. If the leaf is a bit paler, you live
with it, because it has been in the same room, under
the same weather, just on its back.

Then the catalog cousin.

IKEA's extending tables — BJURSTA is the one people
can still name — taught a generation the verb without
teaching the shop. Dovetail-ish sliders screwed under
the top. Hook latches. Alignment pins. Extra half-leaves
lying on a rack underneath. You unhook, pull, lift,
drop in, push. It works until the screws loosen in
particle core, or a pin bends, or someone leans on a
half-open top. I am not sneering. I have eaten on
those. They got more people through more small rooms
than Johnstone & Jeanes ever did.

The honesty gap is materials and service. The Danish
oak expected to be repaired. The catalog table expects
to be replaced. When the slide rackets, you do not
camber it. You look up a part number and hope the
model still exists. That is a different history of
mechanisms: mechanism as consumable.

I put them in one draft because they share a kinematic
family and split on a moral. Both hide the extra board
in the table. Both ask one or two people to perform a
short ritual before guests. Only one of them was built
as if the ritual would still exist in forty years.

If you are designing now, you can steal the ritual
without stealing the particle board. Wooden slides,
real pins, a leaf from the same flitch as the top, a
way to retighten. The catalog already proved the
motion is teachable. The old Danes proved it can last.
That is enough ancestry for a new table. You do not
need to pretend you invented the flip.
