# The circle that grows

March 11, 1835. Robert Jupe, upholsterer, 47 Welbeck Street,
Cavendish Square. British patent 6788. An expanding table
"so constructed that the sections composing its surface may
be caused to diverge from a common centre and that the
spaces caused thereby may be filled up by inserting leaves
or filling pieces."

That sentence is the whole idea. A round top is not one
board. It is a pie. Turn the mechanism — later tables use a
spiral capstan that changes rotation into radial travel —
and the slices slide apart. Gaps appear. You put the wedges
in. The table is still a circle. It is just a larger one.

Sotheby's, writing about a Johnstone & Jeanes example, gives
a useful pair of numbers: about 160 cm up to about 229 cm,
six seats to twelve, extra leaves in two sizes, sixteen
pieces, a fitted case. I take the measurements as belonging
to that table, not as a universal spec. I take the case
seriously. The invention did not make the filling pieces
disappear. It made a second piece of furniture to hold them
and called the set complete.

Jupe worked with John Johnstone of New Bond Street.
Johnstone, Jupe & Co. until the partnership ended around
1840. After 1842 the shop language becomes Johnstone &
Jeanes. Early bosses say JUPES PATENT. Later ones say the
new firm. The Great Exhibition of 1851 showed a circular
table "on the expanding principle." The machine-age dinner
had found its mascot.

I care about this object because it refuses the rectangular
habit. Almost every other extension in this series adds
length or adds flaps. Jupe adds radius. Seating grows around
the roast instead of down a refectory. The host stays in the
middle of the conversation instead of at a distant head.
That is a social design as much as a brass one.

It is also a storage design that catalogs still underplay.
Those wedges are handed. They come in sizes if the table has
more than one open state. They want a case with nests. Lose
the case and you have a very expensive round table that
cannot grow, plus a bag of oddly shaped boards that will not
stack in a closet like an 18-inch rectangle. Self-storing,
in the Jupe world, means "we sold you the box."

Modern "lotus" tables are this idea after a hundred and
ninety years of copying. Some are honest. Some motorize the
capstan and then leave you to handle the wedges anyway. The
circle that grows is only half a trick. The other half is
still a case in the corner, waiting for someone to remember
where the small set lives.
