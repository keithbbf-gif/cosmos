# One hand, two halves

If both ends of a split top do not move together,
the seam becomes a grin.

An equalizer is the part that refuses the grin.
Usually a rack and pinion: a gear at the center,
racks on each slide, so a pull on one half pushes
the other. One person, one hand, a table that
stays rectangular while it grows. Without it you
are asking two people to dance, or one person to
run around the table like a fool, and the first
time someone is late the gap goes trapezoid.

I have opened unequalized tables alone. You pull
your end eight inches, walk, pull the other six
because you guessed wrong, walk back, correct,
and by then a pin is already starting into a hole
at a slant. The leaf you then drop in has to
teach the table what square meant. That is a
lousy teacher.

Shop hardware exists because this problem is
solved. Rockler's 28- and 38-inch equalizer pairs
are the ones American amateurs name. Longer
finger-jointed wood equalizers show up at the
specialty houses — TableLegs, Osborne, the places
forum people send each other to. Size the slide
to the opening, not to the closed table. A 38-inch
slide is about the extra travel, not the dining
length. If you want two 18-inch leaves, you need
travel for 36, plus the overlap the mechanism
keeps in the frame. The shop that sells you the
slide will tell you if you listen. The shop that
does not ask what you are opening for is selling
you a number.

Pedestal tables like equalizers. The stems want to
part in step or the bases scuff and the slides
bind. Four-leg tables can live with plain slides
if you are disciplined. I still specify the gear
if one person will ever open it. Discipline is
not a mechanism.

The failure I watch for is mounting. Two slides
not parallel. A pinion that barely kisses one
rack. Screws in a soft web that later loosen so
the gear climbs teeth. You can build a perfect
top and ruin it with a 3-degree error underneath.
Measure diagonals of the opening as you go. If
the diagonals drift, stop and fix the slides
before you cut a single pin hole.

Equalizing is not elegance. It is the minimum
civility for a split top. If a maker asks you to
admire the wood and shrugs at the grin, they
have not finished the table.
