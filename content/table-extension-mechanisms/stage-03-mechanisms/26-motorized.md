# Effortlessness is a new kind of failure

A motor under a dining table is a promise that
nobody will have to understand the table.

I distrust that promise. Not because I hate
motors. Because the failure mode leaves you
with a smaller table and a dead machine at
the worst time, which is when the extra
people are already in the hall.

Hand motions fail loud and early. A slide
binds, you stop, you wax, you look underneath.
A motor fails polite. A beep. A half-open
gap. A leaf that cannot be lifted because
the travel is in the way. Or worse: it runs
and you do not notice a child' s finger, a
cloth, a chair rail. Effortlessness hides
the last inch, and the last inch is where
tables pinch.

There is also the service scar. Wooden slides
are a local craft. A geared slide is a part.
A motor, a control board, a transformer hiding
in a pedestal — those are appliances. When
the maker is gone, you are in appliance land
with a very large teak shell. I have seen
more than one "smart" table whose owners
opened it by lifting the top off the base
because the base had become a brick.

If you still want a motor, design a manual
override that is not a myth. A clutch you
can reach. A crank. A way to separate the
halves with two people if the gear train
dies. Write it on a card in the drawer, not
in a PDF that lived on a discontinued
domain.

And do not motorize a ritual that was already
one pleasant pull. Equalizers exist. Flip
leaves exist. Saving the host thirty seconds
is not worth a second industry under the
oak. Motorize the Jupe-scale object if the
wedges are heavy and the host is one person
who should not lift them. Even then, I would
rather a good case on casters than a
pedestal full of copper.

Effort is information. The drag of a wood
slide in February tells you the house is
dry. The clack of a pin tells you the gap
is wrong. A silent motor tells you nothing
until it tells you nothing at all.
