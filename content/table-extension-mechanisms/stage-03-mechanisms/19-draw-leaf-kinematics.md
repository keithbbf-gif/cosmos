# Rails, taper, and the drop

A draw-leaf is three boards and a permission to change
which one is in charge.

Start with the center sitting a little high, riding on
the under-leaves or on packs that keep it up. The
under-leaves have rails — slats, runners, sometimes a
pair each — that pass through the frame. You pull both
ends. The rails telescope out. The under-leaves become
cantilevers. If the rail is a constant rectangle in a
loose mortise, the leaf dives as soon as its center of
gravity clears the frame. Dinner goes with it.

The old answer is geometry, not steel. Taper the rail
so that as it extends, a thicker section arrives at the
bearing. Or cut the mortise so the rail can only run
level. Or add a mid-bridge with a second hole, turning
the slat into a beam on two supports. Stops at the
inside ends keep the leaf from coming out of the house.
I have seen repairs where the stop was a later nail.
You can feel that dinner in the scar.

When both leaves are out, something has to bring the
three faces into one height. The museum version lets
the center drop. Gravity is the clamp. The center's
bottom edges, or blocks on them, land on ledges or
simply come down between the leaves until the faces
kiss. If someone later refinishes the center thicker,
the drop is shy and you live with a proud middle. If
they sand the leaves more than the center, the middle
sinks and you have a valley. The mechanism is only as
true as the last person with paper.

Modern draw-leaves that refuse the drop owe you a
different leveling trick: wedges, inclined planes,
lifting cams. Those can be sweet. They can also be
two more parts that jam with bread crumbs. I would
rather tune a drop than debug a cam that was designed
on a screen and never fed pasta.

Hand motion matters. You want a clear run at both
ends. A wall or a sideboard kills one leaf. This is
not a table for a built-in bench on both sides unless
you only ever open one end, which makes a lopsided
beast. Two people help but are not required if the
rails are waxed and the stops are kind.

The sound of a good one is a slide, a pause, a wooden
sigh as the center comes down. If you hear a clack,
something fell the last millimeter instead of sitting
down. That millimeter is the whole craft.
