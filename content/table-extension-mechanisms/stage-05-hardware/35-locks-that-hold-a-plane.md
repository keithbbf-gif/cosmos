# Locks that hold a plane

A latch that only kisses is decoration.

Once the pins have registered the
edges, something has to keep the
halves from opening a hair when a
sitter leans, when the slides want
to creep, when a tablecloth is
tugged. That something is a lock.
Hooks. Barrel bolts. Table forks.
Over-center catches under the top.
I do not care what it is called if
it holds a plane.

I care if it can be worked from the
long side without crawling. I care
if it pulls the joint together
instead of just forbidding travel.
A hook that only prevents opening
will still let the faces breathe
apart vertically if the slides are
tired. A lock that cams the halves
in and down is doing two jobs, which
is the right number.

Victorian forks — the iron pieces
that drop into slots across a joint
on the underside — are ugly and
good. You feel them take. Cheap
zinc catches from a hardware aisle
are pretty in the package and then
they flex. Flex is how a plane
becomes a landscape.

On folding leaves the keys *are*
part of the lock. If you rely on
friction in the slides, you will
watch the table open itself during
a lively story. I have seen it. The
host put a hand on the seam as if
the table were a dog.

Document the lock for the next
owner. A catch that lives six
inches off center on the left apron
is invisible to anyone who did not
build it. A small mark — a dot of
paint, a carved notch — is not a
crime against purity. Purity is how
tables get forced.

And test the lock with weight on the
leaf, not with the leaf empty in a
quiet shop. A roast is a critic. So
is a guest who talks with their
forearms. If the joint opens a
black line under that load, you
built a clasp for a cabinet, not a
lock for a table.
