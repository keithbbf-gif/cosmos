# The rule joint is a tiny roof

A drop-leaf that meets the center on
two square edges will always have a
ditch or a proud lip the first time
the house dries out. The old answer
is a rule joint: a quarter-round on
the fixed top, a cove on the flap,
hinges set so the flap swings around
that radius. Closed up, the round
nests in the cove. Light does not
fall into a crack. Dirt has a harder
time. The line reads as a bead, which
is a choice, not a wound.

I call it a tiny roof because that is
how it sheds. Crumbs go away from
the hinge instead of into it. Water
from a wet glass has a chance to run
onto the flap instead of into iron.
When the flap hangs, the round of
the center still looks finished. A
square edge hanging is a cut. A
rule joint hanging is a profile.

The hinges are the whole craft.
Table-leaf hinges have one leaf
longer than the other so the knuckle
can sit on the centerline of the
radius. If you use a door hinge you
will bind or you will open a gap
that grows as the flap travels.
People do this. Then they plane the
cove until there is no cove, and
then they have a square edge again
with extra work in it.

Seasonal movement still shows. The
round and the cove were cut in one
weather. In another they will kiss
or they will grin. A little grin on
a rule joint is prettier than a
grin on two squares, which is not
an excuse to cut it sloppy. Cut it
in the middle of the shop's year
and leave a paper's thickness for
February.

I do not put rule joints on a
center drop-in. Those edges meet
once and stay. The rule joint is
for a flap that has a down life.
If you see one on a split top, someone
loved a profile more than a
function. Profiles are not
mechanisms unless they move.
