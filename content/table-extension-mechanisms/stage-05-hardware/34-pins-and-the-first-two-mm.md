# Pins and the first two millimeters

Alignment is won before anyone calls it a
lock.

The pins — dowels, steel nibs, wooden
keys — are the first thing that teaches
two edges how to be one plane. If they
are fat, the table never closes without
a shove. If they are skinny, the leaf
can wind and still call itself seated.
If they are long, they engage while the
faces are still apart and then they
steer the last inches like a bad
helmsman. If they are short, they only
kiss at the end, which is what you
want: faces close, then the pins say
*here*.

I like three across a dining width.
Two is a hinge. One is a pivot. Four
starts to fight the seasonal movement
of the boards. The outer pins keep
the long edges in a line. The middle
one keeps the leaf from crowning into
a smile.

Material is a temperament. Wood on
wood swells together and can weld in
August if you made them too proud.
Steel in wood will stay the same size
while the hole shrinks; February is
easy, July is a pinch. Brass is kind
to both and bends if a guest uses the
leaf as a lever. I have replaced more
bent brass than I have praised it, and
I still specify it for small Pembrokes
because it does not blacken a hand.

Cut the holes late. I will say this in
every hardware draft until it becomes
a superstition, which it already is.
Build the motion. Get the slides
parallel. Then, with the table almost
closed and the leaf almost in, mark.
A hole cut from a drawing is a hole
you will oval later with a round file
in front of company.

The first two millimeters of engagement
should be easy. Chamfer the pin. Ease
the hole. If the first two millimeters
are a fight, the last two will be a
scar. You will see it as a shiny
crescent on the pin and a chewed
mouth on the hole. That crescent is
a calendar of every hurried holiday.
