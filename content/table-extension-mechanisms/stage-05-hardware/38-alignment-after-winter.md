# February is the test the shop never runs

Shops are humid compared to the
houses the tables die in.

You build in June, you deliver in
September, you get the call in
February: the leaf will not go in,
or it falls through, or the closed
seam has opened a dark line that
looks like a fault. Nobody sanded
it in the night. The house did
what dry air does. The top shrank
across the grain. The pins did
not. The slides got tighter or
looser depending on which part
was wood.

I run a winter thought in the
shop even when the shop is
sweating. Extra tenth in the pin
holes if the pins are metal.
Slides that still have travel
when the top is at its narrowest.
A lock that can pull a winter
gap without chewing. Breadboards
that are pinned in the middle and
slotted at the ends so the panel
can move without blowing the
joint you are so proud of.

The stored leaf is a different
climate. A closet against an
exterior wall is not the dining
room. A basement is a swamp or a
desert, rarely a dining room. If
the leaf lives there, it will
arrive in February as a stranger
to the top it was cut from. Self-
storing leaves at least sleep in
the same weather. That is one
actual point of the undercarriage.

I ask owners for one midwinter
opening even if no one is coming.
Not as romance. As a service
interval. Wax. A look at the pins.
A straightedge. If the table only
moves when the relative humidity
is an accident of cooking, you
are training it to fail in the
one week you cannot stand a
failure.

The shop never runs this test
because the shop cannot be the
house. The best we can do is
leave room for the house and
tell the truth about it. A table
that only works in the climate
it was born in is a prototype
that escaped.

Houses with tight winter heat are
the hard case. A humid June shop
and a 22-percent-RH dining room
are not a disagreement. They are
two countries. If you build as if
they were one, the pins will
tell on you in front of company.
