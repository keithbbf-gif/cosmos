# Long leaves need a spine

Gravity does not negotiate.

An 18-inch leaf across a 42-inch
table is a short bridge. A 24-inch
leaf across a 48-inch table is a
real one. A pair of 18s with
nothing under the meeting is a
span the Victorians already
admitted by dropping a fifth leg.
If you refuse the fifth leg, you
owe a sag bar, a deep apron on the
leaf, a cambered slide, or a
thicker plate. Choose one. "It's
oak" is not a choice.

A sag bar is a beam under the leaf,
sometimes iron, sometimes wood,
sometimes a truss you can tension.
Old ones are shy: a flat bar on
edge in a groove. New ones can be
loud if the designer is proud of
engineering. I prefer shy. The bar
should be cambered a little against
the future, the way wood slides
are. Wood creeps. Iron sags less
and then stays where it gave.

You will not see the sag in the
shop on day one. You will see it
in a raking light after a summer
of fruit bowls left on the extra
leaf that no one put away. The
plane becomes a shallow valley.
Peas roll to the seam. That is
not charming.

Breadboard ends on a leaf fight a
different war — they keep the end
straight — and do nothing for the
span if the breadboard is at the
short ends. People confuse the two
stiffnesses. I have. Then I put a
straightedge down the long way and
shut up.

A straightedge after a week with a
full leaf left in is worth more than
a straightedge on install day. Creep
is slow. If you only check at
delivery you are grading a
freshman.

If the design cannot take a bar
without ruining a slim mid-century
silhouette, the design cannot take
that leaf. Shorten the leaf. Add a
leg. Or refuse to extend, which is
still legal. What is not legal is
a slim sculpture that becomes a
hammock and then gets photographed
from far enough away that the
hammock looks like a plane.
