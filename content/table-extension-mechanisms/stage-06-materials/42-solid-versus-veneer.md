# Two different contracts with humidity

Solid wood and veneer are not two
prices for the same table. They
are two contracts.

Solid moves. It can be planed, it
can be patched with the same
species, it can take a ding and
still be the same object in fifty
years. It will also cup a leaf,
open a winter seam, and teach
you February. You are signing up
for weather.

Veneer on a stable core — plywood,
a good MDF, a well-made
laminate — stays flatter. Pins
keep their relationships. Slides
see less seasonal drama. The
plane you set in the shop is
closer to the plane in the
house. That is a real gift for
an extension, which is a machine
that hates surprise widths.

The bill comes later. A veneered
leaf that is stored on edge in a
damp closet can still let go.
Edges chip and then there is no
thickness to cut back to. A
burn goes through the skin. A
refinisher sands off the figure
you paid for. And the core, if
it is cheap, swells at a screw
and never forgets.

I specify solid when the table
is meant to be a household tool
with a future shop in its life.
I specify veneer when the
priority is a quiet plane and
the owner will keep the leaves
in the same climate and not
sand them on a weekend. Mixing
— solid top, veneered leaves, or
the reverse — is how you get two
contracts at one seam. Do not
do that unless you like a
ridge that changes with the
news.

There is a third contract:
softwood core with a hardwood
mask, the furniture-store
special. It photographs. It
extends. It does not want a
second decade of openings. If
that matches the life of the
room, fine. Say it. A lot of
pain is just an expensive
word applied to a cheap
contract.

I do not rank them morally.
I rank them by whether the
owner knows which weather they
bought.
