# You cannot stain a year back on

A refinish on the top only is how
you make the leaf famous.

Someone burns a mark. Someone
sands the closed table because
the seam has ridges. Someone
hires a person who does "the
table" and means the thing that
was in the room, not the boards
in the guest closet. The top
comes back a shade. The leaves
arrive at the next holiday as
exhibits.

If you are going to cut film,
cut every board that belongs to
the plane. Bring the leaves into
the same shop, the same week,
the same batch of finish. Number
them. Do the edges that only
meet when open. Those edges are
where a hurried job shows: a
drip, a dry brush, a color that
turns the corner and dies.

I am harsh about stain because
stain is how people try to
negotiate with the sun. The sun
already voted. A toner on a
leaf will look right at 7 p.m.
under bulbs and wrong at noon.
Then someone adds another toner.
The leaf becomes a record of
corrections, which is a kind of
history, just not one I want
under a plate.

Better to plane a proud seam and
re-oil everything than to paint
the proudness a matching brown.
Better to live with a younger
leaf than to give it a fake
middle age. If the family cannot
stand the honesty, store the
leaves in the room for a year
and do nothing. Doing nothing is
an underrated finish schedule.

On veneer you have less room.
Sand through and you have a
different species in a patch.
That patch will never take the
sun like the original skin.
This is why I am cooler to
veneered extension tops than to
veneered fixed ones. The
maintenance path is narrower,
and extensions are maintenance.
If you still want veneer, keep
the leaves with the table and
write a note that travels with
the object: *these boards are
one finish. Do not separate
them for work.* Notes get lost.
The note is still better than
the surprise.
