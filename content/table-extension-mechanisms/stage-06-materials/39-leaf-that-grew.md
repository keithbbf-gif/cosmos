# The leaf that grew

Wood moves across the grain. A table
that pretends otherwise will open a
seam you did not cut.

The usual crime is a leaf whose
grain does not run with the top.
Someone needed a leftover, or a
board that was wide enough the
short way, and they turned it.
In August that leaf is wider than
the gap the slides want to offer.
In February it is narrower and
falls in like a loose tooth. The
pins, if they were cut in a third
season, are wrong in both.

Even when the grain agrees, widths
differ. A top built of three
boards and a leaf built of one
will not shrink as twins. Glue
lines are little dams. A single
wide board cups. I would rather
a leaf of two or three boards
that match the top's construction
than a trophy slab that lives a
private life.

Cross-grain battens on the
underside are an old way to keep
a leaf flat and an old way to
split it if you glue them the
full length. Slot the battens.
One fastener in the middle can
be a prisoner. The rest should
allow travel. This is first-year
shop law and I still see it
violated on expensive tables
because the underside is where
pride takes a night off.

Apron returns on a leaf are
another cross-grain trap. A skirt
glued to the underside, grain
running the long way of the
table, will fight a leaf whose
grain runs that way too — that
part is fine — but if the skirt
turns the corner and gets glued
on the end grain path, you have
built a picture frame around a
living panel. The panel will
win. The win looks like a split
or a sprung joint in the skirt.

I do not try to stop movement. I
try to make the two halves of
the conversation speak the same
dialect. Same species, same cut,
same construction, same room.
Then the growth is a chorus, not
a solo. A solo is how you get a
leaf that will not come to
dinner.
