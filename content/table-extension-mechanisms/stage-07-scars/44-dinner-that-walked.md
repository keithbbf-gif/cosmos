# Racking is a social event

A table that racks does not rack
in private.

Someone leans in to hear. Someone
stands to pour. The open joint
works like a hinge because the
slides are loose or the lock was
never thrown or the fifth leg is
up. The plane becomes two planes
for a second. Glasses nod. A
little red tide moves in a
shallow bowl. Conversation stops
in the way it stops when a truck
goes by, except the truck is the
furniture.

I have done this as a guest and
hated myself for putting a hand
on the seam. I have done this as
a host and hated the table. The
shame is out of proportion to the
millimeters, which is how you
know it is social. The object
failed in public.

Causes are boring and fixable.
Slides mounted out of parallel
so the opening is a
parallelogram. A missing lock.
Pins that no longer teach. A
leaf too thin for the span. A
pedestal foot that does not
touch because the floor is the
other critic in the room. People
will blame the floor. Level the
foot anyway. Then fix the lock.

The scar I watch for in old
tables is the ovaled screw hole
in a slide. Someone felt the
rack and tightened the wrong
thing, stripping the wood,
adding play, making the next
dinner worse. Play invites
force. Force invites play. You
can date a family's patience by
the size of those ovals.

Prevention is the equalizer, the
lock that pulls, the sag bar,
the winter opening, the fifth
leg without shame. Cure, once
the party has started, is
honesty: stop the meal, throw
the catch, put a shim under a
foot, do not pretend. Pretending
is how a rack becomes a story
the family tells for a decade,
which is a very expensive
millimeter.

If you are the guest, do not
diagnose out loud unless asked.
If you are the host, do not joke
the table into being charming. A
charming failure is still a
failure with wine on it.
