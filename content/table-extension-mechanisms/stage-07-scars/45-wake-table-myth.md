# A story that sold more tables than it explained

I put this in scars because a
false origin is a kind of wear.

The wake-table story says a
drop-leaf was built to the length
of a man so a coffin could lie
on it in the parlor. Irish,
Irish-American, sometimes just
"country." It is a good story.
It has death, dinner, and a
secret function. It makes a
plain table feel like an
artifact. Dealers know this.
I have heard it attached to
objects whose hinges were
replaced in 1985.

Using a dining table at a wake
is ordinary history. Houses had
few large planes. A body is a
guest who does not sit. Of
course the table was used. That
is not the same as a form
invented for the corpse.

I have not seen a period shop
book that says *cut it for the
box*. I have seen inventories
that list tables and, elsewhere,
the facts of a death. The leap
between them is ours. If a
reader has a primary source, I
want it. Until then I will call
the coffin origin a sales
myth — not a lie about use,
a lie about intent.

Why it belongs in a mechanism
series: myth hides the actual
supports. People stop looking
at the swinging arms and start
looking for a moral. The leaf
still sags. The gate still
steals a knee. The closed table
is still the Tuesday object.
None of that cares about the
story.

There is a kinder reading. A
community that laid out its
dead on the dining table was a
community whose most important
plane was the same plane as
dinner. That is worth keeping
without the gunsmith precision
of a fake specification. The
table was the house's largest
truth. Sometimes it held a
roast. Sometimes it held a
person. The hinges were for
the room, not for the coffin.

I would rather that kindness
than a dealer script. Kindness
does not prevent you from
checking whether the rule
joint is original.
