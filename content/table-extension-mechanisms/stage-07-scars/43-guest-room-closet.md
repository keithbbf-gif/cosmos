# The leaf that did not make the move

Fine Woodworking published makers who
were hired to build the missing
leaves. That sentence should be on
the inside of every slide carton.

The scar is ordinary. A household
moves. The table is obvious. The
leaves are in a closet that was
already a junk closet. Someone
labels the box WRONG or does not
label it. The new dining room is
smaller. The box stays in the old
garage for a month that becomes a
year. By the time anyone misses
Thanksgiving, the extra sitters
are eating on laps and the
original flitch is a memory.

I have also seen the quieter
version: the leaves made the move
and then lived in a basement that
taught them a new width. They
"made it." They just cannot come
to dinner without a plane and a
fight.

Self-storing is the mechanical
answer to this scar. A fitted case
that is too handsome to leave
behind is the Jupe answer. A
checklist on the underside of the
top — *leaves: 2, case: dining
closet* — is the cheap answer and
it works until someone sands the
checklist off.

The social answer is to stop
treating extra boards as
accessories. They are the table
in another tense. You would not
move a car and leave the wheels
in a guest room. You would not
move a gateleg and leave the
gates. Loose leaves are gates
that detached.

When I am asked to make
replacements I ask for the old
ones if they exist, even if they
are wrong, because a wrong old
leaf still knows the pin spacing.
If they do not exist I warn that
the new boards will be a year
younger forever. People nod and
then are surprised anyway. I
write the warning on the invoice
now. Invoices survive more often
than verbal wisdom.

If you are designing, count this
scar as likely. Design as if the
household will move twice and
argue once. That is not cynicism.
That is the data.
