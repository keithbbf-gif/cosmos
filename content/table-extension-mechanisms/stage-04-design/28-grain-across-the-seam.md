# Grain across the seam

Bookmatch is a dare you issue to the closet.

A maker splits a flitch, opens it like a book, and
runs the figure across the place where the table
will later come apart. Closed, it is a magic trick.
Open, if the leaf came from the next boards, the
trick can continue. Open, if the leaf came from a
different tree because the first tree ended, the
seam becomes a border between countries.

I love a continuous cathedral that survives one
cut. I do not love a catalog that implies the
leaf was always part of the same sentence. Ask
where the extra boards came from. Same log is
the only answer that keeps the dare honest.
Same species is a shrug. "Matching" is a word
that has covered a lot of almost.

There is a second dare: the seam itself as a
design line. Some tops want the figure to die
into a straight joint and start again, like
panels in a door. That can be calmer than a
bookmatch that almost lines up and therefore
looks drunk. Almost is worse than honest
difference. The eye will hunt the missed peak
of a cathedral for the entire soup course.

Veneer makes the dare cheaper and more fragile.
You can bookmatch a skin across a seam that
the substrate does not care about. Then the
leaf lives in the dark and the skins age
apart. Solid wood at least argues in the same
language as the seasons. Veneer argues with
glue and with ultraviolet and with the fact
that a stored skin never got the same sun.

If I am choosing, I would rather see the seam
acknowledged: a thin inlay, a change of
direction, a breadboard that turns the end
grain into a frame. Give the eye a place to
stop on purpose. Accidental mismatch is the
thing that makes guests touch the table and
say *huh*. You do not want *huh* at a table.
You want the roast.

For folding center leaves the hinge line is
another grain cut, usually across the figure.
That one I try to hide in a quieter part of
the board, not through the loudest curl.
Vanity in the wrong place is how a clever
mechanism spends the meal introducing itself.
