# If it only looks right open

I will say this until it sticks. The open table
is a special case. The closed table is the
furniture.

Designers who fall in love with the extended
span — the long run of walnut, the twelve
chairs, the runner that finally has a job —
will steal from the closed object to feed that
love. Overhangs that are correct at 96 inches
become stubby at 60. A pedestal that is a
calm cross under a banquet becomes a heavy
hub under a breakfast. A seam that is a
minor event in a long plane becomes the
entire subject of a short one.

The inverse theft happens too. A closed table
drawn as a perfect six-seater, then asked to
open, will show you every place the drawing
lied. The edge profile does not turn the
corner onto the leaf. The apron stops. The
base is still standing where six people
needed it, not where ten do.

I use a simple studio rule. Draw both states
at the same scale and put them on the wall
together. If I would not photograph the
closed one for the front of the book, the
design is not done. Holiday styling is the
back of the book.

Drop-leaves have an advantage here. Their
closed state is a different silhouette, not
a reduced one. A gateleg shut is a new
object, narrow and vertical in the flaps.
You can make that object handsome. Split
tops have a harder job: they must be the
same object, shorter, with a seam. That
seam needs a reason. A rule joint. A
subtle bead. A line that looks like a
choice. Two raw edges butted in a hurry
look like a table that is waiting for its
real length, which is to say they look
impatient.

Live with a cardboard mockup of the closed
overhangs. Sit at it. Put a laptop on it.
If the mockup feels like a coffee table
that grew legs, your closed dining table
is too small for the base you chose. Fix
that before you order slides. Slides will
not give you back Tuesday.
