# The person who sits on the joint

Someone always gets the crack.

You can pretend the leaves fall in the
aisles between settings. They do not, not
once the chairs are pulled in and a couple
has decided to sit together. A plate lands
on the seam. A wine glass finds the little
ditch. A knife clicks. That person is now
in a relationship with your mechanism.

Design for them.

Keep the seam tight enough that a thin-footed
glass does not rock. That is pins, locks, a
plane, not hope. Keep the height match
inside the thickness of a fingernail. If
you cannot, do not use stemware on that
table, which is a coward's specification
but an honest one.

Place settings can be drawn so the seam
runs under a charger instead of under a
pea. A charger is a bridge. I would rather
specify chargers than pretend the seam is
invisible. On round radials you have more
seams, so you have more of these people.
The wedges should meet with a tightness
that does not catch a tablecloth. Cloths
help and they also snag. I have watched a
host tug a cloth and lift a filling piece
a millimeter. The person on that joint
felt it as a ghost.

Socially, do not put the oldest guest or
the person you are trying to impress on
the worst seam. That sounds like etiquette
from a dead century. It is also engineering:
those seats are where you notice. Put a
child there and they will play with the
crack. Put a bore there and they will
explain how they would have built it.
Neither improves the roast.

If the joint must fall in a setting, give
that setting the best chair and the most
stable leaf — the one with the sag bar,
the one over a pedestal, not the diving
board at the end of a draw. Compensate in
the floor story for what you cannot hide
in the plane.

I measure success by whether that person
forgets. If they leave talking about the
food, the joint did its job. If they leave
talking about the table, the table talked
too much.
