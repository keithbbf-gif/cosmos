# The gap under the top is the tell

People photograph tabletops. People sit
under them.

The shadow line where the top meets the
apron is the first thing a sitter sees
that is not plates. On a closed table
that line should run. On an open table
it should still run, or stop on purpose.
A sudden cave into hardware is a tell
that the designer worked in plan view.

I crawl a chair in before I praise a
prototype. I look for the lantern effect:
daylight punching through the center
gap, lighting the slides like a shop
tour. I look for a ridge where a slide
sits too close to the underside and
reads through as a welt. I look for
apron pieces that do not share a
profile — a fancy ogee that dies into
a flat added skirt. Those are not sins
of craft only. They are sins of where
the eye actually lives.

A small, even reveal around a leaf
apron is handsome. It says the extra
board is a citizen with its own hem.
A ragged dark bay says the extra board
is a patch. You can feel the difference
without knowing the words.

Painted tables hide some of this and
then reveal it in chips. Dark stains
hide less than people think; they turn
the gap into a mouth. A light oil makes
every interruption obvious, which I
prefer, because then the maker has to
deal with it.

If the language of the table is
"honest structure" — trestles, visible
joinery — then show the slides on
purpose. Machine them as objects. Do
not leave galvanized stampings in a
walnut sermon. Honesty is not the same
as neglect.

Candlelight is a second critic. A
gap that is only a gray smudge at
noon becomes a little stage at
dinner: every zinc slide a
highlight, every screw a star.
If you light the table for people,
you have also lit the wound.

The tell never lies. Guests may not
name it. They will remember that the
table felt finished, or that they
could see its bones and the bones
were a mess.
