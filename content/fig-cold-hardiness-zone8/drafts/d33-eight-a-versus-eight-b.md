Eight-b is 15–20°F on the average extreme. Eight-a
is 10–15°F. Five degrees. It does not sound like a
personality. It is one. I have friends in 8b who
honestly never wrap, have Missions that look like
small trees, and think 8a people are anxious. I
have 8a nights that would have rewritten their
yard.

Five degrees is the difference between "wood usually
holds on a hardy fig" and "wood is a coin flip if
the plant is young, windy, or still soft." It is
the difference between a pot that can sometimes
spend a mild winter against a wall and a pot that
is lying to you if it stays out. It is the
difference between wrapping as a rare event and
wrapping as a tool you keep sharp.

Coastal 8b with water nearby is even softer. Inland
8a with a clear still night is harder than the
number. Always look at water, wind, and whether
your 8b is a real 8b or a map courtesy.

I do not try to win the attitude argument. I try
to keep the right plants. If I lived in a true 8b
I would still mulch first-year crowns and I would
still own a frost cloth. I would plant one fig
that is a little less hardy, because I could. In
8a I spend that courage on siting, not on variety
greed.

If the 2023 map moved you from 8a to 8b, do not
throw the cages away the first November. Keep
them until you have seen the decade low, not the
new color. Maps change faster than a fig's memory
of 11°F.

I keep a crude personal rule. In 8b I
will try one less-hardy fig in the
ground. In 8a I will try that fig in
a pot first. Same person. Five degrees.
The rule has saved me from a Mission
in an inland lot that the map painted
pretty.

When friends argue 8a vs 8b they are
often arguing coast vs hill, or last
winter vs the decade. Ask them their
actual low, their wind, their pot or
ground. The half-zone is a start.
The argument is usually about a yard
they have not described.

I drove an hour last winter from my 8a lot to an 8b friend's
and the figs were not in a different country. His were taller.
Mine had more dieback on the open plant. His pots were still
outside. Mine were in the garage. We drank coffee and argued
about whether I was anxious. Then I asked his decade low. He
did not know it. I knew mine: 9°F, once, with wind. That is
not anxiety. That is a number.

If you are new to a half-zone, rent a cheap thermometer for
a winter before you retire the cages. The map is a letter of
introduction. The thermometer is the conversation.
Five degrees is small on a chart and large on a young cane. I budget for large.
