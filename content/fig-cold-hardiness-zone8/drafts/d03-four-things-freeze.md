People say "the fig froze" as if the plant were one object with one death. It
is four objects that fail at different temperatures, and they fail in a
different order depending on whether you are in the ground or in a pot.

Wood is the part you see in March and argue with. Dormant fig wood on a hardy
cultivar can take more than people think. Chicago Hardy is commonly rated near
10°F for stems. A lot of ordinary figs start looking injured in the mid-teens
if the cold sits, if they were still soft, or if the wind was a thief. Wood
injury is not binary. You get tip burn. You get a cane that leafs from halfway
down. You get a trunk that looks fine and then collapses in a warm week because
the cambium was cooked on the southwest side. Scratch the bark. Green and wet
is life. Brown and dry is last year's story.

Buds are ruder. A cane can be alive and the fruit buds can be toast. That is
how you lose the breba without losing the plant. Late cold after the buds have
moved is worse than a colder night in January when the plant is actually
asleep. I have had 19°F in early March do more crop damage than 12°F in
January, because January found closed buds and March found a plant that had
already believed in spring.

Roots are the part pot people learn the expensive way. In the ground, a mulched
fig root system is often the toughest organ on the plant. Published ratings for
Chicago Hardy roots run down around −20°F. I have not personally offered a fig
that number, and I do not need to. The point is the gap: roots in soil, under
mulch, in 8a, are usually not the thing that dies. Roots in a black plastic pot
on a porch are a different animal. The pot is a block of soil standing in the
air. It freezes from the sides. It freezes from the bottom if you set it on
concrete. A pot can kill roots at temperatures that would only sting in-ground
wood. That is why "my in-ground fig is fine, so the patio one will be fine"
is a sentence that empties wallets.

Fruit is the fourth. Unripe figs hanging into a hard frost are finished. They
turn glassy, then brown, then a smell you will not put in your mouth. Virginia
Tech's backyard fig guide says to strip remaining leaves and fruit after a hard
frost (they use less than 29°F). I do it because rotten fruit on a wrapping
project is a gift to mold. The plant does not need those figs. You were going
to lose them anyway.

When someone tells you a variety is hardy to Zone 6, ask which of the four they
mean. Usually they mean the roots will send up new canes and you will get a
main crop if the season is long enough. That is a real kind of hardy. It is
not the same as keeping a six-foot tree with last year's wood and a June
breba. In 8a you can often have both, if you site the plant and do not leave a
pot out like a dare. In 7 you should decide in advance which hardy you are
buying: keep-the-wood hardy, or come-back-from-the-crown hardy. They are both
figs. They are not the same winter.
