The first bad March I had with figs, I almost dug the plant. It looked like I
had installed a coat rack. No buds swelling. Bark dull. I kicked the mulch,
saw what I thought was a dead crown, and went inside to look up replacements.
A neighbor who has been growing Celeste since before the 2012 zone map told me
to leave it until the redbuds were done. I left it. In April it pushed pink
from the base like nothing had happened. By August I was picking a main crop
off wood that had not existed in February.

That is the fig contract in Zone 7 and in the colder 8a winters. The top is
optional. The crown, if the roots are alive, is not. UT Gardens puts it
plainly: "hardy" is as much about the roots as the limbs, and a short-season
main crop on new wood is why these plants still feed you after a kill-back.
Chicago Hardy, Celeste, a decent Brown Turkey — they are built to take that
insult.

Dieback still costs you. You lose height. You lose the breba. You lose the
feeling of having a tree, which matters more than people admit if you planted
the fig as a little shade by the kitchen steps. You also lose time. A plant
that burned to the ground is a shrub that year. It will fruit if you picked a
variety that ripens on new wood in 90 to 110 days and if your 8a summer is not
a monsoon followed by an early October. If you planted a slow, late fig
because the fruit looked like a magazine, dieback can mean no ripe fruit at
all. That is not the plant being dramatic. That is arithmetic.

How you read dieback in March:

- Scratch from the tip down. Stop when you hit green. That is your live wood.
- Do not cut yet. Wait until you see actual buds or obvious collapse. Live
  wood that looks asleep will fool you.
- If the whole cane is tan and dry down to the soil, look at the crown. If the
  crown is firm and you see basal buds, you have a plant. Mulch it. Water it
  when the soil is working. Do not fertilize it into a second childhood until
  it has leaves.
- If the crown is mush or the pot is light and the roots are black, you have
  a stick. Then you can grieve.

I stopped calling a killed-back fig a failure. I call it a year the plant
spent as a perennial rather than a small tree. In 8a that year should be
uncommon on an established in-ground plant in a decent spot. It should be
expected on a first-year cutting, on a pot left out, and on anything you
planted in a windy corner because the sunny spot was already taken by tomatoes.

Zone 9 people use "dieback" for a different event — a freak night, or a young
plant, or a variety that never belonged there in a wet freeze. If their fig
dies to the ground every year, they planted wrong or they have a drainage
problem they are blaming on winter. Zone 7 people should budget for dieback
the way they budget for firewood. 8a is the place where dieback is a weather
story, not a lifestyle, unless you insist on treating a pot like it is in the
ground.
