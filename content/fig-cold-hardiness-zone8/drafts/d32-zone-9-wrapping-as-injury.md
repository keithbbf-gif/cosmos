Zone 9a averages an extreme minimum in the 20–25°F
band. That is a citrus night, not a fig funeral. A
dormant fig of a normal variety, in the ground, in
9, does not need a season-long coat. It needs
drainage, sun, and a person who will throw a frost
cloth on the one ugly advection freeze and take it
off in the morning.

I have watched 9 people wrap like they live in
Lexington because a group chat got loud. They left
the plastic on through a 70°F week in January. They
invented a disease problem and called it bad luck.
The luck was moisture and darkness they installed.

Wrapping as injury looks like: bark slip, mildew on
the bundle, canes that break when you unwrap because
they steamed and then snapped, a crown that stayed
wet under a sealed tarp. The plant would have taken
22°F naked. It could not take three weeks of wet
wool.

What 9 should still do: mulch a young crown, move
pots on the rare hard night, strip rotten fruit,
and not fertilize in the fall. What 9 should not
do: build the 8a cage as décor.

I include this in an 8a set because 8a people move,
and because 8a has 9 winters. Some Januarys here
never go below 25. Those Januarys are when I leave
the established plants alone and I look like I am
neglecting them. I am not. I am refusing to injure
them with a ritual.

If you are in 9 and you lose figs, look at
nematodes, water, and the wet freeze you ignored
for 36 hours. Do not look at a Kentucky wrapping
video first. If you are in 8a and you are about
to wrap a mature Celeste for a forecast of 24°F
and rain, walk away from the tarp. That is a Zone
9 night that wandered north. Treat it like one.

A 9a night that deserves cloth: still
air, 24°F, young bark, or a pot on a
pad. A 9a month that does not deserve
a cage: January in the 40s with one
graphic that scared you on a Monday.
Take the graphic seriously for 36
hours. Then take the cloth off. The
injury happens in the leaving-it-on.

If you moved from 7 to 9, your hands
will want to wrap. Give them a citrus
or a banana, or give them a mulch
pile. The fig does not need your old
job. It needs you to learn a new
kind of restraint.

If you are in 9 and you lost wood, ask
whether the wood was even dormant. Warm
falls keep figs growing. A 25°F night on
soft wood is ruder than a 25°F night on
gray wood. Your job there is to stop
feeding and let the plant sleep, not to
invent a Kentucky coat.

8a people should hear that too. Our warm
falls are getting longer. The wrap date
slides later for a reason.
