"Hardy to Zone 7" on a website means a person in marketing looked at other
websites. Sometimes there is a real trial behind it. Sometimes there is a
plant that lived in a protected courtyard in 7b and sent up new canes after
the top died, which is a kind of success, and then the catalog wrote "Zone 7"
like it was an apple.

Yard hardy is narrower. In my 8a dirt, yard hardy means: established plant,
in the ground, decent drainage, not a wind tunnel, and I still have
scaffolding wood after a normal worst night in the mid-teens. If I only have
a living stump and a late main crop, I will still keep the plant, but I will
not call it yard hardy. I will call it root hardy. Those words should not be
allowed to date.

A short, unromantic list of what I treat as actually useful in 7–9:

- Chicago Hardy / Bensonhurst Purple — the floor. Wood often cited to about
  10°F; roots far colder. Fruit is fine, not a religion.
- Celeste and Improved Celeste — southern workhorses, closed eye, small figs
  you will eat standing up. Better in heat than people from Chicago expect.
- A *known* Brown Turkey clone from a neighbor who fruits it, not a tag.
- LSU Purple, LSU Gold, LSU Tiger — bred for the Gulf South. I want them in
  8a and 9 more than I want them as a Zone 6 bet.
- Violette de Bordeaux, Petite Negra — small, good, worth a pot or a wall if
  you are picky about flavor.
- Olympian, Florea — the "maybe I can keep wood in 7" conversation.

What I do not treat as 8a-automatic: Black Mission, a lot of Kadota, anything
that needs a wasp, anything sold as "Italian honey fig" with no cultivar
name, and any plant whose only credential is a pretty oval on a box-store
tag.

Zone 7 should start with the floor varieties and a plan for dieback or a
plan for a pot. Zone 9 can grow almost anything that is parthenocarpic and
then lose it to nematodes, rust, or a wet freeze they did not take
seriously. 8a can get greedy. Greedy is how Mission ends up on the north
corner of the house.

When a seller says hardy, I ask three questions:

1. Hardy as wood, or hardy as roots?
2. In the ground or in a pot?
3. After a still night or after a windy week?

If they cannot answer, they are selling a story. I will still buy a fig from
a storyteller if the cutting came from a yard I can walk through in March.
A March yard is the only catalog I trust.

I have a shoebox of tags. Hardy. Super hardy. Zone 5. Zone 6 with
protection. None of them mention wind, a pot, or a plant that was
still pushing tips in November. The catalog is not lying in a
courtroom sense. It is answering a different question than the one
I ask in March: do I have wood I can pick from before the main
crop, or do I have a stump.

Yard hardy also includes the summer. A fig that holds wood and then
sours every wet week is not hardy in a humid 8a. It is winter-hardy
and summer-fragile. I will take a slightly tender, closed-eye fig
over an iron cane that feeds yellowjackets. Catalogs almost never
say that, because catalogs are not eating the fruit in a July rain.

If you are shopping this week, buy the plant you can walk to in
someone's yard, or buy the floor variety and a second fig you
actually want to eat. Two plants beat one famous name. The famous
name is how you end up with one data point and a lot of feelings.
