USDA zones are built from the average annual extreme minimum. Read that again,
slower. Average. Annual extreme. Minimum. It is not the coldest night that will
ever happen in your driveway. It is not the coldest week. It is a statistic of
the worst night in a year, averaged across years. Zone 8a means that statistic
lands between 10 and 15°F. It does not mean the air is forbidden from going to
6°F. It does not mean last January's 22°F low "proves" you are really 9a now.

People shop that way anyway. They type a zip code, get 8a, buy a Black Mission
because a tag said 8–10, and feel betrayed when the top dies. The tag was doing
zone math. The plant was doing weather.

The 2023 map shifted a lot of neighborhoods warmer. That is a real change in
the averages. It is also a trap. A warmer average can coexist with a nastier
outlier. I have watched 8a people retire their wraps after two gentle winters
and then meet a night that would have been ordinary in 1985. The fig does not
care that the map got prettier. The fig cares whether the wood had finished
hardening, whether the wind was moving, and how many hours the temperature sat
under 15.

If you want a number you can use, keep two:

1. The zone number, so you know what the *usual* worst night looks like.
2. The lowest temperature your own thermometer has seen in the last ten years,
   or the lowest your county's cooperative observer station recorded.

The second number is the one that kills wood. In much of 8a the usual worst
night is the mid-teens. The decade worst night is often single digits. Plan the
planting and the variety for the first. Plan the wrap, the pot move, and the
mulch pile for the second. That is the whole method. It is not elegant. It
works.

Zone 7 is the same idea with uglier arithmetic. 7b is 5–10°F on the average
extreme. 7a is 0–5°F. Those people should not be talking themselves into
"maybe I am secretly 8" because of one January thaw. Zone 9a is 20–25°F. Those
people should not be building Zone 7 cages because a Facebook group in Kentucky
had a good year with one.

I write the winter down. Date, low, wind, whether the fig was still holding
leaves, whether I had watered the week before. In spring I walk the canes with
a thumbnail. Green under the bark or tan and dry. That pair of notes — what the
night did, what the wood did — is worth more than another zone map printout on
the fridge.

If your house sits in a hole, you are colder than the zip code. If you sit on
a slope above a river with a brick wall behind the fig, you are warmer. The
zone does not know about your wall. You do. Use the zone as a starting rumor.
Then measure.
