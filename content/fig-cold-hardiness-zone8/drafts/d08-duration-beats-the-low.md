A 10°F snap that arrives after midnight and is gone by 9 a.m. is a different
animal from four days that never climb through 20. I used to read forecasts
for the low and go to bed. I still read the low. I also count the hours
under 20, and I care whether the days recover.

Wood has a thermal mass. So does a pot. So does the ground. A short plunge
does not always finish the job the number suggests. A long soak at 18°F can
finish a plant that "should have" been fine at 18. Pots are the clearest
example. A 20-gallon container will ride through one ugly night if it went
into the evening wet-but-not-soggy and sitting on soil. The same pot, after
three sunless freezing days, is a block of ice with roots in it. In-ground
plants have the planet as a battery. Pots have Tuesday.

This is why I do not panic at a single 8a headline number, and why I do panic
at a stalled Arctic pattern. The stalled one is when I move every pot, even
the ones I was being lazy about. It is when I check the wrap for a wet
collapse. It is when I stop telling myself the brick wall will handle it.

Duration also decides whether snow helps. A snow blanket on the crown during
a two-day cold is useful insulation. A snow blanket that melts into the wrap
and then refreezes for a week is a different gift. Same sky. Different stay.

Zone 7 is almost defined by duration. Their cold does not visit. It rents.
That is why they build cages they can live with for months, not emergency
sheets they throw on at 10 p.m. Zone 9 is defined by short insults — a
radiation frost, a one-off advection freeze — and overbuilding for duration
is how they rot a plant. 8a has both winters in the same decade. You need a
light kit for the snap and a heavier kit for the week. You do not need to
deploy the heavy kit every November.

I keep a stupid little note on the calendar: "hours below 20, hours below
15." In spring, when I walk the canes, those hours explain more than the
official low in the newspaper. A year with a 9°F hour and a fast recovery
has left me more wood than a year with a 14°F low that lasted from Thursday
to Sunday.

If you only have energy for one forecast habit, stop screenshotting the low
and start looking at the hourly ribbon. When that ribbon lies down in the
teens, treat the fig like the cold is moving in, not passing through.

I started keeping the hourly ribbon because of one pot I lost while
bragging about the official low. The newspaper said 16°F. True. What
I did not read was Friday afternoon through Sunday morning, never
above 21, wind on the porch, a five-gallon can that went into Friday
damp and came out a brick. The in-ground Celeste ten feet away kept
its wood. Same night on a map. Different stay in the pot.

A useful 8a habit: if the hourly chart shows two or more nights
strung together under 20, I treat pots as a move even if the printed
low looks "only" mid-teens. If the chart shows a V — down to 11 at
6 a.m., 28 by noon — I still protect the young in-ground plant, and
I do not rebuild the whole yard. The V is a slap. The flat line is
a sit-in.

Zone 7 lives on flat lines. Their cages are furniture. Zone 9 lives
on V's. Their cloth is a nightgown. 8a has to own both shapes in
the same decade, which is why a single number on a phone widget
makes fools of us. I screenshot the ribbon now, not the badge.
