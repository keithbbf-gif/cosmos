I plant Chicago Hardy the way I keep a spare key. It is not my favorite fig.
It is the fig that makes the rest of the collection honest. Missouri
Botanical Garden rates the stems to 10°F and the roots to −20°F. Those are
published ratings, not a pact with my thermometer. In 8a, that rating means
the plant is allowed to be in the ground without me hovering like a
helicopter parent — once it has a year of roots.

The fruit is medium, purple-brown, pink inside, sweet enough, a little
one-note if you have been eating Celeste and Violette de Bordeaux in the
same week. It will make a main crop on new wood if the top dies. That is the
feature. In Zone 7 that feature is the whole product. In Zone 9 the plant
is almost overqualified for winter and under-discussed for flavor. In 8a it
is the plant I give people who say they killed two figs and want to know if
they are cursed. They are usually not cursed. They usually had a pot on
concrete, or a Mission, or both.

What Chicago Hardy will not do: make you look like a Mediterranean postcard.
It wants to be a bush if winter keeps knocking it down. Let it. Bush form
is how figs take cold — wood closer to the ground, easier to wrap, easier
to harvest, easier to throw a frost cloth over a late April surprise. If you
insist on a single trunk in 7 or cold 8a, you are volunteering for extra
work so you can mow under it. I have done that. I do not recommend it as a
first fig.

It is also not freeze-proof at 10°F on the nose. Duration, wind, a plant
that still had leaves, a young trunk — all of that moves the real number.
I have seen tip dieback on Chicago Hardy after nights that "should not"
have bothered it. I have also seen it keep wood through weather that took a
neighbor's unnamed Turkey to the dirt. That is why it is a floor. You start
there. You do not stop thinking.

If I could only keep one in-ground fig in a real Zone 7, it would be this
or a proven Celeste, and I would mulch the crown like I was putting a child
to bed. In 8a I keep Chicago Hardy *and* something I like more, because the
floor plant is how I still have figs in the year I get greedy and lose the
fancy one.

Do not pay extra for "Hardy Chicago" versus "Chicago Hardy" versus
"Bensonhurst Purple" as if they were a collector's set. You want a cutting
from a plant that has already survived a winter near you. The name is a
clue. The March wood is the proof.

I have given Chicago Hardy cuttings to three people who "could not
grow figs." Two of them still have the plant. One left it in a pot
on a second-story balcony and then told me hardy figs were a myth.
The myth was the balcony.

If you already own a fancier fig and it lives, you do not have to
rip it out and plant this. The floor is for the hole you have not
filled, and for the year you get greedy. I would rather have a
boring fig in August than a sophisticated stick in March.

One practical note: Chicago Hardy still wants sun and drainage. I
have seen it planted in a wet shade "because it's tough" and then
blamed when it sulked. Tough is winter language. It is not a
license to pick the worst spot in the yard. Put it where a fig
belongs, and let the toughness be the bonus, not the excuse.
