Celeste is the fig I actually want to eat in a humid 8a. Small. Skin
bronze to purple. Pulp pink. Honey-sweet without the jammy weight that
makes some larger figs feel like dessert for someone else. The ostiole —
the eye — tends to stay closed. That sounds like trivia until you have
watched an open-eye fig sour from the inside after a warm rain while the
Celeste on the same fence line stayed clean.

Cold-wise, Celeste is a southern hardy, not a Chicago story. Virginia
extension puts it in 7–9. It will die back in a bad 7 and still be a plant.
In 8a, established in-ground Celeste is the closest thing I have to a
fig that behaves. I still mulch the crown. I still do not leave a young
one naked in a windy 10°F forecast. I do not treat it like a camellia
either.

Improved Celeste (the LSU one people call a sugar fig) is in this family
of usefulness. Heat tolerance is the extra. If your 8a is really a long
hot summer with a wet shoulder — a lot of the interior South — I want that
bloodline more than I want another "hardy" fig bred for a short northern
season and a dry cold.

The closed eye does not make Celeste winter-proof. It makes the *summer*
survivable, which is half of why people think they failed at figs in 8a
when they did not. They kept the plant alive and then the fruit fermented
on the branch. They blame the variety's hardiness because hardiness is the
word everyone knows. The eye was the problem.

Zone 9 should plant Celeste before they plant a romance fig they saw in a
California photo. Zone 7 can plant it if they accept dieback years and a
smaller plant, or if they have a wall. 8a is Celeste's house, especially
if you are tired of wrapping like it is a personality.

One caution: Celeste is a name that gets stuck on small brown figs the way
Brown Turkey gets stuck on everything else. If you can, take a cutting from
a yard where the fruit stays closed and the plant has a winter history. If
you are buying a pot in March, you are buying a hope. Hope is fine. Just
do not build a whole essay of expectations on a sticker.

I pick Celeste before it looks ready by grocery-store rules. The
neck softens. The fruit hangs. The skin dulls. If you wait for it
to swell like a Turkey you will pick a raisin that the birds
already voted on. That is not a winter fact, but it is why I
bother to keep the wood: the fruit is worth the wrap on a young
plant.

In a bad 8a winter Celeste will still usually send a main crop if
the crown lives. The figs will be on new wood and a little later.
I can live with later. I cannot live with an open-eye fig that
ferments in the same week I finally have a crop. That is the
closed-eye tax I am happy to pay.

If your Celeste never closes the eye, you may not have Celeste.
Keep it if it eats well. Rename it in your head. The name on the
stick is not a blood test. The rain will tell you what you bought.
