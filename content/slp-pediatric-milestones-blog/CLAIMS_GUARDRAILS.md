# Claims guardrails — pediatric speech & language milestones

Internal. Not a blog post. These drafts are parent-facing education for **SLPWOW**. They are not a clinic chart, not a screening instrument, and not a diagnosis.

If a sentence would let a parent leave thinking *we just named what is wrong with my child*, cut it. Do not soften it into a how-to.

Counsel / a licensed SLP still owns the last read before any URL goes live.

## What this pack is allowed to be

A magazine-section explanation of how communication usually unfolds from birth through five, written so a tired parent can use it at the kitchen table. The legal and ethical object is **surveillance education**: what researchers and public-health checklists have published, what those numbers mean, and how to *talk with* a pediatrician or an ASHA-certified speech-language pathologist.

It is not evaluation. It is not treatment. It is not “red-flag SEO.”

## The documents, not the vibe

- CDC *Learn the Signs. Act Early.* checklists (revised February 2022; printable set updated 2025) are **communication tools**. They place items at ages when **at least 75%** of children would be expected to show the skill. They are **not** a standardized developmental screen and **not** a diagnostic test. Zubler et al., *Pediatrics* 2022; Abercrombie, Wiggins & Green, *Zero to Three* 2022; CDC checklist footer.
- ASHA’s birth-to-5 communication milestones are also written for **75%** and say on the page: not a screening or diagnostic tool. [asha.org/public/developmental-milestones](https://www.asha.org/public/developmental-milestones/communication-milestones/).
- AAP recommends **universal developmental screening** with validated tools at 9, 18, and 30 months, and autism-specific screening at 18 and 24 months *or whenever a parent or clinician has a concern* (Lipkin & Macias, *Pediatrics* 2020). A blog post does not perform those screens.
- IDEA Part C (birth–3) and Part B (3–21) are **access paths**. Naming the door is allowed. Promising eligibility is not.
- ASHA’s *Identify the Signs* campaign lists observations that warrant a conversation. It does not license us to label a child.

## Never say (diagnosis / labeling)

- “Your child has a speech delay / language disorder / autism / ADHD / apraxia / dysarthria / phonological disorder / social-communication disorder.”
- “This is a late talker, which means…” as if the phrase were a diagnosis. “Late talking” in research is a **description of a measured vocabulary at a measured age**. It is not a clinic stamp you can apply from a blog.
- “If they miss this item, they have X.”
- “This checklist shows your child is delayed.”
- “Most late talkers catch up, so you don’t need to ask.” (The first clause is a research finding about *groups*. The second is the wait-and-see scar CDC revised the 2022 lists to reduce.)
- Any sentence a reasonable parent would hear as a diagnosis of themselves or their child.
- Invented case vignettes with ages + unique details that could be read as a real child (“Maya, 22 months, in our Fayetteville clinic…”). Composite kitchen scenes without names are allowed if they teach an observation, not a label.

## Never say (treatment / DIY clinic)

- Home therapy protocols: cycles, minimal-pair lists to drill, PROMPT cues, “do these ten apraxia exercises,” fluency-shaping steps, PECS phase training, “use this AAC app this way.”
- “Try this at home to fix / treat / catch them up.”
- Dosing of medication, supplement stacks for language, “ask your doctor about [drug] for speech.”
- Guarantees of outcome (“early therapy will prevent…,” “this will stop later reading problems”).
- ASHA’s public tip that getting help early *can reduce later risk* may be quoted as ASHA’s sentence, not rewritten as our promise.

## Never say (myths that still get traffic)

- “Two languages confuse a child” or “bilingualism causes delay.” ASHA: speak the language(s) you know best. [asha.org — bilingualism](https://www.asha.org/public/speech/development/learning-more-than-one-language/).
- “Boys talk later, so wait.” Average sex differences, where they exist, are small and do not replace a conversation with a clinician.
- “Screen time causes autism.” Do not print this. AAP media guidance is about *time with people*, not etiology.
- “Pacifiers / sippy cups / thumb-sucking cause speech delay.” Evidence is mixed and mostly about dentition and duration. Say what is known. Do not convert a habit into a diagnosis.
- “Tongue-tie always causes speech problems” or “get a frenotomy and speech will come.” ASHA and AAO-HNS are cautious. Ankyloglossia is a medical finding, not a blog diagnosis, and speech outcomes after clip are not a settled parent-facing claim.
- “Baby sign / AAC will stop them talking.” ASHA: AAC does not replace speech; it is another way to communicate.
- “They’ll grow out of stuttering, ignore it.” Many preschoolers who repeat sounds do become fluent; that is a **group** finding (Yairi & Ambrose). A parent who is worried still gets to ask. Do not teach wait-and-see as a rule.
- Refrigerator-mother, “screens made them autistic,” vaccines, or any etiology rumor.

## Never say (brand / SEO)

- “Best pediatric SLP,” “#1 speech therapy,” city superlatives.
- Disease keywords paired with a booking CTA on these URLs (“autism speech delay — schedule today”).
- Schema `MedicalWebPage`, `MedicalCondition`, `Drug`, or `treats` / `indication`. `Article` is enough.
- Fake credentials or an invented clinical director.
- Patient testimonials in this folder.
- Affiliate wrappers on CDC.gov, ASHA.org, PubMed, or IDEA.ed.gov.

## Allowed shapes

- Dated, sourced description of what CDC, ASHA, AAP, NIDCD, and named papers actually printed.
- The difference between **surveillance** (checklists, conversation) and **screening** (validated tool) and **evaluation** (licensed clinician).
- “Most children (75% or more) can…” — always with the CDC/ASHA percentile in the same breath the first time it appears in a piece.
- “You know your child best. If you are worried, talk with the doctor. You can ask about screening, a hearing check, or a speech-language pathologist.” That is CDC’s own act-early language, not a diagnosis.
- Loss of skills a child used to have: name it as a reason to call **now**. Do not name a disease.
- Bilingual homes as typical, not a risk factor.
- A short “what you can do this week” that is **interaction**, not a protocol: talk about what you are doing, wait, read, sing, follow their point. ASHA already publishes these tips.
- The educational footer already in each draft.

## Percentiles, ranges, and honesty

A 50th-percentile chart and a 75th-percentile chart are different objects. The 2022 CDC revision moved many items later so a missed item would prompt a conversation instead of “maybe next month.” ASHA publicly worried some language items might *slow* referral. Both facts belong in the pack (essay 01). Do not pick a side and hide the other.

Do not invent n, percentages, or “most children say 50 words at 24 months” if the source you are holding says **30 months** (CDC 2022) or a research definition of late language emergence at **24 months** (Zubrick et al. 2007). When sources disagree, say they disagree.

Mark unverified numbers `[VERIFY]`. Do not fabricate a PMID.

## Hearing is not optional color

Newborn hearing screening (EHDI / JCIH) and later middle-ear fluid are part of language, not a sidebar. “Have you had a hearing check?” is an allowed question. “Your child has hearing loss” is not.

## Living people and real clinics

Do not invent a WOW Therapies / SLPWOW patient. Do not put Keith’s family in the copy. Public researchers (McLeod, Crowe, Zubler, Rescorla, Yairi, Hirsh-Pasek, Romeo) may be named for their papers. No health speculation.

## If asked for a diagnosis or a home program

Print the education. Refuse the diagnosis and the protocol in one sentence: this series does not evaluate children and does not publish treatment steps.
