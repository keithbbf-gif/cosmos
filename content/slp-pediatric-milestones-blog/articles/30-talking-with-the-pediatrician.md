**Disclaimer.** Educational only. Not medical advice. A well-child visit is short. This page is about using the minutes. It is not a script that forces a referral.

You have eight minutes and a child who will not demonstrate the wave on command. That is a normal visit.

CDC built the checklists to be **taken in**. The “other important things to share” box is the same at every age: what you do together, what they like, what concerns you, lost skills, prematurity or special health needs. You can treat that box as the agenda.

## Before you go

**Three stories, not a theory.** A time they got your attention. A time they understood a word. A time you worried. Stories beat adjectives (*delayed, stubborn, fine*).

**A list.** Oral words, signs, favorite gestures. Languages in the house. Who understands them.

**The page.** Print the CDC checklist for the age you are actually in, plus the one below if you want. Mark yes / not sure / not yet. You are not scoring a test. You are showing your work.

**The screen dates.** AAP wants general developmental screening at 9, 18, and 30 months, and autism-specific screening at 18 and 24 months or whenever you or the clinician is concerned (Lipkin & Macias, 2020). Write whether those happened. Ask for the **name of the tool**. “It was fine” fades. “We did the [name]; no follow-up” can travel to the next clinic.

## Sentences that fit in a visit

“I’m not asking you to diagnose. I want to know if we should screen or refer.”  
“They used to say *ba* for ball. I have not heard it in a month.”  
“I understand them. My mother does not.”  
“We speak Spanish at home. I don’t know how to count the English words.”  
“Can we check hearing?”  
“Can I have the early-intervention number even if you’re not sure?”

CDC prints the EI path for a reason. In many states you can **self-refer**. Essay 31. You are not going around the doctor. You are using a public door.

## What the doctor may do

Look at ears. Ask their own questions. Run or order a validated screen. Refer to audiology, SLP, EI, or a developmental clinic. Say “let’s see at the next visit.”

If the last one is the answer and your stomach is still tight, you can say so. “I hear watchful waiting. I am still worried. What would you need to refer now?” That is allowed. It is not rude. If they still wait, you can still call EI and you can still find an SLP (ASHA ProFind). Two doors.

## What not to do in the room

Do not accept a joke as a plan (“he’s a boy”).  
Do not accept “they’ll talk when they’re ready” as the only sentence (essay 39).  
Do not demand a label. There may not be one, and this series does not want one from a hallway.  
Do not perform anger at the child who will not wave. The wave still exists at home. You have the list.

## After

Write down what was decided, the tool name, the referral, the date to return. Put the next appointment in the phone before you leave the parking lot if you can.

If you got a “he’s fine” and you are not fine, you did not fail the visit. You gathered data. Use the other doors.

The pediatrician is a partner in surveillance. You live with the child. Both kinds of knowledge belong on the table. Bring yours in stories. Ask them to bring the screens they already promised every child.

## If the child performs perfectly in the room

They will wave for the doctor and not for you, or the reverse. Say so. “This is not the kitchen.” A good clinician will believe the kitchen. A rushed one may write “normal speech in office.” You can add a sentence to the portal later: “At home I still hear X.” Portals exist for the sentence that did not fit.

If you need an interpreter, ask when you book. Language access is part of the visit, not a favor (essay 23).

## A paper in the pocket, if you like papers

Lipkin and Macias, *Pediatrics* 2020, is the surveillance-and-screening policy. Zubler et al., 2022, is the milestone-methods paper. You do not need to print them. You can say “AAP wanted screens at 9, 18, and 30 months; can we do today’s?” That is enough citation for a hallway. The rest of this series can stay at home.
