**Disclaimer.** Educational only. Not medical advice. Not a screen and not a diagnosis. “Understood most of the time” is not a demand for perfect sounds.

Three is when strangers start having opinions in the grocery line.

CDC’s 3-year language line is the first one that sounds like school: talks with you in conversation using **at least two back-and-forth exchanges**; asks *who*, *what*, *where*, or *why* (“Where is mommy/daddy?”); says what action is happening in a picture; says first name when asked; talks well enough for **others to understand, most of the time**.

ASHA’s 2-to-3-year public page includes saying their name when asked. Identify the Signs invites a conversation if, at 3–4, speech is not easily understood by others. The public documents are aligned enough to use at the table.

## Two turns is a conversation

Not a TED talk. You: “What did you eat?” Them: “Cheese.” You: “Yellow cheese?” Them: “Yeah.” That is two exchanges. The skill is staying in the pocket with you — listening, then adding.

If they can monologue about trucks but cannot answer a simple question, say that pair. If they answer in one word and never offer a question, that is also a pair. CDC wants them *asking* question words, not only surviving yours.

Why-questions will be early and philosophical and exhausting. You can say “I don’t know” (CDC’s 4-year tips even encourage it). You can also answer the ones you can. The item is the ask, not the graduate seminar.

## Actions in pictures

*Running. Eating. Playing.* Verbs, again. A child who only names nouns in a book is doing 30-month work. A child who can say what is happening is doing 3-year work. Try it on a library book, not a flashcard app.

## First name

The name they are called. You do not need last name, address, or a safety lecture in this essay. If they say a nickname and that is the name in the house, that is the name.

## Understood most of the time — by others

This is the intelligibility item. CDC put it at three, not at two. “Others” means people who do not live in your kitchen.

Most of the time is not all of the time. Three-year-olds still smash consonants. *Th* and *r* can be years away (McLeod & Crowe, 2018; essay 13). The question is whether a grandparent or a teacher can follow the message without you translating every line.

If you are the only interpreter, and you are tired of being the only interpreter, that sentence is allowed at the visit. Essay 14 is the longer intelligibility map. Identify the Signs treats unclear speech to familiar people at 2–3 as a reason to ask; CDC’s “others, most of the time” at 3 is a slightly different bar. Bring both if you want. Do not average them into shame.

## After three, the door on the footer changes

Look at the CDC 3-year page carefully. The early-intervention phone number of the first two years is joined, then replaced in the older sheets, by **calling any local public elementary school** for a free evaluation. That is Part B / Child Find language, not a threat. Essay 31. You can still talk to an SLP in a clinic. You can do both. Eligibility is not this URL’s to decide.

## What you can do that is not speech homework

CDC’s 3-year tips: longer sentences than theirs; repeat *need nana* as “I want a banana”; two- or three-step directions in real life; songs and rhymes; questions about pictures — what is happening, what might happen next. Play with other children, because friendship is a language gym.

Correcting every sound is not on the list. ASHA’s general tips say do not interrupt to fix speech sounds; model the adult form in your own talk. That is the whole articulation program this series will give you.

Screen cap: AAP-style limit, adult in the room, no bedroom set. Essay 29.

## What three is not

It is not “all grammar.” Plurals and past tense are just getting going (essay 21).

It is not “ready to read.” Letter names sit on later cognitive lines.

It is not a diagnosis if one box is empty. It is a year when preschool teachers become extra ears. Listen to them without handing them the right to label. Bring their observations to the person who can evaluate.

## “Most of the time” in real places

The grocery line. The classroom rug. Grandma’s house. Those are three “others.” If Grandma understands everything because she raised you, she may not be “others.” If the teacher asks you to translate the whole morning, she is.

Hustad’s intelligibility curves and the old Coplan-and-Gleason staircase (essay 14) are clinic objects. CDC gave you a parent object: most of the time. You can say “the teacher gets about half” without a stopwatch.

## First name and stranger safety, briefly

Saying their name when asked is a language item. It is not a reason to teach an address to a three-year-old in this essay. If they will not say their name to a new adult, that can be temperament. If they will not say it to you either, mention it.

Ask who / what / where at the park about things they already see. If *why* arrives at 6 a.m., you can answer once and then say “more why after breakfast.” Limits are language. So is a real answer. CDC’s later tips even allow “I don’t know.” You are allowed to be a person, not an encyclopedia.
