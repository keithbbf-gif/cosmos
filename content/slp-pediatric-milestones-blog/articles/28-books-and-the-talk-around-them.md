**Disclaimer.** Educational only. Not a reading curriculum and not a diagnosis. You cannot fail bedtime.

CDC keeps dragging books onto the communication line. Looks at a few pages with you (18 months). Points to the bear (2 years). Names when you ask “What is this?” (30 months). Says the action in the picture (3 years). Words from a story or rhyme (4 years). Answers questions after you read (5 years).

That is not “raise a reader” branding. That is a cheap, repeatable place to see language.

## You do not have to finish the book

Eighteen months leaves. Three years argues about the page order. Five years tells the story wrong on purpose. CDC’s 18-month item is **a few pages**. Anyone who made you feel bad about the abandoned flap book was not holding the checklist.

“Reading” to a baby, CDC says in quotes, can be talking about pictures in a magazine. Permission granted.

## Dialogic is a research word for something you already do

Whitehurst and colleagues (early 1990s and after) studied **dialogic reading**: the adult prompts, the child says something, the adult adds a bit. The exact prompts are a method. This series will not print a branded sequence as homework. The idea you can steal ethically: the child should get a turn on the page.

“What’s that?” is a start. “What’s the bear doing?” is a 3-year CDC item. “What happened then?” is a 5-year item. You can live those questions without a card deck.

ASHA’s general tips: tell stories a lot; read as much as you can; ask questions; read in the languages they are learning.

## The talk around the book is the curriculum

Naming. Verbs. Feelings. Prediction. Memory. Rhyme (essay 12, essay 40). Pointing (essay 17). Sitting together, which is joint attention with a spine (essay 18).

A silent video of a book is a different object. A person who answers *dat?* is the object CDC had in mind.

## Two languages

Read the book you can perform. Clumsy English and fluent Spanish (or the reverse) is not a contest. You can tell the same pictures in two languages on two nights. You can keep the home-language stories that never got an ISBN.

## When books are a fight

Sensory: the page is loud, the flap is wrong. Attention: the body will not stay. Language: they cannot point to the bear and they know you wanted them to. Do not turn the fight into a daily test. Try shorter. Try their chosen catalog. Try you reading while they play nearby — still words in the air.

If they never join a page by the ages CDC uses books as a probe, mention it. Mention whether they join other shared attention. The cluster matters.

## Print is coming

Five-year cognitive line: writes some letters of their name; names some letters. Identify the Signs cares about pretending to read and finding the front of the book at 4–5. That is **print awareness**, not decoding. Essay 40.

You do not need to teach phonics at three to honor a flap book at one.

## Ordinary, not virtuous

Library. Bathroom basket. One book in the bag. Phones down for three pages. That is enough holiness for a week.

If someone sold you a $40 “language book set” with a script, you can return it. The CDC items work on a takeout menu. The bear is optional. The turn is not.

## Board books, flaps, and dignity

A destroyed flap is a book that was loved. CDC did not ask for library condition. If the child only mouths the book at ten months, you are in the 9–12 month “talk about pictures” tip, not in a failure to read. If at four they still only mouth and never point to a picture, that is a different note — pair it with other shared-attention stories (essay 18).

Whitehurst’s dialogic work used specific prompts with specific ages. You can steal the spirit: they talk, you add a little, you do not recite the whole page over their turn. You cannot steal a fidelity checklist and call it bedtime.

If reading is the only time they sit, you may be tempted to quiz. Quiz less. Sit more. The 5-year “answers after” item can wait for a night they are not already done with people.
