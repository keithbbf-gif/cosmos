**Disclaimer.** Educational only. Not medical advice. Not a screen and not a diagnosis. Three words is a CDC surveillance item, not a clinic label. Talk with your child’s doctor if you are worried.

Eighteen months is a crowded visit. The AAP wants a general developmental screen *and* an autism-specific screen around this age (Lipkin & Macias, 2020). CDC’s sheet reminds you. None of those tools is this blog.

The language line on the 18-month CDC page looks almost too small after all that: tries to say **three or more words** besides *mama* or *dada*; follows a **one-step direction without any gesture** — “Give it to me,” and the toy moves, no hand held out.

The social line is doing the pointing that parents think of as language: points to show you something interesting; looks at a few pages in a book with you. Keep those in the same conversation as the word count.

## Three words besides the parent names

CDC is still in *tries to say* territory. The forms can be short. They need to be words — consistent, meaningful, not only babble strings.

Three is not fifty. Fifty is a 30-month CDC item (essay 09). Older parent lore (“they should have twenty words at eighteen months”) is a different chart from a different decade. If you bring a paper from a parenting site that disagrees with the fridge checklist, say so at the visit. Do not silently average them.

ASHA’s public ranges for 13–18 months emphasize following directions more than a vocabulary quota. Identify the Signs still invites a conversation if there are only a few words into the 19-month–2-year stretch. Different jobs, same permission: you can ask.

If you have three words and a rich point-and-show life, the communication system is working in more than one channel. If you have three words and no point, or a point and no words, describe both. The description is the useful object.

## Directions without the gesture

This is the leap from 15 months. Then, CDC allowed the package: words plus a hand. Now they want the words to work alone.

Try it when you are not in a fight. “Give it to me” about a toy they were going to hand you anyway is a bad experiment. “Bring the shoes” when the shoes are in sight and they like going outside is a better one. If it only works when you point, say that. That is still information.

Understanding a *no* (12 months) and fetching on a sentence (18 months) are not the same skill. Essay 34 stays with directions as they get longer.

## The show-you point

Social, not language, on the CDC layout. Still language. “Look at that” is a comment, not a request. Comments are the beginning of conversation — I want you to see what I see. Joint attention, again (essay 18).

A child who requests by crying and never comments with a point is a different kitchen than a child who comments all day and talks little. Both kitchens are allowed to ask for help. They will not get the same next questions from a careful clinician. Good. That is evaluation, which this page is not.

## Books without finishing the book

“Looks at a few pages with you” is the bar. You do not need a plot. You need a lap, a page, a finger, a word. Essay 28 is the longer version. Eighteen months is when many adults decide they are bad at reading because the toddler leaves. CDC did not ask you to finish *Goodnight Moon*. They asked whether the pages are a shared place.

## What you can do that is still ordinary

CDC’s 18-month tips (on the printable packet) stay in the same family as 15 months: name, wait, add a word, let them help. The 18-month screen is the extra. Ask what tool they used. Ask what it said in words you can repeat. “It was fine” is not a score you can take to the next visit. “We used [name of tool]; no follow-up recommended” is better. If follow-up *was* recommended, that is not a diagnosis either. It is a next appointment.

## Late talking starts as a research phrase, not a fridge phrase

Essay 24 is the evidence piece. The short version: researchers have used parent-report tools and cutoffs (often around 24 months, often about small vocabulary and no two-word combinations) to study groups of children. That literature is not a stamp you press on an 18-month-old at breakfast. If someone says “late talker” in the hallway, ask what they counted and at what age. Then talk to the person who can actually look.

## The 18-month autism-specific screen is a separate object

Lipkin and Macias (2020) asked that it happen at 18 and 24 months or when someone is concerned. M-CHAT-R/F is a name you may hear. This series will not interpret it. Ask whether it was offered. Ask what “failed” or “passed” meant in a sentence you can repeat. A screen that sends you to a next step is still not a diagnosis. A screen that does not send you, while your stories are loud, is a reason to say the stories again.

If you decline a screen, that is your right. Write down that you declined so the next person does not assume it was done.

## Word lists that do not become a trap

Three words besides mama/dada can be *uh-oh, bye, ball*. They can be *agua, más, no*. Count honestly. Do not pad the list with imitations that never return. Do not delete signs from your life — put them in a second column (essay 15).

You can want a closer look at 18 months without waiting for a researcher’s cutoff. CDC built that permission into the footer. ASHA’s “don’t wait if you’re concerned” is the same permission in another logo. The logo does not need the relative’s permission.
