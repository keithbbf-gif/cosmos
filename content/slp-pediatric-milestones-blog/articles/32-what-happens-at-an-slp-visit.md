**Disclaimer.** Educational only. Not a substitute for a clinical visit. This page describes a typical outline. Clinics differ. A visit is not a diagnosis until someone licensed says so — and many visits end with “let’s watch” or “here are ideas,” not a name.

The waiting room has a toy kitchen that has seen things. Your child will not demonstrate their best language on the letter board. That is allowed.

## Who you are seeing

A speech-language pathologist is a licensed clinician (in the U.S., typically a master’s degree and ASHA certification if they use CCC-SLP). They work on speech sounds, language, fluency, voice, feeding in some clinics, AAC in some clinics. They do **not** automatically diagnose medical conditions. They may describe communication. They may recommend other specialists. ASHA ProFind is one public directory. Your pediatrician is another door.

If the person selling “speech therapy” has no license, leave.

## The first hour is mostly not a test booklet

**Interview.** Your pregnancy and birth story if you want to tell it. Hearing. Ear infections. Languages. Who lives in the house. What you are worried about. What the child loves. You can read your three stories (essay 30).

**Play.** On the floor. Bubbles, toys, books. The SLP is listening for sounds, combinations, how the child gets help, whether they look, whether they take turns. A good one will also watch you and the child together without grading your parenting.

**Looking at the mouth**, sometimes, if sounds or feeding are the question. A quick structure look is not a surgery.

**Formal tools**, sometimes: a standardized language test, a parent questionnaire, a speech-sound inventory. Toddlers often give you one good item and then leave. The clinician should say so. A score from a child who refused half the pictures is a weak score. Ask.

**Hearing** may be a question they send back to audiology. They should not skip ears if the story smells like ears (essay 22).

## What you can ask

“Are you diagnosing something today, or describing?”  
“What did you actually see versus what I reported?”  
“How sure are you, given they didn’t talk much in here?”  
“What would you like us to do before we meet again — not a home drill, just living?”  
“Do you want a recording from home?”  
“Do you have bilingual support?”

If they hand you a thick home program, you can say you can do two things, not twelve. If they use a word you did not ask for — a disorder name — you can ask what criteria they used and whether a second opinion is reasonable. You can decline a label-shopping relationship. You can also want a clear description so school and EI can talk to each other.

## What they should not do

Shame you about screens, work, or the second language.  
Promise an outcome.  
Start therapy without explaining the goal in words you recognize.  
Treat a milestone chart as a test they “failed.”  
Give you medical advice about tubes or frenotomy as if they were the surgeon.

## Therapy, if you choose it

If you continue, it should still look like play for a young child. You should know the target in one sentence: “more ways to request,” “clearer early sounds,” “we are watching fluency.” You should see how it connects to dinner and the park. If you do not, ask.

Frequency is a clinical and insurance conversation. More is not automatically better. This pack will not prescribe a dose.

## After you leave

Write down the plan in the parking lot. If you cried, that is ordinary. If you felt talked-over, you can try another clinician. Chemistry matters. Licensure matters more.

The visit is a look with a trained ear. You brought the child and the stories. They brought the tools. Together you decide the next door — which might be “go live your week and come back.” That can be a good visit. It is still not a diagnosis from a blog.

## If they barely talk in the room

Say so at the ten-minute mark, not in the car. “This is quiet for them. At home I hear X.” A good SLP will change toys, change rooms, or ask for a recording. A score from a silent child should be labeled as limited. Ask them to write that down.

If you need to nurse or snack for the child to stay, say so. A hungry language sample is a bad sample.

## After insurance says no

Ask the clinic what they billed and what “not medically necessary” meant in a sentence. Ask EI and the school door (essay 31). Ask ASHA ProFind for someone who does sliding scale. This pack is not a billing department. It is a reminder that one no is not the end of looking. You can also pause. Pausing with a date is a plan. Pausing forever is essay 39.
