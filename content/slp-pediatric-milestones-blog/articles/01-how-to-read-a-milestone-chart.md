**Disclaimer.** Educational only. Not medical advice. Not a developmental screening tool and not a diagnosis. Milestone charts describe what many children can do by an age. They do not name what is “wrong” with your child. If you have questions, talk with your child’s doctor or a licensed speech-language pathologist.

The chart on the fridge looks like a test. It is not.

In February 2022 the CDC’s *Learn the Signs. Act Early.* program published revised checklists. The American Academy of Pediatrics had convened an expert group. One rule they set was simple on paper and easy to misread at 6 a.m.: put each item at an age when **at least 75 percent** of children would be expected to show that skill (Zubler et al., *Pediatrics*, 2022). ASHA’s public communication milestones use the same 75 percent idea and say, on the page, that the lists are not a screening tool and not a diagnostic tool.

That number is the whole plot of this piece.

## What 75 percent is doing there

Older parent charts often sat closer to the middle of the pack — a 50th-percentile world, where “average” and “expected” blurred. If your child had not done the thing yet, a well-meaning relative could say *give it a month*. The 2022 revision was built to make a missed item mean something quieter and more useful: *this is worth a conversation*, not *panic*, and not *wait it out in silence*.

CDC’s own write-up is blunt about the old habit. “Wait and see” is named as a barrier to finding children who could use extra support (Abercrombie, Wiggins & Green, *Zero to Three*, 2022). The lists added 15-month and 30-month pages so a checklist existed for every AAP well-child visit from 2 months through 5 years. Some older items moved later. Some were replaced. The checklists got shorter in places. None of that is a secret lowering of “standards.” It is a different statistical job.

ASHA was not on the working group. After members wrote in, ASHA published a careful objection: some language items, placed later, might *slow* a referral even as CDC hoped to speed conversations. Both things can be in the same folder. CDC answered that the lists are communication tools, not the way children are evaluated or found eligible for services. ASHA still wants clinicians to use their own tools. You, at the table, get to hold both memos.

## Three words that are not synonyms

**Surveillance** is the ongoing look. A checklist. A question at the well-child visit. You noticing that the wave came, or did not. Anyone who spends days with the child can do this. CDC says it does not take special training.

**Screening** is a formal, standardized comparison with other children the same age. The AAP asks that it happen for *every* child at 9, 18, and 30 months, and that autism-specific screening happen at 18 and 24 months or whenever a parent or clinician is worried (Lipkin & Macias, *Pediatrics*, 2020). A blog post cannot run that tool. A fridge magnet cannot either.

**Evaluation** is what a licensed professional does when someone needs a closer look — a pediatrician, an audiologist, a speech-language pathologist, an early-intervention team. That is where a description can become a clinical opinion. Not here.

If you remember only one triangle from this series, remember that one.

## How to sit with a page

Read the age you are actually in, then glance at the age below and the age above. ASHA says that out loud because skills do not clock in on a birthday. A child who turned two last Tuesday is not “behind” because the 30-month page has more words.

Read the communication line *and* the social line. A point, a show, a look when you call a name — those sit in different boxes on the CDC sheets and they are still language. Essays 17 and 18 stay with that.

Read the footer. Every CDC page repeats it: this checklist is not a substitute for a standardized, validated screen. Most children (75 percent or more) can do these things by this age. Subject-matter experts chose the items from data and consensus. If you skip the footer you will turn a surveillance tool into a verdict.

When a box is “not yet,” the CDC script is not a diagnosis. It is: you know your child best; talk with the doctor; ask about screening; if you are still concerned, ask about a specialist and call your state’s early-intervention program. Loss of a skill the child used to have is on every page as a reason to call *now*. That sentence is about timing, not about naming a condition.

## What the chart will not tell you

It will not tell you that two languages at home are a problem. They are not. ASHA’s public pages say to talk in the language you know best.

It will not tell you that a boy should be given extra months because “boys talk later.” Group averages are not a permission slip.

It will not tell you that a missed sound at three is a lifelong speech disorder. Consonants have their own, later timetable (McLeod & Crowe, 2018; essay 13).

It will not tell you whether your child qualifies for services. Eligibility is a legal and clinical process. The chart is a conversation starter.

It will not replace a hearing check. If you find yourself raising your voice, repeating, or wondering whether sound is getting in, say that at the visit. Essay 22.

## Two lists in the house

Parents meet CDC at the pediatrician and ASHA when they search “speech milestones” at midnight. The ages do not always match, because the jobs do not always match. CDC aligns to well-child visits and wants items you can see in a kitchen. ASHA publishes ranges (“7 to 9 months,” “13 to 18 months”) and keeps a longer clinical literature behind the public page.

When they disagree, do not average them into a homemade cutoff. Bring both pages to the visit if you want. “I am not sure how to read these two” is a complete sentence.

Identify the Signs — ASHA’s public campaign — lists observations that have made families ask for help: little babble, few gestures, few words, speech that stays hard for familiar people. Those are invitations to a conversation. They are not a quiz you fail.

## A way to use this series

The age-band essays (02–12) walk the CDC language/communication lines and, where it helps, ASHA’s ranges. The domain essays stay with one seam: sounds, understanding, gestures, hearing, two languages, books. The parent-guide essays (this one, 30, 31, 32, 39, 42) are about doors — the doctor, early intervention, an SLP visit — not about deciding what is on the other side.

If a relative wants a verdict from a URL, this series will disappoint them. That is the point.

You can print a CDC checklist, mark what you see, and take it in. You can write three ordinary sentences: what your child does to get your attention, what they understand, what you are worried about. You can ask for the 9-, 18-, or 30-month screen if it has not happened. You can ask for a hearing check. You can call early intervention in your state without a doctor’s permission — CDC prints that path on purpose.

None of those steps is a diagnosis. All of them are allowed.
