**Disclaimer.** Educational only. Not medical advice. Not a grammar test and not a diagnosis. *I goed* is a famous ordinary error. It is not a disorder you can name from a blog.

Grammar sounds like school. In toddlers it sounds like *doggie run* and then, later, *doggie running*, and then a messy past.

Roger Brown’s 1970s stages — the ones SLPs still meet in textbooks — ordered little English bits: *-ing*, plural *-s*, *in/on*, possessives, and later past and third-person *-s*. Those stages were a small longitudinal study, not a CDC checklist. This essay will not pretend they are a fridge law. It will use them as a way to hear what is arriving.

## What CDC actually put on the parent page

**30 months:** two words together with an **action** word (*Doggie run*). Words like *I, me, we*.  
**3 years:** says what **action** is happening in a picture (*running, eating, playing*) — those examples are *-ing* forms.  
**4 years:** sentences of four or more words; talks about one thing that happened (past *meaning*, not necessarily a correct verb).  
**5 years:** two-event story; time words like yesterday and tomorrow (cognitive line).

NIDCD’s public milestone lists have long mentioned plurals and *-ing* in the 2–3 year stretch. ASHA’s public pages stay higher-level. If your handout has “uses regular past tense at 3;6,” ask which study and which percent. Mark `[VERIFY]` before you treat it as a court date.

## Verbs first, decorations later

*Run* has to exist before *running* and *ran*. CDC’s 30-month action-word item is the hinge. A vocabulary of fifty nouns is a catalog. A verb is a movie.

You can feed verbs by talking about what is happening, not only what things are called. “You’re pouring.” “It spilled.” You do not need to make them repeat.

## Plurals

*Two cups* will appear as *two cup* for a while. Then an *s* that is a whistle or a *t*. Then *mouses* and *foots*, which are a good sign that the child found a rule and over-applied it. Over-regularization is a famous ordinary moment, not a regression.

If there is never a mark for more-than-one by the later preschool years, and sentences stay telegram-short, mention it. If *foots* appears, celebrate quietly and do not correct at the party.

## The past

Meaning arrives before the right form. *I play park* about yesterday is a past story in a present verb. CDC’s 4-year “talks about at least one thing that happened” accepts that. *I goed* is the rule-machine. *I went* is the exception list, slower.

Do not hold dessert for a correct irregular. Model: “You went to the park.” Move on.

## Pronouns and little words

*I / me / we* at 30 months (CDC) is a start. *Him doing it* will linger. Articles (*a, the*) and *is* are easy for adults to miss because we fill them in. An SLP listening to a recording will hear whether they are there. You can listen too, once, without becoming a copy editor.

## Two languages, two grammars

A Spanish-English child may drop English *is* longer, because Spanish can drop that kind of piece differently. Do not score them on a monolingual Brown chart. Tell the SLP both languages. Bring a speaker.

## When little grammar stays little

The worry is not one missing *-s*. The worry is a cluster: short sentences at four, no verbs, no past meaning, questions that never invert, people still not understanding. Clusters go to a person. Isolated cute errors stay at the table.

ASHA’s tip remains: model, do not interrupt every sentence. Grammar grows in conversations that are about something — the spill, the dog, the unfairness of bedtime — not in worksheets named after morphemes.

If a teacher says “grammar is weak,” ask for two examples. Write them down. Examples beat adjectives.

## *Running* on the 3-year page

CDC’s picture-action examples are *running, eating, playing*. Those are *-ing* forms. If your child says *run* for the picture, they have the verb (30-month work). If they say *running*, they have the decoration. Both are good. Only *dog* for a running dog is a thinner sample — mention it.

Brown’s Stage II–III families used to get excited about *-ing* as the first bound morpheme. You can get quietly pleased. You cannot put a date on the fridge from 1973 and call it CDC.

## Irregulars are a collection, not a rule

*Went, saw, came* arrive one by one. *I seed* means they found *-ed*. Do not un-teach the rule to force *saw*. Model *saw* in your own sentence about the same bird. The bird is the point. The verb is a hitchhiker.

If sentences at four still have no tense meaning — everything is a nameless now — that is bigger than *goed*. That is a cluster for a person, not a worksheet named “past tense pack.”
