**Disclaimer.** Educational only. Not medical advice. Not a bilingual evaluation and not a diagnosis. Two languages are typical human childhood, not a risk factor you invented at the park.

The relative who only has English will say the child is confused. The relative who only has the home language will say the school is erasing them. Both relatives are afraid of losing a person.

ASHA’s public page is calmer than either relative: **communicate in the languages you are most comfortable using.** Children can learn more than one. Mixing words from two languages in a sentence is common, not a defect. A “delay” is not diagnosed by looking at English alone.

This series will repeat that until it sticks.

## Milestones do not split in half

CDC’s lists were built as general surveillance tools, mostly illustrated in English. They are not a rule that a bilingual toddler should have half the words in each language. ASHA’s milestone page says to look at ranges and at the ages above and below. For bilingual children, look at **total** communication: words + signs + what they understand in each language.

A child with 40 words across Spanish and English at 30 months is not “below 50 in English.” Tell the clinician the split. Bring a speaker of the other language to the visit if the clinician does not have it. That is not extra. That is the evaluation.

## One person, one language is optional

Some families like OPOL (one parent, one language). Some speak both at the table. Some switched when a grandparent moved in. Research on input (Hoff & Core, 2013, and a large literature after) cares about **quality and amount of real conversation**, not about a brand of family policy. Do not throw out the language you dream in because a blog sold you a schedule.

If you are more fluent in one language, that language is where your best sentences live. Children need good sentences. ASHA said the comfortable language on purpose.

## Mixing is not breaking

*Quiero juice* is a child using the tools in the drawer. Adults who are bilingual do this too. You can model a full sentence in one language without a scold. You do not need a swear jar for the other language.

## School English and home language can look uneven

Understanding at home can be rich while preschool English is thin in September. The reverse happens in some houses by spring. Time on the playground is not a disorder. A teacher who has never heard the home language may still have a real concern about *how* the child communicates with everyone. Listen to the concern. Add the missing half of the data.

Identify the Signs and CDC footers still apply: if you are worried in the languages the child lives in, ask. Bilingualism is not a reason to wait-and-see (essay 39). It is also not a reason to pathologize an accent or a younger English vocabulary.

## Sounds

An English *r* and a Spanish *r* are not the same assignment. A “lisp” in one language may be a sound that language does not treat as an error. McLeod and Crowe’s cross-language work exists partly so clinicians stop using one English photocopy for the world (essay 13).

## What to take to the SLP

Every language heard, including from YouTube, church, and cousins. Who speaks which, how many hours, how well. A word list in each language. What the child understands in each. Whether anyone is worried in the *home* language, not only at school.

Ask whether the clinician has bilingual training or an interpreter. You are allowed to ask. ASHA’s practice portal for clinicians is about doing this well. You do not need to read it. You need to not accept “drop the Spanish so English can catch up” as a plan. That sentence has harmed families. It is not in this pack.

## What you can do Tuesday

Talk. Read. Sing. In the language that comes out whole. Keep the stories, the jokes, the scoldings that only work in one tongue. Those are not extras. Those are the curriculum.

If you want more English, find people who speak good English to play with — not a silent app. If you want to keep the home language, find people who love it, including you.

Two languages are not a delay. They are a household. Treat them like one.

## Code-switching is not a bug

Adults who live in two languages switch for emphasis, for the only good word, for the person in the room. Children do the same. A teacher who only has English may hear “incomplete sentences.” A grandmother may hear perfect sense. Both reports are true. The evaluation has to include the grandmother’s language or it is half an evaluation.

Hoff and Core (2013) and the input literature keep finding that *how much good talk* in a language predicts skill in that language. That is not a reason to delete the other language. It is a reason to find more living speakers of the one you want to grow — playdates, cousins, a library hour — not a silent app.

## When a professional gets this wrong

“Pick one language” is a sentence you can refuse. “Tell me about both” is a sentence you can reward. If they have no interpreter and they still want to score English only, you can ask to return with a speaker or to see someone who has the language. ASHA’s public page is on your side. Take a screenshot if you need a prop. You should not need a prop. You might.
