**Disclaimer.** Educational only. Not medical advice. Not a screen and not a diagnosis. A four-word sentence is not a writing sample.

Four is the age of the car-seat debrief. “What did you do today?” used to get a stare. Now CDC wants at least one thing that happened — *I played soccer* — plus sentences of four or more words, some words from a song or nursery rhyme, and answers to simple function questions: what is a coat for, what is a crayon for.

ASHA’s 3-to-4-year public note includes telling you a story from a book or a video. Identify the Signs still cares whether early book behaviors are in the room. Narrative is the new work. Sounds can still be messy. That is allowed.

## Four words is a length, not a style

*I want the red cup* counts. *Daddy go work car* counts if those are the words you hear. You are not scoring grammar on a rubric. You are noticing whether talk has outgrown the two-word telegram of two.

If length is there and sounds are not adult, you are in the normal fork. If length is not there and the child is answering with one word or a pull-to-the-fridge, describe the situations. Tired, new place, second language — mention those. Do not explain the whole item away. Do not catastrophize it either.

## Songs and rhymes are memory plus sound play

“Some words from a song, story, or nursery rhyme” is how CDC writes it. The brain is chunking. The ear is catching rhyme and beat, which later help with reading (essay 40). You do not need a perfect recitation of *The Wheels on the Bus*. A line is enough.

If songs are a house without music, poems on a page count. So do the same three words from the same cartoon. Mention the source. An SLP would rather have a true example than a cleaned-up one.

## One event from the day

This is narrative at its smallest. A beginning that is also the end. *I played soccer.* Tomorrow, five will ask for two events (essay 12). Do not pre-assign homework.

You can make the item easier without turning it into therapy. Ask in the doorway, not at 8 p.m. Ask about a specific thing you already know happened: “Did the class go outside?” Then wait. If they tell Grandma and not you, that is still the skill.

If they never recount, but they can answer “What is a coat for?”, you have a split worth describing. Functions are semantic. Recalling an event is memory plus language plus trust.

## What is it for?

Coat. Crayon. A chair. These questions show that words have jobs, not only labels. Wrong answers that are still in the neighborhood (*a coat is for the hook*) tell you more than silence. Write them down if you are the kind of person who writes things down.

## Pretend is on the social line for a reason

Pretends to be a teacher, a superhero, a dog. Asks to go play with children. Comforts a friend. Changes behavior in the library versus the playground. CDC is painting a child who can hold a role and a rule. Language is how roles get negotiated. Essay 33.

If pretend never shows, or play with others is something they never ask for, say what you see. Do not let a search bar finish the sentence.

## Sounds at four

McLeod and Crowe’s reviews put many English consonants at or before five, with a few later (*r*, *th*). A lisp or a slushy *s* at four is a reason some families ask an SLP — and it is not, by itself, a disorder you can name from a blog. If the message is clear to other people most of the time, you are still in CDC’s 3-year intelligibility neighborhood. If the message is not clear, do not wait for the five-year rhyme item to give you permission to ask.

ASHA: do not interrupt to correct sounds. Say them correctly in your own mouth.

## School door

The 4-year CDC footer still points to the local public elementary school for a free evaluation if you and the doctor remain concerned. That feels early if your child is not in kindergarten. Child Find is supposed to feel early. Essay 31.

A preschool teacher’s note is data. “We cannot understand them at snack” is data. “They don’t answer in a group” is data. Data is not a diagnosis.

## Ordinary help

Read and ask what happens next (CDC). Answer some of the why-questions. Give the words for “Can I have a turn?” when grabbing is the current strategy. Keep screens out of the bedroom and out of the last hour before bed if you can — CDC’s own 4-year sleep tip, 10–13 hours including naps.

You do not need a narrative curriculum. You need a person who asks, and then shuts up long enough for a four-word sentence to arrive.

## Songs they get “wrong”

CDC wants some words from a song or rhyme, not a recital. A garbled *twinkle twinkle* still has words. If there is never a remembered line — not from a song, not from a book refrain, not from the same cartoon — mention memory-and-language together. If they remember every lyric and cannot tell you one thing from preschool, mention that split. Memory for songs and memory for events are neighbors, not twins.

## Function questions in the wild

“What is a coat for?” is easy when the coat is in your hand. Try it when the coat is not. If they only answer with objects in sight, that is still useful. If they can talk about yesterday’s coat, you have time talk arriving early (CDC puts time words on the 5-year cognitive line). Do not force yesterday. Notice it.

Four-word sentences will include grammar errors. *I goed park with Daddy car* is length plus meaning. Celebrate the length. Model one cleaner sentence. Do not diagram it at dinner (essay 21).

If preschool sends home a “language concern,” ask for two quoted sentences. Quotes travel. “Language concern” does not.
