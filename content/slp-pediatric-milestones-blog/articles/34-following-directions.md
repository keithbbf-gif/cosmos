**Disclaimer.** Educational only. Not a compliance program and not a diagnosis. Not following a direction can be language, hearing, attention, or a person with a better idea.

“Give it to me.”

At 15 months CDC lets you hold out your hand. At 18 months they want the words alone. At 30 months the cognitive line wants two steps: put the toy down **and** close the door. At 3 years the tips suggest two or three steps in real life. ASHA’s 13–18 month range is full of these sentences: give me the ball, hug the teddy, come here, show me your nose.

Directions are comprehension with a deadline. They are also how adults accidentally test children while holding a car seat buckle.

## Why a direction fails

They did not hear it (essay 22).  
They heard it and do not know the words (essay 16).  
They know the words and cannot hold two steps yet.  
They understood and chose the cat.  
The room is loud.  
You used a paragraph.

If you only tell the doctor “they don’t listen,” you have handed over a moral. If you tell the doctor “they fetch the shoes when I point and not when I only talk,” you have handed over language data.

## How to try once, kindly

One step. Familiar object. Quiet kitchen. Hands still. Then wait. Then show. Then help. You are allowed to help. This is not the army.

Two steps only after one step is easy, and only in a real routine: shoes, then door. CDC’s 30-month example is a real house, not a tabletop trick with blocks they do not care about.

If it works for Grandma and not for you, mention tone, language, and whether Grandma cheats with her eyes.

## Routines are directions in costume

“It’s clean-up time” (CDC, 30 months, social) is a scripted direction. Children often follow the script they live. New directions in a new clinic are harder. Tell the SLP what works at home.

## School directions are a different sport

Group, noise, “everybody line up.” A child who follows one-to-one and melts in a group is giving you an attention-and-noise story. That can still be language. It can be hearing. It can be the room. Do not reduce it to “behavior” before ears and understanding have had a turn.

## Two languages

A direction in the weaker language will fail more. That is not stubbornness. Give the clinician both.

## What you can do that is not a workbook

Talk less when it matters. Gesture when you need success; take the gesture away when you are curious. Thank them when the shoes move. Give the direction before they are already in trouble.

Do not run ten fake directions as a quiz. Do not withhold dinner for a two-step.

Simon Says at five is CDC’s own game tip — following plus fun plus inhibition. Use it as a game.

If directions are the main worry, bring two videos or two stories: one success, one miss, what the room was like. That pair is an evaluation starter. It is not a label.

## “Come here” is the oldest test

ASHA’s range includes it. So does every parking lot. If they come when you have the snack and not when you have the car seat, you have motivation data, not a full comprehension score. Try “give me the cup” about a cup they were done with. Then stop. Two tries. Then live.

Teachers stack directions: “Push in your chair, put your paper in the bin, line up.” That is three. CDC’s 3-year tips allow two or three in real life. If the first of three happens and the rest die, mention working memory and noise before you mention defiance. You still may have a child who understood and declined. Both can be written down. The SLP will not be shocked. They have chairs too.

A visual — a picture of shoes, then door — is AAC-adjacent and ordinary. It is not giving up on words (essay 41). It is a way to see whether the idea is there when the ears are busy.
