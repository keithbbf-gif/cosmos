**Disclaimer.** Educational only. Not medical advice. Not a screen and not a diagnosis. A first name-word is not a vocabulary count. If gestures or looking are missing, or a skill disappeared, talk with your child’s doctor.

The first birthday cake is a trap for milestone talk. People want a word they can spell.

CDC’s 12-month language line wants three things, and only one of them is a word: waves bye-bye; calls a parent *mama* or *dada* or another special name; understands *no* — pauses briefly or stops when you say it. ASHA’s 10-to-12-month range puts pointing, waving, and showing or giving objects in the same breath.

Notice what the lists will not do. They will not demand a ten-word vocabulary. They will not demand a clear consonant. *Mama* can be messy. The job is: a name that means a person, used on purpose.

## A wave is not cute extra. It is the sentence.

Bye-bye is a social script. It has a moment (someone is leaving), a body shape, and often a word from you. CDC put it on the communication line because it is a symbol. The baby is referring to an event, not just grabbing a cookie.

If there is no wave, look at the rest of the gesture set. Do they show you a toy? Hold something out? Shake the head? ASHA treats that cluster as the late-first-year work. Essay 17 stays with gestures. Identify the Signs asks families to notice few gestures around the first birthday. That is a conversation prompt, not a label.

You can teach a wave the way CDC suggests: you wave when you leave, you say the word, you do it again tomorrow. You cannot force a wave into existence at the party for the camera.

## Mama, dada, and the other special name

Some babies pick a sibling’s name. Some pick the dog. CDC allows “another special name.” The item is about a consistent form for a consistent person (or beloved being), not about English kinship terms.

Clinics used to distinguish *mama* said to the wall from *mama* said to you. That distinction is still useful at the kitchen table. A string of *mamama* in the crib (essay 04) is babble. *Mama* when you walk in, with a look, is the 12-month item.

If the special name is not there yet and the wave and the *no* pause are, you still have communication on the page. If none of the three are there, you still do not have a diagnosis. You have a well-child visit and a sentence to say.

## Understands “no”

CDC’s version is small: a brief pause or a stop. Not obedience. Not a child who never touches the outlet again. A flicker that the word landed.

Families get into fights with this item. A baby who smiles and keeps going may have understood perfectly. Understanding and complying are different skills. If you want to test the item, use a calm *no* in a real moment, not a drill in a high chair with an audience.

Save *no* for the dangerous stuff, CDC says on the 12-month tips. Long lectures do not help. Neither does spanking. That is their sentence, not a parenting brand’s.

## Build on the almost-word

CDC’s 12-month language tip is the one SLPs have been saying since the Iowa clinic days: if they say *ta*, you say “Yes, a truck.” If they say *truck*, you say “Yes, that’s a big, blue truck.” You are not correcting a mistake. You are showing the next size up.

When they point, answer with a word and a beat of waiting. “You want the cup? Here is the cup.” If a sound comes, celebrate the try. Essay 15 is about what counts as a first word. You can start that argument now, kindly, with yourself.

## Hearing and name-looking

The 9-month social item “looks when you call her name” is still in play. The 12-month cognitive line wants them looking for something they saw you hide. Together those are about following your attention into the world. If sound seems optional — TV up, no startle, no find-your-voice — ask about hearing before you ask about words (essay 22).

## What this birthday is not

It is not a speech-sound exam. *Dada* with a soft *d* is still *dada*.

It is not a bilingual crisis. Two languages do not split the wave in half. ASHA: use the language you know (essay 23).

It is not the last chance. Fifteen months will ask for one or two words besides the parent names (essay 06). The chart is a staircase, not a trapdoor.

## Hide-and-find is language’s cousin

CDC’s 12-month cognitive line wants them looking for something they saw you hide. That is not a word. It is the idea that things (and people) continue. Combined with a name-word and a wave, you have a child who can hold you in mind and call you back. If hide-and-find is strong and the name-word is not, say both. If both are thin, say both. Do not only report the birthday-party word.

Drinks from a cup you hold, without a lid — also on that movement line — is a mouth item. Mention mess and mention whether they will even try. Essay 27 and 37. It is not an articulation score.

## Relatives and the video

Someone will play a cousin’s first-word video. You can say “we’re using the CDC 12-month line” and change the subject. You can also enjoy the cousin. Comparison is how families do love and harm in the same minute.

If you want a card for the 12-month visit: here is the wave; here is the name they use; here is what they do when I say *no*; here is how they show me a thing. That is a language sample. It fits on a phone note. The cake is optional. The note is not, if you are carrying a question.
