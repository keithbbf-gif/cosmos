**Disclaimer.** Educational only. Not medical advice. Not a language sample scored for question types and not a diagnosis.

*Where daddy?* is a complete intellectual life in two words.

CDC’s 3-year line wants children to **ask** who, what, where, or why. The 4-year line wants them to **answer** a simple function question. The 5-year line wants answers about a book after you close it, more or less. ASHA’s general tips: ask questions that need more than yes/no; encourage them to ask you.

Questions are how children steal information they cannot see. They are also how adults accidentally run a quiz show in the cereal aisle.

## Asking is a different muscle from answering

A child can answer “What’s that?” all day — you have trained it with books — and never offer a question. A child can ask *why* as a stall and not answer anyone else’s *why*. Mention both directions at a visit.

Early questions are often one word with a rising voice. *Dat?* counts as a question in a kitchen. CDC’s 3-year examples are fuller (*Where is mommy/daddy?*), at an age when 75 percent or more would be expected to use those question words. You can still honor the rising *dat?* at two as the seed.

## A rough order, not a law

**What / where** tend to show before **who**, and **why / how / when** trail. Function questions (*what is a coat for*) need the idea that objects have jobs. Time questions need *yesterday* (CDC cognitive, 5 years). If your child is “stuck” on *what’s that*, they may be doing the age-appropriate job of labeling the world. It gets old. It is still work.

Do not teach *why* with a flashcard. Live a life that has mysteries. “I don’t know; let’s look” is CDC’s own later tip.

## Yes/no is a smaller door

ASHA says to ask questions that need more than yes/no. Good for growing sentences. Also: yes/no is a valid comprehension check when the child is tired. “Do you want apple?” is not a moral failure. Mix.

Choice questions (*apple or cracker*) sit in between. They force a word or a point. They are allowed.

## Book questions versus life questions

“Where is the bear?” (CDC, 2 years) is a point. “What is happening?” (3 years) is a verb. “What happens next?” (4-year tips) is prediction. “What did she find?” after the book (5 years) is memory.

If book questions work and life questions do not — or the reverse — say so. Context is the whole game.

## When questions do not come

Some children comment and never ask. Some children echo your question back. Some children find another way to get the cookie and skip the word. Describe the workaround. Workarounds are intelligence. They can also hide a missing form.

Hearing matters. Soft question words get lost (*where* versus *wear*). Essay 22.

Two languages: a child may ask in the home language and answer school questions slowly. That split is typical, not sneaky.

## How not to turn dinner into an exam

One real question, then a comment of your own. “I saw a truck. It was loud.” You are modeling that talk can be offered, not only extracted.

If they ask *why* twelve times, you can answer twice and then say “That’s enough whys for the noodles.” Limits are language too.

Do not refuse the noodles until they produce a *where* question. This series does not do hostage therapy.

## The ask

If, at three, they never ask, and they rarely answer, and they do not point to the bear, you have a cluster. If they ask all day and cannot answer “What is a crayon for?” at four, you have a different cluster. Take clusters to people with tools. Leave the quiz-show buzzer at home.

## *What’s that* as a career

For months the child is a labeling machine. It is how vocabularies get large. You can answer with the name and one extra: “That’s a fire truck. It’s loud.” Then you are done. You do not need a paragraph on municipal services.

If *what’s that* never comes and they still get objects by grabbing, they have a workaround. Write the workaround. If *why* comes before *what*, enjoy the philosophy and still model *what* on the page.

## Teachers’ questions are a different dialect

“What did we just read?” on the rug is memory plus group plus performance. Home *where’s your cup* is survival. If school says they don’t answer questions and home is full of *why*, take both dialects to the SLP. If both places are quiet, that is a fuller quiet.

ASHA’s tip to avoid yes/no is for growth. Your tip to use yes/no when the child is exhausted is for being a parent. Both can live in one day.
