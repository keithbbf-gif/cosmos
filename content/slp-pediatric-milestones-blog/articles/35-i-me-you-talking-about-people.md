**Disclaimer.** Educational only. Not a grammar test and not a clinical label. Pronouns are slippery on purpose. *Me do it* is ordinary. This page will not turn a pronoun slip into a condition name.

*I* is a moving target. When you say it, it means you. When they say it, it means them. That is a lot of philosophy for a person with a sippy cup.

CDC’s 30-month language line wants words like **I, me, or we**. Not a full set. Not correct case. Presence.

The 3-year line wants a **first name** when asked — a different way of pointing at a person. Names are easier. They stay put.

## Why pronouns arrive late

Toys have names you can point to. *I* does not. *You* flips depending on who is talking. Researchers who study pronoun acquisition (a long developmental literature; Chiat and others in the clinic textbooks) treat errors as expected. *Me do it* is a celebrity error. *Him going* will visit. *Your* for *my* happens.

If you correct every one, you will spend preschool as a copy editor. Model: “You can do it.” Move on.

## What CDC did not ask

They did not ask for *he/she* contrast at 30 months. They did not ask for a lecture on gender. Family words for people — including the words your family actually uses for caregivers — count as person-talk. If your household’s special name for you was the 12-month item, that thread continues here.

## When pronoun talk is the worry

The worry is rarely one *me*. The worry is a cluster: no words for people, no names, no *I*, sentences that never include a doer, or a child who talks about themselves only as a name in the third person **and** has other language that is thin. Some clinical descriptions include pronoun patterns; this blog will not apply them. Describe: “They say *baby want* and their name, not *I*.” Take that to a person.

Echolalia — repeating your *you* — can make pronouns look extra weird. Mention scripts. Do not name a condition.

## Two languages

Pronoun systems differ. A language that drops subjects will make English *I* look optional longer. Tell the SLP. Do not score a bilingual child on a monolingual pronoun checklist you found in a drawer.

## Names, kinship, and theory of mind, without the textbook

*Mommy gone* is person + event. *Where daddy?* is a question about a person (essay 20). Comforting a friend (CDC, 4 years) needs the idea that other people have insides. You do not need to teach “theory of mind” as a module. You need stories about people: who was sad, who helped.

Books do this. Family photos do this. “That’s you. That’s me. That’s Abuela.”

## Ordinary help

Talk about what *you* are doing with *I* sometimes: “I’m cutting the apple.” Children steal the frame.

When they say *Baby want juice*, you can say “You want juice” as you hand it over. The juice still arrives. Language is not a hostage.

If 30 months is next week and you have not heard *I/me/we*, look at the rest of the line: fifty words, action combinations, naming in a book. The cluster is the conversation. A missing pronoun is one tile.

People-talk is how children place themselves on the map. The map can be messy for a long time. Messy is not a diagnosis. Silent about people, when everything else is silent too, is a reason to ask.

## *We* is a crowded word

CDC included *we* at 30 months. *We* means more than one, and it includes the speaker. A child may say *we* for a toy group (“we go”) before they say *I*. Mention what *we* points at in your house.

Kinship terms — *tia, nana, brother* — are person vocabulary. If those are rich and *I* is missing, you still have people-talk. If those are missing too, the person map is thin. Thin maps go to a visit with the rest of the language line, not as a pronoun emergency.

You can play “your turn, my turn” with a block without a social-skills brand. The pronouns ride along. The block is the point. If they never take a turn and never use a person word, you have essay 18 and this essay in the same bag. One bag is fine. One label is not.
