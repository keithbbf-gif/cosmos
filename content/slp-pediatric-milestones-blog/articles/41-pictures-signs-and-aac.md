**Disclaimer.** Educational only. Not an AAC evaluation and not a treatment protocol. This page will not teach PECS phases or program an app. It will not tell you a child needs a device.

AAC means **augmentative and alternative communication**: signs, pictures, a book of symbols, a device that speaks, a homemade board on the fridge. ASHA’s public AAC page is the parent door. The word is large. A point is already a cousin (essay 17). Baby sign is a cousin (essay 38).

## The myth that will not die

If we give them pictures, they will not talk.

Romski and Sevcik (2005) wrote about AAC myths in early intervention for a reason. Families still hear the sentence in parking lots. The usual clinical counter — and ASHA’s public tone — is: people use the method that works; having another method does not steal the mouth; many children use AAC **and** speech; some children need AAC as their voice for a long time. That last group deserves respect, not a promise that the device is temporary.

This series will not promise that AAC will make speech appear on a schedule. It will not promise that it never does. Outcomes are individual. Marketing is not.

## Who decides

An SLP who actually does AAC — not a stranger on a video, not this blog — after looking at motor access, vision, hearing, language, and the family’s life. Schools and EI have their own processes. You can ask for an AAC look without accepting a diagnosis you did not request. You can also say not yet.

A device is not a personality. A low-tech board is not a failure to get the expensive thing.

## How to think about it at the kitchen table

If your child already uses a consistent point, a sign, or a picture to request, you are already in the AAC family. You can model a spoken word at the same time. You can honor the picture by giving the juice. Honoring the message teaches that communication works. That is the opposite of a trick.

If nothing works and frustration is high, pictures are one of the doors to ask about — alongside hearing, understanding, and a full SLP look. Pictures are not a consolation prize. They are a bridge or a destination. Either can be a good life.

## What this series will not print

A 12-step picture-exchange protocol.  
A ranked list of apps.  
“Start AAC now or they will never talk.”  
“Never start AAC or they will never talk.”

Both ultimatums are unpaid advertising.

## Two languages

The device or the book should include the words the family actually speaks. An English-only board in a Spanish house is a half tool. Say that out loud at the meeting.

## Ordinary respect

Talk to the child, not only about them, whether they use a device or not. Wait. Let them finish the button. Do not grab the device to make it go faster unless they want help.

If a relative says the pictures are giving up, you can hand them ASHA’s public page. You can also leave the room.

Being understood is the job. Mouth, hand, picture, device — those are tools. A clinician helps pick tools. A blog does not. You are allowed to ask for the conversation. You are allowed to want speech *and* a backup. Wanting both is not confusion. It is a plan that can change.

## Modeling is not drilling

If you use a picture, you tap it and say the word while you hand the juice. You do not withhold the juice for a perfect tap. The same ethic as first words (essay 15). The message working is the lesson.

Romski and Sevcik (2005) wrote for early-intervention audiences who were afraid of devices. Twenty years later the fear is still in parking lots. You can hand them ASHA’s public AAC page. You can also say “we are adding a way, not subtracting a mouth.” Then go inside.

If school offers a device and home never sees it, ask for the charger and a 10-minute show-me. A tool that lives in a backpack is a rumor. A tool on the fridge is a language.
