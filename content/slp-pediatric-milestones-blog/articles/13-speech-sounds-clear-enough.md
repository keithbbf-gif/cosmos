**Disclaimer.** Educational only. Not medical advice. Not an articulation test and not a diagnosis. Sound charts describe groups of children. They do not name a disorder for yours.

Parents get handed photocopies. A column of IPA symbols. A year beside *r*. Someone’s aunt says, “He’ll get that lisp in school.”

The photocopy and the aunt are both doing a job this essay will not do: deciding whether a sound is “late.” This essay can say what the large reviews actually found, and what CDC/ASHA ask parents to notice instead of a phoneme list.

## The 2018 review in one kitchen paragraph

Sharynne McLeod and Kathryn Crowe looked at consonant acquisition across 27 languages (*American Journal of Speech-Language Pathology*, 2018). Tens of thousands of children, many studies, a lot of single-word picture naming — which is easier than conversation. The headline they wrote for clinicians: five-year-olds have acquired **most** consonants in the language around them; individual variability still counts.

For English, their synthesis (and Crowe & McLeod’s 2020 U.S. follow-up) puts a crowd of sounds in the preschool years. Stops like *p, b, t, d, k, g*, nasals like *m, n*, glides like *w, y* — earlier. Some fricatives and *l*, *ch*, *j* — later in the preschool window. A short list, often including *r* and the *th* sounds, can still be settling after five when you use a strict 90-percent-of-children criterion.

If your printout disagrees by a year, it may be using a 50-percent criterion, a different word position, or a study from 1963. Ask which. The number is only as honest as the rule behind it.

## CDC did not give you a phoneme chart

The Act Early language lines care whether **others understand most of the time** at three (essay 10) and whether talk is doing social jobs — combinations, questions, stories. They do not say “says *k* in the middle of words.” That is a clinic instrument.

Identify the Signs is a little more sound-specific for the public: *p, b, m, h, w* used incorrectly at 2–3; *k, g, f, t, d, n* at 3–4; speech unclear even to familiar people at 2–3. Those are invitations to a conversation with an SLP, not a score you compute at dinner.

## Patterns versus one sound

A child who says *tap* for *cap* (a back sound coming out in the front) is doing something systematic. A child who cannot be understood in any context is doing something else. A child who only lisps on *s* and is otherwise crystal is doing a third thing. Families mash these together as “he can’t talk right.” A clinician un-mashes them. You can help by bringing three recorded examples — if everyone in the house agrees to it — or three written translations: what they said, what they meant, who understood.

Do not start drilling minimal pairs from a video. This series will not hand you a home articulation protocol (`CLAIMS_GUARDRAILS.md`).

## What is ordinary in the mouth at two and three

Final sounds drop. Clusters simplify (*top* for *stop*). Long words shrink. Unstressed syllables vanish. These “phonological processes” are described in every textbook because they are common in young speech. They are also the kind of thing an SLP watches for *persistence*, not presence. Persistence is a clinical judgment. Presence at two is often just being two.

If you correct every cute error, you will get a quieter child. ASHA’s public tip: do not interrupt to fix sounds; use the sounds correctly in your own talking.

## Hearing and teeth and “they’re just lazy”

Hearing belongs first (essay 22). Fluid can blur the quiet consonants. Teeth coming in can change an *s* for a season. “Lazy tongue” is not a diagnosis and not a kindness. Effort is usually not the missing ingredient.

Tongue-tie (ankyloglossia) is a medical finding some children have. It does not automatically explain a speech pattern, and a clip does not automatically buy adult consonants. ASHA and ENT groups have been cautious in public for a reason. Do not let a feed-and-sleep influencer finish that sentence (essay 37).

## Two languages, two sound systems

A Spanish-English household is not “behind on English *r*.” Sounds that do not exist in one language will look “late” if you only hold an English list. Tell the SLP every language the child hears. Bring a speaker of the other language if you can. ASHA’s bilingual pages are for this moment (essay 23).

## When to ask, without naming

Ask if the message is hard for familiar people at ages when Identify the Signs says that is worth a look. Ask if other children start asking “what?” and your child starts talking less. Ask if a teacher cannot understand them in the room. Ask if you are tired of being the interpreter at three and four.

Bring the McLeod sentence if you want: most consonants by five, variability allowed. Bring the CDC sentence: understood by others most of the time at three. Then let a licensed person look at *this* mouth, *this* hearing, *this* language history.

Clear enough is a social fact before it is a phonetic one. Can the people who need to understand, understand? That is the parent question. The IPA chart can wait in the clinic folder.

## Single words versus conversation

McLeod and Crowe’s studies, like most acquisition charts, leaned on picture-naming. Conversation is harder: faster, less careful, more grammar in the way. A child who can say *sun* on a card and loses *s* in “I see the sun” is ordinary. A child who is clear on cards and lost in the story is still a story worth telling. Ask the SLP which they used.

Crowe and McLeod (2020) narrowed the English-in-the-U.S. picture. If your photocopy is “Sander 1972” or a speech-therapy company poster, it may be a different criterion. Different is allowed. Unnamed is not.

## The word “lisp”

Families use *lisp* for any *s*. Clinicians split dentalized, lateral, interdental. You do not have to. Say “the *s* sounds slushy” or “the tongue is out.” Do not accept a promise that a six-week app will fix it. Do not accept “all kids lisp” if the teacher cannot understand the rest of the sentence either.

If a dentist mentions a bite and a sound in the same visit, let those two people talk. Essay 37. You are the messenger, not the judge of frena and palates.
