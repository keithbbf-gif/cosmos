**Disclaimer.** Educational only. Not medical advice. Not a screen and not a diagnosis. If you are worried about sound, looking, or a skill that went away, talk with your child’s doctor.

Sometime in the fourth month the living room gets a new soundtrack. Not words. Vowels with a little body in them — *oooo*, *aahh* — the thing English-speaking clinics have called cooing for a century.

CDC’s 4-month language line wants three observations: those open sounds, sounds *back* when you talk, and the head turning toward your voice. ASHA’s 4-to-6-month range mentions vowel sounds, sometimes with a consonant stuck on, *uuuuuummm* or *aaaaaaagoo*. By 6 months CDC has moved the camera: taking turns making sounds with you, blowing raspberries, squealing.

This is still not talking. It is the workshop where talking is built.

## Cooing is a motor and a social event

An open vowel means the jaw and the voice are coordinating well enough to play. That is interesting to a clinician. To a parent it is simpler: the baby has a sound they can offer you on purpose, or almost on purpose.

“Sounds back when you talk” is the item people miss because they are doing it. You say *hi baby* in that high, silly contour adults cannot help. The baby answers with a vowel. You answer the vowel. CDC’s 4-month tips call that teaching turns. You are not drilling conversation. You are showing that voices go back and forth.

If the room is quiet and you never get a sound in return — if the baby watches but the voice does not come to meet yours — say so at the visit. Pair it with the head-turn item. A baby who does not find your voice with their face might be telling you about hearing, or about attention, or about a sleepy afternoon. The pediatrician’s job is to help you sort that. A URL’s job is not.

## Raspberries are on the official list

The 6-month CDC page is almost cheerful: raspberries (tongue out, blow), squeals, taking turns. Families sometimes treat raspberries as a mess. The checklist treats them as a skill. Air, tongue, voice — the same parts that will later make consonants.

Squeals are pitch play. The larynx is learning a range. You do not need to encourage a squeal at 5 a.m. You can notice that the voice can go high and come back.

ASHA’s Identify the Signs campaign asks families to notice if a baby is *not* babbling in the 4-to-6-month window. CDC’s 6-month page is more specific about raspberries and turns than about canonical “ba-ba” strings; those sit on the 9-month page (essay 04). Do not average the two lists into a homemade deadline. If the 6-month items are missing, you still get to ask.

## The social line is doing language work

At 4 months CDC wants a smile that arrives to get your attention, a chuckle when you try to make them laugh, looks or moves or sounds to keep you. At 6 months: knows familiar people, likes the mirror, laughs. None of those are “speech sounds.” All of them are why speech sounds matter.

A raspberry at the ceiling fan is one thing. A raspberry at your face, waiting, is another. The second one is a turn.

## Hearing is still the quiet hinge

Turning toward your voice is an ear item wearing a communication label. If it is inconsistent, mention ear infections, congestion, the newborn screen result, any NICU stay. You are not diagnosing fluid. You are handing the doctor a reason to look.

CDC’s 4- and 6-month tips still treat screen time as a video-call-with-grandparents exception. The recommendation for children under 2 is not “educational apps.” Babies learn from people who answer them. A show does not take a raspberry turn.

## What you can do that is still just living

Copy the sound, then add a real word if you want. Baby: *aaaa.* You: *aaaa — apple?* You are not correcting. You are mapping.

Name what they look at. CDC says to point out cars and dogs on a walk. The pointing is for you at this age; their point comes later (essay 17).

Read the colorful junk mail if that is what is in your hand. “Reading” at six months is talking about pictures. CDC puts the word *read* in quotes on purpose.

Put them on the floor. The 6-month movement line — rolling, pushing up, sitting with hands — is not this essay’s job, but a baby who can sit a little can face you. Faces are where vowels get answered.

## What not to do with a milestone page

Do not count raspberries. Do not decide the baby is “advanced” because the squeal is loud. Do not decide they are “behind” because today’s stretch was quiet.

Do not start a home program you found under “teach baby to talk at 5 months.” There is no such program in this series.

## The mirror and the laugh

CDC’s 6-month social line likes the mirror and a real laugh. Those are not speech sounds. They are why you bother with speech sounds. A laugh is a vocal play with a social target. A mirror look is a face looking at a face. You can sit with the baby in your lap and let them find you in the glass without turning it into a cognitive exam.

If laughs never come and vowels never come and the head never turns, you have a cluster. Clusters go to the doctor as clusters. Isolated quiet days go to tomorrow.

## What “taking turns” is not

It is not a 50/50 conversation. You will still do most of the talking. The item is whether *any* return sound shows up when you offer one. A baby who vocalizes to the ceiling fan and never to your face is a different description than a baby who answers you and ignores the fan. Mention the difference.

Do not keep poking a tired baby for a raspberry so you can film the milestone. CDC’s own 2-month tips already told you to read the “I need a break” signs. Those signs did not expire at four months.

If the 4-month visit is tomorrow, three sentences are enough: they coo; they do or do not answer me; they do or do not turn to my voice. If the 6-month visit is tomorrow, add: we take sound turns; raspberries; squeals. Then the doctor’s questions. Then, if you are still carrying a worry, the sentence CDC printed for you: you know your baby best.
