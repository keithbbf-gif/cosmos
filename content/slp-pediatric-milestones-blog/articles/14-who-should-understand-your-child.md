**Disclaimer.** Educational only. Not medical advice. Not an intelligibility score and not a diagnosis. “I understood that” is a family fact. “Strangers should understand 100 percent” is a sentence that needs a source and an age.

There are two listeners in every kitchen.

You, who know that *gogi* is the neighbor’s dog. And the neighbor, who does not.

Intelligibility — how much of the message a listener gets — is a relationship, not a grade. It depends on the child’s mouth, the noise in the room, the topic, and how well the listener already knows the story.

## What CDC asked parents to notice

At **three**, the Act Early list wants talk “well enough for others to understand, most of the time.” Not at two. Not “perfectly.” Others.

Identify the Signs asks families to notice speech that is not easily understood by others at 3–4, and speech that is unclear even to familiar people at 2–3. Those are slightly different bars. Familiar listeners always hear more. If *you* are lost at two, that is a stronger “ask” than a sitter being lost at two.

## What the older clinic rules of thumb were

For years, pediatric offices quoted Coplan and Gleason (1988) and similar guides: about half of a two-year-old’s speech understandable to strangers, about three-quarters at three, and close to all of it at four. You will still meet that staircase on handouts.

Newer work measures conversation more carefully. Hustad and colleagues have published intelligibility growth curves in typical children (e.g., *JSLHR*, 2021) — percentages that rise with age and still show a spread. If a clinician quotes a percent, ask whether it was single words or conversation, and who was listening. Do not take a 1988 hallway number and a 2021 lab number and average them into a homemade cutoff.

This essay will not print a fake “your child should be 70 percent at 30 months.” The honest parent object is: who understands, in what situation, and are you doing all the translating.

## Why parents understand more

You share context. You saw the cracker hit the floor. You know the one stuffed animal that matters. You are also motivated. That is love, and it is bias. Both.

A useful experiment that is not a test: let a grandparent or a teacher tell you what they caught today, before you interpret. If they caught the topic and missed the details, that is one report. If they caught nothing, that is another.

Video on a quiet phone, if everyone is comfortable, helps an SLP more than your imitation of the child. Do not post it. Do not turn it into content.

## Noise, two languages, and shyness

A gym is a rotten intelligibility lab. So is a birthday party. Mention the setting.

In a bilingual house, a listener who only has English will “not understand” words that are not English. That is not a speech-sound problem (essay 23).

A child who talks in a whisper at school and a paragraph at home is giving you two intelligibility samples. Bring both.

## When unclear speech is the only issue

Sometimes language ideas are rich — combinations, questions, stories — and the mouth is the bottleneck. Sometimes the mouth is fine and the sentences are thin. Sometimes both. Parents feel one blob of worry. Clinicians try to split the blob. You can start the split: “People don’t understand the words” versus “They don’t have many words” versus “They don’t try to tell us.”

Do not accept “they’re just being lazy” or “all boys are mushy.” Those are not sources.

## Hearing, again

If intelligibility dropped after a winter of colds, say that. Fluid can muffle the quiet sounds that make words distinct. A hearing check is a reasonable ask when speech is hard to understand (essay 22). It does not mean you have decided anything about ears for life.

## What to do while you wait for a visit

ASHA’s public advice still fits: model the adult word; do not make them say it again ten times to earn the cracker; reduce the chaos when you really need to hear them; look at their face. You can repeat what you *think* they said — “You want the blue cup?” — and let them confirm. That is conversation. It is not therapy.

If frustration is high — theirs or yours — that is part of the story you tell the doctor. Frustration is not a diagnosis. It is a reason humans ask for help.

## A worked example you can steal

You understand “I want the blue cup.” The sitter hears “ah wah uh boo tuh.” The teacher hears nothing on the rug. That is three listeners, one child. Write it that way. Do not average them into “kind of unclear.”

If all three hear everything and a relative still wants a photocopy of *r*, the relative can wait. McLeod and Crowe gave *r* time (essay 13). If all three hear nothing and the child is three, you do not need the relative to vote.

## Recording, consent, and dignity

A 30-second clip in a quiet room, if the child does not mind and you will not post it, helps. A clip of you humiliating them into talking does not. Ask the clinic how they want media. Delete it when they have heard it.

Clear speech is a gift to the child because it lets the world answer them. You can want that gift without turning the next six months into a drill. Ask. Describe. Let someone trained listen.
