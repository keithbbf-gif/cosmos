**Disclaimer.** Educational only. Not medical advice. Not a screen and not a diagnosis. Babble is not a first word. If babble is missing or a skill disappeared, talk with your child’s doctor.

There is a week when the baby starts giving speeches in no known language. Long strings. *Mamamama. Babababa. Upup.* The cadence of a person with something to say and no vocabulary yet.

CDC’s 9-month language line is short: different sounds like *mamamama* and *babababa*, and arms up to be picked up. ASHA’s 7-to-9-month range is the long babble string. Identify the Signs asks families to notice if there are only a few sounds or gestures in this window.

This is the age people start arguing about whether *mama* “counts.” It does not have to. Not yet. The 12-month page is where CDC wants a parent name used as a name (essay 05). At nine months the win is the *string* — the same syllable cycling, the jaw and the lips in a rhythm.

## Why clinicians care about canonical babble

Researchers who study infant vocal development (Oller’s work is the usual citation in clinics) distinguish goos and isolated vowels from **canonical babble**: a consonant-like closure plus a vowel, repeated. It is a motor milestone wearing a social costume. Cross-language work finds the timing is stubbornly similar even when the adult language is not English. You do not need the journal. You need to hear whether the baby can *play a beat* with their mouth.

A single *ba* in the bathtub is nice. A run of *bababa* while they look at you is the item.

If the mouth is quiet except for crying, or the sounds never get that sing-song consonant beat, write it down for the 9-month visit. That visit is also when the AAP wants a **standardized developmental screen** for every child (Lipkin & Macias, 2020). CDC’s 9-month page says so on the sheet: it is time. Ask whether it happened. A screen is still not a diagnosis. It is the next, more formal look.

## Arms up is language

CDC put “lifts arms to be picked up” on the communication line, not only on movement. Good. The baby is using the body to send a message you can answer. That is the same job a word will have later.

You can answer with words. “Up. You want up.” You are not required to wait for the perfect arm shape before you pick them up. This is not a compliance drill.

## Name, peekaboo, and the social sheet

The 9-month social line is doing heavy lifting: shy with strangers, several facial expressions, looks when you call their name, reacts when you leave, smiles or laughs at peekaboo. “Looks when you call her name” is one of the items families remember later, when someone on the internet has turned it into a scare headline. On the CDC page it is one observation among others, at an age when 75 percent or more of children would be expected to do it.

If they do not look, say that. Mention hearing. Mention what name you actually use — some households have three. Do not let a comment thread name a condition.

Object permanence — looking for a spoon that dropped — sits on the cognitive line. It is the cousin of looking for *you* when you leave. Separation protest is not a speech problem. It is a person problem, which is the right kind.

## What you can do without turning the living room into therapy

CDC’s 9-month tips are almost a style guide. Repeat their *bababa*, then say a real word that starts that way: *book*. Wave bye-bye and shake your head *no* as you leave — you are offering the gesture they will steal. Peekaboo. Dump the blocks out and put them back. Hand a toy back and forth so “my turn, your turn” has an object.

Read pictures. Name them. You are not teaching phonics.

Baby sign shows up in CDC’s own tip list as optional help before words. Essay 38 stays there. It is not required. It does not replace babble.

Screens stay in the video-call exception. A babble string wants a face.

## Hearing, again

Fluid after a cold can dull the edges of *ba* and *ma*. You will not see fluid from the couch. You can say, “They used to turn when I said their name in the kitchen. This month I am less sure.” That sentence is allowed.

If newborn screening was a pass and you have never thought about hearing since, you are normal. If something feels off, you do not need a new personality. You need a question at the visit.

## What this age is not

It is not a first-word deadline. Relatives who heard *mama* at seven months and want a report are hearing a syllable the baby likes. Enjoy it. Do not file it as a résumé.

It is not too late if the strings are just arriving. Skills have ranges. ASHA built ranges on purpose.

## Canonical versus “talking to themselves”

A baby on their back, alone, running *bababa* at the mobile, is practicing the motor piece. A baby in your lap, running *bababa*, then waiting, is practicing the social piece. Both count toward “makes different sounds like mamamama and babababa.” Mention which one you have. An SLP would rather hear that split than a yes/no.

Oller’s stages (phonation, goos, expansion, canonical) are clinic vocabulary. You do not need to memorize them. You need to hear whether a true syllable — a closure plus a vowel — has arrived. *Aaa* is older work. *Ba* is newer work. The 9-month CDC examples are the newer work on purpose.

## The 9-month screen is not this article

Ask what tool they used. Ages and Stages (ASQ) is a common one; there are others. Write the name. If they hand you a CDC checklist and call it the screen, you can politely say AAP wanted a **validated** tool (Lipkin & Macias, 2020) and the CDC footer agrees the checklist is not that tool. You are not being difficult. You are reading the page they printed.

If the screen flags communication, that is still not a diagnosis. It is a next look. If it does not flag and you are still worried, you still have the footer.

It is not a diagnosis if the strings are late. It is a reason to use the 9-month screen you are already owed, and to talk. CDC’s act-early language still fits: you know this baby best.
