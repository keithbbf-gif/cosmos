**Disclaimer.** Educational only. Not medical advice. Not a screening tool and not a diagnosis. This essay explains how researchers used the phrase “late talking.” It does not apply that phrase to your child.

The internet loves a category. *Late talker.* It sounds like a club with a T-shirt and a prognosis.

In the journals, it is usually **late language emergence (LLE)** or a small score on a **parent-report** vocabulary checklist at a **named age**, often 24 months. The child is in a *group* that researchers followed. The group is not standing in your kitchen.

## What Zubrick and colleagues counted

Zubrick, Taylor, Rice, and Slegers (2007) published an epidemiological study of late language emergence at **24 months** in a large Australian sample (*JSLHR*). They used parent report, looked at prevalence and predictors, and treated LLE as a research definition. Read that sentence again. **Parent report. Twenty-four months. A definition for a study.**

They did not hand you permission to wait. They did not hand you a crystal ball. Follow-up papers in this literature (Rescorla’s reviews; Dale, Price, Bishop & Plomin, 2003, on outcomes of early language delay) keep finding the same unsatisfying truth: **many** children in these groups look typical later, and **some** do not, and the early predictors are imperfect.

Rescorla (2011) asked, in a review title, whether good predictors of outcome exist. The honest answer is “not good enough that a parent should gamble.” That is why CDC’s 2022 lists tried to kill casual wait-and-see (essay 01, essay 39). That is why ASHA tells the public not to wait if they are concerned.

## Fifty words, which birthday?

Parent lore: fifty words at two. CDC 2022: **about fifty words at 30 months.** Research screens: often 24 months, sometimes a cutoff around a small vocabulary and no two-word combinations (Rescorla’s Language Development Survey, 1989, is a **screen**, not a toy).

If you mix these, you can make any child look late or early. Name the object in your hand.

## What the studies usually left out, or put in the covariate table

Hearing. Gesture. Understanding. Two languages (sometimes mishandled). How the parent was asked. Whether the child was born early. Whether anyone else in the family had a hard time with language. Zubrick et al. reported predictors and covariates — the point of an epi paper. Your child is not a covariate.

If a blog says “late talkers are usually boys who will catch up,” they are flattening a table. Sex differences, when they appear, are **group** findings. They are not a hall pass (guardrails).

## “Catch up” is a mean of a distribution

Some children in LLE groups later score in the average range on language tests. Some have lingering weak spots in grammar or reading. Some receive clinical diagnoses later — and this essay will not list those diagnoses as destiny. A mean cannot sit with you at 2 a.m.

The useful sentence from the literature is negative: **we cannot tell from a word count alone who will need what.** The useful sentence from CDC/ASHA is practical: if you are worried, act early — talk, screen, ask about evaluation.

## What to do with the phrase if someone uses it

Ask: counted how, at what age, in which languages, with what tool? Ask: what do you want to do next — watch, screen, refer? “Late talker, don’t worry” is not a plan. “Late talker, here’s a validated screen and an SLP referral” is a plan that still does not diagnose.

You can want a closer look without accepting the T-shirt.

## What this series will not say

We will not say your child is a late talker.  
We will not say they will catch up.  
We will not say they will not.  
We will not sell a supplement, a DVD, or a 30-day talking plan.

We will say: CDC’s items and ASHA’s ranges are conversation tools; research definitions are research definitions; your worry is already a reason to talk to a person with a license.

## What “parent report” means in a paper

Someone filled out a form. Often a checklist of words. Sometimes a yes/no about combinations. The form is only as good as the parent’s memory and the form’s language. Bilingual households have been mishandled in more than one study. If you read a prevalence number (Zubrick et al. reported one for their definition), remember whose mothers were asked, in which language, in which country.

Rescorla’s LDS was designed as a **screen** — a first pass — not as a coffee-table diagnosis. If a clinician uses it, they should say what happens next, not stop at a nickname.

## Why this essay exists

Because the T-shirt version of “late talker” sells comfort and sells fear in the same shop. The papers sell neither. They sell a group, a tool, an age, and a lot of leftover variance. Your child is the leftover variance. That is why we do not stamp. That is why we still knock on doors.
