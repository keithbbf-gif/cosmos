**Disclaimer.** Educational only. Not medical advice. Not a language test and not a diagnosis. “They understand everything” is a compliment families say. Sometimes it is true. Sometimes it is hope. A visit can tell those apart.

Talking is the part you can post. Understanding is the part that keeps a toddler alive in a parking lot.

Clinicians split **receptive** language (what goes in) from **expressive** language (what comes out). Parents live in one afternoon. This essay is about not letting the loud half erase the quiet half.

## CDC already hid comprehension on the communication line

Look at the ages again, not as word counts:

- 12 months: understands *no* — a pause, a stop.
- 15 months: looks at a familiar object when you name it; follows a direction **with** a gesture.
- 18 months: follows a one-step direction **without** a gesture.
- 2 years: points to the bear in the book; points to two body parts.
- 30 months: names when asked “What is this?” (that one is expressive); two-step instructions sit on the cognitive line.
- 3–5 years: questions, functions, retell — comprehension wearing harder clothes.

ASHA’s ranges do the same work: following “Give me the ball,” “Show me your nose.” NIDCD’s public milestone page has always been half listening.

If you only track words out, you will miss the child who has a map of the house in their head and a mouth that has not opened the gate.

## The “they understand everything” trap

Sometimes it is true. They fetch shoes you named from the next room. They laugh at a joke that was only words. They bring the right cousin the right cup.

Sometimes “everything” means they read your body. You looked at the shoes. You walked to the door. You held the leash. A careful clinician will try the words without the choreography. You can try it once at home, kindly: same request, hands still, eyes not cheating. If it falls apart, that is information, not a gotcha.

Identify the Signs includes “does not understand what others say” across a wide age band. That item is about the quiet half. It is an ask, not a name.

## When talking is ahead of understanding

Less common in the toddler folklore, real enough. A child can echo phrases from a show and not follow “put it in the basket.” Echolalia — repeating — has clinical meanings this blog will not assign. What you can say: “They have a lot of scripts; I am less sure they follow new directions.” That sentence is allowed. A diagnosis is not.

## Hearing is the first fork

You cannot understand what you do not hear. A child who “ignores” you in the next room may be busy, or may be missing the word. Mention ears, last cold, last hearing screen (essay 22). Do not skip this fork because the child startles at the blender. Blenders are loud. *Cup* is not.

## Two languages, two incoming streams

Understanding in the home language can be rich while preschool English is thin. That is not a delay. That is a time-on-task fact. Test (and live) in the languages the child actually hears. ASHA’s bilingual public pages are stubborn about this (essay 23).

## Why the split matters at the visit

A child with strong understanding and few words is a different question than a child with thin understanding and few words. Both may get a closer look. They will not get the same look. Early intervention and SLPs sort this on purpose. Your job is to bring examples of both columns:

**In:** What they fetch, what they look at when named, what they do with *no*, what happens when you do not point.  
**Out:** Words, combinations, gestures, how they get your help.

Write five of each. That is a better gift than “speech is delayed,” a phrase this series will not print about your child.

## What you can do that feeds understanding

Talk about what they are already looking at. That is the cheapest comprehension work on earth. CDC’s infant tips are this idea in a onesie.

Give the direction, then wait. Then show. Then help. You are allowed to help. This is not a test.

Books: “Where is the bear?” is a comprehension item at two. You are playing CDC’s game when you play that game.

Do not run a homemade “receptive therapy” protocol from a video. Do not withhold objects until they prove they understood. That turns language into a trick.

If understanding is the worry, say it first at the visit, before the word count. The quiet half deserves the first sentence sometimes.

## Body parts and books are not tricks

“Show me your nose” (ASHA’s range) and “Where is the bear?” (CDC at two) are the same skill on different objects. Try them when the child is not already in trouble. If they can show a nose and cannot find a bear, the book may be the problem — a new picture, a busy page. Try a simpler page. Then stop testing.

If they find the bear only when your eyes go there first, you cheated. Mention that you cheated. Clinicians cheat too, then they stop cheating on purpose.

## “They’re so smart, they just don’t talk”

Intelligence is not a language sample. A child can be clever with locks and thin with words. A child can be verbal and still learning to think. Do not use smart as a reason to skip a look, or quiet as a reason to lower your idea of them. Bring the lock story *and* the word list. Let the person with tools hold both.

NIDCD’s public pages have always given listening its own rows. You can too. Write five “in” examples before you write the “out” list, just once, so the visit does not start with performance.
