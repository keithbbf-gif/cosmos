**Disclaimer.** Educational only. Not medical advice. Not a screening tool and not a diagnosis. Newborns vary. If something worries you — especially hearing or a skill that disappeared — talk with your child’s doctor.

The first language lesson is not a word. It is a face.

On the CDC 2-month page, the language/communication line is only two items long: the baby makes sounds other than crying, and the baby reacts to loud sounds. The social line is doing more of the talking — calms when spoken to or picked up, looks at your face, seems happy to see you walk up, smiles when you talk or smile. ASHA’s birth-to-3-months range says a baby may quiet or smile when you talk.

That is the whole early curriculum: sound going out, sound coming in, and a person to look at.

## Sounds that are not crying

Crying is communication. It is also loud enough to erase everything else in the room. The CDC item is asking whether anything else is in the mix — a whimper, a coo-ish vowel, a grunt on the changing table, a little voiced sigh after a feed. NIDCD’s public milestone page has, for years, put “coos” and different cries for different needs in this stretch. You do not need a phonetic transcription. You need to know the voice is being used for more than alarm.

Some days the extra sounds show up after milk, when the body is quiet. Some days they show up at 2 a.m. and you are not in a data-collection mood. That is fine. The item is not a quota.

If you are not hearing any non-cry voice by the 2-month visit, say that. It is a conversation, not a verdict.

## Reacting to loud sounds

A startle, a blink, a stilling, a look — CDC is asking whether the auditory system is in the game. This is not a hearing test. The hearing test already had a job in the hospital.

In the United States, newborn hearing screening is standard under Early Hearing Detection and Intervention. The Joint Committee on Infant Hearing’s 2019 position statement is the professional rulebook: screen, then diagnose, then intervene on a tight timetable when a baby does not pass. A pass in the nursery is good news. It is not a lifetime certificate. Fluid, later illness, or a progressive condition can change the picture. If you later wonder whether sound is getting in, you can ask for another check. You do not have to wait for a word to be late.

A baby who sleeps through the dog is not automatically a concern. A baby who never startles, never stills to a voice, never seems to find your face with their ears — that is worth saying out loud at the visit.

## The face is the first sentence

Looking at you is listed under social/emotional on the CDC sheet. Treat it as language anyway. Infants are wired for faces. When you talk, the mouth moves, the pitch moves, the eyes crinkle. Kuhl and other infant-speech labs spent decades showing that babies are already sorting the sounds of the language around them long before they can answer. You do not need the lab. You need the look.

ASHA’s early tip is almost embarrassingly small: listen and respond. CDC’s 2-month tips say to act excited, smile, and talk when the baby makes sounds, because that is how turn-taking gets practiced. You will not spoil a newborn by picking them up. CDC prints that on purpose. The older advice to let them cry it out so they “learn” is not a language strategy.

If looking at faces is hard — if the baby rarely settles on your eyes, rarely calms to voice — bring it to the doctor. Do not let a blog name a reason.

## What you can do that is not a program

Talk about the boring work. “I’m washing your hands.” “That’s the latch.” The sentences can be short. The voice can be the voice you actually have at 5 a.m.

Sing if you sing. Hum if you do not. The melody is extra timing information. You are not auditioning.

Limit your own screen when you are the person in the room. CDC’s 2-month tips say being responsive is easier when the phone is not winning. That is not a moral ranking of parents. It is physics: a baby cannot take a turn with a forehead.

Watch for the baby’s “I need a break” signs — turning away, yawning, fussing. CDC names those. Language is a social game. Games have pauses.

## Prematurity belongs on the card

The CDC “other important things” box asks whether the baby was born early or has special health-care needs. Corrected age — counting from the due date — is a pediatric conversation for the first couple of years, not a rule you invent from a blog (essay 36). If the nursery stay was long, bring the discharge papers. Hearing rescreens after a NICU stay are common. Ask what was already done.

## What this age is not

It is not “first words.” Anyone quoting a first-word deadline in month two is selling something.

It is not a personality test. A quiet baby is still a baby.

It is not proof of future eloquence if the coos are gorgeous, and it is not a diagnosis if they are scarce. It is a two-item communication line and a social line about faces.

## A Tuesday that counts as data

You are holding the baby against your shoulder. The dishwasher thuds. They flinch, or they do not. You say their name in a voice you would not use at work. They still, or they keep rooting. Later, on the mat, a small voiced sigh that is not a cry — you would miss it if you were only waiting for a milestone word. Write the sigh down if you are the kind of person who writes things down. It is a sound other than crying. CDC asked for that, not for a sonnet.

Partners argue about whether a grimace was a smile. ASHA’s birth-to-3-months line will take a quieting or a smile when you talk. You do not need a photographer. You need to know whether the voice does *anything* to the body.

If the first weeks were a blur of jaundice lights or a NICU, say that at the visit before anyone compares this baby to a cousin. Essay 36. The 2-month page still wants the same two communication items. Context sits in the box labeled special health-care needs.

## What this age will not prove

It will not prove they will be a talker. It will not prove they will not. Infant-speech labs (Kuhl’s work is the name clinicians drop) show that babies are already sorting the vowels of the language around them. That is group science. Your living room is one baby. The science is a reason to keep talking, not a reason to buy a language DVD.

It will not replace the newborn hearing result. If you never got a clear “pass” in language you understand, ask now. JCIH’s clock is measured in months. Language later sits on that clock whether or not anyone in the family is thinking about speech yet.

If you want a sentence to take to the 2-month visit, try: “Here is what I hear that is not crying. Here is how they react to a bang in the kitchen. Here is how they look at me when I talk.” That is surveillance. It is enough.
