**Disclaimer.** Educational only. Not medical advice and not an audiology report. This page cannot tell you whether your child hears. It can tell you why asking is ordinary.

Language is sound dressed up as people. If the sound is muffled, the dress still exists — you just cannot see the pattern from the cheap seats.

## The hospital screen is a beginning

In the U.S., almost every newborn is screened before discharge. The Joint Committee on Infant Hearing’s 2019 position statement is the professional timetable: screen, then confirm, then start services when needed, on a clock measured in months, not years. CDC’s EHDI pages are the public door.

A **pass** is good. It is not a promise that hearing will never change. A **refer** or **fail** is not a diagnosis of lifelong deafness. It is a next appointment. Go to the next appointment. The clock is the point.

NICU stays often mean a different screening path and a higher chance of a later check. Bring the discharge papers. Ask what was already done.

## CDC’s early items are ear items in disguise

Reacts to loud sounds (2 months). Turns toward your voice (4 months). Looks when you call a name (9 months). Follows words without a gesture (18 months). If those wobble, mention hearing before you mention willpower.

Startling at the blender and missing *cup* can live in the same child. Loud is easy. Soft consonants and distant speech are not.

## Fluid is common and still counts

Middle-ear fluid after a cold can dull hearing for a while. Some children get it often. You will not diagnose fluid from the couch. You can say: “Every month this winter they stop answering from the next room.” You can ask the pediatrician to look at the ears and to talk about a hearing test if the story keeps repeating.

This series will not tell you to demand tubes, or to refuse tubes, or to take a supplement for ears. Those are medical decisions. The language fact is simpler: inconsistent access to sound makes inconsistent access to *s, f, th* and to your voice from the kitchen.

## Later-onset and progressive hearing loss exist

JCIH and CDC both remind families that passing at birth is not the end of surveillance. Head trauma, some infections, some genetic conditions, some medications — your doctor knows the list. You know whether the TV got louder and the teacher says they don’t turn.

ASHA’s public hearing pages are for this moment: if you are repeating yourself or using a loud voice, consider a hearing test. That is their sentence, almost word for word.

## Listening is a skill on top of hearing

A child can hear a tone in a booth and still have a hard time listening in a classroom. Those are different questions (auditory processing is a clinical thicket this blog will not enter). Start with the booth when the question is “is sound getting in?” Start with the teacher when the question is “can they follow in noise?”

## Two languages do not break hearing

If a child answers in one language and not another, that is not an ear. That is a language history (essay 23).

## What to take to the visit

When it started. Which situations (next room, group, one-on-one). Last newborn result if you have it. How many ear infections you can remember. Whether speech clarity changed after a winter. Whether they look when you call their name in a quiet kitchen.

You can ask for a hearing evaluation without deciding that words are “delayed.” You can ask for words to be looked at without skipping ears. The honest order is often ears first, then speech-language, then both.

## What you can do at home that is not a treatment

Face them when it matters. Cut the extra noise when you need them to hear a new word. Do not assume they are ignoring you from the next room — walk closer, then decide.

If a child uses hearing technology, this series will not coach you on it. Their audiologist will. Keep those appointments. Language rides on that access.

You are not dramatic for asking whether sound is getting in. You are doing the first language check that exists.

## What a “hearing test” can mean

A pediatrician looking at eardrums is not a hearing test. A sound booth with an audiologist is. OAEs, tympanometry, visual reinforcement — you do not need the alphabet. You need to know who did what. Write the names. If someone says “hearing is fine” after a glance, you can ask for the booth if the language story is loud.

JCIH’s 1-3-6 benchmarks (screen by 1 month, diagnosis by 3, intervention by 6, in the 2019 statement’s spirit) are for *infants who do not pass*. Older children have a different path. Do not apply a newborn clock to a three-year-old with fluid. Do apply urgency if sound access is in doubt.

## Teachers and the back of the room

“They don’t listen at circle” can be hearing, language, attention, or a boring circle. Ask where they sit. Ask whether one-to-one is better. Ask whether the voice is always “what?” Those three answers change the next referral. You can want ears checked without deciding the teacher is wrong. You can want language checked without deciding the ears are fine. Both referrals can be in the same week.
