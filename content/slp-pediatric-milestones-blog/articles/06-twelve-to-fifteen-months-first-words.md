**Disclaimer.** Educational only. Not medical advice. Not a screen and not a diagnosis. One messy word is still a word if it is used on purpose. Talk with your child’s doctor if you are worried.

The 15-month visit is one of the ages CDC added in 2022 so the fridge chart would match a real well-child appointment. The language line is suddenly busier.

Tries to say one or two words besides *mama* or *dada* — *ba* for ball, *da* for dog. Looks at a familiar object when you name it. Follows a direction that comes with a gesture *and* words — you hold out your hand and say “Give me the toy,” and the toy arrives. Points to ask for something or to get help.

ASHA’s 13-to-18-month range talks about following directions like “Give me the ball” or “Show me your nose.” The overlap is the point: words are arriving, and understanding is already at work.

## What “tries to say” is doing in the sentence

CDC did not write “says two adult words clearly.” They wrote *tries to say*, and they gave reduced forms as examples. That is a gift. A ball that comes out *ba*, used when the ball is wanted or seen, is the item. Essay 15 is the longer argument about counting.

If you are keeping a list on your phone, write the form you hear and what it means. *Ba* = ball, bottle, or Baba? Consistency is what makes it a word. A new sound every day that never repeats is still play.

One or two besides the parent names is the 75-percent bar on this page. It is not a personality score. Some children arrive at the visit with six. Some arrive with one and a lot of pointing. The pointing is on the page too.

## Looking when you name it

This is understanding, not talking. You say *shoes* and the eyes go to the shoes by the door. That is a vocabulary item you cannot hear on a recording of the child’s voice.

If talking is thin and looking-when-named is rich, you have a common pattern: comprehension ahead of expression (essay 16). If both are thin — you name the dog and there is no search, no stilling, no look — mention hearing and mention that pair at the visit. Do not let a blog decide which door that is.

## Directions with a gesture

CDC is not asking for a two-step chore. They are asking whether speech-plus-hand works as a package. You put your hand out. You say the words. The toy moves toward you.

A child who only responds to the hand, never the words, is still responding. Mention both. A child who does neither might not be stubborn. They might not have the idea yet, or the sound, or the interest in that toy. Try it with something they love. Then tell the doctor what happened in the kitchen, not in a quiz.

## Pointing to ask or to get help

This is a different point from “look at that airplane” (the 18-month social sheet has *points to show you something interesting*). At 15 months CDC wants the request point: I cannot reach; I want; help.

A reach-and-grunt is older. A point with a look back at your face is a small miracle of social thinking. Joint attention has its own essay (18). If there is no point and no show and no give, that cluster matters as much as the word count. Identify the Signs has been saying so to the public for years.

## Social crumbs on the same page

Copies other children at play, shows you an object they like, claps, hugs a stuffed toy, shows affection. Those are not extra credit. Showing you the object is proto-conversation: I have this; I want you to know.

CDC’s tips for this age are the ones tired parents can actually do. Repeat and add to the stub word. Wait a few seconds after they point before you hand the thing over, to see if a sound comes. Let them fetch the shoes. Sing “Wheels on the Bus” and see if a gesture tries to join. Name feelings without giving a lecture.

Screens: still the video-call exception. Under 2, the AAP and CDC are aligned in spirit — people, not programs.

## What not to harvest from this visit

Do not harvest a delay label from two missing words. Do not harvest “they’re fine, skip it” from two present words if your stomach is still tight. The 15-month page exists because someone decided this visit should have a list. Use the list as a conversation. Use your worry as a conversation. Those can be the same conversation.

## Jargon and the almost-sentence

Some children in this stretch produce long strings with the melody of speech and one real word dropped in — clinicians have called that jargon or “conversational babble.” It is not a CDC box. It is useful to mention: “They give speeches; I catch *ba* and *up*.” Speeches without any islands of meaning are a different mention.

NIDCD’s public milestone page has, for a long time, put first words in a range rather than on a birthday. If your family is fighting about 12 versus 15 versus 18, they may be holding three different public documents. Bring the ones you used.

## Walking and talking, not a trade

The 15-month movement line wants a few steps. Families still repeat the myth that children cannot walk and talk in the same season. They can. Some do one first. That is not a law of conservation of development. Mention both so nobody “explains away” words with walking, or walking with words.

Loss of words they already had — a *ba* that vanished, a wave that stopped — is the item every CDC page treats as call-now. That is not this essay inventing urgency. That is the footer. Write the last day you heard the thing, if you know it. Dates help more than adjectives.
