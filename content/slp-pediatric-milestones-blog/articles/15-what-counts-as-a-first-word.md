**Disclaimer.** Educational only. Not medical advice. Families argue about first words for sport. A clinician counts with rules. This page explains the rules. It does not award a trophy or a delay label.

Someone will want the date for the baby book. Someone else will say *mama* at seven months “doesn’t count.” Both people are trying to be precise and both will be a little wrong.

## The three-part test families can actually use

A word, in the sense milestone lists mean, usually needs three things:

1. **A stable form.** Not a new mouth-shape every time. *Ba* can be the form.
2. **A stable meaning.** *Ba* is ball more often than it is random joy.
3. **A purpose.** They use it to comment, request, greet, protest — something that makes you a listener.

Babble fails (2) and often (3). *Mamamama* in the crib is a rhythm (essay 04). *Mama* when you appear, with a look, is why CDC’s 12-month item is “calls a parent mama or dada or another special name.”

Imitations that never appear again fail (3). If they only say *bye* when you say *say bye*, write “imitates bye.” If they wave and say it at the door tomorrow without a prompt, you can move it to the word list.

## Reduced forms count

CDC’s 15-month examples are *ba* for ball and *da* for dog. The adult word is in your mouth, not theirs. NIDCD’s public pages have long treated early words as approximations. If you only count dictionary pronunciations, you will under-count and scare yourself.

Write the child’s form and the meaning. That list is gold in a clinic. “Ball, bottle, Baba” all mapping to *ba* is also useful — one form, several meanings, a later clarity problem, not a “no words” problem.

## What about animal sounds, signs, and *uh-oh*?

Parent-report tools used in research (MacArthur-Bates CDI; Rescorla’s LDS) have rules for these. They do not all agree. A reasonable kitchen rule:

- **Animal sounds used as names** (*moo* for the cow) — count on a parent list; tell the SLP you did.
- **True baby signs or AAC symbols** — count as words in a **total communication** list; keep a separate oral-word list so nobody erases their mouth (essays 38, 41).
- **Social pieces** (*hi, bye, uh-oh, wow*) — count if they are consistent and used in the right moments.
- **Family-only jokes** — count if they are stable; explain them.

Do not fight your mother-in-law about *moo*. Write two numbers: oral words, and all symbols.

## Names and “another special name”

CDC allows a name that is not mama/dada. The dog counts. A sibling counts. A grandparent’s language’s word for you counts. The item is a person-form mapping, not English.

If the only oral word is a parent name at 15 months, CDC still wants one or two *besides*. That is their 75-percent item. It is not a moral score.

## How many, at what age, on which list

12 months: a parent name (CDC).  
15 months: one or two besides (CDC).  
18 months: three or more besides (CDC).  
30 months: about fifty (CDC).  

ASHA uses ranges and slightly different examples. Research on late language emergence often used 24-month parent report and a small-vocabulary cutoff (essay 24). If you mix these without naming them, you will invent a child who is “behind” on Tuesday and “fine” on Wednesday.

## Words in more than one language

Count them all. A child with *agua* and *milk* has two words, not a confusion. Do not require that every concept exist in both languages before you count. ASHA: mixed vocabularies are typical (essay 23).

## When the first word is late to the party

Look at the rest of the communication system before you stare at the baby book. Gestures (essay 17). Understanding (essay 16). Hearing (essay 22). Turns and looks. Identify the Signs cares about the cluster.

You can ask for help before a magic word arrives. You can also have a late first word and a child who is doing fine on a careful look. Neither sentence is available from this URL. Both sentences are why visits exist.

Put the almost-words on the list. Take the list in. Let the date in the baby book be a celebration, not a court exhibit.

## A kitchen argument, settled without a winner

You heard *ba* at the park looking at a ball. Your partner says that was a grunt. Play the week, not the moment. If *ba* comes back at the ball basket three times, you have a word. If it never returns, you have a nice afternoon. CDC’s “tries to say” is on your side of almost. Honesty is on your partner’s side of not padding. You can both be useful.

Person names that are nicknames count. *Papa* in one language and *Daddy* in another are two words if both are used. A dog that answers to three names still counts as one meaning if the child has one form.

## Protowords

Clinicians use *protoword* for a stable homemade form that is not the adult word — *nana* for whatever, *ghee* for the blanket. Count them. Explain them. They are the opposite of random. They are the child’s first contracts with meaning.

If the only forms are protowords and there are many, you have a system. If there are none, you have a different week. Either way, the list is the object. Not the argument.
