**Disclaimer.** Educational only. Not medical advice. Not a fluency evaluation and not a diagnosis. This page will not teach a home fluency program. It will not tell you to ignore what you see.

Language is speeding up. The mouth is still hiring. For a lot of preschoolers, words come out twice. *I I I want…* A phrase restarts. There is a little *um*.

Clinicians call some of that **typical disfluency**. They call other patterns **stuttering** after they have looked. You do not have to apply either word. You have to decide whether to ask.

## What Yairi and Ambrose actually gave us

Yairi and Ambrose (2013 review; earlier Illinois work) described the epidemiology of stuttering in young children: it often **starts in the preschool years**, more boys than girls in many samples, and a **substantial portion** of young children who begin later speak fluently — a group finding, not a promise about your kitchen. Recovery, when it happens, is not something you create by pretending not to hear.

ASHA’s public stuttering page is the parent door: what it can sound like, that help exists, that you can see an SLP. Identify the Signs includes fluency among the reasons families call.

This essay will not quote a fake recovery percentage. If a clinician quotes one, they can name the paper.

## What parents often notice

**Easy repetitions.** Whole words, a relaxed face, the child keeps playing. Common when sentences are getting longer.

**Tension.** Blinks, a jaw that works too hard, a pitch rise, a child who starts to avoid talking, a fear face. That cluster is a stronger “ask soon.”

**Duration.** A week after a big language burst is one story. Months of struggle is another.

You can write what you hear without labeling it: “He repeats the first sound, his mouth tightens, he stops and says never mind.” That is a gift to an SLP.

## What not to do (and what not to do the other way)

Do not say *slow down* every time. Do not say *stop and start over*. Do not make them perform the sentence. Do not laugh. Do not finish every word unless they want you to.

Do not go silent and look worried. Children read faces.

Do not wait because “all kids stutter” if *your* stomach is tight or the child’s body is working. Wait-and-see as a rule is how families lose a year (essay 39). Group recovery statistics are not a babysitter.

Do not start a YouTube breathing protocol. Guardrails: no fluency-shaping steps in this pack.

## What you can do that is just being a person

Give time. Look like you have time even if you do not. Reduce the pile-on of questions. Take a turn yourself so the conversation is not a spotlight. Keep the day ordinary.

If they ask why talking is hard, you can say talking is working extra right now and we can get a person who knows about that. You do not need a lecture on the brain.

## Hearing, language, and two languages

Disfluency can show up when a second language is new, or when sentences just got longer. Mention the language history. Mention if understanding or words were already a concern. An SLP will want the whole picture. They will not want a parent-made theory that “the bilingualism caused it.” ASHA does not treat two languages as a fluency disease (essay 23).

## The ask

You can call a pediatric SLP who lists fluency without a referral in many states; ask your pediatrician anyway if you want ears and a well-child note in the same week. You can ask how soon they like to see preschoolers. Early advice to *families* — how you listen — is part of care. That is not the same as a home drill.

If the child is in sudden distress, or you are, do not finish this article. Call the people you already have.

Repeating a word does not name a future. Struggle is a reason to let a trained listener in. Both sentences can be true at once.

## What to write down for the SLP

When it started. Whether it came with a leap in sentences. Whether anyone else in the family had a hard time getting words out as children — history, not destiny. What the face does. What the child does after: keeps talking, stops, says “you talk.” Which situations: tired, excited, new people. A 20-second clip only if the child is not performing fear for the camera.

Yairi and Ambrose’s epidemiology is a reason not to promise chronic stuttering from one week of easy repetitions. It is not a reason to hide a month of struggle. ASHA’s public page exists so you do not have to become a fluency specialist overnight.

If a teacher says “he stutters,” ask for two examples, the same way you ask for language examples. Examples, not a hallway noun. The noun can wait for the person who uses it as a term of art.
