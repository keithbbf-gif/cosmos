**Disclaimer.** Educational only. Not medical advice. Not a kindergarten entrance exam and not a diagnosis. Rhymes and stories are skills with ranges.

Kindergarten likes children who can tell what happened and hear that *bat* and *cat* belong to each other.

CDC’s 5-year language line: tells a story they heard or made up with **at least two events** (a cat stuck in a tree; a firefighter saved it); answers simple questions about a book after you read it; keeps a conversation going with **more than three** back-and-forth exchanges; uses or recognizes simple rhymes.

ASHA’s 4-to-5-year public note mentions recognizing and naming letters and writing a name — those sit on CDC’s *cognitive* line (some letters in their name; names some letters). Keep speech-language and early print in the same house without turning this essay into a reading program.

## Two events is a plot

One event was four. Two events is cause and time. *The cat was stuck. Then the firefighter came.* You can get this in the car about recess: we went outside, then I fell. You can get it about a picture book. You can get it about a lie. The item is structure, not virtue.

If they list nouns (*cat, tree, truck*) but never sequence, try “and then?” once. If “and then?” always dies, that is worth saying to someone who evaluates language. It is also worth saying to the teacher. It is not worth a homemade diagnosis.

Made-up stories count. CDC said so. A superhero plot with two beats is enough.

## Questions after the book

Not during, necessarily. After. “Who was afraid?” “What did she find?” This is comprehension you cannot see while they are touching the pop-up flap. If they only answer while looking, try once with the book closed. Then stop experimenting on your own child and just read tomorrow.

## Four turns

Three years asked for two exchanges. Five asks for more than three. That is a longer social rope — topic maintenance, in clinic language. At the table it looks like they do not wander off to the window after the first answer.

You can kill this item by quizzing. You can grow it by being interesting. “I saw a dog that looked like Grandma’s” is a better bid than “Tell me about your day” in a voice that means *perform*.

## Rhyme

*Bat-cat. Ball-tall.* CDC will take use or recognition. Hearing the match is the phonological-awareness seed teachers mean when they say “readiness.” Essay 40.

If rhyme is invisible, try it in song, where the beat does some of the work. If it is still invisible, mention it. Plenty of children need a little more time with sound play. Time is not a plan you wait out in silence if you are worried about later reading — you can ask the school or an SLP what they hear. You still do not get a learning-disability label from a blog.

## Cognitive cousins on the same sheet

Counts to 10. Names some numbers. Words about time: yesterday, tomorrow, morning, night. Pays attention 5–10 minutes in story time or crafts (**screen time does not count** — CDC’s parenthesis). Writes some letters of their name. Those are not “speech.” They share the visit. A child with rich stories and no letter knowledge is one description. The reverse is another.

## Sounds at five

McLeod and Crowe (2018): across languages, most consonants are in by five; English still allows a short late list. A five-year-old who is hard for strangers to understand is a stronger “ask” than a five-year-old who says *wabbit* and is otherwise clear. Essay 13 and 14.

Identify the Signs, for 4–5, also mentions early reading behaviors — pretending to read, finding the front of the book. That is book knowledge, not decoding. Missing it is a conversation, not a verdict.

## The school is already in the footer

Same as three and four: local public elementary school, free evaluation, if you and the doctor stay concerned. Kindergarten screening at your district is a local object. It is not CDC’s list and not this series.

## Ordinary help

CDC’s 5-year tips: ask why and how about their play; time words in the real week; rhyming games; rules in games (Simon Says is following directions plus fun); let them “read” the pictures and tell the story. Memory games. Family talk at meals without a phone.

If the teacher says they cannot retell, ask for one example. Write it down. Bring it to whoever looks next. Two events. That is the unit.

## Rhyme that is not a worksheet

In the car: “What rhymes with *cat*?” If they say *dog*, they heard “animal.” If they say *hat*, they heard the ending. If they say *cat-cat*, they heard the game and not the sound. All three answers are data. CDC will take recognition — you say *bat-cat*, they laugh or pick the pair — if production is shy.

Songs still do some of this work for free. If the house has no music, a library rhyme time is a person plus a beat. Apps that flash rhyming pictures are optional and not a treatment.

## Attention in the parenthesis

CDC says 5–10 minutes in story or crafts, and they say screen time does not count. That parenthesis is a gift. If the only long attention you see is a show, write that. If they can build for fifteen minutes and cannot sit for a book, write that. Attention is not this series’ disorder to name. It is context for language sample quality. An SLP will want to know whether the child *could* talk and did not, or could not stay.

Time words — yesterday, tomorrow — will be wrong before they are right. Wrong is close. Silent is a different note.
