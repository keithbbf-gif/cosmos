**Disclaimer.** Educational only. Not medical advice. Dietary supplements are not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease (DSHEA; 21 U.S.C. § 321(ff); 21 CFR 101.93). This is not legal advice and not a labeling opinion for a specific SKU.

0.5 micrograms per day is an OEHHA reproductive Maximum Allowable Dose Level for lead. It is not a % Daily Value. 21 CFR 101.36 has no lead row. The Supplement Facts box will not tell a buyer whether a serving of powder, a gummy, or a botanical extract pushes a California safe-harbor number. That math lives in a different statute: Proposition 65 (Cal. Health & Safety Code § 25249.5 et seq.) and Title 27 of the California Code of Regulations.

A bottle can carry both systems at once. They do not cancel. A clean 101.36 panel does not answer OEHHA. An OEHHA warning does not make the panel optional.

## Safe harbor is not a Supplement Facts line

OEHHA's lead page lists two safe-harbor numbers that operators mix up. The oral No Significant Risk Level for cancer is 15 µg/day. The reproductive MADL is 0.5 µg/day. Lead is listed for cancer as "lead and lead compounds" and for developmental, male, and female reproductive toxicity as "lead." [VERIFY] those values on oehha.ca.gov/proposition-65/chemicals/lead before you freeze a spec; this draft is written against the page as it stood for this pack.

The MADL is the tighter of the two. Most supplement desks treat 0.5 µg/day as the number that decides whether a California warning is the safe-harbor path. That habit is not a license to forget the NSRL, and it is not an FDA tolerance. FDA has not written a lead %DV for dietary supplements. Do not invent one.

Title 27 CCR is where warning *content* lives when you choose the safe-harbor warning instead of proving the exposure is below the MADL. 27 CCR § 25607.1 and § 25607.2 cover food exposures, "including dietary supplements." The long-form food warning starts with **WARNING:**, **CA WARNING:**, or **CALIFORNIA WARNING:** in all-capital bold, then names the chemical and the cancer and/or reproductive-harm sentence, then points at www.P65Warnings.ca.gov/food. On a product label, § 25607.1(b) wants that warning set off, in a box. [VERIFY] the current 27 CCR text and any short-form options OEHHA has in force on the print date. Short-form rules have been in motion.

The warning is not a Supplement Facts footnote. It does not get an asterisk that says "Daily Value not established." It does not go in the (b)(3) block. Parking it inside the box is how you teach a buyer that lead is a nutrient you meant to sell.

## Serving-day math

Safe harbor is a *day*, not a scoop.

Exposure = concentration × how much a reasonably anticipated user takes in a day. If the panel serving is one scoop and the suggested use is two scoops twice daily, the day is four scoops. Piece 03 already told you that "take 1–3" makes the serving three. Prop 65 does not care that the front of the bottle looks like a single pretty scoop.

Work the arithmetic on the lot you are shipping, not on a sales-deck typical. A COA that reports lead in parts per million of powder has to be converted to µg per labeled serving, then multiplied by servings per day. If the method's limit of quantitation sits above 0.5 µg/serving, you do not have a "below MADL" result. You have a method that cannot see the number you are claiming.

Do not use a 30-day average to talk yourself out of a one-day MADL unless counsel has walked the current OEHHA and case file with you. Averaging has been litigated. This pack will not pretend to settle it. Flag it: [VERIFY] with counsel whether any averaging theory is available for *this* SKU before you omit a warning on that basis.

Protein powders and botanicals are where the math usually breaks. Plant concentrates can carry soil metals. A serving that looks modest in grams can still be a large share of 0.5 µg. A "test once per year" skip-lot program (piece 36) is a weak input to a daily exposure claim.

## Warning vs tested-below

Those are different sentences.

A Prop 65 warning says: consuming this product can expose you to a listed chemical, and California wants the buyer told. It is a notice. It is not a Supplement Facts quantity. It is not an admission you have to reprint as a milligram row. Some firms warn because they did the math and cleared the MADL the wrong way. Some warn because they did not want to do the math. The bottle does not disclose which.

"Tested below the 0.5 µg/day MADL" is a quality-and-exposure sentence. It needs the four fields from piece 36: lot, method, lab, and the rows, plus the serving-day multiplier you actually used. Without those, it is another caption. "Meets Prop 65" on a storefront tile is the same caption in fewer words.

The two sentences can appear together only if they do not fight. A boxed warning next to "tested below safe-harbor" for the same chemical is a 403(a)-shaped mess even before California gets involved. Pick the sentence the file supports.

Never write "heavy-metal free" as product copy. CLAIMS_GUARDRAILS already bans it. A method with a detection limit is not zero. A single lot is not a lifetime. The honest replacement is tested; result; method; lab; lot.

## Press tests vs a spec

Consumer Reports published "High Levels of Lead in Protein Powder and Shakes" on 14 October 2025 and updated the piece on 8 January 2026. CR later published follow-up testing and an FAQ. That file is press. It is not a tolerance. It is not Title 27. It is not 21 CFR 101.36.

CR used 0.5 micrograms per day as *its* "level of concern" for lead in that project. The number matches OEHHA's reproductive MADL. The document does not. A magazine's method, sample list, and risk language are not a specification you can drop into a master manufacturing record. Do not put "CR-tested" or "below CR limits" on a carton. Do not treat a story about 23 products as your lot file. Do not paste CR's brand table into a sales deck as if it were a COA.

Press is useful as a reminder that buyers and plaintiffs read. It is not useful as a substitute for your own serving-day math. If a reporter's lot and your lot disagree, you still own your lot.

Industry replies to that story argued that food already carries lead, and that one serving of powder should be compared to a day's kale. Maybe. That comparison is not a safe-harbor showing. OEHHA's number is still 0.5 µg/day unless a listed exception applies, and "naturally occurring" under Prop 65 is a defense people cite more often than they prove. [VERIFY] with counsel before you rely on it.

## What the bottle can say at once

A single carton can legally hold, if each is true:

- a Supplement Facts box under 101.36
- an other-ingredient list under 101.4
- a FALCPA "Contains" line (piece 37)
- a 101.93 disclaimer if you made a structure/function statement (piece 22)
- a Prop 65 food warning in its own box
- a country-of-origin or qualified Made-in-USA line (piece 39)
- a program mark you actually hold (piece 36)

They are different statutes. Stacking them does not create a new grade called "compliant." Leaving one off because the panel looked crowded is how a 3PL intake fails.

What the bottle cannot say: "heavy-metal free," "Prop 65 approved," "below federal lead limits" when no such supplement %DV exists, or "as seen in Consumer Reports" as if the press test were a certification mark.

Print the milligrams the FDA rule asked for. Do the micrograms-per-day arithmetic the California rule asked for. If the arithmetic needs a warning, box the warning. If it does not, keep the lot file that shows why. Either way, leave the press clipping in the reading pile. It is not camera-ready art.
