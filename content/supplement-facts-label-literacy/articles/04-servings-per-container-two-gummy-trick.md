**Disclaimer.** Educational only. Not medical advice. Dietary supplements are not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease (DSHEA; 21 U.S.C. § 321(ff); 21 CFR 101.93). This is not legal advice and not a labeling opinion for a specific SKU.

A bottle that looks like a month is a month only if serving size is 1. Sixty units in a jar is a thirty-day jar when the serving is 2. That is not a gummy joke. That is 21 CFR 101.36(b) plus arithmetic. The servings line exists so a buyer does not have to do that arithmetic from a net-contents statement that says "60 gummies" and a serving line that says "2 gummies" on opposite sides of the package.

Chapter IV of FDA's Dietary Supplement Labeling Guide (April 2005), question 4-3, is the omit rule. You must list Servings Per Container unless that number is the same information already in the net quantity of contents. One hundred tablets, serving size one tablet: you may skip "Servings Per Container 100." Two gummies, sixty in the bottle: you may not skip "Servings Per Container 30." The skip is for identity of information, not for convenience.

This piece is the container math. Cohen PA et al., *Quantity of Melatonin and CBD in Melatonin Gummies Sold in the US*, *JAMA* 2023, is the dose-versus-label scar that made gummy math a public file. Piece 40 is kids, candy cues, and the children's %DV fences. Stay on the count here.

## When you may drop the line

101.36(b) requires names and quantities of dietary ingredients, Serving Size, and Servings Per Container. The last of those three drops out in one case: when it restates the net-contents line. The 2005 example is exact. Net quantity 100 tablets. Serving size 1 tablet. Servings per container would also be 100 tablets. You may omit it.

The test is identity, not "close enough." 90 capsules, serving size 1: omit is available. 90 capsules, serving size 3: servings per container is 30, and 30 is not 90. Print 30. 120 softgels, serving size 2: 60 servings. Print 60. A 30-packet carton, serving size 1 packet: omit is available because the carton already said 30. A 30-packet carton, serving size 2 packets (morning and night in one occasion — an odd design, but it happens): 15 servings. Print 15.

Small-pack abbreviations may shorten the heading to "Servings" (101.9(j)(13)(ii)(B)). That is still the line. It is not an omit.

If you drop the line when the math does not match net contents, you have made the buyer multiply under fluorescent light. A heavy bar still sits under the serving block (101.36(e)(6)(i)). If Servings Per Container is absent, the bar sits under Serving Size. The line is gone because it was redundant, not because the bottle has no count.

## When two is the serving

Serving size is the maximum recommended per eating occasion (101.12(b) Table 2; piece 03). A great many gummies, two-piece softgel pairs, and "take with breakfast" tablets set that maximum at 2. Then the jar count is not the day count.

Two is not a trick until someone treats the jar as a month. A 60-count with serving size 2 is 30 servings. A 60-count with serving size 2 and a front sticker that says "30-day supply" is at least consistent. A 60-count with serving size 2 and a front sticker that says "60-day supply," or a PDP that says "60 servings," or an Amazon title that says "two-month supply," is three different counts. The panel's Servings Per Container is the one 101.36 asked for. The sticker is advertising. If they disagree, you have two labels.

The two-gummy trick, in the aisle sense, is the mismatch: net contents looks like a month, serving is 2, the servings line is missing or the marketing count uses the net number. Nothing in 101.36 forbids a serving of 2. Plenty of formulas need two units to hit the declared milligrams without making one unit the size of a walnut. The rule forbids *hiding* that 2 when you talk about how long the bottle lasts.

If suggested use says 1–2 gummies, serving size is 2 (Chapter IV, 4-4). Servings per container uses 2, not 1. You do not get to pick 1 for the day-count sticker and 2 for the %DV. One object. One count.

## Net contents vs days of use

Net quantity of contents is a separate statement. It tells you how many units, or how much volume or weight, are in the package. It does not tell you how many eating occasions are in the package. Days of use is net contents divided by serving size, only after serving size is the legal object from piece 03.

A "30-day supply" claim is not a 101.36 row. It is a number you chose to put on the front. If the panel says Servings Per Container 30 and suggested use is one serving a day, the front can say 30 days without doing extra math. If suggested use is twice a day, 30 servings is 15 days. The front has to follow the directions, not the wish.

Travel tins and "bonus" pouches change net contents. If you add 10 gummies to a 60-count promo, servings per container becomes 35 at a serving of 2. Reprint the line. Powder is the same division with a scoop: net contents 360 g, serving 1 scoop (12 g) is 30 servings. Match the tool to the panel, then divide.

## Gummies inherit the same rule

A dietary supplement that happens to be a gummy is still a dietary supplement if that is how it is sold and labeled (piece 01). 101.36 does not have a candy chapter. Serving Size 2 gummies. Servings Per Container = net gummy count ÷ 2, when 2 is the serving. Other dietary ingredients below the hairline. Plant part if a botanical is in the formula. Other ingredients under the box — gelatin or pectin, colors, flavors, citric acid, the sugar system.

The sugar system is why people want to treat gummies as food. If the SKU is a conventional food, it uses Nutrition Facts and you do not get a (b)(3) melatonin row. If the SKU is a supplement, it uses Supplement Facts and you do not get to skip servings per container because the jar looks like a snack. Category first. Then the count.

Kids' flavors do not change the division. They change the intended-use file and the photo file (piece 40). A 60-count jar with serving size 2 is still 30 servings. Print the number of servings the labeled serving actually produces.

## What Cohen showed about declared vs found (no disease story)

Cohen PA, Avula B, Wang Y-H, Katragunta K, Khan I. *Quantity of Melatonin and CBD in Melatonin Gummies Sold in the US*. *JAMA*. 2023;329(16):1401-1402. The paper compared the quantity declared on the label with the quantity found by assay in melatonin gummies sold in the United States. The assays did not match the labels. One product had no detectable melatonin and did contain CBD. Among products that contained melatonin, found amounts ran from well below the declaration to several times the declaration. Most of the tested labels were inaccurate against a ±10% band the authors used. Recheck the paper for the exact table before you quote a row in a catalog meeting [VERIFY: use the *JAMA* 2023 table; do not reconstruct milligrams from memory].

That is a declared-versus-found scar. It is not a permission to write a sleep-disease story next to a cart button. It is not a children's dosing protocol. It is evidence that a gummy serving printed on a bottle is a claim about contents, and contents are a lab result, not a flavor.

101.36(f)(1) is how FDA will sample you: a composite of 12 subsamples or 10% of the packages in the inspection lot, whichever is smaller (Chapter IV, 4-41; piece 05). Cohen's letter is not that composite method. It is a research letter about what was in bottles the authors bought. Different job. Same pressure on the number you print next to "2 gummies."

If the serving is 2 and the row says a milligram amount, that milligram is per 2 gummies, and the 100% rule for added dietary ingredients still applies (101.9(g)(3)–(4); piece 05). The COA pack is where you read the assay. This pack is where you read the object the assay is supposed to match. Print Servings Per Container when it is not a restatement of net contents. Do not let a 60-count face become a 60-day story when two is the serving.
