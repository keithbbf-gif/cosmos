**Disclaimer.** Educational only. Not medical advice. Dietary supplements are not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease (DSHEA; 21 U.S.C. § 321(ff); 21 CFR 101.93). This is not legal advice and not a labeling opinion for a specific SKU.

101.93(f) is the permitted shape. 101.93(g) is the fence. The regulation restates what 21 U.S.C. § 343(r)(6) already allowed in 1994, then — after the 6 January 2000 final rule — tells you how FDA will recognize a disease claim. The 9 January 2002 Small Entity Compliance Guide is the plain-language map of those ten criteria. Piece 24 walks the ten. This piece walks the statutory sentence and the three duties you have before a printer file is honest.

## The statutory sentence

§ 343(r)(6)(A) allows a statement for a dietary supplement that does one of four jobs:

1. Claims a benefit related to a classical nutrient-deficiency disease and discloses the prevalence of that disease in the United States.
2. Describes the role of a nutrient or dietary ingredient intended to affect the structure or function in humans.
3. Characterizes the documented mechanism by which a nutrient or dietary ingredient acts to *maintain* such structure or function.
4. Describes general well-being from consumption of a nutrient or dietary ingredient.

21 CFR 101.93(f) compresses the middle two into the sentence operators actually set in type: labels may bear statements that describe the role of a nutrient or dietary ingredient intended to affect the structure or function in humans, or that characterize the documented mechanism by which it acts to maintain that structure or function, **provided** the statements are not disease claims under 101.93(g).

"Maintain" is doing work. A mechanism that "maintains" a structure is not a mechanism that "repairs a named disease." If the rest of the page does the repair job, (f) will not save the verb.

The statement still has to be about a nutrient or dietary ingredient. A blend name is not a free-floating wellness slogan. If you cannot point at the ingredient that is supposed to have the role, you do not have a 343(r)(6) statement. You have a banner.

§ 343(r)(6) then attaches two more statutory conditions — substantiation, and the bold disclaimer — and a notice to the Secretary within 30 days after first marketing. 101.93(a) through (e) implement those. Fail any one and you are not in the lane.

## Deficiency-disease exception (educational, not a scurvy protocol)

101.93(g)(1) defines "disease" for this section as damage to an organ, part, structure, or system so that it does not function properly (the regulation's examples are cardiovascular disease and hypertension), or a state of health leading to such dysfunctioning. Then it carves out diseases that result from essential nutrient deficiencies. The regulation's own examples are scurvy and pellagra.

That carve-out is how a classical deficiency sentence can exist without automatically being a 101.93(g) disease claim. The statute still requires you to disclose the prevalence of that deficiency disease in the United States. "Vitamin C and scurvy" without a prevalence clause is not the statutory sentence. This pack will not supply a prevalence number. Look up a current, citable figure before you ever print one [VERIFY].

The carve-out is educational here, not a protocol. This piece does not tell you to dose vitamin C for scurvy, niacin for pellagra, or any nutrient for any deficiency disease. A deficiency that is uncommon in the United States, described as if the bottle were the treatment, is how a "classical" sentence becomes ordinary drug talk. Authorized health claims, where they exist, are a different drawer (piece 26).

FDA will still read context. A scurvy sentence on a children's gummy next to carnival photos is not automatically saved by 101.93(g)(1)'s parenthetical. The SECG is about the whole label. Piece 40 is the gummy room.

## Well-being claims still need evidence

"General well-being from consumption of a nutrient or dietary ingredient" is the fourth statutory job. It is not a blank. § 343(r)(6)(B) still requires substantiation that the statement is truthful and not misleading. 101.93(a)(3) still requires a person to certify that the firm has that substantiation. FTC's 2022 guide still reads the sentence as a reasonable buyer would take it (piece 27).

"Feel better" attached to a 12-herb blend is a well-being claim that has to be true for that blend, at that serving, in that form. "Promotes well-being" next to a disease photo is not saved by the word well-being. The SECG's examples of general structure/function shape — including "supports the immune system" without more — still fail if the rest of the page does the disease work (piece 30).

Well-being is also not a nutrient-content claim. "Excellent source of well-being" is nonsense under 101.54. "Excellent source of vitamin C" is a different rule, and it attaches to a DV nutrient, not to a mood (piece 25).

If you do not have evidence, do not use the fourth job as the dump drawer for copy the first three jobs will not bear.

## Three duties

The statute and 101.93 give you three duties. They are ordered.

**Substantiation first.** § 343(r)(6)(B) and 101.93(a)(3): the notifying firm has substantiation that the statement is truthful and not misleading. FDA's 2008-era substantiation guidance (still posted; recheck the dated page [VERIFY]) says the agency intends to apply a standard consistent with FTC's "competent and reliable scientific evidence," and that you should have substantiation for each reasonable interpretation of the claim. You are supposed to hold that file *before* first marketing, not after a retailer asks. Piece 23: the certification is a person swearing the file exists. Piece 28: an ingredient RCT is not automatically this SKU.

**Thirty-day notice.** § 343(r)(6), last sentence; 101.93(a): no later than 30 days after first marketing of a dietary supplement that bears one of these statements, the manufacturer, packer, or distributor notifies the Office of Dietary Supplement Programs (HFS-810) with the contents 101.93(a)(2) lists. Notification is not approval. Silence is not a blessing. Piece 23 is the file.

**Disclaimer.** § 343(r)(6)(C); 101.93(c)–(e): the two official sentences, or the plural form, in boldface, no smaller than one-sixteenth inch, adjacent or asterisk-linked, on each panel or page that carries the statement, boxed if not adjacent. Piece 22 is the type spec. The disclaimer does not wash a 101.93(g) claim.

A fourth practical duty is consistency. The bottle, the site, and the marketplace bullet must say the same permitted sentence (piece 42). A notified statement on the bottle and a disease blog on the cart is intended use assembled from four corners (piece 20).

## This is not a health claim

A health claim characterizes the relationship between a substance and a **disease or health-related condition**. 21 CFR 101.14 is that drawer. Authorized claims have model language and qualifying levels. Qualified health claims have FDA's own letter language (piece 26; *Pearson v. Shalala*, 164 F.3d 650). You do not get there by printing a structure/function verb next to a disease noun.

101.93(f) ends on that split: if the labeling bears a disease claim as defined in (g), the product is a drug unless the claim is an authorized health claim for which the product qualifies. There is no third status called "structure/function about a disease."

Nutrient-content claims ("high in," "good source," "excellent source") are a third drawer again (101.13, 101.54; piece 25). They attach to nutrients with Daily Values. They do not attach to ashwagandha, and they do not become structure/function by adding "supports."

The permitted shape is smaller than the aisle. Role of this ingredient on this structure or function. Or a documented mechanism that maintains it. Or a careful well-being sentence you can prove. Or a deficiency sentence with a prevalence clause you can prove, used as education on a label, not as a treatment protocol. Then the three duties. Then you stop typing. 101.93(g) is the fence because operators would not stop typing. Piece 24 is how FDA listed the ways they tried.
