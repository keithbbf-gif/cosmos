**Disclaimer.** Educational only. Not medical advice. Dietary supplements are not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease (DSHEA; 21 U.S.C. § 321(ff); 21 CFR 101.93). This is not legal advice and not a labeling opinion for a specific SKU.

The study on the PDF is usually a different dose, a different form, or a different product. A sales folder labeled "clinical" is often a trial of an isolated ingredient, in a capsule, at a milligram the formula card does not match, in a population the cart page does not name. Printing "clinically studied" on *this* SKU is then a claim about a bottle that was not in the trial.

FTC's December 2022 *Health Products Compliance Guidance* is the current long form for that mismatch (piece 27). 21 CFR 101.93 still requires substantiation *before* first marketing of a structure/function statement. Neither file lets an adjective launder a different object.

## Ingredient vs SKU

A finished dietary supplement is a serving: the combination, the amounts, the forms, the other dietary ingredients, the excipients that change dissolution, the serving size that 101.12 treats as the maximum per eating occasion. A paper about one constituent is a paper about one constituent.

Example 18 in the 2022 guide is the sports-drink sentence "clinically tested ingredient" for blood flow and endurance. FTC says that phrase, in context, implies not only that the ingredient was tested but that the *test results prove* those benefits — and that the drink will deliver them. Because the drink also contains other ingredients, the marketer is told to ask whether experts in that field would require a trial of the drink itself.

Example 37 is the combination problem from the other side: two stimulant ingredients, each studied alone for alertness and safety. Studies of the parts may not substantiate a safety claim about the pair. If experts would require a study of the actual product, the folder of single-ingredient PDFs is not enough.

Example 38 is the extract problem. Several human trials on a named botanical extract show an effect on accepted markers of immune-system activity. The studied extract is a complex mixture; the active constituents are unknown. The advertiser's product uses a different extraction method from the same plant. The chemical profile differs. FTC says do not rely on those trials alone.

None of those examples is permission to write a disease story. "Supports the immune system" is the SECG's general structure/function *shape*, still needing evidence and a disclaimer, and still a disease claim if the page does infection or antiviral work (piece 24, criterion 8). A trial on markers is not a license to print "resist infection." That last phrase is a forbidden SECG example.

The honest split is three objects: (1) the paper, (2) the ingredient as studied, (3) the SKU as sold. "Clinically studied" on (3) that only has a paper about (2) is a category error. It is also, as FTC reads it, an efficacy claim about (3).

## Dose matching

The 2022 guide's matching questions start with dosage and formulation. How does the advertised serving compare to the studied serving? Is the ingredient the same chemical entity, the same salt, the same extract ratio, the same particle size if the paper made particle size the point?

A trial at 600 mg of a named extract does not carry a proprietary blend that lists the plant somewhere in a 1,200 mg mix (piece 16). You do not know the milligrams of that plant. You cannot claim the trial. A trial of 25 mcg vitamin D does not carry a 125 mcg row, and a 125 mcg row does not carry a 25 mcg trial. Piece 10 is the IU/mcg arithmetic. This piece is the claim arithmetic: the adjective follows the milligram that was studied, or the adjective comes off.

Form is dose's twin. A time-release capsule is not a gummy. A liposomal liquid is not a compressed tablet. Example 36 in the 2022 guide — a probiotic drink leaning on capsule trials of a *different strain* in a *different* population — is FTC's own illustration that form and strain are not interchangeable. Use the example as a method. Do not borrow its disease name as product copy. Crohn's disease in that example is a forbidden disease claim if you put it on a supplement (piece 24, criterion 1).

If the study used the ingredient with a restricted diet and exercise protocol, the ad that omits those conditions is a different claim. Example 8 in the 2022 guide is a weight-loss supplement whose trial paired the product with diet and exercise. FTC says the ad must make that pairing clear. Piece 32 is the weight drawer. The matching point here is simpler: the serving on the panel has to be the serving in the paper, or you say what the paper actually dosed and you do not hang "clinically" on the mismatch.

## Population matching

Who was enrolled is part of the claim. A trial in older adults is not, without more, a trial in children. A trial in people who already have a diagnosed disease is not a structure/function file for a general aisle product — and using that trial as a reason to sell a supplement for that disease is how you walk into 101.93(g) and 21 U.S.C. § 321(g)(1)(B). Disease names in a methods section are not product indications.

The 2022 guide asks: does the study population reflect the people the ad targets? Example 32 (joint flexibility, adults over 65) is written as a case where the population, the outcome, and independent trials lined up — and even there FTC walks through expert review, similar formulation, and similar dose. If your cart page is a general-adult multi and your only paper is a disease-population intervention, you do not have a match. You have a paper you should not turn into a banner.

Sex, diet, baseline nutrient status, and country of the trial can matter when experts in the field say they matter. Example 36 flags a Japanese cohort and a different diet as one of the gaps. That is not a rule that foreign trials never count. It is a rule that you ask the matching question and you keep a record of the answer.

Do not invent enrollment numbers, p-values, or "98 percent responders" to make a thin match look thicker. If the paper is real, cite the paper. If you have not read the paper — Example 23 is the abstract-only folder — you do not have substantiation. FTC says an advertiser who has only abstracts cannot know whether the design, the formulation, or the between-group result will carry the claim.

## "Clinically proven" vs "studied"

"Clinically proven" is an efficacy claim. FTC will read it as: competent and reliable scientific evidence shows the advertised benefit on the advertised product. The 2022 guide's memory-software Example 35 uses "clinically proven to improve memory" and then cuts the claim down because the trial measured only working-memory tasks. The adjective did not save a broader noun.

"Clinically studied" is softer in a copywriter's ear and not softer in the guide. Example 18 treats "clinically tested ingredient" as implying proven benefit plus finished-product performance. If you cannot substantiate those implications, you do not get the adjective.

"Clinically proven to [disease]" is worse. It is a disease claim under 101.93(g) and an unapproved-drug claim under 321(g) if the product is sold as a supplement. The 2022 guide's Example 47 (diabetes plus a DSHEA disclaimer) is the FTC version of piece 22: the box does not wash the verb.

Traditional-use stories are not a third adjective. The 2022 guide allows a carefully qualified historic-use sentence that does not imply efficacy and that matches the traditional form, dose, and indication. A concentrated extract sold as the old tea is Example 45: the qualification may still fail because the object changed. Traditional-use language about treating or curing a serious disease is off the table even with a "no science" clause.

## How to say a true, smaller sentence

The smaller sentence names objects instead of costumes.

**Amount, form, serving.** "Each serving (2 capsules) provides 200 mg of [ingredient] as [form]." That is 101.36 work. It is not an efficacy claim.

**A named paper about a named ingredient, with the mismatch visible.** "A randomized trial of [ingredient] at [dose] in [population] is cited on the science page. This SKU was not the study product; this serving is [dose]." If that paragraph makes a reasonable buyer hear that *this bottle* was proven, it is still too big. Then you drop the trial from the PDP and leave it on a science page that does not sit next to the cart — or you drop it entirely.

**What you do not say.** Do not say the SKU is clinically proven. Do not say "clinically studied formula" when the formula was not the intervention. Do not put a journal PDF behind a button labeled "the science" if the PDF is a different milligram. Do not let a paid creator say the adjective you took off the bottle (piece 29).

101.93 still wants substantiation for whatever structure/function sentence remains. The smaller sentence does not retire that duty. It keeps the duty the same size as the words. If the only true sentence is the milligram line, the milligram line is the claim. That is a better PDP than a borrowed trial and a letter that quotes you back to yourself.
