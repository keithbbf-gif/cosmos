**Disclaimer.** Educational only. Not medical advice. Dietary supplements are not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease (DSHEA; 21 U.S.C. § 321(ff); 21 CFR 101.93). This is not legal advice and not a labeling opinion for a specific SKU.

"Detox" is doing organ work until you prove it is a flavor. On a tea tin the word can mean lemon and a dark color. On a supplement PDP it usually means liver, kidney, "toxins," or a cleanse that stands in for a disease or a drug. 21 CFR 101.93(g) reads implied disease from context. FTC's December 2022 *Health Products Compliance Guidance* reads the same panel as an advertising claim that needs competent and reliable scientific evidence — for weight loss, generally a human trial of the product.

This piece teaches those two reads. It quotes FDA's forbidden examples as forbidden. This piece will not tell a reader to take a supplement to lose weight, to treat obesity, to cleanse an organ, or to replace a drug.

## Weight loss is not a vibe

A claim that a SKU causes weight loss is a health-related efficacy claim. The 2022 guide's general rule is randomized, controlled human testing that matches the product and the claim (piece 27). Ingredient PDFs, mechanism posters, and before-and-after photographs are not that file (pieces 28 and 29).

Example 1 in the guide is a weight-loss brochure: white coats, microscopes, journals. FTC says those pictures likely convey that the product has been clinically proven for weight loss. The costume is the claim.

Example 8 is a placebo-controlled trial the ad cites for a stated average loss over eight weeks. Both groups also dieted and exercised. FTC says the ad must make that pairing clear. The guide's numbers are FTC's example, not a target you should print. If you have a trial on *this* SKU, you state what that trial found, with its conditions. If you do not, you do not have the banner.

Example 41 (in the testimonials chapter) is a before-and-after plus "results not typical." The buried line does not save a dramatic body. Typicality needs the generally expected result, prominently, and substantiation for that result. This pack will not invent an average pound figure. It will not tell you to put a body slider on the carton.

Obesity and overweight-as-disease copy is 101.93(g) criterion 1: an effect on a specific disease or class of diseases. "Protective against the development of cancer" and "reduces the pain and stiffness associated with arthritis" are FDA's forbidden illustrations of that criterion. A banner that says a capsule treats obesity is the same class of sentence. Do not write it. Do not let a review widget write it (piece 29).

"Inhibits platelet aggregation" and "reduces cholesterol" (without the already-normal fence) are SECG's always-implied cardiovascular pair (criterion 2). Weight SKUs that drift into "melts arterial fat" or "lowers cholesterol" without "already in the normal range" have left the weight aisle and entered a disease claim. FDA's rescue wording for cholesterol is "maintain cholesterol levels that are already in the normal range." That fence is not a wink. This piece is not a cholesterol protocol.

## Metabolism as structure/function

Metabolism can stay in 101.93(f) if it stays on normal structure or function: a nutrient's role in normal energy metabolism, a documented mechanism that *maintains* that function, substantiated, notified, disclaimed. The sentence dies when "metabolism" is a synonym for weight loss, a thyroid disease, or diabetes.

Diabetes treatment is the 2022 guide's Example 47: an herbal supplement advertised to treat diabetes, plus a prominent DSHEA disclaimer, is still deceptive if the science is not there — and is a drug claim on the FDA side regardless of the box (piece 22). Do not write "supports healthy blood sugar" as a stealth diabetes ad. If the rest of the page names diabetes, insulin, or "replaces your metformin," you have criteria 1, 6, and 7. Those are **forbidden intended-use shapes**. This pack will not tell a reader to take a supplement for diabetes or for any other disease.

Thyroid, "adrenal," and "starvation mode" theater is the same test. If the words point at a damaged organ or a named endocrine disease, (g)(1) and (g)(2)(i) have you. If they point at normal nutrient metabolism in healthy people, and you have evidence, you may have a small structure/function sentence. The small sentence cannot carry a before/after waist.

"Clinically proven to boost metabolism" is piece 28: whose trial, which dose, which SKU. A different form or a different milligram is not this bottle.

## Cleanse and detox as implied disease

"Detox" and "cleanse" are not in 101.54. They are not nutrient-content claims. They are implied-disease or drug-adjacent words until the rest of the label proves they are tea flavor.

Liver, kidney, "toxins," "parasite cleanse," "gallbladder flush," "chelation" — those phrases point at organs that are not functioning properly, or at treatments for poisoning and infection. 101.93(g)(1) defines disease as damage to an organ, part, structure, or system so that it does not function properly, or a state leading to that dysfunction. A SKU sold to "detox the liver" is claiming an effect on that organ's disease or on a toxic exposure. Criterion 1 and criterion 2 will read the signs you list (yellow skin, dark urine, "after antibiotics") as the disease story.

**Forbidden examples, taught as *no*:** "detox your liver after chemotherapy"; "cleanse parasites"; "pulls heavy metals like chelation"; "cures candida overgrowth"; "resets failed kidneys." Chemotherapy is a named disease therapy (criterion 7). Chelation as a disease treatment is a drug class. Pathogenic overgrowth is the SECG's criterion-9 teaching problem: "to maintain the intestinal flora in people on antibiotics" is a **forbidden** example because it implies prevention of pathogenic bacterial overgrowth. Quote it as forbidden. Do not put it on a probiotic PDP. Do not tell a reader to take a supplement to prevent that overgrowth or to treat an infection (piece 30).

A general sentence about diet and regularity, if it stays off disease and off a drug class, is a different, smaller claim — and still needs evidence. "Detox" is not that sentence. If you cannot define the word without an organ or a toxin, do not print the word.

## Laxative-class names (SECG criterion 5)

Criterion 5: claiming membership in a class of products intended to diagnose, mitigate, treat, cure, or prevent disease is a disease claim. SECG's forbidden classes include analgesic, antibiotic, antidepressant, antimicrobial, antiseptic, antiviral, and vaccine.

Laxative, anti-inflammatory, and diuretic sit on both sides. The SECG says those class names are *not* disease claims if context makes the intended effect a structure/function effect, not a disease effect. The agency's own example: **"diuretic that relieves temporary water-weight gain."** Temporary and water-weight are the fence. A "diuretic cleanse" for edema from heart failure is a disease claim. A "laxative detox" for an unnamed toxin load is usually implied disease plus a class name without the SECG's fence.

If you need the class word, supply the non-disease context in the same breath, and be prepared to substantiate that smaller claim. If you do not need the class word, do not use it. "Supports occasional regularity" is closer to the sleep room's "occasional" (piece 31) than "stimulant laxative for a three-day cleanse." Chronic constipation as a disease story is criterion 2. This piece will not write a constipation protocol.

Stimulant laxative identity also has OTC drug monographs when the article is sold as a drug. A dietary-supplement PDP that talks like an OTC laxative is occupying two chairs. FDA will pick one.

## What you can say about diet and a nutrient

SECG criterion 4 allows general statements about diet and disease prevention that do **not** make the product the actor. Acceptable example: "a good diet promotes good health and prevents the onset of disease" or "better dietary and exercise patterns can contribute to disease prevention and better health." **Forbidden** example: "Promotes good health and prevents the onset of disease" as a product claim.

That split is how you talk about calories, fiber, and protein without writing a weight-loss drug ad. A 101.54 nutrient-content claim — "good source of protein," "excellent source of fiber" — is a percentage of Daily Value (piece 25). It is not "melts fat." A 101.14 authorized health claim, used in FDA's words at FDA's levels, is a different drawer (piece 26). Do not write a homemade obesity health claim.

If the math and the evidence are there, a careful PDP can still state the serving, the protein or fiber grams, a defined NCC, and a structure/function sentence about normal energy metabolism that does not promise weight change. If you actually have a product-level weight trial, you may state what that trial showed, including diet and exercise if the trial used them. If the only true sentence is "contains 20 g protein per serving" and a diet-and-exercise line that does not make the powder the physician, print that. The word "detox" is optional. The letter that quotes it is not.
