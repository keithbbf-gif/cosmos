**Disclaimer.** Educational only. Not medical advice. Dietary supplements are not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease (DSHEA; 21 U.S.C. § 321(ff); 21 CFR 101.93). This is not legal advice and not a labeling opinion for a specific SKU.

"Pharmaceutical grade" is not a checkbox on 21 CFR 101.36. The panel asks for a name, a serving, a weight, and — where a Daily Value exists — a percent. It does not ask whether the powder once sat in a building that also makes tablets for a New Drug Application. The adjective is aisle copy. The milligram is the legal object.

A buyer who has been trained by drug packaging hears "pharmaceutical" as a grade of purity and a grade of oversight. A founder who has been trained by a sales deck hears the same word as a reason to print larger. Neither reading is a defined USP mark for a dietary supplement. If you need a sentence that survives a retailer desk, print the number the rule already wants.

## What USP actually verifies

The United States Pharmacopeia writes monographs and runs a separate, voluntary Dietary Supplement Verification Program. Those are not the same stamp.

A monograph is a specification: identity tests, strength ranges, impurity limits, and performance tests for a named article when that article claims to meet USP. Putting "USP" after an ingredient on a Supplement Facts line is a conformity claim for that ingredient. It is still not FDA approval of the SKU, and it is still not a license to write "pharmaceutical grade" on the principal display panel.

The USP Verified Mark is narrower and more public. USP's own program pages say a verified supplement contains the ingredients listed on the label in the declared potency and amounts; does not contain harmful levels of specified contaminants; will break down and release its contents within a specified time; and is made according to FDA current good manufacturing practice using sanitary, well-controlled procedures. The mark is awarded to products that meet the program's testing and evaluation criteria. USP also describes facility audits against 21 CFR Part 111 and USP General Chapter 〈2750〉, plus continuing off-the-shelf surveillance. [VERIFY] the current USP Verification Services FAQ and the participant manual before you quote those bullets on a carton; program language moves.

What the mark does not do is as useful as what it does. It does not mean the product is a drug. It does not mean FDA approved the formula. It does not mean the SKU was in a clinical trial. It does not mean "pharmaceutical grade." USP's FAQ is blunt that determining the safety of dietary supplements in the broad sense — drug interactions, contraindications, side effects — is outside what the program verifies. The program checks labeled ingredients, labeled amounts, specified contaminants, and manufacturing controls.

There is a directory. If the SKU is not in it, a gold seal that looks like USP's artwork is a costume, and a trademark problem besides. PHOTO_NOTES in this pack already says the marks are trademarks: use them only if the SKU is actually in the program, and follow the artwork rules. A contractor who emails a JPEG of a badge is not enrollment.

Part 111 remains a duty for everyone who manufactures, packages, labels, or holds a dietary supplement. "Quality" in 21 CFR 111.3 is a defined word: the product consistently meets established specifications for identity, purity, strength, and composition, and limits on contaminants. That definition does not mint a consumer-facing grade called pharmaceutical. Piece 34 is the sister file for "FDA approved," "FDA registered," and "cGMP certified by FDA." This file is the pair of adjectives that try to sound like a pharmacopeia without joining one.

## Clinical as a costume

"Clinical strength" does different work. It does not pretend to be a purity monograph. It pretends to be a dose that a clinic, a trial, or a hospital would use.

FTC's December 2022 *Health Products Compliance Guidance* reads the claim as a reasonable consumer would take it. Competent and reliable scientific evidence has to match that reading. A consumer who sees "clinical strength" on a gummy bottle is not required to understand that you meant "we put more milligrams than the SKU next to us." Many will hear: this is the studied dose, or this is medicine-adjacent. If you do not have a human study on *this* finished product at *this* serving, you are borrowing a trial you did not run. Piece 28 is the longer version of that theft.

FDA's structure/function fence is older and still in force. 21 U.S.C. § 343(r)(6) and 21 CFR 101.93 let you talk about the role of a nutrient on a structure or function of the body if you substantiate first, notify within 30 days, and carry the disclaimer. They do not let you dress a supplement as a drug by tone. 21 U.S.C. § 321(g)(1)(B) reads intended use from the whole file: the panel, the PDP, the site, the Reel. "Clinical" is a word hospitals and drug labels already own. On a dietary-supplement PDP it is a costume until the evidence file is real and the claim stays inside 101.93(f).

"Clinically proven" is worse. That is an efficacy claim. FTC will treat it as one. "Clinically studied ingredient" is the usual dodge — a different dose, a different form, or a different product on the PDF. Do not convert a citation into a grade for the bottle in the cart.

The Small Entity Compliance Guide (9 January 2002) already warned that class names and product names can do disease work. You do not need to invent a new example. You need to notice that "clinical" sits next to those class names in the same drawer: it sounds like a professional indication. If the rest of the page also winks at a condition FDA used as a forbidden-claim example, the adjective is not decoration. It is intended use.

## Strength vs serving

101.36 cares about amount per serving. Serving size, for a dietary supplement, is the maximum amount recommended per eating occasion (21 CFR 101.12(b) Table 2; piece 03). "Take 1–3 tablets" makes the serving 3 tablets. A banner that says "clinical strength — just one caplet" while the suggested use still runs to three is a fight between the PDP and the box.

"Extra strength," "maximum strength," and "professional strength" have the same problem as "clinical strength" when they lack a defined comparison. Nutrient-content claims attach to nutrients that have Daily Values (21 CFR 101.13, 101.54; piece 25). There is no "excellent source of clinical." A (b)(3) botanical does not get a strength grade by adjective. The row is a weight and an asterisk that Daily Value is not established.

Composite compliance is still 12 packages or 10 percent of the lot, whichever is smaller (101.36(f)(1)). Added dietary ingredients are supposed to meet 100 percent of what you declared (101.9(g)(3)–(4), applied through 101.36(f)). "Clinical strength" printed on the front does not change the assay. If the lot is short, the adjective is not a defense. If the lot is over, the adjective is not a blessing. The number to the right of the name is the number.

Watch the dual-column trick. A second column that restates "per day" after a three-capsule serving can look like a study arm. Piece 08 is that pane. Do not use a marketing column to imply a protocol you do not have.

## How to replace the adjective with a number

Write the sentence the panel already knows how to host.

"Vitamin D3 (as cholecalciferol) 125 mcg." If you want the old aisle talk in parentheses, the optional IU is a parenthetical, not a substitute for mcg after 81 FR 33742 (piece 10). "Magnesium (as magnesium glycinate) 200 mg" is elemental magnesium, not the weight of the salt (piece 14). "Ashwagandha root extract 600 mg" is a (b)(3) row with a plant part. None of those sentences need "pharmaceutical" or "clinical" to be true.

If the SKU is actually USP Verified, say that with the mark USP permits, and keep a screenshot of the directory listing in the print packet. [VERIFY] the listing on the date you send files to the printer. A mark that expired, or that applies to a different SKU in the same family, is a different product.

If you have a lot-level test, say the four fields piece 36 wants: lot, method, lab, which rows. "Lot 24A031; lead by ICP-MS; [lab name]; result X µg/serving against spec Y." That is longer than "pharmaceutical grade." It is also a fact. Do not replace it with "heavy-metal free" (piece 38). Do not invent a lab, a chief scientist, or a plant you do not operate.

Retailer questionnaires that ask "Is this pharmaceutical grade? Y/N" are asking you to join their costume. The survivable answer is a short appendix: the 101.36 serving, the specification, the program mark if you have one, the COA fields if you have those. A checkbox that only the adjective can satisfy is a checkbox you should refuse.
