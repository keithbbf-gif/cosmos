**Disclaimer.** Educational only. Not medical advice. Dietary supplements are not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease (DSHEA; 21 U.S.C. § 321(ff); 21 CFR 101.93). This is not legal advice and not a labeling opinion for a specific SKU.

December 2022 is the current long form. On 20 December 2022 the Federal Trade Commission published *Health Products Compliance Guidance* and retired *Dietary Supplements: An Advertising Guide for Industry* (1998). The new document is not a supplement-only pamphlet. It covers health-related claims for any product. A principal display panel that already survived a 21 CFR 101.93 notification can still fail this file.

FDA and FTC have split the same bottle since the 1971 memorandum of understanding: FDA on labeling and intended use, FTC on advertising. A product page you can add to a cart is both. The 2022 guide says so. This piece is the FTC half of the panel you are about to print.

## What the 2022 guide replaced

The 1998 guide taught competent and reliable scientific evidence for dietary-supplement ads. The 2022 guide keeps that core and writes it onto a larger set: foods, devices, apps, shakes, the adjacent SKU that is not a 321(ff) article. FTC staff said the basics are largely unchanged and that the evidence still has to match the product and the claim. What grew is the substantiation chapter: randomized, controlled human clinical testing as the general expectation for health-benefit claims; more on study quality, drawn in part from the *POM Wonderful* line; a caution against p-hacking; and a thicker set of examples.

The guide is staff guidance. It does not have the force of a rule. FTC will still bring cases under sections 5 and 12 of the FTC Act. Treating the PDF as optional because it is "only guidance" is how operators discover that the examples were written from cases.

DSHEA did not amend the FTC Act. The 2022 guide repeats that FDA's structure/function category has no legal significance for FTC substantiation. A sentence that is a lawful 343(r)(6) statement, notified to HFS-810, still has to be true as a reasonable consumer takes it. Piece 23 is the notification. This piece is why the postcard is not a shield.

## Net impression

FTC law reads the ad the way a reasonable consumer reads it, not the way the formulator meant it. The 2022 guide's phrase is **net impression**: text, product name, charts, graphs, photographs, and the rest of the page, taken together. If the ad will bear more than one reasonable reading, you substantiate each reading.

Example 1 in the guide is a weight-loss brochure: doctors in white lab coats, microscopes, molecular structures, a stack of medical journals. Those pictures, FTC says, likely convey that the product has been clinically proven to be effective for weight loss. No sentence has to say "clinically proven." The costumes say it. Piece 33 is the white-coat file. Piece 32 is the weight file.

A disclosure that a significant minority of consumers still miss is not a disclosure. The 2022 guide's test: if a significant minority still takes a misleading claim, the qualifier failed. Fine-print "results not typical," a 101.93 disclaimer parked under a disease headline, or an asterisk that leads to a paragraph the cart page collapses — those are the usual misses.

When a disclosure is required, it has to be clear and conspicuous: hard to miss, easy for an ordinary buyer to understand, in the same sensory channel as the claim (visual with visual, audio with audio, both when the claim is both). If you cannot write an effective disclosure, you change the claim or you drop it. You do not shrink the footnote.

Net impression is also how a structure/function line dies. "Supports the immune system" on the panel, plus a virus graphic, plus a blog titled as a protocol, is not the SECG's general sentence (piece 24, criterion 8; piece 30). FTC will not give you a separate grade because the five words on the bottle were careful.

## Human studies vs mechanism posters

Competent and reliable scientific evidence, as the 2022 guide restates it, means tests, analyses, research, or studies that (1) have been conducted and evaluated objectively by experts in the relevant disease, condition, or function, and (2) are generally accepted in that profession to yield accurate and reliable results. The research must be sufficient in quality and quantity, against the entire body of relevant evidence, to show the representation is true.

As a general matter, FTC expects randomized, controlled human clinical testing for health-related benefits. Quality beats a stack of thin studies. Results must be statistically significant *between* groups and clinically meaningful to a person, not only a p-value on a lab marker. The guide warns against mining a subgroup after the main analysis failed.

Animal studies and in vitro work can be background. Without human confirmation, they do not substantiate a health-benefit claim for people. A pathway poster or a petri-dish photograph is a mechanism story. Mechanism is not the claim.

Epidemiology can show association. It does not, by itself, prove causation. FTC will accept high-quality epidemiologic evidence where experts in that field treat it as an acceptable substitute and human trials are not otherwise feasible. Most aisle claims are not that case.

The evidence has to be *this* product and *this* claim. Dose, form, combination, route, and population have to match, or the ad has to shrink. Piece 28 is that matching problem. Example 18 is the sports-drink line "clinically tested ingredient": in context, it implies the ingredient was tested *and* that the drink delivers the named benefit. Example 38 is a botanical extract with a different chemical profile from the studied extract.

A claim that names its own level of support — "Nobel Prize-winning," "NIH-proven" — must have at least that support, about the advertised benefit (Example 17). Borrowed prestige from an unrelated use of the molecule is deceptive even if some other study exists in a drawer.

## Endorsements pointer to 29

Section III.C.1 of the 2022 guide is testimonials and expert endorsements. You may not say through a reviewer what you may not say yourself. A testimonial is not competent and reliable scientific evidence. Typicality cannot be cured with "results not typical." Before-and-after bodies are claims. Paid bloggers are ads. Piece 29 is 16 CFR Part 255 (2023) on the same facts.

Example 39: customers who say an app ended their insomnia are making an insomnia-treatment claim the marketer must substantiate. Insomnia is a disease name FDA already used in the 2000 sleep examples (piece 31). Displaying the review is paying to repeat it. Material connections — payment, free product, a title at the company — have to be clear and conspicuous.

## A PDP that passed a 30-day notice and still fails FTC

101.93(a) is a notice that you are using a 343(r)(6) statement. The signer certifies that substantiation exists (piece 23). FDA silence is not approval. FTC does not treat the postcard as a file of human trials.

Three usual failure modes, after the notice has gone to College Park:

1. **The sentence grew.** The notification said "supports normal muscle function." The principal display panel says "clinically proven to rebuild muscle after injury," or the Amazon bullet names a disease. Intended use is assembled from four corners (piece 20). FTC reads the same corners as advertising claims.

2. **The evidence is the wrong object.** An ingredient RCT at a different dose, in a different form, in a different population, sits in the substantiation folder. The 2022 guide's matching questions — dosage, formulation, combination, outcomes, population — are how that folder fails. Notification does not inspect the folder.

3. **The disclaimer is asked to wash a disease.** Example 47: an herbal supplement advertised to treat diabetes, plus a prominent DSHEA disclaimer, is still a deceptive diabetes-treatment ad if the science is not there. 101.93(c) cannot cancel an express disease claim. Piece 22 said the same thing from the FDA side. FTC says DSHEA did not change the FTC Act, and the labeling disclaimer is not even required in non-label advertising — and if you put it there anyway, it still will not save the headline.

Mischaracterizing FDA's process is its own 2022 problem. Example 48: a marketer gets a qualified-health-claim letter that the relationship is uncertain because the evidence is thin, then banners "Meets FDA Qualified Health Claim." FTC calls that a mischaracterization that likely communicates FDA approval and a high level of evidence. Example 49: "FDA Approved" next to inch-and-pound promises. Piece 34 is that costume. FTC will read those words as efficacy claims, not as facility paperwork.

Write the smallest true sentence; match it to human evidence on this SKU or shrink the sentence; check 101.93(g); place the disclaimer; send the 30-day file; then read the finished PDP, the cart page, and the first three bullets as one impression. If a reasonable buyer would hear a stronger claim than the study will carry, the panel is not done.
