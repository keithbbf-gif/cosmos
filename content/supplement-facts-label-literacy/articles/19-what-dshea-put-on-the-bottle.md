**Disclaimer.** Educational only. Not medical advice. Dietary supplements are not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease (DSHEA; 21 U.S.C. § 321(ff); 21 CFR 101.93). This is not legal advice and not a labeling opinion for a specific SKU.

Pub. L. 103-417 is dated 25 October 1994. The signature date is not a permission slip. The Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act amended the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act. It created a definition, a narrow lane for statements of nutritional support, and a duty to label the product as a dietary supplement. It did not repeal 21 U.S.C. § 321(g)(1)(B). A product intended to diagnose, cure, mitigate, treat, or prevent disease is still a drug.

This piece stays on the bottle. The companion manufacturing pack can talk about plants and certificates. Here the question is what 1994 put on the label, what 1997 had to finish, and what 2007 never was.

## What § 321(ff) is

Section 3 of DSHEA added 21 U.S.C. § 321(ff). A dietary supplement is a product (other than tobacco) intended to supplement the diet that bears or contains one or more of: a vitamin; a mineral; an herb or other botanical; an amino acid; a dietary substance for use by man to supplement the diet by increasing the total dietary intake; or a concentrate, metabolite, constituent, extract, or combination of any of those.

The same definition requires a product that is intended for ingestion in a form the Act already knew (pill, capsule, tablet, powder, liquid — the cross-reference is to the vitamin-mineral special-dietary provisions), that is **not** represented as a conventional food or as a sole item of a meal or the diet, and that is **labeled as a dietary supplement**.

That last clause is on the bottle. "Labeled as a dietary supplement" is how a powder drink stays in 101.36 instead of 101.9. The statement of identity, the Supplement Facts title, and the intended-use language have to agree (piece 01). DSHEA did not let you print Nutrition Facts and then recite (ff) in a footnote.

§ 321(ff)(3) is the drug-first fence. An article approved as a new drug, or authorized for investigation as a new drug with public substantial clinical investigations, is out of the dietary-supplement definition unless it was marketed as a food or supplement *before* that approval or authorization — and even then FDA can write a contrary regulation. The 1994 definition is how a later unapproved peptide or a prescription active gets kept off this panel. The label cannot talk the article back in.

Except for purposes of the drug definition in § 321(g) (and a later adverse-event section), a dietary supplement is deemed a food. Deemed a food is not "deemed unsupervised." Misbranding and adulteration still attach. The bottle still has to tell the truth.

## What § 343(r)(6) added

Section 6 of DSHEA added 21 U.S.C. § 343(r)(6). For purposes of the nutrition-claim paragraph, a statement for a dietary supplement may be made if three things are all true:

(A) the statement claims a benefit related to a classical nutrient-deficiency disease and discloses the prevalence of that disease in the United States, **or** describes the role of a nutrient or dietary ingredient intended to affect the structure or function in humans, **or** characterizes the documented mechanism by which a nutrient or dietary ingredient acts to maintain such structure or function, **or** describes general well-being from consumption of a nutrient or dietary ingredient;

(B) the manufacturer has substantiation that the statement is truthful and not misleading; and

(C) the statement contains, prominently displayed and in boldface type, the two sentences everyone now calls the DSHEA disclaimer: that the statement has not been evaluated by FDA, and that the product is not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease.

The next sentence is the one catalogs skip. A statement under this subparagraph **may not** claim to diagnose, mitigate, treat, cure, or prevent a specific disease or class of diseases. Then the notice: if you propose to make such a statement in the labeling, you notify the Secretary no later than 30 days after first marketing with that statement.

That is the entire statutory gift. Role. Mechanism to *maintain*. Well-being. A deficiency-disease sentence with a prevalence disclosure. Substantiation already in hand. Disclaimer on the bottle. A letter to FDA after you start. Pieces 21–23 are the regulation that implements those sentences. 1994 did not add "and then you may talk like a drug insert."

## What still required a box

DSHEA's section 7 added dietary-supplement ingredient labeling and nutrition-information labeling to the Act, including 21 U.S.C. § 343(s). The statute said the product would be misbranded if it failed those labeling duties. It did not camera-ready the Supplement Facts panel.

You still needed a statement of identity. You still needed net quantity. You still needed an ingredient statement. You still needed nutrition labeling appropriate to a supplement rather than a yogurt cup. You still needed the name and place of business. You still needed to avoid the drug claims § 343(r)(6) had just refused.

The 1994 text also told FDA it could issue good-manufacturing-practice regulations for dietary supplements. That sentence is how Part 111 exists later. It is not a cGMP logo you print in 1995. It is not a Supplement Facts row.

New dietary ingredients (now 21 U.S.C. § 350b) are a premarket notification for certain ingredients that were not marketed in the United States before 15 October 1994 — that date *is* in the NDI statute, and it is not the Public Law's enactment date. An NDI file is not a panel. This piece will not rewrite the NDI rule. If the ingredient does not belong in (ff), it does not belong in the box.

## 1997 labeling rules

On 23 September 1997 FDA published the final rule that actually drew the bottle: 62 FR 49826, "Food Labeling; Statement of Identity, Nutrition Labeling and Ingredient Labeling of Dietary Supplements." Effective 23 March 1999. That is where 21 CFR 101.36 takes its modern shape, where 101.4(g) and 101.4(h) tell you how the list under the box and the botanical names work, and where "dietary supplement" becomes a required piece of the statement of identity (with the permitted substitutions: "calcium supplement," "herbal supplement with vitamins").

The same day, a companion final rule set the notification procedure and the disclaimer mechanics that became 21 CFR 101.93 (62 FR 49859 and the pages that follow; pieces 22 and 23). 1994 wrote the statutory sentences. 1997 wrote the type size, the 30-day address, the adjacent-or-asterisk placement, and the box around a disclaimer that is not adjacent.

If a sales deck says "DSHEA labels," ask which year they mean. The definition and the claim lane are 1994. The panel a printer can set is 1997, in force 1999, then amended — units in 2016 (81 FR 33742), the 101.93 mailing address in 2023 (88 FR 17718). A 1994 reprint of the Public Law is not a dieline.

Chapter IV of the Dietary Supplement Labeling Guide (April 2005) is still the Q&A FDA hosts for how that 1997 box is assembled. Use it for shape. Use current 101.36 for units and mandatory rows (piece 01, piece 09).

## 2007 is manufacturing, not this panel

Current Good Manufacturing Practice in Manufacturing, Packaging, Labeling, or Holding Operations for Dietary Supplements is 21 CFR Part 111, issued in 2007. It is a plant rule: identity, purity, strength, composition, records, quality control. It is how a lot is supposed to match the label you already wrote.

Part 111 is not a Supplement Facts format. It is not FDA approval of the SKU. It is not a certificate you print as "FDA cGMP certified" (piece 34). A product can have a clean 101.36 panel and a dirty plant. A product can have a Part 111 file and a panel that still calls calcium carbonate "1,000 mg calcium." The documents do different jobs.

This pack will not walk you through a master manufacturing record. The companion COA pack is that curriculum. The 1994 statute mentioned that GMP regulations could be written. The 2007 rule wrote them. Neither year replaced 321(g). Neither year made the disclaimer a solvent for disease copy.

What DSHEA put on the bottle, in the end, is a category word, a definition that can fail, a claim lane with a fence, and a command to label nutrition information. The box you set in a printer file is the 1997 implementation of that command, still live, still amended. The sentence you want to put next to the box is 343(r)(6) or it is a drug claim. October 1994 did not merge those two sentences. A later website cannot merge them either (piece 20).
