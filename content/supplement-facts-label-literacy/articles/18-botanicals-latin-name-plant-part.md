**Disclaimer.** Educational only. Not medical advice. Dietary supplements are not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease (DSHEA; 21 U.S.C. § 321(ff); 21 CFR 101.93). This is not legal advice and not a labeling opinion for a specific SKU.

"Root" is a required word, not flavor text. 21 CFR 101.4(h)(1) says the listing of a botanical dietary ingredient is followed by the part of the plant from which it is derived, in English: "Garlic bulb" or "Garlic (bulb)." Flower, not flos. Leaf, not folium. The 2005 Dietary Supplement Labeling Guide, Chapter IV, question 4-2, puts that plant-part duty on the Supplement Facts panel. Nutrition Facts will not take "root" as a nutrient row. Supplement Facts must.

Algae are the exception in 101.4(h)(1). Everything else that is a plant, including fungi as the rule writes them into the botanical sentence, needs the part. A powder that says "ashwagandha 600 mg" and never says root has not finished 101.4(h).

## Plant part

The part is how two products with the same common name stop pretending to be the same article. Echinacea aerial parts and echinacea root are different starting materials. Green tea leaf and green tea extract of leaf are different preparations; they at least share a part. "Echinacea" with no part is a category.

101.4(h) wants the part in English. Do not dress it in pharmacy Latin to look serious. "Radix" on a U.S. retail label is the opposite of the instruction. FDA's own examples are "root" and "leaves" and "bulb."

The part belongs with the dietary-ingredient name on the Supplement Facts panel (DSLG 4-2; 101.36). If the botanical is a (b)(3) other dietary ingredient, the common or usual name, the part, and the weight per serving sit together, with the asterisk for "Daily Value not established" (DSLG 4-30). If the botanical is inside a 101.36(c) blend, the part still attaches to the indented name. The blend heading does not waive 101.4(h).

Seeds, fruits, peels, latex, resin, and mycelium are parts or preparations you have to name honestly. "Mushroom" that is mycelium on grain is a different article from a fruiting-body powder. This pack will not write a mushroom monograph. It will say: the word after the common name has to match the article in the capsule. If you cannot point to a plant part in the batch record, you cannot print one.

A combination part — "root and leaf" — is legal if that is what you extracted or powdered. It is not legal if you powdered root and then borrowed leaf identity from a different SKU's photo.

## Latin name

101.4(h) sets the common or usual name of botanical dietary ingredients, including fungi and algae, to the names standardized in *Herbs of Commerce*, 1992 edition, which the regulation incorporates by reference. That 1992 book is the incorporated text. Later AHPA editions are useful to people who label herbs. They are not automatically the CFR. If you are relying on a later edition for a name that is not in the 1992 book, mark the gap and get a labeling read [VERIFY the 1992 entry for that common name before print].

101.4(h)(2) requires the Latin binomial in parentheses **except** when that binomial is available in *Herbs of Commerce* for the common or usual name you listed. In practice: if the standardized common name is in the 1992 book, you may use that common name (plus plant part) without reprinting the Latin. If it is not, you print the Latin. When a positive identification cannot be made without the author citation, you include the author or authors who published the Latin name, in line with the International Code of Botanical Nomenclature the rule incorporates (Tokyo Code, 1994 edition, as 101.4(h) still writes it).

The binomial may be listed before the plant part when it is required. "Withania somnifera root" and "ashwagandha root (*Withania somnifera*)" are both trying to do the same identification job. Pick a house style and keep it consistent across the catalog so a buyer can scan.

Latin is not a claim. Italic binomials on a principal display panel do not make the extract standardized, do not make the claim substantiated, and do not move the product into drug territory or out of it. They identify the organism. That is enough work for one line.

## Common name collisions

"Ginseng" is how this section earns its keep. *Panax ginseng* (often "Asian ginseng" or "Korean ginseng"), *Panax quinquefolius* ("American ginseng"), and *Eleutherococcus senticosus* (sold for years as "Siberian ginseng") are not one species. *Herbs of Commerce* exists so those collisions have standardized English names. A bottle that says only "ginseng root" has not told a careful buyer which plant was powdered.

"Cinnamon" can be *Cinnamomum verum* or *Cinnamomum cassia* (and other *Cinnamomum* species). The coumarin story belongs in a specification and a COA, not in this panel's claim block. The panel's job is to stop "cinnamon bark" from being an unidentified bark.

"Elderberry" should not silently become *Sambucus nigra* flower, *Sambucus nigra* fruit, or another *Sambucus* species because the photo was purple. Fruit and flower are different parts. Different *Sambucus* species are different plants.

"Turmeric" versus "curcumin" is a common-name collision of a different kind. Turmeric is the botanical (rhizome, if that is what you used). Curcuminoids are constituents you may indent (piece 15). Printing "curcumin 500 mg" when the article is turmeric extract standardized to a curcuminoid percentage is a constituent-versus-parent error, not a Latin error. You still need the plant and the part on the parent line.

Do not invent a folk name to dodge a collision. If the 1992 book has a standardized common name, that is the name 101.4(h) pointed you at. If it does not, the Latin is not optional color.

## Extract vs crude

A crude powdered root and a dried extract of that root share a plant and a part. They do not share a 101.36(b)(3)(ii) amount rule. Crude powder is the weight of the powder. Dried extract is the weight of the dried extract (piece 15). Liquid extract is the volume or weight of the total extract, with fresh marked when the starting material was fresh.

The name still needs the part. "Ashwagandha root extract" and "ashwagandha root powder" are different common-or-usual-name strings. Do not let the word "ashwagandha" do both jobs on alternate printings of the same SKU.

Standardized constituent, indented, is optional (101.36(b)(3)(iii)). "Withanolides 5 mg" under ashwagandha root extract is a constituent line. It does not replace "root." It does not replace the Latin or the *Herbs of Commerce* name. It does not make the parent line a drug monograph.

Solvent remains a label or ingredient-list fact for liquid extracts (101.36(b)(3)(ii)(B)). A crude powder has no extraction solvent. Do not print "in 70% ethanol" on a crude-powder row because the last SKU was a tincture.

## Do not turn a binomial into a disease monograph

This is not an adaptogen efficacy piece. The companion pack on that word is history. This pack is the panel. *Withania somnifera* on a label identifies a plant. It does not authorize a sentence about a named disease, and it does not authorize FDA's forbidden examples — "reduces the pain and stiffness associated with arthritis," "protective against the development of cancer" — to be rewritten with a Latin hat on.

A structure/function sentence about a botanical, if you have one, is 21 U.S.C. § 343(r)(6) and 21 CFR 101.93: role of the ingredient on structure or function, or a documented mechanism to *maintain* that structure or function; substantiation first; 30-day notice; disclaimer (pieces 21–23). The binomial does not write that sentence. The SECG's ten criteria still apply to the whole page (piece 24).

Do not paste a botanical-drug monograph, a German Commission E paragraph, or a PubMed abstract onto the information panel and call it identification. Citation of a publication that refers to a disease use can be a disease claim under 101.93(g) when the context of the labeling implies treatment or prevention. The Latin name is not a citation. A QR code that opens a disease paper is.

Read a botanical row in this order: standardized common name or required Latin, English plant part, extract versus crude, amount rule for that article, optional indented constituent, solvent if liquid. If the principal display panel says "ginseng" and the box cannot pick a species and a part, stop. The rest of the label is decorating a plant you have not named.
