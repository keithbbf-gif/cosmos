164 F.3d 650. *Pearson v. Shalala*, United States Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit, 1999. The case is about commercial speech and how FDA ranks evidence for a health claim. It is not a SKU. It does not authorize a calcium banner you have not earned.

A health claim, under 21 CFR 101.14, characterizes the relationship between a substance and a disease or health-related condition. That is a different drawer from 21 U.S.C. § 343(r)(6). Structure/function talks about normal structure or function, fenced by 101.93(g) (pieces 21 and 24). A health claim *names the disease relationship* and is allowed only through the health-claim rules — authorized after significant scientific agreement, or, after *Pearson*, as a qualified health claim in the words FDA specified.

Do not invent a qualified health claim. Do not shorten one. Recheck FDA's current qualified-health-claim page before any printer file. [VERIFY]

## Authorized health claims are a list

NLEA put health claims on a petition-and-rule path. 101.14(c) is the significant-scientific-agreement (SSA) standard: totality of publicly available evidence, agreement among qualified experts that the claim is supported. FDA then writes a Subpart E regulation with model language and qualifying levels.

The current SSA list is a list, not a theme. This pack's working examples are calcium (and, where the rule allows, calcium and vitamin D) and osteoporosis at 21 CFR 101.72, and folate and neural-tube defects at 101.79. Other SSA pairs live on FDA's hosted SSA page. [VERIFY] that page and the eCFR before you quote a section as still operative. Soy protein's coronary-heart-disease regulation has been the subject of later agency action. A remembered list is how a false claim gets typeset.

101.14(d) and (e) are the general conditions. The substance-disease relationship must be the one in the authorizing rule. All required information appears in one place, except that the principal display panel may point: "See ______ for information about the relationship between ______ and ______." FDA's example is "See attached pamphlet for information about calcium and osteoporosis." If a graphic — 101.14 names a heart symbol — is an explicit or implied health claim, the reference statement or the complete claim sits next to it. Piece 24 already treated the heart symbol as a disease mark when you do *not* have an authorized health claim. Subpart E, if you qualify, tells you what sentence must sit next to it.

The claim has to be comprehensible in a total daily diet. It is a risk-reduction sentence about a substance in a dietary pattern, not "this bottle treats osteoporosis."

## Qualifying levels

101.14(e) plus the specific Subpart E rule set the amount and form tests. If the claim is about eating *less* of a substance, and "low" is defined, the food must meet "low" unless the specific rule writes a different number. If the claim is about eating a substance at other than decreased levels, and "high" is defined, the food must meet "high" (101.54(b): 20 percent or more of the RDI or DRV per reference amount), unless Subpart E writes a different number.

101.72 is the worked example this piece will not turn into an ad. A calcium–osteoporosis health claim may appear on a food that meets 101.14 *and* meets or exceeds "high" in calcium as defined in 101.54(b). The calcium has to be assimilable. The rule also carries other conditions — phosphorus limits, and the vitamin D variant when you use those models. [VERIFY] 101.72(c) and (d) against current eCFR before anyone sets type. FDA's model sentences are about adequate calcium (and, where used, vitamin D) as part of a diet, sometimes with physical activity, and a possible reduction of osteoporosis risk later in life. They are not "prevents osteoporosis." They are not a reason to tell a reader to take a supplement for a disease.

Folate and neural-tube-defect claims live in 101.79. Piece 11 is the mcg DFE arithmetic. If a prenatal panel wants the authorized claim, it uses that rule's level and model language. If it does not, it does not get to write a neural-tube ad as structure/function. Criterion 1 in piece 24 already forbids naming the disease outside this drawer.

Disqualifying nutrient levels in 101.14 keep a high-fat, high-saturated-fat, high-cholesterol, or high-sodium food from wearing a health claim unless the specific rule says otherwise. A calcium gummy still has to clear those gates.

## Pearson and qualified claims

The *Pearson* appellants had asked FDA to authorize four claims FDA would not put on the SSA list: dietary fiber and cancer; antioxidant vitamins and cancer; omega-3 fatty acids and coronary heart disease; and a comparative claim that 0.8 mg of folate in supplement form is more effective against neural-tube defects than a lower amount in conventional food. The D.C. Circuit reversed the district court (164 F.3d 650).

The holding that matters for a label file: on that record, the First Amendment does not let FDA reject a health claim it finds only *potentially* misleading unless the agency also reasonably determines that no disclaimer would cure the deception. The court also told FDA, under the APA, to clarify "significant scientific agreement."

What *Pearson* did not do: create a right to print any disease-risk sentence with a footnote. Inherently misleading claims can still be banned. After the decision, FDA built a qualified-health-claim (QHC) practice: enforcement-discretion letters that specify the exact qualifying language, often ranking the evidence as limited or inconsistent. Later guidance extended QHCs to conventional foods.

A QHC is not structure/function and not an SSA claim. It is not a sentence you draft because a sales deck liked *Pearson*. The letter — if one still exists for that pair on FDA's page — is the copy deck. Shortening "the relationship is uncertain because there is little scientific evidence" into "meets FDA qualified health claim" is FTC 2022 Example 48. Piece 27 is that file.

## Why S/F is a different drawer

101.93(f) permits a statement that describes the role of a nutrient or dietary ingredient intended to affect structure or function in humans, or that characterizes a documented mechanism by which it maintains that structure or function — *provided* the statement is not a disease claim under 101.93(g). If the labeling bears a disease claim, the product is regulated as a drug unless the claim is an authorized health claim for which the product qualifies.

That last clause is the only bridge. An SSA claim or a still-operative QHC, used in the agency's words at the agency's levels, is how a dietary supplement may talk about a substance-disease relationship. Everything else that names a disease, a class of diseases, or characteristic signs is a 101.93(g) problem (piece 24) or an unapproved-drug problem under 21 U.S.C. § 321(g)(1)(B) (piece 20).

The three 343(r)(6) duties — substantiation first, 30-day notice, disclaimer — do not substitute for 101.14. Filing a structure/function notification that says "calcium and osteoporosis" does not create a health claim. The 101.93(c) disclaimer cannot wash a disease-risk sentence you were not authorized to make. Piece 22 is that box.

FTC will still read the same panel as advertising (piece 27). An authorized claim that is true under FDA rules can still fail FTC if a before/after spine or a "clinically proven to reverse bone loss" caption communicates a stronger claim than the model language.

## Do not write a calcium–osteoporosis ad unless you are actually using the authorized claim

The temptation is always the same: put 200 mg of calcium in a blend, print a bone silhouette, and write a sentence that sounds like 101.72. That is not how the drawer works.

To use 101.72 you need a serving that meets "high" in calcium, the other 101.72 conditions, the 101.14 general requirements, and the model language or a truthful paraphrase that does not escalate the claim. If you do not have those, you have a disease name on a supplement. Criterion 1 of the 2000 rule already called "protective against the development of cancer" and "reduces the pain and stiffness associated with arthritis" forbidden examples. Osteoporosis is the same class of word.

A structure/function path, if you have substantiation and you stay off the disease, talks about nutrients and normal bone structure without promising osteoporosis risk reduction. That sentence still needs the 101.93 disclaimer and the 30-day file. It still dies if a blog next to the cart does the 101.72 work you did not qualify for.

Qualified claims are not a workaround for a serving that misses "high," or for a pair FDA has not written a letter for. If the QHC page is silent, the answer is silence on that disease. A third chair labeled "Pearson said we can say it" is how a label becomes a letter.
