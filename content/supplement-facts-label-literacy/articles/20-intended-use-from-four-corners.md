**Disclaimer.** Educational only. Not medical advice. Dietary supplements are not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease (DSHEA; 21 U.S.C. § 321(ff); 21 CFR 101.93). This is not legal advice and not a labeling opinion for a specific SKU.

FRS International, 15 June 2020, letter 606701: website plus Facebook. Dual header. FDA and the Federal Trade Commission signed the same warning letter. They reviewed www.frs.com, www.healthyenergy.com, and www.frs-international.com (1 April 2020 and 4 June 2020), and the Facebook page facebook.com/FRSEnergyEndurance, where the firm directed people to the shopping sites. The products named were quercetin-containing soft chews and liquid concentrates sold as Q-Force and FRS Healthy Energy. The agencies' conclusion was not a taste test. It was intended use: products intended to mitigate, prevent, treat, diagnose, or cure COVID-19, sold as unapproved new drugs and as misbranded drugs.

That letter is a method. It is not a review of quercetin, and this pack does not recommend any ingredient for COVID-19.

## Intended use is not a feeling

21 U.S.C. § 321(g)(1)(B) defines a drug, in part, as an article intended for use in the diagnosis, cure, mitigation, treatment, or prevention of disease. The formulator's private intent does not control. The statute reads *intended use*. Agencies assemble that use from what you said where a buyer can see it.

A dietary supplement is a food category under § 321(ff) (piece 19). It becomes a drug when the four corners say drug. You do not get to keep the Supplement Facts panel as a costume once the website has done 321(g) work. 21 CFR 101.93(f), last sentence, says the same thing in regulation: if the label or labeling of a product marketed as a dietary supplement bears a disease claim, the product is subject to regulation as a drug unless the claim is an authorized health claim the product actually qualifies for.

"I meant structure/function" is not a defense if the page says the other thing. "Our lawyer has not reviewed the Facebook page" is how 606701 gets written. Dual-header letters exist because FTC will read the same URL as advertising while FDA reads it as labeling.

Intended use can be proved by a single sentence. It can also be proved by a pile of weaker sentences that a reasonable buyer adds up. Piece 27 is FTC's 2022 *Health Products Compliance Guidance* on net impression. This piece is the FDA drug definition that sits under the same URL.

## The four corners

Call them four corners even when a firm has six surfaces. The investigator will not stop at the bottle.

**The bottle.** Principal display panel, information panel, Supplement Facts, other ingredients, hang tags, inserts. A name that swallows a disease is already a problem; FDA's 2002 Small Entity Compliance Guide used invented names like "CarpalHealth" and "CircuCure" as examples of forbidden claim-in-the-name. A heart symbol next to a disease story is the same class of evidence (piece 24).

**The site you own.** Product pages, blogs on the same hostname, "Shop" buttons on article pages, comparison charts, FAQ macros. Mercola 607133 (18 February 2021) is the worked example: FDA reviewed mercola.com and mercolamarket.com on 8 February 2021, plus twitter.com/mercola, where readers were directed to the market site. The products named were Liposomal Vitamin C, Liposomal Vitamin D3, and Quercetin and Pterostilbene Advanced. The hook was again COVID-19 intended use, built from articles and a social account sitting next to a place to buy. This pack does not recommend those SKUs for any disease. It recommends reading the letter's method.

**The marketplace.** Amazon bullets, A-plus HTML, backend search terms, a third-party listing you "don't control" but still refill. Piece 41 is that surface. Intended use does not care that the bullet lives in a vendor portal.

**The paid or directed social, and the 9 p.m. chat.** Facebook in 606701. Twitter in 607133. A Reel you paid for. A customer-service macro that says the capsule is for a named disease. Influencer scripts. If you directed the buyer from that speech to a cart, you handed the investigator the stitch.

Four corners is a metaphor for the whole envelope, not a promise that a fifth surface is free. Packaging in another language, a podcast show notes page, a QR code to a preprint — all of it is available to the same read.

## Why a blog next to a cart counts

Labeling is broader than the bottle. If the blog and the cart share a house, a reasonable buyer is not asked to pretend the article is a peer-reviewed journal that happens to have a yellow "Shop" button. 607133 is built on that stitch. The letter quotes website copy and social copy, then names the SKUs for sale.

You do not fix the stitch by putting the 101.93 disclaimer on the product page and leaving the blog naked. Piece 22: the disclaimer does not wash a disease claim. You do not fix it by moving the disease article to a subdomain if the subdomain still sells the bottle. You do not fix it by dating the article "for educational purposes" while the SKU link remains.

A science page that never names your product can still become labeling when you paste it under the PDP, when your ads point at it, or when your CS team sends it as the answer to "will this help with [disease]?" Context is the whole page (piece 24). Intended use is the whole account.

FTC's interest is the same URL. A claim can fail 101.93 as a disease claim and fail the 2022 FTC guide as unsubstantiated advertising in the same week. Dual header.

## COVID letters as a method, not a product review

The COVID-19 warning letters are useful because they are dated, numbered, and public, and because they show the agencies reading websites and social together. FDA's fraudulent COVID-19 products page is the running index. Recheck that page for the current list [VERIFY]. Do not treat a 2020 letter as a monograph on the ingredient it happens to name.

Method, in 606701:

- Agencies name the dates they captured the sites.
- They name the social account and the fact that it directed people to purchase URLs.
- They name the products.
- They apply § 505(a) (unapproved new drug) and § 502 (misbranded drug), and the 301(a)/(d) prohibition on introducing those articles into interstate commerce.
- They quote the speech — including, in 606701, a Facebook post about doctors studying quercetin as a "defense against coronavirus." This pack quotes that only as the letter's example of the speech they captured, not as a use for quercetin.

Method, in 607133:

- Same statutory hooks.
- Two owned sites plus a Twitter account that pointed at the market.
- Three named SKUs.
- Articles on the content site treated as labeling because of the commercial stitch.

Bautista et al. (PMC7528445) counted letters in a 2020 window; piece 30 uses that paper. This piece does not invent a letter count. One letter is enough to learn the read.

Do not recommend vitamin C, vitamin D, zinc, elderberry, silver, or quercetin for COVID-19. Do not reprint the letters' quoted claims as if they were structure/function templates you could trim. The quoted claims are the violation.

## What to strip before you print

Print is the expensive moment. Strip the disease work first.

Take the SKU name off any disease. Take the disease off the PDP, the box, the insert, and the hang tag. Take the protocol, the hashtag, and the "works with your [drug]" sentence off the site. Take the Reel you paid for down, or recut it until it cannot assemble 321(g). Rewrite the Amazon bullets and the backend search terms. Rewrite the CS macro so a night-shift answer cannot diagnose.

Then read what is left as a buyer would. A clean 101.36 panel next to a blog that still does 321(g) is not a clean SKU. A clean 101.93 disclaimer next to a Facebook post that still does 321(g) is not a clean SKU. A structure/function sentence that is true, substantiated, notified, and disclaimed can stay (piece 21). A nutrient-content claim that is true can stay (piece 25). An authorized health claim you actually qualify for can stay (piece 26). Everything else that names a disease as something the bottle is *for* comes off.

606701 and 607133 are still on FDA's site as of this draft's source check. They will paste your sentence back to you (piece 43). Write fewer sentences you would be ashamed to see in that paste. The four corners are already assembled. Your job is to stop handing them a drug.
