**Disclaimer.** Educational only. Not medical advice. Dietary supplements are not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease (DSHEA; 21 U.S.C. § 321(ff); 21 CFR 101.93). This is not legal advice and not a labeling opinion for a specific SKU.

If the formula card says 2 capsules and the site says 1, you do not have a label. You have a dispute. 21 CFR 101.36 will take one serving size. The plant will weigh one batch. The website will sell whatever someone typed into a CMS. Those three documents have to agree on serving, form, amount, blend names, and claims before anyone hits print. A fourth document — the COA — sits in the companion manufacturing pack. Mention it. Do not clone it.

Intended use (21 U.S.C. § 321(g)(1)(B)) is assembled from all of them. A site that adds a disease blog next to the cart is the four-corners problem in piece 20, even if the carton is quiet.

## What each document is for

**The formula card** is the manufacturing object. In a Part 111 plant it shows up as the master manufacturing record and the batch record: the identity and weight of each component, the theoretical yield, the processing steps, the in-process targets. 21 CFR 111.3's definition of quality is that the finished dietary supplement meets established specifications for identity, purity, strength, and composition. The card is how the plant knows what those specifications were supposed to be. This pack will not rewrite Part 111. It needs you to notice that the card is not a mood board. It is grams.

**The label** is 101.36 plus 101.4 plus, if you made a statement of nutritional support, 101.93. Title of the box. Serving Size as those two words. Amount per serving. (b)(2) then the hairline then (b)(3). Plant part. Other ingredients in order of predominance. Allergen line (piece 37). Disclaimer box if you triggered it (piece 22). The label is what FDA's Dietary Supplement Labeling Guide is for. It is not a place to "round" a blend because the formula card looked crowded.

**The website** is labeling and advertising. Product page, blog, FAQ, PDF sell sheet, Amazon listing (piece 41), the comparison chart that only exists in HTML. 101.93(b) wants the disclaimer on each panel or page that carries the statement. FTC's December 2022 guidance wants competent and reliable evidence for the net impression of the URL, not for the sentence you liked best.

When the three diverge, each audience gets a different product. The plant makes the card. The buyer reads the site. FDA reads both and the carton. That is the dispute.

## Serving and amount

Start here. It is the cheapest fight to find and the most expensive to ship.

Formula card: fill weight and count per serving. Label: Serving Size and the amounts to the right of each name. Site: "take one daily" in the first paragraph, or a calculator that assumes one scoop when the card is two.

101.12(b) Table 2 still says the serving is the maximum per eating occasion. If the site says 1–3 and the label says 1, the legal serving is the occasion you actually recommended somewhere. Piece 03. If the site says 1 and the card was costed at 2, someone is shorting the bottle or overstating the days of use (piece 04).

Form has to match. Magnesium oxide on the card and magnesium glycinate on the site is not a style choice. The row is elemental magnesium; the source line is the salt (piece 14). Vitamin D in mcg on the panel and IU-only on the site is a 2016-unit problem (pieces 09–10). Folate as mcg DFE on the card and "800 mcg folic acid" on a prenatal landing page is a 2014 page (piece 11).

Amount per serving is not amount per bottle (piece 05). A site that sums the bottle and puts that number in a burst is fighting the box. Composite compliance will not be run on your landing page. It will be run on packages.

## Blend names

101.36(c) is specific. You may give "Proprietary Blend" or a fanciful name. You must give the *total* weight of the other dietary ingredients in the blend. You must indent the names in descending order by weight. You may not hide RDI/DRV nutrients inside the blend; those come out and get their own rows (piece 16).

The formula card knows the milligrams of each herb. The label, if you used (c), does not have to disclose those milligrams. The website that then says "500 mg of ashwagandha" under a 1.2 g blend you never declared per-herb is either leaking a number the label refused or inventing one. Either way you now have a third document that disagrees with the second.

Fanciful names have to be the same fanciful names. "Night Blend" on the card, "Sleep Matrix" on the site, and "Rest Complex" on the carton are three products. Amazon will create a fourth.

Descending order is information. If the site rearranges the herbs so the expensive one is first, and the label correctly lists the cheap one first, you taught the buyer a lie the panel already corrected. Pick one order: the rule's.

## Claims that exist only on the site

Structure/function statements live in 21 U.S.C. § 343(r)(6) and 21 CFR 101.93. They need substantiation *before* first marketing, a 30-day notification, and the disclaimer. The notice (piece 23) asks for the text of the statement. If the site later grows a paragraph the notice never saw, you have a new statement.

Disease copy that exists only on the site is still intended use. A blog titled with a condition FDA used as a forbidden-claim example, sitting in the same domain as the cart, is the FRS 606701 method (piece 43). You do not need a second CMS number to understand the paste-back. "The letter will quote your sentence" is the opening of that piece for a reason.

Nutrient-content claims and health claims have their own drawers (pieces 25–26). A site that says "excellent source of ashwagandha" is not saved by a quiet panel. A site that writes a calcium–osteoporosis ad without using the authorized health-claim language is not saved by a correct mcg RAE row.

Quality costumes that exist only on the site — "FDA approved," "pharmaceutical grade," "third-party tested" without a lot, "Made in USA" on an imported powder — are still claims (pieces 34–36, 39). The carton does not have to repeat them for FTC to read them.

Reviews, Q&A, and chat macros are part of the website even when a vendor hosts them (piece 41). If you cannot export them, you cannot print-lock them.

## A print lock

A print lock is a dated packet, not a Slack thumbs-up. Minimum contents:

1. Formula-card revision number and date, with the serving and every dietary-ingredient weight.
2. Label PDF, die line, and the exact Supplement Facts encoding (units post-2016; elemental vs salt; blend math; plant part; other ingredients; allergen line; disclaimer placement).
3. Website screenshot and a crawl of every URL that names the SKU, including the blog posts in the same cart path.
4. Amazon export: title, bullets, A+, backend terms (piece 41).
5. Macro export from the helpdesk.
6. 30-day notice file, if any statement is on any of the above.
7. COA or program-directory screenshot for the lot you intend to pack, if you plan to say anything about a test (piece 36). That file is read with the COA pack, not rewritten here.
8. Origin and Prop 65 math if those sentences are on the carton (pieces 38–39).

One human signs that the five claim-bearing surfaces say the same serving, the same form, the same amounts, the same blend names, and the same claims. Science/QA and counsel are empty seats in this pack; the signature is still a person's. 101.93(a) already taught you that the notification's certification is a person. The print lock can borrow that seriousness.

If any row fails, you do not send the printer a new die. You fix the document that is wrong. Sometimes that is the site. Sometimes that is the card. Sometimes that is the label someone "simplified." Print is the expensive moment (piece 44). The PDF is cheap. The dispute is cheaper than a letter that pastes three disagreeing sentences back to you as if they were one product.
