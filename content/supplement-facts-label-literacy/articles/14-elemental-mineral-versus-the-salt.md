Calcium carbonate is not 40% of the front of the bottle unless you did the elemental math and the panel says calcium. The salt is heavier than the mineral. A 1,250 mg scoop of calcium carbonate holds about 500 mg of calcium, because calcium is roughly 40% of CaCO₃ by atomic weight. That 40% is stoichiometry, not a marketing grade. The Supplement Facts row is the 500 mg.

FDA wrote the question in the 2005 Dietary Supplement Labeling Guide, Chapter IV, 4-15: if the calcium carbonate in the product supplies calcium, do you list the weight of the entire salt or just of the calcium? The answer is the calcium. The cite is 21 CFR 101.36(b)(2)(ii). The same sentence governs magnesium, zinc, iron, selenium, and every other (b)(2) mineral you added on purpose.

## Elemental weight is the row

101.36(b)(2)(ii) wants the quantitative amount by weight of the dietary ingredient. The dietary ingredient is calcium, not calcium carbonate. It is magnesium, not magnesium oxide. It is zinc, not zinc gluconate. The salt is the source. The mineral is the row.

% Daily Value is built from that elemental weight. Divide the unrounded milligrams of calcium by the Daily Value for calcium, multiply by 100, round to the nearest whole percent (piece 06). If you divide the salt weight by the Daily Value, you have invented a %DV. A 1,000 mg calcium-carbonate tablet is not "100% DV calcium" on the old 1,000 mg calcium DV, because most of that gram is carbonate.

Compliance follows the same number. Added dietary ingredients must be present at 100% of the declared amount, analytical method aside (101.9(g)(3)–(4), applied through 101.36(f); DSLG 4-43). The declared amount is the elemental row. A lot that assays 1,000 mg of calcium carbonate against a 1,000 mg calcium claim is short. The COA pack is where you read the assay. This pack is where you read which milligrams the assay is supposed to match.

Naturally occurring mineral in a botanical matrix is a different 80% rule (DSLG 4-43, rose hips versus isolated vitamin C). Do not use that exception to hide a weighed-in carbonate salt. If you added the salt, you added the mineral.

## Source in parentheses

You may name the source inside the box. 101.36(d) gives the pattern: "Calcium (as calcium carbonate)." "As" or "from," immediately after the name or indented under it. The 2005 guide treats that parenthetical as a permission, not a decoration.

The source line does work the ingredient statement does not have to repeat. If the source is already in the Supplement Facts panel, you do not have to list that same source again under the box (101.36(d); 101.4(g)). If the source is not in the box, it belongs in the other-ingredient list. A label that says "Calcium 200 mg" with no parenthetical, and then hides "calcium citrate" in a 6-point blend of excipients, is making the buyer hunt.

Multiple sources of one mineral get listed as sources, not as three calcium rows. "Calcium (as calcium citrate and calcium carbonate)" is a legal shape. "Calcium citrate 400 mg" as if citrate were the dietary ingredient is the salt error with nicer handwriting.

The parenthetical is also how oxide, citrate, glycinate, malate, and chelate stop being aisle poetry. Those words name salts and complexes. They do not change the rule that the row is elemental. They do change the grams of powder you weigh to hit the row. That is formulation. The panel reports the result.

## Binders

Chapter IV, question 4-10: if you use a magnesium salt as a binder, where do you declare it? In the ingredient statement below the panel, not as a magnesium %DV row. The cite is 21 CFR 101.4(g). Binders, excipients, and fillers are not dietary ingredients. They do not get a (b)(2) line because they share a cation with a vitamin-mineral row.

Magnesium stearate is the usual example. It is a flow agent. If it appears in the %DV column, someone confused a lubricant with a magnesium supplement. Stearate magnesium that you are not offering as supplementation belongs under the box, in descending order of predominance with the rest of the other ingredients (piece 17).

The same split applies to calcium stearate, silicon dioxide, and a carbonate used only to set pH or as a tablet filler. Intent and quantity matter. A few milligrams of magnesium stearate in a two-piece capsule is a processing aid. A 400 mg magnesium-oxide serving is a mineral. Do not let the shared word "magnesium" collapse those jobs.

If you are actually supplementing magnesium *and* using a magnesium salt as a binder, the supplemented amount is the (b)(2) row, sourced in parentheses. The binder, if it is a different salt and is not being counted toward that row, stays in the other-ingredient list. Do not double-count the binder into the %DV and also list it under the box as if it were only a flow agent. Pick the chair the milligrams actually sit in.

## Oxide vs citrate is not a disease story

Oxide versus citrate is a source line and a weighing problem. It is not a disease story, and it is not a permission to write absorption-as-treatment copy.

Magnesium oxide is a small molecule. A large fraction of its weight is magnesium; the rest is oxygen. A citrate salt is mostly citrate. You weigh more citrate powder to put the same elemental milligrams in the capsule. That is why a "1,000 mg magnesium citrate" front panel can be a 100-and-some-mg magnesium product once you strip the organic acid [VERIFY the exact hydrate and the certificate of analysis for the lot — citrate salts are sold in more than one hydration state]. The panel still wants elemental magnesium.

Zinc oxide versus zinc citrate, ferrous sulfate versus ferrous bisglycinate, calcium carbonate versus calcium citrate: same walk. Name the mineral. Weigh the salt. Declare the mineral. Put the salt in parentheses. Do not write that one salt "treats deficiency better" or "raises serum levels in disease." A serum-level story that names a disease is 21 CFR 101.93(g) work, and it is a drug claim unless you hold an authorized health claim (piece 21). "Supports bone health" is a structure/function shape that still needs substantiation, notice, and the disclaimer. It is not earned by picking citrate.

Bioavailability talk on a cart page is still labeling. If you say the citrate is "better absorbed," you need competent evidence for that sentence as a reasonable buyer will take it, and you still may not turn absorption into a disease claim. This piece will not write the comparison for you. The label job is the elemental milligram.

## A "1,000 mg" that is the salt

Walk a calcium bottle from the principal display panel, not from the brand banner.

If the front says "1,000 mg" and the Supplement Facts row says "Calcium 1,000 mg (as calcium carbonate)," the front and the box agree on the mineral. The capsule is heavier than a gram because of the carbonate and the excipients. That is expected.

If the front says "1,000 mg" and the box says "Calcium 400 mg (as calcium carbonate)" — or worse, lists "calcium carbonate 1,000 mg" as if that were the (b)(2) line — the front is selling the salt. 101.36(b)(2)(ii) does not give you that line. DSLG 4-15 already answered it.

Iron is where the same trick gets dangerous in the aisle without becoming a treatment story in this pack. A ferrous-sulfate heptahydrate tablet has a small elemental fraction. The historic "325 mg" ferrous sulfate tablet is an elemental-iron number once you do the salt math, not 325 mg of iron [VERIFY the exact hydrate on the SKU before you repeat any milligram on a label]. The panel must say iron, in milligrams of iron.

Selenium methionine, chromium picolinate, potassium chloride: the first word in the pair is not automatically the row. Selenium is the row. Chromium is the row. Potassium is the row. The organic piece is the source, unless you are declaring the amino acid or the organic acid as its own dietary ingredient. Do not invent a second %DV for the salt.

A reading habit: ignore the front milligram until you have the row name, the elemental amount, the source parenthetical, and the other-ingredient list. If the front milligram matches the salt and not the mineral, the catalog is not ready to print. Piece 44 is the audit. This piece is the reason the calcium line exists.
