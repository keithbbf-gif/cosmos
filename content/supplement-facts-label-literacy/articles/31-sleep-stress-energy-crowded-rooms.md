**Disclaimer.** Educational only. Not medical advice. Dietary supplements are not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease (DSHEA; 21 U.S.C. § 321(ff); 21 CFR 101.93). This is not legal advice and not a labeling opinion for a specific SKU.

FDA used "Soothing Sleep" as a name that needs context. In the 9 January 2002 Small Entity Compliance Guide, under criterion 4, the agency wrote that **"Soothing Sleep" could be considered a claim to treat insomnia, a disease**, unless other labeling makes clear that the claim relates to a non-disease condition such as occasional sleeplessness. The name alone can do the work. No efficacy sentence is required.

Sleep, stress, and energy are three aisles that share a wall with a diagnosis. The 6 January 2000 final rule (65 FR 1000) and 21 CFR 101.93(g) already drew the wall. This piece stays on the structure/function side and quotes the disease side as **forbidden examples**. It is not a reason to take a supplement for insomnia, an anxiety disorder, or any other disease. It is not a stack protocol.

## Occasional sleeplessness vs insomnia

Insomnia is a disease name in FDA's own sleep examples. Treating it, naming it, or implying it with "difficulty falling asleep" language is a 101.93(g) problem.

The 2000 preamble and the SECG treat **occasional sleeplessness** as a non-disease condition. "For the relief of occasional sleeplessness" is the structure/function shape FDA accepted, because occasional sleeplessness is not treated as a characteristic symptom of a specific disease. That sentence still needs substantiation, a 30-day notice, and the 101.93 disclaimer (pieces 21–23). "Occasional" is doing legal work. Drop it, and the rest of the words drift toward the disease.

SECG criterion 2 is the sign-or-symptom test. Characteristic signs, in scientific or lay words, can imply a disease even when the disease is not named. Sleep copy that says **help you fall asleep if you have difficulty falling asleep** is the teaching contrast: that phrasing implies treatment of insomnia. **Helps to reduce difficulty falling asleep** is the same claim unless surrounding copy keeps the product on occasional sleeplessness. Those two sentences are **forbidden-claim examples** when they are heard as insomnia treatment. Do not put them on a PDP as product advice. Do not tell a reader to take a gummy for insomnia.

"Soothing Sleep" as a brand is criterion 4 (name). Pair it with a before-and-after sleepless face (piece 29) or a five-star review that says "my insomnia is gone," and context has supplied the disease. Pair it with directions that say "for occasional sleeplessness," a panel that does not name insomnia, and surfaces that do not either, and you are in the SECG's narrower room. The room is smaller than the aisle.

Melatonin gummies inherit the same claim rules plus serving-size math and the Cohen *JAMA* 2023 label-versus-assay scar (pieces 04 and 40). This piece will not write a pediatric sleep protocol. Children's serving columns and candy cues are a later file.

## Stress without a disorder

SECG criterion 2 says **"relieves stress and frustration"** might imply treatment of anxiety disorders — and also appears in non-disease states. If nothing on the page links those words to a disease, the SECG treats them as a possible structure/function shape. If a banner, a review, or a blog supplies the disorder, the same words are a disease claim.

**Forbidden examples** when the page is doing disorder work: naming anxiety disorder, panic, PTSD, or "works with your antidepressant." "Antidepressant" is on the SECG's forbidden class list (criterion 5). Criterion 6 forbids substituting for a disease therapy. Criterion 7 forbids augmenting a named disease therapy. A testimonial that says the capsule replaced a prescription is a drug claim you displayed (piece 29).

Criterion 3 (natural states) is the adjacent fence. FDA's acceptable examples include "mild mood changes, cramps, and edema associated with the menstrual cycle." FDA's **forbidden** example is "severe depression associated with the menstrual cycle." Quote that last sentence as forbidden. Do not write it as a women's-health banner. "Mild" and "associated with the menstrual cycle" are doing the same job "occasional" does for sleep. Escalate the adjective, and you have named a disease.

"Relieves crushing chest pain" is a criterion-1 / criterion-2 forbidden example from the same SECG page (angina). Stress copy that drifts into chest-pain or "heart racing" theater is how a calm-aisle SKU inherits a cardiovascular disease claim. Stop before the symptom list becomes diagnostic.

If you have substantiation for a smaller sentence — supports a calm mood in healthy adults, without a disorder name — you still need the disclaimer and the notice. You still lose the sentence if the Instagram tile uses a panic-attack caption. Context is the whole page (piece 24).

## Energy without a drug class

Energy is a crowded noun, not a license. Criterion 5 lists class names so strongly tied to disease treatment that claiming membership is a disease claim. SECG's forbidden classes: **analgesic, antibiotic, antidepressant, antimicrobial, antiseptic, antiviral, vaccine.** A shot-shaped bottle labeled "antiviral energy" has already failed twice (class plus criterion 8). "Natural vaccine" is the same failure with a pandemic costume (piece 30).

Some class words sit on both sides. Laxative, anti-inflammatory, and diuretic can remain structure/function if context makes the intended effect a non-disease function. SECG's example: "diuretic that relieves temporary water-weight gain." Energy SKUs that borrow "stimulant" as a drug-class swagger, or that compare themselves to a prescription for narcolepsy or ADHD, are doing criterion-5 and criterion-6 work. Do not write "replaces your [drug]." Do not write a stack that "works with your Adderall." Those are forbidden intended-use shapes. This piece will not tell a reader to take a supplement for a sleep disorder, a mood disorder, or an attention disorder.

Caffeine milligrams are 101.36 quantities when caffeine is a dietary ingredient you are supplementing. They are not a disease story. A "clinically proven energy" banner is the piece-28 adjective: whose trial, which dose, which SKU. FTC Example 10 in the 2022 guide (an energy drink whose daily ingredient can raise blood pressure) is a reminder that safety omissions are also claims. Do not invent a safety number. If you have a risk that reasonable buyers would need, the 2022 guide says disclose it.

## Stacks that narrate a diagnosis

A three-bottle "PM / calm / focus" kit becomes a diagnosis when the sell sheet writes the patient's day: can't sleep (insomnia), can't cope (anxiety disorder), can't start (depression or ADHD). The kit does not need those ICD words if the captions supply the signs. Criterion 2 does not require every sign. It requires a set that lets a buyer infer the disease.

Forbidden stack copy, taught as *no*: "for insomnia and racing thoughts"; "kills the virus so you can sleep"; "taper off your antidepressant"; "daytime narcolepsy stack." Infection and antiviral language is criterion 8 (piece 30). Drug-substitution language is criterion 6. Disease names are criterion 1. FDA's forbidden illustrations — "protective against the development of cancer," "reduces the pain and stiffness associated with arthritis," "relief of bronchospasm (asthma)" — are the same test in other aisles. Sleep-stress-energy does not get a softer test.

A bibliography on the carton that cites an insomnia trial is criterion 4 if placement makes the citation a treatment claim. A heart-rate graphic next to "calm" can be an EKG problem (criterion 4: tracings as implied heart-disease marks). A five-star widget that says "finally slept through my apnea" is a disease review you paid to display (piece 29).

If the formula card is three structure/function SKUs, keep three small sentences. Do not write a fourth sentence that stitches them into a syndrome.

## Keep the claim smaller than the aisle

The aisle will always be louder than 101.93(g). Retailers file sleep next to a disease the customer already has. Your job is to keep the SKU in the room FDA left open.

Smaller sleep: occasional sleeplessness, substantiated, disclaimed, notified. No insomnia name. No "difficulty falling asleep" as a chronic-disease wink. No "Soothing Sleep" without the occasional fence.

Smaller stress: unlinked to a disorder, no class name, no prescription comparison, no "severe depression" menstrual banner. FDA already wrote that last phrase as a disease claim.

Smaller energy: milligrams and a structure/function sentence about alertness or vitality in healthy people, if you have the evidence — not a stimulant-drug costume, not an antiviral, not a vaccine, not a substitute for a diagnosed sleep or mood disorder.

FTC will read the same words as advertising (piece 27). A testimonial that the gummy "cured my insomnia" is Example 39's problem. A before/after of sundown-to-sunrise is typicality plus a disease implication (piece 29). The 2000 rule did not need a pandemic to crowd these rooms. It needed product names, sign lists, and class words. Those are still the tests. If the only true sentence is the milligram line and "for occasional sleeplessness," that is the claim. The aisle can keep the rest of the vocabulary.
