Sesame became a major allergen on 1 January 2023. The FASTER Act of 2021 (Pub. L. 117-11, signed 23 April 2021) amended 21 U.S.C. § 321(qq)(1) by adding sesame to the list that already had milk, egg, fish, Crustacean shellfish, tree nuts, wheat, peanuts, and soybeans. The amendment applies to food introduced or delivered for introduction into interstate commerce on or after that January date. Dietary supplements are food for this purpose. A gummy that still treats sesame as optional spice-bin text is a 2022 label.

This is not a treatment piece. It is a reading of the other-ingredient list and the "Contains" line. Piece 17 is where that list starts. The disease story stays out.

## The major-allergen list

FALCPA (Pub. L. 108-282, 2004) put major-allergen labeling into section 403(w) of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (21 U.S.C. § 343(w)). The source of a major food allergen must be declared. The statute gives two ordinary homes for that declaration: the common or usual name of the ingredient already names the source, or a "Contains" statement sits immediately after or next to the ingredient list. Parenthetical source names inside the ingredient list are the other familiar pattern — "lecithin (soy)," "tahini paste (sesame)."

21 U.S.C. § 321(qq)(2) then folds in food ingredients that contain protein derived from a listed food, with a carved-out exception for highly refined oil derived from a listed food and ingredients derived from that oil. [VERIFY] the current statutory text and FDA's Edition 5 allergen Q&A before you decide a sesame oil is "highly refined" enough to stay off the allergen line. The exception is not a vibe. It is a fact about that oil.

FDA's consumer page on the FASTER Act lists the nine: milk; eggs; fish (bass, flounder, cod as examples); Crustacean shellfish (crab, lobster, shrimp as examples); tree nuts (almonds, walnuts, pecans as examples); peanuts; wheat; soybeans; sesame. Fish and Crustacean shellfish and tree nuts still need the specific source named. "Contains tree nuts" without the nut is the old incomplete line.

Dietary-supplement specifics live in FDA's allergen Q&A, Edition 5. Section E is the supplement chapter. If the major allergen is already declared inside Supplement Facts — a source in parentheses, or a dietary-ingredient name that is the source — FDA will generally treat 403(w) as satisfied without repeating the word under the box. If you add a "Contains" statement anyway, that statement must list the source of *all* major food allergens in the product, not a decorative subset. When an ingredient list is present, "Contains" goes immediately after or adjacent to that list (403(w)(1)(A)). When the label has no ingredient list, FDA will take a "Contains" printed outside and immediately after, or adjacent to, the Supplement Facts box.

Other ingredients — gelatin, flavors, colors, fillers, capsule shells — are 21 CFR 101.4 work. They are where allergens hide when the (b)(2) and (b)(3) rows look clean.

## Contains vs may contain

"Contains sesame" is a statutory allergen declaration for an allergen that is in the product as an ingredient. "May contain sesame" is a voluntary advisory statement about possible cross-contact. They are not synonyms. They are not a menu.

FDA's staff manual and the Edition 5 Q&A say an advisory statement such as "may contain [allergen]" is not a substitute for current good manufacturing practice or for allergen preventive controls. It must be truthful and not misleading under section 403(a)(1). You do not get to skip the process controls and write a hedge.

Two pairings FDA has already called inappropriate:

- A "Contains peanuts" statement next to "may contain peanuts." Edition 5, D.14: that pairing can mislead.
- A "milk-free" (or similar) claim next to "may contain milk." D.13: allergen-free claims must be true; the hedge fights the claim.

"Contains" plus a missing allergen is also a problem. If you use the statement, you use it completely. A "Contains soy" line on a yogurt-coated gummy that also has milk is an incomplete 403(w) statement, not a partial courtesy.

Cross-contact is a plant fact. Shared scoops, shared coating pans, shared flavor kettles. The COA pack is where environmental monitoring and Part 111 live. The label pack only needs this: do not use "may contain" to hide an ingredient you added on purpose. Sesame oil in the flavor system is an ingredient. Declare it.

## Facility statements

"Produced in a facility that also processes peanuts" is another advisory shape. FDA's Sec. 555.250 lists it next to "may contain" as a voluntary allergen-advisory example. It is not a "Contains." It does not satisfy 403(w) for sesame you put in the formula. It does not make the product peanut-free. It does not make the product peanut-containing.

3PLs like facility statements because they feel like insurance. Sometimes they are the only honest sentence you have: the contract packager runs peanut protein in the same building and will not give you a tighter guarantee. Write that sentence only if it is true, and do not let it replace the statutory line for allergens that are actually in the SKU.

"Allergen-free" and "sesame-free" are still 403(a)(1) claims. Other than "gluten-free" in 21 CFR 101.91, FDA has not written a numerical rule for those adjectives. If you print "sesame-free," FDA expects no sesame allergen in the product. A facility statement about sesame on the same bottle would fight that claim.

Empty seats stay empty. Do not invent a "nut-free facility in [city]" you do not operate.

## Gummies and flavors

Gummies are where the other-ingredient list earns its keep. The Supplement Facts rows can look like melatonin, vitamin C, or a botanical. The formula card underneath is sugar, glucose syrup, pectin or gelatin, citric acid, colors, and a flavor system. Sesame enters as oil, as tahini, as a "natural flavor" whose documentation names sesame, or as a spice blend. FDA's own FASTER Act examples include "spices (sesame, wheat)" and "tahini paste (sesame, canola oil)."

Read the flavor house's allergen letter, not the marketing name of the flavor. "Birthday cake," "tahini vanilla," and "everything-bagel" are not 101.4 names. The letter is. [VERIFY] that letter against the lot you are about to fill; flavor houses reformulate.

Soy lecithin in a coating, milk in a yogurt drizzle, wheat in a starch, egg-white glaze on an older candy-adjacent line — those are FALCPA work even when the dietary ingredient is a vitamin. Gelatin is not on the major-allergen list; customers still ask. Answer with the source (bovine, porcine, fish) on the information you control, and do not pretend fish gelatin is exempt from the fish line if it is fish.

FDA warned after FASTER that some manufacturers added small amounts of sesame so they could use a "Contains sesame" line rather than control cross-contact. That is a recipe change, not a labeling trick you can ignore on a reorder. The bottle you printed in 2022 can be a different food in 2024. Read the current other-ingredient list.

## Why a 3PL cares

A third-party logistics warehouse is where retailer intake, Amazon prep, and returns collide. A missing "Contains" is a chargeback and a recall-shaped conversation. An advisory statement that contradicts a "free" claim is a listing suppression waiting for a complaint. A kids' gummy with undeclared sesame is the file you do not want to explain.

Retailer portals often ask for a separate allergen grid. That grid is not the label. The label still has to be right. If the grid says sesame-free and the flavor letter says sesame, you have a dispute, not a shipment (piece 42).

Amazon bullets that say "allergy friendly" or "school safe" are labeling. They are not a substitute for 403(w), and they are an FTC claim besides. Do not write them.

The reading habit is the same walk as piece 01, with one extra stop. Title of the box. Serving. Rows. Hairline. Other ingredients. Then the "Contains" line, then the advisory line if any. If sesame is in the flavor and missing from both, stop. The rest of the carton is decorating an allergen you have not named.
