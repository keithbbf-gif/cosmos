"Fresh dandelion root extract, x (y:z) in 70% ethanol" is FDA's own pattern. 21 CFR 101.36(b)(3)(ii)(B) writes that sentence with the variables still in it. *x* is milliliters or milligrams of the entire extract. *y* is the weight of the starting material. *z* is the volume of solvent in milliliters. The 2005 Dietary Supplement Labeling Guide, Chapter IV, question 4-31, repeats the pattern in plain language.

A 10:1 sticker on the principal display panel is not that sentence. 10:1 is a concentration claim about how the extract was made. It is not "ten times the herb in your body," and it is not a license to print the starting-material weight as if it were the milligrams in the capsule.

## Liquid extracts

For a liquid extract from which the solvent has not been removed, 101.36(b)(3)(ii)(B) wants the volume or weight of the **total extract**. If the starting material was fresh, you must say so. If it was dried, you may say so. You may add the concentration and the solvent.

That is why the dandelion line carries three numbers and a percentage. The buyer can see how much extract is in the serving, what went in, and what it was extracted with. A dropper bottle that lists only "dandelion 1,000 mg" has not said whether 1,000 mg is the tincture, the fresh root, or a wish.

Partial removal of solvent has its own arithmetic in the same paragraph. If the original extract was 1:5 and you remove 50% of the solvent, and you choose to state a final concentration, the rule's own example says you state 1:2.5. Do not keep printing 1:5 after you have boiled the extract down. The ratio follows the liquid you actually filled.

The solvent has to appear somewhere a buyer can find it. If it is not in the nutrition label, it is required in the ingredient statement (101.36(b)(3)(ii)(B), last sentence). "In 70% ethanol" inside the box satisfies that. A glycerin tincture that never names glycerin in the box or under it does not.

Alcohol, glycerin, and water are not interchangeable decorations. They change what dissolved. They also change what a retailer, a school, or a workplace will allow through the door. Put the solvent on the label you actually ship.

## Dried extracts

When the solvent has been removed, 101.36(b)(3)(ii)(C) and DSLG 4-32 want the weight of the **dried extract**. Not the weight of the plant you started with. Not the volume of the tea you evaporated. The powder in the capsule, after the solvent is gone.

A 250 mg dried extract is 250 mg of that dried extract. If the supplier's certificate says the extract was produced at 10:1 from dried leaf, that ratio is manufacturing history. It may be true. It does not let you print "2,500 mg herb equivalent" as the (b)(3) amount. The rule's amount is the dried extract.

You can still tell a true smaller sentence in labeling that is not the amount column: that the extract was made at a stated ratio from a stated plant part, if that sentence is accurate for the lot. The amount column stays on the dried weight. Mixing the two numbers — putting the "equivalent" in the milligram column and the extract weight in 6-point type — is how a 250 mg capsule becomes a 2,500 mg headline.

Native extract versus extract-plus-carrier is a COA problem (companion pack). On this panel, if the 250 mg includes maltodextrin that was standardized into the powder, you are declaring a mixture as if it were the botanical. Say what the 250 mg is. If the carrier is an other ingredient, it belongs under the box (piece 17), not inside the herb's milligrams.

## Indented constituents

101.36(b)(3)(iii) and DSLG 4-33 let you list constituents indented under the dietary ingredient, each with a quantitative amount by weight per serving. Column or linear display. The parent ingredient still has its own amount.

The usual picture is a botanical extract with a marked compound underneath: the extract weight on the parent line, then an indented "with X mg of [constituent]." That indented number is a fraction of the extract, or a measured constituent in the serving. It is not a second serving of the herb.

(b)(2) dietary ingredients do not get to hide as indented constituents. If the constituent is vitamin C, or calcium, or any other RDI/DRV nutrient, you declare it up in the (b)(2) block under the rules for that nutrient (101.36(b)(3)(iii), exception clause). You do not indent ascorbic acid under a rose-hip line and skip the vitamin C row.

Once you list any constituent of a (b)(3) ingredient, the rule requires the other dietary ingredients to be declared in a column. The constituents themselves may stay column or linear. That is a format consequence, not a style choice. A label that starts indenting "withanolides" and then goes back to a linear dump of six herbs is not following (iii).

Standardized to 1.5% of a named constituent means the constituent milligrams should match the extract milligrams times that percentage, within the assay's honesty. A 500 mg extract "standardized to 5%" with an indented 50 mg is arithmetic you can check on the carton. A 500 mg extract "standardized to 5%" with no indented amount and a front panel that says "50 mg of actives" is doing the check in a headline instead of in the box.

## Ratio theater

10:1 is not 10× the herb in the body. It is a statement about input and output at the extractor: ten parts of starting material, one part of extract, under whatever solvent and process the supplier actually used. Two 10:1 extracts of the same common name can be different articles if the plant part, the solvent, or the starting condition differ.

A 4:1 water extract and a 4:1 70% ethanol extract are not interchangeable SKUs. The ratio matches. The dissolved fraction does not. FDA's dandelion example puts the solvent in the same breath as the ratio for that reason.

"Herb equivalent" math on a cart page multiplies the dried-extract weight by the ratio and sells the product. Sometimes the multiplication is the supplier's own starting-material figure. Sometimes it is a marketing round number. 101.36 does not define "herb equivalent" as a panel heading. If you use the phrase at all, it has to be true, and it cannot replace the required amount.

Fresh versus dried starting material changes the ratio's meaning. Fresh root is mostly water. A fresh 1:1 tincture and a dried 1:1 extract are not the same plant-solids story. The rule makes you mark fresh when the starting material was fresh. That word is doing quantitative work.

A blend of three extracts, each with its own ratio, rolled into one "20:1 complex" is theater. List each extract under (b)(3) or inside a 101.36(c) blend (piece 16), with the amount the rule actually asks for. Do not average ratios.

## Solvent belongs on the label or the ingredient list

101.36(b)(3)(ii)(B) is explicit: if the solvent is not named in the nutrition label, it is required in the ingredient statement. DSLG 4-31 says the same thing in the Q&A voice.

Ethanol, alcohol, grain alcohol, glycerin, glycerol, water, acetic acid, hexane residuals you should not be shipping — name the solvent you used. A "alcohol-free" banner with glycerin in the other-ingredient list is at least a consistent pair. An "alcohol-free" banner with ethanol still in the extract and no solvent line is a fight between the principal display panel and 101.36.

Residual solvent in a dried extract is a specification, not a Supplement Facts row. If a solvent was removed to dryness, (ii)(C) has you list the dried weight; the solvent is no longer the amount. If a solvent remains because this is still a liquid extract, you are in (ii)(B). Pick the paragraph that matches the article in the bottle.

Plant part still has to be on the botanical (piece 18). "Dandelion extract" without "root" or "leaf" fails 101.4(h) even if the ratio and the solvent are perfect. FDA's own example is "fresh dandelion **root** extract." The part of the plant is not optional color.

Read an extract row in this order: common name, plant part, fresh or dried if required, amount of extract (volume or dry weight), optional ratio with the variables defined, solvent in the box or under it, optional indented constituent with its own milligrams. If any of those is a headline on the front and a blank in the box, the box is the document that has to be right. The ratio is allowed. It is not the product.
