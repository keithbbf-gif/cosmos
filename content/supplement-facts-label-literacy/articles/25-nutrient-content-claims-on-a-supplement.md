**Disclaimer.** Educational only. Not medical advice. Dietary supplements are not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease (DSHEA; 21 U.S.C. § 321(ff); 21 CFR 101.93). This is not legal advice and not a labeling opinion for a specific SKU.

20 percent of Daily Value is a defined cut, not a compliment. 21 CFR 101.54(b) lets a label say "high," "rich in," or "excellent source of" a nutrient when the food — including a dietary supplement — contains 20 percent or more of that nutrient's RDI or DRV per reference amount customarily consumed. 10 to 19 percent is "good source," "contains," or "provides" (101.54(c)). Those percentages attach to nutrients that have Daily Values. They do not attach to ashwagandha, to a fanciful blend, or to a (b)(3) row with an asterisk.

A lot of principal display panels treat "excellent source" as atmosphere. It is a nutrient-content claim (NCC). 101.13(a) applies the NCC rules to foods and to dietary supplements. Piece 06 is the %DV arithmetic. This piece is what happens when those percentages leave the box.

## Which words are nutrient-content claims

101.13(b) is the definition. An express NCC is a direct statement about the level or range of a nutrient: "low sodium," "contains 100 calories," "excellent source of vitamin C." An implied NCC describes the food or an ingredient in a way that suggests a nutrient is absent or present in a certain amount ("high in oat bran"), or suggests the food is useful in maintaining healthy dietary practices because of its nutrient content ("healthy").

Information that 101.9 or 101.36 requires or permits *inside* the nutrition label is not, by itself, an NCC (101.13(c)). Repeat the same number on the front of the bottle and it is. "Vitamin C 90 mg 100%" in the box is a quantity. "Excellent source of vitamin C" on the principal display panel is a claim with a 20 percent test, a type-size cap (no larger than twice the statement of identity, 101.13(f)), and, if fat, saturated fat, cholesterol, or sodium blows a disclosure level, a mandatory "See nutrition information for ____ content" line.

"Free," "low," "reduced," "light," "sugar free," and "calorie free" are NCC vocabulary too (101.60–101.62). A gummy that wants "sugar free" has to meet 101.60, not a mood. [VERIFY] current 101.60 numeric cuts against the eCFR before a printer file.

101.13(b)(5) fences calorie, fat, saturated-fat, and cholesterol claims on supplements that already meet "calorie free" or "low calorie" — most capsules will — unless you compare to a similar supplement that normally exceeds "low calorie." You do not get to dress two capsules as a diet food because capsules are small.

## High / good / more

**High / excellent source / rich in.** 20 percent or more of the RDI or DRV per reference amount (101.54(b)(1)). For a dietary supplement, the reference amount is the maximum recommended per eating occasion (101.12(b) Table 2; piece 03). If the directions say 1–3 tablets, the serving is 3, and the 20 percent test runs on 3.

**Good source / contains / provides.** 10 to 19 percent (101.54(c)(1)). "Contains" in this defined sense is a level claim, not a synonym for "this bottle has some."

**More / fortified / enriched / added / extra / plus.** Relative claims (101.54(e); 101.13(j)). The labeled food must have at least 10 percent more of the DV for protein, vitamins, minerals, fiber, or potassium than an appropriate reference food. For "more" and "less," the reference can be a dissimilar substitute in the same dietary slot — 101.13(j)(1)(i)(A) uses "orange juice as a reference for vitamin C tablets." For "fortified," "enriched," "added," "extra," and "plus," the reference must be a similar food: one multi against another multi.

**High potency.** 101.54(f). Single vitamin or mineral: 100 percent or more of the RDI per reference amount. Multi: 100 percent or more of the RDI for at least two-thirds of the vitamins and minerals listed in 101.9(c)(8)(iv) that are present at 2 percent or more of the RDI. That is a math test, not a clinical-strength costume (piece 35).

Percentages of vitamins and minerals against an RDI may appear without a nutrient-specific authorizing regulation (101.13(q)(3)(i)). "100% DV vitamin C" on the front is still an NCC once it leaves the box, and it still has to be true for the labeled serving.

## What cannot wear those words

A (b)(3) other dietary ingredient has no Daily Value. 101.36(b)(3) puts an asterisk and "Daily Value not established" on that row (piece 07). There is no 20 percent of a number that does not exist. "Excellent source of ashwagandha" is not a 101.54 claim. "Excellent source of our focus blend" is the same error.

What you *can* do for a no-DV dietary ingredient is a percentage characterization under 101.13(q)(3)(ii) and 21 U.S.C. § 343(r)(2)(F). Two shapes:

1. **Simple percentage.** State the actual amount per serving next to the percentage. The rule's example: "40 percent omega-3 fatty acids, 10 mg per capsule."
2. **Comparative percentage.** Identify the reference food, the amount of that food, and the actual amounts in both. The rule's example: "twice the omega-3 fatty acids per capsule (80 mg) as in 100 mg of menhaden oil (40 mg)."

Those sentences are still claims. They still take any 101.13(h) disclosure. They do not import "excellent source," "high," or "good source." Those words are reserved for RDI/DRV nutrients that clear 101.54.

A proprietary blend is a total weight (101.36(c); piece 16), not a nutrient with a Daily Value. "Excellent source of blend" is costume. "Excellent source of immune support" is not a 101.54 sentence. If the vitamin C row is 20 percent DV, the legal NCC is about vitamin C. The rest is structure/function — or a disease claim if it names infection (piece 24, criterion 8).

## Implied NCC ("contains as much C as an orange")

101.13(b)(2) and 101.65 cover implied claims. "High in oat bran" implies fiber. "Contains as much vitamin C as an orange" characterizes the level of vitamin C by pointing at a food people already rank. The numbers have to be true for the labeled serving and the named reference, and the claim still has to sit inside 101.13 — type size, disclosure, no false "low" implication.

"Healthy" is an implied NCC when it is about nutrient content and dietary practice (101.13(b)(2)(ii)). It has its own definitional file. Do not drop it on a high-sodium gummy as flavor.

Equivalence copy that is really a disease story is not saved by 101.54. "Contains as much C as an orange, so you can skip the cold" is a criterion-1 problem (piece 24). The orange comparison, if true and fenced to the nutrient, is an NCC. The cold is forbidden disease talk.

A brand name can be an implied NCC. 101.13(q)(7) says implied NCCs in a brand name need FDA authorization (petitions: 101.69(o)).

## Disclosure statements

101.13(h)(1) is the disclosure most operators skip. If you make an NCC, and the food — except meals, main dishes, and foods for children under two — contains more than 13.0 g fat, 4.0 g saturated fat, 60 mg cholesterol, or 480 mg sodium per reference amount, per labeled serving, or (for very small RACCs) per 50 g, you must say adjacent to the claim: "See nutrition information for ____ content," with the blank filled by the nutrient that blew the level.

A protein powder with an "excellent source of protein" banner and sodium above 480 mg per serving is the usual hit. The disclosure is not optional because the SKU is a supplement. 101.13(n) also requires nutrition labeling — 101.36 here — on any food that bears an NCC.

Placement: immediately adjacent, no intervening material except other required claim text. If the claim repeats on more than one panel, the disclosure repeats, except on the panel that already carries the nutrition information. Multiple claims on one panel may share one disclosure next to the largest claim (101.13(h)(4)).

Fiber has an extra line. If you claim high, good source, or more fiber, and the food is not "low" in total fat, disclose fat per labeled serving in immediate proximity, in type at least half the claim size, *before* any 101.13(h) line (101.54(d)).

A statement of amount that does not characterize the level — "100 calories," "5 grams of fat" — needs no "not a low-fat food" disclaimer (101.13(i)(3)). A statement that *does* characterize the level and misses the definition needs an adjacent disclaimer: "only 200 mg sodium per serving, not a low sodium food" (101.13(i)(2)). Relative claims disclose the reference food and the difference (101.13(j)(2)).

The 2005 Dietary Supplement Labeling Guide still hosts Q&A on these claim chapters. Recheck current 101.13 / 101.54 / 101.9 Daily Values before you print; the *cuts* (20 percent, 10 percent, disclosure grams) are the rule, and the DV *denominators* moved in 2016 (piece 09). If the word on the banner needs a Daily Value and the row has an asterisk, stop.
