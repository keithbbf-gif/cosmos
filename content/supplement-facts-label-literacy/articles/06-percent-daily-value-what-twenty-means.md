60 mg of vitamin C is 100% DV in the example FDA still hosts, because the adult Daily Value in that 2005 walkthrough is 60 mg. Chapter IV, question 4-24: divide the quantitative amount by the established Daily Value and multiply by 100. Confirm the current adult DVs before you print [VERIFY current 101.9 RDI table]. The 27 May 2016 final rule (81 FR 33742) moved several of those denominators. Vitamin C for adults and children 4 years and older now sits at 90 mg in 21 CFR 101.9(c)(8)(iv) [VERIFY]. 90 mg is 100% on that table. 60 mg is 67%, nearest whole percent. A printer file that still treats 60 mg as 100% is speaking 2005 in a 2016 denominator.

%DV is a ratio. It is not a grade, not a clinical result, and not a permission to call a botanical an "excellent source." 20% is a nutrient-content threshold on foods that have Daily Values (21 CFR 101.54). It is not a halo you may slide onto ashwagandha.

## The division

Chapter IV, 4-22: the %DV is the percent of the Daily Value — RDI or DRV — of a dietary ingredient that is in a serving of the product. 4-23: you must declare it for dietary ingredients that have Daily Values, with listed exceptions. Protein may omit the percent. On products for infants, children less than 4 years, or pregnant or lactating women, you must not list percents for a specified set of nutrients (total fat, saturated fat, cholesterol, total carbohydrate, dietary fiber, vitamin K, selenium, manganese, chromium, molybdenum, chloride, sodium, potassium). Piece 08 and piece 40 pick up those columns.

4-24 is the arithmetic. Use the **unrounded** quantitative amount, except that for total fat, saturated fat, cholesterol, sodium, potassium, total carbohydrate, and dietary fiber you may use the rounded amount on the label. Protein, if you show a %, has its own 101.9(c)(7)(iii) method. Everyone else: amount ÷ Daily Value × 100.

The Daily Value is the number in 101.9(c)(8)(iv) or (c)(9) for the population the column is for. Adults and children ≥4 years is the default column on a general-population bottle. Infants, children 1–3, and pregnant or lactating women have their own RDI columns in the same table. Four sets. Identify the one you used (4-27).

If the current adult vitamin C RDI is 90 mg [VERIFY], then 45 mg is 50% and 90 mg is 100%. A 2005 slide that still divides by 60 will print the wrong percent. Vitamin D's 2016 adult RDI is 20 mcg; 25 mcg is 125% (FDA's conversion-guidance example). Folate's adult RDI is 400 mcg DFE; 400 mcg folic acid is 680 mcg DFE and 170% (piece 11). Recheck 101.36(b)(2)(iii) and 101.9(c)(8)–(9) before a print lock.

## Rounding and "<1%"

4-25: express the percentage to the nearest whole percent. When the amount is large enough to list but the %DV would round to zero, you must print "Less than 1%" or "<1%." The guide's carbohydrate example: 1 g of total carbohydrate lists as "<1%" or "Less than 1%," not 0%.

4-26: a product with 5 mg of potassium against a 3,500 mg Daily Value in the old example calculates to 0.14% and would round to zero. You still declare "<1%" or "Less than 1%." A label that printed 5 mg and 0% would tell a buyer the potassium is nothing. The amount line already said it was something. [VERIFY the current potassium DRV in 101.9(c)(9) before you reuse 3,500 mg; the 2016 rule moved potassium.]

Nearest whole percent is ordinary rounding, not a brand voice. 12.4% is 12%. 12.5% is 13%. Do not "round toward a nicer shelf" from 19% to 20% so you can say "excellent source." 20% is a different rule (101.54). If the unrounded math is 19.4, the panel is 19.

You may not invent "trace," "nil," or a dash in the %DV column for a (b)(2) nutrient you chose to list. Other dietary ingredients get a symbol and "Daily Value not established" (piece 07).

## Why you cannot list a 1% RDI vitamin as 0%

The note under 4-26 is the trap. The "<1%" instruction does not apply to dietary ingredients that have RDIs, because those ingredients may not be listed when they are present at less than 2% of the RDI (101.36(b)(2)(i)). If you do not have 2% of the RDI, you omit the row — unless you added the vitamin for supplementation or you made a claim about it, in which case you are in a different sentence of (b)(2)(i) and you still do not print 0%.

The 0% vitamin row on some multis is a food habit. Nutrition Facts prints zeros. Supplement Facts omits the nutrient that is not there, and does not list an RDI vitamin under 2% unless a claim or an addition-for-supplementation duty pulled it on — in which case you declare a real amount and a real percent. If your spreadsheet produced 1% of an RDI, you either omit the row or you have a formula problem. Do not print zero.

## More than one column

4-27: you may show more than one %DV column. FDA has established four sets of Daily Values for many nutrients. Appendix B of the 2005 guide (adults and children ≥4) and Appendix C (infants; children under 4; pregnant and lactating women) are the teaching pictures. Current values live in 101.9(c)(8)(iv). When you show more than one column, you must clearly identify each one. "Percent Daily Value for Children Under 4 Years of Age" is a heading, not a color.

101.36(e)(10)(ii) is the sample with adult and child columns (piece 08). You do not pick the smallest denominator so the percent looks huge. A prenatal that uses the adult 400 mcg DFE denominator when the pregnant-woman RDI is 600 mcg DFE [VERIFY 101.9(c)(8)(iv)] is using the wrong table. A "kids %DV" that also quietly uses a half serving is two lies stacked (piece 40).

## %DV is not a clinical result

A row that says "Vitamin D 25 mcg 125%" is a quantity and a ratio. It is not a serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D. It is not a trial. It is not "clinically studied" (piece 28). It is not a structure/function claim. The sentence next to the cart that says what vitamin D *does* is 101.93 work (pieces 21–23). The row does not do that work for you.

Do not convert 100% into "complete." Do not convert 200% into "twice as effective." Do not convert "<1%" into "practically zero, so we left it out of the story." The percent is a labeled fraction of a reference amount FDA published for a population. It is not a health outcome.

ODS fact sheets discuss adequacy and ULs. Those pages are not a %DV, and they are not a disease claim (piece 10, piece 12). Do not write "125% DV means you are 25% past deficient." FTC will read the percent the way a reasonable buyer will read it (December 2022 *Health Products Compliance Guidance*; piece 27). A banner that circles "2000% DV" is not saved by correct division.

## Nutrient-content claims live next door

21 CFR 101.54 is where 20% becomes a word. "High," "rich in," and "excellent source of" attach to a nutrient that has a Daily Value when the food (and, with care, the supplement) provides 20% or more of that DV per the applicable reference amount. "Good source," "contains," and "provides" sit at 10–19%. Those are nutrient-content claims (101.13). They have disclosure rules. They do not attach to a (b)(3) ingredient that has no DV.

You may not call a blend an "excellent source of ashwagandha." There is no botanical Daily Value. You may not slide "excellent source" onto a 20% vitamin row and let the PDP talk as if the 20% belonged to the herb.

Piece 25 is the nutrient-content file. The only point here is the smuggle. 20% on a (b)(2) row is a ratio you calculated from 101.9. 20% as an "excellent source" sentence is a claim you have to qualify under 101.54. They can sit on the same bottle. They are not the same act. A 19% row that you rounded to 20% so the claim would attach is a rounding violation and a claim violation.

"More," "added," "fortified," and implied comparisons ("contains as much C as an orange") are also 101.13 / 101.54 work. They use %DV as an input. They do not turn the %DV column into advertising copy. Keep the column a column. Put the adjective through the claim rule, or leave the adjective off. 20% is a number on that panel. It is a defined claim only when 101.54 says so, and never for an ingredient that has no Daily Value.
