**Disclaimer.** Educational only. Not medical advice. Dietary supplements are not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease (DSHEA; 21 U.S.C. § 321(ff); 21 CFR 101.93). This is not legal advice and not a labeling opinion for a specific SKU.

The title of the box is not a font choice. 21 CFR 101.36(b)(1)(i) calls the nutrition label for a dietary supplement a **Supplement Facts** panel. 21 CFR 101.9 calls the food box **Nutrition Facts**. FDA's Dietary Supplement Labeling Guide, Chapter IV (April 2005, still the Q&A the agency hosts) opens on that split. If a powder drink uses the wrong title, you are not looking at a style error. You are looking at a category error.

A lot of catalogs treat the two boxes as cousins. They are not. One is built for a serving of food that is mostly water, starch, and fat. The other is built for a serving that may be two capsules of a botanical with no Daily Value.

## Five differences the 2005 guide still states

Chapter IV, question 4-2, is the short list. Recheck current 101.36 before you print; the *units* moved in 2016. The *shape* of the split did not.

1. **You must list dietary ingredients that have no RDI or DRV** on a Supplement Facts panel. You may not invent a home for those ingredients on a Nutrition Facts panel. Phosphatidylserine, ashwagandha root extract, and a 400 mg "focus blend" live in 101.36(b)(3) or they do not live on the nutrition label at all.
2. **You may name the source of a dietary ingredient** in the Supplement Facts panel ("Calcium (as calcium citrate)"). Nutrition Facts does not do that work.
3. **If the source is already in the box, you do not have to repeat it** in the ingredient statement below. That is 21 CFR 101.4(h) plus 101.36, not a designer shortcut.
4. **You must name the part of the plant** when the dietary ingredient is a botanical. Nutrition Facts will not take "root" as a nutrient row.
5. **You do not print "0"** for nutrients that are not there. Nutrition Facts requires zeros. Supplement Facts omits the row. A multi that lists "Total Fat 0 g" is talking like a granola bar.

Those five are how you tell, in ten seconds, whether the person who drew the label read 101.36 or copied a yogurt cup.

## What must be in the box

101.36(b) wants names and quantities of dietary ingredients, **Serving Size**, and **Servings Per Container**. The servings line drops out when it merely restates the net-contents line: 100 tablets, serving size 1 tablet, and you may skip "Servings Per Container 100" (Chapter IV, 4-3). That is a space rule, not a permission to hide how many days the bottle lasts when the serving is 3.

The (b)(2) block — dietary ingredients that have Daily Values — comes first, in food order, vitamins and minerals grouped. Then a heavy rule. Then (b)(3) "other dietary ingredients," common or usual name, weight per serving, and a symbol that points at **Daily Value not established**. Then, *under* the box, the other-ingredient statement: cellulose, magnesium stearate, the flavor system. Those are not dietary ingredients. A magnesium salt used as a *binder* belongs under the box, not as a magnesium %DV row, unless you are actually supplementing magnesium (Chapter IV, 4-10; 21 CFR 101.4(g)).

If you remember one walk: title, serving, (b)(2), hairline, (b)(3), other ingredients. Piece 02 is that walk in slow motion.

## The 2005 guide is not the 2026 printer file

Chapter IV still talks about calories from fat, vitamin A and vitamin C as the old mandatory pair, and iron/calcium as the old food pair. The 27 May 2016 final rule (81 FR 33742) rebuilt Nutrition Facts. Supplement Facts followed the unit changes: vitamin D in mcg, folate as mcg DFE, vitamin A as mcg RAE, vitamin E in mg. Compliance staggered into 2020–2021. A label that still speaks only in IU for D3 is speaking 2015.

So: use the 2005 guide for *how the box is assembled*. Use current 101.9 / 101.36 for *which units and which mandatory food-style rows* apply. If a contractor hands you a 2005 sample label as camera-ready art, that is a history handout (piece 09).

## When a product uses the other box

A conventional food that happens to contain a vitamin is still a food. A dietary supplement that happens to be a gummy is still a supplement if that is how it is sold and labeled. The statement of identity, the Supplement Facts title, and the intended-use language have to agree. A "protein powder" that uses Nutrition Facts, lists a scoop as a food serving, and also prints structure/function disease-adjacent copy is trying to occupy two chairs. FDA will pick one.

Drinks and bars are where this fight usually starts. If the SKU is a conventional food, 101.9 is the box and you do not get a (b)(3) botanical row. If the SKU is a dietary supplement, 101.36 is the box and you do not get to skip plant part, serving-as-max-dose, or the 100% rule for added dietary ingredients (101.9(g)(3)–(4), applied through 101.36(f)).

## What the box is not

It is not FDA approval. It is not a cGMP certificate. It is not a COA (that file is the companion pack). It is not a structure/function claim. A row that says "Vitamin C 90 mg 100%" is a quantity. The sentence next to the cart that says what vitamin C *does* is 101.93 work, with a disclaimer and a 30-day notice (pieces 21–23).

It is not permission to print "0 g sugar" as a health story. Nutrient-content claims have their own rule (piece 25). It is not permission to hide a blend. 101.36(c) lets you give a fanciful name and a *total* weight for other dietary ingredients in a proprietary blend. It does not let you skip the names, and it does not let you park RDI nutrients inside the blend without declaring them (piece 16).

## A reading habit that takes one minute

Do not start on the brand banner. Start on the title of the box. If it says Nutrition Facts, you are in 101.9. If it says Supplement Facts, you are in 101.36. Then serving size. Then whether the first rows are food macronutrients or vitamins. Then whether a hairline appears and unlabeled botanicals begin. Then the list under the box. Then, and only then, the claims on the principal display panel.

If the title and the claims disagree — food box, supplement sentences, or the reverse — stop. The rest of the panel is decorating a category you have not identified.

This pack stays on that side of the line. No disease story. No "this SKU treats." The box is how you count what is in a serving. The claim rules are how you are allowed to talk about what that serving is *for*. Those are different chapters. Mixing them is how a label becomes a letter.
