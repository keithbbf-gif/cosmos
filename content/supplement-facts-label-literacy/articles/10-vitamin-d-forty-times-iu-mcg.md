**Disclaimer.** Educational only. Not medical advice. Dietary supplements are not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease (DSHEA; 21 U.S.C. § 321(ff); 21 CFR 101.93). This is not legal advice and not a labeling opinion for a specific SKU.

40 is the conversion factor. It is not a serving. 1 mcg of cholecalciferol (vitamin D3) equals 40 IU. 1 IU equals 0.025 mcg. FDA's August 2019 conversion guidance says both, and the NIH Office of Dietary Supplements still writes the same factor on the Vitamin D Health Professional fact sheet. 25 mcg = 1,000 IU. 125 mcg = 5,000 IU. A spreadsheet that multiplies when it should divide — or divides when it should multiply — will print a panel that is off by forty. That is a recall-class error, not a rounding disagreement.

This is not an immune-disease story. A D row is a quantity. The 2016 rule (81 FR 33742) made mcg the panel unit. IU may follow in parentheses. ODS may still narrate IU for clinicians who trained on them. The box does not get to pick.

## The factor

FDA and ODS agree on the arithmetic. 1 mcg cholecalciferol = 40 IU vitamin D. Ergocalciferol (D2) uses the same IU-to-mcg factor for labeling; the conversion guidance treats D2 and D3 as bioequivalent for the unit change. Do not invent a second factor because a supplier's COA said "IU as D2." Convert, then assay the form you actually filled (the COA pack).

Work the two directions until they are muscle memory.

- mcg × 40 = IU
- IU × 0.025 = mcg
- IU ÷ 40 = mcg

1,000 IU = 25 mcg. 2,000 IU = 50 mcg. 5,000 IU = 125 mcg. 10,000 IU = 250 mcg. The 2016 adult RDI is 20 mcg (800 IU) [VERIFY 101.9(c)(8)(iv)]. FDA's own supplement example in the conversion guidance: 1,000 IU per serving → 25 mcg → 125% DV (25 ÷ 20 × 100).

The 40× error has two faces. Face one: the formula is 125 mcg and someone types 125 IU on the panel or the PDP. The buyer who still thinks in IU takes a fiftieth of what the formulator meant — or, if they invert it in their head, they panic about a 5,000 IU dose that is actually 125 IU. Face two: the formula is 5,000 IU and someone types 5,000 mcg. That panel is 200,000 IU. Forty times the intended fill if the plant follows the panel, or a 40× over-declaration if the plant follows the old formula card. Piece 42 is the three-document fight. Vitamin D is the line that makes that fight expensive.

A "125" with no unit is not a number you can ship. Piece 09's opening: the customer who bought 125 thinking IU received 5,000 IU if the panel was mcg. Write the unit. Then write the parenthetical if you want the old unit visible.

## Optional IU in parentheses

101.36(b)(2)(ii)(B) and 101.9(c)(8)(iv) footnote: the amount of vitamin D may, but is not required to, be expressed in IU in addition to the mandatory declaration in mcg. Any IU declaration must appear in parentheses after the mcg. FDA said why in the 2016 preamble (81 FR 33742 at 33912): D is a nutrient of public-health significance, the National Academy of Medicine still used IU, and a voluntary IU would help people keep using the unit they knew.

The order is specified. **25 mcg (1,000 IU)**, not **1,000 IU (25 mcg)**, as the legal pair on the panel. The mcg is first because the mcg is the unit of the RDI. A designer who puts IU first "because that is what customers search" has reordered a regulation. Search terms are piece 41. The panel is 101.36.

You may omit the parenthetical. A clean "Vitamin D 25 mcg 125%" is a complete (b)(2) row. You may not omit the mcg and keep the IU. That is a 2015 row.

Website tables and Amazon bullets are the same row (piece 41). If the bottle says 25 mcg (1,000 IU) and the bullet says "1000 IU D3" with no mcg, you have taught the aisle to ignore the legal unit. A buyer who writes "is this the 5000?" may mean 5,000 IU (125 mcg) or 5,000 mcg (200,000 IU). Read the panel: 125 mcg, which is 5,000 IU. Then stop. Do not add what that amount *does*.

## 5,000 IU as aisle talk

5,000 IU is how a large part of the U.S. aisle still speaks. It is 125 mcg. It is 625% of a 20 mcg adult DV [VERIFY the 20 mcg denominator]. That percent is a ratio (piece 06). It is not a protocol, not a seasonal story, and not a permission to print "high dose" as if 101.36 defined the phrase (piece 35).

ODS discusses recommended intakes and tolerable upper intake levels on the Health Professional fact sheet. Those pages are ODS, dated, and they change. Recheck the live sheet before you put an ODS number in a catalog footnote [VERIFY current ODS Vitamin D HP UL and RDA tables]. A UL is not a Supplement Facts row. A UL is not a disease claim. Quoting ODS as if it blessed *this SKU* is piece 28 in a lab coat.

If you sell a 125 mcg product and your PDP says "5000" without IU or mcg, you have omitted the unit that makes the aisle word true. Put 125 mcg on the panel. If you want the aisle word visible, put (5,000 IU) after it.

Children's products do not get a special factor. They get a different RDI column and a different serving (piece 08, piece 40). 1 mcg is still 40 IU.

## Assay vs label (pointer to COA pack)

The companion pack, `supplement-coa-manufacturing-literacy`, is where you read a COA: method, lab, lot, the form (D2 vs D3), and whether the result is in IU or mcg. This pack will not rewrite that file.

The pointer, for label people: the number on the COA and the number on the panel must be the same object after conversion. A COA that reports 1,000 IU per capsule and a panel that says 25 mcg per capsule are the same if serving size is 1 capsule. They are not the same if serving size is 2. They are not the same if the COA is per gram of powder and the panel is per scoop and no one multiplied by scoop weight.

101.36(f)(1) composites 12 packages or 10% of the lot (piece 05). Added vitamin D owes 100% of declared (101.9(g)(3)–(4)). "Naturally occurring" D in a mushroom powder you added as a (b)(3) ingredient is a different row. If you declared a (b)(2) D amount from that powder, you are on the 80% band only for the naturally occurring portion — and you still have to be able to show the math. Most D SKUs are added D3. Plan on 100% of the mcg on the panel, in the composite, in a method your lab actually ran.

If the COA unit and the panel unit differ and no one wrote the 0.025 or the 40 on the worksheet, you do not have a release. Convert, then compare. Overages keep the end-of-shelf-life composite at 100%. They are not a reason to declare 25 mcg and fill 10 mcg because "IU is approximate." The factor is exact for labeling. The assay has a method uncertainty. The COA pack owns the second sentence.

## Do not turn D rows into COVID copy

A row that says "Vitamin D 25 mcg (1,000 IU) 125%" is a quantity. It is not a structure/function claim. It is not a health claim. It is not a disease claim. The sentence that says what vitamin D *does* is 101.93 or 101.14 work, with the fences those chapters require (pieces 21–26).

COVID-era websites that tied vitamin D — and C, zinc, elderberry, silver — to a named virus were intended-use files. FDA treated products as drugs when the four corners said diagnose, treat, cure, mitigate, or prevent (21 U.S.C. § 321(g)(1)(B)). Piece 20 is how those letters quote a PDP and a Facebook caption. Piece 30 is immune language after those letters. This piece will not recommend D for that purpose, will not reprint those captions as tips, and will not dress a mcg row as a "protocol."

SECG (9 January 2002) already split "supports the immune system" from "supports the body's ability to resist infection." The second is a disease-claim example. The first still needs substantiation, a 30-day notice, and the disclaimer. A D row does not carry any of that. If the only sentence on the page is the row, you have a quantity. If the next sentence is a virus, you have a letter.

Do not use "D3" as a hashtag costume for a disease story. Do not write "clinically studied vitamin D" as if this SKU were the trial (piece 28). If a spreadsheet wants to multiply where it should divide, stop the print. Forty times is not a rounding error. It is the whole product.
