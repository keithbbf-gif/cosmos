If magnesium stearate is in the %DV column, someone confused a flow agent with a mineral supplement. The 2005 Dietary Supplement Labeling Guide, Chapter IV, question 4-10, puts a magnesium salt used as a binder in the ingredient statement below the Supplement Facts panel. The cite is 21 CFR 101.4(g). Binders, excipients, and fillers are not dietary ingredients. They do not earn a row inside the box.

The list under the box is where the capsule, the color, the flavor system, and the anti-caking agent live. It is also where an allergen usually announces itself. Piece 37 is the allergen statute. This piece is why that statute starts here and not in the %DV column.

## Dietary ingredient vs other ingredient

101.36 is the nutrition label for a dietary supplement. It names dietary ingredients and their amounts per serving. 101.4 is the ingredient-statement rule for food, including dietary supplements. 101.4(g) is the dietary-supplement paragraph.

When a source is already identified inside the box — "Calcium (as calcium carbonate)" — you do not have to repeat that source under the box (101.36(d); 101.4(g)). Everything else that is in the serving and is not a declared dietary ingredient goes in the list. 101.4(g) names the usual suspects: excipients, fillers, artificial colors, artificial sweeteners, flavors, binders.

The heading of that list depends on what you already disclosed. If no sources sit in the box, the list is preceded by "Ingredients." If some ingredients (the sources) are already inside the nutrition label, the list under it is preceded by "Other ingredients." That "Other" is a legal word. It means "the rest," not "unimportant."

Vegetable cellulose is the usual costume. A hypromellose capsule is a shell. It is an other ingredient. It is not a (b)(3) "vegetable cellulose 80 mg" dietary-ingredient row unless you are actually offering cellulose as the dietary ingredient and can defend that declaration. A fiber row, if you have one, is a (b)(2) carbohydrate story with its own rules. Do not smuggle a capsule into the fiber line to make the serving look like food.

Magnesium stearate, silicon dioxide, stearic acid, dicalcium phosphate used as a tablet base, maltodextrin used as a flow aid, rice flour used to fill a capsule: other ingredients, unless a specific amount is being offered as a dietary ingredient and declared as such. Intent and the formula card decide. Shared chemical names do not.

## Order under the box

101.4(a) lists ingredients by common or usual name in descending order of predominance by weight. The dietary-supplement twist is that ingredients already declared in the nutrition label need not be repeated. The remaining list is still a predominance list of *those remaining ingredients*.

The heaviest other ingredient goes first. On a two-piece capsule, that is often the shell, or a filler that outweighs the shell. Magnesium stearate is usually a small fraction and sits late. A list that opens with magnesium stearate and buries "gelatin" at the end is probably out of order. Weigh the batch record.

Water, in a liquid, is often the first other ingredient because it is most of the serving. That can look embarrassing next to a "concentrated" banner. It is still the correct first word if water predominates.

101.4(g) places the list immediately below the nutrition label, or, if there is no room, immediately contiguous and to the right. It is not a footer on the website and it is not a PDF attached to the PO. A cart page that reprints Supplement Facts and omits the other-ingredient statement has omitted 101.4.

Incidental additives that meet 101.100's exemptions are a food-labeling rabbit hole. Do not assume a processing aid is exempt because the contract manufacturer always skips it. If it is in the capsule in an amount that 101.4 requires you to declare, declare it. When in doubt, the honest list is longer, not shorter.

## Capsule and coating

The shell is an ingredient. Gelatin, hypromellose (often sold as "vegetable cellulose" or "HPMC"), pullulan, and softgel gelatin-plus-glycerin systems all belong under the box unless you have a specific, defensible reason to treat a component as a dietary ingredient.

Glycerin in a softgel is often both a plasticizer and a residual from the fill. If it is in the shell, it is an other ingredient. If it is the solvent of a liquid extract, it also has a 101.36(b)(3)(ii)(B) job (piece 15). One substance can appear in two legal places because it is doing two jobs. Do not pick the more flattering one.

Coatings — shellac, carnauba wax, hydroxypropyl cellulose, titanium dioxide in a film coat — are other ingredients. "Enteric coated" on the principal display panel does not move the coating into the (b)(3) block. It is still a processing and other-ingredient fact.

Color in the capsule wall is still color. A "natural" hypromellose cap colored with titanium dioxide or an FD&C dye is not an undyed capsule. Name the color. A clear capsule with a dyed powder fill puts the color in the fill, not the shell. The list still has to tell the truth about which article is dyed.

Empty capsules bought as a commodity still have a composition. If the supplier's spec is hypromellose, gellan gum, and titanium dioxide, those words belong on your bottle if they are in your bottle. "Vegetable capsule" is not a USP monograph you can stop at.

## Colors and flavors

Artificial colors, artificial sweeteners, and flavors are in 101.4(g)'s own list. Certified colors have their own declaration conventions under 101.22. "Color added," "artificial flavor," and the specific FD&C name are not interchangeable. Recheck 101.22 for the SKU's actual color system before you print [VERIFY the current color-declaration sentence for that dye].

"Natural flavor" is a defined term in 101.22, not a vibe. A gummy "natural berry flavor" can hide juice concentrates, oils, and carriers. Those carriers are how major allergens arrive in a product that looks like a vitamin (piece 37). The flavor line is where sesame oil, milk fractions, and tree-nut oils have shown up in other food categories. Read the flavor house's breakdown. Do not take "natural flavor" as a blank.

Citric acid, malic acid, and sodium citrate are often acidulants in gummies and liquids. They are other ingredients when they are there to make the product taste or gel, not when you are offering citrate as a mineral source. If they are the source of a declared mineral, they belong in the parenthetical in the box (piece 14).

Sweeteners — sucrose, glucose syrup, cane sugar, sucralose, stevia leaf extract, monk fruit — sit under the box unless you are declaring sugars or a (b)(3) stevia glycoside as a dietary ingredient. A gummy that prints "0 g sugar" as a health story is in nutrient-content-claim territory (piece 25). The other-ingredient list will still say glucose syrup if that is what you cooked.

## Why this list is where allergen work starts

FALCPA and the FASTER Act (sesame as of 1 January 2023) are piece 37. The reason they attach to this list, not to the vitamin rows, is mechanical. Major allergens in a dietary supplement are usually the shell (gelatin from bovine or fish, if you treat that as an allergen communication problem), the flavor, the oil fill, the stearate's fatty-acid source, or a botanical that is itself a food allergen.

"Contains" statements are built from the ingredients that are actually present. You cannot write a truthful Contains line if the other-ingredient list is a marketing subset. "May contain" and facility statements are a different, voluntary-or-policy layer; they do not replace 101.4.

A 3PL and a retailer will read this list before they read your structure/function sentence. They are looking for FD&C Yellow No. 5, gelatin, soy lecithin, sesame, and milk. A clean (b)(2) block with a sloppy list under the box is not a clean label.

The list is also how you keep a binder from impersonating a mineral, a capsule from impersonating fiber, and a flavor from impersonating a fruit serving. 101.36 counts dietary ingredients. 101.4 names everything else, in order, immediately under the box. If a word in that list could sit in the %DV column, stop and ask 4-10 again. If a word in the %DV column could sit in this list, you have the magnesium-stearate error. Fix the chair before you print.
