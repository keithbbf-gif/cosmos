A stock photo in a lab coat is an endorsement you have not hired. FTC's December 2022 *Health Products Compliance Guidance*, Example 1, is a weight-loss brochure that shows doctors in white coats, microscopes, and a stack of medical journals. The Commission says those images likely convey that the product has been clinically proven for weight loss. No sentence has to say "doctor recommended." The coat says it.

16 CFR Part 255 (2023 revisions) treats that coat as an endorsement when consumers will take it as someone else's finding, not the brand's. This piece is how "doctor formulated," "used in hospitals," and "9 out of 10" become claims you have to be able to prove — and how an empty science seat stays empty.

## Formulated by vs endorsed by

Those two verbs are not interchangeable.

**Formulated by** is a fact claim about who designed the formula. If a named, licensed clinician wrote the formula card, and will stand behind that sentence, you may be able to say so. You still need the person's real credentials, a record of the work, and a material-connection disclosure if the person is paid, employed, or equity-tied (255.5). You do not get to promote the person as a "leading clinician in joint health" if the degree is in another field. Example 42 in the 2022 guide is that costume: a "Doctor" and "leading clinician" who is a psychologist and physical therapist, has not reviewed the literature on the active, and is a paid officer. Overstated expertise, no product exam, undisclosed job.

**Endorsed by** is 255.3 plus the 2022 health-products chapter. An expert endorsement requires actual expertise in the relevant field *and* an examination or testing of the product that the field would recognize as enough. A testimonial from a clinician is still not competent and reliable scientific evidence for a health benefit (piece 29). If the doctor says the SKU treats a disease, you have bought a disease claim (piece 24). If the doctor says it is "clinically proven," you have bought piece 28's adjective.

"Doctor formulated" without a doctor is false. "Doctor formulated" with a stock headshot and a rented name is false and an endorsement problem. "Inspired by physicians" is the same claim in softer type if a reasonable buyer hears a physician's hand on the formula.

Do not invent a medical director to launder the verb. This pack's empty seats stay empty: science/QA partner and counsel are not named here, and a fictional M.D. is not a substitute (CLAIMS_GUARDRAILS). A white-label formula card signed by a contract formulator is not "our chief physician."

## Hospital use

"Used in hospitals" is a specific, checkable claim. Which hospitals, which SKU, which years, which use — foodservice multivitamin, inpatient protocol, gift-shop bottle? If you cannot produce the purchase orders or the formulary listing, you do not have the sentence.

Hospitals buy a lot of things that are not endorsements of efficacy. A case of vitamin D on a kitchen SKU list is not "hospital-grade treatment." Turning institutional purchasing into a disease story ("used in hospitals to prevent [disease]") is criterion 1 plus an endorsement you may not have (piece 24). "Works with your chemotherapy" remains a **forbidden** criterion-7 shape even if a hospital gift shop sold the SKU.

"Hospital grade," "clinical setting," and "ER trusted" are costumes unless the facts are narrow and documented. FTC will read them as efficacy and typicality claims. FDA will read a disease purpose as intended use (piece 20). A photo of a nurse's station you do not have access to is a stock-photo endorsement, same as the coat.

If a real institution endorsed the product, 255.4 (organizational endorsements) wants the endorsement to reflect the institution's collective judgment, not a single employee's weekend Reel. Disclose the relationship. Do not stretch a cafeteria SKU into a formulary claim.

## Survey claims

"Recommended by 9 out of 10 doctors" is a survey claim. You need a real survey: a defined population of the kind of professional the ad implies, a sample that can support the number, a question that matches the banner, and a date that is still current. A poll of ten friends with credentials, or a pop-up on your own site, is not "doctors." A poll of chiropractors is not "physicians" if the coat implies M.D./D.O. practice.

"9 out of 10 users" is typicality (255.2). If the nine are the people who already loved the brand, or the only people who finished a rebate form, the number is not what buyers will generally achieve. The 2022 guide will also ask whether the underlying benefit — the thing the nine recommend the product *for* — is substantiated with competent and reliable scientific evidence. A survey is not that evidence.

Do not invent a percentage. Do not invent an n. If you do not have the tables, you do not have the badge. "Doctor recommended" without a survey or a named endorser is an implied survey. It lands in the same file.

Comparative survey claims ("more doctors recommend our brand than…") need the comparison object, the same population, and the same question. A leftover slide from a prior owner of the formula is not your survey.

## Photos

The coat, the scope, the journal stack, the caduceus, the hospital corridor, the clipboard — 101.93(g)(2)(iv)(E) already treats pictures, vignettes, and symbols as disease-claim vehicles when they imply treatment or prevention. The SECG's heart symbol and EKG tracing are the dietary-supplement version of that rule (piece 24). A white coat on a supplement PDP is the FTC version: implied clinical proof (Example 1) and often an implied expert endorsement (255.3).

A stock model in a coat is an endorser you have not hired and a credential you have not verified. If the model is not a clinician, the ad says they are. If the model is a clinician who has not examined the product, Example 42's second prong fails. If the model is your uncle the dentist, the connection is material.

Rx symbols and the word "prescription" on a supplement are, in the SECG's words, usually misleading even when they are not automatically disease claims, because they dress the bottle as a prescription drug. Do not put Rx on a gummy to make it look serious.

Before-and-after bodies next to a coat are two claims stacked (piece 29, piece 32). A "week 8 lab printout" you designed in a slide deck is a third.

PHOTO_NOTES for this pack already bars fake-lab theater. This piece is why: the photograph is labeling. If you cannot name the person, the license, and the relationship, do not run the coat.

## Empty seats stay empty

This catalog does not name a chief scientist, a medical director, or a hospital partner it does not have. Filling those seats with stock photography, a rented byline, or "formulated in consultation with wellness experts" is how a PDP acquires an endorser and a false credential in one pass.

The sentences that survive are factual and small.

**Who did the work, if anyone real will stand behind it.** "Formula card prepared by [actual role] at [actual firm]." If that person is paid, say so on the same surface. If you cannot name them, say nothing about a doctor.

**What the SKU is.** Serving, form, amount, 101.36 box. No coat required.

**What you can prove about use.** Not "used in hospitals" unless you have the account. Not "9/10" unless you have the survey. Not "clinically proven" unless this SKU was the intervention (piece 28).

**What you do not say.** Do not invent a medical director. Do not put a stranger's coat on the principal display panel. Do not let a paid creator play doctor (piece 29). Do not turn a facility tour photo into "our physicians." Do not hang a disease name under a caduceus.

FTC Example 49 is the adjacent costume: "FDA Approved" next to inch-and-pound promises. Piece 34 is that file. The coat and the approval badge are the same theater in different fonts. A retailer desk that asks "who is the doctor?" is doing your job early. If the answer is a stock URL, the photo comes off before the proof comes back.
