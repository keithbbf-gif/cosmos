**Disclaimer.** Educational only. Not medical advice. Dietary supplements are not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease (DSHEA; 21 U.S.C. § 321(ff); 21 CFR 101.93). This is not legal advice and not a labeling opinion for a specific SKU.

Ninety-eight letters, March–July 2020. Bautista, Quock, Lima, and Melucci counted them in *Therapeutic Innovation & Regulatory Science* (PMC7528445): of 3,139 FDA warning letters in that window, 98 (3.14 percent) focused on COVID-19-related drugs, devices, biologics, and dietary supplements. The paper is a count of letters, not a protocol. It is not a reason to take vitamin C, vitamin D, zinc, elderberry, or silver for COVID-19. Those ingredients appearing in letters is not a recommendation. It is a record of how intended use was assembled.

The immune split was already in the 2000 rule. The letters are how that split was enforced when a pandemic made "immune" a disease word on every cart page.

## The SECG immune split

Criterion 8, 21 CFR 101.93(g)(2)(viii), treats a statement as a disease claim if the product "has a role in the body's response to a disease or to a vector of disease." The 9 January 2002 Small Entity Compliance Guide is the plain-language version.

**Forbidden SECG examples — not product copy, not a brief, not a backend search term:**

- "supports the body's ability to resist infection"
- "supports the body's antiviral capabilities"

FDA called those disease claims because the context is limited to prevention and treatment of infectious disease. Infection is a disease state. Antiviral capability talk is the same work. A vector, in the 1998 proposal and the 2000 preamble, is an organism or object that can carry a disease agent. Claims about killing or neutralizing pathogens, or about the signs of infection, sit in this box.

The SECG's example of a *general* structure/function shape is "supports the immune system," without more. The immune system has structure/function roles and disease-fighting roles. A general sentence does not, by itself, focus intended use on the disease role. That sentence is still a claim. It still needs substantiation before first marketing, a 30-day notice, and the 101.93 disclaimer (pieces 21–23). It still fails if the rest of the page — a virus graphic, a "flu season" protocol, a COVID hashtag, a blog next to the cart — does the disease work the five words avoided.

Piece 24 listed the rest of the ten criteria. Criterion 8 is the one the 2020 letters spent.

Do not tell a reader to take a supplement to resist infection, to treat a virus, or to prevent COVID-19, influenza, or any other named disease. Those uses are drug claims. This pack will not write them as advice.

## What the letters actually quoted

**FRS International, LLC, MARCS-CMS 606701 (15 June 2020).** Dual header: FDA and FTC. The agencies reviewed www.frs.com, www.healthyenergy.com, and www.frs-international.com (1 April and 4 June 2020) and the Facebook page www.facebook.com/FRSEnergyEndurance, which sent buyers to the sites. The products named were quercetin-containing soft chews and liquid concentrates sold as Q-Force and FRS Healthy Energy. FDA's conclusion: those products were intended to mitigate, prevent, treat, diagnose, or cure COVID-19, and were therefore unapproved new drugs under 21 U.S.C. § 355(a) and misbranded drugs under § 352.

The letter pasted the firm's own language back to it. One Facebook line the letter quoted: "IN NEWS: DOCTORS ARE STUDYING QUERCETIN AS A DEFENSE AGAINST CORONAVIR…" Website-plus-Facebook is the method. Quercetin is not the lesson. The lesson is that a study headline plus a cart button is intended use (piece 20). Do not take that quoted sentence as a reason to use quercetin for COVID-19. The letter is an example of a forbidden disease claim the firm had already printed.

**Mercola.com, LLC, MARCS-CMS 607133 (18 February 2021).** FDA reviewed mercola.com and mercolamarket.com on 8 February 2021, and twitter.com/mercola, which directed buyers to the market site. The products named were Liposomal Vitamin C, Liposomal Vitamin D3, and Quercetin and Pterostilbene Advanced. Same statutory hook: intended to mitigate, prevent, treat, diagnose, or cure COVID-19; unapproved new drugs; misbranded drugs.

The letter quoted site copy as disease claims, including statements that vitamins C and D were being adopted in the conventional treatment of SARS-CoV-2; that vitamin C was "a crucial aid, both for the prevention and treatment of viral illnesses," with a recommendation of liposomal vitamin C; and that vitamin C and quercetin "have synergistic effects that make them useful in the prevention and early at-home treatment of COVID-19." Those sentences are what the letter treated as drug claims. They are taught here as **forbidden examples**. They are not a protocol. This piece will not tell a reader to take vitamin C, vitamin D, or quercetin for COVID-19 or for any viral illness.

Bautista et al. reported that among the 98 COVID-focused letters, the ingredients appearing most often in the cited products included vitamin C, cannabidiol/CBD, vitamin D/D3, silver/colloidal silver, elderberry, and zinc (the paper also lists ritonavir, a drug, among frequent mentions). The paper's job was to describe the letters. It is not a ranking of remedies. FDA's fraudulent COVID-19 products page collected the same class of listings. ## Ingredient was not the violation

A Supplement Facts row that says "Vitamin C 90 mg" or "Vitamin D 25 mcg" is a quantity (piece 01, piece 10). 101.36 does not become a COVID claim because the nutrient is on Bautista's frequency list. The violation in 606701 and 607133 was intended use: sites, social posts, and captions that told a buyer the SKU was for COVID-19.

You can misbrand a bottle of water if the site says it treats COVID-19. You can keep a lawful vitamin C panel if every surface stays off the disease. The ingredient does not immunize the claim. The claim does not contaminate every other use of the ingredient.

That is why this pack will not recommend C, D, zinc, elderberry, or silver — or any other dietary ingredient — as a COVID-19, flu, or "viral illness" product. Doing so would repeat the letters' quoted language as advice. Teaching the letters means quoting them as *no*.

CBD as an interstate dietary supplement is a separate FDA conclusion (26 January 2023) unless a later dated page says otherwise [VERIFY]. Silver as a disease treatment is a drug story and, in the COVID letters, was treated as such. Neither is an immune structure/function SKU.

Hashtags do not create a new category. They are labeling when you paid for them or when they sit on a page that sells the bottle.

## Hashtags and protocols

"#immune," "#fluseason," "#covidprotocol," "natural vaccine" — the last two are class-name and disease-prevention problems (piece 24, criteria 5 and 1). "Vaccine" is on the SECG's forbidden class list. "Protocol" next to a named virus is a treatment plan. A blog titled as a protocol, with an add-to-cart module, is the Mercola pattern: the article supplies intended use for the SKU on the same domain.

Piece 20 already said intended use is assembled from label, site, marketplace, Reel, and chat macro. FRS 606701 is website plus Facebook. 607133 is website plus market site plus Twitter. Bautista noted letters that identified products sold through Amazon. A clean 101.36 box does not save a dirty caption. A paid creator who says the gummy is for COVID, flu, or "the thing that's going around" has written a disease claim you bought (piece 29).

## What a 2026 PDP can still say

A 2026 panel can still say, if you have substantiation, a 30-day file, and the 101.93 disclaimer, a general structure/function sentence about the immune system that does not name infection, a virus, a vaccine, a protocol, or COVID-19. "Supports the immune system" is the SECG's example of that general shape. It is not a safe harbor if the rest of the SKU does criterion-8 work.

A 2026 panel can still declare vitamin C, vitamin D, zinc, or a botanical in 101.36 rows. Those rows are amounts. They are not "defense against coronavirus." It cannot say the forbidden SECG pair, "natural vaccine," "antiviral," "protects against COVID," "flu protocol," or the Mercola or FRS sentences the letters quoted. It cannot hang a disease-titled citation on the carton (criterion 4).

FTC's 2022 guide will read the same PDP as advertising (piece 27). "Clinically studied for immunity" is the piece-28 adjective: whose study, which dose, which SKU. If the only true sentence is the milligram line, print the milligram line. The letters are what it looks like when the agency pastes the other sentence back.
