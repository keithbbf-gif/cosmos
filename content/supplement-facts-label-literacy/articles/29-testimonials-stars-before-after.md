**Disclaimer.** Educational only. Not medical advice. Dietary supplements are not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease (DSHEA; 21 U.S.C. § 321(ff); 21 CFR 101.93). This is not legal advice and not a labeling opinion for a specific SKU.

A five-star review that names a disease is a disease claim you paid to display. 16 CFR Part 255, as revised in 2023 (88 FR, 26 July 2023), treats endorsements as advertising. FTC's December 2022 *Health Products Compliance Guidance* says the same thing in health-product language: you may not say through a customer what you may not say yourself, and a testimonial is not competent and reliable scientific evidence.

The principal display panel that kept 101.93(g) clean can still lose the page to a widget. Stars are labeling when you put them on the bottle, the cart, or the A-plus module. Piece 41 is the marketplace surface. This piece is the endorsement file those surfaces inherit.

## Endorsement guides

Part 255 is a guide, not a criminal code. FTC uses it to explain how it reads section 5 of the FTC Act. An endorsement is an advertising message that consumers are likely to believe reflects the opinions, beliefs, findings, or experience of a party other than the sponsoring advertiser. A five-star block, a "verified purchase" paragraph, a creator's Reel, a "Dr." in a lab coat, a hospital logo — those are endorsements when they are used to sell.

Two duties sit on the advertiser, not only on the person who typed the review.

First, the underlying claim must be substantiated as if you had written it. 16 CFR 255.2 says consumer endorsements themselves are not competent and reliable scientific evidence. The 2022 health-products guide repeats it and adds the health-claim standard: if the reviewer says the product ended a disease or delivered a health benefit, you need the same science you would need for that sentence on the panel (piece 27).

Second, the endorsement must be honest and current, and any material connection must be disclosed when a significant minority of the audience would not already expect it (255.5). Payment, free product, affiliate commission, employment, a family tie, a seed round — if it would change the weight a buyer gives the praise, it is material. "Clear and conspicuous" means hard to miss and easy to understand, in the same channel as the endorsement. A #ad buried under a fold of hashtags is the usual miss.

Expert endorsements (255.3) add a third duty. The person must have the expertise the ad claims, and must have examined the product in a way the field would recognize as enough to support the endorsement. The 2022 guide's Example 42 is the infomercial "Doctor" and "leading clinician in joint health" who is a psychologist and physical therapist, has not reviewed the literature on the active, and is a paid officer of the company. Overstated credentials, no product exam, undisclosed job. Piece 33 is the white-coat version of that example.

## Typical results

255.2(b) is typicality. An endorsement about a central attribute will likely be read as what consumers will generally achieve in real, variable use. If you do not have substantiation that the reviewer's result is representative, you must clearly and conspicuously disclose the generally expected performance, and you must have substantiation for *that* number too.

"Results not typical" does not do that work. The 2022 guide is blunt: attempts to disclaim dramatic results with that phrase do not cure the deception. Example 41 is a magazine ad with before-and-after photographs, a woman who says she lost a stated amount of weight in eight weeks, and a fine-print asterisk ("These results are not typical. Your weight loss may not be the same."). FTC walks through a trial in which the average advantage over placebo was much smaller, and calls the vague, buried line insufficient. The guide's illustration of a better qualifier is a prominent, adjacent statement of what the study actually showed for the product and for placebo.

Do not invent your own averages to decorate a review. If you have a trial on *this* SKU, you may be able to state what that trial found, with the conditions the trial used (diet, exercise, population). If you do not have that trial, you do not have a typicality number, and the dramatic review should not be the ad.

Typicality is also how a single five-star disease story becomes a product claim. One reviewer who names arthritis pain is 101.93(g) criterion 1 if you display it (piece 24). FDA's forbidden example "reduces the pain and stiffness associated with arthritis" does not become lawful because a stranger typed it and you hit "feature this review."

## Before/after

Before-and-after photographs are claims. The 2022 guide uses them in the weight-loss example. Bodies, faces, lab printouts, "week 1 / week 8" sliders — they communicate a result, they communicate typicality unless you effectively disclose otherwise, and they often communicate a disease or a drug-adjacent use even when the structure/function line on the bottle is clean.

A body-composition slider next to a "supports metabolism" panel is doing weight-loss work (piece 32). A sleepless-to-sleeping face next to "Soothing Sleep" is doing insomnia work unless the rest of the labeling keeps the claim on occasional sleeplessness (piece 31). A rash-to-clear pair is a skin-disease story.

The photographs do not have to be fake to be unlawful. Example 41 assumes the woman's experience was accurately represented. The problem was the implied typical result and the buried qualifier. Staging a body you do not have, or a lab coat you have not hired, adds a second problem (piece 33).

CLAIMS_GUARDRAILS in this pack already lists before/after bodies and "replaces your [drug]" as never-say. This piece is why: 16 CFR 255 and the 2022 guide treat the picture as the claim, and 101.93(g) treats the implied disease as a drug claim.

## Review widgets

A stars module on a PDP, a cart page, or a sell sheet is not a neutral bulletin board once you choose what it shows. The 2023 Endorsement Guides address consumer reviews as endorsements when they are used in advertising, and they address review suppression and manipulation. You may not hide negative reviews by a filter that keeps only the fives, boost your own ratings, write the review yourself, or pay for a rating without disclosure.

A widget set to "most recent" that you never read is still your advertising if it sits next to the buy button. Intended use is assembled from four corners (piece 20). FTC and FDA can both add to cart. A review that says the product treated a named disease, replaced a prescription, or worked with chemotherapy is a criterion-1, -6, or -7 problem the moment you leave it up (piece 24). Moderation is not optional courtesy. It is how you keep the widget from rewriting the SKU as a drug.

Star averages are claims about satisfaction and, in context, about performance. "Rated 4.9 for joint relief" on the front of the bottle is an express claim plus an endorsement. If "joint relief" is being heard as arthritis, you are in the forbidden SECG examples ("improves joint mobility and reduces inflammation (rheumatoid arthritis)"). If it is being heard as a structure/function comfort claim, you still need substantiation and the 101.93 disclaimer, and the stars still need typicality and honesty.

Do not put a review carousel on a sell sheet you hand a retailer unless you have read every card in the deck. The retailer desk will treat it as your labeling. So will the letter.

## Influencers you paid

A creator you paid, seeded, or commissioned is an endorser with a material connection. The 2022 guide's Example 40 is the paid blogger who writes that a supplement cures acid reflux. The marketer made no such claim. The marketer is still liable for the blogger's sentence, and the post must disclose the payment so readers do not take it as an independent review. Acid reflux as a treatment claim is disease talk. Do not brief it. Do not leave it up.

Scripts, talking points, and "just being authentic" captions are labeling if you paid for them. A Reel is not a private diary. Piece 41 will treat Reels and chat macros as four-corners surfaces. The endorsement rule is prior: the creator may not say a disease name, a typical result you cannot substantiate, "clinically proven" about a SKU that was not in the trial (piece 28), or "FDA approved" (piece 34). The brief should be a list of allowed sentences and a list of forbidden SECG examples, not a vibe.

Affiliate links, seeded jars, and "I just love this brand" posts by employees or relatives are the same file. 255.5 does not care that the caption was short. It cares whether a significant minority would still think the praise was unconnected.

If you cannot monitor the paid surface, do not buy the surface. A clean bottle cannot save a dirty caption. The 2023 guides and the 2022 health-products chapter were written because the caption is now the ad, and the ad is now the label.
