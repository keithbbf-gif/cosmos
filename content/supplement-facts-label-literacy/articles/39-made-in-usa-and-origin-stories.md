"Made in USA" is an FTC file, not a flavor. The Made in USA Labeling Rule is 16 CFR Part 323, published at 86 FR 37022 (14 July 2021) and effective 13 August 2021. It codifies the old "all or virtually all" standard and puts civil penalties behind an unqualified U.S.-origin claim on a label. A flag on a capsule bottle is now a record you have to keep.

Customs marking is a different stamp. 19 U.S.C. § 1304 and 19 CFR Part 134 tell you how to mark an imported article with its country of origin. You can pass Customs and still fail FTC. You can fail both. The Supplement Facts box will not save either analysis. 101.36 wants plant part and quantity, not a patriotic caption.

## All or virtually all

16 CFR 323.2 is a three-part prohibition. It is an unfair or deceptive act to label a product Made in the United States unless (1) final assembly or processing occurs in the United States, (2) all significant processing that goes into the product occurs in the United States, and (3) all or virtually all ingredients or components are made and sourced in the United States.

§ 323.1 defines "Made in the United States" broadly. Unqualified representations count: made, manufactured, built, produced, created, crafted in the United States or in America, and other unqualified U.S.-origin claims. Mail-order catalogs and mail-order promotional material that carry a seal, mark, tag, or stamp are in the same rule (§ 323.3). A storefront tile is not exempt because it is HTML.

FTC's *Complying with the Made in USA Standard* page says "all or virtually all" means no — or negligible — foreign content. There is no published percentage that lets you stop thinking. The Commission looks at the portion of manufacturing costs that are U.S., how far removed any foreign content is, and how important that foreign content is to the form or function of the product. You need a reasonable basis: competent and reliable evidence, in hand, before the claim goes on the carton.

United States, for this file, includes the 50 states, the District of Columbia, and U.S. territories and possessions. A fill in Puerto Rico is not the same sentence as a fill in Ontario. Do not guess.

Encapsulated in New Jersey from a Chinese or Indian powder is the ordinary dietary-supplement fact pattern. Final assembly may be domestic. The ingredient that does the work on the panel usually is not. Unqualified "Made in USA" fails the third prong unless the foreign content is truly negligible. A 500 mg botanical extract is not a rounding error.

## Qualified claims

The 2021 rule is about *unqualified* claims on labels. Qualified claims still sit under Section 5 of the FTC Act. They have to be true in net impression.

Shapes that can be true, if the file is true:

- "Made in USA from imported ingredients."
- "Assembled in the USA."
- "Encapsulated and bottled in [state] from globally sourced dietary ingredients."
- "Packaged in the USA."

Shapes that fail because the qualifier is in 6-point gray next to a flag the size of the logo: those are unqualified claims wearing a footnote. FTC has been down this road since the 1997 policy statement. The 2021 rule did not invent the problem. It attached penalties to the label version.

"Made in USA with domestic and imported ingredients" is only useful if a buyer can tell which is which. If the only domestic inputs are the bottle, the cotton, and the label stock, say that. Do not let "with domestic ingredients" imply the ashwagandha grew in Oregon.

Retailer and marketplace checkboxes that offer only "Made in USA — yes/no" are the same trap as "pharmaceutical grade — yes/no" (piece 35). The survivable response is a short qualifying sentence plus the bill of materials. A checkbox that cannot hold a qualifier is a checkbox you should leave blank.

[VERIFY] the substantiation file — invoices, country-of-origin declarations, processing steps — before any origin claim ships. A brand manager's memory of a 2019 supplier is not a reasonable basis.

## Botanical origin stories

101.4(h) and 101.36 already require the plant part on a Supplement Facts panel. Latin binomial is how you stop "ginseng" from being three species (piece 18). That is identity. It is not origin.

"From the soil of [place]" is a supply-chain claim. It needs the agricultural source, the extract site, and the steps in between. A 10:1 extract manufactured in one country from herb grown in another is not "grown and made in" the growing country, and it is not "Made in USA" because you put it in a capsule in New Jersey. Piece 15 already told you 10:1 is not ten times the herb in the body. Origin theater is the cousin of ratio theater.

"Wildcrafted in the Appalachians," "Himalayan," "from our family farm" — each is an express origin claim plus, often, an implied purity claim. If the lot is a blend of two countries, the romantic singular is false. If you do not have chain-of-custody papers, you do not have the sentence.

Do not turn a place name into a disease monograph. "Peruvian bark" is not a treatment. "Icelandic kelp" is not a thyroid drug. The SECG examples in piece 24 still apply when the adjective is a map.

## Printed in vs made in

Label stock printed in Ohio is a print-shop fact. It can go on a specification. It does not make the capsules Made in USA.

The dangerous layout is an American flag, the words "printed in the USA," and a brand name that already sounds domestic. Net impression is the test. FTC's health-products guidance (December 2022) and the Made in USA materials both care about what a reasonable consumer takes away, not what the art director intended.

Other near-miss captions: "designed in California," "quality you expect from American manufacturing," a map of the lower 48 behind a bottle of imported fish oil. "Designed in" can be true and still imply "made in" if the rest of the panel does that work. Keep design credits in the About page, in type that cannot be mistaken for origin of the goods.

Cartons, inserts, and mailers that repeat the claim are labels for 16 CFR 323.3 when they carry the mark. A welcome booklet with a flag seal is in the file.

## What a white-label bottle usually is

The ordinary contract-manufactured SKU is a stack:

- dietary ingredients purchased on a world market, often already extracted or synthesized abroad
- excipients from more than one country
- a capsule shell from a specialist vendor
- a domestic or near-shore fill-and-pack step
- a carton printed wherever the printer was cheapest
- a 3PL in California, Kentucky, or New Jersey

That stack can be a lawful dietary supplement. It is almost never unqualified Made in USA. It may need a Customs country-of-origin mark if the finished article is imported. If the finished article is substantially transformed in the United States, Customs and FTC can still disagree about what you may say in English on the front.

White-label catalogs that offer a house American-flag template are selling a claim, not a fill. Do not accept the template until the bill of materials clears the three-part test. Showing another company's plant photo as "our U.S. facility" is the guardrail already written: empty seats stay empty.

Country-of-origin marking, when it applies, is a plain country name. "Product of [country]." Do not decorate it into a soil story you have not traced. Do not hide it on the inner lip of the cap because it spoiled the flag.

The panel still comes first. Title, serving, rows, hairline, other ingredients, allergens (piece 37), then origin. If the origin sentence needs a qualifier to be true, the qualifier is part of the sentence. Print it in the same type as the claim. The flag is optional. The file is not.
