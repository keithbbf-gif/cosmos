The 2016 rule retired IU as the panel unit for E. 81 FR 33742 (27 May 2016) rebuilt Nutrition Facts and pulled Supplement Facts with it. Vitamin D went to mcg. Folate went to mcg DFE. Vitamin A went to mcg RAE. Vitamin E went to milligrams of α-tocopherol. A printer file that still speaks only in IU is speaking 2015.

NIH's Office of Dietary Supplements still narrates the old unit in its fact sheet, because people still buy "400 IU" off the aisle talk. The panel does not. The row is mg. The form in parentheses is how you know which conversion you used.

## mg α-tocopherol

The Daily Value counts α-tocopherol. ODS states that α-tocopherol is the form recognized to meet human requirements, and that one milligram of vitamin E on the new label equals 1 mg RRR-α-tocopherol (the natural stereoisomer, commonly written d-alpha) or 2 mg all-rac-α-tocopherol (the synthetic mixture, commonly written dl-alpha). That 1-for-2 identity is how FDA and ODS keep the milligram row honest when the bottle holds the synthetic salt.

ODS, citing FDA, puts the adult Daily Value at 15 mg for people 4 years and older. Recheck the current 21 CFR 101.9 RDI table before you print [VERIFY]. The 2005 Dietary Supplement Labeling Guide still talks in the old IU world. Use it for how the box is assembled. Use current 101.9 / 101.36 for the unit.

You list the milligrams per serving, then the % Daily Value, same walk as any other (b)(2) vitamin (21 CFR 101.36(b)(2)). You do not get a second unit as the legal number. IU in a parenthetical on a website is optional copy. IU as the only number on the panel is a leftover.

A customer who reads "400" and guesses milligrams, or reads "15" and guesses IU, is doing the same 40× style error piece 10 walks for vitamin D. E's factor is not 40. It is 0.67 or 0.45, depending on the form. Get it from ODS. Do not get it from memory.

## Natural vs synthetic factors

ODS publishes the conversion rules in one block. Copy them onto the formula card.

To convert milligrams to IU:

- 1 mg α-tocopherol = 1.49 IU of the natural form
- 1 mg α-tocopherol = 2.22 IU of the synthetic form

To convert IU to milligrams:

- 1 IU of the natural form = 0.67 mg α-tocopherol
- 1 IU of the synthetic form = 0.45 mg α-tocopherol

ODS works the 15 mg example: 15 mg of natural α-tocopherol is 22.4 IU (15 × 1.49). The same 15 mg of synthetic α-tocopherol is 33.3 IU (15 × 2.22). Run that arithmetic on the actual salt in the lot, not on the word "vitamin E."

The d- / dl- prefix is aisle slang for RRR versus all-rac. The acetate and succinate esters are still α-tocopherol after the body hydrolyzes them; ODS says the esters are absorbed as efficiently as the free tocopherol. The label milligram is still α-tocopherol, not "mg of acetate salt." If your spreadsheet reports milligrams of d-alpha-tocopheryl acetate as if they were milligrams of α-tocopherol, you have inflated the row by the ester weight. That is a formula-card error, not a font error.

A 400 IU softgel is not one number. Natural 400 IU is 268 mg α-tocopherol (400 × 0.67). Synthetic 400 IU is 180 mg α-tocopherol (400 × 0.45). Those are ODS's own factors applied to a common aisle claim. If the panel says 180 mg and the cart says "400 IU natural," the two documents disagree. Piece 42 is that fight. This piece is the factor.

Do not invert the factor. Multiplying when you should divide is how a 15 mg row becomes a 22 IU row, or a 400 IU row becomes a 596 mg row. The vitamin D scar in piece 10 is the same class of spreadsheet. E just uses a different pair of constants.

## Mixed tocopherols that are not the DV form

Vitamin E in plants is a family: alpha, beta, gamma, and delta tocopherols, plus the tocotrienols. ODS is blunt that α-tocopherol is the form the requirement is written in. Gamma-tocopherol is common in soybean and corn oil. It does not become the vitamin E row because the oil smells "mixed."

A (b)(3) line that says "mixed tocopherols 50 mg" is an other dietary ingredient if you are not counting those milligrams as the vitamin E Daily Value. A vitamin E row that says "50 mg" because the blend of tocopherols weighed 50 mg is a unit error. Only the α-tocopherol fraction belongs in the (b)(2) vitamin E line.

You may indent constituents under a (b)(3) mixed-tocopherol line (piece 15). You may not park the RDI nutrient inside that line and skip the vitamin E declaration (piece 16). If the product supplies both a declared vitamin E amount and a mixed-tocopherol blend, the two rows have different jobs. One is a Daily Value. The other is a named other dietary ingredient.

Tocotrienols are not a synonym for vitamin E on the panel. If you list them, list them by common or usual name under (b)(3), with the asterisk and the footnote. Do not convert them to α-tocopherol milligrams with a factor you invented.

## Source line

21 CFR 101.36(b)(2) lets you name the source of a dietary ingredient in the Supplement Facts panel. The 2005 guide's own pattern is "Calcium (as calcium carbonate)." The E analog is "Vitamin E (as d-alpha-tocopheryl acetate)" or "Vitamin E (as dl-alpha-tocopheryl acetate)" or "Vitamin E (as d-alpha-tocopherol)." The word in parentheses is the salt or the stereochemistry. It is how a chemist, a buyer, and an investigator all know which ODS factor you used.

If the source is already in the box, 21 CFR 101.4(h) plus 101.36(d) mean you do not have to repeat that source in the other-ingredient statement. If the source is not in the box, it belongs under the box (piece 17). "Mixed tocopherols" used as an antioxidant in the oil fill, and not declared as a dietary ingredient, is an other ingredient. "Mixed tocopherols" sold as the product is a (b)(3) row. Those are different chairs.

The source line is not a claim. "As d-alpha-tocopheryl succinate" does not mean the succinate is better. It means that is the article in the capsule. Do not turn the parenthetical into "natural, therefore superior" copy. Natural versus synthetic changes the IU-to-mg factor. It does not write your structure/function sentence for you.

## Do not write "heart healthy E" as if it were a qualified claim you have

A milligram row is a quantity. It is not a health claim and it is not a structure/function claim. 21 CFR 101.36(b)(2) tells you how to count α-tocopherol. It does not tell you what E *does*.

"Heart healthy" is claim language. Authorized health claims live in 21 CFR 101.14 and the model-language lists. Qualified health claims are letter-by-letter files with FDA's own disclaimer language (piece 26). Recheck the current FDA qualified-health-claim page before you ever print one [VERIFY]. Do not borrow the aisle adjective because a 1990s observational paper still circulates in sales decks.

ODS reviews the cardiovascular literature on its fact sheet. That page is a nutrient monograph, not a label permission. This pack does not convert those paragraphs into cart copy, and it does not recite trial counts or p-values. The fence is simpler than the monograph: if you do not hold the authorized or qualified claim you are about to print, do not print it.

A structure/function sentence about vitamin E, if you have one, still needs substantiation before first marketing, the 30-day notice, and the disclaimer (pieces 21–23). "Supports antioxidant activity in the body" is a different drawer from "heart healthy." Both drawers fail if the rest of the page does disease work. FDA's own disease-claim examples in the 2000 rule include sentences about protecting against the development of a named disease. Do not write the cousin of that sentence and park 15 mg next to it.

The form in parentheses, the milligram, and the %DV are the E job on this panel. IU is history, or a parenthetical. Mixed tocopherols are not a free upgrade to the row. "Heart healthy" is a claim you either have, under the claim rules, or you do not. The 2016 unit change did not give it to you.
