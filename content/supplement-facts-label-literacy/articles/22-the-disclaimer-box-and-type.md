**Disclaimer.** Educational only. Not medical advice. Dietary supplements are not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease (DSHEA; 21 U.S.C. § 321(ff); 21 CFR 101.93). This is not legal advice and not a labeling opinion for a specific SKU.

One-sixteenth of an inch is a specification, not a vibe. 21 CFR 101.93(e) says the disclaimer in paragraph (c) shall appear in boldface type in letters of a typesize no smaller than one-sixteenth inch. 62 FR 49859 (23 September 1997) is the final rule that put those mechanics on paper, the same week FDA drew the Supplement Facts box (62 FR 49826). The statute had already required the two sentences, "prominently displayed and in boldface type" (21 U.S.C. § 343(r)(6)(C)). The 1997 rule told the printer where, how large, and when to draw a box.

If the letters are 1/16 inch on the dieline and shrink on press, you do not have 101.93(e). Measure the printed bottle.

## The two official sentences

101.93(c)(1), one statement:

This statement has not been evaluated by the Food and Drug Administration. This product is not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease.

101.93(c)(2), more than one such statement on the label or in the labeling — each statement may carry the singular disclaimer, or you may place a plural disclaimer:

These statements have not been evaluated by the Food and Drug Administration. This product is not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease.

Those are the official sentences. "These statements have not been evaluated by the FDA" is already a rewrite. "This product is not intended to treat or prevent disease" drops diagnose and cure. A paraphrase that sounds close is not 101.93(c).

101.93(b) tells you when the section applies: label or labeling of a dietary supplement that bears a statement provided for by section 403(r)(6) of the Act, where the manufacturer, packer, or distributor wants the 403(r)(6) exemption from the drug definition in 201(g)(1)(C). If you want the lane, you take the text.

You do not put this box on a product that is making an authorized health claim *instead of* a structure/function statement and then assume 101.93 is the right rule. Health claims have their own qualifying language (piece 26). You do not put this box on a nutrient-content claim and assume you have done 101.54. Different drawers. The two sentences attach to 403(r)(6) statements.

A house "educational only" line in the website footer is not a substitute. This pack's own disclaimer is education for a reader of essays. The bottle needs 101.93(c) if the bottle bears the statement.

## Placement

101.93(d) is the placement rule. The disclaimer shall be placed **adjacent** to the statement with no intervening material, **or** linked to the statement with a symbol (the rule's example is an asterisk) at the end of each such statement that refers to the same symbol placed adjacent to the disclaimer.

On product labels and in labeling — the rule's own examples are pamphlets and catalogs — the disclaimer shall appear on **each panel or page** where there is such a statement. If the statement is on the principal display panel and again on the information panel, both places need the disclaimer or a legal link to it. If the statement is on page 4 of a pamphlet, page 4 carries it. A single disclaimer on the back cover of a 16-page catalog does not cover page 4.

"The disclaimer shall be set off in a box where it is not adjacent to the statement in question." That is the box people mean when they say "disclaimer box." Adjacent and linked-by-asterisk placements do not require that extra box. A disclaimer parked at the foot of the panel, separated from the claim by a block of other copy, does.

Adjacent means adjacent. A structure/function sentence on the front, a brand story, a QR code, and then the two sentences at the bottom is not adjacent. Use the asterisk if you will not give the claim a neighbor.

Each web page that carries a 403(r)(6) statement is a "page" in the same sense the catalog is. A PDP on Amazon that carries the statement needs the sentences on that PDP, not only on a buried "legal" tab. Piece 41 is the marketplace. 101.93(d) is why a tab does not finish the job.

## Type

Boldface. No smaller than one-sixteenth inch. 101.93(e) does not offer a "unless impractical" escape the way some 101.36 small-pack rules do. If the bottle is too small to hold the claim and a legal disclaimer, the claim does not fit.

Color contrast is not written as a 101.93(e) number, but a gray-on-gray 1/16-inch line that a buyer cannot read is not "prominently displayed" under the statute. 101.36(e) wants, whenever practical, all black or one color on a white or neutral contrasting background for the nutrition panel. The disclaimer is often next to that panel. Do not hide it in the varnish.

Do not set the two sentences in a script face, in all caps that shrink the x-height, or inside a photo of a chalkboard. The rule said boldface type. It did not say "fun."

If you use the plural form, you still need 1/16 inch. Plural is not a permission to go smaller because the line is longer. If the line wraps, every letter of the official text still has to meet the typesize.

A hang tag that carries a statement is labeling. The hang tag gets the type spec. An insert that carries a statement gets the type spec. A card in a subscription box that carries a statement gets the type spec. The 1997 rule's "pamphlets, catalogs" list was illustrative ("e.g.").

## Catalogs and pamphlets

101.93(d) names pamphlets and catalogs on purpose. In 1997 those were how a brand made twenty statements at once. The same sentence now describes a lookbook PDF, a sales one-pager, and a retailer sell-sheet.

Each page that carries a 403(r)(6) statement carries the disclaimer. A twelve-page catalog with a statement on pages 2, 5, and 11 needs the disclaimer on 2, 5, and 11 — adjacent, asterisk-linked, or boxed if not adjacent. A single boxed disclaimer on page 12, after the order form, is the design that 101.93(d) was written to stop.

A pamphlet that is "only for practitioners" is still labeling if it is used to sell the SKU. Intended use does not end at a professional audience (piece 20). The disclaimer rule does not end there either.

Electronic catalogs are pages. A swipeable mobile lookbook is a sequence of pages. If a card in that sequence bears the statement, that card bears 101.93(c). Do not argue that a scroll is one page if a buyer can share the claim as a screenshot without the sentences.

The statement of identity and the Supplement Facts panel can be perfect while a catalog page still fails 101.93(d). The 1997 labeling rule and the 1997 disclaimer rule were published the same day because they are different jobs.

## What the box cannot do

The box cannot wash a disease claim. 101.93(f) says that if the label or labeling bears a disease claim as defined in 101.93(g), the product is subject to regulation as a drug unless the claim is an authorized health claim for which the product qualifies. Printing the two sentences under "reduces the pain and stiffness associated with arthritis" — FDA's own forbidden-example shape from the 2000 rule and the SECG — does not turn that sentence into structure/function. It turns the bottle into a drug with a disclaimer on it.

The box cannot replace substantiation. You still needed the file before first marketing (piece 21, piece 23). The box cannot replace the 30-day notice. You still send 101.93(a). The box cannot replace FTC. A claim that a reasonable buyer takes as a disease or as a stronger promise than your study can bear will fail the 2022 *Health Products Compliance Guidance* with or without 1/16-inch type (piece 27).

The box cannot save a name, a picture, or a citation that does 101.93(g) work. "CircuCure" with a perfect disclaimer is still the SECG's example of a name that claims a disease. A before/after body with a perfect disclaimer is still an endorsement problem (piece 29). A QR code to a disease paper with a perfect disclaimer is still a context problem.

The box cannot be a footer you forgot to put on the panel that actually carries the claim. 101.93(d) is each panel or page.

Set the two official sentences, in bold, at 1/16 inch or larger, next to the statement or asterisk-linked to it, on every surface that carries the statement, in a box if you will not give them a neighbor. Then read the statement again. If it is a 101.93(g) claim, take the statement off. The box is a condition of the 403(r)(6) lane. It is not a solvent.
