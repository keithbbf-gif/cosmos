**Disclaimer.** Educational only. Not medical advice. Dietary supplements are not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease (DSHEA; 21 U.S.C. § 321(ff); 21 CFR 101.93). This is not legal advice and not a labeling opinion for a specific SKU.

IU hid the conversion. RAE does not. The old vitamin A unit treated 1 IU as 0.30 mcg of all-trans-retinol or 0.60 mcg of all-trans-β-carotene and then let a single "5,000 IU" stand for whatever mix was in the formula. Retinol activity equivalents do not let that mix collapse. 1 mcg RAE is 1 mcg retinol, or 2 mcg supplemental β-carotene, or 12 mcg dietary β-carotene, or 24 mcg dietary α-carotene or β-cryptoxanthin (21 CFR 101.9(c)(8)(iv) footnote 3; 81 FR 33742). A gummy that stacks retinyl palmitate with β-carotene can look modest in leftover IU and large in RAE — or the reverse — depending on which form is doing the work. You cannot convert a mixed IU number with one factor. FDA said that out loud.

This is not a deficiency-disease ad. ODS discusses upper intake levels on the Vitamin A Health Professional fact sheet. That is ODS, not a disease claim, and not a Supplement Facts row.

## What RAE counts

RAE counts vitamin A *activity* after the conversion the 2016 rule adopted from the National Academy of Medicine. Pre-formed vitamin A (retinol, including retinyl palmitate and retinyl acetate as sources) converts 1:1: 1 mcg retinol = 1 mcg RAE. Supplemental β-carotene in oil is 2:1: 2 mcg of that β-carotene = 1 mcg RAE. Dietary β-carotene — and, FDA said, β-carotene added to *food* — is 12:1. Other dietary provitamin A carotenoids (α-carotene, β-cryptoxanthin) are 24:1.

FDA's August 2019 conversion guidance, Table 2, is the worksheet. Supplemental factors apply to purified provitamin A carotenoids in oil. Food-source carotenoids in a supplement use the dietary factors [VERIFY you are applying that split, not a supplier's IU label].

The 2016 adult RDI is 900 mcg RAE (was 5,000 IU). Infants, children 1–3, and pregnant or lactating women have their own RAE values in 101.9(c)(8)(iv) — 500, 300, and 1,300 mcg RAE in the current table [VERIFY]. %DV is mcg RAE ÷ that denominator × 100 (piece 06). There is no IU in that division.

101.36(b)(2)(ii)(B) pulls those units onto Supplement Facts. The amount line is mcg (as RAE). You do not print IU as the primary unit. Vitamin D still gets an optional IU parenthetical (piece 10). Vitamin A does not get that same parenthetical as a substitute for RAE on the panel.

A source line — "Vitamin A (as retinyl palmitate)" or "Vitamin A (as beta-carotene)" — names what you added. The number to the right is still RAE, not milligrams of the salt or the powder.

## Carotenoids vs retinol

IU used one scale for two biochemistries. 1 IU = 0.30 mcg retinol = 0.60 mcg β-carotene, historically. RAE says supplemental β-carotene has half the activity of retinol on a microgram basis, and dietary β-carotene has one-sixth the activity of the supplemental form. 81 FR 33742 at 33913: IU does not reflect the carotene-to-retinol equivalence ratio. That sentence is why the unit changed.

Conversion guidance Table 3: there is **no** direct conversion from a label IU to mcg RAE when the product is a mixture, unless you know the proportion of retinol and each carotenoid. Individual factors exist only for a single source: IU × 0.30 for retinol; IU × 0.30 for supplemental β-carotene (because 1 IU was 0.6 mcg β-carotene and 2 mcg supplemental β-carotene = 1 mcg RAE); IU × 0.05 for dietary β-carotene; IU × 0.025 for dietary α-carotene or β-cryptoxanthin.

Worked single-source examples from that guidance: 500 IU as pre-formed vitamin A in milk → 150 mcg RAE. 3,500 IU as purified β-carotene in oil in a supplement → 1,050 mcg RAE → 117% of 900. Those two "IU" stories are not interchangeable. A 5,000 IU row that was 100% of the old RDI could be 1,500 mcg RAE if it was all retinol (5,000 × 0.30) or 1,500 mcg RAE if it was all supplemental β-carotene on that 0.30 factor — same factor, different micrograms of *material* in the capsule — or a much smaller RAE if a big share was dietary carotenoid from a powder. If you do not know the split, you cannot convert the old IU. You have to go back to the formula card, in mcg of each form, and apply Table 2.

UL talk is ODS, and it is about *pre-formed* vitamin A. Carotenoids do not sit on that UL the same way. Recheck the live ODS Vitamin A Health Professional fact sheet before you put a UL number in a footnote [VERIFY current ODS UL for pre-formed vitamin A]. A RAE total that is mostly β-carotene is not the same UL object as a RAE total that is mostly retinyl palmitate. The panel will add them. The formula card must still know which is which. Do not write a disease story next to the UL. Do not write "safe because it's carotene" as a claim you have not substantiated.

## Gummy stacks

Gummies like two colors of vitamin A: a retinyl palmitate hit so the RAE is cheap, and a β-carotene hit so the aisle can say "from carrots" or the gummy can stay yellow. Old IU let that stack print as one friendly number. RAE still lets you print one number — the sum — but the sum is honest only after each form is converted.

Example shape, not a SKU: 300 mcg retinol from retinyl palmitate is 300 mcg RAE. 600 mcg supplemental β-carotene in oil is 300 mcg RAE. Together 600 mcg RAE, 67% of a 900 mcg adult DV. In leftover IU, the palmitate piece is 300 ÷ 0.3 = 1,000 IU and the β-carotene piece is 600 ÷ 0.6 = 1,000 IU, total 2,000 IU — 40% of the old 5,000 IU RDI. Same gummy. Old story: 40% DV. New story: 67% DV. That is the "modest in old IU, large in RAE" the spec named, when the stack is heavy in retinol relative to how IU used to score the mix. Flip the ratio toward dietary carotenoid powders and RAE can look *smaller* than a sloppy IU-to-RAE guess that used 0.30 on the whole number.

Do the mcg of each form first. Then apply Table 2. Then add RAE. Then divide by the right RDI column. Do not convert the sum of IU and hope.

Kids' gummies add a denominator change (piece 08, piece 40). The child 1–3 RDI is 300 mcg RAE in the current table [VERIFY]. 600 mcg RAE is 200% on that column and 67% on the adult column. Dual columns must name which is which. Candy cues do not change RAE.

## Optional old units in copy vs the panel

The panel unit is mcg RAE. Full stop. Training pages, a glossary, a "what happened to IU?" FAQ, a clinician sell sheet — those may show the old unit as a translation if the translation uses the right factor for the form you actually sell. They may not replace the panel.

A site table that lists "Vitamin A 5000 IU" next to a bottle that says 1,500 mcg RAE is two documents (piece 42) unless 5,000 × 0.30 = 1,500 and the formula is all retinol. If the formula is mixed, the site IU is a lie or a guess. Delete it or rebuild it from the formula card.

Amazon bullets that still say "5000 IU vitamin A" are labeling (piece 41). If you keep IU in the bullet, put the RAE next to it and make them match. Do not extend vitamin D's parenthetical privilege to vitamin A on the panel. 101.9(c)(8)(iv) allows IU in parentheses after mcg for D, not for A [VERIFY that limit still holds]. Inserts that teach "1 IU = 0.3 mcg RAE" without saying "for retinol" are how mixed stacks go wrong. Write the form. Then write the factor.

## Do not write "prevents night blindness" (that's a deficiency-disease claim shape — name the fence)

Night blindness is a classic vitamin A deficiency sign. A sentence that says a product prevents it is a disease claim in ordinary English: prevent, plus a named deficiency condition. 21 U.S.C. § 343(r)(6) and 21 CFR 101.93(f) allow a narrow nutrient-deficiency-disease shape — FDA's scurvy example in the 2002 SECG is the teaching file — with disclosures about how widespread the deficiency is in the United States, plus substantiation, a 30-day notice, and the disclaimer (piece 21, piece 22, piece 23). That exception is not a PDP slogan. It is not "prevents night blindness" on a gummy.

101.93(g) and the ten criteria (piece 24) are the fence for everything else. This pack will not draft a vision sentence for a SKU. A RAE row is a quantity. ODS may discuss night blindness as a deficiency sign; that monograph is not your product copy. The panel is mcg RAE. The formula card is mcg of each source. If those two disagree, you have a dispute.
