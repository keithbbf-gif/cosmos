A customer who bought "125" thinking IU received 5,000 IU if the panel was mcg. Forty times. That is the conversion factor for vitamin D (1 mcg cholecalciferol = 40 IU), not a serving, and it is how a leftover 2015 eye and a 2016 panel talk past each other. The 27 May 2016 final rule (81 FR 33742) rebuilt Nutrition Facts and pulled Supplement Facts with it. Vitamin D in mcg. Folate in mcg DFE. Vitamin A in mcg RAE. Vitamin E in mg α-tocopherol. IU as the *only* unit on those rows is a 2015 sentence.

FDA later staggered compliance into 2020 and 2021. The 2005 Dietary Supplement Labeling Guide is still the Q&A the agency hosts for how the box is assembled. It is not the printer file for units. Piece 01 already said that. This piece is the unit change. Pieces 10–12 (and 13, next) are the three vitamins that still generate the most spreadsheet errors.

## What the 2016 rule moved

81 FR 33742 (27 May 2016), "Food Labeling: Revision of the Nutrition and Supplement Facts Labels," amended 21 CFR 101.9 and 101.36. The units of measure for the RDI, and therefore for the quantitative amount on both Nutrition Facts and Supplement Facts, changed for vitamin A, vitamin D, vitamin E, folate, and — in the table — niacin as mg NE (the panel still prints "mg"). FDA's August 2019 conversion guidance (docket FDA-2016-D-4484) puts the old and new adult RDIs in one table:

- Folate: 400 mcg (1993) → 400 mcg DFE (2016)
- Niacin: 20 mg → 16 mg NE (declared as mg)
- Vitamin A: 5,000 IU → 900 mcg RAE
- Vitamin D: 400 IU → 20 mcg
- Vitamin E: 30 IU → 15 mg α-tocopherol

Those 2016 adult values are the denominators in 101.9(c)(8)(iv) for adults and children 4 years and older [VERIFY the live eCFR table before a print lock]. Infants, children 1–3, and pregnant or lactating women have their own columns in the same section. Piece 06 is the division. This piece is why the unit on the amount line changed even when the "400" on folate looked the same.

Folate's number stayed 400 and the unit did not. 400 mcg of folic acid is not 400 mcg DFE. It is 680 mcg DFE (× 1.7). A prenatal that still says "folate 800 mcg" without DFE is a 2014 row (piece 11). Vitamin D's old 400 IU RDI was 10 mcg (400 ÷ 40). The 2016 adult RDI is 20 mcg, which is 800 IU. The rule changed the unit and the reference amount. A "100% DV" D row that still treats 400 IU as 100% is wrong on both.

Vitamin A from 5,000 IU to 900 mcg RAE is not a single multiply, because IU hid the mix of retinol and carotenoids (piece 12). Vitamin E from 30 IU to 15 mg α-tocopherol depends on d-alpha versus dl-alpha (piece 13). If your art file still has IU in the amount column as the primary unit for A, D, or E, the file is pre-rule.

A Supplement Facts panel still omits nutrients that are not present (piece 01). Do not import a food-only mandatory row just to look current. Do import the new *units* for any (b)(2) vitamin you actually list.

## Staggered compliance

The 2016 rule's original compliance date did not hold. FDA extended. Manufacturers with $10 million or more in annual food sales were generally expected to use the new labels by 1 January 2020. Manufacturers under that threshold by 1 January 2021 (83 FR 19619 is the extension document the conversion SECG cites). Recheck those dates and the sales threshold if you are writing a history memo for counsel [VERIFY 83 FR 19619 and any later enforcement discretion you intend to rely on]. For a 2026 printer, the stagger is over. "We are a small brand" is not a unit exemption.

Dietary supplements were in the same rule. 101.36(b)(2)(ii)(B) now says the amounts of vitamins and minerals use the units and levels of significance in 101.9(c)(8)(iv). The amount of vitamin D may, but is not required to, be expressed in IU *in addition to* the mandatory declaration in mcg, and any IU must appear in parentheses after the mcg.

If a lot still on a shelf speaks only in IU, it is speaking 2015. That is a labeling problem, not a "customer preference" for the old unit. You may teach the old unit in parentheses on D. You may not make it the only unit on the panel.

## IU in parentheses

For vitamin D, IU is optional and placement is specified: after the mcg, in parentheses. FDA's conversion guidance example for a supplement: Vitamin D 25 mcg (1,000 IU) 125%. The mcg is the legal amount. The IU is a translation for buyers and clinicians who still think in the old unit. ODS still narrates IU on its vitamin D fact sheet. The panel is still mcg.

Vitamin A and vitamin E do not get the same parenthetical privilege as a substitute for the new unit. The 2016 rule retired IU as the panel unit for both. If you want to mention IU in a product insert or a site glossary, that is copy, and it still has to convert correctly (pieces 12–13). It is not a second amount column inside Supplement Facts unless current 101.9 / 101.36 says so — they say it for D. [VERIFY you are not extending the D parenthetical to A or E on the panel.]

Folate prints as mcg DFE, with mcg folic acid in parentheses when folic acid is added or claimed (piece 11). That parenthetical is a form split, not an IU hangover. A parenthetical that says "(5,000 IU)" next to 125 mcg D is the honest translation. A parenthetical that says "(125 IU)" next to 125 mcg is the 40× error in reverse (piece 10).

## Why a 2005 sample label is not camera-ready

Chapter IV still ends with sample labels. Question 4-59 points at them. Those samples are how you see a boxed title, a hairline, a (b)(3) asterisk, an aggregate packet panel. They are also how you see calories from fat, vitamin A and vitamin C as the old mandatory pair, and IU-era thinking in the surrounding Q&A (4-6 still talks about the pre-2016 mandatory list).

Use the 2005 samples as a *history handout* for assembly (piece 01, piece 02). Do not send them to a printer as art. A contractor who says "we used the FDA sample" has told you they used a 2005 Q&A picture. Ask for the current 101.36(e)(10) illustrations and the current 101.9(c)(8)(iv) units. If they cannot produce both, they are not ready to lock type.

The 2005 guide remains the best plain-language walk of serving size, the 100% rule, rose hips versus isolated C, phosphatidylserine, and the omit rule for servings per container. Those answers did not die. The *units and some mandatory food-style rows* did move. A file that mixes a 2005 vitamin A IU row with a 2016 added-sugars sensibility is not conservative. It is a hybrid that exists in no year.

Appendix B and C Daily Value tables in the 2005 PDF are the old numbers. 60 mg vitamin C = 100% lives there (piece 06). Do not paste those appendices into a 2026 spec sheet.

## Printer files that still speak 2015

The scar is a reusable template. A brand updates the front. The Supplement Facts box is a linked graphic from 2015. Vitamin D 1000 IU. Folate 400 mcg. Vitamin A 5000 IU. Vitamin E 30 IU. Sometimes the %DV has been "refreshed" against 2016 denominators while the units stayed IU, which produces a panel that cannot be true both ways. Sometimes the units were converted with the wrong factor and the %DV was left alone.

Ask for the source of the box. If it is a flattened image, rebuild it. If it is a spreadsheet, show the conversion. FDA's 2019 guidance is the worksheet: D, IU × 0.025 = mcg; folate, mcg folic acid × 1.7 = mcg DFE; A, no single IU-to-RAE factor when retinol and carotenoids are mixed; E, different factors for natural and synthetic α-tocopherol. Pieces 10–13 exist because those four lines are where reprints go wrong.

Website Supplement Facts — the HTML table next to add-to-cart — are the same label (piece 41, piece 42). A 2016 bottle and a 2015 grid is two documents. 3PL portals that still offer IU first will turn a compliant bottle into a noncompliant listing if someone picks the first item. The listing is labeling. Convert the template. Then convert the copy. Leave the 2005 sample in the training folder, labeled as history.
