**Disclaimer.** Educational only. Not medical advice. Dietary supplements are not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease (DSHEA; 21 U.S.C. § 321(ff); 21 CFR 101.93). This is not legal advice and not a labeling opinion for a specific SKU.

Phosphatidylserine is FDA's own example of an other dietary ingredient. Chapter IV of the Dietary Supplement Labeling Guide (April 2005), question 4-28, names it when the agency defines the class: dietary ingredients that do not have Daily Values — no RDI, no DRV. You list them by common or usual name. You give a weight per serving. You put a symbol in the % Daily Value column that points at a footnote: **Daily Value not established.** That footnote is the whole status of the row. It is not a decoration you may drop because the panel looks cleaner without it.

A lot of blends look official because they sit inside the box, in the same type as vitamin C, with a milligram and a quiet gap where the percent would be. The gap is the tell. If the symbol and the footnote are missing, the row is borrowing the authority of the %DV column without doing the work. 101.36(b)(3) does not allow that loan.

## What "other dietary ingredient" means

101.36(b)(3)(i) is the class: dietary ingredients for which FDA has not established RDIs or DRVs and that are not regulated under (b)(2). The 2005 guide's one-word example is phosphatidylserine. The aisle's longer list is most botanicals, many specialty lipids, lutein, lycopene, coenzyme Q10, amino acids sold as such, and the contents of a proprietary blend that are not RDI or DRV nutrients.

If it has a Daily Value, it is a (b)(2) row: food-style nutrients and the vitamin/mineral table in 101.9(c)(8)(iv) and (c)(9). If it does not, and it is a dietary ingredient present in the product, it is (b)(3) or it does not appear in the box. Nutrition Facts will not take it (piece 01, Chapter IV, 4-2). You may not invent a %DV for it. You may not print "excellent source of phosphatidylserine" (piece 06, piece 25). You may not park an RDI nutrient inside a (b)(3) blend to avoid declaring its milligrams (101.36(c); piece 16).

"Dietary ingredient" is the DSHEA term (21 U.S.C. § 321(ff)). Cellulose, magnesium stearate, the flavor system, and a magnesium salt used only as a binder are *other ingredients*, 21 CFR 101.4, and they live under the box (Chapter IV, 4-10; piece 17). A (b)(3) row is not the dumping ground for anything that is not a vitamin. If you did not add it as a dietary ingredient, it does not get a milligram in the panel.

Common or usual name is required (4-30). Plant part is required when the ingredient is a botanical (101.4(h); piece 18). Latin binomial is how you keep "ginseng" from being three species. Extract weight and solvent rules are 101.36(b)(3)(ii) (piece 15). None of that is optional because the ingredient "doesn't have a DV so we can be casual." The absence of a DV is why the symbol exists. It is not why the name can be sloppy.

## Where they sit

4-29: list other dietary ingredients in the Supplement Facts panel *following* the listing of dietary ingredients that have Daily Values. 101.36(b)(3)(i) puts them under the heavy bar described in 101.36(e)(6). Walk (piece 02): title, serving, (b)(2) in food order, heavy bar, then (b)(3).

If there are no (b)(2) ingredients to declare — a straight botanical capsule, no vitamins, no measurable food-style nutrients — other dietary ingredients sit directly under the Amount Per Serving heading. You still owe the box, the title, the serving, the weights, and the footnote. You do not owe a fake vitamin block to make the panel look like a multi.

FDA did not specify a mandatory order among (b)(3) rows (4-30). Column or linear display. Weight immediately after the name or in a separate column. Linear is available when you are not also listing constituents. Constituents, if you show them, are indented under the parent ingredient with their own weights (101.36(b)(3)(iii); Chapter IV, 4-33). A 10:1 extract is not ten times the herb in the body (piece 15).

A proprietary blend is a named (b)(3) object with a total weight and indented names in descending order (4-34; piece 16). The symbol still follows those names. The total is the blend, not each herb. RDI nutrients pulled out of the blend sit above the bar, as (b)(2), with amounts and %DVs. That pull-out is how you tell a blend that is hiding calcium from a blend that is actually a list of herbs.

The quantitative amount is the weight of the other dietary ingredient listed, not the weight of a component or source you did not declare (101.36(b)(3)(ii)). "Turmeric (as curcumin 95%)" without an indented constituent is a name-and-weight problem.

## The asterisk is not optional

4-30: you must place a symbol in the % Daily Value column that refers to the footnote "Daily Value not established." If you are not using the column format, the symbol follows the weight. 101.36(b)(2)(iii)(F) and (b)(3) are the paired cites in the 2005 Q&A (4-9, 4-30).

The words matter. "Daily Value not established" is the footnote the guide uses. "No DV," "† not a significant source," or a blank are not the same sentence. A footnote that says "Percent Daily Values are based on a 2,000 calorie diet" is a *different* footnote (below). It does not cover phosphatidylserine. A (b)(3) row that points at the calorie line is pointing at the wrong sentence.

One symbol, used consistently, is enough. You do not need a new glyph for every botanical. You do need the glyph on every (b)(3) row, including each indented name in a blend that is an other dietary ingredient. Skipping the symbol on the blend total and hoping the indented list carries it — or the reverse — is how a reviewer reads the panel as unfinished.

Small and intermediate packages may connect columns with dots instead of hairlines (Chapter IV, 4-53). They do not get a pass on the footnote. The panel asterisk means only this: this ingredient has no Daily Value. It is not the 101.93 disclaimer (piece 22). Do not merge the two footnotes into one blob under the box.

## The calorie footnote is conditional

Chapter IV, 4-55, and 101.36(b)(2)(iii)(D): you are **not** required to place on a dietary supplement the 2,000-versus-2,500 calorie comparison footnote that 101.9(d)(9) requires on conventional foods. You **are** required to include "Percent Daily Values are based on a 2,000 calorie diet" when you declare total fat, saturated fat, total carbohydrate, dietary fiber, or protein.

A vitamin-only tablet with no fat, carb, fiber, or protein row does not owe the 2,000-calorie line. A protein powder that declares protein does. A gummy that declares total carbohydrate or sugars in a way that pulls the carb row onto the panel does. A "greens" drink that lists fiber does. Check which (b)(2) rows you actually triggered (Chapter IV, 4-6). Then decide whether (b)(2)(iii)(D) is on.

The calorie footnote explains the percents on those macronutrient rows. It does not explain a botanical. Printing only the calorie line under a panel that is half phosphatidylserine and ashwagandha is how the (b)(3) rows look like they have DVs based on 2,000 calories. They do not. They need their own sentence.

You may not import the food panel's 2,000/2,500 comparison table to look more like a food. 4-55 said you are not required to. Putting it on in place of "Daily Value not established" is a substitution the rule did not write.

## Skipping the footnote is how blends look official

The costume is familiar. A two-gram "Proprietary Matrix" in the box, names indented or not, no symbol, no "Daily Value not established," sometimes a designer % column filled with dashes or a repeated "†." The milligrams sit in the same field as vitamin C. A buyer who does not know 101.36 reads a fortified food. A buyer who knows 101.36 reads a missing footnote.

That is the point of the symbol. It is the cheap, required disclosure that these rows are not on the DV system. Skipping it is how a blend borrows the look of a (b)(2) nutrient. 101.36(c) already limits what you can hide about weights (piece 16). The footnote is what you cannot hide about status.

A front-of-pack "clinically studied blend" with a clean panel and no asterisk is two costumes (piece 28). Fix the glyph. Then look at the claim. The box is allowed to say that a Daily Value does not exist. Print it.
