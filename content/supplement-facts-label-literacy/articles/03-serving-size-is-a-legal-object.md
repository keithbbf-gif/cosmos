If the directions say take 1–3 tablets with breakfast, the serving size is 3 tablets. FDA wrote that example. Chapter IV of the Dietary Supplement Labeling Guide (April 2005), question 4-4, copies it out of 21 CFR 101.12(b) Table 2, miscellaneous category. One serving of a dietary supplement equals the maximum amount recommended, as appropriate, on the label for consumption per eating occasion, or, if there is no recommendation, one unit — tablet, capsule, packet, teaspoonful. The range is not a menu. The top of the range is the serving.

A lot of catalogs treat serving size as a design choice. Put "1 capsule" on the panel because the milligram looks larger that way, then print "take 1–3 as needed" on the back. That is not a layout decision. That is two documents disagreeing about the legal object the amounts attach to. Piece 42 is the three-document fight. This piece is the object.

## The maximum per eating occasion

101.12(b) Table 2 is the food serving-size table. Dietary supplements sit in the miscellaneous category with a special rule, not with yogurt and not with candy. The reference amount is not a gram weight pulled from a consumption survey. It is whatever the *label* tells a person to take at one sitting, at the high end of that instruction.

"As appropriate" is in the sentence. It is not a loophole that lets you pick the pretty number. It is how the table handles a product that has no directions at all: then the serving is one unit. A bottle that says nothing about how many to take still owes "Serving Size 1 tablet" (or 1 gummy, or 1 scoop). A bottle that says "take 2 in the morning and 2 at night" has two eating occasions. Each occasion is 2. Dual columns for those two servings are piece 08. You do not get to average them into "Serving Size 1" and hope the %DV looks modest.

"Per eating occasion" is also doing work. A suggested-use line that says "take 6 daily" without saying how to split them is an invitation to argue. If the six are one sitting, the serving is 6. If the six are 2 with each meal, the serving is 2 and the day is three occasions. Write the occasion. Then set the serving to the maximum of *that* occasion. Do not leave a reviewer to reconstruct it from a blog.

If you set the serving wrong, every number to the right of every row is attached to the wrong object (pieces 04–06). That is not a rounding issue. That is the panel.

## You may not rename the heading

Chapter IV, 4-5, is one word: no. You must use the term **Serving Size**. 101.36(b)(1) is the cite. You may not print "Dose," "Recommended Use," "Daily Amount," "Usage," or "How to Take" as the heading inside the box. Those phrases can live in a suggested-use sentence *outside* the box. Inside the box, the subheading is Serving Size, left-aligned under Supplement Facts.

Small and intermediate packages may abbreviate to "Serv size" (101.9(j)(13)(ii)(B); Chapter IV, 4-52). That is still the same heading, shortened. It is not a new name.

The value after the heading uses a term that fits the form: tablets, capsules, packets, teaspoonfuls (101.36(b)(1)). "Serving Size 3" with no unit is incomplete. "Serving Size 3 tablets" is the form the rule named. A powder that only says "1 serving" has not named the object. A dropper bottle that says "1 dropperful" owes you a volume or a milligram somewhere the buyer can check — the household term is the serving expression; the metric is how you keep two lots from meaning two different squeezes.

## Household measure plus metric

101.36(b)(1) points at 101.9(b) and at Table 2 together. Food labels pair a household measure with a metric weight. Supplement labels express the serving in the form of the product. For a tablet, "2 tablets" *is* the household measure. For a powder, "1 scoop (12 g)" or "1 teaspoon (5 g)" is how you stop a scoop from being a marketing cup that changes every reprint.

A scoop with no gram weight is a serving you cannot audit. Two tools from two plants will not pull the same heap. Gummies are units: "Serving Size 2 gummies." If you also print a gram weight, that is extra clarity, not a substitute for the count. Liquids use the term the directions speak — teaspoonful, dropperful, milliliters — then the panel matches. Do not write "Serving Size 1,000 mg." A milligram is an amount. The serving is the thing you swallow.

## "Suggested use" that fights the panel

The fight looks like this. Panel: Serving Size 1 capsule. Suggested use: take 1–3 capsules daily with food. The Table 2 example already resolved it. The serving is 3. Every (b)(2) and (b)(3) amount must be the amount in 3 capsules, or you must change the directions so the maximum per occasion is 1.

A softer version: panel says 2 gummies; Amazon bullet says "just one a day." That bullet is labeling if you paid for it (piece 41). The panel is still the legal number. The bullet is now a second serving size. Pick one and make the rest of the catalog agree.

"Take as needed" and "take up to 6" are still maxima. "Up to 6" is 6. "As needed" with no cap sends you back to one unit — and then the sentence is fighting the unit. Write a number. If you cannot stand the %DV that produces, the answer is a smaller recommended occasion, not a smaller heading. A "loading week" on the site that says take 4 is a second maximum and intended-use language (piece 20). "2 before training, 2 after" is two occasions of 2, or one occasion of 4 if they land in the same sitting.

## Per unit is extra, not instead

101.36(b)(2)(iv) lets you declare amounts on a per-unit basis *in addition to* the required per-serving basis. Chapter IV, 4-19, is the yes. Additional columns to the right. "Per tablet" next to "per 3 tablets" is allowed. "Per tablet" *instead of* the serving is not.

The temptation is obvious. A 3-capsule serving of a mineral looks large. A per-capsule column looks like a smaller product. Buyers compare columns. If you print only the small column, you have not labeled the serving. You have labeled a fraction and hoped no one multiplies.

If you add the extra column, label it. "Amount Per 1 Tablet" is language 101.36(b)(2)(i)(A) already permits. The serving column is mandatory. The unit column is a courtesy. Per-day totals are not a substitute. A "daily value pack" that lists 24-hour sums in the only column has skipped the eating-occasion object. You may need more than one column if morning and evening packets differ (101.36(e)(8); piece 08). You may not replace serving with day because the day is a nicer story.

## Kids and prenatal columns (pointer)

FDA has more than one set of Daily Values. Chapter IV, 4-23 and 4-27, point at four: adults and children 4 or more years; infants; children under 4; pregnant or lactating women. When you show more than one %DV column, you must identify each column. "For children under 4 years of age" is a heading, not a color band.

Serving size is still the maximum per eating occasion for *that* product as labeled. A children's gummy whose directions say "ages 4+ take 2, ages 2–3 take 1" is two populations and, if you put both on one bottle, a column problem. Chapter IV, 4-54, and 101.36(i)(1) restrict which %DVs you may print on products for children under 4. Piece 40 is kids, gummies, and candy-dose math. Piece 08 is how the columns are built. Do not invent a "kids serving" that is half an adult serving so the %DV looks like a food.

Prenatal directions that say "take 2 daily" have a serving of 2 unless you split the occasions and label them. The folate row on that serving is mcg DFE, not a 2014 "800 mcg" leftover (piece 11). Fix the object first. Then fix the unit. If the rows look wrong after you do that, the formula is the problem, not the heading.
