Cohen PA, Avula B, Wang YH, Katragunta K, and Khan I published "Quantity of Melatonin and CBD in Melatonin Gummies Sold in the US" in *JAMA* on 25 April 2023 (JAMA. 2023;329(16):1401-1402; doi:10.1001/jama.2023.2296). The paper is a label-versus-assay scar. It is not a bedtime protocol, not a children's dosing chart, and not permission to photograph a child with a handful of gummies. This piece will not reprint their milligram table. The scar is enough: what the labels declared and what the assays found were not the same thing.

Piece 04 is the container math — a 60-count bottle with serving size 2 is 30 servings. This piece is the kids' column, the candy cue, and the paper that made the gummy aisle a records problem.

## What Cohen published

The authors started from melatonin gummy brands entered into NIH's Dietary Supplement Label Database in 2021, purchased what they could in September 2022, and dropped bottles that did not actually say "melatonin" on the label they received. Twenty-five products were analyzed. The paper's own summary is the part you may quote: twenty-two of twenty-five were inaccurately labeled; only three sat within ±10 percent of the declared melatonin quantity; one product had no detectable melatonin and did contain cannabidiol; among products that contained melatonin, assayed quantity ranged from 74 percent to 347 percent of the labeled quantity. Five products declared CBD. Serotonin was not detected. [VERIFY] those summary figures against the *JAMA* page before you put them in a retailer memo; do not reconstruct the per-SKU table from memory.

The authors stated a limitation you should keep in the same paragraph as the scare: one sample per brand. They did not claim to have mapped lot-to-lot drift. They did not assay every gummy form on the U.S. market. They did not convert a dietary supplement into a drug by writing the paper.

What the paper is for, in this pack, is a reading habit. Amount per serving is a legal number (piece 05). Added dietary ingredients are supposed to meet 100 percent of declared (101.9(g)(3)–(4) through 101.36(f)). A gummy is not exempt because it is chewy. If your lot file cannot show the declared melatonin — or whatever else you printed — you have Cohen's scar with your name on the carton.

CBD in a product sold as a dietary supplement is a separate agency file. FDA's 26 January 2023 conclusion still stands unless a later dated page says otherwise: the food and dietary-supplement frameworks are not appropriate for CBD. [VERIFY] that dated FDA page before you argue a listing. This piece will not recommend CBD, melatonin, or any other ingredient for a sleep condition.

## Serving size on a gummy

21 CFR 101.12(b) Table 2 still treats a dietary-supplement serving as the maximum amount recommended per eating occasion, or one unit if there is no such recommendation. "Take 1–2 gummies" makes the serving 2 gummies. The heading must say "Serving Size" — not "dose," not "suggested," not a cartoon of two bears.

Household measure plus metric still applies. "2 gummies (4.4 g)" is a complete serving line if 4.4 g is true. "2 gummies" with no metric is the incomplete version. A front-of-pack "1 gummy bedtime" burst that fights a serving of 2 is the PDP-versus-box fight from piece 03.

Net contents versus days of use is piece 04. A bottle that looks like a month is a month only if serving size is 1, or if the count divides cleanly by the real serving. Sixty gummies at 2 per occasion is thirty occasions. If the occasion is for a child and an adult ends up using the same bottle, you now have two users and a serving that was not written for that math. Do not solve it with a cute calendar.

101.36(b) lets you add a per-unit column. That column is extra, not instead. "Per 1 gummy" next to "per 2 gummies" can help a parent count. It cannot shrink the legal serving to the pretty number.

## Children's %DV fences

The 2005 Dietary Supplement Labeling Guide, Chapter IV, question 4-54, is still the Q&A FDA hosts. On products for children less than 2 years of age, other than infant formula, you must not declare calories from fat, calories from saturated fat, saturated fat, polyunsaturated fat, monounsaturated fat, and cholesterol. On products for children less than 4 years of age, you may not include %DVs for total fat, saturated fat, cholesterol, total carbohydrate, dietary fiber, vitamin K, selenium, manganese, chromium, molybdenum, chloride, sodium, or potassium.

Current 21 CFR 101.36(i)(1) points at the special labeling provisions in 101.9(j)(5)(i) for foods other than infant formula represented or purported to be specifically for infants through 12 months of age and children 1 through 3 years of age. 101.36(b)(2)(iii)(E) says %DV is based on adult-and-children-4-or-more values unless the product is specifically for infants through 12 months, children 1 through 3 years, pregnant women, or lactating women — in which case the column heading names the group. The 2016 rule (81 FR 33742) changed the old "children less than 4 years" category language. Use the 2005 guide for the *shape* of the fence. Use current 101.9 / 101.36 for the *column you are about to print*. [VERIFY] the current RDI/DRV tables for the age group on the label before the file goes to the printer. Piece 09 is the unit-change companion.

An adult %DV column on a bottle purported for a two-year-old is a wrong denominator. Dual columns are legal when the product is for more than one group (101.36(e)(11) pattern). They are not a place to hide an adult serving behind a children's cartoon.

Protein %DV has its own infant omission in 101.36(b)(2)(iii). Do not invent a protein grade to make a gummy look like a food.

## Candy cues as labeling

PHOTO_NOTES for this piece is one line: typeset the serving math; do not show kids with candy. That is not a mood board preference. It is intended-use and typicality.

A gummy that shares a shape, a sparkle, a sugar crust, or a "just like the ones in the dish" line with candy is telling a household that this unit is food-like. Serving size is a maximum per eating occasion. Candy cues push the occasion toward a handful. FDA will read the pictures, the flavor name, and the PDP burst as labeling. FTC will read them as the claim a reasonable consumer takes away (December 2022 guidance; 16 CFR 255 if a child or parent endorsement is in the ad).

Do not write "tastes like candy so kids will actually take it" on a carton, a Reel, or a chat macro (piece 41). That sentence is a use instruction. It is also how you teach a child that the bottle is a snack. This pack will not supply that photograph and will not supply that line.

Color and flavor still belong under the box (piece 17). Allergens still belong on the "Contains" line (piece 37). Sesame in a flavor system is not a candy surprise. It is 403(w).

## Adult bottles with kid flavors

Sour watermelon, "burst," and cartoon fruit are not an age grade. 101.36 asks who the product is *represented or purported* to be for. If the PDP, the Amazon title, and the suggested use point at children, you have a children's column problem even if the brand also sells an adult SKU in the same flavor.

If the product is purported for adults, keep the adult %DV column, keep the serving honest, and keep the candy cues from implying a household snack. A nightstand bottle that looks like the child's bottle is a mix-up you designed. Different cartons, different closures, different serving lines.

Sleep language stays inside the crowded room of piece 31. FDA used "Soothing Sleep" as a name that needs context; occasional sleeplessness is not insomnia. This piece will not write a melatonin protocol, will not name a pediatric dose, and will not turn Cohen into a reason to take more. The paper is about labels that did not match assays. Your job is a serving size that matches suggested use, a %DV column that matches the age you purported, and a lot file that can stand next to the milligram you printed.

If those three disagree, you do not have a gummy brand. You have a dispute (piece 42). Hold the printer.
