FDA will composite 12 packages or 10 percent of the lot, whichever is smaller. Chapter IV of the Dietary Supplement Labeling Guide (April 2005), question 4-41, is 21 CFR 101.36(f)(1) in Q&A form. The packages are randomly selected. The number FDA will compare to your label is not the best bottle in the case and not the front-of-pack milligram. It is a composite, and the composite is judged against the amount you declared *per serving*.

That is why a 30-serving bottle that prints "1,500 mg" on the shoulder when the serving is 50 mg is not a bold claim. It is a different arithmetic object. The panel speaks per serving. The inspector composites packages and asks whether the serving they reconstruct from your directions is in the bottle. Piece 03 set the serving. This piece is the number to the right of the name.

## Per serving is the legal number

101.36(b)(2) requires the quantitative amount by weight of each (b)(2) dietary ingredient in one serving. 101.36(b)(3)(ii) does the same for other dietary ingredients. The heading is **Amount Per Serving**, or language consistent with the serving size: "Amount Per 2 Tablets," "Each Tablet Contains," "Amount Per Teaspoonful" (Chapter IV, 4-18; 101.36(b)(2)(i)(A)). You may place that heading over a column of amounts (4-17). You may put the amount immediately after the name instead of in a column (4-16). You may not put the amount per bottle in that slot and call it a serving.

Units follow 101.9. Fat in grams. Vitamin C in milligrams because the Daily Value is in milligrams. Vitamin D in mcg after the 2016 rule, not IU as the sole panel unit (pieces 09–10). Folate in mcg DFE (piece 11). Vitamin A in mcg RAE (piece 12). Chapter IV, 4-21, still sends you to 101.9(c)(8)(iv) for the unit on each vitamin and mineral. Recheck that table before you print [VERIFY current 101.9(c)(8)(iv) units].

The amount is the weight of the dietary ingredient, not the weight of the source salt. Calcium carbonate is not the row. Calcium is the row (Chapter IV, 4-15; 101.36(b)(2)(ii)). Piece 14. If you declare 1,000 mg and that 1,000 is the carbonate, you have labeled the rock, not the mineral.

%DV is calculated from the *unrounded* quantitative amount, except that for certain macronutrients you may use the rounded label amount (Chapter IV, 4-24). Piece 06. The amount and the percent are two presentations of the same serving. They are not two products.

A proprietary blend's number is the blend total, not each herb (101.36(c); piece 16). That total is still per serving.

## Extra columns

You may add a per-unit column (101.36(b)(2)(iv); Chapter IV, 4-19). You may add columns when servings differ — one tablet in the morning, two at night (4-20). You may add %DV columns for more than one population (4-27). All of that is extra. The required number remains the amount in the serving you declared in the heading.

"Amount Per 2 Tablets" is a legal heading when 2 tablets is the serving. It is not a way to print the 1-tablet number as the only number. If 2 is the serving and you want the 1-tablet split visible, that is a second column, labeled. Piece 08.

A per-day column is allowed as an addition when you have actually constructed the day from labeled occasions. It is not a replacement that hides a 3-capsule serving inside a "daily" sum. The composite FDA will make is packages, opened, measured against the serving on the panel.

## The 100% rule for what you added

Chapter IV, 4-43, is the compliance sentence people file under "overage" and then forget. Dietary ingredients you specifically added must be present at 100% of the volume or weight you declared, except for a deviation attributable to the analytical method. Less than that, and the product is misbranded. Dietary ingredients that are naturally occurring must be present at 80% of the declared value. The cites are 21 CFR 101.9(g)(3) and (g)(4), applied through 101.36(f).

"Specifically added" includes a vitamin you isolated from a natural source and a vitamin you made synthetically. The 2005 guide says that out loud. Isolated ascorbic acid is added. Synthetic ascorbic acid is added. Both owe 100% of the declared milligrams in the composite. Class-II nutrients on a food label — the 80% band — are the naturally occurring ones.

Overage is how a plant tries to still be at 100% at end of shelf life. It is not a permission to declare 50 mg and fill 40. The exception in 4-43 is a deviation *attributable to the analytical method*. The judgment is 100% if you added it, 80% if it was already in the rose hip.

101.36(f)(1) is the sample: 12 consumer packages or 10% of the packages in the same inspection lot, whichever is smaller, randomly selected, composited. You do not get to hand them the retain that tested high. If it is not technically feasible to comply, 101.36(f)(2) points at a written request under 101.9(g)(9). Chapter IV, 4-42, still lists an old office address. Check the current FDA contact before you send [VERIFY current office and address for 101.9(g)(9) petitions]. Until you have that permission, the 100% rule is the rule.

## Rose hips vs isolated C

The guide's own pair is the one to keep. If you add vitamin C that was isolated from a natural source, or made synthetically, the 100% rule applies. If you added rose hips and you are declaring the vitamin C that comes with those rose hips, that vitamin C is naturally occurring and the 80% rule applies (Chapter IV, 4-43).

Those are different formulas. A row that says "Vitamin C 90 mg (from rose hips)" is a naturally-occurring story only if the 90 mg is actually the C in the hips and you did not also spike isolated ascorbic acid. A row that says "Vitamin C 90 mg (as ascorbic acid)" is added. A row that says "Vitamin C 90 mg (as ascorbic acid and rose hips)" is a mix. The added portion still owes 100% of what you attributed to the isolate. Do not use the hips as a costume for a short-fill of the isolate.

The ingredient you *added as the dietary ingredient* is the 100% object. The vitamin that happens to be in a food you added is the 80% object. A (b)(3) blend weight you added still owes 100% of that declared weight. You do not get 80% off a 500 mg ashwagandha extract because ashwagandha grows in dirt.

## A bottle-front milligram that ignores serving

The PDP loves a big number. "1,500 mg" on the front of a 30-serving bottle. "10,000 mg per bottle." "A month of C in every jar." None of those phrases is Amount Per Serving. If the serving is 50 mg, the legal row is 50 mg. The front is either a per-container sum — which a reasonable buyer will take as the serving if you do not say otherwise — or it is a different serving size you did not put in the box.

Nutrient-content claims have their own rule (21 CFR 101.13, 101.54; piece 25). A front milligram can also be an implied claim. This piece is the narrower error: the front number and the panel number are attached to different objects. Fix the object. If you want the front to say 1,500 mg, either the serving contains 1,500 mg or the front must say 1,500 mg *per bottle* in words a reasonable person cannot miss, and you accept that FTC will still read the net impression (piece 27). Hiding "per bottle" in 4-point type under a 1,500 is how a front becomes a second panel.

Per-day banners have the same shape. "500 mg daily" on a product whose serving is 250 mg twice a day is true only if the directions are two occasions of 250. The panel must show 250 if one sitting is the serving. Amazon bullets that repeat the bottle-front milligram and omit the serving are labeling (piece 41). The composite does not read the shoulder sticker. It reads the panel, builds the serving, and assays that serving's worth of contents. Keep the legal number in the box, per serving, in the unit 101.9 named, at 100% of declared if you added it. Do not print the spreadsheet total where Amount Per Serving belongs.
