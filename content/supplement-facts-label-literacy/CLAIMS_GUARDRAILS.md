## The statute, not the vibe

Under DSHEA (Pub. L. 103-417) and 21 U.S.C. § 321(ff), a dietary supplement is a food category. It is not a drug. A product is a drug if it is intended to diagnose, cure, mitigate, treat, or prevent disease (21 U.S.C. § 321(g)(1)(B)). Intended use is read from labels, websites, social posts, influencer scripts, and customer-service macros — not from what the formulator meant.

Structure/function claims are a defined exception (21 U.S.C. § 343(r)(6); 21 CFR 101.93): truthful, not misleading, substantiated *before* first marketing, notified to FDA within 30 days, and paired with the disclaimer that the statement has not been evaluated by FDA and the product is not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease.

FDA's 6 January 2000 final rule (65 FR 1000) added 101.93(f) and (g). The Small Entity Compliance Guide (9 January 2002) is the plain-language map of the ten disease-claim criteria. Those criteria are how this pack teaches "no."

FTC sits on the same URL. *Health Products Compliance Guidance* (December 2022) replaced the 1998 dietary-supplement advertising guide. Competent and reliable scientific evidence must match the claim *as a reasonable consumer would take it*.

## Never-say list (on-site / on-label / in a Reel you paid for)

### Disease and implied disease

- Diagnose, treat, cure, mitigate, or prevent any named disease.
- "Protective against the development of cancer." "Reduces the pain and stiffness associated with arthritis." FDA used those as disease-claim examples (65 FR 1000; SECG).
- Characteristic signs used as a wink: "relieves crushing chest pain," "reduces cholesterol" (without the "already in the normal range" fence), "inhibits platelet aggregation" as a stroke story.
- Product names that swallow a disease: "CarpalHealth," "CircuCure" (SECG examples).
- Class names that are drug classes: analgesic, antibiotic, antidepressant, antiviral, vaccine.
- "Supports the body's ability to resist infection." "Supports the body's antiviral capabilities." FDA called those disease claims. "Supports the immune system," without more, is the SECG's example of a *general* structure/function shape — still needs substantiation, still needs the disclaimer, still fails if the rest of the page does the disease work.
- COVID / flu / "natural vaccine" / "protocol."
- Before/after bodies, "replaces your [drug]," "works with your chemotherapy."

### Quality costumes that become claims

- "FDA approved" for a dietary-supplement SKU.
- "FDA registered facility" as a quality mark. Registration is a list.
- "cGMP certified by FDA."
- "Pharmaceutical grade." Not a defined USP mark for dietary supplements.
- "Heavy-metal free." Say tested; result; method; lab; lot.
- "Clinically proven to [disease or implied disease]."
- "Clinically studied ingredient" presented as if *this SKU* was in the trial.
- Invented science partner, lab director, or plant we do not operate.

### White-label and catalog

- Showing another company's label, lot, or plant photo as if it were ours.
- Promising a 3PL or retailer that a PDF will clear Prop 65, Amazon, or a sports-org screen when the serving-day math has not been done.
- SARMs, BPC-157, unapproved peptides, or "research chemicals" described as dietary ingredients.
- CBD as an interstate dietary supplement. FDA's 26 January 2023 conclusion still stands unless a later dated page says otherwise. Check the date.

## Allowed shapes (still need substantiation)

- "Contains [dose] of [ingredient], as [form]. Serving size [X]."
- "Supplement Facts per 21 CFR 101.36. Other dietary ingredients listed below the hairline."
- "Supports [normal structure/function]" with a notified, evidence-backed claim and the DSHEA disclaimer.
- "This statement has not been evaluated by the Food and Drug Administration. This product is not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease."
- Teaching that a *quoted* sentence is a disease claim, when the quote is FDA's or FTC's example and the draft does not adopt it as product copy.
