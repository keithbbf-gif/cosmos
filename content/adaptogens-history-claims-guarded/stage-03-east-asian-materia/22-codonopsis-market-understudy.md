Every prestige plant grows an understudy.

*Codonopsis* — *dangshen* (党参) in the Chinese file, several species in the Campanulaceae, not an Araliaceae cousin except in the shopkeeper's heart — is the understudy English writers like to call "poor man's ginseng." I hate the phrase and I have to discuss it, because the phrase is the history.

This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent any disease. It is not medical advice. It is a substitution story.

Substitution is ordinary. If a court root is expensive, a kitchen and a clinic will look for something that can stand in a formula without bankrupting the pot. *Dangshen* has a Chinese life as a qi-tonifying root with a milder, more food-like reputation than *Panax* in many practical talks. "Milder" is a traditional contrast, not a laboratory ranking I ran, and not a promise. It is a reason a market could use it when ginseng was the moon.

The insult in "poor man's ginseng" is the same insult as "Siberian ginseng," only ruder. It says the plant's job is to be a cheaper *Panax*. Sometimes, in a formula, that is the job it was asked to do. A job in a formula is not an identity. *Codonopsis* also has its own entries, its own regional species, its own sweet root that people actually like to eat. Reducing it to a discount is a prestige economy talking.

Nörr's 1993 Munich dissertation, which the EMA reflection lists, included *Codonopsis pilosula* among plants investigated in an adaptogen-shaped project. That is a fact about a dissertation, not a coronation. It shows the European bench, like the aisle, was willing to let an understudy sit the Soviet exam. Sitting an exam is not passing a disease test I will not write.

I want to stay with the **market** a moment longer, because markets teach claims. A shop that sells *dangshen* as *dangshen* is doing one kind of honesty. A shop that sells it as ginseng is doing another. A shop that sells it as an adaptogen is doing a third: filing a substitution history under a functional class so that the poverty and the prestige both disappear into a blend. The blend is soothed. The history is not.

Species muddles are real here. *C. pilosula*, *C. tangshen*, others, regional common names, dried roots that look like pale ginseng if you want them to. Identity is not a side issue. It is the issue that determines whether your historical sentence is about a plant or about a wish.

I will not tell you when to use *dangshen* "instead." Instead is a clinical or culinary decision for people who have a formula and a teacher, or a recipe and a grandmother. I do not have your formula. I have a folder that refuses to become one.

The understudy also belongs in this series because it makes the **class look hungry**. Hungry classes recruit substitutes. Substitutes then recruit the class to raise their status. *Codonopsis* gets to stop being poor. *Adaptogen* gets to look like it has a Chinese depth it did not earn in 1969. Mutual flattery, again.

If you take a habit from this draft, take this: when you see "poor man's X," ask who is being called poor, who is being made the rich original, and whether either plant consented to the comparison. Then ask whether a third noun has been added to make the comparison look scientific. If the third noun is *adaptogen*, you are looking at a three-card monte.

A last sentence I want on the page: **a cheaper root can be a more honest food.** Honesty of price is not honesty of indication, but it is not nothing. A pot of *dangshen* that admits what it is beats a capsule that pretends to be a court.
