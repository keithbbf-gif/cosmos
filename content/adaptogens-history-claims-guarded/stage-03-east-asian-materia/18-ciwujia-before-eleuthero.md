Before Vladivostok made a shrub famous in English, the shrub already had a Chinese name.

*Ciwujia* (刺五加) — "spiny five-add," if you want the characters to do a little work — points at *Eleutherococcus senticosus* and at a cluster of related talk in Chinese materia. I am not going to pretend I have a first-print date for every appearance. I am going to insist on a simpler historical order: **the Chinese name is not a translation of *adaptogen*. The Soviet noun is the later arrival.**

This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent any disease. It is not medical advice. It is a priority dispute.

Chinese medical language for this plant, in the accounts I will trust at a distance, sits with wind-dampness talk, with *wei* and sinew talk, with a northern, somewhat rustic reputation compared to *Panax*'s court glamour. Those are system-internal sentences. If I render them as "for arthritis" or "for fatigue" I have changed the system. I will not render them that way. I will say: the plant had jobs in a language that already had jobs.

The characters themselves are a small ethnography. Spines you can feel. Five, which may nod at leaflets or at a naming family that likes fives. *Jia* as a naming element shared with other Araliaceae talk. A farmer could use that name without a theory of nonspecific resistance. A farmer's name is a kind of document.

When Brekhman's school took the shrub, they did not need to erase *ciwujia* to do their work. Erasure happened later, in English, when "Siberian ginseng" and then "eleuthero" became the handles. *Eleuthero* is a decent trade name. It is also a forgetting. Forgetting is how a Chinese rustic plant becomes a Russian discovery story becomes an American powder.

I care about this order because of the **colonizing direction**. If you say "ciwujia is the traditional Chinese adaptogen," you have made the older name a costume for the newer class. If you say "eleuthero is a shrub that Chinese materia already knew, later redescribed in a Soviet functional language," you have kept the sequence. Only the second sentence belongs here.

There is a modern Chinese-English supplement literature that does the costume move in both directions: TCM terms dressed as adaptogens, adaptogens dressed as TCM. I am not the police of that literature. I am the police of *this* folder. In this folder the costume comes off.

A reader who knows Chinese medicine will find this draft thin. Good. Go read a *bencao* entry and a contemporary Chinese monograph with someone who can navigate both. This page is a signpost, not a substitute for that navigation. Signposts that try to be textbooks start inventing indications.

What I will add, because it is easy to miss: **part and preparation again.** Root bark versus root, decoction versus a later standardized eleutheroside talk — these are not the same objects. Soviet papers that chase eleutherosides are doing twentieth-century marker chemistry. Marker chemistry is a tool. It is not the *ciwujia* of a Ming compiler, and it is not a reason to imply a treatment.

The EMA monograph path, as I said in draft 11, grabbed this plant as Eleutherococci radix. Latin pharmaceutical names are yet another layer. A European pharmacist saying "radix" and a Chinese physician saying *ciwujia* may be pointing at related material and still not be saying the same thing about it. Layers are allowed. Merged layers become a blend.

If you take one habit from this draft, take the habit of asking **which name came first in the sentence**. "The adaptogen ciwujia" and "ciwujia, later called an adaptogen" are not stylistic variants. They are different claims about time. Time is the only thing this series has to be strict about besides disease verbs.

I have not told you to prefer Chinese over Soviet descriptions. Preference is a school loyalty. I have told you not to let the later school date-stamp the earlier name. Date stamps are how aisles write history.
