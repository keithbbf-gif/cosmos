A fungus that eats a moth larva on a high pasture is already a story. The story did not need a Soviet noun. It needed a landscape, a season, a pair of hands, and later a price that could wreck a hillside.

*Ophiocordyceps sinensis* (Berk.) G.H. Sung et al. — older labels still say *Cordyceps sinensis* — sits in Tibetan and Chinese material life as *yartsa gunbu*, *dongchong xiacao*: summer grass, winter worm. The name is a life cycle in four characters. I will stay with the life cycle and the trade, because those are things a history can hold without becoming a locker-room rumor.

This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent any disease. It is not medical advice. It is a high-altitude commodity with a late celebrity.

The older use-talk, in the sources I will not pretend to have recension-checked line by line, sits with tonic and restoration language in Chinese and with local Tibetan economies that are not my mine to romanticize. Restoration language is a traditional register. I will not translate it into a modern endocrine panel. I will say: people collected a strange, expensive object and filed it with other strange, expensive objects.

The late twentieth century added a **price spike** and a global rumor. Popular retellings like to drag in Chinese athletes in the 1990s. I am not going to reconstruct those meets, those coaches, or those accusations. Sport rumors are how a fungus acquires a performance claim, and performance claims are how a folder like this gets pulled toward a stack. The historical fact I will keep is smaller: **the fungus became famous enough, and expensive enough, that cultivated substitutes and other *Cordyceps* species (*C. militaris* in factories) became industrial objects.**

A factory fruiting body is not a wild larva-fungus. A powder standardized to cordycepin talk is not a caterpillar. If a label says *cordyceps* without a species and a method, you are looking at a mood.

What about *adaptogen*? The noun shows up because the aisle already had a drawer for "expensive restoration objects from Asia." Eleuthero taught the drawer to speak Russian. Cordyceps filled the drawer with a pasture. The pasture does not owe the drawer a definition.

There is an ethics here that is not a health claim. High-pasture collection can be a livelihood and an ecological pressure at once. A history that only talks about "ancient Tibetan wisdom" and never about price per kilo is a brochure. A history that only talks about overharvest and never about the people collecting is a lecture from far away. I am not close enough to write the village chapter. I am close enough to refuse the brochure.

I will not tell you that a cultivated *C. militaris* "is just as good." Good for what? The *what* is the indication I will not name. I will tell you they are different objects with a shared marketing umbrella, and that umbrellas leak.

If you came to this draft because a blend promised "Himalayan adaptogens," look at the geography. *Ophiocordyceps sinensis* is a Tibetan Plateau and high Himalayan story. *Eleutherococcus* is a Far-East woodland story. *Rhodiola rosea* is a circumpolar and mountain story of another sort. Stacking them as Himalayan is a travel agent's lie. Travel agents are not ethnobotanists.

Form, again, because a pasture object is unusually easy to fake with a photograph. A wild stroma still attached to a larva is a specific, seasonal body. A rice-grown *C. militaris* fruiting body is a factory body. A mycelial biomass on grain is a third body that can be labeled with the same English word if no one is watching. Three bodies, one aisle word: that is the identity problem from draft 28 in a high-altitude coat. I will not rank the three for an outcome. I will say that a history which cannot tell them apart is already a caption.

The life-cycle name remains the most honest sentence in the file. Winter worm, summer grass. A larva, a fungus, a season. You can sit with that sentence and not add a disease. I recommend the sitting. I do not recommend a product. Sitting is free.
