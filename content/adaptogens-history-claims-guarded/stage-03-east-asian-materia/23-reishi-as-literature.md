The immortality mushroom is a literary object that later learned to sit still in a bag.

*Lingzhi* (灵芝), *Ganoderma* in the Latin that made a brand possible, "reishi" in the Japanese-to-English handle that American shelves preferred — this is a fungus with a painting history. You have seen it, even if you have not named it: the varnished, kidney-shaped, almost lacquered presence in a scholar's still life, a Daoist motif, a carved scepter, a wish for long life that belongs to **pictures and poems** before it belongs to a capsule.

This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent any disease. It is not medical advice. It is a warning about what happens when a motif gets a milligram.

Long-life talk in Chinese literature is not a trial endpoint. It is a cosmological and aesthetic vocabulary: peaches, cranes, mountains, a fungus that looks already varnished by time. If I take that vocabulary and say "therefore *Ganoderma* promotes longevity" as if I had a Kaplan–Meier curve, I have laundered a poem. This folder will not launder poems.

The materia-medica file exists too. *Lingzhi* has entries, grades, color talk, a rarity story of its own. Rarity, as draft 16 said, is a political economy. Cultivated *Ganoderma* is now a crop. The crop can be a honest fungus farm. It cannot be the painted mountain, and it cannot be an adaptogen in the Brekhman sense just because an American page needed another ingredient.

Why did reishi get the noun?

Because the aisle needed a mushroom after the 1990s taught shoppers to want mushrooms. Because "immortality" is the most expensive prestige suffix of all. Because a Soviet class about resistance to adverse factors sounds, to a copywriter, like a prose version of not dying. That sound is a claims leak. I am sealing it. **A literary immortality is not a disease-prevention claim we will finish.**

I will not list the conditions modern pages hang on this fungus. The list is long and exactly the kind of wandering a regulator would call a problem. Wandering is the literary motif's revenge: a symbol that meant "the whole wish" gets asked to do the whole wish in biochemistry.

There is also a Japanese file (*reishi*, *mannentake*) and a modern cultivation-and-export file. Those are real. They deserve historians of agriculture and of Kampo. They do not deserve a sentence that begins "reishi is an adaptogen that…"

If you like the paintings, look at the paintings. A fungus that artists treated as a scepter is a fungus that was already doing social work: rank, wish, display. Display is the ancestor of the gift box and the ancestor of the Instagram jar. Seeing the ancestor is a way not to be fooled by the jar.

I have a small affection for the fact that the fruiting body looks almost artificial when it is real. It confuses the handmade and the grown. Adaptogen blends like that confusion. They want a product that looks like nature and like a technology. *Lingzhi* was confusing in that way before factories. That is a visual history note. It is not a reason to chew a slice tonight.

When a blend says "reishi, cordyceps, lion's mane" and then, in smaller type, "adaptogen complex," you are looking at a **mycological celebrity summit** plus a Soviet hat. Hats can be removed. The fungi still have their own names, their own farms, their own literatures. Leave them their literatures. Literatures do not need to become indications to be worth reading.

And if a clerk tells you this mushroom is why Daoists lived a long time, you may tell the clerk that Daoists also had poems, diets, politics, and a talent for metaphor. Metaphor is not a capsule. I like metaphor more than I like the clerk's sentence.
