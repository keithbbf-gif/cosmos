*Wu wei zi* (五味子) is a better teacher than most definitions, because the name is already an argument about the mouth.

Five tastes: the Chinese medical set, not a tasting-menu gimmick. A berry that can be talked about as sour and sweet and the rest is a berry that a system could file under more than one movement. I will not turn the five tastes into a cartoon of five organs. I will say that **the plant was interesting, in that literature, as a sensory and directional object**, not as a 1960s resistance powder.

This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent any disease. It is not medical advice. It is a berry with too many later jobs.

In Chinese materia, *Schisandra chinensis* (Turcz.) Baill. — and the related southern talk that sometimes points at *S. sphenanthera* — shows up with astringing and gathering language, with lung and kidney talk in the Chinese sense, with a reputation that later English pages flatten into "liver" and then flatten again into a cleanse. Flattening is the enemy. I will stop at: the berry had a file, the file had tastes, the tastes had a system.

The Soviet file is different. Reviews say wartime and pre-war Russian journals discussed *limonnik* as a stimulating and tonic sort of preparation, and that the berry sat on the adaptogen shortlist next to eleuthero and *Panax*. Same species neighborhood, different verbs. If you merge "astringe" and "nonspecific resistance" you get a smoothie of systems. This folder serves the systems in separate bowls.

The chemistry that later reviews love — schisandrins, lignans — is a third file. I will mention that the file exists and then close it. Marker compounds are how a berry becomes a standardized product. Standardization is a historical event of the late twentieth century, not a Ming process. A lignan is not a taste. A taste is not a trial endpoint.

There is a fourth file, the one that most threatens our fence: English-language pages that make schisandra a "liver herb" and then make liver a metaphor for everything you regret drinking. I will not give that metaphor any more air. A Chinese system-sentence about *gan* is not a hepatology clinic, and a hepatology clinic is not a place this essay will convert into a product hint.

What I like about the five-taste name, for a claims-guarded series, is that it is **empirical in a household way**. You can argue about the list. You cannot pretend the name was coined in a branding meeting. It asks a reader to notice the berry, not to notice a disease. I would like more plant names to do that job.

Preparation, again, because I will keep saying it until it sticks: berry versus seed, wine versus decoction versus a later extract standardized to a lignan percentage. Nanai hunters, in the trail draft 20 takes up, are described as using berries and seeds on the move. A dropper bottle in a backpack is performing that story, not repeating it.

If you meet *wu wei zi* in a formula, you are meeting a **combinatory** tradition. Chinese herbal writing is often about relationships among items, not about a celebrity solo. Celebrity solos are an aisle habit. The adaptogen class, which wants a plant to be a general answer, pulls against the formula habit, which wants a plant to be a specific move among other moves. I am not saying one habit is truer. I am saying they are different habits, and the celebrity habit is the one that writes disease claims.

A last caution about "five." Western wellness copy loves fives: five tastes, five elements, five minutes of your morning. The copy is trying to make a system look like a lifestyle. Lifestyle is how a berry becomes a brand. Leave the number in the Chinese name. Do not let it become a routine I am not giving you.

I have not said schisandra is or is not an adaptogen. Membership is the Soviet school's exam. The berry did not sit that exam in the Song. It sat in a jar with a name you can still taste if you are lucky enough to meet the actual fruit and not a "berry flavor."
