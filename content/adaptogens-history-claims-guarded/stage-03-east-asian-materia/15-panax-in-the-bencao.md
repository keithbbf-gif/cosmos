The plant later called *Panax ginseng* C.A. Mey. did not wait for a Soviet noun. It waited, if it waited for anything, for commentators.

What English speakers flatten as "the *Shennong Bencao Jing*" is a textual tradition: a materia medica attributed to the Divine Husbandman, compiled and recopyied, dated by scholars in arguments I am not going to fake-settle. Later *bencao* — Tao Hongjing's apparatus, the *Zhenglei*, Li Shizhen's *Bencao Gangmu* — keep returning to ginseng as a superior (*shang pin*) sort of item in a three-tier moral-pharmacological ladder that is not our OTC ladder. Superior, in that old sorting, is closer to "can be taken toward a long-view nourishment without being classed as a harsh attack on a named evil" than to "FDA-friendly daily gummy." Even that paraphrase is already too modern. The Chinese categories have their own teeth.

This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent any disease. It is not medical advice. It is a walk around a plant in a literature.

The literature talks in **qualities and directions**, not in ICD codes. Ginseng is discussed in the language of *qi*, of spleen and lung in the Chinese medical sense (which are not the autopsy organs with the same English names), of a body that can be depleted or collapsed. If I translate those sentences into "for fatigue" I have already done the aisle's first theft. If I translate them into "for immune health" I have done the second. I will not do the third, which would be to translate them into *adaptogen* and then smile as if the Han had been waiting for Lazarev.

What a historian can say without leaving the fence:

- Ginseng had **prestige**. Prestige is political. It shows up in tribute, in gift, in the price of a root that looks like a person.
- Ginseng had **identity anxiety** even before Latin binomials. Wild mountain roots, garden roots, lookalikes, regional names — the tradition is full of warnings about what you are actually holding. That anxiety is a cousin of draft 28.
- Ginseng had **form**. Decoction is not a tea bag. A prepared root (red, steamed; white, dried) is a technological object. A later standardized extract is another. Equating them is a category error with a kettle.

Li Shizhen, in the late Ming, is the name Western herb books like to drop because a name makes a citation. I will drop it only to say: a sixteenth-century encyclopedia is an encyclopedia. It gathers, argues, sometimes repeats. It is not a trial register. Citing Li as if he were a principal investigator is a costume party.

There is a missionary and later colonial layer, too: Jesuits, export, the English word *ginseng* itself traveling with American *P. quinquefolius* as a trade rival. The *Panax* genus name, from Linnaean habits and a Greek "all-heal" flavor, is already a little embarrassing for a claims-guarded series. *All-heal* is a disease claim hiding in a genus. I will use *Panax* because it is the handle, and I will wince when I do.

What I will not do is the "emperor's herb" paragraph that ends with a wink about stamina in the bedroom or clarity in the office. Those winks are how traditional literature gets mined for modern indications it did not consent to. Court medicine had court bodies. Your body is not a court.

The Soviet school put *Panax* on the shortlist because it was the prestige plant of the same forests that offered eleuthero. That is a geographical and rhetorical fact. It is not a finding that ginseng "meets the definition" in a way that should matter to your week. Meeting a 1960s definition is a paper event.

If you grew up with ginseng as a cultural food — a slice in soup, a gift box at a New Year, a story your grandfather told about a mountain — that practice does not need my noun and does not need my fence to be a practice. The fence is for the moment someone turns the soup into a caption that implies a treatment. Soup can stay soup.

I have now written several hundred words without telling you what ginseng "does." That omission is the draft. A *bencao* entry is a dense object. It deserves better than a verb stolen from a supplement fact sheet. Read an entry with a scholar of Chinese medicine if you want the system on its own terms. Read this folder if you want to see how that system gets raided by a later word.

And if a shop tries to sell you "ancient adaptogenic ginseng," you now have two objections ready. *Ancient* does not attach to *adaptogen*. *Ginseng* does not need the adjective to have a history. The adjective is there to make a court plant look like a 1960s class, and to make the class look like a court plant. That two-way flattery is the whole trick.
