Some plants become famous in the century that photographs them.

*Gynostemma pentaphyllum* (Thunb.) Makino — *jiaogulan* (绞股蓝), "southern ginseng" in the prestige-suffix habit I have already complained about — has a local southern Chinese and neighboring life as a tea-ish, weedy, sweet-bitter vine. It also has a late-twentieth-century popularization story that English-language wellness treats as if a Han emperor had personally filed the herb under *adaptogen*.

This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent any disease. It is not medical advice. It is a fame timeline.

I will be conservative about the older file, because conservatism is the only way not to invent a dynasty. A plant can be used locally for a long time and still have a **thin elite textual record**. Thin records get padded by modern prefaces. Prefaces love "used for centuries by mountain people." Mountain people are not a citation. If a local tea habit existed, it existed as a local tea habit. That is already interesting. It does not become more interesting when you add a fake memorial.

The later file is thicker: chemical interest in gypenosides (a name that wants to sit next to ginsenosides and get invited to the same party), longevity-village journalism of the sort that should make a historian reach for a red pen, export tea bags, and the adaptogen filing cabinet. I will not take you through the longevity-village genre except to say it is a **genre**. Genres have plots. Plots want a plant to be the reason a village is old. Villages are old for many reasons, including luck, water, census artifacts, and the journalist's choice of village.

"Southern ginseng" is the naming theft again. *Gynostemma* is a cucurbit relative, not an Araliaceae. The theft is chemical-rhetorical: saponin talk that sounds like *Panax* talk. Sounding like is not being. I will not rank saponins. I will say that a vine named for a prestige root is a vine whose marketers were nervous it would not be bought as itself.

Why does late fame matter for the fence?

Because late fame is **easier to mistake for discovery**. A 1980s paper plus a 1990s tea bag plus a 2010s blend looks, to a shopper, like "science has now confirmed the ancients." The timeline may actually be: local use, then chemistry, then a noun from 1960s Russia that was never about this vine, then a photograph. Confirmation is not what happened. **Recruitment** is what happened.

I have no quarrel with a person who likes *jiaogulan* as a tea in a southern kitchen. Kitchen tea is a practice. I have a quarrel with the caption that makes the kitchen into a laboratory and the laboratory into a dynasty.

If you are a writer, the discipline is to give *jiaogulan* a **short century** unless you can point at a named older text. Short centuries are allowed. Not every plant in this folder needs a millennium. The ones that get millennia without texts are the ones that should make you suspicious.

The vine also teaches a small lesson about weeds. A plant that grows like a weed is a bad luxury object and a good household object. Adaptogen branding prefers luxury. Tea prefers the kettle. When you see the vine in a gilt tin, someone has spent money to make a weed look like a tribute root. Look for the weed. Weeds are more honest.

I will not tell you that gypenosides "do what ginsenosides do." I do not even tell you what ginsenosides do; that would be a mechanism draft with a disease shadow. I tell you the names were seated next to each other on purpose. Purpose is a historical fact.

Late fame is not a moral failing of the plant. The plant was busy being a vine. Fame is something people do to vines when they need a new ingredient that is not already patented by a neighbor's brand. You can watch that happen without buying the moral that the vine was waiting to save you.
