*Insam* is a better word to start with than *ginseng* if you want the Korean file and not the airport file.

I am not a Korean court historian. I am a reader of the fact that a peninsula made a plant into a state-shaped object: tribute to larger neighbors, later a national emblem, a grading craft, a gift economy, a rural crop with a city reputation. The plant is still *Panax*. The story is not "the same as China, smaller." It is a different set of institutions standing on a related materia.

This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent any disease. It is not medical advice. It is a national-plant history without a disease punchline.

Court medicine in Joseon, as later scholars describe it, sat in a Chinese-literate world and also in a Korean one. Formularies, local substitutions, arguments about what grows here versus what must be imported — that is ordinary East Asian medical history. Ginseng sits in it as a Korean product that other places wanted. Wanting is a historical engine. The Qing border, the Japanese market, the later American one, all have their own wanting.

Red ginseng (*hongsam*) as a steamed, dried technology is a **manufactured object** with a national story attached. Steaming is not a footnote. It changes what you are holding: color, storage, a craft standard, a way to make a seasonal root into a year-round gift. When a modern company sells a standardized extract, it is standing on that craft whether it says so or not. Standing on a craft is not the same as continuing it.

I will not recite branding slogans from Seoul department stores. I will say that **the gift box is a document**. A box that presents a root as a filial object, a corporate object, or an export object is telling you which social relation the plant is being asked to carry. Filial is not a clinical endpoint. Corporate is not a *bencao*. Export is not an adaptogen definition.

Why does this belong in an adaptogen folder at all?

Because English-language adaptogen lists always include *Panax* and then, lazily, let Korean advertising do the rest of the sentence. The rest of the sentence is often a stamina wink or a middle-aged wink. I am leaving the winks on the table and walking away. Korean cultural practice around *insam* does not need a Soviet noun to be intelligible. The noun needs *Panax* to look ancient. That is an asymmetric friendship.

There is also a scientific-national story in the twentieth century: institutes, export standards, papers in international journals. Those papers have endpoints I will not turn into advice. A nation-state that funds ginseng research is doing political economy. You can study that without concluding that a root is a treatment, and without sneering at people who grew up with the soup.

I have eaten ginseng in soup. I mention that only to refuse the testimonial form. I do not know what the soup "did." I know it tasted like a particular bitter-sweet and that the meal had a social meaning. Social meaning is allowed. Testimonials about outcomes are how a history essay becomes a stealth label.

If you are Korean, or in a Korean family, and you are reading a white-English folder about your plant, you have a right to be suspicious. This draft is not trying to own *insam*. It is trying to stop a later noun from owning it. The fence is for the noun. The soup was never the noun's to begin with.

A practical reading habit: when a page says "Korean adaptogenic ginseng," remove the middle adjective and see whether the sentence still has any historical content. Usually you are left with "Korean ginseng," which is a real object with a real craft, and a hole where a 1960s class used to be flattering everyone.

I will not rank Korean, Chinese, and American roots. Ranking is a shop gesture. I will say they are different political economies sharing a genus, and that the genus is already a Linnaean convenience. Convenience is not a reason to stack them in a blend and call the stack a tradition.

The last thing *insam* needs from me is a cure. The last thing the adaptogen word needs is another nation's prestige. I am introducing them and then separating them, like guests who should not have been seated together.
