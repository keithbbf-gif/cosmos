I do not have a Nanai elder on this page. I have a chain.

The chain, as later reviews tell it, runs through V.L. Komarov in the 1890s and V.K. Arsenyev in the early 1900s: Russian botanical and travel work in the Far East and northern Manchuria, reporting that Nanai hunters (Goldi, in older exonyms) carried *limonnik* berries and seeds as a tonic sort of provision — something talked about in the neighborhood of thirst, hunger, exhaustion, and night movement. Panossian and Wagner, among others, repeat that chain when they want a pre-war ethnopharmacological start for schisandra research.

This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent any disease. It is not medical advice. It is a walk along a **cited trail**, with the citations still attached.

A trail is not nothing. Hunters in a taiga and river world using a local berry is a completely ordinary human event. Ordinary events are what ethnobotany is for. The trouble begins when the trail is asked to do three extra jobs:

1. Prove that the berry "works."
2. Prove that the Soviet program was only discovering what indigenous people already knew, in the exact Soviet sense of knowing.
3. Prove that you, in a different climate with a different job, should buy a dropper.

Job 1 is a clinical question this folder will not finish. Job 2 is a category theft: Nanai provision-talk is not nonspecific-resistance talk. Job 3 is a sales conversion. I refuse all three.

What I will keep is the **ethics of the chain**. Komarov and Arsenyev are not Nanai voices. They are Russian observers with their own genres (flora, travel, military-adjacent exploration). Later Soviet scientists who liked the berry had reasons to like a hunter story: it makes a laboratory interest look like respect. Respect that does not return land, language, or authorship is a familiar colonial shape. I can name the shape without pretending I have repaired it.

Exonyms are part of the shape. *Goldi* is a name in someone else's mouth. *Nanai* is the name later writing prefers. *Limonnik* is a Russian plant-name that smells of lemon because the fruit can. Each name is a layer. If I only say "Siberian hunters used adaptogens," I have erased every layer and invented a noun they did not use.

What might a hunter's use look like, as a historical reconstruction rather than a recipe? A portable berry, a seed, a season, a night river, a need to keep moving. That reconstruction is already too neat. Real provision is messier: mixed foods, sleep, stories, weather. A single plant celebrity is what *we* want because we have aisles. A hunt is not an aisle.

Night vision talk, which some retellings include, is a good example of a **phrase that wants to become a claim**. I will leave it inside the chain ("later writers say the observers mentioned night-time seeing") and I will not walk it toward an ophthalmology hint. Eyes are how essays get cute and then get sued in spirit if not in court.

If you are Nanai, or from a neighboring Amur people, and this chain has been used to sell a powder without you, you do not owe the powder your approval. This draft is not asking for a blessing. It is asking English-language writers to stop treating your reported practice as a preface to their class.

For the rest of us, the useful discipline is **citation hygiene**. Say "as reported by X, as repeated by Y." Do not say "the Nanai have always known that schisandra is an adaptogen." Always is a lie-clock. Known is a stolen verb. Adaptogen is a stolen noun.

I like this trail more than I like most origin stories, because it is narrower. A people, a landscape, a berry, a pair of named observers, a later laboratory that wanted a preface. Narrow stories are harder to turn into world-historical cures. I will keep it narrow on purpose.

And I will say again, because the fence needs the repetition: a hunter carrying a berry is a historical image. It is not a protocol for your night shift, not a proof, and not a reason to write a disease on the next line.
