A monograph is a filing cabinet with a logo.

The World Health Organization's plant monographs — the series many herb people have photocopied — are easy to wave as a blessing. A blue logo looks like a verdict. A verdict is not what a monograph is. A monograph is a **catalog entry**: names, parts, a gathering of reported uses, a gathering of reported chemistry and reported studies, a gathering of reported cautions, at a time of compilation. Catalogs are useful. Catalogs are not oracles.

This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent any disease. It is not medical advice. It is a genre note.

I am not going to inventory which WHO volume mentioned which plant. Volumes have dates. Dates go stale. The genre does not go stale. The genre says: *here is what a committee or a set of authors was willing to put in one place*. Willingness is already a filter. Filters are historical.

When an adaptogen blog writes "WHO recognizes…" it is usually doing the same move as "FDA-recognized" (draft 36): turning a catalog into a roll call. Recognition, in ordinary speech, means a gold star. A monograph means "we wrote a chapter." If the chapter includes traditional-use sentences, those sentences are still traditional-use sentences. They do not become WHO-endorsed disease treatments unless you are willing to misread a catalog as a prescription pad. This folder is not willing.

Compare a WHO chapter to a *bencao* entry and to an EMA reflection. All three are documents with institutional voices. The *bencao* speaks in a medical system. The reflection speaks in a regulatory grammar about a noun. The monograph speaks in an international public-health compilation grammar. Three grammars. If you collapse them into "the authorities say take this for X," you have invented a fourth grammar called **the aisle**.

Cautions in monographs are the part sellers skip and skeptics enlarge. Both skip-and-enlarge habits are lazy. A caution is a historical object in the file: someone thought a risk was worth typing. Typing is not a ban and not a dare.

Chemistry sections are the part that makes a chapter look modern. Lists of constituents are lists. They are not mechanisms I will narrate toward a condition.

Study sections are the part that ages fastest. A compilation that stopped its search in a given year is a photograph of a literature, not the literature's future. Photographs are allowed if you caption the year.

Why include this draft in an adaptogen series? Because the noun, being hungry, will try to eat any institutional logo that walked near a plant. WHO walked near many plants. The eating must be stopped at the caption: **catalog, dated, not a blessing, not a prescription.**

If you are a student, use monographs as a **bibliography generator**. The references are often the real gift. Then go to the references, if you can, and see whether they bear what the blog said they bear. This series tried to do a smaller version of that with the EMA PDF and the *HerbalGram* dating footnote. We did not re-compile WHO. We are telling you the genre so you can read it without kneeling.

Kneeling is for churches. Catalogs are for shelves. Shelves are where this folder lives, too. I would like our shelf to be honest about being a shelf.

A last, slightly unkind sentence: if a writer's only institutional citation is a logo, the writer has not read the chapter. Read the chapter. Then decide what kind of sentence it can bear. Most chapters can bear "this plant has a file." Fewer can bear what the aisle wants.
