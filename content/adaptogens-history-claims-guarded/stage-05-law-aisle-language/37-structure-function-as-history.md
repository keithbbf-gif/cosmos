"Supports healthy stress response" is not a sentence from the *Bencao Gangmu*. It is a grandchild of a statute.

Structure/function language — the U.S. supplement style that talks about a structure of the body or a function of the body without naming a disease — is easy to mistake for science, because it uses the cadence of science. It is also easy to mistake for tradition, because it uses the warmth of tradition. It is neither. It is a **legal dialect**.

This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent any disease. It is not medical advice. It is a dialect history.

A dialect has tells. The verbs are *supports*, *promotes*, *helps maintain*. The nouns are *healthy*, *normal*, *already-normal*. The forbidden twins are the disease name and the verb that would attach to it. A good copywriter can dance on the line until the dance is the product. This folder refuses the dance even when the verbs are legal, because we are not filing a notification. We are writing history.

Why does the dialect love *adaptogen*?

Because the Soviet definition is already halfway there. "Nonspecific resistance" and "normalizing" are structure/function-shaped hopes. They do not name diabetes. They name a function of an organism. A 1994-shaped aisle could pick the noun up without translation. The translation had been done in 1969, in a different country, for a different reason.

The dialect also loves **stress**, the Selye word that escaped into ordinary English. Stress, in the dialect, is not a 1936 syndrome and not a workplace protocol. It is a feeling the shopper already has. "Supports the body's response to stress" lets the shopper finish the sentence with whatever they brought in the door. Finishing the sentence is the shopper's job and the copywriter's wish. It is not a historian's job.

I will not provide a how-to for "compliant" claims. Compliance is a trade with lawyers. If I wrote a how-to, I would be helping the aisle in the name of analyzing it. Analysis stops at: **this speech style exists; it has a date; it is not a *bencao*; it is not a trial result.**

There is a folklore that structure/function sentences are "weaker" versions of what the company really believes. Sometimes that is true in a marketing meeting. A history essay should not become the place where the stronger sentence is finally said. If a company believes a disease claim, they have a drug gate to think about. If a writer believes a disease claim, they can write a different folder with different ethics. Not this one.

Another folklore: that adding "traditional" to a structure/function sentence makes it ethnobotany. "Traditionally used to support healthy…" is a hybrid that often has no traditional sentence behind it. Traditional sentences, as draft 02 said, have languages and parts and documents. If you cannot name those, *traditionally* is a costume.

I want readers to hear the dialect as **dated**. 1995 English on a bottle is as dated as 1969 English in an *Annual Review*. Dating a sentence takes its glamour off. A dated sentence can still be legal. Legal is not the same as historical.

When I write about plants in this series, I am trying to use a third dialect: objects, names, years, refusals. It is uglier than *supports*. It will not sell a tub. It will let you see the tub as a document. Seeing is the most I can offer.

If you work in the industry and you are angry that I am unkind to your verbs, I will say this: the verbs are doing a hard job in a constrained statute. Hard jobs deserve to be named as jobs. They do not deserve to colonize every other genre. Leave the essays alone. Leave the *bencao* alone. Use your verbs on the label, with the disclaimer, and do not ask a history folder to finish your dance.

And if you are a shopper, try reading a label out loud with the date 1994 inserted after every *supports*. It sounds comic. Comic is a good solvent for a dialect that wants to sound timeless.
