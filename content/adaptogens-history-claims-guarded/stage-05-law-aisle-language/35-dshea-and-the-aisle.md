1947 and 1969 are not the years that built the American shelf. 1994 is.

The Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act of 1994 is the statute-shaped weather under most U.S. bottles that want to talk about a body without becoming a drug. I am not your lawyer. I am not going to walk every subsection. I am going to say, as history: **after DSHEA, a plant powder could live in a grocery as a "dietary supplement" with a structure/function style of speech and a disclaimer style of speech, rather than having to live as a food only or as a drug.** That change is bigger, for the word *adaptogen*, than any *bencao*.

This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent any disease. It is not medical advice. It is a statute as a historical climate.

Before the climate, herbs in the U.S. had a messier retail life: foods, folk products, a post-1990s industry that wanted a lane. After the climate, the lane had a name. Names attract nouns. *Adaptogen* arrived as a useful noun because it sounded functional (structure/function loves a function) and because it did not, on its face, name a disease. "Supports the body's response to stress" is a cousin of the Soviet sentence, rewritten for a disclaimer.

The disclaimer — the one you have read so many times your eye skips it — is part of the climate. It says the statement has not been evaluated by the FDA and the product is not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease. This folder says a version of the same sentence without selling you a product. The difference is that we are trying to mean it in the body of the essay, not only in the footer.

DSHEA did not define *adaptogen*. It defined a **place** where such a word could earn rent. Places matter. A word that lives in a monograph, a word that lives in a *bencao*, and a word that lives next to protein bars are three words.

There is a standard critique of the statute — under-regulated, over-permissive, a gift to an industry — and a standard defense — access, tradition, a middle lane. I am not picking a Senate side. I am pointing at an effect: **the aisle became a museum of functional nouns.** Nootropic, thermogenic, adaptogen. Each noun is a little Soviet in spirit (a class by hoped-for function) and a little American in law (not a drug if you do not say the disease).

For ethnobotany, the climate is a mixed gift. Plants became easier to find and easier to strip. You can buy *wu wei zi* in a strip mall. You can also buy a blend that has forgotten which part of *wu wei zi* it used. Access without identity is how traditional indications go feral.

If you are reading this outside the United States, your weather is different: EU traditional herbal registrations, Canada's natural-health-product lane, Australia's listed medicines, a dozen other grammars. Do not export DSHEA as the world's natural law. This draft is about the climate that made *adaptogen* a grocery word in one large market.

A practical reading habit: date the bottle's language. If the language is "ancient wisdom" plus a 1994-shaped disclaimer, you are looking at a **hybrid document**. Hybrids are allowed. Unlabeled hybrids are how people think a Han compiler invented a structure/function claim.

I will not tell you the statute made supplements safe or unsafe. Safety is a person, a dose, a contaminant, a drug interaction. I will tell you the statute made a speech style common. This series is a long argument that the speech style should not colonize history essays. You can describe a plant without talking like a label. If you cannot, you are not writing history. You are writing packaging without a UPC.

The aisle is not a villain. It is a climate. Plants, workers, shoppers, and copywriters live in it. A guarded folder lives in it too, which is why we keep saying the disclaimer in the body. If we only said it in a footer, we would be doing what the aisle does. We are trying not to.
