The twentieth century did not give *adaptogen* a folk song. It gave the word a stack of reviews.

I have already named the stack as a crossing (draft 14). This draft is about the **stack as a habit** — what review articles do to a field, and what they do to a claims fence.

This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent any disease. It is not medical advice. It is a little sociology of a genre that ate a noun.

A review article is a compression. Compression is a kindness to the outsider and a danger to the file. Kindness: you can learn that a Russian-language pile exists without reading a thousand papers. Danger: the pile becomes a table, the table becomes a paragraph, the paragraph becomes a blog's "research shows." Each compression drops a method and keeps a verb.

Wagner, Nörr, Winterhoff (1994). Panossian, Wikman, Wagner (1999). Davydov and Krikorian (2000). Panossian and Wagner (2011). Later reviews I am not turning into a syllabus. Each of these is a document with authors who had laboratories, institutes, or long-standing arguments. Authors are not oracles. They are people with a point of view. Panossian and Wagner say, in so many words, that the internet is full of bad compilations used by opponents to discredit a field they believe in. That sentence is interesting as a **social fact**: by 2011 the noun had enemies and defenders, and both were reading garbage. Garbage is part of the history.

Davydov and Krikorian, in a skeptical key, are useful as a reminder that a review can be a closer look rather than a parade. Closer looks still compress. They just compress toward doubt.

What a claims-guarded reader does with a review:

1. Names it (authors, year, journal).
2. Names its job (crossing, defense, closer look, mechanism speculation).
3. Refuses to lift its efficacy tables into an indication.
4. Steals, if anything, its **footnotes** — a dating quarrel, a citation to a congress, a remark about Soviet diagnostic categories.

Item 4 is how this series used Panossian and Wagner: we took the 1958 dating footnote and left the clinical enthusiasm on their page.

Reviews also manufacture **canons**. A plant that appears in four successive reviews becomes "classic." A plant that appears in none becomes a footnote (draft 13). Canons feel like nature. They are editorial. Once you see the editor, you can ask who paid for the issue, who supplied the extract, who needed a European face. Asking is not an accusation of fraud. It is literacy.

The century of the review is also the century of the **PDF**. PDFs travel. Travel is how a 1994 *Phytomedicine* paper ends up in a 2020s Shopify caption. The caption will not tell you the paper is a review. "Paper" is enough to sound like a trial. Genre-stripping is a claims technique. Put the genre back.

I will not pretend I have read every paper under every review. That pretense is how writers become oracles. I have read enough of the crossing, and the EMA's reading of the pile, to know that **a pile is not a proof of a class in the authorisation sense**. That is enough for a fence.

If you are a scientist who writes reviews, I am not mocking the genre. I am asking you to imagine a shopper downstream who will never see your hedging. Hedge harder, or accept that your compression will be de-hedged. This folder is a hedge that got out of the lab and tried to stay a hedge.

If you are a shopper, the practical move is to hear "a review" as "a second-hand document." Second-hand is not worthless. It is not first-hand. First-hand would be a specific trial you could describe, or a specific traditional sentence you could locate. This series prefers those, and when it cannot have them, it says so.

The review-article century is not over. There will be another adaptogen review next year, with a nicer diagram of heat-shock proteins. Diagrams are pretty. Pretty is a claims risk. I will look at the year, the authors, the funding if it is stated, and I will not let the diagram finish a disease sentence. That is the whole literacy.
