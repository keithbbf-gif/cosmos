I keep meeting the sentence "FDA-recognized adaptogen" as if a clipboard in White Oak had a roll call.

The U.S. Food and Drug Administration does not maintain an official adaptogen drug class. It does not publish a blessed list of plants that have passed a membership exam. It does not, by having once been cited in someone's review article, anoint the noun. If a label or a blog tells you otherwise, the label is doing folklore about an agency.

This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent any disease. It is not medical advice. It is a negative inventory: **what is not there**.

Absence is a fact that both sellers and a certain kind of skeptic misuse.

Sellers misuse it by implying recognition that does not exist, or by treating a lack of a warning letter as a gold star. Agencies do not gold-star most of the grocery. Silence is not a monograph.

Skeptics misuse it by saying "the FDA doesn't recognize adaptogens, therefore the plants are a scam." The agency's job is not to complete your ethnobotany. Plenty of real cultural practices have no FDA class. Plenty of real chemicals are regulated under other headings. Absence of a class is a fact about a noun's legal status, not a fact about a root's existence.

What the FDA *does* have, in the broad public sense relevant here, is a distinction between **drugs** (disease claims, a heavier gate) and **dietary supplements** (a DSHEA-shaped gate), plus a long ordinary interest in adulteration, contamination, and claims that cross the disease line. I am speaking at textbook altitude, not as a counselor on your warning letter. The altitude is enough to say: *adaptogen* lives, when it lives legally in the U.S. grocery, on the supplement side by not saying the disease. The moment a seller says the disease, they have changed gates.

Later reviews sometimes mention that "some health authorities" have used adaptogen as a functional term, and they wave at late-1990s citations. A functional term in a secondary citation is not a list you can download from FDA.gov. If you cannot download the list, do not print the list.

Compare the EMA paper. Europe, at least, wrote a public reflection that named the noun and refused it as authorisation terminology. That is a document. The U.S. story is more like a **gap** that the industry filled with a word. Gaps are interesting. They are not recognitions.

Why do people want the clipboard?

Because a class feels safer than a plant. A plant has a part, a dose problem, a lookalike, a caution. A class has a definition you can memorize. If an agency would only bless the definition, the memorizing would feel like science. This folder's bet is the opposite: **memorize the plants' names and the documents' dates, and let the class stay an argument.**

I will not tell you the FDA "should" create a class. I am not in that business. I will tell you that writers who invent a class-list are doing the same violence as writers who invent a 1947 certainty. They are filling a silence with a slogan.

A practical test for a sentence: replace "FDA-recognized adaptogen" with "a plant later writers filed under a Soviet functional noun, sold in the U.S. as a dietary supplement." If the second sentence is too long for the label, the label was too small for the truth.

Negative inventories are unsatisfying. They do not give you a hero. They give you a cleared table. The next drafts will put structure/function grammar and WHO monographs on that table, as objects. The table stays cleared of a fake roll call.

If an agency later publishes something I have not seen, the draft can be revised with a URL. Until then, the honest caption is: **no list**. No list is a historical statement you can defend. A fake list is a claims leak that does not even need a disease to do harm. It harms the file.
