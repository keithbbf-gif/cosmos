Definitions look like fences. Some of them are sales floors.

The adaptogen definition that English-language writers keep photocopying comes, with small wording drift, from Brekhman and Dardymov at the end of the 1960s, as later quoted by the EMA herbal committee and by almost every review that wants a paragraph before the plant table. I will give you the committee's four-line version, because it is public and because the fourth line is the one popular pages drop:

- almost non-toxic to the recipient;
- nonspecific in its pharmacological properties, raising resistance to a broad spectrum of adverse biological, chemical, and physical factors;
- a regulator with a normalizing effect on various organ systems;
- the effect is more pronounced the deeper the pathologic changes.

Three clauses if you stop where the aisle stops. Four if you keep the document honest.

This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent any disease. It is not medical advice. It is a reading of a definition as a historical object — a wish-list that later writers treated as a membership test.

**Clause 1, "almost non-toxic."** This is doing ethical work and marketing work at once. Against stimulants and against frank poisons, the school wanted a substance you could imagine using repeatedly. "Almost" is doing a lot. Licorice, which sometimes gets dragged into tonic talk, has a long-known mineralocorticoid-like story around glycyrrhizin. Many plants are "almost" until the dose, the part, the adulterant, or the person changes. A membership test that begins with innocence will be tempted to forget the almost. This series will not.

**Clause 2, "nonspecific."** I give this word its own draft (09) because it is the engine. Here I will only say: a laboratory may mean "we used more than one insult on the bench." An aisle may mean "we need not name a use." Those are opposite ethics. The clause also lists biological, chemical, and physical factors. Psychological factors sneak into later restatements. Each added family of insults makes the class more glamorous and less testable.

**Clause 3, "normalizing."** This is the clause that sounds kind. It is also the clause that eats pharmacology. A drug that raises a low number and lowers a high number toward "normal" is a regulator in a story. In a clinic, you still have to say *which number*, *which person*, *which risk*. Without that, "normalizing" becomes a way to imply benefit in every direction at once — the closest a wellness sentence can get to a perpetual motion machine. Historically, you can see why a school that had read Selye wanted this word: if the organism is displaced by a load, a counter-load that restores is the dream. Dreams are not indications.

**Clause 4, the pathology gradient.** Popular adaptogen writing often omits this line because it is awkward. It says, in effect, that the deeper the pathology, the bigger the effect. That is a research hypothesis with a dangerous shadow. The shadow is: *so use it when someone is really sick.* The EMA quoted the line as part of the historical definition and then, in the same paper, refused the term as an authorisation class and noted that the available clinical packages did not establish efficacy in a clearly defined clinical condition. I will follow the refusal, not the shadow. A historical definition may mention pathology. This folder will not convert that mention into a treatment hint.

Membership tests have a second life as **exclusion tools**. Panossian and Wagner remark that some traditional tonics, when people bother to look, do not meet the "true adaptogen" profile the school wanted. That sentence can be used honestly ("the class, if it exists, is narrower than the aisle") or dishonestly ("buy *our* true adaptogen"). Honesty stops at the first use.

There is a third life, the one that matters for our fence: **a definition that is functional rather than botanical will recruit any plant a writer likes.** Once the test is "nonspecific resistance + safety + normalization," a mushroom, an Andean hypocotyl, a mineral pitch, and a courtyard basil can all sit for the exam. Some will be given honorary degrees they never asked for. Drafts 23, 30, 32, and 29 are about those honorary degrees.

I am fond of old definitions the way I am fond of old maps: they show you what someone needed the world to look like. Brekhman's world needed a class that was not a stimulant, not a single-symptom drug, and not a poison. That is a coherent wish. The wish does not mint a disease treatment, and it does not mint a folk category that villagers already had.

If you must memorize something, memorize the **genre**. This is a 1960s functional definition from a Soviet review culture, restated by later Europeans who then declined to let it authorize a medicine. It is not a verse from a *bencao*. It is not FDA language. It is not a reason to take a capsule tonight.

When a modern page says "adaptogens are defined as…," ask: defined by whom, in which year, with how many clauses, and who refused the definition later? If the page cannot answer, it is not defining. It is incanting.
