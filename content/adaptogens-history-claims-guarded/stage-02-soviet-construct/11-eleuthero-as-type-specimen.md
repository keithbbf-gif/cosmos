If the Soviet school needed a plant that could carry the noun *adaptogen* into quantity, it found a shrub.

*Eleutherococcus senticosus* (Rupr. & Maxim.) Maxim., Araliaceae — eleuthero in the name the U.S. trade was eventually pushed toward, *ciwujia* in Chinese, "Siberian ginseng" in a fight I will not repeat here except to point at draft 12 — is the type specimen of the English-language story even if it is not the oldest plant in the story. *Panax* had the prestige. Eleuthero had the kilos.

This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent any disease. It is not medical advice. It is a portrait of a shrub as a **logistical and rhetorical** object.

Look at the family first. Araliaceae is also *Panax*'s family. That botanical cousinhood made a metaphor easy: if you cannot get enough true ginseng, here is a relative with its own Chinese record. Metaphors are not chemistry. A cousin in a flora is not a substitute in a body, and this page will not say it is. It is a reason a pharmacologist in the Far East could put the two plants in the same sentence without feeling insane.

Look at the Chinese record second. *Ciwujia* does not begin in Vladivostok. It has a materia-medica life that draft 18 takes up. The Soviet program did not invent the shrub. It **redescribed** the shrub in resistance language and then published enough that later Europeans, when they needed an example, reached for Eleutherococci radix. The EMA's 2008 reflection paper says as much: the committee was drafting a community herbal monograph on eleuthero root when it found itself having to think about the adaptogenic concept in general.

That is a remarkable bureaucratic accident. A root monograph forced a philosophy paper. The philosophy paper then declined to let the philosophy authorize a medicine in the ordinary EU sense, while allowing the concept to be considered in the traditional-use assessment. If you want a single public document in which eleuthero is the type specimen, that is the one.

Look at the English reviews third. Wagner in the 1990s, Davydov and Krikorian in 2000, Panossian in several venues — eleuthero is the plant they cannot avoid. Davydov and Krikorian's title even promises "a closer look," which is a polite way of saying the slogan had gotten ahead of the shrub. I am not going to strip-mine that paper for mechanisms. Mechanisms are how essays acquire disease shadows. I will take the *genre*: a long ethnopharmacological skepticism aimed at a star example.

Why did eleuthero travel so well?

**Supply.** A shrub you can cultivate or gather in more than a ceremonial kilo is a program plant. Wild *Panax* is a political economy of rarity (draft 16).

**A functional story already written.** Once the three-clause definition existed, eleuthero was repeatedly pointed at as meeting it. Repetition is a kind of type-specimen designation. The plant becomes the example you use to explain the word, and the word becomes the reason you notice the plant.

**A European monograph path.** Traditional herbal-medicine regulation needs a species, a part, a named preparation. "Adaptogen blend" is not that. Eleuthero root is.

What eleuthero is *not*, in this folder:

Not a proof that the class is real in the authorisation sense. The committee that stared at it hardest said the term was not appropriate for a marketing authorisation.

Not an ancient Russian folk category that villagers called *adaptogen*. They did not.

Not "Siberian ginseng" as if the word ginseng were free.

Not a treatment I am offering you for the conditions that later study lists wander through. Those lists — you will see them in reviews that mention kidneys, moods, hearts, sleep — are exactly the wandering the EMA called a shortcoming. Wandering through conditions is the opposite of a claims fence.

I like type specimens as a metaphor because in botany a type is a reference object, not a "best" object. Eleuthero is the reference shrub for a mid-century noun. It is not the best plant, the safest plant, or the plant your aunt should buy. It is the plant a history of the *word* has to walk through, the way a history of the meter has to walk through a bar of metal.

If you meet eleuthero on a label this week, read the Latin, the part (root, not "herb"), and the rest of the blend. Then ask whether the label is selling you a shrub or selling you a 1960s definition. Those are different purchases. Only one of them can, in principle, be in the bottle.
