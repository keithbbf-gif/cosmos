Common names are where botany goes to be robbed.

*Eleutherococcus senticosus* spent a stretch of its American life as "Siberian ginseng." The phrase does three jobs at once, all of them a little crooked. It borrows the prestige of *Panax*. It paints a map (Siberia) that is only partly the plant's story. And it teaches a shopper that two Araliaceae roots are, in some commercial sense, the same idea.

This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent any disease. It is not medical advice. It is a naming quarrel, which is to say it is a claims quarrel that never has to mention a diagnosis.

*Ginseng*, in the stricter English of trade and of people who like plants, points at *Panax*: Asian *P. ginseng*, American *P. quinquefolius*, a few relatives, a shape of root, a cultural weight that includes tribute, courts, and a long Chinese materia. The word is already stretched when it covers both Asian and American species. Stretching it to a different genus is a second stretch. At some point a word is not stretched; it is stolen.

U.S. labeling pressure, as later herb-trade histories tell it, eventually pushed the market toward *eleuthero* — a clipped genus name that sounds like a supplement and at least does not pick *Panax*'s pocket in public. I am not going to reconstruct every Federal Register notice from memory I do not have. The historical fact I will stand on is simpler: **the fight existed because the name was doing economic work.** If the name had been neutral, no one would have bothered.

Why did the crooked name work in the first place?

Because the Soviet school had already put eleuthero and *Panax* in the same functional sentence. If both were "adaptogens," a marketer could be forgiven, in a lazy way, for thinking they were both ginsengs. The laboratory noun and the folk noun shook hands behind the shopper's back.

Because family resemblance is a pretty lie. Araliaceae includes ivy, too. We do not sell ivy as "English ginseng."

Because "Siberian" is an atmosphere. It suggests cold, hardship, a body that endured. Draft 42 is about that atmosphere as folklore. Atmosphere is not a species.

The name fight is useful for our fence because it is a **pure category theft** with no disease required. You can cheat a reader without promising to treat anything. You simply file a shrub under a more expensive word. Later, the disease claims can be bolted on. First you steal the drawer.

Chinese *ciwujia* is the better common name if you want history rather than heat. It does not flatter a Californian who has only heard of ginseng. That is a feature. A name that makes you ask "what is that?" is a name that still has room for a sentence.

There is a parallel theft later in the aisle: "Indian ginseng" for ashwagandha, "poor man's ginseng" for codonopsis, "southern ginseng" for jiaogulan. Once you see the pattern, you cannot unsee it. *Ginseng* became a prestige suffix. Prestige suffixes are how a single plant's political economy colonizes other plants' stories.

I do not think every use of a wrong common name is a conspiracy. Some of it is translation fatigue. Some of it is a 1980s wholesale catalog. Intent does not repair the file. If you are writing history, you say *Eleutherococcus senticosus*, you say eleuthero, you say *ciwujia*, and you put "Siberian ginseng" in quotation marks as an object of study.

If you are reading a label that still uses the old phrase, you have learned something about the manufacturer's respect for categories. You have not learned whether the shrub in the bottle is the right species, the right part, or a powder that was ever near a Far-East hillside. Names can be cleaned up while the powder stays dirty. Identity is a later draft (28 is about *Withania*; the moral travels).

I will not tell you that eleuthero "is just as good as ginseng" or "is not as good as ginseng." Those sentences assume a *good for* that this folder will not name. The name fight is about honesty of reference, not about a score.

A type specimen that had to go to court over its nickname is a good mascot for the whole series. The word *adaptogen* is also a nickname that wandered. We are here to put the nicknames back on their hooks.
