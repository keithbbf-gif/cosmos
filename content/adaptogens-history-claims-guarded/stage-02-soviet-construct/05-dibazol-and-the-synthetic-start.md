The first bench in this story does not smell like a root cellar. It smells like a synthetic.

That fact is inconvenient for every later telling that wants *adaptogen* to be an ancient plant wisdom that science finally named. If you start where the Soviet toxicology actually started, you meet a compound with a laboratory name, a household name, and a set of jobs that already wander toward disease language — which is exactly why this draft will walk slowly and keep its hands visible.

This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent any disease. It is not medical advice. It is a reminder that the adaptogen program was, at the jump, a pharmacology of resistance that included **made molecules**.

Later Russian memorial essays on Nikolai Lazarev talk about dibazol (bendazol; 2-benzylbenzimidazole hydrochloride in the older naming). They describe work under Lazarev on purine analogues and this particular compound. They describe more than one kind of interest: vascular talk, talk about certain neurological situations, and — this is the thread that feeds our word — a "prophylactic" picture in which the organism's resistance to assorted chemical, physical, and biological insults looked raised. Lazarev's phrase, in the later retellings, is a state of nonspecifically increased resistance. Plants such as ginseng and eleuthero get mentioned in the same memorial breath, as other items that could be pointed at with the new noun.

I am paraphrasing memorials and secondary reviews, not a lab notebook. Memorials flatten. They also, sometimes, put later disease names next to a 1940s compound in a way a claims-guarded essay must not copy. If a later eulogy says a synthetic was used in the setting of this or that named condition, that is a claim about Soviet clinical practice for a historian of medicine to handle with archives. It is not a finding I will repeat as a take-home, and it is not a hint that you should look for dibazol on a wellness shelf.

What I will keep is the **shape**:

- A synthetic sits in the same conceptual basket as roots.
- The basket is labeled with resistance, not with a single receptor story.
- "Nonspecific" is already doing heavy lifting: many insults, one hoped-for raised state.
- Later, the plant side of the basket is what English-language marketing keeps. The synthetic side becomes a trivia item, if it is mentioned at all.

Why drop the synthetic? Because a capsule brand cannot easily sell "we are like a 1950s benzimidazole." Because the romance is in the taiga and the *bencao*. Because a synthetic with a Soviet clinical biography is harder to launder into a dietary supplement. The plant is the portable myth. Dibazol is a reminder that the myth was, at origin, a **pharmacological ambition** that did not care whether the substance grew.

There is a second synthetic ghost at the far end of the century. Later reviews of "adaptogens" sometimes wander into compounds from sports-medicine cultures — names that have shown up in anti-doping monitoring lists. I am not going to turn this paragraph into a doping explainer or a shopping list. I mention the ghost for one reason: once you mint a functional class that means "helps the organism meet load," you will attract both plants and molecules, and some of those molecules will leave the folk story entirely. That is not a reason to call a root a drug. It is a reason to notice that **class-talk is greedy**.

The honest historical use of dibazol in this folder is therefore narrow. It helps us refuse a sentence like "adaptogens are herbs humanity has always used." Humanity did not always use bendazol. A mid-century institute did, in a resistance frame, and then the frame went looking for plants that could be described in the same breath.

If you are a plant person, this should not insult the plants. Ginseng did not need dibazol to have a tribute economy. Schisandra did not need a benzimidazole to have a Chinese name with five tastes. What the synthetic does is keep the *word* honest. *Adaptogen* is a wager about substances and resistance. Wagers can be placed on more than one table.

Keep the claims fence up around this table especially. Synthetic-drug biographies are full of disease names. Repeating those names as if they were our recommendations would blow the series. I am interested in the conceptual start, not in recovering a forgotten pharmaceutical for your cabinet.

When you see a modern blend labeled "original adaptogen formula," ask a rude question: original to whom? If the answer is "the Soviet school," the original formula, in the strict sense, had a synthetic in the room. If the answer is "your ancestors," the word is the wrong word.
