If you have a single date for the word *adaptogen*, you already have a problem.

I do not mean a little academic asterisk. I mean the kind of problem that tells you how a history is going to behave later, when it gets near a bottle. Clean origin years are a marketing asset. Messy origin years are what the paper trail actually offers.

This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent any disease. It is not medical advice. It is a quarrel about a calendar.

The version you will meet first, because it is short, goes like this: in 1947 the Soviet toxicologist Nikolai Vasilievich Lazarev coined *adaptogen* for substances that raise "nonspecific resistance" to adverse influences. The European Medicines Agency's 2008 reflection paper repeats a form of that line and even lists a 1947 congress citation (7th All-Union Congress of Physiology, Biochemistry, and Pharmacology, Medgiz, Moscow, p. 579) among its references. Later popular pages copy the year and lose the congress. By the time the year reaches a blog, Lazarev has "invented adaptogens," as if he had invented the plants.

Then there is the version that sits in a footnote of a 2011 *HerbalGram* review by Alexander Panossian and Hildebert Wagner. The authors say they went looking through original Russian full texts from 1943 onward. They say they did not find the word *adaptogen* in any publication before 1958 — not in Lazarev's 1947 book *Evolution of Pharmacology*, not in early Brekhman pieces, not in the conference abstracts they checked. They date Lazarev's introduction of the term into the scientific literature to 1957, for substances that increase the "state of non-specific resistance" in stress. They add that a 1947 congress abstract and a 1956 paper discussed a synthetic, dibazol, in the neighborhood of nonspecific resistance *without* using the later brand-word.

I cannot adjudicate those two stories from a reading room I do not have. I have not held the 1947 congress page. I have not searched the Russian corpus they searched. What I can do — what a claims-guarded history is *for* — is refuse to pick the prettier year and bury the other.

So the honest sentence looks like this:

**By the late 1950s, Soviet pharmacological writing had a word, *adaptogen*, attached to a program about nonspecific resistance. Writers still argue about whether that word itself is already in the 1947 congress record or only in the air around earlier resistance talk.**

That sentence will not fit on a pouch. Good.

Why does 1947 keep winning the popular telling? Because it is close to the war, close to Selye's English-language stress papers, and close to a hunger for a founding genius. Origin stories like a man and a year. They do not like a decade of abstracts about a synthetic benzylbenzimidazole and a later christening.

Why does the 1957/1958 caution matter beyond pedantry? Because if the *word* is later than the *program*, then a lot of what gets called "the first adaptogen studies" were not, in their own language, adaptogen studies. They were studies of stimulation, tonus, work, dibazol, schisandra, ginseng. Retrofitting the word backward onto those papers is the same habit as retrofitting the word backward onto the *bencao*. It is how a research brand colonizes its own prehistory.

There is a third dating problem people skip: **when the word becomes English.** The handle most later writers can actually pull off a Western shelf is Brekhman and Dardymov in the *Annual Review of Pharmacology*, commonly cited as 1969, volume 9, pages 419–430. Some bibliographies say 1968 and volume 8. That drift is small and telling. Even the "canonical" English paper does not sit still in the notes. If your history needs that paper to be a thunderclap that settled a class, the notes are already laughing.

A fourth dating problem is **when the word becomes a shop.** That is not 1947 and not 1969. In the United States the relevant legal weather is much later: the dietary-supplement statute of 1994, the structure/function style on labels, the long refusal of FDA to treat *adaptogen* as a drug class. In Europe the relevant weather includes the 2008 HMPC reflection: the concept can be considered when assessing traditional herbal medicines; it is not accepted as ordinary pharmacological terminology for an authorisation. Those dates belong in the same folder as Lazarev, or the Soviet story becomes a creation myth for a checkout display.

I am not trying to take Lazarev's name off the door. The man existed. The resistance program existed. Dibazol existed. Brekhman existed. Vladivostok existed. The dating problem is a test of whether we can stand to let a scientific culture be a culture — overlapping papers, delayed words, later English summaries — instead of a parable.

If you are writing a label, you will want 1947, because it sounds like a discovery. If you are writing a guarded history, you will write the quarrel and then move on to what the program actually talked about: nonspecific resistance, Selye's stages as a borrowed picture, a hope that some substances were not mere stimulants. That talk is already ambitious. It does not need a fake first-birthday.

And it still does not get to become a disease treatment on this page. A year is not an indication. Two conflicting years are even less of one.
