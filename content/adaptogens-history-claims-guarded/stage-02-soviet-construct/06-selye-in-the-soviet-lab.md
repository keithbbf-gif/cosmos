Hans Selye did not walk into Vladivostok with a suitcase of roots. His 1936 *Nature* note — a short, famous description of a syndrome produced by "diverse nocuous agents" — is an English-language object from Montreal. Later he built a public career on *stress* and on a three-phase picture that popular science still recites: alarm, resistance, exhaustion.

Soviet writers who wanted a physiology for nonspecific resistance could point at that picture. Later European regulators, when they had to explain what *adaptogen* was even supposed to mean, pointed at it again. The 2008 EMA reflection paper says the term *stress* is used there in Selye's classic sense as a threatened homeostasis, and it repeats animal-work stories in which eleuthero was discussed in connection with the alarm phase.

This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent any disease. It is not medical advice. It is a crossing: a Montreal theory of a general syndrome, a Soviet hope for a general substance.

I want to keep Selye *small* in this folder, because popular adaptogen writing makes him huge in the wrong way. The wrong way looks like this: Selye discovered stress; adaptogens manage stress; therefore a capsule manages the diseases stress is said to cause. That is a chain of shrugs. Each shrug trades a more specific noun for a vaguer one, until you have implied prevention without typing the word.

What Selye's 1936 note actually gave later readers was permission to think that **different insults could rhyme**. Cold, toxin, injury — the body, in his telling, produced a recognizable pattern. That permission is intellectually exciting. It is also how "nonspecific" gets its glamour. If insults rhyme, maybe a counter-substance can rhyme back.

A research culture that is also a labor culture will hear that glamour in a particular key. The question becomes: can we raise the resistance phase, delay the exhaustion phase, keep a radio operator or a miner or a conscript in the middle of the curve? I am describing an ambition, not an outcome. Ambitions leave paper. Outcomes need methods we are not re-grading here.

There is a second Selye problem: **the word *stress* escaped him**. In ordinary English it now means a mood, a job, a traffic jam, a diagnosis-shaped complaint, a lifestyle brand. In Selye's earlier usage it is closer to a physiological state. In a supplement aisle it means "your life." If I say a Soviet paper studied "stress," I need to say what they actually loaded onto the animal or the volunteer — heat, noise, a night shift, a forced march — or I am translating a protocol into a feeling.

There is a third Selye problem: **general adaptation is not a disease name, but it sits next to disease names**. Exhaustion talk slides toward adrenal talk slides toward a panel of blood tests slides toward a sale. This series will not follow that slide. We can say that mid-century writers borrowed a three-phase sketch. We cannot say that a plant therefore "supports adrenal health" as a wink at a disorder.

Did Soviet laboratories "believe" Selye? Belief is the wrong verb for a literature I am reading through translations and reviews. They *used* him. They used him as a shared picture that made a class of substances imaginable. Other pictures were available: Chinese *zheng* patterns, Ayurvedic *dosha* talk, ordinary stimulant pharmacology. The Selye picture was useful because it was general and because it was already international. A general, international picture is easier to export than a local pulse system.

The export has a cost. Once your class is defined against a general syndrome, your evidence will be tempted to stay general: work capacity, "adaptation," quality of life. The EMA committee in 2008 complained, in milder language, that the clinical packages in front of them wandered across conditions, often with small numbers and thin methods, and that none of them would do for a clearly defined clinical condition in the authorisation sense. You can hear Selye's gift in that complaint. A theory of diverse nocuous agents invites diverse endpoints. Diverse endpoints look, to a regulator, like no endpoint.

I am not blaming Selye for the aisle. He wrote a note in *Nature*. Other people built a noun and then a market. The useful historical move is to see the **borrowed skeleton** inside the noun. Alarm, resistance, exhaustion. A hope to thicken resistance. A hope that some substances were not stimulants with a crash. That skeleton can be described without promising you a thicker resistance, and without pretending the skeleton is a folk category from the Han.

If you take one thing from this draft, take the discipline of asking, every time a later writer says "adaptogens help with stress," *which Selye they mean* — the 1936 pattern, the public-lecture stress, or the aisle feeling. Only the first belongs in a laboratory history. None of the three is a prescription, and none of them is a reason to treat this folder as a clinic.
