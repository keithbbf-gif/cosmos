The romance version of this history opens on a battlefield and closes on an Olympic podium. I do not have those files on my desk. I have later reviews that say wartime Soviet journals carried early schisandra work, that the 1960s turned adaptogen talk into a biomedical field in the USSR, and that the same reviews mention Arctic expeditions, factory floors, radio rooms, and sport as settings where preparations were given and work was measured.

This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent any disease. It is not medical advice. It is a note about **settings** and about the difference between a setting and a proof.

Start with war, because the reviews do. Panossian and Wagner date the "modern scientific research" mood to World War II: a state that wanted stamina from soldiers, pilots, sailors, and people in weapons plants. They point at schisandra papers in military journals and at an ethnopharmacological interest in Far-East berries that predates the war (draft 20). I can repeat that *as a claim about a literature*. I cannot show you the journal issue. I cannot tell you the blinding, the dropout, the exact preparation. A wartime study is a kind of document written under a kind of pressure. Pressure is not automatically fraud, and it is not automatically quality. It is a water stain on the page.

Then labor. The stories that travel west are almost folkloric: telegraph operators, miners, people in heat, people in noise. Farnsworth's later English-language review, as cited by others, is often invoked for a large pile of eleuthero human studies. I will not reprint a headcount I did not verify. I will say what a **workplace study** is, as a genre. It has a shift, a boss, a metric that can be counted by a clock or a tally sheet, and a substance someone in authority handed out. It may lack the niceties a 2020s trial registry expects. It may still record something about how a state thought about bodies as productive. That "something" is social history. It is not a reason to put a dropper in your bag for a Monday.

Then sport and spectacle. Later popular pages love to say that Soviet athletes "used adaptogens." Sometimes they mention chess, which is a nice detail because it is not a muscle. I have not seen locker-room ledgers. I have seen a pattern: once a substance class is defined as raising work capacity without being a stimulant, sport will show up the way iron filings show up around a magnet. Sport talk is also how a history acquires disease-adjacent boasts (recovery, immunity, "protecting" a heart) that this folder will not carry. If you want the anti-doping end of the synthetic cousins, look elsewhere and look carefully. Here I will only say that **a podium is not a monograph**.

Then the files we do not have.

We do not have, in this folder, a systematic translation program. We do not have individual-level data. We do not have a uniform diagnostic language; later commentators have pointed out that Soviet psychiatric and "asthenic" categories do not sit still under later ICD codes. The EMA paper, which is public and which I did read, says the clinical material it surveyed had shortcomings in inclusion criteria, medication description, diagnosis, design, and analysis; that conditions wandered; that some studies were very small; that none would substantiate efficacy of eleuthero in a clearly defined clinical condition for authorisation, even if the pile might justify more research.

That last clause is easy to abuse. "Justify more research" is a committee's shrug, not a green light on a label, and not a treatment.

Why include war and labor at all, if the files are this thin from where I sit?

Because omitting them makes the word look like it was born in a California warehouse. It wasn't. It was born in a state that measured people as workers and fighters and expedition members. That origin explains the obsession with **capacity** rather than with the disease categories a Western drug label loves. Capacity talk can be more honest than disease talk. It can also be a way to medicalize a shift without naming a disease — and then, decades later, to medicalize a mood.

If you are a historian of science, the invitation is obvious: go to the Russian pages, if you can, and do the work this draft is not doing. If you are a reader who came for a plant that will "get you through" a job, you are the person this series is most trying not to recruit. Jobs are political. Plants are plants. A 1960s factory protocol is not your consent.

I will keep mentioning radiotelegraph operators, because that image is concrete and a little strange, and strangeness slows the slide toward a cure. A person with a headset, a clock, a paper, and a preparation. That is a scene. A scene is enough.

## Claims box

- Settings in which a literature says studies happened, not outcomes you should expect.
