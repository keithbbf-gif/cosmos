Words do not migrate because they are true. They migrate because someone can cite them.

*Adaptogen* becomes an English word you can find without Cyrillic in a thin line of reviews: Brekhman and Dardymov in the *Annual Review of Pharmacology* at the end of the 1960s; Norman Farnsworth and colleagues on eleuthero in the mid-1980s; Wagner, Nörr, and Winterhoff in the first volume of *Phytomedicine* in 1994; Panossian, Wikman, and Wagner at the end of that decade; Davydov and Krikorian in 2000, staring hard at eleuthero; a cluster of later Panossian papers; a 2011 *HerbalGram* piece that tried to narrate the whole arc for a professional herbal audience. I am naming a chain, not kissing it.

This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent any disease. It is not medical advice. It is a customs record for a noun.

Each crossing document has a **genre**, and the genre is the thing to keep.

An *Annual Review* article is a privileged summary. It tells a wider pharmacological public that a Soviet plant program exists and that it has a definition. Privilege is not omniscience. It is a megaphone.

A Farnsworth-style review is a pharmacognosist's attempt to make a Russian pile legible. Later writers treat it as a warehouse of study counts. Warehouses need audits. This folder is not the audit.

A *Phytomedicine* paper from Wagner's Munich orbit is a European lab putting a German-language research culture into English. Nörr's dissertation, which the EMA lists, sat behind some of that. Dissertations are where plants get compared on a bench; they are also where a school's hopes get a table.

Panossian's line of papers, including work associated with the Swedish Herbal Institute in later tellings, is a different crossing: not only "the Soviets did this," but "here is a continuing research program with branded extracts." The moment a brand enters the notes, a historian should sit up. Brands are not sins. They are interests. Interests belong in the caption.

Davydov and Krikorian are a crossing in a skeptical key. A closer look is still a look at the noun. Even a critic has to repeat the word to dismantle it, and repetition is how words survive.

What did these crossings **not** do?

They did not turn *adaptogen* into an FDA class. They did not persuade the EMA HMPC, in 2008, that the term was appropriate for a marketing authorisation. They did not, by existing, make rasayana and *bu qi* into synonyms. They did not replace the need to name a species and a part.

What they did do is give journalists and copywriters a **cite-shaped object**. "Researchers say" needs a footnote. A review is a footnote that already aggregated the footnotes. Aggregation is catnip. It is also how a thin wartime paper and a later branded trial end up in the same breath as "the evidence."

I want to be fair to the people in the chain. Some of them were trying to get Russian-language work out of a cold-war fog. That is a real service. Some of them were trying to make a functional class make sense in Western pharmacology's less hospitable climate. That is a real intellectual problem. Fairness means describing the service and the problem. It does not mean importing their efficacy tables into a claims-guarded folder.

If you read only one crossing with this series in mind, read the EMA paper alongside the *HerbalGram* review. The first is a committee refusing a class for authorisation while allowing traditional-use consideration. The second is two committed researchers narrating a field they believe in, including a dating footnote that undermines the lazy 1947 slogan. Together they give you the range: belief, refusal, and a calendar quarrel. That range is the English-language weather.

After the reviews come the **glossaries**. Herb textbooks, naturopathic handouts, Wikipedia-shaped pages I am not using as sources. Glossaries flatten crossings into definitions. Definitions wander onto labels. Labels wander into essays that think they are more sophisticated than labels. This folder is trying to break that loop by keeping the crossing *as a crossing*.

A practical habit: when you see *adaptogen* in English, ask **which decade's English**. 1969 English is a Soviet summary. 1994 English is a Munich plant-lab. 2000 English is a long eleuthero stare. 2008 English is Brussels. 2011 English is a herbal-professional magazine. 2020s English is often a store. The word is spelled the same. The job is not.

I have not told you that the crossings "proved" a class. Proved is a verb this series does not spend on disease or on membership. I have told you how a Russian-language program became a word a person in a grocery can mispronounce. Customs records are dull. Dull is safer than a cure.
