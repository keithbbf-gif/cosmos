If Lazarev is the name people hang a year on, Israel I. Brekhman is the name people hang a map on. The map is the Soviet Far East: ginseng country in the Chinese and Korean sense, eleuthero country in the later English sense, a coastline that looks at Japan and a hinterland that looks at taiga. Vladivostok is not a branding exercise. It is a city where a pharmacologist could take the resistance idea and walk it toward plants that already had Chinese and local names.

This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent any disease. It is not medical advice. It is a sketch of a person-as-program, drawn from the English-language crossing and from later reviews, not from a biography I am qualified to swear to.

Brekhman (1921–1994, in the usual reference works) shows up in those reviews as the figure who pushed the adaptogen criteria toward **safety plus normalization**, and as the figure who made *Eleutherococcus senticosus* the plant the rest of the world would meet first when it met the word. He wrote in Russian; the piece most outsiders can actually pull is the late-1960s *Annual Review* article with I.V. Dardymov. He is also attached, in later tellings, to an Institute of Biologically Active Substances and to a pile of workplace and expeditionary studies that this folder will not pretend to have re-read in the original.

I want to resist the "father of" construction. Father-of stories turn a research culture into a mascot. Vladivostok in the 1960s was not a lone genius hut. It was institutes, field collection, extraction, animal benches, human studies of uneven design, and a state that had reasons to care about labor, sport, and cold. Brekhman is a way into that culture. He is not the culture.

What does the culture look like from the outside?

It looks **plant-forward** in a way Lazarev's synthetic start was not. The shortlist that later English reviews keep repeating — *Panax ginseng*, *Eleutherococcus senticosus*, *Rhodiola rosea*, *Schisandra chinensis*, *Rhaponticum carthamoides* — is a Far-East and Eurasian list with one foot in Chinese materia and one foot in Soviet collecting. That list is already a choice. It is not "the world's tonics." It is the set a particular school kept putting on the bench.

It looks **pragmatic about substitution**. Eleuthero is easier to gather in quantity than wild *Panax*. If your program wants kilos, you will fall in love with the shrub that grows. A later American aisle would call that shrub "Siberian ginseng" and start a naming fight (draft 12). In Vladivostok the love affair is easier to understand as supply plus a hypothesis.

It looks **eager to export a function, not a cosmology**. Brekhman's English-facing work does not ask you to learn *qi*. It asks you to learn nonspecific resistance. That is both a translation strategy and a loss. The loss is the Chinese and Nanai and Korean contexts that made the plants make sense before the hypothesis. The strategy is why a German or Swedish lab could later pick up the noun without picking up the pulse system.

I am not going to recite Brekhman's reported study counts as if I tallied them. Panossian and Wagner, among others, describe an enormous Russian-language literature through the early 1980s, much of it hard for outsiders to get. "Enormous" is a historical claim about publication volume. Volume is not quality, and quality is not an indication. The EMA committee, looking at what had been translated and summarized by 2008, still would not accept the noun as a marketing class.

There is a human temptation, when you meet a researcher who spent decades on one idea, to pay him with belief. The better payment is **accuracy about his idea**. The idea was that some plant preparations could be sorted by a functional profile — low toxicity, broad resistance, a normalizing push — rather than by a single disease target. That idea is interesting as philosophy of pharmacology. It is also, as the EMA noted, hard to fit into "one drug, one symptom." Interesting and hard-to-fit is a good description of a research program. It is a bad description of a cure.

Vladivostok also matters because of **direction**. Knowledge did not only flow from "the West" into "the East" as a civilizing science. Here a Soviet periphery with Asian plant neighbors minted a word that later Western shelves would treat as if it had always been English. The irony is almost too neat, so I will not lean on it. I will just say: if your history of adaptogens starts in California, you have started in the export shop.

When later Indian and Chinese scientists, as the *HerbalGram* review notes, began asking whether ashwagandha or ginseng "were adaptogens," they were doing a translation in the dangerous direction — taking a Soviet functional class and laying it over older shelves. Brekhman did not force them to. The noun was sitting there, looking like a bridge. This series exists to put a toll on that bridge. You may walk across it as a historian. You may not walk across it as a prescriber, not on this page.

If I have a soft spot for Brekhman, it is the soft spot I have for anyone who tried to make a messy forest intelligible without pretending the forest was a single molecule. Soft spots are how fences fall. So I will leave him in Vladivostok, with a review article and a shrub, and I will not thank him by turning his shrub into a treatment.
