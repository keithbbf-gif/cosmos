The American shelf keeps a shortlist. The Soviet bench, if you believe the later English reviews, was longer.

*Rhaponticum carthamoides* (Willd.) Iljin — also dragged around under *Leuzea* and under the folk-facing name *maral root* — shows up in the plant sets Brekhman and Dardymov are said to have systematized. *Aralia* species, cousins again in that Araliaceae neighborhood, show up in Soviet pharmacognosy talk that never quite made the Costco display. There are other names that flicker: *Rhodiola* we still have; some *Leuzea* extracts became a sports-nutrition rumor; most of the rest became footnotes.

This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent any disease. It is not medical advice. It is a reminder that **a class, once invented, had members that marketing later fired**.

Why remember the fired members?

Because a shortlist that only contains plants you can buy in 2026 is not a history. It is a catalog. History includes the plants that stayed in Russian-language papers and never learned to smile in English. If *adaptogen* were a folk category, you would expect the folk to have kept the ones they liked. If it is a research category, you expect membership to follow institutes, extractors, and later, exporters. The forgotten bench argues for the second story.

*Rhaponticum / Leuzea* is a useful example because it sits at an awkward junction. It is a steppe plant with a folk name that points at maral deer, which is already a little mythic. It picked up, in later decades, a sports-nutrition reputation that I will not detail, because detailing is how an essay becomes a stack. Some later reviews mention ecdysteroid chemistry. Chemistry is not a reason for this folder to start whispering about muscle. I will say only: **this is a plant whose afterlife was more "performance" than "grandmother,"** which is a different afterlife than tulsi's, and a different one than wild ginseng's. Afterlives are historical facts.

*Aralia* is useful because it shows the Araliaceae metaphor getting greedy. Once ginseng and eleuthero are in the basket, the genus next door looks like a candidate. Candidate is not the same as tradition, and not the same as a trial, and not the same as a product you should hunt for. It is a sign of how a school thinks: by family, by extract, by functional test, not by a village's named recipe.

There are also mushrooms and distant tonics that later writers tried to seat at the same table. I am leaving them to their own drafts. The point here is **the Soviet table's extra chairs**.

A claims-guarded history has a special reason to like forgotten members. They have not yet been covered in quite so many disease-shaped blog posts. The temptation to "explain what they're good for" is therefore easier to refuse. I will refuse it anyway, in writing, so the refusal is on the record. I do not know, from a document I trust and a method I ran, what you should do with maral root. I know that a mid-century list included it. That is enough for this page.

There is a melancholy version of this draft that says: the aisle is a filter that keeps what photographs well. Eleuthero looks like a root. Rhodiola photographs as golden. *Panax* photographs as a man-shaped treasure. A steppe composite with a deer in its nickname photographs as a rumor. Melancholy is fine. Melancholy is not a reason to revive a rumor as a treatment.

If you are a reader with a family story about a plant that never got the English noun, this draft is for you in a sideways way. The noun was never the only way to have a plant. Most of the world's preparations never sat on Brekhman's bench. That is not their failure. It may be their protection. A plant that never became an adaptogen in a catalog is a plant that still has a chance to be talked about in its own language.

The forgotten bench also warns the writer. If I only tell the plants that have U.S. SKUs, I am doing the catalog's work. So here is a draft with almost nothing you are being asked to remember to buy. Remember instead that **lists have editors**. The Soviet list had some. The aisle had others. This folder has a third editor: the fence. The fence cuts disease names. It does not have to cut *Rhaponticum* from a historical sentence.

When later popular science says "the classic adaptogens are ginseng, eleuthero, rhodiola, and schisandra," you may now hear the quiet part: *classic to whom, after which export decisions?* A classic is a curriculum. Curricula omit.
