*Nonspecific* is a respectable laboratory adjective that went feral.

In a bench notebook it can mean something exact: we did not tune the insult to one receptor story; we used heat *and* a toxin *and* a swim test, and we are reporting a pattern. In a grant it can mean: we are looking for a general host response, not a single bug. In an aisle it means: we will not tell you what this is for, and we would like you to feel that this is a virtue.

This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent any disease. It is not medical advice. It is a lexical history of a word that now does too much work.

Lazarev's phrase, in the later English, is a state of nonspecifically raised resistance. Brekhman and Dardymov keep the adverb. The EMA, quoting them, says an adaptogen "tends to be non-specific in its pharmacological properties." Each of those sentences is already a little different. "Nonspecific resistance" is a hoped-for state of the organism. "Nonspecific pharmacological properties" is a claim about the substance. Popular writing mashes the two into "works on everything, gently."

I do not know how to say this more bluntly: **a substance that works on everything is the opposite of what a careful clinician, a regulator, or a historian of indications wants to be handed.**

A clinician wants to know the interaction, the blood pressure, the pregnancy, the particular complaint. A regulator wants a defined condition and a package that matches it. A historian wants to know *which* use, in *which* language. "Nonspecific" shrugs at all three and calls the shrug a philosophy.

There is a charitable reading. Mid-century pharmacology was full of magic-bullet stories. A class defined as nonspecific was a protest: the organism is not a single target; load is various; maybe a useful substance is various too. You can hear, in that protest, a cousin of tonic traditions that also refused one-drug-one-symptom. The EMA paper says as much when it notes that the "one drug – one symptom" idea does not apply to the adaptogen story people were telling. Charity can stop there. A protest against magic bullets is not a license to imply magic buckshot.

Watch how the adverb behaves near **disease names**. A writer says the plant raises nonspecific resistance to biological factors. The next sentence mentions "infections" as an example of a biological factor. The third sentence is already halfway to prevention. Nobody typed a forbidden verb. The adverb did the verb's job.

Watch it near **mood language**. Psychological stressors get added to the list in later restatements. "Nonspecific" then covers a hard day and a diagnosable disorder with the same shrug. This folder will name folk and literary talk about the mind when the texts have it. It will not let *nonspecific* smuggle a psychiatric indication.

Watch it near **sports**. A workplace study about a night shift is a workplace study. "Nonspecific resistance to physical factors" is how that study gets metaphysically promoted into a theory of the body. Promotion is not evidence.

I have a practical rule for the rest of the series. When I need the Soviet sense, I will try to say **"broad-insult resistance talk"** or **"the nonspecific-resistance program."** Ugly on purpose. When I need the aisle sense, I will say **"unspecified."** If I slip and write *nonspecific* without a gloss, you have my permission to scowl.

There is also a **specificity hiding inside the plant list**. Eleuthero is not rhodiola is not schisandra is not *Panax*. They do not share a chemistry. They do not share a materia-medica sentence. They share a later noun. The adverb *nonspecific* is part of what makes that sharing look natural. Take the adverb away and you are left with a pile of different plants and a research culture that wanted a pile to be a class.

Davydov and Krikorian's long 2000 look at eleuthero is useful here as a mood, even if I am not importing their every pharmacological judgment: a closer look tends to split what a slogan joined. Closer looks are the enemy of *nonspecific* in the feral sense. This series is a closer look at *language*. It will not repay you with a more precise dose. It will repay you with a more precise refusal.

If you have been trained by wellness copy to hear *nonspecific* as "safe because gentle," untrain. Gentle is a feel. Toxicity is a measurement that clause 1 of the old definition only *hoped* to keep low. Unspecified use is a legal style. None of those three is the same fact.

And if you have been trained by a certain kind of skeptic to hear *nonspecific* as "therefore fake," untrain that too. A research program can be ambitious, culturally located, and uneven in its methods without being a hoax. Hoax-talk is just *nonspecific* in a hostile key — a shrug that covers every paper at once.

The adverb did too much work. I am taking some of the work away. What remains is a historical program with a name, a set of plants, a set of documents, and a claims fence that will not let "broad" become "cures."
