Three words keep getting stacked as if they were synonyms you could shuffle for SEO. They are not. They come from different rooms, and if you let them collapse you will write a disease claim without meaning to.

This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent any disease. It is not medical advice. It is a short history of three labels that refuse to sit in the same chair.

**Tonic** is the oldest of the three in ordinary European medical talk, and it has cousins everywhere. German and older English physicians used it for preparations meant to address weakness or "lack of tone" in a person or an organ. The EMA herbal committee, when it needed a contrast class in 2008, reached for that traditional sense and for *asthenia* — a word that, the committee was careful to say, does not map cleanly onto a modern ICD line. In Chinese materia, *bu* (to tonify, to supplement) is a verb inside a system of qualities, not a brand. In Ayurveda, rasayana is a whole shelf with arguments about rejuvenation language that are not our arguments. Household English used "tonic" for bitters, wines, and things a grandmother poured when someone looked peaked.

A tonic, in those rooms, is not "a substance that treats a named disease." It is a style of use: repeated, general, aimed at a picture of depletion that the local medicine already had words for. You can study that style as history. You cannot, from the word alone, hang a diagnosis on a bottle.

**Stimulant** is more modern in the sense that twentieth-century pharmacology got a grip on it. A stimulant, in the contrast the Soviet writers liked and the EMA later repeated, is something that can raise work capacity for a stretch and then drop you. Think of the everyday experience of too much tea, or the less everyday experience of a sympathomimetic, without turning this paragraph into a shopping guide. The point for our fence is the *shape of the story*: up, then down. Soviet adaptogen talk defined itself against that shape. Whether the contrast holds as physiology is a question for labs. That it was used as a contrast is a historical fact.

**Adaptogen** is the youngest, and the only one that pretends to be a pharmacological class invented on purpose. The usual English crossing is Brekhman and Dardymov at the end of the 1960s: almost nontoxic; raising resistance to a broad (they said "nonspecific") range of adverse factors; a "normalizing" effect. Some later summaries add a fourth clause about deeper pathology making a bigger effect — a clause that should make anyone with a claims pencil sit up, because "pathology" is how a laboratory word starts eyeing a disease list.

Notice what the three-clause definition does *not* say. It does not name diabetes. It does not name a tumor. It does not name an infection. It talks about resistance, normalization, and low toxicity as aspirations of a research program. Aspirations leak. By the time the word is on a U.S. shelf, "nonspecific" has come to mean "we will not specify," which is the opposite of what a careful clinician wants and the opposite of what a regulator can authorize as a disease treatment.

The EMA reflection paper of 2008 is useful here not because Brussels is oracular, but because the committee was forced to sort the pile. Tonics, they said, belong to traditional talk about weakness. Stimulants belong to a temporary increase in work capacity. Adaptogens were *reputed* to raise work capacity without the crash, and to sit closer to Selye's alarm-resistance-exhaustion picture. Then the committee said the term was not accepted in the pharmacological and clinical language commonly used in the EU for a marketing authorisation. That is a sorting, plus a refusal. It is not a finding that a root is "just a tonic" in the grandmother sense, and it is not a finding that a root is a medicine for a named disease.

Why does the collapse happen in popular writing?

Because each word flatters a different anxiety. *Tonic* flatters depletion. *Stimulant* flatters the afternoon. *Adaptogen* flatters the sense that the problem is the whole environment — cold, noise, night shifts, history itself — and that a plant could answer the whole environment without becoming a drug. That last flattery is why the word sells. It is also why it is dangerous on a page like this. "Answers the whole environment" is one shrug away from "prevents the diseases the environment causes."

I will keep the words apart in this series.

When I say **tonic**, I mean a traditional or household category of repeated, general use, in a named medical language.

When I say **stimulant**, I mean the contrast class used by mid-century writers and later regulators: a temporary push with a recognized come-down story, not a recommendation.

When I say **adaptogen**, I mean the Soviet and post-Soviet research wager and the marketing word that followed it. I do not mean "any old plant that a blog likes."

If a plant has been called all three, that is a fact about writers, not a fact about a molecule. Eleuthero has been a Chinese materia item (*ciwujia*), a Soviet program plant, a misnamed "Siberian ginseng," and a U.S. supplement. Those are successive jobs. They do not add up to a cure.

You will notice I have not told you which plants "are real adaptogens." That question assumes the class is settled. The class is exactly what is not settled — not in the EU marketing sense, not as an FDA drug class, not as a folk category that predates 1950. What is settled enough for an essay is this: people used plants under other names for a long time; a laboratory culture tried to mint a new name; the new name went shopping.

Read the rest of the folder with the three chairs still in the room. If a sentence cannot tell you which chair it is sitting in, it is probably standing in the aisle, hoping you will not ask.
