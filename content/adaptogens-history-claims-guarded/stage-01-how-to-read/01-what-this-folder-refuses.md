I am going to waste the first page on what we will not do. If that sounds like a lawyer got to the manuscript first, good. The word *adaptogen* has spent seventy years sliding off a laboratory bench and onto a bottle, and the slide is almost always greased with a disease name.

This folder is a stack of historical essays. It does not treat, cure, or prevent any disease. It is not medical advice. It is not a shopping list dressed up as folklore.

That is not a humility ritual. It is the only way to talk about these plants without lying in a soft voice.

Here is the usual lie, in its polite form: a writer tells you that Siberian hunters, Chinese emperors, and Ayurvedic physicians were all, without knowing the word, using *adaptogens*. Then the writer names a modern condition — fatigue that sounds like a diagnosis, a mood that sounds like a disorder, a blood number that sounds like a chart — and lets the plant sit next to the condition until your eye does the rest. No one typed the forbidden verb next to a tumor name. The sentence still did the work.

I refuse that sentence, including the version that swaps "cures" for "supports" and hopes the statute will hold.

What I will do instead is slower, and less flattering to anyone who wants a hero plant.

I will keep **folk categories** in their own languages. *Rasayana* is a Sanskrit shelf with its own arguments. *Bu qi* is a Chinese medical move, not a translation of a 1960s Russian abstract. A northern household that boiled a root because winter was long was not waiting for Nikolai Lazarev to baptize the pot.

I will keep the **Soviet construct** in the twentieth century, where it was built. A toxicologist and then a Far-East pharmacologist tried to name substances that would raise "nonspecific resistance." They borrowed a stress picture from Hans Selye. They ran programs that mixed plants, a synthetic compound, workplace studies, and later English-language reviews. That is a research culture with archives, congresses, and gaps. It is not a lost chapter of the *Shennong Bencao Jing*.

I will keep **regulators** in their buildings. In 2008 the European Medicines Agency's herbal committee wrote a public reflection paper that said, in bureaucratic English, that *adaptogen* was not accepted terminology for an EU marketing authorisation. That paper is a fact about a committee. It is not my secret way of saying "the plants are worthless," and it is not a loophole that lets me imply they are a treatment.

I will keep **uncertainty** visible. Secondary literature does not agree on when the word first saw print. Some pages say 1947 and point at a Moscow congress. A later review says the authors could not find *adaptogen* in print before 1958, even in Lazarev's 1947 book. If I flatten that into one confident year, I am already doing the aisle's job.

I will not invent a notebook I have not held. I have not sat in Vladivostok reading institute files. I have not graded Soviet trials. When I mention "more than a thousand studies," I am reporting a claim that circulates in reviews, not a count I performed. A circulating claim is a historical object. It is not a license.

I will not give you a dose. The minute a history essay hands over milligrams, it has changed jobs. Form matters — decoction, powder, wine, standardized extract — and I will talk about form as history. I will not talk about form as a recipe for your Tuesday.

I will not do the "balanced" trick where the last paragraph says "more research is needed" after the first twelve paragraphs have already sold the indication. If a draft cannot live without a disease payoff, the draft should die.

Why bother, then?

Because the plants are real, the texts are real, and the laboratory word is real, and they are not the same object. A person who wants to understand why a gas-station display says ADAPTOGEN BLEND is entitled to a history that does not recruit their pancreas. A person who loves materia medica is entitled to hear *wu wei zi* as five tastes, not as a hack. A person who is tired of both wellness copy and sneering debunking is entitled to a third tone: specific, a little dry, unwilling to convert a grandmother's pot into a press release.

There is a fourth reader I am writing against: the one who thinks a claims fence is cowardice. I do not think it is cowardice to refuse a sentence that would be illegal on a label and still tempting in an essay. Essays have more room than labels. That is exactly why they leak.

If you want a clinician, see a clinician who knows you. If you want a bottle, read the bottle as a legal artifact (drafts 35–37) and then decide what you are actually buying: a species, a part, a solvent, a story. If you want the story, stay here. The story is long. It has Nanai hunters in someone else's travel account, a Korean tribute economy, a Peruvian export boom, a mushroom that entered literature as an immortality motif, and a committee in Canary Wharf that would not let a word become a class.

None of that is a cure. I am repeating myself on purpose. Repetition is how a fence stays up when the next sentence wants a disease.
