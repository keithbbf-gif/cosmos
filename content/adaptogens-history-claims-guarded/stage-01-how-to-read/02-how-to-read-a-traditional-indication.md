A line in a sixteenth-century herbal is not a prescription waiting for a barcode.

I keep having to say that, because the modern eye is trained by aisles. You see a plant name, then a short clause — "for weakness," "to replenish," "used by hunters" — and your hand already wants the rest of the sentence to be a diagnosis plus a result. The old line will not finish that sentence for you. If you finish it anyway, you have left ethnobotany and started writing a label.

This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent any disease. It is not medical advice. It is a reading lesson.

Start with the **object**, not the hope. What part of the plant is named? Root, leaf, berry, exudate, a fungus that ate a moth. A tradition that decocts a taproot is not making the same thing as a factory that sells a leaf-and-stem powder in a vegetarian capsule. When a draft in this folder says "ginseng," it should soon say *Panax*, root, and which market. When it says "schisandra," it should eventually say berry or seed, because Soviet wartime notes and Chinese materia do not always point at the same preparation.

Then name the **language of the indication**. Chinese *xu* (vacuity, deficiency) is not ICD fatigue. Ayurvedic *bala* is not a VO2 max. A Russian workplace study that talks about "work capacity" is measuring something the protocol defined that week, in that shop or radio room, with whatever clocks they had. If you translate all three as "energy," you have made a smoothie.

Then name the **kind of document**. A recension of the *Shennong Bencao Jing* is a textual tradition with arguments about dating and later commentary. A travel account that says Nanai hunters carried limonnik berries is an ethnographer's or soldier-explorer's sentence, filtered by whoever later cited it. A 1969 *Annual Review* article is a review. A 2008 EMA reflection paper is a committee thinking out loud in public. None of these is a randomized trial you can wave at a skeptic, and none of them is nothing. They are different weights.

I like to pencil four margins on a photocopy, even when the photocopy is metaphorical:

1. **Who is speaking?** Court physician, household, hunter, monk, factory doctor, marketing intern.
2. **Who is the body in the sentence?** An emperor's pulse, a laborer's shift, a literary immortal, "the organism" as a Soviet abstraction.
3. **What is the time depth?** "Used since antiquity" in a preface is often a flourish. A dated tribute list is a different animal.
4. **What would falsify this as history?** If the species was misidentified, if the part changed, if the common name drifted, the indication is now a rumor about a rumor.

Notice what is not in those margins: *Did it work?* That is a fair question for a pharmacologist and a clinician. It is a crooked question for a historian of use, because "work" requires a modern endpoint the old writer did not sign. You can still care about effects. You just cannot smuggle them in through a folk line and call the smuggling respect.

There is a fifth margin I add when the plant has reached a U.S. shelf: **What job is this sentence doing now?** Sometimes a traditional indication is being asked to launder a structure/function claim. Sometimes it is being asked to make a Soviet laboratory word look ancient. Sometimes it is being asked to make an Andean cultivar look like a Himalayan myth, or the reverse. The sentence may be accurate as folklore and still be dishonest as a caption.

People who dislike this kind of caution say it erases indigenous knowledge. I think the erasure happens the other way. If I take a Nanai practice, strip the season, the hunt, the berry, and the person who wrote it down, then paste the remainder onto a capsule that also contains caffeine and a trademark, I have not honored a knowledge system. I have strip-mined it for a verb.

The opposite error is also common: treating every traditional line as superstition with no social content. A court that demanded wild ginseng as tribute was not confused about rarity. A household that grew tulsi by the door was not waiting for a randomized trial to justify a plant in the yard. Practice has reasons that are not our reasons. History can hold those reasons without converting them into a treatment.

When a later writer — including me — says "traditionally used for…," watch the preposition. "For" is doing a lot. In this series I will try to say "described in X as Y," or "prepared as Z in the context of W." It is uglier. Uglier is the point. Beauty is how disease claims sneak back in.

If you are reading ahead to the plant drafts and you feel the itch to ask "so should I take it," you are asking a question this folder is built not to answer. Take that itch to a clinician who knows your medicines, your pregnancy status, your blood pressure, your actual bottle. Take the history for what it is: a map of names, parts, trades, and arguments.

A traditional indication, read this way, gets more interesting, not less. You start seeing substitutions (codonopsis standing in for ginseng in a market). You start seeing literary motifs (the immortality mushroom). You start seeing statutes (1994 in the United States) that changed what a sentence on a bottle is allowed to be. You start seeing a European committee that would not let *adaptogen* become a class. That is a richer file than a cure, and it has the advantage of being a file we can actually keep.
