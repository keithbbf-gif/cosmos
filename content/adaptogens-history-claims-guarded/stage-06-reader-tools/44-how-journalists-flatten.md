I do not hate journalists. I hate a shape that journalism, blogs, and "explainer" videos share when they meet this noun.

The shape is: one word, four centuries, zero hedge.

You have seen the piece. It opens on a tired office. It says adaptogens are herbs that help the body handle stress. It names ashwagandha, ginseng, rhodiola. It says they have been used for thousands of years in China, India, and Russia. It mentions a study, singular. It tells you to talk to a doctor, in a footer. It has done the crime in the lede and paid the fine in the last line.

This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent any disease. It is not medical advice. It is a field guide to **flattening**, so you can watch it happen in real time.

Flattening move one: **the office**. The office makes the reader the subject. History is then obliged to arrive as a solution. Solutions want verbs. Verbs want conditions. Conditions want the footer.

Flattening move two: **the three-plant chorus.** A Soviet shrub, a rasayana root, a prestige *Panax*. The chorus implies a natural kind. Natural kinds do not need dates.

Flattening move three: **"used for thousands of years as adaptogens."** The category crime and the calendar crime in one phrase. Our linter hunts a version of this phrase. Journalists will find a synonym. Your eye has to hunt the synonym.

Flattening move four: **the singular study.** A study is a document with a method. In the flat piece it is a talisman. Talismans do not have exclusion criteria.

Flattening move five: **the hedge footer.** "Not a substitute for professional care." The hedge is true and too late. This folder puts the hedge in the body, repeatedly, because we have seen what footers are for.

I have written explainers. The shape is tempting because it is kind to the clock. A dating quarrel about 1947 versus 1958 is not kind to the clock. A Nanai citation chain is not kind to the clock. Kindness to the clock is how a noun eats the world.

There is a better journalistic piece, and I have seen a few. It opens on a bottle, not an office. It names a statute year. It says the word is contested. It refuses a disease. It lets a historian or a pharmacognosist sound unfinished. Unfinished is a virtue. The comments will be angry. Angry comments are not a reason to flatten.

Scientists flatten too, in abstracts. "Adaptogens are…," then a mechanism diagram. Diagrams travel farther than the methods. Journalists pick up the diagram because it is already flat. The review-article century (draft 39) manufactures the pre-flat object. I am not letting science off. I am saying the explainer is the last press.

A reader-tool, then. When you meet a piece about this noun, score it on five ugly points:

- Does it keep folk categories and the Soviet noun in separate sentences?
- Does it name a document (EMA 2008, DSHEA 1994, Brekhman 1969, a *bencao* recension)?
- Does it name a part and a form?
- Does it avoid finishing a disease sentence?
- Does the hedge arrive before the sell?

If the score is two or less, you have a flat piece. You may still learn a plant name from it. Then come back here, or go to the PDF, or go to a practitioner of the system the piece raided.

I will not ask journalists to write 45 drafts. I will ask them to write one sentence that cannot be put on a tub without looking wrong. If the sentence looks right on a tub, it was copy. Copy is a job. It is not this job.

The office, if you are still in it, remains an office. A noun will not unionize it. A plant will not shorten it. A footer will not save a lede. I can be unkind about this because I have been the tired person the lede was written for, and I have bought the chorus, and I have later had to take the chorus apart to write this folder. Taking apart is slower than flattening. Slowness is the quality we were asked for.

If you write the piece anyway, in the old shape, at least do not say you read this series. We refused to give you the lede.
