A plant by the door is doing a different job than a plant in a blister pack.

*Tulsi*, *Ocimum tenuiflorum* L. (still *O. sanctum* in a lot of older writing), holy basil in the English that cannot leave holiness alone — this is a household and temple basil with a daily life: watering, circumambulation, a leaf in tea, a presence in a courtyard that is as much kinship as it is materia. I have watched people greet a plant. Greeting is not a randomized protocol. It is a fact about a relationship.

This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent any disease. It is not medical advice. It is about a courtyard practice that later writers tried to promote into a class.

The Ayurvedic and broader Hindu files give tulsi more than one face: a *sattvic* household ally in some talk, a plant with pungent and heating language, a formula ingredient, a ritual being. Ritual is the part secular herb writing is shy about, so it gets stripped until only "respiratory" and "stress" are left. Stripping is a claims engine. Once the god is gone, the leaf has to justify itself with a condition. I will not provide the condition. I will say that **a ritual plant already had a justification** that was not a diagnosis.

Species muddle is quieter here than with *Withania* but real: *O. tenuiflorum* versus sweet basil versus other *Ocimum* that smell like a kitchen. Rama and Krishna tulsi talk in popular Indian English is a cultivar-and-color folk taxonomy. Folk taxonomies are data. They are not a barcode.

Nörr's dissertation, again, listed *Ocimum sanctum* among plants an adaptogen-shaped European project was willing to look at. A dissertation's table is not a courtyard. The courtyard did not ask Munich whether it was allowed to water the plant.

Why the adaptogen filing? Because English-language wellness needed a "daily" plant, and tulsi was already daily. Daily is a gift and a danger. A daily ritual can be a life. A daily capsule can be a way to medicalize the morning. I am not telling you to stop a tea you like. I am telling you that **daily plus adaptogen** is a sentence that wants to become "protects you all day," which is a prevention shadow.

I will not write about "tulsi for the lungs" or "tulsi for the mind" as if those were conclusions. I will write about steam in a kitchen, a leaf torn by hand, a smell that is clove-and-pepper rather than Italian basil. Sensory notes are allowed. They keep the plant a plant.

There is a colonial and missionary layer in the "holy basil" name: holiness as an export category, basil as a European genus handle. *Tulsi* does not need to be basil to be tulsi. Genus is a convenience. Holiness is a relationship. Convenience and relationship should not be sold as a standardized ursolic-acid story without admitting the drop.

If you do not come from a tulsi household, you can still write about the plant without converting it into your nervous system. Curiosity is allowed. Appropriation usually begins at the moment curiosity decides it has a right to an outcome. This folder stops at curiosity plus history.

A courtyard plant also offers a gentle joke on the Soviet definition. Clause 1, almost non-toxic, is a hope about a substance. A courtyard basil is a neighbor. Neighbors are not substances first. They are presences. I would rather write about a presence than about a clause. Presence does not scale to a shipping container, which is why the aisle has to change the plant to keep it.

Water the plant or do not. I am not in your yard. I am in a folder saying that the yard came first, the ritual came close behind, the materia came in the same breath, and the Soviet noun came from another country and another decade. If you put the noun on the pot, the pot is still a pot. The noun is the thing that does not belong there without a caption.
