If you want a plant that actually did travel through many systems without needing a 1960s passport, look at licorice.

*Glycyrrhiza glabra* L., *G. uralensis* Fisch., and their cousins — *gancao* (甘草) in Chinese, *yashtimadhu* in Sanskrit talk, a Greek and Mediterranean sweet-root with a long ordinary-pharmacy life — show up as a diplomat: they enter other formulas, they sweeten, they "harmonize" in some Chinese formula talk, they sit in European cough-shop memory, they sit in an Ayurvedic file. I am listing **presences**, not jobs I want you to copy.

This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent any disease. It is not medical advice. It is a widely shared root with a long-known caution, which is exactly why it belongs in a folder about a class that wanted to be "almost non-toxic."

The caution is the point, so I will put it in historical language. Ordinary pharmacology has discussed glycyrrhizin's mineralocorticoid-like effects for decades: a sweet root that can, in enough of the wrong preparation for the wrong person, sit next to stories about blood pressure and potassium. I am not going to specify a dose, a product, or a person. I am going to say that **clause 1 of the adaptogen definition ("almost non-toxic") meets, in licorice, a plant that already had a publicly discussed almost**. Almost is doing work. The work is not "ban this root." The work is "a class founded on innocence will be tempted to forget the plants that taught humility."

Did the Soviet shortlist need licorice? Not as a star. Did later tonic and adaptogen blends recruit it? Of course. Diplomats get invited. Invitation is not membership, and membership is not a reason to ignore the caution.

Chinese *gancao* is often a formula's secretary: present in many classical prescriptions, not always the celebrity. Secretaries are easy to underestimate and easy to overdose in a Western "more is more" extract culture that takes a diplomat and makes it a solo. A solo licorice extract is a different object from a pinch in a decoction. Objects matter.

The sweet taste is a historical fact that also caused trouble. Sweetness reads as food, as kindness, as harmless. The adaptogen aisle loves harmless sweetness. History is allowed to say: sweetness is not a safety certificate.

I will not write "licorice is good for the adrenals" or "licorice is bad for the heart." Those sentences are the two faces of the same leak. I will write: this root has a wide traditional presence and a caution that made it into ordinary textbooks. If you are a person with a blood-pressure story, you already have a reason to talk to a clinician before you talk to a blend. That is not an indication. It is a pointer off this page.

Why "diplomat"? Because the plant's historical talent is **entering rooms**. Greek, Chinese, Indian, European shop, later American blend. Rooms have rules. The adaptogen room's rule was supposed to be nonspecific innocence. Licorice is the guest who smiles and then reminds you that innocence was always a hope.

If a blend lists licorice in the same line as eleuthero and ashwagandha, you are looking at a diplomat plus a Soviet shrub plus a rasayana root. That is a summit, again. Ask what the diplomat is doing there: sweetening a powder, filling a traditional-sounding slot, or riding a class that does not want to talk about mineralocorticoid-like effects. If the label is silent on the caution and loud on the class, you have learned something about the label.

I like this plant as a teacher for the whole series. It is ordinary. It is everywhere. It is not exotic enough to support a harsh-climate myth. It has a textbook caution. It refuses, by existing, the idea that tonic-shaped plants are a gentle cloud. Clouds do not have glycyrrhizin stories. Roots do.

See a clinician if you need one. Read a pharmacognosy text if you want the molecule. Stay here if you want the diplomat to keep his caution in the minutes.
