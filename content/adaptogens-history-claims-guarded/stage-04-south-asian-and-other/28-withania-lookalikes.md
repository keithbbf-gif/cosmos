The most boring draft in the folder may be the most useful. Celebrity plants attract other people's powder.

*Withania somnifera* is not the only *Withania*. It is not the only ashy root in a dry landscape. It is not the only powder that can be cut with starch, with a cheaper Solanaceae, with a spent marc, with a species that shares a common name in one district and not in the next. I am not going to publish a fraud manual. I am going to insist that **identity is a historical and an ethical problem**, not a footnote under a glow.

This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent any disease. It is not medical advice. It is about whether the noun on the bag matches a plant that existed.

Ethnobotany, when it is being a grown-up, spends a lot of time on names that drift. A village name that pointed at one root in 1910 may point at another in 1980 because the first was depleted. A Sanskrit name in a printed nighantu may have been applied by a colonial botanist to a specimen that a practitioner would not have used. A Latin binomial can be right on a website and wrong in a drum. Each of those is a different failure. They all produce the same shopper sentence: "I take ashwagandha."

What does this have to do with adaptogens?

The class, being functional and prestige-hungry, **increases the value of being in the class**. Value attracts substitution. Substitution then gets covered by the same functional language ("it's all adaptogenic") that made identity seem beside the point. If the definition is nonspecific, why care which shrub? That is the adverb from draft 09, back with a knife.

A claims fence that ignores identity is a fence around an empty lot. You cannot even describe a traditional use if you do not know which plant the tradition was holding. "Traditionally used" plus the wrong species is a kind of lie that does not need a disease verb.

Tools exist: herbarium vouchers, macroscopic tests, microscopy, chemical markers, DNA methods in labs that have them. I will not pretend this folder is a pharmacognosy course. I will say that a serious historical or commercial claim names **who identified the plant, by what method, from where**. If a page cannot do that, the page is not serious.

Wild-harvest pressure is the ecological twin. When a celebrity root is gathered faster than it returns, lookalikes and younger roots enter the sacks. Conservation is not a health claim. It is a reason to be suspicious of cheap abundance.

There is a colonial layer, again. Some of the Latin identities we now treat as stable were assigned by people who were passing through with a press and a theory. Some Ayurvedic synonyms were flattened to one Linnaean species because flattening is what empires do to lists. A living practitioner's "this is ashwagandha" may be a sensory and lineage judgment that a barcode does not capture. I am not saying barcodes are useless. I am saying **two identification cultures exist**, and a capsule sits awkwardly between them.

For this series, the rule is: I will not write "ashwagandha does" sentences at all, and I will write "ashwagandha is" sentences only with a genus-species and a note that the powder in the world may be otherwise. That is an ugly rule. Ugliness keeps you from making a traditional indication about a plant that was not there.

If you are a shopper, I will not tell you which brand to trust. I will tell you that a Latin name plus a part plus a lot number plus a refusal to stack disease language is the beginning of an adult label. The beginning is not a guarantee.

If you are a writer, do not use *Withania somnifera* as a spice you sprinkle on a paragraph about stress. Stress-paragraphs plus an unstable identity are how a thousand blogs become one blur. Blur is the lookalike of thought.

The horse-smell test from draft 27 is not a lab. It is a reminder that plants used to be objects you could interrogate with a nose. A lookalike problem is, in part, what you get when the nose is replaced by a story. Stories travel farther than smells. That is why we need a fence.
