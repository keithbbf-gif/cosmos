The name is better than the branding.

*Ashwagandha*: smell of a horse, in the usual Sanskrit gloss, with a second implication about a horse's strength that English-language catalogs lean on until the plant becomes a cartoon of virility. *Withania somnifera* (L.) Dunal, Solanaceae. *Somnifera* is a Linnaean sleep-bearing epithet that has caused its own later trouble, because sleep-bearing sounds like a disease-adjacent promise. It is a species name. Species names are not labels.

This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent any disease. It is not medical advice. It is a plant with a smell, a shelf, and a recent American celebrity that would surprise a 1960s Vladivostok list.

The Ayurvedic file — *Charaka*, *Sushruta*, later nighantus, a living practice — places the root in rasayana and other talks: astringent and bitter language, a warming reputation in some descriptions, a use-picture that includes depletion and a kind of steadiness I will not translate into a psychiatric term. I am staying high on purpose. High is how you do not accidentally write an indication.

The horse in the name is an ethnographic gift. It points at a sensory test (the root's odor) and at a culture that thought in animals as well as in dosha theory. English branding keeps the horse and loses the smell. A standardized withanolide extract does not smell like a stable. That is not a joke about quality. It is a fact about **what got discarded when the plant became a SKU**.

The Soviet file did not need this plant to invent the noun. The later English file could not leave it alone. Once *adaptogen* meant "tonic we have heard of from Asia," ashwagandha was an obvious recruit: already famous in yoga-adjacent America, already rasayana, already a root. Recruitment accelerated in the 2010s until the plant became, for a while, the face of the whole noun in U.S. retail. Faces replace histories.

I will not walk through the trial literature that exploded with the celebrity. Trial literature has endpoints (sleep scales, stress scales, hormone numbers) that a claims-guarded essay should not pick as favorites. Existence of trials is a historical event of the 2000s–2020s. It is not a reason for this folder to become a reviewer.

Identity, next door in draft 28, is the unglamorous twin of celebrity. The more a plant costs, the more the powder wants to be something else. *Withania* has cousins and lookalikes and exhausted wild stands in some regions. A name that smells like a horse should, at minimum, still be that species.

Form: milk-decoction talk in some traditional preparations, ghee, a household powder, a later KSM-shaped branded extract I will not endorse or attack. Form is history. A milk decoction is a food-medicine object in a household. A capsule is a statute-shaped object in a shipping container. They can mention the same Latin name and not be the same practice.

If you came here because a friend said this root "is an adaptogen for anxiety," you have hit the fence at speed. I will not finish your friend's sentence. I will say: your friend is using a Soviet noun plus a modern diagnosis-shaped word to describe a rasayana root. That is three systems in one breath. See a clinician if you have a condition. See a qualified Ayurvedic practitioner if you want that system. See this page if you want to watch the breath and slow it down.

I like the smell. I have opened a jar of the actual root, and the horse is not a metaphor you can entirely joke away. Sensory honesty is a kind of claims hygiene. If the product has no smell and a great deal of mood language, you may be in the presence of a story rather than a root.

The horse, the sleep-bearing epithet, the rasayana shelf, the 2010s celebrity, the Soviet noun that arrived as a hat: keep them in five piles. When they merge, you get a centaur in a gym advertisement. Centaurs are for myths. Myths are not medical advice, and neither is this.
