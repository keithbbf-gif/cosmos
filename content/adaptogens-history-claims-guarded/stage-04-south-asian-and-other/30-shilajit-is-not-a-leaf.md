The aisle is a plant aisle even when the thing in the jar never had a leaf.

*Shilajit*, *mumijo* in some Central Asian and Russian talk, a rock-exudate tradition with a smell you do not forget — this is a tarry, mineral-organic stuff associated with cliffs, heat, and a long Ayurvedic rasayana file. It is not a root. It is not a mushroom. It is not *Panax*. It shows up in adaptogen blends because the noun stopped requiring a plant.

This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent any disease. It is not medical advice. It is a plea for **material honesty**.

If your class is functional ("raises nonspecific resistance, more or less harmless, normalizing"), you will recruit anything that looks like a legendary tonic, including a cliff exudate. The recruitment tells you about the class. It tells you the class is a filing cabinet. Filing cabinets do not do botany.

The Ayurvedic file treats shilajit as a substance with a source story (mountains, rock, a season of oozing in some tellings) and with purification talk. Purification talk should make a modern reader sit up. A wild exudate can carry what a cliff carries, including things you do not want. I am not going to turn this into a toxicology sheet. I will say that **traditional purification is evidence that the tradition did not think "natural" meant "ready."** Modern resin that skips that conversation is not more scientific. It is more impatient.

Russian *mumijo* talk is a parallel file, sometimes folded into the same jars, sometimes not. Folding two source-traditions into one powder is a third object. Identity, again: whose cliff, which season, what purification, what heavy-metal test if we are in a statute-shaped market. I am not certifying tests. I am saying the questions exist, and the adaptogen hat is not an answer to them.

Why do I care enough to give a non-plant its own draft?

Because "adaptogen" on a label has become a way to **stop specifying the kind of thing you are selling**. Plant, fungus, exudate, synthetic (draft 05) — the noun covers a sin. A reader of history should uncover it. Kind is the first ethnobotanical fact. Kind is not a disease.

There is also a masculinity-marketing problem I will name and then leave. Exudate plus mountain plus "strength" is a billboard waiting to happen. Billboards finish sentences this folder will not finish.

If you are a chemist, you will want fulvic-and-humic talk. That talk exists in modern sales. It is a twentieth/twenty-first-century chemical costume, the way eleutherosides are a costume for a shrub. Costumes can point at real constituents. They can also make a cliff-tar look like a vitamin. I will leave the costume on the rack.

A guarded sentence looks like this: *Shilajit is a traditional exudate substance in Ayurvedic rasayana literature and in related mountain economies; later supplement culture filed it under adaptogen, a noun that does not require a leaf.* That sentence has no outcome. It has kinds and times. It is enough.

If a blend lists "ashwagandha, shilajit, maca, reishi," you are looking at a **root, an exudate, a hypocotyl, and a fungus** from four political economies. The only thing they share for certain is a warehouse. Warehouses are not traditions.

I have not said shilajit is "not an adaptogen," because that sentence still treats membership as the prize. I have said it is not a leaf. Start there. If a label cannot tell the truth about a leaf, it will not tell the truth about a disease, and we are not asking it to do the second thing anyway.

## Claims box

- Material kind (exudate, not plant) and a later filing, not a potency story.
- This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent any disease.
- Traditional purification talk is a caution about "natural = ready," not a method I am prescribing.
- Not medical advice. Not a mountain resin protocol.
