Not every plant in the adaptogen drawer is from a Soviet forest or a Sanskrit shelf. Some of them arrived by airport in the 1990s.

*Lepidium meyenii* Walp. — maca, a Brassicaceae hypocotyl from high Andean agriculture — has a local food history that is older than the boom, and a global supplement history that is not. Distinguishing those two is the only reason this draft exists.

This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent any disease. It is not medical advice. It is an export timeline.

Andean cultivation of a mustard-family storage organ at altitude is a serious agricultural fact. People grew a food that could handle a hard place. Spanish colonial records and later ethnographies mention the plant; I will not pretend I have a complete colonial dossier on my desk. I will say: **food first**. A dried hypocotyl in a stew is a food. A gelatinized powder in a plastic tub in Los Angeles is a later technology wearing the food's name.

The 1990s and early 2000s export boom — Peru, then the world, then arguments about yellow and red and black as if they were superhero suits — is a political economy you can read without a lab. Price, planting, identity (other *Lepidium*, other powders), sexual-marketing billboards I will not finish, and a filing under *adaptogen* because the drawer was open and the plant was from a "harsh climate." Harsh-climate logic is draft 42. Sexual-marketing logic is a claims leak this page will leave in the leak category.

Did Brekhman list maca? Not in the classic shortlist this series has been using. The recruitment is Western and late. Late recruitment is honest if you call it late. It becomes dishonest when the tub says "ancient Andean adaptogen," which is two times (Incan-flavored antiquity plus 1960s Russia) neither of which is the 1990s airport.

I care about the food-first sentence because it is a fence. If maca is a food, it can stay in the kitchen without becoming a treatment. If it is an adaptogen, it is being asked to join a functional class and then to pick up the class's wandering endpoints. The kitchen is safer for our purposes. Safer is not a nutrition rating. It is a genre.

There is a processing history worth naming: fresh root, dried, "gelatinized" (a starch-gelatinization industrial step), extracts. Each step is a technology. Technologies change what you are holding. A history that says "Peruvians used maca" and shows a scoop of industrial powder is doing a costume drama.

Identity and fraud showed up, as they do when a boom outruns a field. Other mustard-family powders, starches, a color story that outpaces a cultivar. I will not write a detective chapter. I will point at draft 28 and say the moral travels across oceans.

If you are Andean, or from a maca-growing district, the boom may have been money and trouble at once. A folder written in English does not get to narrate your district as a spiritual resource. It can say: a crop went global under a noun that was not yours. That sentence is available to any honest customs record.

The adaptogen hat on maca teaches a final, simple lesson. **The class, in retail, is now a prestige tag for 'botanical from somewhere difficult.'** Somewhere difficult can be a plateau, a taiga, a cliff, a courtyard (less so), a pasture. Difficulty is not a phytochemistry. It is a story. Maca's story, told cleanly, is: high farm, local food, late boom, later hat. You can stop after *food* and still have a history.

I have not said whether you should put the powder in a smoothie. Smoothies are not my jurisdiction. Captions are.
