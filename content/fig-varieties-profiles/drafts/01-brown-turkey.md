I have said on [The Fig Jam](https://www.thefigjam.co/p/interview-with-a-fig-grower-keith) that I am not a fan of Brown Turkey for eating, so far. We still have the trees. That sentence is the whole profile if you came here for a score. If you came here because a neighbor, a box store, or a county agent handed you a “Turkey,” keep reading. The name is doing more work than the fruit.

Ira Condit counted **at least fourteen synonyms** on Brown Turkey in 1955 and then warned that Brunswick had another dozen. Starnes and Monroe said in 1907 that no fruit was so badly mixed. They were not being poetic. They were tired. The mix did not get better because the internet learned to print tags.

## The pile

In the Southeast, “Brown Turkey” usually means a common fig — persistent, no wasp — with copper to violet-brown skin, strawberry-to-amber pulp, and a reputation for coming back after a freeze or a hard prune. University of Arkansas Extension files it with Texas Everbearing. NC State says the Brown Turkey they mean is also sold as Texas Everbearing and Harrison, and it is **not** the Brown Turkey of California. That last clause has wrecked more threads than a late freeze.

California’s commercial Brown Turkey is the plant Condit wanted people to call **San Piero**. Alabama Extension still has to write “few call it that.” It is larger, more of a shipping fig, and not the wood you want to judge an 8a winter by. Florida’s sheet calls the California plant Black Spanish in one breath and San Pedro in another. See the mess? The tag used two English words. The climates did not merge.

Texas Everbearing is the Southern sales name for a Turkey-type that fruits over a long stretch on new wood. It is not a machine that fruits in a shop in January. It is not a promise of breba. UAEX already said breba rarely overwinters in Arkansas. Main crop on this year’s wood is the crop. Mississippi State gave Southern Brown Turkey about a **60-day** pick. That is a harvest job. If you will not walk the tree, you will wear it.

If two neighbors have “Everbearing” and the fruit does not match, believe the fruit. Write unknown on the pot until the plate repeats.

## What the county agent is actually buying

UAEX does not recommend a Turkey type because it tastes like Negra d’Agde. They recommend it because it **lives**. A bit less hardy than Celeste. A bit later. A longer pick. It fruits after freeze or a severe prune. Celeste will not forgive that prune. I wrote the comparison already in the how-to set. This page is the Turkey side of the hardware.

Texas A&M’s disease handbook still files Texas Everbearing with the closed-eye set, next to Celeste and Alma. Magnolia and Kadota take more open-eye trouble. That is disease talk. Look at the ostiole on *your* fruit. [VERIFY] on the plate. I have seen “Turkey” tags on eyes I would not trust in a wet August. The name does not sew the eye shut.

Souring is water in the eye plus heat after rain. Tight is a door, not a vault. Split is a balloon that ran out of skin. Do not call every ruined fig a beetle and do not call every sour fig a split. They can arrive in the same week.

The eye on a Turkey type often colors early — reddish while the fruit is still hard. Celeste’s eye stays green until the fig is almost ready. Alabama wrote that down as a field mark. Use it. It will not settle a fourteen-name pile, but it will keep you from calling a sugar fig a Turkey because both are brown.

## Flavor, if we are being honest

Very sweet. Not rich. That is the old Southern line, and it matches my mouth so far. Sugar. Useful. A kid will eat it. I will put it in a bowl if the berry figs are still cups. I will not pretend it is a conversation.

A grocery Turkey is not the test. Grocery figs are picked for the truck. Tree-ripe in a dry week is the only fair plate. Taste that before you write the tree off, and taste it before you plant a row of them because a catalog used the word “classic.” Classic means the name printed. It does not mean I changed my mind.

Jack ranks berry figs. I do too. Red Sicilian, Negra d’Agde, Maryland Berry, Black Madeira — those are the names I said out loud. A Turkey type is how a lot of us started, or how a grandpa started, which is how Jack started. You can add the berry conversation later. You cannot add it if the first tree died and the kid decided figs are a hassle.

## How it behaves in 8a

Common fig. No wasp. Do not buy pollen for it.

Wood: it will die back in a bad winter and still try to feed you on the resprout. NC State said Brown Turkey and Brunswick produce fair-to-good crops on suckers the season after freeze injury. TAMU’s freeze chapter — five or six trunks when the shoots are about two feet — is how you turn that thicket back into a bush you can pick. Leave the wood if you wanted a shape. Leave the tree if you wanted a hole.

Season: later than Celeste, longer than Celeste. In a short, cool year the last figs will still be hard when the first frost writes on them. In a long, wet August the middle of the crop will sour if you let it hang for a prettier color. A ripe fig is a morning job. Neck wilt. Give. Soft. Not a calendar date I will invent for Saline County. [VERIFY] your first soften and write it on the tag.

Sun: **6–8 hours**. A Turkey in deep shade is still shade. Afternoon cloth in July is not a personality. It is South Arkansas.

Pots: it will fruit in a **#3** if you pot up before June and fertigate once it is a plant. In-ground it becomes the tree people mean when they say “the fig.” Shallow roots. Mulch. Do not drown the hole in clay.

Birds: dark fruit is a billboard. A green fig lasts longer on the tree. If your only fig is a Turkey type, net it or pick it. Do not write a forum post about “the year the birds won” as if that were weather.

## What I tell a neighbor

Buy wood from a person who has fruit, or take **6–8 inch**, three-node cuttings off the grandpa tree that already proved the county. Do not buy a California shipping photo. Do not buy a marketplace “Brown Turkey” from a seller with no address. The introduction page already said the review window closes before you taste type.

Plant one tool tree before you plant twelve internet names. Eat the tool fruit in a year the celebrities are still cups. Then spend money.

If you already have a Turkey and you do not like it, keep the hole. We graft. We do not throw a live root system in a pile to prove we have taste. Relabel the day the scion takes. Suckers off. The tool tree did its job: it taught the hole, it fed somebody, it waited.

If you like it, good. You are easier to live with than I am. Pick it in the morning. Do not hat-rack it in January unless you wanted a leaf bush that still might fruit. Write the real name on the pot once the fruit repeats — Southern Turkey type, Texas Everbearing type, or unknown. The English words on the nursery stick are not a pedigree.
