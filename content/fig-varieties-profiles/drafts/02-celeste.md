I am not a fan of Celeste for eating, so far. I said that on [The Fig Jam](https://www.thefigjam.co/p/interview-with-a-fig-grower-keith) and I will say it until the fruit changes my mind. We still have the trees. We give a long trial. LSU still talks Celeste as the Louisiana yard standard. UAEX still puts it first for Arkansas. None of those institutions asked my mouth.

If your grandpa has one fig, this is often it. Small. Brown to violet. Pink-strawberry pulp. Tight eye. Early. The sugar fig. People who grew up on it will fight you if you call it boring. People who grew up on berry figs will call it a date that forgot to happen. Both can be telling the truth about *their* tree.

## Malta, Celestial, Sugar Fig — and the tree in the yard

Condit wrote that the proper name is **Malta**. Almost nobody in a Southern yard says Malta. Alabama lists Celestial, Conant, Sugar Fig, Tennessee Mountain Fig. Florida adds Blue Celeste and Little Brown Sugar. The pattern matters more than the spelling: small, early, closed eye that stays green until the fig is almost ripe, wood that takes more cold than most common figs and less saw than a Turkey type.

“Improved Celeste” is a different fight. The official LSU release is **O’Rourke**, tested as a Celeste improvement, tan-brown, long peduncle, hangs when ripe. A lot of “Improved Celeste” in the trade is ICON or a leak or a wish. Do not eat this page and that tag as one plant. O’Rourke has its own draft.

If a neighbor’s “Celeste” is large, late, and open-eyed, it is not Celeste. Write unknown. Wait for a second year. The introduction page already said it takes **1–3 years** to prove type. A first-year cup can lie.

## The prune that deletes the year

UAEX’s words, not mine: if you severely prune a Celeste in late winter, you will greatly limit that season’s fruit. I have watched people treat every fig like a Turkey — hat-rack in January, congratulate themselves on a clean bush, then call Celeste a bad variety in July.

Celeste wants last year’s wood if you want this year’s crop. Dead wood is a different job. Crossing limbs are a different job. A hard freeze that kills to the ground is a different job — then you are in TAMU’s five-or-six-trunk chapter with everybody else, and you may owe yourself a lean year while the new wood learns to be a crop.

I will not give you a grid. I will walk it.

**Eye.** Tight. That is why county agents like it in a wet August. Dried-fruit beetles and souring want a door. Celeste makes a small one. Physics can still split a balloon. A week of rain plus heat can still put water where you did not invite it. Tight is a screen.

**Season.** Earlier than a Turkey type. NC State puts Celeste’s start around **mid-July** in North Carolina. That is their calendar. [VERIFY] your first soften and write the date on the tag. Earlier fruit in 8a is a gift when August turns into a steam bath. It is also how a tree finishes before you get tired of picking.

**Cold.** Often the hardiest common yard fig the Southern sheets will name. Alabama still groups it with English Brown Turkey, Alma, and Hardy Chicago when they talk 15–20°F wood. That is a conversation number, not a warranty. Unprotected tips still burn. A pot still cooks or freezes at the rim. Chicago Hardy is the resprout story. Celeste is the “please do not chop me” story.

**Flavor.** Sugar. Clean. Short. Alabama will call it arguably the finest Southern fig and then admit it disappoints in California and the Southwest. That sentence is climate, not a contest. Dry heat makes a different plate. Our humidity makes this plate. I still prefer a berry fig. I still taste a tree-ripe Celeste in a dry week before I bad-mouth a grandpa tree. Then I keep my opinion.

Jack does not have Celeste on his short list. He has Jack Lilly, which is a small tight-eye fig with a nicer balance in his mouth. Different plant. Do not “improve” a Celeste by renaming it.

## How it fails

Alabama’s trouble table is blunt: fruit turns tough and falls in hot, dry weather — typical of some varieties such as Celeste. We see the same sulk when a pot cooks on black plastic in July or an in-ground tree sits in a west fry with no water. Afternoon shade in the South is not a rumor. The Pick The Right Fig page already said it.

Birds will still find a brown fig. They find the dark ones faster. Net or pick.

A late freeze after budbreak is cruel to any early fig. Celeste moves early because it is Celeste. Pots you can roll. In-ground you cover or you take the hit. The late-freeze draft in the how-to pack is that night.

Mosaic mottles leaves on a lot of old yard figs. Condit and the Florida sheet mention it on Mission; Celeste gets it too. Mottled leaves are not a reason to discard a tree that fruits. Dwarfed fruit is a reason to take cuttings off a cleaner neighbor tree and start a new hole.

## How I would plant it

Common fig. No wasp.

Wood from a fruiting tree, not a parking-lot tag. **6–8 inches**, three nodes, lignified if you can. Root it like any other stick. Pot up to a **#3** before June. Fertigate once it is a plant, not while it is a stick.

In-ground in 8a: east wall if you have one, **6–8 hours** of sun, clay that drains, mulch over shallow roots. Alabama wants a full eight so fruit dries after rain. We give morning sun and afternoon cloth in July. A sugar fig in a west fry on black plastic is how Alabama’s “tough and falls” sentence gets written on our bench. Do not plant it in last year’s tomato hole if nematodes live there. Celeste is not a nematode solution. Alma and some LSU talk get that rumor. Pots if the ground is dirty.

Do not fertilize late. Succulent September wood is how a hardy variety writes a tender will.

If you want one fig for a child, this is still a correct answer. It lives. It finishes. It sours less than an open-eye celebrity. If you want a fig for *me*, wait until I change my mind or graft the hole. Those are different compliments. I can respect a tool and still want a berry fig on the plate.

Write Celeste on the pot when the fruit is small, early, tight, and sugar-leaning two years running. Until then it is a brown fig we have not finished arguing with.
