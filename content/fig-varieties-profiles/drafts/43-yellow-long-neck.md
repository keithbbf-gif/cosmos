On 25 July 2025 I told [The Fig Jam](https://www.thefigjam.co/p/interview-with-a-fig-grower-keith) I was looking forward to varieties we had not tried yet. Yellow Long Neck was the first name in that sentence.

This page exists so a future editor does not write a tasting note I did not earn. If we have plated it since, [VERIFY] the date and replace the middle of this profile. Until then, the honest magazine piece is a honest stub.

I am not a fan of Brown Turkey or Celeste so far. That sentence is plated. This one is not. I will not borrow a mouth from a forum average to fill the hole.

## What the name is claiming

Yellow. A long neck. A common fig in every listing I trust — persistent, no wasp. Jack already defined that on [Types of Figs](https://figroots.com/2025/08/17/types-of-figs/). If a numbered or named yellow drops at marble size every year, look at water and type before you start a wasp project in Bradley County. We will not.

Collectors treat it as a honey-leaning fig with a dramatic shape, sometimes a breba, a plant people photograph because the neck is the photograph. That is collector talk. It is not our plate. I will not write “rich honey with notes of…” in a caption. I will not steal a Commons long-neck and call it ours.

[An Introduction to Figs](https://figroots.com/an-introduction-to-figs/) already said trusted sellers matter and it takes **1–3 years** to prove type. A tag is a claim. A neck in a marketplace photo is a claim. The review window closes before you taste type. Yellow Long Neck is a pretty name. Pretty names move the wrong wood.

If a marketplace photo is a yellow fig with a short fat neck, it is not this. If it is striped, it is Flanders or Panache. If it is brown, it is Hunt or a Turkey. If it is grass-green and huge, it may be Dalmatie wearing a yellow wish.

## Sister slugs — four long things are not one cultivar

**Flanders** is Condit’s 1975 Verdone hybrid: greenish yellow with violet stripes, amber pulp, pyriform with a long slender neck, fine flavor, vigorous, not particularly hardy, good on the West Coast. [Alabama ANR-1145](https://www.aces.edu/blog/topics/crop-production/fig-production-guide/) already wrote that sentence. If your “Yellow Long Neck” is striped, you have Flanders or you have Panache. Panache is the chimera — green fruit with yellow stripes, mediocre in the South on Alabama’s sheet. Different profile.

**Hunt** has a long *stem* — a peduncle to 3/4 inch — and a small brown body. Eatonton, 1920s. No synonyms. Do not merge a yellow neck and a brown stalk because both are long.

**Col de Dame** — blanc or noire — has a neck and a late clock. Different color families. Different profiles. A neck is a family of shapes, not a cultivar.

**LSU Gold** is the official large yellow. Upright tree, late enough that 8a has to finish the year. Not a long-neck photograph. **Champagne** is the other official LSU yellow. **Excel** is Condit’s 1975 Kadota hybrid, oblate to spherical — Alabama’s shape words are the opposite of a dramatic neck. **Kadota** is the canned parent, pyriform, open-enough eye. **Peter’s Honey / Lattarula / Blanche** is the Northwest honey argument. I-258 is another looking-forward yellow I had not plated. Same interview. Different unplated profile.

Do not merge six yellow ideas because the cut looks amber. We have not earned a cut.

## Climate without a plate

A long-neck yellow fig still has an eye, a clock, and a split risk. I will not guess whether it is Gold-late or Champagne-early. I will not guess tight or open. [Pick The Right Fig](https://figroots.com/2025/07/02/pick-the-right-fig-for-you/) already said GDD is a count and the ostiole is a humidity judge. Those rules apply to a fig I have not met.

TAMU’s [disease handbook](https://plantdiseasehandbook.tamu.edu/food-crops/fruit-crops/fig/) puts Magnolia and Kadota in the open-eye souring column and Celeste, Texas Everbearing, and Alma on the closed side. Yellow Long Neck is not on that table. Photograph the ostiole after the first storm. [VERIFY]. If it sours like Kadota, we have a yellow fig with a door. If it holds through a wet week, we have a yellow fig worth the neck.

UAEX’s souring sentence is water in the eye plus heat after rain. Alabama’s trouble table says fruit sours and many split in an unsuitable variety or an unusually wet year. Split is a balloon. Even water. A drought then a flood is how any yellow fig unzips. I will not write that this cultivar splits. I will write that we will watch.

Birds: yellow hides less than green and more than black. A ripe yellow fig still smells like lunch. Net the week it softens, if it softens.

Rust is the Gulf leaf tax. Yellow-green flecks, then yellowish-brown, blisters under the leaf. A late yellow that drops leaves in August will not finish a long neck. Rake. I will not advertise rust-proof on a plant we have not fruited.

Breba: collectors mention it. In Arkansas, UAEX already told you breba rarely overwinters. If this plant brebas, that is a bonus on last year’s wood, not a reason to buy it as a June machine. Main crop on current wood is the crop we can plan for. Do not caption a wish on the breba folder.

Cold: Alabama’s 15–20°F talk names Celeste, English Brown Turkey, Alma, and Hardy Chicago. This name is not on that list. Treat it as a pot we shuffle. Our [winter protection](https://figroots.com/winter-protection/) page named Smith and Chicago, not a looking-forward yellow. Barely moist in the shop. Not a heated den.

Nematodes: pots if the ground is dirty. A new yellow is not Alma.

## How I would run it

We already wanted it. That is in the interview. Wood from a source that has fruit photos of a *long* yellow neck, not a blurry lemon. **6–8 inches**, three nodes. Alabama likes 3–6 inch pencil wood. We run a little longer. Label like it might be wrong. Pot up to a **#3** before June. Fertigate once it is a plant, not while it is a stick.

Sun: **6–8 hours**. Alabama wants a full eight so fruit dries after rain. We give morning sun and afternoon cloth in July. A yellow fig in a west fry on black plastic will cook the pot and then split when you panic-water it — if it fruits.

In-ground in 8a only after we have eaten it two years running. East wall if we get that far. Clay that drains. Mulch over shallow roots. Do not plant a looking-forward name in last year’s tomato hole. Do not fertilize late. Succulent September wood is how an unknown yellow writes a tender will.

Pick in the morning if anything softens. Write the first soften on the tag. [VERIFY]. Walk it after a storm. Photograph the neck, the ostiole, and the cut on the same day. Future-us will need all three.

If it never finishes, it joins the late-yellow museum and we graft a clock we already eat — Champagne, a true Celeste if you like sugar, Excel if that tree already proved a rain week. Keep the hole. We graft. We do not perform funerals for live roots.

If it finishes, the next editor writes the cut.
