Boyer’s grant has a long name and a simple picture. You train
a fig as a Japanese stepover: a permanent cordon at or just
above the ground, fruiting verticals every 14 inches, those
verticals cut back, new ones next year. The fruit is on new
wood, which figs already do. The trick is that the *permanent*
wood is where a blanket can find it.

He started cuttings in cells, transplanted at about a foot,
pinched, chose two leaders, ran a wire an inch off the
ground, clipped with tomato clips, let the tip grow vertical
for vigor, then laid the lower part over as it gained
length. Year one was thickness, not fruit. Voles got boxes
with traps. In winter, when nights in the hoop sat in the
mid-20s for more than an hour or two, 64-inch wire hoops
and DeWitt AG19 went over the cordon. That is the method. I
am not improving it from a desk.

What he measured is why I bother to write it. Ground under
the cover ran on the order of **20–23°F** warmer than
outside ground in Feb–Mar 2024. Buds on the 27th of
February. First fruit the 21st of July. 230.98 lb in 2024
off a house that had been a disappointment the year before.
He also cooked a row by leaving the cover on into a hot
April. The plants lived. The lesson lived louder.

I will not tell an 8a backyard to copy 120 trees at 7×6.
I will tell a Zone 7 person who wants *wood* that this is
the most honest way I have read to keep wood in a cold
house without heating the sky. You are marrying the plant
to the soil battery and putting a blanket on the marriage.
That is work every week in the training years and a dance
every spring. It is not a weekend kit.

8a can steal a lighter version: one or two low arms you can
pin and chip if a 12°F night is coming, and ignore if the
winter is kind. You do not need 14-inch verticals on a
wire grid to eat. You need a plant you can get a blanket
over. A six-foot bush is a monument. A two-foot arm is a
tool.

Zone 9 almost does not need a stepover. The wood lives. A
fan on a wall will do. If you stepover in 9A for fun, you
are choosing a shape, not a survival method. Do not tell
yourself it is required.

Labor is the cost Boyer named. Setup is slow. Clips are
weekly. Leaves come off the cordon as you lay it down.
Vole traps in winter. The payoff is a cover that fits and
a harvest that can start when open-field trees are still
waking. If you will not do the clips, do not start the
wire. A sagging cordon is a weed with a grant vocabulary.

I have not run his trial. I have run enough low wood to
believe the blanket. If I build a fruit house in a county
that takes tops, I will build it near the ground on
purpose. The peak of a gothic is for air. The crop is at
your shins. That looks wrong until January. Then it looks
like the only plant in the room that still has a plan.

If I only steal one measurement, it is the July 21 first pick
after a late-February wake — a 6b year with a second cover,
not an 8a promise. The mechanism is the steal: wood where a
blanket fits, days bought at the front. The 230 lb is his
house. The cooked Longue d’Aout is his warning. I will take
the mechanism and the warning and leave the 120-tree math on
his page.
