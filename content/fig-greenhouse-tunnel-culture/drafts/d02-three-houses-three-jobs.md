I use three words in this pack and I am going to keep them mean
something. A **greenhouse** has a door you think of as a room. It may
have glass or twin-wall or film. It is where I park pots for the fig
shuffle, where Jack looks at cups, where a bench can hold a tray
without the wind stealing the label. [Winter Protection](https://figroots.com/winter-protection/)
already named it: a climate-controlled garage, shed, or greenhouse
that stays above **40°F**. That is a winter shop. It is not an orchard.

A **hoophouse** is the backyard cousin. Pipe, film, a crank or a
sandbag. You can build one ugly and it will still work if the sides
open and the floor drains. I do not sneer at ugly. I sneer at sealed.

A **high tunnel** is the farm word. Gothic or Quonset, roll-up sides,
end walls you can drive a cart through, enough length that a row is a
row. OSU used one at Piketon. Zhi used a 34×72 in Maryland 7A. Boyer
used about 5,000 square feet in Pennsylvania 6b. Those are not
hobby sentences. They are also not a command that you need 5,000
square feet to eat a fig.

The job is the thing, not the brand on the bow. If the job is *keep
the collection alive without dragging fifty #3s through a door every
November*, you want a winter room with a floor you trust and a
thermometer you read. That can be a greenhouse. It can be a corner of
a barn. It should not be a hoop you cannot get a wagon into on a
wet night.

If the job is *finish fruit that dies green in October*, you want
season. That is a hoophouse or a high tunnel over in-ground wood, or
over pots you are willing to leave in place. The roof is doing the
work. The heater is usually not invited.

If the job is *sell a flat in September*, you want the high-tunnel
sentence: spacing, training, a variety that actually ripens, and a
vent schedule you will keep when it is 95°F at 2 p.m. Gao was plain
that they did not run a cost-benefit on the Piketon house. Ernst’s
Kentucky floor — about 10 lb a plant at $3 — is a paper floor. I
will not pretend our yard is a farm trial.

Zone 7 leans toward the orchard house because the year is short and
the wood is the argument. Zone 9 leans toward no house, or a house
that is only a rain roof you can strip. 8a steals both and gets in
trouble when it steals the sealed winter from 7 and the sealed summer
from a catalog.

I have used a greenhouse as a shop and I have wanted a tunnel as a
season. I try not to ask one building for both jobs at once. A shop
that is also a fruit house is how you get pots in the way of a cordon
and a hose that waters the wrong climate. Jack can have a bench. The
row can have a floor. If they share a roof, they still need two
rules.

Name the job on a card before you order steel. Winter shop. Season
extension. Rain roof. Market row. If the card has two jobs, you need
two rules, not one kit. If the card has four jobs, you are buying a
barn and calling it a fig method. I have watched that happen. The
figs did not read the invoice.
