Vegetable people argue about ridge lines the way fig people argue
about names. East–west for winter sun. North–south for even summer
light on two sides of a row. Both sentences are true in a book. A
fig house in Zone 7–9 has to pick which season it is lying to.

If the house is a winter shop for cans — the shuffle room — I want
the long wall to steal winter sun without turning the peak into an
oven the first February week we get 70°F. A south face with a
vent at the top is a room I can work in. A sealed south face is a
car. Jack does not need a solar cooker over a tray of cups.

If the house is a fruit tunnel, I care more about summer air than
about a perfect December beam. Figs in a row want light on the
wood you kept, and they want a breeze that is not a theory. A
north–south ridge with roll-up sides on the long walls lets the
prevailing wind do something. An east–west ridge can put one side
in a permanent shade stripe and the other in a fry pan. I have
walked both. I have also walked a house that was sited for the
driveway, which is how most backyard kits get their compass.

Do not put the door on the winter-wind end and then wonder why
January feels like a chute. In our county the ugly wind has a
direction. Yours does too. Stand outside in January before you
order steel. The catalog does not know your chute.

Trees and buildings already on the lot are the real orientation.
A hoop in the shade of a pecan is a mushroom house. A hoop on the
south side of a metal barn can steal heat and also steal a glare
that cooks the first pot row. I have used a wall as a thermal
mass. I have also used a wall as a reflector that made one side
of a hoop ten degrees ruder than the other. A cheap thermometer
on two stakes will tell you more than a forum thread.

Zone 7 can lean toward winter sun. The year is short. Every
February photon is a week you might not get in October. Zone 9
should lean toward summer air and a way to strip the film. 8a
has to do both, which is why I will not give you a single
compass rose and a blessing. I will give you a question: which
failure are you building against? October green fruit, or July
cook? Face the house at the failure.

Leave room to walk the long side with a wagon. Leave room to
stack snow or a shade-cloth roll. Leave the south wall of the
*yard* available for an outdoor tree. I have seen a kit eat the
only good wall on a lot. Then the outdoor Celeste went into the
side yard and sulked, and the hoop got the sun, and nobody was
happy except the person who sold the pipe.

Siting is not a vibe. It is drainage plus wind plus a neighbor’s
walnut plus the path you will actually take at 6 a.m. when the
row cover has to come off. Face that path. Then face the sun.
If those two fights, the path still wins, because a house you
will not vent is a cooker pointed at the sky.
I have sited a hoop for the hose and regretted it every July. The hose
can be longer. The wind cannot be talked out of a chute. If the only
flat pad is the chute, I would rather terrace a ugly slope that drains
and takes a south glance than win the hose and lose the year. A wagon
on a slope is a habit. A cooker in a chute is a climate. Habits are
cheaper.

Walk the lot at 8 a.m. and at 2 p.m. before you order. Morning tells
you frost pockets. Afternoon tells you the fry pan. If those two walks
disagree, believe the afternoon for siting the side, and believe the
morning for where you put the tender cans. The compass rose on the kit
page does not do those walks. You do.
