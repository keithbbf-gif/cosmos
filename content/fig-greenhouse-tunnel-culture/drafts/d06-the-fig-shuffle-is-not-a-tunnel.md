[Winter Protection](https://figroots.com/winter-protection/) already
named the move we actually do. Relocation. The fig shuffle. You
take the pot to a garage, a shed, or a greenhouse where the
temperature stays above **40°F**, and you do not park it next to a
heat vent because a fig still wants a dormant period. Roots in a
can can freeze below **15°F** without insulation. We shuffle
because it is less work than wrapping fifty cans.

That paragraph is about pots. It is not about a high tunnel full of
in-ground cordons. I am saying this in the first week of the pack
because people mash the two and then write to us as if we promised
them an orchard when we promised them a room.

A shuffle house is a winter shop. The floor is dry enough to roll a
wagon. The door is wide enough for a 5-gallon without a fight. The
thermometer is at pot height, not at the peak where the sun lies.
Water is a sip. Jack can set a tray of cups on a bench and not have
the wind steal the tape. The plants are allowed to look like sticks.
If they look like a June flush in January, the room is too kind.

A tunnel orchard is the opposite personality. The plants are in the
ground, or in cans you have decided not to move. The roof is there
so fruit can finish and so rain can miss the eye. You want light.
You want sides that open. You want a winter method that does not
depend on you dragging a hundred pounds of wet Promix through a
door in the dark.

I have used one roof for both jobs. I do not recommend it as a
plan. The shop wants 40–50°F and a dim week. The orchard wants
every December sun it can steal and then a second cover on the
wood. If you heat the shop to keep Jack’s cups from stalling, you
may wake the orchard row. If you vent the orchard row like a July
tomato house, you may freeze the pot block you parked along the
north wall. Two rules under one film is a dance. Most people step
on a plant.

Zone 7 shuffles harder because the night is meaner and the
collection is often all cans. Zone 9 shuffles less because the
yard will hold a pot if you group them and wrap the cans. 8a is a
pile of #3s and #5s and a smaller in-ground set. The pile goes
inside. The in-ground set gets a wall, a mulch, and a decision
about a hoop. That decision is not “the greenhouse.” The greenhouse
is already spoken for.

If you only have one structure, pick the job that matches the
plants you actually own. A collection in cans wants a room. A row
you intend to sell from wants a tunnel. A single backyard tree
wants a south wall and a blanket before it wants steel. I have
watched people buy a 20-foot kit for three pots. They did not get
an orchard. They got a place to trip in the dark.

Jack will write the shop bench when this pack needs a cup. I am
writing the row. Do not hand either of us a building and ask it to
be the other man’s job without saying so on a card.

A practical test: can you move the plants in one evening without
cursing the door? If yes, you have a shuffle house. If the plants
are in the ground, or the cans are too heavy and too many, you
have a tunnel problem and you need a winter method that does not
depend on your back. Woodchips, a low hoop, a blanket — those
are orchard tools. A wagon and a 40°F room are shop tools. I
have used my back as a bridge between the two. My back has
opinions now.

The fall-airlayer page already hinted at this split. In-ground
wood has a shorter season; you take the layer sooner. Potted wood
can sit in a greenhouse warm enough for roots. That is not
permission to turn the orchard row into a propagation bench in
January. It is permission to admit that a warm shop and a cold
row are different kindnesses. If you only remember one sentence
from this draft, remember that. The shuffle is a winter shop. A
tunnel is a season. Buy the one you will operate.
