This is the fork I keep putting back on the table. In-ground
in a tunnel is an orchard decision. Pots in a tunnel are a
collection decision that borrowed a roof. Both can fruit.
They do not ask the same winter, the same hose, or the same
back.

In-ground is why people build a fruit house. The earth is the
battery. Boyer clipped cordons to it. Zhi tried to keep wood
on it and learned that chips on the wood beat barrels in the
aisle. Roots that have a county do not freeze like roots in
a #3. FigRoots already said a can can be toast below 15°F
without insulation. In-ground roots have a blanket you did
not have to wrap, if the water is not sitting on them.

In-ground is also a marriage. You will not shuffle a cordon
into the garage on a Sunday. The winter method has to come
to the plant: cover, bury, pray, or lose wood and fruit on
new growth. In Zone 7 that marriage is the whole project. In
8a it is a choice you make for the names you are willing to
lose the top of. In Zone 9 it is often just a planting.

Pots under the same roof keep the shuffle option. That is
worth more than people admit. A late name, a tender honey
fig, a one-of-one trade — those can live in a #5 on the
south side of the hoop from April to October and roll into
the 40°F room when the night gets honest. You used the house
as a season and the shop as a winter. Two jobs, two
addresses. I like that better than pretending a #3 is
in-ground because it sat on the dirt all year.

Pots also lie about water. A can on a woven floor dries from
the side and cooks from the sun on the black plastic. A
tunnel pot in July is a thirstier animal than a tunnel
in-ground on a berm. If you mix them in one hose zone you
will drown the ground to save the can, or starve the can to
be Mediterranean about the ground. Two valves. Or two days.

Zone 7: in-ground if you have a winter method you will
perform; pots if you will not. Do not in-ground a museum.
Zone 9: in-ground is the default; pots are for the names you
do not trust in nematodes or in a flood. 8a: in-ground for
the short list that already behaves on a wall; pots for the
rest, even under film.

I will not plant the whole collection in a hoop and call it
simplification. The collection is a shop problem. The hoop,
if I build one for fruit, is six or eight names with air and
a winter plan. The rest of the names can visit as cans and
leave when the night says so. That is not indecision. That
is two tools. The house pack has been trying to keep those
tools from marrying badly since d02.

A hybrid I will allow: plant the short list, and set the museum
cans *on* the fabric aisle from April to October, then roll
them out. They use the roof. They do not marry the clay. They
do not share the winter blanket with the cordon. That is three
addresses and one film. It only works if the aisle stays an
aisle. The day the cans eat the walk, you have a thicket with
handles. Handles are not a training system.

If a can sits on dirt long enough, roots will find a
hole in the fabric and marry the county anyway. Then
the shuffle is a lie and the winter method is a surprise.
Lift and turn a quarter each month, or set cans on a
board. A quiet marriage is still a marriage.
