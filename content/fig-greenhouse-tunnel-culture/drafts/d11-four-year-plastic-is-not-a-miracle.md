The film has a name on the roll and a year in the name. Four-year
IR/AC. Six-year. Greenhouse plastic, not the clear tarp from the
hardware aisle. I will say the hardware-aisle sentence first
because I have watched it fail. Construction sheeting goes
brittle, sweats the wrong way, and cooks a July into a science
fair. If you are going to spend on steel, spend on film that was
made to be a house.

IR means the plastic hangs onto a little more of the day’s heat
after the sun leaves. AC means anti-condensate: the water sheets
instead of raining back down on a leaf. Those are real features.
They are not a furnace and they are not a fungicide. Zhi’s
tunnel still fell to the outside night. Boyer’s high tunnel still
needed a second cover on the wood. If a seller tells you the
four-year film is a Zone 7 heater, the seller is selling film.

Replace it on the year, not on the vibe. UV does not care that
the house “still looks clear.” A cloudy film is a shade cloth
you did not choose. A brittle film is a storm away from a fig
row wearing a kite. I have patched. Patching is a winter chore,
not a personality. When the tape is holding more house than the
roll, you are in a new kit and denying it.

Zone 7 can justify the better roll because the house is a
season tool and a winter windbreak. Zone 9 can justify a cheaper
film if the plan is to strip it for summer, but then you have to
actually strip it. A cheap film left on through a Gulf summer
is how you grow a yellow cave. 8a should buy the roll it will
leave on through a winter, and then treat July as a vent problem,
not a film-upgrade problem. I have never fixed a cooker by
buying a clearer plastic.

Whitewash and shade cloth are summer cousins of the same
sentence. You can paint a cheap shade onto good film. You can
throw a 30-percent cloth over a gothic for August. You cannot
make bad film into a climate. Start with a roll that was meant
to be a house. Then decide, month by month, whether the house
needs a hat.

Do not stretch film like you are wrapping a boat and then walk
away. Wiggle wire, a proper channel, a baseboard that does not
saw the edge — those are boring and they are why the roll lasts
the years in the name. A staple-through-lath job from a weekend
video will saw a hole by March. I have seen the hole. The wind
found it first.

If you only remember one thing: the film is a filter you
replace. It is not a cultivar. It will not make a late fig
early. It will not make a sealed hoop breathe. Buy the roll
that matches the years you will actually keep the house, hang
it like you mean it, and put the rest of the money into the
crank that opens the side. The crank is the crop. The roll is
the roof.
Storage of the spare roll matters. A roll on a barn floor in the sun
is a three-year roll when you hang it. Keep it cool, on end, out of
the UV it is supposed to survive *after* it is a house. I have cooked
a spare and then blamed the brand. The brand was fine. The floor was
a skillet.

If a storm windows the house, patch the same day if you can. A flap
is a sail. A sail takes the next bow. The four-year name assumes you
are a host, not a spectator. Host the film. It will host the fruit.
