Zhi planted forty one-year trees in a 34×72 and then told on
herself. The growth inside was too vigorous. The distance
should have been farther. She would thin half. Ten feet was
the number she kept in the learning-outcomes paragraph; eight
showed up in the summary. I will not pretend those are
lab-precise. I will pretend they are a person who ran out of
aisle.

A fig under plastic is a different animal than a fig on a
windy fence. The house wakes it early, the rain is a drip you
control, and the plant has nothing to do but grow. People
plant at four feet because the baby trees look lonely. Then
year two is a thicket, rust lives in the middle, and you
cannot crank the side without a face full of leaf.

Boyer’s cordon spacing was tighter — 7 ft in the row, 6 ft
between rows — because the training was a two-dimensional
job: one low wood, verticals every 14 inches, suckers gone.
That is a system. It is not a bush planted on a 7-foot
center and left to invent its own architecture. If you copy
his numbers without his pruners, you copied the crowding and
not the method.

I would rather have six names at 8–10 ft in a 14×32 than
fourteen names I cannot walk. The house is not a collection
shelf. The collection shelf is the pot block in the winter
shop. The orchard row is a short list with air. Air is how
you pick, how you see rust start, how a breeze gets through
when the side is up.

Zone 7 people crowd because they want every bow to earn. Then
the winter cover cannot sit on the wood without tearing on a
neighbor. Zone 9 people crowd because the year is long and
the plant is a weed. Then August is a jungle and a mite
farm. 8a people crowd because we have too many names and not
enough dirt. I am the problem I am describing. The house is
where I have to stop.

First-year loneliness is a feeling. Second-year air is a
yield. Zhi said more branches meant no fruit, and that two
or three were enough. That is a spacing sentence as much as
a pruning sentence. A plant with room can keep two good
arms. A plant in a crowd keeps twelve and a story.

If you already planted tight, do not wait for a grant to
thin. Move every other tree in the dormant season — pots
are easy, in-ground is a job — or train a cordon and evict
the extras as cuttings. Jack will take the wood. The row
needs the hole. A stump in a house is less expensive than
a thicket you fertilize.

Walk the aisle with your arms out. If you cannot, the house
is full. Full is not the same as productive. Full is how a
34×72 becomes a lesson about half as many trees.

A first-year photo of a lonely row is the photo people hate. I
would rather hate the photo than hate year three. If a visitor
says it looks empty, I say it looks like August air. Empty is
a stage. A thicket is a stage that does not end. Plant the
empty. Fill it with two arms and a walk. The names you evicted
can live in cans. Cans do not need 10 ft. They need a wagon.

If the kit is already full, the next name goes in a can.
That is not a consolation prize. That is how the aisle
stays an aisle. I have a lot of names. I do not have a
lot of 10-foot holes. The house is allowed to say no.
