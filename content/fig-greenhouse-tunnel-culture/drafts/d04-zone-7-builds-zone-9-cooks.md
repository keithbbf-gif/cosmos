Zone 7 and Zone 9 both talk about houses. They are not talking about
the same building.

In Zone 7 the year is a short fuse. Figs die back. Even when the
crown lives, last year’s wood is a maybe. Fruit on new wood needs
the whole summer, and the first average frost in a Maryland 7A
county sits near mid-October. Zhi wrote that outdoor fruit there
often ripens in October and then meets that frost. A tunnel that
moves ripe into September and holds a few more weeks into November
is not a luxury. It is the difference between a crop and a story
about a crop.

Zone 7 also has a night that will take wood. FigRoots already put
numbers on the table I will not restage: cold snaps below **17°F**
may take limbs; **10°F** will take unprotected trees to the ground
and may take them outright. A single-layer hoop does not cancel
that. It changes the wind and the daytime. Night is still a night
unless you add a second cover, a bury, or a room. People who skip
that sentence build a pretty barn and then inventory dead cordons
in April.

Zone 9 is a different fight. The tree keeps wood. Celeste and the
LSU names and a south-wall Brown Turkey will often finish without
asking you for steel. The house, if you build one, is a rain roof
and a bird roof. The danger is not October. The danger is that you
copied a Zone 7 winter habit and left the film tight in July. Then
you have a cooker, a rust chamber, and a spider mite party. Zone 9
should be able to strip a hoop to a skeleton. If the kit cannot
open, it is the wrong kit.

8a hears both conversations and wants a single answer. There is not
one. Our average annual extreme minimum is 10–15°F. That is a Zone 7
number on a bad night and a Zone 8 shrug on a kind one. Our summer
is a Zone 9 argument with clay. So I steal Zone 7’s respect for
wood and for the last six weeks of fruit. I steal Zone 9’s refusal
to live in a sealed bag. I do not steal Zone 7’s heater lifestyle.
I do not steal Zone 9’s “never cover anything” when a 12°F night is
on the radio.

The build decision is a climate decision. If your last ripe week
and your first killing frost are the same week most years, build
for season. If your wood dies every January and you want breba, you
are asking for a winter method — bury, wrap, pots in a room — and
the tunnel is only half of it. If your fruit already finishes in
August and the rain is the thief, build a roof you can open. If
none of those are true and you just want a project, plant two
outdoor names first. A house is a second crop of work.

I have watched a Zone 7 neighbor wrap a hoop like a gift and never
open it. I have watched a Zone 9 neighbor nail film to a frame and
then vanish until September. Both lost fruit. The first lost it to
heat and mildew. The second lost it to a freak freeze they were not
emotionally prepared for. 8a is allowed to be emotionally prepared
for both. That is the job.

The calendar makes the argument physical. In a 7A year the last
green figs and the first killing frost are often the same ten-day
window. A roof that holds 35°F fruit a little longer is a harvest
tool. In a 9A year the same ten-day window is August rain on an
open eye. A roof that you can peel is a quality tool. Same steel,
different month, different win. If you cannot name the month you
are buying, you are buying a hobby.

I write Zone 7 and Zone 9 into every draft because 8a people
borrow like magpies. We steal a 7A bury and then leave it on
through a 70°F January Thursday. We steal a 9A “sides always
open” and then get a 12°F Friday. Steal the *question* from each
zone — what kills the crop this month — not the costume. The
house is allowed to change clothes. The fig is not required to
wear your identity.
