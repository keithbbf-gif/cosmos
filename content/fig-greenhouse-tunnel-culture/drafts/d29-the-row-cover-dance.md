Boyer named the weeks from March to mid-May the row-cover
dance. I will keep his name. Under a floating cover in a
high tunnel he saw afternoons at 120°F and nights that
could still freeze. Humidity high, plants not wilting —
until the April he left the cover on Longue d’Aout and
cooked the row to the ground. The plants came back. He
still picked 73 lb off the re-grown wood. That is a
survival story. It is not a recipe.

The dance is: cover on when the night will collect the
flush, cover vented or off when the sun will collect the
flush. Daily in the rude weeks. Not “check on Saturday.”
A sensor helps. A sensor you do not read is a decoration.
He had minute-by-minute logs. You can have a $12 min-max
and a phone alarm at 10 a.m. I would rather have the
alarm.

Fabric weight matters. His DeWitt AG19 is a light
agricultural cover, not a moving blanket. A furniture
blanket under film on a 80°F noon is a steamer. FigRoots
already said to stake blankets so you do not break wood.
In spring I want something I can throw and something I
can pull in two minutes. If I cannot pull it in two
minutes I will leave it, and then I will be Boyer in
April.

Zone 7 should learn this dance or not use a second cover
in spring. A second cover that stays on is a cooker. A
second cover you will not buy is a dead flush. There is
no third option that is “set it and forget it.” Zone 9
should almost never be in this dance for figs. If you
are, the film is still on too tight for the year you
live in. 8a will dance in March and April some years and
skip it in a late spring. The skipping year is how you
get sloppy the next year.

Venting a low tunnel is a crack at the ends, not a
theory. If the cover is plastic instead of fabric, the
crack is mandatory or you will paint the leaves. Boyer
noted that in the early months his figs took heat that
would have stopped a textbook plant, as long as humidity
stayed high. That is a 6b observation under fabric. I
will not use it as permission to ignore a 140°F peak in
8a under clear film. Our sun is a different argument.

When I pull the cover for the season, I want the house
sides to be the new dance. Mid-May in Pennsylvania was
his move. Our move may be earlier. The plant will tell
you: if the cover is a sauna at 9 a.m. and the night was
45°F, you are late. Pull. The wood is awake. The blanket
has become a hat it does not want.

Write the dance on the calendar like a chore, not like a
feeling. Feelings sleep in. Chores get a crank. The cover
is a chore that saves a year, and a chore that can erase
one. Treat it with the same respect you treat a heat mat
without a thermostat. That is to say: do not run it
unattended and then act surprised.

I stage the cover on a reel or a folded pile at the end
wall, not in the barn. A cover in the barn is a cover you
will not fetch at 6 p.m. The dance is a distance problem
as much as a temperature problem. If I cannot reach it
in two minutes, I will leave it, and then I will be a
grant report. I would rather be sweaty at 6 than famous
for a cooked row.
