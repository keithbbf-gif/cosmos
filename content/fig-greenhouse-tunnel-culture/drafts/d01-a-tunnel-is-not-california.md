People send me a photo of a California fig row and a photo of a hoop kit
and they want those two pictures to be the same sentence. They are not.
A tunnel is a bag of air with a roof. California is a climate. If you
confuse them you will buy plastic for a problem you do not have, or you
will skip plastic for a problem you do.

I have eaten California figs. Some of them were good. Most of them were
picked for a truck. A fig that can ride to Arkansas is a fig that left
the tree before it was jam. That is the whole fresh-fig argument, and it
is why a backyard tree in 8a can beat a grocery fig without trying. It
is not why you need a thirty-foot gothic.

What a house buys in Zone 7–9 is narrower than the catalog. It keeps
rain off a softening fruit. It knocks the wind down. It steals a few
weeks on each end of the year. In a county that kills the crop in
October, those weeks are the crop. In a county that already finishes
Celeste on a south wall in August, those weeks are a hobby you will pay
for in July when the same bag of air hits 110°F and the rust likes the
stillness.

I do not grow Mission in a row for drying. I do not run deficit
irrigation as a program. Kong, Lampinen, Shackel, and Crisosto put
numbers on split under California water. That paper can stay in
California. Our swing is a pot on gravel and a Gulf thunderstorm, or a
hose you ran because you felt guilty the night before the front. A roof
helps the rain-split. It does not help you if you binge the drip and
then congratulate the plastic.

Zone 7 people sometimes want the house to be California. They have been
told figs are subtropical, so they buy a heater and a thermostat and
they try to keep January at 55°F. Then the wood never hardens, the
buds wake in a warm week, and a 22°F night in March writes the rest of
the story. Zone 9 people sometimes want the house to be California too,
but from the other side: they want a dry roof over an open-eye fig in
a rainy August. That half is closer to true. The other half is that
Zone 9 July under film is a cooker unless the sides come off like you
mean it.

8a sits in the doorway. Some years I could almost skip a house for
in-ground Celeste and Chicago Hardy and still eat. Some years the last
ripe week and the first hard night arrive in the same forecast, and I
want a roof over the fruit I already paid for. That is not California.
That is a county with a personality problem.

If you only change one habit this year, change this one: stop pasting
a Central Valley orchard onto a kit from a farm-supply flyer. Ask what
fails in *your* year. If the failure is October fruit still green, a
tunnel is a tool. If the failure is July leaf rust and cooked pots, a
tunnel is a new way to fail. If the failure is that you have never
tasted a ripe fig, plant a name that finishes outdoors and learn the
fruit before you learn the film.

I will write the rest of this pack as if you already know a fig can
be better than a strawberry when it is actually ripe. That sentence is
already on FigRoots. The house does not make it true. The house, used
like a tool, gives the fruit more days to become true. Used like a
fantasy, it gives you a plastic barn full of tender wood and a July
you cannot vent.
