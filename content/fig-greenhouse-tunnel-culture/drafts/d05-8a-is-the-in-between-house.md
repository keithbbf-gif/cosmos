Zone 8a is where fig people get cocky about houses the same way they
get cocky about wrapping. The map says the average annual extreme
minimum sits between 10 and 15°F. Some winters you could leave an
established Celeste against a wall and eat two crops. Some winters
you look at gray sticks in March and wish you had spent November on
something other than a YouTube from coastal 8b.

A house in 8a inherits that personality problem. In a kind year the
tunnel is a rain roof and a late-September blanket. In an ugly year
it is the only reason you have wood above the mulch — and only if
you added a second cover or a bury, because the film will not hold
an 11°F wind by itself. Both years are legal 8a. I keep a cheap
min-max on the north side of the shed for this reason. The zone map
is a thirty-year average of the worst night. Your house is a night.

I do not treat 8a as “hardy enough, skip the house.” I treat it as:
most winters the earth and a south wall are enough for the right
in-ground name once the plant has a real root system. Young plants
do not get that deal. Neither do pots. Neither does a fig you stuck
in a wind tunnel because that was where the hose reached. A hoop
over those three is a tool. A hoop over a fifteen-year Celeste that
already finishes is a hobby. Both are allowed. They are not the
same invoice.

The in-between also messes with advice. A grower in 8b coastal air
will tell you they never cover. A grower in 7a will show you a
leaf-stuffed cage and a gothic kit with two layers of film. Both
are telling the truth about their nights. If you copy the 8b person
in an inland 8a pocket that drops to 10°F twice a decade, you will
eventually pay. If you copy the 7a double-cover every year in a
mild 8a, you will spend November building a monument to a cold that
does not arrive, and you may hold moisture against the bark until a
warm spell in January steams it.

Summer is the other half of the in-between. We get Zone 9 heat and
Zone 7 humidity on the same week. A house that was a kindness in
October becomes a rust room in July if the sides stay down. I have
opened a hoop at 2 p.m. and felt the air come out like a dryer. The
figs were not grateful for my winter personality. They wanted a
skeleton and a breeze.

So the first fact about an 8a house is not a kit SKU. The first
fact is that you will use it as two buildings. From leaf-drop until
the wood is brown, it is a windbreak and a place to hang a second
cover. From the week the buds swell until the week the fruit
softens, it is a vented barn that happens to have a roof for rain.
If you cannot switch personalities, do not build it. Plant outdoors
and shuffle the pots. FigRoots already told you how.

Write down what the thermometer did inside and out. Next September
you will remember the figs and forget the low. The house will not
remember for you. I have a notebook with nights that embarrassed
me. That notebook has saved me more money than any kit page.

The in-between also changes how you spend. A Zone 7 farm can
justify a gothic and a second cover because the alternative is no
fruit. A Zone 9 backyard can justify almost nothing because the
wall already works. 8a has to look at the collection. If the
collection is cans, spend on a winter room and a wagon. If the
collection is three in-ground names that already finish, spend on
pruners and a rain year. If you want one structure to cover a
bad night *and* a late fruit week, budget the vent hardware as
seriously as the steel. A house without a crank is a Zone 7
costume in a Zone 9 summer.

I do not owe 8a a romance. I owe it a switch. Off in July. On in
October. Half-on in March with a hand on the cover. People who
want a house that is “set and forget” should forget the house and
plant Chicago Hardy on a wall. The in-between is a job. It is
not a setting on a thermostat.
