This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 1928 the *American Journal of Diseases of Children* printed Clara M. Davis’s “Self Selection of Diet by Newly Weaned Infants.” Three children — Earl H., Donald R., Abraham G. — sat at Mount Sinai in Chicago after a few days of their mothers’ milk. Then the experiment proper: a list of simple foods, unmixed, unseasoned, some raw, some plainly cooked. Nurses were told to sit still. When a child pointed, the food moved. No coaching. Davis wanted to know whether a weaned infant, offered a wide honest tray, would keep himself alive without a percentage card.

In June 1939 she told the Canadian Medical Association, at Montréal’s Windsor Hotel, a longer story. Later popular tellings say fifteen children, two boys followed for four and a half years, something like 36,000 daily food records. The 1928 paper is three infants. The 1939 address is the cafeteria people remember. Both numbers are in print. Do not collapse them.

This essay is that cafeteria as a historical object. It is not permission to let a toddler live on crackers.

## The problem she thought she was solving

Holt’s catechism and Rotch’s arithmetic had made refusal a medical event. Pediatric offices filled with children who would not eat the prescribed mixture. Davis, a Chicago pediatrician (M.D. 1901 in the Hektoen note; 1878–1959), thought the experts had been too sure. Her wager: if the tray is real food and the adult shuts up, the child will not invent scurvy.

She was not offering candy and tinned dessert. The list is meat, organs, bone marrow, eggs, cereals, vegetables, fruit, milk. One child with rickets was given access to cod liver oil on the tray; Davis reported he took it until radiographs looked better and then stopped. That sentence has been doing cultural work for ninety years. It is a report from a closed ward, not a supermarket study.

## What the cafeteria was

It was closer to an orphanage than to a family kitchen. Davis arranged for infants of unmarried young mothers and widows who could not keep them — Stephen Strauss’s later *CMAJ* account is blunt about this. The children lived where the food could be weighed. Every spill counted. Height, stool, bone films. The adult in the room was a nurse under orders to be a piece of furniture.

That is not Ellyn Satter’s later family table, and it is not a modern “baby-led” Instagram. It is an institution with a research question. The question was powerful because the surrounding medicine had made mothers feel stupid. The institution is uncomfortable because the children were not free to leave.

Sometime after Davis died in 1959, the boxes of raw records disappear from the trail Strauss could find. We have the papers. We do not have the ledgers. A magazine that treats the cafeteria as a settled law of appetite is leaning on a missing basement.

## Who was left out

Children who could not point. Children who could not sit. Children whose swallow was unsafe on thin liquids — a category Davis’s ward was not built to name. Children whose households already offered family food without a physician’s permission. The cafeteria argues with Holt. It does not argue with a tracheostomy.

Also left out: the mothers who placed the infants. Their hunger is not in the calorie table.

## Residue

Every later American fight about control at the high chair — Satter’s division of responsibility, Toomey’s sensory hierarchy as a *named method*, Klein’s permission language, the parent who says “he’ll eat when he’s hungry” and the parent who says “the doctor said ounces” — walks through Davis’s hotel ballroom. The 1939 audience heard a pediatrician tell them the child had a wisdom the prescription card lacked.

They also heard a study that chose the foods first. Wisdom inside a curated tray is not wisdom in a gas station. Davis said as much if you read her, and the popular memory forgot the tray.

A clinician who quotes Davis to end a conversation has not read her. A clinician who quotes Holt to end a conversation has not read the cafeteria. The two objects need each other. The catechism made the cafeteria necessary. The cafeteria made the next century’s relationship literature possible. Neither object is a home program.
