This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

The sibling dysphagia pack already walked this hallway once, as a swallow-heritage stop. This essay stays longer and looks at the name war.

By the late 1970s and 1980s, American children’s hospitals had a new census: infants who survived, children with tubes, children whose scales would not rise, children whose swallow pictures were ambiguous. Someone had to own the meal. Speech-language pathology, occupational therapy, gastroenterology, nutrition, nursing, pulmonology, surgery, sometimes psychology. The phrase that let them sit down without settling the credential fight was *feeding team*.

Joan C. Arvedson’s service at Children’s Hospital of Wisconsin became the name a generation of students met. *Pediatric Swallowing and Feeding*, first with Linda Brodsky (Singular, 1993), later editions that bring Maureen Lefton-Greif onto the spine, made the hallway portable. A textbook is a meeting you can mail. Milwaukee did not invent hunger. It made a meeting assignable.

## The problem the decade thought it was solving

Adult dysphagia had Jeri Logemann’s 1983 book and a cookie swallow. Pediatrics had Bosma’s films and a lot of opinions. NICU nurses owned the clock. GI owned the reflux sentence. ENT owned the airway. OT owned the hands and, in many buildings, the word *feeding*. SLP owned the swallow — if the state practice act and the medical staff bylaws agreed, which they did not always.

A team is what you invent when the note war gets expensive. It is a truce with minutes. It is also a way to keep a parent in a chair while five adults take turns being sure.

## Two rehabilitation credentials, one high chair

This pack will not settle whether feeding “belongs” to speech-language pathology or occupational therapy. The historical fact is that both walked in, both taught courses, both wrote paperbacks, and both sometimes treated the other as a trespasser. Wolf and Glass (1992) are occupational therapists writing an infant book that speech pathologists still wear out. Morris and Klein’s *Pre-Feeding Skills* (1987) sat on both shelves. Rona Alexander’s neurodevelopmental teaching crossed the same border.

Parents did not care about the alphabet after the name. They cared who answered the pager. Hospitals cared who could bill. Universities cared who could place a student. Those three cares are not the same care.

## Who was left out of the meeting

The school therapist who sees the child at lunch and is not invited to the tertiary-team note. The early-intervention SLP in a living room whose state will not let her call it swallowing. The rural hospital with no team, one PRN therapist, and a barium schedule on Tuesdays. The parent who is listed as “historian” when the story is convenient and “noncompliant” when it is not.

Also left out: the child who is not a NICU graduate. Cardiac. Oncologic. Autistic. The child with a typical swallow study and a diet of three foods. The team that only knows the premature mouth will miss that child, then call the miss a behavior problem.

Lefton-Greif’s Hopkins pediatric line is the other American academic address. This pack does not give her a forty-fifth profile; the sibling pack already did. Mention her here as the reminder that Milwaukee was not the only hallway.

## Residue

Every later “interprofessional” slide inherits this furniture. So does every clinic that has a feeding team on the website and a six-month wait. Assignable is how a job survives a charismatic NICU nurse. Assignable is also how a job becomes a bottleneck.

A modern reader who wants a seating chart will not find one on this page. The historical residue is the parent still in the chair, listening to a truce. The truce is better than a single certainty that is wrong. It is not the same thing as a meal.
