This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Across Longwood Avenue from Boston Children’s, cows once grazed for a hospital that did not trust the city’s milk. Thomas Morgan Rotch’s laboratory at the Infants’ Hospital treated cream as a number you could order. The percentage method — fat, sugar, protein adjusted as if the infant were a flask — made him the American name on a fashion that ran, in archive language, from about 1890 to about 1915.

He was born 28 December 1849 and died 9 March 1914. Harvard’s early pediatric chair is the job title. The milk lab is the object.

## The work

Rotch wanted cow’s milk to behave like human milk without pretending it was human milk. Centrifuged cream, known percentages, a prescription the milk room could fill. Boston Children’s later exhibit text still quotes a sample mixture; this pack will not reprint it as if it were advice. The historical fact is that a physician could telephone a laboratory and receive a bottle that matched a card.

He also repeated the old doubling-and-trebling weight milestones beside curves that did not always show a normal band. Weaver’s work on infant weighing is the secondary warrant for that habit. A number next to a missing range is a style. Later growth charts will try to supply the range. Rotch is the style’s American pediatric ancestor.

## What later people kept

Clean milk as a civic achievement. The idea that composition matters. What they discarded: the belief that ever-finer percentages were the same thing as care. Jacobi was rude about it at the time. British pediatricians were bored. Pasteurization and simpler dilutions made the card a museum label. The Hunnewell cows were gone by the 1930s.

What they still argue about, without his name: whether a feeding order is a laboratory act or a relationship. Every branded infant formula that advertises a “scientifically designed” ratio is speaking Rotch’s language with a marketing department.

## Close

Boston’s medical geography matters. Longwood Avenue, a hospital herd, a laboratory that could take a telephone order — that is a city rich enough to industrialize milk. Rotch’s percentages are what that city sounded like when it talked about infants. A later clinician in a county without a milk lab is not failing Rotch. She is living in a different economy.

The sample mixture in the hospital archive is a temptation. Reprinting it would make this profile a recipe card with a Harvard accent. The accent is the history. The recipe is refused. If a reader wants the arithmetic, the archive exhibit is still there. This page will keep the cows and the card as furniture, not as homework.
