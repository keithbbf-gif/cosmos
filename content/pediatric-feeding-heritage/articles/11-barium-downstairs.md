This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Jeri A. Logemann’s *Evaluation and Treatment of Swallowing Disorders* (College-Hill, 1983) taught a generation of speech-language pathologists to watch a swallow on fluoroscopy. The sibling dysphagia pack owns that door. This essay is what happened when the door opened onto a child.

Pediatric videofluoroscopy is not a small cookie swallow. The body is smaller. The cooperation is a negotiation. The radiation argument is louder. The parent is in the room, or is fighting to be. The meal may be breast milk, formula, a puree the child has never accepted, a barium mixture that tastes like a science project. Susan E. Langmore’s FEES papers (1988, with Schatz and Olsen) gave adults a scope through the nose. Children’s hospitals later asked whether a small nose could bear it. Consent grew teeth.

This essay is that migration as a historical object. It is not a guide to which test to demand.

## The problem the decade thought it was solving

Bedside guesses were failing in both directions. Children were being held NPO on a rumor. Children were being fed on a rumor. Pictures promised an end to rumor. Pictures also promised a profession that could stand next to radiology and GI and not look like a visitor.

Maureen Lefton-Greif’s Hopkins pediatric work and Memorie M. Gosa’s later pediatric VFSS literature are part of the American academic residue. Pamela Dodrill’s reviews sit on the same shelf. None of those names are a reason to treat a fluoroscopy suite as a first visit.

## What the picture could not decide

Whether the child will eat at home. Whether the parent can sleep. Whether a trace of barium in the airway on one sip is a life sentence. Whether a normal picture means the feeding disorder was “just behavior.” The last one did real damage. A clean study became a way to discharge the swallow and keep the insult.

Silent aspiration — the adult literature’s scare — walked downstairs too. Useful when it prevented casual reassurance. Harmful when it made every cough a verdict. The ethical literature on pediatric instrumental assessment is, in part, a literature about fear.

## Who was left out

Children whose hospitals had no pediatric-capable suite. Children who could not be held still without a fight the family would remember for years. Children whose insurers would pay for the picture and not for the six months after. Children who needed a meal more than they needed a frame rate.

Also left out: the school SLP who receives a report written in adult abbreviations and is asked to “follow the diet” in a cafeteria that does not have the diet.

## Residue

IDDSI and the National Dysphagia Diet are adult-texture furniture that later tried to speak pediatric. The next essay takes thickening as inheritance. Here the residue is simpler. A picture is a tool. A tool is not a team. A team that starts with a picture because the picture is billable has learned the wrong lesson from 1983.

A Tuesday barium list that treats every child as a small adult cookie swallow has not heard the radiation argument or the parent in the room. The list can still be the right list. It has to be able to say why.

Logemann did not invent the pediatric meal. She invented a professional confidence that the meal could be watched. Confidence travels. In a children’s hospital it has to travel with a physicist, a parent, and a reason that is not curiosity. The reason is the heritage worth keeping. Curiosity dressed as care is the heritage worth naming.
