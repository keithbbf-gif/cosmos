This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

American feeding heritage likes a hallway and a paperback. Australian speech-language pathology likes a cohort. Sheena Reilly’s work — Murdoch Children’s Research Institute and university addresses in the public record — treated pediatric feeding and communication as things you could count in a population, not only describe in a tertiary clinic.

She was living when this pack was staged. Published epidemiology and clinical research only.

## The work

Reilly is in this pack so the 2019 U.S. consensus cannot pretend it was the first time anyone counted. Prevalence papers make a field look like a public health object. Public health objects get policy. Policy is how a rural child might, someday, meet a service that is not a three-hour drive.

She also sits on the communication side of the profession. This file stays on the feeding residue: children who do not eat like the chart, counted without being reduced to a Milwaukee case.

## What later people kept

A reminder that most children do not live in a children’s-hospital ZIP code. What they still argue about: whether a prevalence number travels across foodways and health systems.

Goday’s word needed a world that had already been counting. Reilly is one of the counters this series refuses to leave in a footnote.

## Close

Murdoch Children’s Research Institute is a Melbourne engine. Melbourne is not a suburb of Milwaukee. Reilly’s cohorts are why this pack’s last era essay can say most children are not tertiary-hospital children without waving a hand. Someone counted.

Communication and feeding share a mouth and a research career in her line. This file does not annex the language papers. It steals one habit: if you cannot say how common a problem is, you are still in anecdote. Anecdote built this field. Census is how it grows up.

Cohorts take years. Hallways take minutes. A field that only funds hallways will keep being surprised by prevalence. Reilly’s career is a long argument against that surprise.

Australian speech pathology’s research habit is easy for U.S. readers to treat as a cousin they will visit later. Later is how a census stays foreign. Read a prevalence paper before the next hallway story, and notice who is missing from the U.S. hallway version of the same counted child at home.
