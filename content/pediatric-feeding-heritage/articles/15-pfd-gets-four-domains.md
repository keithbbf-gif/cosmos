This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In January 2019 the *Journal of Pediatric Gastroenterology and Nutrition* printed “Pediatric Feeding Disorder: Consensus Definition and Conceptual Framework,” Praveen S. Goday and a long author line. The paper did not invent children who could not eat. It gave a name that could travel across GI, nutrition, therapy, and psychology without each shop using a private dialect.

Pediatric feeding disorder, in that consensus, is impaired oral intake that is not age-appropriate and is associated with medical, nutritional, feeding-skill, and/or psychosocial dysfunction. Four rooms. A child may live in one or in all four. Pediatric dysphagia remains a swallow-function word. The two can sit in the same body. They are not synonyms. ASHA’s later practice-portal language follows that split. This pack will not copy the portal. It will say the split was a relief to people who had been using “dysphagia” as a suitcase.

## The problem the decade thought it was solving

Billing, research, and humility. Without a shared name, studies could not be compared. Without a shared name, a swallow-normal child with three foods and a falling curve had to be called something insulting or something vague. “Failure to thrive” blamed the outcome. “Picky eater” blamed the character. “Oral-motor dysfunction” blamed the tongue. “Infantile anorexia” blamed a relationship. Each older word was sometimes accurate. None of them could carry a team.

The 2019 paper is a diplomatic object. Diplomacy leaves someone unhappy. Swallow specialists worried the word *feeding* would dilute dysphagia. Behavior specialists worried medical rooms would still own the meeting. Parents worried a new acronym was a new way to wait six months for an appointment.

## What four domains cannot do

They cannot feed the child. They cannot create a rural team. They cannot stop a clinician from stuffing every child into the domain she knows. A framework is a map. Maps do not walk.

They also cannot settle culture. A diet that is “restricted” in one clinic is a family foodway in another. Age-appropriate oral intake is a phrase that still smuggles Gesell’s chart unless someone watches the smugglers.

## Who was left out of the consensus table

This pack will not pretend to have the full author-politics. Consensus papers always have a backstage. What a reader can say without gossip: American academic medicine has a zip-code problem. International feeding epidemiologists — Sheena Reilly’s Australian line is a profile later — had already been counting children while some U.S. services were still arguing about the word.

Also left out: adults who grew up in the older words and do not recognize themselves in PFD. Language change is not a cure. It is a filing system.

## Residue

Every slide deck that now opens with four boxes is this paper’s afterlife. The useful afterlife is a meeting where GI does not get to send the child away because the swallow study was clean, and therapy does not get to ignore weight, and psychology does not get to ignore the airway. The cheap afterlife is four checkboxes on a form.

A rural reader who meets PFD only as an insurance code has met the cheap afterlife first. The paper is still worth opening. Codes are not rooms.

Goday’s name is on the lead line. The work is a committee’s. This pack will profile him as the person who midwifed a word, not as the inventor of hunger. Words are how fields grow up. They are also how fields hide. PFD is a better suitcase than “poor feeder.” It is still a suitcase.
