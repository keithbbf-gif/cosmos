This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Some clinic rooms of the 1980s and 1990s had a chair that was not for the parent. The child sat. The adult ran a program. Bites were counted. Escape was blocked or bargained. Applied behavior analysis and older feeding-compliance traditions gave those rooms a scientific accent. Children gained weight. Parents sometimes left feeling like the problem that had been solved.

Other rooms tried to put the parent back in the chair as a person with a job. Ellyn Satter’s Division of Responsibility — parent provides, child decides, in the language of her books — came out of dietetics and family therapy, not speech-language pathology. Irene Chatoor’s work at Children’s National classified infant feeding disorders, including the phrase *infantile anorexia*, and treated the meal as a relationship with a diagnosis attached. Marsha Dunn Klein’s later Get Permission stance made consent a clinical word. Kay A. Toomey’s SOS Approach to Feeding put a sequential-oral-sensory name on a hierarchy that courses then carried across the country.

This essay names those objects. It does not teach them.

## The problem the decade thought it was solving

Children were not eating enough, or not eating enough kinds, and the swallow picture was not the whole story. Medicine offered reflux drugs, then different reflux drugs. Therapy offered motor stories. Someone had to talk about learning, fear, power, and the fact that a hungry child and a terrified child can look alike from the doorway.

Behavior rooms said: the child can learn. Permission rooms said: the child will not learn if the meal is a siege. Both sentences can be true. The professional fight was about which sentence gets to go first, and who is allowed to say it.

## What the names hide

SOS is a brand and a published method. This pack will not print the steps. ABA feeding protocols are a literature and a controversy. This pack will not print a discrete-trial script. Satter is a relationship claim that popular parenting culture simplified into “he’ll eat when he’s hungry,” which is not what a careful reading of her work supports when medical risk is in the room. Chatoor’s classifications moved; DSM and DC:0–3 moved with and against them. A magazine that freezes any of these at one edition is doing museum work without a label.

Klein’s permission language is easy to steal and empty out. “We got permission” can mean a child leaned in. It can also mean a therapist narrated over a child who had no choice. Heritage includes the emptied-out version.

## Who was left out

Parents who were already being told they were the treatment. Parents who could not attend a ten-week group. Parents whose culture treats a clean plate as love and a leftover as insult. Autistic adults who later described childhood feeding programs as trauma. Those testimonies are part of the record whether a clinic liked the tone or not.

Also left out: the medically fragile child for whom “wait for hunger” is not available, and the child with a typical body for whom a compliance chair was used because it was the only chair the county funded.

## Residue

The 2019 PFD consensus will put “psychosocial” in a domain beside medical, nutrition, and feeding skill. That fourth room is this essay’s afterlife. It does not pick a winner between Satter and a reinforcement schedule. It says the meal has a social life.

A clinician who only has a compliance chair will hurt some families. A clinician who only has permission language and no medical partners will miss a child who is not safe to wait. The parent chair is the residue worth keeping: the adult who already lives with the meal gets a seat that is not the defendant dock.
