This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

The book is small enough to lose in a nurse’s bag. *The Care and Feeding of Children: A Catechism for the Use of Mothers and Children’s Nurses* (D. Appleton, New York, 1894). Question, answer, next question. Luther Emmett Holt wrote as if the household had been waiting for a recitation. Decades of reprints suggest the household, or at least the class of household that bought Appleton books, had been.

He was born 4 February 1855 and died 14 January 1924. He made his name at the Babies’ Hospital in New York and in the American pediatric establishment that was still inventing itself. The catechism is why he is in a feeding-heritage pack. The hospital is why the catechism sounded like law.

## The work

Holt backed Thomas Morgan Rotch’s percentage method and then out-published him for the nursery audience. Later editions multiplied mixtures — ten, thirteen, nineteen in the secondary reconstructions — until the kitchen was a satellite laboratory. He also told readers that weight was to infant feeding what temperature was to a fever. A scale in the home is Holt’s residue as much as a cream percentage.

He wrote for mothers and children’s nurses, not for the foundling register and not for the tenement that had no ice. The catechism’s confidence is a class confidence. It is also a civic one: dirty milk was killing children, and a profession that could prescribe a clean mixture felt like progress. Sometimes it was.

## What later people kept

The idea that infant feeding is a subject a physician may own. The idea that a parent can be taught by Q&A. What they discarded, or should have: the fantasy that arithmetic settles a mouth. Clara Davis’s cafeteria is the argument with this man. Pierre Budin’s Paris scale is a cousin who preferred the breast and a return visit. Commercial tins were the cousins who did not need a physician at all.

Do not call Holt a speech-language pathologist. He would not have known the job. He is here because later feeding therapists still meet his ghost every time a discharge note treats ounces as character.

## Close

A catechism is a teaching form borrowed from the church. Holt used it for milk. That borrowing is why the book felt like law in nurseries that already believed cleanliness was next to godliness. It is also why later relationship writers sound like heretics when they tell a parent the child may refuse. Heresy is a strong word for a Tuesday. It is the right word for a 1894 object that still sits in the back of a discharge note.

New York’s Babies’ Hospital was not a rural kitchen. Readers who treat the catechism as a census of American childhood have already missed the ice wagon. The ice wagon is the unwritten chapter. Holt wrote the written one. This series needs both, and it will not mix him a formula to prove it read the written one.
