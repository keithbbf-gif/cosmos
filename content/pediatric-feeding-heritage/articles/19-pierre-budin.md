This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

A balance, a sterilizer, a return visit. Pierre Budin’s *Consultations de Nourrissons* treated the city’s infants as a population you could argue with. *Le Nourrisson* appeared in 1900. William J. Maloney’s English *The Nursling* (Caxton, 1907) is the object many Anglophone readers actually met. The translator’s preface is a sermon about degeneration and motherhood. Budin’s pages are more practical: temperature, weight, milk that will not kill, a physician who shows up again.

He was born 9 November 1846 and died 22 January 1907. Professor of obstetrics at Paris, Clinique Tarnier, Academy of Medicine. The feeding-heritage claim is not the title. It is the consultation as furniture.

## The work

Budin preferred the breast. Sterilized cow’s milk was the fallback, not the identity of the field. He treated the weight curve as the infant’s fever chart — a sentence later American writers, including Holt, would echo in their own keys. Premature infants sit in his book as nurslings who need heat and a plan, not as “NICU graduates.” The incubator and gavage are period tools in this life. This pack will not teach them.

Infant-welfare clinics across Europe and then the United States borrowed the shape: weigh, advise, come back. Weaver’s history of baby-weighing is the secondary map. Budin is a source of the map’s French origin story, not the only clinic on earth.

## What later people kept

The return visit. The scale as a moral object. The idea that a city owes its infants a room that is not only a delivery ward. What they discarded, or should have: the translator’s racial degeneration talk, which is in the English book whether modern readers want it or not. What they still argue about: whether a number on a scale is a conversation or a verdict.

Do not baptize him an SLP. Do not baptize him the inventor of the NICU. He is the man who made the premature mouth a public-health object before speech-language pathology existed to inherit it.

## Close

English readers met Budin through a translator who wanted to save a race. That is in the 1907 preface. Leaving it out would be a kindness the book does not deserve. Infant-welfare work in the Belle Époque is tangled with natalism and with genuine grief over dead babies. Both can sit in one consultation room.

The premature mouth in *The Nursling* is not yet a speech-language pathology object. It is an obstetric object. The later NICU that never says his name is still using his furniture: come back, bring the infant, look at the curve. Coming back is the heritage worth stealing. The degeneration sermon can stay in 1907.
