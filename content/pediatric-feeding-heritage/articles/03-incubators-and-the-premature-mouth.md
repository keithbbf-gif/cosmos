This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Pierre Budin’s Paris did not look like a modern NICU. It looked like a consultation room with a balance. *Le Nourrisson* (1900), Englished in 1907 as *The Nursling*, taught that a weight curve was the infant’s fever chart. Sterilized milk if the breast failed. Regular return visits. The *Consultations de Nourrissons* were infant-welfare furniture: a scale, a stove for sterilizing, a physician who believed the city’s death rate could be argued with.

The premature mouth arrives in that room as a problem of heat and of milk. An infant too small to suck is not yet “pediatric dysphagia.” He is a nursling who may need a tube, a dropper, or a wet nurse who will sit longer than the clock. Gavage — feeding down a tube — is already a hospital verb. This essay will not teach it. It will say that the verb is older than the speech-language pathologist who later inherited it.

## Heat as a feeding device

The incubator is a box that pretends to be a uterus. French obstetric services, then German and American hospitals, then — uncomfortably — world’s-fair sideshows. Martin Couney’s infant-incubator exhibits on Coney Island and at expositions are part of the American memory whether clinicians like the company or not. Parents paid to see the babies. The babies were, some of them, alive because of the box. The ethics sit on the table with the glass.

A warm infant can be fed. A cold infant is a different physiology. Budin’s pages spend time on temperature and weight before they spend time on the mouth. Later NICU feeding literature will rediscover that order after a century of machines. The sibling dysphagia pack owns James Bosma’s cine of infant heads. This pack owns the prior fact: someone had to keep the head warm enough to matter.

## The tube as a historical object

Gavage in a premature infant is a nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century hospital practice, not a 1990s invention. The materials change: rubber, then plastic, then the branded tubes a modern unit stockroom recognizes. The social fact does not change. A tube lets a staff count milliliters when a mouth cannot. It also lets a staff stop asking what the mouth is doing.

That is the scar this series will keep returning to. Volume is visible. Skill is slower. A profession that can graph ounces will graph ounces. A profession that wants to talk about coordination will have to fight the graph. Budin’s scale is already a graph. The later cue-based NICU is an argument with that graph, not a virgin birth.

## Who was left out

The sideshow infants were a public. The tenement premature who never reached a consultation was not. Rural births, midwife births, infants who died before a box existed in their county — they are the denominator the incubator photograph hides. Black American infants in Jim Crow hospitals met a different box, or no box. A history that only walks the Clinique Tarnier has already chosen a zip code.

Wet nurses appear again. A premature infant who can suck may still need a woman whose milk has come in. Hospital dairies and later formula rooms will try to replace her. Replacement is a policy. It is not a small thing.

## Residue

Every modern NICU that times a first oral attempt is standing in Budin’s doorway whether it knows his name or not. The doorway is a scale and a belief that the city owes the infant a return visit. What the doorway is not: a speech-language pathology origin story. SLPs will enter this room much later, after survival curves change the census, after Wolf and Glass write a 1992 paperback, after someone decides the swallow is a rehabilitation object.

The sibling pack’s pediatric-team essay starts when the hallway already exists. This essay is why the hallway had infants in it. The box kept them. The tube fed them. The mouth waited. Waiting is not a protocol. It is the historical condition the later clock will try to manage.
