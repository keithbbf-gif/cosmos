This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 1992 Therapy Skill Builders put out a 475-page paperback that a certain kind of therapist still recognizes from across a room: Lynn S. Wolf and Robin P. Glass, *Feeding and Swallowing Disorders in Infancy*. Tucson imprint. Catalog number 4190 on some copies. Bosma wrote a jacket sentence that treated therapists as people who had already grown insights the physicians needed. That sentence is a passing of keys.

The same shelf already held Suzanne Evans Morris and Marsha Dunn Klein, *Pre-Feeding Skills* (1987; second edition 2000). A few years later Arvedson and Brodsky’s *Pediatric Swallowing and Feeding* (1993) arrived as the thicker, more medical sibling. Three spines. Three claims on the same infant.

This essay is the shelf as a historical object. It is not a reading assignment with homework.

## The problem the paperbacks thought they were solving

Graduate programs taught adult swallow and pediatric language. The NICU and the special-care nursery hired people anyway. Staff learned from nurses, from each other, from a workshop, from a bad night. A paperback is how a field pretends that is not the training model.

Wolf and Glass wrote from a Seattle Children’s occupational-therapy line, infant-shaped, bottle-and-breast-and-tube in the same chapter architecture. Morris and Klein wrote the sensory-motor and pre-feeding vocabulary that let a therapist talk before the first sip. Arvedson and Brodsky wrote the team that included an airway surgeon’s name on the title page. Students who only met one spine think that spine is the field.

## Imprints as minutes

Therapy Skill Builders, Psychological Corporation, Pro-Ed, Singular, Plural — the logo trail is how you date a pediatric paperback in the wild. Early copies smell like clinic photocopy rooms. Later hardcovers smell like a graduate syllabus. The move from spiral-adjacent clinician manuals to university-press-ish objects is a class move. It is also how pediatric feeding tried to look like a science to people who fund sciences.

Do not confuse a second edition with a settled science. A second edition is a publisher saying the first one sold.

## Who was left out of the bibliography

Parents as authors. Nurses as first authors. Therapists outside the coastal children’s hospitals. Writers in languages other than English. The Australian and British feeding literatures that Sheena Reilly and others will later make impossible to ignore. The disability community that was already eating, or being fed, without waiting for Tucson.

Commercial course binders from the 1990s and 2000s sat *beside* these books and sometimes replaced them in a clinician’s actual week. This pack will name SOS, SOFFI, NOMAS, Get Permission as published method-names in later essays. It will not pull their worksheets. The shelf is already crowded.

## Residue

A 2026 student who buys the newest Arvedson edition and never touches Wolf and Glass has missed the infant-only argument. A student who worships Wolf and Glass and never reads a GI paper has missed the body under the bottle. A student who only knows a weekend course has missed the shelf entirely.

The residue is not which book is “right.” The residue is that pediatric feeding became something you could assign over a summer. Assignable books create assignable jobs. Assignable jobs create people who think the meal began when the ISBN did.

Bosma’s jacket line is the tell. He is the cine-and-atlas ancestor blessing the therapists. The blessing is real. It is also a date. Before 1992 the work was already happening in nurseries that did not have a paperback. After 1992 the work could be cited. Citation is a kind of safety. It is also a kind of forgetting.
