This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

A speech-language pathologist’s name alone on a feeding book can look like a raid. Linda Brodsky’s name beside Joan C. Arvedson’s looked like a truce. *Pediatric Swallowing and Feeding* carried an airway surgeon on the title page. Students who never met her still met the implication: this meal has a larynx, tonsils, a nose, a surgeon who might be in the next room.

She was born 25 May 1952 on Long Island and died 13 February 2014 in Buffalo, age 61. Bryn Mawr 1974, Women’s Medical College of Pennsylvania, Albert Einstein residency, then 1983 to Children’s Hospital of Buffalo and SUNY Buffalo. Chief of pediatric otolaryngology, director of a center for pediatric otolaryngology and communication disorders, tenured professor of otolaryngology and pediatrics. Vineyard Gazette and AMWA obituaries agree on the death date. The third edition was in early talk when she died; Lefton-Greif’s later spine is part of that afterlife.

## The work

More than a hundred papers, twenty-seven chapters in the memorial counts. The Sylvan Stool teaching award from SENTAC. Triological Society membership, rare enough in Buffalo to be mentioned in the notices. Mentorship of women in medicine is in those notices too. This pack will not turn a life into a eulogy contest. The feeding-heritage object is the printed truce.

An ENT who takes communication disorders as part of the center’s name is already refusing the idea that the ear, nose, and throat stop at the tonsil. Feeding is where that refusal becomes a meeting.

## What later people kept

Airway in the feeding conversation from the first page, not as a consult you remember later. What they still argue about: whether a surgeon’s name on a therapy book medicalizes a meal that is also a relationship. Both things can be true. Brodsky’s career is the medical half standing still long enough to be edited.

## Close

Buffalo winters, a children’s hospital that changed names around her, a Vineyard summer in the obituaries. The life is larger than a title page. This pack keeps the title page because that is the feeding object: an ENT who let her name stand next to a therapist’s and did not treat that as a demotion.

SENTAC and the Triological Society are club furniture. Club furniture is how a field decides who is serious. A communication-disorders center with a surgeon at the head is a seriousness claim. Later teams that forget the airway until the third visit have forgotten her half of the truce.
