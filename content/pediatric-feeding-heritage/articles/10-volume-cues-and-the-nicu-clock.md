This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

A NICU clock is a moral object. It says when a feed is due. It says when a nurse is late. It says when a volume is a success. For a long stretch of late-twentieth-century American nursery practice, the successful feed was the one that disappeared into the infant on schedule. Gavage finished what the mouth did not. Discharge math loved a number.

Then a literature grew that tried to give the infant the gavel. Infant-driven, cue-based, infant-led — the nicknames proliferate and do not have a single inventor year. Catherine S. Shaker’s teaching and papers made the argument audible to speech-language pathologists who were new to the unit. Erin Sundseth Ross’s SOFFI program put a published name on a way of watching late preterm and term infants. Nurses had been watching the whole time. The papers made the watching citable.

This essay is that argument as a historical object. It is not a bedside card.

## The problem the decade thought it was solving

Volume-driven culture produced infants who “took” feeds and still looked wrecked — desaturations, apnea, a suck that was a panic, a parent who was told the baby was a good eater because the bottle was empty. Empty is not the same as coordinated. Budin’s scale from 1900 was still winning.

Cue-based language tried to steal the win back. Look at the infant. Stop when the infant says stop. Do not make the clock the attending. That sentence is ethics. It is also, in a unit that is paid for throughput, a labor fight. A feed that follows an infant takes longer than a feed that follows a schedule. Longer feeds need staffing. Staffing is a budget.

## What the nicknames hide

“Cue-based” is a family of practices, not a brand with one manual. Some units meant “watch before you uncap the bottle.” Some meant a whole quality-improvement bundle. Some meant a poster. A magazine that treats the phrase as a settled method has not stood in two different nurseries.

SOFFI is a published program name. This pack will not print its steps. Shaker’s work is a teaching lineage. This pack will not print her handouts. The historical fact is that speech-language pathology entered the NICU as a guest of nursing and neonatology, then stayed long enough to argue about the clock.

Wolf and Glass’s 1992 book is already in this argument, earlier, from an OT infant shelf. The 2000s did not invent infant observation. They professionalized it for a new credential.

## Who was left out

Units without an SLP. Units whose neonatologist thought feeding therapy was a luxury. Families who could not room-in, and therefore could not be the “cue” the poster wanted. Infants on ventilators for whom oral feeding was a future tense. The late preterm infant on a postpartum floor, not in a NICU at all, going home on a clock that still belonged to the nursery’s old habit.

Also left out: the infant whose cue is hunger the unit cannot see because the tube has been the meal for weeks. Cue-based language can become a cruelty if the only legal cue is a perfect suck.

## Residue

Every modern discharge meeting that argues about “ad lib” versus a number is still in this fight. So is every parent who was told, in the same week, “never wake a sleeping baby” and “he has to take thirty.” The contradiction is the heritage.

A clinician who wants a winner will be disappointed. Volume kept children alive. Cues kept some children from being bullied by a bottle. The honest residue is a unit that can hold both sentences without turning either into a religion. Religions photocopy well. Infants do not.
