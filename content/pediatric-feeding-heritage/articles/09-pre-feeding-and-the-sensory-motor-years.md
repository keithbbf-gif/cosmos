This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

*Pre-Feeding Skills* is a title that does a lot of work. Suzanne Evans Morris and Marsha Dunn Klein put it on a Therapy Skill Builders spine in 1987. The claim inside the title: there is a job before the job. Tone, sensation, the way a body organizes toward a spoon, the way a child learns that a mouth is a place things enter on purpose. You could teach that job without pouring anything.

The 1970s and 1980s had already prepared the vocabulary. Neurodevelopmental treatment. Sensory integration after A. Jean Ayres. Rona Alexander’s oral-motor teaching. Marjorie Meyer Palmer’s neonatal sucking scale (NOMAS papers from 1983 onward). A field that had been talking about swallow pictures discovered it also needed to talk about children who never got near barium because the meal failed earlier.

This essay is that fashion as a historical object. It is not a list of exercises.

## The problem the decade thought it was solving

A swallow study can be normal and the child still will not eat. A bottle can be finished and the child still cannot move toward a spoon. Adult dysphagia tools looked silly in those rooms. Therapists reached for motor and sensory stories because those stories had institutions, courses, and a way to write a note.

Sometimes the story was right enough. A child with cerebral palsy does not meet a spoon as a typical toddler does. A child with a sensory history of tubes and tape may treat a texture as a threat. Sometimes the story was a way to keep working when medicine had shrugged.

The scar is isolation. Isolated oral-motor drills — lips in a mirror, a tongue that is supposed to do homework — became a cottage industry. Later evidence fights would treat some of that industry as a scar tissue. This pack will not referee every paper. It will say the fight is part of the heritage. A method that can be photocopied will be photocopied. Photocopies outlive the caution in the original chapter.

## Two shelves, one argument

Morris and Klein’s book let a clinician talk about readiness without pretending readiness was a moral quality. Klein’s later Mealtime Notions and Get Permission work moves the same household toward consent and relationship — a different essay. Palmer’s NOMAS tried to make a preterm suck a score you could agree on. Alexander’s teaching tried to make a whole body the context for a mouth.

These are not the same objects. Students who collapse them into “oral motor” have already lost the plot. Pre-feeding, as a title, was a brake: do not pour yet. Isolated exercise, as a habit, was an accelerator: do something with the mouth while you wait. The field needed the brake. It often bought the accelerator.

## Who was left out

Children whose problem was never motor. Children whose problem was hunger in a house without food. Children whose problem was an undiagnosed airway. Children whose problem was a tube that had become the whole identity of the meal. A sensory-motor year that cannot see those children will treat them as noncompliant mouths.

Also left out: the parent who was already doing the “pre-feeding” work without a workbook — rocking, waiting, offering a finger, stopping. The book can honor that parent. The course industry can forget her.

## Residue

Every later “feeding skill” domain, including the 2019 PFD consensus, owes a debt to the idea that the meal is not only a swallow. The debt is real. So is the bill: a generation of photocopied hierarchies that treated a child’s face as a gym.

A clinician who can say “something has to happen before the sip” and then sit on her hands is closer to the title *Pre-Feeding Skills* than a clinician who fills the hour with a mouth gym because the hour was scheduled. The title was a warning. Warnings do not survive contact with a productivity standard unless someone keeps saying them out loud.
