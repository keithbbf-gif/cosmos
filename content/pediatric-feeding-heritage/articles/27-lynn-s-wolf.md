This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Therapy Skill Builders, Tucson, 1992. *Feeding and Swallowing Disorders in Infancy*, with Robin P. Glass. Four hundred seventy-five pages. A jacket line from James F. Bosma treating therapists as people who had already grown a craft. Lynn S. Wolf’s name is the first one on the title page that a certain generation can still see with their eyes closed.

She was living when this pack was staged. Occupational therapist, Seattle Children’s line in the public professional record. Published book only as the object. No home detail.

## The work

The book is infant-shaped. Bottle, breast, tube, and the nursery’s clock share architecture instead of living in separate manuals. That architecture is why speech-language pathologists wore out an OT paperback. It is also why the 1990s could pretend a summer of reading was a training model.

Wolf is not a solo inventor of infant feeding. She is a first author who made the infant stay the subject when adult swallow furniture was trying to walk downstairs without taking off its coat.

## What later people kept

An infant-only argument you can hold. What they discarded, or should have: the idea that one paperback replaces a nurse’s night and a neonatologist’s doubt. What they still argue about: cue versus volume, a fight the book sits inside without ending.

Glass shares the labor. This pack gives her the next file so the second name does not become “et al.”

## Close

Seattle Children’s in the 1980s and early 1990s was a building full of infants who had not read Logemann. Wolf’s first-author line is a West Coast occupational-therapy claim on those infants. East Coast SLP fame does not get to annex the paperback without saying so.

Catalog number 4190 is a small ugly fact this pack likes. Ugly facts keep a book from becoming a myth. You can still find used copies with a clinic stamp and a coffee ring. The coffee ring is the real citation index.

Bosma’s jacket sentence can be read as a physician noticing therapists. It can also be read as a field asking for a blessing it no longer needed. Both readings can be true. The paperback did the work either way.

Used copies travel farther than citations. A stamp from a hospital that has changed names twice is a better provenance note than a syllabus week. Keep the stamp. It is how infant work actually moved.
