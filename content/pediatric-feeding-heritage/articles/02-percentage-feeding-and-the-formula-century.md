This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Sometime in the 1890s a Boston physician could order infant food the way a chemist orders a solution. Fat, sugar, protein — named as percentages, mixed from cream, milk, water, milk sugar, limewater. Thomas Morgan Rotch’s laboratory at the Infants’ Hospital treated cow’s milk as a problem that had been waiting for arithmetic. Luther Emmett Holt, in New York, wrote the catechism mothers and nurses actually held: *The Care and Feeding of Children* (D. Appleton, 1894). The book went through edition after edition. The kitchen became a satellite of the hospital.

This essay is that fashion as a historical object. It is not a formula.

## The problem the decade thought it was solving

Urban cow’s milk killed children. Tuberculosis, diarrhea, summer complaint — the names shift; the funerals do not. Boston Children’s kept a herd across Longwood Avenue, the Hunnewell cows, because the open market was not a clinic. Pasteur later becomes the civic answer. Before pasteurization is ordinary, the answer is a laboratory and a prescription.

Rotch’s percentage method, in hospital archive language, ran roughly from 1890 to about 1915 as a common American teaching habit. No one mixture suited every infant, so the arithmetic multiplied. Holt’s later editions listed more and more “formulas” — ten, then thirteen, then nineteen in the tellings Rima Apple and others have unpacked. British pediatricians were unimpressed. G. F. Still at Great Ormond Street said there was nothing new in it. Abraham Jacobi, who had already been building American pediatrics, was scathing. The fight is the residue: is infant feeding a science of numbers, or a craft that numbers dress up?

Commercial houses were not waiting for the fight to end. Liebig’s food, Nestlé, Eskay’s — late-nineteenth-century advertisements sold a tin as if it were a physician. The percentage method and the tin are cousins. Both say the household cannot be trusted with milk. One says the doctor will calculate. The other says the factory already did.

## What the numbers hid

A percentage does not name the woman who cannot stay home. It does not name the immigrant dairy, the ice wagon, the tenement that has no ice. It does not name the wet nurse who has been priced out. Holt’s catechism is written for mothers and children’s nurses — a household that can read English and buy milk sugar. The foundling register from the previous essay does not disappear. It just stops being the frontispiece.

The word *formula* in this period is still close to its laboratory sense. Later American English will use it for a branded can. Do not read 1894 with a 2026 supermarket in your head. The can is coming. The arithmetic is the dress rehearsal.

## Who was left out

Breastfeeding does not vanish. Pierre Budin’s Paris consultations — the next essay — will treat the scale and the breast as the default and sterilized cow’s milk as the fallback. American percentage teaching often reads as if the breast were a preface. Rural households that never saw a milk laboratory kept doing what they had done. Black and immigrant mothers met a profession that was busy professionalizing whiteness and city practice. The archive is loud about Longwood Avenue. It is quieter about everyone who never walked it.

Jacobi’s scorn matters here. A German-trained New York pediatrician telling Boston that the arithmetic is a performance is not a modern “breast is best” campaign. It is one physician refusing another physician’s claim to the meal.

## Residue

By the 1930s the Hunnewell cows are gone. Pasteurization and simpler dilutions make the percentage card look like a museum label. The hunger does not go. Every later “scientifically designed” infant food, every hospital feeding order that treats the parent as a transcription error, every argument about whether a feeding therapist may touch volume — they inherit this century’s belief that the meal is too important to leave to the household.

A modern clinician who writes a number on a page and calls it feeding has not escaped 1897. She has only changed which number. The catechism is useful. It is also late literature. The household was already feeding. The laboratory taught a profession how to sound like it had invented the work.
