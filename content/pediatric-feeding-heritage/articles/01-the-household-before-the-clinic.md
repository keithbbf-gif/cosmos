This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

The oldest feeding specialist in most houses was not a specialist. She was a woman with milk, or a woman with a spoon, or a grandmother who had already buried a child and did not want to do it again. Physicians wrote when they were paid to write. The meal happened anyway.

This essay is the household as a historical object. It is not a lesson in how to feed a baby.

## Milk that was a job

Wet nursing is older than pediatrics. Contracts survive from Italian cities, French foundling hospitals, and English parishes: a woman takes a child, a household pays, a parish officer checks whether the child is still alive at the quarter. The language is labor language. Duration, price, whether the nurse may take a second infant, what happens if her own child dies. The modern clinic will later call the same transaction “breastfeeding support” or “donor milk” and put it behind a refrigerator door. The older version was a person who lived in, or a person who took the infant away.

Foundling hospitals made the transaction visible. Paris, London, Florence — the wheel in the wall, the register, the wet-nurse countryside. Mortality was not a secret. Administrators counted. The count is why later reformers could claim a science of infant feeding: the household had already produced a pile of deaths large enough to graph.

Who is missing from the contract is the infant’s taste. The nurse’s own child is sometimes named, sometimes not. Enslaved women in the Americas were conscripted into the same labor without a parish fee. A history of feeding that starts in a Boston milk laboratory has already skipped the people who did the work.

## Spoons, pap, and the first solids

Before “first foods” became a chart, households mashed what they had. Pap — bread and liquid — shows up in English and colonial American notes as the thing you give when milk is short or when a grandmother believes the child is ready. Fermented grains, broth, pre-chewed meat: the archaeological and folkloric record is wider than the later American rice-cereal aisle. The clinic will spend the twentieth century arguing about when a spoon should appear. The household had been arguing in other words for a long time.

Medical writers noticed when they could moralize. A wet nurse who drinks. A mother who weans too early to return to work. A servant who leaves the pap too thick. The advice is class advice wearing a white coat. It is not a physiology paper.

## What the household already knew

Infants pause. Infants refuse. Infants sleep through a feeding and then make up the volume at the next one, or they do not, and someone stays up. Those observations do not require a credential. They require a night. Later NICU literature will rediscover “cue” as if the grandmother had been waiting for a journal.

The household also knew failure. A child who could not suck, a child who vomited everything, a child whose chest rattled after a swallow — these were not “pediatric dysphagia” until a profession needed a billing word. They were reasons a priest was called, or a physician if one could be afforded, or a neighbor who had seen it before. The later feeding team inherits that night without inheriting the neighbor.

## Three rooms that are not clinics

A wet-nurse cottage is not a NICU. A foundling register is not an IFSP. A kitchen table with pap is not a videofluoroscopy suite. What they share is a public penalty. A child who does not thrive is heard as a woman’s failure before it is heard as an airway. The later American academy will spend decades arguing whether the trouble lives in the swallow, the gut, the sensory system, or the parent’s anxiety. The older world already had an answer: it lives in the household, and the household should try harder.

This pack will not romanticize that answer. Households killed children with dirty milk and with kindness. They also kept children alive while medicine was still arguing about cream percentages. Both facts sit on the table.

A modern first visit that begins by asking who already feeds this child, and what that person has already tried, is closer to the better old rooms than to a laboratory that treats the parent as a contamination source. The household is useful. It is also the place the clinic keeps forgetting it did not invent.
