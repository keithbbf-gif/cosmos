**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

A person can download the APA ethics text from apa.org on a Tuesday afternoon and finish it before dinner. That download is not a trial. No one has filed. No one has answered. No one has sat in a room with a record. The PDF is a public instrument. The case, if there ever is a case, is a private machinery that this folder will not pretend to run.

The distinction sounds obvious until a headline erases it. "Psychologist violated the ethics code" can mean an association found a member out of bounds, or a board touched a license, or a newspaper decided the code was a useful measuring stick. Those are three different sentences. Only the first is the association talking about its own book.

## Three rooms, three kinds of paper

**The association** owns a membership. It can admonish, require education, suspend, or expel — if its bylaws say so and if the person is still a member. People practice without belonging. A code that only reaches members does not reach them. APA, ACA, and NASW have all spent decades living with that gap. It is one reason they lobby legislatures to put pieces of their language into statute or into board rule.

**The licensing board** owns a state permission. An LPC in Arkansas is not an LPC in the same sense as an LPC in Virginia; the letters look alike and the statutes do not. Some boards adopt an association code by reference. Some write their own. Some cite "generally accepted professional standards" and leave the fight for the hearing. A board action can end a livelihood. An association letter, by itself, cannot.

**The court** owns a docket. Malpractice, duty to protect, privilege, custody evaluations, billing fraud: these are legal objects. A lawyer may cite a code in a deposition. A judge may or may not care. *Jaffee v. Redmond* (1996) is a Supreme Court opinion about psychotherapist-patient privilege in federal courts. It is not an ACA standard. *Tarasoff* (1976) is California. It is not a national ethics clause. Draft 36 will keep those cases in their courthouses.

## Why associations publish the book anyway

Because a secret code cannot teach, and a craft that cannot teach will not stay a craft. Hobbs said as much in 1948: the point of gathering incidents was to change psychologists' behavior, not to decorate a letterhead. NASW's 1979 revision, in the historical accounts, added enforcement language because members had complained that the 1960 page was too vague to use when someone actually misbehaved. ACA's public archive exists so that a counselor can see what the house said in 1988 and what it said in 2014.

Publication is also a political act. Insurers, hospital credentialers, and university programs want a text they can point to. "We have a code" is a sentence that opens doors. The cost of the sentence is that outsiders will read the text as if it were a statute. It is not.

## What you cannot see from the PDF

You cannot see the complaint form. You cannot see the investigatory file. You cannot see whether the member hired a lawyer. You cannot see whether the association settled for a quiet educational letter. Those absences are not a conspiracy. They are how membership bodies try to hold two duties at once: protect the public name of the craft, and not try members in the newspaper.

This folder will not reconstruct a file it has not been shown. It will not name a living clinician as an example. It will not walk a reader through "how to file." The existence of ethics offices is a historical fact. Their phone trees are not our content.

## Effective dates, again, because this is where they bite

APA's prefatory language says complaints are adjudicated under the code in force when the conduct occurred. That sentence is how a 2002 standard stays alive for a 2004 session even after a 2010 amendment. It is also how a historian has to write. "The code says" is incomplete. "The 2002 code, as of a June 2004 session, said" is closer. The closer sentence is uglier. Good.

NASW's 1979 text, by the association's own historical notes, took effect 1 July 1980. A social worker's 1979 conduct is not a 1980 case. The calendar is the ethics.

## What a small-city clinic inherits from this distinction

A speech-language pathologist in Southeast Arkansas has a license, a certifying body (ASHA, if they hold the CCC), and possibly an employer policy. A counselor down the street has a different board. A social worker in the same building has a third. They may share a fax machine and a coffee pot. They do not share a trial.

WOW Therapies, on its live homepage, is not staffing an ethics court. These essays exist so that a reader who hears "she broke the code" can ask: whose code, which year, which room. That question is not a defense. It is literacy.

If you came here because you are angry at a clinician, this is the wrong door. Anger is allowed. A history pack will not process it into a filing. If you came here because you want to know how a craft learned to write rules for itself, stay. The next essay will tell you why this lane is not the Freud-to-Herman calendar, and why that refusal is a kindness.
