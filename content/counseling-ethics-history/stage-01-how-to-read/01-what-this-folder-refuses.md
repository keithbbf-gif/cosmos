I am going to waste the first page on what we will not do. If that sounds like a lawyer got to the manuscript first, good. The words *ethics code* have spent a generation sliding off association letterhead and onto continuing-education slides, and the slide is almost always greased with a case that sounds like yours.

This folder is a stack of historical essays for WOW Therapies. It does not diagnose, treat, or prevent any condition. It is not medical advice. It is not legal advice. It is not a silent conversion of a Southeast Arkansas therapy site — occupational, physical, and speech on the live homepage — into a mental-health menu or an ethics hotline.

That is not a humility ritual. It is the only way to talk about these documents without lying in a soft voice.

Here is the usual lie, in its polite form: a writer tells you that Hippocrates, a 1953 psychology book, a 1961 guidance pamphlet, and a 1960 social-work page were all, without knowing it, saying the same thing. Then the writer names a modern distress, or a modern quarrel with a clinician, and lets a numbered standard sit next to the trouble until your eye does the rest. No one typed the forbidden verb. The sentence still did the work.

I refuse that sentence, including the version that swaps "treats" for "can help you decide" and hopes a history page will hold.

What I will do instead is slower, and less flattering to anyone who wants a hero code.

I will keep **APA, ACA, and NASW** in their own buildings. The American Psychological Association published *Ethical Standards of Psychologists* in 1953 after a 1952 adoption. The American Personnel and Guidance Association — later the American Counseling Association — printed a five-page *Ethical Standards* in 1961. The National Association of Social Workers' Delegate Assembly approved a one-page code on 13 October 1960. Those are three professions, three membership machines, three revision calendars. They borrowed from one another. They are not a single "counseling ethics" tablet.

I will keep **a code** as a dated object. A 1992 APA text that first split aspirational principles from enforceable standards is not the 2002 text Celia Fisher's task force sent to the Council of Representatives. A 2005 ACA sentence about "clear and imminent danger" is not the 2014 sentence about "serious and foreseeable harm." A 1996 NASW list of six values is not a 1960 first-person proclamation. Year first. Parable later, if at all.

I will keep **the three rooms that punish** separate. An association can expel a member. A state board can touch a license. A court can enter a judgment. A blog that collapses those rooms into "you could get in trouble" is already doing a risk-manager's job, and badly.

I will keep **copyright** visible. The living codes are public *to read* on the associations' sites. They are not public *to reprint*. This folder summarizes arguments and names sections. It does not host a PDF that would save you a click and steal a publisher's page.

I will keep **uncertainty** visible. Secondary literature does not agree on how many APA revisions to count, or whether the 2018 Ethics Code Task Force has finished a replacement. As of 14 September 2026 I have not seen a adopted successor to the 2002 code as amended in 2010 and 2016. ACA announced a Fall 2026 final text while a draft sat in committee. If I flatten that into "the new code says," I am already doing a webinar's job.

I will not invent a notebook I have not held. I have not sat in Hobbs's committee reading the thousand-odd critical incidents. I have not graded the Hoffman binders. When I mention a town hall or a Delegate Assembly, I am describing a public process as the associations and the newspapers told it.

I will not give you a script. The minute a history essay hands over a decision tree, a "what would you do" card, or a complaint checklist, it has changed jobs. Form matters — membership trial, licensing hearing, civil suit — and I will talk about form as history. I will not talk about form as a recipe for your Tuesday.

I will not do the "balanced" trick where the last paragraph says "see a professional" after the first twelve paragraphs have already sold a method, a lawsuit strategy, or a moral panic. If a draft cannot live without a symptom payoff or a "protect yourself" payoff, the draft should die.

Why bother, then?

Because the documents are real, the votes are real, and the borrowed word *ethics* is real, and they are not the same object. A person who wants to understand why a speech clinic in Southeast Arkansas and a psychology department in Washington now share a vocabulary of confidentiality and competence is entitled to a history that does not recruit their chart. A person who loves association history is entitled to hear a 1953 book as a 1953 book, not as a hack. A person who is tired of both wellness copy and sneering debunking is entitled to a third tone: specific, a little dry, unwilling to convert a committee report into a press release.

There is a fourth reader I am writing against: the one who thinks a claims fence is cowardice. I do not think it is cowardice to refuse a sentence that would be reckless on a service page and still tempting in an essay. Essays have more room than service pages. That is exactly why they leak.

If you want a clinician, see a clinician who knows you. If you want a named ethics consultation, that belongs with a supervisor, an association ethics office, or an attorney — and WOW Therapies does not, by hosting this history, claim to be that office. If you want the story, stay here. The story is long. It has a 1948 article by Nicholas Hobbs, a five-page guidance pamphlet, a one-page social-work oath, a California duty-to-protect opinion, a 2015 law-firm report, and a 2021 sentence about self-care that surprised people who thought codes only talked about clients.

None of that is a treatment plan. I am repeating myself on purpose. Repetition is how a fence stays up when the next sentence wants a worksheet.

## Claims box

This essay is educational history for a general reader. It is not medical advice, not legal advice, not a diagnosis, and not a treatment plan. It does not teach a session, an ethics-complaint procedure, or a decision worksheet. Reading it is not therapy, not counseling, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician or for counsel from a qualified attorney. WOW Therapies does not claim, by publishing this history, to adjudicate ethics complaints or to replace APA, ACA, NASW, or a state board.

If you are in danger, call local emergency services.
