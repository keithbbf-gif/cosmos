**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In American English a person can say "I saw a therapist" and mean a psychiatrist, a psychologist, a licensed counselor, a clinical social worker, a marriage-and-family therapist, or — on a site like this one — a speech-language pathologist who is doing something that also gets called therapy. The waiting-room word is a convenience. The guilds are not.

APA, ACA, and NASW each printed a public ethics code because each guild decided it had a membership to teach and a membership to punish. The dates do not line up, and that is the point. Psychology's first book-length code is 1953. Social work's first NASW page is 1960. Counseling's first APGA pamphlet is 1961. Three neighboring crafts, three postwar professionalization waves, three printers.

## Psychology's room

The American Psychological Association dates to 1892. For a long time it was a learned society more than a licensing machine. World War II changed the employment graph: more psychologists in hospitals, industry, the military, and clinics. A 1938 committee began handling complaints informally. A 1947 Committee on Ethical Standards, first chaired by Edward C. Tolman, was asked to write something members could actually hold. Nicholas Hobbs's 1948 *American Psychologist* article explained why they would not do it from an armchair.

The resulting 1953 *Ethical Standards of Psychologists* is a psychology document. It talks to scientists, teachers, and practitioners in one breath because APA still wanted to be all three. Later revisions would keep that stretch. A counseling code does not have to police a rat lab. A social-work code does not have to police a psychometric shop. APA's code does, or tries to.

## Counseling's room

Counseling did not begin as the treatment of madness. It began as vocational guidance, school guidance, and college personnel work. Four associations merged in 1952 as the American Personnel and Guidance Association. They printed *Ethical Standards* in 1961 — five pages, already talking about client welfare, competence, and a duty to keep learning. The association renamed itself the American Association for Counseling and Development in 1983 and the American Counseling Association on 1 July 1992. The code kept being rewritten as the job slid toward clinical mental-health work, insurance panels, and, later, screens.

ACA's later texts are often more particular than APA's about the counseling relationship: how long after termination a sexual or romantic involvement stays forbidden (five years in the 2014 ACA text, as publicly discussed; two in the APA text, as publicly discussed), how values conflicts are supposed to be handled, what a social-media wall is for. Commentators have made a small industry of those contrasts. The contrast is real enough to name. It is not a scoreboard.

Virginia's 1976 counselor-licensure statute and CACREP's 1981 birth belong to the profession essay in the sister pack. They belong here only as a reminder: a code without a license is a club rule. A license without a code is a state permission with an empty bookshelf. American counseling spent the late twentieth century trying to own both.

## Social work's room

NASW was assembled in 1955 from older social-work organizations. The Delegate Assembly approved a first-person, one-page code on 13 October 1960. The voice is "I" — I respect privacy, I put professional duty ahead of personal interest — which is a different literary choice from APA's later third-person standards. 1967 added a nondiscrimination line. 1979 built six sections and an enforcement preamble. 1996 wrote a mission and six core values that students still recite: service, social justice, dignity and worth of the person, importance of human relationships, integrity, competence.

Social work's code has always been more willing to name poverty, oppression, and the employing agency as ethical objects. That is not a personality. It is a craft that began in charity organization societies and public bureaus, not in a university clinic's fee-for-hour. When NASW spends 2017 on technology and 2021 on self-care and cultural competence, it is revising from that spine.

## Why the waiting room blurs them

Markets like blur. So do intake forms. So do journalists. A family in Pine Bluff or Dumas meets a person, not an org chart. The person may be an LCSW on a community-mental-health badge, an LPC in a group practice, a psychologist who still says "testing" first, or — if they walked into WOW Therapies — a speech-language pathologist whose ethics home is ASHA, not these three.

ASHA's code is a neighbor. It is not this pack. The sister SLPWOW lane owns that heritage. The live WOW Therapies homepage, as last fetched, is speech, with occupational and physical named in the title bar. These essays do not pretend the clinic has quietly become a counseling shop. They exist because the *word* therapy is shared, and because a curious reader deserves to know that "the ethics code" is not one object.

## What a historian does with the blur

Keeps the letterheads. Dates the mergers. Refuses the sentence that begins "all helping professionals believe." They do not. They wrote different pages, under different lawsuits, for different employers.

A comparative essay later in this pack will talk about confidentiality, multiple relationships, competence, consent, screens, and conversion practices as **arguments that crossed the letterheads**. That is allowed. Annexing the three codes into one "counseling ethics" brand is not.

If you are a student, your program assigned you one of these books and pretended the others were electives. If you are a client, no one assigned you anything. This folder is for the second reader first. The first reader is welcome if they can stand not being flattered.

Three professions. Three codes. One waiting-room noun. History's job is to un-blur the noun without sneering at any of the crafts.
