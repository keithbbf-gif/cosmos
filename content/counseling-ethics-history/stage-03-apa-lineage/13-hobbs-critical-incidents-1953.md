Nicholas Hobbs asked APA members to describe a situation they knew firsthand in which a psychologist made a decision with ethical implications, and to name the issue. More than a thousand such incidents came back. A committee read them for themes: patients, students, research participants, colleagues, the public. Drafts went to the membership. The association adopted a text in 1952 and published *Ethical Standards of Psychologists* in 1953.

That is the origin story APA still tells when it wants to sound unlike a trade group that copied the AMA overnight. The story is mostly true. It is also a method story, which is why this essay is longer on process than on quotations. We will not reprint the 1953 book.

## What "critical incident" meant in 1948

John C. Flanagan would publish "The Critical Incident Technique" in *Psychological Bulletin* in 1954. The ethics committee was already using the idea: do not start from a philosopher's list; start from a worker's memory of a hard day. Hobbs called the rejected alternative the armchair. He wanted a code "truly indigenous to psychology, a code that could be lived."

Indigenous is a charged word now. In Hobbs's mouth it meant: grown from our own cases, not borrowed from medicine's oath or law's canons. The borrowing still happened — everyone in 1948 could hear a medical silence rule — but the *claim* of the method was empirical.

Whether a thousand anecdotes are a sample or a pile is a fair later question. The committee did not pretend to have a random national draw. It pretended to have listened. Listening is a political act inside a membership body. It is not a survey of patients. The 1953 book is a psychologists' book about psychologists.

## What the incidents were about

Secondary accounts, including Celia Fisher's later textbook chapters, say the pile included McCarthy-era fights over academic freedom and industrial psychologists asked to design tests that would keep a workplace racially segregated. I am reporting those examples as circulating in the secondary literature. I have not read the raw incident cards.

If the examples are right, the first APA code was never a polite clinic manual. It was a postwar document. Loyalty oaths and Jim Crow were in the room. A later reader who treats 1953 as an age of innocence has not read the method's own publicity.

The themes Hobbs's committee pulled out — public responsibility, competence, confidentiality, research, teaching — are the ancestors of later numbered standards. Ancestry is not identity. The 1953 prose is longer, more hortatory, and, by the decade's end, too bulky for members who wanted something they could carry.

## Book-length, then a problem

People who have held the 1953 object describe it as book-length, sometimes as on the order of 170 pages. `[VERIFY]` a library copy before a live page number. What is not in dispute: by 1959 APA was already shortening. A method that honors incidents produces more sentences than a membership can memorize. Every later revision is, among other things, an editing argument.

ACA's 1961 five pages and NASW's 1960 one page look thin beside 1953. They were different literary bets: a short public oath versus a long empirical catalog. Both bets have descendants. APA's descendants got shorter, then grew again in 1992 and 2002 as enforceable standards multiplied. Length is a historical variable, not a virtue.

## Hobbs after the book

Nicholas Hobbs went on to other public work in American psychology and education; this pack will not become a biography. The 1948 article is the object we need. It is short, named, and checkable. A student can read it without reading the 1953 book. A website can point to it without pasting the 1953 book.

## What 1953 did not settle

It did not settle the scientist–practitioner split. It did not settle licensing; that is state work. It did not settle whether a code is a conscience. It did not keep later psychologists out of national-security interrogations. Origin stories that treat 1953 as a completed ethics are advertisements.

It did settle this: APA would revise. The 1959 trial adoption already assumed the document would move. "Living document" is a later slogan for a 1950s editing habit.

For WOW Therapies, 1953 is the first thick American *helping-craft* ethics book in this lane's spine. Counseling and social work would print shorter first pages. They would thicken later. The thickening is Stage 4 and Stage 5. The method — ask the members what went wrong — is the thing to carry forward when a 2026 ACA task force says it read comments. Comments are incidents in a new coat.

I have not sat with the 1951 draft segments in *American Psychologist*. A later editor with JSTOR can thicken the paragraph that names those installments. Until then, the published 1948 manifesto and the 1953 title are enough to keep the story honest.
