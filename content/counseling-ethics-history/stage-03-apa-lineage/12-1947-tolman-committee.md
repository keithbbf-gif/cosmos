**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Edward C. Tolman chaired the American Psychological Association's first Committee on Ethical Standards for Psychologists in 1947. He was not an obvious ethics celebrity. He was a University of California psychologist whose rats had opinions about mazes, and whose name still sits on an undergraduate lecture about purposive behavior. APA put him at the head of the table anyway.

The choice is the history. If the first formal ethics committee had been staffed only by the new clinicians pouring out of wartime hospitals, the association might have split along the old wound: scientists versus practitioners. A code that began as a practice pamphlet would have been easier to ignore in the lab. A code that began with Tolman in the chair announced, before a sentence was adopted, that ethics was a problem of *psychology*, not a problem of the applied wing.

## What 1947 was asking

The informal 1938 committee had already learned that an unwritten code does not scale. Postwar APA was reorganizing, taking in applied divisions, and watching psychologists take jobs that touched hiring, schooling, and patients. Newspapers could find those jobs. Legislatures could find them. A society that wanted to remain self-governing needed a text.

Hobbs, writing the next year, said the Tolman committee did not want "a document with an imposing title." They wanted something that would change how psychologists behaved. That sentence is ambitious. It is also a researcher's sentence. Change of behavior is an empirical claim. The method that followed — critical incidents — is how a 1940s psychologist tries to make an empirical claim about morals.

## Armchair versus incident

Hobbs named the rejected method: gather the mature, the wise, the well-read; study other professions' codes; draft a dignified page; mail it to the membership for a yes. Hundreds of American associations had done it that way. APA, he said, would not.

Whether that contrast is entirely fair to the armchair, I will leave to historians of professionalization. What matters for this lane is that APA *told on itself* in print. The 1948 article is a public methodological manifesto. Later task forces — Fisher's in the years before 2002, the 2018 ECTF — still genuflect toward member comment and incident. They are genuflecting toward 1947–48.

## Tolman as a political fact

Tolman had already been a public man. His University of California years included a loyalty-oath fight that later readers, including this one, should not reduce to a sticker. `[CITE NEEDED]` before a live sentence that over-specifies that episode. The usable fact is simpler: the chair was a scientist with a spine in university politics, not a clinician writing a shop manual.

That does not make the 1953 code "scientific ethics" in a flattering sense. The incidents members sent included McCarthy-era academic freedom *and* industrial testing for segregation. Science's house was already political. The committee's job was to notice.

## What the 1947 committee did not finish

It did not publish the book. Publication is 1953, after a second committee chaired by Hobbs gathered the incidents, after drafts went out in 1951, after the association adopted a text in 1952. Anniversary speeches that say "Tolman wrote the code" are false in the way anniversary speeches are often false. He opened the shop. Other people ran the shop.

He also did not write a counseling code. Guidance workers were still a few years from APGA and a decade from five pages. Social workers were still eight years from NASW. 1947 is a psychology date.

## Why a speech clinic in Arkansas should care

Because every later "we surveyed the membership" ritual in ACA and NASW is a cousin of this shop, and because every later fight about whether researchers and clinicians can share a book begins here. WOW Therapies will not staff a 1947 committee. It can tell a reader that the first American psychology code was born in a scientific society's fear of becoming a guild it could not explain.

The next essay is the method itself: more than a thousand incidents, Flanagan in the background, a 1953 book that members would soon call too long. Tolman hands Hobbs the problem. Hobbs hands the membership a questionnaire.
