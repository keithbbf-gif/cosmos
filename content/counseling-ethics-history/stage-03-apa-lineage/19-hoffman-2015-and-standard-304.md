David H. Hoffman and colleagues at Sidley Austin delivered an independent review to a special committee of the APA Board of Directors on 2 July 2015. The *New York Times* had the report, in leaked form, on 14 July. A revised text with an errata sheet followed on 4 September. Those are documentary dates. They are not a verdict this website is competent to retry.

The review had been commissioned in November 2014 to test allegations that APA's 2002 and 2005 ethics-policy decisions were shaped by improper coordination with government agencies that wanted permissive rules for psychologists near interrogations. Hoffman's principal findings, as the executive summary states them, focused on the 2005 PENS process and on close relationships between some APA officials — the Ethics Director among them — and Department of Defense actors. On the 2002 code language itself, the report said the important 1.02 changes were pre-9/11 and not designed as interrogation policy, while also saying a Nuremberg-problem warning was not followed far enough.

I am paraphrasing a public executive summary. I am not pasting the 500-plus-page report. I am not adopting every sentence as gospel. Named psychologists and military-psychology groups later disputed parts of the account. A later editor who wants a "Hoffman was right / Hoffman was wrong" essay should write it somewhere else, with the binders. This essay is about what the association *did with* a public report.

## 7 August 2015

APA's Council of Representatives voted 157–1, as publicly reported, to prohibit psychologist participation in national-security interrogations. Policy is not yet a code standard. Policy is a Council sentence. The ethics book still needed a suture if the association wanted the sentence to be enforceable in the membership sense.

That distinction is the same 1992 lesson in a harsher year. Principles and policies inspire. Standards bind — if you write them.

## Standard 3.04, adopted 3 August 2016, effective 1 January 2017

APA called for comments on proposed language in early 2016. Council adopted a revision of Ethical Standard 3.04 — Avoiding Harm — on 3 August 2016. It took effect 1 January 2017. *American Psychologist* printed the revision at 71:900.

The amendment, in public descriptions, put an explicit prohibition on participation in torture and in cruel, inhuman, or degrading treatment into the enforceable book, aligning the code with the August 2015 policy. I will not quote the standard. The bibliographic facts are the history: a 2002 house, a 2010 suture on 1.02/1.03, a 2016 suture on 3.04.

People who say "the 2017 APA Ethics Code" are naming the morning the last suture turned on. The spine is still 2002.

## What a law-firm report is

It is not a peer-reviewed history. It is not a criminal verdict. It is a hired investigation with a client (the Board's special committee), a method (interviews, documents), and a set of conclusions that then become political objects. Treating Hoffman as scripture is a category error. Treating Hoffman as a rumor is also a category error. It is a dated public document that moved a membership body.

Supplemental review work in 2016 — APA re-engaging Hoffman on DoD-policy questions — is part of the documentary trail. I will not narrate it as a sequel novel.

## Living people

Hoffman is a living attorney. Psychologists named in the report are not public property. This essay uses the report's public existence and the association's public votes. No health, no family, no unsourced motive.

## Why this belongs beside a speech clinic

Because "APA ethics" in the American ear now includes this decade, whether a continuing-education slide admits it or not. A curious reader who meets only the five principles has met a poster. A curious reader who meets PENS, Hoffman, and 3.04 has met a craft that had to write "avoiding harm" again after harm had become a national argument.
