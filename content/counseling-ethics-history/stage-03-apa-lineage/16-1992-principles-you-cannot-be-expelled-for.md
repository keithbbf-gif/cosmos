**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 1992 the American Psychological Association published *Ethical Principles of Psychologists and Code of Conduct* in *American Psychologist* 47:1597–1611. The double title is the news. For the first time the association's public ethics book officially contained two kinds of sentences: General Principles that aspire, and Ethical Standards that bind.

A student who highlights "Beneficence" as if it were a chargeable count has not read the 1992 preface. A workshop that treats a Principle as a checkbox has flattened a compromise. The compromise is the history.

## Why split the page

Members had spent decades living inside texts that mixed sermon and rule. Sermons are hard to adjudicate. Rules are hard to inspire. The 1992 architects — I am not naming a single hero chair from memory; `[CITE NEEDED]` for the revision-committee roster — tried to give the association both a ceiling and a floor.

The ceiling: principles a psychologist should aim at, in language closer to a moral vocabulary (competence, integrity, responsibility, respect, welfare, social responsibility, in the 1992 list as later textbooks recount it). The floor: numbered standards about records, fees, sex, assessment, research, teaching.

NASW would make a cousin move in 1996: values and principles up top, a long body of standards below. ACA's lettered sections stay closer to house rules, with a preamble doing some of the aspirational work. 1992 is psychology's cleanest public admission that not every ethical sentence is a charging document.

## What the split does to a complaint

It tells an ethics committee what it is allowed to pretend to prove. "You failed to be a beneficent person" is a Sunday sentence. "You did this named thing that Standard X forbids" is a file. The split protects members from vague piety as a charge. It also protects the association from having to define piety.

Critics later said the split let people hide: keep the principles pretty, keep the standards survivable. Supporters said the split kept the book from becoming either a poster or a penal code. Both criticisms are historical objects. This folder will not pick a winner.

## 1992 as a 1990s document

Managed care was already in the waiting room. HMOs, session limits, and utilization review would be named more loudly in the 2002 revision notes. 1992 sits at the start of that squeeze. It also sits after *Tarasoff*, after the first wave of duty-to-protect statutes, after a generation of licensing. The enforceable standards thickened because the jobs had thickened.

The 1992 sexual-and-romantic-relationship rules, as later comparison charts have it, included a two-year post-termination floor that ACA would later outrun with five. Draft 37 will keep the year-counts in one place. Here the point is architectural: those sentences live in the *standards* half. They are not "principles." They are rules a membership body can try.

## What 2002 kept and what it renamed

The 2002 code kept the split and rewrote the principle list into the five names a current student can recite: Beneficence and Nonmaleficence; Fidelity and Responsibility; Integrity; Justice; Respect for People's Rights and Dignity. It also rebuilt the standards into ten sections. 1992 is the architecture. 2002 is the renovation.

A writer who says "the APA principles" without a year is already mushing 1992 and 2002. This pack will keep the years.

## For a reader who likes inspirational posters

The 1992 split is a warning label on the poster. You may hang a principle in a break room. You may not pretend the poster is the enforcement manual. WOW Therapies, if it ever hung such a poster, would be hanging a neighbor's ceiling, not a speech-license floor. ASHA is the speech floor. The neighbor's ceiling is still worth reading as history.
