Celia B. Fisher chaired the Ethics Code Task Force that spent the late 1990s and early 2000s rewriting APA's book. The Council of Representatives adopted the result on 21 August 2002. The new text governed new complaints beginning 1 June 2003. *American Psychologist* printed it at 57:1060–1073.

Those three dates should be on any slide that says "the current APA code." As of 14 September 2026, the living public text is still this renovation, plus a 2010 suture and a 2016 suture. People who say "the 2017 code" as if Fisher had been replaced are naming an amendment year, not a new book.

## What the task force said it was answering

Fisher's later process notes, as they circulate in *Decoding the Ethics Code* and in Sage chapters, list a 1990s weather: managed care and HMOs; internet-mediated research and practice; culturally and linguistically diverse clients; psychologists in court; a shift from paternalism toward patient access to records; and the same old need for a book members would actually use. I am summarizing a secondary list, not reprinting her table.

The method was self-consciously 1953-shaped. Member surveys. Critical-incident thinking. Observers from divisions. Open forums at conventions. Calls for comment. The 1948 manifesto still had a job. Whether the 2002 result is "empirical" in Hobbs's sense is a later argument. The *claim* of lineage is a 2002 fact.

## Five principles, ten sections

The 2002 General Principles are the five names that now live on posters: Beneficence and Nonmaleficence; Fidelity and Responsibility; Integrity; Justice; Respect for People's Rights and Dignity. They remain aspirational. The Ethical Standards run in ten sections, from resolving ethical issues through research and publication, with therapy, assessment, privacy, and competence among the rooms in between.

I will not outline the ten sections as a study guide. A study guide is a how-to. A history sentence is: the 2002 book is a practice-and-science handbook with more enforceable doors than 1981, built on the 1992 split.

## Standard 1.02 as a time bomb that was also a workaday sentence

The 2002 language on conflicts between ethics and law — later nicknamed, by critics, a "Nuremberg defense" because it allowed a psychologist, after certain steps, to follow law — was, in Hoffman's 2015 account, drafted in a pre-9/11 revision process and not designed as interrogation policy. Hoffman also said the nickname-problem was raised and not adequately chased.

Both things can be true. A workaday sentence about subpoenas can become a national-security sentence when the jobs change. Draft 18 is the jobs changing. This draft keeps 2002 as a managed-care-and-internet renovation that happened to carry a law-conflict clause into a worse decade.

## Effective June 1, 2003

Why the lag? Associations need to print, to train, to warn members, to tell boards. I have not seen a memo that says "we picked June so summer internships would…" and I will not invent one. The lag is the lesson from Draft 02: adoption is not effectiveness. Complaints about pre-June-2003 conduct stayed under the old book.
