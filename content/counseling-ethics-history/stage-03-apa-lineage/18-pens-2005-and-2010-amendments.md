On 26 June 2005 the APA Presidential Task Force on Psychological Ethics and National Security — PENS, in the acronym that would not stay quiet — finished a report with twelve ethical guidelines about psychologists and national-security work. The Board of Directors adopted the report as association policy on an emergency calendar measured in days, not in the multi-year comment cycle Celia Fisher had run.

Speed is the first historical fact. The second is that a large part of the membership did not treat the speed as efficiency.

## What PENS was as a document

PENS was not a revision of the 2002 code. It was a policy report about a job some psychologists were already doing after 11 September 2001: consultation to interrogations and related national-security work. Supporters said the report set limits and kept psychologists in rooms where they could restrain harm. Critics said it blessed presence in rooms that should not have had a psychologist's name on the badge.

This folder will not retry the interrogations. It will not teach a "should a psychologist…" lesson. It will say: an ethics *policy* was written beside a code, adopted fast, and then spent a decade as a wound.

## 1.02 and 1.03 become famous

The 2002 standards on conflicts with law (1.02) and conflicts with organizational demands (1.03) were workaday sentences about subpoenas and employers. In the mid-2000s they were reread as sentences about orders. Critics called the "follow the law if you cannot resolve the conflict" clause a Nuremberg defense. The nickname is harsh. The nickname is also, by then, part of the public record.

APA's Council, in the years after PENS, directed a change meant to block that reading. The 2010 amendments — printed in *American Psychologist* 65:493, effective 1 June 2010 — altered the Introduction and Standards 1.02 and 1.03. In the versions I have seen described, the amendment removed the leftover permission to follow law once a conflict was called unresolvable, and added language that those standards may not be used to justify violating human rights.

I am paraphrasing. I am not pasting the 2010 text. The association's own pages and the 2010 *American Psychologist* note are the checkable objects.

## What 2010 did not do

It did not rewrite the rest of the 2002 book. It did not end the argument. It did not prevent the 2014 Board from hiring Sidley Austin. It did not, by itself, write a torture prohibition into Standard 3.04. That sentence is 2016.

People who say "APA fixed it in 2010" are telling a victory lap the next five years refused to let stand. People who say "2010 changed nothing" are ignoring a real textual suture. History can hold a suture without calling it a cure.

## Member revolt as a historical engine

Petitions, convention floor fights, the resignation-or-refusal politics of a scientific society — I will not catalogue them as a thriller. The usable fact is that PENS produced an internal opposition with names, letters, and, eventually, enough weather to force an independent review. Codes do not only change because task forces plan a cycle. They change because members make the cycle impossible to skip.

ACA and NASW have their own member-weather stories (conversion-practice statements; taxonomies of technology). PENS is psychology's loudest twenty-first-century version.

## Living people, public documents

Psychologists named in later reports are living or recently living. This essay uses roles and public documents. It does not invent hallway conversations. It does not diagnose anyone's motives beyond what a public report already claimed — and Draft 19 will treat even those claims as claims.

## For a small-city reader

A speech clinic in Southeast Arkansas did not sit on PENS. The reason the episode belongs on an educational site is narrower: "the ethics code" in American psychology is not a serene pamphlet. It is a text that has been to a war-on-terror argument and come back amended. Readers who only meet APA as a poster of five principles deserve the suture.

The next essay is the review the suture did not prevent: Hoffman, 2 July 2015; a Council vote on 7 August 2015; Standard 3.04 amended in 2016, effective 1 January 2017.
