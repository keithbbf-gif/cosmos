**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 1959 the American Psychological Association published a shortened *Ethical Standards of Psychologists* in *American Psychologist* and adopted it on a trial basis. The 1953 book had been a triumph of incident-collecting. It had also been a brick. Members wanted sentences they could find.

The next twenty years are not a single moral awakening. They are an editing cycle. 1963, 1968, a March 1977 *APA Monitor* printing, a 1979 pamphlet: same family title, different trim. A historian who skips from 1953 to 1992 misses the way a profession learns to live with a text — by cutting it.

## Trial adoption as a tell

1959's "trial" language, in the circulating accounts, assumed future revision. The committee that hoped the code would move with conditions of practice was not being modest. It was describing a product cycle. Counseling would later say "every 7–10 years" as if that were a law of nature. Psychology had already been doing a version of it.

Trial adoption is also a legal-adjacent fact for later complaint calendars. If a text is on trial, which sentences bind? I have not reconstructed 1959 enforcement practice. `[CITE NEEDED]`. The usable public fact is the word *trial* itself: APA did not treat the first short code as Sinai.

## Where you can still check the printings

The 1959, 1963, and 1968 texts sit in *American Psychologist* at pages later bibliographies still cite: 14:279–282; 18:56–60; 23:357–361. The 1977 text appeared in the *Monitor*. The 1979 text went back between covers. A student with a library can do this work without this website pasting the standards.

What I will not do is pretend I have a line-by-line diff in my pocket. Secondary literature talks about condensation, rearrangement, and the long presence of a "Moral and Legal Standards" type principle before 1981 gave conflicts with law a sharper sentence. Draft 15 takes that sharper sentence. This draft takes the *habit* of republication.

## The 1960s and 1970s as weather, not as a rewrite of everything

Those decades were not quiet. Civil-rights litigation, campus revolt, the rise of community mental health, the first state psychology boards thickening, women and Black psychologists organizing inside and against APA — all of that weather hit the association. A shortened code can still be a political object even when the visible change is editorial.

I will not invent a 1968 clause to match a headline. If a later editor has the 1968 PDF and wants to name a specific added or dropped duty, that is honest work. Until then, the honest sentence is: APA kept the title and changed the length while the country changed the jobs.

Belmont is 1979. Tuskegee's public exposure is 1972. Research-ethics weather thickened at the same time as the 1977–1979 printings. Coincidence is not a claim of causation. It is a reason to read those printings next to the National Commission's report rather than next to a nostalgia for "simpler times."

## Shortness as a professional fashion

NASW in 1960 and APGA in 1961 had already bet on short. APA spent 1959–1979 moving toward them, then spent 1992–2002 moving away again as enforceable standards multiplied. Fashion is a rude word for ethics. It is also accurate about page count.

A short code is easier to teach and easier to hide behind. A long code is harder to teach and harder to hide behind. Neither length guarantees a conscience. Draft 11 already said that. The 1959–1979 cycle is the evidence: APA believed length was a problem to be solved. Later APA believed underspecification was a problem to be solved. Both beliefs produced paper.

## For readers who want a villain or a hero

There is no villain in a shortened reprint. There is a membership that would not carry the 1953 brick. There is no hero either. Shortening is not courage. It is maintenance.

WOW Therapies does not need a 1963 *American Psychologist* on the reception table. It needs the habit named: when an association says "we updated the code," ask whether they rewrote the spine or republished a trim. 1959–1979 is mostly trim. 1992 and 2002 are spines. 2010 and 2016 are sutures.

The next essay is 1981, when the law-versus-ethics sentence steps forward and the title shifts toward *Ethical Principles of Psychologists*. The short-code era ends not with a bang but with a rename.
