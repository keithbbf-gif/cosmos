**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Every code in this pack's spine talks about competence. The 1961 APGA pamphlet, in later summaries, already told guidance workers to keep learning. NASW put competence in the 1996 six. APA's principles and standards have said, in various years, that psychologists stay inside what they can actually do.

The sentence is easy. The edge is not. A license is a state permission. A degree is a school's permission. A weekend workshop is a vendor's permission. A code tries to talk to all three edges and cannot police any of them alone.

## Three edges, three disappointments

**The license** disappoints because it is a floor. Passing a board exam is not a specialty. An LPC, an LCSW, and a psychologist may all be "allowed" to see a grieving adult and only one of them may have been trained for the particular grief in the room. Codes that say "practice within competence" are trying to add a second floor the state did not build.

**The degree** disappoints because curricula change and memory fades. A 1996 master's is not a 2014 telehealth. A 2002 internship is not a 2021 cultural-competence sentence. Continuing education is the official patch. CE is also a market.

**The workshop** disappoints because it is how new names (EMDR, a "trauma-informed" brand, a third-wave acronym) enter a small-city practice without a training clinic behind them. The ACT sister pack already refused to become a protocol. This pack refuses to become a credential-checker. It will say: competence sentences exist because workshops exist.

## APA versus ACA as a teaching contrast

A 2019 *JISS* commentary, answering an article that tabulated APA/ACA differences, reminded readers that psychology and counseling are distinct professions with distinct qualification cultures — doctorate versus master's as a crude national picture, with exceptions that ruin the crude picture. The comment is useful here only as a brake. If you use ethics-code differences to decide who is "more qualified," you have left history for a turf war.

This folder will not run a turf war. It will say the codes *assume different training stories*. APA's book assumes a psychologist's education. ACA's book assumes a counselor's. NASW's book assumes a social worker's, including the agency. Comparing a competence clause without comparing the assumed education is a category error.

## Cultural competence as a moving noun

NASW's 2017–2021 title fight (Drafts 33–34) is the loudest recent version. ACA's 2005 cultural-sensitivity thickening and 2014 values sentences are cousin objects. APA's 2002 weather included, in Fisher's process notes, culturally and linguistically diverse populations. The noun moves. The job — do not pretend a workshop sticker is a life — remains.

This site will not define the noun for a caseload.

## Impairment

NASW's 1993 additions named impairment. Other codes have cousin sentences about the worker who is too sick, too drunk, or too undone to sit in the chair. 2021's self-care language is a later, softer relative. Competence is not only skill. It is the body that uses the skill. A history pack can name that without writing a fitness-for-duty protocol.

## For WOW Therapies

The live clinic's competence home is speech-language pathology: ASHA, a state license, a swallow or an articulation that is not a counseling hour. Hosting a history of *other* crafts' competence sentences is literacy for readers who share a waiting-room word. It is not a claim that this clinic practices those crafts.

If you want to know whether your clinician is competent, a blog cannot tell you. A license lookup, a conversation, and, if needed, a board are the rooms. This essay is about why the codes keep saying a word that none of those rooms can finish.
