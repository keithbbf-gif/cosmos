**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

The American Counseling Association published a *Code of Ethics* in 1995. The archive still lists it. Later teachers often treat 1995 as the "before" picture for the 2005 revision talks: before the end of "clear and imminent danger," before a more particular dual-relationship conversation, before a technology section that 2014 would then enlarge.

That "before" is a later photographic trick. 1995 was, for its own year, a current book. It sat three years after the ACA name, in the same decade as APA's 1992 split and NASW's 1996 rebuild. Counseling was deep in the clinical turn: DSM on the master's desk, insurance panels, the same managed-care squeeze Fisher's APA task force would name.

## What I will not pretend to have memorized

I have not produced a section-by-section 1995 commentary. A later editor with the archive PDF should name the exact danger phrase, the exact relationship rules, and the exact assessment language. This essay's job is to keep 1995 from disappearing between 1988 and 2005.

Disappearing is how a craft loses its own middle. Students learn 2014 as if it were the first serious code. 1995 was already serious. It was also about to look dated, which is what serious codes do.

## 1995 as a clinical-turn object

By the mid-1990s a counselor in many states could be licensed, could diagnose for reimbursement, and could be sued. A guidance pamphlet's bones — welfare, competence, research — were no longer enough particularity for those three facts. 1995 is the particularity catching up. 2005 would decide it had not caught up enough.

The multicultural-competencies paper by Sue, Arredondo, and McDavis is 1992. Bayer's book on the 1973 psychiatric diagnosis fight is 1981. Both sit in the climate of a 1995 counseling code whether or not the code cites them. A craft that had begun as vocational match-work was now in the same moral weather as psychiatry and psychology, including the official cruelties those crafts had to unlearn.

## Dual relationships before the 2005 nuance

Popular memory says older counseling codes "banned dual relationships" and 2005 "allowed" some. Popular memory is a cartoon. The 2005 interview series itself is titled, in one part, "Allowing dual relationships," which tells you how the revisers wanted the contrast remembered. Historians should read both texts before repeating the cartoon. `[VERIFY]` 1995's actual relationship language against the PDF.

This pack's later comparative essay (Draft 37) will keep year-counts and relationship rules in one place. 1995 belongs in that table as a column, not as a myth.

## Technology before Section H

1995 is a pre-Facebook, pre-HIPAA-compliance-date document. HIPAA becomes law in 1996; the Privacy Rule's common compliance date is 14 April 2003. Email was already in clinics. The 2005 code would add a technology conversation; 2014 would give it a whole section. 1995's silence or thinness on screens is not innocence. It is a date.

## 1995 beside 1992 and 1996

APA's 1992 split and NASW's 1996 rebuild are the same-decade neighbors. Counseling's 1995 book is the middle craft's version of "we need more particularity now that we diagnose and bill." A reader who only compares 2014 ACA to 2002 APA is missing the 1990s cluster: three crafts, three architectures, one managed-care squeeze.

NBCC's National Certified Counselor credential, from 1982, was already a second noun on the wall. 1995 had to live beside a certificate that was not a license and a new association name that was not yet a household word. The code does not settle those nouns. It inherits them.

## For a reader who only has 2014

Read 1995 as the last full book before the 2005 rewrite, and as the first full book that already knew its association was named ACA. That is enough to stop the jump-cut. WOW Therapies does not need a 1995 poster. It needs the habit: when someone says "the old ACA code," ask which old.

The next essay is 2005, where ACA did the unusual thing of recording its own revision as a ten-part interview series. That series is a historian's gift. We will use it as a map, not as a sermon.
