**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

At the end of March 2014 the American Counseling Association released a new *Code of Ethics*, replacing the 2005 book. *Counseling Today* later described three years of work: forty conference calls, face-to-face meetings, two convention town halls, and comments from more than a hundred members. Perry Francis, who coordinated a training clinic and sat on the Ethics Revision Task Force, is named in that account as a public voice of the revision.

Those process facts are the first object. The 2014 book is the second. This website will not host the second.

## Values, without a culture-war worksheet

The sentences later teachers cite most often are about personal values and referral. In the public commentary, ACA said counselors should not impose their values on clients and should not use referral as a way to avoid a client whose life they morally dislike. Competence, not comfort, was supposed to be the hinge.

I am paraphrasing a public argument. I am not pasting Standards A.4.b or A.11.b. I am not applying them to a case. The historical fact is that a national counseling association, in 2014, chose to write more particular sentences about values conflicts than many readers expected from a "wellness" craft.

The fight was already in the courts and in training programs — conscience clauses, dismissal-from-program cases, state bills. 2014 did not invent the fight. It put the association's name on one side of a particular version of it. Later state laws would sometimes point the other way. A code is not a statute. Draft 04 still holds.

## Section H and the wall

2014 added an entire section on distance counseling, technology, and social media. Email had been in the 2005 conversation. By 2014 the objects were Facebook walls, search boxes, and the cheap intimacy of a screen. The section is a dated inventory of those objects. It is not a 2026 telehealth manual. It is not a HIPAA textbook. It is a membership body saying: the hour has a new furniture.

NASW would spend 2017 on a cousin technology revision. APA's 2002 book had already tried to see the internet coming. ACA's 2014 section is the middle craft's most public "we see the screen" moment.

## Five years, two years

Public comparison charts say ACA's 2014 text asks counselors to wait five years after termination before a sexual or romantic involvement with a former client, and that APA's text uses two. I am reporting the circulating comparison, which is consistent across teaching slides I have seen. `[VERIFY]` the current living texts before a live caption, because amendments happen.

Year-counts are a crude instrument. They are also one of the few places a general reader can *see* that two professions wrote different rules about the same temptation. Draft 37 will come back to the crudeness. Here the count is a 2014 fact.

## Glossary, length, the 7–10-year story

Commentators noted a glossary of about fifty words and a jump from five 1961 pages to about twenty-four. Lynn Linde's circulating remark that ACA revises every 7–10 years is a habit, not a bylaw. 2005 to 2014 is nine years. 2014 to a hoped-for 2026 is twelve. Habits slip.

Perry Francis and other task-force members were living at last verification. Public process only. No portraits. No gossip.

## What 2014 is on this site

It is, as of 14 September 2026, still the living ACA book while a revision sits in committee (Draft 28). A reader who wants the sentences should use counseling.org, not a blog's memory. WOW Therapies will not interpret 2014 for a counselor's Tuesday and will not pretend a speech clinic is bound by it.

The 2014 object is also why this pack exists: a small-city educational lane can tell the story of a national pamphlet that learned screens and values fights without becoming either a culture-war poster or a telehealth vendor.
