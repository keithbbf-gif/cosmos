**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In August 1996 NASW adopted a rebuilt *Code of Ethics*. The preamble named a mission — enhancing well-being, meeting basic needs, particular attention to people who are vulnerable, oppressed, and living in poverty — and six core values: service, social justice, dignity and worth of the person, importance of human relationships, integrity, competence.

If you have been in a social-work classroom, you have heard the list. The list is a 1996 committee distillation. It is not a revelation and it is not eternal. Reamer, whose later textbooks describe the revision committee's literature review, says the six were chosen from a crowded field of proposed value lists. Distillation is a historical process. Recitation can make it sound like scripture. This essay is on the side of distillation.

## Architecture, cousin to APA 1992

1996 stacked four layers: mission and values; purpose of the code; ethical principles (one broad sentence per value); ethical standards (a long, particular body). APA's 1992 split had already taught American helping crafts that aspiration and enforcement can share a binding and not a sentence. NASW's version is more explicit about *mission*. Psychology's book does not open with poverty. Social work's 1996 book does.

That opening is why later readers hear NASW as "more political." The politics are in the 1915–1917 ancestry (Flexner, Richmond, the agency) as much as in a 1990s culture fight. A historian who treats "social justice" as a 2015 slogan has not read the 1996 preamble.

## How many standards

Secondary accounts mention on the order of 155 specific standards in the 1996 architecture. `[VERIFY]` the count against a 1996 printing if a live caption wants a number. The usable fact is *much larger than 1979's principle list*. Particularity was the point. Members had asked for it since the 1960 page.

Particularity has a cost. Students outline. Agencies laminate. The laminated card is not a conscience (Draft 11). It is a 1996 achievement that can become a 1996 fetish.

## 1999: a small suture

NASW notes a 1999 clarification about disclosing confidential information. Small sutures matter because they prove the 1996 book was already being lived hard enough to need a tweak. They also prove that "the 1996 code" is a short series, not a monolith.

## The six as a classroom object

Service. Social justice. Dignity and worth of the person. Importance of human relationships. Integrity. Competence. The list is short enough to write on a board and long enough to start a fight about which value wins when two collide. 1996 did not write a collision rule. It wrote a list and then a body of standards that try, case by case, to do the colliding.

Kitchener's 1984 counseling-psychology principles are a cousin list (autonomy, beneficence, nonmaleficence, justice, fidelity). Students mash them. Historians should not. One is a 1984 journal paper. One is a 1996 association vote. Different rooms.

## What 1996 did not finish

It did not name gender identity, sexual orientation, and immigration status in the 2008 way. It did not write the 2017 technology sentences. It did not write the 2021 self-care sentences. It did not settle whether "cultural competence" or "cultural humility" is the better English. It built the house those fights would happen in.

## For a speech clinic's neighbors

An LCSW in the same Arkansas town as WOW Therapies was trained, if the training was typical, to recite six values before they could bill a unit. An SLP was trained to a different catechism. The child on the caseload does not recite either. The educational lane can say: the social worker's catechism has an August 1996 publication date and a poverty sentence in the mission. That is more useful than "social workers are the justice people."
