**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

The National Association of Social Workers' Delegate Assembly approved the first NASW *Code of Ethics* on 13 October 1960. The document fit on one page. It set out fourteen proclamations in the first person: I respect privacy, I give professional responsibility precedence over personal interests, I offer appropriate service in public emergencies, I contribute knowledge to programs of human welfare — the list as later historians, including Frederic Reamer, have summarized it.

NASW hosts a historical copy. This folder will not retype the page. The literary fact is enough: social work's first national code sounded like an oath, not like a handbook.

## 1955 as the letterhead

NASW itself is 1955, a merger of older social-work organizations. Five years from merger to page is faster than APGA's 1952-to-1961, slower than a myth of instant ethics. The Delegate Assembly — a representative body, not a staff office — is the machine that would keep voting on later revisions. Draft 35 is that machine. 1960 is its first ethics product.

Flexner had asked in 1915 whether social work was a profession. A one-page "I" is a 1960 answer: we can at least speak as a single first person. Whether that answers Flexner's knowledge-base taunt is a different fight. Codes soothe status wounds. They do not close them.

## What a first-person page can do

It can be memorized. It can be hung. It can shame, a little, if the "I" on the wall is not the "I" in the office. It cannot easily support a formal complaint that needs a particular count. Reamer's later history says members came to find the 1960 page vague, thin on dilemmas, and weak as an enforcement tool. That complaint is why 1979 exists.

APA's 1953 book had the opposite problem: too much incident, too many pages. Social work and psychology, in their first printed ethics, missed in opposite directions. Both misses are informative. Length is a bet about what a membership can use.

## 1967: a fifteenth line

On 11 April 1967 the Assembly added a principle on nondiscrimination. NASW still points to that revision as a public historical copy. One line, seven years later, is not a diversity paragraph at the end of a textbook. It is an admission that fourteen proclamations had not said a thing that 1967's politics made unsayable to omit.

The later 2008 naming of gender identity, sexual orientation, and immigration status, and the 2021 fight over the title of Standard 1.05, are grandchildren of this 1967 line. Grandchildren argue. The 1967 line is the first public NASW ethics sentence that says the "I" is not only a private virtue.

## Neighbors in 1960

APGA's pamphlet is 1961. APA is already on its first short-code cycle (1959). The three first printed helping-craft codes in this pack's spine are, within eight years, a book, a page, and a pamphlet. That cluster is the postwar professionalization wave, not a coincidence of virtue.

## For a clinic that is not a welfare office

A clinical social worker in a Delta community agency in 2026 is a long way from the 1960 page and still closer to it, literarily, than a psychologist is to 1953. The "I" remains in the profession's self-talk even after 1996 moved the book into third-person standards. WOW Therapies is not NASW. A reader who meets a social worker in the same town as a speech clinic deserves to know that the social worker's oldest national ethics sentence was a first-person oath on a single sheet.

The next essay is 1979: Charles Levy's task force, six sections, a preamble, enforcement language, a 1 July 1980 effective date, and, in later counts, eighty-two principles.
