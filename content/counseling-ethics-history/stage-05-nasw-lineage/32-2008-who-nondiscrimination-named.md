In 2008 NASW revised its code to put gender identity, sexual orientation, and immigration status into already existing nondiscrimination language. The association's later teaching materials describe the change as a revisiting, not as the invention of nondiscrimination. The 1967 fifteenth principle is the ancestor. 2008 is a naming.

Naming is a historical act. A list that does not include a category can still be used, sometimes, to protect people in that category. A list that includes a category makes the protection harder to talk around. It also makes the list a political object in a country that was, in 2008, arguing about both marriage and the border.

## What a historian does with a list

Does not treat the list as complete. 2008's names are 2008's names. Later years would argue about other words. Does not treat the pre-2008 code as a permission to discriminate. Absence of a noun is not always a permission; it is often an older generality. Does not turn the list into a worksheet about "how to be affirming." Affirmation as a clinical method is a different literature. This folder does not teach it.

Does say: social work, as a national association, chose in 2008 to spend its revision energy on who counts in a nondiscrimination sentence, while ACA was three years past the 2005 cultural-sensitivity thickening and six years from the 2014 values fight, and while APA was living in the 2002 house under PENS weather.

## Immigration status as a tell

Many American ethics lists in the 2000s named sexual orientation and were slower to name immigration status. NASW's inclusion of immigration status is a documentary tell about the craft: public agencies, child welfare, medical social work, and border-adjacent practice were already ethics rooms. A counseling code written from private-practice anxiety may be slower to see a green card as an ethics object. A social-work code written from agency anxiety is faster.

That contrast is a tendency, not a scoreboard. Individual counselors at the border and individual social workers in boutique practice will ruin the tendency. The *documents* still differ.

## Gender identity in 2008

2008 is before a later decade's louder legislative fights, and after a long activist and clinical argument. Putting the words in a national code is not the end of the argument. It is a dated public sentence. Later NASW statements and later state laws would sometimes rhyme and sometimes collide. A code is not a statute. A statute is not a clinic. Draft 04 remains the furniture.

## What this essay will not do

It will not narrate a culture war. It will not diagnose a profession as "captured." It will not diagnose a profession as "finally moral." It will not reprint the 2008 sentences. It will not offer a client a script for confronting a worker.

It will say that a curious reader who hears "the NASW code is inclusive" is hearing, in part, a 2008 naming decision with a 1967 grandfather.

## 2008 as a mid-architecture suture

This is not a 1996 rebuild and not a 2017 technology cycle. It is a naming suture on the 1996 house, the way 1967 was a naming suture on the 1960 page. Sutures are easy to skip in a greatest-hits lecture. Skipping them is how a craft looks like it jumped from "values" to "tech" without admitting who it learned to name.

APA's 2002 Respect principle and ACA's 2005 cultural-sensitivity thickening are same-era cousins. They do not contain the same nouns. Nouns are the history.

## For Southeast Arkansas

Immigration status, sexual orientation, and gender identity are not coastal abstractions in a Delta town. They are neighbors, schools, churches, and clinics. A speech-language pathologist meets some of the same families. ASHA's ethics language is a neighbor document. NASW's 2008 naming is one craft's public list. This site will not collapse the lists into a brand slogan.
