**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Judges at Nuremberg, in 1947, appended a ten-point statement about permissible medical experiment to a verdict on Nazi physicians. The statement later traveled under the name Nuremberg Code. It is a court-adjacent document, not an APA pamphlet. It still sits in the weather of every later American research-ethics sentence about consent, coercion, and the right to quit.

Counseling and psychology codes did not begin at Nuremberg. They began, as this pack will show, in membership anxieties about clinics, classrooms, and tests. But the postwar research scar is why a 2002 APA code has a long research section, why IRBs exist in the universities that train counselors, and why "informed consent" sounds, to a modern ear, like a form. The form is a descendant. The descendant is allowed to be thinner than the trial.

## Three documents, three jobs

**Nuremberg, 1947.** A list. Voluntary consent. Fruitful results for the good of society. Avoidance of unnecessary suffering. The right to end participation. Historians argue about how much the list described existing American research practice and how much it invented a usable past. `[CITE NEEDED]` for the strongest version of that argument; I am reporting the quarrel, not scoring it. What a later ethics teacher can honestly say: after Nuremberg, "we were only following orders" became a sentence a profession had to answer. APA would meet a version of that sentence again in Standard 1.02. Drafts 15 and 18 will not let that echo become a cartoon.

**Helsinki, 1964.** The World Medical Association wrote a declaration for physicians doing research on human beings, and then rewrote it, repeatedly, for the rest of the century. Helsinki is a medical-association object. Psychologists and social workers met it as neighbors: through journals, through funders, through the embarrassment of being asked whether their "subjects" had been treated like patients. The declaration's later revisions are a reminder that even a famous ethics text is a series.

**Belmont, 18 April 1979.** An American National Commission, created after the U.S. Public Health Service's Tuskegee syphilis study became a public scandal, published a report that named three principles for research with human subjects: respect for persons, beneficence, justice. Those English words then leaked everywhere. APA's later General Principles are not a copy of Belmont. They are written in a climate Belmont helped name. Students who treat Belmont as a counseling code are mixing a research-commission report with a membership handbook. The mix is common. It is still a mix.

## Tuskegee and Willowbrook as the American weather

Tuskegee (the USPHS study of untreated syphilis in Black men, exposed in 1972) and the Willowbrook hepatitis studies are not association codes. They are the reasons Congress and universities built review machinery. Counseling trainees meet that machinery as CITI modules and IRB stamps. The modules are not this pack's subject. The weather is.

A profession that tests children, interviews prisoners, or runs analog "experiments" in a clinic cannot pretend that research ethics is someone else's campus. APA's 1953 incidents already included research and teaching. Later codes thickened those sections as the federal apparatus thickened. NASW's standards on evaluation and research sit in the same weather. ACA's later texts do too.

## Consent as a traveling word

"Informed consent" in Belmont is a research protection. "Informed consent" in a 1992 or 2002 practice standard is a clinical protection. The phrase moved house. Draft 39 will treat the clinical house as its own object: fees, recordings, the right to refuse a technique, the problem of court-ordered evaluation. Here it is enough to say that the phrase arrived in American helping crafts carrying a research passport, and that the passport still shows at the border.

A speech-language pathologist in Southeast Arkansas who hands a parent a consent form is not performing Nuremberg. She is performing a later, local, licensed ritual that would be unintelligible without that passport. History can name the passport. History cannot turn the ritual into a downloadable form on this site.

## What leaked, what did not

What leaked: suspicion of the bare "trust me"; a taste for written explanation; a committee that sits between the investigator and the body.

What did not leak cleanly: Nuremberg's severity. Most counseling research is not a camp. Using camp language to decorate a survey study is a kind of kitsch. This folder will not do it. Using camp history to explain why 1.02 became a fight inside APA is not kitsch. It is the profession's own later argument.

Hoffman and PENS are twenty-first-century objects. They belong in Stage 3. They are intelligible, in part, because Nuremberg made "orders" a dirty word in medical ethics. The 2010 APA amendments did not "apply Nuremberg." They answered a domestic fight about psychologists and interrogations. Keep the metaphor on a leash.

## For a site that is not a lab

WOW Therapies is not an IRB. These essays will not teach you how to write a protocol. They will say, when the later codes mention research, that the sentence has grandparents. The grandparents are 1947, 1964, and 18 April 1979. The children are membership standards. The grandchildren are the forms in a university clinic.

If you want the full research-ethics library, it is elsewhere: the Common Rule, OHRP, the later Helsinki versions. This pack names the three objects a counseling-history reader keeps tripping over. Then it goes back to the guilds.
