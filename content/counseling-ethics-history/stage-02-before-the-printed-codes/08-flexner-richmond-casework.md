Abraham Flexner stood up in 1915 and asked whether social work was a profession. He had already remade American medical education with a famous report. He knew how to wound with a definition. A profession, in his mouth, had a learned knowledge base, a school, a kind of altruism, and a way of teaching the next cohort. Social work, he suggested, was still too much a pile of good will and other people's sciences.

Social workers have been answering him ever since. The 1960 NASW code is one of the answers. So is the 1996 list of values. So is every later sentence about competence. You cannot hear those sentences if you start the story in 1960 as if a page appeared from nowhere.

## The notebook before the code

Mary Richmond's *Social Diagnosis* (Russell Sage Foundation, 1917) is a book about how a charity worker gathers and weighs facts about a household. It is earnest, of its time, and sometimes hard to read without flinching at class and race. It is also a founding object for the idea that social work has a *method*, not only a heart.

A method wants a notebook. A notebook wants a rule about who else may read it. Confidentiality as a social-work anxiety is older than NASW. It is the anxiety of a visitor who has been in a kitchen and then returns to an office where other visitors gossip. The 1960 proclamation "I respect the privacy of the people I serve" is a late, first-person compression of that office problem.

Richmond is not a saint of the code. She is a documentary ancestor. Later critics would say casework individualized poverty and let structures off the hook. Those critics are why NASW's later texts talk about social justice as a core value and not only about the dyad. The quarrel is internal. This folder will not settle it. It will keep both objects on the table: the notebook and the justice sentence.

## Charity organization and the public bureau

Nineteenth-century Charity Organization Societies invented "scientific charity" — investigate, coordinate, do not duplicate alms. The tone can sound cold. It was a reaction to a fear of indiscriminate giving, and it produced a craft identity: we are not the lady with a basket, we are the people with a file.

Twentieth-century public welfare and child-welfare bureaus then employed thousands of people whose boss was the state. NASW's later standards about employers and employing organizations come from that employment graph. A psychologist's 1953 code is more worried about the university and the clinic. A counselor's 1961 pamphlet is more worried about the school and the guidance office. A social worker's code has to talk about the agency, because the agency is often the real other party in the room.

## Flexner's wound as a professional engine

Flexner did not write an ethics code. He wrote a status anxiety that codes try to soothe. "We have standards" is a sentence aimed at Flexner's ghost as much as at a client. The 1979 NASW revision, in Frederic Reamer's later historical essays, answered members who said the 1960 page was too vague to enforce. Enforcement is a Flexner move: if you cannot throw someone out, are you a profession?

Counseling and psychology have their own Flexner-shaped nights. APA's postwar reorganization and ACA's march toward licensure are cousin anxieties. Social work's version is simply earlier and more explicit, because Flexner named the craft to its face.

## What a 1960 page inherited

A first-person voice (the oath habit). A privacy sentence (the notebook). A duty that outranks personal interest (the agency vs. the self). A promise to contribute knowledge (the school Flexner said they lacked). A later 1967 nondiscrimination line that admits the 1960 page had not said enough.

It did not inherit a 155-standard architecture. That is 1996. It did not inherit "self-care" as an integrity sentence. That is 2021. Ancestors are not the whole house.

## For a clinic that is not a welfare office

WOW Therapies is not the county office. A speech evaluation in Southeast Arkansas is not *Social Diagnosis*. The reason this essay exists on this site is narrower: when a later draft says NASW's code sounds more structural than APA's, that sound has a 1915–1917 address. Readers who only know the counseling hour will otherwise hear "social justice" as a 2010s slogan. It is a 2010s fight. It is also a century-old professional argument about whether the file is enough.

I have not reproduced Richmond's case examples and will not. They are period objects with living descendants. A history site that turns a 1917 case into color is already halfway to a composite patient. We do not do composite patients.

The next essay leaves the charity office for the vocation bureau — Parsons, guidance, the job that would later call itself counseling and print five pages in 1961. Different notebook. Same Flexner weather: are you a profession, or a helpful person with a title?
