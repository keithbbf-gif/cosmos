The Hippocratic Oath is a short Greek text that later physicians treated as a founding promise: do some kinds of harm, refuse others, keep some kinds of silence. Classicists argue about date, authorship, and whether a working ancient doctor ever spoke it. That argument is already the lesson. An oath is a literary object with a reception history. It is not a membership PDF with an effective date.

American counseling and clinical codes sit on a pile of older promises. If we flatten the pile into "ethics is ancient," we flatter the present. If we pretend the pile is irrelevant, we miss why a 1953 psychologist and a 1960 social worker reached for the word *ethics* as if everyone already knew the tune.

## What an oath could do that a code later wanted

An oath binds a speaker in the first person. NASW's 1960 page still sounds like that: I will, I respect, I give precedence. APA's later third-person standards sound like a handbook. ACA's lettered sections sound like house rules. The literary distance from "I swear" to "psychologists do not" is a twentieth-century professionalization. It is not a moral upgrade.

An oath also travels without a bureaucracy. You can copy it. You can refuse it. You can hang it in a waiting room and never join an association. Codes, by contrast, want a printer, a committee, and a list of members. That is why this pack starts its numbered spines in 1953, 1960, and 1961. The older sentences are climate, not statute.

## Silence, harm, and the household

Two themes in the Hippocratic reception matter for later counseling rules: a promise about harm, and a promise about what must not be gossiped.

Later medical ethics would spend the nineteenth and twentieth centuries turning those themes into hospital bylaws, then into research codes, then into licensing statutes. Counseling and psychology borrowed the *shape* — a public sentence about harm, a public sentence about silence — while working in rooms the Hippocratic writers had not imagined: a fifty-minute hour, a test file, a school counseling office, a charity caseload.

The household is the third theme. Ancient and medieval care happened among kin, priests, and local specialists. A modern code's anxiety about "multiple relationships" is, in one sense, an anxiety about the household leaking back into the hour. Rural Arkansas still lives that leak. A clinician and a client may share a grocery store. The 2014 ACA text and the NASW standards on dual relationships are late attempts to write a rule for a small town. They are not translations of Cos.

## Clerical seals and the later lease

Confession, spiritual direction, and rabbinic or pastoral counsel taught generations to put a life into words for a person who was not supposed to carry the words to the market. Historians who call that "early psychotherapy" are flattering the clinic. Historians who ignore it miss the emotional technology that codes later tried to *lease* from the state: confidentiality as a professional duty, sometimes backed by privilege, sometimes punched through by a court.

The seal and the license are not the same object. A priest's silence is a religious rule. A counselor's silence is a mix of association language, board rule, and statute. Draft 36 will keep privilege in the courthouse. Here it is enough to say: the desire for a quiet room is older than APA. The paperwork that makes the quiet enforceable is not.

## Guilds, apprentices, and the American association

European craft guilds wrote rules about who could practice, who could train, and who could be thrown out. American learned societies — AMA, ABA, later APA — inherited the ambition without always inheriting the medieval structure. Flexner's 1915 question, "Is social work a profession?," is a later American version of the same anxiety: are you a craft with standards, or a pile of good intentions?

When APA in 1947 and NASW in 1960 and APGA in 1961 print codes, they are answering Flexner in different dialects. They are also answering clients, newspapers, and, soon, licensing boards. The oath is the ancestor they could point to when a skeptic said, "You made this up last year."

## What this essay will not do

It will not print the Hippocratic Oath. The text is short and widely reproduced; this site does not need another copy. It will not claim that Hippocrates invented informed consent. He did not. It will not claim that a 1953 psychologist was "really" an ancient physician. She was a mid-century American employee with a new kind of power.

It will not turn "do no harm" into a poster. Nonmaleficence as a named principle in later APA and counseling-psychology writing (Kitchener 1984 is a marker) is a twentieth-century academic object. The English slogan is a reception. Receptions are allowed to be thinner than the Greek.

For WOW Therapies, the useful inheritance is modest. People have been promising not to gossip and not to use a craft as a weapon for a long time. Associations later wrote those promises down, dated them, and built rooms to punish the people who broke them. The next essays in this stage are about the twentieth-century scars — Nuremberg, Helsinki, Belmont — and about the charity notebook that social work brought to the table. Those are closer ancestors than Cos. They still are not the 1953 book.

If a reader leaves this page thinking "ethics is old, so we are fine," the page has failed. If a reader leaves thinking "the old sentences were already arguing about harm and silence," the page has done its job.
