**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 1938 the American Psychological Association created a Committee on Scientific and Professional Ethics and let it handle complaints on an informal basis. There was no book to hold up. There was a reputation, a network of men (mostly men) who knew one another, and a sense that some conduct was "not what we do."

Fourteen years later, as the first printed code was being adopted, an APA note looked back and called the unwritten code "tenuous, elusive, and unsatisfactory." I am taking that phrasing from circulating secondary quotations of a 1952 retrospective. `[VERIFY]` the page if a later editor has the scan. The sentiment is the history: the association had already discovered that informal shame does not scale.

## Why 1938 is a date and not a code

A committee is a room. A code is a text. Confusing them is how anniversary essays get written. 1938 matters because it shows APA admitting that psychology had become a public power — testing, advising industry, teaching, touching patients — before it had sentences a stranger could read.

The Depression and the coming war sit in the background. Psychologists were leaving the campus. The 1945–47 reorganization of APA, which folded applied divisions into a new association structure, made the lack of a text more embarrassing. You cannot welcome clinicians and testers into a scientific society and then tell the newspapers that ethics is a gentleman's understanding.

## Informal process as a historical object

Informal process has virtues. It is fast. It can be kind. It can tell a young psychologist to knock it off without ending a career. It also has vices that later codes exist to constrain. It favors insiders. It disappears when the accused is powerful. It leaves no public memory, so the next committee repeats the education.

When later readers meet an association ethics office, they are meeting the descendant of this informal room *plus* a text the room can point to. The text does not make the process public. It makes the *standard* public. That is a narrower transparency than journalists want and a wider one than 1938 offered.

This folder will not reconstruct 1930s complaint files. I have not seen them. A history that invents a 1939 case "for color" is a composite patient in a different coat.

## The 1947 ask

By 1947 the informal committee was ready to say the obvious: write it down. Edward C. Tolman chaired a Committee on Ethical Standards. Tolman is a name experimental psychologists already know for rats and purposive behavior. His presence on an ethics committee is a tell. APA still wanted the scientists in the room. A code written only by clinicians would have split the association it was meant to hold together.

Hobbs's 1948 article is the next chapter. This chapter ends on the threshold: a craft that had already been trying members, in a fashion, without a pamphlet. Counseling, in 1938, was still mostly guidance and did not have an equivalent national ethics committee with a public later myth. Social work had older association fragments and would merge NASW in 1955. Psychology's 1938 room is specific.

## Unwritten rules in a small city

Southeast Arkansas still runs on unwritten rules. So does every clinic that has a senior person who "knows how we do things." A printed code is a later attempt to make those things checkable by a stranger. It never fully replaces the senior person. Anyone who has worked in a small shop knows this. The 1952 APA retrospective knew it too: the unwritten was unsatisfactory *and* it had been doing work.

WOW Therapies, as a live speech practice, has its own house habits and its own ASHA neighbor-code. This essay is not a demand that a small clinic become a 1947 committee. It is a warning against nostalgia. "We didn't need a code in my day" is a sentence 1938 already tested. The association that tested it decided, after the war, to print.

## What "tenuous" bought us

It bought a method argument. If the unwritten failed because it was elusive, the written would have to be specific. Hobbs would answer with critical incidents — more than a thousand stories — rather than a dignified page of abstractions. NASW would later decide the 1960 abstractions were too thin and write 1979 and 1996. ACA would thicken five pages into twenty-four.

Tenuousness is the engine of revision. Every later "living document" speech is a grandchild of 1938's failure to stay informal.
