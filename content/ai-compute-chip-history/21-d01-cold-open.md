# Draft 01 — cold open only

Job: get two pictures on the wall. Do not explain CUDA. Stop before
the lecture starts.

---

In 2012, a neural net learned to see well enough to embarrass the
field, and it did it on two video-game cards.

The cards were NVIDIA GTX 580s. Each one had three gigabytes of
memory, which sounded like a lot if you were playing a game and not
enough if you were stuffing 1.2 million pictures into a deep
convolutional network. Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever, and Geoffrey
Hinton split the work across the pair, trained for five or six days,
and entered the result in that year's ImageNet contest. They won by
enough that computer vision had to rethink its week.

The paper is almost offhand about the hardware. A single 580 could
not hold the net they wanted. The two GPUs could talk to each other
without walking the long way through the computer's main memory.
Results, they wrote, would get better if you simply waited for
faster GPUs and bigger data. They were not being cute. They were
describing the next decade.

The same season, another picture. Researchers at Google were
training enormous nets on warehouses of ordinary processors —
DistBelief, tens of thousands of CPU cores, a famous experiment
that found a neuron that liked cats in YouTube frames. It was real
science and a perfect magazine story. It also needed a building.

Two cards on a desk. A building full of CPUs. Same hunger. If you
only remember one thing from this history, remember that contrast.
The math wanted a certain shape of machine. One of those setups
already had it, and it had the shape because teenagers had been
buying graphics cards to make games look less like painted boxes.

We will get to the language that made those cards programmable, and
to the other chip Google built when buying more cards was not the
answer it wanted. First, sit with the two pictures. The rest of the
story is people noticing what they already imply.
