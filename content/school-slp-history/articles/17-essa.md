This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

President Barack Obama signed the Every Student Succeeds Act, Public Law 114-95, on 10 December 2015. ESSA is again an ESEA reauthorization, not an IDEA reauthorization. IDEA 2004 stayed. What changed was the weather in the regular building: no more federal adequate-yearly-progress machine of the NCLB type, more state-designed accountability, a continued demand for disaggregated data, and a set of literacy and school-improvement nouns that states could interpret.

For school speech-language pathologists, ESSA felt like a release and a shrug. Release: fewer buildings living in weekly terror of a subgroup cell. Shrug: the literacy block did not shrink, MTSS graphs did not vanish, and “evidence-based” remained the password. The Department of Education’s 2017 IDEA regulation tweaks even cleaned some ESEA-related definitions after ESSA. The two statutes still share a building.

## Specialized instructional support personnel

ESSA uses *specialized instructional support personnel* (SISP) for the cluster that includes school counselors, psychologists, social workers, and related-service providers such as speech-language pathologists. The noun is a seating chart. It puts the SLP in a federal K–12 list that is not “special education staff only.” Title I and Title II conversations can, on paper, include that list. Whether a particular district’s professional-development money ever reaches the closet is a local budget story.

The noun also marks a claim: this person supports instruction, not only a separate clinic. That is the 1997–2010 ASHA line in ESEA clothing. Jean Blosser and Barbara Ehren had been writing it in professional books for years.

## What ESSA did not do

It did not rewrite FAPE. It did not set a caseload. It did not settle English-learner identification versus speech-language eligibility (essay 20). It did not fund the itinerant’s second building. It did not end alternate assessments; it constrained some of the “modified” assessment experiments of the late NCLB years, as the Department’s 2015 IDEA regulation note records.

Parents sometimes heard “ESSA replaced NCLB” as “the tests went away.” They did not. States still test. Schools still publish. Children with disabilities are still a group whose scores are reported. The federal punishment script changed. The child’s Tuesday did not automatically change.

## A quieter alignment

If 2004 was IDEA chasing NCLB, 2015 is ESEA loosening so IDEA does not have to chase as hard. School SLPs who had rewritten every goal to sound like a reading intervention were free, in theory, to write a communication goal again. Some did. Some had forgotten how. A profession that survives accountability regimes by mimicry will mimic the next regime too.

English learners remain a reported group under ESSA, as do students with disabilities. The two groups overlap in the speech office and still get confused in the evaluation (essay 20). A state plan that names “evidence-based literacy” without naming language is a plan that will send the SLP a slide deck and not a schedule.

The residue is a state plan. An SLP who wants to know what her building is now judged by must read her state’s ESSA plan, not a 2006 AYP worksheet. That is dull civic advice. It is also the only accurate map. This series will not summarize fifty plans. It will say the federal scoreboard moved upstairs and left the closet intact. The 2010 ASHA roles statement, five years older than ESSA, already described the job the state plan would keep asking for: curriculum, collaboration, a literacy seat. ESSA did not invent that seat. It changed who grades the building that refuses to offer it.
