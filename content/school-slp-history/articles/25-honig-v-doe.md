This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

*Honig v. Doe*, 484 U.S. 305 (1988), is a San Francisco Unified School District case that arrived at the Supreme Court under the names of two students, called John Doe and Jack Smith in the reports to protect their identities. Both were labeled emotionally disturbed. Both had been involved in serious disciplinary incidents. The district wanted them out. The 1975 Act’s “stay-put” provision said that during the pendency of proceedings, the child was to remain in the then-current educational placement unless the parents and the agency agreed otherwise.

Justice Brennan, for the Court, held that Congress meant what it wrote. Schools may not unilaterally exclude a child with a disability from the classroom for dangerous or disruptive conduct during the pendency of a review, except by the statutory process — including, if needed, a court injunction — and except for a short “cooling off” suspension the opinion allowed as a traditional tool (the famous ten-day discussion). There is no implied “dangerousness” exception that lets an administrator skip the Act.

## Why a speech series keeps a discipline caption

Because stay-put is how a related service survives a crisis week. A child who receives speech-language pathology as part of an IEP cannot simply be disappeared from the specialist’s list while adults argue about a fight in the cafeteria. The placement includes the services. A unilateral “he’s not coming to speech until this is settled” is, in stay-put weather, a change.

Because 1997 and 2004 will write more elaborate discipline rules — manifestation determinations, 45-day interim alternative placements for weapons and drugs and serious bodily injury — on top of Honig, not instead of it. The later code is a statute trying to give administrators a valve without returning to the pre-1975 sidewalk.

Because children with communication disabilities are over-represented in some discipline counts, and children of color with disability labels more so. Honig is not a race case. The 2016 Equity rules are the later federal admission that removals were not evenly distributed. Essay 20.

## What the opinion is not

It is not a permission slip for a child to injure people. Brennan is explicit that schools can go to court. It is not a speech-language holding. It is not a biography of Doe or Smith; the Court used those names so this magazine will too, and will not hunt their later lives.

It is not the last word on “current educational placement.” Later cases argue about what the phrase means when the last agreed IEP is stale, or when the parent has placed the child privately. This profile stays with 1988’s spine: Congress put the burden on the district that wants the child gone.

School speech work is rarely the reason a Honig fight starts. It is often part of what the fight would erase. The residue is a calendar: ten days, a meeting, a stay. The closet’s sign-out sheet is, for once, a civil-rights document.
