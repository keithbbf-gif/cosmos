This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 1910, Chicago’s superintendent, Ella Flagg Young, hired ten teachers out of the Department of Expression at Chicago Teachers College and sent them into the schools as speech workers. The same year, Detroit opened two speech centers under Clara B. Stoddard and Lillian Morley. Judy Felson Duchan, writing in *Language, Speech, and Hearing Services in Schools*, treats those two hires as the first recorded district-paid speech staff in the United States. The date is not a brand origin story. It is a payroll.

New York had already tried something smaller. In 1908, John F. Reigart, principal of Public School 2, organized a class for children who “stammered.” The class did not survive his transfer. In 1911, at Public School 166, he hired Mary C. Crahen, a grade teacher, to work with children who used “baby talk.” That is a principal improvising, not a bureau. Duchan’s point is that most large American cities had someone on the job by 1921, and that supervisors to coordinate them arrived in the 1920s.

## What the cities thought they were hiring

They were hiring a correction of the mouth that embarrassed the classroom. Stammering. Lisping. “Infantile speech.” Foreign accent, in some high-school add-ons. The early reports do not say *language impairment*. They do not say *autism*. They do not say *swallowing*. They say the child cannot recite, or cannot be understood at the front of the room, or cannot stop repeating a sound. The job sat next to elocution and next to the special class for the “defective.” It was a teacher’s job with a narrower noun.

Pauline B. Camp published “Correction of Speech Defects in a Public School System” in the *Quarterly Journal of Speech* in 1917. The paper is a city report, not a laboratory. That is the genre: how many children, how many hours, which building. Mabel Farrington Gifford’s 1925 San Francisco paper in the same journal is the West Coast twin. In 1917 she had already ridden the train east to look at Minneapolis, Saint Paul, Detroit, Chicago, Boston, Newark, and New York. Alfred Donald Cross’s 1961 thesis on San Francisco quotes that itinerary. The profession was comparing notes by streetcar and Pullman before it had a federal statute.

## Who was not in the room

Deaf education was already a century-old argument about signs and speech. Blind children had their own residential schools. Children labeled “feeble-minded” were, in many states, someone else’s institution. The new speech teacher did not open those doors. She (almost always she) took the children the regular teacher could still imagine as fixable. Duchan is blunt that school speech work has been the neglected half of the field’s memory. The academy later wrote itself as Iowa and Wisconsin. The cities had already hired.

Walter Babcock Swift’s National Society for the Study and Correction of Speech Disorders, affiliated with the National Education Association from about 1918, was the school people’s club. Duchan’s 2002 *ASHA Leader* piece notes that nine of the twenty-five charter members of the 1925 American Academy of Speech Correction were already tied to public or private school programs, seven of them as administrators. The hotel meeting in New York is a different essay, in a different pack. The residue here is simpler: the school job is older than the association that later certified it.

## What later people kept

The itinerant model. The survey of a building. The habit of grouping “stammerers” in one hour and “lispers” in another. What they discarded, or should have: the correction of “foreign pronunciation” as if a home language were a defect; the assumption that a ten-teacher hire could cover a city; the idea that a principal’s side class is a program. What they still argue about: who was first. Local pride will always find an earlier classroom. Duchan’s range — 1895 to 1921 for first clinicians in most major cities — is the honest bracket. A single American birthplace is a later convenience.

The 1975 law did not invent the speech teacher. It found her already in the building, already underpaid, already carrying a bag between closets, and wrote *speech pathology* into a parenthetical list called related services. The cities of 1910 would not have recognized the statute. They would have recognized the bag.
