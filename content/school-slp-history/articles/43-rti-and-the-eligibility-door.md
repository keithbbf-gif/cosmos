This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Response to Intervention, later folded into many states’ Multi-Tiered Systems of Support, is a general-education idea with a special-education legal hook. IDEA 2004 allowed states to find a specific learning disability by looking at how a child responded to scientific, research-based intervention, instead of requiring an IQ-achievement discrepancy. The 2006 regulations talked about research-based interventions in the process of helping students with learning difficulties or determining eligibility. Early intervening services let districts spend a slice of Part B money on children *not* yet identified.

School speech-language pathologists were pulled in as language people and as convenient interventionists. Some of that was a good use of a scarce professional in a tiered system. Some of it was a way to park a child in a “speech RTI group” until the graph looked like a delay the building could live with.

## The legal sentence that has to be repeated

Child find does not pause for a tier. A parent request for an evaluation does not have to wait until someone finishes a six-week cycle. Federal guidance has said this in more than one administration’s voice because buildings like cycles. ASHA’s implementation-guide language on workload says the same: RTI/MTSS may address mild communication needs and must not delay a student’s movement into special education when that movement is due.

This page will not design a tier. It will say the historical risk is delay dressed as care. Mills was about children left out. A modern version can be children left in a hallway labeled Tier 2.

## What the graph cannot do

It cannot hear. A child who is not responding to a language intervention may have a middle-ear problem, or a home language the intervention ignored, or an autism identification no one wanted to write, or a curriculum that is too hard for reasons that are not a disability. Nelson’s caution — classroom data is necessary and not sufficient — belongs here. So does essay 20: a dialect is not a failed tier.

It cannot replace an IEP for a child who needs specially designed instruction. A successful tier is a success. It is not a finding that the child was never disabled. A failed tier is a signal. It is not, by itself, an eligibility decision.

## Who RTI left out

Children whose parents did not know they could ask for an evaluation while the graph ran. Children whose “mild” stutter or voice problem never got a tier because the building only graphed reading. Children whose AAC need is not a tiered reading intervention. Adolescents, again: many MTSS systems are elementary machines.

Workload (essay 18) is where RTI becomes a staffing trick. If every “mild” referral is a tier the SLP must run, the evaluation calendar dies. If the SLP is forbidden to run tiers, the building invents a waiting room staffed by someone with less training and calls it prevention. Both tricks have been used. Neither is in the 2004 text as a mandate.

The residue is a door with a window in it. You can see the child. You can also watch the child wait. 2004 built the window. Child find is still the hinge. A school SLP who only does tiers and never does evaluations is staffing a 2004 experiment as if it had repealed 1975. It did not. The parent who asks for an evaluation in October is not being impatient. She is reading the older statute, the one that still governs the door.
