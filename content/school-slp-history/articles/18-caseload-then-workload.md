This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 1993, ASHA published “Guidelines for Caseload Size and Speech-Language Service Delivery in the Schools.” The number people remember is 40: a recommended maximum caseload, regardless of service-delivery model. The 2000 roles-and-responsibilities guideline still cited that 40, and cited an ASHA 1995 schools survey in which about 80 percent of a school SLP’s time went to direct intervention and diagnostics and 20 percent to meetings, paper, training, and travel.

Forty is a clean number. Administrators can print it. Unions can bargain toward it. Families can hear it as a promise that their child will not be the forty-first. It is also a fiction about the job, and by 2002 the association said so.

## The 2002 turn

“A Workload Analysis Approach for Establishing Speech-Language Caseload Standards in the Schools,” approved by ASHA’s Legislative Council in July 2002, is a position statement, not a guideline with a cap. Co-chairs Frank Cirrin and Ann Bird; committee members including Larry Biehl, Sally Disney, Ellen Estomin, Judy Rudebusch, Trici Schraeder; Kathleen Whitmire as ex officio. The position: total workload activities must be taken into account to set caseload standards. Caseload is the students you serve. Workload is everything you do for them and for the statute: evaluations, IEPs, consultation, travel, Medicaid notes, child-find screens, the meeting after the meeting.

Cirrin and colleagues restated the argument in *Seminars in Speech and Language* in 2003. ASHA’s current Practice Portal page on caseload and workload still points at the 2002 statement and now adds Endrew F. (2017): progress, not administrative convenience, is the point of the arithmetic.

## Why forty failed as a civic tool

Because a child who needs an AAC system, three teachers consulted, and a bus from a separate school is not the same unit as a child who meets twice a week for a single sound. Because IDEA after 1997 is a meeting-heavy statute. Because Medicaid in the schools (essay 21) added a billing clock. Because the itinerant’s windshield is not a name on a list. A cap that ignores those hours produces the old compromise: everyone gets twenty minutes, twice a week, whether the IEP needed that or the schedule did.

States and districts still print numbers. Some statutes and contracts still say 50 or 55 or 60. ASHA’s 2002 document does not bind a legislature. It binds, at most, the association’s mouth. School SLPs use it as an advocacy vocabulary: stop asking me only how many children; ask me how many hours the children require.

## What this essay will not do

It will not tell you the correct caseload for a building. It will not offer a worksheet. It will not say 40 was “gold-standard.” It was a 1993 guideline number that later policy retired as the *method*, while many workplaces kept it as folklore.

Jean Blosser’s textbooks had been telling administrators to look at program design, not only headcount, before 2002. The position statement is the association catching up to the closet. States that still print a number are not automatically wrong. A number can be a floor against a 90-child list. The 2002 statement’s fight is with a number that pretends to be the whole arithmetic. Forty children who all need AAC trials and three teachers consulted are not the 1993 forty.

The residue is a word — *workload* — that a principal may still hear as a complaint. It is a description of the 1975 Act’s true cost. Ford, who worried about paperwork, would have recognized the 20 percent. He would not have known it was an underestimate. Endrew F. makes the underestimate more expensive: progress appropriate to the child is hard to claim on a schedule designed for administrative convenience. ASHA’s portal now prints those two citations on the same page. That pairing is the 2017 residue of a 2002 memo.
