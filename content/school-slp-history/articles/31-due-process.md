This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

The 1975 Act promised parents a way to contest a district’s identification, evaluation, placement, or provision of FAPE. The later name is a due-process hearing: an administrative proceeding, evidence, a hearing officer, an appeal path into court. PARC and Mills had already required notice and a hearing before a child was moved or excluded. Congress federalized the furniture.

This essay will not tell a family how to file, what to say, or how to win. That would be a script, and the guardrails forbid it. The historical object is the right, and two Supreme Court captions that changed who carries the file.

## Schaffer v. Weast (2005)

*Schaffer v. Weast*, 546 U.S. 49 (2005), asked who bears the burden of persuasion at the hearing when the statute is silent. Justice O’Connor, for the Court, put it on the party seeking relief — usually the parents. The opinion is a procedural allocation, not a judgment about a particular child’s speech minutes. School districts heard a victory. Parent attorneys heard a reminder to build a record. Speech-language pathologists heard, if they were paying attention, that their notes and test tables would be exhibits.

## Winkelman v. Parma (2007)

*Winkelman v. Parma City School District*, 550 U.S. 516 (2007), held that parents may pursue IDEA claims in court pro se, because they have their own rights under the Act, not only rights as next friends of the child. The caption is a door for families who cannot hire counsel. It is not a recommendation. Hearings are technical. A magazine that pretends otherwise is selling hope. A magazine that omits Winkelman is selling the idea that only lawyers own the statute.

## Mediation and resolution

1997 added mediation. 2004 added a resolution session after a parent complaint. Both are attempts to keep some fights out of the hearing room. Both can also be delay dressed as process. The historical fact is the extra rooms. The moral fact is that a tired parent can be mediated into exhaustion. Stay-put (essay 29) is supposed to hold the child while the rooms are used.

## Who due process leaves out

Families who cannot miss work. Families whose first language is not the hearing’s language, even with an interpreter of uneven skill. Families who reasonably fear retaliation in a small town. Children whose parents cannot organize a file. The right is real and unevenly usable. That is not a reason to sneer at it. It is a reason child find and IEP meetings are supposed to work well enough that a hearing is rare.

Attorneys’ fees — available to prevailing parents under later statutory language, with limits the Court has also policed — changed the market for hearings without making them cheap. Districts buy insurance and counsel. Parents buy time. A speech-language pathologist’s calendar is often the exhibit both sides want: who canceled, who implemented, who wrote the progress note that said “continued.”

School speech-language pathologists are sometimes witnesses. They are rarely parties. The residue is a way of writing — dates, data, who was at the meeting — that exists because a stranger with a gavel might one day ask. Van Riper’s textbook did not imagine that stranger. The 1975 Act did. Ford’s paperwork complaint and a parent’s hearing right are the same pile of paper seen from two chairs. Schaffer tells you who must pick the paper up first. Winkelman tells you the parent may carry it without a bar card. Neither case writes the speech goal.
