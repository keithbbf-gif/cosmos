This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In March 2020, American public schools closed for a virus. The speech-language pathologist’s route — six buildings, a bag, a sign-out sheet — ended at a kitchen table and a district laptop, if the district had a laptop. The Department of Education issued COVID-era guidance on FAPE, IEPs, and the duty to serve children with disabilities even when the building’s method changed. The guidance moved as the pandemic moved. This essay will not reprint those memos as current advice; they were emergency documents. It will say what they revealed: FAPE had always been a service, not a room, and almost no one had practiced that sentence at scale.

## Telepractice as policy, not as a gadget

ASHA already had telepractice guidance for clinical work. States already had patchwork licensure rules. Schools had barely used either. Then a million IEPs needed a new “location.” Some children got a real adapted service. Some got a worksheet and a hope. Some got nothing until a parent yelled. Compensatory services — the later argument about what was owed for the months of nothing — are a legal residue still being scheduled in some districts at this pack’s date. This page will not compute a particular child’s hours. It will say the 1975 Act did not contain a pandemic clause, and the 2004 Act did not either, and the field improvised under letters from Washington and from state chiefs.

Licensure showed its seams. An SLP certified in one state and living in another, serving a child who had moved to a grandparent, walked into the compact-and-exception maze. The Audiology & Speech-Language Pathology Interstate Compact was already in motion; the pandemic made its purpose obvious. Essay 41 is the three tickets. 2020 was the year the tickets did not match the map.

## Who the screen left out

Children without broadband. Children whose AAC system lived at school. Children who cannot look at a face on a laptop and also look at a symbol board. Deaf children whose interpreter was a small rectangle. English learners whose parent was told to “just translate.” Children whose disability includes not being able to do the school day from a bedroom. The equity story of pandemic school speech work is not a success narrative. It is a list of who could log on.

It is also a list of who was asked to become the therapist: parents. A home visitor in Part C already knew that risk. A school itinerant who had never asked a parent to run a session suddenly had no other hands in the room. That is not a protocol we will write. It is a labor fact. Some families could. Some were working the “essential” shift.

## What came back

The building reopened in pieces. Masks changed speechreading. Distancing changed group size. Quarantine turned the route into a lottery. Then, in many places, the caseload that had been frozen added the children who were found late, the language that had not been practiced, the stutter that had worsened in isolation, the teen who had disappeared. Workload, not caseload, was the accurate noun, and almost no district hired as if that were true.

Telepractice remained as an option in some state plans and union contracts. Used well, it is a way to reach a homebound child. Used as a staffing trick, it is a closet without a building. The statute still wants LRE and an IEP that names location. A permanent grid of Brady-Bunch faces is not automatically LRE.

The residue is a profession that now knows it can work through a screen and should not have to pretend the screen is the same as the room. March 2020 did not invent school speech work. It took attendance on every weakness the previous essays named: the itinerant model, the thin staffing, the billing clock, the children already on the wrong side of a door.
