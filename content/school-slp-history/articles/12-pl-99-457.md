This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Public Law 99-457, the Education of the Handicapped Act Amendments of 1986, is the year the federal bargain grew downward. The Department of Education’s IDEA history page says the 1986 reauthorization addressed early intervention and required states to provide services to families of children born with disabilities from birth — services that had not, as a national mandate, been available until age three. The same page describes a national concern in the 1980s for young children and their families, and a preschool system already half-built by EHA for ages 3–21 “consistent with state law.”

Two rooms, two later names. The infant-toddler program became Part H, then Part C: a lead agency that is sometimes education and sometimes health, an Individualized Family Service Plan, services in “natural environments.” The preschool program stayed with education, ages three through five, and grew into the Part B preschool child count. School speech-language pathologists met both rooms, often as the same person with two sets of forms.

## Why a school series cares about babies

Because the children arrive. A toddler program that names communication on the IFSP becomes a preschooler with an IEP becomes a kindergartner in the closet. The 1910 itinerant did not have a birth-to-three caseload. The 1990 itinerant did, or sat next to someone who did. Transition at age three — the handoff from Part C to Part B — is one of the most failed seams in the statute. This page will not write a handoff checklist. It will say the seam is where families get lost and where speech files start over as if the child had just been invented.

The 1968 Handicapped Children’s Early Education Assistance Act and the Head Start amendments of 1972 were the rehearsal. 99-457 was the mandate with money and timelines. States phased in. Some already had preschool speech programs because city correctionists had never limited themselves to first grade. Gifford’s San Francisco work began with primary and grammar grades and added high school in 1917; preschool as a right is a different legal object.

## Family, not only child

Part C’s IFSP is a family plan. That was a philosophical fight with the school IEP’s child-centered page. Early-intervention people will tell you the family is the client. School people will tell you the child is enrolled. Both sentences can be true and still produce a meeting where no one knows who talks first. Speech-language pathology, which already lived in kitchens and in closets, was used to both rooms. The paperwork was not.

## What 1986 did not do

It did not settle who pays. Health insurance, Medicaid, education — the infant program is a braid. It did not create a national curriculum for late talking. It did not make “wait and see” illegal. It did not staff rural counties. The Department’s later Part C counts (299,048 infants and toddlers in 2005–06; 409,315 in 2018–19) are growth from a small base, not saturation.

A school SLP who says “we don’t do birth to three” is describing a local org chart, not a professional border. ASHA’s scope does not end at kindergarten. The statute’s preschool mandate is why a three-year-old can have an IEP with speech as special education before anyone has assigned a reading level. The residue is a younger chair in a room that was built for eight-year-olds who lisped.
