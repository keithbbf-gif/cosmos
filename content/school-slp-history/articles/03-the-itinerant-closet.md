This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Chicago’s 1910 teachers did not get a wing. They got a route. Duchan notes the itinerant approach in that first hire: ten people, a city, buildings that already had desks for everyone else. Detroit opened “centers,” which sounds more permanent until you watch the children walk to them for a half day. Gifford’s San Francisco plan, as the 1926 California superintendent’s report later described it, chose five schools as centers. Pupils assembled half a day a week. On the other days a teacher who had gone with them ran a twenty-minute drill. That is not a clinic. It is a circuit.

The closet came later as a joke and then as a fact. A storage room. A stage wing. A nurse’s office on Tuesdays. A table in a hallway when the storage room was full of chairs for the winter concert. Anyone who has done the job can furnish the room without a photograph. This pack will not invent the furniture. It will say that the itinerant stop is the oldest American design for school speech work, and that later statutes never quite replaced it with a wing.

## Why districts liked the bag

It was cheap. One specialist could touch six buildings. The regular teacher kept the child for reading and recess. The specialist did not need a full classroom’s worth of desks. Supervisors could report a citywide number — so many “defects,” so many “corrected” — without opening a new school. Gifford’s first-year San Francisco survey found 1,486 children with “speech defects.” The number is in the pioneer literature. The staff was Gifford and one assistant. Arithmetic like that invents the circuit whether you want it or not.

It also matched the theory of the day. If the trouble was a mouth habit, you did not need the child’s whole morning. You needed a short, repeated visit. The later language-in-the-curriculum argument (Ehren, Nelson, the 2010 ASHA roles statement) is a fight with that arithmetic. A twenty-minute stop can mark a sound. It cannot sit inside a science lecture. The closet was designed for the first problem. The statutes of the 1990s started naming the second.

## What the closet hid

It hid travel time. ASHA’s later workload papers are, in one register, an attempt to make the minutes between buildings visible. It hid the children who were not allowed in the building at all — essay 04. It hid the fact that a “center” could be a basement. It hid race and language. A circuit that corrects “foreign pronunciation,” as San Francisco’s 1917 high-school add-on did, is a circuit with a theory of whose English counts.

It also hid collaboration, which is a polite word for the thing the closet made optional. A teacher who never sees the specialist except as a person who removes four children at 10:15 does not share a curriculum. The 1991 ASHA model for collaborative service delivery, and the 1997 IDEA’s general-education teacher on the IEP team, are paperwork attempts to drag the closet back toward the classroom. Some districts did. Some reprinted the memo and kept the folding table.

## Pull-out, push-in, consult

Those are later names for where the table sits. Pull-out: the child leaves. Push-in: the specialist enters. Consult: the specialist talks to the teacher and may not take the child at all. None of them is a protocol on this page. All of them are answers to the same old district question: how many buildings can one salary cover? Jean Blosser’s school-program textbooks spent decades walking administrators through the question without pretending the closet had vanished.

The 2002 ASHA workload position statement is the association finally saying, in public, that a list of names is not a job description. The closet did not write that statement. The closet caused it.

A new school can still open with a dedicated speech room, a window, and a door that closes. The itinerant will still be assigned the three buildings that did not get the window. The bag is older than the IEP. It will outlive a few more statutes.
