This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

The U.S. Department of Education’s public IDEA history page is blunt about the years before Public Law 94-142. In 1970, American schools educated about one in five children with disabilities. Many states had statutes that excluded children who were deaf, blind, “emotionally disturbed,” or labeled with what federal law later renamed intellectual disability. The same page says nearly 1.8 million children were out of public school before the Act was implemented. Those are agency numbers, not a magazine’s. They are the census the speech teacher did not take, because the children were not on her list.

## What a speech program looked like beside an exclusion statute

A city could hire itinerant correctionists and still bar a child who used a wheelchair, a child who did not speak, a child whose file said “unable to profit.” The speech room was not a civil-rights office. It was a service for the children the board had already decided to enroll. Pauline Camp’s 1917 paper and Gifford’s 1925 paper are full of counts. They are not full of the children in the state school on the hill.

Families who wanted a desk used other doors: parent associations, church basements, the occasional private tutor, the residential institution that promised “training.” The Department of Education’s history page says many of those institutions offered food, clothing, and shelter, and not much that a later statute would call education. Parents were often not in the placement conversation. That sentence is easy to skim. It is the whole plot of the next essay’s consent decrees.

## Federal half-steps

Washington did not arrive in 1975 from nowhere. The Training of Professional Personnel Act of 1959, captioned films, the Teachers of the Deaf Act of 1961, the 1963 construction and training expansions, ESEA in 1965, the State Schools Act, the 1968 Handicapped Children’s Early Education Assistance Act, Head Start amendments in 1972 — the IDEA history page lists them as the 1950s–1970s “initial federal response.” They trained people and funded model rooms. They did not, by themselves, give a child a right to walk into the neighborhood school.

Brown v. Board of Education (1954) is not a special-education case. It is the equal-protection weather the later disability cases breathed. PARC and Mills lawyers knew that. So did the people who wrote 94-142’s findings: more than a million children excluded entirely; more than half of all children with disabilities in the country with only limited access. Those findings are in the statute. Ford signed the statute and then complained, two days later, that it promised more than the government would pay.

## Who is missing from the speech-correction photograph

The photograph, when a later archive has one, shows a small group at a table. It does not show the child who was sent home as “uneducable.” It does not show the Black child counted twice — once by Jim Crow, once by a disability label. It does not show the child whose home language was treated as a defect (essay 20). A history of school speech work that stops at “we have always been here” without the locked door is a staff memoir, not a civic record.

Rosa’s Law (2010) later replaced “mental retardation” in federal statute with “intellectual disability.” This essay uses the older public phrases when it is quoting a 1970 state code or a 1971 caption, then leaves them there. The house noun is the later one. The exclusion was not a noun problem.

The speech teacher of 1968 could be excellent, exhausted, and beside the point for the child who had no desk. The 1975 Act did not make her excellent. It made the missing desk a federal problem. Whether the closet then grew to fit the new children is the rest of this series.
