This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 2010 the American Speech-Language-Hearing Association issued “Roles and Responsibilities of Speech-Language Pathologists in Schools,” a professional-issues statement that replaced and absorbed earlier school-roles documents, including the long 2000 guideline. The 2000 guideline is the one that still quoted the caseload of 40 and the 80/20 time split. The 2010 statement is the one later university syllabi assign. It is not a statute. It is the association’s official description of the job after 1997, 2002, 2004, and NCLB had already rearranged the building.

## What the statement tried to name

A school SLP as a person who works on the language of the curriculum, not only on a sound in a closet. Collaboration and consultation as core work. Prevention and RTI-shaped support as possible roles, with a warning — in ASHA’s later portal language — that those supports must not delay a needed evaluation. Workload as the honest noun. Cultural and linguistic competence as a responsibility, not a poster. A range of service-delivery models. A relationship to literacy. A relationship to federal law.

Readers who want a protocol will be disappointed. The document is a seating chart and a claim. The claim is that the 1910 itinerant’s job has grown to the size of the 2004 statute, and that a district which still schedules only direct minutes is staffing a previous century.

## What it could not do

It could not make a principal hire. It could not bind a state caseload statute. It could not settle the fight between push-in as a religion and pull-out as a right. It could not make “roles and responsibilities” into FAPE. Hearing officers read IDEA, not ASHA, though ASHA sometimes appears as persuasive professional literature.

The 2000 guideline had already listed the disability categories a school SLP might serve beyond SLI — intellectual disability, emotional disturbance, multiple disabilities, sensory impairments, autism, TBI, and the rest of the IDEA list. 2010 is that list plus a louder curriculum. Ehren and Nelson are in the weather even when they are not in the footnote a student remembers.

## Who the statement left exposed

Clinicians whose buildings never printed it. Assistants whose roles are a separate, thinner document. Families who hear “ASHA says” as if ASHA were the district. Children whose needs are on the statement’s list and not on the master schedule.

University training programs assigned the 2010 statement because it is shorter than the 2000 guideline and more current than Van Riper’s school chapters. Students memorize “roles” and then take a job that is still a bag and a sign-out sheet. The gap between the PDF and the master schedule is the lived history of the 2010s.

The residue is a PDF that can be attached to a bargaining proposal. That is not nothing. The 1993 number (40) was easier to attach. The 2010 statement is harder and more accurate. A profession that wanted to be more than a correctionist needed a job description that did not fit on a sticky note. It got one. The closet is still there, now with a longer list of things that are supposed to happen inside it. Endrew F., seven years later, added a progress adjective the statement did not yet have. The seating chart stayed. The ambition requirement got a caption. A later editor can update the PDF. The closet will still need the hours the PDF assumes.
