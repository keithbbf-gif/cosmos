This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Gifford’s San Francisco high-school add-on in 1917 included “the correction of foreign pronunciation.” The phrase is in the 1926 superintendent’s report Duchan quotes. It is a perfect specimen of the old job: a home language heard as a defect to be drilled out. A century later, ASHA’s public pages say to assess a child in a way that does not confuse difference with disorder, and IDEA’s evaluation rules require that tests not be racially or culturally discriminatory and that they be administered in the child’s language unless it is clearly not feasible. The rule is newer than the habit.

## Three public landmarks, none of them a speech protocol

*Diana v. State Board of Education* (N.D. Cal. 1970), a consent decree, concerned Spanish-speaking Mexican American children placed in classes for the “educable mentally retarded” on the basis of English IQ tests. The order required testing in the child’s native language or with nonverbal measures. It is a special-education case about language, which is why school SLPs still owe it a citation.

*Lau v. Nichols*, 414 U.S. 563 (1974), is a 504-and-equal-protection story about Chinese-speaking students in San Francisco who received no English-language help. The Court said treating them exactly like English speakers was not equal treatment. ESL and bilingual programs are the residue. So is the standing warning that a speech evaluation conducted only in English, on a child who is learning English, is a *Lau* problem wearing a lab coat.

*Larry P. v. Riles*, 495 F. Supp. 926 (N.D. Cal. 1979), limited the use of standardized IQ tests for placing Black children in California’s classes for the “educable mentally retarded.” The opinion is about racial bias in a number. Speech-language eligibility that leans on a single standardized score, normed on someone else’s English, is in the same weather even when the label is SLI instead of EMR.

## The 2016 regulation

The Department of Education’s 2016 “Equity in IDEA” rules require a standard methodology for significant disproportionality by race and ethnicity in identification, placement, and discipline, and they require remedies — including comprehensive coordinated early intervening services — when a district is identified. The Department’s history page is frank: over-identification of certain students, more restrictive placements, lower academic expectations. Under-identification is the twin injury, less often in the headlines: children who needed a service and got a dialect lecture instead.

None of this is a kitchen-table test. A parent cannot run a disproportionality formula at dinner. A clinician can refuse the 1917 sentence. That refusal is professional ethics and, in many states, regulation. It is not a treatment plan.

## Who was left out of the “we don’t do accent reduction” era

Children who needed language help *and* were treated as accent cases. Deaf children funneled through oral-only school speech programs that ignored sign. Black children whose African American English was marked as error on an articulation test. Indigenous children whose boarding-school history is a language-suppression history the speech profession was not innocent of. A magazine series that mentions “multiculturalism” as a 1990s ASHA poster has not read its own early city reports.

The residue is a two-part sentence school people still get wrong in both directions. Difference is not disorder. Disorder can exist *in* a bilingual child, *in* a dialect speaker, and pretending otherwise is a second way to close a door. The evaluation is supposed to hold both. This page will not tell you how to hold both in an afternoon. The statutes and the cases say you must try, and that a single English score is a poor try.
