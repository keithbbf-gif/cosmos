This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Amy Rowley was a deaf student at Furnace Woods School in the Hendrick Hudson Central School District, Peekskill, New York. The Supreme Court, in *Board of Education v. Rowley*, 458 U.S. 176 (1982), recites the facts the lower courts had: minimal residual hearing, excellent lipreading, parents Clifford and Nancy Rowley, who are also deaf. Before kindergarten, the family and the school talked. Administrators took a sign-language course. A teletype went into the principal’s office. Kindergarten was a trial. An FM hearing aid followed. She completed the year and moved on.

The IEP for first grade kept the FM system and added other supports the district favored. It did not include a full-time sign-language interpreter. A two-week interpreter experiment in kindergarten had ended. An independent examiner later said she was achieving educationally, academically, and socially without that assistance. The New York Commissioner agreed. The Rowleys went to the Southern District of New York.

## What the district court saw

Judge Vincent Broderick found a remarkably well-adjusted child, at ease with classmates, in rapport with teachers, performing better than the average child and advancing easily — and also understanding considerably less of the classroom than she would if she were not deaf. The gap between achievement and potential, he thought, meant FAPE was missing. The Second Circuit affirmed. The Supreme Court, 6–3, Justice Rehnquist, reversed.

The Act, Rehnquist wrote, did not require a state to maximize a child’s potential. It required procedures and a program reasonably calculated to provide educational benefit. For a child in a regular classroom, passing marks and grade advancement were strong evidence. Amy had those. She already had personalized instruction and related services, including speech therapy. The interpreter was not, on those facts, a statutory command.

Justice White dissented: the Act pointed toward an education commensurate with children without disabilities. Justice Blackmun concurred separately on a more equal-opportunity reading that still did not require the interpreter on this record.

## Why a speech series keeps her caption

Because the opinion names speech therapy as part of what she was already receiving, and then says that package can be FAPE without the additional support the parents wanted. Because every later IEP argument about “enough” walks through Peekskill. Because “some educational benefit” became, in careless mouths, “any benefit,” which is not what the opinion’s better readers found — and which Endrew F. had to say out loud in 2017.

This profile will not invent Amy’s inner life. It will not say what she should have been given. The Court said what the 1975 Act required on these facts. Deaf communities and interpreters have been arguing with that sentence for forty years. The argument is the residue. So is a school speech file that learned it could be listed among “related services already provided” and still be on the winning side of a case about a different support.

Furnace Woods is an ordinary school name. That is the point. FAPE was first defined at the top of the judiciary in a neighborhood building, about a child who was succeeding on the district’s report card. The success was real. The access debate did not end.
