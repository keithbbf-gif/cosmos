This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Before ASHA’s 2002 workload position statement had a number, school supervisors had a textbook. Jean L. Blosser’s *School Programs in Speech-Language Pathology: Organization and Service Delivery* — earlier editions with Elizabeth A. Neidecker under a slightly different title — is the book principals were handed when they inherited a related-service staff and did not know what the staff was for. Later editions (Plural Publishing, with Jennifer Means on recent ones) kept the same claim: this is a *program*. It has a design, a budget, a continuum of service delivery, a relationship to the curriculum, and a workload that is not identical with a caseload.

Neidecker and Blosser’s 1993 edition is already in the bibliography of papers that describe the late-century school job. The date matters. 1993 is also ASHA’s caseload-40 guideline year. Blosser was writing the longer object while the association was still printing a cap.

## What the book is not

It is not a treatment manual. It is not an IEP goal bank. It is not a protocol this magazine will excerpt as homework. It is administrative literacy for a profession that spent fifty years pretending administration was someone else’s problem. The closet existed because no one designed a room. Blosser’s genre is the design.

## What later people kept

The vocabulary of service-delivery options as *choices with costs*, not moral identities. The insistence that collaboration and consultation are work, not personality. The later ASHA workload turn looks, in hindsight, like the association adopting a textbook’s common sense. What they discarded, or should have: any edition used as a club (“the book says you must push in”). What they still argue about: whether program design can survive a district that only funds headcount.

Birth year: not independently confirmed at pack date. Living public author and ASHA contributor. No private biography.

## Residue

A generation of school SLPs who can say “workload” without sounding as if they are complaining about children. The children were never the complaint. The list was. Blosser’s pages are where the list became a program on paper. The 1910 itinerant did not have those pages. She had a superintendent who wanted a number. The textbook is a long refusal to let the number be the job.
