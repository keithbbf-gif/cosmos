This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

When the 1975 Act landed on school speech programs, someone at ASHA had to translate it into workshops, Q&As, and angry phone calls from supervisors who had just been told their lists were now legal instruments. Stan Dublinske’s public title for years was Director of the School Services Program at the American Speech-Language-Hearing Association, Rockville. The letterhead is the portrait this pack is allowed.

In 1978 he published, with W. C. Healey, *PL 94-142: Questions and Answers for the Speech-Language Pathologist and Audiologist*. In 1981 he finished an Ed.D. dissertation at the University of Southern California: *Impact of the Education for All Handicapped Children Act (PL 94-142) on Speech-Language Pathology and Audiology Programs in Public Schools*. The title is the whole career in one line. He went back to a university to measure the statute he was already explaining.

## The OSERS letter

The Office of Special Education and Rehabilitative Services sent him a policy interpretation, later circulated by ASHA, on the phrase “adversely affects educational performance.” Districts were requiring academic assessments that showed classroom failure before they would serve a child as speech impaired. OSERS said that reading was unreasonably restrictive and inconsistent with the Act: a communication impairment can be the educational performance that is affected. Dublinske’s later public summaries (including a 2002 ASHA schools piece) said the interpretation had not changed. Districts kept inventing the academic-failure gate anyway.

Kathleen Whitmire and Dublinske’s 2003 *Seminars in Speech and Language* paper, “Provision of Speech-Language Services in the Schools: Working with the Law,” is the next generation of the same translation: eligibility as an educational-planning process, not a clinic severity score.

## What later people kept

A profession that can quote a federal letter when a principal says “he’s getting As.” A habit of Q&A as a genre. What they discarded, or should have: the idea that a Rockville memo settles a local fight without a file. What they still argue about: how wide “educational performance” is. The letter is wide. Some state eligibility manuals are narrow. This profile will not reconcile them for a particular child.
