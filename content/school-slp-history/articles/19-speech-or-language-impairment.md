This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Federal special-education law has always needed a list. In 1975 the list said “handicapped children” and named impairments. Later regulations said “speech or language impairment”: a communication disorder — such as stuttering, impaired articulation, a language impairment, or a voice impairment — that adversely affects a child’s educational performance. The examples are in the Code of Federal Regulations (34 C.F.R. § 300.8(c)(11) in the modern numbering). They are examples, not a closed set, and they are not a protocol for how to test a child.

The category is huge. The Twentieth Annual Report to Congress, as quoted in ASHA’s 2000 school-roles guideline, put speech or language impairments at 20.2 percent of children served, second only to specific learning disabilities at 51.2 percent. Later annual reports move the decimals. The civic fact stays: a large share of the American special-education census is, on paper, a speech-language file.

## Special education, or only related

States may treat speech-language pathology as special education when it is the specially designed instruction. That is how a child with no other IEP services still counts. It is also how a district can, if it wishes, pretend the category is a waiting room for “real” disabilities. The OSERS letter to Stan Dublinske refused one version of that pretend: you may not require academic failure as the price of entry. “Adversely affects educational performance” includes the performance of speaking, listening, reading, writing, and participating — not only the report-card average. Dublinske’s later public summaries said the interpretation had not changed between 1980 and 2002. Districts kept asking.

## What the category hid

It hid children whose communication disability was filed under autism, hearing impairment, intellectual disability, or “other health.” The speech-language pathologist still worked with them; they were not on her “SLI” list. It hid children whose dialect or home language was treated as an impairment (essay 20). It hid children who stuttered and were told they were fine because they were bright. It hid voice. A hoarse child is in the federal example list and still easy to miss if the building only funds articulation groups.

It also hid the opposite error: children placed in the category because a screening was convenient and a full evaluation of language and hearing was not. Child find without a differential is just a net.

## Dismissal, the unwritten category

Leaving the count is a legal event. It requires data, a team, and notice. Informal “we’ll just fade the minutes” is a 1910 habit the 1975 Act made risky. This page will not write a dismissal rubric. It will say that a category this large is always under pressure to shrink — from administrators counting FTE, from NCLB-era subgroup anxiety, from clinicians who know some children were never impaired. Shrink it wrongly and you have Mills in miniature: a child without a service and without a process.

Voice and fluency sit in the federal example list and still lose to the articulation group when a schedule is tight. A hoarse child and a child who blocks are not luxuries. They are in the regulation’s own parentheses. A category this large will always be tempted to serve only the easy third.

The residue is a noun on a child-count table that a family may never use at home. They say “speech.” The state says “SLI.” ASHA says “speech-language.” The statute’s “adversely affects” is the fight in the middle. It is a legal phrase, not a clinical severity scale. Treating it as a scale is how a district invents a cutoff the Secretary of Education already rejected on letterhead. Dublinske’s file cabinet is full of that invention. The letterhead is still the reply.
