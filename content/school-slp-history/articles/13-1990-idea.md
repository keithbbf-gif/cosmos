This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 1990 the Education for All Handicapped Children Act became the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act. The Statutes at Large citation is Public Law 101-476, 104 Stat. 1103. The Department of Education’s public history page has, at this pack’s verification date, printed “Public Law 110-476” for that rename. That is a page error. 110-476 is not the 1990 Act. Uncertainty is a fact; so is the *Statutes at Large*.

The name change is not cosmetics. *Handicapped children* was the 1975 noun. *Individuals with disabilities* is person-first committee English, and it is also a statement that the law is about people who keep being people after the bus ride. School speech files changed their headers more slowly than Congress changed the title.

## Two new categories

The 1990 reauthorization added autism and traumatic brain injury as disability categories. Children in both groups had been served under other labels, or not served, or served in programs that did not have to say the word. Adding the categories did not create the children. It created a child-count line and a permission for districts to hire and train toward those lines.

Speech-language pathologists already knew both groups in clinics and, unevenly, in schools. The school residue was a caseload that could no longer pretend it was mostly lisps and “hard of talking.” Autism, in 1990s school practice, pulled in social communication, alternative communication, and arguments about whose job the behavior plan was. TBI pulled in a child who had been fine in September. Neither sentence is a protocol. Both are why the 1910 circuit’s categories — stammerers in class 1, lispers in class 2 — look like a different planet.

## Transition as a required page

The Department’s history page says Congress mandated, as part of the IEP, an individual transition plan to help the student move to post-secondary life. Transition had been encouraged in the 1983 amendments (Pub. L. 98-199). 1990 is when many school people remember it becoming unavoidable. Essay 33 stays with the page. Here the point is 1990’s appetite: the law wanted an exit, not only an entrance.

A high-school speech-language pathologist who writes only articulation goals for a seventeen-year-old is, after 1990, easier to describe as out of date. The statute does not prescribe a particular goal. It prescribes a horizon.

## ADA the same year

The Americans with Disabilities Act is 1990 too. It is a different statute, a different enforcement world, a wider set of public accommodations. School lawyers learned to say IDEA and ADA in the same breath and then to keep the files apart. 504 was already there. The 1990s building had three disability laws on the same bulletin board. The speech closet did not get three extra hours.

Person-first English in a statute title does not clean a closet. Teachers still said “the handicapped kids” in 1991; parents still said “speech.” The useful change was the two new lines and the exit page, not the adjective.

The 1990–91 child count on the Department’s page is 4,710,000 from birth through 21. The 1976–77 count had been 3,694,000 aged 3–21. Growth is real. So is the renaming of children who would have been counted under older nouns. A history that treats the rise as pure prevalence, not as a legal invitation to find and name, is doing epidemiology with a statute. School speech-language pathologists felt the invitation as new referrals that did not look like Gifford’s class 1 and class 2. They also felt it as a high-school schedule that now had to imagine a job, not only a sound.
