This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

President George W. Bush signed the No Child Left Behind Act, Public Law 107-110, on 8 January 2002. It reauthorized the Elementary and Secondary Education Act. It is not a special-education statute. It still rearranged the speech closet, because the closet sits in a building that can be labeled failing.

Adequate yearly progress. Subgroups, including students with disabilities. Highly qualified teachers. Scientifically based reading. Public report cards. The nouns are familiar now because they were shouted for a decade. A child whose IEP already named language as the disability suddenly sat inside a subgroup whose test scores could move a principal’s career. That is not a clinical observation. It is an organizational one.

## What the speech office was asked to become

A reading office, sometimes. Title I reading coaches and speech-language pathologists found themselves in the same professional-development slide deck. ASHA had been saying for years that spoken language and written language share a system. NCLB gave administrators a reason to hear it — and a reason to treat any speech goal that did not look like a reading intervention as a luxury. Fluency, voice, swallowing, AAC for a child who would never take the grade-level test: those files did not disappear, but they had to compete with the subgroup.

“Scientifically based” became a weapon and a filter. Some of the filter was overdue; school speech work has always had room for habit without evidence. Some of the weapon was a misunderstanding of what a randomized trial can say about a child who uses a communication board. This page will not adjudicate a particular method. It will say that a testing law changed the *burden of explanation* in the IEP meeting.

## Disproportionality under a scoreboard

When a subgroup is small, a few scores swing the label. Districts felt that. Some responded by finding fewer children, or by exiting them faster, or by writing IEPs that promised grade-level proficiency a team did not believe. The 2004 IDEA alignment (essay 15) and the 2016 Equity in IDEA rules (essay 20) are later attempts to keep identification honest under that pressure. NCLB did not invent racial disproportionality. It added a spreadsheet that made some districts more cautious and some more cynical.

## Highly qualified, and the three tickets

NCLB’s teacher rules collided with related-service credentials. A speech-language pathologist might hold a state license and an ASHA card and still be asked whether she was “highly qualified” in the Title I sense. Essay 41 is the three tickets. The 2002–2015 years are when secretaries of education and state certification offices had to explain, repeatedly, that a related-service provider is not a classroom teacher of record in the same way.

ESSA (2015) will walk some of this back — less AYP theater, more state discretion. The speech room will not forget the decade in which every untested minute felt like a theft from the reading block. That feeling is historical weather. It is not a protocol for how to spend Tuesday.

The residue is a profession more fluent in literacy talk and more suspicious of it. Both are earned. A 1910 correctionist was hired to make recitation possible. An NCLB-era SLP was asked to make a proficiency percentage possible. The child in the chair was asked to be both a talker and a data point. Some of the literacy talk was overdue; spoken language and print are one system. Some of it was a building using the speech office as unpaid Title I staff. Distinguishing those two is the adult job. The statute did not distinguish them. It only published the percentage.
