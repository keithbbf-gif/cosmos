This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

The Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act, 20 U.S.C. § 1232g, is a 1974 spending-clause statute. Schools that take certain federal funds must give parents access to their child’s education records, a chance to seek amendment, and a limit on disclosure without consent. There are exceptions — school officials with legitimate educational interest, health and safety emergencies, some directory information, transfers to another school — but the default is not the faculty lounge.

A speech-language pathologist’s file on a named student is an education record if the district maintains it. Test protocols, IEP drafts, session notes tied to a child, emails that sit in the official record — the later Department of Education guidance on what counts is picky, and this page will not pretend to be that guidance. The historical turn is simpler: the closet’s notebook became a record someone else had a right to see, and a record the clinician could not read aloud at the grocery store.

## Two privacy cultures

Health care has HIPAA. Schools have FERPA. A district-employed SLP is usually in FERPA-land for the school file. A contracted clinician who also bills Medicaid may be standing in both weathers. The 1980s–2010s billing turn (essay 21) made that overlap ordinary and still poorly taught. Mixing the two statutes in a staff meeting is how a building invents a rule that is neither.

IDEA has its own notice and consent rules for evaluation and placement. FERPA is the records statute. They cooperate. They are not synonyms. A parent’s right to inspect the speech file is a FERPA-shaped right even when the IEP meeting is an IDEA-shaped meeting.

## Who was left out of the old notebook

Parents who were never shown the scores. Children who became adults and could not get their own files. Teachers who needed to know a goal and were told it was “confidential” as a power move, not as a statute. FERPA’s “legitimate educational interest” is supposed to let the science teacher see the accommodation and not let the whole building see the family story. Buildings get both sides wrong.

Directory information — the exception that lets a yearbook exist — is not a speech diagnosis. Treating a disability label as directory information is a category error with a human cost.

## What this essay will not do

It will not tell you how to respond to a subpoena. It will not list every exception. It will not say whether a particular text message is a record. Those are office-of-general-counsel questions. The magazine fact is that 1974, a year before 94-142, already told the future speech-language pathologist that the child is not a story you own.

Email made the record problem worse. A joke in a thread about a named child can become an education record if the district maintains it. So can a voice-mail saved to the official system. The 1974 Congress was thinking about cumulative folders, not inboxes. The statute still applies. Buildings that train staff on phishing and not on FERPA have chosen a different fear.

The residue is a locked cabinet that is also an open cabinet for one family. The 1910 itinerant could keep impressions in a private hand. The post-1974 itinerant writes as if the parent will one day sit with the page. Some of the resulting prose is careful. Some of it is empty, because people write for auditors and not for the child. FERPA required access. It could not require courage. It could require a log of who looked. The log is the unromantic civic object. The hallway story is the thing the log was invented to embarrass.
