This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

FAPE, in the 1975 Act, was four parts: special education and related services at public expense, under public supervision, without charge; meeting state standards; including the state’s preschool, elementary, or secondary education; and in conformity with the IEP. That is a definition, not a temperature. The temperature arrived when parents and districts asked *how much* education is “appropriate.”

## Rowley’s first reading

*Board of Education v. Rowley*, 458 U.S. 176 (1982), is the first Supreme Court look. Amy Rowley, deaf, Furnace Woods School, Hendrick Hudson district, Peekskill, New York. FM system, tutoring, speech therapy. Her parents wanted a sign-language interpreter. She was passing and advancing. The district court said she understood less than she would if she were not deaf, and that the gap from her potential meant FAPE was missing. The Supreme Court, Justice Rehnquist writing, reversed. The Act did not require a state to maximize potential. It required access to specialized instruction and related services individually designed to provide educational benefit, and — for a child in the regular classroom — a program reasonably calculated to allow passing marks and advancement. Essay 23 is her caption at human scale.

School speech offices heard Rowley as permission and as a warning. Permission: you are not required to produce the theoretical best mouth. Warning: “some benefit” became, in some circuits, a very small some.

## The small some

Lower courts spent thirty years arguing how much benefit is enough when the child is not Amy Rowley — not fully integrated, not advancing on grade-level tests. The Tenth Circuit’s phrase, later made famous, was an educational benefit “merely more than de minimis.” That is lawyer Latin for barely more than nothing. Districts could, under that weather, defend an IEP that repeated itself year after year if anyone could point to a sliver of movement.

Speech goals that never changed except the date are part of that weather. This page will not diagnose a particular file. It will say that a profession that lives on repeated minutes is vulnerable to the charge that repetition was the plan.

## Endrew F.’s second reading

*Endrew F. v. Douglas County School District RE-1*, 580 U.S. 386 (2017), is Chief Justice Roberts for a unanimous Court. The IEP must be reasonably calculated to enable the child to make progress appropriate in light of the child’s circumstances. For a child fully integrated in the regular classroom, Rowley’s passing-and-advancing picture still helps. For a child who is not, the program must be appropriately ambitious — not a formula, Roberts said, but “markedly more demanding” than the Tenth Circuit’s de minimis test. Aiming that low would be “tantamount to sitting idly” until dropout age. Essay 27 is the caption.

ASHA’s public caseload-and-workload page now cites Endrew F. next to the 2002 workload statement. That is the association noticing that “progress” is no longer a shrug. It is still not a protocol for how to write a goal.

## What FAPE never was

It was never a medical guarantee. It was never a Cadillac, in the old courtroom metaphor, and after 2017 it was not supposed to be a broken bicycle either. It was never the same as Section 504’s accessibility duty, though the two can overlap. It was never a reason to skip the procedural requirements; Rowley itself is a two-part inquiry, procedure and substance.

The moving target is the word *appropriate*. Congress left it. Courts filled it twice at the top and a hundred times below. The speech-language pathologist sits in the fill. The honest residue is that FAPE is a legal standard you can quote and a child’s year you cannot outsource to a quote.
