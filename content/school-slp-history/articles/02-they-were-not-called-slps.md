This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

A 1910 Chicago hire was a *speech teacher*. A 1925 San Francisco paper said *speech correction*. A mid-century district advertisement said *speech therapist*. A 1975 statute said *speech pathology*. A 1978 association added *language* to its own name. The person in the closet collected four nouns before the federal file settled on one credential. Calling those early workers “SLPs” is a courtesy to the present. It is a lie about their pay stub.

## Correction

*Correction* is the word that did the most work and the most damage. It promised a before and after. It sat comfortably next to “speech defects” and “nervous speech disorders.” Gifford’s California bureau, when it arrived in the late 1920s, was the Bureau of Correction of Speech Defects and Disorders. The noun told the public what the job was for: to make the child sound like the room. It did not tell the public that some mouths would not, and that some differences were not defects.

School people used the word because they were teachers. The classroom already corrected spelling. Speech was another paper to mark. University clinics, a decade later, preferred *pathology* because they wanted a science and a hospital corridor. Both nouns are historical. Neither is a method on this page.

## Therapist, clinician, specialist

By the 1940s and 1950s, city reports slide toward *therapist* and *clinician*. The first sounds medical. The second sounds like a person who keeps notes. Districts used them interchangeably, the way they used *itinerant* and *special teacher*. ASHA’s own names moved in parallel: American Speech Correction Association (1934), American Speech and Hearing Association (1947), American Speech-Language-Hearing Association (1978). The school job lagged the letterhead. Many state education codes still said *speech correctionist* into the 1970s.

Britain took a different public noun — *speech therapy*, then *speech and language therapy* — and a different employer, the National Health Service. That is a sibling continent, not this building. American school workers were, for a long time, employees of a board of education with a teaching certificate and a bag.

## Pathologist, then the hyphen

*Speech-language pathologist* is a 1970s settlement. *Language* was the admission that children who did not put words together were not a side clinic for the lisp. *Pathologist* was the admission that the job wanted a health-profession prestige the classroom title had never quite delivered. The hyphen is a committee. School districts adopted it slowly. Some still print *speech therapist* on the door because parents know the word and the board does not want to reprint the directory.

The 1975 Act said “speech pathology and audiology” inside the definition of related services. It did not say SLP. It did not say CCC-SLP. Those are association and state-license later. Essay 41 keeps the three tickets — teaching certificate, state license, ASHA card — in separate pockets. Mixing them in 1910 is how a history page starts sounding like a recruitment brochure.

## What the names hid

They hid who was left out. A *correctionist* was not assigned to the child who had been excluded from the building. A *therapist* in 1960 might never see a child who used a board to point. A *pathologist* in 1980 might still be scheduled as if every student were a twenty-minute articulation group. The noun upgraded. The schedule often did not.

They also hid gender and class. The early city staffs were overwhelmingly women teachers. The early academy officers were often university men. Duchan’s charter count — fifteen women and ten men in 1925, nine of the twenty-five already in schools — is the census, not a morality play. The school noun stayed feminine in the public mind long after the doctorate arrived.

A modern job posting that says “School SLP” is accurate about today. It is a poor caption for a 1916 photograph. Use the word on the letterhead of the year you are in. Then, if you must, translate once.
