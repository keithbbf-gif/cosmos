This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

The 1975 definition of related services is a list that begins with a bus. Transportation, then a parenthetical menagerie: speech pathology, audiology, psychology, PT, OT, recreation, medical and counseling services (medical only for diagnosis and evaluation), and early identification. The list is not a menu a parent orders from. It is a category: developmental, corrective, or supportive help *required to assist a handicapped child to benefit from special education*.

That last clause is the hinge. A related service is not owed because it would be nice. It is owed because, without it, the specially designed instruction cannot do its job. School speech work has lived inside that hinge ever since — sometimes as the related service next to a learning-disability IEP, sometimes as the specially designed instruction itself.

## Why speech is in the list twice, in practice

If the child’s disability is a speech or language impairment and the specially designed instruction *is* the speech work, many states count that work as special education. If the child’s disability is something else and speech work helps the child use the instruction, it is related. The same clinician can be both things before lunch. Administrators who want a single box on a form have been fighting that grammar since 1978.

The OSERS letter to Dublinske on “adversely affects educational performance” is the federal reminder that a child can need the speech work without failing spelling. The letter is not a protocol. It is a refusal of a local invention: no academics, no service.

## The cases that strained the list were not speech cases

*Irving Independent School District v. Tatro*, 468 U.S. 883 (1984), asked whether clean intermittent catheterization was a related service or an excluded medical service. Amber Tatro, a child with spina bifida, needed CIC during the day. The Court drew a bright line: if a physician must perform it, medical and (except for diagnosis/evaluation) out; if a nurse or layperson can, it may be related. Essay 24 is her caption.

*Cedar Rapids Community School District v. Garret F.*, 526 U.S. 66 (1999), asked whether continuous nursing for a ventilator-dependent student was still on the related-services side of Tatro’s line. The Court said yes. Cost was not the test. Essay 26 is his caption.

Speech pathology was already named; it did not need those cases to exist. It needed them to understand the neighborhood: related services are the supports that make the school day possible, and the Court will not let a district rename an expensive support as “medical” just to drop it. A later IEP team that treats speech minutes as a budget valve is playing a game Tatro and Garret F. already lost for other nouns.

## What the list left out, then added

Augmentative communication is not in the 1975 parentheses. Interpreting, orientation and mobility, school health — the list grew in later regulations and statutes as practice named the gaps. Audiology was there from the start, which is why the school SLP and the educational audiologist have been arguing about whose equipment it is for fifty years.

Recreation is in the 1975 list and almost never in the public memory of the speech closet. That is a useful embarrassment. Congress was thinking about a child’s whole access, not about one profession’s identity.

A related service is a legal relationship, not a personality type. The person who provides it may be a district employee, a contractor, or a private clinician the district pays. Essay 45 is the contract next door. The parentheses do not say who signs the paycheck. They say why the paycheck exists.
