# Coil Springs: Junigl, Pratt, Hancock, Crofton

## Learning objective

No one on the brand leaves training saying “Samuel Pratt invented the sofa spring in 1828, full stop.” They can tell a **cluster of 1820s patents and a shop manual**, plus an eighteenth-century novelty (the chamber horse).

## Thirty-second floor version

“Coil springs show up in odd furniture in the 1700s — Sheraton describes making them for a chamber horse. They become practical for ordinary seats in the 1820s: Junigl in Vienna in 1822, American elastic sofas around the same years, Pratt’s British patents in 1826 and 1828. Crofton’s 1834 book assumes the trade already knows spring stuffing. We did not invent this. We inherited a nineteenth-century tool.”

## History

**Before the 1820s.** Helical wire exists. The chamber horse (exercise chair) used stacked boards and coils; Sheraton’s *Drawing-Book* describes winding iron wire on a block so turns nest under load. That is gentry novelty, not parlor default. Carriage coils (Tredwell and others in the eighteenth century) are a parallel metal story. Do not collapse them into “sofas had springs under George III.”

**1822, Vienna.** Georg Junigl, a bourgeois upholsterer, receives an Austrian *Privilegium* for a wire spring used with upholstery filling (announced 24 April 1822). This is one of the earliest **seat-specific** legal protections.

**1822–25, United States.** Sherman Blair of New Haven patents an elastic spring sofa (1822) and cushion (1823). William Hancock advertises elastic spring-seat chairs and sofas and, by 1825, rocking chairs. American shops are not waiting politely for Pratt.

**1826 and 1828, London.** Samuel Pratt of New Bond Street, a camp-equipage / trunk / upholstery supplier (BIFMO), takes GB 5418 (1826) for beds, bedsteads, couches, seats — including naval furniture meant to fight seasickness — and GB 5668 (1828) for elastic beds and cushions, with hourglass coils illustrated. Pratt’s 1826 specification even refers to a communication from a foreigner abroad plus his own discoveries. He is a **major patentee and supplier**, not a lone inventor of the coil.

**1834, Crofton.** *The London Upholsterer’s Companion; The Art of Spring Stuffing* describes nine circular steel coils, 7 inches high and 3½ inches across, of No. 8 charcoal wire, for easy chairs; lighter gauges for sofas, beds, coaches. He says coils have been in the trade “a number of years.” He tells the shop to sew them to the web, tie them so they help each other and stay perpendicular, then fasten the twine to the frame. That is the ancestor of hand-tied springing (UH-25).

**Why adoption takes time.** Early coils wander, collapse, and cost too much. Steel quality and tying method have to catch up. Mid-century chairs (V&A c. 1845) show the matured sandwich: coils + hair. Brands should not date “the sprung sofa” to a single birthday party in 1828.

## What a brand may honestly say

- “Sprung seating becomes a normal luxury in the 1800s after a cluster of 1820s patents and a shop manual in 1834.”
- “We use coil springs in the 19th-century sense: they sit on a web and work as a group.”
- “Pratt’s patents were partly about ship furniture. Springs were never only a parlor toy.”

## What a brand must not say

- “Invented by us in 1828.”
- “Springs since Chippendale.”
- “Pratt’s hourglass coil is a registered feature of this SKU” without a patent.
