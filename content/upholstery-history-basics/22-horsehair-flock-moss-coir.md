# Horsehair, Flock, Moss, Coir, and Tow

## Learning objective

Product and sales can list historic **staple fills** and can say what each was *for* (resilience, cheap bulk, regional supply) without claiming horsehair is magically cooler or forever.

## Thirty-second floor version

“Before foam, shops stuffed with what they could stitch and buy: horsehair for springy, lasting pads; flock and tow for cheaper bulk; moss in the American South; coconut fiber in later trade. Hair is still the quality layer above springs on traditional work. It is not automatically ‘healthier’ than a specified foam.”

## History

**Horsehair** (and hoghair mixes) is the prestige vegetable-animal fill of European traditional seats. Long, slightly oily, resilient, it can be **stitched into shape** — the rolled edge, the firm nose of a seat, the smooth back. V&A object texts return to it on both 1730s and 1840s chairs. Above coils, a stitched hair layer stops the sitter from feeling each spring as a lump. Conservators love it because it holds a shape for centuries if dry. Sitter complaints: it can feel hard; it can smell when damp; it is animal.

**Flock** is shredded wool or cloth, the historic bedding fill in Houston’s title. It bulked mattresses and cheaper seats. Quality varies from decent wool flock to filthy floor sweepings — which is why guild search rights (UH-06) existed.

**Tow** (hackled flax or hemp waste) and **straw** are the cheap, crispy fills. They flatten. They are the reason reformers talked about lumpy, unsanitary seats.

**Spanish moss** (*Tillandsia usneoides*), ginned and processed, was a major **American Southern** fill and mattress fiber in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. It is a regional supply story (Gulf South, furniture and bedding plants), not a European guild material. A brand in that geography can tell it. A brand that prints “old world moss” on a polyester wrap cannot.

**Coir** (coconut husk fiber) enters as a cheap, springy, tropical commodity — often as rubberized coir pads in later twentieth-century seats. It is a **global trade fill**, not a Tudor secret.

**Cotton batting and cotton felt** become the American factory wrap: a smooth layer under the cover. They are not structural.

Health history: dusty organic fills are why late Victorians and moderns reached for **foam and rubber**. Allergy and mold are real. So is the opposite marketing lie — that all foam is poison and all hair is pure. Training should send people to **spec sheets**, not to folklore.

Animal ethics: horsehair today is a by-product market. If a brand uses it, answer the question. If a brand uses a “hair effect” polyester, do not call it horsehair.

## What a brand may honestly say

- “Traditional seats use stitched hair to hold a shape above the springs.”
- “This SKU uses [hair / cotton wrap / foam / coir pad]. Here is why we chose it.”
- “Spanish moss was a historic Southern fill. We do / do not use it.”

## What a brand must not say

- “100% horsehair” when the core is foam with a hair wrap.
- “Organic fill, so no fire rules apply” (see UH-40).
- “Museum-grade hair” without a conservator.
