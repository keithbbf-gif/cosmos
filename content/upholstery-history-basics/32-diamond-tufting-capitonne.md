# Diamond Tufting and Capitonné

## Learning objective

Sewers and sales can describe **diamond tufting / capitonné** as a nineteenth-century way to quilt a thick pad with buttons on the diagonal, and can distinguish it from channeling and from fake foam stamps.

## Thirty-second floor version

“Diamond tufting — capitonné in French shops — pulls the cover down to the deck on a diagonal grid and dresses the pulls with buttons. It showed up when seats got thick enough to need it. A stamped foam ‘tuft’ with no tie is a picture of this work, not the work.”

## History

Once springs and extra hair deepen the sit (1820s–1840s onward), the cover wants to **bag**. Through-ties at a regular interval fix the cover to the stuffing and the deck. Arranged on the diamond, the folds make the familiar quilted lattice. French *capitonné* is the shop name American luxury catalogs still borrow.

Labor: each button is a measurement, a stab, a pull, a knot, a cover-up. Leather capitonné is harder than cloth because leather does not ease. That is why a leather diamond sofa costs. History here is **hours**.

The Independent’s interview with Fleming Howland is useful as a **negative source**: even a specialist Chesterfield maker calls the origin of the *name* a mystery and treats deep-buttoning as an evolving description, with eighteenth-century buttoning more often in velvet than in leather. Do not use that interview as a date for “first diamond sofa, 1750.” Use it to humble origin stories (UH-36).

Twentieth-century modernism sometimes **keeps** tufting as a graphic (Barcelona cushions; Knoll leather buttons) and sometimes **refuses** it as Victorian dust. Both are literate choices.

Fake tufting: CNC-cut foam with a sewn or glued button, or a printed diamond, with no through-tie. Legal to sell. Illegal to describe as “hand-tufted capitonné.” Training photos should include a wrong example.

**A factory measurement.** On a true diamond, you can lift a button and see twine or a linen stay. Spacing is a drawing: too wide and the cover bags; too tight and the leather starves and cracks. Historic deep-buttoning on hair sits *on* a sprung deck; the pull goes down to webbing or to a hessian layer. Pulling a button into a 2-inch foam slab and calling it “Victorian capitonné” is a category error — the Victorians were restraining a thick, springy sandwich (UH-17), not decorating a foam brick.

**Velvet vs leather.** Eighteenth-century buttoning, as Fleming notes, is easier to document in velvet. Leather diamonds become the club cliché later, when hides and springs are both ordinary. A velvet diamond on a 2026 sofa can be the more historically cautious “18th-century-looking” choice than a distressed brown hide — if anyone on the brand is still trying to look 1750. Most should not try.

## What a brand may honestly say

- “This is diamond tufted: each button is a through-tie on a diagonal grid.”
- “Capitonné is the French shop word for that work.”
- “This SKU uses a tufted foam insert. It is a look, not a tied pad.”

## What a brand must not say

- “Hand-tufted” on a stamp.
- “Eighteenth-century leather capitonné, documented to the 4th Earl…” as fact.
- “Buttons reduce lingering guests” as history.
