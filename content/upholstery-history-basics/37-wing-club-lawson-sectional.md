# Wing Chair, Club Chair, Lawson, Sectional

## Learning objective

Sales can date four living-room forms to **different centuries** and can stop calling every rolled arm a Lawson and every deep chair a club.

## Thirty-second floor version

“Wings are an English easy-chair idea from the late 1600s — they blocked drafts by the fire. Club chairs are low, deep, usually leather, from clubrooms around 1900. Lawson is a twentieth-century American sofa with low rolled arms. Sectionals are modular sofas for open rooms. Four forms, four jobs.”

## History

### Wing / easy chair

The English **easy chair** with lateral wings appears in the late seventeenth century and becomes a standard eighteenth-century invalid and fireside chair. The wings shelter the head from draft and from the bite of a coal fire. Upholstery is full, often with a loose cushion later. American “wingback” is the same family. A 2026 wing chair with a USB port is a silhouette revival. The historic job was **microclimate**, not “reading nook aesthetic.”

### Club chair

Not a Georgian type. The **club chair** is a low, deep, usually leather easy chair of gentlemen’s clubs, hotels, and smoking rooms in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. It is a cousin of the Chesterfield *sit* (deep, male-coded interiors) without requiring equal-height arms. The Eames lounge (1956) is explicitly a modern club-chair rethink (UH-20). If a brand’s “club chair” is a high-back wing, the word is sloppy.

### Lawson

In American retail, **Lawson** means a sofa with **low or mid rolled arms**, usually loose cushions, a relatively square, informal drawing. Origin stories that name a single store founder or a single 1910s patent are messy and often unsourced in lifestyle blogs. This curriculum’s rule: **use Lawson as a type name**, the way shops do, and do not print a founder myth until a primary is in the folder. If a brand has an archive that names a Lawson, use *that* paper.

### Sectional / modular

Pieced sofas for flexible plans are a **twentieth-century** answer to apartments, ranch houses, and conversation pits. They are not Ottoman divans (though a corner sectional can *behave* like a divan). Shipping and FOAM make sectionals a factory product. History for a brand: **logistics invented this form as much as style did**. Upholstered modules that cannot be re-covered at the zipper are a sustainability problem (UH-44).

### Related named sits (one line each)

- **Bridgewater** — low rolled arm, often with a dressmaker skirt; American traditional.
- **English roll arm** — higher, tighter roll; more “country house.”
- **Camelback** — UH-13.
- **Recamier / fainting couch** — UH-16; do not diagnose Victorian women.

## What a brand may honestly say

- “Wing chair: English easy chair with draft wings, late 17th–18th century type.”
- “Club chair: deep, low, club-interior sit, circa 1900 type.”
- “Lawson, in our line, means [exact arm height and cushion plan].”
- “Sectional: modular 20th-century living-room sofa.”

## What a brand must not say

- “Lawson, 1712.”
- “Club chair from Chippendale’s *Director*.”
- “Sectional, ancient Persian.”
