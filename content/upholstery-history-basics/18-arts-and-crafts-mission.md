# Arts and Crafts and Mission: The Cover Recedes

## Learning objective

A merchandiser can explain why an Arts and Crafts or Mission chair **shows its wood and uses leather or canvas as a plane**, and can stop calling that “no upholstery.”

## Thirty-second floor version

“Arts and Crafts and Mission chairs often put the frame back in charge. The seat may be leather, rush, or a simple stuffed pad. The point was to escape the Victorian stuffed box. It’s still upholstery — just thinner and more honest about the wood.”

## History

Late-nineteenth-century reformers (Ruskin, Morris, the English Arts and Crafts shops, the Vienna Secession’s related honesty-of-materials talk) attack the overstuffed parlor as dust, sham carving, and hidden construction. The chair that answers them has:

- **visible joinery** (through-tenons, keys, expressed posts)
- **flat leather or canvas** instead of buttoned velour
- **rush or leather sling seats** on some lines
- **a pad, not a mountain**

Gustav Stickley’s Craftsman furniture is the American teaching set: oak, iron hardware, leather seats that are a **plane of hide** over webbing or slats. “Mission” in the American trade is a broader, often cheaper, related look (and a disputed name). A brand should not print “Stickley” unless it is Stickley. “Mission oak with a leather seat” is the safe adjective.

Upholstery did not vanish. Morris interiors are famous for **textiles** — printed cottons, embroidered hangings, carpets. The sit may be simple while the *room* is richly cloth. That split is useful for brands that sell both case goods and COM: you can be “honest” in the chair and still sell meters of cloth.

The construction lesson: a leather seat on a Mission chair is closer to a **Cromwellian nailed plane** (UH-10) than to a Victorian easy chair. If the leather is a 1/4-inch foam-backed vinyl, say so. The style can survive a material update; the honesty claim cannot.

British and American variants (Voysey, Greene and Greene, Prairie school settles) each have a different relationship to the cushion. Greene and Greene can be luxurious and still express wood. Do not mash them into one “Craftsman sofa.”

**What Morris actually sold.** Morris & Co. is a textile and decoration firm as much as a chair shop. The “honest chair” in an Arts and Crafts room often sits on a **printed cotton or embroidered cloth** that is the expensive object. A brand that copies a square oak arm and then covers the room in a no-name poly floral has taken the cheaper half of the argument. If you want the Morris half, license or cite a documented print (UH-29) and keep the seat simple.

**Mission vs Craftsman in a price book.** In American retail, “Mission” became a catalog grade: oak color, slats, a leatherish seat, sometimes plywood. “Craftsman” is both a Stickley trademark neighborhood and a general adjective. Legal and historical hygiene: use **Mission-style** for the generic slat chair, **Craftsman** only when the lineage is real, **Stickley** only on Stickley. Customers use the words as synonyms. Training should not.

## What a brand may honestly say

- “Arts and Crafts seating pulled stuffing back so you could see how the chair was built.”
- “This leather seat is a plane of hide over [webbing/slats], in the Craftsman/Mission family.”
- “Morris’s argument was about honest making and better textiles, not about abolishing comfort.”

## What a brand must not say

- “Stickley designed this” on a non-Stickley frame.
- “No upholstery, just like 1905” on a foam-and-staple pad.
- “Mission” as a California mission founding myth for a Tennessee mill.
