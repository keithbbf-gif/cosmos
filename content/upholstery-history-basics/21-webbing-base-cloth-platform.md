# Webbing, Base Cloth, and the Platform

## Learning objective

A salesperson can kneel beside a sofa, point at the underside, and name **webbing, spring deck, rubber webbing, or a hard platform** — and know that “solid wood base” is not automatically better.

## Thirty-second floor version

“Every upholstered sit needs a platform. Traditionally that’s interlaced webbing — often jute — tacked to the rails, then a base cloth. Springs stitch to that web. Mid-century European chairs may use Pirelli rubber webbing instead. A plywood deck is a platform too. Ask us which one this SKU uses.”

## History

Before coils, the seat is a **woven trampoline of textile**. Eighteenth-century museum chairs show linen webbing and a linen or ticking base cloth, then hair. The webbing pattern (usually interwoven, sometimes with a closer front edge) is the first engineering decision. Too loose: the sit bottoms out. Too tight: the hair has no give and the tacks tear the rail.

When coils arrive, they are **sewn to the webbing** (V&A 1845: six springs stitched down, then compressed under a cover). Crofton (1834) already tells the shop to fasten springs firmly to the web and then tie them to each other. The web is not decoration. It is the ground.

Materials history:

- **Linen and hemp** in early fine work.
- **Jute** as the industrial webbing of the later nineteenth and twentieth centuries — cheap, strong, ugly, correct.
- **Steel webbing / metal grids** under some high-end or later factory decks.
- **Pirelli rubber webbing**, from Pirelli’s GRG (General Rubber Goods) division in Burton, created in **1953** to find an outlet for surplus tire rubber. Still associated with Ercol, Parker Knoll, G-Plan recover jobs. Later products include the **Sisiara** wired rubber-and-fabric mat (from 1974, per the successor-company history). “Pirelli webbing” in a shop today may be the authentic rubber-nylon strap or a generic elastic copy. Specify.

The **dust cover** (black cambric) hides the web from the customer and from pets. It is not structural. A ripped cambric is a maintenance issue, not a failed spring.

Hard platforms (plywood with foam) are a twentieth-century factory simplification. They can be excellent for tight seats and terrible for a sit that promised “hand-tied bounce.” The educational rule: **name the platform**. “Hardwood frame” on a hangtag does not tell you what you sit on.

Pirelli-type webbing fails by **stretch and heat-aging**. Coil-and-jute fails by **rot, tack rust, and broken ties**. Training a service department on failure modes is part of heritage: you cannot offer a lifetime sit without knowing how the platform dies.

## What a brand may honestly say

- “This seat is interlaced jute webbing, [with / without] coil springs.”
- “This mid-century chair uses rubber webbing in the Pirelli tradition (1950s onward).”
- “This tight seat is foam on a plywood deck. That’s a platform choice, not a missing soul.”

## What a brand must not say

- “Solid wood foundation” when the sit is webbing.
- “Lifetime webbing” without a climate and weight caveat.
- “Genuine Pirelli” on unbranded elastic.
