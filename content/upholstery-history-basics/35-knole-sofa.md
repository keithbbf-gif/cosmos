# The Knole Form

## Learning objective

Merchandisers can describe a **Knole sofa** as a high-backed, high-armed seat whose arms (or ends) hinge and tie with cords to finials — a **Stuart-court form associated with Knole House** — and can separate that from a Chesterfield.

## Thirty-second floor version

“A Knole has tall sides, often as high as the back, that can drop and are held with cords at the finials. The type is tied to Knole in Kent and to seventeenth-century court furniture that ended up there. It’s an ancestor people point to for the enclosed sofa. It is not a buttoned leather box.”

## History

Knole House, Kent, holds an extraordinary run of royal and aristocratic furniture. Popular histories (and the house’s own fame) present a high-sided, cushioned, cord-tied seat of about the mid-seventeenth century as the **type ancestor** of the enclosed sofa. Charles Sackville’s role as Lord Chamberlain / Master of the Great Wardrobe under William and Mary is the usual explanation for why Stuart court furniture from Windsor and Hampton Court sits at Knole: the office could remove ‘outdated’ royal furniture. That **Wardrobe path** is the documented kind of story brands should prefer — offices and inventories — over bedroom-intrigue folklore.

The form’s working parts:

- high back
- high ends, often hinged
- **cords and finials** (the adjustable, tie-able bit)
- deep cushions (squabs) on a base
- a more throne-like or settee-like sit than a sprung Victorian sofa

Later commercial “Knole sofas” (twentieth-century decorators; 1940s and after revivals) copy the **cords and ears** and usually add modern springing. Soho House and others have made the silhouette a lifestyle object. That is revival, and it is allowed if named.

Folklore to park:

- that the drop end exists so a woman’s farthingale can fit (possible as a *later* explanation; not a substitute for the object)
- that the sofa is a discreet lovers’ seat (decorator fiction)
- that every sofa descends from this one object (the *lectus*, the Ottoman room, and the French *sopha* also exist)

For brands: if you sell a Knole, **show the cord**. If the arms do not move, you are selling a high-arm settee. Do not use the house name as a trademark you do not own; use “Knole-type” or “in the Knole manner.”

## What a brand may honestly say

- “Knole-type: high ends tied with cords, after the seventeenth-century seats associated with Knole, Kent.”
- “Court furniture reached that house through Wardrobe office practice, not through our factory.”
- “Our version uses [modern suspension]. The historic sit was cushions on a base.”

## What a brand must not say

- “The original sofa, 1640, our license.”
- Invented Sackville bedroom stories.
- Confusing Knole with Chesterfield.
