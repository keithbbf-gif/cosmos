# The Upholsterer as Interior Contractor

## Learning objective

Product and sales teams stop picturing a historic upholsterer as a person with one hammer, and start picturing a **contractor who could furnish a room, a funeral, or a sale of goods**.

## Thirty-second floor version

“For centuries the upholsterer was the person you hired for the soft interior — beds, hangings, seat covers, sometimes the whole room. Cabinet-makers made the wood. The upholder might also value the estate. Today those jobs are split across factories and designers. We should not pretend one SKU is a full historic workshop.”

## History

By the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries in London and Paris, the person of textiles in a house was not a junior sewer. In France the *tapissier* (later *tapissier-décorateur*) coordinated hangings, beds, and seat covers; the *menuisier* made seat frames; the *ébéniste* made veneered case furniture. In London the upholder’s shop could invoice a duke for chairs, a chaise, a patent sofa seat, and camp furniture in the same decade (see BIFMO on Samuel Pratt: Kilruddery chairs 1813; Stoneleigh chaise 1833; Stafford House 1838).

The educational point for brands is **division of labor**:

| Historic role | Typical delivery | Modern fragment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Upholder / tapissier | Covers, stuffing, hangings, beds, sometimes whole rooms | Cut-and-sew; trim; sometimes merchandising |
| Menuisier / chairmaker | Seat frames, show wood | Frame mill |
| Ébéniste | Veneered case goods | Case-goods factory |
| Appraiser / auctioneer | Estates, second-hand | Liquidators; “design trade” sales |
| Undertaker | Funerals (cloth, coffins, procession) | A split profession |

When Chippendale, Hepplewhite, or Sheraton put *upholsterer* in a book title, they were speaking to this contractor class as much as to a gentleman buyer. The *Director* (1754) and the later Guides are pattern books for shops that had to price a room, not just a splat.

Firms like **Morel & Hughes** (Regency London) show the suite logic: in 1823 they supplied furniture and upholstery for Northumberland House, including cane-seated chairs with **squab** cushions built like small mattresses (V&A). The brand analog is a **to-the-trade showroom** that sells frame plus COM plus trim as one order — not a big-box cardboard box.

Undertaking sounds like a tangent. It is not. Cloth, lining, and display were shared skills. Houston’s work on the Company keeps this in view so that “heritage upholstery” is not sanitized into a lifestyle magazine. A brand does not need a funeral line. It does need to stop calling a 1990s merchandising calendar “the same business as a Georgian upholder.”

For product development, the contractor model is a warning against **orphan SKUs**. Historic invoices sell *sets*: chairs, stools, a sofa, a screen, sometimes the hangings that match. A modern brand that launches one “Chippendale chair” without a suite story is using a name and discarding the practice.

## What a brand may honestly say

- “Historically the upholsterer often contracted the soft interior of a room, working with chairmakers.”
- “We still sell seating as a specified package: frame, suspension, fill, cover, trim.”
- “Pattern books were written for shops that had to furnish rooms, not just carve a splat.”

## What a brand must not say

- “Our designer is a tapissier to the Crown.”
- “We are the original undertaker-upholsterers of [town]” without an archive.
- Implying that a COM program is identical to a 1823 ducal commission.
