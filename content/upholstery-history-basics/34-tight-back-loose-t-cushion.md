# Tight Back, Loose Cushion, T-Cushion

## Learning objective

Every salesperson can sketch a **tight back**, a **loose back**, and a **T-cushion**, and can explain the recover and sag implications.

## Thirty-second floor version

“Tight means the upholstery is attached to the frame — no zip-off pillows on that surface. Loose means removable cushions. A T-cushion is the seat pad that sticks out in a T to wrap the front of the arm. Loose is easier to fluff and recover. Tight shows the designer’s line and hides less sin.”

## History

Eighteenth-century chairs are mostly **tight panels** (or drop-in seats). Victorian easy chairs are often tight-backed mountains. The twentieth-century American factory, especially after foam, normalizes **loose seat and back cushions** because:

- foam cores can be replaced
- covers can be zippered (and, later, washed or COM-swapped)
- the sit can be sold as “plush” without restuffing a carcass

That is a **service history**, not a moral decline. A brand that only sells loose-cushion “traditional” is selling a twentieth-century convenience in an eighteenth-century silhouette. Say so when it helps the customer choose.

**T-cushion.** The pad’s front corners extend beyond the inside arm so the cushion reads as one plane in front of the arm stump. It is a twentieth-century American upholstery drawing-board standard more than a Georgian one. Pair it with rolled arms (Lawson, UH-37). A tight-seat Georgian revival should not grow accidental T-wings.

**Semi-attached backs** (backs that look loose but are tacked or zippered to stay) are a factory hybrid. Honest catalogs name them. They exist because loose backs slouch on the showroom floor.

**Knife-edge vs boxed.** Knife-edge is two panels, maybe a welt, no boxing strip — softer, cheaper, harder to keep square. Boxed is the mattress logic of the squab (UH-31). History favors boxed for “mattress-like” luxury seats; fashion favors knife-edge every few years.

English vs American vocabulary: *cushion* vs *pad* vs *pillow*; *tight seat* vs *fixed seat*. Align the brand glossary (UH-48) so a UK buyer and a Carolina mill are saying the same thing.

**Why loose won American factories.** After foam (UH-26) a cushion became a **replaceable SKU**. A plant can inventory three core densities and six COM covers. A tight-back Victorian revival cannot. That is why so many “18th-century” sofas on a 2026 floor have loose backs: not because Chippendale drew zippers, but because the plant is a 1960s–2000s machine. Teach that sentence. Customers who want the historic *line* should be offered a tight SKU, not a pillow mountain with ball-and-claw feet.

**Sag diagnostics.** Loose down backs pancake in a year if they are not baffled (UH-23). Tight backs show a “smile” at the front rail when the deck dies. T-cushions walk forward if the deck is slick and the T is undersized. Each failure points at a different historic layer — cover, fill, or platform — which is why the five-layer read (UH-42) belongs on the sales floor, not only in a museum.

## What a brand may honestly say

- “Tight back: the line you see is the frame’s line. Loose back: you can flip and recover the pillows.”
- “T-cushions wrap the arm. That’s a 20th-century American lounge convention.”
- “This collection is historically a tight sit; we offer a loose-cushion comfort option and we label it as such.”

## What a brand must not say

- “Authentic 1780 T-cushion.”
- “Loose cushions mean eight-way” (unrelated).
- “Never sags” on a knife-edge down back.
