# Women, Workshops, and Invisible Labor

## Learning objective

Leadership can name **women in the historical record** (Alice Hepplewhite, Lilly Reich, needlewomen, sewing rooms) and can stop writing heritage copy as a parade of surnames of men who “invented the sofa.”

## Thirty-second floor version

“A lot of the work that makes a sofa — sewing, tufting, needlework, first-up, the 1788 *Guide*’s publication, the Barcelona interiors — was done by women whose names catalogs drop. Our factory floor still runs on sewers. Heritage that only lists Chippendale is incomplete.”

## History

**Pattern books.** The Hepplewhite *Guide* is issued by **A. Hepplewhite & Co.** after George’s death. Alice is not a mascot; she is why the plates exist in the world. Teach her name in the same hour as George’s absence from documented bench work (UH-15).

**Modern interiors.** Lilly Reich’s role on the 1929 German Pavilion furniture is a documented corrective to the lone-genius hangtag (UH-19). Florence Knoll’s 1954 “fill-in pieces” are the corporate interior as a woman’s architectural job (UH-20). Ray Eames is not a footnote to Charles on the 670.

**Needlework.** Canvas-work and Berlin wool seats were often **designed in shops and stitched by women at home or in workshops**. That is skilled labor, sometimes unpaid in the family sense, sometimes waged. A brand that sells “hand-needlepointed by artisans” should say **where and for what wage**.

**Sewing rooms.** Cut-and-sew is the industrial descendant of the tapissier’s workroom. In American upholstery plants the sewing line is still heavily female in many shops; upholstery (the male-coded tack-and-spring job) is a different bid. Both are the product. Training tours that only stop at the spring deck are lying about the BOM.

**Guilds.** London livery history is male in its early freedom lists; that is a historical exclusion, not a natural law. The modern Upholders support training across genders. Do not print “brotherhood of upholsterers” as charm.

**Invisible because unpaid:** dressing down cushions, slipcover laundry, the country-house maid who changed the chintz (UH-38). A care card that assumes a servant is a historic document. Rewrite it.

This draft is not a complete labor history. It is a **minimum correction** so the other forty-seven files cannot be read as a men-only craft.

## What a brand may honestly say

- “The Hepplewhite *Guide* was published by A. Hepplewhite & Co.; Alice Hepplewhite is part of that fact.”
- “Barcelona: Mies van der Rohe and Lilly Reich.”
- “This cover was sewn in [plant], [shift size], [union if any].”

## What a brand must not say

- “Our girls in sewing” as a caption.
- Erasing Reich or Ray Eames to shorten a hangtag.
- “Hand-needlepointed” on a machine tapestry.
