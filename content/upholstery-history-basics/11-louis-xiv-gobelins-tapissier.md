# Louis XIV, the Gobelins, and the Tapissier

## Learning objective

A brand can use “Louis XIV” as a **court-style adjective** and still explain that the Gobelins was a royal manufactory of *meubles* (movable furnishings), not a High Point upholstery line.

## Thirty-second floor version

“Under Louis XIV, Colbert turned the Gobelins into the royal manufactory for the Crown’s movable furnishings — tapestries, cabinets, silver, the coordinated look of the court. Seat-upholsterers were tapissiers; tapestry weavers were liciers. ‘Louis XIV sofa’ on our ticket means a formal, rectangular, richly covered silhouette — not a chair from 1680.”

## History

In 1662 Colbert installed workshops at the Gobelins, on the Bièvre, where dyers had worked for generations. Letters patent of **1667** formalize the **Manufacture royale des meubles de la Couronne**. Charles Le Brun is the first director. The French word *meubles* means movables: everything that is not the building. Tapestries, carved and gilt seats, silver furniture, pietra dura cabinets, and diplomatic gifts come out of a coordinated machine of royal taste.

Vocabulary a catalog desk must not blur (French trade usage, as summarized by furniture historians):

- **Licier** — weaves tapestry or carpet.
- **Tapissier** — garnishes seats and beds with cloth; the upholsterer.
- **Menuisier** — solid-wood seat frames and carved chairs.
- **Ébéniste** — veneered case furniture (Cucci, Gole, and others at or around the Gobelins).

Le Brun’s job was unity. The king’s visit to the Gobelins, woven in the *Histoire du Roi* series, is propaganda: artisans displaying objects to the monarch. A brand that wants “atelier” language can teach this picture — and then admit that a modern COM mill is not a royal manufacture.

Savonnerie carpets and Gobelins tapestries are the **room-scale textiles** that seat upholstery had to live with. State chairs of the period are carved, gilt, and covered in tapestry, velvet, or brocade, often with fringes that cost as much as a lesser chair. The sit is still pre-spring: stitched stuffing on a webbed or boarded seat. Comfort is not the point; ensemble is the point.

After Louis XIV, the names **Régence, Louis XV, Louis XVI** become the European period ladder every American mill still uses. Train staff that these are **reign-style labels**. A “Louis XV” cabriole and a cartouche back can be honest as geometry. A “signed by the ébéniste to Louis XV” claim on an unsigned factory chair is fraud.

Gobelins still exists as a historic tapestry manufacture. That continuity is public. It does not extend to a private sofa brand.

## What a brand may honestly say

- “Louis XIV style means court formality: straight lines, rich cover, matched room textiles.”
- “At the Gobelins, weavers and seat-upholsterers were different trades under one royal roof.”
- “Our fringe and braid package is in the state-seat tradition of visible trim. It is made by [supplier].”

## What a brand must not say

- “Woven at the Gobelins” unless it was.
- “Colbert appointed our founder.”
- “Louis XIV invented the armchair.”
