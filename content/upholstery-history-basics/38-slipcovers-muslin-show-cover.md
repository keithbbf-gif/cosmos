# Slipcovers, Muslin First-Up, and Show Covers

## Learning objective

Product and sales can distinguish **show cover**, **loose cover / slipcover**, and **muslin first-up**, and can treat COM as a historic client-textile habit, not a startup feature.

## Thirty-second floor version

“The fabric you see is the show cover. A slipcover is a removable dress — country houses used them for summer and for protection. Muslin first-up is a white fitting so the shop and the client can approve the shape before the expensive cloth goes on. We’ve been changing clothes on chairs for a long time.”

## History

Museum chairs prove the concept. The Houghton gilt set’s velvet is **hooked on pegs**; silk scarves could fold over to save the back (UH-13). That is a **designed** loose cover, not a cheap afterthought.

Eighteenth- and nineteenth-century accounts and surviving suites show **case covers** (sometimes linen or leather) for travel and storage, and **seasonal slips**. Chintz in summer, wool or silk in winter, is a country-house cliché because it is true enough as a practice class. The slip is why printed cotton belongs on furniture (UH-29).

**Muslin first-up** (or white calico) is shop practice: stuff and sew in a cheap cloth, stand the piece in the showroom or the client’s room, then cut the show fabric. High-end COM (customer’s own material) still needs this when the cloth is $200 a yard. A brand that skips first-up on a sculptural sofa is gambling with a client’s textile.

Twentieth-century department-store slipcovers (elasticized, printed, “transform your sofa”) are a **different, democratic chapter**. They can look poor if the pattern isn’t matched. They also extend the life of a carcass (UH-44). Do not sneer in training; teach fit.

Zippers, velcro, and hidden snaps are modern hardware on an old idea. The historic fastener set is ties, hooks, pegs, and the client’s maid.

For brands: a “slipcovered collection” should specify **whether the slip is the only cover** (fashion sofa) or **a second dress** over a proper show cover (historic logic). Those are two products.

**Fit is history’s hard part.** A historic loose cover was cut from the object in the room, often with darts at the arm and ties under the frame. A 1990s universal “T-cushion sofa cover” is a different industry: it assumes a Lawson-ish box. When a brand sells a “slipcovered Knole-type,” the slip must honor the cords and finials or it will look like a bag on a throne. Train the sewing room on the form (UH-35) before promising machine-washable court furniture.

**COM risk.** Client cloth that is not upholstery-rated (drapery silk, a sari, a vintage quilt) is a historic *and* modern request. Shops used to refuse, or they lined and quilted. Write a refusal path: abrasion, seam slippage, lightfastness, fire (UH-40). Saying yes to every COM is not hospitality; it is an unlabeled liability.

## What a brand may honestly say

- “Show cover is the finish cloth. This model also has a washable slip.”
- “We first-up in muslin on COM orders over [threshold]. That’s old shop practice.”
- “Loose covers on gilt chairs exist in museum objects. Protection is historic, not a hack.”

## What a brand must not say

- “Machine-washable silk velvet.”
- “COM invented in 2018.”
- Selling a poorly cut universal slip as “bespoke loose cover.”
