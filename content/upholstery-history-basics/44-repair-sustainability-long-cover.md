# Repair, Sustainability, and the Long Cover

## Learning objective

Product can define **long cover** as a design goal: a carcass that can be re-covered, a fill that can be specified again, a cloth that can be removed — and can refuse “sustainable” as a sticker on a stapled orphan.

## Thirty-second floor version

“The greenest sofa is the one whose frame and deck can take a second cover. That is how historic seating already worked: slip seats, loose covers, restuffing. Foam slabs glued into odd shapes are hard to recycle. If we claim sustainability, we show the recover path.”

## History

Medieval cushions moved house (UH-05). Queen Anne pads dropped out (UH-12). Gilt chairs wore hooked velvet (UH-13). Country houses slipped chintz (UH-38). Victorian seats were stripped and restuffed as a trade. **Repair is the default history.** Disposable upholstery is the novelty.

What broke the default:

- **Staples and cardboard** instead of tacks and webbing you can read
- **Shaped polyurethane** that crumbles and is not a single resin stream
- **Wrapped frames** you cannot open without destroying
- **Fashion cycles** faster than a cover’s life
- **Offshore case goods** that left upholstery onshore — but still priced recover as a luxury

A brand that offers a **named recover program** (parts, zippers, documented decks) is aligning with the longer history. A brand that prints a leaf logo on a 3-year foam warrant is placating (UH-41).

Metrics that count as artifacts: years of frame warranty; whether cushions unzip; whether the deck is photographable; take-back; recycled content **with a percentage**; a partner shop (UH-39). “Sustainably sourced mahogany” needs a chain-of-custody, not a Georgian adjective (UH-13).

Latex vs PU vs hair vs down are **tradeoffs** (UH-22, UH-23, UH-26), not a purity ladder. Hair lasts and is animal. Down needs farms. PU is cheap and bulky in landfills. Acrylic outdoor cloth is plastic that lasts in the sun (UH-30). Teach the tradeoff table.

**A minimum repairable spec.** If the brand will print any green word, the engineering packet should answer four questions in writing: (1) Can a shop reach the deck without destroying the rail? (2) Are cushion cores a standard rectangle a foam house can recut? (3) Is the cover a sewn envelope with a zipper, or stapled into a grave? (4) Who will do the work in five years — a named program, or “good luck”? Historic seating already answered (1)–(3) with slip seats and loose covers. The fourth answer used to be “the upholder in the next street.” A brand that has killed the local recover trade and then claims circularity is performing.

**What “buy once” inherited.** A 1900 parlor sofa that is still in a family is usually on its third cover. That object is the sustainability case study. Photograph one in training. Count the staple generations. The lesson is not nostalgia; it is a BOM that expected a future sewer.

## What a brand may honestly say

- “This frame is designed to be re-covered. Here is the labor estimate.”
- “Cushions unzip. Cores are replaceable at [density].”
- “We do not call this sustainable. We call it repairable.”

## What a brand must not say

- “Eco foam” without chemistry.
- “Zero waste” on a cut-and-sew floor.
- “Heirloom” on a cardboard-rail promotional sofa.
