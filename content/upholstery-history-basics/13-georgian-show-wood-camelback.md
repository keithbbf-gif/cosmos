# Georgian Show Wood and the Camelback

## Learning objective

Merchandisers can define **show wood** (visible carved frame) versus **fully upholstered carcass**, and can describe a camelback as a **top-rail profile**, not a breed of comfort.

## Thirty-second floor version

“Georgian seating often lets you see the carved wood — legs, arms, a shaped top rail — with the stuffing in the seat and back panels. A camelback is the sofa whose top rail rises in a hump. That’s a silhouette. The sit inside can be horsehair or, on a new piece, whatever we specified.”

## History

“Georgian” in the trade covers the long British eighteenth century (George I–IV, with overlap into Regency). For upholstery education, two construction families matter:

1. **Show-wood seating.** The frame is the design. Cabriole or straight legs, carved knees, arm supports, a splat or a padded back inset. Upholstery is panels and a seat. The Houghton-related gilt chairs at the V&A (1730s, Kentian world) keep linen webbing, ticking, horsehair, and linen stuffing-covers. The green silk velvet is a **loose cover** hooked over metal pegs. Some chairs in the set show evidence of silk “scarves” — flaps that could fold over to protect the back when in use. That is a conservation-and-use story: the show cover was already understood as sacrificial.

2. **The stuffed sofa.** Longer seats with upholstered backs appear as settees and “sopha’s” in pattern books (UH-14). The **camelback** is the English sofa whose top rail is serpentine or humped in the center, often with rolled or outscrolled arms. It is a drawing-room object. Depth is still pre-spring or, later in the century, still stitched hair. A 2026 camelback with sinuous springs and foam is a **profile revival**. Honest: “camelback silhouette.” Dishonest: “identical to a 1750 drawing-room sofa.”

Palladian and Kentian interiors put gilt chairs against architectural walls. The upholstery had to read at the scale of a saloon. Fringe, braid, and matched stools are part of the architecture. A brand selling a single camelback into an open-plan loft should not claim “Palladian authenticity.” It can claim “a Georgian profile in a modern room.”

Mahogany replaces walnut as the international wood mid-century. That change is colonial and commercial (Caribbean and Central American timber). If a brand uses “Georgian mahogany” in copy, it should be ready for a sourcing question. Historical mahogany is not a blank luxury word.

American Georgian (colonial) seating follows English pattern books with local woods and local shops. Newport, Philadelphia, and Boston are not “late copies of London” in every detail; they are regional schools. A brand in the United States can say “American Georgian tradition” if it means that — not if the frame was carved last month in a style file named `georgian_04.obj`.

## What a brand may honestly say

- “Show wood means you can see the carved frame. Fully upholstered means the frame is buried.”
- “Camelback names the humped top rail of a Georgian-type sofa.”
- “Some 1730s gilt chairs used loose silk covers on hooks so the fabric could be protected or changed.”

## What a brand must not say

- “Hand-carved by William Kent” on an unsigned mill chair.
- “Georgian horsehair sit” when the core is polyurethane.
- “Palladian certified.”
