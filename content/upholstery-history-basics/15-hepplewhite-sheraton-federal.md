# Hepplewhite, Sheraton, and American Federal

## Learning objective

Trainees can tell Hepplewhite from Sheraton as **book-born style languages** (shield backs vs square-tapered lightness) and can place American Federal as a **reception of those books**, not as a third English shop.

## Thirty-second floor version

“Hepplewhite’s *Guide* came out in 1788, after George Hepplewhite’s death, under A. Hepplewhite & Co. — Alice is in the firm name. Sheraton’s *Drawing-Book* was issued in the early 1790s. American Federal chairs often follow those plates. ‘Hepplewhite sofa’ on our floor means a shield or an oval and lighter legs — not a signed London piece.”

## History

### Hepplewhite, 1788

*The Cabinet-Maker and Upholsterer’s Guide* is first published in London by I. and J. Taylor in 1788, with on the order of 125 engraved plates. A slightly revised 1789 follows; the “improved” third edition (1794) has 128 plates and drops many cabriole-legged patterns. George Hepplewhite is the name later attached to a style of shield-back and oval-back chairs, tapered legs, and a lighter sit than high Chippendale Rococo. Little or no documented furniture can be proven as George’s own bench work. Alice Hepplewhite and the firm published the book. Training should say **“the Hepplewhite *Guide*”** more often than “George invented the shield back.”

The Met notes written evidence of the *Guide* in Baltimore and Hartford by the end of the century, and surviving furniture in Salem, New York, and those towns. That is how a London plate becomes an American dining room.

### Sheraton, 1791–94

Thomas Sheraton (1751–1806) published the *Drawing-Book* in parts from 1791; the first complete edition is 1793 (with accompaniment plates into 1794; a later revision 1802). It is more didactic than the *Guide*: geometry, perspective, then furniture. Square backs, delicate inlay, and a very thin visual weight become the trade’s “Sheraton.” A Met copy associated with Thomas Seymour shows the book in Boston-world shops. Salem, New York, Philadelphia, Baltimore, and southern ports all produce furniture that repeats *Drawing-Book* ideas.

Sheraton’s own preface comments on Chippendale and on Hepplewhite’s 1788 *Guide*. The three books are a **conversation**. A wall of “the three greats” in a showroom is fine if the caption is “pattern books,” not “three men who personally upholstered America.”

### Federal

American Federal (roughly 1780s–1820s) is the patriotic, classical, post-Revolution reception of these light styles: eagles, urns, inlay, mahogany and satinwood, painted fancy chairs. Upholstery remains slip seats and stuffed panels, still mostly pre-spring in the domestic chair. The **lolling chair** (Martha Washington chair) is an American easy-chair type with open arms and a stuffed seat and back — a Federal answer to the English easy chair, not a recliner.

Duncan Phyfe in New York is the famous later Federal / Grecian shop. Use him as a **New York workshop** story, not as a synonym for all Federal.

## What a brand may honestly say

- “Hepplewhite style comes from a 1788 pattern book published by A. Hepplewhite & Co.”
- “Sheraton’s *Drawing-Book* (1790s) taught a lighter, square-backed language that American shops copied.”
- “Federal is the American chapter of that language.”

## What a brand must not say

- “George Hepplewhite upholstered this frame.”
- “Sheraton licensed our collection.”
- Collapsing Phyfe, Hepplewhite, and Sheraton into one hangtag adjective.
