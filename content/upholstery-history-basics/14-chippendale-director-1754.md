# Chippendale’s *Director* (1754) and Upholstered Suites

## Learning objective

Everyone who will write the word *Chippendale* on a ticket can state what the *Director* actually is: a **1754 pattern book** of designs in Gothic, Chinese, and modern (Rococo) taste — not a proof that Thomas Chippendale stuffed this SKU.

## Thirty-second floor version

“Thomas Chippendale published *The Gentleman and Cabinet-Maker’s Director* in 1754 so gentlemen could choose and cabinet-makers could build. It includes chairs, settees, and sofas. ‘Chippendale’ on a modern sofa usually means a carved leg or a splat type from that book’s world. It does not mean he signed our frame.”

## History

Thomas Chippendale (1718–1779), of St. Martin’s Lane, issued the *Director* in 1754: one hundred and sixty copper plates, “Gothic, Chinese and modern taste,” with dimensions and an explanation of the orders. The title page lists chairs, settees, *sopha’s*, and beds among the household furniture. The preface says the book is calculated to assist the gentleman in choice and the cabinet-maker in execution, and that if no single drawing answers, the plates still offer hints to construct a new one. That sentence is the ancestor of every “inspired by” collection — and Chippendale said it out loud.

A third edition (1762) expands the plate count (literature cites on the order of 200 plates in the full set). Dedications move with patronage (Northumberland; later royal hopes). This is a **commercial publication competing with other shops**, not a secret workshop diary.

Upholstery implications:

- The *Director* designs **frames and silhouettes**. The stuffing is assumed trade practice: webbed seats, hair, show covers specified by the client’s tapissier or upholder.
- French elbow chairs and sofas in the plates need **allowance for seat stuffing** in the dimensions. Product engineers who scale a plate without adding foam/hair height repeat an eighteenth-century error in reverse.
- “Chinese” and “Gothic” in the *Director* are **English tastes**, not imports of Chinese or medieval upholstery methods. Pagoda canopies and fretwork are London Rococo chinoiserie.

Almost no domestic sofa branded “Chippendale” in a 2026 market is a documented St. Martin’s Lane object. Documented Chippendale commissions (Dumfries House, Nostell, Harewood — the literature is large) are museum and country-house territory. A brand may say “in the *Director* taste” if a splat or leg honestly follows a plate type. A brand may not say “Chippendale original” or “the Chippendale sofa.”

Hepplewhite’s *Guide* (1788) and Sheraton’s *Drawing-Book* (1791–94) are the next two books in the “big three” (UH-15). Sheraton himself comments on Chippendale and on Hepplewhite. The conversation is **among pattern books**. Modern mills that put all three names on one wall unit are running a greatest-hits file.

## What a brand may honestly say

- “Chippendale’s 1754 *Director* is a pattern book. Our carved [leg/splat] follows that taste.”
- “He published sofas and beds as well as case furniture. The upholstery would have been the shop’s and the client’s textiles.”
- “Chinoiserie in that book is an English Gothic-and-Chinese *fashion*, not a Chinese construction method.”

## What a brand must not say

- “Hand-made by Thomas Chippendale, 1754.”
- “The only authentic Chippendale sofa.”
- Reproducing plates as if they were the brand’s copyrighted designs.
