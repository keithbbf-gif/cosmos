# Words That Built the Trade: Upholder, Sofa, Divan, Ottoman

## Learning objective

Trainees stop treating *sofa*, *couch*, *divan*, *settee*, and *ottoman* as interchangeable mood words, and can give a one-line etymology that does not invent a British inventor.

## Thirty-second floor version

“*Sofa* came into European languages from Ottoman Turkish and Arabic, first as a raised, carpeted place to sit, then as a stuffed long seat. *Ottoman* names a backless padded form after the same cultural world. *Upholsterer* is the English trade name; *upholder* is the older London word. We use the words as forms, not as proof that we invented them.”

## History

### Upholder / upholsterer

*Upholder* is the City of London name. The Livery records variant spellings (*Upheldere*, *Uphouldesterr*) and settles on Upholder. The company formed 1 March 1360 (old style) and was chartered in 1626. The older sense is broader than “person who stuffs chairs”: bedding, flock, feathers, small wares, second-hand dealing, and valuation. Karin Walton’s listing of freemen (1678–1803) and later freedom admissions show how often the trade overlapped cabinet-making, auctioneering, and undertaking. When a brand says “our upholsterers,” it is using a nineteenth- and twentieth-century factory job title. That is fine — if the hangtag does not imply a medieval charter.

*Upholstery* as the covering-and-stuffing of seat furniture is the sense customers hear. Keep the medieval breadth in training so nobody claims the company “invented upholstery.”

### Sofa

The Oxford English Dictionary treats *sofa* as of multiple origins: partly a borrowing from Ottoman Turkish *ṣofa*, partly from French *sofa*. The French record is early and useful for brands that sell in both languages. CNRTL cites 1519 *soffa* for an eastern raised platform covered with carpets and cushions; 1560 *sopha*; 1654 *sofa* as an *estrade*. By 1689–1694 the word in French has become a rest-bed with backs “in the Turkish manner,” and the Académie (1694) defines *sofa* as a kind of *lit de repos* with two backs, lately used in France.

English follows the same path: first the raised, carpeted eastern floor-place (seventeenth century), then the long stuffed seat with back and ends. A brand that writes “the sofa was invented in London in 1700” is simply wrong. Europe *adopted and redesigned* a seating idea that already had a name.

### Divan

*Divan* (Persian *dīvān*) is a council, a register, then the cushioned bench around a reception room. In European interiors it names a long, often backless or low-backed, wall-aligned seat. It is not a synonym for “sectional.” If a collection is wall-hugging and cushion-forward, *divan* is the honest historical cousin — with the Persian and Ottoman rooms attached, not a Mayfair club.

### Ottoman

As a furniture word, *ottoman* is late: English in the late eighteenth century for a low upholstered seat without back or arms, sometimes a box with a hinged lid. French *ottomane* is cited from 1729. The name points at “Ottoman / Turkish style,” not at a single documented maker. An ottoman is a **form**, not a trademark origin story.

### Couch, settee, canapé, chesterfield

- **Couch** in English is older and messier: to lie down; a daybed; later a synonym for sofa in American retail.
- **Settee** is the English long seat with a back, often lighter than a stuffed sofa; related to *settle*.
- **Canapé** is the French formal long seat; do not use it as a fancy synonym unless the collection is actually in that line.
- **Chesterfield** is a later English nickname for a sofa whose arms meet the back height (see UH-36). In Canada it can still mean “sofa” in ordinary speech. That dialect fact is worth one sentence on a Canadian hangtag and no sentence as an origin myth.

Words travel faster than constructions. A 2026 “divan sectional” can be honest if the form is wall-like and modular. It becomes dishonest when the copy adds “ancient Persian royal seating, hand-built in our factory since 1890” without a factory date or a Persian object.

## What a brand may honestly say

- “Sofa is a word Europe borrowed. The stuffed long seat is a European redesign of an older eastern seating idea.”
- “This ottoman is the backless padded form that English and French named in the 1700s.”
- “Our upholsterers build the seat layers. The medieval upholders also sold bedding and appraised goods — we are not claiming their charter.”

## What a brand must not say

- “We coined the word sofa.”
- “Ottoman means Turkish leather pouf invented by [brand].”
- Using *chesterfield*, *canapé*, and *divan* in one paragraph as if they were quality grades.
