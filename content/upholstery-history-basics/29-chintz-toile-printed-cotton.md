# Chintz, Toile, and Printed Cottons

## Learning objective

Merchandisers can locate **chintz** in the Indian-to-European cotton trade and **toile** at Jouy, and can stop treating every pastoral print as “French country heritage we invented.”

## Thirty-second floor version

“Chintz is a glazed printed cotton that Europe learned from India and then copied. Toile de Jouy is an eighteenth-century copperplate print from Jouy-en-Josas — usually a single color story on linen or cotton. If our sofa wears a pastoral scene, we should say ‘toile-type print’ unless it is actually from that lineage.”

## History

**Chintz.** The word comes from Indian printed and painted cottons that flooded European markets in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. Glaze, bright mordant colors, and floral trees of life made chintz a **global textile**, then a protectionist problem (bans and import fights), then a European industry. Furniture uses: loose covers, summer slipcovers (UH-38), bed hangings, later tight upholstery. English country-house “chintz sofas” are a **nineteenth- and twentieth-century interior fashion** (Colefax, later American country-house style), not a Tudor practice.

**Toile de Jouy.** Christophe-Philippe Oberkampf’s manufactory at Jouy-en-Josas (from the 1760s; the famous copperplate toiles later in the century) prints narrative and pastoral scenes, usually monochrome. “Toile” in a 2026 mill book is often a **layout** (scenic repeat, one color) printed by rotary screen or digital. Honest: “toile-style print.” Dishonest: “Oberkampf, 1780, our archive” without an archive.

**Other printed cottons.** Copperplate and woodblock chair seats; roller-printed Victorians; Morris’s block-printed cottons for rooms that also had simple seats; twentieth-century barkcloth (which is more a mid-century American drape and occasional upholstery cloth than a historic European pile). Each has a date. “Printed linen” is not a period.

Slipcovers made printed cottons practical: they wash, they change with the season, they protect silk (UH-38). The history of chintz on furniture is partly a history of **removable covers**.

Colonial and labor note: Indian chintz and later plantation cotton are not politically neutral. A brand that sells “plantation chintz” as nostalgia should expect a deserved argument. Teach the trade route; do not sell a racial idyll.

**Glaze and finish.** Historic chintz is often **calendered or waxed** so it sheds dust and reads glossy in a candlelit room. A 2026 “chintz” that is a dull digital floral on poly-cotton is a print, not a finish. If the collection name is Chintz, specify glaze. If there is no glaze, rename.

**Scale.** Jouy scenes are drawn to a cloth width and a viewing distance. Blown up onto a wide sofa, a tiny pastoral becomes a giant milkmaid across a cushion seam. Historic upholsterers matched or centered. A brand that “doesn’t match at this price” should not use a scenic toile; it should use a small sprig. That is a period-sensitive specification, not a taste lecture.

## What a brand may honestly say

- “Chintz is a glazed floral cotton in an Indian-to-European trade tradition.”
- “This print follows Jouy-style toile: one color, a scenic repeat.”
- “We use this cotton as a slipcover cloth, which is how country-house seating often wore prints.”

## What a brand must not say

- “Original Jouy document” without a document.
- “Colonial charm” as a substitute for history.
- “Hand-blocked 1780” on a digital print.
