# The London Upholders, 1360–1626

## Learning objective

Leadership and training can tell the Livery story with **dates that exist**, and can separate a living City company from a furniture brand’s marketing department.

## Thirty-second floor version

“London’s upholsterers organized as the Upholders in 1360, got a coat of arms in 1465, and a royal charter in 1626. They could search out bad work. Their hall burned in 1666 and was never rebuilt. They still exist. We are not them — we *learn from* that idea of a trade that polices its own quality.”

## History

The Worshipful Company of Upholders is the single best-documented institutional ancestor of the English-language trade. Brands should use it as a **public history**, not as a stolen pedigree.

**Organization.** The Company’s own history quotes the Saturday after the Feast of St. Matthias in the 35th year of Edward III: 1 March 1360, old style. Wardens were elected “to survey and govern the men of the mistery.” *Mystery* here is Latin *misterium*: professional skill, not a secret handshake for catalog copy.

**Search.** In 1474 the Company gained the right to search for and seize wares in the City that were insufficiently or not truly made. This is the sentence worth teaching to a QC manager. Quality was not a brand promise; it was a civic power with teeth. That power eroded as the trade diversified and statute law shifted. Do not tell customers that a modern factory “still has search rights.”

**Arms and charter.** Coat of arms: 1465, Edward IV. Royal charter: Charles I, 14 June 1626. The original charter burned in the Great Fire of 1666; an exemplification in 1668 confirmed the grant under Charles II. Motto: *Sustine Bona* — Uphold the Good. Precedence among Livery companies: forty-ninth.

**Hall.** In 1645 Andrew Yardly gave £500 toward a hall. In 1646 the Company bought Wingfield House / Wingfield Place between Lambeth Hill and St. Peter’s Hill, Castle Baynard. It burned in 1666 and was not replaced. A blue plaque marks the site on the walk from the Millennium Bridge toward St. Paul’s. The Company today is among the Liveries without a hall. That absence is a better story than a stock photo of a paneled dining room captioned “our ancestral guildhall.”

**Who they were.** Houston and Walton’s work on freedom admissions shows a trade that is not only stuffing: second-hand dealing, appraisal, auctioneering, cabinet-making, furniture renting, pawnbroking, sworn broking, undertaking. Featherbeds and flock beds are in Houston’s title for a reason. If a brand’s heritage page says “we have always only made sofas,” it is not describing this company.

**Apprenticeship.** Surviving bindings (e.g. 1704–72 extracts) and later apprenticeship schemes (the Company notes a 2017 completer under the Livery Companies’ scheme) show a living educational function. The Association of Master Upholsterers and Soft Furnishers (AMUSF) and the Guild of Traditional Upholsterers are the modern craft partners. A brand that funds training can say so. A brand that does not should not wear the Livery badge.

**What the charter does not do.** It does not certify a North Carolina SKU. It does not make “upholder” a protected title in the United States. It does not prove that coil springs are medieval.

## What a brand may honestly say

- “The London Upholders organized in 1360 and were chartered in 1626 to govern the quality of their trade.”
- “Their motto is *Sustine Bona*. We borrowed the *idea* of upholding good work — not their arms.”
- “If you want the living company, it is still in the City and still supports training.”

## What a brand must not say

- Reproducing the Company’s arms on a hangtag without permission.
- “Chartered 1626” in the brand’s own founding line.
- “Destroyed with the Great Fire, we rebuilt in [American town].”
