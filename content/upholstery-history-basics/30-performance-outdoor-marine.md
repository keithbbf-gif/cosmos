# Performance, Outdoor, and Marine Cloths

## Learning objective

Sales can place “performance fabric” in a **late-twentieth-century chemistry and contract** story, and can connect marine seating to a much older problem (Pratt’s ship furniture) without claiming acrylic is Georgian.

## Thirty-second floor version

“Ships needed seating that didn’t rot or make people sick — Pratt’s 1820s patents are partly naval. Today’s outdoor and ‘performance’ cloths are usually solution-dyed synthetics and barrier weaves from the late 1900s. They are a real technical chapter. They are not ‘traditional linen, but magic.’”

## History

**Marine is old; acrylic is not.** Eighteenth- and nineteenth-century ship furniture uses leather, painted canvas, cane, and, after Pratt, sprung cushions meant to live with salt and motion. Yacht and rail seating in the twentieth century use leather and mohair. The **problem** (moisture, sun, abrasion, ignitability) is historic. The **solution-dyed acrylic** answer is not.

**Solution-dyed acrylic** (Glen Raven’s Sunbrella is the famous US trade name, postwar development into a furniture outdoor standard) puts pigment in the fiber, not on it. Fade resistance is the sales fact. The history to teach: **awnings and boats first, patio cushions later, then indoor “performance” sofas.** If the brand is not Glen Raven, do not say Sunbrella unless the mill goods are that brand.

**Barrier and moisture-control systems** (Crypton is the widely known 1990s trademark) add a moisture barrier and stain chemistry for healthcare, hospitality, and family rooms. They are **systems**, not a fiber. Training should mention cleanability codes and the need to use the specified cleaner — history of contract interiors, not of Louis XVI.

**Vinyl and coated cloths** (Naugahyde and others, mid-twentieth century) are the diner and waiting-room chapter. They belong in the same hour as performance: **wipeable public sits**. Do not call vinyl “vegan leather” without also saying it is a coated fabric.

**Outdoor foam and quick-dry constructions** (open-cell, reticulated foams, slings) are a late chapter. Sling furniture is closer to a **canvas chair** than to a sprung carcass. Name it.

Environmental caveat: performance often means **plastic**. A sustainability story that hides that is placation (UH-41). Recycled content, take-back, and recoverability are the honest add-ons.

**What “outdoor” used to be.** Painted canvas, oilcloth, rattan, and slings of canvas on folding frames are the pre-acrylic patio. They rot, mildew, and look honest about it. A brand that sells a deep-cushioned outdoor “Chesterfield” in solution-dyed acrylic is inventing a form that could not have lived on a Victorian porch. Allowed — if the ticket says **new form, new fiber**. Not allowed: “the porch sofa they had in 1890, now improved with space-age cloth, no plastic.”

**Sunscreen, chlorine, and salt** are the real 21st-century marine brief. Pratt worried about motion and damp. He did not worry about SPF 50 on a white cushion. Spec the chemical family or do not promise “yacht tough.”

## What a brand may honestly say

- “This is a solution-dyed acrylic outdoor cloth, [brand if true], made for sun and wet.”
- “Marine seating is an old problem. Our answer is a modern synthetic system.”
- “Performance indoor fabric here means [barrier / cleanable code]. It is not historic chintz.”

## What a brand must not say

- A competitor’s trademark on generic goods.
- “Waterproof antique linen.”
- “No care needed.”
