# Squabs, Tufts, and the Ancestor of the Button

## Learning objective

Product people can define a **squab** as a separate mattress-like cushion and a **tuft** as a through-tie that predates the decorative button.

## Thirty-second floor version

“A squab is a cushion built like a little mattress — top, bottom, boxed sides, often tufted so the filling stays put. The V&A has an 1823 luxury squab whose tufts are thread, not leather buttons. Buttons are a later, dressier way to do the same job.”

## History

**Squab** is the English trade word for a stuffed pad made to sit *on* a chair, a cane seat, a bench, or a window. Construction, as the V&A describes the Morel & Hughes 1823 example: a top and bottom joined by borders; sides stitched for a firm upright edge; **tufts of thread** on top and bottom, joined by twine through the horsehair. The silk show cover on that object is lost; the linen underside survives. That is enough to teach a factory: **the engineering is the through-tie**.

Tufting in this sense is not diamond capitonné (UH-32). It is a **quilt-like stab** at intervals to stop the fill from migrating — the same idea as a mattress tuft. Brands that sell “biscuit tufting” on foam are imitating the *look* of those stab points.

Loose medieval and Renaissance cushions are squabs without the later boxed vocabulary. The continuity is portable softness. The discontinuity is the sewn, side-stitched, tufted object of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries.

When cane and rush seats remain fashionable (Regency, some country chairs), the squab is how you add winter comfort without destroying the summer seat. A modern brand that sells a cane chair with a zippered boxed pad is in this line. Call it a squab on the ticket; customers will learn a word.

**Buttons** arrive as a **hardware and leather/cloth finish** on the through-tie: a show button on the face, a stay button or tie on the back. They become fashionable in the nineteenth century as seats get thicker (UH-17, UH-32). Do not tell children that buttons exist to hurt visitors; tell them the fill got deeper and needed more discipline.

**How many tufts.** The 1823 squab’s tufts are functional, not a diamond fashion grid. A chair squab might take a handful of ties; a mattress takes a field. When a brand “tufts” a 3-inch foam box with one center button, it is making a decorative dimple. When it tufts on a 6-inch grid through hair, it is in the V&A object’s family. Put the grid on the shop drawing.

**Zippers vs tufts.** A modern boxed cushion often replaces tufts with a foam core and a zipper. That is a better service object (UH-44) and a worse historic replica. Offer both: a tufted hair squab for the cane reproduction line, a zippered foam box for the hospitality line. Do not mix the copy.

## What a brand may honestly say

- “This chair takes a squab: a boxed, tufted pad, like Regency luxury seats on cane.”
- “Tufts are through-ties. Our buttons are the dressed version of that tie.”
- “The 1823 Northumberland House squab at the V&A is the object we show trainees.”

## What a brand must not say

- “Buttons invented by the Earl of [x] to keep callers from staying.”
- “Hand-tufted” on glued foam dimples with no tie.
- “Squab” as a brand-new trademarked form.
