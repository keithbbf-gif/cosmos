# Art Nouveau, Art Deco, and Bauhaus Covers

## Learning objective

Catalog writers can keep three modern-adjacent styles apart: **whiplash organism** (Nouveau), **streamlined luxury surface** (Deco), **reduced industrial frame plus cushion** (Bauhaus / International), and can name Lilly Reich beside Mies.

## Thirty-second floor version

“Art Nouveau chairs treat wood and cover as one curved plant. Art Deco likes leather, geometry, and liner luxury. Bauhaus and International Style often show a metal or wood frame with separate cushions. The Barcelona chair of 1929 — Mies and Lilly Reich — is the teaching example: an X-frame and leather pads, not a buried Victorian carcass.”

## History

### Art Nouveau

In Paris, Nancy, Brussels, and Barcelona, seating around 1890–1910 follows the whiplash line. Majorelle and others carve and upholster so the velvet seems to grow from the wood. The sit can still be spring-and-hair; the news is **silhouette**. A brand that prints “Nouveau” on a rectangle with a floral pillow is using wallpaper, not a chair language.

### Art Deco

Post-1925 (and the wider 1920s–30s luxury modern), Deco seating is the **club, the cinema, the liner**: sunburst channels, stepped arms, pigskin and calf, metallic thread, lacquer next to velvet. Deep comfort remains. The cover becomes a graphic. Ocean-liner and hotel commissions matter: upholstery as **public interior**, not only parlor. Brands selling hospitality lines can honestly stand here.

### Bauhaus and International Style

The educational break is the **visible frame**. Marcel Breuer’s tubular steel, Mies’s furniture with Reich, and related Dutch and French modern seats often treat cushions as **objects placed on a structure**. The Barcelona chair (1929, German Pavilion, Barcelona International Exposition) has a bolted, later seamless, steel X-frame and hand-tufted leather cushions. Knoll has manufactured it since 1964; the familiar name “Barcelona” is later (Knoll literature: 1987 naming). Original covers included ivory-colored pigskin; later production uses bovine leather. Foam fills modern cushions. The chair was made for a ceremonial pavilion, not for a mass housing program — Time called the setting sumptuous. Tom Wolfe later mocked it as a luxury totem. Train staff to hold both facts: **modern look, elite object**.

Lilly Reich’s co-authorship is not optional in 2026 training. She was responsible for significant material and interior decisions. A hangtag that says only “Mies” repeats a twentieth-century credit failure.

Other modern seats (Breuer Wassily — leather slings, not stuffed upholstery; Rietveld — planes) show that “modern” sometimes **refuses** the stuffed carcass. A brand that sells only overstuffed “mid-century” is not covering this chapter.

## What a brand may honestly say

- “Nouveau seating joins carved frame and cover in one curve.”
- “Deco hospitality seating treated leather and velvet as graphic luxury.”
- “The 1929 Barcelona chair — Mies van der Rohe and Lilly Reich — is a steel frame with separate leather cushions. Licensed production is Knoll’s story, not ours, unless we are Knoll.”

## What a brand must not say

- “Mies-only” credit.
- “Bauhaus furniture for the people” as a caption on a $8,000 lounge without the elite-pavilion asterisk.
- Copying the Barcelona X and calling it original.
