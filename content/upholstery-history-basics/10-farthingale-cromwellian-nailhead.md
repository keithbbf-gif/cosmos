# Farthingale, Cromwellian, and the Brass Nail

## Learning objective

Sales staff can tell a customer that **nailhead is a seventeenth-century construction finish**, not a 2010s “industrial” invention, and can separate farthingale width from Cromwellian leather.

## Thirty-second floor version

“Farthingale chairs are wide, so a hooped skirt could sit. Cromwellian chairs are the plain leather-and-brass-nail seats of mid-seventeenth-century England. When we offer nailhead, we are using a very old way to fasten and decorate a leather or textile cover — not a new ‘loft’ trend.”

## History

### Farthingale chairs

Early-seventeenth-century “farthingale” chairs take their nickname from the farthingale (and later, wide Jacobean skirts). The seat is broad; arms are low, short, or absent so the skirt is not crushed. Upholstery, when present, is a stuffed panel and a textile or turkey-work cover. The teaching use is **fashion drives seat geometry**. A brand that designs for sitters in winter coats or for plus-size comfort is in the same problem-space. Do not claim the farthingale as a “wellness chair.”

Turkey-work (knotted pile imitation of carpets) appears on some English chairs of this world. If a collection uses a pile geometric, “turkey-work tradition” is a better caption than “Kilim sofa” unless the textile is actually a kilim.

### Cromwellian / Commonwealth chairs

Mid-seventeenth-century English chairs associated with the Commonwealth are typically **oak or walnut, turned legs and stretchers, a rectangular leather back and seat, large brass nails**. The look is severe because the construction is honest: the nail is the fastener and the ornament. Restoration and later revival chairs copy the silhouette. A 2026 “Cromwellian” SKU is almost always a revival, not a 1650 survivor. Say “in the Commonwealth leather-chair manner.”

### The brass nail as technology

Decorative nails (and later close-nailed outlines) do three jobs: they hold the cover, they protect the edge, and they read as a border from across a room. French and English shops both use them. The modern “nailhead trim” applied with a tape of fake heads is a **labor-saving picture** of this technique. Honest copy: “nailhead outline, applied as [individual nails / nail-head trim].” Dishonest copy: “hand-forged 17th-century nails” on a steel tape.

After the Restoration (1660), caned chairs and richer velvets return; the leather-and-nail chair does not vanish. It becomes one option among many — including the walnut and gilt suites of the William and Mary and Queen Anne years (UH-12).

Political warning for brand voice: “Cromwellian” is a historical style name. It is not a personality for a leather collection in a divided country. Teach the chair. Do not write manifesto copy around it.

## What a brand may honestly say

- “Nailhead trim comes from early modern chairs where big brass nails really held the leather on.”
- “Farthingale chairs were cut wide for hooped skirts. We kept the wide sit for another reason: [state it].”
- “Cromwellian is a trade name for a plain mid-17th-century leather chair type.”

## What a brand must not say

- “Hand-forged Commonwealth nails” without a metals report.
- “Oliver Cromwell designed this sofa.”
- Selling a narrow tub chair as a farthingale.
