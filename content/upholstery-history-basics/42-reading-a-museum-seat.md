# How to Read a Museum Seat

## Learning objective

A content or product person standing in a gallery can **read a seat as a stack of decisions** and take notes a factory can use — without touching the object.

## Thirty-second floor version

“Look at the frame first, then the platform, then the fill, then the cover, then the trim. Read the label for what is original. Photograph the underside if you’re allowed. Never invent the inside from the silhouette.”

## History (as method)

Museum furniture is why this curriculum can be sourced. The method is repeatable:

1. **Identity.** Accession number, date, maker if any, later alterations. “About 1845” is a date. “Victorian style” is not.
2. **Frame.** Wood species, show wood vs buried, repairs, worm, replaced legs. A recovered sofa can have an older frame and a 1990s sit.
3. **Platform.** Webbing material and pattern; springs (count, tie, gauge); hard deck; cane. The V&A 1845 chair tells you *six* coils. Steal that specificity.
4. **Fill.** Hair, flock, moss, foam, down. Smell and stitch pattern (from published conservation notes — not from poking).
5. **Cover.** Show vs loose; fiber; whether the cover is original. Houghton velvet on hooks is a different object from a tacked show cover.
6. **Trim.** Nails, fringe, gimp, buttons, tuft spacing.
7. **Use traces.** Wear on the front rail, repaired webbing, replaced cambric. Use is history.

The late-seventeenth-century settee study (Conservation Journal 33) shows how **tacks and tack holes** tell sequence. A brand photographer who only shoots the three-quarter beauty angle is not collecting evidence.

**What not to do in a gallery.** Sit. Flip. Pull a cover. Export a high-resolution plate from a book and reprint it as your own (copyright). Use a flash if the museum forbids it.

**What to do with the notes.** A one-page object card in the PLM or the training deck: photo, accession, five-layer list, “what we will *not* copy” (the gilt, the arms, the exact needlework). That card is the artifact behind “inspired by.”

National Trust houses (Knole) and American collections (Winterthur, Met, MFA) are as useful as the V&A. The rule is the same: **the object page beats the mood board.**

**A one-hour assignment for content hires.** Pick one V&A or Met seat online. Write a five-layer card without using the words *luxurious*, *timeless*, or *iconic*. If you cannot name the platform, you may not write “inspired by.” If the object page says the cover is later, your card must say the cover is later. File the card next to the SKU it supposedly inspired. That is how a brand builds a private corpus instead of a Pinterest folder.

**Photography ethics.** Beauty shots flatten springs into a silhouette. Training shots include the underside, a raking light on nail heads, and the accession label. Keep both. The beauty shot sells. The underside shot keeps the copy department honest (UH-41).

## What a brand may honestly say

- “We based the seat depth on V&A W.93-1921’s sprung sandwich, not on a Pinterest silhouette.”
- “The show cover is a loose-cover idea from the Houghton chairs. The frame is ours, [year].”
- “We could not inspect the underside; our reconstruction is labeled as a guess.”

## What a brand must not say

- “Exact replica” without a measured drawing and a materials match.
- Using a museum photo as a product shot.
- Touching.
