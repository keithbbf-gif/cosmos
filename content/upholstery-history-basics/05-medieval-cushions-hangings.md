# Medieval Portable Luxury: Cushions, Chests, Hangings

## Learning objective

Trainees can explain why a medieval great hall looks “under-furnished” to a modern sofa shopper: **wealth was in textiles that moved**, not in bolted-down stuffed carcasses.

## Thirty-second floor version

“In medieval houses the expensive sit was often a cushion you could carry to a bench or a chest. Hangings warmed the wall. The upholder’s trade grew up around those goods. A built-in stuffed sofa is a later, heavier idea.”

## History

Northern European rooms between roughly 1100 and 1500 were cold, smoky, and more mobile than a showroom photograph suggests. Elite households moved between residences. The furniture that stayed put was structural: trestle tables, benches, settles, chests, beds with frames. The furniture that announced rank was often **textile**.

Three objects do most of the educational work:

1. **The cushion.** Inventories list them by the dozen, in tapestry, embroidery, leather, and silk, sometimes with armorials. They turn a chest into a seat and a window ledge into a day perch. They are the ancestor of the **squab** (UH-31) — a separate, mattress-like pad — not of a sprung T-cushion.
2. **The hanging.** Tapestries and painted cloths are climate control and display. The French *tapissier* is, first, the person of hangings. English upholders overlap this world: bedding, flock, feathers, and the policing of shoddy wares. In 1474 the London company received the right to search for and seize goods “insufficiently or not truly made.” That is a consumer-protection story a brand can tell without pretending to own a guild hall.
3. **The chest and the settle.** A chest is storage and a sit. A settle is a bench with a back, sometimes a box seat. Both can take cushions. Neither is a sofa.

Beds matter more than chairs in this period. The great bed — testers, curtains, valances, mattresses of flock or feathers — is where the upholstery trades spent their skill. A brand that only sells sofas still needs this fact: **the padded interior began as a bed-and-hanging interior.** Seat furniture catches up later, when rooms are warmer and more specialized.

What did not exist as a normal domestic object: the deep, coil-sprung, buttoned sofa. Metal springs as seat foundations are a nineteenth-century popularization (UH-24). Teaching the gap prevents the catalog line “medieval luxury sofa.”

Social history for the sales floor: cushions and hangings were **portable capital**. They could be bequeathed, seized, or taken to another house. A sofa that weighs 120 pounds and is stapled to a kiln-dried frame is the opposite economic object. If a brand’s sustainability story is “we make things that last and can be recovered,” the medieval analogue is the cushion that outlived the bench — recover the cover, keep the fill, move the wealth.

Church and choir stalls show another medieval sit: wood, sometimes a hinged misericord, rarely a stuffed envelope. Do not use a cathedral photograph to sell a foam bench.

## What a brand may honestly say

- “Medieval comfort was often a high-value cushion on a bench or chest, plus hangings for warmth.”
- “The London upholders were already policing badly made bedding and furnishings in the 1400s.”
- “This collection’s loose cushions are in that portable-luxury family. The frame is [date/method].”

## What a brand must not say

- “Our sofa is a medieval design.”
- “Guild-certified since 1360” on a private company hangtag.
- Calling a tapestry-print polyester a “true arras.”
