# What Upholstery Is — And What It Is Not

## Learning objective

A furniture-brand trainee can define upholstery as a **layered construction** (frame, suspension, fill, cover, trim) and stop calling every soft seat “traditional upholstery.”

## Thirty-second floor version

“Upholstery is the built-up seat: what holds you up, what cushions you, and what you see and touch. A cushion dropped on a wood chair is not the same craft as a sprung, stitched, covered sofa. When we say *upholstered*, we mean a construction — not a mood.”

## History

The English trade name is older than the padded sofa most customers picture. In London the craftsman was an **upholder** (also *upheldere*, *uphouldesterr*). The Worshipful Company of Upholders dates its organization to 1 March 1360 (old style), in the 35th year of Edward III, and received a royal charter from Charles I on 14 June 1626. The Livery’s own history is explicit that members did not only “stuff chairs”: they dealt in bedding, soft furnishings, appraisal, auctioneering, and, for a long stretch, undertaking. “Upholstery” in the medieval and early modern city was a **goods-and- interiors trade**, not a single factory department.

That breadth matters because modern catalogs collapse three different things into one adjective:

1. **Soft furnishings** — hangings, bed testers, loose cushions, slipcovers. The French *tapissier* lineage starts here: cloth in a room, not necessarily a sprung seat.
2. **Structural upholstery** — webbing or springs on a frame, stitched stuffing, a cover tacked or stapled to the rail. This is what a Victorian easy chair and a contemporary eight-way sofa share.
3. **Cushioned furniture that is not upholstered in the trade sense** — a molded-foam shell with a zip-on cover; a sling; a leather hide laced over a board. Comfortable, saleable, and historically important — but not “hand-tied upholstery” unless the construction is actually there.

Museum objects make the distinction physical. A V&A chair of about 1845 (W.93-1921) still carries its original under-upholstery: coil springs stitched to webbing, compressed under a tacked cover, with a stitched horsehair layer above the springs. That sandwich — **suspension + fill + cover** — is the thing this series means by “the seat.” A 1730s gilded chair in the same museum (Houghton type) uses linen webbing, ticking, horsehair, and a linen stuffing-cover, with a *loose* velvet show cover hooked to pegs. Same craft family, different century, no springs.

What upholstery is **not**:

- **A period name.** “Louis XV sofa” on a 2026 SKU is a silhouette adjective unless the piece is a documented eighteenth-century object.
- **A comfort promise.** Horsehair can be harder than cheap foam; foam can outlast neglected down. Comfort is specification, not heritage.
- **A moral.** Hand work is not automatically more honest than machine work. Honesty is whether the hangtag matches the inside.

For a brand, the useful definition is operational: **upholstery is the sequence of operations that turns a frame into a sit.** Everything else in this curriculum is a history of those operations and of the rooms that paid for them.

## What a brand may honestly say

- “Our upholstered seating is built in layers: frame, suspension, fill, and cover. We can show you each layer.”
- “The word *upholstery* comes out of a London trade that once covered beds, hangings, and valuation as well as chairs.”
- “This piece uses [named suspension and fill]. That is why it sits the way it does.”

## What a brand must not say

- “We’ve been practicing traditional upholstery since the 1300s” — unless the legal entity is the Livery, which it is not.
- “All our sofas are fully upholstered” when some SKUs are foam-on-board with a staple gun and no suspension.
- “Upholstery means handmade” — staples, CNC frames, and cut-and-sew cells are part of the modern craft.
