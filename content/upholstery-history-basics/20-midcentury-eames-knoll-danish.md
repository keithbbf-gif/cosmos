# Mid-Century: Eames, Knoll, Danish, Italian

## Learning objective

Sales staff can name four postwar upholstery attitudes — **American molded-and-cushion**, **corporate architectural**, **Danish organic**, **Italian sculptural** — and can refuse to call every tapered-leg sofa “Eames.”

## Thirty-second floor version

“Mid-century isn’t one sofa. Florence Knoll’s 1954 lounge is a geometric fill-in for modern offices. The Eames lounge of 1956 is a plywood-and-leather club-chair rethink — Charles said it should look like a well-used mitt. Danish designers often floated thin upholstery on wood. Italian houses treated the stuffed form as sculpture. We should say which attitude we mean.”

## History

### Florence Knoll, 1954

Knoll’s own text is the training quote: she called the lounge pieces “fill-in pieces that no one else wants to do.” The collection is spare and geometric, learned from Mies, scaled to postwar American office interiors. Construction notes from KnollStudio: solid-wood inner frame, **No-Sag (sinuous) springs**, steel legs, leather with matching buttons. This is modern **and** conventional upholstery — not a sling. Brands that copy the block sofa should not print her signature.

### Eames Lounge, 1956

Charles and Ray Eames, model 670/671, Herman Miller. Three molded plywood shells, leather cushions, aluminum base, a recline. Charles, on CBS in January 1956, wanted “the warm, receptive look of a well-used first baseman’s mitt.” It is a modern **club chair** (UH-37), not a sofa program. Early cushions are discussed in collector literature as glove leather with feather and down; later production is foam. Licensed makers (Herman Miller; Vitra in other territories) own the authentic product. A “Eames-style” lounge is a silhouette homage and must be named as such.

### Danish modern

Finn Juhl, with cabinetmaker Niels Vodder, pulled upholstery into **sculptural pads on visible wood** (Chieftain as the extreme). Børge Mogensen and Kaare Klint’s measuring-and-type furniture kept seats disciplined. Jens Risom’s early Knoll chair used leftover parachute webbing on cedar — wartime scarcity as aesthetic. Hans Wegner’s paper-cord and upholstered variants show the Danish habit: **the frame is a craftsman object; the pad is enough**. A foam-heavy “Danish” sectional with hidden legs is using a stain color, not a school.

### Italian postwar

Cassina, Gio Ponti, later Bellini, Magistretti, and others treat the stuffed volume as a **designed industrial object** — sometimes foam as form, not foam as cheap fill. The Italian lesson for a brand is that **polyurethane can be a design material** (UH-26), not only a cost cut. Still, “designed in Italy” is a geographic claim. Prove it or drop it.

### Saarinen and the Womb

Eero Saarinen’s Womb chair (Knoll) is a shell plus loose cushions: comfort as a nest, frame as plastic form. It belongs in the same training hour so staff do not file every organic lounge under Eames.

## What a brand may honestly say

- “Florence Knoll’s 1954 lounge used a geometric, architectural sit with sinuous springs.”
- “The 1956 Eames lounge rethought the club chair in plywood and leather. Ours is a [licensed product / inspired silhouette].”
- “Danish modern often shows the wood and keeps the pad thin. Italian houses sometimes let foam be the shape.”

## What a brand must not say

- “Eames” on a generic recliner.
- Forged Knoll or Herman Miller marks.
- “Danish oil” as proof of Copenhagen design.
