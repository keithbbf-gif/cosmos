# Velvet, Mohair, Silk, and Pile Cloths

## Learning objective

Merchandisers can tell **silk velvet, cotton velvet, mohair velvet, and polyester pile** apart as different histories, and can stop calling every plush “royal velvet.”

## Thirty-second floor version

“Velvet is a pile cloth. Historic state chairs used silk velvet — sometimes as a loose, changeable cover. Theaters and trains used mohair velvet because it wears. Turkey-work was a knotted pile on seventeenth-century English chairs. Our plush may be polyester. We should say which pile.”

## History

Pile is older than the sofa. What furniture brands sell as “velvet” is a **family**:

- **Silk velvet** — the state-room cloth. The Houghton chairs’ loose green silk velvet (UH-13) is a museum example of velvet as a *sacrificial show cover*. It abrades; it is stored; it is replaced. A brand that sells silk velvet without a wear clause is not reading the object.
- **Cotton velvet / velveteen** — later, more democratic pile. Victorian and revival seating lives here.
- **Mohair velvet** — pile from the Angora goat, usually on a cotton ground. The prestige **contract** pile of theaters, cinemas, and transport. It is why old cinema seats outlast living-room velour. If a brand sells “theater mohair,” the fiber should be mohair.
- **Turkey-work** — knotted pile imitating carpets, nailed to seventeenth-century chairs (UH-10). Ancestor of “carpet upholstery,” not of crushed velour.
- **Polyester and acrylic piles** — twentieth-century chemistry: crush-resistant, stain-resistant, often hotter to the hand. Legitimate. Not silk.

Utrecht velvet (mohair, sometimes with a stamped pattern) is a historic upholstery name still used in the trade. Do not print it on a poly print without the mill’s right to the name.

Pile direction (**nap**) is a making issue: panels must run the same way or the sofa looks two-toned. That is why velvet SKUs cost sewing time. History of the cloth and history of the labor meet in the cutting room.

Crushed and embossed velvets are nineteenth- and twentieth-century fashion finishes. They are not “medieval.”

**Wear numbers a catalog can print.** Historic silk velvet on a dining chair can fail at the front rail in a season of daily use — that is why Houghton-type chairs had loose covers and scarves. Mohair contract velvets are specified in double rubs (Wyzenbeek or Martindale; do not mix the scales on one hangtag). A “performance velvet” that is polyester pile may beat silk on rubs and lose on hand and heat. The history lesson is that **pile was always a tradeoff between show and abrasion**. State rooms accepted replacement. Theaters accepted mohair. Living rooms that want both silk hand and toddler durability are asking for a cloth that did not exist in 1730.

**Cutting.** Nap direction, pattern match on diamonds, and the extra yardage velvet drinks are why a velvet Chesterfield-style sofa costs more than the same frame in a flat cloth. If sales cannot explain yardage, they should not explain “luxury.”

## What a brand may honestly say

- “This is mohair velvet, the historic theater pile, [x] % mohair.”
- “Silk velvet is in the state-chair tradition. It will wear; we offer a recover path.”
- “This pile is polyester, chosen for [cleanability / price].”

## What a brand must not say

- “Royal silk” on poly.
- “Utrecht” as a vibe word.
- “Velvet never wears.”
