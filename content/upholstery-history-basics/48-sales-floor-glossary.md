# Sales-Floor Glossary and Teaching Script

## Learning objective

A new hire can survive a Saturday with **one page of words** and a **90-second tour script** that does not invent history.

## Thirty-second floor version

“If you only remember five words: *platform, fill, show cover, a named test, and I don’t know*. Everything else is in this glossary. ‘I don’t know — I’ll get the spec’ is a premium sentence.”

## History (of the script)

Sales floors already have a history of bad sentences. “Chippendale” became a wood-species adjective in American retail. “Chesterfield” became a dialect word in Canada and a leather trademark fight in Britain. “Eight-way” became a decal on a drop-in unit. This page exists so a new hire inherits a **corrected dialect** instead of the last associate’s folklore. The glossary is compiled from UH-01–UH-47; if a word here fights a draft, the draft with the source wins.

## 90-second showroom script (memorize)

“This is an upholstered sofa, which means a frame, a platform, a fill, and a cover. The platform on this one is [jute + eight-way / sinuous / rubber web / plywood]. The fill is [hair / foam density / down blend]. The cover is [fiber and finish]. If you care about heritage names, we use them for **shapes** — camelback, Chesterfield-style, Lawson — and we don’t tell Earl stories as fact. If you need a fire or leather spec, I get the file instead of guessing. If you want this to last, ask me how we recover it.”

## Trainer notes

- Role-play the customer who says “Is this a real Chippendale?” Answer: “It’s a [splat/leg] in the *Director* taste. Chippendale published plates in 1754. He did not build this frame.”
- Role-play “Why is this $2,000 more?” Answer with **labor you can photograph**, not with a guild year.
- Reward “I’ll look that up.” Punish a confident Earl.

## What a brand may honestly say

- The script above, filled with SKU facts.
- “Our training series is staged drafts in `content/upholstery-history-basics/`.”

## What a brand must not say

- Anything in the “Do not say” column.
- A script that ends with “trust me.”
