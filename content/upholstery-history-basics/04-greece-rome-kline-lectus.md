# Greece and Rome: Klinē, Lectus, Pulvinus

## Learning objective

A merchandiser can describe Greco-Roman elite sitting as **reclining on a webbing-and-mattress couch**, not as a tufted club chair, and can use *klinē* / *lectus* correctly in collection notes.

## Thirty-second floor version

“Greeks and Romans who could afford it dined and talked on couches — a wood frame, straps or a solid support, a stuffed mattress, pillows. The luxury was the textile and the inlay, not a sprung English sofa. When we say *chaise* or *recamier*, we are in a later European memory of that couch.”

## History

The Greek **klinē** is a reclining couch: a rectangular frame, raised at the head, used for the symposium. The Roman **lectus** is the same family with Roman room jobs — sleeping, reading, and, in the dining room, the *lectus* of the triclinium. Standard archaeological and handbook descriptions agree on the construction that matters to this series:

- a **wooden frame**
- **criss-crossed straps** (often leather) or, in some Pompeian rooms, a masonry podium that made the wooden frame unnecessary
- a **mattress** stuffed first with straw, later with wool or feathers
- **pillows and a coverlet** (*pulvinus* is the cushion)

That is a complete comfort system. It is also **not** Victorian spring-stuffing. The sit is a mattress on a platform. Depth comes from the mattress and pillows, not from tied coils.

Decoration was where money went: legs inlaid with ivory, tortoiseshell, or metal; coverlets in dyed cloth and gold thread; silver frames in literary boasts. Brands that want “Roman luxury” should talk about **surface and ritual** (who reclines, who sits, the three-couch dining room), not about eight-way hand-tying.

Two later design facts belong in the same training hour because catalogs mash them together:

1. **The recamier and the chaise longue** are eighteenth- and nineteenth-century European forms that *remember* the antique couch. They are not archaeological replicas. Empire and Directoire designers were looking at wall paintings and sculpture, then asking a *menuisier* and a *tapissier* for a new sit.
2. **The Barcelona chair** (Mies van der Rohe and Lilly Reich, 1929) is often compared to the Roman **curule** seat — a folding X-stool of office. Knoll’s own notes treat the resemblance as formal, not as a surviving Roman workshop. Train sales staff to say “X-frame, like the ancient stool of office” and stop there. The leather cushions are modern upholstery on a modern steel frame.

Pompeii is useful for humility. Some cubicula show the mattress support built from the floor; the wood is gone. Organic fill is gone. What visitors photograph is plaster and stone. A brand that prints “Pompeii-inspired upholstery” and ships polyurethane is doing costume, not archaeology. Costume is allowed if labeled.

For product teams, the classical lesson is **platform logic**. A tight seat on webbing, a boxed mattress cushion, and throw pillows is a closer structural cousin to a *lectus* than a buttoned Chesterfield is. If a collection is a daybed with a bolster, the honest ancestor is this couch, not a 1920s club.

## What a brand may honestly say

- “Roman dining couches used a strapped or built platform and a stuffed mattress with pillows.”
- “This chaise follows a European neoclassical memory of the antique couch.”
- “The Barcelona’s X-frame is often compared to the Roman curule stool; the leather sit is twentieth-century.”

## What a brand must not say

- “Our sofa is a replica of a lectus from Pompeii.”
- “Romans invented coil-spring comfort.”
- Using *klinē* as a font name on a recliner with a cupholder and calling it scholarship.
