# Seedling versus cutting understock

Most backyard understock in 8a is a rooted cutting. That is correct.
You know the variety. You know it already lives here. You can make
fifty of them in a winter without asking a seed for permission.

Seedlings show up anyway. Birds. A forgotten fruit. A forum rumor that
seedlings make "stronger" roots. Sometimes they do. Sometimes they make
a caprifig or a plant with no interest in sweet fruit, which is fine
for a root and a problem if a bud below the union takes over and you
think you still have *Celeste*.

I use cutting understock when I want a known winter behavior. *Chicago
Hardy* cuttings for insurance. *Celeste* cuttings for a southern 8a
stool that does not sour. *LSU Purple* if I want vigor and I am willing
to prune. Known names, known sins.

I use a seedling when I am experimenting or when I already have a
two-year seedling that is in the right hole. I do not plant a row of
unknown seedlings as my orchard plan. I have better cuttings to root.

The research curiosity is interspecific stock: *Ficus palmata*,
sometimes others. Hosomi and others got clean compatibility on
*palmata*. Florida growers have watched at least one *palmata* clone
eat root-knot nematodes and die. So the sentence is not "*palmata*
solves soil." The sentence is "this clone, in this soil, after three
summers." I do not import a tropical *Ficus* as understock for 8a
without asking whether the root dies at 15°F. A hardy top on a tropical
root is a house on a sandbar.

Nematodes are real in warm 8a sand. Grafting onto a random seedling
does not fix that. If your old figs decline with galls on the roots,
you have a soil problem. Raised beds, fresh stock, maybe a clone with
a reputation — not a prayer and a different scion.

Age of understock. A pencil cutting rooted last winter is perfect for
whip and chip-bud this spring. A two-inch trunk is a bark-graft
project. A massive old stool is a two-year topwork. Match the method
to the diameter you actually have, not the diameter in the video.

If you only keep one kind of understock in 8a, keep rooted cuttings of
a variety that already survives your worst night. Boring is a feature.
You can put the interesting wood on top.

One more cut on seedlings: a bird-sown fig under a fence is not a
rootstock program. It is a plant in the wrong place until you decide
otherwise. I have dug those, potted them, and used them as chip-bud
stock the next spring. That is fine. I have also left them and then
grafted in place and discovered the "stool" was two plants twisted
together. Know how many trunks you have before you spend good wood.

If a seller offers "nematode-resistant seedling understock" with no
clone name and no three-year hole in soil like yours, you are buying
a hope. Hope is allowed in a pot at the edge. It is not the tree
that shades the porch.
