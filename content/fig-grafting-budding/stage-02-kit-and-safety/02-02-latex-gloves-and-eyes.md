# Latex, gloves, and your eyes

Fig sap is not a cute rustic detail. The latex is a skin and eye
irritant. Some people welt. Some people do not, until they rub a temple
on a hot day and then they do. I graft in nitrile. I keep water on the
table. I do not touch my face.

Spring grafts spit less than a July prune, but a bark graft on a moving
tree will still weep. The white beads on your wrist are not milk. Rinse.
If you get it in an eye, you rinse a long time and you do not wait to
see if you are "tough." This is the one paragraph in the series that is
not optional.

The knife is sharp because a dull knife slips. You cut toward the body
once, you learn, or you bleed into the parafilm and then you have a
different problem. I sit for chip-buds. I stand for heading cuts and I
use a folding saw, not a knife, on anything over an inch. The saw is
the adult tool. The knife is for the cambium.

Hats. Not style. April sun on the back of your neck while you fuss a
stub for twenty minutes is how you do sloppy last cuts. Shade the work
or do it in the morning.

Kids and dogs. Fresh unions look like toys. A hanging bag is a piñata.
I cage the interesting ones for a week or I lose them to a golden
retriever who "just wanted to see."

Clothes. Latex stains. It also sits in a cuff and finds your wrist
again at lunch. Long sleeves you do not love. I throw grafting shirts
in a pile that does not go near my face towels.

If you are the person who already knows they react to figs — the itch
from picking fruit, the rash from a broken leaf — do not discover your
limits on a four-hour topwork day. Gloves, glasses, and a shorter
session. The tree can wait until Saturday. Your eyelid cannot.

I am not a medic. I am a person who has had sap in the crease of a
finger for a day and did not enjoy it. Treat the latex as the plant's
opinion about being cut. It is allowed to have one. You are allowed to
wear gloves anyway.

An 8a April session is long because the stock is finally moving and
you want to finish the row. That is when people get sloppy with sap.
Set a water jug at the first tree, not at the house. Rinse as you
go. If a child wants to "help hold the bag," give them the labels
and the marker, not the headed stub. The latex on a fresh bark graft
will find a sleeve and then a mouth.

I keep a cheap pair of glasses in the grafting tote. I do not wear
them for chip-buds at a table. I wear them when I am splitting a
stub at belt height. The first chip of bark that jumps is the one
that teaches you. You only need that lesson once if you are lucky.
