# What a dead scion looks like

Hope is not a diagnostic. Figs bluff in both directions. A scion can
look tan and still be alive. A scion can stay green on the surface
and be cooked underneath. You need a small scratch and a little
patience.

Alive: a shallow fingernail scrape shows green or pale green that
is moist. Buds are plump, not caved. On a wrapped chip, the bark
of the chip matches the stock in humidity and has not gone the color
of tea. A petiole left on a green chip yellows and falls without
taking a black crater with it.

Dead: the scrape is brown and dry, or greenish but stringy and sour.
The bark wrinkles like a raisin. The bud is a husk. Under parafilm
you see a dark wet smear or a gray fuzz. The wood is light in your
hand in a way live wood is not — you learn that after you have
held enough of both.

The liar: a scion that pushed a pale shoot, then the shoot collapsed.
The push used whatever water was in the stick. There was never a
union. People call that "it took and then died." It never took. The
stick just spent itself. Scratch the base, not the corpse of the
shoot. If the base is brown, you had a vase of flowers, not a graft.

The other liar: a scion that sits quiet for six weeks and looks
asleep. Chip-buds especially. I have cut off "dead" chips in May
that were green at the cambium and would have flushed in a warm
spell. If the scratch is green, you wait. If you need the stub for
another try, wait until you are sure, then recut into fresh stock.

Storage death versus field death. Fridge-dead wood is often moldy
or slipped. Field-dead wood is often baked — uniform tan, tight to
the tape, no mold, just dry. Same result. Different lecture. Mold
means your bag. Dry means your seal, your sun, or wood that was
already tired.

Do not sniff a scion as your only test. Do scratch. Do wait three
days after a frost or a heat spike before you hold a funeral. And
when it is dead, cut it off clean and try again on fresh wood. A
dead scion left in a cleft is a wick for rot into the stub. I have
left those wicks because I was busy. The stub paid me back in June.

A second pair of scions on a bark-graft stub will not all die on
the same day. Read each one. I have cut off a whole ring because
two were tan and the third was still a sleeper. The sleeper was
the one that became the tree. If you are going to be ruthless,
be precise.

Take a photo of the union on day one. Memory edits color. The
photo does not. When you argue with yourself in week five, use
the picture, not the mood.
