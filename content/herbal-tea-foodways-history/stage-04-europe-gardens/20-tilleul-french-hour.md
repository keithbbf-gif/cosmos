The paper cone from the pharmacie still rustles like a small animal. Inside: pale bracts, a few twigs you are meant to pick out, a smell that is honey and old wardrobe and the tree that shaded the square. French households have a word that does not need a Latin sermon. They say *tilleul*. They mean a kitchen hour.

*Tilia* is the genus. Small-leaved lime, large-leaved lime, the common hybrid that lines a boulevard — *Tilia cordata*, *Tilia platyphyllos*, *Tilia × europaea* — are the trees English also calls linden. The drink is not the timber and not the honey, though both sit in the same summer. The drink is the dried inflorescence: flower, pale wing-like bract, a bit of stalk. Grocery and pharmacy tins in France have sold that cut as an ordinary *tisane* for a long time. I have not inventoried every provincial grocer. I have stood in kitchens where the jar was as unsurprising as sugar.

Hour matters more than protocol. *Tilleul* is what you put on when the meal is over, when a guest is not being offered coffee, when the child is allowed the pale cup and not the adult roast, when January wants something that came from a June tree. Travel writing never gets tired of this scene. The scene is still a foodways object: a pot, a strainer, a handful that has to be cleaned of wood. A café will do the same work with a bag and call it *infusion*. The bag is a later labor transfer. The hour is older than the bag.

I will give Proust one sentence and then leave him alone. In *Du côté de chez Swann*, the madeleine is dipped in aunt Léonie's *tilleul*; the novel uses a domestic lime infusion as the liquid of an ordinary Combray morning, not as a shrine. That is all I need. The rest of the essay is not a fan page. Most people who drink *tilleul* have not read the novel, and the hour does not require a narrator.

What the kitchen actually does is unglamorous. You heat water. You pour it over the blossom. You wait. You fish out the twigs that survived the sort, because a twig in the teeth is a different hospitality from a petal on the tongue. Dried lime is bulky for its weight. A careless handful is all bract and no flower; a factory cut is more even and tastes flatter. People who grew up with a tree they picked themselves will tell you the sachet is not the same drink. I believe them as witnesses of form, not as priests of authenticity. Form is history. A porch tree, a village dryer, a pharmacy paper, a hotel sachet — four labor stories, one French name.

Cafe and home are not rivals. A zinc counter that keeps a tin of *tilleul* next to the coffee is saying that not every customer wants Camellia or a digestif. A parlor that pours it after dinner is saying the same thing with better china. The word *tisane*, which this folder has already dragged back to barley, is the category the hour sits in: the mild pot that is not wine and not coffee. Dictionaries still carry a sickroom aftertaste for that category. I am mentioning the dictionary as a fact about language. I am not writing a protocol for your evening.

Season, again. Lime trees flower in a short early-summer window. You pick on a dry day or you pick a damp disappointment. You dry thin, in the shade, or you brown the lot. Village lime harvests — the kind that get a sentence in a local paper when the trees are municipal — are civic foodways: a public tree, a private jar. I will not invent a tonnage. I will say the obvious: a boulevard of limes is a drink pantry if someone bothers to climb.

English "lime blossom" and German *Lindenblütentee* are the same kitchen object wearing different grammar. The French hour is useful to isolate because it stayed ordinary. It did not need a Boston harbor or a wartime leaflet to explain itself, though wartime cupboards used linden when Camellia was dear — that is a later draft. Here the point is smaller. A plant can be a national habit without being a campaign.

This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent. It is not medical advice. *Tilleul* is a dried blossom, a twig you pick out, and a time of day. If a box in another country has already printed a mood-word over the same flowers, that is the aisle doing its job. This folder will not finish the aisle's sentence.
