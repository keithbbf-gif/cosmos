Fireweed colonizes a burn and a railway cutting with the same indifference. The drink made from its leaves has spent two centuries trying on costumes: cheap stand-in, fraud, peasant pot, then — quite recently, for a lot of drinkers — a fashionable fermented "ancestral" cup. The plant is *Epilobium angustifolium*, also *Chamaenerion angustifolium*, rosebay willowherb, *kiprei*, *Ivan-chai*. The costumes are not the same garment.

Koporye sits west of St. Petersburg, a fortress-village whose name attached itself to a processed leaf. *Koporskii chai*, Koporye tea, is the trade nickname that tea-history retellings keep handing around: fireweed darkened and dried so that it could sit beside Chinese black tea, or sit *in* it, or sit in the place of it when the imported leaf was dear. I have not stood in a Koporye shed and I will not invent a court record. What I can say is the shape of the story as it circulates. Eighteenth- and nineteenth-century Russia had a Camellia habit that outran a Camellia budget for many tables. A local leaf that could be bruised, oxidized, and dried brown was a substitution technology. Substitution becomes fraud at the moment the seller lets the buyer think the crate is Fujian.

Prohibition notices dated to the 1810s — 1816 is the year that popular secondary pieces like to print — circulate in that same literature, sometimes joined by later rural-police stories about forbidding the gathering of the plant for mixing. I am reporting the circulation. I have not opened an imperial *ukaz* and I will not quote a penalty I have not seen on a page. Fiscal logic is easy to understand without a folio: import duties and honest Kyakhta merchants both dislike a brown leaf that dilutes a taxed one. Whether a particular year's notice existed as printed, and what it actually banned (the passing-off, the mixing, the gathering), is the sort of question a specialist in imperial food law can settle. A foodways essay can keep the question visible.

Kalle, Sõukand, and colleagues spent the late 2010s asking a ruder question than the aisle likes. In fieldwork reported around 2020 — the Appetite paper on popularization and the Heliyon paper on the rewritten narrative — the fermented drink that social media calls ancient was, for many of their interviewees, a practice of the previous few years. Dried, unfermented leaves and flowers had shown up sporadically in earlier Slavic ethnographies, and the eighteenth-century fake-black-tea trade is not in dispute as a *kind* of fact. What is thinner is the "everyone always fermented Ivan-chaj as a mass national tradition" line. The 2018 Russian interviews found a sudden recreational use; the internet found a glorious past to dress it in. Hobsbawm and Ranger would recognize the sewing.

That gap is the essay. A plant can be old in a landscape and young as a fashion. Fireweed is a pioneer species; it does not need a myth to be abundant. The myth does other work. It recasts an outcast imitation as a cultural marker, then watches neighboring countries try to claim the marker. The Heliyon group watched that spread along post-Soviet linguistic lines. I am not awarding a nationality to a willowherb. I am noticing that substitution stories, once shamed, can be laundered into heritage if the package is pretty enough.

Method, again, because the costume change is a method change. Boiling a fresh or dried leaf is a pot. Wilting, rolling, oxidizing, and drying a leaf to resemble black Camellia is a small factory, even when the factory is a kitchen table. Contemporary makers talk about a controlled ferment. Nineteenth-century hostile accounts talk about passing and coloring. Those are not one ancestral gesture. If I collapse them, I am doing the viral narrative's job.

I will not give you a Koporye tonnage, a clay-floor recipe presented as archive, or a speech from a merchant I have not read. Subbotin and later tea-trade historians exist; Morinaga's overview of Russian tea culture is a reminder that Camellia itself was a learned habit in Russia, not an eternal samovar essence. Fireweed enters that story as a shadow of the default — the same gravitational field the unmarked-Camellia draft described.

This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent. It is not medical advice. Ivan-chai is a plant name, a fraud-and-substitute history, a village name near St. Petersburg, and a recent fashion that borrowed a deeper past than the interviews support. The leaf can stay. The invented court scene cannot.
