The red flower is not in dispute. *Monarda didyma* grows, smells of citrus-mint, and takes a kettle. The flag people plant on it is another matter. Popular history likes to say that after Boston Harbor took the Camellia in 1773, colonists poured bee balm and called the refusal liberty. Sometimes someone did pour bee balm. The story, as a flag, is partly patriotic work. I will mark it as such and not polish it.

Oswego is a place-name before it is a brand. The river mouth on Lake Ontario — the "pouring-out place" in the Iroquoian geography that English spelled *Oswego* — sat in a Haudenosaunee world and then under a British fort and then under an American town. Garden writers later attached *Monarda* to that name: Oswego tea. Popular pages then invent a tidy nation called "the Oswego tribe" who "taught the settlers." I will not invent an Indigenous quotation to make that tidiness feel kind. Peoples of the Great Lakes and the Northeast used *Monarda* species in their own kitchens and their own registers; the paper trail in English is colonial, thin, and fond of speaking for other people. Absence of a quoted sentence is not absence of a drink. It is a reason to keep my mouth smaller.

Linnaeus named the genus for Nicolás Monardes, a Seville physician who wrote about New World plants he had not seen in the ground. The scarlet bee balm itself moved in the eighteenth-century seed traffic. Later garden histories say John Bartram of Philadelphia sent seed to Europe; I have not held the letter, and I will treat that as a circulating attribution. Once in English and continental gardens the plant acquired other nicknames — bergamot, because the leaf reminds people of the citrus used in Earl Grey, which is a perfume coincidence, not a bloodline. Shaker herb businesses grew *Monarda* among many other plants. A Shaker field is a production site. It is not a harbor myth.

The refusal story needs a wider cupboard if it is going to be honest. "Liberty tea" in American popular writing is a pile: raspberry leaf, currant, goldenrod, loosestrife, and — with better contemporary newspaper legs than bee balm — *Ceanothus americanus*, New Jersey tea. A 1768 *Boston Gazette* item, as tea historians quote it, has a Newburyport circle tasting a shrub infusion and calling it near Bohea. That is a dated hospitality scene. *Monarda* gets written into the same paragraph in museum blogs and Independence Day garden lists. I will not pretend I have a 1774 household book that says "we poured bee balm because of the Tea Act." I will say the plant can take a pot, the boycott was real, and the pairing of this particular red flower with the harbor is a later, satisfying picture.

Satisfying pictures do work. They let a garden center sell a "liberty" six-pack. They let a foodways writer collapse Haudenosaunee knowledge, colonial substitution, and a night in Boston into one moral. The collapse is the problem. A Haudenosaunee or other Indigenous use of *Monarda* — if we are going to mention it at all — is not a prequel that exists to make settlers look humble. A colonial cup of bee balm is a substitution for a taxed import, or a garden experiment, or a pretty pot. A twenty-first-century "Oswego tea" sachet is a heritage flavor. Three foodways. One Latin name.

I placed this draft in the Europe stage because the refusal story is an Atlantic loop: a Camellia habit learned from the British table, a North American plant, a European garden that took the seed, a later American aisle that sold the flag back. The unmarked default is still Camellia. Oswego tea is one of the shadows. The earlier method draft already used it as an example of a receipt that is a clue, not a banner. Here the banner is the subject.

How to read the name, then. Believe the flower. Believe that people infused the leaves. Be slower to believe any sentence that begins "the Indians taught" and then supplies fluent English. Be slower still to believe that a patriotic substitute must have been this plant and not the shrub that got the newspaper. Ask who is unnamed. Ask what was expensive. Ask whether the same drink appears in a garden list with no harbor at all — as a bee plant, as a border perennial, as a Shaker article. Those scenes are the foodways object without a borrowed climax.

This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent. It is not medical advice. The plant is real. The flag is a story. I will not finish the story with a condition, and I will not put words in a mouth that the archive, as I have it, does not give.
