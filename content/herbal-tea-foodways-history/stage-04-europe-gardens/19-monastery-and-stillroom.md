The walled bed is a factory with better manners. A monastery garden, when it is doing its ordinary work, is a production site: labeled plots, a drying rack in a draft, a store of last summer's blossom that will go into a winter pot. The popular caption prefers a different picture — a monk as proto-clinician, the cloister as the origin story of every later sachet. That caption is a medical monastic myth. I am not writing it.

What I can say from public objects is smaller and more useful. The Plan of Saint Gall, drawn on five sewn parchments in the early ninth century, is an idealized Benedictine compound, not a photograph of a built house. Garden historians still use it because it bothers to separate work. There is a *hortus* for kitchen plants, a *pomarius* for orchard, and a *herbularius* of rectangular beds with a plant name written in each. The infirmary and the physician's house sit in the same neighborhood on the parchment. That adjacency is architecture. It is not a license to convert every later lime-blossom cup into a cloister sermon.

Enclosure is the first labor. A wall keeps goats out and a calendar in. Someone has to know which week the flowers are worth cutting, which week they have gone to seed, which week the leaves will blacken if you dry them in a damp corridor. Someone has to copy the labels when the paint flakes. The Plan's beds are a copying problem as much as a planting problem: a name that is not rewritten is a name that dies. Later printed herbals and household books inherit that habit of listing. Listing is not the same as using. A plant can sit in a margin for a century as a word.

I have not walked the Carolingian garden that the Plan imagined, and I will not invent a harvest ledger for it. Real houses were messier. Cistercian and Benedictine houses kept kitchen gardens because they ate. They kept drying rooms because winter is long and a green plant does not keep. They copied, because a rule that wants self-sufficiency still needs a written memory when the brother who knew the beds is buried. Lay workers did a great deal of the stooping. The romance that puts only tonsured hands in the soil is already doing brochure work.

The English stillroom is a later production site, and it is a household room, not a chapel with a still. The name comes from the still — the alembic, the limbeck — used to raise waters and cordials. Inventories of country houses list glass, a furnace, bottles of vinegar, a cupboard. By the seventeenth century the same room was routinely making confectionery as well as scented waters, because the techniques overlap: sugar, heat, a plant part, a vessel that can be stoppered. Mary Evelyn, in a 1674 remark that household historians like because it is blunt, put "stilling" in a list with pigs, cakes, salves, and sweetmeats. That is a work list. It is not a clinic.

Receipt books that came out of those rooms are mixed literature. Cookery, stain-remover, conserve, distilled water, and a sickroom register can share a hand and a binding. I said so in the method draft and I will say it again: the mix is a fact about the book. It does not authorize me to park a modern condition next to a drying screen. When I mention a stillroom manuscript here, I am mentioning a kitchen that also stored scent and household chemistry. I am not listing what the margins thought a drink was *for*.

Copying, again, is labor. A literate woman — or a literate servant, or a neighbor who could write — copied a receipt so a sibling could have it, or so a daughter would not have to remember the sugar weight. Printed "closets" sped the traffic. Manuscripts after print start to change every few decades instead of sitting still for three centuries. That is a history of paper and leisure, not a history of a plant's essence. The surviving books over-represent households that could afford a bound book and time. Oral drying instructions leave thinner paper. If I treat the bound stillroom as the whole European past, I will write a history of people who owned desks.

Season sits under both the cloister wall and the stillroom window. Blossom is a week. Leaf is a month if you are lucky. Root is a different calendar and a dirtier knife. You dry in thin layers, in shade, in a draft, and you lose the work if the weather turns. A French *tilleul* harvest and an English chamomile cut are not "monastery medicine" just because a ninth-century plan had a herb bed. They are the same *kind* of fact as making hay: a short window, a drying problem, a store against a season when the plant is not in flower.

What the two rooms share, if they share anything I am willing to claim, is this: they are places where a household or a house of religion turned a garden surplus into a pantry object. Dried plants used in drinks and cookery are pantry objects. They can later be sold in a Hamburg crate or a grocery tin. The tin likes a monk on the label. The monk on the label is branding. Stage 4 will meet that branding again in Alpine shops. It is not evidence that a particular infusion was born in a scriptorium.

This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent any disease. It is not medical advice. A walled garden is a labor map. A stillroom is a room. If a later sentence needs a monk to bless a sachet, fail the sentence. The beds can stay. The blessing cannot.
