The elder does not give you a month. It gives you a fortnight if the weather holds, three weeks if you live far enough north and the heads open late. *Sambucus nigra* — the European elder of hedges, railway cuttings, and the damp corner of a churchyard — is a foraging calendar before it is a flavor. Miss the cream plates and you are waiting a year, or you are buying a bottle that remembers June for you.

Flower and berry are different kitchen objects. I want that sentence nailed to the cupboard door, because the grocery aisle and a certain kind of winter story like to mash them. The flowers arrive in late spring to early summer: flat cymes, pollen that dusts your cuffs, a smell that is honey and cat and citrus if you are generous. They want to be used quickly. Cordial, a sparkling "champagne" that is really a wild ferment if you let it, fritters (*beignets de fleurs de sureau*, elderflower fritters in an English pudding basin), a pale infusion if you only have a kettle. The berries arrive later, dark, and they are a preserve problem: jam, wine, a cooked syrup. Cooks who work with European elder treat the ripe fruit as something you heat. I am stating a kitchen caution, not a clinic. Leaves, bark, and green berries are not this essay's pantry.

A short season trains a household. You walk the same hedge you walked in March for something else. You pick dry heads, not the ones beaten by rain, and you keep them upright so the pollen does not all sit in the bag. You do not strip the shrub if you also want fruit, because a flower taken is a berry not born. That trade is foodways: two harvests, one plant, a decision. Woodland Trust notes and ordinary country columns repeat the same window — late May into June or mid-July depending on the island and the year. I am repeating a calendar, not a secret.

Cordial is the British and Irish fame of the flower: sugar, water, lemon, a night or more of steeping, a bottle that will meet soda water in August. Fritters are the older show-off: a head dipped in batter, fried, eaten before the scent dies. The infusion is the quiet third — hot water on fresh or dried blossom — and it is the one the aisle later bagged. All three are June work even when you drink them in November. A dried flower is a stored season. A berry syrup is a different stored season. If a winter tin prints the shrub without saying which part you bought, the tin is being lazy.

I will not write the essay the internet wants, the one that parks the flower next to a winter complaint and calls the hedge a pharmacy. Household literature had a sickroom register; this series has already admitted that. The destination here is the calendar. People pick elder because it is suddenly everywhere and then it is gone. That is a reason. Thirst in heat, a fete, a child's first foraging bruise, a restaurant that wants a floral note in a glass without buying a perfume house — those are reasons. "For" a condition is a different job.

Identity stays messy at the edges. American elder (*Sambucus canadensis*) and blue elder have their own kitchens. Red elder and dwarf elder are plants cooks are told to leave alone. I have not botanized every roadside. "Elderflower" in a British supermarket cordial is, as a rule, the European shrub or a flavor built to recall it. "Elderberry" in a winter bottle is the fruit side. Do not let a shared genus become a shared recipe.

Labor hides in the pleasantness. Picking is scratches. Shaking insects out of a head is not photogenic. Stripping flowers from stems for a large cordial batch is a sink full of tiny pale stars and a backache. Commercial cordial made the season available to people who do not have a hedge, which is a kindness and a flattening. A café that serves "elderflower" in January is serving sugar, acid, and a memory of a fortnight. That is still hospitality. It is not the hedge.

This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent. It is not medical advice. *Sambucus* flowers are a short season: cordial, fritter, infusion. The berries are another season and another pot. If a sentence needs a winter illness to make the hedge interesting, the sentence is in the wrong folder.
