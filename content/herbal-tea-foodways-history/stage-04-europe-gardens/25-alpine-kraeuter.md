The word *Alpenkräuter* on a box is doing landscape work before it does kitchen work. It wants you to smell hay and see a peak that has never had a road. Then it wants you to buy a blend. I do not mind the purchase. I mind the compression that turns pasture labor into a postcard and a postcard into a cure-all.

Alpine herbs exist. Meadows exist. People cut them. The brand is the extra noun. Swiss, Austrian, Bavarian, South Tyrolean, and Engadine shops have spent a century learning that a gentian or a lady's-mantle or a handful of mint looks more expensive if the Alps are standing behind it. Tourist railways, hotel balconies, and later ski weeks taught a public to want a portable meadow. The portable meadow arrived as a tin, a drop, a syrup, a bath salt, and a tea bag. Ricola's herbal-drop Alps are not this essay's cup, but they are cousins in the same advertising weather: edelweiss as a logo, a choir of peaks, a promise that altitude is an ingredient.

Monastery-brand packaging is a second weather. Convent shops — St. Johann at Müstair is a living example you can click — sell *Kräutertee* blends with house names, sometimes with cultivated edelweiss in the ingredients list, sometimes with lemon balm and sage that would grow in any valley garden. A UNESCO cloister is allowed to have a shop. The shop is not proof that a ninth-century sister dried this exact blend. It is proof that "kloster" still sells. The monk on an Alpine sleeve and the monk on a stillroom-myth sleeve are the same graphic. Stage 4 keeps meeting him.

Hay-meadow romance is the third weather, and the one that most needs a sweat stain. An *Alm* or *alpage* is a work site: animals moved up for summer, grass cut, hay dried on racks, weather as an enemy, a season that is not a wellness retreat. The plants that end up in a "meadow tea" are a selection from that work, or they are a lowland farm standing in for it, or they are a Bio-Suisse plot in the Simmental planted because a 1991 company needed a story and a dryer. Swiss Alpine Herbs in Därstetten, to take a dated public example, processes mountain-farmer herbs into teas and spices and offers Wednesday tours to people with guest cards. That is contemporary agro-tourism. It is not a folk dawn.

I will keep the labor visible because the brand keeps deleting it. Someone weeds. Someone harvests before the flower goes over. Someone dries without cooking the leaf. Payment may be a side income that lets a farm stay a farm. That sentence is already more honest than "gifts of the Alps." When the same blend is cut in a valley industrial park, the peak on the box is a rented view. Foodways uncompresses: name the cut, name the shop, name the uncertainty.

What is in the cup is usually modest. Mint, lemon balm, yarrow, lady's-mantle, sage, mallow, a flower for color — the European cupboard, not a rare cliff. Edelweiss in a tea is often a cultivated cameo. The blend tastes like hay if the hay plants are in it, and like mint if the mint won. A hotel breakfast in Zermatt or Innsbruck that offers "Alpenkräuter" is offering a hospitality category: the not-coffee, not-Camellia pot for the guest who wants the landscape to enter the room. That is a real use. It becomes a dishonest one when the sleeve implies that altitude itself is doing a clinical job, or that a monastery garden certified the blend in a century the printer cannot name.

I have not audited every hut. I have watched the same vocabulary travel: *Alpenkräuter*, *Almkräuter*, *herbes des Alpes*, *tisana alpina*. Trade associations already keep Camellia and herbal infusions in two bins. The Alpine adjective is a third bin that marketing invented inside the second. It is a geography claim. Sometimes the geography is true. Sometimes it is a font.

This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent. It is not medical advice. An Alpine blend is a landscape brand, a tourist object, and — when the hay was actually cut — a record of pasture work. It is not a cure-all, and this folder will not let the peak do that work by silhouette.
