Ask for *mate* in a Cusco kitchen and you have not yet named a plant.

You have named a kettle habit. Andean Spanish uses *mate* for an infusion in a cup long before a tourist learns that the word is also a Paraguayan holly and a gourd. *Mate de coca* is one drink. *Mate de hierbas* is another — a string-tied bundle from the market, or a pinch of *muña*, or *manzanilla*, or a mix the seller swore was for after the meal. The grammar is household. The plants are not interchangeable. Collapsing them into "herbal tea at altitude" is how the aisle does the Andes with a suitcase.

*Muña* (*Minthostachys*, in the keys I have seen) is the sharp, oregano-mint smell that follows a heavy lunch in a lot of highland kitchens. *Manzanilla* is chamomile under a Spanish name, a table flower as ordinary as it is in a Madrid cupboard. Anise, *hierba luisa*, a twist of something the bundle does not label — markets sell mixtures the way European stillrooms once mixed a drawer, without owing you a Latin sermon. After a meal is a table hour. I will believe the hour as a foodway: the plates are cleared, the pot is already hot, a child is allowed the milder cup. Feeling like drinking it is a use. "Feeling" is not a diagnosis.

Coca leaf is the object that will try to hijack the paragraph.

*Erythroxylum* leaf, toasted or dried, poured over in a glass, is a household infusion in much of the Andes. It is also a political and legal object. National law in Bolivia and Peru does not tell the same story as a customs schedule in Washington or a United Nations list. People chew leaf with an alkali, offer it, tax it, fight over it, and also just make *mate de coca* because that is what the house drinks. Isolated alkaloid chemistry is a different industry with different cops. This folder will describe the kettle and the contest. It will not write a drug scare. It will not write a salvation caption. It will not hand you an arrival protocol for thin air. Altitude is a geography the kettle lives in — hotels press a cup on the newly arrived; night buses smell like leaf — and geography is not a dosage chart.

I have not kept a kitchen in La Paz. I am working from the public face of the drinks: restaurant menus, market bundles, travel writing that I will treat as travel writing, and the obvious legal headlines a reader can open without my help. Traditional use is evidence of practice. It is not permission for me to launder a contested commodity into a wellness caption, or for me to sneer a household out of its cup because a treaty is louder than a grandmother.

The market bundle is the foodways object I want on the table. A woman in a stall with a hanging scale does not owe the European two-bin model a speech. She is selling smell, habit, and a price. *Mate de hierbas* can be cheaper than coca, or mixed with it, or a substitute when the leaf is awkward to carry across a border. Substitution again — not for *Camellia* this time, or not only, but for a sister infusion that politics has made heavy. A traveler who cannot take leaf home still takes *muña*. That is a legal geography in a suitcase, not a conversion story.

Compare the hardware to mate's gourd and you will see a different social form. Andean *mate* in this sense is often a personal cup, a pot for the table, a thermos for a climb. Sharing happens; it is not the *cebador*'s circuit. The word overlap is a linguistic weather system: Quechua *mati* helped name a vessel; Spanish then used *mate* for almost any steeped household drink. Foodways has to live with the overlap without pretending the holly and the coca leaf are one plant. They share an hour more than they share a botany.

There is a class fact inside the after-meal pot. *Manzanilla* is cheap and imported-or-local depending on the town. *Muña* can be gathered or bought. Coca leaf carries prestige, stigma, sacrament, and police in different ratios by neighborhood. A hotel breakfast that offers all three is performing hospitality for visitors who have read one paragraph about elevation. A household that only ever boils *manzanilla* is not failing a tradition. It is keeping a kettle.

This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent. It is not medical advice. I will not tell you which bundle to pack, and I will not translate a table drink into a modern indication because a guidebook did. If a later sentence in this series starts handing out altitude instructions, fail it. The Andes in this folder are kitchens, markets, and a leaf that governments cannot stop arguing about. The argument is historical. The cup is ordinary. Ordinary is enough.
