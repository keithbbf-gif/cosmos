Smell the root first, then argue about the century.

*Sassafras albidum* announces itself: mitten leaves, a citrus-spice bark, a tree of eastern woodlots that children can name before they can spell it. The foodways object is not "a tea" in the *Camellia* sense. It is a flavor that kept changing jobs — pot herb, export crate, soda-fountain ghost, a powder stirred into a stew at the end so it does not go stringy. If I let the word *tea* run the essay, I will miss the kitchen.

Start with the leaf, which never needed a kettle. Louisiana kitchens still grind young sassafras leaves as filé, a thickener and a perfume for gumbo. Writers argue, productively, about how much of that practice is Choctaw, how much is a later Creole synthesis, and whether *gumbo* itself answers to a West African word for okra or to a Choctaw word for the leaf. I will not referee an etymology I have not sat with in three languages. I will keep the bowl. A powder of leaves is a flavor history. It is also why sassafras belongs in an Americas stage that is supposed to get beyond the tin: the plant was food before English needed it to be a drink.

The root and the root-bark are the parts that boarded ships. Early Virginia paperwork treats sassafras as a cargo beside tobacco. Company instructions and later court-book lines name it as something a vessel should carry home. I have not held those ledgers. I will not invent a rank ("second only to…") that popular captions love and specialists keep trimming. What I can say without a brochure voice is smaller: sixteenth- and seventeenth-century European demand made a southeastern tree into a commodity. Spanish, French, and English shops knew a New World bark with a loud smell. The Timucua name *pauame* shows up in the careful commodity histories; *sassafras* is the word that traveled. Shop goods pick up medical rumors the way wool picks up burrs. This folder will not unpack those rumors. The crate is the plot.

Why, then, does the tree keep showing up in "tea" stories?

Because colonists and later households poured water over the root-bark and called the result tea, or root tea, in the elastic English way this series has already complained about. Because the smell taught a generation of soda fountains what "root beer" should be. Charles Hires's late-nineteenth-century syrup is one famous American packaging of a flavor that was already in kitchens; I will not make him the inventor of a forest. Because wartime and hard-times cupboards, here as with yaupon and roasted grain, reached for what the woodlot had when the crate did not. The kettle is a late, English-shaped chapter of a longer flavor life.

Safrole is the twentieth-century bureaucratic chapter, and I want it kept as food-additive history. Safrole is a natural constituent of sassafras oil. In 1960 the U.S. Food and Drug Administration refused added safrole, oil of sassafras, and related flavor compounds in food; the order sits in the *Federal Register* for 3 December 1960 (25 FR 12412), and the prohibition is now restated in 21 C.F.R. § 189.180. The same rule treats sassafras bark offered mainly as a vehicle for those substances — "sassafras tea" as a grocery idea — as adulterated food. I am quoting a category, not writing a scare. Commercial root beer learned to smell like itself without the old oil. That is a flavor-industry fact. It is not a clinic, and it is not a reason to park a disease name next to a childhood mug.

I will be blunt about the temptation. Popular tree essays want to climax in a ban and a warning. A claims-guarded foodways series can say that a regulator reclassified a flavoring. It cannot do the other essay. If you need the toxicology, you need a different folder and a person who knows you. If you need to understand why a supermarket root beer still winks at a woodlot, stay here. The wink is historical. The woodlot is still a woodlot.

There is a class story inside the smell. Filé in a home pot is cheap if you have leaves and time. An early modern crate of root was an investor's object. A soda fountain in 1890 sold a democratized perfume of the crate. A twenty-first-century "sassafras tea" on a wellness shelf is often a nostalgia brand standing in a legal awkwardness it would rather not print. Form is history. A gumbo finish, a colonial hogshead, a paper label with a mitten leaf — these are not the same foodway wearing different hats.

This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent. It is not medical advice. I am writing about a flavor that got drafted into the English word *tea* because hot water is a common tool, not because sassafras is a cousin of Yunnan leaf. Keep the leaf in the stew. Keep the crate on the quay. Keep the fountain syrup in the nineteenth century. Leave the body to someone who has met you. The Americas beyond the tin include plants that were never trying to be an aisle.
