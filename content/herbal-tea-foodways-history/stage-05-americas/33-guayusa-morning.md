Three in the morning is a social hour if the pot is already on.

*Ilex guayusa* is a caffeine holly of the western Amazon — eastern Ecuador first in the stories that reach English, with reports into Peru and Colombia along the Andean piedmont. It is kin to mate and yaupon, sweeter in the travel-writing cliché, still a holly, still not *Camellia*. I will not invent a village. I will not walk you into a house I have not entered. What I can say is that travel accounts and ethnography widely report a dawn gathering around a pot: Kichwa *wayusa upina* (the spellings wander), a fire, talk, the day's work named out loud, dreams told to people who already know the teller. That is a reported social form. It varies. It is not a franchise.

A 2025 *PLOS One* paper on Napo Kichwa cropping and brewing is the sort of public object I mean when I say "widely reported" without pretending I did the interviews: families still know how to plant and pick; consumption has also moved into restaurants and other hours; export has changed the garden. Earlier tourism and anthropology pieces describe the same dawn pot. Jesuit-era Quito shops, in the secondary histories, sold the leaf at a luxury price after first scolding it. I have not held those account books. A Tiwanaku-period burial in the Bolivian highlands has been published as containing *Ilex guayusa* leaves — a long-distance hint, not a morning circle I can reconstruct. The trail is real and thin in different places. I will not thicken it with a scene.

The form that matters for foodways is ordinary and specific. A pot, not a gourd circuit like mate (though hardware travels). Leaf boiled or infused, depending on the household. Served, in the accounts, by people with a job in the room — often women of the house — into bowls or *pilches*. Children present. Elders talking. Weaving or net-mending in the same hour, in some descriptions. Chili in the eye as discipline shows up in the same literature; I mention it as a reported companion practice, not as color, and I will not make it the climax. The climax is the pot as a clock. Morning is not a brand. Morning is when you say what the day will be.

Then the bottle arrived, late.

English-language commerce learned guayusa as an "energy" leaf in the twenty-first century. Company histories and later reviews place a social-enterprise export push around 2008–2010 (RUNA is the name the papers keep using); Ecuadorian export tallies in the 2010s send leaf to the United States and then to a longer list of buyers. I will not invent a first-crate date more precise than "late, and after mate was already a global curiosity." The export object is a caffeine holly in a can, a carton, a sachet — sometimes blended, sometimes sweetened, sometimes standing in a cooler next to drinks that have never seen a dawn fire. Official production notes from Napo, Pastaza, and Orellana describe agroforestry lots and smallholder kilos. Those are livelihood facts. They are not the circle.

The export bottle is not the morning gathering. I will keep saying that until the caption stops collapsing them. A Kichwa household can sell leaf on Tuesday and still sit to a pot on Wednesday; commodification is not a morality play with one ending. It is also not proof that the bottle "preserves tradition." Tradition, if we need the word, is the hour and the talk. The bottle is a market. Foodways can hold both without making the market the ancestor.

Compare the three hollies and the aisle gets less clever. Yaupon was filed under a vomit-name and a substitute-tea. Mate kept a national hourly life and then grew a Berlin latte. Guayusa was, for a long time, almost invisible in English grocery language, then suddenly visible as a rainforest stimulant with a story attached. The story is the expensive part of the carton. The plant is an *Ilex*. This series will not do caffeine cheerleading. It will not do a productivity caption. It will say: there is a reported morning form, and there is a late export form, and they share a leaf the way a wedding glass and a street bag of hibiscus share a calyx — which is to say, not enough to be the same sentence.

I have not been to Napo. I am working from papers, travel prose, and the honesty rule that a travelogue is a traveler's sentence. If a Kichwa writer wants to tell me the dawn hour is overstated, or understated, or belongs more to tourism than to Tuesday, I will listen. Until then I will refuse two lies: the lie that there was no social drink until a brand arrived, and the lie that the brand is the drink.

This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent. It is not medical advice. A holly pot at dawn is hospitality and talk. It is not a protocol for your jet lag, and the export cooler is not a clinic. If you came for an energy origin myth, the folder will give you a kettle and a late carton instead.
