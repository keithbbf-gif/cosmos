The Latin name is already an argument, and a bad one.

*Ilex vomitoria* is a coastal holly: small serrated leaves, red winter berries, thickets from Virginia around the Gulf. It is, as Florida's Division of Historical Resources says in a public foodways note, the only plant native to the southeastern United States that carries caffeine. That is geography plus botany. It is not a dare, not a grocery warning label, and not a finding about a body I have not met. The plant is a holly. The drink is a social object. The epithet is a colonial scar.

English *yaupon* is usually traced to a Catawban tree-and-leaf word. I am repeating a dictionary trail, not a lesson I took on a river. Sixteenth-century Spanish more often wrote *cacina* or *cassina*, borrowed from Timucua. Later English preferred *black drink*, because a strong brew is dark in the cup. The same Florida note, sitting in a Hudson-school literature, says that Apalachee, Creek, Seminole, and Muskogee speakers whose homelands included north Florida called the hospitality a *white drink*, for its association with peace and purity — not because the liquid was pale. One holly. Several names. The names are doing the work this series cares about: who is in the room, what the hour is for, which language got to file the plant.

I will not write the sensational version. You have met it. A colonial witness watches a council. Someone vomits. The holly is sentenced. William Aiton put *Ilex vomitoria* into *Hortus Kewensis* in 1789, and the insult stuck to the binomial the way a bad nickname sticks to a family. Charles Hudson's *Black Drink: A Native American Tea* (1979), the later Palumbo–Talcott–Putz *Economic Botany* note (2009), and Hammett's horticultural reminders out of UF/IFAS keep repeating a duller point: an ordinary infusion of toasted leaves is not a grocery emetic. Historic vomiting reports sit next to quantity, fasting, other plants in the pot, and the demands of a ceremony. Quantity and mix are kitchen facts. They are not a personality of the species. They are not a reason to make this essay a purge story.

What the historic record will support, if we stay with hospitality, is a drink for councils, for guests, for Green Corn and other annual gatherings, and — in the Florida Department of State account of Apalachee practice — for preparation before a ballgame. Marine-shell cups show up often enough in southeastern descriptions that the vessel is part of the foodway. Toasted leaves, hot water, a dark liquor, a person poured for and a person doing the pouring. I have not sat in those councils. Oral cultures leave thinner paper than a Linnaean epithet. Absence of a colonial notebook is not absence of a cup.

Then Cahokia, which is the argument that will not stay on the coastal plain.

Patricia Crown and colleagues, in *PNAS* in 2012 ("Ritual Black Drink consumption at Cahokia"), reported *Ilex* biomarkers — caffeine, theobromine, ursolic acid — absorbed into beakers from Cahokia and nearby sites in the American Bottom, dated about A.D. 1050–1250. The paper places those vessels some 500 to 650 kilometers overland north of the historic natural range it cites for *I. vomitoria*. *I. cassine*, dahoon, would be farther still. The residues cannot, the authors say, settle *vomitoria* versus *cassine*, or a mix. They can argue that holly drinks were in those beakers centuries before Spanish ink, and that the leaves or the bushes had to travel: trade in dried leaf, cultivation beyond the later mapped range, or both. University of Illinois flotation, they note, had not found carbonized *Ilex* in nearly 10,000 liters of Mississippian soil. The chemical argument is not a stump in a garden bed. I have not counted the beakers. I am reporting a published argument, not a dig I performed.

That is already a foodways plot without a clinic in it: a stimulating holly, a vessel, a distance, a name fight, a trade-or-garden problem. It is also a cousin story. *Ilex paraguariensis* and *Ilex guayusa* are caffeine hollies with their own hours and hardware. *Camellia* does not own the idea of a morning cup. Yaupon looks like a surprise only if the unmarked English default is allowed to hog caffeine and then lend the leftover word *tea* to everything else.

Colonists later drank cassina as Carolina tea, South-Sea tea, Appalachian tea — cheap stand-ins when the imported caddy was taxed or simply not there. Substitution is a plot this folder already knows. The nineteenth-century southern cupboard that reached for local holly when the crate failed is a cousin of roasted grain and darkened fireweed, not a secret origin for a wellness aisle. A twenty-first-century grocery revival is a new tin on an old plant. None of those afterlives erase the council, the guest, or the beaker.

I keep the fence where the epithet wants to drag the sentence. This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent. It is not medical advice. A naming scar is historical evidence about observers. It is not a property list for your kettle, and it is not a ritual I am inviting you to reenact from a screen. If you came for a purge, or for a native "super-caffeine" caption, you are in the wrong folder. The folder will stay with hospitality, ceremony, and the awkward fact that a holly reached the Mississippi centuries before English needed a color-word for the cup.
