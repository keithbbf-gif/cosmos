The paper trail for a northern kettle is often a man who did not grow up with the shrub.

Trader journals, Hudson's Bay factor notes, missionary letters — those are the pages English readers still meet first when they want "Labrador tea on the trapline." The drink is older than those pages. The pages are not a village census. I will say so every time the temptation is to treat a Company pot as an origin story. A factor who learned to like a bog leaf has given us a sentence. He has not given us the names the leaf already had.

The plant, when people mean the thing in a Quebec or James Bay muskeg, is a low evergreen of wet, acid ground: leaves rolled at the margin, undersides woolly, white at first and then rusty. Older books file it under *Ledum*. Newer keys put the cluster in *Rhododendron*. *Rhododendron groenlandicum* is the bog Labrador tea of a thousand camp captions. *R. tomentosum* is the more northerly cousin. A western shrub still called trapper's tea in some guides is another relative, not a synonym. English also says Hudson's Bay tea, muskeg tea, swamp tea. Those are camp names. They are not a species certificate.

Identity is the first kitchen problem, and it is a food-safety problem before it is a romance. The heath family, Ericaceae, is generous with lookalikes that share a bog and a silhouette. A person in a hurry can pick the wrong shrub. Nancy Turner's note in *The Canadian Encyclopedia*, and a stack of modern field guides, urge caution about identity and about quantity. I will leave the caution there. This is not a foraging manual. It is not a clinic. I will not name a damage. I will not turn a winter pot into a dare, and I will not write the cure story that some eighteenth-century post journals could not resist.

What the trapline picture actually holds, when you strip the calendar art, is a portable drink. Leaves that keep. A pot that can sit on a small fire between sets of traps. Aromatic, resinous, bitter in a way that reminds the mouth it is not *Camellia*. Indigenous households across a very large north — Dene, Cree, Inuit, and others — have their own names and rules. I have not collected those names, and I will not invent a single "Native tea" out of three language families. The written English that reached libraries often belongs to Company men and missionaries who borrowed the pot and then explained the country. Borrowing is not authorship. Explanation is not a census.

Secondary notes like to quote Hudson's Bay commentators on a "pleasant" *Ledum* infusion, "much in use" at the coast. I am working from those later notes, not from a post journal I held. Samuel Hearne's generation of travelers sits in that stack. So do Moravian missions on the Labrador coast, whose ink is thick on harbors and thin, in the pages I have, on the shrub's older names. The same notebooks sometimes wander into a sickroom register — a pint a day, a nervous complaint, a boast. This folder will not follow them. A camp drink is a camp drink. Traditional use, here, is evidence that people prepared and shared a liquid. It is not a finding.

Labor hides in the leaf. The shrubs are slow. Guides that bother with harvest etiquette talk about taking little and leaving the plant standing. That is ecology as kitchen sense, not a virtue slogan. Drying a winter store is household work: a string, a loft if you have one, a tin if you don't. Calling the result "tea" is the *Camellia* shadow again — the pause, the hot water, the noun that traveled farther than the crate. Foodways should keep the noun and the plant in separate hands. The hour on the trapline is a hospitality hour even when the only guest is the person who set the next trap.

Substitution sits in the story whether we like it or not. When the imported caddy was dear, absent, or a rumor at a post, a bog shrub filled a cup. When coffee was a luxury counted by the ounce, the same shrub did. That does not make Labrador tea a grocery "herbal tea" waiting for a canoe illustration on a box. It makes it a northern foodway that English later filed under a borrowed word. The aisle likes a caption about wilderness purity. The actual pot is wet boots and a decision about which leaf you are sure of.

I keep a pencil for the identity fights. *Ledum* in an 1850 book and *Rhododendron* in a 2020 key are the same argument in different decades. "Trapper's tea" in the Rockies may not be the Labrador shrub a child picked in a Quebec bog. If a draft cannot survive the sentence "as named in the source," it should not drop a Latin binomial with a straight face. Confusion inside Ericaceae is not a trivia item. It is why the modern guides sound nervous, and why a claims-guarded series will not reward the reader with a harvest recipe.

This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent. It is not medical advice. I am writing about a kettle that moved with winter labor, and about the kind of paper that recorded it from one side of the fire. If you came for a wilderness protocol, or for a plant that "the Natives always used for" a complaint, you will have to go to a different literature. The literature here will stay with the pot, the naming mess, the borrowed English noun, and the unshowy fact that a trapline drink is still a foodway when no one is watching.
