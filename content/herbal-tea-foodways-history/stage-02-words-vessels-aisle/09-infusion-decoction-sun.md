Method is not a personality. It is a decision about heat, time, and the part of the plant you bothered to keep.

An **infusion**, in kitchen English, is what you do with something that will give itself up to hot water without a long boil: petals, many leaves, a bag. You pour, you wait, you strain or you don't. A **decoction** is what you do when the thing is tougher — a root, a seed, a grain, a calyx you want to bully — and you keep it on the fire. The two words come from cookery and from pharmacy cabinets; I am using them as cookery. They describe labor, not virtue.

A foodways series that blurs them will lie about work. Japanese *mugicha* is roasted barley boiled or steeped depending on the household and the weather; the roast is already a method before water arrives. Nigerian *zobo* is commonly described as a boil of roselle with ginger and whatever the seller has, then cooled. French *tilleul* is often a pour-over of dried blossom. Moroccan *atay* is a boiled or repeatedly poured green tea with fresh mint and a frightening amount of sugar — a hybrid method, Camellia plus herb, not a sachet. If I call all of those "steeping tea," I have flattened four kitchens into a stock photo.

Cold and sun methods belong on the same list. "Sun tea" in late-twentieth-century North America — a jar on a porch, bags hanging like tired flags — is a domestic fashion with food-safety arguments attached that I will not litigate here. I mention it because it is a method people actually used, and because it shows that "herbal tea" in American English was willing to leave the kettle. Hibiscus drinks in the Sahel and the Caribbean are often cooled on purpose: the point is thirst in heat, spice, sugar, ice if you have it. A hot linden in January and a cold bissap in May are not the same foodway wearing different hats. Temperature is part of the meal.

Fermentation is a method too, and a troublemaker. Koporye fireweed was processed to look and taste nearer to black Camellia: bruising, oxidizing, drying, sometimes — if the hostile nineteenth-century accounts are right — tricks with color. Contemporary Ivan-chaj makers talk about a controlled wilt and a dry. Kalle and Sõukand's fieldwork suggests that the fashionable fermented drink in parts of Eastern Europe is, for many drinkers, recent. I will not collapse "people boiled fireweed" and "people ran a mini tea factory on fireweed" into one ancestral gesture. The second is a craft with a learning curve. The first is a pot.

Roasting sits beside fermentation as a way to make a non-Camellia drink brown, fragrant, and bitter-sweet. Barley, buckwheat (*sobacha*), chicory, and various wartime grains got the oven or the pan because brown is what "tea" and "coffee" had taught the eye. Color is foodways. A pale chamomile and a dark roasted barley do not occupy the same hour of the day in the same house.

I want one caution about method-talk. Wellness copy loves to imply that a long decoction is "stronger" in a moral sense, or that a delicate infusion is "gentler." Those adjectives are doing a job this folder will not hire. Longer heat changes flavor, color, and what dissolves. That is cooking. It is not a finding about a body I have not met. This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent. It is not medical advice. If a source says a household boiled a root for an hour, I will believe the hour as a kitchen fact. I will not translate the hour into a dose.

Vessels follow method. A tall glass for mint and sugar, a small handleless cup for a bitter holly, a saucepan for barley, a thermos for a commute, a gourd for mate, a shell cup in a southeastern council — these are not interchangeable "mugs." A later draft will stay with the objects. Here I only need the rule: if you change the vessel, you have often changed the drink, even if the plant Latin stays still.

When you read the rest of the folder, punish me if I write "brewed" for everything. *Brewed* is a blur word, beer and tea and a bag in a microwave. I will use it when a source uses it. I will try to use the more honest verb when I have one. The honest verb is the one a person could argue about in a kitchen: did you boil it or did you pour?
