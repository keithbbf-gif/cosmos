Industry prose is rarely lovely. It is sometimes more honest than a lifestyle caption.

Tea & Herbal Infusions Europe — THIE — sits in Hamburg and says, in the flat voice of a trade association, that tea comes from *Camellia sinensis* L. Kuntze, and that herbal and fruit infusions are something else: popular, various, convenient, and not the same plant. The association in its present name dates to 22 January 2015. It is the successor of the European Tea Committee and the European Herbal Infusions Association, which had been around, as the current site puts it, for more than sixty years under older letterheads. I am repeating their self-description, not awarding them a monopoly on truth. I am interested in the fact that European trade bothered to keep two bins.

Why does a foodways series care about a Hamburg office?

Because categories that stay separate in trade law and customs paperwork are the categories that marketing keeps trying to glue. "Herbal tea" is a glue-word. It lets a sachet borrow the dignity and the habit of Camellia — the kettle ritual, the afternoon, the word *tea* itself — while also borrowing, when convenient, the freedom of a non-tea plant to be sold as a mood. THIE's page is not a moral document. It is a reminder that processors, packers, and regulators have long needed to say which plant they mean when they fill a crate.

The older herbal-infusions association existed because there was already a European industrial object: dried lime blossom, peppermint, fennel, hibiscus, rosehip, and the blends that taste like a cupboard. That object had food-safety problems, pesticide residues, and labeling fights long before Instagram found chamomile. A claims-guarded history should notice the factory. A paper mint is not a handful of leaves from a courtyard. A hibiscus blend packed in Germany may be calyces grown in Sudan or Nigeria or China. The cup in a Düsseldorf kitchen can be an African or Asian harvest with a European cut and a multilingual sleeve.

I do not have THIE's internal minutes, and I will not invent them. What I can say from the public face is modest: the association talks about food safety, standards, and research as its job. That is the voice of a commodity sector that wants to stay a food. Food is a jurisdiction. Dietary supplements are another. Drugs are another. When a sachet starts talking like a third thing, it is not because Hamburg got poetic. It is because the aisle pays better for a story than for a plant part.

There is a second, quieter honesty in the two-bin model. Fruit infusions — rosehip, apple, hibiscus-as-fruit-adjacent tartness — get their own phrase. That matters in a series that will spend a stage on roselle. *Hibiscus sabdariffa* calyces are food in Senegal and Jamaica before they are a European "herbal tea." Calling the crate "fruit infusion" at least admits acidity and color, which are kitchen facts. Calling it "tea" admits only the kettle.

I will use THIE the way I use a dictionary: as a public categorization, dated, interested, incomplete. It does not settle indigenous names. It does not settle whether a Japanese roasted-barley drink should be filed under grain or under *cha*. It does not bless a plant. It does prove that, in late-twentieth- and early-twenty-first-century Europe, enough money moved through non-Camellia infusions to justify a letterhead.

Compare that with the American grocery phrase. In a U.S. supermarket, "herbal tea" is often a shelf talker, not a legal class with a Hamburg address. The same parent company may pack Camellia and chamomile in identical envelopes. The sameness is the product. A foodways reader should see the envelope as a historical artifact: the tea bag taught the herbal infusion how to look like tea. We will get to the bag in the next draft. The point here is prior: once the package is identical, the plant difference has to be restated on purpose or it disappears.

This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent. It is not medical advice. A trade association is not a clinic and not a villain in a cape. It is a place where someone had to write down that mint is not Camellia. I would like the rest of the folder to stay at least that dull, and that clear.
