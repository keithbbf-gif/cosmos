Before there was a "herbal tea" aisle, there was a problem of portion.

Loose leaf — Camellia or otherwise — wants a caddy, a spoon, a strainer, and a person who has done this before. A household that already drank tea had those tools. A household that wanted a single cup of mint at a desk did not. The tea bag, patented and fought over in the early twentieth century and industrialized after, solved a Camellia problem: how to sell a measured, tidy, fast infusion to people who were not going to own a pot. Once that solution existed, every other dried plant in the cupboard could climb into the same envelope.

I am not going to pretend I have the first chamomile bag's invoice. The sequence that matters for foodways is mechanical, not mythical. The bag taught a shape. The tin taught a pantry life. The aisle taught a neighborhood: herbal to the left of black, decaf as a cousin, "wellness" as a later paint job. You can watch the neighborhood change without opening a single clinical paper. Look at the box. Look at who is pictured. Look at whether the plant is named in Latin, in English, or in a mood.

The tin is older than the bag as a storage idea. Apothecary and grocer tins kept dried leaves dry. Shakers and Moravians and ordinary farm households used crocks and paper. What the twentieth-century branded tin added was a face: a company, a blend name, a promise of sameness from box to box. Sameness is a food-industry virtue. It is also how a local plant becomes a flavor. Peppermint from one field and peppermint from another are not the same oil. The tin says they are.

I want to stay with the bag a moment longer, because it changed labor in the cup. A handful of linden blossom in a French kitchen is a small chore: pick out twigs, judge the handful, clean the pot. A bag is a chore that has already been done in a factory, including the chore of deciding how much plant you get. That decision is economic. It is why café "herbal" can taste like hot paper. It is why a person who grew up with loose *tilleul* may not recognize the sachet. Form is history. A tincture is not an ancient decoction, and a bag is not a stillroom.

The aisle, as a physical place, is younger than it looks. Mid-century American supermarkets had tea. They did not always have a foot of boxes pretending to be tea. The health-food store and the later supermarket "natural" set carried the category before the main aisle fully absorbed it. I will not invent a year for your town's first Celestial-style box. I will say the obvious: by the late twentieth century, "herbal tea" was a grocery object in the United States, with blend names that did cultural work ("Sleepy," "Tension," "Zinger") whether or not the box crossed a line. This series will not repeat those blend names as indications. It will notice that a mood-word on a box is a marketing genre.

There is a class fact inside the aisle. Loose Chinese or Japanese Camellia can be sold as connoisseurship. A box of mixed rosehip and hibiscus is sold as easy. The easy box is not morally worse. It is a different foodway: cheaper, sweeter, aimed at people who want the kettle ritual without caffeine or without leaf-skill. Wartime and 1930s hard-times households had already learned substitution. The aisle industrialized substitution and then forgot to call it that.

Packaging also hides geography. A hibiscus-forward blend may be Sudanese or Nigerian or Chinese calyces, cut in Europe, scented in a lab, sold under an English cottage illustration. A rooibos tin may carry a Cederberg silhouette and a German or American corporation. The illustration is not a lie exactly. It is a compression. Foodways uncompresses: name the cut, name the port if we have it, name the uncertainty if we do not.

This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent. It is not medical advice. I am writing about paper, metal, and shelf talkers. If your memory of "herbal tea" is a childhood box with a smiling flower, that memory is a primary source for the aisle as a domestic object. It is not a primary source for what a plant does in a body. Keep the memory. Leave the body to someone who has met you.
