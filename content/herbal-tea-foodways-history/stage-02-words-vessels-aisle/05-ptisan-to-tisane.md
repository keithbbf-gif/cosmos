If you want a history of "herbal tea" that does not start in a wellness warehouse, start with porridge that learned to be a drink.

Greek *ptisanē* is peeled or crushed barley. The verb behind it is a kitchen verb: to pound, to winnow, to take the husk off. Latin borrowed the noun as *ptisana* or *tisana*. Anglo-Norman scribes wrote *tysanne*. Medieval French kept *ptisane* and *tisane* for a decoction of hulled barley, sometimes used as the liquid in which other plants were cooked. The *Dictionnaire du Moyen Français* is blunt about the older sense: barley decoction, plants optional. The barley is the headword. The herbs are guests.

I like putting this at the front of the aisle stage because it reverses the prestige gradient. A modern café *tisane* is supposed to sound lighter than tea, more French, less caffeinated, more intentional. The medieval word is heavier than tea. It is grain. It is a pot that has already done work before anyone adds a leaf. It belongs next to gruel and small beer, not next to a linen-sachet lifestyle.

How did the grain leave the cup?

Slowly, and not on a date I will invent. French lexicographers note that by the nineteenth century the name had widened toward any mild everyday drink made with little "active" material — a sentence that still smells like a sickroom, which is a fact about dictionaries, not a finding I am offering. English *ptisan* shows up in the late Middle Ages as barley water; *tisane* as a fashionable spelling is later. Etymonline's casual "by 1931" for the herbal-tea sense is a reminder that the pretty French spelling is not medieval just because the barley word is. When a menu writes *tisane* in italic, it is usually buying the nineteenth- and twentieth-century sense and hoping you will hear the thirteenth.

Foodways should care about that gap. A word can keep its prestige while swapping its staple. Barley water is a caloric, cheap, household liquid. A modern chamomile bag is a flavor and a pause. They share a lexical ancestor and almost no labor process. If I say "tisane is traditional," I have to say traditional *of what*. Traditional of pounding grain is a different kitchen from traditional of opening a box.

There is a second swap inside the first. Once the word meant "the mild drink you take as ordinary liquid," it became easy to pour any garden plant into it. Mint, linden, verveine — the French domestic hour that English-language travel writing never gets tired of — sit in that widened cup. They are real foodways objects. They are not barley. They are also not *Camellia*. The widened *tisane* is a hospitality category: the thing you offer when you are not offering coffee or wine. That is a useful category. It becomes a dishonest one when it is reverse-engineered into "Europe always had herbal tea, therefore herbal tea is a therapeutic class."

I will keep the barley visible for another reason. Roasted-grain drinks never left. Japanese *mugicha*, Korean *boricha*, and various European barley coffees and wartime grain infusions are not descendants of *ptisanē* in a straight line — I am not doing folk etymology with a map — but they are the same *kind* of fact: a cereal can be a table drink without becoming a medicine chest. When the English aisle later sold "barley tea" as a curiosity, it was rediscovering something the medieval word already knew and the modern word had forgotten.

A note on method, because this is the sort of essay that tempts a fake primary. I am working from dictionary entries (OED, CNRTL, Anglo-Norman Dictionary, DMF) and not from a pot I watched in 1280. Dictionary etymologies are arguments. They can be wrong in the fine grain and still be right about the barley. If a specialist in medieval cookery wants to tell me that a particular manuscript used *tisane* only for herbs, I will listen. I will not, in the meantime, let a café italic erase the grain.

This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent. It is not medical advice. It is a history of a noun that got lighter as it got prettier. When later drafts say *tisane*, they mean the French word and its fork: grain behind, garden ahead, grocery to the side. They do not mean a clinical class, and they do not mean "tea" with a discreet cough.
