A bibliography in this folder is not a flex. It is a list of what I opened, what I only saw the face of, and what I refused to treat as a pot I had stood beside.

I will write it as an essay because a bullet list would pretend completeness. Completeness is a marketing tone. This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent. It is not medical advice. It is a map of paper.

**Words.** The OED entry on *tisane*, the CNRTL etymology, the Anglo-Norman Dictionary, and the *Dictionnaire du Moyen Français* are how I kept barley in the cup. They are lexicographers' arguments. They can be wrong in a manuscript I have not seen and still be right that the Greek and Latin point at grain. Etymonline's later date for the pretty French spelling is a reminder, not a last word.

**Meaning.** Sidney Mintz, *Sweetness and Power* (1985) and *Tasting Food, Tasting Freedom* (1996), for the stubborn rule that meaning arises from use in social relationships. I used him as a method, not as a mint specialist. He is not a source on hibiscus. He is a source on why sugar in a Maghrebi glass and sugar in a British factory cup are cousins.

**Maghreb.** Graham Cornwell, *Sweetening the Pot: A History of Tea and Sugar in Morocco, 1850–1960* (Georgetown PhD, 2018), including the public copy that circulates as a PDF. I leaned on his insistence that herbs predate the green-tea boom and that mint can hide a lesser leaf. I did not re-do his archives.

**Industry bins.** Tea & Herbal Infusions Europe's public site: the 2015 letterhead, the two bins, the self-history of ETC and EHIA. A trade page is a category claim.

**Cape.** Van Wyk and Gorelik, "The history and ethnobotany of Cape herbal teas" (2017), for the Thunberg / *Aspalathus cordata* correction. Gorelik's ethnographic notes for the South African Rooibos Council, and the secondary fight about Ginsberg 1904 versus prior local sale. Joubert and colleagues on cultivation after Nortier c. 1930 — used as industry history, not as a lab story. The 2021 EU protected name for rooibos / red bush, as reported.

**Atlantic roselle.** Judith Carney and Richard Rosomoff, *In the Shadow of Slavery*, for kitchen gardens and African plants that crossed the ocean. Michael Twitty, *The Cooking Gene*, for the red drink as joy and survival. Serious Eats' hibiscus-diaspora essay as a competent popular synthesis I did not treat as an archive. A 2022 *Open Agriculture* review for the name-table (bissap, zobo, karkadeh, sorrel) and the hedge that China and Thailand are major calyx producers.

**Holly.** Crown et al., "Ritual Black Drink consumption at Cahokia," *PNAS* (2012). Florida Department of State notes on yaupon and the white-drink / cassina vocabulary. Hudson-school cautions, as repeated in UF/IFAS horticulture pages, about the *vomitoria* epithet. I have not held a Cahokia beaker.

**Fireweed.** Kalle, Sõukand, and colleagues (2020) on *Epilobium angustifolium* as food and recreational tea, and the 2020 *Journal of Ethnopharmacology* paper on the rewritten Ivan-chaj narrative. Nineteenth-century Koporye / counterfeit-tea notices as they circulate in secondary tea-trade histories — I did not open the Russian State Historical Archive.

**Mountain.** Slow Food Ark of Taste on Cretan *malotira*. Theophrastus on the iron-name, as a botanical-tradition etymology, not as a modern indication. I refused the popular pages that wanted a body complaint in the next sentence.

**What I did not open.** Stillroom manuscripts in the original hand. Clanwilliam farm ledgers. SGH Chrome DOM as a live research rail — this pass worked from named public documents and the sibling packs' fence style. Clinical trials, on purpose. The herbal-medicine-history folder and the mid-century laboratory-word folder, except as neighbors not to impersonate.

**What stays uncertain.** Khoisan names for rooibos in the early written record. The exact mix in any given Cahokia beaker. How ordinary, in what decade, fermented fireweed was before the recent fashion. The private path by which *jamaica* became the Mexican drink's name. Whether a cheap lotus tin is flower or flavor oil. Almost every "always" in a travel article.

If you want to go further, go further as a reader of kitchens and crates, not as a collector of indications. The fence stays up when the bibliography does. A paper can be real and still not be a reason to finish a sentence this folder refused.
