The flatten has a shape. Once you see it, you cannot unsee it, which is annoying in a supermarket and useful in a chair.

Paragraph one: a kettle, a grandmother, a word like *ancient* or *soothing*. Paragraph two: a plant Latin that may or may not match the bag. Paragraph three: a body complaint, or a wink that does the work of a complaint without a verb the lawyer would circle. Paragraph four: a shop. Paragraph five, if the piece is "responsible": "more research is needed," which is how a flatten pretends it was a review article.

This series is a long refusal of paragraph three. I will not name the complaints here. You can fill them in from memory; that is the problem. The flatten depends on your memory doing the illegal work so the writer does not have to.

The first flattening is **the word tea**. One syllable is asked to hold Camellia, roasted barley, hibiscus calyces, a holly, a mountain *Sideritis*, a sachet of mixed "bedtime," and a Maghrebi glass that is mostly sugar. A journalist who does not split the word will then borrow prestige from the Camellia library — clipper ships, Japanese bowls, British hours — and lend it to a plant that has a different ship or no ship at all.

The second flattening is **the calendar**. "For thousands of years" is not a date. It is a fog machine. Rooibos has a thin late written record for the species that is actually in the tin. Ivan-chaj as a fashionable fermented drink is, for many drinkers in recent fieldwork, new. Moroccan *atay* as a national glass is nineteenth-century as a commodity system. A flatten that needs antiquity will steal a century from the nearest herbal and glue it to the bag.

The third flattening is **the grandmother**. Grandmothers exist. They are not a citation. A specific woman in a specific kitchen with a specific plant is a source. "Grandmothers used to" is a brand. Notice whether the piece can name a language, a region, and a plant part. If it cannot, the grandmother is furniture.

The fourth flattening is **the condition-without-a-verb**. I am staying inside the fence: I will not park a diagnosis next to a cup. I will say what the prose trick is. The plant sits in sentence A. A body complaint sits in sentence B. The verb that would join them is missing, or is "supports," or is "traditionally associated with." Your eye supplies *for*. That supplied *for* is the product. A claims-guarded reader should treat a missing verb as a loud verb.

The fifth flattening is **the lab noun**. Antioxidant, polyphenol, "studies suggest." Sometimes a paper exists. Existence of a paper is a historical fact about a journal, not a kitchen instruction. A flatten uses the lab noun as incense. You can smell it from the headline.

The sixth flattening is **the single origin**. Hibiscus is bissap and sorrel and jamaica and karkadeh. A flatten picks one postcard. Yaupon is a southeastern holly with a Cahokia residue argument; a flatten makes it a colonial curiosity or a startup. Linden is a tree and an hour; a flatten makes it a European bedtime. Ask who got deleted.

How to read a piece, then, as a practical tool:

- Split tea / infusion / decoction / cereal drink / scented Camellia.
- Demand a plant part and a place.
- Circle every year. If there are no years, the calendar is fog.
- Watch for the missing verb between plant and complaint.
- Ask what the writer is selling: a book, a tin, a trip, a self.
- Reward hedges. A journalist who says "the trail is thin" is doing our job.

This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent. It is not medical advice. It is a reader's kit. If I have flattened anyone in this folder — and I will have, because forty-five short essays are a form of violence against particularity — fail me by the same rules. Quote the sentence. Ask who is missing. Ask whether the verb is doing a clinic's work. Then go back to the pot, which was never a paragraph.
