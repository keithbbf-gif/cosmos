Turn the box around. The front is a story. The back is a shorter story with a legal department.

I am not going to tell you what to buy. I am going to give you questions that a foodways reader can ask a tin without turning the kitchen into a clinic. This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent. It is not medical advice. If the box is doing a job a drug label would not be allowed to do, notice the job. Do not finish it for them.

**What plant?** A Latin binomial is not holiness, but it is a claim you can check. "Chamomile" without *Matricaria* or *Chamaemelum* is a category. "Mint" is a crowd. "Mountain tea" without *Sideritis* and a country is a landscape painting. "Proprietary blend" is a shrug.

**What part?** Leaf, flower, calyx, root, roasted seed, bark, "natural flavors." Hibiscus that is actually calyx is a different object from a petal picture. Rooibos is a needle-leaf shrub, not a Camellia. Barley is a grain. If the picture and the part disagree, believe the part if it is stated, and distrust the picture if it is not.

**What form?** Cut herb, powder, extract, "essence," tea bag of dust. Form is history, as we said in the method draft. A tincture is not a grandmother's decoction. A bag is not a courtyard pot. An extract on a "tea" box is a category leak toward supplement-land.

**Who packed, who grew?** "Packed in" is not "grown in." Hibiscus packed in Europe may be Sudanese or Nigerian or Chinese calyces. A Cederberg silhouette may be a licensed name on a multinational brand. Country of origin rules vary; many boxes are allowed to be shy. Shyness is information.

**What words are moods?** Sleepy, calm, defense, cleanse, zinger, ancient, temple, shaman, grandma. Mood-words are a genre. In the United States, dietary-supplement structure/function language is a grandchild of a 1994 statute (DSHEA). A grocery "herbal tea" sold as food may not use the same grammar as a supplement bottle, which is why some boxes stay in food-voice and some creep. I am describing a legal neighborhood, not offering compliance advice. If you need compliance advice, you need a lawyer, not this folder.

**What is the serving?** A bag weight tells you how much plant the company thinks is a cup. Compare that, in your head, to a handful of linden a person actually put in a pot. You do not need a milligram sermon. You need to notice that the factory already decided.

**What is missing?** Allergens sometimes appear. Sugar in a "tea" may be in a separately sold syrup, or in a prepared bottle. A prepared hibiscus drink can be a soda in all but name. The dry tin and the cold bottle are different foodways wearing the same flower.

A practical sequence I use in a store, without romance:

1. Read the ingredient list before the poem on the front.
2. Count the plants. More than four and you are drinking a flavor design.
3. Look for the part.
4. Look for a place that is not a P.O. box.
5. Put the box back if the only fact is a mood.

None of that makes you a better person. It makes you harder to flatten. Label literacy is not purity. People will still buy the pretty tin. I do. The point is to know you bought a pretty tin, a cut, and a story, not a courtyard, not a Cederberg farm, and not a finding.

If the tin is honest — plant, part, cut, packer — you can decide whether you like the flavor. Flavor is a legal reason to buy a food. A body complaint is not a reason this folder will help you shop. Go to a clinician who knows you if you have a complaint. Go to the kettle if you have a pot. The two trips are not the same trip, even when the box wants them to be.
