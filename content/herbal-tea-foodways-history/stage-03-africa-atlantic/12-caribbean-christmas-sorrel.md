December stains a Caribbean saucepan before anyone writes a menu. The calyces go in early because the spices need time, and the holiday will not wait for a botanist.

English-Caribbean *sorrel*, in this sense, is not the green leaf salad Europeans mean by the same word. It is a drink from the calyces of roselle — again *Hibiscus sabdariffa* — boiled or steeped with sugar and a spice list that usually includes ginger and cloves, often cinnamon, and, in islands that grow it and say *pimento*, allspice. Rum goes in some pots and not others. The liquid is left to sit. Families argue about how long. The color is deep red. The hour is Christmas, or the stretch of December that behaves like Christmas whether or not a church calendar is in the room.

Jamaica is the name most export copy reaches for, and Jamaican kitchens have every right to the association. The drink is not Jamaica's private property. Trinidad, Barbados, Antigua, Grenada, and other English-speaking islands have their own pots, their own rum decisions, their own arguments about whether the drink is ready. I have not sat at all of those tables. Food pages and family talk agree on the seasonal object more than they agree on a single recipe, which is what you would expect of a holiday drink that lives in households rather than in a franchise manual.

The name collision is worth a pause. *Sorrel* in British English can mean *Rumex*, a sour leaf. In Caribbean English it can mean the hibiscus drink and the plant that makes it. A traveler who hears "sorrel" and pictures a salad has already left the kitchen. Foodways starts with the local noun. The plant is the same species as West African *bissap* and Mexican *flor de jamaica*. The meal is not the same. A Dakar street glass is thirst and traffic. A Kingston December pot is a calendar. Same calyx, different sentence.

Michael Twitty has insisted, in *The Cooking Gene* and in the public talk that travels with that book, that a red drink in African-diasporic foodways can be joy and survival in one glass. I am using that as a foodways claim, not as a lab result. The cup is allowed to be festive. It is allowed to be a thing people made and kept making. It does not have to climax in a condition to be historically serious.

How the plant got into island gardens is a longer Atlantic story than a Christmas essay can finish. Judith Carney and Richard Rosomoff describe African botanical knowledge moving with enslaved people, and provision grounds — the plots captives were forced to feed themselves from — as places where African and American plants were grown, remembered, and cooked. Roselle belongs in that garden conversation. I will not turn this draft into a catalog of violence. The violence is the condition of the garden. The draft's job is to keep the drink and the garden visible: who knew the plant, who boiled it, who sweetened it with sugar that was itself a plantation product, who poured it at a holiday that the islands did not invent and then remade.

Sugar is not innocent in this pot, and it is not optional in most of the pots people actually serve. The drink is tart without it and a party with it. Cloves and cinnamon are ship spices. Ginger may be garden or market. Rum is molasses with a memory. A claims-guarded history can say all of that without converting the punch bowl into a sermon and without converting it into a clinic. Household literature in the wider Atlantic world sometimes filed hibiscus in a sickroom register. Christmas sorrel, as served, is a table drink. This series stays with the table.

Labor, again, is the part the garnish photograph deletes. Someone grew or bought the calyces. Someone stood over a pot in a hot kitchen in a cool month. Someone strained the liquid and washed the stain out of the cloth. Children are often allowed the sweet version; adults decide about the rum. Those are ordinary hospitality rules. They are also a history of who does the December work.

I will hedge what I cannot see. I do not have a plantation ledger that lists roselle by the pound. I do not have a first Christmas date for the drink on any island. Popular writing likes to imply that the pot is as old as the first enslaved gardener who recognized the plant. That may be true as a kind of continuity and still be unprovable as a dated event. What is easy to attest is later and nearer: twentieth- and twenty-first-century Caribbean households and bakeries treating sorrel as a seasonal given, bottled versions in groceries, and the drink traveling with migrants to London, Toronto, and New York, where December still wants the color.

Substitution sits at the edge. When fresh calyces are scarce, dried ones from a shop bag do the job. When rum is scarce, the drink is still sorrel. When the household is not Christian, the calendar may still offer a red pot because the neighbors are making it and the weather has turned to the spices. Foodways is allowed to be messy about belief. The saucepan does not take attendance.

This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent. It is not medical advice. A Christmas pot is a ceremony of season and company. If an export tin later sells "hibiscus" without cloves, without December, and without the argument about steeping time, it has sold a flavor and deleted a holiday. The plant can survive that deletion. The foodway cannot.
