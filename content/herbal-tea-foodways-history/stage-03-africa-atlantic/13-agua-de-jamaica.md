A comida-corrida plate arrives with a sweating jug, not a ceremony. The red liquid is a cooler among other coolers — horchata, tamarindo, the day's cheaper thirst.

In Mexico and much of Central America that cooler is *agua de jamaica*, made from *flor de jamaica*: again the dried calyces of *Hibiscus sabdariffa*, boiled, strained, sweetened, and served cold. The plant is the same species as Senegalese *bissap* and Jamaican Christmas sorrel. The meal structure is not. This is not a December pot that sits for days with rum and pimento. It is an *agua fresca*: a fruit-or-flower water in a glass or a plastic bag, sold in mercados, at street stalls, and as the house pitcher beside a set lunch. You can drink it on a Tuesday in May because the plate is salty and the afternoon is hot. No holiday needs to authorize the jug.

I want the ordinary objects in view. A barrel or a glass dispenser with a spigot. A bag knotted at the corner, drunk by biting or by straw. A restaurant pitcher that has already gone pale from ice. Sugar measured by the cook, not by a sachet company. The drink is tart enough that unsweetened versions exist, but the public default is sweet. That is a food fact, not a virtue contest.

The name is the part English-language writing likes to over-explain. *Jamaica* in the drink is a toponym sitting inside Spanish. It names, in everyday Mexican speech, the flower and the water. It does not, by itself, prove a single origin story — that the plant arrived from the island of Jamaica on a dated ship, or that Mexican kitchens borrowed a Jamaican Christmas recipe and then forgot the spices. Plants and names move on different clocks. Roselle was already an Atlantic garden and commodity plant; Spanish American kitchens had reason to use a cheap, staining, sour calyx as a cooler. How the word *jamaica* attached to that calyx, and through which ports, is a trail I have not walked in an archive. Food historians and popular accounts offer routes. I will not promote one of them to a certificate. The honest sentence is smaller: the drink-name carries a place-name, and a place-name in a glass is not the same thing as a proven itinerary.

Compare the hour with the other hibiscus pages in this stage. West African *bissap* and *zobo* are street and party drinks with ginger arguments. Caribbean sorrel is a calendar. *Agua de jamaica* is a table cooler in a meal system that already had a category for such waters. *Aguas frescas* are a hospitality and commerce form: they make a lunch feel finished, they sell from a stall without requiring a kettle ritual, they let a cook stretch fruit, rice, tamarind, or a dried flower into a pitcher. Filing *jamaica* only as "hibiscus tea" steals that form and replaces it with an English grocery pause. The stall is not trying to be a tin.

Geography of the crate still matters, even at a mercado. Dried *flor de jamaica* in a Mexican shop may be grown in Mexico, or it may be an import. Trade talk, as in the West African draft, often names large Asian producers of calyces for export. I have not traced a particular stall's sack to a field. A foodways reader should keep the possibility open: the Tuesday jug can be a local harvest or a global one poured into a local meal. The meal does not become less Mexican because the dried flower traveled. Compression is the enemy, not travel.

I will say something about color and labor that the tourist photograph flattens. The calyces stain. Someone's hands, cloth, and plastic barrel take that stain. Someone boiled the pot before the lunch rush. Someone decided how much sugar the neighborhood will pay for. Children get the red glass; adults may drink the same glass with the same plate. There is no rum requirement. There is no wedding toast required, though a party can use the pitcher. The foodway is repetition: the cooler that is always there, the way bread is always there, until it isn't.

Household literature and later wellness copy have both tried to recruit the sour red water into a sickroom or a "functional" caption. Mexican domestic talk, like other domestic talk, can carry a register of "what we give when." I will acknowledge that such a register exists in print and in kitchens. I will not name it as the point of the jug. This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent. It is not medical advice. A comida-corrida cooler is a way of finishing a plate. That is already enough history.

One more naming caution, because this folder is a machine for category errors. English speakers sometimes say "hibiscus tea" for the hot infusion and "jamaica" for the cold one, as if temperature created two species. Temperature is a serving choice. The same boiled liquid can be drunk warm in a house that wants it that way. The *agua fresca* form prefers cold because the form is a heat answer. Do not let a café menu convince you that the plant changes passport when it hits ice.

If you only remember the contrast, remember this: Christmas sorrel is a season that commandeers a pot. *Agua de jamaica* is a pitcher that commandeers a lunch. The Atlantic hibiscus belt is a belt of uses. The uses do not collapse because the Latin stays still.
