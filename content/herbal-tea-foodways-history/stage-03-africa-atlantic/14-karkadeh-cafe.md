Cairo's open-air tables have room for a red glass that is neither coffee nor Camellia. Vendors and cafes sell it as a drink people already know how to order.

The Arabic name you will meet most often in English is *karkadeh* or *karkade* — spellings wander — for an infusion or decoction of roselle calyces in Egypt and Sudan. Hot in weather that wants heat. Iced when the city is doing what cities do in summer. Street sellers pour it. Open-air cafes in Cairo are widely reported as places you can sit with it beside tea and coffee, without anyone treating the order as a novelty. I will not invent a particular cafe, a particular waiter, or a particular corner in Zamalek or downtown. The type is attested in travel writing, food writing, and ordinary talk. The type is enough: a public cup, a color, a price, a pause.

Sudan belongs in the same sentence and not as a footnote. Sudanese harvests of *Hibiscus sabdariffa* show up in commodity talk as a source of dried calyces for export as well as for local drink. I have not counted sacks at Port Sudan. I can say the plant is a farm and market fact, not only a cafe flourish. Egypt drinks it; Sudan grows and drinks it; the crate, as in other hibiscus drafts, may travel farther than the story on a tourist menu.

Menus are where the worst nickname lives. "Tea of the Pharaohs" appears in modern tourism and export copy as if a New Kingdom protocol had been waiting for a glass. I have not seen an attested ancient serving ritual that would license that phrase as history. Treat it as a modern nickname — a sales adjective with a monument in it — and then put it down. Ancient Egypt had plants and drinks. That is not the same as a chain of custody from a tomb painting to a cafe kettle. A claims-guarded folder should be allergic to borrowed antiquity.

The cafe matters because it is a hospitality machine that is not a holiday and not a clinic. You sit. You order. You look at the street. The red glass is one option among caffeine options and water. That is a different social object from Christmas sorrel, which commandeers December, and from *agua de jamaica*, which commandeers a lunch plate. *Karkadeh* in this use commandeers a chair. Foodways should care about chairs. A drink that can be ordered in public without a speech is a drink that has already won its category.

Wedding-toast stories circulate around *karkadeh* in Egypt and the wider region: the red drink on a tray, the color as a festive sign, the pour as a wish. I will mark that as a circulating custom, not as a census. I have not counted weddings. I have not been handed a tray in a hall I can name. People tell the story often enough that a foodways essay would be coy to omit it and dishonest to promote it into "always." Keep the tray if the household keeps the tray. Do not invent a liturgy.

Method is familiar if you have read the other hibiscus pages, and still local in its habits. Calyces boiled or steeped, sugar to taste, sometimes spices, served in a glass that can stand heat or ice. I will not freeze a spice list and call it national. Egyptian and Sudanese households are allowed to disagree the way Senegalese and Nigerian sellers disagree. The honest constant is the calyx and the color. The rest is kitchen.

Household literature and later pamphlet talk have given roselle a sickroom register in Arabic as in other languages. Dictionaries and domestic advice can carry a "when" that this series will not finish. The cafe glass does not need that "when." People drink it because they like it, because they are sitting, because it is what the vendor has that is not coffee. This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent. It is not medical advice. A public cup is a public cup.

I want one comparison that keeps the Atlantic belt from becoming a single smear. The plant is shared. The words are not. *Karkadeh* is not a mistranslation of *bissap*. It is how Arabic speakers named the drink in a Nile and Red Sea world that had its own sugar history, its own cafe furniture, its own relationship to imported Camellia and to Turkish and Egyptian coffee habits. Substitution is in the room: the red glass can stand in for a pause that might otherwise be tea. It is not pretending to be Yunnan. It is sitting next to Yunnan and winning some of the orders.

If a European "fruit infusion" crate of hibiscus is cut in Hamburg and grown in Sudan, the cafe and the crate have met without being the same object. One is a chair in Cairo or Khartoum. The other is a supermarket pause in a climate that needed a tart bag. This stage exists to keep those objects from collapsing. Order the glass by the name the waiter already uses. Leave the pharaohs on the postcard.
