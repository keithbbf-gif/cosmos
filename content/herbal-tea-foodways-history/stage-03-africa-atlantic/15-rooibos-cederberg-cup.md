Paper arrived late to the Cederberg shrub. The mountain did not file a birth certificate, and the first confident year on an export sleeve is still a fight between brochure and quieter notes.

The plant in the modern cup is *Aspalathus linearis*, a Cape fynbos species tied, in the commercial story, to the Cederberg and to Clanwilliam. People drink a reddish infusion of the fermented, cut, dried stems and leaves — "red bush" in older English — and a greener, less oxidized version when the factory wants that color. I am interested in the drink as a regional foodway that later learned to travel. I am not interested in the sleeve's health paragraph. This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent. It is not medical advice.

The written record of *this* species as a drink is thinner and later than popular history likes. Ben-Erik van Wyk and Boris Gorelik, in their 2017 paper on Cape herbal teas in the *South African Journal of Botany*, are usefully dull on a point the brochures blur. Carl Thunberg's 1772 note about country people making tea from a Cape shrub is a note about *Aspalathus* / *Borbonia cordata*, not about *A. linearis*. The sentence is a sentence about a plant. It is not a birth certificate for rooibos. If you have been told that a famous botanist "discovered" the cup in 1772, you have been told a compression.

What, then, can be said without inventing a notebook?

Oral use in the region is widely assumed and poorly documented in the way oral cultures are poorly documented: the paper is thin, not the lives. I will not put a Khoisan name on a brochure and call it certainty. I have not got a dated indigenous recipe card to wave. Absence of a colonial notebook is not proof of absence of a drink. It is proof that the archive is the wrong genre for a lot of ordinary cups. A claims-guarded foodways essay can live in that gap without filling it with a speech.

On the commercial side, the popular line is that Benjamin or Barend Ginsberg began buying and selling rooibos around 1904 and thereby "started" the industry. Gorelik's ethnographic and historical notes, as this folder's citation list records them, push back in a usefully small way: the plant was already being sold locally; Ginsberg opened a wider market. 1904 is a commercialization story, not an invention story. Keep both dates in the room if you meet a third. I have not held Ginsberg's ledgers. I am reporting a dispute that is already public.

Cultivation as a farm crop, rather than a veld gathering, is usually attached to P. le Fras Nortier and to experiments around 1930 with farmers named Bergh and Riordan — the cluster that later industry histories (Joubert and De Beer and their neighbors) repeat. I will leave the year approximate. I will not invent a first successful hectare. The foodways point is labor: a wild shrub became a planted one, with the usual consequences for who picks, who dries, who owns the name, and who drinks the cheap bag versus the "estate" story.

Export scale came later. I will not quote a tonnage I have not seen. What I can say is institutional and recent. In 2021 the European Union registered a protected designation of origin for rooibos / red bush. Reporting around that decision often called it the first African food PDO. I will leave "first" in the reporter's mouth; firsts are a sport. The fact that does not need the sport is smaller and sharper: a European name-protection attached to a Cape plant geography. The unmarked default in English hot drinks is still Camellia. Rooibos learned to travel *like* the default — a crate, a tin, a restaurant pot — while remaining a different species. That is a late twist in the substitution story this folder keeps telling.

Hospitality on the ground is less cinematic than the mountain photograph. A kettle in Clanwilliam, a farm kitchen, a tourist cup on a stoep, a supermarket bag in Johannesburg or Hamburg: those are different hours. The fermented cut is a method, not a mystique. Someone has to chop, oxidize, dry, and grade. The red color taught European and American drinkers to see "tea" when the leaf was not Camellia. Color is foodways. A pale green rooibos and a dark red one do not occupy the same marketing hour, even if the shrub is the same.

I will keep the wellness copy out of the last paragraph on purpose. The plant has been asked to do too many jobs on boxes. Household talk and older Cape literature may have given local infusions a sickroom register; that register is a fact about genres, not a finding I am offering. The cup I can stand behind is the one poured because it is what the household has, because Camellia is expensive or unloved, because the guest sat down, because the region grew a shrub that takes well to a pot.

If you need a moral, do not take a medical one. Take this: a Cederberg cup can be a farm, a market, a name fight, and a European legal category without becoming a clinic. The paper will always arrive late. The kettle does not care.
