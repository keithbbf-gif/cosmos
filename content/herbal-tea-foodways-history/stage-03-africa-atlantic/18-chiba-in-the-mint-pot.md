Height is part of the recipe in a Maghrebi mint pot. The host lifts the teapot so the stream cools, foams, and announces that the glasses are for company.

The drink English speakers flatten into "Moroccan mint tea" is, in Graham Cornwell's account, *atay*: Chinese green tea — often gunpowder — boiled or repeatedly poured with a great deal of sugar and a handful of fresh herbs. Mint is the herb the postcard knows. It is not the only leaf that goes in. Cornwell's 2018 Georgetown dissertation, *Sweetening the Pot: A History of Tea and Sugar in Morocco, 1850–1960*, is the document this folder actually leans on here. I have not reconstructed his archives. I am using his argument as a public object: the nineteenth-century boom in imported tea and sugar sat down next to an older pot of herbs, and the herbs did not politely leave.

*Chiba* — also *sheeba*, wormwood as the house names it — is one of those herbs. Sage, thyme, and oregano show up in the same kind of sentence: seasonal, local, a flavor the mint pot can carry when the garden or the market has them. I want *chiba* in this draft as availability and taste, not as a cameo from a vermouth bottle or an absinthe poster. Those European drinks have their own histories. They are not a license to turn a Maghrebi handful into a medical story. The wormwood in *atay* is a kitchen herb among kitchen herbs. It is bitter in a useful way when mint alone is too simple, or when mint is scarce, or when the household likes the edge.

Cornwell is careful about sequence, and this series should stay as careful. Herbal infusions in the Maghreb predated the volume arrival of Camellia. People already poured herbs. Then, in the nineteenth century, cheap green tea and cheap sugar became a mass hospitality form — not an ancient Camellia ceremony wearing a fez. The postcard likes antiquity. The commodity record likes steamships and prices. Foodways should prefer the prices. Mint, in that boom, had a second job that is almost embarrassing to marketing: it hides low-grade gunpowder tea. A handful of fresh *nana* will perfume a pot whose leaves would not survive a blind tasting. That is not a scandal. That is how households make an imported staple taste like the house.

Sugar is the other imported staple, and it is not a garnish. Cornwell's title is the plot. Sweetness made the pot a daily social fact: glasses refilled, foam rebuilt, guests kept sitting. Sidney Mintz, from another shoreline, would recognize the structure. A claims-guarded essay can say "a frightening amount of sugar" as a hospitality measure. It cannot say the pot is therefore a clinic, or that taking the sugar out would make the drink more serious. Seriousness, here, is the pour.

I will stay with hardware, because hardware refuses to become a cure. Small heatproof glasses. A metal pot. A tray. A first glass that may go back into the pot. A second filling of the same leaves. The host's arm is part of the method. Height aerates and cools; it also shows that the drink is being made *for* someone. A bag of "Moroccan mint" in a supermarket, already blended and already portioned, can approximate flavor and cannot approximate the arm. Form is history. The sachet is a different foodway that borrowed a photograph.

Season matters for *chiba* more than the postcard admits. Fresh wormwood is not a twelve-month factory cut in every town. Households use what they have. Mint may be the year-round face of the pot; *chiba* may be the winter or market-day face. I will not freeze a calendar I have not lived. I will say the honest thing: a "mint pot" that never varies is a restaurant menu. A house pot varies.

There is a gender and labor fact in the performance, and I want it without a sermon. Someone bought the tea and the sugar. Someone washed the glasses. Someone stood with the pot while other people talked. Tourist versions often put a man in a good robe in front of the tray. Domestic versions are less costumed. Foodways notices who is unpaid in the photograph.

Household literature and later talk have given some of these herbs a sickroom register. I will mention that the register exists. I will not make *chiba* the payoff of a complaint. This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent. It is not medical advice. The glass is a social device. If a later sentence starts explaining wormwood as if the pot were a bottle behind a European bar, that sentence has left Morocco without buying a ticket.

One naming caution before the tray is cleared. *Atay* is a hospitality word that already includes Camellia. It is not "herbal tea" that happens to have tea in it. It is tea, in the Moroccan social sense, that happens to have herbs in it — herbs that were there first and then made the import drinkable, cheap, and local. THIE's two bins, from an earlier draft, still apply as botany: the leaf in the crate is *Camellia sinensis*; the handful is not. The pot refuses the bins because the pot is a mixed object. Mixed objects are allowed. They are most of what people actually pour.

When you are offered the glass, take the height as the citation. The citation is not a pharaoh, not a clinic, and not a French bottle. It is a nineteenth-century boom that learned to taste like a house.
