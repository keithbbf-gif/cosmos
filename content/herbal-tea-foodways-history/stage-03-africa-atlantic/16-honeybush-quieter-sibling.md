Fynbos does not owe the world a second export celebrity. Honeybush grew in the same Cape scrub as the louder cup and then watched the louder cup take the microphone.

The plants are *Cyclopia* species — several of them, not a single shrub with one farm face. Van Wyk and Gorelik's survey of Cape herbal teas treats honeybush as part of that regional drink list: local infusions from fynbos, with names that English later flattened into one sweet-sounding commodity word. *Heuningbos* in Afrikaans is already a landscape name. English *honeybush* followed the scent and the marketing. I will not pretend I have a complete map of every local name for every *Cyclopia* used in a pot. The paper trail is patchier than rooibos, and the industry is smaller. That patchiness is the story, not a problem to be patched with a founder myth.

I am not going to invent a first commercial year. Rooibos got Ginsberg-in-1904, Nortier-around-1930, and a stack of later industry papers, whether or not those dates do all the work brochures ask. Honeybush's twentieth-century passage into a packaged drink is later, thinner, and easier to over-tell. I have not got a dated invoice I can stand in front of. What is easy to see from the public record is comparative: by the time rooibos had an export voice, a restaurant pot, and then a European protected name, honeybush was still the other Cape herbal tea — sold, yes, gathered and later cultivated, yes, but not the sleeve the world learned first.

Geography is not a backdrop. Fynbos is a specific vegetation, a winter-rain shrubland with a reputation among botanists and a problem among harvesters: slow plants, patchy stands, a temptation to overgather when a fashion arrives. Conservation writing about that landscape, when it appears, is useful as a warning about picking, not as romance. A wild-cut *Cyclopia* and a planted one are different labor. I will not assign a hectare count I have not seen. I will say the quieter sibling has had to live with the louder sibling's market: buyers who already think "red bush" when they think Cape, and who may meet honeybush as a blender's afterthought or a specialty tin.

Taste, as far as a foodways essay should go, is a hospitality fact. The dried, often fermented cut is described as sweeter and more floral than rooibos — "honey" is doing scent work, not grocery-aisle morality. Households and small packers sell it as a pot drink, a guest drink, a caffeine-free kettle when Camellia is not wanted. Those are substitutions and preferences. They are not findings. This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent. It is not medical advice.

Branding is the contrast I actually care about. Rooibos learned to speak in crates: Clanwilliam silhouettes, "red bush" as an English phrase, later a PDO that taught European lawyers to spell a Cape word. Honeybush got the leftover adjectives — rarer, artisanal, the other one. Some of that is ecology and scale. Some of it is path dependence. Once a region has one successful non-Camellia export, the second plant has to explain itself in the first plant's shadow. Foodways should notice the shadow without turning it into a contest of virtue. Neither shrub is a clinic. Both are Cape drinks that industrial packing taught to look like tea.

I will keep indigenous naming in the same hedge I used for rooibos. Oral culture leaves thin paper. Local names existed and exist. A brochure that prints one name as *the* original word is doing brochure work. Van Wyk and Gorelik are historians of a literature as much as of a pot; they can tell you what was written down. They cannot conjure a complete oral census. This series will not either.

Household literature in the Cape, as elsewhere, sometimes filed herbal infusions in a mixed stillroom register. I can say the register existed. I will not finish its sentences. The cup in this draft is the one poured on a stoep, in a kitchen, in a small tasting room that would rather you buy the packet than a story about your body. If the packet still tells that story, read the packet as a commercial artifact. Leave the body to someone who has met you.

A last comparison, because this stage is a belt of names. Hibiscus drinks in West Africa, the Caribbean, Mexico, and the Nile world share a calyx and split a meal. Rooibos and honeybush share a vegetation and split a market. The split is not botanical trivia. It is who got the microphone: which plant a Hamburg tin will bother to explain, which plant a traveler is told to ask for, which plant a farm child grows up packing. The quieter sibling is not lesser vegetation. It is a different hour of the same hills — less photographed, less legally crowned, still a pot.

When you see honeybush on a shelf next to rooibos, do not assume you are looking at a matched export set invented as a pair. You are looking at two foodways that industrialization filed in one aisle. The aisle is the late object. The hills were first.
