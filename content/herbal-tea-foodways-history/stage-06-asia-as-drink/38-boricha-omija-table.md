In a lot of Korean restaurants, the first liquid on the table is not water as a European diner means water. It is roasted barley, pale brown, free, refilled without a performance. *Boricha* is so ordinary that writing an essay about it feels like writing an essay about the napkin. That is why it belongs here. Foodways is often the napkin.

I will keep *mugicha* in the other room. The drinks are cousins, not twins. Korean barley tea lives as a default meal-liquid: next to banchan, next to a metal pot of rice, next to a grilled something that wants a sip between bites. It is not a ceremony and it is not a sachet hour. If you ask for "tea" after the meal you may get something else. The barley was already there, doing the job of making the table wet in the right way.

Omija — *Schisandra* berries, "five flavors" in the name — is a different Korean liquid: tart, red or pink, seasonal, closer to a punch or a cordial than to a water-cousin. It shows up as a sweet-tart drink, sometimes with sugar or honey, sometimes as a restaurant special, sometimes as a home jug. English wellness copy loves the five-flavor name because it sounds like a system. The system, in the kitchen, is a taste description and a fruit. I will not import the plant into the mid-century laboratory-word folder, and I will not import it into a *bencao* lecture. I will say: Koreans have a tart berry drink with a poetic name, and it sits at a different hour than barley.

The table is the method. Banchan logic — many small plates, refills, a shared center — wants a liquid that does not interrupt. Barley does not interrupt. A flavored "herbal" performance would. That is why the American aisle's idea of herbal tea (a special mug, a pause, a mood-word) maps so badly onto *boricha*. The mapping error is the essay.

I have not done a national survey of what households boil. Some boil corn silk or other pantry plants; some buy barley in tea bags; some drink water; some drink barley in winter and cold barley in summer. A foodways draft that says "Koreans always" is already wrong. The restaurant default is the public fact I can stand on. The home pot is varied and I will leave it varied.

There is a labor fact in the free refill. Someone roasted or someone opened a bag. Someone keeps a urn. The cheapness is the point. A claims-guarded series should love cheap drinks. Cheap drinks are what most people actually pour. Prestige Camellia and prestige mountain herbs get the documentaries. Barley gets the table, which is a better primary source.

Home pots vary more than restaurants, and I will not flatten them. Some households keep a barley urn because that is what their parents kept. Some switched to filtered water and kept the word *boricha* as a memory. Some buy tea bags of roasted corn or a mix and still call the pot barley. A foodways writer who needs a single national liquid will lie. The public default — the free brown cup on the table — is the fact that can travel.

Omija lets me say something about season without a harvest myth. A tart berry drink in a hot month is a weather answer, like hibiscus in Dakar and chrysanthemum in a Chinatown bakery. The plant Latin is *Schisandra chinensis* in the grocery of many countries; the Korean name is doing taste work. Five flavors is a tasting note that became a brand. Brands flatten. The drink can still be good. A syrup from a bottle and a home steep of dried berries are not the same glass; ask which one you were poured.

This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent. It is not medical advice. If you have seen omija sold as a specialty "tea" with a body-complaint in the next sentence, that sentence is the flatten we will scold in draft 43. At the table, the questions are smaller: is it barley or water, is it tart or brown, who refills the cup, and did anyone bother to call it tea. Usually they did not need to. The cup was already there.
