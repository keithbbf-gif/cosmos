Gunpowder green tea did not arrive in Morocco as a ceremony. It arrived as a crate, and the crate needed sugar.

Graham Cornwell's 2018 dissertation, *Sweetening the Pot*, is the named public object I am leaning on. I have not sat in his archives. I have sat with the argument as it circulates: between about 1850 and 1960, Chinese green tea and loaf or cone sugar became, together, a Moroccan daily fact. The drink that resulted — *atay* — is now so thoroughly "traditional" in travel writing that the nineteenth century has to be restated on purpose. Traditions have birthdays. This one is later than the brochures.

Draft 18 stayed in the pot with *chiba* and the other local herbs. This draft stays with the political economy that made the pot expensive. Tea and sugar were imports. Herbs were often not. The genius of the glass, if we are allowed a little admiration, is that mint — loud, cheap where it grows, fragrant enough to announce itself from a doorway — taught a sometimes low-grade Camellia how to be drinkable, and taught sugar how to be the point. Cornwell is blunt that mint can hide the astringency of the leaf that actually showed up. That is a sensory fact and a class fact. The best leaf does not need as much help. Most households are not buying the best leaf.

Hospitality is the plot that survived the prices. A tray, small glasses, foam from a high pour, a second filling of the same leaves, an insistence that the guest drink — these are social technologies. They are not ancient Camellia rituals transplanted intact from Fujian. They are what a Maghrebi house did with a new pair of commodities and an old habit of offering something hot and sweet. The herb tradition, Cornwell notes, is hard to sketch from written sources and older than the boom. I will keep that hedge. Oral pots leave thinner paper.

Urbanization in the twentieth century moved the glass from a rural performance to a city constant. I will not invent a census of cafés. Anyone who has walked a Moroccan or Maghrebi city street in the last half-century has seen the drink as furniture. Television and advertising later sold togetherness back to the people who were already pouring it. That loop is ordinary for staples. Bread does it. Tea does it.

Empire sits in the crate. British and other European trades moved Chinese leaf; colonial and semi-colonial markets moved sugar; Moroccan drinkers made a third thing. A foodways series that only says "Moroccan mint tea" as a flavor profile has deleted the ship. A series that only says "colonial imposition" has deleted the house. Both deletions are popular. Both are incomplete.

Class sits in the second filling. The same leaves poured again are economy and hospitality at once: we are not wasting the crate, and we are not done with you. I have not timed a pour in a particular house. I have read enough descriptions, including Cornwell's, to treat the refill as a structural fact of the drink rather than a flourish for tourists. Tourists get the flourish. Households get the structure.

I will not make this a health story. The glass is sweet enough to be a dessert. That is a dental and caloric fact I am not going to turn into a lecture, and not going to turn into a virtue. Sweetness in Mintz's sense is labor and status. Factory sugar in a Maghrebi glass is a cousin of factory sugar in a British working-class cup, even when the herb is different. Meaning arises from use.

This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent. It is not medical advice. If a caption says mint was "used for digestion" and then winks, that wink belongs to a mixed household register we already refused to climax on. The climax here is a price, a pour, and a plant that grew nearer than the crate. When mint taught the leaf to sit, it did not teach it to become a clinic. It taught it to become a guest.
