The first time I understood *cha* as a hospitality syllable rather than a plant, it was from a refrigerator, not a tea caddy.

Japanese *mugicha* is roasted barley poured over ice, or kept in a fat plastic pitcher that lives on the bottom shelf all summer. School jugs, vending-machine bottles, a household pot that makes the kitchen smell like toast — those are the objects. *Sobacha* is the buckwheat cousin, nuttier, often a winter or gift-tin drink, still not Camellia. English calls both "tea" because English is lazy in a way this series has already complained about. Japanese keeps the *cha* and does not thereby claim a Yunnan bush. The syllable traveled with the pause: a cup, a table, a break in heat or work.

I want the roast in the first paragraph of method. Barley is not infused like a blossom. It is browned until it smells like a cereal that has met a pan, then boiled or steeped in quantity. The roast is why the drink can stand in the same visual family as coffee and black tea without being either. Color is a teacher. A pale barley would look like dishwater; a dark one looks like a decision. Wartime and scarcity kitchens in more than one country learned that lesson. Japan's summer barley jug is not a wartime relic in the present tense — it is an ordinary grocery — but it belongs to the same family of cereal drinks as the medieval *ptisanē* we already met. I will not draw a folk-etymology arrow between Athens and a Tokyo supermarket. I will say: grain can be a table drink. The fact keeps arriving.

Vending machines matter. They are how a foodway becomes infrastructure. A cold barley bottle in a station is as Japanese-summer as a cicada joke, and as unromantic as a fluorescent tube. If your history of "herbal tea" cannot include a PET bottle, your history is a boutique. The bottle also tells you the drink left the connoisseur's *kyusu* and entered commute time. That is a class and labor fact.

Household labor sits on the other side of the bottle. Someone still roasts or someone opens a bag of already-roasted grain and boils a pot in the morning so the children can drink it at four. The pot is large because the drink is a water-cousin: you do not ration it like gyokuro. Korean *boricha*, in the next draft, will look familiar. The Sea of Japan is not a wall for cereal drinks.

*Sobacha* lets me say something about gift economy. Buckwheat is a plant with its own noodle prestige. As a drink it shows up in souvenir tins and winter hotels. I have not audited the gift market and I will not invent a percentage. I will say the tin is doing "local flavor" work, the way Alpine blends do landscape work. The plant is real. The tin is a story about the plant.

Children's hours belong in this draft. A pitcher that can be drunk by a child at four in the afternoon is a different social object from a small bitter cup reserved for adults. I will not romanticize that as innocence. I will say the household sorted liquids by who was in the room, and barley often won the large-volume slot because it was cheap and brown enough to look like a decision.

Caffeine-absence is the English aisle's favorite fact about these drinks. It is a fact about Camellia, not about barley. People drink *mugicha* because it is cold, cheap, and tastes like roasted grain, not because they read a label about a stimulant they are avoiding. A claims-guarded series should not become a decaf sermon. The sermon is how "herbal tea" got medicalized in American marketing. The pitcher does not need that sermon.

A last method note: some bottles are blended with other grains or with a flavor. The supermarket SKU is not a farm. If you want the roast, smell the bag. If you cannot smell the bag, you bought a design.

This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent. It is not medical advice. If a wellness page has tried to make barley a "cleanse," that page is in the wrong genre and we will not finish its sentence. We will stay with the refrigerator, the school jug, the station bottle, and the syllable *cha* that learned to mean a pause even when the leaf in the pot was never a leaf.
