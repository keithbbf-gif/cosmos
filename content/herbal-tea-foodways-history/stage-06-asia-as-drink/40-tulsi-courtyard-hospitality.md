The plant I want is the one by the door.

In a great many Indian households — not all, not as a law, not as a brand — *tulsi* (*Ocimum tenuiflorum* and related basils) stands in a pot or a small masonry shrine in the courtyard or on a balcony. People water it. People walk around it. A leaf may go into water or into a tea-glass or into a prayer. The English aisle later sold "holy basil tea" in a sachet that has never seen a courtyard. Those are not the same foodway. This draft is about the courtyard plant as a household object, and about the drink only as something that plant sometimes becomes. It is not a *rasayana* lecture. It is not the mid-century laboratory-word folder. If you want those arguments, they already have a fence elsewhere.

I am not Hindu, and I am not going to perform a temple tour from a search snippet. The public, ordinary fact is enough: a basil with a sacred name is kept alive as part of the day's domestic religion, the way other houses keep a kitchen god, a saint's card, or a garden that is not only salad. Domestic religion is foodways-adjacent because it decides who may pick the leaf, when, and for whom. A guest may be offered a glass. A fast day may have a permitted liquid. A child may be told not to break the plant. Those are rules of a house.

When the leaf is drunk, the honest description is small. A few leaves in hot water, or in tea that is already Camellia, or in a sweetened glass. I will not give a handful count. I will not call it a protocol. I will say the plant is near the drinking water of the house, which is already a stronger fact than a blend name.

Export and the sachet change the object again. "Holy basil" on a kraft tin in a North American shop is a Latin binomial plus a piety word. The piety word does work. It lets a grocery object borrow a courtyard. Sometimes the borrow is respectful; sometimes it is a costume. I cannot audit the companies. I can say the courtyard does not need the tin, and the tin should not pretend it *is* the courtyard.

There is a naming mess I will put on the table without solving it. Tulsi is not the same plant as sweet basil on a pizza, and it is not automatically the same as every "holy basil" in a Thai kitchen (*kraphao* is a different culinary life). Grocery English collapses basils. A claims-guarded series should refuse the collapse. If a source says tulsi, keep tulsi. If a Thai recipe says a different basil, do not steal this draft for that wok.

Gender and labor hide in the watering. In many of the households that keep the plant, the person who tends it is not the person who writes the English blog. I will not invent a statistic. I will notice that a living plant is daily work, and a sachet is not. Foodways that only photograph the shrine and never the watering can are doing the same lie as foodways that only photograph the leaf and never the pot.

This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent. It is not medical advice. I am repeating the fence because tulsi is one of the plants English wellness most likes to recruit. The recruitment usually begins with respect ("sacred") and ends with a complaint the plant is supposed to handle. We will not finish that sentence. Sacred is a household fact. A body complaint is someone else's genre. The courtyard can hold the first without owing the second.

If you take nothing else: when you see "holy basil tea," ask whether anyone is still watering a pot. If yes, you are nearer this essay. If no, you are in the aisle, which is a legitimate place to buy a flavor, and a bad place to buy a courtyard.
