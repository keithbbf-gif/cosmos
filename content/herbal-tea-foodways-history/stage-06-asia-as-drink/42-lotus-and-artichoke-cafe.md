Dalat has a souvenir problem, and the souvenir is a drink.

Vietnamese *trà atisô* — artichoke as a sweet, brown, sometimes canned infusion associated with the highlands around Đà Lạt — is a regional cafe and gift-tin object. I have not stood in the market counting cans. I have seen the cans in the way anyone who has walked a Vietnamese grocery has seen them: artichoke on the label, a landscape behind it, sweetness you do not expect if your only artichoke is a Mediterranean vegetable on a plate. The plant is *Cynara*, grown as a crop in a highland that French colonial agriculture helped remake. That last clause is the kind of sentence that wants a monograph. I will leave it as a hedge: highland vegetable economies have colonial plots, and this drink sits in one of them. I will not invent a founder or a first factory year I have not checked.

Lotus sits in a different Vietnamese and wider East Asian drink life: scent more than vegetable. Lotus tea in the Camellia sense is often green leaf scented with flowers — which means it may be "real tea" plus a flower, not an herbal infusion at all. Lotus as seed, stem, and plant also enters sweet soups and other liquids. A claims-guarded series has to say when the word *trà* means scented Camellia and when it means a plant that never saw Yunnan. The cafe does not always say. The tin does not always say. Ask.

I grouped these two because they are how a region sells a landscape in a liquid: highland vegetable, water flower, gift bag for the train or the airport. That is foodways as tourism without reducing the drinkers at home to tourists. People in Đà Lạt drink artichoke because it is around, because it is sweet, because it is what the shop poured. People elsewhere drink it because the trip needs a portable hill.

English marketing of artichoke infusions has a favorite body-story I will not finish. You have seen it if you have seen a European or American "detox" caption. This folder's job is to refuse the caption and keep the can. The can is already interesting: a thistle crop, a highland town, a sugar level, a gift economy. It does not owe anyone a cleanse.

Lotus marketing prefers emperors and moonlight. Sometimes there is a real scenting craft — leaves and flowers layered, overnight, a skill. Sometimes there is a flavor oil. I cannot tell them apart from a photograph. A foodways reader should assume the cheap tin is the oil until someone proves the craft. That assumption is not cynicism. It is how you avoid buying a poem.

Cafe geography: a low table, a glass of ice, a slow afternoon, condensed milk in some drinks and not in these. Artichoke and lotus sit nearer to the "something brown or floral in a glass" family than to the espresso family. They are pause drinks. Pause drinks are this series' home.

Gift labor is the part a tourist forgets. Someone has to carry the can, choose the label, decide whether the hill town is for a teacher, an aunt, a host. The drink at home may be whatever is open. The drink on the train is a message about a place. Foodways should keep both. A history that only sneers at souvenirs will miss how regions teach themselves to strangers, and how strangers are sometimes cousins returning with a bag.

I will not invent a first-year factory or a French planter's diary I have not read. Highland vegetable economies in Vietnam have a colonial plot; the can has a postwar plot; the grocery in San Jose or Prague has a diaspora plot. Three plots can share a thistle without becoming one myth.

This draft does not treat, cure, or prevent. It is not medical advice. If an artichoke page tries to become a protocol, it has left the cafe. If a lotus page tries to become a dynasty, it has left the tin. Stay with the highland crop and the flower that may only be a scent. Those are enough objects for an honest cup.
