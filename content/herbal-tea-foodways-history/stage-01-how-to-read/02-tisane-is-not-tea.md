The grocery aisle is a poor linguist. It prints TEA on a box of chamomile, a box of roasted barley, a box of hibiscus calyces, and a box of actual *Camellia sinensis*, then lets the shelf do the rest of the thinking. English has been doing this for a long time. It is convenient. It is also a category error with a string tag.

Tea, if we are going to be dull and accurate for one paragraph, is an infusion of the tea plant. The plant has a Linnaean name and a trade history that already fills libraries. Almost everything else that Americans call "herbal tea" is a drink made by pouring hot water over some other plant part: leaf, flower, fruit, seed, roasted grain, bark, calyx. The European trade association that now sits in Hamburg — Tea & Herbal Infusions Europe, stitched together in 2015 from an older tea committee and an older herbal-infusions association — bothers to keep the bins separate. That is not pedantry for its own sake. It is how a food category stays describable when the marketing department would rather it not be.

The French word that English sometimes borrows to look precise is *tisane*. It looks like a solution. It is not. Open the Oxford English Dictionary and the word arrives with two lives. The older life is barley water: Anglo-Norman *tysanne*, Middle French *ptisane*, from Latin *ptisana* / *tisana*, from Greek *ptisanē*, peeled or crushed barley, related to *ptissein*, to peel or pound. A thirteenth-century kitchen that said *tisane* was talking about a cereal drink, sometimes used as the liquid in which other plants were cooked. The later life — "a mild herbal infusion" — is the one cafés prefer. Dictionaries still carry a sickroom sense for that later life. I am mentioning the dictionary, not writing a clinic. The point for foodways is simpler: the word began as grain, not as mint.

I like that origin because it ruins the romance in the useful direction. If your tongue wants "tisane" to mean "the elegant European way to say herbal tea," the barley is waiting in the etymology like a pebble in a shoe. Barley water is a household drink. It is cheap. It is filling in a thin way. It is the sort of thing a receipt book can boil without a Latin sermon. When the word later widened to cover linden blossom in a Paris kitchen or a paper sachet in a Minneapolis cupboard, it dragged that household past with it, whether the sachet admits it or not.

English "tea" did the opposite journey. It began as a specific imported leaf and then became a social event, a meal, a verb, and finally a generic for any hot steeped drink. That is why a sober-minded writer can say "I will put the kettle on for tea" and mean peppermint. The sentence is good English and bad botany. Foodways has to live with both facts. People name what they pour by the hour and the company, not by the flora of Yunnan.

So what do we do in this folder?

We use **tea** when we mean *Camellia*, or when we are quoting a speaker who used the word as a social form ("tea" as the afternoon, "tea" as the wartime substitute). We use **infusion** or **decoction** when the method matters: leaves steeped versus roots or grains boiled. We use **tisane** when we are talking about the French word and its barley past, not as a prestige sticker. We use the **local name** first: *bissap*, *mugicha*, *tilleul*, *cassina*, *atay*. If a draft cannot survive without "herbal tea" in the first sentence, the draft is already in the aisle.

None of this is a purity cult. I do not think you are a lesser person for saying "chamomile tea." I think a history that wants to be claims-guarded has to notice when a grocery phrase is doing medical work. "Herbal tea" in American English often means "the caffeine-free one," which is already a half-truth (yaupon and mate are not Camellia and are not caffeine-free). It also often means "the one that is for something." For something is where the fence breaks. A drink can be for hospitality, for a fast day, for a harvest, for a child who is allowed the sweet glass and not the coffee. Those are uses. "For" a disease name is a different job, and this series does not take that job.

There is a third confusion I want on the table before we leave the dictionary. Some Asian languages use a *cha* / *chay* / *chai* root for a wide range of infusions, including roasted barley and tart berries, because the social form — a cup, a table, a pause — traveled with the Camellia trade. Korean *boricha* and Japanese *mugicha* are not pretending to be Yunnan leaf. They are table drinks that borrowed a syllable. That is the opposite of the American aisle, which borrowed the English word *tea* and then stuffed every sachet into it. One is a hospitality word widening. The other is a commodity word widening. Both are historical. Only one of them tends to show up next to a structure/function claim.

I will keep repeating the dull sentence because it is the one the kettle keeps trying to steam off: this draft does not treat, cure, or prevent. It is not medical advice. A naming essay is still an essay about language and shelves. If you came here for a list of which "teas" to drink for which complaint, you are in the wrong folder, and the folder will not become that list just because the word *tea* is elastic.

The useful test is this. Can you describe the drink as a foodways object — plant part, vessel, hour, company, price, substitute — without a payoff in a condition? If yes, it belongs here. If the sentence dies without the condition, it belongs to a different literature, and we will not smuggle that literature in through a French noun.
