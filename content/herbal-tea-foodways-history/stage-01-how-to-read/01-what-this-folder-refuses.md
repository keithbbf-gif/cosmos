I am going to spend the first page on what we will not do. If that sounds like a lawyer got to the kettle first, good. The English phrase *herbal tea* has spent a century sliding off a kitchen table and onto a claim. The slide is almost always greased with a condition name.

This folder is a stack of foodways essays. It does not treat, cure, or prevent any disease. It is not medical advice. It is not a shopping list dressed up as folklore. It is not the herbal-medicine pack that already lives in this repo, and it is not the mid-century laboratory-word pack either. Those folders have their own fences. This one stays at the table.

That is not a humility ritual. It is the only way to talk about these drinks without lying in a soft voice.

Here is the usual lie, in its polite form: a writer tells you that a grandmother, a monastery, and a "Pharaoh" were all, without knowing the word, brewing a functional beverage. Then the writer names a modern condition — a sleep that sounds like a diagnosis, a digestion that sounds like a chart, a blood number that sounds like a lab — and lets the cup sit next to the condition until your eye does the rest. No one typed the forbidden verb next to a tumor name. The sentence still did the work.

I refuse that sentence, including the version that swaps "cures" for "supports" and hopes the statute will hold. I also refuse the version that keeps the plant and deletes the people: no harvest hour, no sugar price, no street glass, no wartime substitution, just a Latin binomial and a vibe.

What I will do instead is slower, and less flattering to anyone who wants a hero leaf.

I will keep **Camellia** and **not-Camellia** in separate bins. Tea, in the narrow botanical sense, is an infusion of *Camellia sinensis*. Almost everything else that the grocery aisle calls "herbal tea" is an infusion, a decoction, or a flavored water of some other plant. The French word *tisane* is older than the aisle and younger than barley. It does not make a mint leaf into a tea plant.

I will keep **foodways** as the plot. Sidney Mintz's useful stubbornness still applies: meaning does not inhere in a substance. It arises from use — who pours, who is poured for, what the cup costs, what it stands in for when the real tea is taxed or gone. A hibiscus glass at Christmas and a hibiscus glass on a Dakar street are not the same sentence just because the calyx is the same.

I will keep **names** in their languages. *Bissap*, *zobo*, *sorrel*, *jamaica*, *karkadeh* are not a branding problem waiting for "hibiscus tea" to solve them. *Tsai tou vounou* is a mountain name, not an export slogan. *Ivan-chai* is a Russian plant-name and a drink-name, and the fashionable fermented version is younger than the story that usually travels with it.

I will keep **uncertainty** visible. Thunberg's 1772 note about country people making tea from *Borbonia cordata* is a sentence about a Cape plant. It is not a birth certificate for *Aspalathus linearis*. The popular "Ginsberg, 1904" line for rooibos commercialization sits next to quieter evidence that the plant was already being sold locally. If I flatten that into one confident year, I am already doing the aisle's job.

I will not invent a notebook I have not held. I have not stood in Koporye watching fireweed leaves darkened to look like Chinese black tea. I have not graded Soviet harvest ledgers or counted Cahokia beakers. When I mention a *PNAS* paper on *Ilex* biomarkers, I am reporting a published argument, not a dig I performed.

I will not give you a dose. The minute a foodways essay hands over milligrams, it has changed jobs. Form matters — infusion, decoction, sun tea, roasted grain, fermented leaf — and I will talk about form as kitchen history. I will not talk about form as a protocol for your Tuesday.

I will not do the "balanced" trick where the last paragraph says "more research is needed" after the first twelve paragraphs have already sold an indication. If a draft cannot live without a disease payoff, the draft should die.

Why bother, then?

Because the drinks are real, the gardens are real, and the grocery category is real, and they are not the same object. A person who wants to understand why a gas-station tin says HERBAL TEA is entitled to a history that does not recruit their pancreas. A person who loves a Maghrebi glass of mint is entitled to hear about sugar, gunpowder tea, and the older pot of herbs without a wellness caption. A person who is tired of both wellness copy and sneering debunking is entitled to a third tone: specific, a little dry, unwilling to convert a grandmother's pot into a press release.

There is a fourth reader I am writing against: the one who thinks a claims fence is cowardice. I do not think it is cowardice to refuse a sentence that would be illegal on a label and still tempting in an essay. Essays have more room than labels. That is exactly why they leak.

If you want a clinician, see a clinician who knows you. If you want a bottle, read the bottle as a legal and commercial artifact and then decide what you are actually buying: a species, a part, a cut, a story. If you want the story, stay here. The story is long. It has a barley-water word that outlived the barley, a holly that reached Cahokia, a red drink that crossed the Atlantic in more than one direction, a Cederberg shrub that picked up a protected name in Europe, and a wartime cupboard that learned to call roasted grain "tea."

None of that is a cure. I am repeating myself on purpose. Repetition is how a fence stays up when the next sentence wants a disease.
