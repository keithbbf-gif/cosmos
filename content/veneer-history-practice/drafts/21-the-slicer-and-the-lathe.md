A pit saw makes a leaf and a pile of dust that used to be a leaf. A slicer makes a stack of pages. A rotary lathe makes a ribbon. The nineteenth century did not invent veneer. It invented a way to spend a log that made the apology possible later, because the sheet got cheap enough to put on objects that did not deserve a face.

I have never stood next to a full-size slicer in a mill I could name in this county. [VERIFY] plant lists; I will not invent a head and dogs in Warren. I have stood next to the result: a numbered bundle, a cooked smell still in the crate, a knife mark on the tight face. The machine is present in the leaf.

## Cook, dog, knife

The log or the flitch is heated so the knife can take a thin sheet without shattering the figure. The cook is a climate you apply on purpose. Too little and the leaf checks. Too much and the color shifts, the figure goes muddy, the mill has cooked the money. I cannot give you a mill’s schedule. I can say the bundle I open is a cooked object. It will keep moving as it dries to my shop.

Dogs hold the flitch. The knife is a plane iron the size of a door. Flat slice, quarter, rift: the mill decides the angle the way I decide a saw. The stack comes off in order. That order is the only reason bookmatching is a job and not a prayer.

## Nobel and the ribbon

Immanuel Nobel’s work on rotary peeling in the mid-nineteenth century is the name plywood histories keep using. [VERIFY] date and place against a monograph, not a motivational paragraph about dynamite. The lathe turns the log. The knife peels a continuous sheet. Yield jumps. Plywood becomes possible at a scale that is not a cabinetmaker’s glue-up of sawn leaves.

Rotary birch, rotary maple, a crossband that no one will see: the lathe is why a modern core exists. It is also why a rental door looks like a tree that was spun. I do not hate the lathe. I hate a caption that calls the ribbon a quartered oak.

## What the shop gained

Sequence. Width. A crotch that can be a pair of dining leaves instead of one split slab. Architectural panels. A price that lets a church face a wall. A price that lets a factory face a million drawers.

The shop also gained a thickness that a belt can erase. Sawn veneer had meat. Sliced architectural veneer is a business card. The machine did not make sand-through. The machine made sand-through cheap and common.

## What the county may not have gained

A lumber mill and a veneer mill are different capital. A town can saw furniture stock for a century and buy its faces from a mill that cooks and slices. That is not a failure. It is a speciality. [VERIFY] before a heritage page says we sliced. What I can say: we live among the trees that become faces somewhere. We can still put those faces on furniture here, with a pot or a bag, and tell the truth about the cut.

If a photograph ever turns up of a slicer in Bradley County, I want it captioned with a date and a plant name and a rights line. Until then the slicer is a machine I buy from.

## Checks, barber pole, the mill’s scars

Lathe checks: the ribbon bent off the log and broke a little. They show under stain. Slicer chatter: a dull knife, a washboard in the face. Barber-pole: a mismatch of tight and loose faces, or a slip that flipped, a stripe that blinks. Some of this is mill. Some of this is me. I look at the bundle in raking light before I blame the finish.

A mill grade is a promise about defects they already saw. It is not a promise about my July.

## Sawn meat and the sliced card

I keep a sawn oak leaf in the rack that still has meat. You can feel the edge. A block can take a mistake. I also keep sliced architectural walnut that is a business card. A belt will erase it before lunch. The nineteenth-century machines did not invent the thin face — sawyers had already spent a log on dust — but they made the thin face ordinary. Ordinary is how a thickness becomes a habit you stop naming.

When a bundle arrives I feel the edge before I fall in love with a cathedral. If the leaf powders, it is dry and it will take shop air and grow. If it limp-rags, it still remembers the cook. I sticker and weight. I do not tape a dining top the afternoon the crate comes off the truck. The cook is a climate the mill applied. My shop is a different climate. The leaf has to walk from one to the other before I ask it to sit.

Sawn leaf I will use on a piece I expect a sander to love later — a family table, a desk that will be “refinished” in twenty years. Sliced leaf I will use when the match is the job and I can control the sanding. I write the cut on the back if I know it: *sawn* or *sliced* or *rotary*. I often do not know. I write what I can defend. I do not write quartered on a ribbon that was spun.

## The rental door I called quartered

A rental house had a hollow door with a rotary maple face that someone had stained toward oak. The tenant called it quartered oak because the catalog had. I stood in the doorway and said maple, rotary, a print of a tree that was spun. I said it too sharply. The tenant had not chosen the door. I should have saved the sharpness for the catalog.

The lesson I can use is the caption. Rotary is a useful core face and a bad dining-table story. Half-round and stay-log — the mill words for a flitch held so the knife takes a wider, flatter sheet — are how you get a cathedral without spinning the log. I have never stood next to a full-size head and dogs in a plant I can name in Bradley County. I will not invent one in Warren so the heritage page has a machine.

I have captioned my own shop door wrong in a photograph: a rift oak face I called quartered because the flake was shy. Rift and quarter are cousins. They are not twins. The mill decides the angle. I decide the sentence. If I cannot see flake, I say rift or I say I do not know. The lathe is innocent. My mouth is not.

## A cooked crate that checked in July

I opened a crate in a July that was already arguing and fanned the flitch to show a client. The leaves were still cooked-soft in the middle of the stack. By the time we had talked, the top pages had taken shop air and the edges had started to speak. By morning I had checks I had not bought. I bought them by fanning a wet book in a dry room.

I now open a crate the way I open a book I intend to close: a few pages, a weight, a look, back into the binding. If I must show a client a walk of cathedrals, I show photographs of the fan I already made under control, or I show three leaves under a board. I do not perform the whole novel on the bench while the air conditioner runs.

Barber-pole and chatter are mill scars I look for in raking light. Checks I caused are my scars. I mark them on the crate map so I do not put a check at an elbow on a dining top and then blame the knife. The knife got faster. My July did not get kinder.

## The knife got faster. The job did not.

I still tape. I still match. I still write both faces on the back. The slicer did not remove the ébéniste. It removed the pit saw as the default. That is enough history for a machine essay.
