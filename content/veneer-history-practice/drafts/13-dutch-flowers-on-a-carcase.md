A moth on a table in the Rijksmuseum is a scrap of a pale wood, antennae like a knife mistake you meant. The tulips are stained, or they were green once and went brown, or they are a species that reads as a petal in north light. Jan van Mekeren’s shop and the Amsterdam makers around him sold gardens that you could set a glass on. [VERIFY] a named table against the Rijksmuseum collection before a caption uses his name. I look at the moth first. If the moth is cut clean, the shop was awake.

Dutch floral marquetry is veneer as painting. The carcase is oak, often, a box that can be joined in a damp country. The face is a bouquet assembled from a rack of leaves: ebony, rosewood, stained maple, olive, a green whose chemistry is a conservation file. The still-life painters were in the same town. The furniture wanted the same wall.

## Why flowers

Tulip mania is a cliché I will not lean on. Flowers were money, science, a garden you could own in a climate that did not always play along. A wooden bouquet does not need water. It needs hide glue and a cartoon and a client who wants the table to do what a painting does, plus hold a plate.

The cartoon is shared language with the studiolo, but the goal is different. Urbino wanted space. Amsterdam wanted a surface that beat a painting at permanence and lost to a painting at sky. A table cannot have sky. It can have a parrot and a lily.

I think about that when a client wants a “live edge” as if a slab were automatically more honest than a picture. A van Mekeren table is honest about being a picture. A slab can be honest about being a tree. A printed flower on an MDF table is honest about being a catalog. I know which one I will repair.

## The oak box

The Netherlands had oak. The tropics had the dark woods. The sentence is Antwerp’s sentence with a different city. The floral table is a local carcase and an imported palette. When the palette fades — greens to brown, reds to rust — the bouquet becomes a memory of a bouquet. Museums talk about this in condition reports. I talk about it when a client wants “that Dutch look” in aniline dyes that will also shift. I test a cutoff in a window.

## Sand-shading and the round petal

Hot sand turns a leaf edge brown so a petal reads round. The method is simple and the mistakes are permanent. I have shaded a leaf for a small repair and I have overcooked it into a crisp. The Dutch shops did this all day. The evenness is the flex. A petal that is scorched in a blotch is a petal that was rushed.

I do not sand-shade oak flake and call it Dutch. Flake is a ray. A petal is a cartoon. Mixing the religions makes a joke.

## What lifts

Floral work lifts at the small pieces first. A stamen is a toothpick of wood. A moth antenna is a dare. Hide glue lets you put it back if you are patient and the piece has not been flooded with a modern finish that sealed the crime scene. PVA under a later repair is a fight. I will meet that fight in the repair stage of this pack.

If I ever cut a floral panel, I will keep a map of the species. The original shops had a rack and a habit. I have a messy bench. The map is the habit I can choose.

## Arkansas is not Amsterdam

I will not put a tulip on a Saline River dining table and call it heritage. Bradley County’s heritage is oak, pine millwork, a mill town, a furniture stock. [VERIFY] plant product mix before a brochure. Flowers can be a commission. They are not a county story.

What I will take from Amsterdam is the refusal to apologize for a picture on a box. The leaf is allowed to be a moth. The carcase is allowed to be oak. The client is allowed to set a glass on the moth if the finish can stand it. I would not. I would use a coaster. The moth took someone an hour.

## The garden and the later shops

English restoration work, French flowers, a Victorian revival: floral marquetry keeps coming back because it photographs and because it is obviously skilled. The skill is real. The revival is sometimes a paste. I look at the moth. I look at the oak box. I look at whether the flowers sit in a ground that still has a grain direction you can believe.

Boulle will take the picture into metal and shell. The Dutch table stays in wood. I like that better for a shop that sells wood. Brass is another bench. A moth in holly is my bench, if I ever earn it.

When I bookmatch oak flake, I am not making a bouquet. I am making one thought. The Dutch shops made a hundred thoughts and glued them into one plane. Both are veneer. Only one of them needs a cartoon of a moth. I keep a photograph of a good moth on the shop door anyway, so I remember that a leaf can be a wing.

## The sewing table with one flower

I cut a single tulip on a sewing-table lid because I wanted the moth lesson without a shop’s reputation. Holly for the petal if I had it, maple when I did not, a stained scrap for the leaf, walnut for a stem, a ground of quiet oak. [VERIFY] the holly species on that leftover; “holly” in a scrap bin can be more than one pale wood. The cartoon was simple. The afternoon was not. I sand-shaded the petal edge and I overcooked the left wing. I recut. The second petal sat.

The table is still in the shop. Clients touch the tulip and then they look at the oak box under it. That is the Dutch sentence I wanted: the flower is a picture, the carcase is a joined thing in a damp country, and you can set a glass on the picture if the finish can stand it. I would not. I use a coaster. The tulip took me longer than a door.

I keep a species map in the folder: petal, leaf, stem, ground, the dye I used on the green. When that green goes brown — and aniline will shift; I test a cutoff in a window — a future me can recut the leaf without guessing. The original Amsterdam shops had a rack and a habit. I have a messy bench. The map is the habit I can choose.

## The stamen I lost in the pot

Floral work lifts at the small pieces first. I know that sentence and I still lost a stamen. A toothpick of pale wood, hide a little too wet, a hammer pull that was a tap instead of a squeegee, and the piece slid into the pot. I fished it out. I could not use it. I recut from the same scrap and I taped it before I breathed on it.

## How the cartoon gets onto the lid

I draw the tulip on thin paper at the size of the lid, not at the size of my pride. I prick the lines onto the ground, or I glue the paper to a packet of the pale woods and saw the petal as a stack. A degree off and the vase sits like a drunk. I learned that on the first petal I overcooked; I remembered it on the second.

The ground stays a field with a grain direction I can believe. I do not tile flowers over a featureless sheet and call it Amsterdam. A bouquet with no plane under it is a sticker. I leave oak showing so the lid is still a lid. Then I shade only the edges the drawing asked for, and I stop. The sewing table is a commission I gave myself so I would have one flower I could stand next to. A table of them is a shop’s reputation I have not earned. I have a lid, a map, and the pot I will not fish another stamen out of if I tape first.
