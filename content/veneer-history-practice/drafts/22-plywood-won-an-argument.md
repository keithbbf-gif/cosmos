Look at the edge of a Baltic birch drawer side. You are looking at veneer. Not a face. A stack of faces, each one a rotary leaf, glued in disagreement so the panel stays a rectangle. Plywood won an argument that the cabinet centuries were already having: the core should be the furniture, and the furniture can be made of leaves.

The apology never caught up. People say plywood the way they say veneer, as a confession. Then they sit in an Eames lounge and call it design. The lounge is plywood that went to art school. The closet shelf is plywood that went to a price. I use both. I caption both.

## Portland and the industrial sheet

U.S. softwood plywood as an industry is often dated to Portland Manufacturing Company and a 1905 fair demonstration — a sentence I want [VERIFY] against a mill history before a caption. The idea is older: stacked leaves, crossed grain, hide or a protein, a cabinetmaker’s panel. The industry is the lathe plus a press plus a building trade that wanted a sheet the size of a wall.

Furniture took the sheet and put a prettier leaf on it. That is my Tuesday. Construction took the sheet and put a stamp on it. I do not veneer a CDX and call it a sideboard. I said that in the core essay. I say it again because plywood is a family name.

## Crossed grain is the joint

Each ply’s grain fights the next. Movement is still there; it is smaller and in a rectangle. That is why a plywood door can stay a door when a solid glue-up of the same width becomes a propeller. It is also why an edge is ugly and true. The stripes are the argument made visible.

I band the edge or I finish the stripes as stripes. I do not fill them with a crayon and say solid oak. The crayon is the twentieth-century apology in a stick.

## The Eames shell

Charles and Ray Eames’s molded plywood chairs — LCW and the cousins — are leaves stacked and bent on a form, a compound curve that a solid could not take without a carve and a waste. [VERIFY] date and manufacturer for a photographed chair. The object admits what it is. The beauty is the bend and the face together. That is veneer as structure, not veneer as a hat.

I have bent a two-ply for a jig. I have not made an LCW. The lesson I can use: if the leaf is the structure, the glue and the form are the joint. If the leaf is a hat, the core is the joint. Do not mix the religions in one panel without a recipe.

## Formaldehyde and the shop air

Modern plywood glues are a chemistry I will not cook. I will buy a sheet with a stamp I can read — CARB, TSCA, whatever the invoice uses this year — and I will [VERIFY] the stamp we actually stock. I ventilate when I saw. I do not write a homebrew resin.

The cabinet centuries used hide on a ground. The plywood century used a press and a resin that does not want to reverse. That is why a blister on a 1970s door is a different essay from a blister on a Phyfe-manner leaf.

## Voids I sold as a drawer side

I built a run of drawers from a construction sheet because it was in the rack and the face was going to be painted. The sides were not painted. The edges showed voids like missing teeth. A client opened a drawer in the light and asked if the box was hollow. It was not hollow. It was cheap. I remade the sides in Baltic birch and I ate the sheet. The face of the drawer was a walnut leaf I had been proud of. The side was the confession.

Baltic birch — the dense birch stack with fewer voids if the grade is the grade I paid for — is a drawer side I will show. A CDX stamp is a sheathing I will not veneer and call a sideboard. I said that in the core essay. I say it here because plywood is a family name and families have members you do not invite to dinner.

When I band a plywood edge, I am hiding stripes or I am framing them. I decide before the glue. A crayon fill that wants to be oak is the motel century in a stick. A solid band thicker than a thumbnail is a hip that can meet the case. I have filled voids with a putty that shrank and left a dotted line under the band. The band was honest. The putty was a delay. I now reject a sheet with voids at the arris or I cut the arris off and lose width. Width is cheaper than a dotted line.

## Lumber core is a cousin, not a secret

A dining-table top — the second object next to the Eames shell — is often a lumber core or a veneer-core plywood with a better leaf on both faces. Lumber core is strips, a stable argument, an edge that can take a screw if you plan the strip. Veneer core is all leaves, crossed, an edge that is stripes. MDF is a paint panel I will face only where the piece can stand MDF and a hip will not drink. I write the core on the back. I do not say “wood” and stop.

I have used a veneer-core sheet as a dining top with a mahogany face and a mahogany backer and a solid band. The top stayed a rectangle. I have used a lumber-core sheet with a face and no backer because I was listening to a voice that said the core was enough. The top cupped toward the room that had the humidifier. Both faces, or a cup. Plywood won the argument about crossed grain. It did not repeal the argument about balance.

The Eames shell is plywood that is the object. The dining top is plywood that wears a hat. I do not mix those religions in one panel without a recipe. A shell wants a form and a glue that can take a bend. A top wants a band and a pair of faces. If I bend a dining top on a form, I have built a sculpture that wants to be a trough.

## One face of paint, one face of oak

A closet door came back to the shop with a cup you could see from the hall. I had faced one side with a quiet oak and painted the other because the closet was dark and the client wanted “less wood.” The paint and the leaf did not agree about July. I pulled the door, faced the paint side with a backer that should have been there, and reset it. The rectangle returned. The sentence on the back now says both faces even when one face is a paint-grade leaf.

Plywood does not forgive a one-sided hat any more than a solid core does. The plies are already an argument. The show leaf is another argument. If you only dress one side, you have picked a winner for a week, not for a year.

## What I take

I take plywood as honest furniture when the edge is treated as honest. I take the Eames lesson that a leaf can be a shell. I take the closet-shelf lesson that a leaf can be a price.

I leave the habit of saying “real wood, not plywood,” as if the plies were not wood. They are wood. They are rotary leaves. The question is which leaf, which glue, which edge, which sentence in the room.

Grand Rapids is next: the factory that put faces on a million cases and taught America that furniture could be a catalog. Plywood and the slicer made that factory possible. The apology was optional. The factory did not always decline the option.
