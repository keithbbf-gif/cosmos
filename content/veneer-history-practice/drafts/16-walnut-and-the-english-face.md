An oyster cabinet looks like a pond of coins. Each “oyster” is a slice across a small branch, end grain showing a ring, laid like flagstones on a ground. The English shops of the late seventeenth century — Restoration into Queen Anne — loved this because a branch is cheap compared to a wide walnut board and because the pattern is a flex that still reads as wood. I have cut a few oysters from a walnut limb in the yard. They are not a cabinet. They are a sample that made me respectful. The rings do not lie flat. End grain drinks glue and still wants to lift.

This is veneer without Versailles. A cushion-drawer dressing table, herringbone banding, a seaweed marquetry that is really a tight arabesque in sycamore and walnut: the English face is quieter than Boulle and more domestic than a Dutch bouquet. The carcase is often oak. The face is walnut. The sentence is becoming a habit in this pack because it is the habit.

## Adam Bowett and the dates

Adam Bowett’s English furniture books are the ones I want on the table for dates and for the walnut-to-mahogany shift. [VERIFY] a named piece against Bowett or a catalog. I will not freeze “Queen Anne” as a personality. It is a span of legs, a veneer fashion, a cabriole that is a menuisier problem sitting under an ébéniste box.

Seaweed marquetry — the tight scrolling that looks like a drowned plant — is a knife skill. I have not earned it. I have taped a herringbone banding and called that enough English for a Tuesday. Herringbone is two leaves, opposite grain, a band. It is a drawing. It is also a place a belt loves to sand through because the grain keeps changing its mind.

## Oysters are a loaf the tree made

Yosegi builds a loaf. Oysters slice a loaf the tree grew as a branch. Sequence is the order of slices from the same stick. If you mix sticks, the pond of coins becomes a pond of strangers. I number the slices from one limb. I do not mix a pecan limb into a walnut pond and hope the finish unifies them. It will not.

End grain in a dining top is a crumb and a finish problem. Oysters belong on a vertical face or a box lid that is not a plate. I have seen oysters on a coffee table that became a map of white rings where glasses sat. The English shops were not thinking about condensation from an air-conditioned glass. I am.

## Walnut as a local word

*Juglans regia* in Europe, *Juglans nigra* here: both walnut, different color, different figure, different client expectation. Arkansas black walnut is a shop wood I will veneer and I will build solid. English “walnut period” furniture is not a claim that our walnut is that furniture. I say black walnut. I say European walnut if that is the leaf. I do not say “Queen Anne walnut” as a stain name.

A mill town that cut hardwood for furniture stock knows walnut as a board. The leaf is how you get a matched pair of drawer fronts from a tree that did not give you two boards that agreed.

## The cushion and the band

Cushion drawers — a convex face — are a ground that is not flat. Veneer on a curve is a bag or a hammer and a lot of tape. The English shops did it with hide and cauls I have not seen. I do it with a vacuum bag and a form. The leaf still has to go around without splitting. I cut reliefs in the tape, not in the show face. I have split a show face. I remade the drawer.

Banding is the English habit I use most: a herringbone, a string, a crossband around a field. It frames the picture. It also gives the edge a little meat if the band is thick enough. A paper-thin inlay at an arris is a moth antenna. I band thicker than a thumbnail when the arris is a hip. I can go thinner on a box lid that lives a quiet life.

## Laburnum, yew, and a pond I mixed

English oysters are not always walnut. Laburnum, olive, yew, lignum: the branch woods show up in the books and on the better cabinets. [VERIFY] a named piece’s species before a caption says walnut oysters as a rule. I have a walnut limb I cut in a yard that was mine to cut. I have looked at yew in a photograph and wanted a tighter ring. I did not put the photograph into the pot.

I cut oysters on a bandsaw. The slice wants to be thick enough that a ring reads and thin enough that the pond will flush. Too thin and the end grain is a cracker that checks before the glue gels. Too thick and you have cobbles. I number the slices from one stick, same corner, same hand, the way I number a flitch. I dry them under a board and a weight. They still cup. End grain is a drinker. I size it with a thin hide so the pot does not disappear into the rings. I still get a lift if I rush.

A jewelry-box lid is the object I will put oysters on. I made one the size of a book: three slices from one limb, a small walnut frame, hide, a caul. They sat. I added a fourth slice from a second limb I told myself was close enough. It sat a shade colder and a ring too wide. Oil did not marry them. I warmed the stranger, lifted it, and put a sibling in. The lid is in the shop. It is not a Restoration cabinet. It is a pond that taught me not to mix quarries.

## The dressing table and the pull

A cushion-drawer dressing table is the English object I think about more than the grand oyster cabinet. The convex drawer is a face you open every morning. The pull is a wound waiting. I veneered a small dressing-table drawer for a client who wanted Queen Anne in the room and black walnut we could name. The carcase was oak I had. The face was walnut I sawed from a board because I did not have a flitch that wide. That is allowed on a drawer that size. The leaf still had to go around the cushion without a split.

I drilled the pull holes from the face as if the leaf were a board. The bit walked a fiber and lifted a crescent the size of a fingernail. Hide and a patch from the same board saved it. The patch is visible if you know. I now drill from the back until the point kisses, then finish from the face with a sharp bit and a scrap caul. The English shops had drops and handles too. I do not have their jig. I have that crescent.

Herringbone around that drawer is where I live on a Tuesday. Two leaves, opposite grain, a band that frames the cushion. I shoot the edges on a light box. I tape. I once flushed a herringbone with a block that loved one grain and hated the other. The band became a washboard. I remade the band. I did not remake the drawer. That was luck I do not budget for.

## Seaweed I will not fake

Seaweed marquetry — the tight scrolling the books also call arabesque — is sycamore or holly on walnut, or the reverse, a drowned plant that is a knife drawing. [VERIFY] woods on a named door before a caption. I traced a photograph of a London door and told myself I could cut a corner. I cut a card the size of a playing card. The background crumbled. The scroll fattened. It looked like a vine drawn with a marker. I stopped. The card sits in the job folder so I do not try the same pride on a paid door.

The English face I will sell is the quiet one: a bookmatched walnut field, a herringbone band, a string, an oak box underneath. Seaweed belongs to a shop that has earned the knife. Bowett will date the fashion. I will date my own work with the year I made it and the woods I used. I will not call a herringbone a seaweed to make a tag prettier.

If a client brings a photograph of an oyster cabinet and a dressing table in the same room, I ask which object they want me to attempt. The cabinet is a pond I can sample. The dressing table is a drawer I can finish. The seaweed door is a referral or a no. That is an English lesson too. The court is across the Channel. The bedroom is enough.

## Quiet as a judgment

I like the English walnut face because it does not shout. A bookmatched walnut drawer with a herringbone band is a sentence I will stand next to in a bedroom. A Boulle revival in a ranch house is a shout I will not help with unless the client is doing a joke they can afford.

The next essay is mahogany and the Director: when the English face goes red and the pattern book goes public. Walnut does not end. It gets a rival. The oyster pond stays as a reminder that figure can be a branch you sliced, not a crotch you were lucky to find.
