I dry-fit a door on the bench: rift oak stiles and rails, a quartered flake panel bookmatched down the middle. The flake is a church window. The rift is a quiet stripe that does not compete. The rectangle is a door. If I had made the whole door flake, the rays would have shouted at the edge and the match would have been a propeller in the client’s mouth even if the core stayed flat. If I had made the whole door rift, I would have been honest and a little dull. This layout is the sentence I want in a lot of rooms.

One species. Two cuts. One object. That is matching as joinery.

## Why the stile is rift

A stile is a vertical at a hand and a hinge. Rift reads as a straight grain that does not throw a cathedral off the edge of the door. It also hides a little dirt and a little finger. Flake on a stile is a mirror at a knuckle. It will chip. It will catch light in a way that makes the door look bent when it is not.

I cut stile leaves from the quieter pages of the same book if I can, or from a rift bundle I bought to marry the flitch. Same tree is a luxury. Same species and a married color is the working version. I finish a cutoff of both in the same rag before I promise a marriage.

## Why the panel is flake

The panel is the thought. Flake is why anyone bought quartered oak. Bookmatched, it becomes a field the old shops would have spent a wide board on and then watched move. I let the core take the climate. I let the flake take the room.

The panel gets the pretty pages. The stiles get the straight pages. I do not rob the panel to make the stiles fancy.

## Rails agree with stiles

Rails are rift too, or a quiet quarter that does not throw flake at a hip. A flake rail is a horizontal mirror that will be a shelf for dust and a place a belt starts. I keep the rails in the stile religion.

Mitered vs butted at the stile/rail leaf: this is a picture joint, not a mortise. The structural joint is in the core or in a solid frame if I built a frame-and-panel the old way. On a flat faced door, the leaf joints are tape and glue. I miter the leaf if the rift stripe would otherwise make a T I do not like. I butt if the miter is a wish. I do not do both on one door unless I am repairing a mistake.

## The band is not a stile

A solid oak band at the arris is structure for a hip. It can be the same oak as the stile leaf or a little quieter. It is thicker than a thumbnail. It is not a veneer stile. I have seen doors where the “stile” was a 2-inch rift leaf and the edge was still a tape. The hip found the tape.

Frame-and-panel in solid, with a veneered panel only, is another religion and a good one: the stiles are solid rift, the panel is a flake leaf on a core that floats. Movement is allowed in the panel. That is the medieval clamp chest talking to the ébéniste. I like that door more than a flat slab door when the piece is furniture. I use the slab door when the brief is a flush modern and the core is honest.

## The walnut door that wanted flake stiles

A client brought a magazine photograph of a walnut door with busy figure on the stiles. I dry-fitted that layout on a cutoff. The stile flake caught the shop fluorescent and the door looked twisted when the core was flat. I showed them the cutoff next to a rift-stile sample from the same species. They picked rift. I did not have to argue a propeller. The light argued.

Walnut flake is rarer in my crates than oak flake, and I do not pretend a walnut cathedral is a ray. The religion is the same: quiet grain where a hand and a hinge live, the thought in the panel. If the book has no quiet pages, I marry a rift bundle and I finish both cutoffs in the same rag before I promise the marriage. Same tree is a luxury I have had twice. ## Dry-fitting the rectangle

I tape the stile and rail leaves to a scrap core the size of the door. I stand it in the opening, or I stand it against a wall that has the room’s light. I look from a hallway distance. If the flake shouts at the knuckle, I recut the stile from a quieter page. If the bookmatch in the panel walks, I swap pages before glue. Glue is a late tool.

I mark hinge side and lock side on the back of the leaves. A bookmatched panel does not care which stile is which. A door does. I have hung a door with the prettier stile on the hinge and then watched the lock stile look like a leftover. The rectangle is a use. The photograph is a Tuesday.

I dry-fit the solid band at the same time if the door is a slab. The hip should meet oak, not a tape. If the flake panel runs to the arris on a sample, I recut. The flake never does the hip. That is the second figure in this pack and I will say it until the drawing agrees.

## Leftover flake and the next pair

The pretty pages leftover from a door are not kindling. They go in the job folder for a coin patch, or they wait for a smaller door, a drawer, a jewelry lid. Leftover rift waits for the next stiles. I have robbed leftover flake to fancy a stile on a later job and I have regretted the knuckle. The leftover has a job. The job is not to decorate a hinge.

Frame-and-panel with a floating flake leaf on a thin core is the door I like when the piece is furniture that will live in a house with a woodstove and an August. The stiles are solid rift. The panel can move in its groove. The slab door is for a flush kitchen that asked for a flush kitchen. I do not sell a slab as a floating panel. I write which religion on the back.

A house oak-door spec, if we have one, should say rift stiles, flake panel, solid band, both faces. [VERIFY] before a brochure prints a millimeter religion. What I print here is the sentence I can dry-fit: quiet frame, thought in the field, hip meets wood.

## Light on flake, light on rift

Flake needs a light you have asked. Rift can stand a hallway. I do not put a flake panel in a dark corridor and then wonder why the client thinks they bought plain oak. I do not put flake opposite a west window without a sample in that window. The ray can go metallic and then the client thinks I used metal.

## What I promise

I promise this layout more than I promise a diamond. I can dry-fit it. I can repair a stile without remaking a sun. I can keep leftover flake pages for the panel and leftover rift for a later door.

I promise the band. I promise both faces. I promise the word veneer in the room.

This is the last matching essay. If you can read a flitch, bind a book, keep the numbers, flatten a chip, frame a field, and put rift around flake, you are ready for the glue. The shop stage is the pot, the bag, the tape, the band, the sand-through, the knife, the crate in the corner. The door on the bench is the critic. If the flake shouts at the knuckle, I recut the stile. If the rectangle holds and the hip meets oak, I write the note on the back and I let the glue set without a speech.
