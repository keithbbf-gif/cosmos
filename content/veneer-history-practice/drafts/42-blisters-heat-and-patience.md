I tap a dining top with a fingernail and I stop at a coin that sounds like a drum. The finish is still a film. The leaf is a tent. If the glue is hide, I have a path: heat, a little moisture, a seat, a weight. If the glue is PVA, I have an argument. If the glue is a urea from 1974, I have a museum problem and a shop problem and I may remake the field.

Blisters are local. I map them with tap before I heat anything. I have heated a whole field to chase one coin and I have stained a maple I did not need to stain.

## Hide, the open door

Warm iron, a cloth that is damp not wet, a short visit. The hide relaxes. I press the tent down. I add a little hide if the line starved — a syringe or a knife I did not want, a warm glue, a wipe. I weight until the gel and then some. I tap again tomorrow.

I do not scorch. A scorch is a finish problem and a leaf problem. I test the iron on a cutoff. I keep the iron moving. I am not pressing a shirt.

A knife slit, along the grain if I can, is how I get glue in if the tent will not take it from the edge. The slit is a scar. I place it where a figure will hide it, or I accept a line. I do not cut a flap like a trapdoor on a show face unless the alternative is a remake and the client chose the flap.

## PVA, the argument

PVA does not relax like hide. Heat can soften some of it and can smell and can fail. I test. I have injected a thin glue into a PVA blister and I have made a bigger blister. I have slit and peeled a coin and patched from the folder. I have remade.

I do not lecture a 1990s factory door about hide. I diagnose and I pick a repair that will hold in their July. Sometimes that is a patch. Sometimes that is a new leaf on a new backer. Sometimes that is a polite refusal.

## Urea and the industrial press

A lot of mid-century and later plywood faces are a resin that does not want me. I can still patch. I can still remake. I cannot pretend I am in a Phyfe shop. I write *urea?* on the ticket if I am guessing. I do not guess in the invoice.

## Finish on top of a tent

If I have already finished, the blister is a sealed tent. I may have to cut the film, do the glue, then spot-finish. The finishing pack has the chemistry. I will say: the spot will show if I pretend it will not. I tell the client. I blend as far as the film allows. I do not strip a dining top for a coin unless the coin is a weather system of coins.

## Bubbles in the bag, the same day

A blister I tap the day I open the bag is a gift. No finish. Hide or PVA still fresh enough to be kind. I fix it that day. I do not wait until the delivery because I liked the photograph.

## Water stains and the fake blister

A white ring is often finish, not a tent. I tap. If it is solid, I do not iron it as a blister. I treat it as a film. If I iron a white ring I can make a tent. I have made a tent.

## The maple that remembered the iron

A coin blister on a maple field, hide, a path I trusted. The cloth had a stain from a previous oak job. The iron was warm. The hide seated. The maple took a brown ghost the shape of the cloth. I remade the field. I now keep a white cloth for pale woods and I test the iron on a cutoff. I am not pressing a shirt. I am not pressing yesterday’s oak into today’s birch.

Scorch is the other ghost. I keep the iron moving. A parked iron is a shine that is a burnish, then a sand, then a core. I have parked an iron. The dining top became a sample I keep to show why I tap before I finish and why I test before I heat.

## A white ring I promoted to a tent

A client pointed at a white ring on an oiled table and said blister. I did not tap. I ironed it as a tent. The film softened. The leaf lifted. I had a ring and a drum. I slit, I seated, I spot-finished. The spot showed. I told them. I should have tapped first.

A urea face from a mid-century dresser — I wrote *urea?* on the ticket because I was guessing. I patched a coin from a cousin leaf, not a sibling. I did not lecture the factory about hide. I did not print a year I did not have. [VERIFY] a dated glue claim before an invoice states a chemistry. I guessed in the shop. I did not guess in the invoice.

## Mapping coins before I heat

I tap the whole field before the iron comes off the hook. I mark coins with a chalk that will wipe. I heat the coins, not the county. I have heated a whole top to chase one drum and I have stained more maple than I needed to stain.

A weather system of coins is a remake. A single coin on hide is an iron, a cloth, a weight, a tap tomorrow. A coin on PVA is an argument I may lose. I inject only when I can see where the glue is going. I slit along the grain if I must. I place the slit where the figure will hide it, or I accept a line, or I remake.

## A syringe on a drawer lip

A hide blister at the lip of a bow-front drawer, small enough that a flap would have shown in every open. I warmed the edge, worked a thin hide in with a syringe from the arris, wiped, and weighted with a caul cut to the bow. Tapped the next day. Solid. The folder kept its page 24.

I do not syringe a field I cannot map. The drawer lip had an edge I could see. The dining tent I once flooded from a hole in the middle became a pond I have already named in the flatten essay. Local, visible, hide, a weight that fits the curve: that is the syringe I will still use. A weather system still gets a remake.

## What I write

Map of taps, glue I believe, slit or not, iron or not, weight hours, tap the next day. If I guessed the glue, I write guessed. A future heat on a PVA I called hide is a smell and a failure.

The folder is next: the offcut that makes a coin patch a sibling instead of a stranger. A blister without a folder is a patch from a cousin. A blister with a folder is why I kept page 24. I tap. I map. I heat if hide. I argue if PVA. I remake if the map is a weather system. I do not iron a shirt into a dining top. The coin should disappear to a fingernail. If the fingernail still finds a drum, I am not done.
