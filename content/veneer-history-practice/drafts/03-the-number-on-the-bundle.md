The bundle said 1/42. The caliper said 0.022 on one edge and 0.018 on the other. That is not a scandal. That is a leaf. Thickness in veneer is a range dressed as a fraction, and the fraction is a sales courtesy, not a machinist’s dimension.

I still read the stamp. Grade, cut, species, flitch number if I am lucky. Then I caliper three places. Then I look at the back. Paper, phenolic, fleece, raw. The backing is a handling decision that becomes a sanding limit and a glue decision. People buy “oak veneer” and mean four different products. The bundle is trying, in its blunt way, to tell you which.

## Raw leaf

Raw sliced veneer is wood. It curls. It cracks if you store it on edge in a dry week. It takes hide, PVA, urea, the bag, the hammer. It is what matching is for. Sequence numbers live here. This is the furniture leaf if you have a press and the patience to tape.

It is also the leaf that will shatter when a helper snaps it like a bedsheet. I unroll on the bench, weights on the corners, a damp towel in a plastic if it arrived like a potato chip. Flatten first. Glue second. The flattening essay comes later in this pack. The number on the bundle will not flatten it for you.

Raw leaf thickness varies by mill and species. Architectural hardwood is often in the 0.5–0.7 mm neighborhood. Thick-cut decorative can be a millimeter and change. [VERIFY] the bundle. A “1/16” claim on a modern architectural oak is a claim I would like to meet in person.

## Paper-backed

Ten-mil paper, sometimes twenty, sometimes a fleece. The leaf is already flattened to a sheet you can handle like a map. Cabinet shops love it. Iron-on cousins exist and I will use them in a closet. On a dining top I would rather explain the price of raw leaf and a press.

Paper-backed is not thicker wood. The paper is the number you can feel. Sand through the wood and you are in fiber that will not stain like oak. The pale wound is paler. I mark the thickness in my head as *wood*, not as *sheet*.

Glue: most paper-backs are meant for contact or a press with a glue the paper can stand. Hide and a hammer is a fight; the paper wants to pill. I do not fight it. I pick a glue the backing was sold for, or I buy raw.

The gift is yield and speed and a helper who will not crack a $200 crotch. The cost is the match. Paper-backed is often not sequence-matched the way a flitch is. You get a grade and a look. You do not get leaf 17 next to leaf 18 unless you paid for that and checked.

## Phenolic and the other armors

Phenolic-backed, laminated, “2-ply” with a crossband already on the back: architectural panels, elevator cabs, a wall that will be cleaned by a night crew. The leaf is a wearing surface on a small laminate. I use it where the specification says it, and I treat the edge like a laminate edge, because that is what it is.

Do not scrape phenolic the way you scrape raw oak. You will dull a scraper and tell yourself a story about the mill.

## Grade is a face, not a soul

A, AA, architectural, “premium,” “select,” “rustic”: the words move by mill. What I want to know is whether the face will take a clear finish without a baseball of mineral streak in the sightline, and whether the flitch can be sequenced. Rustic is a grade, not a mood. If I want knots, I buy knots on purpose. If I want a quiet quartered oak, I do not buy rustic and hope the stain will hide a hole.

Species names lie less than trade names and more than Latin. “Rosewood” on a bundle in 2026 is a legal sentence. “Mahogany” might be *Swietenia*, sapele, khaya, or a hope. I write the Latin on the job folder when the finish or the CITES desk will care. [VERIFY] the invoice species against the leaf; I have opened “walnut” that was stained something else.

## What the number is for

The number is how you plan the sanding. A 0.6 mm face has almost no meat for a wide-belt. A sawn 1/16 has a chance. A paper-backed 10-mil has a wood thickness I treat as a rumor until I caliper a cutoff.

The number is how you plan the edge band. The band must be thicker than the leaf and thicker than a thumbnail, and flush-trimmed with a tool that will not dive. The number will not save you from a hungry bit.

The number is how you talk to a client. “This face is about the thickness of a business card, on a core that is the furniture” is a better sentence than “1/42, A-grade, sequenced.” Both can be true. One can be heard in a kitchen.

## Storage is part of the number

A bundle left on a concrete floor in August has a new thickness: swollen, wavy, a glue surprise. A bundle left near a woodstove in January has a new thickness: brittle, a tape surprise. I store flat, stickered or in the crate, off the slab, in the same air the core lives in. The meter on the core matters more than a meter on a leaf — a leaf fools a pin meter — but the air is the same argument.

I write the open date on the crate. Veneer does not spoil like milk. It spoils like a door left in a weather change: slowly, then all at once when you glue it.

## How I open a crate

I do not stand a fresh bundle on its edge and peel the tape like a Christmas box. I lay the crate or the cardboard on the bench, lid off, and I look at the mill’s packing before I touch a leaf. A bundle that arrived with a crushed corner has a crushed leaf. That leaf is now a sample, not a door. I cut it free and I caliper it and I write the number on the crate lid so I stop guessing.

Then I read the stamp out loud to myself: species, cut, grade, flitch if they gave me one, thickness as a fraction. I write those words on the job folder before I unroll anything. A helper who only wanted to see the figure has already shuffled a sequence I cannot put back. The number on the bundle is also a permission structure. Until it is copied, the stack stays a stack.

I slide one leaf, not a handful. I look at the back for a sequence stamp or a pencil number. I look at the face under raking light for knife marks, mineral, a bark inclusion the grade allowed. If I am buying for a dining top, I dry-fit two neighbors on the core and I walk around them. If I am buying for a closet interior, I am faster and I still caliper. The closet will still show a pale wound if I sand like the sheet was 1/16.

Paper-backed stock I treat as a map: unroll, weight, do not fold. Raw leaf I treat as a fish: it wants to curl, it wants a damp towel if it arrived as a chip, and it wants the same air as the core for a day if I can spare the day. The flattening essay later in this pack is the long version. The short version is: the stamp will not flatten it, and a press full of wavy leaf is a press full of bridges.

## The secretary that was two numbers

I built a fall-front secretary with a walnut face I thought was one flitch. The invoice said one thickness. The doors calipered 0.024. The fall-front, from a second bundle I grabbed because the first had run out of quiet figure, calipered 0.017 on one edge. I sequenced the figure by eye and I ignored the meat. After finish, the fall-front went pale at the hinge stile where I flush-sanded to the doors. The doors still had wood. The fall-front had paper-thin walnut and then the core. I pieced a patch from the offcut and I can still see it at 4 p.m.

The second object in that room is a pencil-post bed I did not veneer. Solid posts, a headboard panel in the same walnut leaf as the secretary’s doors — the leftover of the first bundle, the thicker one. The bed and the secretary were supposed to be a set. They are a set if you do not open the fall-front in raking light. I wrote both caliper numbers on the folder after the fact, which is the wrong time to write them.

I do not blame the mill. I blame the way I treated two stamps as one story because the species name matched. “Walnut” is a pile. A thickness and a flitch number are a sentence.

## Backing is a sanding limit you can feel

I keep a cutoff of each backing type in the same cigar box as the cut samples: raw, 10-mil paper, a phenolic scrap from an architectural leftover. When a new hire asks why I will not scrape a paper-back, I hand them the scrap and a cabinet scraper. The paper pills. The phenolic laughs. The raw leaf takes the burr and smells like walnut.

That demonstration has saved more dining tops than a lecture. It has also stopped me from buying a pretty paper-backed crotch for a table I intended to hammer. Hide and paper is a fight I have already lost once, on a jewelry-box lid that looked fine until the paper moved in a wet week and the crotch blistered in a crescent over the lock. I remade the lid in raw leaf and I used the paper-backed remainder on a closet shelf nobody will scrape.

The number on the bundle is how you plan the sanding, the band, and the sentence in the kitchen. It is also how you refuse a product that cannot do the job you already promised. Caliper. Read the back. Write the species you believe. Then decide if this bundle is a flitch or a product. The rest of the pack is easier if you know which you bought.
