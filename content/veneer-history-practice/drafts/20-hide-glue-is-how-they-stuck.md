The pot on the hot plate ticks. Hide glue smells like a wet animal and a kitchen that has been honest for a long time. I keep it because a blister on an old leaf still answers to heat and moisture and a knife, and because a joint I may want to take apart in twenty years should not be a plastic crime scene. The cabinet centuries — the Dutch flowers, the Boulle sandwich, the English oysters, the mahogany tops, the birch facades, the Phyfe folds — spent their lives on some version of this pot. I cannot prove every shop’s recipe. I can say protein glue is the reasonable method and the method that still lets me in.

This is the last cabinet-stage essay and the first that is mostly a bench. History earned the pot. The pot earns the repair stage.

## What hide is doing

Hide glue is collagen, cooked, dried into granules or cakes, remelted. It grabs, it gels, it can be reversed with heat and water. It stains some pale woods. It hates a cold room and a draft on a big leaf. It likes a hammer: a veneer hammer is a squeegee, a short wide blade you pull to squeeze out the excess and seat the leaf while the glue is still willing.

I do not use the hammer as a mallet. I have marked a leaf that way. The hammer is a pull. The pot is a temperature I check and do not invent for a caption. [VERIFY] house pot temperature and gram strength before anyone prints a number as SOP.

Liquid hide in a bottle is a cousin. I use it for small repairs and I do not pretend it is the pot. The pot is fresh and hot and a little dangerous to a helper who thinks it is coffee.

## Why the old shops stayed

Clamps on a large leaf are a circus. A hammer and hide let a shop face a carcase without a press the size of a room. Cauls and sandbags existed. The hammer existed. Both are in the literature and in the scars on old grounds.

Reversibility was not a museum theory. It was how you fixed a mistake and how a later shop fixed a climate. I have lifted a Phyfe-manner leaf with a heated palette and a little water and put it back. I have also met a PVA repair that turned that operation into a tear. The cabinet centuries did not owe me reversibility. They gave it to me anyway.

## What hide will not forgive

A leaf that is too dry will take the water from the glue and gel the line before you have seated the field. A shop that is too cold will do the same. A ground that is dirty or finished will not take. I scrape the ground. I warm the ground. I work a field I can finish before the gel. Big doors are a bag now, or a team, or a lie I do not tell.

Hide on oak can stain. Hide on maple can stain. I test. I size a thirsty figured leaf so the glue does not disappear into a crotch and starve the line. Starvation is a blister with a delay.

## The hammer path

I start from the center or from a taped seam and I pull to the edge, like squeezing a bubble from a sticker, except the sticker is a tree. Tape holds the seams. The hammer does not replace the tape. Excess glue is a wet line I wipe. Dried hide on the show face is a scrape, not a sand, if I can help it.

A blister I missed will tell me in a day, a hollow tap. Heat, a little moisture, a knife if I must, more hide, a weight. The repair essay will slow this down. The cabinet-century point is: they could do this. I can do this. A urea door from 1972 often cannot.

## Modern glues at the edge of this essay

PVA, urea, contact, epoxy: the shop stage will give them their due. They are faster. They are stronger in ways that are not always a gift. They are the reason a lot of twentieth-century veneer is a fight. I use them in the bag for work I do not expect to reverse. I use hide when the object is old, or small, or likely to be loved by someone with a pot.

I write the glue on the back. “Hide, pot, date.” That sentence is a kindness to a century I will not meet.

## Sizing a thirsty crotch before the pot

A figured leaf drinks. Crotch mahogany, quilted maple, a walnut burl I should have flattened first: the glue can disappear into the figure and starve the line. The blister shows up a day later, a hollow tap in the heart of the thing you were proud of. I size. A thin hide, brushed, left to gel, a scrape of the glaze, then the pot for the real seat. I do not invent a dilution number for a caption. [VERIFY] the house habit on the day we print a recipe. What I can say is that an unsized crotch has cost me a dining-top afternoon.

I sized a walnut crotch for a small Pembroke-style leaf — a second object next to the big cabinet faces — and I still starved a corner because I hammered too long and the shop was cold. The corner lifted overnight. Heat, a little water, more hide, a weight. The repair held. The note on the back says *hide, pot, sized, date* and a word about the corner so I do not pretend the first seat was clean.

Pale woods get a test. Hide on maple can stain a gray that looks like a bruise. Hide on birch can do the same. I have pushed a birch Biedermeier-manner sample because I liked the pot that day. The stain was a tide line. I scraped and I still see it. PVA on that sample would have been the better first glue. I write that on the sample. The pot is not a religion. It is a tool with a prejudice.

## The bottle I used on a Phyfe-manner fold

Liquid hide in a bottle is a cousin I reach for when a hinge mortise lift is the size of a coin and the object is already in the house. I used the bottle on a Phyfe-manner card-table fold that had lifted in a half-moon. The bottle grabbed. It did not grab like the pot. A year later the half-moon was a whisper again. I went back with the pot, a heated palette, and a weight I should have used the first time. The bottle is not a crime. Treating the bottle as the pot is a crime against a fold.

I have also met a PVA repair on a similar leaf — someone had injected a white glue and clamped a caul that left a dent. The leaf would not come up with heat and water. I had to knife a window, lose a patch, and put in a dated later leaf. The cabinet centuries gave me reversibility. A living-room repair in 1994 took it away. I still use PVA in the bag for work I do not expect to reverse. I do not use it to “improve” an old protein joint.

Gram strength, pot temperature, open time: those are numbers I will not invent. [VERIFY] house notes with a thermometer if an editor wants SOP. What I will print is the order: scrape the ground, warm the ground, size if the leaf is thirsty, work a field you can finish before the gel, hammer from the seam or the center, wipe the wet line, knock it the next day. A hollow tap is a sentence. Heat and hide are the reply.

## The pot and the county

A hardwood shop in a mill county is allowed a hot plate. This is not a reenactment. It is a tool. The smell bothers some clients. I can do the hammer work when they are not in the room. I will not switch to a bottle to spare a nose if the object wants the pot.

[VERIFY] any heritage claim that a local mill ran a veneer press or a hide room. I have a pot. I do not have a plant ledger for a pot.

When the pot ticks, I am in the same problem as a Paris shop and a New York shop and an Amsterdam shop: a face that must sit, a ground that must take, a time limit that is chemistry and not a calendar invite. The machines come next and they will slice faster than a pit saw. They will not make the pot stupid. I keep the pot. I keep the hammer. I keep the note on the back. The cabinet centuries stuck. That is the inheritance I can actually use.
