A bundle that says rosewood in 2026 is a legal sentence before it is a shop sentence. *Dalbergia* species went onto CITES Appendix II in a listing that took effect in 2017, with paperwork that made a lot of small shops stop guessing. I am a shop that files the invoice species line with the job and does not invent a Brazilian look with a can of dye.

The woods that left are part of veneer history. Riesener’s mahogany, Deco’s macassar, a thousand “rosewood” pianos: the faces were trees. Some of those trees are not ours to spend. The history pack would be a lie if it only loved the shine.

## Stop is a shop skill

When a species is listed, I stop. I do not write a workaround. I do not name a substitute as the same wood. I do not coach a client on how to move a board across a border. If they have a family piece, we talk about repair with what is already in the piece, or with a legal wood they accept as a visible later leaf, dated on the back.

Old stock in a crate is not a personality. It is a paperwork problem. I will not tell a story here about how to use it. I will say: talk to the people who do the permits, or leave the crate shut.

## Look-alike is a different lie

“This walnut looks just like rosewood” is a sales sentence I will not use. Walnut looks like walnut. Sapele looks like sapele. A client who wants a dark stripe can have walnut, or a legal dark hardwood I can invoice, or a stain we test and name as stain. The Deco appetite is not a warrant.

I have been asked to match a missing rosewood drawer face on a nineteenth-century box. The honest repairs are: a saved offcut from the same object, a documented conservation fill, or a dated later leaf in a legal wood. I prefer the dated later leaf if the offcut does not exist. The box keeps its history. The new face does not pretend to be 1840.

## Mahogany, ebony, the moving list

Trade names are how the motel century ruined the word *oak*. They are also how *mahogany* became a color. I want Latin on the folder when the law or the finish will care.

## Museums and the shopping list

A Ruhlmann caption is a historical document. It is not a cut list for Wilmar. I can admire macassar in a case. I cannot order it as if the caption were a catalog. This is the same discipline as not calling a Director plate a cut list. The plate is a suggestion. The appendix is not a suggestion.

## What I tell a client

If they want “that piano look,” we look at legal woods and we look at whether they want a historical copy or a new object. If they want to sell an old rosewood piece, I am not their broker. If they want to repair it, we photograph, we date the new work, we do not sand off the only evidence of the original face.

If they are angry at the law, they can be angry. I will not join a workaround. I will join a design that uses oak flake, walnut crotch, maple quilt — faces this county and this invoice can stand next to.

## The piano that is a document

A parlor piano with a “rosewood” nameboard is the second object that arrives after the Deco photograph. Families want a missing cheek block matched. I do not open a crate. I photograph. I look at what is already on the instrument. If an offcut exists in a bench I do not own, that is the conservator’s business. If it does not, I offer a dated later leaf in a legal wood, or I offer to leave the wound clean and labeled. I will not dye a walnut to “go back to rosewood.” Walnut looks like walnut. The piano is a document. A look-alike is a second lie on a first history.

I am not their broker if they want to sell the piano. I am not a customs lesson. Old stock in a crate is a paperwork problem I will not narrate into a how-to. Talk to the people who do the permits, or leave the crate shut. The shop skill is stop.

## Latin on the folder, nothing cute on the tag

I file the invoice species line with the job. I want the scientific name when the law or the finish will care. Trade names are how *oak* became a photograph and how *mahogany* became a color. I have accepted a bundle tagged with a poetry word and found a different genus in the fine print. I called the seller. I did not “make it work” with a stain.

The folder also gets a cutoff with the finish schedule and a note about glue. If a listed species is already in a family piece, the folder gets photographs of the original face before I touch it. I have sanded a mystery dark wood on a sewing table — a third small object in this habit — to “see what it was” and taken off the only clean evidence. I do not do that anymore. I scrape a hidden edge. I write *unknown dark hardwood, do not assume Dalbergia*. I do not write a shopping name to satisfy a curiosity.

## A stain I mixed and then poured out

A client asked for “that piano look” on a new box. I mixed a dye on sapele until a wet rag looked like a memory of rosewood. The dry rag looked like dirty sapele. The pore was wrong. The smell was wrong. I poured it out. We built the box in walnut with an ebonized string, dated, honest. The box does not pretend to be 1840. It does not pretend to be a piano. It is a new object in woods I can stand next to in this county.

Look-alike is a different lie from the motel print. The print pretends a photograph is a species. The look-alike pretends a legal wood is a listed one. I will not do either. A dark stripe can be walnut, or a legal dark hardwood I can invoice, or a stain we name as stain. The Deco appetite is not a warrant. The piano memory is not a warrant.

Ruhlmann’s caption remains a historical document. It is not a cut list for Wilmar. The appendix is not a suggestion. The plate was a suggestion. I can tell those apart in a shop. That is the job.

## The flitch you cannot replace, twice

Sequence numbers are a flitch you cannot replace because the tree is spent. CITES is a flitch you cannot replace because the tree is protected. Both make me careful with offcuts. I keep the job-folder scrap. I keep the invoice. I keep the note on the back.
