The door would not latch. The straightedge showed a bowl. I had veneered one face in a hurry in July. I did not flatten that door. I remade it. A cup in a balanced-looking panel is sometimes a moisture gradient you can even out if the glue will allow a movement. A cup in a one-sided panel is a spring. I do not fight a spring with a belt. I add a backer on a new core, or I add a backer if the construction allows a second press, and I write both faces this time.

Flattening a failure is not the same as flattening a chip before glue. Before glue, the leaf is free. After glue, the leaf is a skin on a decision.

## Diagnose the bowl

Is the core a bowl, or is the leaf a bubble, or is the door hinged into a twist? I take the door off. I read the back. I look for a backer. I look for a finish on one side only. I look for a wet wall. I have planed a reveal on a door that was a climate and a hinge. The reveal came back.

A twist is a hanging problem and a panel problem. I do not flatten a twist with weights on a hung door.

## The free leaf that failed

A leaf I flattened badly, glued anyway, now a telegraph of a wave: I may be able to heat and moisture a local lift. I may not. If the wave is glued down as a wrinkle, the wrinkle is a ridge. Scraping a ridge on a thin face is how I meet the core. I remake.

A paper-backed that tunneled: the paper is a clue. I do not flood it. I cut a slit on the back if I can reach the back, or I remake. I do not inject a mystery glue into a tunnel I cannot see.

## Heat and moisture, after the fact

On hide, heat and moisture are the original tools. A warm iron over a damp cloth on a blister — the next essay — is a cousin of flattening a small lift. On PVA, heat is a fight and a smell. I test a corner. I do not iron a dining field of PVA and hope.

Pale woods stain from the cloth. I use a clean cloth and I test. I have maple that remembers a dirty iron.

## Weights on a hung piece

I do not stack bricks on a sideboard in a client’s house and call it a repair. I take the door home. I have left bricks. The client has a story. I have a liability. The shop is where the weights live.

## When the failure is the crate

A bundle I stored wrong, not yet glued: that is the flattening essay from matching, still available. I do not pretend it is a repair of furniture. It is a repair of my storage. I write it on the crate so I do not do it again.

## Remake as a kindness

A remake is expensive and honest. A forced flatten that comes back in October is expensive and a liar. I would rather say remake. I show the straightedge. I show the back. I do not blame the leaf. The leaf did what a one-sided face does.

If the client refuses a remake, I can refuse the repair. I will not belt a cup into a wound and a still-cupped door.

## A wet wall I blamed on the leaf

A bookcase door bowed a month after hanging. Both faces were there. The core looked like a panel I would trust painted. I took the door off anyway and set a straightedge on it in the shop. A shallow bowl, then less of one after a night in our air. The wall behind the case was a chimney chase that wept in January. The door was a sensor. I did not remake it first. We moved the case. The bow eased.

I still write the backer note. I would have remade if the wall had been dry and the door still a bowl. Diagnosis is a walk around the object: back, finish on one side, hinge bind, wet plaster, a slab the crate sat on. The leaf is the last thing I blame. ## Bricks in a dining room

A sideboard door, a small lift at a corner, a client who wanted it fixed in place. I stacked bricks on a towel. I left. They had a story and a dent in a finish I then had to rub out. I have a liability I did not need.

The weights live in the shop. I take the door home. A hung piece is not a flatten bench. A twist is not a bowl. I do not flatten a twist with bricks on a hinge.

## A tunnel I injected blind

A paper-backed maple on a closet door tunneled — a ridge you could roll under a finger. I injected a glue I liked into a hole I could not map. The tunnel became a pond. The pond telegraphed. I remade the door.

If I can reach the back I will slit the paper, seat the leaf, weight. If I cannot see the tunnel I remake. A mystery glue in a mystery cavity is how I stain a pale wood from the inside.

On hide, a small lift can still take heat and a damp cloth and a weight. That is the cousin of the blister essay. On PVA I test a corner. I do not iron a dining field and hope. Pale maple remembers a dirty iron. I have that maple. It is a sample now. It is not a door.

I keep that July one-face door as a sample, backer missing, bowl you can rock. The next hurry gets to set a straightedge on it before I agree to a one-sided rush.

## Two diagonals on the bench

I set a straightedge on both diagonals, not one. A bowl reads on both. A twist reads as a high corner that swaps when I turn the stick. I mark the highs with chalk. A twist on a hung door is often a hinge and a plane I will not flatten with bricks. A bowl in the shop air that was not a bowl in the house air is a climate I need to even before I invent a glue.

A dining leaf I glued as a wrinkle — not a cup, a ridge of extra face — is a third animal. Scraping the ridge on a thin face is how I meet the core. I remade that field from the next pages in the folder. I do not call a wrinkle a blister. The iron will not unsay a fold I bagged.

## What I write

*Cup, one face, remake, date.* Or *lift, hide, heat, weight, date.* The words are different. I do not write *flattened* on a cup I remade. I write *remade*. The next person should not think weights will do it again.

Blisters are next: the local hollow, the tap, the iron, the knife. A cup is a weather system. A blister is a coin. I treat them as different animals. I have treated a cup like a blister. The iron did nothing. The latch still missed. The straightedge is the tool that keeps me honest. The remake is the tool that keeps the room honest. The crate off the slab is how I meet fewer bowls.
