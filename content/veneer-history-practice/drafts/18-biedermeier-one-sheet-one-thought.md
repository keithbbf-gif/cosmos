A Biedermeier secretary in birch can look like one board the size of a door. It is not. It is a flitch spent as a single thought: bookmatched or slip-matched so the cathedral or the quiet stripe runs the facade without a garden, without a moth, without a brass vine. The ebonized line — a colonette, a capital, a string — is punctuation. I like this furniture because it agrees with how I want a lot of doors to behave.

The word Biedermeier is a later nickname for a Central European domestic style, roughly the decades after Napoleon, Vienna and Berlin and a hundred shops. [VERIFY] a named piece’s city and date. I will not turn the nickname into a personality. I will talk about the sheet.

## The luxury of enough leaf

To face a secretary with one thought, you need a flitch that is wide enough and long enough and even enough. That is luxury without a tropical name. Birch, cherry, pear, maple, walnut: local and regional woods, figured or plain. The flex is the match, not the import. Egypt spent an import. Biedermeier spent a tree that agreed with itself for eight feet.

I have tried to fake this with mismatched leftovers and a stain. The stain unified nothing. The leftovers kept being leftovers. One thought is a buying decision at the flitch, not a finishing decision at the rag.

## Ebonized punctuation

The black line is often ebonized pear or a stained local wood, not ebony. I will say ebonized. The line is a relief from the field and a way to hide a seam or to mark a bay. It is also a place people chip. Black on an arris is a chip magnet. I ease the arris. I do not sharpen a piano-key edge on a hallway piece.

## Light wood, light rooms

Biedermeier rooms in the photographs are pale: birch, a plaster, a window. The furniture was meant to take daylight. A dark shop LED will make birch look cheap. I approve a birch match in daylight or a lamp that is close to the room. I said this about quilt. I will say it about birch. Light is part of the leaf.

Birch also yellows and dirties. A “fresh birch” restoration that makes a secretary look like a kitchen cabinet is a choice. I would rather clean and leave a little age than bleach a field into a door from a catalog. [VERIFY] any bleach protocol; I am not writing one here.

## The fall-front that wanted two thoughts

A Biedermeier secretary is a facade until you drop the fall-front. Then it is a writing surface and a little theater of cubbies. The facade wants one grain. The fall-front is a second object that has to agree with the facade when it is closed and still be a desk when it is open. I have seen a fall-front bookmatched on a different axis than the case, a butterfly that became a shrug when you shut it. The shop had spent two thoughts and hoped the ebonized line would hide the argument.

When I face a fall-front, I dry-fit it against the case with the hinge mock in place. I mark the leaf so the cathedral continues or so the slip current does not break at the mouth. I veneer the interior writing face too, or I use a leather I can replace, or I use a solid that can take a pen. A fall-front that is a leaf on the show side and a raw plywood on the write side is a secretary that tells on itself every morning. Hide on birch is a stain risk. I test. PVA can creep on a hinge line. I test that too. The hinge screws go into a core with meat, not into a business card of birch.

I split a fall-front leaf once by folding it over a radiused lip without a relief in the tape. The show face opened like a smile. I remade the front from the next pages of the same flitch. The first front is in the folder. The secretary — if I ever build a true one — will get the pages that learned.

## Fruitwood on an oak box in a county July

Pear and cherry move unlike the oak box they sit on. The band is small, so a string can get away with it. A pear door the width of a wardrobe cannot. I built a small sofa table — a cousin of the Viennese ones in the photographs — with a cherry field and an oak apron. The field was one thought, slip-matched, pale, a quiet stripe. I balanced the underside. I still got a whisper of cup after a week of south-Arkansas July because I had left the table in the shop under a vent. The vent was a climate I had not counted. I bagged it again with a little correction in the cauls and I moved the table off the vent. The leaf was not the failure. The air was.

I will not pretend Vienna’s rooms are Wilmar’s rooms. I will pretend I can put a table where the air is honest. Pale fruitwood shows a glue line like a map. I scrape. I do not belt. I have stained a glue line into a cherry field by wiping hide as if it were a finish. The line went gray. I steamed and scraped and still see it. The next cherry field gets a test rag and a smaller pot.

## Vienna, Berlin, and a nickname I will not freeze

The nickname Biedermeier landed later, a cartoon of a comfortable citizen, then a useful word for a domestic style in the decades after Napoleon. Vienna and Berlin are the cities the catalogs repeat. A hundred shops in a hundred towns did the work. [VERIFY] a named secretary’s city and date before a caption. I will not turn the nickname into a man. I will not turn a taper and a light wood in a furniture ad into a city.

What I can take from the cities without a ticket is the habit: a local or regional sheet spent as one thought, a black line that is often ebonized pear, a carcase that is joinery, a room that wanted daylight. The luxury is enough leaf that agrees with itself for eight feet. Egypt spent an import. These shops spent a tree that behaved. That is a different wealth. It is the wealth I can still buy in a birch or maple crate if the book is long enough.

I leave “Biedermeier-style” as a sales weather. If I mean a one-field birch secretary with an ebonized colonette after a Viennese idea, I say that. If I mean a wardrobe in rift oak with one thought and a shadow reveal, I say that. The nickname can sit in the bibliography. The sheet has to sit on the door.

## What I take to Wilmar

A facade as one thought: I use this on a pair of wardrobe doors, on a sideboard, on a headboard. Rift oak can do it. Walnut can do it if the flitch is quiet. Flake oak can do it and it will shout; that is a different thought, still one thought.

I take the punctuation: a solid band that is a different wood, or an ebonized string, or just a shadow line at a reveal. I do not put a Dutch moth on a Biedermeier idea. The religions fight.

I leave the nickname as a sales word. “Biedermeier-style” in a furniture ad is often a taper and a light wood. Fine. I will say “one-field birch, ebonized line, after a Viennese secretary” if that is true. I will not say Biedermeier as a stain.

## The seam is the only confession

On a good facade you see the bookmatch as a faint butterfly or you see a slip as a current. That seam is the honesty. The rest is the tree. I spend more time on that seam than on the punctuation. Tape, a joint that does not ridge, a glue that does not stain the pale wood. Hide can stain. PVA can creep. I test on birch before I trust a pot.

Pale woods show everything. That is why shops hide in walnut. Biedermeier did not hide. I respect that more than I respect a bouquet. The next essay is Phyfe and the Federal leaf: America taking the English mahogany sentence and making it a New York shop. The one-thought field will show up again in Deco and in Danish teak. It is a habit that survives costumes.
