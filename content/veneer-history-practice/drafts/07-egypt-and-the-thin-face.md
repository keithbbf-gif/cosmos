The chair is gold and wood and a missing century of humidity. What you see in the museum photograph is a reconstruction as much as an object: George Reisner’s team found the tomb of Hetepheres I at Giza in fragments, a queen’s furniture crushed and oxidized, and then the carpenters of the modern century built the idea back into a chair. [VERIFY] which members on the current Museum of Fine Arts, Boston, labels are original wood and which are the reconstruction’s honesty. The point for a shop is older than the argument about the reconstruction. Old Kingdom Egypt already knew that a thin piece of a better wood, or a thin sheet of gold, could do the work of a face while the structure was something you could raise in a country that did not grow a forest of ebony.

Veneer, here, is not a brand. It is a scarcity method. Ebony, cedar from the Levant, ivory, gold: the face is an import. The core can be a local wood that will take a joint. That sentence still works in Bradley County, if you swap the species and lose the gold.

## What the tombs actually hold

New Kingdom boxes and chairs keep the evidence in a form a bench person can read. Tutankhamun’s tomb held furniture that used ebony and ivory in thin applications, gold foil, and woods that were not all grown in the Nile valley. [VERIFY] a specific box against Carter’s object cards or the Grand Egyptian Museum’s current caption before you name a species in print. The method is consistent: the show surface is expensive; the carcase is possible.

Egyptian joinery is not a footnote. Mortise-and-tenon, webbing, a lion’s foot that is a carving and a structure — the leaf or the foil sits on a carpenter’s object. I care about that because a lot of popular history talks as if “inlay” and “veneer” were decorations sprinkled on a box. They are faces on joints. The joint is why the box is still a box in the photograph.

Hide and protein glues are the reasonable shop guess for how a thin face stayed. I will not invent a pot recipe for a Fourth Dynasty bench. I will say: reversible protein glue is how a lot of later veneer was stuck, and Egypt had animal glue. [VERIFY] residue studies on a named object before a chemistry paragraph gets confident.

## Scarcity is the teacher

A wide board of a dark imported wood is a treasury item. A thin board of the same wood covers a chest. The saw is the tax. Every kerf is a leaf you did not get. That is why sawn veneer is old and why it stayed thick: the sawyer was spending the import on the kerf as well as on the face.

I think about that when I throw away a cracked edge of a modern sliced leaf. The Egyptian carpenter would have recut the waste into a stringing. The modern bundle makes me sloppy. The tomb does not.

Local woods — sycamore fig, acacia, tamarisk — appear in the literature as the possible cores and the possible ordinary furniture. [VERIFY] species lists against a current materials paper; I will not freeze a 1920s guess as a fact. The geography is the lesson: you veneer what you cannot grow, and you grow what you can joint.

## Gold is a leaf

Gold foil on furniture is veneer in metal. It has the same problems: a core that moves, a face that does not stretch the same way, an edge that tells the truth. The gold is why the objects were robbed and why they were saved. The wood is why they are furniture.

A shop that gilds a carved frame is in that family. A shop that irons a photograph of oak onto particleboard is not. The difference is not “natural versus fake.” The difference is whether the face is a material with a thickness and a joint, or a picture of a material.

## What I take to the bench

When I unroll a quartered oak leaf, I am not pretending to be an Old Kingdom carpenter. I am in a county that grows oak. The scarcity is different: figure, match, a crotch that would split as a slab. The method rhymes. Thin face, better argument, a core that can be had.

I also take the reconstruction problem. Hetepheres’ furniture comes to us through a modern shop that had to decide what a chair was. Every repair I do on an old veneered piece is a smaller version of that: how much to replace, how much to leave as a scar, how to label the new wood so the next century does not think I was the first shop. I write the date on the back. I wish more reconstructions did it where the public can see it.

## The apology, again

If veneer were a Victorian trick, the tombs would be a problem. They are not a problem. They are early. The apology is late. A client who hates the word can still be shown a photograph of a named object and a sentence: the face is thin because the wood was worth more as a face than as a carcase.

I do not use the tombs to sell a kitchen. I use them to stop a false history that makes my hammer look like a shortcut. The hammer is a later tool. The idea is a carpenter looking at an imported board and a local core and deciding not to waste the import on the part you never see.

## Ivory, foil, and the other thin faces

Ivory on a casket or a chair is a leaf in a different animal. It has the same shop problems a wood face has: a ground that moves, an edge that chips, a glue that can let go in a damp century. I will not cut ivory. I will not name a plastic as “the same.” If a museum label says ivory, I read it as a scarcity method and as a death. The ethics are not a modern discovery just because the shop is in Bradley County.

A client pointed at a tomb photograph and asked for that look in a kitchen. They wanted the shine. They did not want the scarcity method. I can give them a thin face of a better wood. I cannot give them a Fourth Dynasty treasury.

## A cedar chest I will not fake

The second object in this essay is not Egyptian. It is a cedar chest I repaired for a family that wanted the inside to smell like a grandmother’s attic and the outside to look like a dark imported face. The carcase was aromatic cedar, honest, a little tired at the lid. Someone had already glued a paper-backed “mahogany” on the front panel and sanded through at the lock rail. The pale wound was cedar. The lie was the paper.

I pulled the paper. I did not replace it with a darker lie. I scraped the cedar, I let the smell stay, and I wrote the date inside the lid. If they want a dark face later, we can talk about a real leaf and a backer and a lock rail that will take a hip. We will not talk about a photograph of mahogany on a chest that already had a wood.

That repair is the tomb lesson without a museum: the face is allowed to be thin and better, and the ground is allowed to be the wood you can have, and the label has to say which is which. Reisner’s carpenters had to decide what a chair was. I had to decide what a chest was. I left the cedar as the sentence.

## What I write on a reconstruction

When I replace a leaf on an old piece, I write the date, the glue, the species I believe, and the word *repair* on the back, in pencil, where a belt will not take it first. I keep the offcut of the new leaf in the job folder. I keep a chip of the old leaf if it came off clean. I do not stain a new oak to “match a tomb photograph.” I do not distress a leaf to look excavated. The Egyptian carpenter was not making a ruin. He was making a chair. On my bench the pot is a tool, not a costume.

Egypt is not the only beginning. Rome will spend more money on a citrus table than on a farm. The medieval chest will hoard a wide board. The Renaissance studiolo will turn a wall into a picture of wood. This essay only has to do one job: put a date under the method that is older than the shame. The chair in the photograph, reconstructed or not, is enough.
