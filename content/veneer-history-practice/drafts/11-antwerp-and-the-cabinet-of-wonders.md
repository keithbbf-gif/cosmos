Open the doors and the box becomes a street. Little columns, little arches, a perspective that is a cousin of the studiolo, drawers that are rooms, a hidden well for a letter, tortoiseshell and ivory and a wood so dark the room called it ebony whether or not the tree was *Diospyros*. Antwerp in the seventeenth century made cabinets for a Europe that wanted a portable palace. The carcase is often oak. The face is a fortune. [VERIFY] a specific piece before you name the woods; ebonized pear has fooled a lot of labels.

I think about these cabinets when I build a jewelry box that wants to be more than a box. I also think about them when I refuse. A drawer that does not run is a theater with a broken floor.

## The port is the materials list

Antwerp was a port. The cabinet is a map of ships: tropic woods, shell, ivory, silver mounts, a lock from a smith who did not work in the same shop as the veneer. Furniture as collaboration is not a modern discovery. The ébéniste later is a French guild name for a job Antwerp was already doing — the face specialist.

Oak carcases make shop sense. Oak is local, jointable, a ground that takes hide and a nail. The face can be a tropical leaf that would have been a stupid carcase: too hard, too short, too expensive, too likely to split. That is the Egyptian sentence again, in a colder harbor.

## Closed and open

Closed, the cabinet is a dark architecture: pilasters, a broken pediment, a quiet flex. Open, it is a riot that was designed. The two states are the object. A bookmatched sideboard that only has one state is a different brief. I ask a client which they want. Most want closed. They want a quiet face. The Antwerp cabinet is for the person who wants a reveal.

The reveal is also a conservation nightmare. Every little column is an edge. Every edge is a place for a leaf to lift. Museums spend decades on these interiors. I spend an afternoon on a blister the size of a coin and I am humbled.

## Ebony and the name

Ebony is a word that means a dark face and sometimes a species. Ebonizing — iron and tannin, a stain, a paint — is a shop method for getting the word without the tree. Both exist in the seventeenth century and after. I will not call a blackened pear ebony in a sales tag. I will say “ebonized” if I ebonized it. I will say “ebony” if the invoice and the leaf agree and the law agrees.

CITES and modern ebony are a later essay. Antwerp’s appetite is the ancestor of the problem. The port did not invent restraint.

## The lock and the runner

A cabinet of wonders that cannot lock is a display case. A drawer that binds is a wonder you will not open twice. The Antwerp shops knew hardware. The little drawer still has sides and a bottom and a runner. I look at those before I look at the ivory.

When I build a small box now, I cut the drawers first in cheap wood if the interior is a picture. The picture is allowed to be late. The runner is not allowed to be late. That is not a historical claim about Antwerp sequence. It is a shop claim I will not give up because a photograph of a cabinet made me romantic.

## What I take and what I leave

I take the idea of a carcase in a local wood and a face in a spent wood. I take the open/closed object. I take the collaboration — lock, mount, leaf.

I leave the ivory. I leave the hawksbill. I leave the habit of calling every dark wood ebony. I leave the portable palace if the client lives in a house that needs a dresser.

A sideboard with a quiet oak face and a drawer that runs is a descendant if you squint. The Antwerp cabinet is a peak of veneer as theater. The next essay is a Japanese cousin — yosegi and the other way to make a picture out of wood — so this stage does not pretend Europe invented the patterned leaf.

## The jewelry cabinet and the blister on a column

I built a jewelry cabinet that wanted to be more than a box: two doors, a little architecture on the inside, drawers like rooms, a hidden well for a letter. The carcase was oak. The face was a dark walnut I did not call ebony. The little columns were leftover walnut, turned, with a thin face of a quieter leaf on the flat pilasters beside them. Every column was an edge. Every edge was a place for a leaf to lift.

One pilaster blistered over the lock after a week of shop heat and a door I kept opening to show people. The leaf had not seen enough caul at the edge. I warmed it, I slid hide under the bubble with a thin blade, I hammered it down, and I taped it overnight. The repair holds. The column still looks like a column if you do not hunt. I hunt.

I had cut the drawers first in poplar, fitted them, and only then faced the fronts. That sequence is the one part of the cabinet I would not change. The blister taught the rest: a pilaster edge wants a caul that reaches the arris, and a door I keep opening to show people is a door I have heated on purpose.

## A writing box from the same harbor idea

The second object is a traveling writing box I made later: oak carcase, no columns, a fall front, a lock from a smith who does not work in my building. Closed, a dark rectangle. Open, a small desk — a well, two drawers, a leather I did not date. The face is walnut, sequenced, both faces on the fall. The client lives with it closed and opens it when they sit down. That is a reveal without a portable palace.

The lock rebate was too proud. The leaf chipped when the plate went in. I pieced a patch from the offcut and I filed the plate until the key’s hip did not scrape the face. I will hire the smith. I will not sand a finished leaf to make a catalog lock sit.

Ivory and hawksbill stay off this box. I can take the local-carcase sentence without taking the turtle.

## Oak ground, hide line, and what I refuse to ebonize

I scrape the oak clean before the leaf. Dust in the ground is a blister later. I size if the oak looks thirsty. I do not flood. I have flooded a pilaster and telegraphed glue at the edge, a shiny line in the dark face. I scraped, I warmed, I lived with a slightly hungrier arris.

Ebonizing the oak to “be Antwerp” is a temptation I have now refused twice. Iron and tannin will blacken a solid molding. They will also blotch a leaf if I get sloppy at the edge, and they will stain a hand. I will say “ebonized” if I ebonize. I will not call a blackened pear ebony on a sales tag, and I will not call my walnut ebony because the room is dark. [VERIFY] a museum cabinet’s materials line before a caption chooses ebony over ebonized pear; the labels have been fooled and so have I, in photographs.

The jewelry cabinet stays in the shop as a warning about edges. The writing box left. Hakone is next, a loaf tradition that makes a patterned leaf without a Flemish street, so this stage does not pretend Europe invented the habit.
