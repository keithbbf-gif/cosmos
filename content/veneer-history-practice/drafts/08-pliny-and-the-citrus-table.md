Pliny the Elder writes about tables the way a shop writes about a flitch that got away: with prices that make the rest of the house look cheap, and with a moral that does not stop anyone from buying. Citrus wood — *citrus*, the North African tree whose burled and wavy timber the Roman rich wanted — appears in the *Natural History* as furniture that could cost more than a farm. Roman luxury furniture used thin applications of expensive wood and other materials — *crustae*, veneers, tortoise, ivory — over cores that could be built. The word in the shop now is veneer. The Latin is a crust, a skin, a facing. Same job.

## What we can actually see

Wood from the Roman world survives when it was waterlogged, carbonized, or lucky. Herculaneum’s carbonized beds and tables are a shop in a fire: joinery you can still read, a carbon that used to be a plank. They are not Pliny’s citrus. They are the ordinary and the good that happened to be in the wrong town in 79.

Ivory furniture fittings, bronze couches, marble tables: the atrium was a materials catalog. Wood was one face among faces. A citrus table is special because it is wood trying to be as expensive as stone and metal. Figure is the reason. A burl or a wavy grain that read as a pictured surface, a map of the tree, was the point. The Romans were buying a picture that grew.

I do not have a citrus leaf on my bench. I have walnut crotch and oak flake. The buying is the same disease. A client will pay for a face and then ask why the core is “just plywood.” Pliny’s reader paid for a face and then asked why the rest of the Republic was in debt. The shop answer is older than the Republic: the face is what you can see; the core is what you can joint.

## Crustae and the other skins

Marble *crustae* on walls are the cousin everyone knows: thin stone, a core of cheaper masonry, a room that looks like a quarry. Wood veneer is the same sentence in a different material. Once a culture learns to slice a luxury thin, it applies the lesson everywhere. That is not a theory of progress. It is a tax on imports.

Tortoise and ivory on furniture will come back with Boulle. Rome already had the appetite. The ethics are not a modern discovery. Hawksbill and ivory were already a death and a trade. I name that because a history of veneer that only loves the shine is a sales catalog. The shop can love the work and still say what the face cost a living thing.

## Cicero’s taste and the shop floor

The literary tables — Cicero, the moralists, the satires — are not shop drawings. They are a class talking about objects. I use them the way I use a client email: as a record of what the object was supposed to mean, not as a cut list.

What I take as shop fact is simpler. Expensive figure was applied thin. Cores were built. Glue existed. The luxury was the face. ## Heat, carbon, and the lesson from the bay

Herculaneum’s wood is a reminder that furniture history is a survival bias. We have stone and we have carbon and we have Pliny. We do not have the atrium on a Tuesday. The carbonized beds show rails and tenons and a carpenter who was not writing to the emperor. That carpenter is my company. The senator’s citrus is my client’s crotch leaf.

When I flatten a buckled burl, I am in a smaller version of the Roman problem: a figure that does not want to be a plane, a price that makes me careful, a core that has to be worthy of the face. I do not gild the story. I tape the leaf.

## The price list as a warning

Pliny’s anger at luxury tables is a moral set-piece. I am not a moralist for the Republic. I am a shop that has watched a client spend the budget on a face and then cheap out on the core. The cup that follows is not a tragedy of empire. It is both-faces-or-a-cup. Rome knew how to put a skin on a wall. Rome also knew how to go broke on a table. The two facts can sit in the same room.

If I ever caption a photograph “in the tradition of Roman citrus,” I have failed this essay. The tradition is scarcity, figure, and a thin face. The tree is not the same. The empire is not the same. The hammer is not a toga.

## What the tree was, as far as a shop can say

The citrus of the *Natural History* is not a fruit-bowl orange. The usual scholarly guess is a North African *Thuja* or a related sandarac tree, a burled and wavy timber the Latin called *citrus*. That is enough to keep a shop honest. We still buy figured faces from shores we do not grow. We still argue about the core. We still have clients who want the map of a tree and a price that pretends the map is a board.

## A parlor table that asked to be a senator

A client brought a photograph of a burled slab — modern, glossy, no collection number — and said they wanted a “Roman citrus table” for a parlor in a house that already had oak floors and a heat pump. I talked them into a walnut-burl leaf on a balanced core, both faces, a band, a size I could press. I did not caption the job as citrus. I wrote *walnut burl, both faces, dining height* on the folder.

The burl did not want to be a plane. I flattened, I taped, I sized the thirsty eyes, and I still got a blister the size of a coin over a swirl that had spit hide. I lifted the leaf locally with a warm iron, added a brush of glue, and hammered it down. The repair is a slightly hungrier eye in the finish. The table is not a senator’s atrium. It is a parlor table that knows what it cost a tree and a Tuesday.

The second object in that room is a carbonized-looking cousin I will not build: they asked for scorched oak as a Herculaneum costume. I refused. Herculaneum’s beds are a fire. A torch on a new rail is theater. I will show a photograph of a carbonized bed for the joints — rails, tenons, a carpenter who was not writing to the emperor — and I will not sell the carbon.
