I pull the hammer from the center of the panel and a wet line of hide gathers at the edge like a tide. The leaf seats. The tape holds the seam. The ground is warm. This is not a demonstration for a camera. It is a field I sized so I could finish it before the gel. The pot ticks. I do not talk while I pull.

The cabinet-century essay said why hide stays. This essay is the pull.

## The ground

Scrape. Do not sand a ground into a round and then wonder why the leaf bridges. A ground with finish on it is a refusal. A ground with dust is a refusal. A cold ground gels the glue on contact. I warm the carcase with a lamp or a moment in a warm room. I do not torch a leaf.

Pores in oak will telegraph if I starve them and then flood them. I size a thirsty ground with a thin hide, let it gel, then I do the face. I have skipped size on a crotch and bought a blister with a delay.

## The pot

Granules, soak, melt. I do not print a temperature as SOP without a house note. [VERIFY] the thermometer we actually use. Too hot and the glue weakens and the shop smells like a mistake. Too cold and the hammer is a plow through pudding.

I brush the ground, or I brush the leaf, or I do both on a small field. I do not paint a door I cannot finish. A helper with a second hammer is how a door gets done. A helper with a conversation is how a door gets a gelled island.

Liquid hide in a bottle is for a coin blister, not for a field. I have used it on a field when the pot was dirty. I remade the field.

## The pull

Center out, or seam out if the seam is the spine I trust. Overlap the strokes. The hammer face is clean enough to not scratch and dirty enough to be hide. A dry hammer drags. A dripping hammer floods the show face.

I wipe the show face with a rag that is wrung. Dried hide on oak flake is a scrape. Dried hide on a pale birch is a stain I tested for and still swore at.

The edge gets a little extra attention because the leaf wants to lift there first. I pull off the edge, not toward a trapped bubble at the band.

## Tape and the hammer

Tape holds the drawing. The hammer does not replace tape. If I hammer a seam that was not jointed, I have a ridged butterfly. If I hammer so hard I stretch a wet leaf, I have a leaf that will shrink into a split. The pull is firm and not a performance.

Gummed veneer tape on the show face: I damp it off later. I do not belt it off. I have belted it off.

## When I put the hammer down

A door I cannot finish in the gel window. A leaf that needs a bag’s evenness — a burl, a curve, a field the size of a dining top in a cold shop. A glue I have already chosen as PVA for a reason. The hammer is not a religion that beats a bag. It is a tool that beats a circus of clamps on a small carcase.

A curve — a cushion drawer, a column — can still be hide and a hammer and a lot of tape, or hide and a caul. I have done both. I have also used a bag on a form with hide and watched the gel win. Warm form, warm glue, fast team.

## The cushion drawer and the column

A bowed drawer front — a cushion — is a field the bag does not swallow unless I have a form. I have a form now. I did not always. Hide, a hammer, a lot of tape, a warm shop: I pulled from the crown out, overlapping, a helper feeding glue. The leaf wanted to wrinkle at the spring line. I slit nothing. I pulled faster and I taped the wrinkle before it became a ridge.

A column is worse. The leaf is a rectangle trying to become a tube. I cut the leaf long, I spring it around a form, I tape the seam as a book if I can, and I pull. Or I use a caul that is the form and I skip the hammer. I have used the bag on a form with hide and watched the gel win because I talked. Warm form, warm glue, no speech.

These are the objects that keep the pot on the bench. A flat door can go in a bag. A built carcase, a repair on a rail that is already a piece of furniture, a column: the hammer still faces them.

## A gelled island I remade

I painted a door I could not finish. The pot was fine. The ground cooled as I worked the far stile. The center gelled into an island the hammer plowed around. I thought I could heat it back. I heated. I got a smear and a blister with a delay. I remade the field.

The rule is the size of the field I can finish, not the size of the door I wish I could finish. A helper with a second hammer is how a door gets done. A helper who asks about lunch in the middle of a pull is how a door gets an island. I assign jobs before the brush hits the ground. I do not assign conversation.

Liquid hide from a bottle went onto that remake as a spot at a corner I had starved. I wrote *bottle* on the back. A future iron needs to know the corner is not pot hide. The field was pot. The corner was a confession.

## Two hammers, one pot

I warm the carcase. I scrape the ground. I size oak if the pores look thirsty. I brush. I lay the leaf. I pull. The second hammer starts at the other half if the field is a door. We meet at a taped seam we already trust. We do not chase each other’s strokes into a stretch.

The hammer face is hide. I wipe it when it drips. I do not clean it to a furniture shine mid-pull. A dry face drags a wet leaf into a scratch on flake. A dripping face floods birch. I have stained birch with hide I then had to bleach toward, which is a chemistry I do not print as SOP. I remade the birch.

I tap before I clean the pot. A hollow is still hide’s problem today. Tomorrow it is a sealed tent. The repair essays slow the iron down. This essay is the pull that tries not to need them.

[VERIFY] the thermometer we actually use before a house temperature becomes a caption. I will print the habit: warm ground, sized oak, field I can finish, center out, tap before the film. I will not print a number I did not read off our pot.

## Blisters in the first day

I tap. A hollow is a place I missed. Heat, a little moisture, a knife if the leaf must lift, more hide, a weight. The repair essay slows this down. The shop rule is: tap before you finish. Finish on a blister is a sealed crime scene.

## What I write

Hide, pot, date, sized or not, who pulled. If a bottle was used on a spot, I write bottle. A future heat iron needs to know.

I clean the pot because a dirty pot is a dark speckle in a pale field. I clean the hammer. I turn off the hot plate. I have left the hot plate. That is a different safety essay and a short one: I do not leave the hot plate.

The bag is next. It is the tool that made a lot of shops put the pot on a shelf. I did not put the pot on a shelf. I added a bag. The hammer still faces a carcase that the bag cannot get around — a built piece, a repair, a column. The pot still ticks. The pull is still a pull.
