I opened a crate in September that I had parked on the slab in June. The leaves were a lasagne of waves. The numbers were still in order. The book had become a climate experiment. I flattened for two days and I still lost a page to a split. The crate is a door I have not glued yet. It deserves the same air as the core.

Shop air is the last shop-stage essay because it is the one that makes the other tools look stupid. A sharp knife on a sponge, a bag on a chip, a match on a bundle that took a rain: I have done all three.

## Off the slab

Concrete is a reservoir and a cold. A bundle on concrete takes a gradient. I store on stickers, on a shelf, in the crate, off the floor. I have used a pallet and called it enough. It is enough if the air goes under.

A woodstove in January is the other slab. A crate next to a stove is a chip factory. I keep flitches in the same room I keep panels, not in the room I keep the stove if I can help it. South Arkansas July is a sponge factory. I do not store in a leak.

## The same air as the core

I glue a leaf to a core. If they have lived in different climates, they will equalize after I have already hung the door. I bring the crate into the finishing room a week early if the finishing room is the house. I do not glue a swamp to a desert. I said it in the core essay. The crate is how I fail it.

I do not print a humidity percent as SOP without a house meter note. [VERIFY] if we have a target. I will say: even, boring, the same.

## Closed crate, open crate

A sealed plastic is a sweat if the shop is a swing. A wide-open bundle is a curl if the shop is a swing. I store flat, weighted or in a closed crate that can still breathe a little, labeled. I open to work. I close to wait.

I write the open date. Veneer does not spoil like milk. It spoils like a door: slowly, then all at once in the glue.

## Pests and the other climate

A mill crate can bring a hitchhiker. I look. I do not write a pesticide recipe. I isolate a dirty crate. I keep the job folder paper from becoming a nest. This is boring and I have skipped it. I do not skip it after a moth in a flitch I needed.

## The rush job that bought the reserved book

Two crates said walnut. One was reserved for a dining pair, numbered, a note on the lid. The other was a pretty grade bundle for a rush cabinet. A helper took pages from the reserved book because they were on top and they were walnut. I sold a cousin to the dining client and I owed them an apology and a wait.

Labels are not decoration. Flitch number, species, Latin if the law cares, date in, date opened, which job reserved the pages. I write the reservation on the crate and on the job folder. I do not smile at unlabeled pretty wood. Pretty is how a shuffle starts.

## January stove, July leak

A woodstove in a shop in this county is a winter fact. A crate beside it is a chip factory. I moved a flitch in January because the corner was warm. In a week the edges crackled. I flattened. I lost a page to a split I should have taped before I moved the crate toward heat.

July is the other factory. A leak I ignored because it was just the roof at the eaves dripped on a closed crate. The top leaves were a sponge. The bottom leaves were a wave. I opened, stickered, weighted, and I still threw a page that had mildew I would not glue under a film. I do not print a humidity percent. [VERIFY] if we have a house target. I will say: the crate lives in the same air as the cores, off the slab, away from the stove, away from the leak.

South Arkansas air is not a mystery. It is a swing. The crate is a door I have not glued yet. I treat the swing as a clamp I did not ask for. I sticker a wet arrival the way I sticker a panel: air on both faces, weight, a note on the lid before I forget which bundle took the rain.

## Bringing the crate into the house air

If the finishing room is the house — a piece that will live in a room I can visit — I bring the crate into that climate a week early when I can. I do not glue a shop swamp onto a house desert. The core essay said it. The crate is how I fail it.

I write the open date. I write *arrived waved* or *arrived brittle* if that is the truth. The next person should not discover the wave in the bag. A sealed plastic that sweats in a swing is a climate I applied. I store flat, weighted or in a crate that can breathe a little. I open to work. I close to wait.

The job folder paper is not a nest. The crate is not a rag bucket. Oil rags and a veneer crate are a novel I will not write. I look for hitchhikers. I isolate a dirty mill crate. I skipped this until a moth in a flitch I needed. I do not skip it now.

## Fire, again

The crate is the expensive corner. I think about a hose. I think about a sawdust pile against a crate. I keep the pile away. I keep the hot plate away. I keep the rags from the finishing pack away — oil rags are a fire the finishing essays already named. A veneer crate next to a rag bucket is a novel I will not write.

## Labels

Flitch number, species, Latin if the law cares, date in, date opened, which job reserved the pages. An unlabeled pretty crate is a novel with the binding gone. I have sold the wrong pages to a rush job because the crate said “walnut” and the reserved book was also walnut. The reserved book had numbers. The rush job had a smile. I owed a client a cousin. I do not smile at unlabeled crates now.

## What I write on the crate

The map. The reservation. The climate notes if the bundle arrived wet or brittle. *Arrived waved, flattened 9/12.* The next person should not discover the wave in the bag.

This is the end of the shop stage. Hide, bag, tape, band, scrape, knife, crate. The repair stage is what you do when the climate and the hip and the belt still win: flatten a failure, chase a blister, use the folder, finish a thin face, tell the client the word without a flinch. The crate in the corner is how you lose before you start. I move it off the slab. I label it. I give it the same air as the door. Then I open the book on purpose.
