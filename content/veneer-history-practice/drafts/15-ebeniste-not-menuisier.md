Jean-Henri Riesener’s stamp on a commode is a small rectangle that has to do a lot of work. It says the guild allowed this man to make veneered case furniture in Paris. It does not say he cut the marble. It does not say he chased the mounts. It does not say the chair in the same room is his. [VERIFY] a specific commode — Waddesdon, the Louvre, the Met — before a caption uses Riesener as if all commodes were his. I use his name because readers know it. I mean the job.

*Ébéniste*: the specialist in ebony and then in all the faces that followed ebony. *Menuisier*: the joiner, the chair, the solid wood that is a frame. The split is French and guild and not a law in Bradley County. It is still a clean way to talk. A chair that tries to walk is a menuisier problem. A door that cups is an ébéniste problem. This shop does both. It should not pretend they are the same bench.

## Why the split existed

Veneered casework is a materials racket: imported leaves, glue, mounts, locks, marble, a store of flitches. Chairmaking is a geometry racket: rake, splay, a joint that takes a hip. Training both to mastery in one body is possible and rare. The guild made it a legal rarity. The market made it a shop rarity.

Oeben’s mechanical tables, Cressent’s mounts, Riesener’s mahogany and pictorial tops: the eighteenth-century Paris names are a catalog of faces plus mechanisms. The mechanism is a third shop in the building. I think about that when a client wants a “secret drawer” in a veneered box. The secret is easy. The drawer that runs after a season is the job.

## Stamps and the later lie

A stamp is not a fingerprint. Shops used stamps. Widows used stamps. Revivals used stamps the way they used Boulle’s name. Pierre Verlet and Alexandre Pradère are the kind of books that slow a caption down. [VERIFY] the stamp rules before a sales paragraph. I will not write “stamped Riesener” as a romance.

In my shop the equivalent of a stamp is the note on the back: both faces, date, glue, core, who sanded. It is not a guild. It is a trail. I like it better than a burned logo on a rail that a belt will take off.

## One building, two benches

We build chairs. We veneer doors. The chair rail is a tenon. The door is a leaf and a band. On a good week they share a finish schedule and a delivery truck. On a bad week someone sands a chair like a door or a door like a chair. The chair loses an arris. The door loses a face.

I keep the wide-belt away from leaves. I keep the leaf tape away from a chair that is still being fitted. The guild split, translated, is: do not let the tools of one job finish the other job.

## Ebony was a starting word

The ébéniste is named for ebony, not because every commode is ebony, but because the dark imported face was the job’s origin story. Oak and walnut grounds, mahogany faces, satinwood, pictorial trophies: the word grew. My word is just “shop.” I still like ébéniste when I mean the face-on-a-case problem. It keeps me from calling a veneered dresser a “solid oak piece with a look.”

## What I tell a client

If they want chairs, we talk about rack and the front rail. If they want a case, we talk about the leaf, the core, the band, the backer. If they want both in a set, we talk about the finish agreeing and the joints remaining themselves. I will not veneer a chair seat that has to take a flex and a sitting bone unless the brief is a period copy with a solid frame and a face that can fail. Most briefs are not that.

The split is a kindness. It keeps the apology from spreading. Veneer is not a way I cheat a chair. Veneer is how I face a case. Solid is how I build a rail that takes a hip. A dining set can have both and be one family. The stamp, if I had one, would have to say that in a rectangle too small for the sentence. So I say the sentence out loud.

## A commode hip and a chair hip

A commode’s hip meets a band and a leaf. A chair’s hip meets a rail and a tenon. I sanded them like the same job once, on a dining set that wanted to leave on a Friday. The chair lost an arris I had spent a day shaping. The commode — a low chest, walnut face, oak ground — lost a comma of leaf at the front corner where the wide-belt kissed the band. I pieced the leaf from the folder offcut. I recut the chair arris and I was late anyway.

I now hang the chairs first and I face the case second, or I face the case and I leave the chairs in clamps until the leaf is safe. The order changes. The fence does not. The scraper that is right for a crotch is wrong for a crest rail I still have to carve.

## The secret drawer that bound in August

I built a spring well and a false back in a veneered cabinet because a client had seen a photograph of an Oeben table and wanted a secret. [VERIFY] a named Oeben piece against a current catalog if a caption uses his mechanism as more than a warning. The well was easy. In August the oak sides took shop air and the walnut face did not take it the same way. The drawer bound. The secret became a drawer you needed a story to open.

I eased the sides. I waxed the runners. I wrote *secret is a runner* on the folder. I still build a well. I will not sell the mechanism as the furniture. The lock now meets a rebate I cut before I face the door, so I am not sanding a leaf to make a plate sit.

## When a dining set mixes the benches

Chairs get rake, splay, a front rail that takes a hip. The table gets a leaf if the top wants one thought of figure, a core if it wants to stay a rectangle, a band, both faces. The sideboard is the ébéniste problem in full — flitch, glue, a lock, a drawer that runs. I finish them as a family and I keep the joints themselves. A sitting bone is a flex I will not face. A case is a picture I will.

Finish day is where the benches collide again. I film the chairs first, off the leaf bench, so overspray and a hungry scuff pad cannot find a walnut face. The case waits on stickers, both faces seeing air, until the chairs are safe to stack. Then I finish the commode like a face: no wide-belt, a scraper, a block, stop early. I have scuffed a leaf with a pad that had grit from a chair rail. The pale comma was small and I still pieced it. Now the pads do not travel.

The note on the back of that set says more than a burned logo: both faces, date, glue, core, who sanded, chair rails solid, case faced. Riesener’s stamp still does a lot of work in a museum caption. [VERIFY] Waddesdon, the Louvre, or the Met before a caption uses his name as if all commodes were his. My rectangle is a pencil trail. English walnut is next: a different country, a different guild story, the same face-on-a-case habit in a darker, quieter wood. The ébéniste word stays French. The job crosses the Channel without permission.
