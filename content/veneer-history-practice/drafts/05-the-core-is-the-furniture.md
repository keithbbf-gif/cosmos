I have blamed glue for a door that cupped. The glue was fine. The face was a handsome quartered oak. The back was empty air and a coat of something I told myself would “seal it.” A week of July, a shop without enough air, and the door became a shallow bowl. The leaf did what leaves do. The core did what an unbalanced panel does. I remade it with a backer. I wrote *both faces* on the job folder in a hand I could read from the other end of the bench.

The core is the furniture. The leaf is the face you were allowed to spend.

## Plywood

A good hardwood plywood, even, dry, the plies in disagreement with each other so the panel stays a rectangle: this is the modern default and it earned it. I want a core that is already balanced before I add a decorative face. I still veneer both sides when I add a heavy or a thirsty leaf, or I buy a panel the mill balanced for that leaf.

Baltic birch and its cousins are honest in drawers and jigs. They are a look. They are not “oak” when I put oak on them, and I do not say they are. ApplePly and the better domestic hardwood cores are what I reach for when the door is furniture. Construction plywood with a voids and a stamp from a house wrap is a house wrap. I will not put a crotch on it.

Screws: plywood takes a screw if you drill and you do not ask the face ply to be a cross-dowel. Hinges want meat. A leaf does not give you meat. The core does.

## Lumber core

Lumber core is old and good and heavy: strips of solid, a crossband, a face. The old shops liked it because it machines like wood, takes a screw like wood, and stays flatter than a wide solid. It also moves a little like wood, which is better than pretending MDF is oak.

The strips can telegraph in a low-angle light if the glue-up was proud or the moisture was a fight. I check the core under raking light before I spend a quiet rift on it. A telegraphing core under a satin finish is a telegraphing core. The leaf will not hide a ridge. It will shine it.

Weight is a door-closer problem and a shipping problem. I like lumber core on a table that will be dragged, on a desk that will take a clamp, on a piece I want to feel like furniture in the hand. I do not like it on a huge wall panel I cannot lift without a helper I do not have.

## MDF

MDF is flat. That is the sentence. It is also heavy, a sink for screws if you do not plan, a swell if it drinks, and a dust that wants a mask. I use it where the piece can stand MDF: a painted box, a speaker, a shop jig, a panel that will live in a stable room and never see a wet glass on an unprotected edge.

I will put a leaf on MDF for a stable, quiet face in a closet interior or a shop cabinet I am not selling as hardwood furniture. I will not put a $200 leaf on MDF and call the result a craft object unless the brief was “flat and cheap and honest.” Honesty is the hinge. “Stable core, oak face, MDF” is a sentence. “Solid oak” on that object is a different sentence and I will not write it.

Edges: MDF needs a band or a fill and a film. A leaf wrapped over an MDF arris without a solid band is a leaf waiting to chip back to gray.

## Particleboard and the price

Particleboard is a price. I will paint it. I will laminate it. I will not dress it in a sequenced flitch and stand next to it in a dining room. The screw will pull. The edge will swell. The leaf will be the only honest material in the object, and that is a kind of lie.

There are engineered cores I will use when a specifier names them and the test data is on the ticket. There is no romance in this paragraph. There is a refusal.

## Solid as a core

A solid panel as a core for a decorative face is usually a mistake unless you are doing traditional hide work on a carcase that already moves as one piece — a ground of pine or oak, as the old shops did, with the leaf and the ground in a conversation about season. That is a different system: hide, hammer, a ground that was never trying to be MDF. It works. It also moves. I do not mix that system with a PVA bag and a hope.

A breadboard is not a veneer core. A tabletop with a leaf on a solid slab is a cup waiting for a lawyer if the other face is empty.

## Moisture and the meter

I meter the core. I do not really meter the leaf — a pin in a 0.6 mm face is theater. The core should be in the same air the piece will live in, or as close as a shop can get. A wet core under a dry leaf is a glue joint that lets go when the core shrinks. A dry core in a wet week swells and the leaf did nothing wrong.

## The screw, the hinge, the hip

Hinges go into the core, or into a solid band, or into a block you planned. They do not go into a leaf. Shelf pins, lid stays, a desktop monitor arm: the core and the insert. The leaf is a wearing surface, not a fastener.

The hip meets the band. The band meets the core. If those two sentences are true, the leaf can be thin and still be furniture.

I knock the panel when it comes out of the press. I look at the back. I write the core species or brand on the note, not just “ply.” The next repair will thank me, or curse the person who wrote “wood.”

## The desk that telegraphed a void

I put a quiet rift-walnut leaf on a hardwood plywood I had used before and trusted too far. Raking light after finish showed a thumb-shaped dip in the kneehole writing surface, a void in an inner ply that the leaf had followed like cloth over a bruise. The glue was fine. The leaf was fine. The core had a hole the mill had graded through and I had not asked the panel to confess under a straightedge before I spent the face.

I filled from the back where I could reach. I could not fill the telegraph. I remade the writing surface. The leftover leaf was a lesser page and the match to the drawer fronts is a cousin, not a twin. I now roller a panel under raking light and I run a hand and a straightedge before the leaf leaves the crate.

Construction plywood with a stamp from a house wrap has given me the same bruise on a shop cabinet I was not selling. I deserved that one. I will not put a dining leaf on a void I can feel with a knuckle.

## A hall table on the wrong meat

The second object is a narrow hall table I built with an MDF core because the drawing asked for dead flat and the budget asked for less. The leaf was a decent cherry, sequenced, both faces. The top stayed flat. The apron screws, which I had run into the MDF edge like a fool, pulled in the first move across a threshold. I plugged, I added a solid band I should have started with, and I re-screwed into wood. The table is still in the house. I will not sell the next one as “cherry furniture” without saying the core’s name.

A lumber-core cousin of that table — same leaf family, strips of poplar, a crossband — takes a clamp and a hip and feels like furniture when you lift one end. It also weighs enough that a solo delivery is a second person. I like that weight on a desk. I do not like it on a wall panel I have to hang on a Friday afternoon with the light going.

The lesson is not “never MDF.” The lesson is that the fastener is a core problem, the hip is a band problem, and the leaf is a wearing surface. If I skip the band, the cherry chips back to gray. If I skip the species name, the sales sentence becomes a lie I will not write.

## How I choose before I unroll

I write the core on the folder before I write the flitch. Door: hardwood plywood I have used, or lumber core if the door is a table’s cousin and will be slammed. Drawer bottom: Baltic birch, honest, sometimes left as itself. Closet interior: MDF I will name. Dining top: the better hardwood core, balanced, both faces, a band thicker than a thumbnail. Particleboard: paint, or a refusal.

I meter the core and I store it in the same air as the leaves. [VERIFY] any later SOP number against a meter and a room, not against a forum.

Then I knock the panel. I look at the edges for voids. I look at the back for a mill stamp I can copy. If the stamp says a grade I do not know, I treat the panel as a suspect until raking light says otherwise. The leaf can be the prettiest page in the county. If the core is a price, the page is a costume.

Bradley County grew the oak that becomes furniture stock. [VERIFY] which plants sold panels and which sold boards before a heritage sentence names a core. What I can say without a file is that I buy cores the way I buy leaves: by a stamp I check and a behavior I have already met. The core is the furniture. I try to choose it like furniture, even when the photograph wants only the face.
