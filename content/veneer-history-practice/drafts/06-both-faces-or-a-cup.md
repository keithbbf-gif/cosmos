The door was beautiful on Monday. Wednesday it would not latch. The strike had not moved. The door had. A straightedge on the face showed a shallow bowl, the ends high, the middle proud toward the room. I had veneered the show face in a hurry for a photograph. The back was sealer. I had told myself sealer was a backer.

Sealer is a film. A backer is a leaf that pulls.

Wood moves. A leaf wants to shrink and swell across its grain. Glue it to one face of a panel and that face has a new idea about width. The other face does not. The panel becomes a curve. The curve is not a mystery. It is a spring.

## What balance means

Balance means the two faces ask the core for roughly the same thing: same thickness class, same grain direction, same moisture at glue-up, same glue, same press. The backer does not have to be the same species. It has to be a similar pull. A cheap rotary backer under a heavy quartered oak can still work if the mill or the shop knew the pairing. A coat of shellac under that oak is not a pairing.

I have used the same species, lesser grade, on the back of a good door. I have used a rotary hardwood the mill sold as backer. I have used a second leaf of the face flitch when the client might ever see the inside of a pair of doors. I have not used paint as a structural backer and then been surprised.

Plywood that is already balanced can take a thin additional face on both sides. One additional face is how you buy a cup. If I add 0.6 mm of thirsty oak to one side of an already-balanced sheet, I have unbalanced it. I add a backer or I buy the face already on the sheet from a mill that did both.

## Orientation

Grain direction of the backer should agree with the face unless you are building a specific cross-pull you understand. Most doors: face vertical, backer vertical. A tabletop: face as the picture requires, backer the same way. Crossbanding is a layer, not a backer substitute, unless the construction is a built-up panel with a known recipe.

I mark the inside face. I have glued two show faces on one door because I was tired and the leaves looked similar. That door is still somewhere, both sides trying to be the room.

## Moisture is a face

If the face leaf is damp and the backer is dry, you have built a time-delay cup. They will equalize. The panel will move after you have already hung the door. I store face and backer in the same crate, same air, same days. I glue them in the same press cycle when I can. A day apart is a different weather.

Shop fans on one face of a drying panel are how you invent a banana. Let both faces see the same air.

## When people skip it

They skip it because the back will not be seen. A bookcase against a wall. A dresser against a dresser. A tabletop that “only has one side.” The tabletop has two sides. The underside is a face as far as the climate is concerned. I have seen tops cup toward the room because the underside was bare and the finish on top slowed that face down.

Finish is a mild backer and a bad one. A film on one side only is a moisture gradient you applied on purpose. If you film the top, film the bottom. If you oil the top, oil the bottom. If you veneer the top, veneer the bottom. The rule is boring and it is the rule.

## The latch and the reveal

A cupped door shows at the latch and at the reveal. You can plane a reveal. You cannot plane a cup out of a veneered door without going through the leaf. I remake. I do not “correct” a cup with a belt on the face. That is how you get a pale wound and a still-cupped door.

A slight spring in a large architectural panel can be pulled by hardware and a frame. I do not count on that for furniture. Furniture is picked up, slammed, leaned on, moved to sweep. The panel has to be a panel.

## The note on the back

Both faces. Date. Glue. Core. If the backer is a different species, write it. The next person may sand the inside of a cabinet door and think they have found “the real wood.” They have found the backer. If you wrote it, they might stop.

I knock the panel. I look at the edges for a glue line that says two leaves. I have been fooled by a sealer that looked like a leaf in bad light. Now I look.

## How I lay a backer

I cut the backer first, a little oversize, and I mark an arrow for grain. I cut the show face after I am sure the backer will exist. That order has saved me from a handsome door with a sealer back I invented because I was already in the bag. If I am short on the face flitch, I still cut a backer from a lesser grade of the same species or from a rotary hardwood the mill sold as backer. I do not cut a second show face “for later” and leave the first door naked. Later is a climate.

Same glue, same press cycle when I can. I spread both leaves, or I spread the core both sides, depending on the glue and the size. Hide wants a field I can finish before it skins. PVA wants an even film and a press I will not open to peek. I have opened a press to peek. The cup that followed was not mysterious. One face had seen air and a fan. The other had not.

I keep the cauls the same. A thick caul on the show face and a thin scrap on the back is how you invent a banana in the bag. Both faces see the same pressure, or I do not call the panel balanced. I have used a cheap rotary backer under quartered oak and it held because the mill pairing and the thickness class agreed. I have used a coat of shellac and it did not hold, because shellac is a film.

After the press I sticker the panel so both faces see shop air. I do not lean a fresh door show-face-out toward a window. That window is a moisture gradient I applied on purpose.

## The tabletop that lifted its own finish

A dining top I veneered on the show face only — “the underside will never be seen,” which is a sentence I now treat as a dare — cupped toward the room after a wet week. The finish on top slowed that face. The bare underside drank. The top became a shallow lid. I filmed the underside too late. The cup stayed. I remade the top with a backer of the same walnut, lesser pages, and I oiled both faces on the same afternoon.

The second object in that house is a pair of wardrobe doors I did later, same client, both faces, a rotary backer inside that nobody praises. Those doors still latch. The table, the remade one, is flatter than my pride. I wrote *underside is a face* on the job folder in a hand I can read from the other end of the bench.

A bookcase against a wall is the other place people skip. I veneered the insides of a pair of doors and left the outsides as factory plywood “because they face the wall.” The wall was a chimney breast. The chimney was a climate. The doors bowed into the room and the books started to walk. I pulled them, added backers, and I stopped using the wall as an excuse.

## Rebalancing is a remake

People ask if I can “flatten” a cupped veneered door with clamps and a wet towel. I can make a different cup. I cannot put the missing leaf on the back by wetting the face. I remake. I salvage the hardware and the band if the band is still thicker than a thumbnail. I do not compete with the climate by sanding.

If I inherit a one-sided antique — hide on a pine ground, the old system — I am in a different conversation. That carcase already moves as one piece. I do not add a PVA backer to a hide ground and hope the centuries agree. I repair in the system I found, or I explain why I will not. Mixing the systems is how you get a cup with a scholar’s story.

This is the last foundation essay. If you know what the leaf is, how it was cut, what the number means, what the figure is arguing, what the core is, and why both faces exist, you can read the history without thinking veneer was invented by a catalog. The history is older than the apology. The cup is older than the apology too. The old shops balanced. They just did not always write it on the back.
