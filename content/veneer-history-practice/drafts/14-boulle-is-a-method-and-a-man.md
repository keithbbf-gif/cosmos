André-Charles Boulle (1642–1732) was ébéniste to Louis XIV. The Wallace Collection’s cabinet F16, attributed to him and dated to the early 1670s, still has floral wood marquetry of an older luxury and, on the frieze, the metal-and-shell language that would take his name. [VERIFY] the Wallace’s current materials line and attribution sentence; they have been careful, and I will be too. The Met’s commode 1982.60.82 is later workshop Boulle: ebony face, engraved brass, tortoiseshell, gilt-bronze mounts, a marble top. William Rieder’s Linsky catalog entry is the spine I want an editor to re-read. [VERIFY] 1982.60.82 against the current Met page.

I am a wood shop. I am not going to teach you how to saw a brass-and-shell sandwich. I am going to teach you how to caption one, and why the method is veneer even when the leaf is metal.

## Première and contre-partie

You saw two sheets together — brass, shell — and you get two panels: one with brass in a shell field, one with shell in a brass field. Economy and dazzle. The leftover of one picture is the other picture. That is bookmatching for people who can afford a foundry and a turtle.

Hawksbill shell is a legal and an ethical stop now. I will not write a workaround. I will not name a plastic as “the same.” If a restorer uses a documented modern substitute, that is a conservation file, not a shop flex.

Brass engraving, black fill, a ground of oak or walnut, an ebony face: the stack is a collaboration. Mounts are a founder and a chaser and a gilder. Marble is a third shop. Boulle’s name sits on the agreement.

## He did not stamp

Later copies are everywhere. The nineteenth century made “Boulle” an industry. The Beurdeley shop that sold F16 to Wallace in 1872 is part of that afterlife. [VERIFY] that purchase sentence against Wallace. Caption a piece as Boulle technique when it is not the man’s hand. Caption it as workshop when the catalog does. Caption it as revival when the mounts are too clever and the oak is too new.

I care because clients say “Boulle” the way they say “Chippendale”: as a mood. A mood is not a joint. A mood is not a leaf.

## Ébéniste

In French guild language an ébéniste is not a chairmaker (*menuisier*). The ébéniste is the veneered-case specialist, originally in ebony. Boulle’s shop is the type of that split. Chairs at Versailles belong to another bench. This pack is mostly the ébéniste’s problem: a face on a box. The next essay stays on that word.

The silver furniture of the early Versailles phase was melted when the wars required it. What remains in the photographs is glass, gilt, and later replacements. Veneer outlasted the silver because it was not worth melting. That is a dark shop joke and a true one.

## Fire and stock

Boulle’s workshop stock burned in 1720. [VERIFY] Samoyault before a caption uses the fire as a tearjerker. The point is not pathos. The point is that a veneer shop is a warehouse of faces. I have a crate of flitches that is the most expensive corner of the building. I think about fire. I think about a hose. I think about what I would grab. I would grab the numbered bundles and the job folders with the offcuts. The machines are replaceable. The sequence is not.

## What I take to a hardwood bench

I take the sandwich idea in wood only: two leaves sawn together for a bookmatch is already our habit. I take the honesty of première and contre-partie — use both halves, do not waste the leftover of a picture. I take the collaboration: I am not a founder. I will hire a metal shop if a mount is required. I will not glue a stamped “ormolu” from a catalog onto a walnut box and say Boulle.

I leave the shell. I leave the court. I leave the habit of putting a man’s name on a century of copies.

When I look at F16 in a photograph, I look at the place where floral wood and metal marquetry share a door. That seam is the history of veneer in one line: the older garden, the newer metal, the same glue problem, the same oak underneath. The man is a shop. The method is a recipe that escaped the shop. Keep them labeled.

## A clock case someone called Boulle

A clock case arrived in the shop already named. The owner used the man’s name. The brass was thin and the “shell” was a plastic I will not advertise, and the oak underneath was too new and too clean for the man’s century. The mounts were catalog clever. I said Boulle technique as a mood, and revival as a guess, and I would not write the man’s name on a repair ticket. [VERIFY] any later attribution if that case ever gets a catalog; I have a clock, not a Wallace number.

I repaired a lifted corner of the brass with a glue the plastic could stand and I told the owner I would not chase a foundry finish. I am a wood shop. I filed the proud edge so a hip would not catch. I wrote *revival sandwich, not the man* on the folder. The clock keeps time. The caption is the work I actually did.

The owner still wanted the man’s name on the invoice. I wrote the clock’s movement maker, the plastic I would not advertise, and the glue I used on the lifted corner. That is a caption. A mood is not a caption.

## The sandwich in wood: two doors from one sawing

I taped two walnut leaves face to face and cut a simple cartouche so each door of a pair got the leftover of the other. No turtle. No brass. Hide in the packet. The left cartouche sat high by a sixteenth. The right sat true. I recut the left from the next pages of the flitch and I sent the shifted pair to a pair of closet interiors where a cousin is allowed. I now pin the packet, I mark a centerline on both faces, and I saw slower than my pride.

The doors taught me a shop use for première and contre-partie that does not require a foundry: keep both halves, number them as a pair, and do not throw the leftover of a picture into the stove. The clock case’s plastic sandwich taught me the other half: a revival material is a repair problem, not a heritage sentence.

## When I call the other benches

A true Boulle stack is an agreement among shops. Face, ground, mounts, marble, a lock. I will hire a metal shop if a mount is required and I will send a drawing, not a mood. I will hire a stone shop if a top is stone. I will not chase a gilt on my own bench with a rub-on wax and a photograph of the Met commode 1982.60.82. [VERIFY] that commode’s current materials line before anyone uses it as a recipe.

What I can do here is the oak or walnut ground, the wood face, the note on the back, and a refusal to melt the word Boulle over a clock that is not the man. The ébéniste essay is next because the guild word is still the right word for what I do when I put a face on a case. I am not sworn to a king. I am sworn to a hip and a band and a sentence in a dining room.
