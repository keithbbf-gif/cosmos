A Phyfe card table in a museum is often a hinge problem wearing a mahogany face. The top folds. The edge of the fold is an arris that will tell on a thin leaf. The lyre support is a carving and sometimes a veneered ground. Duncan Phyfe (1768–1854), Scottish-born, New York shop, is the name Americans hang on a whole Federal-to-Empire manner. [VERIFY] a specific table against the Met, Winterthur, or the Kenny/Brown Phyfe literature before the caption says his hand. Shops copy. The manner spreads. The leaf is still mahogany that came from somewhere the Director already knew.

I care about Phyfe in an Arkansas pack because the American shop story is not only Grand Rapids and not only a mill town. It is also a coastal cabinetmaker buying imported faces and selling them to a republic that wanted to look like it had been here longer than it had.

## Federal is a taste, not a joint

Shield backs, tapered legs, satinwood inlay, a light stringing: Federal is the American name for a Hepplewhite/Sheraton conversation. The joinery is still a chair or a case. The face is still a leaf when the surface wants a picture. Phyfe’s earlier work sits in that talk. The later work goes heavier, Empire, a lyre, a claw, a darker mahogany.

I will not veneer a shield back and call it Federal if the chair cannot take a hip. Menuisier first. Face second. The split travels.

## The card table as a shop test

Folding tops are a test of thickness, edge, and hinge screws that go into a core, not a leaf. I have repaired a fold where the leaf lifted in a half-moon around the hinge mortise. Someone had mortised through the face as if it were a board. The hinge was fine. The face was a wound.

When I veneer a folding top, I band the fold edge like I mean it, or I use a solid lip, or I accept that this object should be solid. A card table that is a leaf on both faces of a thin core is a card table that will telegraph a hinge. Plan the meat.

## New York as a port

Like Antwerp, like Amsterdam, New York is a materials list. Mahogany, satinwood, brass, a marble, a looking-glass plate: the shop is a harbor. Arkansas is a forest. I can buy a harbor leaf. I cannot pretend Wilmar was Phyfe’s yard. What I can say is that an American shop has always been allowed to put an imported face on a domestic carcase. That is not a betrayal of a hardwood county. It is how a lot of American furniture was always made.

A local walnut face on a local oak core is the other American sentence. I like that sentence more for a house on the Saline. I will still buy mahogany if the brief is a parlor that wants it and the invoice is legal.

## Attribution is a climate

“Phyfe” on an auction label is a weather. It changes. The 2011–12 scholarship tightened some shops and loosened some romances. [VERIFY] current labels. I treat the name like Riesener: useful, over-applied, not a glue.

My note on the back is the attribution I control. Year, glue, core, face species, both faces. A hundred years from now someone can argue about my taste. They should not have to argue about my materials.

## The sideboard and the string I erased

A Federal sideboard is the second object I keep next to the card table. Long, a little architectural, satinwood stringing, a light band around a mahogany field, legs that are a chairmaker’s problem under a case. I inlaid a maple string in a walnut sideboard that wanted a Federal sentence without a harbor invoice. The string was a knife job. The field was a leaf. I flushed the string with a block and 180 because I was proud of the fit.

I sanded the string into a pale ghost in two feet of the front rail. Maple dust filled the pores of the walnut and made a fog. I scraped. I did not get the ghost entirely gone. The sideboard has a quieter string on the left and a memory on the right. I tell that story when a client wants “just a little inlay.” Stringing is a leaf with no meat. You scrape to the line and you stop. A belt is a remake.

The sideboard also taught me that Federal taste is not a joint. The case still needed a web frame that could take the weight of the drawers. The face still needed both sides. I had balanced the doors and forgotten the long top. The top whispered a cup toward the window. I pulled it, added the backer I should have put on the first day, and reset the top. Phyfe’s shop would have known. I knew and I hurried.

## Reeded legs stay solid

Phyfe’s reeded legs are a menuisier sentence. I will not wrap a leaf around a taper and call the reeding a carving. I have tried a test: a poplar blank, a mahogany leaf, a scratch stock. The leaf furred in the reed and tore at the arris. The blank looked like a period leg from across the room and like a wound from the chair. I threw the test in the burn pile I am not supposed to have and I went back to a solid blank I could reed and scrape.

If a client wants the Federal parlor and the harbor leaf, the legs can be solid mahogany or a legal cousin I can invoice. The top can be a leaf. The lyre — if they want a lyre — is a carving that may sit on a faced ground. I will face the ground. I will carve the lyre from meat. Mixing those religions in one stick is how you get a reed that is a furred stripe.

A dining chair that has to rack is not a veneer job. I said that in the ébéniste essay. I say it again because Phyfe’s name gets hung on chairs more than on card tables in the American mouth. The chair is joinery. The card table is a hinge and a face. The sideboard is a case. I will not sell a manner as a joint.

## Empire weight and the darker leaf

Phyfe’s later work goes heavier: Empire, a claw, a lyre that is a thicker thought, a darker mahogany, brass that wants to be Roman. [VERIFY] a named piece against the Met, Winterthur, or the Kenny/Brown literature before a caption moves a table from Federal to Empire. The leaf does not change its job. The taste changes the thickness of the shadow.

I will not bolt a claw onto a Federal taper and say the shop evolved. I will not stain a maple and say satinwood to keep a Federal light after the client has asked for Empire. If they want the later weight, we talk about a darker field, a heavier band, a support that is a carving, a fold that still has to survive. The card table remains the shop test. Empire does not excuse a thin arris at the fold.

Arkansas is still a forest. New York is still a port in this story. I can buy the harbor leaf if the brief is a parlor and the invoice is legal. I can also put a local walnut face on a local oak core and let the reeded leg be a solid walnut I grew up seeing as a board. That sentence is American too. It just never got Phyfe’s name on the auction label.

## What I take

I take the card-table warning. I take the American right to a harbor leaf. I take the lyre as a carving that may sit on a faced ground.

I leave the claw on a dining chair if the client thinks Phyfe is a foot style you can bolt on. I leave the habit of staining a local maple and saying satinwood.

Hide glue is next, because all of these cabinet-century faces — Dutch, Boulle, English walnut, mahogany, Biedermeier, Phyfe — spent a lot of their lives on a protein pot. The machines will change the knife. They will not make hide stupid. I still keep a pot. The Federal leaf, when it lifts, still prefers the pot to a modern fight.
