The Gubbio studiolo at the Metropolitan Museum is a small room that is not a room. It is a set of walls made of wood pictures: lattices that recede, benches that have no seat you can use, a lute that will not play, cabinets whose doors are open on objects that are leaves. Federico da Montefeltro’s study at Urbino is the cousin that stayed home. [VERIFY] dates, workshop names, and the Met accession 39.153 against the current collection page before a caption locks them. I have stood in front of enough photographs to know the feeling. I have not yet stood in Gubbio’s reconstructed box in New York with a notebook. When I do, I will check the shadows. The shadows are species.

Intarsia is veneer that learned to draw. The shop word later is marquetry. The Italian word is inlay as a picture. The difference from a bookmatched door is intention: the door wants one thought of figure. The studiolo wants a space that is not there.

## How a shadow is a wood

A perspectival shadow on those walls is often a darker species, or a scorched edge, or a grain turned so it reads as a plane changing direction. The craftsmen — the *intarsiatori* — were joiners and draftsmen. They cut leaves to a cartoon. They did not hope a stain would make a lute. They built a lute out of maple and walnut and a green wood that was once a local color and is now a museum problem.

[VERIFY] the woods list on the Met page. Walnut, oak, fruitwoods, spindle, a stained plane: the palette is a lumber rack plus a dye. I care because a shop that “does marquetry” with a laser and a single walnut sheet is doing a different job. The studiolo job is a map of species.

Hide glue, a ground, a cartoon transferred: the method is closer to my hammer than to a CNC. The patience is not. A door is a week. A studiolo is a workshop’s year.

## Why a duke wanted a fake bench

The studiolo is a humanist flex: the man of arms who also reads, the cabinet of objects that prove it, the perspective that proves he can afford Brunelleschi’s math on a wall. Furniture here is propaganda you sit next to. The fake bench is a joke and a status. You cannot sit. You are supposed to notice that you cannot sit, and that the man who paid for it could have built a real bench and chose a picture.

I think about that when a client wants a “statement wall” of bookmatched walnut. They want a picture. They often do not want a studiolo. A bookmatch is one tree agreeing with itself. A studiolo is twenty trees pretending to be a room. I ask which.

## The cartoon is the joint

Layout is the joint. In a mortise-and-tenon, the gauge line is the joint. In intarsia, the cartoon is the joint. If the lute’s neck is a degree off the perspective grid, the wall wobbles. If my bookmatch seam is a degree off the door’s center, the door wobbles in a quieter way.

I tape seams. They taped a picture. The tape is not the inheritance. The inheritance is: decide the drawing before the knife.

Scorching — *sand-shading* in the later English shop word — is how a leaf gets a burned gradient that reads as roundness. I have shaded a small leaf in hot sand for a repair. I have also burned a leaf I needed. The sand is not romantic. It is a heat you do not control as well as you think.

## What survived and what was moved

The Gubbio room in New York is a transplantation. Rooms move. Veneer rooms move worse than stone rooms. A leaf cycle of heat and winter in a palace, then a dealer, then a museum HVAC, is a long press cycle with no cauls. Losses are filled. Colors shift. The green that was a plant dye becomes a brown. [VERIFY] conservation notes on 39.153; do not invent a fill.

That is shop-relevant. My bookmatched door will live in a house with a heat pump and a July. The studiolo lived in a political climate and then a market. Both are climate. The leaf does not care about the politics. It cares about the air.

## What I will not copy

I will not build a fake bench that cannot be sat on and call it a dining nook. I will not stain a single species to fake a studiolo palette for a restaurant. I will take the lesson that a wall can be furniture, and that furniture can be a drawing, and that the drawing is species plus knife plus glue.

A modern architectural panel of sequenced oak is a descendant in the way a straight road is a descendant of a Roman road: same idea of a surface covering a structure, different brief. The studiolo is the brief at full volume.

When I set a diamond match on a drawer front, I am in a faint echo. The diamond is a drawing. The drawer still has to open. That is the difference I like: the picture has to survive a hand. The duke’s lute did not have to play. My drawer does.

## A drawer front that tried to be a lute

I cut a small intarsia lute on a jewelry-drawer front because a photograph of Gubbio had made me proud. I transferred a cartoon, I cut maple and walnut and a dyed plane I will not call a Renaissance green, and I sand-shaded the soundboard edge in hot sand. The neck was a degree off the centerline of the drawer. The drawer, once hung, made the lute lean as if it were drunk. The picture had to survive a hand, and the hand had to pull a drawer that was also a drawing.

I remade the front. I redrew the cartoon with the drawer’s rectangle as the first line, not the lute’s. The second lute sits. The first lute is in the job folder, a failure I can hold. Hide glue let me lift the first picture without destroying the ground. PVA would have been a different fight.

The jewelry drawer lives in a cabinet that also has a quiet bookmatched walnut door. That door is the object I will actually sell next to the lute. A client who wants a “statement wall” usually wants the door, not twenty trees pretending to be a room. I ask. Most want the one tree. A studiolo is a workshop’s year. A bookmatch is a week I can stand behind. [VERIFY] Met 39.153 woods and workshop against the current collection page before a caption borrows the lute as if I had stood in the box with a notebook.

## Sand-shading a repair leaf

Hot sand is a gradient you do not control as well as you think. I keep a small iron pot of sand on a hot plate when I need a burned edge for a repair that has to read as roundness — a petal, a column, a lute’s belly. I bury the edge, I count, I pull. I have counted too long. The leaf went crisp and the repair became a patch of charcoal I had to recut.

I test on a cutoff from the same leaf. Species burn at different speeds. Maple browns in a way I can use. Oak flake goes dirty and looks like a mistake. Walnut can go red-black and then dead. I do not sand-shade a quartered-oak door to fake a studiolo shadow. The ray is not a cartoon.

Scorching is also how later restorers “improved” a faded wall. [VERIFY] conservation notes on 39.153 before anyone claims which shadows are original and which are a later heat. I will not invent a fill. I will say: colors shift, greens go brown, rooms move, and a leaf cycle of palace heat and museum HVAC is a long press cycle with no cauls.

## Ground, hide, and a cartoon on paper

The studiolo method I can actually use is older than my pride: a cartoon, a ground, hide, a knife. I draw on paper. I glue the paper to a packet or I prick the lines. I cut. I shade only what the drawing needs. I keep a map of species so a future me does not stain a repair into the wrong tree.

I have used a laser-cut packet for a geometric diamond and still drawn the cartoon first. The laser is a knife I do not pretend is Urbino. The first lute taught me that a drawing which ignores the drawer’s rectangle will lean once a hand pulls it. I now draw the rectangle first.

Urbino and Gubbio are the proof that veneer was already an art history problem in the fifteenth century, not a kitchen problem in the twentieth. The next essay is Antwerp, where the cabinet becomes a portable studiolo with doors.
