The crotch leaf does not want to be flat. It came from the fork of a tree that spent fifty years deciding which trunk would win, and the fibers there are a traffic accident that light loves. I weight the corners. I mist the back. I wait. If I glue it in the shape it arrived, I have glued a potato chip to a door and called the bubbles “character.”

Figure is not a finish. It is anatomy plus an angle. The same white oak is flake on the quarter, stripe on the rift, cathedral on the flat. The same walnut is quiet on a straight rift and theatrical in a stump. People buy “figured walnut” the way they buy “loud.” I buy a specific argument: this crotch, this burl, this quilt, this bee’s-wing, this ray.

## Flake and the quarter

Quartered oak is a religion in some shops and a headache in others. The ray fleck is the medullary ray, a ribbon the tree used to move water and sugar, sliced so it shows as a mirror. On a leaf, the flake can be matched into a field that no two solid boards would agree to stay flat for.

The headache is chatter and tear. Flake is hard and shiny and will chip if a knife is dull. A dye sits on the ray differently than it sits on the fiber. I test a cutoff. I do not invent a uniform oak from a flitch that is half mirror and half stripe.

Rift is the diplomat: the ray subdued, the stripe straight, the door that does not shout. I like rift stiles around a flake panel. That is a sentence this pack will say more than once, because it is how a door stays a door and still earns the oak.

## Crotch

Crotch is the fork. Mahogany crotch paid for a lot of eighteenth-century dining. Walnut crotch pays for a lot of twentieth-century boardroom. The figure feathers out from a dark heart. Bookmatched, it becomes a butterfly or a Rorschach. Slip-matched, it becomes a current.

The leaf is unstable. Tension wood, compression wood, a fork that was never meant to be a sheet. Flatten it. Tape it. Do not sand it like a board. The dark heart will go through first; the pale wings will still have meat. That is how you get a raccoon face in a $400 leaf.

I keep the offcut. Crotch does not have a cousin at the lumberyard.

## Burl

Burl is a wound the tree healed by making a knot of buds. The leaf is eyes and swirls and a thousand grain reversals. It will not plane. It will barely scrape. It wants a scraper, a cabinet scraper, sandpaper you change before it loads, and a finish that does not puddle in the eyes.

Burl as solid is a turning. Burl as leaf is furniture. The old shops knew this; the catalogs that sold “solid burl tables” were selling a split they had not met yet.

Flattening burl is a project. Glycerin recipes exist and I treat them as shop folklore until I have a note that they did not stain later. [VERIFY] any flattening bath before it becomes house SOP. I have had luck with controlled moisture, heat, and time. I have also had a burl that returned to a potato chip in the bag. The bag is not a personality. The leaf is.

## Quilt, curl, bee’s-wing

Quilted maple is a three-dimensional bruise in the fiber that light walks across. Curl (fiddleback) is a tight ripple, the back of a violin if the violin were a drawer front. Bee’s-wing is a tiny mottled figure in satinwood and a few cousins, the eighteenth century’s quiet flex.

These figures are why veneer exists as a luxury. A quilted maple slab that wide would move like a sail. A quilted maple leaf on a balanced core is a desk that can live in an office with a HVAC system and a weekend of no HVAC.

They are also why people sand through. The high spots of a quilt are the first to go. You think you are flattening. You are making islands of core. Scrape. Use a block. Stop early. The finish will show the quilt if you leave it wood.

## Wild grain is a glue problem

Figured leaves drink glue in patches and spit it in others. End grain that is really a swirl will starve a PVA line and stain a hide line. I size when the leaf looks thirsty. I do not flood. A starved crotch is a blister later. A flooded burl is a telegraph of glue in the eyes.

The core still has to be the furniture. Figure does not excuse a one-sided panel. A quilted face with no backer is a cup with a fancy name.

## Buying the argument

I look at the whole flitch when I can. A pretty top leaf over a flitch of waste is a pretty first door and a problem for the set. Sequence matters more in figure than in plain rift, because the figure is a story that continues or dies.

I look at defects the mill graded through. A bark inclusion in a burl can be a feature. A bark inclusion in a dining top at elbow height is a crumb trap. Mineral streak in walnut can be handsome. Mineral streak across a bookmatch can be a mustache.

I write the figure name on the folder the way I write the species. “Walnut” is not enough. “Walnut crotch, flitch 4, leaves 12–15, bookmatch, dining” is a sentence a future me can use.

Light is the last critic. Raking light on a quilt. North light on flake. A warm bulb on mahogany crotch that will go redder in a year. I do not approve a match under the shop’s cool LEDs only. I have approved a match that died in a client’s dining room at 6 p.m. The leaf was fine. The light was honest and I had not asked it.

## The dresser that went raccoon

I sanded a walnut-crotch dresser front as if it were a board. The dark heart is harder and thinner in the way that matters: it sits a little high after glue, and a wide-belt or a proud block finds it first. I found it. Two pale commas of core looked back at me, one on each wing of the bookmatch, a raccoon in a leaf I had paid for and could not replace. The flitch’s next pages were already on the companion doors. There was no cousin at the lumberyard.

I pieced from the offcut I almost threw away. The patches sit in the heart, and I can still find them if I know where to look. The client was kinder than the light. I wrote *no belt on crotch* on the inside of the top drawer, which is a note I should have written on my own wrist.

The procedure I use now is slower and duller. I flatten the leaf first — weights, a controlled mist on the back, a night if it arrived as a chip. I tape the weak feathers. I size if the swirl looks thirsty. I scrape after the press, a cabinet scraper, short strokes, and I stop when the glue ghosts are gone. I do not flatten a quilt or a crotch with the finish grit. The finish will show the figure if I leave it wood. If I keep going, the finish will show the core.

## A music stand in bee’s-wing

The second object I keep next to that dresser is a small music stand I faced in satinwood with a bee’s-wing flicker, a leftover from a larger job that did not need the quiet leaf. [VERIFY] the invoice species; “satinwood” in the trade can be more than one tree. The stand is a picture of a luxury the eighteenth century spent on a drawer front. It is also a glue problem: the mottled fiber drinks in patches. I sized, I did not flood, and I still got a telegraph in one eye of the figure where hide sat too wet.

I keep the stand in the shop. Clients who ask for “figured wood” hold it and then hold a rift-oak sample. Most of them want the stand’s light and the oak’s manners. I tell them those are two arguments. I can put bee’s-wing on a box. I cannot put bee’s-wing manners on a six-foot table without a core and a backer and a budget that admits the leaf is the fortune.

Stump walnut is a third argument I almost treated as crotch. Stump is swirl and eyes near the root, a cousin of burl and a cousin of trouble. I used a stump leaf on a jewelry-box lid because the figure was a storm I liked. The lid cupped in the bag — one face of figured walnut, a thin core I had told myself was “small enough not to matter.” I remade it with a backer of the same flitch, lesser page. The storm stayed. The lid stayed a rectangle. Figure does not excuse a one-sided panel. I have now written that sentence on two folders.

## Tape is part of the figure

A figured leaf wants tape the way a quiet rift leaf wants a clean seam. I tape the feathers of a crotch before I carry it. I tape a burl across the eyes that look like they will pop. I do not tape a quilt so hard that the adhesive ghosts under a thin finish; I test a cutoff. Hide tape, veneer tape, a painter’s tape I will regret — the brand matters less than whether I can get it off without lifting a wing.

I have lifted a wing. A mahogany crotch, a tape I pulled across the grain instead of with the feather, and a triangle of face came up like a scab. I glued it back with hide and a warm iron and a caul, and the repair is a slightly hungrier line in the finish. The leaf was arguing with gravity before I touched it. I added a direction it did not grow.

When I buy the argument now, I buy the offcut, the next two pages, and an evening of raking light. The raccoon dresser taught me to pay for the cousins I will need after a mistake. The music stand taught me that a quiet luxury still wants size and a backer. Tape is how I carry the argument across the shop without adding a direction the tree did not grow.

Figure is a tree arguing. The shop’s job is to let the argument be seen, and to keep the door a rectangle while it talks.
