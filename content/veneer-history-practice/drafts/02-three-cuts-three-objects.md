A sawn leaf of walnut is a board that learned to be thin. You can still see the pores the way a plane would leave them. A sliced leaf of the same log is smoother, tighter, and a little more like a photograph of itself. A rotary leaf is a sheet that remembers being a cylinder. The figure goes cathedral or wild in a way no quartered board ever did, because the knife walked around the tree.

People say “veneer” as if those three were a thickness. They are three objects. If you glue the wrong one, you have not made a cheaper version of the right one. You have made a different door.

## Sawn

Before the slicer, veneer was sawn. A thin-kerf saw, a pit, later a frame, later a circular that wasted less than a pit but still wasted. The leaf is thick by modern standards — often 1/16, sometimes 1/20, old work thicker still. [VERIFY] a given piece with a caliper before you write a number on a repair ticket.

Sawn veneer sands. That is the gift and the danger. You have meat. You also have a belt that will still find the core if you are proud. Old hide-glue work is often sawn. You can feel it at a chip: the leaf has a little boardness. It will take a scraper. It will take a plane if you are careful and the grain agrees.

The saw marks, if they survived, are a document. A restorer who belts them off has erased the mill. I scrape. I leave the thickness I can.

Yield is the tax. Every kerf is a leaf you did not get. That is why sawn veneer became a specialty and a conservation preference, not a production default. It is also why a sawn leaf of crotch or burl is a serious purchase. You paid for the waste and for the fact that the figure was not stretched around a drum.

I use sawn when I am matching an old piece, when the customer wants a face that can be repaired in twenty years by someone with a scraper, and when the figure would look wrong stretched. I do not use it to save money. It does not.

## Sliced

A slicer is a giant plane. The flitch is cooked, dogged, and the knife takes a leaf the width of the log’s best face. Flat-sliced (plain-sliced) gives you the cathedral you know from a flat-sawn board. Quarter-sliced gives you the flake or the tight stripe, depending on the species and the angle. Rift sits between, the ray subdued, the stripe straight.

This is the furniture leaf. Architectural grade, sequence-matched, numbered on the back. The thickness in a modern bundle is often about 0.6 mm, a little more or less by mill and species. [VERIFY] the bundle in your hand; the stamp lies less than the catalog and more than the caliper.

Sliced veneer keeps the figure in the order the tree grew it. Leaf 17 is the neighbor of leaf 18. That is the whole matching game. Rotary cannot give you that neighbor in the same way, because rotary is a spiral, not a stack of pages.

The knife burnishes. Sliced oak can look tighter than sawn oak of the same tree. Finish will tell you. A dye that should have gone into an open pore sits on a smeared wall and looks like a stain mistake. I raise the grain lightly, or I wash, or I pick a finish that does not need the pore to drink. I do not pretend the leaf is a board.

Sliced is what I buy for doors, casework, a table that wants a picture and a climate. It is what I mean when I say “a flitch” without an adjective.

## Rotary

A rotary lathe turns the log and the knife peels a continuous sheet, the way a pencil sharpener peels a ribbon if the pencil were four feet across. Immanuel Nobel’s mill work in the mid-nineteenth century is the name the plywood histories keep reaching for. Yield is the point. You get almost the log. You get a sheet wide enough for a plywood face without a seam. You get the wild cathedral of rotary birch, the face that built a century of cabinets and a century of cheap doors.

Rotary figure is not quartered figure. It is not even flat-sawn figure. It is a spiral unrolled. Some of it is handsome. A lot of it is “oak” in a rental that is actually rotary birch printed or dyed. Crossband rotary is a structural layer, not a dining-table story. I will use rotary as a backer, as a crossband, as a paint-grade face, as a Baltic-birch cousin I trust in a drawer side. I will not sell a rotary oak print as quartered oak.

The leaf is also more likely to have lathe checks — small breaks from being bent off the log. They show under stain. They show under a film that goes in and does not come out the same. I look at rotary under raking light before I spend a dye on it.

## Which object for which job

A sunburst of crotch: sliced, from one flitch, numbered.  
A repair on an 1810 card table: sawn if you can find it, or sliced thicker than the architectural default, matched to the old meat.  
A plywood panel that must stay flat behind a paint: rotary, balanced, no apology.  
A quartered-oak door with flake: quarter-sliced, bookmatched, solid band.  
A “rustic oak” kitchen in a box store: often rotary or a printed cousin. I do not compete with that object by making a worse version of it. I make the sliced door and I say the word.

Thickness is not the identity. I have seen sliced leaves thick enough to scrape and rotary leaves so thin the paper was the structure. Ask how it was cut. Then ask what it will sit on. Then ask whether both faces exist.

## How I tell in the hand

I do not trust a catalog line that says “wood veneer.” I bend a corner. Sawn has a little board stiffness. Sliced flexes and still remembers the flitch as a stack of pages. Rotary often comes as a wider sheet, and the cathedral will not decide which way the tree ran. Under raking light the rotary checks show as hairlines. The sliced knife burnish shows as a dull ribbon. The sawn leaf, if anyone left the marks, shows a faint track I will not belt off.

I caliper three places. I look at the back for paper or fleece. I ask the seller how it was cut if the stamp is shy. If the seller does not know, I treat the sheet as rotary until the figure proves otherwise. That prejudice has saved me a dining top. It has also made me send back a handsome sliced flitch I should have trusted. I would rather lose a week than glue the wrong object and call it a cheaper version of the right one.

A helper who snaps a leaf like a bedsheet is learning the same lesson the hard way. Sawn may survive the snap. Sliced may split on a weak stripe. Rotary may tear along a lathe check you had not seen yet. I unroll on the bench, weights on the corners, and I do the telling before anyone waves the sheet in the air.

## The same log does not make the same door

If I could take one walnut log and saw a leaf, slice a leaf, and peel a leaf, I would hang them on one wall and stop arguing with catalogs. I cannot do that here. I keep three offcuts in a cigar box on the finish bench, labeled sawn / sliced / rotary, same species when I can get it, and I make a client hold them before we talk about a dining table.

The sawn piece has pores you can feel with a thumbnail. The sliced piece photographs cleaner. The rotary piece looks like a tree that was asked to be a map. People who hate the word veneer often hate the rotary map and think they hate the other two. I let them keep the sawn piece in their hand while I talk.

I mixed the cuts once on a pair of closet doors for a hall that wanted “the same oak.” One door was leftover quarter-sliced from a kitchen. The other was a rotary sheet I had as a paint-grade backer and decided, in a hurry, was close enough under a dark dye. The dye found the lathe checks. The hall got two different oaks and a photographer who was kinder than the light. I remade the rotary door. The leftover sliced flitch did not have enough neighbors left. I bought another bundle and I wrote *cut* on the folder in the same line as *species*.

## A card table and a kitchen door

The second object I keep in mind when I choose a cut is not a museum piece. It is a Federal-style card table in the shop with a sawn mahogany face that still takes a scraper, and a kitchen door I built last year in quarter-sliced white oak with rift stiles. The card table is a repair ticket: hide, hammer, a leaf with meat, a ground of pine that moves like old work. [VERIFY] the table’s date if anyone writes it on a tag; I have a style, not a bill of sale. The kitchen door is a climate ticket: sliced, sequenced, both faces, a core I would trust painted.

If I put the kitchen cut on the card table, I am thin in a place that will be scraped again. If I put the card-table cut on the kitchen door, I have spent sawn yield on a face that will live next to a dishwasher and never see a scraper. The glue does not care which story I told myself. The room will.

A third object sits in the same lesson: a Baltic-birch drawer box with a rotary face I am happy to leave honest. I do not stain that face to “oak.” I oil it or I paint it. Rotary as itself is a useful sheet. Rotary as a lie about quartered oak is how the word got a bad century.

## The mill and the county

A hardwood county can saw furniture stock for a hundred years and never own a slicer. Those are different machines, different cooks, different customers. [VERIFY] Bradley County plant lists before anyone writes that Warren sliced faces. What I can say without a file is that the oak that became furniture stock here is the same species that becomes a sliced face somewhere else. The tree does not care which knife.

If I ever put a slicer claim in a heritage paragraph, it will be because a ledger or a photograph showed the head, the dogs, the cooked flitch. Until then the county sawed boards and I buy leaves from mills that slice.

Three cuts. Three objects. The rest of the pack assumes you can tell them apart in the hand, because the glue does not care and the room will.
