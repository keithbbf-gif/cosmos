A yosegi box from Hakone looks like marquetry until you look at the edge. The pattern does not stop. It goes through. The maker glued a loaf of sticks — a geometric sausage of species — and then sliced it, the way a cook slices a jam roll, except the jam is walnut and white-bark and a yellow wood I will not misname. [VERIFY] the species palette against a Hakone craft text. The slice is a leaf. The loaf is the flitch, if a flitch were built instead of grown.

I put this essay in the ancient stage not because Hakone is pharaonic, but because this pack refuses a Europe-only memory. Patterned wood as a thin face is a human habit. Japan has a shop tradition that makes the habit obvious in the hand.

## Built figure

Most of my figure is grown: crotch, burl, flake. Yosegi figure is built. The “bookmatch” is a geometry that was decided in the glue-up. If the loaf is true, every slice is a sibling. That is sequence-matching as a carpenter’s choice, not as a tree’s accident.

I have glued scrap into a cutting board and sliced it and told myself I understood. I understood a party trick. Yosegi at the level of a named Hakone maker is a tolerance and a palette and a life. I will not pretend a weekend loaf is that.

What I will take: if you want a repeating geometric face, building a loaf and slicing it is more honest than printing a geometry on a sheet. The shop cost is time. The sales cost is that clients think they can buy the look as a vinyl.

## Tansu, fusuma, the other Japanese faces

Yosegi is not all of Japanese furniture. Tansu is hardware and a carcase and a wood that can be plain. Fusuma and screens can be paper. A lacquer ground can be a face that is not a leaf. I am not writing Japan in one essay. I am writing one method that rhymes with veneer so the pack does not sound like a Versailles tour.

Sawn and sliced leaves exist in Japanese joinery and in export furniture of the later centuries. The point is the loaf. Europe’s marquetry is usually a cartoon cut from leaves and assembled on a ground. Yosegi is assembled first, then cut. The order flips. The glue is still the joint.

## Sliding lids and the runner, again

A Hakone box with a sliding lid is a runner problem. The pattern is the sale. The lid is the furniture. I have bought a cheap export box whose lid bound in a wet week. The pattern was fine. The carcase was a souvenir.

When I tape a geometric match on a drawer, I still cut the drawer like a drawer. The Antwerp essay said it. The yosegi lid says it. Pattern does not excuse a bind.

## What I will not steal

I will not call a shop herringbone “yosegi.” Herringbone is a floor and a drawer end and a European word. Yosegi is a loaf tradition with a place name. If I build a loaf, I say shop loaf, species list, slice thickness. I do not hang a Japanese word on an Arkansas bench to make a photograph look traveled.

I will not use ivory or tortoise to “complete” a palette. The Hakone tradition has its own ethics and its own woods. Mine has oak and walnut and the law.

## Sequence, again

The loaf is a reminder that sequence can be manufactured. A flitch sequence is a gift of the slicer. A loaf sequence is a gift of the saw and the glue. Both let leaf 17 sit next to leaf 18. Both punish a shop that loses the numbers.

I number my slices. I number my flitch leaves. I have lost the numbers once and spent a day trying to re-stack a geometry that only looked random. It was not random. It was a loaf I had forgotten how to read.

## The weekend loaf that drifted

I glued a loaf of oak and walnut and a pale maple I had as offcuts, sticks squared in a hurry, and I sliced it on the bandsaw as if Hakone were a Saturday. The pattern drifted. One stick had been a hair proud. The glue lines were a river that wandered. Every slice was a sibling of a mistake. I used the better slices on a cutting board and I stopped calling the board anything Japanese.

The failure was useful. Built figure punishes a loose glue-up the way a flitch punishes a lost number. If the loaf is not true, every leaf inherits the lie. I now joint the sticks, I dry-clamp, I look down the length for a winding, and I glue in batches I can keep flat. I slice only after the loaf has sat in shop air with the future lid. A loaf that is still moving is a lid that will stain two colors: one at the glue line, one in the thirsty maple.

Hide will stick a loaf. PVA will stick a loaf. I have used both. Hide lets me open a bad joint and try again. PVA is a refusal to try again. For a shop loaf I am still learning, I use hide. For a loaf I trust, I use what the finish can stand. I will not flood. Starved end grain in a stick is a later crack that reads as a white river through the geometry.

## A tea box and a dresser that asked for Hakone

The second object is a small tea box with a sliding lid, shop loaf on the lid only, oak carcase, a runner I fitted in dry week and again after a wet one. The lid is the furniture. The pattern is the sale. I had bought a cheap export box years ago whose lid bound in August. The pattern was fine. The carcase was a souvenir. I kept that box on the shelf so I would not repeat it. The tea box I built still slides. I wax the runner. I do not let the loaf be the excuse for a bind.

A dresser client asked for that Japanese pattern on five drawer fronts. I showed them the tea box and a herringbone sample and a bookmatched walnut. I said shop loaf, species list, slice thickness. I did not say yosegi. They chose the walnut bookmatch, which was the right object for a bedroom that needed quiet. I was glad. Five matching loaf slices is a true loaf and a life I have not spent. A named Hakone maker’s tolerance is not a weekend inheritance.

Tansu hardware and a plain carcase are the other Japanese sentence I will actually use: the face can be quiet, the drawer can run, the iron can be honest without a geometry. Yosegi is one method that rhymes with veneer. It is not a costume for a Saline River dresser.

## How I slice without stealing the word

I mark the loaf with a face arrow and a sequence number before the first cut. I take slices thicker than a modern architectural leaf if I can — I want meat I can scrape — and I caliper. [VERIFY] any claim about traditional Hakone slice thickness before a caption; I have a shop saw, not a designated-craft workshop. I number the slices as they come off. I stack them in order, paper between, the same crate logic I use for a flitch.

End grain on a loaf slice is a finish problem. It drinks. I size. I test a cutoff. I do not flood a dye into maple and oak and expect one color. The geometry is a species map. If I stain it like a single board, I have erased the map.

The tea-box lid got oil, not a film that would sit white in the maple. I sanded the end grain with a block and I stopped before I dished a walnut stick. A loaf slice is a lot of arrises in a small rectangle. A hungry grit will make a relief map I did not glue.

This is the last ancient-stage essay. Egypt spent an import on a face. Rome spent a fortune on citrus. The Middle Ages hoarded width. Urbino drew a room. Antwerp built a portable city. Hakone built a loaf and sliced it. None of that is a kitchen catalog. All of it is the method.

The cabinet centuries come next: Dutch flowers, Boulle, the French ébéniste, English walnut, mahogany, Biedermeier, Phyfe, and the hide pot that made the faces stay. The history gets denser and the shop gets closer. The leaf does not change its job. The rooms get more expensive.
