A medieval chest is often a width. Three boards, four, a lid that is a plank, iron straps that admit the wood will move and the thief will try. The luxury is not a match. The luxury is that the tree gave you a board that wide and the sawyer did not waste it on a house you cannot see.

Veneer does not disappear in the Middle Ages. It gets quieter in the survivals, because the objects that lasted were oak boxes and church wood, and because a thin face is the first thing a damp century eats. What you can say without a fantasy is that a wide board was wealth, a joined panel was work, and a thin imported face was a court taste that the next essays will pick up in Italy and Antwerp.

## Boarded, clamped, joined

Victor Chinnery’s English furniture and Penelope Eames’s medieval furniture are the books I want an editor to keep on the table. [VERIFY] any specific chest against those pages or a catalog before a date goes in a caption. The types are shop types.

A boarded chest is planks and nails or pegs and a hope about movement. A clamped chest is a panel in a frame that lets the panel be a panel. A joined chest is mortise-and-tenon, a rectangle that can take a season. These are not veneer methods. They are the reason veneer had a job later: when you want a picture larger than a board, you either glue boards or you glue a leaf.

I build doors both ways. A glue-up of rift oak is a solid door. A flake leaf on a core is a picture of a glue-up that would have been a propeller. The medieval carpenter with a wide oak did not need the leaf. The medieval carpenter without a wide oak needed a frame and a panel. The court carpenter with an imported dark wood needed a knife and a glue.

## Churches and the other wood

Choir stalls, rood screens, a bishop’s chair: the Gothic carpenter could carve a forest on a stall end and still be looking at a shortage of clean width. Oak in northern Europe was the furniture wood and the ship wood and the house wood. Figured luxury woods were a Mediterranean and an eastern trade. When they appear, they appear thin, or as inlay, or as a treasure.

I do not turn a stall into a veneer sermon. Most of it is solid oak because oak was what you had. That is the opposite of Egypt’s ebony problem and a cousin of Arkansas’s oak plenty. A county that grows oak still veneers oak when the figure and the flatness want different things. A county that does not grow oak veneers what it can buy.

## Iron, damp, and what ate the faces

Iron straps on a chest are a climate device and a security device. They also stain. Tannin and iron are a black ink. A leaf on an oak chest in a damp keep is a leaf that may not be there when the keep becomes a museum. Survival bias again: we have the boarded oak. We have less of the thin imported face.

That is why people think veneer “starts” in the Renaissance. It does not start. It becomes documentable again when the objects are kept as art and the glue is hide and the rooms are slightly kinder. The medieval gap is a damp gap and a taste gap, not a missing idea.

## What I take to the bench

When a client wants a “medieval” chest in quartered oak, I ask whether they want width or a picture of width. A real wide board, dried and honest, is a beautiful object and a movement problem. A leaf of quartered oak on a lumber core is a picture and a rectangle. A glue-up of three boards with honest joints is the middle and often the right object.

I will not distress a leaf to look like a keep. Distressed veneer is a pale wound with a story. Distressed solid is a worn arris. They are not the same theater.

The iron hardware on a modern chest can be honest without pretending to be a parish chest. I oil the oak. I let the tannin be. I do not pour a vinegar-and-iron brew on a leaf unless I have tested a cutoff and accepted a blotch. [VERIFY] any house “ebonizing” recipe before it becomes a caption.

## Wealth, still

A wide walnut slab in a showroom is still wealth. A sequenced flitch of walnut is a different wealth: repeatable, matchable, flat. The medieval chest chose the first wealth because that was the furniture idea — a box from a tree. The later cabinet chose the second because the furniture idea became a picture on a box.

Both ideas are alive in a hardwood shop. I do not owe the century a side. I owe the object a decision. If the object is a bank for blankets, I think about the boarded chest. If the object is a face that has to stay flat in a dining room, I think about the leaf.

## The linen chest that split on the lid

I built a blanket chest from a wide white-oak lid I wanted too much. The board was honest, dried as well as a shop can dry a board, a width you still stop and look at in a county that grows oak. I framed the box as a joined chest — mortise-and-tenon, a rectangle — and I left the lid as a single plank because the client wanted wealth they could measure with a tape. The lid split on the heart side after a winter of heat. The iron strap I had put on as a “medieval note” stained a black tannin line across the crack.

I inlaid a butterfly, I eased the strap, and I wrote the date inside. The chest still holds blankets. The lid is now two boards and a butterfly that admits the tree did not owe me a panel. If I had started with a clamped lid — a panel in a frame — I would have had movement I could live with and less theater. If I had started with a quartered-oak leaf on a lumber core, I would have had a picture of width and a rectangle. I chose the first wealth and I paid the medieval tax.

The iron stain is a shop fact. Tannin and iron are a black ink. I do not pour a vinegar-and-iron brew on a leaf unless I have tested a cutoff and accepted a blotch. [VERIFY] any house ebonizing recipe before it becomes a caption. On that lid the stain was an accident I now expect whenever iron and oak share a damp night.

## A parish chest I will not reproduce as theater

The second object is a photograph of a boarded chest in a museum catalog — oak, iron, a lock, a width. [VERIFY] accession and date before any caption uses a collection name. A client wanted that photograph, distressed, for a mudroom. I offered a joined chest in local oak, honest hardware, no fake worm, no beaten leaf. They took it. The mudroom chest is a bank for boots. It is not a keep.

I oiled the mudroom oak and I let the tannin be. I will not belt a fake wear-through on a quartered face and call it age.

Choir stalls and rood screens taught me the other half of this. Most Gothic carpenter-work is solid oak because oak was what you had. That is the opposite of Egypt’s ebony problem and a cousin of Arkansas’s oak plenty. A county that grows oak still veneers oak when the figure and the flatness want different things. A stall end carved into a forest is not a veneer sermon. It is a shortage of clean width spent on a picture that is still a board.

## Width on stickers, width on a core

When a wide board comes into this shop I sticker it, off the slab, and I give it time I will not invent as a number of days. I meter it. I do not invent a percentage as religion. [VERIFY] house targets before anyone prints a schedule. I watch for cup. I decide whether the object wants that board or a picture of that board.

A leaf of the same oak, sequenced, is a different drying problem. The leaf fools a pin meter. The core is the meter I trust. I store the flitch in the same air as the core. I do not treat a sliced bundle as if it were a medieval plank. The wealth is repeatable now. The medieval chest chose a box from a tree because that was the furniture idea. I can still choose that idea. I can also choose the later idea: a picture on a box that has to stay flat in a dining room with July and January.

The next essay is the studiolo, where the wall becomes a picture of wood so complete that the wealth is no longer a board at all. It is a trick you can walk into. The Middle Ages hoarded width. Urbino spent width on an illusion.
