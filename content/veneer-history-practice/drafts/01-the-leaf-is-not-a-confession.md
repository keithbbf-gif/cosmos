I unroll a leaf of quartered white oak and the flake repeats, a bookmatch that would have taken two perfect solid boards and a winter they both agreed to. The core is plywood I trust, or a lumber core I have used before, or an MDF I will only use where the piece can stand MDF. The face is the oak. The joint at the edge is a band of solid so a hip does not meet a paper-thin arris.

That is veneer. It is not a confession.

The word has a bad century in the public mouth because cheap print and cheap peel taught people to hate it. A kitchen catalog in 1978 could say “oak” and mean a photograph of oak glued to a box that drank a splash and let go. Furniture shops used veneer for four thousand years because a crotch is a gift and a slab of crotch is a split. I use it when the face wants to be one thought and the structure wants to be another. I do not use it to lie about a particleboard box unless the box is an honest painted box and the veneer is not invited.

## What the leaf is doing

A leaf is a slice of a tree thin enough that the figure can be repeated, matched, and spent on a surface instead of on a propeller. Quartered oak as a two-inch slab will move. Quartered oak as a 1/42-inch face on a balanced core will still move a little, but the core takes the climate argument and the flake stays a flake.

Burl as a solid is a bowl, a clock, a thing you turn and hope. Burl as a leaf is a door, a drawer front, a table that can stay a rectangle. Crotch mahogany as a dining top in the solid is a beautiful problem. Crotch mahogany as a matched pair of leaves is how a lot of the eighteenth century ate dinner without the top becoming a trough.

The leaf is not weaker wood. It is the same wood, thinner, asked to do a different job. The job is appearance and match. The job of staying flat belongs to the core. If you ask the leaf to be the structure, you have built a card.

## The apology and where it came from

People who say “I don’t want veneer” usually mean they do not want a lie. They have a right to that. They have seen a hotel nightstand whose edge band peeled in a humid week and showed a biscuit-colored core. They have seen a “solid oak” tag on a top that was a photograph. They have sanded a desk and gone through the face in one pass of 80.

That is not the history. That is a sales channel and a thickness.

Egyptian carpenters glued thin boards of better wood over poorer cores. Roman tables wore *crustae* of citrus that cost more than the house. Dutch floral marquetry is veneer. Boulle is veneer. Riesener is veneer. Phyfe’s best parlor work is veneer. The Thonet chair is bent wood; the Eames lounge is veneer that learned to be a shell. A mid-century Danish teak sideboard that is still flat is often a leaf on a stable panel, and that is why it is still a rectangle.

The apology is newer than the method. It arrived when the factory learned to print a photograph of a flitch and call the photograph a species. It arrived when the leaf got thin enough that a belt sander could erase it before lunch. It arrived when the edge was a tape you ironed on in a closet.

I will not defend those objects. I will not apologize for a bookmatched oak door with a solid band and a note on the back that says both faces, date, glue.

## Honesty in the room

I say veneer when it is veneer. I say solid when it is solid. A mixed piece — solid legs, veneered panel — is a lot of good furniture. The customer can hear that in one sentence. If the sales channel cannot say the sentence, the sales channel is the problem.

A “solid oak” tag on a veneered top is a problem I will not help with. A “quartered oak face, stable core” is a sentence I will stand next to.

Some rooms want solid and should have it. A cutting surface. A child’s desk edge that will be kicked. A workbench. A stair tread. A porch table that lives in weather. Those are not leaf jobs. A reception desk that would be a ton in solid and a movement circus is a leaf job. A door that must stay flat in a house with July and January is a leaf job. A sunburst that would take a whole crotch and still split is a leaf job.

The judgment is not moral. It is which argument you want the object to win.

## What this pack is for

The next essays will tell you how the leaf was cut, what it sits on, and why a one-sided panel cups on a wet week. Then the history, because the apology is historically illiterate. Then the flitch, because matching is the work. Then the glue and the hammer and the bag. Then the blister you will get anyway.

I am writing from a hardwood shop in a county that cut oak for a long time. Veneer does not make us less of a hardwood shop. A mill that sliced oak for faces would be in the same family as a mill that sawed oak for solids. [VERIFY] any historic slicing claim before a brochure; I do not have a plant record of veneer slicers in my hand.

What I have in my hand is a leaf that lets me put rift stiles and a flake panel in one door without building a propeller. The door stays a rectangle. The flake stays a flake. The hip meets a band. That is enough craft for the word to stop being an apology.

## The first test

Unroll a leaf. Look at both faces. One face will be tighter; one will show more of the knife. Feel the edge. If it powders, it is too dry. If it limp-rags, it has taken shop air and will grow when you glue it. Smell it. Oak smells like oak. A paper-backed birch smells like paper and a warehouse.

Then look at the core you meant to put it on. If the core is a price, the leaf is a costume. If the core is a panel you would trust painted, the leaf can be a face.

When the press releases, listen. A crackle can be a bond that is not a bond. A quiet panel is a quiet panel. Knock it. Look at the seams. Tape a note to the back: both faces, date, glue. The next person, sanding, might read it before they reach for 80. I hope they read it. I still sand as if they will not.

That is the whole series, if you want it in one motion: the leaf is a face, the core is the furniture, the word is older than the shame, and the hip meets a band. The rest is how not to waste a flitch you cannot replace.
