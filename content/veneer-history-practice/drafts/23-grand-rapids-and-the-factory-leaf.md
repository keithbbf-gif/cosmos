A Grand Rapids catalog page is a drawing of a bedroom that could be shipped. The city became a common noun for American factory furniture: carved-enough, mahogany-enough, a suite, a price. Some of those shops were serious about leaves and cores. Some of them put a photograph of a flitch on a box and taught a century to hate the word. I will not sort every company in this essay. I will sort the habit.

Scale is not a sin. A press that does ten doors is a tool I want. A sales tag that says solid on a leaf is a sin I will not help with. Grand Rapids is where those two facts shared a rail yard.

## A catalog is a flitch you cannot hold

The Director was a plate. The Grand Rapids catalog is a plate plus a price plus a shipping crate. The client in Kansas ordered a drawing. The factory pulled a grade of leaf, not a numbered flitch the client had seen. Sequence-matching at scale becomes a grade-matching. That can still be handsome. It is not one thought from one tree unless the line paid for that.

I sell a door from a flitch I can show. I also understand a factory that cannot show every client a crate. The honesty is in the tag, not in the tour.

## Carving and the face

Factory carving on a veneered case is a place the leaf dies. A deep relief through a 1/42 face is a core showing, then a fill, then a stain. Some factories carved solids and faced flats. Some carved the idea of carving into a composition that was already a picture. I look at the edge of a carved “leaf” on a revival suite. If the edge is a gray core, I know the religion.

When I want a carved line on a faced door, I carve a solid band or a solid appliqué. I do not ask a business card of oak to be a Gothic plate.

## Arkansas is not Michigan

Warren and Wilmar have their own mill and furniture-stock story. [VERIFY] dates and companies on a heritage page; do not borrow Grand Rapids as a costume. A Southern mill town that cut oak for a market is a cousin of a Northern factory city that assembled suites. Cousin is not twin. I will not write “the Grand Rapids of the South” unless a primary source used the phrase and I want to argue with it.

What I take from the cousin: catalogs lie or they don’t. Presses scale or they don’t. Leaves are graded or sequenced. Those choices exist in any county with a sander.

## The suite and the match

A bedroom suite that matches is a factory flex: same grade, same stain, same hardware. A dining set that matches from one flitch is a shop flex. I have done a set from one crate and I have done a set from a grade. I tell the client which. If they want the crate, they pay for the crate. If they want the grade, they get a handsome cousin, not a sibling.

Grand Rapids taught America the suite. The suite taught America to expect match without understanding flitch. That is not evil. It is a translation loss. I translate back when it matters.

## The ranch-house dresser

I repaired a revival dresser in a ranch house south of town: mahogany-enough face, a carved crest, cabriole knees that were a composition, a softwood carcase that had been eaten in the feet. The owner called it Chippendale. The tag, if it had ever had one, was gone. The edge of the carved “leaf” on the crest was a gray core. The factory had carved the idea of carving into a picture that was already a leaf. Then someone had belted the top and gone through at the front arris.

I did not try to make it 1754. I consolidated the feet with a solid I could name, I banded the top arris with a mahogany I could invoice, and I left the crest as a document of a factory religion. A new leaf on that crest would have been a lie about a carving that was never meat. The dresser is a second object next to the catalog page. It is Grand Rapids or a cousin city — [VERIFY] before a caption names a company — and it is still in the house. The sentence I wrote on the back of the new band is the year I worked, the glue, and the word *repair*. I did not write Chippendale.

Scale is not what failed. The tag failed, and the belt failed, and the idea that a 1/42 face can be a Gothic plate failed. A press that had done that top honestly would have been a tool I want. The sales weather around the top is the weather I will not help with.

## Grade on a press, flitch on a bench

I have a bag and a small press. I can do a run of doors. That is scale enough to teach me what a factory knew: you pull a grade, you keep a schedule, you do not walk every client through a crate. Honesty then lives in the tag. I can also do what a factory often would not: show the flitch, number the doors, keep the leftover pages.

When a client wants a bedroom suite that matches, I ask grade or crate. Grade is a handsome cousin. Crate is siblings. If they want the crate, we buy the book and we spend it in a planned order — headboard, dresser, nightstands — not prettiest-first. If they want the grade, I stain a sample set and I refuse to promise a cathedral that walks from piece to piece. Grand Rapids taught the suite as a stain and a hardware package. I can do that package. I will say it is a package.

I have pulled three “matching” doors from a grade bundle and found three different pores under the same invoice word. The press would have hidden that under a toner. I showed the doors in daylight and we picked a pair and a cousin. The cousin became a linen closet. The pair became the room. That is a factory lesson run backward: sort before you finish.

## The headboard that was a picture of a carving

A factory headboard arrived in the shop as a favor: walnut stain, a relief of flowers, a panel that wanted to be a Dutch idea and a Grand Rapids price. The flowers were a composition pressed or carved through a face that was already thin. At the high points the core showed the color of oatmeal. I was asked to “bring the walnut back.” There was no walnut to bring back. There was a picture of a carving on a picture of a flitch.

I declined the belt. I cleaned what would clean. I filled nothing that wanted to be a petal. I told the owner the headboard was a document of a line, and that a new sequenced walnut panel would be a different object. They kept the document. I kept my belt on the hook. That refusal is the other factory lesson. Scale can be honest. Scale can also spend a leaf as a poster. I will press a poster if the brief is a painted closet. I will not press a poster and say flitch.

Warren and Wilmar remain a cousin story: furniture stock, oak, a mill town, not a Michigan rail yard. [VERIFY] dates and companies on a heritage page. What I will write is that catalogs lie or they don’t, in any county with a sander.

## What I take

I take the press as a tool. I take the catalog as a warning. I take the suite as a conversation about grade versus flitch.

I leave the carved-through leaf. I leave the solid tag on a faced top. I leave the habit of using Grand Rapids as a sneer or as a halo. It was a city of shops. Some were better than others. Mine has to be better than my last door.

Art Deco is next: the factory leaf and the luxury leaf both go geometric, exotic, a sunburst that is a flitch spent like a firework. Grand Rapids could do a Deco suite. A Paris shop could do a Deco cabinet. The word veneer still covers both. The tag has to do the rest.
