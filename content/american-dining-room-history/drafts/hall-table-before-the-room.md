# The hall table before the room

A New England hall in 1710 is a machine with a hearth. It cooks, hears cases, holds a loom, sleeps a child on a truckle, and twice a day becomes a dining room that does not keep the name. The table that makes that conversion possible is usually a gateleg or an oval drop-leaf in maple, oak, or walnut, sometimes pine painted. When the leaves hang, the piece is a narrow console against the wall. When the gates swing out, it is dinner. Afterward the cloth is folded, the trenchers stacked, and the hall goes back to being a hall.

This is the furniture of eating before the room. It is not primitive. It is a different contract with square footage. Most colonial houses could not spare a chamber that earned its keep only at two or three in the afternoon. The table had to earn its keep the other twenty hours as well.

## What “hall” meant

In English usage the hall is the principal room of a vernacular house, not a corridor. American inventories of the early eighteenth century still talk that way: hall, parlor, chamber. The parlor, if there is one, is the better room — better chairs, a looking glass, the tea table. Dinner might happen there on a Sunday or when a minister calls. Everyday eating stays near the fire. A separate kitchen, when it exists, is often a lean-to or an outbuilding. Food travels. The table does not travel far. It unfolds where people already are.

Winterthur’s early rooms and the house museums that copy them sometimes over-set the hall: too much pewter, too many chairs in a row, a table left open as if the family never did anything else. The honest picture is messier. A leaf down is the default. Open is an event. If you are looking at a surviving gateleg and both leaves are always displayed, you are looking at a museum habit, not a 1720 habit.

Urban houses complicate the picture sooner. A Boston or Philadelphia townhouse of 1740 may already have a back room used mainly for meals, especially if the household is mercantile and the front parlor is for callers. That room is still not always called a dining room in the documents. Berman’s later inventory work starts in 1794 for a reason. The name trails the use. The furniture of 1700–1760 is mostly still dual-use: tables that fold, chairs that migrate, a cupboard that holds both food and the good earthenware.

## Forms that fold

The gateleg is the English inheritance. Two gates — framed legs hinged to the stretcher — swing out to hold leaves that hang on rule joints. The rule joint is a molded meeting of leaf and top that sheds crumbs and, when it is cut well, looks like a single board from above. American shops cut it in maple and cherry as readily as London shops cut it in oak. The underside tells the truth: pintles, wear on the gate’s inner stile, a patch where a child kicked the hanging leaf.

Drop-leaf tables with swing legs rather than full gates are cousins. So are oval tables whose leaves are supported by lopers — sliding rails — instead of gates. Butterfly tables, with wing-shaped supports pivoting from the stretcher, are a New England specialty the market loves and the documents thin out on. Trestle tables exist, especially in institutional and very plain houses: a board on a frame, sometimes knockdown. They photograph as “medieval” and are often later or agricultural. A Shaker trestle is a different century and a different essay. Do not drag a 1710 farm board into that purity.

Chair tables — a top that tips to become a back — turn a seat into a table. They are real. They are also over-reproduced. A chair-table in a hall is a space argument: one object, two jobs. It is not the high-style story.

## Woods and the local forest

Early eighteenth-century American dining surfaces are local first. Eastern white pine for tops that will be scrubbed. Maple for turned legs that take a bead. Oak where it is still the structural habit. Walnut where the household can show it. Mahogany is not yet the default; Bowett’s English mahogany story after the 1721 Naval Stores Act is a London clock. Coastal American shops feel that clock later, and inland shops may never feel it. A “colonial dining table” in Cuban mahogany with a 1705 date on a dealer tag wants an argument.

Secondary woods — the rails you do not see — are the attribution tool. Pine in New England. Tulip poplar in the Mid-Atlantic. Oak or pine in the Chesapeake. Cypress appears as you go south. Hurst and Prown’s Colonial Williamsburg southern furniture catalog is the book for that geography. A hall table with poplar rails and a maple top is a Mid-Atlantic sentence even if someone later stained it “Pilgrim oak.”

Paint is not a confession of poverty. A red or black painted frame with a scrubbed top is a finished object. Later strippers who take a hall table to bare maple “to show the wood” are making a twentieth-century taste. The Colonial Revival dining draft returns to that vandalism.

## Who sits, who serves

A hall meal is not a Federal dinner. People may eat in shifts. Children may stand. Enslaved workers in the Chesapeake and the Carolinas eat elsewhere, later, worse, or not in the documents at all. Indentured servants in Pennsylvania eat in the kitchen if there is one. The hall table is a household hierarchy made visible: who gets a back to their chair, who gets a trencher, who gets the pewter.

Silver is rare on this table. Pewter, wood, and earthenware do the work. A court cupboard or a dresser holds the display. The sideboard has not been invented for this room. A slab table with a stone top — marble if you are Charleston, soapstone or nothing if you are not — can hold a keg or a joint. That slab is an ancestor of the sideboard table MESDA keeps from Charleston (object 3163, 1750–1765), which already implies servants mixing drinks while guests sit. The hall of 1710 is earlier than that implication, or poorer, or both.

Women set and clear. That is not a sentimental sentence. It is the labor the inventories hide when they list “1 oval table” and do not list the person who unfolded it. The dining-room draft for enslaved labor takes the later, more specialized service. Here the work is still mixed with cooking and watching the fire.

## Joint stools, settles, and who gets a back

Seating at a hall table is mixed long after a merchant can buy a matching set. Joint stools — boarded or framed, four turned or square legs, a slab seat — slide under the leaf when the meal is over. They photograph as medieval and are ordinary. A settle by the fire is a bench with a back that is also a draft stop; it does not move to the table as easily as a stool. Chairs, when they exist, go to the heads or to the guests.

Rush-bottom and splint-bottom chairs are the country volume. Leather chairs, if the household has them, are the better seat and the one that gets listed. Caned high-backs belong to the William and Mary hour and to parlors more than to everyday halls; they break, and a hall is a hard room. A reconstructed 1710 hall with six matching leather chairs in a row is a museum deciding to be legible. The inventory is usually “chairs” and a number, not a suite.

Look at the stretchers on the stools. They wear on the top where feet rest, not only on the floor. A stool with mint stretchers and a scrubbed table is a mixed marriage or a mixed restoration. Fairbanks and Bates, in the long survey *American Furniture, 1620 to the Present*, keep stools in the dining story instead of treating them as prelude. That is the right instinct. A hall meal that cannot find enough backs is still a meal.

## The cupboard wall

Before the sideboard there is a wall of storage that is also display. Court cupboards, press cupboards, and dressers hold pewter, earthenware, and the cloth. The show face may be oak or walnut; the interior is the local secondary. A dresser with a pot-board below and a plate rack above is a working wall. It is not a sideboard. It has no bottle wells, no lockable plate drawer in the Federal sense, and no obligation to stand in a room that is only for dinner.

The cupboard stays when the table folds. The pewter comes down and goes back up. A cupboard that still has spoon scratches in a drawer is a better document than a cupboard that has been waxed into a collector’s object.

Southern houses will grow the slab table — MESDA 3163 is the later, elite version — as a standing-service wall. New England halls keep the dresser longer. The two walls are not the same furniture. They solve the same problem: dinner produces objects that have to live somewhere when the leaf comes down. If you are in a house museum, look at what the wall holds when the table is folded. If the wall is empty and the table is open, you are looking at a photograph, not a day.

## How to look at a survivor

Stand the table up and walk around it with the leaves down. Does it become a decent narrow table against a wall, or an awkward stump? A good gateleg has a job in both positions. Look at the feet. Ball feet and trumpet turnings say William and Mary. Pad feet and a cabriole say Queen Anne and a later hour. Straight turned legs with a plain stretcher can be anytime a shop was cheap.

Look at the hinges. Hand-wrought straps and a slightly drunken pin are earlier than stamped butts. Look at the rule joint: a clean cove and bead, or a later carpenter’s approximation. Look at wear. If the only wear is on the show face and the gates are mint, someone restored it for a collector who never folds it.

The hall table is not a failed dining room. It is the reason most Americans ate together at all. When Biddle letters a dining room in 1805, he is describing a house that can afford to stop folding. This series starts here so that later mahogany pedestals have something to be late against.
