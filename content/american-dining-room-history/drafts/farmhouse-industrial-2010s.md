# Farmhouse and industrial tables

The 2010s dining table wanted to look like work. A thick pine top, a X-trestle or a black steel base, a distressed finish that was applied last week, a bench on one side and mixed chairs on the other. Restoration Hardware and a thousand Etsy and Wayfair cousins sold it. Open-plan kitchens wanted a table that photographed with a bowl of lemons. The hall trestle and the Shaker board and the factory base were all raided. The result is a style, not a farm.

A later fashion brief asked for country house, not “farmhouse content.” This page is the content: what it took from history and what it invented.

## A Revival with a camera

Every Revival in this series edits a past for a room that past never saw. 1876 edited 1720. 1925 edited Nutting’s photograph. 1974 edited television’s grandmother. 2014 edited a warehouse and a feed of other people’s kitchens. The camera is the new pattern book. A table that does not photograph from the sofa is a failed table, even if it holds dinner.

RH, Pottery Barn, West Elm, Magnolia’s later retail, and the marketplace sites are the volume names. Cite a year if you name a SKU. This page will not invent one, and it will not invent a price. The type is stable across brands: a thick top, a trestle or a steel frame, a finish that is gray or brown-gray or a washed oak, a bench. Joanna Gaines’s television rooms (the mid-2010s) taught a national audience the same photograph the catalogs were already selling. Television and retail is the Nutting-and-factory pair again.

The open-plan essay takes the wall that disappeared. Here: the furniture that filled the hole. A mahogany D-end looks wrong in a great room. A gray trestle looks styled. That styling is a job. Someone — a brand, an algorithm, a couple on a Saturday — chose the look of labor over the look of Hepplewhite. American dining keeps choosing.

## Distressed as a finish

The 1720 hall table was worn because it was used. The 2014 table is worn because a chain hit it in a warehouse or a shop applied a gray wash. Both have texture. Only one has a date in the wear. Look at the underside. A farm table that worked is dirty in the pin holes and clean on a later repair. An industrial-farm table is evenly dirty, or dirty only on the show edges.

Pine is soft. The USDA *Wood Handbook* will give you the numbers; the dining-room fact is a knife mark in a month of real use, which then makes the fake distress honest. That accidental honesty is why some of these tables will be documents in 2070. Use is a finish. A pine top that was gray-washed in 2014 and then lived through ten years of homework will have two layers of history. Keep both. Do not sand to “start over” unless you want a 2026 object.

Oak in this style is often flatsawn red oak stained gray, or a veneer over a constructed core that wants to be a slab. Quartersawn white oak — the Mission fleck — is rarer in the marketplace version because the fleck fights the wash. When you find it, you are closer to Stickley than to the lemon-bowl photograph. Walnut appears as a “refined farmhouse” top: darker, less gray, still a trestle. The woods essay takes the species. Here the species is wearing a costume.

Painted tops — white, cream, a pale gray on the base and wood on the top — are the 1970s maple suite after a weekend. The country-revival essay already owned that maple. This hour paints it and calls the result farmhouse. Look at the brush or the spray. Look at the chips at the feet. Look at whether the paint goes under the top (a shop job) or stops at the show faces (a house job).

## Steel bases and the salvage story

A cast-iron sewing-machine base under a wood top is a twentieth-century salvage idea. A new steel trestle made to look like salvage is a 2010s product. Both put metal in a dining room that American high style had reserved for casters and mounts. The dinette already did chrome. This is black and heavier. The slum of legs becomes a slum of one welded frame. Knees are happier than with four fat oak posts.

Look at the weld. A clean mill weld with a powder coat is new. A pitted cast iron with remnants of a maker’s mark is older, or is trying to be. Sewing-machine bases (Singer and peers) have a literature; a base with a ground-off badge and a fresh top is a marriage. Marriages can be good tables. They are not “reclaimed factory tables” unless the top is also a factory.

Reclaimed factory maple and oak — real mill floors — are a different and better object when the provenance is real. They are also a woods-and-conservation story (nails, finish, what the floor lived, the holes you fill or do not). Do not call every gray oak top reclaimed. Most are new oak stained gray. A floorboard top will show nail patterns, a wear that is traffic, a thickness that was a floor. A new oak top will show a planer, a consistent thickness, a stain in the pores that is too even.

Hairpin legs — the mid-century wire V — return as an industrial cousin, usually too light for a thick top. They bend. They rack. They are honest about being cheap. A hairpin under a two-inch pine slab is a mismatch you can feel when someone leans.

## The bench and mixed seating

Shaker and banquet benches return as a photo. Mixed seating — a bench and two Windsors and a folding chair — is actually closer to 1740 than the matched suite of 1974. The 2010s got that part right, sometimes by accident. A bench is hard for older hips. The photo does not care.

Look at the bench height against the table. Dining height is stubborn. A bench that is a coffee-table leftover, or a garden bench, will put diners at the wrong rib. A cushion on a bench is a confession that the photograph hurt. Keep the cushion. The hall already knew a pad.

Windsor copies, metal tole chairs, a slipcovered host chair at the end — the mix is the style. A matched set of five-splat oak chairs around a gray trestle is a household that bought two catalog pages and did not get the mix memo. Both are documents.

## What it took from Shaker and Stickley

The Shaker trestle: a board, a shoe, a stretcher you find with your feet, no carving. The 2010s X-trestle is a cousin that wants more drawing. The X is a picture from the sofa. The Shaker shoe is a joint. Look at the connection of upright to shoe. A through-tenon or a keyed joint is a shop. A hanger bolt into a blind hole is a factory or a weekend. Both hold dinner. Only one is the ancestor’s grammar.

Stickley’s no. 445 bungalow trestle and the two-inch plank of no. 631 are the other ancestors. The 2010s table stole the thickness and not the fume. It stole the medial stretcher sometimes, and made it a decorative iron rod. Fumed oak is ammonia and tannin. Gray wash is pigment. A scratch will tell you.

Do not call the marketplace table Shaker or Stickley. Call it a Revival that knows those names from a mood board.

## How to look

Get on the floor. The underside of a 2014 farmhouse table is often clean plywood or a consistent stain, pocket screws, a metal plate, a brand stencil. The underside of a working farm table is dirt, a repair, a different secondary wood, a nail that does not match. Pocket screws are not a moral failure. They are a date.

Check the top’s edges for a router radius that is the same on all four sides. Handwork is less even. Check the ends for breadboards. A breadboard that is pinned and slotted is a shop that understands movement. A breadboard that is glued tight across a wide pine top will split, and then the split will be sold as character.

If you make tables in a Southern hardwood shop, this trend is a customer who may want oak and a trestle and a color that is not oak’s. You can build the form without the wash. You can refuse the wash. Both are answers. The historical point is that 2010s “farmhouse” is a Revival as surely as 1876: a past edited for a room that past never saw. The hall table folded. This one never folds. It is a resident in a room that is not a dining room. That contradiction is the style.

A twelve-foot gray trestle in a great room is a claim that dinner still has a place even without a door. A small gateleg in the same room is a refusal of the always-on table. Both are American. The lemon bowl is optional.
