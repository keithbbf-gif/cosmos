# Prairie School at table

Frank Lloyd Wright’s dining chairs are tall on purpose. They make a room inside the room: a fence of slats around the table so that dinner is an enclosure, not a floating suite in a box. The table itself is often built-in or aligned to a grid, oak, a low lamp or a ceiling fixture that is part of the same drawing. This is architecture that happens to hold plates. It is the most designed American dining of the century, and the least typical. Most Prairie-influenced dining rooms are a square oak table and some high-back chairs from a catalog that saw a photograph.

Wright’s early dining rooms — the Robie House the public one, the Dana-Thomas and others the fuller stories — are the citations. ## Oak Park first, then Robie

The dining table and eight high-back chairs Wright designed for his own Oak Park house, about 1895, are the earlier fence. The Frank Lloyd Wright Trust calls that suite revolutionary for its verticality and simplicity: an intimate secondary space, the table shielded by the chair backs. Robie (Frederick C. Robie House, Chicago, 1908–10) repeats the idea and then ties it to light. The table, modeled on the Oak Park design, has wood piers at the corners mounted with electrified fixtures of leaded glass. The 1911 interior photograph — high, flat chair backs around a rectangular table, lamps at the corners — is the image this hour owns. The lamps pull attention to the center of the cloth. Side conversations are architecturally discouraged. That is a dining-room politics as much as a fixture schedule.

HABS and later photographs show the chairs as a fence. Wikipedia and the tour scripts say “room within a room.” The phrase is accurate enough if you sit. The backs cut off the living room, the windows, the rest of the prairie plan. Federal dining opened to a parlor. Gilded Age dining opened to a pantry. Prairie dining opens upward to a fixture and sideways to nothing. You eat inside a piece of furniture that is also a room.

The Robie table was expandable. Leaves exist in the Prairie dining room. Do not make Wright a Mission plank by accident. The difference is not that the table never grew. The difference is that when it sat closed, the chairs still made a room.

## A grid, not a suite

Stickley’s dining suite can move to another bungalow. Wright’s dining furniture often cannot without losing the room. Built-in benches, radiators under windows, art glass that matches a table lamp — the meal is site-specific. That is a break with Hepplewhite, Grand Rapids, and even Eastwood. It is also why so little of this furniture is in dining rooms that were not Wright’s. Museums have chairs. Houses have the rest.

George Mann Niedecken and other interior collaborators complicate the “Wright designed every slat” story. Shop drawings, millwork firms, and later restorations all stand between a 1910 dinner and the chair you cannot sit on behind a rope. Say so.

Other Prairie architects (Mahony, Griffin, Purcell and Elmslie) designed tables. A dining-room series that only says Wright is a celebrity series. This slug uses him as the type specimen and leaves room for the others. A Purcell and Elmslie high-back is not a Wright copy. It is the same conversation.

## Niedecken and the shop drawing

Hoffmann and Connors put the Niedecken-Walbridge Co. on the Robie furniture about 1909–10. George Mann Niedecken, Milwaukee, muralist and interior architect, worked with Wright from 1904 into the late 1910s: Coonley, Robie, and others. A Canadian Centre for Architecture photograph of the Robie dining room is stamped, on the mount, “PROPERTY OF / NIEDECKEN - WALBRIDGE CO. / INTERIOR ARCHITECTS,” Milwaukee. That stamp is the document. The chairs are oak. The company built them. Wright’s name is on the house. The slats went through a shop.

Restorations replace leather, refinish oak, rebuild a lamp that was lost. A chair behind a rope in a house museum is a reconstruction even when the wood is old. Say which. The Cassina reissue (model 601 Robie, 1986) is a later product: cherry or walnut, foam, a dining chair for a collector who does not live in Hyde Park. It is not the 1910 dinner. Do not use a Cassina dimension as a Robie dimension. The restoration report is the pin we do not yet have on this page.

Dana-Thomas in Springfield, and the other early houses, have their own tables. Pin each to a monograph. The type is the fence. The specimen is the house.

Art glass in the Robie dining room is not a garnish. Wall sconces of leaded glass, oak, and bronze, and the table lamps on the corner piers, share a palette with the windows. The Trust’s furniture notes treat that match as the point: light, wood, and chair back as one drawing. A factory Prairie chair next to a store-bought dome fixture is not that drawing. It is a slat-back under a different ceiling. Built-in benches along a window wall, radiators under the glass, a carpet Wright specified or an Austrian import the house histories mention — those are dining furniture if you eat with them. They do not leave when the family does. That is why museums own chairs and houses keep the rest.

## Oak, again

Quartersawn oak, dark stain, sometimes a gold or a green in the room, not the red of Empire mahogany. The woods are Midwestern. The labor is millwork plus a famous architect. Clients are businessmen, not planters, not Newport. The servant, if present, uses a pantry Wright also drew. The Robie House plan is a dining-room plan: the room is a terminal, not a passage. That terminal is the furniture’s condition.

Chairs are uncomfortable if you expected a club chair. They are correct if you expected a choir stall that has learned a grid. People complained. Wright did not recant. The dining room is not the lounge.

Marion Mahony’s drawings and the Griffin work in Australia pull the Prairie dining room out of Chicago. Purcell and Elmslie’s Minnesota and Midwestern houses give high-backs that share the slat and refuse Wright’s celebrity. Look at the proportion of the back to the seat before you say “Wright.” A factory Prairie chair that is too short to enclose is not a failed Robie. It is a catalog that saw a photograph and kept the customer’s ceiling in mind.

Mahony’s presentation drawings taught the fence as an image before most Americans could visit a house. A high-back in ink is easier to copy than a lamp built into a table pier. That is why the catalog echo is a chair, not a room. Griffin’s later work abroad is a reminder that Prairie dining is a Midwest conversation that traveled, not a single Chicago trademark. If you only have Wright on this page you have the type specimen. You do not have the school.

## Factory Prairie

Grand Rapids and smaller factories sold “Mission” and “Prairie” high-backs that are too short to enclose and too thin to be millwork. They are fine chairs. They are not a Wright dining room. A suburban 1915 house with a square pedestal oak table and six slat-backs is the American echo, cousin to the Mission bungalow suite. Cross-link Stickley. Do not upgrade the suite with a famous name.

The factory chair often has a slat-back and a leatherette seat and a stain that is a color. It does not have a lamp built into the table. It does not have art glass that matches a sconce. It can move. That movability is why it survived in dining rooms Wright never saw. The architectural dining room survives as a tour. The catalog dining room survives as Tuesday.

If the chair back does not reach a standing diner’s shoulder, it will not make a fence. Measure that before you say Prairie. A 1915 catalog “high-back” that stops at the shoulder blades is Mission with better advertising. The enclosure is the design. Height is not a mood. It is a dimension the restoration report will give when we have it.

## After the fence

Wright’s later dining (Taliesin, the Usonian houses) changes height and formality. Usonian dining can be a table at the kitchen. That is a preview of the open plan. The Prairie dining room is still a room. The Usonian table is a different essay’s last paragraph. Keep the high-back fence as this hour’s image.

If you sit at a reconstruction, notice how the chairs cut off the rest of the house. That cut-off is the design. When it does that, the architect, not the cabinetmaker, is the author. That authorship is the history, and the reason most of us will eat at a table that still moves.
