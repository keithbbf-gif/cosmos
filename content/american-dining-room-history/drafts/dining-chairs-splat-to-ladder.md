A pierced Chippendale splat — yoke crest, compass seat, claw-and-ball on the front legs — is built to take a table rail, a coat, a long sit, and crumbs. Parlor chairs can be weaker and prettier. The American dining chair runs from the joint stool and the leather side chair through that splat, Federal shields, Grecian sabers, Hitchcock tablets, press-backs, Mission slats, and the mid-century plywood shell. This page is a spine so the shop essays do not each invent seating from nothing. It does not replace those essays.

Seat height is the quiet number. Period dining chairs often sit lower and more upright than a 1990s “dining chair” sold for kitchens. Tables were lower or diners sat higher on cushions. If a chair’s seat has been recut, the rail will tell. Museums recane; they also quietly alter heights. Sit when you are allowed.

## Stools to splats

Early eighteenth-century dining is mixed: stools, benches, leather chairs, then caned William and Mary high-backs, then Queen Anne vase splats with compass seats. The splat is a splat because it is a central splat of wood, solid, a vase in outline. Chippendale pierces it. Federal turns it into a shield, an urn, a lyre, a harp. Each change is a drawing problem in a thinner wood. Mahogany makes piercing easier than oak. The Philadelphia essay owns the claw. This page owns the sequence.

A Queen Anne vase splat is a solid board, sawn and shaped, a silhouette against a rush or a leather seat. Chippendale opens that board: ribbon, tassel, Gothic tracery, a hole a sleeve can catch. Federal thins it again and draws a picture — shield, urn, Prince of Wales feathers — that is inlay and piercing as much as structure. Empire throws the splat away for a saber and a tablet, or a curule, or a pile of upholstery. The dining chair follows each change a year or two after the parlor, because dinner abuses a back that the parlor only displays.

Haircloth — horsehair — is the formal seat from Chippendale through Phyfe. It shines and itches. Leather is earlier and also later. Cane and rush are the breathable seats. Upholstered sprung seats arrive with the nineteenth-century comfort project and never fully leave.

## Seat stuffs

The seat is the part that fails first, and the part that tells the meal. Rush is farm, tavern, kitchen, Shaker, Colonial Revival, and also a high-style New England choice when the chair is a Hitchcock or a fancy. It sags in twenty years. Re-rushing is expected. Original rush with an oxidized butt on the underside is a prize. Cane is lighter, cooler, a Federal and a fancy-chair breath. Recaning is a trade; a museum cane can be too tight and too even. Leather wipes. Herter’s Vanderbilt dining chairs wanted stamped leather because gravy is not theoretical. Haircloth is the formal American seat for a century: shiny, a little hostile, correct under a mahogany table. It splits at the front rail. Replacement horsehair is a specialist job. Vinyl is the dinette’s answer and the 1970s captain’s-chair answer. Foam is mid-century. Springs under a dining seat are a nineteenth-century comfort that Grand Rapids kept.

Do not date a chair by its present seat alone. Date the frame, then read the seat as a history of meals. A 1790 shield-back with a 1950 foam pad is a dining chair that stayed in use. A 1790 shield-back with a perfect period-style haircloth and no wear is a chair that has been curated.

## Windsors and the other line

Stick-built, turned, a saddle seat, painted. Boston and Philadelphia Windsors at table are not failed mahogany. They are the other dining chair: light, cheap, summer, kitchen, tavern, also the merchant’s breakfast. They continue in factories long after the splat has become a Mission slat. A dining-room history that omits Windsors is a high-style history.

Ladder-backs — slats across, often rush — are the vernacular and the Shaker and the later Colonial Revival. They stack better than claws. They appear at farm tables and then at 1930s “Early American” dinettes. The country-revival essay takes the later mood. The ladder is older than the mood.

Nutting’s Windsor reproductions, after 1917, taught a generation that the dining chair of the ancestors was a bow-back. Factories agreed and then cheapened the turning. A 1925 Windsor set around a gateleg is Revival dining. A 1765 Philadelphia Windsor at a drop-leaf is the other line before it was a costume.

## The rail is the brief

If you line six American dining chairs from 1720 to 1960, the table rail is the constant they all have to clear. That clearance is the brief. Fashion is everything above the rail. When a chair fails at dinner, it is usually the rail: too thick, too low, an apron that hits a thigh. Shops that designed chairs without a table in the room made parlor chairs. The good dining chairs were drawn next to a leaf. Look at a period painting or a catalog page that shows both. The gap between seat and apron is the history.

Seat height is the other number. A period dining chair that has been recut to take a later table will show it at the tops of the legs or at a seat rail that has been pieced. Museums recane; they also quietly alter heights. A 1990s “dining chair” sold for a kitchen island is often taller in the seat and more slouched in the back than a Phyfe side chair. People who say old chairs are uncomfortable are sometimes sitting at a new table. Measure both.

Armchairs at the ends — host and hostess — are a hierarchy written in wood. The arms must still clear the table, or the armchair is a parlor chair parked at dinner. Shaker benches refused the hierarchy. Banquettes and built-ins refuse it another way. The open-plan kitchen stool is the latest refusal: no back, no host.

A compass seat — the rounded-front Queen Anne and Chippendale sit — is a dining seat if the rail still clears. It is also a chair that turns a little as you eat, which a square Federal seat does not. Saber legs of the Grecian chair kick back and take a coat. Gothic points catch a sleeve. Mission posts do not catch; they occupy. Each of those behaviors is a dining-room fact, not a parlor fact. The coat, the crumb, the long sit: if a chair cannot take those three, it was drawn for a room that did not eat.

## Fancy, factory, reform

Hitchcock and Baltimore fancy: paint as the splat. Grand Rapids press-backs: a machine splat. Eastlake spindles: a reform splat. Mission posts: the splat becomes a set of slats and a sermon. Prairie high-backs: the splat becomes a wall. Each is a dining chair if it sat at dinner. Many “parlor” chairs did.

The press-back is the factory’s most American splat. A die stamps a floral or a strapwork or a “lion” into a cheap oak or elm back. You feel it when you sit. It is not Belter’s lamination. It is not Chippendale’s piercing. It is ornament as a press act, cousin to Hitchcock’s stencil, and it filled more dining rooms than either of those famous names. Look at the back of the splat. If the pattern is only on the front and the back is flat machine-planed wood, you are in the factory. That is the type.

Ladder-backs stack. Claw-and-ball chairs do not. That is why farmhouses and later “Early American” dinettes kept the ladder when they needed to clear a room. Windsors nest less well than a legend claims, but they hang. A Hitchcock hangs. A Belter-type rococo dining chair does not hang and does not stack; it occupies a dining room even when no one is eating. The specialized room and the unstackable chair arrive together. The dinette’s tubular chair that shoves under the enamel top is the later refusal of that occupancy.

## Mid-century shells

Eames, Saarinen, Danish teaks: the splat disappears into a molded back. The dining chair becomes a single surface. The mid-century essay owns the table. Here: note that a shell chair at a Saarinen pedestal is a solution to the slum of legs *and* to the slum of splats. Copies in polypropylene are the Hitchcock of 1970 — volume, brand, a dining room that is a kitchen.

A shell still has to clear a rail if the table has an apron. Saarinen’s pedestal removed the slum and also removed the apron problem. Eames chairs at a rectangular table meet the old brief again. The gap returns. Fashion did not repeal dinner.

If you sit in a trefoil Gothic, a pierced Chippendale, a Mission slat, and a tulip shell in one week, the table rail is the only common sentence. Everything above it is a drawing problem. The crumbs are the same.
