Boston eats first and carves less. That is a slander with a lot of evidence. From the William and Mary hour through Chippendale and into Federal, Boston and then Salem shops supply New England tables that prefer a clean turning or a quiet cabriole to Philadelphia’s wet claw. The woods begin as maple and white pine and become mahogany when the ships allow. The dining is mercantile: a counting-house family, a minister on Sundays, a leaf that still folds in 1760 and a pedestal that will not in 1810.

Salem is the sharper late chapter. Samuel McIntire carves baskets of fruit for rooms and chairs. John and Thomas Seymour’s shop, Boston-based with Salem clients, puts Federal veneer and inlay on tables that already know Hepplewhite’s plates. The colonial dining table in these towns is the earlier object: maple gatelegs, walnut and mahogany drop-leaves, chairs with vase splats that never quite become a Philadelphia piercing.

## A port’s reserve

Boston’s seventeenth-century oak is behind us by 1700. The eighteenth-century shop is a turning and joining shop. Spanish feet linger on chairs. Pad feet take over. Claw-and-ball appears and stays neater, smaller, less anatomical than the Philadelphia paw. Dining chairs in haircloth or leather sit around a rectangular leaf. The set is often not a set. Inventories are stubbornly unromantic.

Harvard and the townhouses around the Common produce a clientele that reads English books and still wants a table that can leave the room. The dedicated dining room arrives here as it arrives in Philadelphia — late, uneven, faster in the bigger houses. Berman’s thesis is Mid-Atlantic in its inventory sample. Boston needs its own counted rooms. `[CITE NEEDED: a published run of Boston household inventories 1750–1820 listing dining rooms by name.]` Until that count exists, treat the architecture as a lag, not a refusal.

## Maple, pine, then mahogany

Eastern white pine is the hidden wood and sometimes the top. Maple is the New England show wood before mahogany: tiger and birdseye will have a factory future; in 1740 the prize is a clean hard maple that takes a turning. Cherry appears. Walnut is imported or rare. Mahogany, when it comes, is a coastal luxury with the same Atlantic cost Anderson names.

A Boston dining table with a pine top and maple legs is not a kitchen exile. It may be a painted object that lost its paint. A mahogany top on maple legs is a hybrid the shop was happy to sell. Secondary pine, not poplar, is the New England tell. If you see tulip poplar in a “Boston” table, ask why.

## Salem’s carved Federal

By the 1790s Salem’s merchants are rich on a trade that will collapse later. McIntire’s architectural carving — mantels, doorways, the occasional chair — sets a fruit-and-sheaf language that belongs in the dining room as woodwork as much as furniture. The table under that mantel may be a Federal pedestal or D-end from Boston or a local shop. Montgomery’s *Federal Period* is the Winterthur survey; Salem objects sit in it beside New York.

The Seymour shop’s veneers — satinwood, maple stringing, a mahogany ground — are a dining-room luxury on card tables and worktables more often than on an eight-foot dining leaf. When they do a dining table, the inlay is a border, not a picture. Keep the baskets of fruit on the mantel and the chair crest. Do not invent a Seymour dining table with a documented McIntire basket unless the object record says so.

## Chairs at the New England table

Bannister-backs and splat-backs mix in the first half of the century. Windsor chairs — Boston and Philadelphia both make them — come to the dining room as the cheap, the summer, and the kitchen set. They are not a failure of Chippendale. They are a different job: light, stick-built, painted, stackable in a way a claw-foot chair is not. A merchant may own both. The Windsor sits six for breakfast. The mahogany splat sits four for a minister.

Hitchcock’s later factory will industrialize a painted fancy chair that New England already understood. That is a later essay. Here, the painted chair is a country cousin, not yet a brand.

## Spanish feet and the small claw

The Spanish foot is a ribbed, scrolled bracket that looks like a clenched scroll. It often begins as a separate piece glued or nailed to the front of the ankle, then carved into the leg. A foot that is one stick with the leg is a different shop decision. The type lingers in New England Queen Anne seating longer than in Philadelphia. When the claw-and-ball arrives, Boston’s version stays smaller and less anatomical than a Society Hill paw. Think of a bird, not a lion. The webbing is tighter. The ball is often a bit flattened. Heckscher’s late colonial catalog and the MFA’s Boston rooms are the comparison set.

Rural Massachusetts and New Hampshire shops copy the pad and miss the curve. That country Queen Anne is its own object and will still hold dinner. A heavy Philadelphia rail on a “Boston” chair is a problem. Secondary white pine — not poplar — remains the New England tell.

## The dining room as a carved box

Samuel McIntire (1757–1811) is Salem’s carver-architect. Baskets of fruit, sheaves, an Adam vocabulary in wood, belong first to doorways, cornices, and mantels. Dining rooms in the richer Salem houses are McIntire rooms even when the table is from Boston. The woodwork is dining furniture you cannot pull out from the wall. The table has to live up to a carved box.

Salem’s merchant money in the 1790s is a trade that will thin later — the embargo years, the War of 1812, a harbor that loses to Boston and New York. The furniture of the peak stays in the houses and then in Peabody Essex. Do not invent a Seymour dining table with a McIntire basket on the rail unless an object record says so. The two names share clients and a town. They do not share every object.

## How a New England leaf gets mistreated

Colonial Revival shops added breadboards to leaves that never had them and cut fake wear into stretchers with a rasp that does not know where a foot actually rests. Pine that still shows a pit-sawn or hand-planed face, not thickness-planer chatter, is the older sentence. Circular-saw arcs on a supposedly 1710 rail are enough to stop a Boston story.

## Paint as the first finish

A Boston dining table with a pine top and maple legs may have been a painted object. Red and black frames with a scrubbed top are a finished New England sentence, not a kitchen exile. Strippers who take the frame to bare maple to “show the wood” are making a twentieth-century taste. The Colonial Revival dining draft returns to that vandalism. Here, look for pigment in the pores of the turning and in the corner of a stretcher mortise. A clean, even maple with no paint ghost and a dealer tag that says 1740 wants an argument.

Hard maple takes a clean turning. It does not love a wet claw. That is one reason Boston’s claw stays small. The turner and the carver are not always the same man, but the wood votes. A maple knee carved as if it were walnut will look busy and then dent.

## Stringing without a picture

When a dining table from the Seymour orbit carries inlay, it is usually a border: a trough cut near the edge, a strip of holly or maple, hide glue, a surface scraped flush. Card tables and worktables take more of the pictorial inlay — satinwood, dyed woods, a mahogany ground. Montgomery’s *Federal Period* treats that precision as a New England luxury school. Keep the baskets of fruit on the mantel and the chair crest. A dining leaf that is a picture is a later taste or a card table someone is trying to eat on.

The stringing line will telegraph a repair. A replacement strip that is too white, or a trough that has been widened with a modern cutter, is a later hand. Original holly has yellowed in the glue. Look at a chip, not at a catalog adjective.

## Looking at a survivor

New England dining tables suffer from the Colonial Revival. They were stripped, darkened, given fake breadboards, sold as “Pilgrim.” Look for oxidation in the pin holes, spoon wear in a drawer, a dish in the top, pine that has never seen a thickness planer. Look at the clip of the claw: Boston’s is a small bird. Look at the secondary wood. Then look at the room. If the table is in a reconstructed 1680 hall, it is probably too late for the room or the room is too early for the table. Museums have gotten better. Dealers have not all gotten the memo.

The Seymour hour ends this page because it ends the colonial sentence. After 1815 the story is Empire pedestals, factory chairs, and a dining room that Biddle had already named. Boston will still make furniture. It will not own the American dining table again the way New York will.

Stand under a McIntire mantel if you can. The carving is the town’s money in fruit. The table is the town’s money in mahogany. Both are dining furniture. Only one gets sat at.
