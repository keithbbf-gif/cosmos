# Extension tables and patents

Robert Jupe’s British patent 6788, March 1835, describes a circular table whose sections diverge from a center so that filling pieces can drop in. The first tables were made with John Johnstone of New Bond Street; brass bosses read “JUPES PATENT,” later “Johnstone & Jeanes.” They exhibited the expanding principle at the Great Exhibition of 1851. These are English luxury objects. American dining rooms felt them as an idea more than as a population of tables.

The American mechanical story that actually filled rooms is the slide and the crank. Cornelius C. Briggs of Boston — proprietor of a large shop, patent 1843 in the Bray and Carr account (*Antiques and Fine Arts*, 2010) — made accordion, hand-crank extension tables. A labeled example, “Briggs Extension Dining Table / C.C. & J. Briggs, 507 Washington Street / Boston,” shows a shaped top, accordion crank, carved supports, casters. A related table is at the Museum of Fine Arts, Boston. That is the document. The later factory table with wooden telescopic slides is the volume.

## Before the crank

D-ends and loose leaves are extensions without a patent story. Federal shops already added length. What the nineteenth century wants is speed and alignment: a single person (or a servant) making the table grow without lifting a pedestal. Slides — two or three beams that telescope — are the ordinary solution. They are wood, later metal, and they gum up. Wax, not oil, if you are asking.

Drop-leaf extensions and draw leaves (leaves that hide under the top and pull out) are other families. Draw-leaf tables have medieval ancestors and twentieth-century cousins (the 1950s draw-leaf is a dinette fact). Keep the patent names on the mechanisms that claimed them.

A D-end table that comes apart on purpose is already a mechanical object. Brass forks, buttons, pins, rule joints. The Federal essay owns that grammar. This page owns the moment the mechanism gets a lawyer and a brass boss. The dinner does not change. The number of people who can make the table grow without calling the yard man does.

Draw-leaf tables — leaves that live under the top and pull out at the ends — skip the closet. Medieval boards already knew the trick. A 1930s enamel dinette will remember it in metal. Between those hours, American shops used draw leaves on breakfast tables and on some dining ovals that did not want a pantry stack. The meeting at the rule or the butt is still the craft. A draw leaf that has been planed by a later owner to “fix” a seasonal gap will never sit flush again. Leave the seasonal gap. It is the wood talking, not a defect in the patent.

## Jupe’s circle

*Nature*’s calendar of patent records gives the date as 11 March 1835. Jupe, upholsterer (the Sotheby’s note says upholder) of 47 Welbeck Street, Cavendish Square, patented “an improved expanding table so constructed that the sections composing its surface may be caused to diverge from a common centre and that the spaces caused thereby may be filled up by inserting leaves or filling pieces.” The principle works on a round top: segments swing or slide out, wedges or rings drop in, the diameter grows. Early tables from the Johnstone partnership carry a brass boss engraved JUPES PATENT. After 1842 the firm is Johnstone & Jeanes; later bosses read that name. Some tables keep a fitted case for the leaves. Christie’s has described a William IV example with triangular sections, a later circular inset, eight rectangular leaves, and the original leaf case.

The Furniture History Society’s BIFMO entry tracks the partnership: Johnstone, Jupe & Co. from the early 1830s, a lawsuit in 1836 against Samuel Luke Pratt over a similar table, Jupe out by 1839, Johnstone keeping the patent for manufacture. The 1851 Great Exhibition showed a circular table on the expanding principle. In 1893 the later Johnstone firm appeared at the Columbian Exposition in Chicago. That is an English firm in America, not an American invention. Mention it as a world’s-fair sight.

Do not invent a Jupe table in an American dining room you have not documented. Do not hang a twenty-first-century hexagonal wedge table on patent 6788 without the mechanism.

## Jupe as a rumor

A true Jupe in an American house is a rare import or a later reproduction. The radial idea — sections that spread, wedges that fill — shows up in later American patents and in twenty-first-century “Jupe tables” that are a furniture-maker’s party trick. Do not call a hexagonal wedge table of 2010 a Jupe without the mechanism. Do not call a Briggs accordion a Jupe. The clocks and the countries differ.

Johnstone’s firm took a version of the expanding table toward the 1893 Columbian Exposition in Chicago. The Midwestern dining room still bought slides. A radial table wants a leaf case and a servant who knows the order of the wedges. A slide table wants wax and a closet. American houses had closets.

## Briggs and Boston

Bray and Carr are the article to foot. I will not invent the patent number if the article is not in my hand. `[CITE NEEDED: U.S. patent number and exact date for Cornelius Briggs’s 1843 extension table.]` The labeled Boston tables and the MFA relative are enough to say: a crank, an accordion, a classical carved base, a merchant clientele. New Orleans labels on Briggs tables show the coast as a market, the way Phyfe’s shop sold south.

The accordion is a concertina of wooden slats or frames that stretch as the crank turns. The top’s sections follow. Alignment is still the craft; the crank is the speed. A labeled Briggs table with a shaped top, carved supports, and casters is an Empire object that learned a mechanism. The pillar-and-claw essay is the costume. This page is the windlass.

After Briggs, hardware houses sell slides to every factory. The genius moves from a shop to a part. Grand Rapids does not need to invent extension. It buys it.

## Slides as a hardware part

Wooden telescopic slides — two or three beams, tongues and grooves, sometimes a steel tongue later — are the American volume. They come from a hardware supplier as often as from the factory that cuts the top. Look under a golden-oak pedestal table from 1905. The slides may have a maker’s name that is not the furniture maker’s name. That split is the industrial fact. Briggs’s shop sold a table. The twentieth-century factory sells a table that contains someone else’s slide.

Metal slides appear as the century turns. They run smoother if they are not bent. They rust if the dining room is a porch. Wooden slides swell in a wet summer and refuse to close. Wax, not cooking oil, not motor oil. A slide that has been painted shut is a table frozen at one length. The dinner you can actually extend to is the dinner the shop meant.

Pedestal tables that split in the base when they open — a three-part pedestal, a pair of pillars that walk apart — are the Gilded Age and Grand Rapids answer to a long cloth. Four-leg tables keep their corners and hide the slides in the apron. Both can sag. A valley in the cloth is a slide that has lost a wedge or a runner that has worn. Repairs are a trade. A block screwed on in 1960 to take up wear is a document. Removing it to “restore” the underside can put the valley back.

Casters are part of the mechanism. A table that grows also rolls, or it scars the floor. Brass cups, porcelain wheels, later rubber. A missing caster on one foot is a table that lists when the slides are out. Look at all four, or all six, before you blame the crank.

## Numbered leaves and the pantry

Leaves need a closet. The butler’s pantry is partly a leaf barn. Original numbered leaves — chalk, pencil, a stamped digit that matches a digit on the underside of the top — are a gift. Mixed leaves are a valley waiting: different thickness, different finish, a seam you feel through the cloth. A table that grows to fourteen wants a room that was drawn for fourteen, or a room that looks wrong on Tuesday. Gilded Age dining rooms were drawn for the large number. Bungalow dining rooms were not; they bought smaller slides or a fixed Mission plank.

A leaf case, English, fitted, is a luxury Jupe accessory. An American closet with a stack of leaves on edge, felt between them if anyone cared, is the ordinary storage. Warped leaves are a climate document. Do not plane them flat to “improve” a meeting that was never meant to be furniture-grade after fifty summers.

If you crawl under an old extension table, you will find pencil math, a maker’s stencil, dried wax, and a block someone added in 1960. That underside is the history. The carving on the base is the costume. This series has been saying that about joints since the gateleg. The patent is just a joint that went to a lawyer.

Use the crank if it still cranks. Do not force a painted-shut slide. A table frozen at six because no one will touch the mechanism is a Federal D-end without the D.
