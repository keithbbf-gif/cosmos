# Rococo Revival, Belter, Meeks

John Henry Belter’s patents of the 1840s and 1850s are parlor patents: laminated rosewood, pierced and carved, a chair back that is a bouquet. The dining room borrowed the look. It did not always borrow the lamination. A Rococo Revival dining table in America is more often a carved mahogany or rosewood pedestal with a marble or wood top, cabriole legs that have learned a new plumpness, and chairs that may or may not be Belter. Joseph Meeks & Sons and a dozen New York shops sold the same C-scrolls. “Belter” on a dining tag is a brand people want. Construction is the check.

The dining room of 1850 that could afford this furniture is a middle- or upper-class room with a carpet, a heavy sideboard, and a taste for France that is not Lannuier’s Empire. It is Louis XV through a New York mill. Downing is still in print. Many of his readers bought rococo anyway.

## Lamination and the chair

Belter (born Germany, 1804–1863, New York) ran a shop that became J. H. Belter & Co., 1854–1865, on the Met’s labels. The method — layers of rosewood steamed and pressed, then carved through — makes a chair back that is thinner and more open than a solid plank would survive. Dining chairs in this technique exist. They are less common than parlor side chairs because dining takes more abuse and because a pierced rose is a snag for a sleeve at table. Haircloth and then more stuffed seats. The set may be six or twelve. Matching an existing parlor suite into the dining room is a period habit and a later dealer habit.

The Met’s attributed side chairs 57.130.4 and 57.130.6, 1850–60, rosewood (the second with ash), about thirty-eight to thirty-nine and a half inches high, are the parlor language. The slipper chair 51.79.8, about 1855, rosewood and ash, forty-three and one-eighth inches, is still not a dining chair. Brooklyn’s attributed pair 64.153.1–2, about 1855, rosewood, is the museum’s useful comparison: one New York chair traditionally carved (Bembé & Kimbel in that installation), one laminated and bent. Brooklyn’s label is the sentence this page needs. Belter’s patented laminated, bent-plywood chair departs further from the eighteenth-century model than the hand-carved rival. By the 1850s consumers would take innovation in the service of conservative revivalism.

Unlaminated rococo chairs — solid walnut with applied carving — are the volume. They are not “fake Belter.” They are the trade. Meeks’s labels and advertisements show a factory-scale shop already. The firm’s 1833 illustrated advertisement is the famous plate of a New York warehouse talking to the country. Grand Rapids will take that scale west. This hour is still New York-centered for the high style.

## How to look at a laminated back

Look at the edge of a piercing. Laminated rosewood shows its layers in the cut — thin dark lines, not a single plank. A solid walnut chair with applied grapes will not. Look at the spring. A laminated back can flex in a way a thick splat cannot. That spring is the process. Then look at the seat rail and the cabriole. Ash in the Met’s secondary lists is a structural wood under a rosewood show. The bouquet is the costume. The ash is the chair.

Repairs tell on laminated work. A broken piercing that has been filled with composition, or a back that has been split and plated, is common. Original finish is a dark, slightly cloudy rosewood, not a 1970s red. Upholstery is almost never original. Brooklyn is honest about “modern upholstery” on 64.153.2. Haircloth, then sprung stuffing, then whatever the last decorator liked. The frame is the document.

Never call every pierced rosewood chair a Belter. Meeks, J. & J.W. Meeks, and unnamed New York shops laminated too, or imitated the silhouette in solid wood. Construction, provenance, and a label — rare — do the job. “Belter” on a dining tag without a look at the edge of a piercing is a wish.

## The table

Marble-top center tables drift from parlor to dining in smaller houses. Dedicated dining tables keep extension slides and a carved base: four cabrioles to a central plinth, or a pillar with a rococo sheath. Rosewood is the luxury wood; mahogany the work luxury; walnut the American stand-in. Crotch veneers continue. The top is often plain compared to the chairs — a cloth will cover it — while the base is the show when the cloth is off.

Castors, heavy. Leaves, numbered. A rococo dining table that does not extend is a center table someone is eating on. The patents essay takes Briggs’s 1843 crank as the American mechanical story, and Jupe’s British patent 6788 (1835) as the circular rumor. Rococo shops used slides of several kinds. Look at the mechanism before the carving. A C-scroll on a sagging slide is a Sunday humiliation.

The Gothic essay’s pointed chair can sit at the same crank. Same decade, different plate. Meeks sold both. A dining room of 1855 can be Gothic on the sideboard wall and rococo at the chairs. Mixed rooms are normal. A fully rococo suite is a statement and a later photograph.

## The dining room that wanted fruit

The room itself is heavier than Phyfe’s Grecian parlor. A carpet that hides caster tracks. Curtains. A sideboard that is already climbing toward the Renaissance Revival mountain the later essay will describe. Gas beginning to replace candles in town. Service moving toward *à la russe*: more hands, more trips from the pantry, a table that stays a field of plate while the board does the staging. The rococo chair’s pierced back is a nuisance in that traffic. Sleeves catch. That is one reason dining sets in this look are often the solid-walnut cousins, not the most open laminated roses.

Meeks’s 1833 plate is a warehouse of forms, not a single style. The firm sold pillar tables, sideboards, and chairs to a national market before Belter’s laminated hour was famous. A labeled Meeks rococo dining chair is as useful a document as an attributed Belter, and rarer in the conversation because collectors want the patent name. Look for a paper label or a stencil on the frame. Then look at the carving. Applied grapes on a solid splat are a different shop hour than grapes cut through a steamed laminate.

The table base is joinery under the C-scroll. Four cabrioles meeting a plinth have to carry an extension top without racking. Glue blocks, a stout stretcher or a hidden iron, numbered leaves, slides that want wax. A restorer who tightens a carved knuckle with a drywall screw has not improved the fruit. Crawl under. The underside is the history. The bouquet is the costume.

## Taste, labor, the later joke

The twentieth century called this furniture “Victorian” and then “horrible.” Colonial Revival dining rooms were a refusal of these C-scrolls. So was Eastlake, earlier, from inside the century. If you are looking at a Belter-type chair now, the joke is stale. The object is a technical feat and a class signal.

Women in the house maintained the polish and the seats. Factory men and boys did the laminating. The labor is industrial even when the look is courtly. Rosewood’s later CITES story is a woods-series problem. A dining room that still holds a rosewood suite is holding a regulated wood. This page will not issue a permit. It will say the word rosewood and point at the other pack.

Downing is the rival moral voice in the same years. He wanted pointed honesty and a counted staff. His readers still bought fruit. The American dining room of the 1850s wanted a bouquet at its back. Federal America wanted a shield. Gothic America wanted a trefoil. The meal was still dinner. The silhouette was a fashion, and fashions at table are this series’ subject as much as joints.

If you sit in a laminated back, you feel a spring the solid chair does not have. Then you eat, carefully, because the piercing is still a bouquet. Look at the edge of a cut. Count the layers. That is the patent, whether or not you have the number.

A rosewood dining suite that has been broken up — chairs in one house, table in another, sideboard sold as “Belter” with no laminate — is the ordinary survival. Reassemble on paper before you reassemble in the room. Woods, seat heights, and the profile of a cabriole do more than a matching finish. The shop could repeat a carving. It could not make two houses keep a set together for a hundred and seventy years.
