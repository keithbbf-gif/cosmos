The Brooklyn Museum’s dining table 1997.150.15a–c is the object to start with if you want dinner, not a sofa. Attributed to Duncan Phyfe, about 1805, mahogany with beech, twenty-nine and a half by forty-eight by seventy-eight inches, Matthew Scott Sloan Collection, gift of Lidie Lane Sloan McBurney. It is a table with parts — the a–c in the accession — the way Federal dining tables are parts. The Met’s better-known Phyfe pieces in the dining line are smaller or adjacent: Pembroke 31.44.15, drop-leaf Pembroke 22.98, sideboard table 1971.160. The sofa 42.16 is a masterpiece of the shop and not a dining table. This page keeps the cloth on the wood you eat from.

Duncan Phyfe (born Duncan Fife, near Lock Fannich, Ross-shire, 1768/70–1854) ran a New York shop that made Federal and later Grecian furniture into a recognizable product. The Met’s Heilbrunn essay on Phyfe and Lannuier puts the family in the United States in 1784, in Albany first, then New York by 1791. Peter M. Kenny and Michael K. Brown’s *Duncan Phyfe: Master Cabinetmaker in New York* (Met, 2011) replaced a century of wishful “Phyfe” with addresses, apprentices, and the move from Partition Street when it became Fulton. Attribution to the workshop is the honest phrase. Specialists carved, turned, and caned. The dining table is a shop sentence in mahogany: reeded legs or a pedestal, a leaf that meets, a secondary of pine or poplar or, in Brooklyn’s case, beech.

## A shop on a renamed street

Phyfe had settled at 35 Partition Street by 1795, bought next door, and ran dwelling, salesrooms, and workshop on one side of the street. After Robert Fulton’s death the street was renamed and renumbered, 1816–17. The Met’s watercolor of 168–172 Fulton Street (22.28.1) is a later image of that block: a row of brick fronts, not a romantic garret. Clients were merchants who wanted a Grecian dining room without Latrobe’s archaeologist paint. Heilbrunn is blunt about the port. The shop sold south as well as to the city — Mid-Atlantic and Southern states, and, on a speculative or consignment basis, islands including Cuba and Guadeloupe. The Met’s Richmond Room exists because New York made for a coast.

An 1816 letter from Phyfe to the Philadelphian Charles Bancker, with illustrations and prices for chairs, is in the Heilbrunn record. The Museum of Fine Arts, Houston, holds a lyre-back side chair from that order and publishes the cane-bottom option as twenty-two dollars on the surviving papers. Dining tables were priced the same way: a product, not a unique poem each time. Quality is in the reeding, the leaf meeting, the balance of a pedestal. It is not in a new type every year.

Labels are scarce. “Phyfe” on a brass plate is often later. Construction, provenance, and comparison to documented shop work do the job. The 2011 show made the shop countable. A table without a paper trail goes back to “New York, attributed to the workshop.” That is not a demotion.

## Three jobs at dinner

Phyfe’s dining furniture is three jobs, not one masterpiece. The Brooklyn table is the long meal: mahogany show, beech where you do not see it, a length that wants eight or ten when the parts are together and a shorter board when they are not. The a–c in the accession is the Federal idea made visible. Ends can stand as pier or side tables. A center fills the gap. Alignment is the craft. A bead that does not meet is a shop that rushed or a house that heated the wood after the fact.

The Pembrokes are the small meal. Met 31.44.15 — workshop of Duncan Phyfe, 1810–20, mahogany, brass, tulip poplar, white pine, twenty-eight and five-eighths by forty-five and five-eighths by thirty-five and a quarter inches, Sylmaris Collection, gift of George Coe Graves, 1931 — is four people and a drawer. Met 22.98, Rogers Fund 1922, is the drop-leaf cousin: mahogany, white pine, cherry, twenty-eight and three-quarters by forty-eight and three-quarters by thirty-five and three-quarters. Sheraton’s *Cabinet Dictionary* (1803) already called the Pembroke a breakfast table. In a New York house that had invented a dining room, the Pembroke stayed useful in the parlor, the breakfast room, or the end of a long cloth when the extra leaf was still in the closet.

The sideboard table is the standing job. Met 1971.160, attributed to Duncan Phyfe, about 1815, mahogany and marble, thirty-six and one-eighth by seventy-eight and three-quarters by twenty-six inches, gift of Mrs. Stanley B. Ineson, 1971. It is not a cabinet sideboard with bottle wells and a lock. It is a slab on a Grecian frame: cool stone, a wipeable top, a height for a person who is not sitting. Charleston’s MESDA 3163 is the colonial Lowcountry cousin of that idea. Phyfe’s version is quieter, later, and New York. All three objects can live in one room. Matching suites are the later dealer’s dream. Mixed shops in one room are the document.

## What the joints actually do

Reeded straight legs on early tables; sabre and pedestal as the Grecian thickens. The reed is a series of parallel convex flutes run with a plane or a scratch stock, then cleaned. It catches light the way a turned vase does, without asking for a carved paw. Brass ferrules and casters are not Victorian additions when the legs are cut for them. The table moves for the extra leaf and for the weekly clean. Look for the original caster plate and a later replacement wheel.

A drop-leaf Pembroke hides its engineering in the fly rail or knuckle joint under the leaf. The rule joint — a matched pair of convex and concave profiles along the meeting edge — is the older poetry. When it is cut well, the underside of the leaf does not flash a raw square when the leaf is up. When it is cut badly, or when a later restorer flattened the profiles, you see a dark line and a gap. Brass clips and pins on a three-part dining table replace some of that poetry with metal. The click is the sound of 1805 deciding the hall was no longer enough.

Glue blocks under a pedestal top radiate like a wagon wheel. A restorer who removes them to “clean up” the underside has removed the table’s brain. A pinned pedestal that has been glued solid by someone who hated the seasonal wobble is a common surgery. The wobble was humidity. The glue is forever.

Beech in the Brooklyn table is a seating and English-adjacent wood; note it and do not force a poplar-only story. Shops used what the yard had. The Met Pembrokes are more typical of the New York sandwich: mahogany show, tulip poplar and white pine (and, on 22.98, cherry) underneath. The 2011 catalog is the place to pin a particular secondary. If the catalog is silent on 1997.150.15, say so. `[CITE NEEDED: Kenny and Brown 2011 discussion, if any, of the Brooklyn dining table.]`

A dining table attributed to Phyfe that is oak, or that has machine screws in an 1805 joint, is someone else’s wish. Horsehair on the formal chairs. Cane on some seating at the same dinner — the Pearsall suite at the Met is seating, but it tells you the shop spoke more than one dialect in a decade. Heilbrunn’s curule chairs (60.4.4) sit next to the Bancker drawings without being the same order. A saber leg is not a signature.

## Beside Lannuier, not under him

Charles-Honoré Lannuier arrived in 1803 and died in 1819. His labeled work is the other New York Grecian: more gilt, more Paris. The next essay is his. A marble-top pier table can be either shop or neither. Dining tables from Lannuier’s orbit are rarer in the public conversation than his pier tables; do not invent a labeled Lannuier dining table to balance this page.

After 1820 Phyfe’s shop follows the Grecian into heavier pillars, then into a revivalism the 2011 catalog does not try to make fashionable. The later firm names — Phyfe & Sons, D. Phyfe & Son — are on pier tables and parlor furniture. Dining rooms of the 1830s wanted the pillar-and-claw the Empire essay describes. Phyfe retired in 1847 and died in 1854. The Brooklyn table remains an 1805 idea: mahogany, parts, a merchant’s length.

## Labor on Fulton Street

Apprentices have names in the 2011 book. The watercolor of the Fulton Street block is a labor picture if you let it be: more than one building, more than one hand, a salesroom that is not the bench. Enslaved and free Black workmen in New York — gradual emancipation running out in 1827, inside Phyfe’s working life — are part of the labor history older catalogs skipped. `[CITE NEEDED: specific documented Black workmen in Phyfe’s shop — Kenny 2011 notes, if any.]` This page will not invent them. Jennifer L. Anderson’s *Mahogany* is again the forest at the other end of the ship. A reeded leg in Manhattan is still a receipt for island labor whether or not anyone at the bench was unfree.

Caners and upholsterers are easy to forget because the table does not wear their work. The chairs around it do. A horsehair seat that has been replaced in leather is a lost hour. Original cane, oxidized on the underside, is a prize. The shop could fill a room and bill it. That is a workforce, not a lone genius with a chisel.

## The room that bought the shop

The dining room that bought Phyfe was already named, or was trying to be. Berman’s inventories watch the specialized room spread down the urban ladder through the first half of the nineteenth century. A three-part table assumes a floor that belongs to it. A marble slab assumes a wall and a standing worker. Leaves assume a closet that is not a damp passage. The butler’s pantry essay takes that closet later. In 1810 it may still be a dining-room press or a passage shelf.

Service is still mostly *à la française* in the Federal years: courses on the cloth, guests helping themselves. *À la russe*, dishes presented from the sideboard, is a later American fashion. The furniture is ready for that shift before most houses adopt it. The slab and the lockable cabinet sideboard are staging furniture. The long table is the sitting furniture. Phyfe made both.

If you are in the Met, look at 31.44.15 and 1971.160 in Gallery 774 and ask what meal they serve. The Pembroke is four people and a drawer. The sideboard table is the standing worker and the marble. Then go to Brooklyn, if you can, for the long table. Read the attribution language on the label. “Workshop” is the accurate word. The suite is the point. Phyfe’s dining room is not one masterpiece. It is a shop that could fill a room and bill it.
