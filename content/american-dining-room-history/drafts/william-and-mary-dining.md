# William and Mary at table

The turning is the giveaway. A William and Mary dining table in America does not announce itself with a royal cipher. It announces itself with a trumpet: a leg that swells, cups, and drops to a ball foot, often with a high stretcher tying the legs in an X or a box. The fashion is Dutch and English, post-1688, and it reaches Boston, New York, and Philadelphia in the 1690s and the first quarter of the new century. The dining it serves is still a hall dining. The style is what you notice when the leaves are up and the candles are lit.

American William and Mary is a trade name. No Boston shop in 1705 wrote it on a bill. Collectors and curators use it for a cluster of turnings, caned chairs, trumpet legs, and veneered case pieces that sit between seventeenth-century oak heaviness and the Queen Anne cabriole. At the table the cluster means: turned legs you can see, stretchers you will kick, a top that is still a drop-leaf or a gateleg, chairs with caned or rushed seats and high backs.

## What crossed the water

William III and Mary II are a political date, not a shop date. Dutch joiners and turners had been feeding London before the Glorious Revolution. After it, the court taste — walnut, strong turnings, caning, a certain verticality — is what pattern-conscious American merchants wanted in a parlor and, when they could, at dinner. Boston had the ships and the turners. New York had a Dutch joinery tradition that did not need a new king to justify a trumpet leg. Philadelphia was younger and faster to catch the next fashion; its William and Mary dining furniture is thinner on the ground and argued when it appears.

Caned chairs are the seating half of the story. English cane chairs, imported and then imitated, put a tall back and a woven seat at a table that had been using joint stools. The cane is not comfortable for a long sermon of a dinner. It is fashionable. It also breaks. American rush and later splint are the repairs and the cheaper first choices. A “William and Mary dining set” with six matching caned chairs in a 1700 farmhouse is a dealer’s wish. Mixed seating is the document.

High-back chairs at a gateleg create a silhouette later rooms will call “Pilgrim,” which is a lie. The Pilgrim century did not look like this. The 1690s did. The Colonial Revival dining essay is where that lie gets a factory.

## Turned legs and the ball

The trumpet turning wants a lathe and a turner who has seen a print or an imported chair. American maple takes the shape well. Walnut is finer and rarer in big sections. Oak is stubborn. Pine is for the top. The ball foot is a separate turning or the end of the same stick; it chips on stone floors and gets replaced. A table with four perfect ball feet and no scars is either lucky or later.

Inverted cups, reel turnings, and spiral twists appear on the better chairs and on some table stretchers. Spiral twist in America is often later than people want it to be. `[CITE NEEDED: a firmly dated American spiral-twist dining table before 1720 with a shop attribution.]` Do not promote a Victorian revival twist into 1701.

Stretchers sit high. You feel them. The X-stretcher, sometimes with a finial at the crossing, is a show version. Box stretchers are the work version. Both rack if the tenons shrink. A glue block added in 1890 is not original and is not a crime.

## Veneer and the case at the wall

William and Mary is also a veneer moment: oyster walnut, crossbanding, a fall-front desk against the parlor wall. Dining-specific casework is still rare. You get a court cupboard, a dresser, a chest. The sideboard has not arrived. A serving table is a slab or a narrow table without drawers. If someone offers you a “William and Mary sideboard,” they are using a nineteenth-century word for a seventeenth-century wall.

Chests and cupboards at dining height hold pewter and the good earthenware. The show face may be walnut veneer over pine. The American humidity is unkind to hide glue and thin walnut. Bubbled veneer on a 1710 piece is expected. A perfectly flat oyster surface is often a later face.

## Boston, New York, a little Philadelphia

Boston’s early eighteenth-century furniture is the best documented of the three for this hour: turned chairs, maple tables, a merchant clientele that imported cane and then asked local turners to match it. New York’s Dutch families kept older habits longer and also made some of the most exuberant turnings. Philadelphia will own the next two essays. For William and Mary it is a port still assembling its shops.

Attribution at this date is mostly regional, not a named maker. Labels are scarce. Construction and secondary wood do the work. A ball-foot table with white pine secondary and a Boston history is a Boston argument. The same table with yellow poplar is not.

## Labor in the turning shop

Turners and joiners were not always the same man. A table can be a joiner’s frame and a turner’s legs, wedded at the mortise. Apprentices kicked the wheel. Enslaved turners appear in later Southern documents more clearly than in 1705 Boston, but unfree labor is not absent from northern ports. Women did not usually turn the trumpet. They did sit on the cane, mend it, and scrub the top. The hall-table essay keeps that labor in the room. This essay keeps it at the lathe.

## How a cane seat is made

A cane seat is a woven panel set in a rabbet or a drilled rail, not an upholstered pad. English chairmakers had the rind, the soak, and the pattern. American shops that could not get cane, or could not spare the time, rushed the seat or never tried. Rush — twisted, wrapped around the rails — is a different craft and a local one. A “William and Mary” chair with a later machine-woven cane panel in a widened rabbet is a repair that erased the first seat.

The high back is also a draft stop in a hall that leaks. Comfort for a two-hour dinner is not the brief. A repaired cane panel that sags in the middle has been sat in. A tight, even, unused weave on a 1705 chair is a later skin.

## The lathe and the two-man table

A trumpet leg is a turning problem before it is a style. The blank is maple or walnut, mounted on a lathe — a great-wheel lathe with an apprentice or a hired man at the wheel, or a spring-pole lathe the turner works alone. The cups and reels are cut with gouges and skews in a sequence the turner has in his hands, not on a measured drawing. Two legs from the same man will be cousins, not twins. Four machine-identical trumpets are a later century or a later recut.

The mortise is the marriage between joiner and turner. If the tenon is round and the mortise is a bored hole, the turner delivered a round tenon and the joiner accepted it. If the tenon has been pared square, someone adapted. Indentured labor in Pennsylvania and the Delaware valley is a better-documented northern category than 1705 Boston and still thin on named turners tied to dining tables. `[CITE NEEDED: a named indentured turner in a Mid-Atlantic shop whose work can be tied to a surviving table.]`

## Reading a trumpet without a bill

Start with the foot. A ball that has been kicked for three centuries is faceted by accident. A ball that is a perfect sphere with a crisp fillet above it has been recut or is new. Then the stretcher height. William and Mary stretchers sit where a sitter’s ankle finds them. A trumpet-leg table with no stretchers and pad feet is already the next hour, or a later amputation.

Secondary wood next. White pine is a Boston argument. Yellow poplar is not. Oak in the hidden rails can be English or a conservative American shop; do not call it Boston because the top is maple. A revival table can copy a trumpet. It has trouble copying three hundred years of scrubbing and a pin that has oxidized in place.

New York’s Dutch families complicate the tidy English sequence. A trumpet in a Hudson Valley house may be a local habit that did not need 1688. Trust region before you trust a royal name. The name is a collector’s. The turning is the object.

## How the hour ends

The cabriole arrives. The stretcher drops or disappears. The ball foot becomes a pad, then a slipper, then a claw. Walnut stays; the turning calms. That is Queen Anne, and it is the next dining hour. William and Mary tables keep getting made in the country for another twenty years, the way all “ended” fashions do. A 1740 rural gateleg with trumpet legs is not confused. It is late and local.

If you are dating a table, trust the tightest feature, not the loudest. A trumpet with a machine-cut screw in the pintle is a revival. A trumpet with a hand-whittled pin, oxidized maple, and a dish in the top is the hour this page is for. Sit down. Kick the stretcher. That kick is the style.
