John Hemmings (1776–1833), enslaved at Monticello, joiner, made furniture and interior woodwork for Thomas Jefferson’s houses. He is a name. Most of the people who made and served at American dining tables before 1865 are not. This page is a dining-room labor history, not a complete census. It exists so the other essays cannot treat mahogany, marble, and Hepplewhite as if they arranged themselves.

Three jobs meet at dinner: the person who cut and joined the table, the person who carried the dish, the person who sat. American furniture history has been lavish with the third, intermittent with the first, and almost silent with the second until house museums were forced to speak. A dining room is a labor diagram. The sideboard’s height is a standing worker. The kitchen stair is a route. The lock on the plate drawer is a politics.

## Makers with names, and without

Hemmings was born at Monticello on 24 April 1776, son of Elizabeth (Betty) Hemings. The Monticello encyclopedia puts him first as an out-carpenter — felling, hewing, fences, barns, log dwellings on Mulberry Row — then, at seventeen, under hired white woodworkers. He was principal assistant to James Dinsmore, the Irish joiner responsible for much of the house’s interior, and succeeded Dinsmore as head joiner in 1809. He made cabinets, chairs, tables, a writing desk for Jefferson’s granddaughter Ellen, the wooden parts of an 1814 landau, and, after Jefferson’s death, the coffin. He was literate. Some of the letters between him and Jefferson survive.

The documented furniture list is short. Robert Self’s MESDA Journal essay of 2020 — “I Have a Job of 4. Pembroke Tables on Hand at Monticello” — is the piece to foot. Eight documented Hemmings furniture jobs include painted bedsteads, a round table sent to Poplar Forest in 1811, two dressing tables of 1818 made with the enslaved woodworker Lewis, at least one campeachy chair sent in 1819, a chess table, and Ellen’s cherry writing desk, later lost at sea. The revolving round table now at Monticello (1976-34), cherry primary, yellow poplar, walnut, and southern pine secondaries, brass, twenty-eight and a half by thirty-five and three-quarters inches, 1811, is the object the Journal calls the only surviving example that can be definitively documented as Hemmings’s and the best-documented American furniture by an identified enslaved cabinetmaker. Jefferson told Edmund Bacon to “tell Johnny Hemmings to finish off immediately the frame for the round table for this place.” It is a writing and sitting table with a revolving top, not the parlor dining board. Do not make it into one.

The four mahogany Pembrokes loaded for Poplar Forest in June 1808 are dining-adjacent and better documented than most plantation tables. The same MESDA Journal essay attributes those 1808 tables to Dinsmore, not to Hemmings: timing, correspondence, and construction point to the hired master, with Hemmings still in the shop. Two survive, at Poplar Forest and at Monticello. Use that distinction. A plantation joinery is a shop with more than one hand. Celebrity sites get the paper. The mass of rural shops do not. `[CITE NEEDED: a published list of named enslaved cabinetmakers tied to dining forms, beyond Hemmings.]` MESDA and Chipstone have been doing this work. Auction catalogs still lag.

Free Black cabinetmakers — Thomas Day the most visible — complicate any binary. Day’s shop made sideboards for planters and used enslaved labor. The Day essay holds that contradiction. Northern unfree and indentured labor in the eighteenth century belongs in Boston and New York shops as a `[CITE NEEDED]` with names, not a gesture. Phyfe’s working life overlaps New York’s gradual emancipation (legally complete 1827). The 2011 catalog is the place to look for Black workmen; this page will not invent them.

The mahogany itself is an enslaved product for most of the eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries. Anderson’s *Mahogany* is required. A Phyfe reeded leg is a receipt for island labor whether or not anyone in the Fulton Street shop was unfree.

## The Monticello dining room as a diagram

Monticello’s dining room is a useful machine because the house still exists and the paper is thick. Side chairs in the room are attributed to Thomas Burling, New York, 1790 — imported seating around a Virginia meal. Cornelia Jefferson Randolph’s plan of the first floor, about 1826, marks a console, a sideboard, and dining tables stored in the dining-room alcove. The tables did not have to live fully open. They could be stacked in the architecture. That is a labor fact: someone lifts, someone stores, someone opens the leaves when the company comes.

Jefferson’s dining room also used a revolving serving door and dumbwaiters — architectural furniture that reduces the number of bodies in the room without reducing the number of bodies in the house. The people are in the passage and the kitchen. The guest sees a shelf turn. Chipstone’s 2023 essay on Jefferson’s New York furniture publishes the room and the alcove. Read those pictures as a service diagram, not only as a taste in Federal chairs.

Hemmings’s best-documented surviving table is not in that alcove. His woodwork at Poplar Forest — doors, architraves, mantels, blinds — burned. The dining room at Monticello still has to be told through his bench and through Dinsmore’s. A named joiner is a start. The unnamed remain the majority. Mark the gap. Do not fill it with atmosphere.

Hemmings wrote. In November 1821 he described a workday that began when he could see and ended the same way, dentils and quarter-rounds on a skylight frame, an architrave to go on before the scaffold came down. Self quotes the letter in the 2020 essay. That is a joiner’s voice, not a caption. The dining room’s woodwork — even when it is architecture rather than a table — is still a meal’s enclosure. Someone made the door the dish came through.

## Servers

Charleston’s MESDA 3163 label says the quiet part: a sideboard table implies access to servants, usually enslaved, who mix and serve while guests remain seated. Marble is cool, water-resistant, and expensive. Paired with carved mahogany it telegraphs elite status. It also telegraphs a standing worker. Dumbarton House’s Federal essay names Fran, a Nourse servant who supplied the table from markets. Service *à la russe* later increases the number of hands in the room and the specialization of those hands. Women, men, children — dining-room staff is a hierarchy of its own.

In Northern hired-service houses the furniture is similar and the legal status is not. Do not flatten. Do not use “servants” as a euphemism when the household’s own documents say otherwise.

The hall-table essay is the earlier, mixed labor: cooking and serving not yet split. The specialized dining room splits them. That split is progress for the guest and a new cage for the worker. The hunt-board essay is the Piedmont version: a tall server in a hall that may not yet be called dining, bottle drawers, yellow pine, a standing station in a hardwood county. The furniture changes height and wood. The station remains.

## Sitters who were not guests

Enslaved people ate. They did not eat at the mahogany table in the dining parlor. Kitchen tables, benches, yards, later “dependencies” — the furniture of those meals is almost uncollected. A history of American dining furniture that only catalogs the guest table is a history of one meal in the house. This series cannot reconstruct every kitchen board. It can refuse to call the parlor table “the” table.

After emancipation the dining room remains a service room in middle-class houses: hired Black and white labor, then the servantless house and the diner, then the open plan. The twentieth-century essays pick that up. The violence of the earlier furniture is that it still exists, polished, in museums, often without a name on the label except the owner’s.

## How a label should read

Maker (named or “shop of,” free or unfree if known). Woods, including the forest. Owner. Then: who was expected to stand at it. A marble slab without that last sentence is incomplete. MESDA’s 3163 text is a model. Many older Winterthur and Met labels have been rewritten. Some have not. Read the date of the label.

If you own a Southern Federal sideboard, you own an object that was almost certainly used in a coerced service. That does not mean you cannot use it. It means the caption is part of the piece. The same is true of a Newport mahogany leaf. The hunt-board romance — horsemen, hospitality — is one of the ways collectors washed this. This series will not wash it.

Hemmings’s surviving documented table is a revolving cherry round for Poplar Forest, not a famous banquet board. Dinsmore’s 1808 Pembrokes are the plantation shop’s dining-adjacent paper trail. Day’s Richmond sideboard is a free Black shop’s dining-room wall, made in a slave economy, using enslaved labor. Three different sentences. None of them is a mahogany leaf that arranged itself.
