# Hollywood Regency and the formal fifties

Not every postwar table was a tulip. A large American dining room of 1955 might still be mahogany, or mahogany-colored, with a breakfront, a crystal fixture, shield-back chairs that are Colonial Revival’s grandchildren, and a table that extends to Thanksgiving. Hollywood Regency — the decorator name for lacquer, brass ormolu, a little Empire, a little chinoiserie, a glamour that photographs — sits at the expensive end. The suburban “formal dining room” sits at the volume end. Both refuse the chrome dinette as a public room. The dinette is in the kitchen. Company sees mahogany.

Dorothy Draper and later decorators are the loud names. Most rooms were done from a store’s “traditional” floor. This page is the refusal of modernism at dinner, which is as American as Saarinen.

## Two rooms again

The split returns: kitchen eat-in and a closed dining room with a carpet that is off-limits to crayons. The closed room’s furniture is a suite from a Southern or Midwestern factory, “mahogany,” often veneer over a core, Queen Anne or French Provincial or Italian Provincial legs. Provincial is a 1950s word that means a cabriole and a distressed paint or a cherry finish. It is not Provence.

Hollywood Regency proper — mirrored leaves, black lacquer, a brass sabre, a paler upholstery — is a decorator’s dining room in Los Angeles or a rich suburb. It shares Empire’s love of the pedestal and the gleam. It does not share Empire’s mahogany piety. Gloss is the brief. William Haines’s interiors and Tony Duquette’s denser glamour are the Los Angeles citations people use; Draper’s earlier, louder classicism is the national one. James Mont’s lacquered and parcel-gilt furniture sits in the same glamour file. Pin a room to a published photograph when you name a decorator. A “Hollywood Regency dining set” on a resale site is usually a black-and-gold suite from a later factory.

The suburban formal room is the volume. Thomasville, Henredon, Drexel’s traditional lines, Baker at the higher end, and a dozen North Carolina and Virginia names filled the closed box. `[CITE NEEDED: a 1955 catalog page for a named “mahogany dining group” with list prices.]` This page will not invent a SKU or a dollar figure. The type is the history: table, six or eight chairs, a breakfront or china cabinet, sometimes a serving piece that is a sideboard’s grandchild.

## What the suite is made of

Veneer, often. A mahogany face on a lumber-core or plywood core, a banding that is a thin stripe, a finish that is sprayed and rubbed to a plastic shine. The shine is not French polish. It is a conversion varnish or a catalyzed film that does not oil and does not want a hot casserole. The film is the date. An Empire table of 1830, refinished in 1958 with the same shine, becomes a fifties object on top of a nineteenth-century body. Look at the underside. The shop will tell you which century made the rails.

Chairs: shield-back, splat-back, a cabriole with a pad foot, a saber that wants to be Regency. Foam seats, often over webbing or a cheap spring. The foam goes to dust. Original fabric — a damask, a sateen, a pale silk on the decorator chairs — is rarer than the frame. Vinyl appears on the kitchen chairs, not here, unless the household was honest about wipeability and gave up the formal claim.

The table extends. Wooden slides or metal. Leaves that live in a closet or, less often, in the table. Alignment is factory-good when new and factory-loose after forty Thanksgivings. A table lock under the top is a small brass or steel catch. Numbered leaves are a kindness some factories skipped.

The breakfront is the monument the sideboard used to be. Glass doors, lights (later fluorescent), a lower bank of drawers, a cornice that is a little too tall for a ranch ceiling. Silver plate and the wedding china go here. The lock is often a joke: a small key, a catch that no longer catches. Display is the job. The Federal sideboard’s bottle wells and butler’s work have moved to the pantry, if the house still has one, or to the kitchen.

## Etiquette after the war

Service is family-style even when the room is formal. The remaining hired help is rare. The wife is the pantry. Women’s magazines teach a table setting that is a Gilded Age memory without the footmen. Silver plate comes out. The breakfront displays what the sideboard used to lock. *House Beautiful* and *Better Homes and Gardens* are as much the designers of this room as Draper is. They teach a centerpiece, a cloth, a rule about candles, a child’s place that is not in this room on a school night.

This room is why later generations called dining rooms stuffy. They were stuffy. They were also a claim that dinner was not television. The television was in the living room. The dinette took Tuesday meatloaf. Sunday and Thanksgiving kept the mahogany. The open plan will reject the claim. The farmhouse table will reject the mahogany. For twenty years the claim held.

The racial and class facts of the formal room are quieter in the catalog than in the house. A closed dining room in a 1955 suburb is a white middle-class stage more often than not. The labor that made the suite is factory labor in North Carolina, Virginia, Michigan — not a joiner’s bench, not an enslaved waiter. The labor that served the meal is unpaid family labor, usually women’s. The Gilded Age essay has the footmen. This page has the hostess who read the magazine and then carried the roast.

## Hollywood gloss versus factory traditional

Do not let the decorator name eat the factory floor. Hollywood Regency is a look: lacquer, brass, a mirror, a pagoda or a palm, a chair that photographs beside a swimming-pool window. Factory traditional is a look: brown, shiny, a cabriole, a breakfront, a crystal fixture on a dimmer. They share a refusal of the chrome dinette and of Saarinen’s stem. They do not share a shop.

A true Regency-revival chair with a saber leg and a cane back can be a careful reproduction. A “Regency” chair with a gold-sprayed plastic mount and a black lacquer that chips at the knee is a 1960s product. Look at the brass. Solid cast mounts have weight. Stamped thin brass has a tin sound. Look at the lacquer edge where a leaf meets a rail. A factory has trouble there. A decorator shop has a little less trouble. Both chip.

Chinoiserie on a 1950s dining cabinet is a surface: a transfer, a painted panel, a bit of fret. It is not an eighteenth-century export table. The Baltimore painted essay already taught painted furniture as a real American shop practice. This chinoiserie is usually a print.

## How to look at a 1955 suite

Veneer, often. A plasticized finish that does not oil. Chairs with foam that has gone to dust. A table that is heavy and a little too shiny. If the label is inside a drawer, a North Carolina or Virginia factory name is likely. The later Southern factory story (Thomasville, Henredon, and peers) is this hour’s Grand Rapids. Look also at the chair rail’s underside for a stencil or a paper tag that a refinisher missed.

A Hollywood Regency chair with original fabric is rarer than the suburban suite. Date by foam, by screws (Phillips is expected; a square-drive is later), by the plug on any attached lighting in the breakfront. Fluorescent tubes and a yellowed diffuser are a 1960s–70s clock. LED strips in the same cabinet are a 2010s renovation. The cabinet can be older than its light.

Check the table’s slides for a hardware maker’s stamp. Check the legs for caster sockets that have been plugged. Check the chairs for stretchers that do not match the set — replacements after a rack, or a marriage of two suites. Seat-to-apron clearance: if the chairs hit the rail, they did not come with this table, or someone recut a seat.

If you eat in a closed formal dining room today, you are in a 1950s idea even if the table is 1810. The closure is the style. Modernism wanted the slum of legs gone. This room wanted the slum of everyday gone. Both are edits. The meal in the closed room is still the meal the Federal house invented a name for. The furniture is a conservative last stand. Last stands, in dining rooms, last longer than architects predict. Plenty of these suites are still set for Thanksgiving and covered the rest of the year. That cover is the history: a room too good to use, which is how rooms die.

Lift the cover. If the finish has alligatored under it, the room was closed in a house that still heated and cooled around it. If the finish is perfect and the chairs’ seats are split, the room was used and then abandoned. Both readings are American. Neither needs a decorator’s name to be true.
