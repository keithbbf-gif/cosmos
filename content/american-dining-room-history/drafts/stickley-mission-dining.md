# Stickley and Mission dining

Gustav Stickley’s Dining Table no. 631 is a plank. The Stickley Project notes put the idea in 1901 — a drawing for Things Wrought by the United Crafts at Eastwood, and the first issue of *The Craftsman*, then with a medial stretcher on the floor — numbered about 1904, made circa 1902–15, thirty by ninety-six by forty-eight inches in a recorded example. Catalogue D called it heavy construction, a two-inch top, a table for a large dining room that did not need to close, also used as a director’s table. Fumed oak. A price, in one list, of sixty-six dollars. That is a dining table that has stopped pretending to be French.

The Bungalow Trestle, model no. 445, circa 1901, twenty-eight and a quarter by seventy by twenty-nine inches in the Sotheby’s example, is rarer — two known, Cathers cites a period photograph from Stickley’s Rochester Arts and Crafts exhibition. Most American Mission dining tables are neither 631 nor 445. They are factory cousins: square oak legs, a mediocre top, a stain that is not a fume. Stickley is the sermon. The bungalow is the congregation.

## Two tables, two briefs

No. 631 and no. 445 are not the same argument in different lengths. The 631 is a resident plank: shoe feet, a deep apron in the better examples, a top thick enough that a plate sounds different. Catalogue D and the January 1909 Craftsman catalogue (Winterthur’s library holds a copy) keep the sixty-six-dollar figure; a Western edition of April 1912 lists eighty-four. `[CITE NEEDED: page images for the 1912 Western price if you are quoting it as a change, not a rumor.]` The 1901 *Craftsman* drawing still has a medial stretcher on the floor. The February 1902 publication drops it. That is a design being edited in public, in a magazine that was also a catalog.

No. 445 is a trestle for a smaller room. Seventy inches is not ninety-six. Twenty-nine inches of width is not forty-eight. The Sotheby’s table, Craftsman Workshops, Eastwood, oak, marked twice with the firm’s decal, is one of two known. Gray’s *Early Work of Gustav Stickley* and Cathers’s 2003 monograph are the pins. A trestle is a medieval and a Shaker thought. Stickley puts keyed tenons on it and calls it bungalow. Most bungalows could not take a 631. They could imagine a 445. They bought, more often, a square factory table that borrowed the stain.

Custom extension Stickley tables exist — the Vancroft square table with many leaves, unmarked, a Rago story. The published ideal is still a fixed plank. That is a class assumption. Space is the hidden material.

## Eastwood, not a lone bench

The Craftsman Workshops at Eastwood, New York, were a factory with a magazine. *The Craftsman* taught rooms. The catalogs sold them. Through-tenons, exposed pins, a hardware of iron, leather seats on chairs no. 366 and its kin — the dining suite is a moral suite. Eastlake asked for honesty and got incising. Stickley asks for honesty and shows the tenon. The tenon can still be a style. Shops that copied the tenon without the fit are the volume.

Stickley adopted a Morris motto, *Als ik kan*, and a joiner’s compass as a mark. Over a hundred retailers, in later firm history, carried Craftsman goods. The Home Builders Club, Craftsman Farms, the house-building company — those are the brand expanding past a table. In 1915 he filed for bankruptcy. The brothers’ later firm absorbed the workshops. None of that makes Eastwood a lone bench. It makes Eastwood a factory that advertised itself as a workshop, which is a different honesty problem from Grand Rapids, and not a smaller one.

## Marks, fume, and the scratch

Fumed oak is ammonia vapor on tannin-rich oak, not a stain from a can. The color goes in. A later stain sits on. Look at a scratch. If the brown is only a skin, you are in a catalog that borrowed the sermon. If the color is in the wood and the ray fleck is bright, you are closer to a fume. Quartersawn figure is the show. Flatsawn Mission is a cheaper look. The woods-series quartersawn draft, if present, is the cut hour.

David Cathers’s *Gustav Stickley* (2003) and the catalogs (January 1909 at Winterthur’s library, among others) are the pins. Red decals and branded marks date chairs and tables. The large red manufacturer’s decal with the compass and *Als ik kan* is the mark collectors want on a 631 leg. Branded marks come in later production. Paper labels die. Unmarked “Stickley” dining tables are a field of tears. A Grand Rapids mission suite with no mark and a stained finish is not a lost Eastwood piece. It is the congregation.

Hardware is iron, dark, a plate that looks like a plate. Brass that has been shined to gold on a Craftsman escutcheon is a later household deciding the sermon was too dull. The Newark show notes have seen that polish. It is use. It is also a refusal of the magazine.

## What the table refuses

It refuses crotch mahogany, gilt, and the extension banquet as an ideal — though custom extension Stickley tables exist. The ideal published table is a fixed plank for a room that has the space. Bungalows are not huge. Many Mission dining rooms used smaller square tables and a settle against the wall. The settle is dining furniture if people eat with a plate on their lap, which they did, to Stickley’s dismay or not.

Chairs are posts and slats. Corseted backs. Leather. They are heavy. They do not stencil. They do not stack. Model no. 366 — side chairs and a captain’s chair in the auction record, branded marks, leather or oilcloth later — is the dining chair that belongs with the plank. Recovered seats are expected. Original leather that has gone stiff is a document. They are the opposite of Hitchcock and they sell to some of the same children of Hitchcock buyers, now reading a magazine.

Look at a 366 the way you look at a 631. Through-tenons at the back posts should read as pegs on the back, not as painted circles. A slat that is only nailed into a rabbet is a factory cousin. Seat rails that have been recut for a later cushion will show a second set of holes. The corset — the inward curve of the back posts — is the sit. People who find Mission chairs upright are sitting as they would in a club chair. Dinner here is a choir stall with leather. The captain’s chair at the end is a host armchair that has learned not to gilt. Arms must still clear the plank’s thick apron. If they do not, someone is using a parlor settle as a dining chair.

A 631-type top is two inches in the catalog language. You feel the thickness in the way a Federal leaf does not feel. The plate makes a different sound. That sound is the style.

## Mission as a wider word

Stickley, the Roycrofters, Limbert, the Stickley brothers who were not Gustav, California shops, Grand Rapids “Mission” that is only dark oak — the word leaks. A dining-room history that says Mission and means only Eastwood is a brand history. This slug is Stickley-centered because the documents are good. Limbert’s cutouts and a Grand Rapids mission suite deserve their own sentences: factory, cheaper, sometimes good. Berkey & Gay, the Grand Rapids essay notes, largely passed on Mission. Other factories did not. Dark oak and a square leg were enough to steal the word.

Prairie School dining (the next essay) shares the oak and not the magazine. Wright’s tables are architecture. Stickley’s are furniture with a magazine attached. A Stickley 631 can move to another bungalow. A Wright dining table often cannot without losing the room. That is the fork in the oak.

*The Craftsman* published dining rooms as much as it published tables: a settle, a board, a fixture, a sideboard with iron pulls, a rug the magazine also wanted to sell. A household could buy the table and ignore the rug. Many did. The sermon was a suite. The congregation bought a piece. That gap is why so many “Mission dining rooms” are a stained oak table and six unmatched chairs. The magazine called that incomplete. The meal did not.

## Labor and the fume

Eastwood workers were factory workers. The rhetoric is workshop. Both can be true. The fuming room is a chemical room. Leather seats were a trade. Women did not become the advertised craftsmen. The dining room that bought 631 employed, or did not, a servant; bungalow life is often servantless. The table’s two-inch plank is a lot of oak.

Look under for pins that go through and pins that stop. Then look at the finish in a scratch. Then decide whether you are in Eastwood or in a catalog that only borrowed the sermon. Either can hold dinner. Only one is the document this page is for. The fume, the tenon, and the magazine are the three tests. Fail one and you still have a table. Fail all three and you have dark oak with a story.
