The Federal sideboard was a long table with locks. The Renaissance Revival sideboard is a building. Pedestals at the ends, a high back, a pediment, carved heads, fruit, game, a marble slab in the middle, walnut or ebonized wood, sometimes incised gold. Herter Brothers at the top. Grand Rapids and a hundred factories at the volume. The dining room of the 1870s hangs its identity on this wall. Eliza Leslie’s “closet” has become a monument.

This is the form after the Civil War, when a broadened middle class and a factory industry can deliver carving by the linear foot. It is also the form the next generation will hate. Eastlake will incise instead of fruit. Colonial Revival will put a slender Hepplewhite board back. The mountain of walnut will go to the barn and then to the antiques mall.

## Architecture on a plinth

A typical high-style board is wider than a Federal serpentine and much taller: a serving height plus a back that can reach a picture rail. Mirrors appear. So do cabinets for glass. The marble is back, not as Charleston’s mixing stone only, but as a washable luxury in a sooty city. Game and fish in the carving advertise a dining room that wants you to think about food even when the table is bare. That is a change. Hepplewhite inlay advertised gentility. Renaissance carving advertises appetite.

The plan of the piece is a plinth, two pedestals or a run of cupboard doors, a serving slab, then a superstructure. The plinth is where the board pretends to be masonry. The pedestals hold bottles, plate, or nothing but the idea of storage. The superstructure is a gable, a broken pediment, a pair of finials, sometimes a clock niche that never held a clock. You read it the way you read a facade: base, piano, attic. A Federal board had no attic. This one does, and the attic is the style.

Herter Brothers (New York) made custom interiors in which the sideboard is part of a paneled room. Most Americans bought a factory board that suggested Herter from across the street. Applied carving, composition ornament, and walnut veneer over pine do that suggestion. Look at the back of a bunch of grapes. If it is a glued bun, you are in the factory. If it is carved through the solid, you are paying.

## The Vanderbilt buffet

Christian Herter’s dining room for William H. Vanderbilt at 640 Fifth Avenue, built and furnished 1879–82, is the monument at its most complete. The room was oak, Neo-Renaissance, dark on purpose. Contemporary photographs — the plate of “Mr. William H. Vanderbilt’s Dining-Room” in *Artistic Houses* (D. Appleton, 1883) — show a built-in buffet and cupboards treated as architecture, not as a piece you could sell apart from the house. Pairs of plump putti hold fruit swags. The cupboards held continental porcelain. An iconography of bounty, the Met’s notes say, governed the room: flowers, nuts, berries, the same motifs on furniture and paneling.

The Metropolitan Museum’s side chair 1994.80 is one of the chairs from that room. Herter Brothers, New York, 1879–82. Oak, brass, and leather (the leather now a replacement). Thirty-five by twenty-three and seven-eighths by twenty-one and three-quarters inches. The crest rail is a lush swag of flowers, nuts, and berries. A period source described the original covering as dull-red stamped leather, “relieved with gold in Cordova fashion.” Dining seats of the decade were often leather because gravy is not a parlor problem. Haircloth still existed. Leather wiped.

The Met is careful about what is copied and what is not. The fruit is Renaissance precedent. The legs and stretchers of 1994.80 are not: interlocking parts, a pendant loop, square rings, a carved sash, palmettes — a New York shop showing off in oak. That distinction matters for this page. The American Renaissance Revival sideboard is rarely a copy of a single Italian cassone. It is a nineteenth-century wall that borrowed fruit and pediments and then invented a factory scale.

Most Americans never stood in Vanderbilt’s dining room. They stood in front of a walnut board in a house with one maid or none, and they recognized the fruit from across a showroom. The high-style room is the citation. The factory board is the population.

## Walnut’s American hour

Black walnut is the Reconstruction dining-room wood: available, stainable, “honest” compared to rosewood, dark enough for the taste. Oak is coming. Mahogany has not left. Ebonized furniture — black with gilt lines — is the reform-adjacent cousin, sometimes still Renaissance in shape. The woods-series walnut drafts, if you have them, are the species hour. This page is the wall.

Secondary pine, lots of it. The monument is a skin. That is not a sneer. It is how you move a monument through a door in pieces. Look at the backboards: wide pine, often unfinished, sometimes numbered in pencil for reassembly. Look at the dust panels over the drawers, if there are drawers. Look at whether the marble is a full slab seated in a rebate or a thin skin on wood that will telegraph every heat ring. A board that comes apart at the pedestals is a board that was designed for a stair.

Burl panels, often walnut or ash, sit in the cupboard doors like pictures. They are veneer. They photograph as solid luxury. A later refinisher who sands through the burl has dated the piece by accident: the ground wood shows, usually pine or a cheaper walnut. Crotch mahogany still appears on expensive New York work. It is not the type wood of this hour. Walnut is.

## Applied fruit and composition

The grape is the tell, and the grape is a product. Shop carving cuts fruit from a block and leaves a back. Factory carving glues a bun, a spray, a stag’s head, a fish, onto a flat field. Composition ornament — sawdust, glue, a mold — photographs as carving from eight feet and chips as a different material. Gilding hides the seam. Incised lines around a panel can be a router’s cousin already, even before Eastlake’s name is stamped on the catalog page.

Look at the pediment first. That is where the factory spent the last dollar, because that is what you see from the parlor door. Then look at the returns: the sides of the superstructure, the backs of the finials, the underside of a carved lambrequin. Economy lives where a guest does not stand. A board with carved faces on the front and raw machine marks on the top of the plinth, under the marble, is an honest factory document. Do not strip the finish to “find the walnut.” The finish is part of the date.

Game and fish are not still-life for its own sake. They tell a guest that this room is about eating, even on a Tuesday when the table is a bare oval. A Federal inlay urn told a guest about taste. This carving tells a guest about appetite and about the money that could buy a carver, or a mold that looks like one. Both messages sat in American dining rooms at the same time, at different prices.

## Service, women, the lock

The lockable cabinet remains. Plate is still counted. The person who counts it is often a woman of the house as hired labor thins in some middle-class homes and thickens in others. Gilded Age dining will restore a staffed service at the top. The Renaissance board sits across that split: useful to a wife who keeps her own silver, and to a butler who keeps someone else’s.

*À la russe* is now ordinary in etiquette books. Dishes come from the board, or from a pantry through the board’s wall. The monument is a station. A Federal board could be a station too. This one looks like an altar, which is why later taste found it comic. Comedy is a later problem. In 1875 the altar was the point: a dining room that had specialized now wanted furniture as ceremonial as the service.

The marble slab is the working top. Hot plates, wet decanters, a carving rest. Stains that never appear in a catalog photograph are the board’s real life. If the slab has been replaced with wood, or with a later marble that does not fit the rebate, someone in the twentieth century decided the monument should be a side table. The holes for the old slab’s pins or the empty slot will say so.

## The china closet as a second wall

The dining-room “suit” of the 1870s–80s — table, chairs, sideboard, sometimes a serving table — often adds a china closet: a tall glazed cabinet, a drawer stack under glass, a companion rather than a replacement. The closet displays. The sideboard works, or pretends to. When the butler’s pantry later takes the working closet next door, the sideboard in the dining room can become even more of a monument. Leslie’s 1844 closet has split. This page’s wall is the half that stayed in the dining room to be seen.

Mirrors on the back of a sideboard multiply glass and plate. They also date the taste. A Federal board rarely wants a looking-glass that size. The Renaissance board wants you to see the room twice: the table, the cloth, the guests, and then the same scene in a dim rectangle over the marble. That is architecture talking. It is also a factory trick that makes a cheap board look deeper.

A serving table — lower, no pediment, sometimes with a single drawer — is the suit’s quieter member. It lives against a wall toward the pantry door. Collectors ignore it. Households used it. If you find a pedimented board without its serving table, you are looking at a fragment of a room, not a failure of the form.

## Grand Rapids

The factory city in Michigan is already making dining-room “suits” — the word they used — by the 1870s: table, chairs, sideboard, sometimes a serving table and china closet. The Renaissance Revival sideboard is the expensive face of that suit. Berkey & Gay and their neighbors exhibited the look at the 1876 Centennial in Philadelphia. `[CITE NEEDED: official Centennial award listing, if any, for a Grand Rapids Renaissance dining or chamber suite.]` The Grand Rapids essay takes the industry. Here, note that the monument scaled. A farm-town dining room in 1880 can have a smaller walnut board with a pediment and no Herter anywhere near it. That board is still this form.

If you face one in a museum, look up. The pediment is where the factory spent the last dollar. Then look at the marble for stains that were never meant to be seen in a photograph. Then look for casters or a split plinth. These things were moved by strong people. The Federal board was furniture. This is furniture pretending to be the house. When the house style changed, the pretense became a problem. Eastlake’s incised line is a criticism of this fruit. You can still put a plate on it.
