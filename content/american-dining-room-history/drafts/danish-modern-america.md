A teak dining table with tapered legs, a draw leaf at each end, and six chairs in paper cord or wool is not an American invention. It is an American dining room. After the war, Danish makers (and Swedish, and then American copies) sold a modern that was warmer than Knoll’s marble and more wooden than chrome. Department stores and specialty shops — the names collectors recite, from Georg Jensen’s shops to later mall “Danish” — put oil finishes into houses that had been golden oak or mahogany revival. The table extends. The chairs are light. The sideboard is a low credenza. The dining room, if it exists, looks like a living room.

Wegner, Juhl, Mogensen, Jacobsen (the last more chair than table) are the design names. Most American families bought unsigned teak. Both are history.

## Why a ranch house wanted teak

G.I. houses and ranch houses have low ceilings and a picture window. A high Renaissance board looks absurd. A low teak credenza looks like it was drawn for the window. The oil finish repairs with more oil. Children can sit on paper cord if you tell them not to kneel. The draw-leaf table is a dinette idea in a better wood: everyday four, holiday six or eight.

The draw leaf (the Danish *tillægsplade* habit, a leaf that stores under the top and pulls out) is older than the fashion. What the export table did was make the mechanism light. Wooden slides, sometimes metal, a top thin enough that the leaf’s thickness has to match or the cloth will tell. Alignment is the craft. A leaf that sits a sixteenth high is a shop that rushed or a house that warped the top beside a heater. Numbered leaves, when they exist, are a kindness. Many unsigned tables have no numbers. You learn the pair by the grain.

Teak (*Tectona grandis*) is a tropical hardwood with a colonial and then a plantation story. The USDA *Wood Handbook* treats it among the imported hardwoods: oily, stable relative to oak, a color that goes from golden to dark brown in light. The woods-series teak drafts, if present, are the ethics hour. This page will not wash the oil. It will say the suburban table is an imported forest. Old-growth teak and plantation teak are not the same board. A dealer who cannot say which is selling a color. `[CITE NEEDED: a dated Danish export figure for furniture teak into the United States in a sample year, 1958–1965.]`

Oak and rosewood appear too. American buyers learned “Danish” as a teak synonym. That is a market compression. Mogensen’s oak is a different piety: lighter, more cabinet, less tropical glow. Jacobsen’s molded chairs (the Ant, the Series 7) often sat at tables they did not match. The American room accepted that mix faster than it accepted Knoll’s marble.

## How the chairs are made, and how they fail

Paper cord is a twisted kraft paper yarn, not rush and not cane. It is wrapped around a rail in a pattern you can read from the underside: even tension, a clean turn at the corner, no staple pretending to be a wrap. Original cord darkens and sags. Re-cording is a craft with a literature. A chair re-corded in a polypropylene lookalike is a different object. Vinyl that wants to be cord is a 1970s confession.

Wegner’s Wishbone (CH24, Carl Hansen, 1949) is the chair Americans can name. The Y-back, the steam-bent arm, the cord seat — copies are a second forest. Look at the steam-bend’s smoothness, the join of arm to back, the depth of the seat. A wishbone that is too upright or too thick in the spindle is not Carl Hansen’s. This page will not invent a tell that a specialist would fight. It will say: sit. The chair reports.

Juhl’s chairs are more sculptural, more joint as jewelry. Mogensen’s are more carpenter. Unsigned dining chairs with a slightly scooped splat and tapered oak or teak legs are the volume. They are not failures. They are the Hitchcock of this hour: a type, a mill, a dining room that needed six of something light.

Wool upholstery on a teak frame is the winter chair. Foam fails. Webbing fails. A recovered seat on an honest frame is expected. A frame that has been stripped of its oil and sprayed with a hard lacquer is a 1990s object wearing a 1962 silhouette.

## American copies

Drexel, Lane, and others made “Danish” walnut and pecan suites. Drexel’s Declaration line is the named American example collectors use; pin a catalog year when you cite a particular suite. Pecan is a Southern wood doing teak’s job. The forest can be local even when the fashion is Copenhagen. Quality varies. Dovetails and a real oil finish are one grade. Printed grain and a spray lacquer are another. Both sat under meatloaf.

American of Martinsville, Founders, and a long list of “contemporary” suites borrowed the taper and not the oil. A pecan table with a plasticized film and a credenza that is a pecan-colored print over particleboard is still a dining-room document. It is not Danish. It is High Point answering a photograph from a shop on Fifty-Seventh Street.

Chairs: the wishbone and its cousins, then a hundred splats that are almost Wegner. The American copy is often heavier. Teak is expensive; walnut and pecan are what the Southern factories had. A “Danish” chair in pecan with a vinyl seat is a cross of three essays in this series: this one, the formal fifties, and the factory South.

## The credenza instead of the monument

Silver goes into a low cabinet. The lock is quieter. Sliding doors, thin legs, a long plinth or a set of tapers. Service is family-style again more often than *à la russe*. The dining room is less a theater. That is a social change as much as a furniture change. The servantless house, already true in the dinette, becomes stylish.

The credenza’s height is the tell against a Gilded Age sideboard. You can set a serving dish on it and still see the picture window. You cannot hide a punch bowl behind a splash of carved grapes. Display is horizontal. Plates lean in a shallow rack or they live in the kitchen. The butler’s pantry, if the ranch house ever had one, is a joke. The credenza is the pantry’s low ghost.

Some American copies added a hutch top to the credenza and made a breakfront in teak clothing. That hybrid is a 1960s compromise: the household still wanted to show china. The Danish low line did not. Look at the joint between upper and lower. A factory screw block is not a cabinetmaker’s cornice.

## How to look

Oil a teak table and you are in the maintenance the shop expected. Polyurethane it and you have made a 1990s object. Paint it white and you have made a farmhouse object (later essay). The Danish dining room in America lasted until Mediterranean pecan and then oak Colonial came back. Survivors are everywhere because the furniture was better than its decade’s jokes.

Sit. The chair will tell you if the cord is original. Turn it over. Count wraps. Look for a maker’s stamp on the rail — Danish factories marked more often than American “Danish” lines. The table will tell you if the leaves match: same oil, same fade, same thickness. A leaf that is raw on the underside and glossy on top has been stored in a closet while the main top took thirty years of sun from the picture window. That fade line is a date.

The credenza will tell you if anyone still owns that many plates. Empty shelves behind sliding glass are a 2020s document inside a 1962 body. Full shelves of stoneware are closer to the original brief. Silver, if present, is usually plate, and usually from the formal-fifties household that bought teak later and kept the service.

Check the legs for caster holes that should not be there (a later owner wanted a roll) and for stretchers that were added after a rack developed. Teak is stable for an imported hardwood; it is not immune. A table that has lived beside a sliding glass door will cup toward the glass. The *Wood Handbook*’s movement tables are the numbers. The dining-room fact is a bowl that no longer sits flat.

If the suite is pecan and the tag says Danish, keep the pecan and drop the tag. The Southern factory that made it is as American as Grand Rapids. The Copenhagen photograph was only the brief.

Georg Jensen Inc. in New York and a handful of specialty importers taught the expensive end: a signed Wegner, a Juhl table, a catalog that named the cabinetmaker. Department stores taught the middle: a teak-colored suite, a tag that said “Scandinavian,” a credit plan. Both routes put oil in American houses. The importer’s chair is the one monographs photograph. The department-store table is the one still in a basement in Ohio, a little dry at the end grain, a leaf that no longer sits flush. Take the leaf out and look at the slide. Wooden runners that have been waxed are a shop that expected you to live with the table. Metal slides that have been painted over are a later repair or an American copy. Either can hold a plate. Only the first is the export object this page is for.
