# Empire pillar and claw

After about 1815 the Federal dining table puts on weight. The tapered leg thickens into a pillar. The foot becomes a carved paw, a scroll, or a hairy claw. The top goes from a quiet mahogany board to a crotch-veneered disc or oval that looks like a flame under polish. Leaves still add length, but the image of the table is now a central support and a cloth that falls onto a carved animal. American dealers call this Empire. The word is borrowed, stretched, and applied to shops that never saw a Bonaparte.

Lannuier’s New York was the accurate French import. This page is the American product: Phyfe’s later shop, anonymous New York and Philadelphia factories, Boston’s Briggs-type extension tables already thinking about cranks, Southern walnut versions of the same silhouette. The dining room that holds this table is named, specialized, and, in town, middle-class as well as mercantile.

## The pillar as a knee solution

A pedestal puts the structure in the middle. Diners keep their shins. The engineering problem moves to the top: a wide leaf on one pin, seasonal wobble, a split if the crossbanding fights the ground. Glue blocks radiate under the top like a wagon wheel. A restorer who removes them to “clean up” the underside has removed the table’s brain.

The pillar itself is a turned baluster, often with carved acanthus at the throat, sitting on a plinth or on four splayed saber legs. Early Federal pedestals can still be a thin Sheraton urn. The Empire pillar is a fat column. If you are on the floor, the tell is diameter and the carved paw. Brass lion-paw casters are common. The table is heavy. Two people move it, or they do not. The dining room’s carpet begins to show a track.

Double pedestals for long tables are a banquet answer. Each end has a pillar; leaves drop in between. Alignment is still the craft. Brass clips and pins replace some of the D-end’s rule-joint poetry. The click is metal now. A three-part Federal table could already do this with reeded legs. The Empire version does it with two carved animals and a valley of leaves that must be numbered.

Paw feet want casters. Look for the original plate and a later wheel. A paw that has been cut down to sit still on a new rug is a common insult. The shop meant the table to roll for the extra leaf and for the weekly clean.

## Crotch, flame, and the poplar ground

Crotch mahogany veneer on a yellow-poplar drawer or apron is the late Federal and Empire sandwich. On a dining top it is a picture. The crotch is the fork of the tree, figured like a feather or a flame. The poplar moves; the crotch wants to stay a picture. If the glue holds and the ground was dry, the picture lasts. If a later refinisher sands through the leaf, the green tulip streak shows. That chip is the lesson. The USDA *Wood Handbook* is the species behavior. Here it is a dining-room look: darker, redder, more “mahogany” than a 1790 Federal table ever was.

American Empire stain-and-varnish habits run toward a red that 1795 shops would not have recognized. Refinishers in 1900 made it redder. An original surface, when you find one, is browner, deeper, less ketchup. Anderson’s *Mahogany* is again the forest at the other end of the ship. A flame veneer in Boston is still a receipt.

Crossbanding around a circular top is a place the glue fails first. The grain of the band runs across the ground’s movement. Seasonal play is not always a death sentence. A top that has been planed flat in 1960 and re-veneered is a different object. Rock the top gently. Count the blocks. See whether the veneer is a skin or a later wrap.

## Chairs to match

Klismos and saber legs, then heavier scrolled chairs with haircloth or more comfortable stuffings. The Grecian is still the language. It is louder. The *London Chair-Makers’ and Carvers’ Book of Prices* (1802, with an 1808 supplement) had already priced curule and Grecian seating for English shops. American Empire chairs are that price-book language thickened: a tablet crest, a saber, a paw, sometimes a brass mount that is a cast cousin of Lannuier’s ormolu.

Gilt stenciling on black — fancy chairs continuing — sits at the cheaper table. Mahogany veneered chairs with brass mounts sit at the richer one. Suites become more thinkable because factories and large shops can repeat a carving. Hitchcock will flood the middle with painted Grecian. That is the next seating essay. The Empire table does not need Hitchcock, but many rooms mixed them.

A saber chair at a pillar table is a knee conversation. The table’s structure is in the middle; the chair’s structure is a backward sweep that wants space. The combination works if the pillar is truly central and the chairs are not too deep. It fails if someone has put a trestle foot where a saber wants to land.

## Mechanism waits in the same decade

Robert Jupe’s British patent 6788, March 1835, is a circular table whose sections diverge so filling pieces can drop in. American dining rooms felt it as an idea more than as a population of tables. Cornelius Briggs of Boston — patent 1843 in the Bray and Carr account — made accordion, hand-crank extension tables that actually filled rooms. The patents essay owns those objects. This page owns the carved base they often sat on. A pointed Gothic chair can sit at a crank table later. An Empire paw can sit at a slide now. Gothic and mechanism are not enemies; neither are Empire and a crank. The costume and the joint are different jobs.

## Region and the word “classical”

New York owns the high style. Philadelphia and Boston make their own pillars. The South makes walnut and cherry pedestals that catalogs call “classical” when they do not want to say Empire. Thomas Day’s mid-century boards share the weight if not the New York paw. Inland shops cut the silhouette in whatever hardwood they have. A pillar table in figured maple is a New England sentence.

The word “American Classical” in auction catalogs is a peace treaty between Empire, late Federal, and a claw that looks Roman if you squint. Do not fight the catalog at dinner. Look at the pillar. If it is a thin Sheraton urn, you are earlier. If it is a fat baluster with acanthus and a paw, you are here.

## Service and the heavier room

Sideboards grow pedestals too. Cellarets become objects. The dining room darkens: red walls, more curtains, a carpet that hides the paw’s caster track. Service *à la russe* is coming. The pillar table’s clear knee space is convenient for a servant moving behind chairs. Whether shops thought that is a `[CITE NEEDED]`. The convenience is real.

Eliza Leslie’s *House Book* (1844) already treats the sideboard as a closet the middle-class dining room must have. Berman’s inventories watch the specialized room spread. The Empire table is the resident that room was invented to hold. It does not fold against the wall. It sits there on Tuesday, slightly in the way, proving you dine.

Enslaved labor in Southern dining rooms of the 1830s is at its most specialized: a room, a board, a prescribed service, a person who cannot leave. The labor essay takes the names. This page takes the furniture’s complicity in that specialization — a room that only works with staff. In Northern hired-service houses the furniture is similar and the legal status is not. Do not flatten.

Leaves should be numbered or at least obviously mates. A beautiful pillar with two leaves from another marriage is a common antique-trade animal. Check the underside color. Check the clip pattern. Check whether the crotch figure continues across a joint that was never meant to be a joint. A later wrap of crotch on a tired solid top is a skin. The blocks underneath will tell you if the shop or the 1920s did the work.

If you own a pillar table, get on the floor. Count the glue blocks. See whether the veneer is a skin or a later wrap. Rock the top gently. Then sit and notice your knees. That comfort is why the form ate the swing-leg. The gateleg was clever. The pillar is selfish in the middle and generous at the chairs. Empire dining is that selfishness polished.
