The sideboard made for Caleb Hazard Richmond, now at the North Carolina Museum of History, is the dining object to stand in front of. Mahogany and mahogany veneer, yellow pine, tulip poplar, walnut, 1840–1855, attributed to Thomas Day, Milton, North Carolina. Chipstone’s 2013 essay, “The Missing Chapter in the Life of Thomas Day,” publishes a detail of the scrolled mirror support and names the donors: Museum of History Associates and Mr. Thomas S. Erwin. The S is too big. That is the point. Day’s shop took a conservative sideboard plan and put exuberant scrolls on it until the piece moved.

Thomas Day (1801–1861) was a free Black cabinetmaker in a slave state. His father, John Day, was a cabinetmaker. The family moved from Virginia — Dinwiddie County in the Chipstone account — into North Carolina. Thomas advertised in the *Milton Gazette & Roanoke Advertiser* on 1 March 1827: mahogany, walnut, and stained furniture on hand, fashionable and common bedsteads, repairing and varnishing. He had been in Milton since about 1823 (a later letter to his daughter implies it). He bought property in 1827. In 1830 he married Aquilla Wilson, a free Black woman from Virginia; an 1826 law blocked her migration until sixty-one white men petitioned the Assembly to let her in. That petition is part of the furniture. It says what the town thought the shop was worth.

## A shop that also built the room

The Smithsonian American Art Museum’s Day exhibition text is the clean public summary: the only documented American cabinetmaker to offer clients both architectural elements for their Greek Revival houses and furniture using the same classical motifs. Woodwork in about eighty houses in rural North Carolina and Virginia has been attributed to the shop. Newel posts spiral or take a shape like his initials. Chipstone publishes twenty-five distinctive S-shaped newels in the attribution group, including the post at Woodside, about 1838 — Caleb Hazard Richmond’s house, the same client as the museum sideboard. The dining room and the stair can be one hand.

Pews at Milton’s Presbyterian church are walnut with tulip poplar, still in use. Chipstone dates the church about 1837 and the pews to that campaign; Day joined the congregation in 1841. Rev. Nehemiah Henry Harding, pastor 1835–1849, is in the same essay as a friend whose words appear in Weld’s *American Slavery As It Is* (1839). The oral tradition then starts arguing about templates stored at Union Tavern. Chipstone is where the pew story gets disciplined.

Dining rooms in those houses received sideboards, tables, and chairs from the same vocabulary as the newels. The S-scroll on a mirror bracket is a cousin of the S on a stair. A planter could buy a Day interior and a Day board and have a house that was one hand. That is rarer than a matching suite from a catalog.

Patricia Phillips Marshall and Jo Ramsay Leimenstoll, *Thomas Day: Master Craftsman and Free Man of Color* (2010), is the monograph. A three-part sideboard with cabinet, marble workspace, and Day’s relief-carved feet — the Waverly / Cuningham type discussed at auction with reference to Marshall and Leimenstoll figs. 5.62–5.63 — shows how far the shop could push a dining-room wall. Do not treat every scrolled Piedmont board as Day. Attribution is a specialist’s job. Construction, history, and the motif’s particular energy are the evidence.

## Union Tavern and the yard

Union Tavern in Milton was home and workshop. Chipstone publishes the brick front, about 1818, formerly the Yellow Tavern, a stagecoach stop on the route to Petersburg. The Dan River and the rails made a town that could support a cabinetmaker of this scale. The 1827 advertisement is a shop talking to a tobacco county: stock on hand, not only commissions, stained furniture as well as mahogany, repair as well as new work. That mix is how a Piedmont shop stays open.

The shop produced more than dining furniture. Chipstone lists cribs, caskets, architectural components, whatnots, open-pillar bureaus, and at least twelve documented “lounges” — settees. The North Carolina Museum of History’s Bass-Engle lounge, walnut with yellow pine, 1845–1855, is one. A rosewood-veneered side chair of 1855–60 in the same museum is the shop keeping up with an “exuberant (French)” mid-century. Day did not become Phyfe. The fluidity Marshall and popular writers describe — serpentine curves, three-dimensional ornament, motion that makes neighboring shops look staid — is a Piedmont sentence. Tobacco money in Caswell County paid for it. Enslaved labor in the fields paid for the tobacco.

Day’s shop itself used workmen. The labor politics of a free Black master in a slave economy are not a children’s story. He owned enslaved people. That fact belongs next to the petition and the pews. `[CITE NEEDED: the documented count and dates of enslaved workers in Day’s shop — Marshall and Leimenstoll.]` Chipstone is plain: he was treated as an exception in part because he owned slaves and because his trade served the planter class.

When the economy turned and the war came, the shop’s world ended. Day died in 1861.

## What the board is made of

S-scrolls that are structural-looking, not appliqué timid. Veneer over pine and poplar. A gallery or mirror that is more animated than a Baltimore Federal eagle. Feet that are carved, not just turned. A history in Caswell, Person, or the adjacent Virginia counties. A “Day” sideboard from Ohio with no paper is a wish.

The Richmond board is a conservative case with an immoderate top. Chipstone’s phrase is “otherwise conservatively designed sideboards” with “exuberant and decidedly oversize ornamental scrolls.” Look at the secondary woods the museum lists: yellow pine, tulip poplar, walnut. That is a Southern shop sandwich. Mahogany is the show. The pine and poplar are the passport. Dovetails in those secondaries, glue blocks under the top, a back meant for a wall — the ordinary cabinet work has to be good or the S-scrolls are a costume on a weak box.

Look at a chip in the veneer. The pine or poplar ground will show, pale, sometimes with a yellow-pine resin streak. That chip is the Southern sandwich, the same secondary contract Charleston writes in cypress and New York writes in tulip poplar. Day’s distinction is not a secret wood. It is what he did on top of the wood: an S that behaves like architecture.

Tables from the shop exist in the literature and in houses. They are less published than the newels and the boards. `[CITE NEEDED: a firmly attributed Day dining table with published photos and woods.]` Until that pin, do not invent a trestle. The sideboard is the dining-room proof. Chairs exist — the 1855–60 rosewood-veneered side chair is one published type — and they are still not the long board.

A whatnot or étagère, like the one Chipstone publishes for Milton merchant John Wilson, 1853–60, mahogany veneer over pine, poplar, and walnut, is dining-adjacent: a place for ceramic that is not the lockable sideboard. Keep it in a clause. This slug owns the board and the room around it.

## Style without New York

Day kept up with fashion: Greek Revival, then the heavier mid-century. He did not become a New York product. Inland shops cut the Empire silhouette in whatever hardwood they have. Day cut it in mahogany when the client could pay, and in walnut when the forest was closer. The Empire essay’s pillar-and-claw is the coastal thickening. Day’s S is a different sentence in the same decade.

The dining room in a Caswell Greek Revival house is not a Fulton Street copy. It may have a Day newel you passed on the way in, a Day board on the serving wall, and a table from another bench or another year. Mixed shops in one room are the document here too. The difference is that the architecture and the board can be the same shop. Phyfe did not carve your stair.

## Why this page is in a national series

American dining furniture history that runs Boston–Newport–Philadelphia–New York and then jumps to Stickley has erased the Piedmont. Day is not a regional curiosity. He is a documented master whose dining-room work is in a state museum and a Smithsonian show. He is also a reminder that “Southern furniture” is not only Charleston marble and not only anonymous enslaved joiners. It is both, and it is a free Black shop that made the planter’s wall.

If you stand in front of the Richmond sideboard, look at the scroll until it seems too much. Then remember the petition. The town needed the shop enough to break its own law for Aquilla. The furniture is that need, in mahogany. Then look at the pine on the underside. The forest and the labor are there whether the label says so or not.
