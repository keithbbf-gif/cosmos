Eero Saarinen wanted to clear “the slum of legs” under the table. Knoll’s Pedestal Collection — designed 1956–57, in production from 1956 — is a cast-aluminum stem and a round or oval top in marble, wood, laminate. The Tulip chairs match. A dining room that buys this set has decided that Empire paws, Mission posts, and four oak legs are clutter. The decision is aesthetic and also practical: fewer legs to mop around, fewer collisions with shins. The pillar table of 1820 already knew the shin problem. Saarinen made the pillar a single stem and called the old forest a slum.

Charles and Ray Eames are the other American modern dining: molded plywood chairs (the DCW and later fiberglass and wire), tables that are thinner, a California and Cranbrook intelligence that is less formal than Knoll’s marble. People eat at Eames tables. People also put Eames chairs at older tables. The mix is the American room.

## Cranbrook, MoMA, and the office

Eliel Saarinen ran Cranbrook. Eero grew up in that argument: architecture as a total interior, furniture as a sentence in a building, not a suite bought after the walls dried. Florence Schust Knoll, a Cranbrook friend, pulled Eero into Knoll Associates’ orbit after the war. The Womb chair (1948) is lounge, not dining. The Pedestal group is the dining brief. Knoll stamps the logo and Saarinen’s signature on authentic bases. That stamp is the Hitchcock stencil of the luxury line. Copies are a forest. Look at the stem’s seam, the coating (Rilsan on aluminum), the knife-edge of the top. A heavy fake stem is a slum of its own.

The 1940 Museum of Modern Art Organic Design in Home Furnishings competition, which Charles Eames and Eero Saarinen won together, is the earlier collaboration. The prize chairs were molded plywood shells, still experimental, still closer to a studio than a shipping dock. Dining as a product comes later. Herman Miller puts the Eames plywood dining chairs into production after 1946. Knoll puts the Pedestal tables and Tulip chairs into production a decade after that. MoMA’s taste and Knoll’s sales force are the institutional half of American mid-century dining. The suburban half is a knockoff tulip in a breakfast nook, a walnut-colored laminate oval, a set of fiberglass shells around a table that never saw a Knoll invoice.

Florence Knoll’s planning unit sold offices first: a desk, a conference table, a lounge that photographed as architecture. Houses followed the office photograph. A Pedestal dining table in a suburban dining room is an office idea that came home. That route matters. Empire and Federal tables were shop objects that entered houses through cabinetmakers and later factories. This table entered houses through a showroom that already knew how to sell a corporation a floor.

## What the table is made of

Marble is cold and proud and stains. Laminate is the honest American top. Wood veneer is the compromise. The base is metal, always, if it is Saarinen’s idea. A wooden tulip is someone else’s. The stem is cast aluminum in two halves, joined, coated. The joint is a vertical seam you can find with a fingernail. Original Rilsan is a nylon coating, slightly waxy, not a thick paint skin. Later refinishes and copies often look wetter or chalkier. `[CITE NEEDED: Knoll’s own language for the coating change over time, if any, and a dated specification sheet.]`

Tops come round and oval. The oval is the American dining move: more places, more turkey, more of the old banquet problem without the old banquet legs. A round Pedestal in a small room is sculpture. An oval Pedestal in a room that still has a dado is a fight between two centuries. The knife-edge — a thin rim, not a breadboard, not a thumbnail molding — is the date. A thick bullnose on a “tulip” is a later or cheaper object.

Eames tables are a different engineering. Plywood tops, later laminates, a wire base or the segmented metal base that reads as a cluster of stems rather than one wine-glass column. Do not collapse the two shops. Both are modern. Only one called legs a slum. Herman Miller’s marks (a medallion, a paper label, later a stamp) sit under the top or on a rail. Absence of a mark is not proof of a fake; presence of a wrong mark is.

Fiberglass chairs at a marble table are a period mix. So are upholstered Knoll chairs. Haircloth is gone. Foam is here. The sit is lower and more relaxed than Phyfe’s upright. Dinners are longer and less formal. The dining room, if it still exists, is opening toward the living room. The open-plan essay takes the wall that disappeared. This furniture is ready for that disappearance: it looks like sculpture in a space, not a suite against a dado.

## Eames chairs at other people’s tables

The DCW (dining chair, wood legs) and DCM (dining chair, metal legs) are plywood shells. The later fiberglass armchair and side chair, and the wire chair with a pad, are the ones that turn up at oak farm tables and at IKEA tops fifty years on. That reuse is not a betrayal. It is how American rooms work. Hitchcock chairs sat at mahogany tables. Press-backs sat at golden-oak pedestals. An Eames shell at a 1925 extension table is the same habit.

What to look at on the chair: the shock mounts that join shell to base (they dry, they creak, they are a repair item), the edge of the plywood (original thinness versus a later thick copy), the fiberglass’s rope edge or its absence, the color (early colors are a collector literature; this page will not recite a price list). A shell that has been drilled for a different base is a document of use. A shell with a new base sold as “original configuration” is a wish.

Ray Eames’s share of the work is not a caption courtesy. The plywood compound curve, the fabric and color decisions, the films that taught the public how the furniture wanted to be seen — those are dining-room facts if the room learned its taste from a photograph. Charles alone on a label is a shop habit the literature has been correcting for decades.

## Who bought it, and who copied it

Offices, then houses. Architects, then the readers of *Playboy* and *House Beautiful*. Prices were never Hitchcock prices. The copies were. A 1965 suburban “modern” dining set in walnut-colored laminate and brass-anodized aluminum is the Grand Rapids of this hour — different city, same job. North Carolina and Midwestern factories learned a tapered metal leg and a thin top the way they had learned a cabriole. The mechanism underneath is often a simple set of screws into inserts, not a Saarinen casting.

Labor is factory labor in Michigan, Pennsylvania, Italy (for some Knoll), California for Eames. Not a joiner’s bench. The studio-furniture essay is the bench that continues anyway. Knoll and Herman Miller are factories with designers’ names on the ticket. That is a different authorship than Phyfe’s shop, and a different one than Grand Rapids’ anonymous carving room, but it is still a factory. Assistants, mold-makers, polishers, upholsterers. The romantic pair in a California studio is a caption problem if it erases the plant.

Imports and unlicensed copies arrive quickly. Italian and Asian stems, marble of a different grade, a top that is too thick, a chair whose cup is a little wrong. The American dining room of 1968 might hold an authentic Knoll table and six unsigned chairs, or the reverse. Look at each object. Suites are a sales idea. Rooms are mixtures.

## How to look

Get under the top. On a claimed Pedestal, find the Knoll / Saarinen marks on the base. Follow the seam. Check whether the coating is a thin original skin or a later paint. From a chair, look at the underside of the shell: old fiberglass is a particular roughness; new polypropylene is another. On an Eames plywood chair, the inner face of the legs and the condition of the rubber mounts will tell you more than the show veneer.

Put your feet out at a real Pedestal table. There is nowhere to hit. That nowhere is the design. Then look at the stem until it looks like a wine glass. Then look at a Federal pedestal in your memory and admit the family. Saarinen did not invent the single support. He edited it until the claw died. American dining furniture is often that: an edit, named as a revolution. The slum quote is a good edit. Knoll’s longer quoted language — an “ugly, confusing, unrestful world” of typical undercarriages — is the same edit in a full sentence.

Measure height. Dining height is still dining height. A coffee-table tulip in a dining room is a later confusion. A conference-scale oval in a small house is an office leftover. The meal still needs a cloth if the marble is to survive the wine. The cloth is older than Cranbrook. It is the one piece of dining furniture modernism never quite killed.

If the table is a copy and the chairs are honest shells, say so at dinner. The history is the mix, not the invoice. A suburban laminate oval with one original stem and five replacements is a document of how long the slum of legs stayed cleared — and of how badly the copies wanted the same photograph.
