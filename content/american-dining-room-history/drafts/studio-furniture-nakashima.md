# Studio furniture after Nakashima

George Nakashima’s dining tables are slabs. The live edge stays. Butterfly keys in contrasting wood stitch a split the way a cabinetmaker stitches, not the way a factory hides. The bases are trestles or a late modern geometry. The wood is named — walnut, cherry, English oak, a particular tree. The Conoid chair and the long table at New Hope, Pennsylvania, are the public images. The Mira Nakashima continuation of the shop is a living fact. This page is the one-off dining table as an American type after the factories won everything else.

Wharton Esherick’s earlier, more sculptural furniture, Sam Maloof’s chairs (more rocker than dining, but the shop ethic), Wendell Castle’s stacks, a hundred less famous makers — the studio movement is a dining-room option for people who will wait and pay. It is also a cousin of any hardwood shop that still works a bench: a named wood, a client, a refusal of the suite.

## New Hope

Nakashima settled the shop at New Hope, Bucks County, after the war. The campus — house, workshop, later the Conoid Studio — is the place the photographs come from. This page will not invent a date for every building or an accession for every table. *The Soul of a Tree* (1981) is the maker’s own language: a particular board, a tree’s history, a joinery that lets a split stay a split. Mira Nakashima’s continuation of the shop, and her own catalog of the work, is the living pin. If you need a chair or table identified, the shop’s records and the monographs are the route. A “Nakashima-style” slab on a marketplace site is a silhouette.

The Conoid chair — a sculpted seat, a back of spindles, a geometry that is not Windsor and not Knoll — is the dining chair the public can name. It is lighter than it looks in a photograph. The long dining tables, walnut or otherwise, are slabs on trestles or on a Conoid-related base. Guest count is limited. That limit is a design. The Federal banquet table wanted twenty. The slab wants eight and a reservation. Leaves are not the point. Many studio tables do not extend. If you need Thanksgiving to grow, this is the wrong brief, or you need a second table.

Internment and Nakashima’s wartime history belong in any honest biography and are not this dining essay’s center. They are in the monographs. The table does not wash them. A dining-room history that treats New Hope as a style only, without the shop’s actual century, is a mood board.

## A tree, not a species-as-product

Nakashima’s language is a particular board. The woods-series drafts speak in species and Janka. The studio table speaks in a flitch. Dining happens on a surface that was a tree’s diameter. American black walnut (*Juglans nigra*) is the wood Americans can see from across a room. Cherry, English oak, a Persian walnut, a claro — the shop’s catalogs name them. The USDA *Wood Handbook* will give you walnut’s shrinkage against oak’s. The dining-room fact is a slab that moves a little across a heating season and a finish that wants oil, not a film that locks the board into a crack.

Live edge is the bark-side contour, cleaned, not a factory’s routed “live” wave. Look at the edge. A real slab edge has a history of the tree and of the sawyer. A routed edge has a bit radius that repeats. A live edge filled with epoxy until it is a smooth river is a different object (the farmhouse-industrial cousin, the 2010s product). A Nakashima top is oiled and will take a ring. Maintenance is the theology.

Butterfly keys are structural and graphic. They are also a signature people now apply as a decal on tables that do not need them. A key in a stable top is an ornament. A key across a moving split is a joint. Look at whether the split goes through. Look at whether the key is let in from the show face only (a picture) or is a full-thickness butterfly (a stitch). Contrasting wood — walnut in cherry, cherry in walnut — is a choice you can see from a standing height. Matching wood is a quieter shop.

Breadboards and battens are the other ways to hold a wide top. Studio practice varies. A slab that has been dried badly will move no matter how pretty the keys are. Ask about the drying. A shop that cannot say how the flitch was dried is selling a photograph.

## Labor and the name

A studio is a shop with a name on the ticket. Assistants exist. The romantic lone maker is a caption problem, as Phyfe’s workshop was. Nakashima’s shop had hands. Say so. The difference from Grand Rapids is scale and authorship, not the absence of other people. Mira’s shop has hands. Esherick’s shop had hands. Maloof’s shop had hands. The name on the underside is a responsibility, not a claim that one man cut every joint.

Wharton Esherick’s Paoli, Pennsylvania, work is earlier and more sculptural: a stair, a hammer-handle chair, a dining interior that is closer to a carved room than to a slab on trestles. Sam Maloof’s Alta Loma, California, shop is more chair than table — the rocker is the public object — but the dining chairs and the shop ethic belong on this page. Wendell Castle’s stack-laminated furniture is a third pole: the table as a carved mass, not a flitch. Do not mash them into one “craft” caption. They disagree, and the disagreement is the history.

The American Craft Council, the university shops (RISD, Rochester, a long list), and the furniture programs that trained a generation after 1960 are the institutional half. The suburban one-off — a maker in a pole barn, a walnut slab, a steel base from a fabricator — is the volume of the 2010s studio idea. Quality spans. A signed butterfly on a wet slab is not New Hope.

## After New Hope: the product slab

Live-edge tables became a 2010s product (the farmhouse essay’s cousin, often epoxy-river and walnut). The studio original and the product share a silhouette and not a brief. A river table with a glass-smooth epoxy is a wipeable postmodern top on a rustic body. It photographs. It also photographs every fingerprint. The epoxy is a finish history: UV yellowing, a seam, a pour that hid a defect. Look at the underside. A product slab often has a flattened, sanded back and a steel channel. A studio slab may have tool marks, a different finish on the underside, a base that is wood.

Contemporary makers — including Southern hardwood shops — sit on this line: one-off, named wood, a client, a wait. They do not need to copy butterflies. A square oak dining table with honest tenons is studio furniture if the shop is a shop. Mission already said that. Nakashima added the slab’s irregularity. Both are available to a maker who does not want to be a factory.

Price is a collector literature and a commission literature. This page will not invent one. Wait time is part of the object. A table that takes a year is a different social fact than a table that takes an afternoon with a hex key. Both held dinner. Only one is this slug.

## How to look

Get on the floor. Look at the base join. A trestle with a keyed tenon is a shop. A hanger bolt into a steel plate is a product, or a shop that chose metal on purpose — ask. Look at the keys. Are they structural? Look at the edge. Live or routed? Look at the finish in a raking light. Oil will show a ring and a repair. Film will show a scratch that does not oil out. Epoxy will show a surface that is a plastic.

Look at the chairs. Conoid chairs at a slab are a suite in the studio sense: they belong to the same brief. Mixed chairs at a slab are an American room. Eames shells at a walnut slab are a common honest mix. Captain’s chairs at a river table are a house that bought two decades.

If you eat at a slab, you will learn the board’s weather: a little movement, a finish that wants oil, a guest who comments on the edge instead of the food. That comment is the modern dining-room talk. Federal guests commented on the silver. The object of pride moved from the lockable board to the tree. Anderson’s mahogany book would have things to say about pride in a tropical log. A Pennsylvania walnut slab is a different forest and a cleaner story, not a perfect one. Name the tree. Name the shop. Pass the plates. The series’ factory pole is Grand Rapids. This is the other pole. A healthy town has both, or at least knows both exist.

A New Hope table in a great room is sculpture that still wants a cloth if the wine is red. A New Hope table in a closed dining room is a modern object in a 1955 box. Both readings work. The shop did not require Biddle’s recess. The board requires a room that will oil it.
