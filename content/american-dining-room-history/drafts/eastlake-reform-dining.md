# Eastlake and the reform dining room

Charles Locke Eastlake’s *Hints on Household Taste* (1868; American editions soon after) is a book against the fruit. He wants furniture whose construction you can see, ornament that is incised or geometric, wood that is not a composition grape. American factories read him the way they read every English book: they stamped “Eastlake” on a chair with a few incised lines and a factory finish and sold a dining suite to people who wanted to be good. The reform is real at the high end and a decal at the volume end. Both ended up at American tables.

The dining room of the 1870s–80s can be Renaissance on the sideboard wall and Eastlake at the chairs. It can be a consistent incised-oak room. It can be ebonized, gilt-lined, a little Japanese, a little Gothic leftover. Reform is a cluster, not a police.

## What Eastlake actually asked for

Honesty of construction, no sham carving, furniture that a joiner could defend. Medieval and early-Renaissance models rather than Louis XV. Oak. A moral tone cousin to Downing and to Pugin, milder than Pugin. American readers, many of them women running houses, used him to argue with husbands and with salesmen. The book is prescriptive literature in Berman’s sense: it tells the room what it ought to be.

The dining-room chapter is not a museum lecture. Eastlake walks through chairs that are still “very far from what they ought to be,” tables that are too varnished, sideboards that hide their construction. He likes a type the trade called a Cromwell chair: square seat, legs partly square and partly turned, a padded frame between two uprights, leather and brass nails, sometimes “American cloth” instead of leather. He has seen a suite of that kind in Tottenham Court Road. He uses the shopkeepers’ word *suite* with a wince and then uses it anyway, because that is how dining furniture was sold. The V&A’s oak sideboard after his published design is the plate made solid: storage and serving in one, upper shelves for china he thought worth looking at, construction you can read.

What shops made: rectangular tables with chamfered legs, chairs with turned spindles and incised crests, sideboards that are still tall but flatter, less fruit, more panels. Incising is a router’s cousin — a line cut in and sometimes gilt. It photographs as “artistic.” It is faster than grapes.

## The American book

The first American edition of *Hints* is 1872, James R. Osgood and Company, Boston, after the 1868 London book. Later American printings followed; the V&A notes a seventh American edition in 1886. The book was reprinted more often in the United States than in England. That is not a compliment Eastlake wanted. In the preface to the fourth London edition (1878) he wrote that American tradesmen were advertising what they were pleased to call Eastlake furniture, “the production of which I have had nothing whatever to do, and for the taste of which I should be very sorry to be considered responsible.” Pin the sentence to that preface. Do not hang it on a chair tag.

What the American reader received was a household manual with a dining-room chapter, plates, and a permission to dislike the walnut mountain. Downing’s *Architecture of Country Houses* (1850) had already given a moral vocabulary to rooms and servants. Pugin had already called sham a sin. Eastlake is the milder Englishman who reached the American parlor table as a Christmas book. Women’s magazines and department-store catalogs did the rest. Chippendale’s *Director* was a shop book. Eastlake is a consumer book. That is new, and it is why a factory in Michigan could sell “Eastlake” without reading him twice.

Herter’s later interiors sit in this conversation at a price most readers will never touch. The Art Institute of Chicago’s Herter sideboard in a “modern Gothic” taste — foliate panels, arches, chamfered pillars, Aesthetic sunflowers — is reform-adjacent architecture, not a Grand Rapids chamfer. Do not collapse the two. Both refused fruit. Only one was a book the middle class could own.

## Factory Eastlake

Grand Rapids and New York factories loved a style you could incise with a machine. “Eastlake” in an 1880 catalog is a drawer of motifs: a linear flower, a chamfer, a brass pull that is a rectangle. The dining suite is still a suite. The reform did not return us to the hall gateleg. It changed the costume on the specialized room.

Quality: a shop-made Eastlake table with through-tenons and a readable oak is one thing. A stained basswood table with printed “incising” is another. Both were called Eastlake. The word is not a grade.

Japanese and “art furniture” influences mix in. Ebonized cherry with gilt lines can sit in the same dining room as an oak Eastlake sideboard. The ebonized line is not Eastlake’s oak sermon. It is the Aesthetic cousin that used his rectangular discipline and then blackened the wood. A dining room of 1882 can hold both without apology. Consistency is a later museum habit.

Bamboo-turned spindles, a gilt line in a chamfer, a brass pull cut as a rectangle with a tiny flower incised on the plate — those are the American mix. A catalog will call the whole suite Eastlake. A shop that had read the book might have called only the oak table his. The ebonized chairs are someone else’s Japan. The sideboard’s panels may still carry a leftover Gothic quatrefoil because the same machine could cut it. Do not clean the mix up on the page. Households sat in it.

## Incising, chamfers, and the line

Incising is a cut, not a carving. A V-tool or a machine equivalent opens a line in the surface; gilt or a darker stain sits in the groove. Under a raking light, hand-cut lines wander. Machine lines do not. Printed “incising” — a transfer, a stamped depression in a finish — does not catch light at all. That is the first test. The second is the chamfer: a planed or machined bevel on a square leg or an upright, the reform’s favorite way to take the fat off a post without turning it into a cabriole. A chamfer you can feel with a thumb is construction. A chamfer that is only a painted highlight is costume.

Hardware tells the same story. Eastlake wanted visible, useful metal: a rectangular plate, a ring, a pull that looks like a pull. Factory “Eastlake” often keeps the Renaissance board’s lock and adds a stamped brass rectangle as a badge. Look at the screws. Look at whether the plate is let in or glued on. Then open the drawer. If the dovetails are machine-cut and even, you are in the factory, which is not a crime. If there are no dovetails, only nails and a printed grain, you are in a grade the book would not have defended.

Oak is the advertised wood. Ash, maple, and birch take the stain and the name. Quartersawn figure — ray fleck — is the better oak and the later Mission boast. Flatsawn “Eastlake” oak is the ordinary American table. The woods-series quartersawn draft, if present, is the cut hour. This page is the line cut into the wood.

## The table and the chair

Extension tables continue. The crank and the slide do not care about incising. Chairs lose the rococo back and gain a rectangular splat or a row of spindles. Seats are still sprung in the better line. Comfort improves a little because the points are gone. The Gothic catch at the shoulder relaxes.

Women’s magazines spread the look. So do department stores. The dining room becomes a place you update from a catalog without moving house. That is new. A Renaissance pediment was a commitment. An incised crest rail is a season. Households mixed them: the walnut board stayed, the chairs changed. A 1875 sideboard with 1885 Eastlake chairs is a document, not a mistake.

The table top is often plainer than the chairs. A cloth will cover it. The chamfered legs are the show when the cloth is on. Look under for slides, numbered leaves, and a stencil. The patents essay owns the mechanism. This page owns the costume on the apron: a line of incising where a grape used to be.

## After reform

Colonial Revival will claim a deeper honesty: not medieval England, but American ancestors. Mission will claim a deeper honesty still: the joint as the only ornament. Both owe Eastlake a debt they do not always pay. If you see a chamfered oak dining table from 1885, you are looking at the missing step between the walnut mountain and Stickley’s plank.

If you inherit “Eastlake” chairs, look at the incising under a raking light. Hand-cut lines wander. Machine lines do not. Then look at the tenons. Then sit. The reform, when it worked, made a chair you can live with. When it did not, it made a rectangular rococo. Either way the dining room stayed a dining room. Eastlake never asked it to become a hall again. He asked it to stop pretending it was Versailles. American factories, being factories, pretended they had stopped pretending.
