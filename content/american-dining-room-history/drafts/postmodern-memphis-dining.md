The Memphis dinner — a table that looks like a toy, a laminate that is a pattern, a chair that is a color first — was an Italian argument that a few Americans ate at. Ettore Sottsass and the Memphis group (1981) designed objects that refused both teak piety and Knoll marble. Michael Graves’s chairs and tables for American makers brought a cartoon classicism into showrooms: a pediment on a chair back, a pastel, a squashed column. Most American dining rooms ignored them and bought oak. This page is the ignored argument, because it is part of the history of what a dining table was allowed to look like.

A postmodern dining table is allowed to be funny. American dining furniture had not been funny on purpose since the painted fancy. Hitchcock’s fruit was not a joke. Memphis’s pattern is a joke that you still have to put a plate on.

## Milan, laminate, and a short argument

Memphis Milano showed in 1981. Sottsass, Michele De Lucchi, Matteo Thun, George Sowden, Nathalie Du Pasquier, and others used plastic laminate — Abet Laminati’s patterns are the ones the literature names — on forms that refused the good-taste wood grain of the previous thirty years. A table might be a laminate box on a laminate sled, or a color-block pedestal, or a piece that looks like a child’s drawing of a table. The dining problem is the same as it was for Saarinen: height, a wipeable top, somewhere to put your knees. The answer is the opposite of the slum-of-legs edit. Pattern returns. Legs return as a display. Color does the work veneer used to do.

American museums and a few showrooms in New York and Los Angeles took the work. A *New York Times* reader saw it and did not buy. That is not a sneer. Most households cannot eat on a collector object, and Memphis was priced and distributed as design, not as a dinette. `[CITE NEEDED: a dated U.S. retail listing for a named Memphis table, without inventing a dollar figure if the page is not in hand.]` Survival of high Memphis is a collector fact. This page will not invent an accession. If you are standing in front of a documented piece, read the museum label and quote it.

What arrived in ordinary American dining rooms is a milder postmodern: a black lacquer table, brass, a chair with a squiggle, “contemporary” in the 1980s catalog. The formal fifties suite and the Early American maple both look suddenly old. Some households switched. Many put a glass-top table on a brass or chrome base and called it a night.

## Glass, brass, and the slum on view

Glass tops are a dining problem (rings, elbows, a view of knees). They are a postmodern and high-tech problem. The slum of legs returns as a display. Saarinen had tried to delete it. The glass table frames it. A brass or chrome cylinder cluster, a zigzag, a single thick stem, a pair of inverted cones — the undercarriage becomes the show. You polish it. You also see crumbs on the floor through the top, which is a service fact the catalogs skipped.

Glass thickness and edgework date a table better than a tag. A beveled edge on a thick plate is one shop. A flat polished edge on a thin plate is another. A glass that sits in a channel and rattles is a cheap one. A glass that is clipped with brass buttons can be any decade after 1975. Look at the clips’ screws from below. Look at chips on the underside, where a leaf or a belt buckle found the edge.

Laminate on a particleboard core is the other volume top. It is a cousin of the dinette enamel: wipeable, proud of not being wood. The edge banding tells the truth. A thick ABS edge in a contrasting color is an 1980s clock. A thin iron-on edge that is lifting is a later cheap clock. Memphis’s laminates are patterns. The American catalog’s laminates are often faux granite, faux marble, or a speckled beige. Both wipe. Only one was an argument.

## Graves, and the factory’s cartoon classicism

Michael Graves’s furniture for American manufacturers — Sunar and later related contract lines are the citations scholars use; pin a maker when you name a chair — brought a toy classicism into the showroom: a pediment, a ball, a pastel column that is too short to be a column. The dining chairs are better than the joke suggests, or worse, depending on the foam and the angle of the back. Some are surprisingly good chairs. Some are not. Sit.

Graves’s later Target work (from 1999) made postmodern a kitchen utensil and a toaster. Dining tables in that later cheap line are not this hour. This hour is the showroom. Do not collapse 1982 and 2002 because the same architect’s name is on a kettle. The dining-room fact is a chair with a pediment that a household actually used, if any did, and a lot of chairs that sat in a lobby.

Swid Powell and other tabletop collaborations put Graves and peers on plates more often than on tables. A postmodern dinner can be a Memphis plate on a glass table with unsigned chairs. The room does not have to be a single brief. American rooms rarely are.

## American factories

The 1980s “lifestyle” dining set — black, brass, a vinyl chair, a table that is almost a desk — is the volume. It shares Memphis’s refusal of teak and not Memphis’s intelligence. It photographs under a halogen lamp. It chips. It is already in landfills. Survival of the black-and-brass set is a garage fact.

Lane, Bassett, Ashley’s earlier years, and a long list of “contemporary” suites did the black lacquer and the brass ferrule. `[CITE NEEDED: a named 1984–1988 catalog suite in black and brass, page and year.]` The chairs are often a splat that has become a squiggle, or a cane that is a vinyl cane, or an upholstered oval on a lacquered frame. Casters appear. So do glass inserts in wood frames. The extension mechanism is a metal slide. Alignment is luck after a few moves.

High-tech — the chrome-and-grid cousin, more office than Memphis — shares the glass top and the metal and not the joke. A wire-grid chair at a glass table is a different brief (more Bertoia’s grandchildren than Sottsass). Look at the chair: if it is trying to be funny, you are closer to Memphis. If it is trying to be a lab, you are closer to high-tech. Both sat in American dining rooms that had just painted the walls gray.

## What it did to the room

It made the dining room a design statement again, briefly, for a class that had been modern or colonial for thirty years. Then the statement looked dated, which is the fate of statements. Farmhouse-industrial and the live-edge slab are later statements. The cycle is the history.

The closed formal room could take a black lacquer table and keep its chandelier. The ranch dining alcove could take a glass oval and keep its captain’s chairs for a year, then replace them with vinyl-and-brass. Open plans were not yet the default; they were coming. A postmodern table in a still-closed dining room is a 1980s argument inside a 1955 box. The box usually won. The table went to the basement.

## How to look

If you sit in a Memphis chair, the joke reaches your back. Check the laminate for chips at the corners — that is where a vacuum found it. Check the underside for a maker’s mark; Italian pieces are more often labeled than American “Memphis-style” ones. A pattern that is a photograph of a pattern (a later print) is not Abet’s sheet. This page will not pretend a flashlight makes you a laminate scholar. It will say: a pattern that continues around an edge in one skin is a better object than a pattern that dies at a strip of black banding.

On a glass-and-brass table, get on the floor. The base will tell you if it was a dining table or a parlor cocktail raised on later stems. Dining height is stubborn. A table that hits you at the wrong rib is not a dining table, whatever the tag says. Look at the feet for caster sockets and for scratches that match a tile floor, not a carpet. The 1980s dining room often had a parquet or a vinyl that pretended to be parquet. The scratches are a document.

On a black lacquer factory suite, look at the chips. Look at the brass ferrules: are they brass or brass paint? Look at the chairs’ foam. Look at whether the table’s extension leaves are the same lacquer or a later matte. Then decide if you are keeping a period room or a pile of parts.

American dining keeps inventing wipeable tops (marble slab, enamel, laminate, glass, epoxy on a river table) and then repenting back to oil and oak. This series is that pendulum. Postmodern is a swing toward the wipe and the joke. The next essays swing toward the slab and the island. You can still eat on a joke if the height is right. Height is the only piety the table cannot drop.
