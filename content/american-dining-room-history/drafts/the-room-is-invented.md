# The dining room is invented

The Metropolitan Museum’s Baltimore Room, Gallery 724, is a parlor pretending to be a dining room. The woodwork came from Henry Craig’s townhouse on East Pratt Street. Craig (1767–1832) was a Baltimore merchant and shipowner. The room served his family as a parlor. Since the American Wing opened in 1924 the Museum has set it for dinner: table, chairs, sideboard, plate. The curators of the 1920s preferred this architecture to the house’s actual dining room. They were staging a fashion that had only recently become ordinary.

That is the first fact of American dining furniture. The table is older than the room. For most of the eighteenth century a prosperous household ate in a hall, a parlor, or whichever heated room could take a leaf. The furniture folded. The cloth came out. After the meal the gateleg went back to the wall. A dedicated dining room — a room that keeps its table, its sideboard, and its name when no one is eating — is a late colonial and early national invention, and it arrives unevenly.

## England had the room first

Leslie Susan Berman’s 1997 Penn thesis, *The Evolution of the Nineteenth-Century American Dining Room*, traces the idea from French *salles à manger* of the 1630s into English country houses in the early eighteenth century, then across the Atlantic in pattern books. The English gentry could afford a room that did one thing and a staff that carried dishes from a distant kitchen. American inventories and builder’s guides lag that luxury by two generations.

William Pain’s English handbooks were reprinted in Boston in the 1790s. *The Practical Builder* (Boston, 1792) gives house plans with dimensions and no room names. *The Practical House Carpenter* (Boston, 1796) is the shock: seven of eight plans label breakfast or dining rooms, five of them both. Those plates are still English aristocratic houses. They taught American carpenters a vocabulary they did not yet build for ordinary clients. Asher Benjamin’s *Country Builder’s Assistant* (Greenfield, Massachusetts, 1797) — the first architectural handbook written and published in America for Americans — dimensions the rooms and refuses to name them. A villa, in Downing’s later definition, needs three or more servants. Benjamin’s readers were not all there yet.

Owen Biddle’s *Young Carpenter’s Assistant* (Philadelphia, 1805) is the American turn Berman isolates. Plate 36, a small house, still has no labels. Plate 37, a large house, letters the rooms and sends the reader to a key. There is a dining room at the rear of the principal floor, and a recess for a sideboard. From that plate forward, Berman argues, room labels become normal in American builder’s books. The recess is as important as the name. A sideboard is not a table you put away. It wants a wall niche and a room that stays a dining room overnight.

## What the inventories say

Prescriptive books tell a carpenter what a gentleman ought to want. Inventories tell you what was in the house when someone died. Berman’s sample — 146 urban Philadelphia and New York inventories, 1794–1855, rooms labeled, country counties excluded — is the check. The dining room does not snap into existence in 1805. It spreads down the income ladder through the first half of the nineteenth century, faster in town than in the country, faster where servants (enslaved, indentured, or hired) can work a service from a sideboard.

Until the late eighteenth century most American families had no separate room for dining and no regular way to entertain at a formal dinner. Dumbarton House’s Federal-era essay puts it bluntly and then shows the Nourse household at work: markets, servants, cloths stacked for courses. Service *à la française* laid a course on the table so guests could choose. Service *à la russe*, dishes presented from the sideboard by a butler or footman, is a later nineteenth-century fashion in America — forty years after the Federal table, in the Dumbarton House account. The furniture follows the service. A gateleg can do *à la française* in a parlor. *À la russe* wants a sideboard and a room that is not also the sitting room.

The Met’s own label on the Baltimore Room is careful about class. Beginning in the late eighteenth century, wealthier Americans dedicated a room to dining and used it with a formal parlor. In the early Federal period the fashion reached middle-class houses. The kit is specific: dining table, suite of chairs, sideboard, silver, ceramic, glass. In Craig’s house the dining room sat on the main floor, connected to the parlor, to the rear — a common townhouse layout. The Museum chose the prettier parlor to tell that story. A visitor who takes the furniture as architecture has already been edited.

## The table that waits

Once the room has a name, the table changes jobs. A hall gateleg is a transformer. A dining-room table is a resident. D-ends, pedestals, and later extension slides assume a floor that belongs to them. The sideboard assumes a wall. Chairs assume a count: six, eight, twelve — a suite, not a mixed pile from three rooms.

That residency is also a labor claim. Someone polishes the sideboard when no guests are coming. Someone keeps the leaves in a closet or a pantry. Someone carries from the kitchen, which in a townhouse is often down a stair and through a passage that was never designed for a parade of hot plates. Enslaved workers in Southern and, until the 1820s, some Northern houses did that carrying. Free Black and white servants did it in others. The room’s specialization is their specialization. A history of the dining room that names Hepplewhite and not the person who set the cloth is half a room.

Women’s prescriptive books make the furniture a moral object. Eliza Leslie’s *House Book* (1844), as Commonplace notes, tells a middle-class reader that a large closet is indispensable to a dining room — and by closet she means a sideboard. She will accept two small sideboards in recesses if a large one is too greedy for the wall. That sentence is the middle of the century talking: the form is no longer a Federal novelty. It is storage you can see, silver you can count, gentility you can stage.

## The kitchen is another floor

Biddle’s Plate 37 puts the dining room at the rear of the principal floor. The kitchen, in the townhouses that plate describes, is rarely on that floor. Philadelphia, New York, and Baltimore row houses of the Federal decades put cooking in a cellar, a rear ell, or a detached building. Food travels a stair, a passage, and a door hung for privacy, not for a tray. The dedicated dining room is also a dedicated route. Someone has to learn it, keep it clear, and take the blame when a sauce cools.

The service stair, when the house has one, is narrower than the stair guests use. A tray turns badly on a wind. Grease marks on a painted stringer are a document museums rarely keep. A dumbwaiter, when it appears later in the century, is an admission that the route was already too long. `[CITE NEEDED: the earliest dated dumbwaiter in an American townhouse dining-room plan.]`

Country houses invert the diagram without canceling the labor. A kitchen in a wing or an outbuilding is a horizontal carry instead of a vertical one. Heat and smell stay out. So does the person who cooks. The dining room’s cleanliness is their absence, staged.

Take a townhouse plan and draw the walk from hearth or range to the dining-room door. Count the turns. If the walk is long and the room has no sideboard wall, the house is asking a person to be the closet. If the walk is short and the table is a gateleg, you may still be in a hall that never took the name.

## Loudon, Downing, and the villa that needs staff

John Claudius Loudon’s *Encyclopaedia of Cottage, Farm, and Villa Architecture* (1833) is the English book Berman names as the influence American writers digest. Loudon assumes labeled rooms and a servant count. Andrew Jackson Downing’s *Architecture of Country Houses* (1850) is the American villa version: rooms named, servants numbered, a dining room treated as a moral as well as a practical chamber. The furniture essays that follow in this series — Gothic suites, Renaissance Revival mountains of walnut — sit in rooms those books had already argued for.

Biddle’s recess is a carpenter’s move in 1805. Loudon and Downing are a generation later, telling a reader who already has the name what the room ought to feel like. Between them, Asher Benjamin’s later titles begin to speak the vocabulary Pain had imported and the 1797 *Country Builder’s Assistant* had refused. `[CITE NEEDED: the first Asher Benjamin edition that consistently labels dining rooms.]` The name, once it is normal in the books, becomes normal in the house. Inventories catch up down the income ladder, which is Berman’s mid-century finding, not a builder’s wish.

## What this series will not do

It will not treat 1700 as a dining-room year in most American houses. It will not treat a Queen Anne drop-leaf in a one-room house as a failed Chippendale suite. It will not call every tall Southern serving table a hunt board without saying that the name is a twentieth-century dealer’s romance (the vernacular draft takes that hour). It will not give Phyfe every New York table.

It will walk the shops: Newport and Philadelphia, Charleston marble slabs, Federal D-ends, Thomas Day’s S-scrolls in Milton, Hitchcock’s stenciled rails, Stickley’s two-inch plank, Briggs’s crank, Saarinen’s one leg, the dinette, the flat-pack, the open plan that ate the room. Cross-links sit at the end of each essay. The primary keyword on this page is the room itself. Other slugs own the table types.

Biddle’s recess is still a good way to look at a house. If the dining room has a niche or a stretch of blank wall that is too wide for a painting and too low for a secretary, someone expected a sideboard. If the table is a gateleg in a hall with a settle, you are earlier than the name, or poorer than the plate, or both. Either reading is a history. Neither is a failure.
