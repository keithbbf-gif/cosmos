# How to read an American dining table

Get on the floor. The show top will lie. The underside keeps the shop. This page is a method, not a price guide. It exists so the series can end in a room you actually have, with a table you did not buy at the Met.

Bring a flashlight, a notebook, and no certainty. Attribution is a specialist’s fight. What you can do is refuse the tag’s century until the joints agree.

## The underside

Secondary woods: white pine (New England), tulip poplar (Mid-Atlantic — green and purple streaks), yellow pine (South), cypress (Lowcountry), oak or chestnut (various), plywood or particleboard (twentieth century and after). A “Boston 1760” table with poplar rails is already in trouble. A “Phyfe” with oak secondaries and Phillips screws is a wish. Hurst and Prown on Southern furniture, Heckscher on the Met’s late colonial, and Montgomery on Federal work are the regional grammars. The USDA *Wood Handbook* is the species tell: poplar’s streak, pine’s resin canals, oak’s rays. You do not need a lab. You need a raking light and a willingness to say “I do not know” when the wood is painted.

Oxidation: the unseen wood should be darker than a fresh cut. A uniformly pale underside on an “18th-century” table has been planed or is late. Pin holes that are oval and dark are old. Round bright holes are new. A pocket-screw scar under a “Federal” top is a date, and the date is last Tuesday.

Tool marks: pit-sawn or riven surfaces, then circular-saw arcs (nineteenth century on), then planer chatter, then nothing because a factory sanded. Circular arcs on a supposedly 1710 gateleg are a problem. A perfectly sanded underside on a table that claims handwork is a problem unless a restorer has been there — and then the restoration is the chapter you are reading, not 1710.

Chalk, graphite, a shop number, a later mover’s scrawl: keep them. They are captions. A stenciled factory name (Grand Rapids, a North Carolina town, an IKEA article number) is a gift. Photograph it. Do not sand it off to “clean up.”

## The joints

Rule joint: a clean bead when open is a shop that could cut. A butt joint with surface hinges is cheaper or later. Pintles versus steel butts. Wooden knuckles on swing legs — original fingers versus a replaced maple knuckle on a walnut table. The gateleg essay owns the mechanism. Here: if the gate’s wear does not match the floor’s wear, someone has been under there with new wood.

Dovetails in a drawer: hand-cut, slightly irregular, thin pins on better early work; machine-cut, even, later. A drawer that is too clean has been refinished inside or added. A table without drawers can still have a skirt whose miters or tenons will talk. Look at the glue. Hide glue is dark and brittle and can be revived with heat and moisture; a later aliphatic glue is creamy and does not behave the same. Smell when you warm a joint. None of these are dates by themselves. Together they are a room.

Pedestal blocks: original hide-glue blocks in a radial pattern. Missing blocks and a later steel plate are a repair. A single lag screw up the pillar is a confession. Empire and Federal pedestals need those blocks the way a wagon wheel needs a hub. A restorer who cleaned them off to make the underside “pretty” removed the table’s brain.

Slides: wooden telescopic, accordion crank (Briggs and the patents essay), metal, cam locks. Numbered leaves. Profiles of rule joints that match. A marriage of D-ends is common; mismatched molding is the tell. IKEA cams are a joint. So is a hanger bolt into a steel trestle. Name the joint you have, not the joint the tag wished.

Screws: a handmade screw with an off-center slot is early. A blunt-start machine screw is later. Phillips is twentieth century. A square drive is later still. Do not date a whole table by one screw; screws get replaced. A pattern of mixed screws is a repair history. A pattern of identical bright screws on an “18th-century” rail is a warning.

## The feet and the floor

Wear on the ball of a claw, caster holes, replaced feet, a table that has been cut down because a Victorian owner wanted it lower or a later owner wanted it higher. Seat-to-apron clearance on the chairs that “came with it” — if the chairs hit the rail, they did not come with it, or someone recut something.

Casters: brass, leather, porcelain, later rubber and plastic. Socket holes that have been plugged are a chapter. A table that has lived on carpet has a different foot than a table that has lived on pine boards. Look at the very bottom. Sand and grit embed. A foot that is clean and sharp on a table that claims two hundred years of dinner has been recut or is late.

Levelers and felt pads are 2010s honesty. They tell you the table is being used on a finished floor. They do not make the table new. They do make the floor’s story harder to read. Lift a pad.

## The finish

Alligator varnish, French polish, a red 1900s mahogany dye, a 1970s film, oil, epoxy. Original surface is rare and valuable as a document. A stripped table can still be old. It has lost a chapter. Painted Baltimore and Hitchcock furniture that has been stripped has lost the book. Do not strip paint to “find the wood” on a fancy chair.

The USDA *Wood Handbook* will not date a finish. Your nose and a raking light will get you closer. Shellac melts with alcohol. A later film does not. A gray wash on oak is a 2010s clock. A fumed oak that goes into the wood, not onto it, is a Mission clock if the rest of the table agrees. A teak oil that has been replaced with polyurethane is a 1990s clock on a 1962 body.

Smell: hide glue when you warm a joint; later aliphatic glue; smoke; mothballs from a drawer. A dining table that smells of cigarettes lived in a century that smoked at dinner. That is a finish too.

## The story on the tag

“Colonial” means nothing. “Hepplewhite” means a style, not 1788. “Phyfe” means New York envy. “Hunt board” means a dealer has been reading 1932 — the name is a twentieth-century romance; the Southern vernacular essay already dropped the horse. “Farmhouse” means 2014. “Shaker” on a glossy cherry four-top means a catalog. “Danish” on pecan means High Point. “Nakashima” on a river of epoxy means a mood board. Compare the tag to the underside. Keep the table if the underside is interesting even when the tag is a lie. Return the table if the underside is plywood and the price is mahogany.

Accession numbers belong to museums. Do not invent one, and do not treat a dealer’s stock number as one. A Met or Winterthur or MESDA number is a pin this series will quote when the label is in hand. A tag that says “ex-Met” without paper is a story.

Prices are a market, not a method. This page will not give you one. A table can be old and cheap. A table can be new and expensive. The flashlight does not care.

## Use

A dish in the top is use. A perfectly flat 1730 leaf has been planed. Burns and rings are a finish history. A table that has never been wet in two hundred years of dinner is a table that lived in a collection. Prefer the wet table if you are going to eat.

Leaves that do not match the fade of the main top lived in a closet. That is ordinary. Leaves that do not match the wood or the molding are a marriage. Numbered leaves that skip a number are a missing leaf. A dining table of 1810 that is now a console is a pair of D-ends that lost their middle. Look at the ends of the rails for empty hardware.

A table that racks when you lean is a joint problem, not a style. A little seasonal play in a pedestal top is not always a death sentence. A lot of play is a missing block or a failed glue. Rock it gently. Then decide whether you are buying a table or a project.

## A short field order

1. Photograph the whole, then the underside, then every joint you can reach.
2. Name the primary wood and the secondary wood, or write “unknown” and a color.
3. Name the joints and the extension, if any.
4. Name the finish in ordinary words (red film, oil, gray wash, alligator).
5. Write the tag’s claim in one sentence. Write what the underside will allow in the next.
6. Only then stand up.

This method will not make you Heckscher. It will keep you from buying a 1925 gateleg as a 1725 one, which is the most common American dining-table error. It will keep you from calling a tall Southern server a hunt board because a 1932 magazine wanted moonlight. It will keep you from paying mahogany money for a foil-grain box. The series’ other slugs give you the hours. This slug gives you the flashlight. When the underside and the hour agree, you can stand up and put a plate down. That is the only test a dining table must pass in the end. The rest is history you can now name.
