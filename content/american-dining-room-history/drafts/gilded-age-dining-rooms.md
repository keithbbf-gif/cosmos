# Gilded Age dining rooms

The dining room at a Newport “cottage” is a European room that happens to be in Rhode Island: paneling shipped or copied, a table that extends toward twenty, chairs that are English or French or Italian in style and American in their last polish, a sideboard that is architecture, silver that needs a room of its own. McKim, Mead & White and Richard Morris Hunt built the envelopes. Herter, Allard, and a chain of decorators filled them. This is not how most Americans dined. It is the image that made the middle-class suite feel small and the Colonial Revival feel like a relief.

Berman’s specialization is complete here and then over-complete. The breakfast room, the butler’s pantry, the china closet, the silver safe, the servants’ dining hall — the house fragments dining into a campus. The furniture follows: a state table, a family table, a kitchen table, chairs that never meet.

## The long table

Extension mechanisms of every kind — Briggs’s descendants, English telescopic slides, Jupe-style rarities for people who had been to London — make a table that can be a family oval on Tuesday and a banquet on Saturday. Leaves live in the pantry. A man (or two) winds or pulls. The cloth is a field. Service *à la russe* is the rule at this level: a footman, a sequence, a plate that changes. The sideboard is a pass-through more than a display, though display is part of the pass.

The Breakers (Richard Morris Hunt, 1893–95, for Cornelius Vanderbilt II) is the public type specimen. The Preservation Society and the National Historic Landmark documentation describe an Italian Renaissance dining room of more than two thousand square feet, alabaster columns with gilt Corinthian capitals, Baccarat chandeliers in an imperial pattern Hunt had a hand in, and a carved oak table inlaid with lemonwood set for a large company — the NHL text says thirty-four persons and thirty-four Renaissance-style chairs in red damask. Pin those counts to that documentation, not to a tour script in your head. The chairs are the chorus. The doors to pantry and hall are the design.

Marble House (Hunt again, 1888–92, for William K. and Alva Vanderbilt) is the other Newport citation: Allard’s French interiors, a dining room sheathed in marble, chairs heavy enough that a footman was part of the furniture. `[CITE NEEDED: period weights or service notes for the Marble House dining chairs, from Preservation Society files rather than later travel copy.]` McKim, Mead & White built other Newport envelopes (Rosecliff among them). Do not give Hunt every cottage. Do not give McKim every table.

## Imported rooms

Entire paneled rooms bought in Europe — or copied so closely the difference is a customs stamp — mean the furniture may be older than the house. A 1680 English oak table in an 1890 Newport dining room is a Gilded Age object in use, not a colonial American table. Keep the clocks honest. This series is American dining furniture. Imported rooms are an American purchasing habit.

Herter’s Vanderbilt dining room on Fifth Avenue (the Renaissance Revival essay’s citation) is the New York indoor version: a designed American room in a historicist style, not a crate from a Loire château. Allard et fils and other Paris firms supplied the French rooms. American shops polished, repaired, and then made “the same” in walnut for people who had seen a photograph. Chicago’s large houses of the 1880s–90s run a parallel: Prairie is coming, but the Gilded Age table in a Prairie Avenue dining room is still a long extension board under a heavy fixture. The furniture is not yet Wright. It is Hunt’s language spoken with Midwestern oak and a different guest list.

World’s fairs taught the look. The 1893 Columbian Exposition in Chicago showed English expanding tables (Johnstone’s firm among the sights) and a world’s-fair idea of a state dining room. The Midwestern household that went home still bought slides from a hardware house and a suite from a catalog. The fair is a rumor with tickets. The suite is the meal.

Imported paneling — a Loire room, an English oak parlor, a ceiling that crossed the Atlantic in numbered crates — makes the American table a guest in someone else’s architecture. Date the paneling and the table separately. A Phyfe-period D-end under a sixteenth-century-style ceiling is a Gilded Age mix, not a Federal room. The purchasing habit is the American fact.

## The middle-class echo

Grand Rapids sells a shorter version: a golden-oak suite, a pedestal table with lion feet leftover from Empire, a sideboard with a mirror, six chairs with press-back or carved backs. The Gilded Age, for most towns, is that suite on the installment plan. It is not Hunt. It is not nothing. The factory essay takes the industry. This page names the aspiration. Etiquette books and women’s magazines teach the sequence of forks to people who do the washing-up themselves or with one hired girl.

Silver plate (the alloy, not only the collection) multiplies. The sideboard’s lock still matters. The dining room is the house’s show room in a way the Federal parlor used to be. Callers may be received here. The table stays.

A 1905 catalog pedestal table with a lion foot and a golden-oak finish is the same century as The Breakers and a different number of hands. Count the chairs in the catalog plate: six, sometimes eight. Count the leaves: two or three, not a pantry of sixteen. The mechanism is a descendant of Briggs. The costume is leftover Empire plus leftover Renaissance. The buyer is a household that wanted the word *dining room* to mean something a photograph from Newport had taught.

Chicago’s Prairie Avenue dining rooms of the 1880s–90s are the Midwestern state version: a long board, a heavy fixture, a staffed pantry, not yet Wright’s fence of chairs. The guest list is meatpacking and rail, not Newport’s summer colony. The furniture language is still Hunt’s historicist oak or Allard’s French, spoken with a different freight bill. A house that later gets a Prairie interior is not a Gilded Age dining room that failed. It is a later author taking the same terminal room and changing the chairs.

## Silver, plate, and the pass-through

At the top, silver needs a room. A silver safe, a vault, a locked closet off the pantry — the dining-room sideboard cannot hold a service that is also a treasury. Display still happens: a plateau, candelabra, a row of cups that will not be drunk from. The working silver lives where a servant can count it without walking through the guests. Plate (the alloy) does the same job in the middle-class house at a price that is not Tiffany. Both need a lock. Leslie’s closet has become a security problem as well as a serving problem.

The sideboard in a staffed dining room is a pass-through more than a monument, even when it looks like a monument. Dishes arrive from the pantry, rest, go to the table, return. A board that is only display has already lost its job to the pantry. A board that is only a station has lost its job to later taste, which wanted the wall back. Gilded Age dining uses both functions at once and needs the bodies to make that possible.

## Staff

At the top, a hierarchy: butler, footmen, cook, kitchen maids. Their dining furniture is pine, late, and rarely kept. The servants’ hall has a table. It is not in the house museum’s photograph of the state dining room. At the middle, one servant and a wife. At the farm, no Gilded Age. The labor essay’s afterlife is this staffed house and then its collapse after the First World War. The butler’s-pantry essay takes the architecture. Here, the furniture implication: a state table that cannot function without bodies who are not eating at it.

A chair that weighs what a footman must move is a labor tool. A leaf that takes two people to carry is a labor tool. A cloth the size of a lawn is a labor tool. When those bodies leave, the table is too long. That is not a metaphor. It is why so many Gilded Age tables now live at half-extension in museums, frozen at a number of places that looks like dinner and is actually a compromise with the fire code.

## After the gold

Taxes, wars, and taste close the cottages or turn them into museums. The long table is too long. Colonial Revival and then modernism offer smaller dinners. Some Gilded Age dining rooms survive as restaurants and house museums, which is why Americans think this was the dining room. It was a class’s dining room. The Hitchcock chair and the Grand Rapids suite were others.

If you walk a Newport dining room, count the chairs, then count the doors to pantry and hall. The doors are the design. The chairs are the chorus. Then go home and look at an oak pedestal table from a 1905 catalog. Same century. Same meal name. Different number of hands in the room. The history is the number of hands.
