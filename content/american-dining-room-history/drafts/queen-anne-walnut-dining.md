# Queen Anne walnut

The cabriole is a bent knee. On an American Queen Anne dining table it is usually walnut, sometimes maple painted to look like walnut, later mahogany pretending the hour is still this one. The leg springs from a rounded knee, tapers, and ends in a pad foot — a disc, a slipper, a trifid in Philadelphia. Stretchers have mostly gone. The table stands on four (or six) legs that you can see under a hanging leaf, and the silhouette is the fashion: open, curved, a little proud.

Queen Anne the person died in 1714. The American furniture name covers shops from the 1720s into the 1750s, overlapping Chippendale in the same town. At dinner the name means: drop-leaf or swing-leg table, vase-splat chairs, a compass seat, walnut the color of a wet leaf. The room may still be a parlor. The table is already trying to be furniture you leave out.

## Walnut before mahogany

English walnut had a run and a blight story; American *Juglans nigra* is a different tree and a different color — chocolate, sometimes purple in the sapwood, a smell in the shavings that a shop still knows. It carves. It dents. It moves more than the mahogany that will replace it on show surfaces. A Queen Anne dining leaf in solid walnut is a wide, nervous board. Shops used it anyway because it was the fine wood they had, and because the cabriole looks better in a close-grained hardwood than in oak.

Maple stands in. So does cherry. Paint and stain close the gap for chairs. A “walnut” suite that is maple under the knee is not a fraud if the shop always meant paint. It is a fraud if a later owner stripped it to satisfy a 1950s idea of honesty.

Mahogany enters this hour as a luxury, not yet the default. Bowett’s English customs numbers after 1722 are a London flood. American coastal shops see the logs; inland shops see walnut. A Philadelphia drop-leaf with a mahogany top and walnut legs is a transitional object, not a puzzle.

## The dining table in this hour

The typical high-style table is rectangular or oval, drop-leaf, swing legs at the ends or the sides depending on how the shop wanted the open shape to look. Closed, it is a side table. Open, the swing legs must land where a diner’s knees are not. That geometry is harder than it looks. A leg that is beautiful in profile can be a bruise at dinner.

Circular and oval tilt-top tea tables are not dining tables. They get used for cards and cups. A large tilt-top in mahogany with a piecrust edge can seat two for a late supper and still not be a dining table in the inventory. Keep the jobs separate. The tea table has its own literature. This page is the leaf that holds a joint of meat.

Chairs: vase splat, yoke crest, compass seat, cabriole front legs, pad feet. The splat is a drawing in walnut, not yet a Chippendale piercing. Seat heights vary; museum chairs have often been recaned and recut. Sit in one that has not been “improved” and you will feel a shallower pitch than a 1950s diner chair. People sat up. They also sat shorter.

## Philadelphia, Boston, Newport

Philadelphia’s Queen Anne is the deep one: trifid feet, a muscular knee, shells on the chair crest, a shop culture that will become Chippendale without changing streets. Dining tables from those shops are fewer in the literature than high chests, which survived better and sold harder to collectors. `[CITE NEEDED: a labeled Philadelphia Queen Anne dining table with a firm date before 1750.]` Construction and secondary poplar still do most of the attribution.

Boston’s Queen Anne is tighter, often maple, sometimes with a Spanish foot lingering from the earlier hour. Newport is already a carving town; the Goddard-Townsend essay takes the later mahogany. In this hour Newport walnut chairs and tables exist and are argued. Do not hang a whole dining room on a single shell.

Rural shops copy the pad foot and miss the knee. The result is a country Queen Anne that is its own object. It will still hold dinner. It will not hold a Winterthur label.

## Joinery you can see from the floor

The cabriole is sawn from a thick blank or laminated — lamination is more often a later repair than an original method, but check. The knee is carved after the joinery is cut. A mortise that enters the knee from the rail is a weak point; glue blocks appear early and get replaced. Look for an original block with hide glue and a hand-cut angle. Look for a pine block with a Phillips screw and sigh.

Drop-leaf hinges on Queen Anne tables are often still wrought, moving toward cast butts. The rule joint remains the quality test. Swing-leg knuckles — wooden hinges with interlocking fingers and a pin — are a shop signature when they survive unrestored. A replaced knuckle in maple on a walnut table is a confession.

## Pad, slipper, trifid

The foot is the regional sentence once the cabriole has arrived. A pad foot is a disc or a thickened slipper under the ankle — the common American ending, Boston to the backcountry. A slipper foot is longer, flatter, a little like a pointed shoe; it reads as more stylish and kicks off more easily on a stone floor. The trifid — three toes, sometimes with a web — is a Philadelphia and Irish-related form that collectors treat as a passport. It is a passport only with the rest of the construction. A trifid recut onto a country ankle is a known sin.

Look at the foot from the front and from the side. A pad that is round in plan and thin in profile can be early and good. A pad that is a hockey puck under a thick ankle is often country or late. Philadelphia’s muscular knee wants a foot that can finish the curve. Boston’s tighter cabriole wants a smaller pad.

Replaced feet are common. The ankle is a break point. A scarf joint or a dowel through the ankle, visible from below, is a repair. Four perfectly matched feet on a table whose knees do not match each other is a later set of feet. Prefer the table with one honest replaced foot to the table with four new ones and a story. Heckscher’s Met late colonial catalog is the comparison set for these endings. A foot without the knee, the secondary wood, and the rule joint is just a foot.

## The knuckle that swings the leg

The wooden hinge is interlocking fingers cut on the fixed rail and the swinging rail, a pin through the stack, the whole joint hidden under the top. The knuckle has to carry the leaf’s weight at the end of a lever. If the pin wears, the leg droops and the leaf sags. If a restorer replaces the knuckle in a different species — maple fingers on a walnut rail — you can see the repair from the floor as a color change.

The fingers must mesh without slop and without binding when the humidity rises. Look for scribe lines that were not sanded off. Look for a pin that is wood, oxidized, slightly oval. A steel rod in a 1740 knuckle is a repair. It may be a good repair. It is still a repair.

Some tables use an iron hinge for the swing instead of a knuckle. That can be original on cheaper work and a replacement on better work. The rest of the table arbitrates. A single hardware type does not date a table.

## Walnut as a board that moves

The USDA *Wood Handbook* puts black walnut’s shrinkage above mahogany’s. A dining leaf in solid walnut will cup if it is finished on one face and left raw on the other, and it will check if a breadboard is glued tight across the end grain. Shops that understood this pegged the breadboard through slotted holes, or they skipped the breadboard and accepted a little opening at a glued seam.

Look at the underside of a walnut leaf for old finish versus raw wood. A leaf finished both sides moves less. A leaf raw below and heavily varnished above is a cup waiting. Later oilers who soak only the show face repeat the mistake. The USDA *Wood Handbook* has the shrinkage numbers. The proof is the meeting at the rule joint.

## The hour at table

Service is still *à la française* when it is formal at all: dishes on the table, diners helping themselves, a servant or an enslaved waiter at the wall if the household has one. The sideboard is not here yet. A serving table or a slab does the overflow. Silver is increasing in merchant houses. Pewter is still the volume.

The room, if it is a parlor, has to be cleared. The drop-leaf’s virtue remains the same as the gateleg’s. What has changed is the silhouette under the cloth. Guests see curves. They do not see stretchers. That visibility is status. It is also why the legs get kicked and patched.

When Chippendale’s *Director* (1754) hits American benches, the splat pierces, the foot becomes a claw, and mahogany takes the show surfaces. Walnut does not vanish. It becomes the secondary wood, the country wood, the chair no one photographed. A dining room that kept its Queen Anne table into the 1770s was not backward. It was paid for.

If you are buying one now, ignore the word Queen Anne on the tag until you have seen the knee, the foot, the secondary wood, and the joint. Then sit. If a swing leg meets your shin, the shop chose the pretty open shape over the diner. That choice is the style, not a defect. You can put a different chair there. You cannot put a different century.
