# Shaker trestles

A Shaker dining room is a long room with a long table and benches, not a suite of claw-foot chairs around a crotch pedestal. The table is often a trestle: a board on uprights and a shoe, sometimes with a stretcher you can find with your feet, maple or pine, a finish that is a stain and a varnish, not a French polish picture. The seats are benches. People sit on both sides. The meal is communal, sex-segregated in many communities, timed, silent or nearly silent in the popular telling, and — this is the furniture point — designed so that no one has a sideboard to perform at.

Shaker furniture is an American design religion that museums have made into a style. The dining objects are among the most copied: trestle tables sold as “Shaker” that were made in 1985 in a color the Believers would not have oiled. This page is the communal table, not the side table in a collector’s kitchen.

## A table that is a board

Trestle construction is older than the Shakers. What they did was refine the shoe, the swallowtail or keyed tenon, the thickness of a maple top that will be scrubbed, the absence of carving. Hancock, New Lebanon, Canterbury, Pleasant Hill, South Union — communities from Maine to Kentucky — had joiners and a shared preference. Local woods vary. The theology does not want a ball-and-claw.

Hancock Shaker Village’s trestle table of about 1830 is attributed to a Hancock craftsman on the strength of its turned legs. The museum’s label is the communal sentence: men and women ate separately and silently at tables like these in the large dining rooms in dwelling houses. An undated photograph of the East Family dining room shows two tables set for a meal with the same support brackets, turned legs, and arched feet. That photograph is the room. The single table on a platform is the fragment.

Edward Deming Andrews and Faith Andrews gave Hancock a New Lebanon communal table the National Gallery library caption still describes as a collector’s problem: about twenty feet, pine top of four boards, three birch trestles, a length that could seat twenty, or five “squares” of diners so dishes could pass. Benches instead of chairs speed the room and remove the hierarchy of armchair and side chair. An elder’s seat may still be a chair. The community’s bulk is a bench. That is a dining-room politics. Federal America was busy inventing the individual dining chair as a matched commodity. The Shakers invented a refusal.

## How the trestle comes apart

A trestle table has no perimeter apron. The stiffness lives in a center stretcher and in the uprights that rise from the shoes. That is why your knees are free and why a long top needs a third trestle in the middle, as the Hancock-related cherry table now at Fruitlands is described in the woodworking literature — almost eleven feet, three trestles. Do not take a reproduction’s eight-foot reduction as the document.

The shoe is a long foot, sometimes arched so diners at the end do not bark a shin on a flat slab. The upright is tenoned into the shoe and into the top, or the top sits on brackets and is pegged. A keyed or swallowtail tenon on the stretcher is the knockdown joint: drive out the wedge, the stretcher releases, the table goes through a door. Studio furniture in the twentieth century will steal that joint. Nakashima’s trestles are a different theology. Cross-link, do not collapse.

Hancock’s other tables help you read the dining trestle. The museum’s double drop-leaf of about 1830 is light, movable, a drawer at the end, a square leg that becomes a turned taper — sisters’ workrooms and smaller meals, not the family dining room. Built-in cupboards and drawers in the Brick Dwelling, about 1830, take the wall the sideboard would occupy. Hancock, Tyringham, and Enfield share a tapered drawer-side that has mystified later woodworkers. Storage is a room lining, not a piece of furniture that locks silver from the staff. The staff *is* the community.

## The dwelling-house dining room

Shaker meetinghouses are for worship. Shaker dwelling houses are for sleep, work, and meals. The dining room is a designed institution: long, well-windowed, peg rails for chairs and clothing, built-ins for crockery, a clock, a silence that later tourists will treat as atmosphere. It is not a farm hall. It is not a Federal parlor. It is an institutional dining room that happens to be beautiful to people who do not share the faith.

Sex segregation at table is a rule, not a caption flourish. Sisters and brethren ate at separate tables or on separate sides, depending on the community and the year. `[CITE NEEDED: a particular community’s seating plan with a dated Millennial Laws clause.]` The furniture supports the rule: two long tables in the East Family photograph, not one mixed suite. Children and hired help complicate the picture in later decades. This page will not invent a seating chart.

Peg rails take the overflow. A bench that is not wanted at breakfast hangs. A chair for an elder hangs. Hats hang. The wall is a storage system, not a place for a looking-glass and a marble slab. Hancock’s Brick Dwelling built-ins, about 1830, with dovetailed drawers and the bishopric’s tapered sides, are the other half of that wall. A visitor who photographs only the trestle has missed the room.

Pleasant Hill and South Union are not New Lebanon sentences. As the communities move west, cherry and walnut enter the dining room the way they enter any Kentucky or Ohio shop. The theology still refuses a claw foot. The forest changes. The species list does not have to.

The difference only goes so far. Shakers used hired and, in contested cases, unfree labor in the wider world they sold to. At the table the design is communal. The sideboard’s lock is someone else’s politics.

## What they sold versus what they used

Shaker chairs sold outside. The ladderback with a tilter button is a commercial object. The dining trestle was more often for the family. Later commerce and still later reproductions blurred that. A small “Shaker dining table” for four in cherry with a glossy finish is a twentieth-century retail object. A twenty-foot maple or pine trestle with bench wear on one side is the document.

Finishes are thin. Red stain, yellow, a residual ochre, later misunderstood as “natural wood.” Strippers have destroyed a lot of Shaker color in the name of honesty. The honesty was the paint. Hancock’s grain-painted blanket chest, attributed to Elder Grove Wright, is a later taste inside the community, not a license to strip a dining trestle to blond maple.

June Sprigg, Timothy Rieman, and Jean Burks are the monographs. Community histories hold the dining-room architecture. This page will not guess a lost Millennial Law from a catalog caption.

## Relation to the American dining room

Berman’s urban inventories do not include Hancock. The Shaker dining room is a parallel modernism: specialized, designed, and opposed to gentility. Arts and Crafts will later steal the trestle and the bench and put them in bungalows with a sideboard anyway. Stickley’s no. 631 plank table is a cousin, not a copy. The Stickley essay can say so. The Shakers did not need *The Craftsman*.

Look at the underside for community marks, paint ghosts, and repairs in kind. Look at the bench height against the table. A reproduction often gets the rise wrong — too chair-like. Look at the length. A “Shaker dining table” under seven feet is probably a later domestic reduction. Look at the wood: maple and pine in the east, walnut and cherry as you go west. Kentucky Shaker (Pleasant Hill) is not a New Lebanon maple sentence. The Andrews table’s pine-and-birch mix is a New Lebanon sentence sitting in a Hancock museum. Read the label for the village, not only for the word Shaker.

If you sit at one in a museum, notice the absence of an armchair at the end. That absence is the design. The American dining room spent a century putting the host at the head. The trestle spends the meal refusing a head. You can buy the look and miss the refusal. Many have.

A square, honest trestle in oak can sit near this tradition without claiming Believers. Simple and square is a Southern shop habit as well as a Shaker one. Do not put a peg rail in the caption unless the room has pegs.
