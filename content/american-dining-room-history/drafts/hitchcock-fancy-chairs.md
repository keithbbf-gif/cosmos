# Hitchcock chairs at table

The stencil on the back of the seat rail is the document: “L. HITCHCOCK. HITCHCOCKS-VILLE. CONN. WARRANTED.” Sometimes the wording shifts with partners. The chair under it is a painted fancy — turned legs, a pillowed tablet or a slat, rush or cane seat, gilt fruit and leaves in bronze powder. It is light. It was cheap. By the late 1820s a three-story brick factory on the Farmington River was turning out on the order of three hundred a week, something like fifteen thousand a year in the Henry Ford account, at prices the same essay puts between forty-five cents and a dollar seventy-five. That is not a cabinetmaker’s dining chair. It is a mill’s dining chair. America sat on it anyway.

Lambert Hitchcock apprenticed with Silas Cheney of Litchfield, who already made Federal sideboards and fancy painted chairs. Clockmakers like Eli Terry were nearby, thinking in interchangeable parts. In 1818 Hitchcock rented space and power at the Benham-Doolittle sawmill in Barkhamsted, at the fork of the Farmington and Still Rivers, and began making unfinished chair parts for stores and peddlers. Parts went as far as Charleston. Then came knockdown chairs, then finished chairs, then, in 1825, the brick factory. The hamlet had already been called Hitchcocksville (later Riverton). The dining room of the 1830s middle class is that factory’s market.

## A floor plan for a chair

Donna R. Braden’s Henry Ford essay is the clearest popular description of the 1825 building as a process: ground floor for sawing, turning, planing; second floor for glue and a kiln; third for paint and stencil. Precut stencils, bronze powders, theorem-painting skills that young women had learned in academies. Men made the chairs. Children put on early coats in some accounts. Women stenciled and rushed. The Connecticut mills record puts the 1820s workforce near a hundred — woodworkers, finishers, rush-seat weavers — one of the largest furniture workforces in the state. Roth’s description of the mill, as the mills inventory quotes it, is a 60-by-32-foot, three-story brick gable with a cupola, S-shaped wall anchors tying floor beams into the walls, later ells to the east and southwest.

This is not yet Grand Rapids. It is the argument Grand Rapids will win: a dining chair as a repeating product with a brand on the rail. Hitchcock opened stores in Hitchcocksville and Hartford, sold wholesale to dealers, and used Yankee peddlers. Braden is frank about the geography: isolated roads, a later bet on a Unionville canal that was not finished, a railroad that bypassed the town. He died in 1852 at fifty-seven with few assets. The name outlived the man. John T. Kenney’s 1946 revival is a Colonial Revival sequel, not this hour. Do not date a Kenney chair to 1828 because the stencil looks familiar.

## What the chair is made of

The Henry Ford’s marked side chair 00.3.4586.2, 1825–1835, lists hickory, maple, and rush, stenciled, thirty-four inches high. That is a typical species mix: hard maple or birch for turned parts that take paint, hickory in spindles or stretchers that take spring, rush for the seat. The wood is a substrate. The paint is the point. Grounds run black, dark green, sometimes a simulated rosewood. Striping, in the trade literature, is often yellow ocher rather than gold; gold is reserved for banding and the bronze-powder fruit. Later touch-ups reverse that economy and look louder.

Stenciling is not carving. It is faster, flatter, and, when the powder is original, surprisingly spatial in its shading. Each stencil is a part of a composition — a leaf, a fruit, a horn — positioned on the tablet or the slat and rubbed with bronze powder into a tacky coat. Professional male stencilers probably cut the patterns, Braden writes; women did much of the rubbing. Caroline Bennett’s 1835 theorem at the Henry Ford is the comparison object, not a Hitchcock employee claim. The academy taught the skill. The mill bought the skill.

Never strip the paint. A stripped Hitchcock is a ruined brand. The maple or birch under it was never the show.

## How the mill chair is joined

A Hitchcock is a turned frame, not a carved splat chair. Front legs are often one piece from floor to seat rail, ring-turned, then painted. Back posts run from floor to crest and take the tablet or slat in mortises. Stretchers — front, side, and often a medial — keep the box from racking. The rush seat is woven around or through the rails; it is structural once tight and a sag once the rush dries. A later plywood seat in a marked frame is a common repair and a lost document. Cane, when it appears, is woven through drilled rails the Baltimore way.

Knockdown is the early product. Parts left the Barkhamsted shed unfinished for stores and peddlers; Charleston already knew those parts before the brick factory painted a finished chair. A chair that ships as pieces is a chair whose joints were designed to be assembled by someone who is not the turner. Hide glue and a kiln on the second floor of the 1825 building are Braden’s next sentence: the mill took the assembly back in-house and sold a finished object. Either way the joint is a repeating mortise, not a one-off carving.

Breaks concentrate at the back posts, where a sitter leans, and at the rush, where a sitter sits. “Warranted” did not mean unbreakable. It meant a brand on a volume product. Look at the post where it meets the rail. A clean old break with hide-glue residue is a life. A dowel from 1970 is a repair. Neither makes the stencil a lie.

## What the chair does at dinner

It is Grecian without mahogany. The saber or turned front legs, the tablet, the gilt, are Empire language in paint. Around a pillar table it reads as a suite if you have six or eight. Around a kitchen drop-leaf it is just chairs. The rush seat breathes in summer and sags in twenty years. Re-rushing is expected. Original rush with an oxidized butt on the underside is a prize. Cane appears on some seats; it is the same summer logic as Baltimore fancy.

The chairs ship. The Connecticut mills record already has Hitchcock sending parts to Charleston in the early years. A “Southern” dining room of 1835 may be a local walnut table and Connecticut painted chairs. That mix is American. Matching mahogany suites are the expensive minority. The labor essay and the hunt-board essay are the other half of that Southern room: who stood at the board, what the tall server was actually called. This page is the chair that got off the wagon.

Ammi Phillips’s portrait of Julia Barton Hunting, about 1830, in the Henry Ford collection, puts a sitter on a Hitchcock-type chair as if it were already ordinary furniture. That is the documentary point. The chair had left the mill and entered the parlor and the dining room as a fact, not a novelty.

## Warranted, then common

“Warranted” is advertising. The chairs break at the back posts and at the rush. They were always a volume product. Survival of marked examples is good because there were so many, and because the Revival factory taught people to keep the stencil. Unmarked painted fancies of the same decade may be Hitchcocks, or rivals, or Baltimore’s older school. The rail mark is the only easy yes.

Partner wording matters more than folklore about backward letters. The draft stencil is Hitchcock alone. Later rails read Hitchcock, Alford & Co. after the partnership with Arba Alford, Hitchcock’s brother-in-law. Dealer literature makes a dating system out of reversed N’s; treat that as a `[CITE NEEDED]` unless you have a marked chair and a monograph in hand. Kenney-period stencils and an HCCo stamp are the twentieth century. The dining-room history does not need a fake 1828.

Hitchcock also made cabinet furniture and ran stores in Hartford. The chair is what the dining room remembers. A Hitchcock cabinet in a dining room is an extra, not the type.

## Labor, gender, the river

Water power is the shop’s condition. Bad winter roads and no rail are the shop’s limit. The labor is mixed-sex and partly child, which is ordinary for New England mills and still belongs in a furniture essay. The theorem-painting pipeline from female academies is a rare documented path from “accomplishments” to wage work on a dining chair. Rush-seat weavers are a trade of their own. Children striping legs are not a romantic caption.

Baltimore’s fancy is the older urban shop version of painted Grecian. Hitchcock is the mill version. IKEA will be the flat-pack version a century and a half later. The handmade suite does not disappear. It loses the middle of the market.

If you put a marked Hitchcock at a Federal D-end, you are mixing hours and that is fine. If you put eight marked Hitchcocks at a farmhouse table, you are in the 1830s middle. If you put Kenney reproductions at a 1790 table and call it colonial, you are in 1946. The stencil, read carefully, is a date. The dining-room history is a factory history from this page forward.
