# Butler’s pantry and breakfast room

Once the dining room has a name, the house grows rooms to serve it. The butler’s pantry — a passage of glass-front cabinets, a sink, a plate warmer, a pass-through — is furniture that is also architecture. The breakfast room is a second dining room with worse chairs and a better morning. Berman’s pattern-book counts already show breakfast rooms alongside dining rooms in English plates Americans copied. By the Gilded Age and the large suburban house of 1910, the campus of eating is ordinary at a certain income. The dining table can stay formal because toast has somewhere else to go.

This page is about those satellite rooms and the furniture they stole from the dining room.

## The pantry as a piece

Built-in cabinets with glass, a counter at serving height, drawers for linen, a silver drawer with a lock, sometimes a dumbwaiter. The sideboard in the dining room can shrink or become display because the working closet has moved next door. Leslie’s 1844 “closet” splits: monument in the dining room, function in the pantry.

Leaves live here. So do extra chairs. So does the good china that is not good enough for the sideboard’s glass. A butler’s pantry without a butler is common after the First World War. The wife or a hired girl uses the sink. The architecture outlives the job title. Mid-century houses drop the pantry and put the glass cabinets back in the kitchen. The open plan drops the door.

Period joinery manuals treat the butler’s pantry as a dense problem: a long serving table, a sink under a window, a dresser opposite for washed dishes, closets and silver drawers below, a sliding door to save swing. A suburban pantry adds dry-goods drawers and a flour bin and becomes a storeroom as well as a station. The millwork is the furniture. You cannot buy it at an antiques mall without buying the house.

The silver drawer is Leslie’s lock moved next door. Felt, a divided interior, sometimes a lock that still has a key on a hook. Linen drawers below or beside: cloths the size of a Gilded Age table need volume, not a sideboard cubby. Extra chairs lean. Leaves stand on edge. A high chair hangs. If you empty a pantry for a photograph you have already edited the dining-room history. The full pantry is the suite’s missing pieces.

## Sink, warmer, dumbwaiter

The sink is the pantry’s machine. It is why the room is not only a closet. Glass and silver can be washed out of sight of the guests. A plate warmer — steam, a hot closet, later a gas or electric box — keeps the next course from dying in a cold passage. Janet McKenzie Hill’s service manual *The Up-to-Date Waitress* puts a copper breakfast warmer with alcohol lamps on the serving table when the breakfast place is remote from the pantry: water, coffee, milk, a double boiler of cereal. That object is furniture. It is also a small stove. The dining-room sideboard, she is clear, holds cold things. Hot things go on the side table or the warmer. The split between display and heat is a furniture split.

The dumbwaiter is a vertical slide: a box on ropes or a later mechanical lift, kitchen to pantry or pantry to dining. It is why a townhouse can keep the kitchen in the basement and the dining room on the principal floor without a parade of open dishes on the stair. When the lift dies, the pantry becomes a landing and the service becomes a person on the stairs again. Look for the shaft. A boarded-up dumbwaiter is a dining-room history that still occupies volume.

A pass-through window between kitchen and pantry, or pantry and dining, is the horizontal cousin. It is a hole in a wall that is also a serving table. Mid-century kitchens will keep the hole and lose the pantry. The island is a later slab in the same argument.

## Breakfast furniture

Pembrokes, drop-leaves, painted chairs, a smaller extension table, Windsors, later a dinette. The breakfast room is allowed to be fashionable in a cheaper line. Hitchcock chairs that would look ambitious in a mahogany dining room look right here in 1835 and look Revival here in 1925.

Hill notes that some handsome houses set apart a sunny room, smaller and less decorated than the dining room, as a breakfast room, and that this room, like the dining room, opens into the pantry. Sometimes the “room” is only a bay or an alcove of the dining room with its own table and cabinet. The furniture follows the light: painted wood, a wipeable top, chairs that can take crumbs without a ceremony. Sun rooms and “breakfast nooks” of the 1920s — built-in benches, a table on a pedestal, oilcloth — are the architectural dinette. The Depression essay takes the factory chrome set. The nook is the carpenter’s version.

## The 1920s nook

A breakfast nook is a built-in: two benches, a table between them, often against a window, often in a kitchen rather than in a named breakfast room. The table may have a pedestal so knees can slide. The benches may have lids and hold linen. Oilcloth, then enamel, then a later laminate. This is satellite dining without a second room. Plan books of the 1910s–20s sell it as efficiency. The dining room, if the house still has one, can stay closed until Sunday.

The nook trains Americans to eat next to the stove again — a hall habit with better paint. It also trains the body: you slide in, you do not pull a chair, you do not sit at the head. Host and hostess arms have no job here. Four people, maybe six if the table pulls out. The dinette will chromify this idea and sell it to apartments that have no carpenter.

Look at the bench lids. If they lift, the nook is also a linen closet. If they do not, someone later nailed them shut or the carpenter never cut the hinge. A table pedestal in a nook is a knee solution, the Empire pillar’s grandchild in a smaller room. Four legs in that slot fight the benches. The carpenter who put a pedestal there had eaten in the space.

## What this does to the dining room

It becomes more ceremonial and less used. That is a risk. A room used only on Sunday still needs heat, polish, and a reason. Some households close the dining room except for holidays, which is how the room begins to die. The open-plan essay is the end of that death. The pantry and breakfast room are the middle: a specialization so complete the main room can be empty.

Wright’s dining rooms sometimes refuse the split by making dinner the event. Apartments refuse the split by having no space. Most American houses between 1890 and 1940 that could afford it built the split. Look at a plan. If there is a pantry, count the doors. Two doors (kitchen, dining) is a working pantry. A pantry with a window and a chair is a servant’s room in disguise.

Downing’s *Architecture of Country Houses* (1850) already counted servants as a condition of a villa. Berman tracks breakfast rooms on English plates Americans copied, then on American plans as the named dining room spreads. The pantry is the American carpenter’s answer to a kitchen that has moved farther from the table than a hall cookfire was. Three rooms (kitchen, pantry, dining) are a labor diagram. When the labor leaves, two of the rooms look like waste. Households close the dining room and eat in the breakfast room or the nook. That is not a failure of taste. It is a headcount.

Leaves stacked on edge, felt between them if anyone cared, are pantry furniture. Extra chairs hung or stacked, a high chair, a tray. The dining room’s suite looks complete because the incompleteness lives next door. When the pantry is gutted for a breakfast bar, the leaves go to the garage and the table freezes at six. The patents essay has met that table.

## Built-ins as dining furniture

A glass cabinet in a pantry is a china closet that cannot be sold apart from the house. That is why so much of this furniture is still in place and so little of it is in museums. The movable dining suite is over-represented in collections. The pantry is under-represented. If you are documenting a house, photograph the pantry empty and full. The full is the dining-room history.

A single good table in a room that is also the kitchen is an old hall idea and a new open-plan idea — closer to the refusal of the pantry split than to a butler’s parade. The pantry years are the years between. They produced excellent millwork. They also produced a dining room that forgot how to be everyday. When people say they want a dining room but eat at the island, they are living the afterlife of the breakfast room. The island is a slab table in the kitchen. MESDA 3163 would recognize the height. The labor is now the owner.
