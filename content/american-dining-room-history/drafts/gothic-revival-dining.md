# Gothic Revival dining

Andrew Jackson Downing’s *Architecture of Country Houses* (1850) is a dining-room book as much as a villa book. He wants rooms labeled, servants counted, and a moral style. Gothic — pointed arches, clustered columns, a certain earnest darkness — is one of the styles he will allow a country house. The dining furniture that follows is oak or walnut, sometimes rosewood, chairs with trefoil backs, tables with clustered or octagonal pillars, a sideboard that looks like a choir stall if the budget ran that far. Alexander Jackson Davis’s villas (Lyndhurst the famous one) gave the style a Hudson Valley architecture. Shops in New York and later the factories gave it a chair you could buy without a villa.

Gothic Revival dining is not a medieval hall. It is a nineteenth-century Protestant mood applied to a room that had only recently been invented. The specialized dining room and the Gothic suite arrive together for a class that read Downing and Loudon.

## Pattern books and the pointed

Berman tracks Loudon’s 1830s English influence into Downing’s 1850s American one. John Claudius Loudon’s *Encyclopaedia of Cottage, Farm, and Villa Architecture* (1833) is the English book named in that thesis. House pattern books now label dining rooms as a matter of course. The furniture plates — Davis, later commercial catalogs — give a pointed-arch back as a dining chair. Oak is the honest wood in the rhetoric. Walnut is what many shops actually used. Rosewood appears on the expensive set.

Downing’s 1850 volume, issued by Appleton in New York and Philadelphia, used Davis as illustrator. The Met holds a copy (24.66.111). Davis also prepared furniture illustrations for Downing’s earlier cottage books. Some of those published designs were later made as furniture; a Decorative Arts Trust note on the Lyndhurst Davis exhibition puts one Downing-plate chair in the orbit of Alexander Roux, the French émigré *ébéniste* in New York. Clustered colonnettes on a table base are the type’s tell. So is a quatrefoil piercing that would have looked like a church to a guest and like a fret to a joiner. Quality varies from architect-designed suites to a single Gothic chair in an otherwise Empire room. Mixed rooms are normal. A fully Gothic dining room is a statement house.

## Lyndhurst as the dining citation

Lyndhurst, Tarrytown, on the Hudson, is the architectural citation this page can actually stand in. Davis built it as Knoll, 1838–42, for William S. Paulding, a former New York mayor. He returned to enlarge it for George Merritt, who renamed it Lyndhurst; Jay Gould was the later private owner. The house keeps more than fifty pieces of furniture Davis designed for it. *The Magazine Antiques*, reviewing the Davis exhibition, notes Davis’s 1842 furniture for Lyndhurst’s first dining room, now shown in the Grand Picture Gallery. A 1989 account of the house describes a dining table Davis made for Merritt “in the style of Pugin,” still in place: a massive center base of molded columns, Gothic arched panels, and tracery, scaled to the room.

That is a particular table in a particular house, not a factory type. Do not assume every pointed chair in Tarrytown is Davis. The shops of Meeks and others supplied Gothic along with rococo. The Belter essay is the rococo hour. This page is the pointed one. Same decade, different plate.

Davis presented furniture designs with the house. Period accounts speak of dozens of drawings offered to Paulding. Built-ins and movable pieces together make the villa a single pointed argument. A wheel-back chair with “gears,” a pointed side chair, a library chair for another client — these are architect’s furniture. The factory chair with a trefoil fretted into a walnut splat is the portable villa. Downing would have had feelings about the difference. The dinner would still have been served.

## How the pointed chair and table are joined

A Gothic dining chair is a frame with a church in the back. The trefoil or pointed arch is cut through a splat or built up from moldings. Points catch a coat. That catch is the style. The Federal shield splat was already a little hostile to a shoulder. Gothic is more so. Comfort is not the brief. Atmosphere is the brief.

Look at the underside of a clustered base. If the colonnettes are three applied half-round moldings, you are in the factory. If they are carved in the solid or properly built up as grouped shafts with capitals, you are closer to a shop or an architect’s maker. Quatrefoils that are pierced through a solid plank will split if the wood was not dry; quatrefoils that are applied frets will pop. Extension slides begin to appear in the same years (the patents essay). A pointed chair can sit at a crank table. Gothic and mechanism are not enemies. The costume and the joint are different jobs.

Oak is the moral wood in Pugin and in Downing’s better sentences. American shops used walnut because they had it and because it carves. Rosewood appears when the client is buying a courtly darkness. Graining — walnut painted on a cheaper ground — is the factory’s way of agreeing with Downing while ignoring him. The tension is the century.

## Oak, morality, dinner

Gothic in England (Pugin) is a Catholic and then a national argument about truth to materials. *Contrasts* and *The True Principles of Pointed or Christian Architecture* are not American shop manuals. American Gothic dining is milder: a look, a library, a dining room that feels serious. Downing’s moral tone still matters. He is against sham. American factories will then sham the pointed arch in walnut graining and composition ornament.

Service in these rooms is increasingly formal. The sideboard grows. The table is a pillar or a long oak board. Eliza Leslie’s 1844 reader already needed a closet she called a sideboard. Berman’s inventories watch the specialized room become ordinary. Gothic costumes that room. Renaissance Revival will costume it again with a mountain of walnut. Eastlake will try to reform the fruit without returning to Hepplewhite.

Hired and, in the South, enslaved labor still work the board. A pointed chair does not change the station. It changes what the guest is asked to feel while someone else stands.

## The room Downing wanted counted

Downing’s villa is a labor count as well as a style. A house that needs three or more servants is his later definition of a villa, already in play in Berman’s reading of the builder’s guides. The Gothic dining room in that house is rear or side, connected to a pantry or a passage, dark enough to flatter oak, large enough for a table that does not fold. The sideboard that looks like a choir stall is a serving wall with a moral costume. It still holds plate. It still needs a person who is not eating.

Factory catalogs of the 1850s and 1860s sell the pointed chair without the villa. A trefoil splat on a walnut balloon-seat, a clustered-base table with a slide already in it, a sideboard with applied tracery in composition — these are Grand Rapids waiting to happen, still stamped New York or Boston or unnamed. The patents essay’s Briggs crank (1843) and Jupe’s British patent 6788 (1835) are the mechanical hour underneath the costume. A pointed chair at a crank table is not a contradiction. It is the decade.

Look at the seat. A sprung, stuffed Gothic chair is already leaving medieval furniture behind. The back is a church. The sit is a nineteenth-century comfort project. That split — pointed eyes, padded sit — is the American Gothic dining chair in one object. A hard oak bench in a hall is a different revival, later, and often a Colonial Revival mistake.

## What survives

Chairs survive better than tables. Tables were used and refinished. A Gothic dining chair with its original dark finish and a tired seat is a better document than a stripped “oak” revival piece from 1910. The Colonial Revival will later prefer Pilgrim over Gothic. The Gothic dining room becomes Victorian excess in the next generation’s eyes.

If you sit in a trefoil-back chair, the points catch a coat. Then look at the room. If there is no pointed architecture, the furniture is a portable villa. Downing wanted the house and the chair to agree. Most American diners bought the chair. The American dining room, having specialized, now costumes itself. That costuming will run through Renaissance Revival, Eastlake incising, and the Gilded Age’s imported rooms. Gothic is the first full costume after Empire’s Roman one.

Lyndhurst remains the place to see the agreement Downing wanted: pointed architecture, Davis furniture, a dining table that belongs to the room’s scale. A factory trefoil in a Greek Revival farmhouse is the other American fact. Both sat at dinner. Only one needed a Hudson villa to make sense.
