This series does not use student records or any protected health information (PHI). Local IDEA, FERPA, Medicaid, and district rules govern practice. Talk with your district, a licensed speech-language pathologist, or counsel before changing services.

Two students can share a disability category and not share a week.

One needs a thirty-minute group and a progress note. The other needs an AAC system that lost a button over the weekend, a classroom observation before the IEP, an interpreter at the meeting, a Medicaid consent form that is not this essay’s job to fill out, and a teacher who wants to know what to do during the science talk. If your district counts both of them as “1,” it is telling the truth about **caseload** and almost nothing about **workload**.

ASHA has been trying to get that sentence into administrative English since the 2002 position statement. The Practice Portal page still opens with it. The 2024 Schools Survey still has to ask both “how many” and “what is manageable,” because the first number will not sit still for the second.

## What caseload is allowed to mean

On ASHA’s page, caseload is the number of students with IEPs, IFSPs, and 504 plans the SLP serves through direct or indirect options. It can also be quantified as sessions in a time frame. Some districts fold in MTSS / RTI students who do not have a disability identification. That last group is easy to forget when a board packet prints a single integer.

The portal lists five groups school SLPs actually serve:

- School-age students whose *primary* IDEA category is speech or language impairment
- School-age students with another primary category who receive speech-language as a **related service**
- Students with 504 plans who need accommodations or supports
- Preschoolers eligible for speech-language services
- Students in pre-referral / MTSS work meant to reduce later difficulty with language and literacy

A headcount that only names the first group is a different object from a headcount that names all five. Essay 19 stays with the doors. Essay 17 stays with the SLI category. Do not average them in a hallway.

## What workload is doing that caseload cannot

Workload is all activities required and performed. Face-to-face time is one slice. The rest of the slice is what makes those minutes a free appropriate public education instead of a hallway conversation.

ASHA sorts the rest into four clusters. They are not poetry. They are a filing system for a meeting with a principal (essay 43).

**Direct services.** Screening, assessment, intervention, IEP/IFSP implementation, counseling of students. The 2024 survey’s clinical providers put a mean of 22.6 weekly hours here, plus 4.0 on diagnostics. That is the part everyone can see through a door.

**Indirect services that support the educational program.** Planning with teachers, preparing materials, programming AAC, writing the present levels a general educator can use (essay 22), progress notes that are not a secret (essay 29).

**Indirect activities that support LRE and the general curriculum.** Co-teaching, classroom observation, adapting the language of a unit, showing a paraprofessional how a device is backed up. Least restrictive environment is a legal requirement, not a vibe. It costs time.

**Compliance.** IEP meetings, prior written notice, Medicaid documentation if the district bills, trainings the building assigned, bus duty, the other duties that still appear as 2.4 mean hours of “other” on the 2024 activity list.

When ASHA says the average *caseload* has been relatively stable while *roles* have expanded, this is the paragraph they mean. The headcount did not explode. The week did.

## Why ASHA will not give you a number

The portal’s refusal is easy to misread as softness. It is the opposite.

There is no research that supports one caseload size for every building. Student needs vary — AAC maintenance is not a lisp in a group of four, and this series will not pretend they are the same week. And a published number becomes a minimum: if you are under it, you can be given another building.

That is why the 2002 position statement asks districts to **analyze workload**, then set caseload standards from the analysis, not the other way around. The implementation guide and the Workload Calculator are the tools. They are not this blog’s job to operate. They are the documents to name in a meeting.

*Endrew F. v. Douglas County* (2017) is the Court case the portal now cites next to IDEA: an IEP must be reasonably calculated to enable progress appropriate in light of the child’s circumstances. “Merely more than de minimis” is not the standard. A caseload so large that model and intensity are chosen from the leftover minutes, not from the student, is the practice pattern Brandel and Loeb described. It is also how a district can meet a ratio and still miss FAPE.

## A hallway test

If you can replace the word *caseload* with *headcount* and the sentence still works, you were talking about caseload.

If you need the word *week* — evaluations open, devices down, meetings stacked, documentation hours — you were talking about workload.

If the sentence only works with *minutes missed*, you have left both words and arrived at compensatory services (essay 41).

Keep the three words in different pockets. The 2024 median of 50 (essay 04) is a caseload statistic. The 6.0 mean hours of documentation (essay 06) is a workload statistic. The 37 percent who are always or almost always required to make up missed sessions (essay 07) is a policy statistic. Mixing them is how a headline gets you to nod at the wrong problem.

You can list this week’s clusters on one page. You can take that page to a principal without attaching a student name. You can refuse a protocol and a billing code while you do it.

That list is not a complaint. It is the job, counted.
