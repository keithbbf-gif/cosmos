Four tools. One closet habit.

**Pull-out** is the student coming to the SLP. **Push-in** is the SLP coming to the classroom for a defined service. **Consult** is the SLP working with the adults so the educational program changes. **Collaboration / co-teaching** is shared planning and shared delivery, not a drive-by.

ASHA’s FAQ will not pick a winner. Inclusive-practices documents say combine and change models as needs change. The traditional pull-out model remains viable *and* is only one option. Cirrin and colleagues’ systematic review of service-delivery models is the evidence trail; it does not hand you Tuesday.

The caseload portal’s warning still stands: large caseloads shrink the menu. People pull out because the schedule can see a group of four more easily than it can see a co-taught writing block. That is leftover design (essay 24), not science.

## What each tool is good at

Pull-out is good for a skill that needs a quieter stage, a new AAC map, a fluency or voice session that the classroom would drown, an evaluation. It is bad as the only adult conversation the student gets about language. It is also a removal. Write it that way.

Push-in is good when the goal lives in the classroom language (essay 23) and the teacher wants another adult who knows the unit. It is bad when the SLP becomes an extra pair of hands for center management and the specialized minutes evaporate. It is not automatically “more inclusive” if the student is singled out on the carpet.

Consult is good when the bottleneck is adult practice: how a paraprofessional prompts, how a science teacher unpacks a prompt, how a device is charged. It is bad when it is the only service on a page that promised direct minutes. The 2024 mean of 2.5 consultation hours a week is the national smear. Limited time for collaboration ranked sixth among challenges. The ranking and the hour are the same story.

Collaboration is good when both adults planned the lesson. It is bad when “collaborate” means an email at 10 p.m. The 3:1 model (essay 26) is one attempt to put collaboration on a calendar the IEP can name.

## What the 2010 roles document wanted

Prevention, classroom-based work, and interprofessional practice — not as slogans, as the job. The document’s “don’ts” include being the only person who owns communication and treating the closet as the program. Fifteen years later, the 2024 survey still shows most time in direct intervention (22.6 hours) and a sliver in consultation (2.5). That is not a moral failure. It is a schedule.

## How to choose without a religion

Start from the present level (essay 22). If the problem is audible only in the speech room, ask whether you have the wrong present level or the right pull-out.

Write the model on the service page. A parent who thinks their child leaves the room twice a week should not discover in March that the child has been “pushed in” to a class the parent cannot picture.

Do not stack four models on one student to look modern. Do not refuse pull-out to look modern.

Count the adults. A push-in session without a teacher who knows you are coming is not push-in. It is a visit.

## Caseload honesty

If your menu collapsed to pull-out groups because you have 51 elementary students (essay 11), say that to the principal with the portal sentence in your hand. The fix may be another FTE, a 3:1 calendar, fewer buildings, or a real MTSS so not every worry becomes an IEP (essay 19). The fix is not a new noun for the same Thursday group.

You can refuse a protocol for what to do inside any of the four models. You can refuse a student vignette.

The tools are furniture. The IEP is the room. The leftover slot is not a philosophy.
