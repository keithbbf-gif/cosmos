This series does not use student records or any protected health information (PHI). Local IDEA, FERPA, Medicaid, and district rules govern practice. Talk with your district, a licensed speech-language pathologist, or counsel before changing services.

Two languages are not a disorder. A dialect is not a disorder. Limited English is not, by itself, SLI.

ASHA’s public bilingualism page and the multilingual service-delivery portal are the documents that keep saying so. IDEA’s evaluation rules expect teams to rule out limited English proficiency and lack of appropriate instruction as the *primary* explanation before they attach a disability label. State operational definitions often repeat the exclusion. This essay will not evaluate a student. It will keep a caseload from becoming unofficial ESL.

## Difference

The student is acquiring English on a typical path for their history: age of first exposure, richness of input in each language, interrupted schooling, the dialect of the home. Skills may look “low” on an English-only booklet and typical in the home language — or the reverse, if the booklet was the only place they have ever done that task.

Dynamic assessment and a true bilingual evaluation, when needed, are the professional tools. Interpreters and translators are a legal and ethical requirement when the adult in the room does not share the language. ASHA has a portal page for that collaboration. This pack will not write the script.

## Disorder

A communication disability that shows up across languages and cannot be explained by exposure, dialect, or the test’s hole (essay 30). The educational adverse effect still has to be there (essay 17). The team still has to find it.

The error in both directions is expensive. Calling difference a disorder fills the caseload with students whose educational need is language *instruction*, not special education. Missing a disorder in a multilingual student is how a child sits in a hallway for years while adults say “give them time.” CDC’s “wait and see” critique in the pediatric pack has a school cousin.

## How caseload pressure picks wrong

English-only tests because they are on the shelf. No interpreter because the meeting is at 7:15. A goal about “using complete English sentences” that is really an ELD goal. Dismissal that never comes because English is still arriving.

The portal already said large caseloads limit equitable services for linguistically diverse students. The extra labor of being a cultural broker — also on that page — is workload that does not appear in the 50-student integer.

## 504, MTSS, IEP

An English learner can walk through any of the three doors (essay 19). MTSS may be the right first teaching. It may not delay an evaluation once requested (essay 20). 504 may be about access to language accommodations. IEP is for a disability. Do not use SLI as the only way to get an interpreter.

## Language for the table

*We will not treat bilingualism as a risk factor. We will not treat an English-only score as a fact if the student is not that test’s world. We will not diagnose from this page.*

You can print ASHA’s public bilingualism URL for a family. You can refuse a protocol. You can refuse a named child.

Limited English is not, by itself, a related-service ticket. A dialect is not a delay, and a translation app is not an interpreter. ASHA’s public bilingualism page is the document to hand a relative who says two languages confuse a child. They do not. If the file has no home-language sample and no interpreter log, you do not have an evaluation of a multilingual student. You have an English booklet. Write that gap down before you write a category.

Difference is typical. Disorder is a team finding. English class is a class. Keep the three nouns in different pockets.
