**Disclaimer.** Educational only. Not legal, billing, union, or clinical advice. Not a diagnosis and not a treatment protocol. This series does not use student records or any protected health information (PHI). Local IDEA, FERPA, Medicaid, and district rules govern practice. Talk with your district, a licensed speech-language pathologist, or counsel before changing services.

Snow day. Field trip. Fire drill. The SLP is out with strep. The student is at the nurse. The IEP still says thirty minutes, twice a week.

ASHA asked clinical providers whether they were required to make up missed sessions. Most said yes in some form. Forty-two percent: only for a few circumstances. Thirty-seven percent: always or almost always. Twenty percent: never or almost never. One percent said the question did not apply. Differences by facility were not statistically significant once “not applicable” was set aside.

That split is the news. It is also not the law. The law lives on the service page of an individual IEP and in the district’s procedure for when FAPE is interrupted. This essay will not write your procedure. It will keep the survey, the statute, and the hallway from pretending they are the same clock.

## What the IEP actually promised

Related services are part of a free appropriate public education when the team writes them there. Frequency, location, and duration are not decorations. They are how a parent, a teacher, and a later investigator know what “speech” meant this year.

A missed session is not automatically a denial of FAPE. A pattern of missed sessions, a month of vacancy, a make-up policy that exists only as a rumor — those are how districts end up in compensatory-services conversations (essay 41). *Endrew F.* did not invent make-up rules. It did say the program must be reasonably calculated for appropriate progress, not for “merely more than de minimis.” Minutes that never occur are a hard way to calculate anything.

ASHA’s caseload portal lists make-up ability among the things large caseloads threaten, next to ethical concerns and litigation risk. That is a professional-issues sentence, not a finding about your Tuesday.

## Three kinds of “missed”

**The building closed.** Everyone’s clock stopped. Districts differ on whether related services are made up when the calendar loses a day. The answer is local. Ask for the written rule, not the lounge.

**The student was unavailable.** Absence, dismissal, testing, a reward assembly. Some IEPs anticipate this. Some make-up policies distinguish excused from unexcused as if speech were detention. The clean question is still: what did the IEP promise, and what procedure did the LEA adopt?

**The provider was unavailable.** Vacancy, illness, IEP meetings stacked through the only therapy block, a contractor who did not arrive. This is the row that turns into headlines. The 2024 workforce report’s job-market item — 79 percent said more openings than seekers — sits behind a lot of these weeks (essay 08). A make-up rule that says “always” without adding hours is a rule that steals from someone else’s IEP.

Notice what the survey did *not* ask: who missed, why, whether make-ups happened in groups of eight, or whether they were written as compensatory services. Do not invent those answers.

## How caseload swallows the clock

Brandel and Loeb’s line, cited on ASHA’s portal, is that caseload size often drives intensity and model more than student characteristics do. Make-up week is where that sentence becomes a schedule. If the only open slots are Thursday lunch and a group that already has four different goals, you are not “catching up.” You are stacking (essay 27).

The 3:1 model (essay 26) is one attempt to put indirect work on the calendar so IEP meetings do not cancel the only direct block. It is optional. It still has to be written so a parent can read it. A make-up policy that ignores the model you already wrote is how you get two clocks.

## What to do that is not a protocol

Read the service page. Read the district make-up / compensatory procedure. Read the 2024 split so you know your building is not the only one arguing. Keep student names off the argument.

If you are a parent reading this: “How does this school make up related-service minutes when the provider or the child is out?” is a complete question. You are owed the procedure, not a diagnosis.

If you are an SLP: track *reasons* at the category level — building, student, provider — without building a shadow record that becomes PHI in a personal notebook. The official record is the official record.

The IEP clock is not a vibe. It is a page. The survey only told us how often people are told to chase it.
