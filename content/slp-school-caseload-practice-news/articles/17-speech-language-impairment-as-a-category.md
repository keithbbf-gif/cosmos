**Disclaimer.** Educational only. Not legal, billing, union, or clinical advice. Not a diagnosis and not a treatment protocol. This series does not use student records or any protected health information (PHI). Local IDEA, FERPA, Medicaid, and district rules govern practice. Talk with your district, a licensed speech-language pathologist, or counsel before changing services.

Federal law names **speech or language impairment** as one of the disability categories under which a child may be eligible for special education. The federal definition is short: a communication disorder — stuttering, impaired articulation, a language impairment, or a voice impairment — that adversely affects educational performance.

That sentence is doing three jobs. It names a **communication disorder** (a clinical-sounding object). It requires an **educational adverse effect** (a school object). It sits inside a **team process** (a legal object). A blog that treats any one of those jobs as the whole category is lying.

This essay will not tell you a child is eligible. It will not reprint your state’s cut scores. It will keep the category from being used as a slur, a shopping list, or a caseload dump.

## Adverse effect is the school word

A lisp that does not affect educational performance is not, on the federal sentence, SLI. A language disability that wrecks reading comprehension and classroom talk may be — if the rest of the state’s criteria and the team’s evaluation say so.

“Educational performance” is broader than a reading percentile. States and courts have argued about grades, state tests, social communication, and vocational performance. ASHA’s eligibility-and-dismissal materials for schools walk clinicians through that argument. They do not let a blog finish it.

The opposite error is also common: “They are passing, so they cannot be SLI.” Passing is not a legal eraser. *Rowley* already refused to treat grade advancement as automatic FAPE. *Endrew F.* refused trivial progress. A student can pass and still need a related service under another category — or still meet SLI under state rules. The team has to look.

## State operational definitions do the real work

Some states require a standard-score discrepancy. Some require a process that looks more like classroom performance and dynamic assessment. Some list exclusions (dialect, limited English, lack of instruction) that the evaluation must rule *in or out*, not wave at (essay 36).

If you move districts, the category name stays and the door changes. That is why this pack will not print a national cut score. Open the state regulation. Open the district evaluation manual. If they disagree, that is a compliance problem, not a blog problem.

## SLI is not the only way an SLP serves a student

ASHA’s five groups (essay 02) are the reminder. A student whose primary category is autism, specific learning disability, or other health impairment may still receive speech-language as a related service. Counting only SLI will make a caseload look small and a week look mysterious.

Conversely, stuffing every communication difficulty into SLI to “get them help” is how categories become junk drawers. MTSS and 504 exist (essay 19). So does “not eligible, here is what the classroom can do.” The last sentence is allowed. It is not a diagnosis.

## Dismissal is the category’s other door

Ten percent of 2024 respondents named difficulty with dismissal as their top barrier to a manageable caseload. That is not a license to dismiss. It is a description of a bottleneck: criteria that never quite get met, teams that fear the parent, or goals that were written so they can never be mastered (essay 23).

ASHA’s dismissal materials ask whether the educational need remains, not whether the clinician is tired. Tired is workload. Eligibility is a team finding.

## How to talk about the category without labeling a child

Say “the federal SLI category requires a communication disorder that adversely affects educational performance, and the state writes the operational definition.” Say “related-service students are not in that category and still count.” Do not say “your child is SLI.” Do not say “SLI means autism.” Do not say “SLI is just sounds.”

The category is a door in a statute. The student is a student. Keep the nouns apart.
