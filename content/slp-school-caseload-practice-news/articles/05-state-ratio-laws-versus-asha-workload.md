A legislature can print a number. ASHA, on purpose, will not.

That sentence is the whole fight inside a lot of “caseload” bills, and it is easy to flatten. Advocates want a ceiling a superintendent cannot shrug off. ASHA’s Practice Portal and the 2002 position statement want a **workload analysis** because a ceiling becomes a floor and because two caseloads of 55 are not the same week.

Both instincts can be true in the same statehouse. They are still different tools.

## What a ratio law is

Some states publish caseload guidelines or maximums for school SLPs. Some leave the number to the local education agency. ASHA keeps a state caseload chart for members; this pack will not reprint it, because the cells move and a blog that freezes a statute is a liability. Open the chart. Open the state education code. If the bill on your desk is an amendment, open the amendment.

A ratio can be written as students per FTE, as a “weighted” count (AAC or multiple disabilities counting as more than one), or as a “soft” guideline a district may exceed with a waiver. Those three sentences are not interchangeable. A waiver culture is how a printed 55 becomes a practiced 80. A weighted count is how a printed 40 becomes a practiced 28 *or* a practiced 40 that still wrecks the week.

A ratio is also silent about evaluations, MTSS, 504, supervision, and Medicaid documentation. It counts the object the statute named. Essay 02 is the vocabulary for everything the statute forgot.

## What ASHA is doing instead

ASHA’s FAQ is short: the Association does not recommend a maximum or a minimum. Schools should consider the total workload. The portal lists the reasons: no research supports one number; needs vary; a number will be read as a minimum and attract extra buildings.

The prescribed move is analysis: list the roles, sort them into the four clusters (direct; educational-program support; LRE/curriculum support; compliance), attach time, and let the leftover minutes tell you how many students that FTE can carry *this year*. The implementation guide and the Workload Calculator exist for that meeting. They do not pass a bill. They do not fire a contractor.

When ASHA staff talk to SEALs (State Education Advocacy Leaders), they are often walking this exact ridge: support people who want relief, without handing opponents a sentence that says “ASHA’s cap is 40.” The 2024 survey’s manageable median is 40. That is a survey answer, not a policy.

## How a bill and a survey collide in the paper

A reporter who writes “ASHA recommends 40, but State X allows 70” has merged two documents. Correct the sentence: *ASHA does not recommend a number. In 2024, the median ‘manageable’ answer from full-time clinical providers was 40. State X’s statute / guideline / silence is [whatever the code says]. The survey median actual caseload in State X, if n ≥ 25, was [table].*

Indiana’s 2024 actual median was 78 against a manageable 50. New York’s actual median was 32 against a manageable 30. Those figures do not tell you which state has a better statute. They tell you what certified people reported. A state with a tight ratio and a vacancy crisis can still print a low median because the students are sitting on a contractor’s list in the next county, or because they are not being evaluated. Essay 14.

A state with no ratio and a strong workload practice can print a median that looks “fine” while paperwork still ranks first (essay 06). The integer is not the week.

## What to take to a hearing

If you testify or write a letter — this series is not telling you to — the clean packet is three pages: the ASHA portal printout that refuses a national cap and defines workload; the 2024 executive summary with the n visible; and the state code or bill text with the definition of “student” underlined. If the bill counts only primary SLI and ignores related-service students, say that. If it ignores preschool, 504, and MTSS, say that.

Do not promise that a printed 50 will satisfy *Endrew F.* Do not promise it will cut burnout. Do not put a student on the record.

A ratio can be a floor against the worst weeks. A workload analysis is how you describe the week you actually have. Use the word that matches the document in your hand.
