A goal that can never be met is not ambitious. It is a trap.

IDEA wants measurable annual goals, including academic and functional goals. *Endrew F.* wants those goals to be appropriately ambitious in light of the child’s circumstances — “the chance to meet challenging objectives.” ED’s Q&A told teams to take goal-setting seriously enough that the standard is real. None of those documents asked for “100 percent accuracy in all conversational settings across all school environments for three consecutive data days forever.”

This essay will not hand you a goal bank. Goal banks are how every student on a caseload ends up with the same /r/ sentence. It will name the failures that make dismissal impossible (essay 21) and make progress reports fiction (essay 29).

## Educationally relevant, again

If the only place the goal can be observed is the speech room, ask why. Sometimes the answer is good: a new skill is being shaped and the classroom is not the first stage. Sometimes the answer is habit. ASHA’s 2010 roles document wanted intervention tied to the curriculum. A goal about “explaining a three-step science procedure using temporal words” can be measured in the lab. A goal about “improving language skills” cannot be measured anywhere.

Secondary goals that ignore credit-bearing classes are closet goals (essay 11). Elementary goals that ignore the language of the reading block will collide with the science-of-reading conversation (essay 37) whether you want them to or not.

## Measurable without being cartoonish

Measurable means a later reader — parent, teacher, next year’s SLP — can tell whether the year did anything. A number can help. A number can also lie: 80 percent of what, collected by whom, in which setting, with which level of support?

Name the setting. Name the support you will fade, if fading is the point. Name a curriculum-shaped product (a retell of the week’s story, a request on the device during centers, a two-sentence claim in social studies). Do not name a protocol. Do not name a proprietary program as if it were the goal.

Baseline belongs in the present level (essay 22). A goal that restates the baseline with a bigger percentage is a slide, not a plan.

## The 100 percent problem

Some skills do need a high criterion — safety words on a device, a swallow-related school plan this series will not write. Most communication goals do not need perfection. Perfection is how you keep a student on the caseload after the educational need has moved. It is also how you ignore generalization: 90 percent in the closet and silence in the classroom is not success.

*Endrew F.* is not a demand for 100 percent. It is a demand that the objective be challenging *and* calculated. Calculated includes “this student, this year, this curriculum,” not “the template.”

## AI and the cloned goal

If a generator can write the goal without the present level, throw the goal away (essay 40). If two students who do not share a week share a goal word-for-word, at least one IEP is decorative.

## Workload honesty

Writing fewer, better goals is faster in November and slower in September. Large caseloads push people toward the bank. The portal already said caseload size drives model and intensity. It also drives prose.

The 3:1 indirect week (essay 26) is one place to sit with a teacher and ask, “What would you notice if this goal were working?” If the teacher cannot answer, the goal is not in the curriculum.

You can refuse a bank. You can refuse a protocol. You can refuse to publish a student example.

The goal is a bet on a year. Make it a bet a classroom could settle.
