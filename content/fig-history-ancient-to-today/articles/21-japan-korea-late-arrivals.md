# Ichijiku and a 16th-Century Landing

Choon-Ki Kang, writing in Korean journals in 1989 and 1990, put *Ficus carica* among economic plants that arrived from outside. His fruit history of Korea names loquats in early Chosŏn and figs around the 16th century. His companion paper on introduced plants says the same: common fig “around 16th A.D.” The abstracts do not give a ship’s log. China is the obvious neighbor. A southern European ship in East Asian waters is thinkable in that century and not proven in this pack.

Japan’s common-fig word is *ichijiku*. Popular and horticultural histories place the tree in the early Edo period, sometimes via China, sometimes via Portuguese routes through Macau and Nagasaki. This pack has not opened a Tokugawa planting diary. It will treat Edo introduction as the working consensus of garden historians and will not invent a temple origin or a first monk.

## A drawing that traveled the other way

Naturalis in Leiden holds a pencil-and-wash *Ficus carica* by Ishizaki Yūshi in the Cock Blomhoff collection — a Japanese hand, a Dutch factory man’s album, a Mediterranean tree. That is the late-arrival story in one sheet: the plant is foreign, the attention is local, the paper ends up in a European museum. Check the file’s institutional licence before a commercial WP import (WC-013).

The drawing is not a proof of Edo introduction. It is proof of Edo *attention*. Attention is what a late guest gets.

## What late arrival means

It means East Asia’s *carica* history is garden history, then nursery history, then a small processing industry (China’s jam plants in Wang’s paper, Japan’s Western-style orchards, Korea’s limited plantings). It is not a Neolithic argument. It is not a missing chapter of Gilgal. Readers who want “figs in all civilizations” will be tempted to pad this. Do not. A 16th-century landing is already a story. It is a story about ships, curiosity, and a climate that is often too wet for the crate.

Hardy-fig culture in cold East Asian districts is a modern conversation that belongs with Chicago Hardy and the northern garden (articles 36, 57), not with Tang (article 20). The Chinese name *wúhuāguǒ* may have traveled with the wood or ahead of it. The Korean and Japanese stories are receptions of that guest, not second origins.
