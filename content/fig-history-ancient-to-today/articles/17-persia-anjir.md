# Anjīr: The Iranian Fig and Its Place-Names

Houshang Alam’s *Encyclopaedia Iranica* entry (1999; updated 2013) is the adult way to write the Iranian fig. He starts with taxonomy that will not sit still — *Ficus carica* and its varieties, *rupestris*, *F. johannis*, Browicz in *Flora Iranica* 153 — and with vernaculars that are almost insults: jackal fig, wind fig, demon fig, fool’s fig. Then he counts places named for the tree: Anjīra, Anjīrak, Anjīrān, Anjīrābād, a mountain Anjīrkūh. Afghanistan’s gazetteer adds another nineteen. A country that names villages for a tree is a country that ate it.

## Before Islam, without romance

Strabo (2.1.14) gives Hyrcania — roughly Gorgān — trees that yield sixty *medimni*. That is a topos of fertility, kin to Pliny’s Onesicritus number. Alam lets it stand as an indication, not a weighbill. Babylonian and Assyrian *tittu* (article 06) is Laufer’s warrant that the plateau’s neighbors knew the fruit; Alam quotes him and does not pretend the warrant is a Persepolis find.

The oldest Iranian *word* we can hold is Middle Persian *anjīr* in the *Bundahišn* (16.26, Anklesaria): one of ten fruits edible outside and inside. That is Sasanian taxonomy of eating, and it is enough to underwrite cultivation on the plateau in late antiquity. Alam uses it to push back against Dymock’s line that Muslims brought the fig to India. The name *anjīr* in Indian languages, and Laufer’s old Chinese *a-ži* matching an n-less Iranian form, are a linguistic wake toward the east (articles 19–20). Linguistics is not a caravan list. It is a reason not to start India’s clock at the Delhi Sultanate.

## Medieval notices

Muqaddasī names Arrajān, Sābūr (Shapur), Fasā in Fārs, and places in Jibāl. Masʿūdī says the *wazīrī* figs of a Samarra quarter beat Arrajān, Ḥulwān, and Syria for sweetness, thin peel, small seeds — a tasting note across an empire. Ibn Ḥawqal names Gorgān. Rashīd al-Dīn claims he made the fig succeed in Tabrīz “where it had never existed before,” and that grafting on mulberry gave extremely fine fruit. Report the claim. Do not certify the graft. Abūnaṣrī Heravī lists white, black, red, and yellow.

## Estahban, which you can still walk

Fars is dried-fig country in the modern market: *anjīr-e shīrāzī*, much of it from Estahbanāt. The FAO GIAHS file on Estahban rainfed orchards is strong on slope, altitude, water-knowledge, and the labor of previous generations. It is weaker when it wanders into “4000 BCE in Egypt” and “southern Arabia” as origin. Use the file for the living system: rainfed trees on hills that hold humidity, a landscape at least a couple of centuries deep in its present form, Fars holding most of Iran’s rainfed fig area. Alam, writing earlier, already had about twenty-four edible varieties and a complaint that no national selection program was keeping export quality from slipping.

Stringed half-dried figs — *anjīr-reshta* — are a processing form, not a cultivar. Jam and compote are household and factory. The Kurdish decoction Alam notes as a laxative is the last general medical use he will allow.

Iran’s fig is not a footnote to Turkey’s. It is a plateau crop with its own words, slopes, and medieval tasting notes. The next article follows the dried fruit east on roads that did not care which empire named them.
