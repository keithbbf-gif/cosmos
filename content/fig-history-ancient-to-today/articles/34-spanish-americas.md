# A Cutting in the Mission Cart

A Smyrna-type fig on a sixteenth-century Atlantic crossing would have been a cruel joke: the tree would have lived, the fruit would have dropped, the wasp would have died in the hold if she came at all. The figs that took in Hispaniola, Mexico, and the Andean coastal valleys were common types — parthenocarpic, the only clones that can found an orchard without a caprifig culture. Morton’s *Fruits of Warm Climates* gives Spanish plantings in the 1560s. Condit and Eisen treat the later California Mission as a descendant of that Iberian-and-Mexican wood, not as a separate creation.

This pack has not opened a 1560s *relación* with a fig line item. It treats Morton as a compilation that matches the biology. If a later historian finds an earlier island date, the biology will not change.

## Mexico to the north

New Spain’s central valleys and the Pacific slope can ripen figs. The mission system — a church, a garden, a cutting from the last garden — is how a clone walks a thousand miles without a seed. Baja and Alta California are the end of that walk (article 35). Peru and Chile are a different walk, down the Pacific (article 37).

Indigenous American agriculture did not need this tree. The fig is a colonial passenger that became a village tree, then a market tree, then, in a few places, an industry. It did not become a sacred native *Ficus*. The Americas were not a *Ficus* civilization in the South Asian sense. They became, in spots, a *carica* backyard.

## Le Moyne’s other fig

Jacques Le Moyne de Morgues drew *Ficus carica* as a European artist of plants. His plate (WC-010) is not a Florida native. It is the same passenger, seen by a man who also drew the Atlantic world. Use it as a 16th-century European eye, not as an American wild fig.
