# Two Figs and a Loaf, Under Ash

The painting is almost rude in its smallness. A sill, a round loaf, two figs. Museo Archeologico Nazionale, Naples, inventory 8625. Fourth Style, from Herculaneum. ArchaiOptix’s photograph on Wikimedia is CC BY-SA 4.0; the museum notes that commercial reuse of the photo of the object wants a conversation. The object itself is a wall that died in 79.

This is how a Campanian house wanted fruit to be seen: not a harvest, a portion. Bread and figs as the modest pair — the file’s older title says “the most modest and frugal of foods,” which is a modern caption’s moral. Pliny, who died in the same eruption, had just written that life’s softness could be measured in fig names. The painter of 8625 is on the other side of that softness: enough, not twenty-nine.

## Orchards on the wall

Pompeii’s Casa del Frutteto (I.9.5) keeps a Third-Style fresco of a fig tree with a serpent in it. That is garden and omen together, a painted orchard in a city that also had real ones. The House of the Fruit Orchard is not named by us; it is named by what the rooms show. Figs here are trees, not dessert.

Casa dei Cervi at Herculaneum offers a composite of fruit vignettes: peaches and a glass of water; a silver tray with plums, dried figs, dates, a coin; a cup of red wine. Dried figs in the same tray as dates — the long-keeping pair — next to money. That is a still life that knows trade.

None of these paintings is a cultivar key. Bimbi will do that in 1696. What they are is proof of *attention*. You do not fresco a fruit you do not eat or sell or dry.

## Carbonized gardens

The same ash that kept the paint kept wood and, in some shops, food. This article will not list every carbonized fig from the Bay of Naples; the publications are uneven and easy to overclaim. The safer sentence: Campania was fig country in the year the mountain closed the book. Pliny’s Pompeian drying type (article 10) and the city’s name on a clone are not accidents. The villa hinterland (article 09) is just up the road.
