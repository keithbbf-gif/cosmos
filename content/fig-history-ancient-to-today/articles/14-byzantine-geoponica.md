# The Geoponica Keeps the Orchard Awake

The *Geoponica* is a book that looks like a committee. Advice sits next to advice, names still stuck to paragraphs, a tenth-century imperial frame around material that had already been compiled, in some form, under the name of Cassianus Bassus. It is not charming. It is not a mosaic of court figs. It is the reason a planting note can survive a broken century.

Ibn al-ʿAwwām, farming west of Seville in the late 1100s, would draw hundreds of citations from this Greek line. The Filāḥa Texts Project’s count — about 615 of his 1,900 citations, roughly a third, most of them this tradition — is the fig’s late antique job in a number. The tree did not need Constantinople in order to fruit. The *handbook* needed Constantinople, or someone like it, in order not to die.

## What a digest is for

A digest is conservative on purpose. It keeps caprification from sliding into superstition or into a forgotten peasant trick. It keeps soil notes, planting times, storage jars, the sort of sentence Cato would have recognized and Pliny would have catalogued. A farmer in Bithynia and a later farmer in the Aljarafe can be using cousins of the same sentence without either man knowing the other’s name. That is not plagiarism in our sense. It is how premodern agriculture remembered.

The eastern Mediterranean orchard in these centuries is still the old mix: olive, vine, fig, where water and tax allow. Date country is further south and east. The fig is the tree that does not need a palace irrigation crew, which is why it survives a fiscal crisis better than a citrus garden. When the Arab conquests remade who collected the tax, they did not remake the wood. They changed who wrote the next handbook and in which language.

Ibn Ḥajjāj, the Andalusi whom Ibn al-ʿAwwām liked to put first on questions of “the ancients,” was himself a reader of this Greek pile. The relay is not East-then-West as a cartoon. It is a library that keeps being copied while the trees ignore the library.

## What the capital thought worth compiling

That an emperor’s circle thought agriculture worth a compilation at the highest level is itself a fact. Grain gets the panic. Fruit gets the handbook when someone in the palace still eats like a landowner. The fig’s presence in that compilation is not a starring role. It is membership. Membership is how a practice stays speakable.

Late antique and Byzantine Egypt still had both figs (article 03). Anatolia still had the Aegean drying country that would later be called Smyrna by grocers (article 22). The Levantine household tree (article 04) did not pause for a dynasty. The *Geoponica* is the written weather over that ground.

## What we will not decorate

We will not invent a Byzantine court fig festival. We will not attach a cultivar to a particular emperor without a text. We will not pretend to have a critical edition of every fig paragraph in our pocket; the point is the *relay*, which the Filāḥa citation counts make visible, not a line-by-line commentary.

Pliny’s Italian list does not die in 79. It becomes, through this relay, part of the Islamic west’s library (articles 15–16). The tree on the ground never needed the library. The library is how types and practices survive when the people who knew them in their bodies are dead.

A blank on a timeline between Pliny and Ibn al-ʿAwwām is a failure of our pictures, not a failure of orchards. This article is there to stop a reader from filling the blank with atmosphere.
