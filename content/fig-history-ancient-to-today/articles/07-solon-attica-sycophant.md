# Solon’s Oil, Attic Figs, and a Word That Got Away

Plutarch, writing centuries after Solon, says the lawgiver allowed only oil to be sold abroad among the products of the soil. Other exports were forbidden; the archon who failed to curse the exporter paid a hundred drachmas. “His first table is the one which contains this law.” Then Plutarch makes a move a careful historian has to keep visible: “One cannot, therefore, wholly disbelieve those who say that the exportation of figs also was anciently forbidden, and that the one who showed up, or pointed out such exporters, was called a ‘sycophant,’ or fig-shower.”

*Solon* 24.1 is the passage. It is not an Archaic inscription. It is a later biographer saying the oil law makes the fig story plausible. Athenaeus has a similar explanation. The *Oxford English Dictionary* (2nd ed., print 1989), as etymonline reports it, says the informer-against-fig-export story *cannot be substantiated*. The Greek word is older than the cute origin. A *sykophantēs* in classical Athens is a man who brings prosecutions, often dirty ones, in a legal system that ran on private litigants.

So we have two histories that must not be glued.

## The Attic fig, which is real

Athens grew figs. Attic dried figs were a known good in a world that already named fruit by place — later Pliny will do it with a clerk’s glee (article 10). Solon’s law, if Plutarch has the table right, is a food-security and class law: keep the calories home, let the oil go, oil being a surplus the elite could ship. Figs, if they were included in “other products,” were too useful as poor sugar to alienate. That reading does not require the folk etymology. It only requires that figs were worth mentioning in the same breath as the soil’s produce.

Aristophanes’ Athens is full of informal prosecutors. The legal history (MacDowell and the rest) is about fines for failed suits, not about customs officers in fig orchards. A magazine piece that tells schoolchildren “sycophant means fig snitch” has chosen the charm and dropped the city.

## What we can keep

Keep the law about oil, as Plutarch reports it, with the date-gap showing. Keep the fact that later Greeks *wanted* the fig in that story — which means figs were already a charged Attic plant, something you could imagine the state hoarding. Keep Theophrastus’s Attic and island observations for the next article, where a botanist watches insects come out of wild figs and cultivated fruit swell.

Drop the capital-offense-for-stealing-figs embroidery that wandered into Blackstone. Drop any claim that we have Solon’s fig clause on stone.

The Attic fig’s later life is island and mainland drying, Kymi’s doubled fruit, a PDO that still uses the old pride (article 24). That pride has a classical shadow. The shadow is not a custom house. It is a city that knew what it liked to eat and invented, later, a joke about who told.
