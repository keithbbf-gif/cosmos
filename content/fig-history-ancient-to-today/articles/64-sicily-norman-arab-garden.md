# Sicily: The Island That Already Knew the Tree

Pliny’s Italian list already assumes the south. Sicily, which fed Rome sugar of other kinds — grain, then later cane in a brief cruel season — grew figs as default. The Norman-Arab centuries (11th–13th) are famous for gardens, water, a court that spoke more than one language about plants. The *Filāḥa* bench (article 15) is Andalusi, not Palermitan, but the same library weather reached the island: irrigation as prestige, fruit as court display, a peasant tree that did not need the court.

Do not write “the Arabs brought the fig to Sicily.” Cato and Pliny will laugh. Write: they brought a way of talking about water and a set of garden plants that made the existing fig look like one citizen among citrus and sugar and cotton, depending on the decade.

Modern Sicilian figs are a village and market crop, sometimes Dottato-ish, often local names Condit only half-caught. They are not the Cilento PDO. They are the island version of article 25’s south.
