# Tittu: The Fig in the Cuneiform Orchard

In Akkadian the fig is *tittu* (also *tiʾtu*, *tittum*), written with the logogram GIŠ.PÈŠ. The Sumerian tree is *ĝešpeš*. A Concise Dictionary of Akkadian gives the forms, the plural *tīnātu*, and a pressed cake, *libittum ša tīnātim* — a “brick of figs.” That brick is the first honest picture this article wants: not a goddess, a ration.

J. N. Postgate’s “Notes on Fruit in the Cuneiform Sources” (*Bulletin on Sumerian Agriculture* 3, 1987) is still the paper to walk through the third millennium and then the Assyrian evidence. This pack will not pretend to have reread every Ur III tablet. It will not invent yields. It will say what the secondary literature that sits on those tablets actually says.

## A lexical orchard

The archaic “Tree List” from Uruk III (3200–3000 BCE) is treated, in recent Liverpool work on the southern alluvium, as documenting a fundamental fruit set: date palm, grape, fig, apple. Olive and pomegranate sit awkwardly in that early southern list — olive for climate, pomegranate appearing more clearly after the Sargonic period. The fig is already in the conversation when the cities are learning to write trees.

That is not Gilgal. It is not domestication. It is administration noticing a plant worth a sign.

## Palms first, figs in the shade

Late third-millennium orchards in southern Mesopotamia are date country. Texts and, now, more environmental work around Nippur put palms along channels, with grape, fig, pomegranate, and apple in the irrigated mix (Postgate 1987; Focke 2015; Greco 2015; Powell 1992 — as cited in the 2019 *Iraq* landscape paper). The state is louder about grain than about fruit. Horticulture looks more private, more garden, more the *ĝeš-nu-kiri6* — the gardener — than the cereal overseer. Figs show up because someone counted them, not because someone wrote a georgic.

Old Assyrian traders know a fig cake. Old Babylonian texts can specify “Amorite figs.” Middle Assyrian notes include crushed figs by the seah. These are the dictionary’s examples, not a cookbook. They are enough to kill the idea that the fig was unknown on the plateau’s western door. Laufer, and after him Iranica, used Babylonian and Assyrian figs to argue that ancient Persia knew the fruit too (article 17). That is a warrant, not a find from Persepolis.

## What the tablets will not give us

They will not give Blastophaga. They will not give Sarılop. They will not tell us whether a Babylonian *tittu* was a common type or a caprified one. A pressed brick could be either. The climate of the southern alluvium is hard on some Mediterranean trees and kind to the date. A fig that lives there is an irrigated decision.

Do not translate every GIŠ.PÈŠ as a still-life. Translate it as a line in an account, then go back to the Levant if you want shade poetry. Mesopotamia’s fig is a clerk’s fruit. That is a civilization too.
