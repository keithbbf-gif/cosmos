# Cato’s Soil, and the Villa That Ate Figs

Cato the Censor did not write a poem about figs. He wrote where to put them. *De Agri Cultura* 8.1, as Pliny later quotes him: plant the marisca on chalky or open ground; plant the African, Herculanean, Saguntine, the winter fig, and the black long-stalked Telanian on richer soil or ground that has been manured.

That is a working list from the second century BCE. Already the names are places and people: Africa, Herculaneum, Saguntum, a Telanian type with a long pedicel. Already there is a winter fig, which means someone is cheating the calendar. Already there is a sense that one clone wants lean ground and another wants fat. California extension circulars would still be making that kind of sentence two thousand years later. The crop did not get more mysterious. It got more names.

## The villa as a fig machine

Columella’s *De Re Rustica* is the handbook of a later, richer villa world — slave gangs, preservation jars, a countryside that feeds a city of a million rumors. Figs in that world are fresh for the table, dried for the year, and part of the laborer’s ration. Pliny says as much when he gets to the fruit’s place in the diet: a food you can legislate into a worker’s day. The villa is not a hobby farm. It is a firm.

Italian names for the caprifig crops — mamme, profichi, mammoni — will become, Eisen said, household words in every country that took the wasp seriously. They are Italian because the villa and the Mezzogiorno kept the practice alive while northern Europe forgot it. Cato does not lecture on *Blastophaga*. He assumes you know how to keep a tree. The silence is not ignorance. It is competence.

## Marisca and the rest

We cannot taste Cato’s marisca. Condit and Eisen spent their lives trying to match living clones to dead names and often failed. What we can say: Romans already thought in *types*. They imported from Chalcis and Chios. They named a fig for Livia and a fig for Pompey. The villa orchard was a collection as much as a field. That collecting habit is the ancestor of Bimbi’s Medici painting and of Condit’s monograph — elite desire to have the list.

Saguntine figs point to Iberia already in Cato’s lifetime, or at least to a type associated with Saguntum. African figs are in the ground before Cato uses one as a prop in the Senate (article 11). The peninsula is not waiting for Islam to discover the fruit. It is already sending names east.
