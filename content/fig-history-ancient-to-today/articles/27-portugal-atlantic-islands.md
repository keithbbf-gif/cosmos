# The Atlantic Lesson: Portugal, Algarve, Islands

Portugal’s south is a fig shore with an African glance. The Algarve — Arabic *al-gharb*, the west — kept an Andalusi sense of orchard after it became a Christian kingdom. Dried figs from that coast entered Atlantic trade because Lisbon and Lagos were already looking at water, not only at Castile.

The islands are the lesson. Madeira, the Azores, the Canaries (Castilian, but in the same ocean school) taught Mediterranean plants to live with Atlantic humidity and a different year. Sugarcane was the brutal cash. Figs were the quieter passenger: a tree for a farmstead, a dried fruit for a ship that might be out for weeks. Morton’s compilation and Condit’s notes treat these islands as way stations in the crop’s west. This pack will not invent a 1420s planting date we have not opened a chronicle for. It will say the biology is obvious: once you can keep a cutting alive on a caravel, the New World is a season away (article 34).

## Names that slipped

Portuguese types in Condit — names that later show up in California and in the PLOS ONE re-evaluation of NCGR (San João Branco as a parent in profile space) — are a reminder that Lisbon’s nursery world was not small. A fig that learned the Atlantic is a fig that might like California fog better than a Sarılop does.

Do not make Portugal a second Aydın. Production numbers do not support a crate empire. Make it a hinge.
