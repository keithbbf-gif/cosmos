# White Marseilles at the Edge of the Possible

The tree in Lambeth Palace’s courtyard is a White Marseilles. The Palace’s own 2014 telling — when a cutting went to Pope Francis — says Cardinal Reginald Pole brought it as Archbishop in 1556, probably from southern Italy, fruit white-fleshed, rich, ripe when the skin goes chartreuse and splits. Loudon, visiting in 1836, said Pole planted in 1548 and found the old trees already destroyed in a repair, only young plants from cuttings left. Garden historians have cleaned the popular “1525” date: Pole was abroad. Matthew Paris, describing the rotten year 1257, mentions figs among fruits spoiled in jars — a medieval English fig as a preserved thing, not a proof of a standing orchard at Lambeth.

So: possible Roman or medieval figs in Britain; a documented early-modern reintroduction associated with Pole; a clone that keeps being recut. The 2014 gift is the clone performing diplomacy, which is a kind of history too.

## Jefferson’s notebook

Thomas Jefferson’s garden records, as Monticello’s published notes use them, include figs from early in his planting life and Marseilles cuttings from France in 1789. This pack cites that published line, not a souvenir tea towel. Virginia is not Aydın. A wall, a slope, a hot summer, a clone that will ripen without a wasp — the northern garden’s entire syllabus.

Pocock’s Oxford tree (1648 in the old garden-book story) and Kennicott’s stolen fig are too good to be first sources and too famous to pretend we never heard them. File under anecdote.

## What the northern garden invented

Not a new species. A culture of walls, pots, and disappointment. Every Chicago backyard fig (article 57) is Lambeth’s problem with worse January. The White Marseilles is one of the clones that made people keep trying.
