# A Fig in the Senate

Cato had a sentence he liked: Carthage must be destroyed. Pliny, in *Natural History* 15.20, gives him a prop. The old man brings into the Curia a ripe fig from Africa, asks the senators when they think it was picked, and when they say “just now,” tells them it was plucked in Carthage the day before yesterday. So near is the enemy. The Third Punic War follows. Cato dies before the city burns. Pliny, who cannot leave a good astonishment alone, asks whether we should admire the man’s cunning or the speed of the passage, and then decides the most marvelous fact is this: a city that had been Rome’s rival for a hundred and twenty years fell at last to an illustration drawn from a single fig.

Trebia, Trasimene, Cannae, Hannibal at the Colline Gate — none of them, in Pliny’s rhetoric, did what the fruit did.

## What the story is

It is Pliny’s story. It is told in a book about trees, in the chapter after the variety list, as a historical anecdote “connected with the fig.” The African type is already in Cato’s planting note (article 09). The fruit in the Senate is that type’s political shadow: Africa is close enough to send dessert still swollen.

Is the two-day crossing possible? With a fast ship and a ripe fig, maybe. Is it likely Cato staged it? Pliny himself offers accident or ingenuity. We do not have a contemporary minute of the session. We have a first-century encyclopedia enjoying a moral.

## What the story does

It makes the fig a measure of distance. Empire is a problem of days. A fruit that cannot travel is a local fact. A fruit that can travel is a threat, a trade, a reason to build a better crate. The rest of this series — Smyrna boxes through İzmir, Adriatic figs east from California in 1889, air-freight trays in January — is Cato’s stunt without the Senate.

It also makes North Africa a fig country in the Roman mind before the Maghreb’s later GI names (article 29). Carthage’s hinterland grew the fruit. The name “African” on an Italian tree is already a confusion Pliny flagged in 15.19: recently introduced into Africa, yet wearing the country’s name. The Senate fig is “from that province.” Whether the clone was African by breeding or by last address, the point was the address.

We will not say a fig caused a war. We will say a writer who had seen Rome’s appetite believed a fruit could stand in for a fleet. That belief is itself evidence: by the 70s CE the fig was a language Italians expected senators, and readers, to speak.
