# Aljarafe: The Hill That Wrote the Fig

The Aljarafe is a raised, fertile country west of Seville. Ibn al-ʿAwwām farmed there. So, the Project tells us, did Abū l-Khayr and Ibn Ḥajjāj. Olive is the crop the district is still willing to brag about. Fig is the crop the books will not shut up about. A tableland that can hold water in winter and bake in summer is fig weather if you choose the right type and do not get greedy.

This is not Córdoba’s mosque as a fruit bowl. This is estates, wells, and a class of men who wrote like colleagues.

## Huerta as politics

Andalusi irrigation — *qanāt* thinking, river takes, garden geometry — is a political fact. Who owns the water owns the late fruit. The fig is less thirsty than some neighbors and more particular about drying weather if you want a crate, not a jam. Fresh figs for a city, dried figs for a ship: Seville could imagine both. After the Christian conquest the huerta did not vanish. It changed landlords. Article 17’s Persian gardens and article 16’s Andalusi ones are cousins in water law, not copies.

Granada’s late gardens — Generalife as a name a tourist knows — are court versions of the same habit: fruit in sight of water. Use a Commons Generalife file only with its CC tag; this pack does not embed one yet because the point is the *system*, not the postcard. The Aljarafe is uglier and more important.

## After 1248

Seville falls. The books remain, some of them to be translated in Madrid in 1802 as antiquarian agriculture. The trees remain in a different way: as Andalusian village figs, as types that will later be called by Spanish names Condit will try to index. The Reconquista is not a biological event. It is a legal one. The fig did not convert.

Portugal’s Algarve and the Atlantic islands (article 27) take the same Iberian wood to sea. That is the Aljarafe’s long wake.
