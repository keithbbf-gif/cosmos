# Under His Vine and Under His Fig Tree

Micah 4:4 is not a proverb about fruit. It is a picture of a man who does not have to be afraid: “they shall sit every man under his vine and under his fig tree, and none shall make them afraid.” 1 Kings 4:25 uses the same pair for Solomon’s peace. The politics are in the sitting. A vine and a fig at the door mean the levy has not taken the son, the army has not taken the yard, and the year was good enough that the trees were not cut for siege.

This article stays inside named passages. It will not derive a “fig goddess of Israel.” It will not pretend Genesis 3 is a cultivar note.

## The seven species and the spy’s load

Deuteronomy 8:8 lists a land of wheat, barley, vines, figs, pomegranates, oil, and honey. The fig is a member of a legal-theological inventory, not a mascot. Numbers 13:23 sends the spies back from Wadi Eshcol with a cluster of grapes on a pole, and pomegranates, and figs. The fruit is evidence: this is the kind of country it is. You can argue about the date of the text. You cannot argue the authors thought figs belonged in that country.

Genesis 3:7 — sewn fig leaves — is a different register. The tree is large-leaved and at hand in the storyteller’s world. It is not a botanical identification of Eden. Later European art made the leaf a decency device (article 44). That is reception. The Hebrew line is shorter: they knew they were naked, and they used what grew.

## Work, not emblem

Amos 7:14: the prophet says he is not a prophet’s son but a herdsman and a *boles shiqmim* — a dresser, gash-er, tender of sycomore-figs. That is *Ficus sycomorus* work, the Galil cut, the lowland and Shephelah tree, not a highland *carica* dessert orchard. A history that files Amos under “biblical figs” without changing species has not read the word.

2 Kings 20:7 / Isaiah 38:21: Isaiah has them lay a cake of figs on Hezekiah’s boil. Kitchen medicine. Dioscorides will later write a more elaborate fig simple (article 45). The Judahite story does not need him.

## Peace as horticulture

The vine-and-fig formula works because both plants are perennial investments. You do not get a fig tree from a season’s labor the way you get barley. You get it from years of not being driven off the terrace. When later readers (including Tissot, painting Nathaniel under a fig tree for a Paris-and-Brooklyn public) turn the tree into a pious shade, they are not wrong about comfort. They are downstream of Micah’s politics.

New Testament fig episodes — Nathaniel (John 1:48), the barren tree, Zacchaeus’s sycamore-fig in Luke 19:4 (*sykomorea*, again not *carica*) — belong to a Greek-speaking Levant that still knew both trees. Article 49 takes the symbolism without folding the species together.

The Hebrew fig is a working tree that writers borrowed. The borrowing is the history. The rest is Sunday-school furniture, and we can leave it in the hall.
