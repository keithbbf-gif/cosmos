# Smyrna on the Label, Orchards Inland

English-speaking grocers learned “Smyrna fig” the way they learned “Smyrna carpet”: as a port. The orchards were in the Menderes country — Aydın, Germencik, Nazilli, the Küçük Menderes towns — baked slopes that know how to finish a Sarılop. İzmir (Smyrna) took the freight, the commission, the reputation, and, in 1922, a fire that rewrote the city. The fruit did not move its hills.

Ottoman fig export to Europe and America in the nineteenth century is the immediate background of California’s inferiority complex (articles 35, 54). Eisen and Condit write as men who have tasted the imported article and found the local Adriatic wanting. The Turks, California rumor said, had sent sterile cuttings on purpose. Condit records the rumor in order to bury it: the cuttings were the right clone. The wasp was missing.

## A trade that is also a culture

Caprification in the Aegean was not a USDA discovery. It was farm practice. Profichi hung in the trees, a calendar of insect and heat, a drying yard that could make or lose a year. Women and seasonal hands did the turning (article 52). Packers in the city did the rest: grades, boxes, the word *Smyrna* in Roman type.

The Ottoman state was a customs and concession state as much as a planting state. This article will not invent a sultan’s fig monopoly. It will say the empire’s Aegean shore was one of the few places on earth where a Smyrna-type fig, a wasp, and a dry harvest month agreed for long enough to feed a world market.

## After the empire

The Republic kept the hills and changed the stationery. “Aydın fig” and then Aydın İnciri as a PDO (2016) are a legal effort to put the orchard back on the label (article 23). The old export name still turns up in American mouths because Calimyrna taught it a second life.
