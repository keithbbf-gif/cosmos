# Wúhuāguǒ: A Fruit Without a Flower, on China’s Clock

*Flora of China* (vol. 5, p. 52) treats *Ficus carica* as a cultivated shrub, native to the Mediterranean eastward to Afghanistan, “introduced to China during the Tang Dynasty (618–906 CE)” and grown especially in Xinjiang. The Chinese name is 无花果 *wúhuāguǒ* — fruit without a flower. That is a better popular description of a syconium than most European vernaculars managed. The flowers are inside. The fruit you see is the room.

Julia Morton’s compilation puts the tree in Chinese gardens by 1550. An ISHS paper by Wang Lianju and colleagues says introduction “about one thousand years ago” and commercial cultivation only in recent decades, with jam and preserved fruit leading the factory list. These dates do not agree, and this article will not pick a winner to look tidy. Tang is the Flora’s line. Ming gardens are Morton’s. “About a thousand years” is a modern horticultural shrug that can cover both if you squint. Laufer’s *a-ži*, reconstructed as an Iranian loan, is the linguistic reason a pre-Ming arrival is thinkable.

## What China already had

A *Ficus* flora of its own, including species that are not dessert. The common fig is a western guest, as in India. Xinjiang’s dryness is why the Flora points there: the crop recognizes a familiar summer. Eastern China can grow the tree as a garden curiosity and a processing fruit; it does not become Aydın.

Do not recruit Daoist or Buddhist tree lore for *carica*. That lore already has other trunks. The flowerless name is botany, not metaphysics.

## What modern China did

Wang’s paper is a late-twentieth-century industry note: cultivars split between fresh and dual-use, twenty-odd processing plants, a research interest in flavonoids and, in their mouse work, extracts and tumors. Report that they reported it. Do not turn a cooperative lab result into a health claim on a FigRoots label (article 45).

The historical point is smaller. China received the tree late relative to the Levant and wrote a perfect name for its strangeness. The belt on our schematic map does not honestly include the North China Plain as old fig country. It can nod toward Xinjiang as a dry-land extension.
