# Sugar That Would Travel: Figs on the Long Roads

Fresh figs are a rumor that spoils. Dried figs are freight. The difference is the whole commercial history of the crop. Aydın’s Sarılop, Estahban’s rainfed fruit, Kymi’s doubled cakes, a California Adriatic in 1889 — all of them are answers to the same physics. Remove water, keep sugar, pack against beetles, write a place-name that a buyer in another climate already wants.

“Silk Road” is a nineteenth-century ribbon laid over many routes, some of which carried more rhubarb and horses than silk. Figs moved on pieces of that mesh because they could. Rashīd al-Dīn mentions good figs in districts of Transoxiana and Khwarazm. Afghanistan’s *anjīr* toponyms (Iranica) are not caravan invoices, but they are a map of a fruit people thought worth naming a pass for. The Chinese loan-name Laufer reconstructed from Iranian (article 20) is evidence of *talk* about the fruit, which usually follows the fruit itself, or follows a pharmacopoeia.

## What a sack needs

A dry autumn. A type that sugars in the sun without turning to leather. Labor — often women’s labor (article 52) — to turn, sort, and keep the dust down. A pack that can take a month of camel or a week of rail. The 19th-century Smyrna box is the famous version. The older version is a skin, a basket, a brick (article 06).

Banditry and customs are the rest of the freight. Dried fruit is taxable because it is countable. A fresh fig is gone before the clerk arrives.

## What the roads do not prove

They do not prove that China grew commercial *carica* in the Tang (article 20 will stay in that fight). They do not prove a single “Persian fig” conquered India. They prove that once a region dries fruit for itself, a surplus can walk.

Modern FAOSTAT still shows the same physics: Turkey’s dried export share dwarfs its fresh; Afghanistan and Iran appear in production tables as countries that understand dry fruit. The table is not a caravan. It is the same idea with a better scale.
