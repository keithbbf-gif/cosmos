# Pollination Without Folklore

*Blastophaga psenes* is not a spirit of fertility. She is a fig wasp. The female enters a syconium through the ostiole, loses wings, and tries to lay eggs in short-styled flowers. In a caprifig she can breed. In a long-styled edible fig she usually cannot, but she can leave pollen if she came from a male crop that had ripe anthers near the door. Then she dies in the room. That is the whole romance.

The year of the insect, as Italian and California growers still name it, runs in crops of the caprifig: *mamme* (overwintering), *profichi* (spring, the pollen crop hung in Smyrna orchards), *mammoni* (summer). The names are not mystical. They are a labor calendar. You hang *profichi* when the edible Smyrna crop is at the right receptive window. You do not hang them because a story says so. You hang them because the fruit will drop if you do not.

## What pollination buys

Seed. Crunch. A flavor people in the Aegean and in California packing houses learned to call “the real fig.” Hormonal swell that holds the syconium on the tree. A crate that can stand next to an imported Smyrna box. Common-type figs — Mission, many “Adriatic” names, the backyard tree — do not pay this tax. They persist. They can be excellent. They are a different machine. Condit’s navel-and-Valencia analogy still earns its keep: Mission and Adriatic were navels. Smyrna was a Valencia.

San Pedro types split the bill: brebas without the wasp, main crop often with. If a nursery catalogue collapses those four boxes into “needs a pollinator” / “doesn’t,” it is not lying so much as giving up.

## What folklore adds

Folklore adds a goddess, a secret Turkish method, a claim that the wasp “stings the fig to ripen it,” a claim that caprification is a pagan rite. Gashing a *sycomorus* fruit is a different species and a different chemistry (ethylene). Do not import Galil’s Cairo knife into a Fresno Smyrna row. Do not say the wasp fertilizes the tree the way a farmer fertilizes a field. She pollinates flowers inside a room.

There are other fig wasps on other *Ficus* species. *Ceratosolen arabicus* on *F. sycomorus* is the Egyptian story. African and Asian *Ficus* have their own pairs. *B. psenes* is the partner of the common fig as Mediterranean people grow it. A backyard tree in Oregon that fruits without a wasp is not a miracle and not a broken law. It is a Common-type clone doing Common-type work.

## Other wasps, other rooms

*Ceratosolen arabicus* is the sycomore’s partner, and Egypt’s gashing habit exists because that partner left. African and Asian *Ficus* species have their own pairs; Berg and Wiebes mapped a continent of them. A magazine that calls every fig wasp *Blastophaga* has already failed biology class.

Pollen on the female’s body, not a “sting that ripens,” is the transfer. Galil and Ne’eman’s 1977 paper is the English place to send a skeptic. Seed set in the “male” syconia (1978) is the other half: the caprifig is not sterile decoration. It is a nursery that also sheds pollen.

California’s insectaries, when they existed as a commercial habit, were caprifig orchards timed to the *profichi*. They were not temples. They were calendars with wings.


## Calendar with wings

*Mamme, profichi, mammoni* are labor words. She enters, often dies, sometimes leaves pollen. Galil and Ne’eman 1977–78 are the skeptic’s papers. *Ceratosolen* is the other Egyptian story. A backyard Common type in a cold state is not a broken law. Folklore that says “sting to ripen” has imported a sycomore knife into the wrong room. Insectaries were timed caprifig orchards, not temples.
