# Courtyard Shade, Not a Miracle Tree

Mediterranean cities already know the fig as a courtyard and vacant-lot tree: a stick in a rubble pocket, a crown over a table, a fruit that stains a sidewalk and starts a fight with the neighbor. That is urban forestry as accident. It is also a real cooling habit. Shade and evapotranspiration lower a surface temperature. They do that for plane, mulberry, and fig alike. This article will not invent a peer-reviewed “fig heat-island coefficient.” If a named study lands, cite it. Until then, stay with the courtyard.

## What cities actually do

They tolerate the tree until the roots lift a pavement. They harvest or they waste. They argue about wasps (usually they are arguing about fruit flies). They plant “ornamental” *Ficus* that are other species — rubber figs, banyans, *F. microcarpa* — and then a magazine writer conflates them with *carica*. Do not. A Moreton Bay fig is a civic monument. A *carica* is a dooryard crop that escaped.

## Accident as forestry

A lot tree is not a municipal plan. It still shades a table. Other *Ficus* species do the monumental civic job and confuse writers. Keep *carica* in the courtyard and *microcarpa* in the plaza unless a city list says otherwise.


## Plaza trees that are not this tree

Municipal *Ficus microcarpa* and Moreton Bay figs are civic monuments with roots that lift pavements and reputations that steal this series’ name. *F. carica* in a city is usually a courtyard accident, a vacant-lot escape, a table under a crown that stains a sidewalk. Shade and evapotranspiration cool surfaces. They do that for plane and mulberry too. Until a named heat-island study isolates *carica*, this pack will not invent a coefficient.

Cities argue about fruit flies and wasps and then cut the tree. That argument is older than the phrase “urban heat island.” The climate-belt article is about commercial edges. This one is about where a person can sit without a farm.


## The lot tree and the plaza liar

Mediterranean cities already run an accidental forestry program. A cutting in a rubble pocket, a crown over a plastic table, fruit that stains a sidewalk and starts a fight about wasps that are usually fruit flies — that is *Ficus carica* in town. It cools a courtyard the way any broadleaf shade cools a courtyard: intercept sun, move a little water, hold a person in a place that is not the asphalt.

The phrase “urban heat island” is a research program with sensors and grids. This pack will not invent a *carica*-specific coefficient and hang a fake DOI on it. Plane, mulberry, and a neglected fig do related work. If a named study isolates this species later, cite it. Until then, stay with the table.

The liar is the other *Ficus*. Municipal *F. microcarpa*, rubber figs, Moreton Bay figs in former British cities — those are civic monuments with roots that lift pavements and names that steal this series’ noun. A writer who illustrates “the urban fig” with a banyan has left the crop. Australia’s DPI opens its fig note by warning that native figs are not edible *carica*. The same warning belongs on a city page.

What cities actually do is tolerate the tree until the pavement lifts or the fruit ferments, then cut it, then miss the shade in August. That cycle is older than climate-adaptation brochures.
