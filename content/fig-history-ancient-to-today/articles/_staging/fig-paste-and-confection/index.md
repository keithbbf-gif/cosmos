# Paste, Newton, and the Factory Sweet

Grind a dried fig and you have a paste that will sit in pastry, in a roll, in a box of sweets that can travel farther than a tray of whole fruit. Levantine and Aegean kitchens knew that before any Massachusetts bakery. So did Maghrebi and Italian convent sweets. The factory Newton — fig paste between cake, a trademarked American childhood — is a late industrial child. It is not the origin of fig paste. It is the origin of a certain lunchbox.

This article will not invent a 1891 recipe card or a first baker’s name that cannot be walked to a corporate history the pack has not sourced. It will say: by the time California and İzmir were arguing about crates, someone in a New World factory had already learned that paste hides a grade the eye would refuse in a whole fruit. That is a quality fact, not a sneer. Paste is also how a good fig becomes a bite that does not look like a fig.

## Older sweets

Fig in baklava fillings, in Sicilian and Calabrian Christmas pastes, in Spanish *pan de higo* (a pressed cake more than a pastry), in Jewish Sephardi trays — these are family names, not a single Ottoman decree. Cite a regional kitchen when you have a cookbook history. Do not universalize.
