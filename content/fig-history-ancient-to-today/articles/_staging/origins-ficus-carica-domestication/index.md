# Nine Figs and a Stick

The figs were already old when someone finally looked at them properly. Tamar Noy’s team lifted nine carbonized fruits and a few hundred drupelets from a house at Gilgal I in the Lower Jordan Valley in the 1970s and 1980s. Then Noy died, the boxes sat, and the fruit waited in the kind of museum quiet that swallows field seasons. When the Israel Museum asked Ofer Bar-Yosef and colleagues to reopen the excavation’s finds, the figs went to Mordechai Kislev at Bar-Ilan. He and Anat Hartmann did not see a snack. They saw a sterile, soft, edible type that does not make good seed — a parthenocarpic fig — stored with wild barley, wild oat, and acorns in a village dated about 11,400 to 11,200 years ago.

Their paper ran in *Science* on 2 June 2006, volume 312, pages 1372–1374. Not *Nature*. The distinction matters because the press that followed treated the find like a crown: first domesticated crop, a thousand years before cereals, the Neolithic beginning with a stick in the ground instead of a sickle. Kislev, Hartmann, and Bar-Yosef were more careful than their headlines. They *suggested* that people had planted branches of trees that set fruit without a wasp. A fig cutting roots if you give it half a chance. You do not need a graft or a seed bed. If a mutation made a tree that held sweet fruit instead of dropping hard little syconia, a person who noticed could have a grove without waiting on *Blastophaga*.

That is a beautiful machine. It is also a claim that other archaeobotanists immediately tried to take apart.

## What a founder fruit tree is

Daniel Zohary, Maria Hopf, and Ehud Weiss, in the fourth edition of *Domestication of Plants in the Old World* (2012), still treat the fig as a founder fruit tree of the Mediterranean and Southwest Asia: a woody crop that enters human management early, travels as wood, and keeps a wild belt you can still walk. Olive, grape, and date are the other names in that sentence. Cereals still own the Neolithic in most textbooks, and they should — calories, fields, tax. The fig’s claim is narrower. It is among the first fruit trees people could have *copied* rather than sown.

Wild *Ficus carica* in the Levant is a dioecious-leaning tree tied to a wasp. Female edible figs have long-styled flowers; the caprifig has short-styled flowers that become the wasp’s nursery and, in the pollen-bearing crop, the male flowers near the ostiole. A parthenocarpic female can swell a syconium without pollination. She pays for that ease: no fertile seed, no seedlings of herself. Persistence of that clone in a landscape is either a lucky wild sport that people kept visiting, or a clone people moved as wood.

Kislev’s group chose the second reading. They argued that the Gilgal type already looked like a cultivated fig, and that such a change is slow enough that planting must have been underway for a long time before the house was filled. Three hundred and thirteen drupelets is not a garnish. Someone was putting fig material in the same place they put other staple plants.

## The gathering objection

Simcha Lev-Yadun and others answered in the same journal later that year (*Science* 314: 1683). Parthenocarpy occurs in wild figs. People who gathered wild fruit would have preferred the soft ones. A pile of sterile figs in a house can be a harvest, not a plantation. Tim Denham put the fork in a sentence that should be nailed over every “first farm” story: early fig domestication, or gathering of wild parthenocarpic figs?

Kislev’s team replied. The debate did not end. A 2019 synthesis on arboriculture and the first cities notes that collection from wild gallery woods along the Jordan cannot yet be ruled out for the Pre-Pottery Neolithic A, and that finds *outside* the projected wild zone become persuasive in the fourth millennium BCE — southern Iran, the Nile Delta — when someone is moving the tree.

So the honest sentence is not “figs were domesticated at Gilgal in 9400 BCE.” The honest sentence is: people at Gilgal stored a parthenocarpic fig eleven thousand years ago, and the easiest way to keep such a tree is to plant a stick, and we do not know whether they had already started.

## Genetics will not crown a village

Later nuclear and chloroplast work — Khadari’s circle, Aradhya’s *Genetica* paper, the 2025 “diffuse domestication” argument — does not point at one Jordan Valley hamlet. It points at structured pools: a Moroccan–Algerian group, a northern Mediterranean group, a Levantine group, with *F. carica* in the strict sense sitting apart from *rupestris* and *F. colchica*. That is what you would expect if people kept planting wood in more than one valley for a very long time. It is death to a single-origin press release. It is life to the stick.

The later chapters of this series — Smyrna cuttings that would not crop, Mission figs that would, Estahban rainfed groves, a White Marseilles at Lambeth — are all footnotes to a plant that travels as wood. Even if Gilgal is a gathering site, the biology that made the argument possible is the biology that made the crop.

Bar-Yosef, in the 2006 coverage, described a mixed life: wild cereals and acorns, and fig trees that may already have been planted. That mixed life is more useful than a race between fig and barley. It is also closer to how figs still sit in a Levantine village — at the edge of a field, against a wall, in a wadi, not as a monoculture and not as a weed.

The nine figs are still the right place to start a history, because they force the reader to hold two ideas at once: the tree is old in human hands, and the first handhold is slippery. Everything that follows — Pliny’s list, a Senate trick, a wasp in a Fresno orchard — is what people did after they learned the stick would grow.


## Diffuse, not crowned

Khadari’s pools and Aradhya’s structure sit beside Kislev’s house. A single-village domestication press release dies on that evidence. The stick remains the technology. Gilgal remains an argument. The series starts there because the handhold is slippery, not because the crown is heavy.
