# *Anjīr* Among Native Figs

South Asia’s *Ficus* flora is a civilization of its own. Pipal (*Ficus religiosa*) is a religious tree with a Bodhi story this series will not steal. Banyan (*F. benghalensis*) is timber, shade, and another set of gods. Cluster fig (*F. racemosa*) is a native food and medicine economy with its own markets. *Ficus carica* is not that civilization. It is a guest with a Persian-Arabic name, *anjīr / anjeer*, in the drug and fruit books.

A writer who illustrates *anjeer* with a Bodhi-tree photograph has left the species. Different leaves, different gods, different crops. The leaf plate in this article is there as a warning, not as an identification key for a Deccan orchard.

## A Victorian desk and a word-trail

Dymock, Warden, and Hooper’s *Pharmacographia Indica* put *anjīr* in the Indian lexicon of drugs and fruits and repeat an older “Muslim introduction” line. Print the line as theirs. It is a Victorian pharmacognosy desk, useful for names, dangerous as a century-picker for a communal story.

Laufer’s *Sino-Iranica* (1919), pages 410–12, is a trail of sounds: Babylonian *tittu*, Iranian *anjīr*, forms that wander east into Chinese *a-ži*. Houshang Alam’s *Encyclopaedia Iranica* article “Fig” is the modern crosswalk. Iranica and Laufer complicate Dymock’s introduction sentence without replacing it with a landing-party diary. Do not pick a century to please a narrative. Say what the desks actually say: the common fig is not native in the way *F. religiosa* is native, and commercial *anjeer* districts are late as industries.

## Purandar and the import jar

Purandar, in Maharashtra, and other Deccan stories appear in trade writing as early-twentieth-century commercializations. This pack will not treat a blog date as a gazetteer. Local industry histories place commercial Purandar figs in the early twentieth century when that is all we have. A later agricultural report or a dated gazetteer can replace that hedge. Trade blogs are not that report.

Medicine shops and sweet shops use dried imported fruit as often as local. That is allowed. It is also why a Mumbai box can be an Aegean fruit in a Marathi sentence. Admit the import. FAOSTAT’s India line, when it appears, is often quieter than the grocer’s *anjeer* jar. India as importer in some trade notes is the present tense of a guest crop. Do not crown a Vavilov center for *carica* on the Deccan.

## What a drug book is for

*Pharmacographia Indica* is a late-Victorian survey of drugs and economic plants. It will give you names in several Indian languages and a habit of hanging introductions on conquests. Use the names. Quote the introduction line as theirs. Do not let it become a communal century. Alam’s Iranica article is the modern place to send a reader who wants Persian *anjīr*, Bundahišn 16.26, and Estahban in one crosswalk. Laufer is the place to send a reader who wants the sound *tittu* → *anjīr* → Chinese *a-ži*. None of those desks is a Purandar gazetteer.

Cluster fig (*F. racemosa*) has its own medicine and its own markets. A grocer in a hurry will still say *anjeer*. The leaf plate is there so a writer who has never stood under a pipal does not caption one as *carica*. The silk-road map fades on purpose: dried fruit moved; the native forest did not need the crate.

If a later agricultural yearbook puts hectares on Purandar or another Deccan district, replace the hedge. Until then, local industry history of the early twentieth century is the most this pack will say. A Mumbai sweet-shop jar that is already Aegean is not a failure of Indian antiquity. It is a guest crop telling the truth.

## Three civilizations, one guest suitcase

Pipal is a religious tree. Banyan is timber and shade. Cluster fig is a native food and medicine economy. Those are three civilizations this series will not steal for *carica*. The guest suitcase is smaller: a Persian-Arabic word, a Victorian drug book, an Iranica crosswalk, a Deccan industry hedge, an Aegean jar in a sweet shop. That is already a full article.

Do not pick a century for a communal story. Do not crown a Vavilov center on the Deccan. Do not caption a Bodhi tree as *anjeer*. FAOSTAT’s India line, when it is quiet, is the present tense of a guest. A later gazetteer can replace the Purandar hedge. Until then, local industry history of the early twentieth century is the most this pack will say, and an import crate is allowed to be the honest jar.

## Word-history is not a landing party

Laufer’s pages 410–12 and Alam’s Iranica crosswalk are the honest trail of *anjīr*. They will not give you a dated ship. Dymock’s “Muslim introduction” will give you a Victorian sentence you can quote. A communal story that needs a century will try to turn that sentence into a fleet. This pack will not. The guest arrived with a word and, often, with a crate. The native *Ficus* forest did not wait for either.

A Mumbai jar can be Turkish fruit. A pipal grove cannot. Say both. The China and Japan–Korea essays hold later East Asian guests. This one holds the continent that already had too many figs to need another origin myth.

A Bodhi-tree photograph in an *anjeer* caption has left the plate and the species. Cluster fig (*F. racemosa*) keeps its own medicine markets even when a hurried grocer says *anjeer*. Laufer’s sounds and Alam’s Iranica crosswalk remain the word-trail. They will not give a dated ship. Dymock’s introduction line remains a Victorian sentence you can quote, not a fleet.
