# The Clones That Made a Market

Ask an American nursery for a fig and you will still get one of three sentences: Mission (black, rich, common type), Kadota (honey, canning, the old commercial white), or Brown Turkey (a name that means too many clones). Condit’s 1955 monograph is the last great printed attempt to hold those sentences still. A 2024 *HortScience* paper extracted 717 cultivar attributes from that monograph. The number is a warning. The market used five.

California’s commercial five, in the mid-century circulars, cluster around Mission, Kadota, Calimyrna, Adriatic, and a Brown Turkey that already needed adjectives. Fresh grocery culture later added Black Mission as a retail spelling of Mission, and a swarm of “Italian” and “French” names that may or may not match Condit’s cards.

## Brown Turkey is a pile

English Brown Turkey, Texas Brown Turkey, Eastern Brown Turkey, a dozen backyard trees called Turkey because the fruit is brown — this is not one clone. Say so. If a grower has a numbered selection, use the number. If not, use the pile.

## Factory white, corridor black

Kadota’s canning life is a retort preference: firm enough, pale enough, a honey a factory could put in a cup next to a peach. It is a related conversation to Dottato and this pack will not finish the match in a grocery caption. Mission’s dried life is a dark paste and a friar corridor that could not wait on Algeria — Common type, 1769 story, later retail spelling Black Mission. Calimyrna’s life is a wasp, already painted in Fresno in 1912 as the stitch. Adriatic’s life is a pale drying hope that disappointed next to seeded Sarılop. Brown Turkey’s life is a pile.

Celeste at Cape Charles in 1911 is the reminder that American fig culture was never only San Joaquin. Eastern dooryards grew Common types that never paid the Smyrna bill and never needed to.
