# Cape Gardens, Then a Census, Then Export

Karsten (1951), citing Godée Molsbergen, puts figs among plants introduced to the Cape between 1652 and 1662. For two and a half centuries after that, the tree is a garden and farm-row fact: fresh for the house, dried and preserved for the year, not a board crop. Lötter (2014), as later plant-pathology papers cite him, is the modern South African desk for the swings that follow.

In 1902/03 the Cape Department of Agriculture imported 140 Smyrna and capri trees — Calimyrna, Kassaba, Bardajie, Capri Nos. 1–3. *Blastophaga* came later under control, because someone had read California’s lesson. Piet Cilliers of Wellington, in industry retellings, plants the first commercial orchard from Californian wood in the 1910s. Treat the hero-name as industry memory; treat the 1902/03 import as the dated state act.

## A forest of jam trees, then a thinning

The Deciduous Fruit Board census of 1946 counted over 618,000 fig trees, about 4.6 percent of South African fruit trees — drying and jam, green brebas for preserve, almost no fresh export idea. By 1960 the count had fallen to about 73,000 trees (0.4 percent). The Board nudged the Agricultural Research Council; California imports followed in 1970 and 1981; French fresh types in 2001. The South African Fig Producers’ Association dates to 2003. Industry summaries put commercial area at 222 hectares in 2019, then note removals when the sums failed. Fruitnet’s later note repeats the arc: from hundreds of thousands of trees to less than 30 ha in the early 2000s, then a fresh-export rebuild.

Production sits in the Western Cape: Porterville, Riebeeck, Malmesbury, Paarl, Wellington, Worcester, Napier in the Overberg, Klein Karoo around Oudtshoorn and Prince Albert. Bonnievale is named for green figs in preserve. Prince Albert still dries White Genoa and Adam in local accounts. Rust likes the humid coast; the Karoo is drier and less kind to the fungus.

Do not print “one of the world’s biggest exporters” from a tourism page as if it were FAOSTAT. South Africa is a southern-hemisphere counterseason story, not a Türkiye rival. Porterville drip rows of Ronde de Bordeaux for Europe are a 2000s niche. They are real. They are small.


## Rust, regions, and the export niche

Western Cape rust likes humidity. Pathology papers from 2021 note defoliation near Napier after wet spells. The Klein Karoo and Prince Albert sell dryness as a defense. That is why a drying White Genoa can still make sense in a town that also sells tourism.

Ronde de Bordeaux and Parisian in Porterville rows (Farmer’s Weekly’s Knoetzen profile) are a 2000s European-fresh bet: prune low, drip, fly to a winter supermarket. 95 percent export in that one farm story is a farm story, not a national share. The 222 ha (2019) and later removals are the scale check. Do not let a single successful exporter become “the industry.”


## Garden centuries, jam forest, export niche

1652–62 introduction (Karsten / Godée Molsbergen). 1902/03 Cape import of 140 Smyrna and capri trees; *Blastophaga* later. 1946 DFB: 618,000 trees, 4.6 percent, jam and dry. 1960: ~73,000. California 1970/81, French 2001, SAFPA 2003, 222 ha (2019) then removals. Porterville–Paarl–Karoo geography; rust on the humid coast. Ronde de Bordeaux rows for Europe are a farm-scale 2000s bet. Not a FAOSTAT rival. A southern counterseason.
