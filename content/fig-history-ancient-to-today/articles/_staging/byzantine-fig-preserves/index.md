# The *Geoponica* Kitchen

The *Geoponica* is a tenth-century Constantinopolitan compilation that claims an older farm literature, much of it associated with Cassianus Bassus and, behind him, a stack of Greek agronomists. It is not a single author’s orchard diary. It is a digest. Digests survive because someone still needed the advice.

Figs in that advice are not a wonder. They are a crop you plant, a fruit you dry, a sweet you keep. Preservation recipes — sun, must, leaves, the winter jar — are the Byzantine gift to this series. Fresh figs are a week. A preserve is a policy.

## What a compilation is

The *Geoponica* quotes and compresses. A sentence about hanging caprifigs or choosing a slope may be Roman, or Hellenistic, or a tenth-century editor’s idea of what an estate should do. The Filāḥa Texts Project, when it later counts Ibn al-ʿAwwām’s sources, will find a third of that Seville book sitting in this Greek-Byzantine pool. The kitchen is a relay.

The capital is a mouth. The islands and coasts are the hands.

## A capital that eats other people’s August

Constantinople’s dried figs are a supply problem. Aegean islands, western Mediterranean *sporte*, the occasional Ligurian basket in a Black Sea warehouse — Jacoby’s food-trade work is the map. The *Geoponica* is the house book that pretends the estate can do it all. Both are true in part. A palace pantry is a port. A monastic pantry is a drying house and a purchase.

## The Geoponika as a library, not a farm diary

The *Geoponika* is a tenth-century compilation, assembled at Constantinople
under Constantine VII Porphyrogenitus, of older agricultural excerpts. It is
not a diary of a Macedonian farm in 950. When book 10 treats *συκῆ* it is
recycling Theophrastus, Anatolius, Cassianus Bassus, and the late-antique
*Geoponica* tradition those names already sit on. A reader who wants a
tenth-century Byzantine orchard should not treat every recipe as current
practice. Treat it as a library that still thought the fig worth repeating.

That library is specific. Book 10, in the Beckh numbering most English users
meet through Owen’s 1805 translation (not a critical edition), discusses
planting figs from cuttings, the preference for certain soils, and the old
Mediterranean conviction that a fig will take from a cutting more readily
than from seed. Another chapter discusses caprification — not under that
Latin name, but as the hanging of wild figs so the insects in them will
visit the cultivated trees. The compiler does not invent that advice. He
copies it because the eastern Mediterranean farm still needed it.

## What a Byzantine estate actually grew

The *Geoponika* sits next to the documentary estate. The *praktika* of Athos
and the cadasters of the themes do not list “fig” as often as they list vine
and olive, but they do list it. A *sykea* or a *sykomorea* in a will is a
tree with a rent, not a metaphor. The distinction matters: *sykea* is usually
*Ficus carica*; *sykomorea* is *Ficus sycomorus*, which the eastern
Mediterranean still grew as shade and timber. A translator who collapses both
to “fig” erases a species the Bible and the papyri kept apart.

Monastic typika sometimes specify fruit for the refectory. Figs appear there
as dried stores more often than as fresh luxury. A dried fig keeps through a
fast; a fresh fig does not. The *Geoponika*’s drying notes — sun, mats, a
second drying after the first sweat — are the same sequence this pack’s
sundrying essay walks on a California or Aydın yard. The physics did not
wait for a tunnel dryer.

Constantinople’s pantry is also a port. David Jacoby’s food-trade work puts
Aegean dried figs, western Mediterranean *sporte*, even a Ligurian basket
expected in a Black Sea warehouse, into the capital’s supply problem. The
house book pretends the estate can do it all. A palace pantry is a quay. A
monastic pantry is a drying house and a purchase. Both sentences can sit in
one week.

## Why the manual still matters

A magazine that only quoted Pliny would miss the medieval hinge. The
*Geoponika* is how Greek farm prose survived into Arabic *filāḥa* and, later,
into the Latin West’s curiosity about “Greek husbandry.” Ibn al-ʿAwwām’s
one-third Byzantine share is not a compliment. It is a citation trail. When
an Andalusi chapter on fig cuttings sounds like Theophrastus, the path often
runs through this book.

The honest limit: we do not have a tenth-century Byzantine fig variety list
with the density of Condit 1947. We have a compiled library and a scatter of
estate nouns. That is enough to keep the fig on the medieval eastern
Mediterranean farm. It is not enough to name the clone on a specific Athos
terrace without a living tree and a document that match.

## A capital that eats other people’s August

Constantine VII’s compilation project is a library of estates, not a
Macedonian farm diary. Owen’s 1805 English is how most magazine readers
will meet book 10’s *συκῆ*; it is not a critical edition. Cuttings, soils,
the hanging of wild figs so insects will visit — those chapters survive
because the eastern farm still needed them, not because a tenth-century
clerk discovered a tree. Ibn al-ʿAwwām’s later one-third Byzantine share
is this book traveling west as citation, not as compliment.

The documentary estate is thinner and more honest than the digest. A
*sykea* in an Athos *praktikon* is a rent. A *sykomorea* is often the other
species. Typika that mention figs usually mean dried stores that survive a
fast. Jacoby’s food-trade work puts Aegean trays and even a Ligurian
*sporta* expected toward the Black Sea into the capital’s pantry. The house
book pretends the estate can do it all. A palace pantry is a quay. We do
not have a Byzantine Condit. We have a library and a scatter of nouns.
Name a clone on a specific terrace only when a living tree and a document
match.
