# A Wall, a Palace, a Garden Book

Lambeth Palace associates a White Marseilles fig with Cardinal Reginald Pole, planted — in the Palace tradition — in 1556. A cutting went to Pope Francis on 24 June 2014. John Claudius Loudon, in 1836, said 1548 and found the old trees gone, young cuttings remaining. Popular garden writing sometimes prints 1525. Morton’s compilation uses a 1525–1548 English range. Do not collapse 1525 / 1548 / 1556 into one date. Print the disagreement. A collapse into “the 1525 Lambeth fig” is how a palace becomes a brand.

Matthew Paris’s 1257 English fig notice, as garden historians cite it, is a medieval northern sighting. It is not the Palace tree. The monastic essay’s Carolingian wish — *ficus* on the *Capitulare de villis* estate list, a fig label on the Plan of St. Gall, and Walahfrid’s *Hortulus* which does **not** name fig — is the long preface. Do not collapse a royal-estate ordinance, an ideal plan, and a palace postcard into one “English fig.”

## Two walls, two climates

Thomas Jefferson’s *Garden Book* and Monticello’s published notes put Marseilles figs among the fruits he wanted, with French cuttings associated with 1789. Use those notes, not a souvenir spoon. A Virginia wall is a different climate bet than a London palace wall. Both are bets: heat the brick, shelter the stick, hope the summer is Mediterranean for a month. Do not expect Aydın.

Loudon walked Lambeth in 1836 and found the old trunks gone. That sentence should sit next to every Palace postcard. White Marseilles is a name that traveled. The trunks did not all survive the visit. The 2014 cutting is living politics of a name. It does not collapse the dates.

## A northern seeing

Johannes Simon Holtzbecher’s Gottorfer Codex *Ficus carica* — already in the still-life essay’s folder — shows what a princely northern book wanted the species to look like when fruit was a rarity. It is a seeing, not a harvest total. Reuse it here as the cabinet cousin of Lambeth’s boast: a leaf painted as diagnosis, a fruit as a type, a collector who might never own an orchard.

## What a wall is stealing

A south-facing brick wall in London is a theft of a Mediterranean month. A brick wall at Monticello is the same theft in a Virginia winter. Heat stored in masonry, a stick trained flat, a hope that August will pretend to be Aydın for three weeks. The climate essay will talk about ripening weeks at the belt’s edge. This page’s job is to keep the theft honest: northern wall culture is rarity and boast, not a drying industry.

The *Capitulare de villis*, chapter LXX, lists *ficus* among royal-estate plantings. That is a Carolingian ordinance, not a monastic rule and not Lambeth. The Plan of St. Gall labels fig in an orchard-cemetery among apple, pear, plum, quince, peach, almond, mulberry, walnut — an ideal plan, not a survey, already in the monastic folder as a PD raster. Walahfrid’s *Hortulus* names twenty-four garden plants and does not name fig. Do not invent the stanza. Do not hang those three Carolingian facts on Cardinal Pole.

Jefferson’s French cuttings associated with 1789 are a Garden Book fact Monticello has published. Use the notes. A souvenir spoon with a fig on it is not a source. Loudon’s 1836 missing trunks are the honesty a palace postcard skips. Print the date spread. A brand that needs 1525 has left Loudon, the Palace tradition, and Matthew Paris in a blender.

## A diplomatic fruit is not a chronology

Pope Francis’s 2014 cutting is living politics: a palace, a name, a gift. It does not collapse 1525 / 1548 / 1556. Loudon’s missing trunks remain the honesty. Matthew Paris in 1257, as garden historians cite him, remains a medieval sighting that is not the Palace tree. Jefferson’s 1789 association remains a Virginia desk with published notes. Holtzbecher remains a northern seeing in another court.

Morton’s 1525–1548 English range is a compilation. It is not a third primary next to Loudon and the Palace tradition. Popular pages that print only 1525 have chosen the cleanest souvenir. This series promised not to. Extra warm weeks, if they come, will make the rare fig ordinary. Ordinary is the tree’s old ambition. Until then a wall is an orchard of one face, and a date spread is the only honest caption.

## Two politics of a wall

Lambeth is an archbishop’s garden that later gave a pope a cutting. Monticello is a republican *Garden Book* that wanted French fruit after 1789. Both walls steal a Mediterranean month. They do not share a politics. A palace tradition dated 1556 and a Garden Book association dated 1789 are not one “northern fig.” They are two bets in two winters, both refusing Aydın, both hoping August will pretend.
