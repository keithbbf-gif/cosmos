# *Tittu* on the Tablet

The fig enters Mesopotamian writing the way other useful trees do: as a word among words, then as a ration, a garden, a simile. J. N. Postgate’s 1987 notes in the *Bulletin on Sumerian Agriculture* remain the sober English door. Marvin Powell’s discussions of the archaic “tree list” put date, vine, fig, and apple in a lexical set already in the late fourth / early third millennium (Uruk III discussions). That is not a farmer’s diary. It is a school text’s idea of what a civilized tree is. The idea matters. Scribes do not waste wedges on weeds they never see.

Akkadian *tittu* (and the Sumerian line behind it) is the common fig in this literature. Laufer, in *Sino-Iranica* (1919), still liked to hang later Iranian and Chinese names on a Babylonian peg. Treat that as a history of words, not as a claim that every *anjīr* east of the Zagros is a Babylonian export. Houshang Alam’s *Encyclopaedia Iranica* article “Fig” is the better modern crosswalk: Mesopotamian notices sit beside Elamite, Persian, and later Islamic ones without forcing a single orchard.

## Gardens of the alluvium

Southern Mesopotamia is a date country first. The fig is the other irrigated fruit that can live in a courtyard and on a canal bank. Third-millennium texts that mention fig trees in gardens are not surprising. They are also not a census. When a royal inscription boasts of planting, it is doing royal work. When a lexical list stacks fruit names, it is doing school work. Between them, somewhere, a person ate a fig in the shade.

Archaeobotany in Iraq and Syria is patchier than the Levant for early *F. carica*. That is a sampling fact, not a proof of absence. Carbonized figs do not always survive the way date stones do. A history that waits for a Gilgal-style house in Uruk will wait too long. The textual fig is already a cultivated idea by the time the lists are stable.

## Offerings, oil, and the other fig

Keep *Ficus sycomorus* out of the alluvial story unless a find or a wood identification puts it there. The sycomore is an Egyptian and East African tree with a different wasp. Mesopotamian *tittu* is *carica* in the standard reading. Do not decorate a kudurru with a Theban garden.

Figs as offerings and as medical simples show up later in the cuneiform medical and lexical streams. This article will not forge a recipe. Dioscorides and the Arabic physicians are later doors (see the medicine pieces). What can be said without invention: the fig is edible fresh and dried; drying is how a river country stores a perishable fruit through a month that is not harvest. That logic is the same logic as Aydın, and it does not require a Greek name.

## What we will not do

We will not invent a Sumerian fig goddess. We will not turn Inanna’s garden imagery into a cultivar list. We will not quote a “translation” that cannot be walked back to Postgate, Powell, or a standard edition. We will not say Mesopotamia domesticated the fig. The Jordan argument is older as archaeology; the Mesopotamian argument is older as writing. Those are different kinds of first.

The tablet’s gift to this series is tone. A fig that has a word is a fig someone expected to meet again — in a garden, in a ration, in a line a student had to copy. That is already culture. It is not yet Pliny’s twenty-nine names. It does not need to be.


## Lexical orchard

Uruk tree-lists and *tittu* rations are a school and a storehouse seeing a tree they expected to meet again. Date remains the alluvial monarch. Fig remains the courtyard second. No goddess. No yield report dressed as a hymn. Postgate is the door; stay near it.


## Wedges on a useful tree

Postgate 1987; Powell’s tree-list (date, vine, fig, apple); Akkadian *tittu*. School texts and rations, not yield hymns. Date is the alluvial monarch; fig is the courtyard second. Keep *sycomorus* out unless a wood ID puts it in. Laufer’s word-pegs are not a Babylonian export of every later *anjīr*. No invented goddess. Writing is a different first than Gilgal’s house.
