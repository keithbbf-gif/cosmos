# The Winter Fig and the French South

Marseille — Massalia, a Phocaean colony in the old story — sits at a fig door. The type English gardens still call White Marseilles is a pale, splitting, honey-fleshed fig that traveled to Lambeth in the sixteenth century and to Monticello in the eighteenth (article 36). Whether the Phocaeans “brought the fig to Gaul” is the kind of sentence garden books like and inscriptions do not confirm. What is confirmable: the French Mediterranean is fig country, and it is colder and windier than Aydın, so the culture learned walls.

Pliny’s Moesian winter fig, banked in manure and finished in a stolen spring, is the extreme of a southern French instinct. A tree against a south wall, a *restanque*, a courtyard that holds last night’s heat: that is how a fig lives at the north edge of the belt. Violette de Bordeaux, Marseillaise, the various “celeste” and “dauphine” names Condit tried to unknot — these are garden and market types, not a PDO empire. France never made the dried crate that Turkey made. It made a table fruit and a jam fruit and a tree for the mas.

## Languedoc and the other south

West of the Rhône the same weather, a different accent. Figs in the *garrigue* edge, in village squares, in the same labor as vines. The wine is the money. The fig is the neighbor. That neighborly status is why French types dominate northern amateur lists: they were selected to ripen in a shorter, cooler summer.

Do not claim a medieval monastic “first.” Monasteries grew figs because they grew everything that would eat the sun. The record is ordinary, which is a kind of evidence.
