# A Tree That Travels as a Stick

Theophrastus, who had the patience to watch roots, said figs were among the easiest trees to propagate. He was not advertising a nursery. He was sorting the living world into the ways it continues: seed, bulb, spontaneous appearance, a piece of the parent stuck in the ground. The fig took the last door so readily that later writers treated the fact as a proverb. You do not need a graft to move *Ficus carica*. You need a winter stick, a hole, and a climate that does not laugh at the idea of a fig.

That is the quiet engine under every regional story in this series. Empires move laws and coins. Figs move because someone put wood on a boat, a donkey, a mission cart, a USDA crate. The clone that arrives is already a decision made years earlier, in another valley, by a person who liked a fruit enough to cut the tree that made it.

## What vegetative travel does to history

Seeded crops forget their parents in a generation. A fig cutting is a rumor that stays true. The Mission trees of Alta California, the Sarılop hills behind Aydın, a White Marseilles associated with Lambeth Palace — these are not “the same species,” which is cheap. They are particular clones, or clone-groups, dragged through time. Condit’s 1955 monograph is, among other things, a catalog of how often those clones changed their clothes. One fruit, six names, three ports.

Vegetative travel also explains the speed. Zohary and Spiegel-Roy (1975) put fig with olive, grape, and date at the beginning of Old World horticulture: trees you copy, not grains you sow. The archaeobotanical fog around Gilgal (article 01) does not cancel the horticultural point. Once people understood the stick, they could move a preferred type faster than a breeding program. They could also trap themselves. A Smyrna-type fig moved to California in the 1880s was still the same clone that needed a wasp. The stick had traveled. The insect had not. Ten thousand flourishing trees and no dessert is what a missing partner looks like.

## How a cutting is not a seed

A seed of *F. carica* is a lottery ticket written by a wasp. The seedling will not be the mother. It may be a caprifig. It may be inedible. Peasant and villa culture both learned to ignore that lottery when they had a tree they liked. Theophrastus already pairs the fig’s ease with the date palm’s fussiness — the palm wanting irrigation, dung, salt, several seeds together. The fig, in his sorting, is the opposite temperament.

Condit, writing from Riverside and the San Joaquin with a generation of California failure and success behind him, still begins from the same wood. Commercial orchards are planted from cuttings. Variety is identity. The “Adriatic” that dried and went east in 1889 was a clone, not a region. When that clone is caprified it changes color and acid in the pulp — Condit says so — which means even a “common” fig is not a sealed object. The stick holds the genotype. The wasp, if she arrives, edits the fruit.

## Wildness that will not sit still

Spontaneous figs grow in Mediterranean masonry, wadi walls, and the skirts of orchards. Birds eat a seeded fruit and paint a cliff. Those seedlings are not the mother’s clone. Over centuries they refill the landscape with genetic noise. Khadari’s recent Mediterranean sampling (article 42) finds cultivated and spontaneous trees looking like kin *within* regions. That is what you expect if people keep planting local wood and local seedlings keep coming home. It is not what you expect from a single Levantine Eden sending one clone west.

So the stick is both conservative and leaky. Conservative: a named orchard type can last a thousand years if people keep cutting it. Leaky: every seeded fruit is an escape. Caprification, which makes Smyrna figs worth drying, is also the leak’s tap.

## A method that humiliates invention stories

Nation-stories love a first planter. The stick makes those stories hard to police. A Phoenician hull, an Andalusi peasant, a Franciscan brother, a Chinese caravan account that may or may not be Tang — any of them can move the same biology. The historian’s job is not to pick a hero. It is to notice when the wood moved without its wasp, without its drying weather, without its name.

The next articles stay in places: Egypt’s two figs, the Levantine household, a cuneiform orchard. Under each of them is this article’s claim. The fig is a portable room of flowers. People learned to carry the room.
