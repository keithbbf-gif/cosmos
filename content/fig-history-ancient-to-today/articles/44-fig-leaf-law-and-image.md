# The Leaf That Did the Censor’s Job

Genesis 3:7 is a sewing of fig leaves. The species is the large-leaved tree of the storyteller’s world, which in the Levant is a fair match for *Ficus carica*. That is the entire biblical warrant. The rest — plaster leaves on classical statues in papal and Victorian collections, a schoolroom giggle, a thousand cheap essays titled with the leaf — is reception. It is not an ancient Hebrew art practice. It is not a pomological note.

A history of the fig that leans on this leaf for knowingness has already left the orchard.

## What the leaf did in Europe

It became decency hardware. A statue that had been a body became a problem; the leaf was a removable (and sometimes not so removable) solution. Dates and decrees vary by museum and century; this pack will not invent a single papal year we have not opened in a catalog. The structural fact is enough: Europe taught itself to *see* the fig first as a cover. Growers who have been asked whether their fruit is “the biblical fig” are living in that hardware.

Ehret’s 1771 plate (WC-001) is the corrective picture: a leaf as botany, palmate, scabrous, useful. Tissot’s Nathaniel (WC-011) is the other biblical job: shade, not a gag (article 05). Keep both. Do not let the plaster win.

## What this series will not do

The ostiole and the syconium have attracted their own smirking literature. Article 38 is the room, written straight. Household shade is articles 04–05. Fertility frescoes, if they appear, stay in the art/labor file with a caption, not a thumbnail (PHOTO_NOTES). The leaf gets one essay because reception can outweigh botany for centuries, and a content lane that sells trees will be asked the Genesis question until someone answers it and sends the reader back to the yard.
