# Theophrastus Watches the Fruit Swell

*Historia Plantarum* is not a chapbook of garden tips. It is an attempt to say how plants differ, and how they go on. In the pages on propagation Theophrastus puts the fig among the trees that grow from a piece of themselves (article 02). In the pages on wild and cultivated life he records something a later Italian professor would try to abolish: gall insects come out of wild figs and make the cultivated figs swell, which helps keep the fruit from shedding.

That is caprification, seen without a microscope and without a USDA bulletin. The wild fig is the caprifig. The insect is the fig wasp. The swell is seed set and the hormonal answer of the syconium. Galil and Ne’eman, in the 1970s, wrote the modern papers on pollen transfer and seed set in *Ficus carica*. They are not correcting Theophrastus so much as giving him genera and species.

## What he could see

He could see that figs are easy to plant and greedy of root. He could see regional trees — the book is full of “in this place, not in that.” He could see a practice already old in the Aegean: hanging or bringing wild fruit to the tame tree. He did not need a theory of mutualism. He needed not to lie about what growers did.

Aristotle’s circle was not sentimental about insects. The *Historia Animalium* tradition knows fig wasps well enough that later classical writers treat the relationship as a known oddity. This article will not forge an Aristotle paragraph. It will say the practice is in the Greek agronomic air Theophrastus breathes.

## What he could not see

He could not see *Blastophaga psenes* walking pollen on her back through the ostiole. He could not split common figs from Smyrna types with Condit’s labels. Some Greek cultivated figs would have been parthenocarpic — they swell without the insect — and some would have been the types that drop. A keen observer still sees “the insect helps.” A later dogmatist, Gasparrini in the nineteenth century, would say the custom is useless and spoils the flavor. California believed him long enough to waste a decade of Smyrna wood (article 54). Theophrastus, who did not have a theory to defend, is the better witness.

## A method of looking

The gift of *Historia Plantarum* to this series is tone. Name the part, name the place, say when you are guessing. The fig articles that go wrong — origin myths, medical miracles, “the Greeks worshipped the fig” — fail that tone. Theophrastus on the fig is almost dry enough to be modern, except he is better company.
