# Pliny’s Twenty-Nine Names

Pliny the Elder, who died in the eruption that sealed the still lifes of Herculaneum, gave the fig a chapter that still reads like a merchant’s daybook. *Naturalis Historia* 15.19: twenty-nine varieties. He has already, he says, mentioned the miraculous figs of Egypt and Cyprus among the exotics. Then he gets down to the Italian list, which is not Italian only.

From Mount Ida a red fig the size of an olive, called Alexandrian there — and he is already in trouble, because that description (no milky juice, linden-like leaves) may not be *Ficus carica* at all. Onesicritus, via Pliny, claims Hyrcanian trees yield two hundred and seventy *modii* from a single trunk, which is traveler’s arithmetic. Then the real catalog: introductions from Chalcis and Chios; purple Lydian figs; mamillana; callistruthiae, “the coldest by nature of all the figs.” The African fig, preferred by many, is a recent immigrant to Africa that nevertheless wears Africa’s name — Pliny enjoys the quarrel. Alexandrian black figs with a pale cleft, nicknamed delicate. Rhodian and Tiburtine, early and black. Figs named for people: Livian, Pompeian. The Pompeian, he says, is among the best for drying in the sun and keeping year to year, with the marisca and a kind whose leaf is spotted like a reed. Herculanean, albicerata, a white aratia, very wide, tiny stalk. At Tarentum the *ona*, extremely sweet, grows only there.

Then Cato’s sentence, quoted as if from a grandfather who knew dirt (article 09). Then the moral Pliny cannot resist: since Cato’s day so many names have arisen that this fact alone shows how life has changed.

## A historian of appetite

That last turn is why the chapter is more than a list. Pliny thinks variety inflation is a moral weather report. He is not wrong that empire multiplies desire. He is wrong if we read him as saying the fig was simple before Rome. Cato already had six types and two soils. What has changed is the audience: a city that can ask for Lydian purple and a Tarentine sweet in the same week.

He knows winter figs in some provinces — Moesia — “a product of art and not of nature.” Bank the tree in manure, let winter catch the fruit unripe, uncover it in mild weather, let it finish in a year that is not its own. That is greenhouse thinking without glass. It is also a warning to anyone who calls a late fig a miracle.

## Names that will not map

Livian and Pompeian are lost as sure matches. Condit would spend a career on that grief. Chalcis and Chios tell us the Aegean was already an exporter of *types*, not only of dried freight. Lydian purple tells us Anatolia was in the Italian mind as color. African tells us the name of a fruit and the name of a province can lie to each other — a lesson for Calimyrna, which is a California word for a Turkish clone.

Pliny’s Hyrcania, via Onesicritus, is the same Caspian-facing country Strabo would stock with impossible yields (article 17). Treat it as a topos: the east is fertile beyond counting. Do not put 270 *modii* on a fact card.
