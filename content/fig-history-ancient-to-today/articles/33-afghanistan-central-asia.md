# Anjīr in the Dry East: Afghanistan and Central Asia

The *Gazetteer of Afghanistan*, as Alam cites it, is cluttered with Anjīr, Anjīrak, Anjīrān, Anjīro — villages, passes, valleys. A country that names a pass for a tree is a country that expected to eat it on the other side. Browicz puts *F. johannis* subsp. *afghanistanica* in the same dry east. Rashīd al-Dīn’s good figs in districts of Transoxiana and Khwarazm (article 17) are the medieval sentence. FAOSTAT’s recent tables put Afghanistan in the world’s front ranks of fig tonnage — 2023 compilations near 60,000 tonnes — which is not a hobby.

This is apricot-and-mulberry weather that also understands fig. The fruit is dried because the roads are long and the winter is real. Fresh figs exist in the valleys; the reputation that travels is the dry one. Article 18’s sack is this country’s ordinary object.

## Do not invent a Silk Road brand

There is no “Afghan Smyrna” in the legal sense. There is a set of landraces this pack has not DNA-fingerprinted. *F. johannis* and *F. carica* both live in the conversation; a market fruit may be one, the other, or a farmer’s name that ignores the botanist. Say so. Khadari’s three Mediterranean pools (article 42) do not license a fourth Afghan sermon without samples.

Uzbekistan appears in some recent FAOSTAT lower ranks. That is a note for the atlas (article 63), not a second essay. The toponyms and the tonnes are enough to keep Central Asia from being a blank east of Estahban.

Iranica’s Afghan list is nineteen places. That is a map, not a poem.
