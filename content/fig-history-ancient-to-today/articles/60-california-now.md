# What Is Left of the Valley

FAOSTAT’s recent U.S. line is a few tens of thousands of tonnes — 2023 compilations near 27,000 — which is not Aydın and not 1925. The San Joaquin still dries. It also picks for a fresh market Eisen did not have to imagine at this scale: clamshells, cold chain, a fruit that bruises if you look at it. Black Mission and the Brown Turkey name-pile and a few others do that job better than Calimyrna, which still wants a wasp and a dry week and a consumer who knows what a seeded fig is for.

Land that grew figs grows houses and almonds. Water that grew figs has other claimants. Ferrara’s world graph — area halved since 1960, yields up — has a California verse.

## Two Californias, both smaller

The coast is still Mission country in the cultural sense: a yard tree, a farmers’ market, a restaurant that wants a fig in September. The interior is still the crate, thinned. The collector backyard (article 58) now holds more named clones than some old commercial lists. That is a cultural success and a tonnage irrelevance. It is also, for FigRoots’ California reader, the actual orchard they have.

Do not print a current acreage we have not pulled from the latest NASS or county crop report. Print the direction: smaller industry, louder hobby, a fresh window that did not exist when the stencil was king (article 56).

## What 1900 looks like from here

Roeding’s 62 acres and Schwarz’s seeded crop are a preface. They are not a promise that the valley would stay a fig republic. The wasp is still here. The land market is not the 1900 land market. The January fig (article 61) can come from Peru. The dried fig can come from Aydın cheaper than a San Joaquin crew can turn a tray.

The reader who plants a Violette de Bordeaux against a west wall is not failing the valley. They are living in the other California, the one that started in 1769 and never needed a coop.
