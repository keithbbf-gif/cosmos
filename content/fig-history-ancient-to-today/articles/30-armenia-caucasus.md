# Colchis, Hyrcania, and the Caucasian Fig

Aradhya and colleagues, opening their 2010 *Genetica* paper, still have to step around a mess. Large-fruited figs in the Colchic woods of northern Turkey and the Hyrcanian woods of Iran and the Caspian — some botanists call them an ecotype of *F. carica*, others *F. colchica* and *F. hyrcanica* (Zhukovsky 1962). Vavilov (1951) wanted Transcaucasia as a center where wild, transitional, and orchard trees could be seen at once. Khadari’s newer sampling treats *F. colchica* as something to keep distinct from *F. carica* sensu stricto, and notes *F. carica* subsp. *rupestris* in drier country where true wild *carica* is missing.

This article will not award a species. It will say the wet Black Sea and Caspian forests grow a fig that looks like a cousin and has been eaten as one.

## Armenia and the highland habit

Armenian and Georgian village figs are a terrace and courtyard crop, often common types that can finish in a short summer, dried when the autumn allows. We do not have a PDO text. We have a cuisine that uses dried fruit the way all the highland Near East does: in stews, on the table, as traveler’s sugar. Do not invent a pagan “fig of Ararat” story. The tree is there. The myth can stay in the tourist folder.

## Why Vavilov still helps

He reminds the Levant-first story that the north and east have their own wildness. He does not get to overrule Gilgal or Khadari’s three pools. He gets a chapter because a history of the fig that stops at the Mediterranean shoreline has not walked the wet woods.
