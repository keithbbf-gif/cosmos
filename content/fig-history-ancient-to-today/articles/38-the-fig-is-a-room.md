# The Fig Is a Room

You do not eat a fruit in the grocer’s sense. You eat an inverted bouquet. The syconium is a fleshy peduncle, hollow, shut almost, with an ostiole — the eye — at the far end. On the inner wall sit the true fruits, the drupelets, each a flower’s ovary. Chinese *wúhuāguǒ* (article 20) is the vernacular that told the truth. English “fig” is a sweetness that hides the architecture.

Condit and Flaishman, and every pomology handbook after, have to start here because nothing else — wasp, seed, “inside-out flower” talk — makes sense if the reader still thinks of a peach.

## Long styles, short styles

Edible female figs carry long-styled flowers. The wasp cannot easily lay in them; she can pollinate them if she enters. Caprifigs carry short-styled flowers that are a nursery, and, in the profichi crop, stamens by the door. Galil and Ne’eman mapped the transfer. The 1911 *Britannica* diagram (WC-009) is a public-domain way to show the room without pretending we photographed a first-century dissection.

Parthenocarpy — the common fig’s trick — is the room swelling without a wedding. Smyrna types refuse. San Pedro types refuse on the main crop and agree on the breba. That is article 39. This one stays in the room.

## Why history needs the room

Because Pliny’s “fruit” and a tomb painter’s oval and a supermarket tray are all pictures of a stem. Because caprification is not magic; it is an insect finding a door. Because “seedless” in a market is sometimes empty achenes (Cosenza’s boast) and sometimes a common type that never saw pollen.
