# Africa’s Name on the Fruit

Pliny’s quarrel about the “African” fig — preferred by many, only lately introduced into Africa — is the right irritant to start with. The name and the country do not sit still. Carthage’s hinterland grew figs Cato could wave (article 11). The Maghreb, a long time later, would grow Nabout of Taounate and Bouhouli of Djebba under legal names (article 29). Between those poles the tree did not take a holiday. It became a village crop from the Atlantic Rif to the Tunisian tell, in a climate that understands drought and a kitchen that understands dried sugar.

## Roman and late antique

Tripolitania and Africa Proconsularis were olive countries first in the surviving stone. Figs in the same villas are expected and poorly counted. This article will not invent a mosaic inscription. It will say the diet of a Roman African town — bread, oil, wine, fruit — has room for the syconium, and the later Arabic agronomists of the west (article 15–16) did not introduce an unknown plant. They inherited one and wrote it better.

*Ficus sycomorus* has an African story of its own (article 03). *F. carica* in the Maghreb is the Mediterranean crop. Do not merge them because both are “African figs” in English.

## A gene pool with its own weather

Khadari, Grout, Santoni, Kjellberg and colleagues, sampling hundreds of cultivated and spontaneous figs, found a Moroccan–Algerian cluster distinct enough to stand as one of three major pools, alongside a northern Mediterranean pool and a Levantine one. Cultivated and spontaneous trees look like each other *inside* the Maghreb. That is local planting, local escape, local selection — not a single Roman clone still wearing a toga.

The paper’s implication for this series is rude to origin sermons. If Morocco and Algeria hold a real wild-or-spontaneous heritage, then “the fig came from the Levant and the rest is copies” is too small. The Levant remains a strong, differentiated group. It is not the only workshop.

## Continuity into Islam

When Arabic writers of the medieval west describe fig culture, they are describing a landscape that already had the tree in its stone. Ibn al-ʿAwwām is Seville, not Fez, but the Maghreb is in the same agronomic weather: irrigation where you can, rainfed where you must, caprification where the type demands it. Later Ottoman and French colonial markets would pull Maghrebi figs toward ports. The village tree did not wait for them.
