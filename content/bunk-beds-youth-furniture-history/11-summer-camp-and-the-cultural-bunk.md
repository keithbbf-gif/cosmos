# Summer camp and the cultural bunk

Ask a room of Americans where they first met a bunk bed. A lot of them will say camp, even if they never went.

The cabin is the bunk's folk stage. Screen door. Bug spray. A counselor who says "lights out" like it is a spell. Someone always claims the top. Someone always falls off once and gets a story they will tell at forty. The bed is a character.

Culture is not a test lab. Culture is why people **forgive** the object.

## What camp actually is

Camp is an institution that pretends it is a forest. The beds are procurement. Metal or wood, bought in dozens, refinished when the board votes yes. Mattresses of whatever thickness the last catalog offered. Rails that may have been unscrewed because a sixteen-year-old wanted to sit on the edge and talk.

Is a camp an "institution" under 16 CFR 1213.1? The text exempts beds **manufactured only for institutional use**, and it excludes facilities for children under six from the word *institution*. A typical U.S. summer camp is full of children over six. That does not automatically place every rusty frame in the exemption. Manufacture-purpose and facility-type are not the same sentence. Draft 27 is the lawyerly one. The cultural point is: camps have been treating bunks as **durable folklore** for decades, and folklore does not keep a certificate in a file cabinet.

## The story camp taught the suburb

Camp said: the top bunk is a prize. Camp said: whispering after lights-out is the point. Camp said: a bruise is a souvenir. Suburbia imported the prize and the whisper and was slower to import the bruise-as-normal. Then television finished the job.

When CPSC later said no horseplay, one person on the upper bunk, use the ladder, under 6 stays down — it was writing against **camp script**. The script is older and funnier and better at spreading. That is why you still see listings that brag "the kids can jump, it's solid wood." Solid wood is not a clause.

## Media bunks

I will name the ones people actually remember, because memory is how this object stays sold.

- *The Brady Bunch*: brothers, blue, suburban-clean. Jail sentence, Greg said. Camera said cozy.
- *Diff'rent Strokes*: L-shape, wood, apartment. Reid coveted it. A generation learned the L as aspiration.
- Three Stooges and Chaplin: the bunk as slapstick collapse. Funny because we know height is a punchline until it is not.
- *Big* (1988): the top bunk as a wish about growing up. The film is about a child in an adult body. The bed is doing thematic work it did not consent to.

I am not writing media studies. I am writing the **demand function**. People buy the picture they already loved. The picture rarely includes a wedge block.

## Horror and the other camp

There is a darker camp-bunk picture: overcrowding, neglect, the institution that does not get a color catalog. I will not exploit it for atmosphere. I will say that stacked sleep in unsupervised children's settings is exactly the situation the under-6 carve-out in the institutional definition is trying to keep **inside** the consumer rule. If your facility is for little kids, you do not get to call yourself a barracks.

## What to do with the feeling

If camp made you love bunks, that is allowed. Love is not the enemy. Unexamined love is. The educational move is to keep the smell of pine and still ask: when was this frame made? Is there a continuous rail on the wall side? What mattress thickness is on it? Who is on top, and how old are they?

A cabin can be a good night. A cabin can also be a room full of pre-2000 steel and a counselor who is seventeen. Those are not the same night. The culture wants them to be. This series does not.
