# College dorms and the adult bunk

The catalog word is "lofting." The legal word may still be bunk.

Every August, someone in a residence hall jacks a twin mattress toward the ceiling and slides a futon or a mini-fridge under it. Universities publish PDFs about approved loft kits. Parents buy "dorm bunks" that look like scaffolding. The occupants are nominally adults. The product, if sold into the civilian market with a foundation over 30 inches, is still in 16 CFR 1213 unless it was manufactured **only** for institutional use.

That "only" is doing a lot of work. A bed made for a university system's bid, never sold at retail, might be on the institutional path. A bed sold at a big-box store as "perfect for college" is a consumer adult bunk. It needs the geometry and a GCC. If a younger sibling later sleeps on it at home, the geometry is why part 1213 exists at all.

## Why "adult" was not an escape

The 1999 Commission was explicit. Adult bunks — beds not specifically intended for children — are **often used by children**. The CPSA rule (part 1213) and the FHSA rule (part 1513) carry the same entrapment provisions so that a label cannot launder the opening.

College is the cleanest illustration. Year one: eighteen-year-old. Spring break: a nine-year-old cousin in the off-campus apartment. Five years later: the same frame in a first apartment, then a first kid's room, because students leave furniture on curbs like snakes leave skins.

The adult bunk is a **time bomb with a long fuse** if the openings were forgiven. The rule refuses the forgiveness up front.

## Dorm practice versus product rule

Housing departments regulate **behavior and fixtures**: no cinder-block stacks, no unapproved loft, sprinkler clearance, a rail of some kind. That is occupancy policy. It can be stricter or sloppier than CPSC. It is not a substitute citation.

I have seen university pages that care a lot about not setting off sprinklers and not much about wedge-block language. That does not make those staff foolish. It makes them fire-and-facility people. You still have a furniture object. Two books can apply. Read both.

## Metal, pine, and the rental market

The civilian "college bunk" is often black metal, modular, a ladder that hooks. Easy to ship. Easy to unscrew. Hook-on rails are exactly the kind of attachment the rule wants to be **intentional** — a fastener you release on purpose, or a two-direction motion — not a piece that walks off when someone sits on it.

Used-dorm Facebook groups are a river of missing rails and "we never used the ladder." Draft 39 again. The adult market is the main feeder of the secondhand children's market, because adults are the ones who move.

## EN 747's non-domestic clause

The European standard covers domestic **and non-domestic** use, then excludes prison, military, fire brigade. A university hall in that system looks more like **non-domestic furniture** than like a special-purpose exemption. I will not pretend I have audited a European university bid. I will say the ISO/EN scope is written to catch hotels, hostels, and similar sleeping rooms, not to wave them off.

If you operate a bunk room for paying guests or for students, "we bought them as adult beds" is the beginning of a compliance file, not the end.

## The educational punchline

Adult bunk is a **certificate type** and a **marketing mood**. It is not a different physics. The 1999 rule was written by people who had already watched the mood fail.

When a salesperson says "this is for college, so the kids' rules don't apply," answer with the scope paragraph. Children's **intent** sends you to part 1513 and CPSIA extras. Adult **intent** sends you to part 1213. Both send you to the same openings. The cousin who visits does not read the hangtag.
