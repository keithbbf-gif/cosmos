# ISO 9098 and the shared idea

ISO 9098-1:2023 is the second edition of an international bunk/high-bed safety-requirements standard. Published May 2023. The first edition was **1994** — older than the U.S. mandatory rule, younger than the idea of stacking sleep.

I have the ISO catalogue abstract, not the 16-page paid text. Abstract facts only:

- Safety, strength, durability of bunk beds and high beds, domestic and non-domestic.
- Internal length **greater than 1 400 mm**; maximum bed-base width **1 200 mm**; upper surface of a bed base **600 mm or more** above the floor.
- Other incorporated products (table, storage) not covered here.
- Does not apply to special purposes including prison, military, fire brigades.

That scope is the same family as EN 747-1:2024. Europe and ISO have been looking at each other for a long time. I will not claim they are word-for-word identical in 2026. Harmonization is a process, not a tattoo.

## Why an ISO bunk standard exists

Ships and barracks were never only American. Neither is the children's loft. A manufacturer in one country shipping to twenty needs a book that procurement officers in the twenty have heard of. ISO is that kind of book. It is not automatically law. Countries **adopt**, or cite, or ignore.

The U.S. did not adopt ISO 9098 as the CPSC bunk rule. The U.S. wrote 16 CFR 1213/1513 out of an ASTM-plus-deaths proceeding. Two origins. Some shared physics (height, barriers, openings, under six as a use line in the European/ISO world).

If a U.S. listing says "ISO 9098" and does not say 16 CFR 1213 or 1513, that is a **category error** for a bed sold here. Nice international homework. Wrong house.

## 1994 to 2023

I have not done a redline of the two editions. A 29-year gap says the committee thought the first book needed a second life. Second editions are where fingers, mattresses, and access language usually get stricter or clearer. That is a pattern, not a clause list.

If you are writing a paper, get both editions and do the redline. If you are buying a bed, get the edition the certificate names and the law of the destination country.

## Strength and durability in the title

ISO puts "safety requirements and tests" in the name and "strength and durability" in the abstract. That is the furniture-committee worldview: a bed that passes a hole test on day one and loosens into a hole on day 400 is not safe. Cyclic loads. Fasteners. The upper bunk still attached to the lower.

U.S. mandatory bunk text is thinner on that worldview. ASTM F1427 is where a U.S. buyer goes hunting for it. Different filing cabinets, same fear: **the bed ages.**

## Special purpose, again

Prison, military, fire brigade. The international text is willing to name the institutional bed and walk away. That honesty helps the consumer standard stay a consumer standard. It also means those special beds need **some other** specification — a defense standard, a corrections spec, an NFPA conversation. "ISO doesn't cover it" is not "anything goes." It is "wrong book."

## The shared idea, said small

Around the world, people who write furniture standards have converged on a few judgments:

- Height above a threshold makes a different product.
- Upper surfaces need barriers.
- Openings need a pass/fail tool, not a vibe.
- Young children should not live on the upper surface.
- Lodging is not automatically exempt.
- True custody/command beds are someone else's file.

That convergence is the opposite of bunkum. It is what it looks like when different committees read similar injury patterns and refuse to be original for sport.

Originality is for the stain color. The openings can be boring.
