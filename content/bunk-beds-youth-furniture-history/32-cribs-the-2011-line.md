# Cribs: the 2011 line

The bunk is not the first sleep product we had to federalize twice.

On December 15, 2010, CPSC voted unanimously for new mandatory crib standards. **June 28, 2011**, importers, distributors, manufacturers, and **retailers** had to offer only cribs meeting the new full-size and non-full-size rules — now 16 CFR parts **1219** and **1220**. Child-care facilities and public accommodations (hotels, motels) had until **December 28, 2012** to **use** complying cribs.

CPSC's June 28, 2011 press release is the public scar:

- Recalled more than **11 million** dangerous cribs since 2007.
- Traditional drop-side designs with detaching side rails associated with **at least 32** infant suffocation and strangulation deaths since 2000.
- More deaths from faulty hardware.
- New rules: stop traditional drop-sides; strengthen mattress supports and slats; more durable hardware; tougher tests.
- CPSIA had required the update. The old crib standards had not had a major revision in **more than 30 years**.

OnSafety, June 14, 2011: you **cannot tell by looking** whether a crib meets the new standard. A drop-side immobilizer kit is not a pass. Resale is in scope — if you sell, you sell the new geometry.

## Why this page is in a bunk series

Because families treat sleep products as a **career**. Crib, then toddler bed, then bunk. The hand-me-down stream is one stream. A grandmother's drop-side and a 1996 bunk travel the same Facebook group.

Also because the **lesson is the same**: a volunteer or outdated mandatory book plus hardware that loosens plus a child who cannot speak. Then a year when the law changes the object.

2011 is to cribs what 2000 is to bunks, with a harder retail cut. CPSC did not only regulate manufacture. They regulated **what may be sold**, including used, on that June day. Possession in a home was a different conversation; **commerce** was not.

## Drop-side, said plainly

A traditional drop-side crib has a moving rail so you do not bend as far. The moving rail is a **gap machine** when hardware fails, warps, or is assembled wrong. Infants slipped into the space between mattress and a detached side and suffocated.

The new standards were written so those traditional designs **could not comply**. That is a ban by geometry, not by a poster that says "drop-sides are illegal" as folklore. Folklore is how people miss the date and the resale rule.

## Full-size versus non-full-size

Full-size cribs have a defined interior box — the 1219 text I sampled uses the familiar 28-by-52-ish inch family. Non-full-size (1220) covers the smaller, the portable, the odd. Both had to grow up in 2011. A "travel crib" is not a free pass. Play yards have their own ASTM/CPSC path (F406 and friends). Do not smash them into one word.

## Hotels and day cares

The 2012 date exists because those places **are** the secondhand market with a business license. A hotel crib that is a 1998 drop-side is the exact object the rule wanted out of commerce-affecting lodging. If you run a facility and you are still asking "does it look sturdy," you are asking the 2010-wrong question. Ask: manufactured when, certified to 16 CFR 1219 or 1220, CPC on file.

## The educational carry into bunks

1. Dates on sleep products are not nerdery.
2. Resale is how old geometry outlives a rule.
3. "Looks solid" is how 11 million recalls happen.
4. Immobilizer kits and homemade fixes are not a standard.
5. Unanimous votes happen when the file is ugly enough. Bunks were 2–1. Cribs were 3–0. Both are law.

If your household still has a pre-2011 crib "for when cousins visit," you have a museum piece. Museums do not put infants in the exhibits.
