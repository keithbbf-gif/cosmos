# Mattress thickness is a safety dimension

Comfort is a sales word. Thickness is a measurement that eats a rail.

The 5-inch guardrail height and the 5-inch end-structure height are both taken **with the manufacturer's maximum mattress (and, for ends, foundation) stack on the bed**. The instructions must state that maximum. 16 CFR 1213.6(a). Length and width too — twin, twin XL, or numbers.

If you put a taller mattress on, you have not upgraded the bed. You have **shortened the fence** and you may have changed the lower-end openings the second time they are probed.

## Why makers specify a maximum, not a vibe

Because the rail is designed against a plane. Raise the plane, and a sleeping roll that would have hit wood hits air. Raise the plane, and a gap that was below the mattress line becomes a new slot. Raise the plane enough and a child on the top is lying on a platform with a decorative rim.

Pillow-top, "hotel luxury," memory-foam toppers, futons (draft 10), two mattresses "because they were on sale" — I have heard all of these. A topper is thickness. It counts in the real world even if a lawyer wants to argue about whether a topper is a mattress. The child does not care what you called it.

## The no-mattress tests are not a contradiction

Some probes run with the mattress **off**. That is to find gaps the bedding hides. Some heights run with the mattress **on**. That is to find whether the fence still exists when the bed is used as a bed.

You need both. A bed that only looks safe dressed, or only looks safe naked, is a bed that was styled for one photograph.

## Foundation is in the sentence

Foundation: the base or support the mattress rests on. Slats, platform, a box the maker sold as a pair. If you swap in a thicker foundation — a bunkie that was not the one in the drawing — you have moved the underside (maybe still over 30 inches; maybe you just changed fall distance) and you have moved the top plane.

Bunkie boards are a whole aftermarket. I will not bless or curse a brand. I will say the rule's heights assume the **specified** pair. Improvisation is a new design.

## Labels you should be able to find

CPSC guidance and the CFR together expect:

- Permanent label: name and address, model, month and year of manufacture.
- Warning label inside an upper end structure, where bedding cannot hide it but a pillow might.
- Instructions with mattress size and **maximum thickness**, plus the warning list (under 6, rails both sides, no horseplay, one person on top, use the ladder, full-length rail to the wall if you only have one full-length rail).
- For children's products: tracking information, which may live on the same physical label.

If the thickness number is gone, you cannot honestly claim the 5-inch condition. You can guess. Guessing is how we got this series.

## Replacement mattresses

The original mattress dies. You buy another. This is the most common silent modification in the youth room.

Take the number off the instruction sheet or the label **before** you shop. Shop with a tape, not with "it looks like a twin." Twin widths vary enough to jam or to leave a side gap. A side gap between mattress and frame is another slot. The bunk rule's wedge work is about the **structure**. A mattress that does not fill the structure is a second product problem.

## Europe marks thickness on the bed

EN 747-1:2024, in the public summaries, wants the bed **permanently marked** with the maximum mattress thickness, not only a paper that can go missing. That is a good habit even in a U.S. room. If you still have the sheet, copy the number onto the slat with a marker. Ugly. Legible. Future-you at a garage sale will not have to invent it.

## The educational sentence

A thicker mattress is not "more bed." It is a **change to the guardrail equation**. Treat it like changing a tire size on a speedometer. The car still moves. The numbers lie.
