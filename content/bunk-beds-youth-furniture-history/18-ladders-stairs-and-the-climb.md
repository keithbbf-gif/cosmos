# Ladders, stairs, and the climb

The upper bunk is a second story. Stories need a way up that is not a dresser drawer.

U.S. mandatory bunk law is mostly an **entrapment and rail** law. It is not a full ladder-performance code in the way EN 747 writes means of access. That difference matters. People assume "federal bunk standard" means every climb was dynamometer-tested. The 1213/1513 text I have in front of me spends its energy on openings, rails, labels, and the instruction **use the ladder**. ASTM F1427 has additional design/performance language the CPSC guidance points at as extra, not as the mandatory floor.

So: I will not invent federal rung-spacing numbers that live only in a paid ASTM PDF I did not open. I will talk about what the public rule **does** say, what Europe **is known** to say, and what the injury file already taught.

## What the U.S. rule does say about access

- If the bed is designed for a ladder on one side, the **continuous** rail is on the other side. The ladder **is** the planned interruption.
- Instructions: use the ladder to enter or leave the upper bunk.
- Guardrails attach in a way that is meant to survive ordinary use, including the forces of people climbing nearby.

The 1990s table coded **seven** entrapment incidents on the ladder (two fatal). Ladders are openings. Hook-on ladders grow gaps. A ladder that is also a decorative slat wall can fail a wedge the same as a headboard.

There is a grim footnote in Table 2: a seven-year-old, blind, confined to the upper bunk by **removal of the ladder**. That is not a product defect. That is a use decision that turned a bed into a cell. I am including it because "we took the ladder off so they can't climb" is a sentence adults still say about younger siblings. It does not keep a determined child down. It keeps a child up.

## Stairs

Staircases on bunks feel safer. Sometimes they are, for climb and for a child's sense of balance. They are also **drawers**, which means they are cousins of the dresser. A stair drawer pulled out is a step. A stair chest that meets CSU dimensions may owe STURDY a conversation. A stair with open backs may owe someone an entrapment conversation.

I have not seen 16 CFR 1213 treat stairs as a separate mandatory chapter. Do not hear that as "stairs are unregulated." Hear it as "stairs may drag **other** books onto the bed." Changing-table rules if someone changes a baby on the stair landing. CSU rules if the volume and height qualify. Ordinary good sense about handholds.

EN 747's public table of contents lists means of access, including ladders, stairs, and handrails, with strength and gap language. If you are in that house, the climb **is** a first-class object. If you are in the U.S. house, ask what ASTM F1427 edition the maker used for the ladder, and do not stop at the federal hangtag.

## Falls versus entrapment on the way up

NEISS, 1998: CPSC estimated about **34,300** bunk-related injuries to children under 15 treated in U.S. emergency rooms that year. Forty-one percent younger than five. The narrative comments said **falls** were the majority of those injuries. Deaths that decade were mostly entrapment. Injuries that year were mostly falling.

Those two facts are not a contradiction. Death and injury have different favorite mechanisms. A ladder that is slick, short on the top tread, or left unhooked is an injury machine even when it is not a noose.

Horseplay is on the warning list because the climb becomes a game. Two kids on the ladder. A jump from the third rung. The instruction "one person on the upper bunk" is trying to keep the destination from being a wrestling mat. It cannot keep the ladder from being a jungle gym if the adults have left the room.

## Built-in steps and "just use the drawers"

Captain-bed logic applied to a bunk: climb the storage. I will be short. **Do not.** Drawers roll. Drawers tip the case. Drawers are the STURDY problem wearing a bunk costume. If the maker did not give you a ladder or a stair, the bed is incomplete. Incomplete is not rustic. Incomplete is unfinished.

## Literacy at the climb

Is the ladder attached so it cannot walk off? Is the opening beside it the **allowed** 15-inch story, or a second missing rail? Are the treads actually wide enough for a foot in socks? Is there a night-path — a light, a habit — or are we expecting a half-asleep child to improvise?

I cannot score your socks. I can tell you the file already contains the child who climbed and the child who could not climb down. The ladder is not an accessory. It is the road.
