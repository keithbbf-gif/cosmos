# How to read a bunk label

The label is the part that survives the catalog.

16 CFR 1213.5 and 1213.6 (and their 1513 twins) tell you what must exist. I am going to tell you how a human looks at it in a dim bedroom.

## The permanent plate

Somewhere on the frame, not on the quilt:

- Name and address (city, state, ZIP) of manufacturer, distributor, or retailer
- Model number
- Month and year of manufacture

Address is how you know who to call. Model is how a recall finds you. Month and year is draft 24. If any of the three is a smear of leftover sticker adhesive, you are already in the unknown.

Children's products add tracking: source, date, batch as practicable. Same plate is allowed.

## The warning that pillows may hide

A warning label, permanently attached to the **inside of an upper bunk end structure**, where bedding cannot cover it but a pillow may.

That location is a compromise with real beds. People bury warnings under comforters. The Commission picked a wall a pillow occupies — which means **you have to move the pillow** to do this job. Do it once when the bed arrives. Do it again when you buy used.

I am not reprinting the exact warning artwork here. Artwork revisions happen. Read the one on the bed. If there isn't one, that is a finding.

## The paper that walks away

Instructions must accompany the set:

- Mattress and foundation **size** (twin, twin XL, or numbers)
- **Maximum thickness** for compliance with the 5-inch rail and end heights
- Warnings:
  1. No upper bunk for children under 6
  2. Guardrails both sides of the upper bunk
  3. No horseplay on or under the beds
  4. No more than one person on the upper bunk
  5. Use the ladder
  6. If next to a wall and you do not have two full-length rails, put the full-length rail against the wall

Item 6 is the wall-gap instruction in parent clothes. People skip it because it is the last bullet. It is the one that matches the ASTM-conforming deaths.

## What a label cannot do

It cannot make a pre-2000 opening legal.  
It cannot survive a mattress swap that eats the 5 inches.  
It cannot be trusted if it was printed on a paper hangtag that left the store.  
It cannot replace a certificate.

A beautiful laser-etched logo with no model and no date is decoration.

## Counterfeit and cargo-cult labels

I have seen "meets CPSC" as a graphic in a marketplace listing, no part number, no date, photo of a rail that stops a foot short of the post. That graphic is not § 1213.5. It is advertising.

Cargo-cult: someone copies the look of a warning — orange, bold, a ladder pictogram — and invents the sentences. Compare to the instruction list above. If "under 6" is missing, they did not copy the rule. They copied a mood.

## A ten-minute ritual for a used bunk

1. Move the pillow. Photograph the warning and the plate.
2. Write down model and date.
3. Search CPSC recalls for that maker and model. (I will not pretend I ran yours.)
4. Measure foundation underside to floor. Over 30 inches? You are in this series.
5. Look at wall-side ends. Continuous or a slot?
6. Find a thickness number. If none, you cannot honestly size a replacement mattress.
7. Ask for CPC/GCC if the seller is a dealer. A neighbor may not have one. That is information.

Ten minutes. Less than the time to argue about stain color.

## The educational sentence

Read the label like a serial number on an aircraft part, not like a brand story. The story is for the quilt. The plate is for the file.
