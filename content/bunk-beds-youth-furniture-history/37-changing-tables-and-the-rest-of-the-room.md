# Changing tables and the rest of the room

A youth room is a small industrial site. Every raised surface is a claim.

## Changing products — 16 CFR 1235

CPSIA § 104 again. CPSC adopted ASTM **F2388** (with whatever modifications the current § 1235.2 names). A changing table, in F2388's public definition, is an elevated freestanding structure generally meant to hold a child up to **30 lb (13.6 kg)** on their back so someone can change a diaper.

Contoured pads, add-on changers that sit on dressers, fold-down murals — the guidance treats a family of "baby changing products," not one IKEA table. If it is a changer, it is in the book. If it is a pad thrown on a dresser, you may have invented a changer **and** kept a CSU.

Restraint straps on changers are a fight I have watched parents have for twenty years. The strap is not a babysitter. The standard's job is to keep the surface from being a slide or a collapse. **Your** job is to keep a hand on a child who has already rolled at 4 months and will again. I am not writing a pediatric how-to. I am writing: the object has a rule, and the rule is not a third parent.

Effective-date tables exist (CPSC lists F2388-18 after June 26, 2019; F2388-21 after July 31, 2022 — verify current). CPC cites 16 CFR part 1235.

A changer on top of a dresser is the **combo** that makes this series one series. Tip-over plus fall from elevation plus a child who cannot crawl but can arch. If the dresser is pre-STURDY and unanchored, the changer is a reason to fix the dresser, not a reason to add more stuff to the top.

## Toy chests

Lids that slam. Latches that trap. The old toy-chest file is suffocation and neck. ASTM has had toy-chest language for a long time; CPSC has dealt with it in rules and recalls. I am not going to fake a current part number I did not open today. If you have a hinged lid and a child, you want a lid that will not stay down on a neck and will not slam on fingers. A blanket box with a soft close is a different object than a "hope chest" from 1972.

## Bookcases

Often **out** of 1261. Still a tip-over. Anchor It applies in spirit. Kids climb shelves because objects live on high shelves. The educational move: do not confuse "not a CSU" with "stable."

## Nightstands and "small"

A 25-inch nightstand may miss F2057 gates and still bruise. The rule's 27 / 30 / 3.2 cut is about the **death** pattern, not about every bump. Small is not innocent. Small is out of **this** statute.

## Lamps, cords, monitors

The hanging file from the bunk table is a room file. A baby monitor cord on a crib rail. A lamp cord on a bunk post. I will not write an electrical code. I will say the 1990s bunk hangings were often **ordinary objects** plus a frame. Walk the room for strings.

## The rest-of-room habit

When you change one object, walk the others.

New bunk? Check dresser date and strap.  
New dresser? Check what you just put on top of it.  
New crib? Check the monitor cord and the bumper situation (bumpers are their own sleep-safety fight; AAP has opinions; I will not paste a pediatric policy I did not re-read today).

The category silos of draft 8 are convenient for certificates and fatal for rooms.

## The educational sentence

If you can only name the bunk rule, you are literate in one machine. The child is in a factory of machines. Learn the changer number, the dresser number, the crib year. Then stand in the doorway and look for cords.
