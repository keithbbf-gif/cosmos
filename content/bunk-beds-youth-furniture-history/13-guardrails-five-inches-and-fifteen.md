# Guardrails: five inches and fifteen

A rail is not a vibe. It is a set of measurements that only make sense together.

I am going to walk the U.S. mandatory rail the way the CFR walks it. Not because the CFR is poetry. Because every informal "make sure it has a rail" conversation I have heard dropped at least one of these numbers, and the dropped number is usually the one that killed someone.

## Two rails, one on each side

Any bunk — foundation underside more than 30 inches — needs **at least two guardrails**, at least one per side, for that high surface. A lower bunk whose foundation is 30 inches or less does not owe you those rails under this rule. Height is still the gate.

"At least two" is the floor. Some designs run full rails on both sides and use a ladder gap that is not a missing rail so much as a planned opening. That planned opening is where the next numbers live.

## Continuous versus the 15-inch end

**One** guardrail shall be **continuous** between the end structures. Continuous, in the Commission's mouth, means any gap between rail and end is **no more than 0.22 inches** (5.6 mm). That is a finger-entrapment ceiling, not a "pretty close." The rail does not have to be bolted to the post. It has to **finish the length**.

**The other** rail may stop short. But not by much: **no more than 15 inches** (380 mm) between either end of that rail and the nearest end structure.

If the bed is designed for a ladder on one side, the **continuous** rail goes on the **other** side. That sentence is the wall-side sentence in disguise. The long rail belongs where a child would otherwise slip toward a wall or toward open air opposite the climb. The ladder side is allowed a gap so a person can get in. Fifteen inches is the allowed door. Nineteen inches is not a door. It is the old ASTM-shaped hole that two children died in while the bed still "met ASTM."

I will say that last part again in draft 16 and draft 23. It is the reason this rule exists as a **mandatory** text.

## Attachment that takes a decision

Rails shall be attached so they cannot come off without **either** intentionally releasing a fastening device **or** applying forces **sequentially in different directions**.

That is a human-factors sentence. A rail that lifts off when you sit on it, or when a kid yanks once, is not a rail. A rail that needs a deliberate unlatch, or a two-step motion, is treating removal as a task, not an accident.

I have watched people unscrew rails "so the room looks cleaner" and leave the screws in a kitchen drawer. The rule cannot follow you to the drawer. It can refuse a design that unscrews itself.

## Five inches above the thickest allowed mattress

The upper edge of the guardrails shall be **no less than 5 inches** (130 mm) above the top of the mattress when the **maximum thickness specified by the manufacturer** is on the bed.

This is why mattress thickness is not comfort. It is the rail's remaining height. A thicker mattress is a shorter fence. The instruction sheet must state that maximum. If you lose the sheet, you have lost a safety dimension, not a suggestion.

The Commission carved one design courtesy: a wall-side rail may terminate in a quarter-circle bend and attach to the side rail of the upper foundation. That bend is not a license to open a torso gap. The **no-mattress wedge-block** test still owns the space under the rail.

## What the rail is for, and what it is not

The definition: a rail or guard on a side of the upper bunk to prevent a sleeping occupant from **falling or rolling out**.

It is not a cage for a toddler. It is not a substitute for the under-6 instruction. It is not a chin-up bar. It is not "safe enough" if the wall-side length is a rumor.

Falls still happen. The 1990s file had nine fall deaths in that incomplete count. Rails reduce a class of roll-outs. They do not make the top bunk a crib and they do not cancel horseplay.

## How to look at a rail without pretending you are a lab

Stand at the foot. Can you see daylight at the wall-side end bigger than a pencil? Then "continuous" is already in trouble.

Look at the ladder side. Can you lay a standard sheet of paper (11 inches) in the end gap and still have room, twice, with leftovers? You are in the neighborhood of 15 inches. Neighborhood is not a test. It is a reason to stop and read the label instead of saying "looks fine."

Look at the mattress. Is it ballooning over the rail? Then the 5-inch story is over, whatever the wood looks like.

That is literacy. Draft 14 is the opening under the rail, which is a different death than the roll-out.
