# Comparing houses: US, EU, ISO

A comparison table is how this topic usually gets ruined. People put checkmarks in cells they have not read. I will compare **public, structural** differences and leave the rest marked unknown.

## How you get in the door

| | United States (16 CFR 1213 / 1513) | EN 747-1:2024 / ISO 9098-1:2023 (public scope) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Trigger | Underside of **any foundation over 30 in (760 mm)** from floor | Upper surface of a bed base **≥ 600 mm** from floor |
| Size gate | None in the definition (a bunk is a height) | Length **> 1 400 mm**, base width **≤ 1 200 mm** |
| Lofts | Caught if height matches ("bunk" includes them) | Named: bunks **and high beds** |
| Extra-wide / extra-short | Still a bunk if tall | May fall **out** of EN/ISO and into another book |

A U.S. loft that is 28 inches underside-to-floor might be **out** of 1213 and **in** EN 747 if the top of the base is over 600 mm. Reverse cases exist. This is why importers hire people.

## What the book is

- **US CFR:** entrapment hazard standard. Rails, ends, probes, labels, instructions. Two statute doors (CPSA / FHSA).
- **US ASTM F1427:** volunteer performance/design extras. Still alive. Not a substitute for the CFR.
- **EN 747:** product standard: holes, barriers, **access**, strength, stability, marking. Part 1 requirements, part 2 tests.
- **ISO 9098:** international cousin of that product-standard idea. Not U.S. law.

## Institutions and lodging

- **US:** exemption for beds **manufactured only** for institutional use; under-6 facilities clawed back into the rule.
- **EN/ISO:** special-purpose (prison, military, fire brigade) **out**; domestic **and non-domestic** (hotels, etc.) **in**.

A hostel bunk is the thought experiment. In Europe, EN 747 wants to see it. In the U.S., a retail hostel bunk is a consumer bunk; a true institutional-only frame is an argument I will not win in an essay.

## Under six

Both cultures: upper/high bed not for children under six. US: instruction sheet (and the cultural label). EN: permanent mark or pictogram on the product, per public summaries.

## Mattress thickness

Both: maximum thickness is a compliance dimension. EN public summaries emphasize **permanent marking**. US: instructions must state it; smart people copy it onto the slat.

## Climb

EN TOC: means of access as its own world. US CFR: ladder as the planned rail gap plus "use the ladder." ASTM for more.

## Chemistry and children's extras

US children's bunks: CPSIA lead, phthalates, third-party CPC, tracking. That stack is **American children's-product law**, not an EN 747 clause. Europe has its own chemicals and GPSR/GPSD-style general safety story. I am not going to fake REACH in a bunk series. Know that **passing EN 747 does not print a U.S. CPC**.

## What I will not put in the table

Wedge dimensions versus EN gap probes. Exact barrier heights. Cyclic-load counts. Whether ISO 9098-1:2023 and EN 747-1:2024 are aligned clause-for-clause. I have not opened those PDFs.

A table that invents those cells is worse than no table. It is a green log.

## How to use this page

If you sell **into the US**, start with 16 CFR 1213 or 1513, add F1427, add CPSIA if children's.

If you sell **into the EU/UK**, start with EN 747-1/2:2024, plus whatever general product-safety and chemicals regime applies, plus other EN books for attached storage.

If you sell **everywhere**, you do not get the union of marketing stickers. You get the **intersection of duties**. The bed has to survive every house's door.

If you are a parent who will never import a container, keep one fact: **there is more than one serious house, and they agree on height, barriers, holes, and young children off the top.** That agreement is your bullshit detector when a listing claims a standard you cannot spell.
