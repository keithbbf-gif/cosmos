# Why this series exists

I keep walking past the same object and watching people lie about it.

Not always on purpose. A parent says "they're bunk beds, so they're safe now." A listing says "meets all safety standards" and names none. A camp director points at a rail and calls it a wall. A used-furniture group posts a drop-side crib "for a weekend at grandma's" a decade after the sale of that design became illegal.

The lie is usually a compression. Safety is a stack of dated decisions. Compress the dates and you get a sticker. Stickers are how children get put in geometry that was already known to kill.

This series exists because youth furniture is one of the few consumer categories where the **history and the hazard are the same story**. The bunk is a space-saving idea that left the ship and moved into a child's room. The dresser is a Victorian storage box that met a toddler who climbs. The crib is an infant enclosure whose moving side was a convenience until it was a gap. You cannot understand the rule if you do not understand the room the object used to live in — and you cannot trust the room if you do not read the rule.

I am writing it educationally. That word is doing work. Educational means: I will teach you enough to **ask a better question**. I will not certify your purchase. I will not tell you which SKU to click. I will not pretend I sat in the ASTM committee room. I will quote the public record and mark the edge of what I have not opened.

## The scar that belongs on page one

From January 1990 through August 9, 1999, CPSC's files held **91** reports of bunk-bed deaths of children under fifteen. Fifty-seven were entrapment. Twenty-five were hangings from belts, ropes, clothing, bedding. Nine were falls. The Commission said, in the same table, that this was not a complete count. Capture-recapture math put bunk-bed **entrapment** deaths at about **ten a year** through that decade.

Over 96 percent of children who died from entrapment were age three or younger. All but one were younger than five.

That is not a mood. That is section B of a final rule published December 22, 1999.

If you remember one paragraph from this whole folder, remember that one. The rest of the series is furniture around it.

## Why "youth furniture" and not just "bunks"

Because the child does not live on one product. The same body that must not sleep on an upper bunk at age four will pull out a dresser drawer at age two and use it as a stair. The same household that retired a drop-side crib in 2011 may still have a changing table whose restraint is in a junk drawer. Category walls are for lawyers and SKUs. Gravity does not honor them.

So the series is a **room history**: how stacked sleep became a kids' product, how the rest of the youth suite grew up next to it, and how the United States — then Europe and ISO — wrote tests after the room failed.

## Why drafts, and why this many

One overview is a pamphlet. Forty-plus drafts are a chance to be wrong in public on purpose — to take one object, one clause, one myth, and sit with it until the sentence is honest.

Quality over speed is not a slogan here. It is the only way this topic does not become SEO. I would rather forty-five pieces that a careful reader can argue with than one "ultimate guide" that names ASTM and moves on.

Each draft is staged. History first, because the bunk is older than the children's aisle. Anatomy second, because the rule is a drawing. U.S. bunk law third, because that is the house I can cite from the Federal Register I actually read. Other jurisdictions fourth. The rest of the youth room fifth. Practice last, because practice without the file is just vibes.

## Who I am in this

I am not a pediatric injury epidemiologist. I am not CPSC staff. I am a person who reads primary text and refuses to report a wish as a fact. That is enough for an educational series and not enough for a courtroom.

If you came here from a store, slow down. If you came here from a standard, stay for the history — the 15-inch gap makes more sense when you have seen why people put beds against walls. If you came here because a kid is sleeping in your house tonight, skip to drafts 21, 26, 39, and 42, then come back and read the rest when you can breathe.

The bed will still be there in the morning. The question is whether you can still see it.
