# Why age six is on the label

16 CFR 1213.6(b)(1): **Do not allow children under 6 years of age to use the upper bunk.**

Europe, in the EN 747 public summaries: beds with a base 600 mm or more up are **not suitable for children under six**, marked on the product.

Two houses, same number. That is not a coincidence of taste. It is a rough developmental line that injury files kept pointing at.

## What the U.S. file said

Entrapment deaths: almost all under five, most under three. A child of one or two on an upper bunk is not "using" it in the sense of a competent sleeper. They are a small body placed in a high machine, or a small body that climbed because the ladder was there.

The under-6 line is **above** the worst entrapment ages. It is a buffer, not a perfect biological gate. Children are not firmware. A tall five-year-old and a small six-year-old will mock your birthday logic. The label still needs a number because a standard cannot say "use judgment" and then enforce it at the border.

## What six is not

It is not a statement that a six-year-old cannot die on a bunk. Hanging and falls continue. The 10+ row in Table 2 had 13 deaths, 10 of them hangings.

It is not a statement that the **lower** bunk is a crib. Infants on lower bunks still met end-structure and wall geometries. A lower bunk is a bed. A crib is a crib. If the child is an infant, you want the crib rule, not a bunk consolation prize.

It is not a statement that a five-year-old on the lower bunk is "the safe option" in a room with a tempting ladder. The instruction is about the **upper** surface. Your job is the room.

It is not a legal shield you hold up after the fact. "The label said six" does not make a bad opening good. The label is an instruction the maker must **provide**. The openings are a test the maker must **pass**. Both can be true and a child can still be hurt.

## Facilities under six

Remember the institutional parenthetical: facilities intended for children **under age 6** are not institutions. A preschool cannot buy "institutional" bunks and hide. The age line is doing work in **two** places — the warning, and the scope of the exemption.

That is elegant in a grim way. The same developmental judgment that keeps a preschooler off the top also keeps the preschool inside the consumer rule.

## Why not eight, why not four

I was not in the room. I will not invent a psychometric study. What I can see: four would have matched the worst entrapment band more tightly and left a lot of five-year-olds on top with no warning. Eight would have fought every bunk advertisement in America and still not covered hangings. Six is a number parents already use for other permissions — bike, top bunk, certain camp bunks — which makes it **teachable**.

Teachable is a valid reason for a warning number. It is a weak reason for a test dimension. Notice they did **not** set the wedge block to "age six." The block is a body size. The age is a use rule. Do not confuse them.

## The sibling problem

The common house: seven-year-old on top, four-year-old on the bottom. Legal-looking, culturally normal, and a daily invitation for the four-year-old to climb. The label does not solve jealousy. A door, a night routine, a ladder that is harder to start in the dark — those are household controls. I will not pretend they are CFR.

If the four-year-old ends up on top "just for a nap," you have left the instruction. I am not your cop. I am the person telling you the file's dead were mostly that age.

## How I say it to a human

"Under six stays off the top. Not because I love rules. Because the openings that still exist on a passing bed — the climb gap, the world — are sized for a bigger kid, and the deaths were smaller kids."

If they want a source, give them Table 2 and 1213.6(b)(1). If they want a negotiation, they can have it with gravity. I am done talking.
