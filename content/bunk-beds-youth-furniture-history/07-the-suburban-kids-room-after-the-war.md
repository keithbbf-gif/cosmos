# The suburban kids' room after the war

This is the chapter the catalogs think they are in.

After the Second World War, the United States built a lot of houses that were bigger than a tenement and smaller than a myth. Two, three, four children. A room that was supposed to be a childhood. Not enough square feet for every child to get a four-poster and a desk that did not touch a bed. The bunk — already proven in camps, trains, and barracks — walked into that room and put on paint.

I am not going to give you a first-year-of-sale for "the suburban bunk." Furniture trade history is messier than a single SKU, and I have not sat in the Baker Library with the old *Furniture World* bound volumes. What I will stand on: by mid-century the bunk is **visible as a children's object** in advertising, in floor plans, and then, loudly, on television.

## Baby boom, small bedroom, new idea of the child

Jason Reid's *Get Out of My Room!* is the book I want in your hand for the **teen bedroom** as a historical invention. The idea that a child is owed a room with a door, posters, and a territorial sulk is not ancient. It is modern, American, and tied to prosperity plus a certain psychology of adolescence. The bunk is a compromise inside that invention: you get the room, you do not get the exclusive floor.

Greg Brady, on television, called sharing with the bunks a jail sentence. Reid remembered coveting the *Diff'rent Strokes* set. Both reactions can be true in the same culture. The bunk is status and sentence at once. Top bunk is "big kid." Sharing is "not enough house." Sitcoms sanded that contradiction until it looked like fun.

The *Brady Bunch* blue bunks and the Jacksons' L-shaped wood set did more for demand than any ASTM committee. That is not a complaint. It is how consumer objects actually spread. First the picture. Then the store. Then, much later, the death file.

## What the suburb changed

Supervision thinned. A barracks has a schedule. A camp cabin has a counselor in a creaking bunk by the door — sometimes. A suburban hallway has a parent who is also doing dishes, also tired, also believing the rail is "good enough."

The bed moved from a **watched density tool** to an **unwatched furniture purchase**. Hardware got prettier and, in some decades, lighter. Flat-pack arrived later and made the stack a Saturday project with an Allen key and a missing dowel.

The wall became part of the bed. Small rooms push frames flush to drywall. That is the exact geometry of the **bed/wall entrapment** deaths in the 1990s file: a child slips through a gap at the end of a rail and wedges between mattress and wall. Institutions sometimes left a walkway. Suburbs often did not.

## Gender, class, and who got the top

I am not going to fake a sociology paper. Observation is enough: catalogs sold bunks as brothers, then as sisters, then as "sleepovers," then as gender-neutral clouds and dinosaurs. Working-class urban apartments used them as necessity. Middle-class suburbs used them as a story about togetherness. Vacation houses now use them as a story about capacity.

Same object. Three class dialects. The safety rule does not change dialect. A 15-inch opening does not care whether the duvet is heirloom or Target.

## The hangover

By the time CPSC opened the bunk proceeding in the late 1990s, the suburban bunk was ordinary. Ordinary objects are the hardest to regulate. Everyone already owns one. Everyone already has a story about not falling off. The voluntary standard had been sitting there. Compliance was uneven. Kids were still dying in the gap the suburb invented: bed, wall, rail that did not finish the job.

Draft 19 is the file. This page is only the room the file walked into.

If you grew up in that room, you remember the ceiling being closer on the top, the dust on the slats, the whispered after-lights-out parliament. Keep the memory. Then measure the rail anyway. Nostalgia is not a test method.
