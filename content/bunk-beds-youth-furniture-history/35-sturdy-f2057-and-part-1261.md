# STURDY, F2057-23, and part 1261

This is the dresser rule at the level of a person who will not sit through the ASTM PDF.

**Law:** 15 U.S.C. § 2056f (STURDY), December 29, 2022.  
**Rule:** 16 CFR part 1261, incorporating ASTM F2057-23 (approved February 1, 2023).  
**When:** clothing storage units **manufactured after September 1, 2023**.  
**Who it is for:** children up to **72 months** — CPSC and AHFA both say that age band accounts for about **80 percent** of CSU tip-over deaths. I am repeating their public figure, not a study I ran.

## The three stability tests, in human

AHFA and CPSC describe F2057-23 as requiring the unit to pass **all** of these (interlocks, if present, get their own evaluation; I am not going to fake that clause):

1. **Loaded drawers / clothing load.** Storage volume filled at a specified density (CPSC guidance mentions 8.5 lb per cubic foot of volume in the loaded-test description). Open the extendible elements the test names. The unit must remain stable. This is "full of clothes, drawers out."

2. **Horizontal force.** All doors open, all elements extended. A **10-pound** horizontal force at the highest handhold not over 56 inches. Held. The unit must not go over. This is a child pulling, or a sibling yanking, or a walker catching a knob.

3. **Carpet plus child weight.** A **0.43-inch** block under the rear legs (carpet crush). Doors open, elements out. A **60-pound** apparatus hung on the front of the door or drawer most likely to tip, for **30 seconds**. This is the toddler-as-weight on the open drawer, on the rug you actually own.

If you only remember one picture, remember **3**. That is the one the old living-room "but it's heavy" argument loses.

## Anti-tip device

A device meeting **ASTM F3096** must be **packaged with** the product. Packaged with is not installed. The federal stability tests are about the **unanchored** unit surviving those simulations. The strap is extra, for the real room, the real carpet, the sibling who climbs anyway.

CPSC still tells consumers to anchor. Draft 36. The device in the box is a duty on the maker. The screw in the stud is a duty on the household.

## Certificates

CSUs need a CPC or GCC citing **16 CFR part 1261**. Children's vs general-use follows the usual product-intent rules. A dresser sold as nursery furniture is not going to talk its way out of children's-product extras if it is intended for that room.

## What STURDY demanded the standard be able to do

The statute wanted performance that included, in CPSC's public paraphrase:

- stability on carpeting
- loaded drawers and multiple drawers open
- simulation of children up to **60 pounds** interacting with the unit

F2057-23 was judged to have those. That judgment is why we are not living under the November 2022 CPSC-unique test protocol. Two roads, one destination: a **mandatory** stability floor.

## What the rule does not do

It does not fix every bookcase (often out of scope).  
It does not fix TVs (a different tip-over file).  
It does not re-test your 2015 MALM-lookalike.  
It does not make a 26-inch, 28-pound cube into a CSU if it misses a gate — and a short cube can still hurt; it just is not this part.

Scope games will happen. They always do. 27 inches, 30 pounds, 3.2 cubes. Measure.

## Next to the bunk

A bunk stair-chest that is 40 inches tall with four drawers may be a CSU **and** part of a bunk. Two books. A captain bed's drawers, same thought. The youth room is a **stack of CFR parts**, not a vibe called "kids' furniture."

## The educational sentence

STURDY is the rare statute whose acronym is the whole argument. Unstable dressers. Youth. Stop. The test that matters is the 60-pound weight on an open drawer on a carpet block. If a salesperson cannot talk about that picture, they cannot sell you this object honestly.
