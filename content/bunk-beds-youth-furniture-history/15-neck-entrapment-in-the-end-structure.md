# Neck entrapment in the end structure

This is the clause that arrived late, after the first proposal, because people were still dying in a shape the first draft did not own.

The 1999 preamble is blunt about the timeline. ASTM meetings after the original proposal showed that extra requirements were needed for **children's necks in the end structures**. The Commission voted 2–1 to propose those additions (64 FR 37051, July 9, 1999). They landed in the final rule. Commissioner Gall voted no on the final. The neck language is not a footnote. It is a second scar sewn onto the first.

## Head-first, not torso-first

Different death than draft 14.

The child puts their head into an opening, then moves into a **narrower** part of that opening where the head cannot come back out, then loses their footing. The neck takes the weight. The preamble names this as head-first entrapment.

You can picture a V, or a decorative oval that pinches, or the space between a curved member and the underside of the upper deck. One of the three ASTM-compliant deaths the Commission discussed was a 22-month-old whose head went into an opening between the upper foundation support and a **curved** end-structure member. Curves are pretty. Curves pinch.

## The template, at the level you need

Figure 2 is a test template with an "A" section and a "B" section. I am not going to dimension it from memory. The CFR does that.

The idea, in human language:

1. You only run this on openings that already **passed the wedge and the 9-inch sphere** — the large openings in the lower end structure.
2. You present the "A" section in the plane of the opening. If both sides of "A" touch the boundary at once, you have a shape that can catch a head-like width. You mark those contact points.
3. You then present the neck portion of "B" perpendicular to the opening. If that neck portion **enters completely** — the text says passing 0.75 inch or more beyond the "A" contact points — the opening **fails**, unless the lower boundary slopes downward at **45 degrees or more** all the way from the pinch to the part that already passes a 9-inch sphere.

The 45-degree escape is the "this is a slide, not a noose" exception. A neck can travel toward a hole big enough for the head to follow. A neck that travels into a tightening pocket cannot.

CPSC's business guidance refuses to summarize this procedure in cute form. So do I, beyond the purpose. The detail is the protection. A casual paraphrase is how a factory "almost" complies.

## Why ends, why lower

The lower-bunk end is at child height. A toddler lives there even when the "under 6" label keeps them off the top. They climb the endboard. They look through cutouts. They wear the headboard as a window.

Upper-end openings above the foundation have a simpler assignment: do not pass the wedge at all. Different height, different expected body.

The wall-gap deaths are a third geometry (draft 16). Neck-in-the-end is the one people miss because the bed "has rails" and the headboard looks solid from across the room. Solid from across the room can still be a necklace up close.

## What a designer should feel in their hands

I am not a designer. I have read enough of this file to say: **avoid tapered holes at toddler head height.** If you want a cutout, make it obviously too small for a head or obviously large and open downward. The in-between oval is how you get a standard named after you.

If you are a buyer, you do not need to love the template. You need to distrust cute shapes in the lower end until someone shows you a certificate that includes 16 CFR 1213.4(c)(3) or 1513.4(c)(3).

## Respect for the late addition

Rules that grow in public, mid-proceeding, are easy to mock as sloppy. I read this one the other way. The Commission **updated the proposal when the deaths said the first geometry was incomplete**. That is the opposite of fabricated compliance. They let the file write the second sentence.

If you ever sit on a committee, this is the move worth copying. Do not defend the first draft because it was first. Defend the child who did not fit the first draft.
