# Boarding houses and the stacked city

The city stacks people. Sometimes it stacks their sleep.

It is tempting to write a Dickens paragraph here — tenement, boarding house, a rope bed over a rope bed, the 19th-century poor inventing the bunk out of rent. Tempting, and sloppy.

Larson's work, as reported in that 2021 bunk-room feature, is a useful brake. In the ordinary household and in hospitality, she found **almost no bunks** before the twentieth century. Homes wanted beds they could take apart, move, sell. A built tier is capital you cannot carry down the stairs when the rent is due. Inns and taverns, when they packed guests, more often **shared the mattress** than stacked frames. Strangers shoulder to shoulder. That is a different horror and a different furniture history.

So I will not give you a tidy "tenement bunk" origin story with a year I cannot source. I will give you the real urban pressure: **density without a product**.

## How cities slept before the SKU

Lodging houses, sailors' rests, flop houses, YMCAs, immigrant "hot beds" rented by shift — the historical literature is full of **shared and sequential sleep**. One mattress, three men, eight hours each. That is stacking in time, not in space. Hammocks in some waterfront rooms. Built-in berths in some purpose-built hostels later on.

When a physical stack does appear in the city, look at who owns the wall. Prisons. Hospitals. Workhouses. School dormitories. The institution again, even when the address is urban. The boarding-house keeper with a carpenter and a leftover closet is a maybe. The documented pattern is the administered building.

If you have a primary inventory that shows household bunks in, say, 1870 New York, I want it. I have not seen it in the sources I trust enough to cite. Until then, the stacked city is mostly **rhetoric**. The stacked barracks is evidence.

## Why the myth wants to be true

Because it makes the children's bunk feel democratic. "We've always stacked the kids in small rooms." It naturalizes a mid-century retail object as folk wisdom. Folk wisdom is harder to regulate. If the bunk is ancient and domestic, then a 1999 rule looks like bureaucracy. If the bunk is institutional equipment that we invited into the nursery, the rule looks like what it is: a conversion tax.

I would rather the tax.

## Hostels, capsule hotels, and the later city

The late 20th and early 21st centuries *did* productize urban stacking: youth hostels with purpose-built bunk rooms, capsule hotels, pod hostels, "bunk-bed dorms" sold by the pillow. Those are real. They sit after the consumer bunk already exists. They are not the origin. They are the city borrowing the camp and the barracks once the manufacturing line was cheap.

Notice the paperwork problem they create. A hostel bunk may be sold as contract furniture. A capsule is almost architecture. A pod may fail the EN 747 width or length gate and fall into some other standard — or into a gap. I am not going to map every city's lodging code. I am going to say: **urban stacking today is a procurement choice**, not an excuse to skip the tests that apply to the product you actually bought.

## The children's room is a small city

A 10-by-11 suburban bedroom with two kids is a density problem with nicer curtains. The boarding-house myth survives because the feeling is shared: not enough floor. The honest history says we solved household density with **low beds and shared rooms** for a long time, and only later with a manufactured stack.

When you put a bunk in that room, you are not completing an ancient folk practice. You are installing a piece of institutional logic in a space that does not have a night watch.
