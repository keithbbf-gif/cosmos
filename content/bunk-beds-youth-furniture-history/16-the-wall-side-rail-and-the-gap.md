# The wall-side rail and the gap

Put a bed against a wall. Of course you did. The room is small. That is why you bought a bunk.

Now you have invented a third surface: mattress, rail, **drywall**. The gap between bed and wall is not empty. It is a slot the width of whatever the carpet and the baseboard and the slightly-out-of-square frame left you.

The 1990s file knew this slot. Eleven entrapment incidents were coded **bed/wall** on the top bunk (nine fatal). Six more bed/wall on the bottom (all six fatal in that table). Bottom-bunk wall deaths are a thing people do not expect. They happen.

## The two ASTM children

Section B of the final rule: of three deaths in beds that **conformed to the then ASTM standard**, two were the same story. An 18-month-old and a child almost five. Each slipped through the space between the **end of the guardrail and the end structure**, then wedged between bed and wall.

ASTM F1427-96 allowed up to **15 inches at each end** of the upper bunk. Both ends. That is a door at the head and a door at the foot. Against a wall, either door dumps a small body into a slot.

The mandatory rule's answer is not "never use a wall." The answer is: **one rail must run the length.** The wall-side — or the side opposite the ladder — is the continuous one. The 15-inch courtesy remains only on the other side, as a climb gap, not as a pair of wall funnels.

CPSC guidance says it in buyer English: the rail next to a wall, or on the side opposite the ladder, must run continuously from end to end. If it does not attach to the end, the leftover gap cannot exceed **0.22 inches**. The away-from-wall rail may stop, but not more than **15 inches** from an end.

The instruction sheet adds the behavioral twin: if you place the bed next to a wall and you do **not** have two full-length rails, put the **full-length** rail against the wall.

## Why "we'll pull it out six inches" is not a plan

People say they will float the bunk in the room. Then a nightstand, then a laundry basket, then the vacuum lives there, then the frame walks back to the wall in an inch-a-month migration. Beds migrate. Rules that depend on a forever aisle are rules for a showroom.

A continuous rail still works when the bed kisses the paint. That is the point.

## Bottom bunk and the wall

The guardrail requirements are about the **high** foundation. The wall can still kill on the low side if the frame, a decorative rail, or an add-on bar makes a slot. The file's bottom-bunk wall deaths are a warning against homemade "safety rails" and against assuming the floor is forgiveness.

Add-on rails show up in the table as their own row. One top-bunk add-on death. Two bottom-bunk add-on deaths. Aftermarket rails are a product. They can create the opening they were bought to close. I will not recommend a brand. I will say: an add-on that makes a new in-between gap is not a kindness.

## Hotels, Airbnbs, and the styled bunk room

The 2021 bunk-room fashion — timber, cutouts, built-in — often **is** the wall. The bed is architecture. That can be good (no gap) or bad (built-in gaps, decorative openings, no label, no mattress spec). A built-in that eliminates the slot still has ends and climb access. Architecture does not automatically pass Figure 1.

If you are the one specifying a bunk room, you are a manufacturer in spirit even if your contractor disagrees. Pick a tested product or accept that you are now in the custom-furniture risk business. Draft 40.

## A habit

When you walk into a room with a bunk, look at the wall-side **ends** before you look at the wood stain. If you can see a child's-torso-shaped invitation at the head or foot, you are looking at the 1990s. The date on the label had better be after June 19, 2000, and the rail had better actually be the long one.

If there is no label, you are not looking at a mystery. You are looking at a question you have not answered.
