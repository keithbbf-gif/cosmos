# What a bunk bed actually is

People point at two mattresses and call it a bunk. The law points at a tape measure.

**16 CFR 1213.2:** *Bunk bed means a bed in which the underside of any foundation is over 30 inches (760 mm) from the floor.* Foundation means the base or support on which a mattress rests. Not the top of the pillow. Not the decorative canopy. The underside of the thing the mattress sits on.

That definition does a lot of violence to common speech, and it is supposed to.

A loft bed with a desk underneath is a bunk if the foundation hangs high enough. A "twin over twin" that someone built low to the floor might not be. A triple stack is three arguments with the tape, not one product name. A trundle that slides out at shin height is usually not a bunk; the upper bunk on the same frame probably is.

CPSC's own business guidance repeats the same line in plainer English: a bunk bed is a bed with the bottom of its mattress foundation more than 30 inches above the floor. The ends are the uprights at head and foot that the side rails attach to. Those ends are where a lot of the later tests live.

## Why height, not "two beds"

Entrapment and falls scale with height. A child who rolls off a mattress six inches up meets the floor differently than a child who rolls off a mattress four feet up. A child who slips into a gap between an elevated frame and a wall meets the gap with more of their body weight hanging. The Commission wrote a height gate so that the rule would catch **any** sleeping surface in that danger band, including the ones catalogs refused to call bunks.

This is the same move you see in other product rules. Define by the hazard geometry, not by the merchandising name. "Youth loft." "Sleep+study." "Castle bed." "Adult dorm bunk." If the foundation underside clears 30 inches, the bunk rule is in the room.

## Two beds that are not the same product

Once the tape says bunk, U.S. law splits the paperwork:

- Intended for children → 16 CFR part 1513 (FHSA path), plus the children's-product stack under CPSIA.
- Not intended for children → 16 CFR part 1213 (CPSA path).

The **entrapment geometry is substantively identical**. That phrase is in the scope section of both parts. I will say it until it sticks. Manufacturers have tried, for decades, to talk as if "adult bunk" were a lighter duty. It is a different certificate. It is not a different opening.

Children's bunks also pick up sharp-point and sharp-edge rules (16 CFR 1500.48 and 1500.49), lead and phthalate limits, third-party testing, a Children's Product Certificate, and tracking labels. Adult bunks need a General Certificate of Conformity against the bunk standard. Draft 25 sits with the paperwork. Here I only need you to see that **intent** is a label the maker puts on, and **use** is what happens in the house.

## What the definition refuses to be

It is not "a bed for two siblings." Plenty of bunks sleep one person on top and hold a desk, a sofa, or empty air below.

It is not "a bed with a ladder." Some lofts use stairs. Stairs do not cancel the height.

It is not "a bed sold in the kids' department." College bunks, military-style metal stacks sold to civilians, and Airbnb "bunk rooms" all meet the same tape.

It is not "a bed that looks like the Brady Bunch." Looks are culture. The foundation is physics.

## Europe uses a different tape

EN 747 and ISO 9098 do not copy the 30-inch sentence. They talk about bunk beds and **high beds** whose bed-base upper surface is **600 mm or more** above the floor, with an internal length greater than 1 400 mm and a maximum bed-base width of 1 200 mm. Six hundred millimeters is about 23.6 inches — a lower height trigger than the U.S. foundation-underside rule, and a different measurement point (top of base vs underside).

Do not convert in your head and declare them the same. Different houses, different gates. Drafts 29–31 compare them. The point for this page: **every serious bunk standard starts by saying what counts**, because if you let the catalog decide, the dangerous ones rename themselves and walk out of the room.

## A small practical habit

If you are standing in front of a bed and you want to know whether the U.S. bunk rule even applies, get on the floor and look at the **underside of the slats or platform**, not the top of the comforter. Measure to the floor. Write the number down. Then read the label for mattress thickness, maker, month and year.

That is not testing. That is literacy. The rest of this series assumes you are willing to do at least that much.
