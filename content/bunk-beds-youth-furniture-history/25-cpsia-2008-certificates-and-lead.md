# CPSIA 2008: certificates, lead, and the second stack

The bunk rule is a 1999 geometry. The Consumer Product Safety Improvement Act of 2008 is a **children's product operating system**. When a bunk is intended for children 12 and under, both stacks apply.

I am not writing a CPSIA treatise. I am writing what the CPSC bunk guidance actually lists, because that is the public artifact a buyer can hold.

## Chemistry

- **Surface coating:** children's bunk paint may not contain more than **0.009 percent** lead. (The old furniture-paint story. 16 CFR part 1303 sits in the background.)
- **Lead in accessible components:** no more than **100 ppm** (0.01 percent) total lead.
- **Phthalates:** plasticized components of children's bunks, as child care articles, not more than **0.1 percent** of eight named phthalates: DEHP, DBP, BBP, DINP, DIBP, DPENP, DHEXP, DCHP.

If your bunk is powder-coated steel and solid wood, you still have coatings and whatever plastic caps, wheels, or decorative bits a factory added. "It's wood" is not a chemistry waiver.

## Testing and the two certificates

**Children's bunk:** tested by a CPSC-accepted third-party lab for the applicable children's product rules. Then a **Children's Product Certificate (CPC)** issued by the domestic manufacturer or importer.

**Adult bunk:** **General Certificate of Conformity (GCC)** based on a test of each product or a reasonable testing program, certifying the bunk standard (and any other applicable rules).

This is the paperwork twin of draft 22. Same openings, different proof style. Third-party vs reasonable-program is not a small difference. It is why a dinosaur bunk and a college loft do not share a PDF template.

Sharp points and edges (16 CFR 1500.48 and 1500.49) apply to children's bunks. CPSC says third-party testing is **not** required for those two. They still apply. "Not third-party" is not "not real."

## Tracking labels

Section 14(a)(5) of the CPSA wants a children's product and its packaging marked, to the extent practicable, with source, date, cohort (batch/run). The bunk rule already wanted name, address, model, month and year, plus a warning label and mattress instructions.

CPSC's guidance is practical: **one label can do both jobs** if it carries the combined facts. A single plate that is missing the batch is a half-done merge.

## What CPSIA did not do

It did not rewrite the wedge block. It did not move the effective date of the bunk geometry. It did not make adult bunks into children's products by magic.

It **did** make it much harder to import a painted children's bunk with a shrug. The 2007–2008 lead-in-toys crisis is the scar under this statute. Youth furniture rode along because children chew rails and lick paint whether or not the object is a toy.

## How a buyer uses this without becoming a customs broker

Ask to see the CPC or GCC. Not a marketing badge. The document. Children's bunk CPC should cite the bunk rule (16 CFR 1513) and the chemistry/testing stack as applicable. Adult GCC should cite 16 CFR 1213.

If the seller says "we don't give those out," you have learned something. If the seller sends a certificate for a **different model**, you have learned something else. Certificates are model-and-date objects. A PDF from a cousin SKU is bunkum.

## Section 104 is the other CPSIA child

CPSIA § 104 told CPSC to make mandatory standards for durable infant and toddler products. Cribs, toddler beds, changing products live there. Bunks do **not** owe their existence to § 104. Bunks already occupied the building.

When a salesperson says "it's JPMA" or "it's a durable infant product," map the object. A bunk is usually the 1999 rule plus, if children's, the CPSIA extras. A crib is § 104 plus 16 CFR 1219/1220. Mixing the slogans is how you get a crib sold as a "baby bunk."

## The educational sentence

After 2008, a children's bunk is not done when the openings pass. It is done when the openings pass **and** the chemistry, the lab, the certificate, and the tracking mark exist as artifacts you can request.

A claim of compliance is not compliance. Ask for the paper.
