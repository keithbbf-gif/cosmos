# Loft, captain, triple, and the cousins

The bunk has relatives. Some of them are bunks in costume. Some of them are other products wearing the same bedding.

## Loft

A loft is a high bed with the floor claimed for something else: desk, sofa, empty "clubhouse." Parents love lofts because they look like a solution to a small room. The tape loves lofts because the foundation is almost always **over 30 inches**.

In U.S. consumer-product language, that loft is a bunk bed. Guardrails, labels, mattress thickness, the under-6 instruction — the whole stack. The missing lower mattress does not grant a pardon. If anything, the open floor underneath changes how a fall ends and how tempting the frame is as a jungle gym.

Europe says "bunk beds and **high beds**" in the same EN 747 breath. That pairing is the honest one. Height is the family name. The second mattress is optional.

## Captain

A captain's bed is a mattress on a chest of drawers. Low. Storage in the volume that a bunk would leave as air or as a second sleep. It is usually **not** a bunk under the 30-inch-underside definition. It can still be a **clothing storage** problem if the drawers are deep, the unit is tall enough, and a child treats the drawer as a step.

This is the cousin people confuse in the other direction. "It's like a bunk, but safer because it's low." Low is safer for falls from sleep. Low is not a tip-over waiver. Measure height, weight, enclosed volume. If it meets the CSU gates, STURDY's world is in the room. See drafts 34–36.

## Triple and L-shape

Triple: three tiers, or a twin over a full with a third sleep stuffed in. L-shape: two beds sharing a corner post, one high, one low at a right angle — the *Diff'rent Strokes* picture.

The tape still decides. Each foundation over 30 inches is a bunk surface. An L-shape can create **two** wall conditions and a corner gap the old rectangular mental model does not cover. I will not invent a special L-shape test that is not in the CFR. I will say: openings are openings. Ends are ends. Wall gaps are wall gaps. Fancy plans do not get fewer probes.

Triples also tempt people to put a younger child "in the middle" as if the middle were a hug. The middle is still a height and still a gap. The under-6 instruction is about the **upper** bunk. It is not a blessing on the middle shelf.

## Futon stack, daybed, trundle

A futon that folds is not a bunk. Two futons in a room are not a bunk. A Western "futon bunk" — a frame sold to hold two futon mattresses — might be a bunk if the upper foundation is high enough. The word futon does not decide. Draft 10 is the Japanese comparison.

A daybed with a trundle is usually a low pair. Measure anyway. Some "daybed + loft" hybrids are just bunks with a pillow story.

## Canopy, tent, curtain

Soft goods change the **hanging** file. The 1990s deaths included children hung from belts, ropes, clothing, and bedding. A canopy kit, a play tent thrown over the upper bunk, a string of flags, a charging cable — the frame becomes an anchor. The mandatory instructions already say no horseplay and no extra person on top. They cannot list every object a child will drape.

I am not writing a ban on pillow forts. I am writing a reminder that the hanging deaths were not all "the bed's fault" and not all unforeseeable. The bed is a rack. You choose what hangs from it.

## Convertible language

"Converts from crib to toddler bed to full." "Converts from bunk to two twins." Conversion is a **second product** hiding in the first. When you convert, you change which standard's geometry you need. A crib kit that becomes a toddler bed is walking from 16 CFR 1219 toward 16 CFR 1217. A bunk you split into two twins may leave the bunk rule and become ordinary beds — unless one twin is still lofted on leftover posts.

Read the conversion instructions as a legal event, not as a lifestyle tip. If the hardware pack for the second life is missing, you do not have the second product. You have a project.

## Family portrait

Loft = bunk by height.  
Captain = storage that may be a CSU.  
Triple / L = bunks with extra openings.  
Trundle = usually not.  
Soft canopy = hanging risk on a bunk frame.

If a salesperson uses any of those words as if they cancelled a CFR part, they are selling dialect. Ask for the tape and the certificate.
