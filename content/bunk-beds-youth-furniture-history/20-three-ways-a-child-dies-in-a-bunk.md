# Three ways a child dies in a bunk

The preamble names three geometries. I am going to keep them separate so they do not smear into "bunks are dangerous."

Dangerous is a fog. A geometry is a drawing you can change.

## 1. Entrapment

A child is trapped between the upper bunk and the wall, in an opening under a rail, or in other structure — ends, ladder, add-on bar.

Two sub-drawings:

**Torso-first.** Body through, head not. The wedge block is the argument against this.

**Head-first.** Head in, then into a narrower pocket, footing gone, neck loaded. The template is the argument against this.

Age: almost entirely the under-five, mostly under-three, in the 1990s file. A toddler's head-to-torso ratio and a toddler's inability to reverse a panic are the reasons this is not a "be careful" problem. Careful is for people who can still think. A wedged airway is a clock.

The rule's personality is **remove the hole**. Warnings are backup. You do not warn a two-year-old out of a slot.

## 2. Hanging

Twenty-five of the 91. Belts, ropes, clothing, bedding. The bed is the anchor. The ligature is often something the house already owned.

Age: mostly six and older in that file. An older child has the motor skill to put a belt somewhere and not the immortal luck to take it off. A younger child can still hang in a sheet; the 1998 NEISS narrative had a two-year-old. The column in the death table just leans older.

The mandatory instruction sheet does not say "no belts." It says no horseplay, one person on top, under 6 down, use the ladder. The hanging file is wider than those sentences. Canopies, strings of flags, charging cables, hooded sweatshirts on posts — the frame is a rack (draft 9).

I will not write a catalog of ligatures. I will write the principle: **do not add vertical toys to a bunk post**, and do not treat "they are old enough for the top" as "they are old enough for a noose-shaped game."

This is the mechanism a rail does **not** fix. A perfect continuous rail and a belt on the end post can still share a room.

## 3. Falls

Nine deaths in that incomplete 1990s count. A minority of deaths. A majority of the 1998 ER injuries.

Rails, mattress thickness, ladder habits, one person on top, no horseplay — those are fall controls. So is the under-6 rule, because a preschooler on a high mattress is a roll-off waiting for a nap.

Falls feel intuitive, so people over-index on them. They buy a rail and think they are done. The death file says you are not done. The injury file says you still needed the rail.

## Why the rule is an entrapment rule

Look at the titles: *Safety Standard for Entrapment Hazards in Bunk Beds* (part 1213). The Commission went where the **deaths** were, not where the ER visits were. That is a values choice. You can argue they should have written a fuller fall-and-ladder code into the CFR. Europe puts more access language in EN 747. The U.S. mandatory text is the entrapment text plus labels.

I do not treat that as a scandal. I treat it as a **scope**. When someone says "it meets the CPSC bunk standard," they have said something important and incomplete. Ask about ASTM F1427 for the rest of the performance picture. Ask about the mattress. Ask about the room.

## Near-miss is the fourth way, almost

Sixty-five near-miss entrapments in the same window. A parent heard a sound. A sibling yelled. The geometry was already the death geometry. Luck and proximity wrote the ending.

I want near-miss treated as data, not as a family story that ends with a nervous laugh. If you have pulled a child out of a bunk gap, you do not have a funny anecdote. You have a failed opening and a second chance.

## How to talk about this without terror theater

Name the mechanism. Name the age band. Name the control.

"Toddlers and openings."  
"Older kids and things that hang."  
"Everyone and height."

If a conversation cannot do that, it is not an educational conversation. It is a mood. Moods do not pass wedge blocks.
