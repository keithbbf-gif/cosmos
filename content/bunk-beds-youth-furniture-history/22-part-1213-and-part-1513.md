# Part 1213 and part 1513

Two doors. Same house.

When CPSC issued the bunk rule in December 1999, it used **two statutes** because bunks live in two legal personalities.

## FHSA for children's bunks — 16 CFR part 1513

The Federal Hazardous Substances Act is the old children's-product hammer. A children's bunk that does not meet part 1513 is a banned hazardous substance under 16 CFR 1500.18(a)(18). Meet 1513, and you are exempted from that ban.

This is the path for beds **intended for use by children**.

## CPSA for the others — 16 CFR part 1213

The Consumer Product Safety Act is the general product hammer. Part 1213 is a consumer product safety standard for bunks **not** intended for children — the adult, college, "military-style" civilian bed.

The Commission said the quiet part in the preamble: those beds are **often used by children anyway**.

## Substantively identical

Both scope sections say the entrapment requirements are **substantively identical**. I have read enough of both to believe them. Guardrails, continuous rail, 15-inch gap, 5 inches, wedge, sphere, neck template, labels, instructions — the geometry is the same conversation.

So why two parts?

Because the **legal hook** and the **downstream paperwork** differ. Children's products, after 2008, drag CPSIA: third-party testing at a CPSC-accepted lab, Children's Product Certificate, tracking labels, lead, phthalates, surface coatings. Adult bunks: General Certificate of Conformity, reasonable testing program, the bunk standard as the cited rule.

If you smash them into one part, you confuse every certificate template in the country. If you let them have different **geometry**, you create the laundromat: "it's adult, so the openings can be prettier." The Commission refused the laundromat.

## Who decides "intended for children"

Intent is a manufacturer's claim with legal consequences. Design, packaging, advertising, where it is sold. A dinosaur bed in the juvenile aisle is not a hard case. A plain metal loft sold next to mini-fridges is not a hard case in the other direction. The gray middle — "youth/teen," "guest room," "cabin" — is where lawyers earn money.

For an educational series, the useful move is: **do not take the word "adult" as a safety downgrade.** Take it as a certificate type. Then look at the openings anyway.

## Effective date, shared

Both: manufactured in the United States or imported **on or after June 19, 2000**. Both: institutional-only carve-out, with the under-6 facility clawback.

A bed made in May 2000 is not "illegal" for existing. It is **pre-effective**. It may still be a bad idea in a child's room. Grandfathering is not a blessing. It is a date line. Draft 24.

## Votes

The Commission voted **2–1** to issue the final rule. Chairman Ann Brown and Commissioner Thomas H. Moore yes. Commissioner Mary Sheila Gall no. The ANPR was 2–1 the same way. The NPR was 2–0–1 (Gall abstained). The extra neck requirements were 2–1.

I name the dissent because unanimous safety rules are easy to mythologize. This one was argued. Gall's statements are in the Commission's files; I did not reproduce them here. Disagreement is part of how you get a record.

## How to cite like an adult

Children's bunk: 16 CFR part 1513 (and 1500.18(a)(18) as the ban you escape).  
Adult bunk: 16 CFR part 1213.  
Definition: § 1213.2 / the twin in 1513.  
Tests: § 1213.4 / § 1513.4.  
Business English: CPSC bunk guidance page.

If a listing says "CPSC approved," ask which part. CPSC does not "approve" a consumer bed the way a stamp-collector thinks. They write a rule. You certify against it. Words matter.
