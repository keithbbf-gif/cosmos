# Openings, wedges, and the block

Kids fit through holes that furniture designers thought were decoration.

The U.S. bunk rule's central tool is not a slogan. It is a **wedge block** — Figure 1 in parts 1213 and 1513 — that stands in for a child's torso. If the block can pass, the opening is the wrong size for a body that might follow.

I am going to describe what the test is **for**, not how to machine a block and go to war with a store display. The procedure lives at 16 CFR 1213.4. If you need to run it, open that. If you need to understand a death, stay here.

## Torso-first

One of the ways a child dies in a bunk is ugly and specific. The torso goes through an opening. The head does not. The child is stuck, airway compromised, time limited. The Commission named this in the 1999 preamble: an opening too small for the head after the body has already gone through.

The wedge is tapered. Orientation matters. The rule says to use the **most adverse** orientation — the way the block is most likely to pass. That is the opposite of a marketing photo. Marketing photos show the pretty axis.

On guardrails, with **no mattress** on the bed, there shall be no opening between the lower edge of the uppermost rail member and the underside of the upper foundation that lets the wedge pass when you apply **33 lbf** (147 N) for **one minute**, perpendicular to the big end of the block.

No mattress. That detail is easy to skip. Bedding hides gaps. The test undresses the bed.

## Ends of the upper bunk

With no mattress, no opening in the end structures **above** the upper foundation may let the wedge pass **freely**. Freely is a different verb than the 33-pound pull under the rail. Read the section. Do not blend the tests into "they poke a thing in holes."

The upper end structures also owe you height: at least **5 inches** above the maximum mattress/foundation stack for at least **half** the distance between the posts. A fancy cutout that drops the endboard to mattress height along most of the width is not a headboard. It is a missing fence.

## Lower bunk ends: small or big, not in-between

This is the clever — and grim — part.

Openings in the lower-bunk end, between the underside of the upper foundation and the upper side of the lower foundation, shall **not** pass the wedge **unless** they also pass a **9-inch (230 mm) rigid sphere**.

Translation: if a torso can go through, a head must be able to go through too. The in-between opening — big enough for a chest, small enough to catch a skull — is the forbidden band.

Then, if an opening is in that "wedge passes and sphere passes" world, it still has to face the **neck-entrapment** template. Draft 15. Passing the sphere is not a gold star. It is the entrance to another test.

They run the lower-end wedge/sphere check **without** mattress and foundation, then **again** with the manufacturer's maximum stack. Bedding changes the hole. The rule knows.

## Why a block and not a sentence

"No dangerous openings" is not enforceable. A block is. Two labs can argue about a curve on a headboard; they can also shove the same Figure 1 at it. The 1999 rule is full of this personality: **stop describing virtue, start describing a tool.**

ASTM F1427-96 already had a 3.5 by 6.2 inch tapered block idea. The federal rule did not invent the metaphor. It **federalized** a geometry and then added neck language ASTM had not finished.

## What I will not do

I will not publish a homemade "does my bed pass" protocol with kitchen objects. A 9-inch sphere is not a soccer ball you almost remember. A 33-pound force is not "pull hard." False pass and false fail are both how people get hurt — one by trusting a bad bed, one by throwing out a good one and buying a worse replacement in a panic.

Literacy looks like this: you know **which openings are in scope**, you know **why in-between sizes are the enemy**, and you know that a decorative heart cutout in a headboard is not automatically innocent.

If the cutout looks like it would make a good handle for a child-sized torso, you have enough suspicion to ask the seller for the certificate and the standard year. Suspicion is not a test. It is the beginning of one.
