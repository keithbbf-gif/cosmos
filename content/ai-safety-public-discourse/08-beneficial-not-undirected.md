# "Beneficial," not "undirected": a 2017 word fight

Principle 1 is only one sentence: the goal of AI research should be to create not undirected intelligence, but beneficial intelligence. It looks like a platitude until you notice the insult hiding in the first adjective.

**Undirected** is a charge. It says there is a way of chasing capability that treats the point of the work as "more intelligence" without a beneficiary. **Beneficial** is the replacement goal. It does not say beneficial *to whom*, in the first principle. Later lines try: as many people as possible (14), all of humanity (15, 23), dignity and rights (11). The opening move is still just a fork in the road.

## Why the fork had to be said in public

By 2017, "intelligence" as a research trophy was already a marketing word. Benchmarks, grandmaster games, image-net medals — the public scoreboard rewarded capability demonstrations. Principle 1 is a small attempt to change the scoreboard's legend. It does not name ImageNet. It does not need to. Anyone in that room knew the gravity well.

The sentence is also a peace offering to critics who thought AI safety talk was anti-progress. "Beneficial" keeps the project. It relocates the adjective. You can still research hard problems. You are asked not to treat the problems as directionless.

## What the sentence cannot do

It cannot define benefit. A military research office and a disability-rights clinic will not share a utility function. The list knows this somewhere — cultural diversity is in Principle 11 — but Principle 1 stays upstream, as a mood correction.

It cannot rank near-term benefit against speculative future harm. A system that helps a radiologist today and, in some later story, contributes to a dangerous capability cascade would be "beneficial" on one clock and not on another. Asilomar's bin structure is the only hint about clocks. Principle 1 sits in the research bin, so the immediate audience is a lab's purpose statement.

## Echoes you can hear without forcing them

OpenAI's later public mission sentence — ensure AGI benefits all of humanity — is in the same adjective family, with a more specific noun (AGI) and a universal beneficiary. The 2015 founding posts already liked "benefit humanity." Asilomar did not invent the word. It put it in position one of a numbered list that thousands of people were invited to sign.

European official English preferred **trustworthy** (HLEG, 2019) and then **harmonised rules** (the Act). Trustworthy is a different moral flavor. It sounds like a consumer can pick the product up. Beneficial sounds like a researcher chose a telos. Both words do public work. They are not synonyms.

## A slow-reading habit

When a later keynote says "we are building beneficial AI," ask three ordinary questions:

1. Is *beneficial* doing the work of Principle 1 (a goal for research), or the work of a press office (a glow around a launch)?
2. Is there a named beneficiary, or only a cloud?
3. Is there any admission of a trade-off, or has the adjective erased the conflict?

You do not need a new ethic to ask this. You need to treat Principle 1 as a fork that later speakers keep driving through at speed.
