# The twenty-three principles, read slowly

The Asilomar AI Principles page is easy to skim and easy to mythologize. Skimming turns it into "be good with AI." Mythologizing turns it into a constitution. It is a list. The useful exercise is to read it as a list: twenty-three sentences, three published headings, a dateline.

FLI's public page has carried a publication stamp of **11 August 2017**, even though the conference was in January and FLI described the list on 18 January. Treat the January note as the process story and the August stamp as the open-letter page's own date. Do not collapse them.

## The three printed bins

**Research Issues (1–5).** These are about the culture and funding of a field. The goal should be beneficial intelligence, not "undirected" intelligence. Money for capability should come with money for the hard adjacent questions. Researchers and policymakers should talk. The culture should be cooperative. Teams should avoid a race that cuts safety.

Notice what is *not* here: a definition of intelligence, a compute threshold, a licensing scheme. The unit is the research community.

**Ethics and Values (6–18).** This is the widest bin, and the most mixed. Safety and failure-transparency sit next to judicial explanation, privacy, shared prosperity, human control, and a clause against an arms race in lethal autonomous weapons. A reader who lives in European product-safety law will feel at home in some of these sentences and stranded in others. A reader who lives in long-termist essays will feel the reverse.

**Longer-term Issues (19–23).** Here the list stops pretending the subject is only today's classifier. Capability caution: do not assume a ceiling. Importance: advanced AI could be a profound change in the history of life. Risks: catastrophic and existential risks deserve planning commensurate with impact. Recursive self-improvement gets its own line. Common good: superintelligence, if developed, for widely shared ideals and all humanity, not one state or organization.

That last clause is the most science-fiction sentence on the page. It is also the one that later political documents usually do not copy. The EU Act does not legislate superintelligence. Asilomar did not pretend the topic was unsayable.

## How to read a principle without reciting it

Take Principle 10, Value Alignment: highly autonomous systems should be designed so goals and behaviors can be assured to align with human values throughout operation.

Three ordinary questions:

1. **Who assures?** The sentence uses a passive. Assured by the builder? A regulator? A test suite that does not exist on the page?
2. **Which human values?** Principle 11 tries to help — dignity, rights, freedoms, cultural diversity — and immediately inherits every political fight those words contain.
3. **Throughout operation** is a time word. It is how the list admits that a system can drift, be updated, or be put to a new use.

You do not need a new theory to ask those questions. You need to refuse the skimming that treats "alignment" as a settled noun.

## What the list is good for

It is a map of what a particular 2017 room could still say together. The high-support filter means the sharpest disagreements were probably edited out or never nominated. Absence is information. There is no principle that says "do not build this," except in the weak form of arms-race avoidance and the superintelligence common-good clause. There is no principle that names a company. There is no enforcement annex.

If you assign this page to students, assign it with a red pen and the three headings. Ask them to mark each principle as *today's product*, *today's politics*, or *notional future system*. They will not agree. The disagreement is the lesson the list accidentally teaches.
