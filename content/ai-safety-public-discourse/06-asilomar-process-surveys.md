# Ninety percent in a selected room

FLI's process note from 18 January 2017 is short and worth teaching on its own. It describes a pipeline: literature pile, distillation, pre-conference survey, breakout edits, competing versions, final survey, keep what clears ninety percent. That is enough structure to talk about **how public consensus was manufactured** without sneering at the people who manufactured it.

## Why they needed a number

A principle list without a threshold is an editor's taste. A threshold gives the list a story: we did not keep the pet ideas of the loudest person at dinner. Ninety percent is a high bar. It is also a bar that rewards mildness. A sentence that almost everyone can live with is often a sentence that does not force a choice.

That is not a scandal. It is a property of the method. If you want a document that a mixed room will sign, you will lose the clauses that split the room down the middle. The public list is the residue of agreement, not a transcript of conflict.

## What the method cannot tell you

It cannot tell you how the attendee list was built. Invitation is the first filter, and it is the one least visible on the principles page. It cannot tell you how much time a breakout spent on lethal autonomous weapons versus shared prosperity. It cannot tell you who wrote the first draft of a line that later looked inevitable.

It also cannot tell you what "support" meant. In ordinary survey English, support can mean *I would sign this* or *I will not die on this hill.* Those are different acts. The process note does not publish the instrument.

A careful educational use of the note is therefore negative as well as positive. Positive: there was a method, and it was described. Negative: the method's unstated choices are where the politics hid.

## Later signatories are a second process

The open-letter page invited signatures from people who were not in Pacific Grove that week. That is a standard web move. It changes the sociological object. A principle that survived a room survey and then collected thousands of remote signatures is doing **circulation work**. Circulation is not the same as the original ninety percent.

Signatory counts on FLI's page have moved over the years. An archived 2023 snapshot showed thousands of names; contemporaneous 2017 notes cited smaller figures. If you quote a number, date the snapshot. Do not treat the latest counter as the 2017 fact.

## A classroom exercise that stays novelty-safe

Hand students the process note and the list. Ask them to invent, on paper only, one principle that would likely *fail* a ninety-percent cut in a mixed 2017 room — for example a hard pause on systems above a stated compute level, or a ban on all workplace scoring. Then ask why it would fail. The point is not to smuggle in a new principle as a proposal. The point is to feel the filter.

Then stop. Do not "improve" Asilomar. The draft set's job is to understand a public object, not to issue Asilomar 2.0.

## Why bureaucratese helps

People who want AI safety to sound like prophecy skip the process essay. That is a mistake. The essay is how you see the meeting as an ordinary professional ritual: surveys, breakouts, a website. Rituals can still matter. They matter more honestly when you can describe the paperwork.
