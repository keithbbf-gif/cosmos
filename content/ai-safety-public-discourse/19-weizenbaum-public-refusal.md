# Weizenbaum's refusal, sitting next to the safety story

Joseph Weizenbaum's ELIZA (1960s) and his 1976 book *Computer Power and Human Reason* belong on any honest public timeline of "people worrying out loud about thinking machines." They do not belong *inside* the FHI folder as if they were drafts of *Superintelligence*. They are a different refusal.

## What the public remembers

ELIZA was a simple pattern-matching therapist script. Weizenbaum watched people, including people who knew the trick, attach feelings to it. The unease he later wrote down was not "this thing will recursively self-improve." It was: humans will delegate judgments that should stay human; institutions will hide behind the computer; the mere fact that a task *can* be automated will be taken as a reason that it *should*.

*Computer Power and Human Reason* is an argument about the proper limits of instrumental reason. It is closer to a humanist lecture than to a control-theory paper. That is why some later AI-ethics teachers love it and some later existential-risk teachers treat it as a side exhibit.

## Why it sits beside this series anyway

Because public discourse is not a single bloodline. When a 2023 columnist says "we have been warned for fifty years," they may be thinking of Weizenbaum, or of Good, or of a film, or of a mix. The warnings do not agree with each other.

Weizenbaum's target is often **use and authority** — a bank, a court, a hospital letting a program speak in a human's place. Asilomar's longer-term bin targets **capability trajectories**. The EU Act's high-risk annex targets **product categories**. If you flatten all three into "AI safety," you will misread every document.

There is also a biographical public fact that teachers still use: Weizenbaum became a critic of the culture that celebrated his demo. That arc — builder turns scold — later attached to other names (Hinton in 2023 is the mass-media version). The arc is a genre. Genres repeat. Persons differ.

## How to read him without recruiting him

Do not recruit Weizenbaum as an early alignment researcher. He did not ask your 2016 research question.

Do not dismiss him as unrelated because he lacks a loss-of-control chapter. A large part of today's statutory AI politics is about automated decisions in public life, which is his neighborhood.

Do not treat ELIZA as a lesson that "people are stupid." Weizenbaum's published point is harsher: people are lonely, institutions are lazy, and a thin program can occupy both facts.

## A pairing that stays honest

Assign a short Weizenbaum excerpt with Asilomar Principles 8 and 16 (judicial transparency; human control of delegation). Ask what each text thinks the failure looks like. Then add a page from the EU Act's recitals on human oversight. The three texts will not harmonize. The non-harmony is the history of the discourse, not a problem for a later synthesizer to erase.
