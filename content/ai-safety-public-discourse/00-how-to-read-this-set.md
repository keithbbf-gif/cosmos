# How to read this set without making a myth

Public talk about AI safety is easy to flatten. A reader meets a beach town, an Oxford institute, a lab charter, and a Brussels regulation, and the mind wants a single plot: *the scientists warned, the labs promised, the law arrived.* That plot is a cartoon. The public record is lumpier than that, and the lumps are the education.

This draft is a reading rule, not a history. The numbered pieces that follow are close readings and timelines. This one is about how not to smash them together.

## Four objects, four jobs

**Asilomar** in this set is two public meetings that later writers keep tying together. The 1975 recombinant-DNA conference is a precedent people *cite*. The 2017 Beneficial AI conference is a meeting that *produced* a numbered list. Citing is not the same as repeating. When a 2017 organizer mentions 1975, that is a public rhetorical move. It does not make the two conferences the same event, and it does not make DNA guidelines into AI law.

**FHI** is a university institute with a founding year (2005), a famous book (2014), a closing date (16 April 2024), and a final report the institute itself published. An institute is not a movement. Alumni go elsewhere. Ideas travel without letterhead. When a draft says "FHI," it means the Oxford group and the documents issued under that name, not every later talk that used the word *existential*.

**The OpenAI charter era** is a window of *published* sentences: the December 2015 founding post, the 9 April 2018 Charter, the March 2019 capped-profit note. Later lawsuits, blog revisions, and restructurings are public too, but they are later. This set treats the charter years as a document era, not as a verdict on what any company became.

**The EU AI Act public timeline** is a legislative file. It has COM numbers, Ordinary Legislative Procedure steps, an Official Journal cite (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689), and an application calendar in Article 113. A White Paper is not a statute. A political agreement in December 2023 is not the signed act of 13 June 2024. The education is the difference.

## Prefer the dull page

If a claim in these drafts matters, it should survive contact with a primary page:

- a conference write-up with a date
- a numbered principle list
- a university closing notice
- a blog post with a dateline
- a COM document or an OJ citation

Secondary journalism is used as a pointer, not as scripture. When a newspaper headline is livelier than the official text, believe the official text first and keep the headline as evidence of *how the event was sold*.

## Do not upgrade a letter into a law

Open letters, principle lists, ethics guidelines, and summit declarations are **speech acts**. They can be signed, quoted, ignored, or later written into a recital. They do not, by themselves, create duties. The EU AI Act does. A lab charter creates, at most, a public standard the lab can be asked about. Mixing those force-levels is how educational writing turns into fan fiction.

## Do not upgrade a date into destiny

2014 is when *Superintelligence* reached a wide non-specialist audience. 2015 is when several labs and institutes made public safety-flavored announcements. 2017 is Asilomar's twenty-three principles. 2018 is the Charter. 2019 is OECD plus Europe's HLEG guidelines plus a lab structure change. 2021 is the Commission proposal and UNESCO's recommendation. 2023 is a pile of letters and a Bletchley declaration. 2024 is the Act in force. Those years are real. They are not a proof that "the world woke up." Plenty of fairness, labor, and surveillance work was already public in those same years under other banners.

## Two vocabularies, one word

In public English, **safety** now covers at least two conversations that only partly overlap:

- **Product and rights safety:** discrimination, privacy, product liability, biometric surveillance, consumer harm. This is the main grammar of the EU file.
- **Long-horizon or catastrophic safety:** loss of control, recursive improvement, "superintelligence," existential risk. This is the grammar of much FHI-adjacent writing and of several Asilomar "longer-term" clauses.

Both are public. Neither owns the word. Drafts in this set keep the vocabularies labeled so a reader can see when a speaker has switched tracks without saying so.

## What you will not find here

You will not find a new framework with a clever acronym. You will not find a ranking of who was "right." You will not find unpublished emails. You will not find a claim that this folder contains a discovery.

If a paragraph starts to sound like a thesis defense, it has failed the stage.
