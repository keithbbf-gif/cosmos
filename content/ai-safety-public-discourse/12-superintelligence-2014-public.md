# 2014: when *Superintelligence* became a public object

Nick Bostrom's *Superintelligence: Paths, Dangers, Strategies* was published in 2014. For a general reader of AI safety talk, that year is less important as a philosophical milestone than as a **distribution** milestone. A university press book (Oxford University Press) left the seminar and entered airport bookstores, podcasts, and the bedside tables of people who funded laboratories.

This draft is not a book report. It is a note about what happens to a public conversation when a long, hedged academic argument acquires a single-word title.

## What the public heard

The portable version, the one that showed up in magazine rooms, was roughly: if we build machines that outthink us, the default result may not be a helpful colleague; control is a research problem, not a slogan; waiting until the machines arrive is late. Specialists will protest that this version is unfair to chapters on pathways, oracles, and collective decision-making. They are right, and the portable version still won the airport.

Famous readers did some of the distribution work. Public recommendations from entrepreneurs and scientists are part of the 2014–2016 record. So is the backlash: reviewers who called the book a fable, or a distraction from present harms. Both the recommendations and the backlash are discourse. Neither replaces the table of contents.

## What the book did to vocabulary

After 2014 it became easier, in ordinary English, to say **superintelligence**, **control problem**, and **existential risk** in the same paragraph as Silicon Valley. Those phrases had academic homes already. The book gave them a trade spine and a citation that a journalist could spell.

It also gave opponents a target with an ISBN. That is a service. Vague unease is hard to argue with. A book you can hold is easier to praise, parody, or assign.

## How to read it now without writing a sequel

Three educational rules:

1. **Separate the scenarios from the existence claim.** The book is full of conditional trees. Public talk often promotes a branch to a forecast. When someone says "Bostrom proved X," go back and see whether the page said *if*.
2. **Separate the book from the institute.** FHI is not the book; the book is not every later FHI working paper; later nonprofit blogs are not chapters.
3. **Separate 2014 from 2023.** The year large chatbots became a mass product changed the audience for control-problem talk. It did not rewrite the 2014 table of contents. People who quote 2014 as if it were a documentary about 2023 are doing propaganda, not history.

## Why this set starts a whole era here

Because several later public objects — the 2015 letters, the 2017 longer-term bin, the Charter's AGI sentences — are easier to hear once *Superintelligence* has been in the room. Influence is not authorship. OpenAI did not "come from" the book. FLI did not "come from" the book. They share a weather system the book thickened.

If you read only one chapter for this series' purposes, read the early framing chapters and the pages on control, then stop and write down what the author marks as speculation. That notebook page will be more useful than a dozen thread summaries.
