# Puerto Rico, 2015: the meeting before the principles

Asilomar 2017 did not arrive from nowhere. The Future of Life Institute had already hosted a public-facing research meeting on Puerto Rico in January 2015 — often remembered as the Puerto Rico AI conference, formally a gathering on the future of AI under FLI's banner. Videos, recaps, and an open letter from that season are still in ordinary web circulation. The 2015 meeting is easy to skip if you start the story at the twenty-three principles. Skipping it makes 2017 look like a thunderclap. It was a second pass.

## What 2015 did in public

The Puerto Rico meeting put a particular mix of people in one place and then put some of the conversation on the internet: researchers who built systems, researchers who wrote about long-term risk, a few industry names, and a surrounding press narrative that "the people who make this stuff are worried." That narrative was already forming around Nick Bostrom's 2014 book and around public remarks by physicists and entrepreneurs. The meeting gave the narrative a room and a date.

FLI also circulated, in that period, an open letter on research priorities for robust and beneficial AI. The letter is a different object from the later Asilomar list. It is shorter in spirit: a call to treat beneficial AI as a research topic that deserves funding alongside capability work. It is an agenda-setter, not a twenty-three-point constitution.

If you read only later coverage, you will see 2015 flattened into "the year everyone woke up." The flatter version is useful for magazine timelines and bad for education. 2015 was a year when a small network became more visible, not a year when a field converted.

## Why a second conference needed a different souvenir

Puerto Rico produced conversation and a letter. Asilomar produced a **surveyed list**. FLI's own January 2017 process note is almost bureaucratic, and that is why it is valuable. Organizers compiled views, distilled candidate principles, surveyed attendees before the meeting, revised, broke into groups, surveyed again, and kept a principle only if it cleared a high support threshold.

That process is a public answer to a predictable criticism of 2015: *a few famous people talked, therefore the field has spoken.* 2017 tried to look more like a consensus document. Whether ninety-percent support in a selected room is "the field" is a fair question. The organizers at least published the method.

## How to place 2015 without romance

Treat Puerto Rico as **scene-setting**. Names that later appear on Asilomar's signatory page, in lab safety blogs, or in 2023 open letters were already in circulation. Themes that later harden — arms races, value alignment, the fear of a capability jump — were already speakable in a conference hotel.

Do not treat 2015 as the origin of AI ethics. Fairness, labor, and surveillance literatures were already thick. Do not treat it as the origin of computer-safety engineering. Those communities had their own workshops. 2015 is the moment a particular **long-horizon safety network** became easier for a newspaper to find.

If you are building a personal timeline, pencil this:

- 2014: *Superintelligence* reaches a general audience.
- January 2015: FLI Puerto Rico meeting and the research-priorities letter.
- December 2015: OpenAI's founding announcement (a different institution, same public weather).
- January 2017: Asilomar, then the principles page.

The weather is shared. The documents are not interchangeable.
