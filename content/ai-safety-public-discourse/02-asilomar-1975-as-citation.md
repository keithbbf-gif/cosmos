# 1975 as a citation, not a sequel

If you only know Asilomar from AI essays, you could be forgiven for thinking the 1975 recombinant-DNA conference was a dress rehearsal. It was not. It was a meeting about circular DNA and containment, held because a specific experimental technique had just become easy enough to scare the people who invented it. The AI world inherited the *story*, not the protocol.

## What the public story usually includes

The standard public version, the one that later AI writers keep retelling, has a few stable beats:

- In the early 1970s, researchers learned to cut and splice DNA across species lines.
- A group of them, including Paul Berg, called for a pause on certain experiments.
- The February 1975 Asilomar meeting gathered working scientists with lawyers and press in the room.
- The output was a graded approach to risk: some work could proceed under specified containment; some work should wait.
- U.S. NIH guidelines followed, and the pause ended in a regulated form rather than a permanent ban.

That is the version that travels. Professional historians of biology have spent decades arguing about who was left out of the room, whether the press presence was enlightenment or stagecraft, and whether "self-regulation" is the right name for a process that quickly became federal. Those arguments are public too. An educational reader does not need to pick a winner. The reader needs to know that **the traveling story is already a compression**.

## What AI discourse borrowed

Three portable objects left 1975 and showed up in 2010s AI talk.

**The pause.** A research community can, in public, say "not yet" about its own tool. Later open letters about large training runs used that shape. A letter is not a lab shutdown. The shape is still recognizable.

**The mixed room.** 1975 made a point of not being only bench scientists. 2017 invited economists, lawyers, philosophers. The guest list became part of the claim: this is not a club talking to itself.

**The written output.** Guidelines, then principles. Something numbered you can point at when a journalist asks "so what did you decide?"

## What AI discourse could not borrow

DNA work in 1975 had a relatively tight technical object. You could say which ligation, which host, which containment hood. "Advanced AI" in 2017 did not have an equivalent object that a facilities manager could lock. There was no agreed test for "this model is the one we pause." There still is not, in any public standard that commands the field the way NIH containment levels commanded recombinant work.

So when an AI essay says "we need an Asilomar," ask which of the three borrowed objects it wants. A pause without a specified object is a mood. A mixed room without a decision rule is a conference. A numbered list without a downstream agency is a press page.

## Why this still belongs in an AI-safety reading list

Because the citation did real public work. It told a non-specialist audience: scientists have done something like this before; the genre exists; you are not watching a brand-new panic. It also told researchers: there is a respectable ancestor for talking about harm in the same week you talk about grant proposals.

That is influence at the level of **genre**. Genre is not law, and it is not a proof that 1975 "worked" in a way 2017 can copy. It is why the beach name keeps appearing.

If you want the biology, read the 1975 documents and the NIH guideline history on their own terms. If you want the AI story, keep 1975 in a footnote where the speakers put it — as a borrowed costume for a different machine.
