# "Benefits all of humanity": one sentence, many later uses

The Charter's mission clause and the founding post share a beneficiary that is hard to oppose in a sound bite: **all of humanity**. Asilomar's Principle 23 uses the same phrase. UNESCO will later speak in the key of humanity and dignity for 193 member states. The words are doing cosmopolitan work. They are also doing *anti-local* work. A particular workforce, a particular city, a particular language community can disappear inside the glow.

## What the sentence claims

It claims a fiduciary direction. The Charter makes that almost explicit: "Our primary fiduciary duty is to humanity." Fiduciary is a legal-sounding word. In U.S. ordinary talk it suggests duties to someone whose interests you must put first. Pointing that duty at a species is a moral gesture, not a filing in a specific chancery court. The gesture still changed the public standard. Journalists and, later, officials could ask: *is this decision for humanity, or for a round?*

It claims distribution. "Broadly distributed benefits" is the heading. The text says the organization will use whatever influence it obtains over AGI's deployment to avoid uses that harm humanity or unduly concentrate power. "Unduly" is doing a lot of quiet work. Every antitrust lawyer knows that adverb.

## What the sentence hides

**Conflict among humans.** Humanity is not a committee with a whip count. A benefit to a translation user can be a loss to a translator. The sentence does not give a rule for that fight. No one-sentence mission does. The honesty is to notice the missing rule, not to demand the blog post be Rawls.

**Near versus far.** A system that pays out now and risks a tail later can be described as beneficial on one ledger and not on another. The Charter's long-term safety heading is the attempted patch. Whether the patch holds is a later empirical and political question.

**Who speaks for the beneficiary.** "All of humanity" has no elected voice on the page. The organization speaks, in first person, *on the way to* that beneficiary. This is the oldest NGO problem in a new font.

## How the sentence was reused

Supporters treated it as a north star. Critics treated it as a shield. Parodists treated it as a punch line. All three uses are public and predictable. An educational reader can watch the same eight words move from a 2018 blog to a 2023 congressional hearing clip to a 2024 lawsuit exhibit without needing to join a camp.

Compare, if you have time, the EU Act's beneficiary language. The Act is for the internal market and for people whose rights the Union already knows how to name — health, safety, fundamental rights. That is narrower and more justiciable. "All of humanity" is wider and harder to sue over. Different genres, different teeth.

## Stay with the sentence

If you write about the charter era and you skip this clause because it sounds soppy, you have skipped the clause the organization chose to stand on. Soppy sentences are often the load-bearing ones.
