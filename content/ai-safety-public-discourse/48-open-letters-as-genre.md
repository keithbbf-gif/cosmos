# Open letters as a public genre

Several objects in this set are letters with signature counters: the 2015 research-priorities letter, the Asilomar principles page, the 2023 pause letter, the CAIS sentence. Treating them as if they were all "the community speaking" is the main genre error.

## What an open letter is good at

It is good at **date-stamping a claim**. 22 March 2023 is now a fact in the timeline because a URL and a press cycle made it one.

It is good at **assembling faces**. A journalist can write "signed by X" faster than "a working paper argues."

It is good at **forcing a yes-or-no** on a mild or sharp sentence. The ninety-percent Asilomar filter and the one-line CAIS statement are opposite techniques toward the same end: reduce the ask until a name can sit next to it.

## What an open letter cannot do

It cannot show that non-signers disagree; they may not have seen it, or may dislike the organizer.

It cannot bind a signer's employer. People sign as individuals more often than press implies.

It cannot replace a statute, a standard, or a paper with a method and a result.

It cannot keep its signatory count still. Always date the snapshot.

## A small typology from this set's own pile

- **Agenda letter** (2015 priorities): please fund this research topic.
- **Principles letter** (2017): please initial this list.
- **Action letter** (2023 pause): please stop this class of run for a stated time.
- **Ranking letter** (2023 CAIS): please put this risk on the top shelf.

If you name the type first, you will not judge an agenda letter for failing to be a statute.

## Signature theater

A letter's press cycle often spends more inches on *who signed* than on *what the sentences do*. That is theater, and theater is part of discourse. A famous name can make a mild sentence look like a turning point. An unexpected refusal to sign can make a sharp sentence look like a factional test.

The educational counter-move is boring: print the letter without the names, ask what type it is (agenda, principles, action, ranking), then reveal the names. If the type-assessment changes when the names appear, the room has just watched theater work.

Counts move. FLI's principles page has shown different totals in different years. Always write the date next to the integer. An undated "thousands of researchers" is a mood, not a citation.

## Classroom hygiene

When assigning a letter, assign the organizer's about-page in the same packet. FLI is not CAIS. CAIS is not FHI. Students should be able to say who paid for the domain name, at the level the organization itself discloses. Deeper funding forensics are a different project and, past a point, not novelty-safe recap.

Then ask: what would falsify the letter's implicit claim to represent "AI researchers"? The question is enough. You do not need a new survey.
