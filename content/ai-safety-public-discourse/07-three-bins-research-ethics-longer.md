# Research, ethics, longer-term: the list's own map

The Asilomar page does a reader a favor that later commentary often undoes. It splits the twenty-three principles into three headings. The split is an argument about time and audience. Ignore the headings and the list becomes a smoothie called "AI ethics." Keep the headings and you can see which public conversations were being stapled together in 2017.

## Bin one is a labor conversation

Principles 1–5 talk the way a research field talks to its funders and to itself. What is the goal of the work? What travels with the money? How do you speak to government without becoming a lobbyist cartoon? How do you keep a culture from turning into a race?

This bin would be intelligible to someone who had never heard the word *superintelligence*. It would also be intelligible to someone who had lived through software-security races or pharmaceutical races. "Race avoidance" is the most operational sentence in the bin, and even it is a norm, not a schedule.

If you want to compare Asilomar to later lab charters, start here. Charter-era OpenAI language about not wanting a late-stage race without safety precautions is in the same family as Principle 5, with more corporate first-person.

## Bin two is a coalition

Principles 6–18 are where the list behaves like a coalition document. Product safety (6, 7) sits beside judicial process (8), designer responsibility (9), alignment (10), dignity and diversity (11), privacy and liberty (12, 13), distribution of benefits and prosperity (14, 15), human control (16), civic non-subversion (17), and weapons (18).

That is a lot of politics for one heading. It is also how you get ninety percent. Each constituency can find a line that sounds like home. A privacy advocate can live with 12–13. A military-ethics reader goes to 18. A philosopher of agency goes to 16. A long-term alignment researcher hears 10 and stays in the room.

The cost is conceptual. "Ethics and Values" becomes a drawer. Drawers hide joints. The joint between *this system's logged failure* and *this system's compatibility with cultural diversity* is not a small joint. The list does not theorize it. It inventories it.

## Bin three changes the subject on purpose

Principles 19–23 are the reason some later writers treat Asilomar as an existential-risk document, and the reason other writers treat those five lines as a science-fiction annex. The heading itself — "Longer-term Issues" — is a time claim. It says: the first eighteen are not the whole file.

Principle 19 is the most underrated. "There being no consensus, we should avoid strong assumptions regarding upper limits on future AI capabilities." That is a truce sentence. It does not say capabilities will explode. It says do not bet the planning process on a ceiling. Skeptics and believers can both initial it, for different reasons.

Principle 21 names "catastrophic or existential risks" in plain text. That phrase is the bridge to FHI-adjacent vocabulary. Principle 22 names recursive self-improvement and self-replication. Principle 23 names superintelligence and forbids (as a should, not as a statute) national or organizational monopoly over it.

## Teaching the map

A good classroom use is to ask which bin a later document lives in.

- The 2019 OECD AI Principles live mostly in bin two, with a growth-and-well-being accent.
- The EU AI Act's operative articles live in bin two's product-and-rights neighborhood, with extra machinery.
- *Superintelligence* as a public book lives in bin three and then walks backward into research culture.
- A 2023 "pause" letter tries to turn bin-one race avoidance into a time-limited action.

The map does not rank those documents. It stops you from quoting Principle 23 as if it were the whole of Asilomar, or quoting Principle 12 as if the longer-term bin were a rumor.
