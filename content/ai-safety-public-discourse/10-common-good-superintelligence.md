# Principle 23: the common-good sentence

The last Asilomar principle is the one a skeptical reader remembers, and the one a legislative drafter usually leaves behind. It says superintelligence should only be developed in the service of widely shared ethical ideals, and for the benefit of all humanity rather than one state or organization.

That is a complete political program in one breath. It assumes a thing called superintelligence can be "developed." It assumes "widely shared ethical ideals" can be named. It assumes a distributional rule: not one state, not one organization. And it uses *should*, the verb of principles, not *shall*, the verb of regulations.

## Why it is last

Lists end on the widest claim. Principle 23 is the widest. It also completes a climb: from research culture (1–5), through present ethics (6–18), through caution about unknown ceilings (19) and existential language (21), to a noun that 2014 had already made famous in trade bookstores.

If you read only Principle 23, Asilomar looks like a Bostrom meeting with extra privacy clauses. If you read only Principles 12 and 8, Asilomar looks like a European ethics checklist with a science-fiction coda. The page wants both readers to stay. The last line is how the second reader is asked not to walk out.

## What the sentence shares with other 2015–2018 public texts

"Benefit all of humanity" is not unique here. OpenAI's founding and charter language uses the same beneficiary. UNESCO's later 2021 recommendation uses a human-rights and human-dignity frame for all member states. The UN system has been saying "all humanity" about nuclear and climate files for decades.

The distinctive Asilomar piece is the **anti-monopoly clause** aimed at both states and organizations, attached specifically to superintelligence. That is a sharper object than "AI should be nice." It is also unenforceable as written. There is no Asilomar court that can dissolve a monopoly over a hypothetical system.

## How later public law did and did not echo it

The EU AI Act regulates AI systems and general-purpose models as products and practices on a market. It does not have a superintelligence article. It does have concentration-adjacent worries in the GPAI and systemic-risk conversation, but those are about model capability, compute, and downstream harm, not about a single organization owning the future.

Summit declarations after 2023 sometimes speak of frontier systems and catastrophic risk. They still do not enact Principle 23. They coordinate research and ask for safety testing. The common-good sentence remains, as of the public record this set can see, a **signed norm**, not a binding rule.

## Reading it without sneering or kneeling

Two unhelpful postures: treat Principle 23 as obvious moral truth, or treat it as a punch line. The educational posture is drier. In 2017, a high-support filter in a selected room kept a sentence that (a) named superintelligence, (b) universalized the beneficiary, and (c) rejected exclusive national or corporate title. That tells you what that room was willing to put on a page that would be passed to journalists.

It does not tell you whether superintelligence is a coherent technical target. Principle 19 already admitted there was no consensus on ceilings. Principle 23 lives next to that admission. The list is more interesting when you let those two lines irritate each other.
