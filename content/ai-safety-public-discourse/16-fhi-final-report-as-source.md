# The FHI final report as a primary source

When an institute dies, it sometimes leaves a document that wants to be the last word. FHI's *Future of Humanity Institute 2005–2024: final report* — deposited in Oxford's research archive and circulated from the institute's sites — is that document. Anders Sandberg is listed as author on the ORA record. The report calls itself, in the archive abstract, an oral history and research summary.

Those genre labels are a gift. Oral history is interested. Research summary is interested. A reader who treats the PDF as a court finding has already slipped.

## What to use it for

**Use it for names, dates, and the institute's own periodization.** Founding in 2005 on a three-year clock, the mix of disciplines, the story of later administrative freezes — these are the institute speaking about itself with time to write.

**Use it for a map of outputs.** A final report is where a group lists papers, themes, and students it wants remembered. That list is a bibliography with a spotlight. Compare it, if you are doing real history, to the library record. This staged set only needs the existence of the spotlight.

**Use it for tone.** How a closing institution narrates its enemies (bureaucracy, freezes, non-renewal) is part of discourse. The tone here is wounded-professional, not apocalyptic. That matters because outsiders often wrote about FHI in apocalyptic fonts.

## What not to use it for

Do not use it as a complete account of criticisms. Final reports are bad at steelmanning the dean.

Do not use it as proof that every affiliated idea was intellectually fruitful. Lists of projects are not replication studies.

Do not use it as a novelty claim of your own. Quoting a final report is recap. Building a new theory of institutes on one PDF would be a different, later, non-staged project — and even then, one archive document is a thin beam.

## A method for any closing file

The FHI report is a chance to practice a general rule this set will reuse on the OpenAI Charter and on COM(2021) 206:

1. Identify the **speaker** (the institute, the company, the Commission).
2. Identify the **occasion** (closure, launch, legislative proposal).
3. Separate **facts that can be checked elsewhere** from **moral adjectives**.
4. File the adjectives as public self-presentation.

If you can do that on a farewell PDF, you can do it on a triumphant blog post. Farewells are easier practice because the incentives are obvious. Triumphs hide the same incentives under better lighting.

## Placement in this series

Read draft 11 (what FHI was), draft 12 (the 2014 book as public object), draft 15 (the closing date in the press), then this one. The report is the institute's last long exhale. After it, the letterhead stops. The phrases it helped popularize do not.
