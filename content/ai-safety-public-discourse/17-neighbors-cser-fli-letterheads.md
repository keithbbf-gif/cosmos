# Neighbors with their own letterheads

A sloppy timeline says "Oxford and the community" and then lumps everyone who ever said *existential* into one noun. A slightly less sloppy timeline at least keeps **letterheads** separate. This draft is a seating chart, not a family tree with proven DNA tests.

## FLI is not FHI

The Future of Life Institute is a U.S.-based nonprofit, visible in the public record as the organizer of the 2015 Puerto Rico meeting, the 2017 Asilomar meeting, the principles letter, and later letters (including the March 2023 pause letter). Max Tegmark's public role is part of that story. FLI's website is the home of the twenty-three principles.

FHI was an Oxford university institute. Different country, different legal form, different accountability path, different closing date. They shared conferences, citations, and some funders in the public telling. Sharing a conference is not a merger.

If you remember only one pairing in this whole set, remember this one. The names are too close. The closeness has confused undergraduates and headline writers for a decade.

## CSER sits on another English faculty map

The Centre for the Study of Existential Risk at Cambridge is another university center, publicly launched in the mid-2010s, with its own directors, working papers, and media hits. Huw Price, Martin Rees, and Jaan Tallinn appear in the founding public story. CSER's bibliography has included AI, biosecurity, and other global catastrophic risks. It is a neighbor. It is not FHI-in-Cambridge.

## Lab safety groups are not institutes

By the late 2010s and early 2020s, companies published safety blogs, "prepardness" pages, and evaluation notes. Those are **firm documents**. They belong in the charter-era and post-chatbot chapters, not in the university-institute chapter. A researcher can move from FHI to a lab. The PDF changes letterhead when they do.

## Why the seating chart is educational

Because influence arguments go bad when names collapse. "FLI said" is a claim about a nonprofit letter. "FHI said" is a claim about an Oxford group. "Asilomar said" is a claim about a surveyed list. "Bostrom said" is a claim about an author. You can trace overlaps after you can tell the objects apart.

The chart also protects novelty-safety. This set does not owe you a secret history of funding flows. Some donation stories are public (the widely reported 2015 Musk gift toward FHI-adjacent AI work is one). Most bank details are not. Stay on the public side of the glass.

## A reader's annotation habit

When a secondary article says "AI safety researchers," pencil in a margin question: *which payroll?* University, nonprofit, company, government office, or freelance. The answer will not settle who is right. It will stop the sentence from pretending the field is a single choir.
