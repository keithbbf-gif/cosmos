# Steam, Beech, and Chair No. 14

The MAK in Vienna holds an early Sessel Modell Nr. 14 as H 2762: designed by Gebrüder Thonet, Vienna, 1859; this example perhaps executed by Josef Neyger about 1863; beech, partly laminated and bent, brown-stained; 93.5 by 39.5 by 49 cm, seat height 47 cm; bought in 1984. The museum’s license on the file is mixed (CC BY-NC-ND and a PD mark — check before reuse). The type was put into production in 1859. It is the chair of the café, the chair that unscrews into a few pieces, the chair whose numbers — tens of millions over a long life — are repeated in every history and still worth repeating if the source is a factory history and not a slogan.

Michael Thonet (1796–1871) was a joiner from Boppard. In the 1830s he was already bending wood. A Koblenz fair in 1841 brought him to Metternich’s notice; he moved to Vienna. An 1842 privilege allowed him to bend wood “by chemical and mechanical means.” The 1856 patent (the one the Powerhouse Museum and the MAK both cite) covers chairs and table legs bent by steam or boiling liquids. Solid rods of beech, not the earlier laminated experiments only: that is the industrial sentence. The factories that followed, especially in the beech forests of Moravia (Koryčany, later Bystřice pod Hostýnem and others), are the reason the chair could be a world object.

## What No. 14 is

A continuous bent back-and-rear-leg member, a front-leg-and-seat-rail logic, a round cane seat, screws instead of a cabinetmaker’s glue-only carcase, a weight a waiter can lift. The chair ships knocked-down. It is repaired with a screwdriver. Adolf Loos, later, would put Thonet chairs in interiors that refused other ornament; the chair was already, in the 1870s, a default. The MAK’s *Bentwood and Beyond* exhibition (2020, guest curator Wolfgang Thillmann) set No. 14 next to tubular steel and plastic to make the obvious point: this is the ancestor of the cheap, strong, repeatable seat.

Competitors — J. & J. Kohn especially — hired Josef Hoffmann and others around 1900 and sometimes out-designed Thonet at the luxury end (Hoffmann’s Purkersdorf chair, the “Sitting Machine”). The bentwood idea was not a trademark that could be held forever. The 1856 patent expired. The type remained.

## Industry without a myth of the lone inventor

Thonet’s sons (the Gebrüder from 1853) and the forest factories matter more than a eureka moment. Beech is a coppice-and-steam wood. Workers — including, in the Moravian plants, a labor history that is not all Viennese design — cut, steamed, bent, stained, caned. A furniture series that only loves the silhouette will skip the factory. Christopher Wilk’s *Thonet* (1980) and the MAK’s own furniture collection texts are the start. Factory catalogs (the famous lithographed books) are primary sources.

## From Boppard to a privilege

Michael Thonet (1796–1871) was a joiner in Boppard on the Rhine. In the 1830s he was already bending wood in laminated experiments — thin strips glued and formed, closer to a carriage-maker’s trick than to a café chair. It is a legal sentence about process, not a eureka about a silhouette.

## After the patent, after the war

The company splits, reforms, survives wars, licenses, and nostalgia. Marcel Breuer’s tubular steel (chapter 37) is a material translation of the same problem: a light chair for a new room, a joint that is a bend. Breuer knew Thonet. Thonet later made some of the steel. The family resemblance is a matter of record, not of metaphor. A 1987 Polish No. 14 in Sydney is not a revival in the Chippendale sense. It is the type continuing because the screws still work and the beech still bends.

What to take forward is small. A privilege in 1842, a patent in 1856, a model in 1859, an early chair in Vienna numbered H 2762, a forest in Moravia, a café in which strangers share a seat. The Victorian parlor of the next chapter will stuff and spring a different body. No. 14 has already left that parlor. It unscrews. It travels as a bundle. It lives as a café.


## A crate, a fair, two coffeehouses

The Crystal Palace of 1851 showed Thonet’s bentwood to a public that was learning to read furniture as industry. Koryčany (Koritschan) opened in 1857; Bystřice followed. Factory literature claimed that a cubic meter of crate could hold three dozen dismantled No. 14s. Treat the number as advertising until a shipping book confirms it. The claim’s job was to say what the chair is: a knockdown object for a café in Buenos Aires or Odessa.

Vienna’s Central and Griensteidl made the silhouette famous. Moravia made it possible. Identifying a “real Thonet” became a hardware-and-stamp sport after the 1856 patent expired. The more interesting fact is that sitting in public space became globally similar, cheaply.

## Jigs, cane, and a number that expects a reorder

A No. 14 is not a unique carving. It is a set of jigs. Beech rods go into steam or boiling liquid, then into iron forms that remember the loop of the back-and-rear-leg member. The front legs and the seat rail are a second logic. Cane is woven into a round seat that can be recaned when a café wrecks it. Screws hold what a village joiner would have glued. The chair that leaves Moravia as a bundle can be assembled by a waiter who has never seen Boppard.

Wilk’s 1980 monograph and the lithographed factory catalogs are the sources that keep this from becoming a design-myth. Model numbers accumulate. Competitors copy. Kohn’s Hoffmann chairs around 1900 prove that bentwood could be architecture; they do not retire No. 14. The MAK’s H 2762, perhaps Neyger’s about 1863, is early enough to show the type before nostalgia. Seat height 47 cm: café height, not a parlor’s sink into springs. The next chapter’s Victorian room will stuff a different body. This chair has already left that room. It unscrews. It travels. It is repaired with a screwdriver. That is the industrial sentence the 1856 patent was trying to protect, and that the expired patent could not keep as a monopoly.

## Cane, women, and a competitor’s machine

Caning the round seat was piecework. Factory histories and later labor notes put women and girls at the cane more often than at the steam chest; the jig and the stain were other stations. `[CITE NEEDED: a Moravian factory payroll or Wilk 1980 page that names caning as women’s work.]` Until that page is on the table, the honest sentence is only that a No. 14 is several trades bolted together: bender, caner, packer, waiter.

Josef Hoffmann’s so-called Sitting Machine, made by J. & J. Kohn about 1905, is the competitor’s argument: adjustable, geometric, still bentwood, an architect’s chair for a sanatorium (Purkersdorf) rather than a café. The MAK and other collections hold the type. It did not replace No. 14. It proved the patent’s expiration had opened a luxury end. Kohn’s catalogs numbered their chairs too. The number is the design.

No. 18, the other café workhorse, uses a similar logic with a different back. Shopmen argued the models; sitters did not. What traveled in the crate was a public posture. What remains in H 2762 is an early witness that a screwdriver could keep that posture in repair.
