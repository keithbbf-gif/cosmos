# Vienna, Glasgow, and the New Art

Charles Rennie Mackintosh’s high-backed chairs for the Ingram Street and Willow Tea Rooms (Glasgow, from 1900) are not comfortable in the Victorian sense. They are screens for a sitter. The back is a wall of lattice or a tall rectangle that makes a booth in a public room. The Willow’s white and silver, the dark oak of other suites, the Glasgow School of Art’s library furniture: a local geometry that Europe filed under Art Nouveau and that Mackintosh’s own circle would have called something more structural.

In Vienna, the Secession and then the Wiener Werkstätte (Josef Hoffmann, Koloman Moser, 1903) treated furniture as part of a total interior. Hoffmann’s bentwood for Kohn (chapter 33) and his painted, gridded chairs for Palais Stoclet sit at two budgets. Adolf Loos, who disliked the Werkstätte’s total design, still used Thonet. The argument is Viennese and it is about whether a chair should belong to a house’s manifesto.

## Brussels, Paris, Nancy

Victor Horta’s houses (Hôtel Tassel, 1893) put the iron line into a stair, then into a chair. Henry van de Velde wrote and designed as if furniture were an ethical line. In Paris, Siegfried Bing’s Maison de l’Art Nouveau (1895) gave the movement its shop name. Guimard’s Métro is architecture; his furniture is rarer. In Nancy, Émile Gallé and Louis Majorelle made a provincial capital into a marquetry city: watery woods, plants that are not Chippendale’s Chinese plants, a luxury that Arts and Crafts would have called dishonest and that is, in fact, extremely skilled. The Musée de l’École de Nancy is the place to see the chairs without a Paris filter.

Barcelona: Gaudí’s Casa Calvet and Batlló furniture, the undulating wood that follows his buildings. A Gaudí chair is a building sample. It does not found a factory.

## A booth in a tea room

Charles Rennie Mackintosh (1868–1928) designed furniture for Catherine Cranston’s Glasgow tea rooms: Buchanan Street, Argyle Street, Ingram Street, and the Willow on Sauchiehall Street, opened 1903. Glasgow Museums hold the largest public group of that interior world — panelling, light fittings, tables, chairs, racks, tableware — some 3,500 objects across the Cranston commissions. Roger Billcliffe’s *Charles Rennie Mackintosh: The Complete Furniture* is the catalogue that a caption should answer to.

The high-backed chairs for Ingram Street and the Willow are not comfortable in the Victorian sense of chapter 34. They are screens. A lattice or a tall rectangle makes a booth in a public room. LACMA’s ladderback from the Willow (M.2011.128.7, gift of Max Palevsky) is oak and horsehair, 41⅛ in. high. The Saint Louis Art Museum’s dining chair (474:1979) is ebonized oak and rush, 41⅜ in., with a provenance that runs from the Willow (1903–20) through a private collection to Christie’s in 1979. The Glasgow School of Art’s armchair MC/F/45, used in the ground-floor saloons, the Gallery, and the Smoking Room, is the boxier cousin. Billcliffe called it sturdy and not particularly comfortable. The original upholstery on those and the Willow settle was a green-and-gold striped horsehair; GSA reupholstered MC/F/45 in blue horsehair in 1984. After the 2018 fire in the Mackintosh Building the chair was assessed again. A tea-room seat has a conservation life as well as a design life.

Margaret Macdonald Mackintosh’s gesso panel *O Ye, All Ye that Walk in Willowwood* (Glasgow Museums E.2001.6, 1903) was the Salon de Luxe’s focus: pencil and oil-painted gesso, glass beads, a Rossetti sonnet made into white faces. Frances Macdonald and the Glasgow circle are in the archives more than in the old monographs. Name them when the chair or the panel is theirs or jointly theirs. The “Glasgow Style” was a group, not a signature.

## Hoffmann’s two budgets

The Vienna Secession (1897) and then the Wiener Werkstätte, founded by Josef Hoffmann and Koloman Moser in 1903 with Fritz Waerndorfer’s money, treated furniture as a paragraph in a total interior. Hoffmann’s bentwood for J. & J. Kohn — the Purkersdorf chair, the Sitting Machine — sits at the industrial budget already met in chapter 33. His painted, gridded chairs for Palais Stoclet, Brussels (1905–11), sit at the other. The house is still private. Photographs and the MAK’s Werkstätte holdings are how a reader sees the furniture without a knock at the door.

Adolf Loos disliked the Werkstätte’s total design and still used Thonet. The argument is Viennese and it is about whether a chair should belong to a house’s manifesto. Loos’s interiors — marble, mirror, a café seat — refuse the manifesto chair. They do not refuse sitting. A plain bentwood in a rich room is a position. It is also a purchase order to a factory that already existed.

## Nancy without a Paris filter

Siegfried Bing opened the Maison de l’Art Nouveau in Paris in 1895 and gave the movement its shop name. Victor Horta’s Hôtel Tassel, Brussels, 1893, put the iron line into a stair before it reached a chair. Henry van de Velde wrote and designed as if furniture were an ethical line. Hector Guimard’s Métro is architecture; his furniture is rarer and should be captioned as such.

Nancy is the provincial capital that makes the luxury argument without a Paris filter. Émile Gallé and Louis Majorelle built a marquetry city: watery woods, plants that are not Chippendale’s Chinese plants, a skill Arts and Crafts would have called dishonest and that is, in fact, extremely skilled. The Musée de l’École de Nancy holds the chairs and desks. Debora Silverman’s *Art Nouveau in Fin-de-Siècle France* (1989) is the book that keeps the politics of the line in view — nature, science, the Third Republic — when a caption only wants a stem.

A Majorelle desk is finished. It does not need a later modernist to forgive the veneer. The veneer is the point: a picture in wood that a Cotswold chamfer would have refused. Both are furniture histories. This chapter’s job is not to award honesty. It is to say what the line is doing.

## Gaudí as a building sample

Antoni Gaudí’s furniture for Casa Calvet and Casa Batlló, Barcelona, is undulating wood that follows the buildings. A Gaudí chair is a building sample you can move. It does not found a factory. Barcelona museums hold documented pieces; the houses themselves, now visited to exhaustion, still show how a seat belongs to a stair and a door. Do not file Gaudí under “Art Nouveau” as if the term explained the carpentry. The carpentry is local, structural, and odd. Billcliffe’s discipline — one object, one commission, one date — is the model even when the city is Barcelona rather than Glasgow.

A manifesto house and a tea-room booth are enough argument for one decade.


## Cranston’s rooms and a library of timber

Miss Cranston’s tea rooms were a business. Good food, good service, and a designed interior that made a customer stay. Mackintosh’s greatest patron was not a museum. She was a Glasgow entrepreneur who also hired George Walton and Jessie M. King. The Ingram Street Oak Room, reconstructed in Glasgow’s holdings and discussed in V&A tea-room literature, is the public afterlife of a room that sold tea. Furniture historians who treat the high-back chair as a sculpture miss the saucer.

The Glasgow School of Art’s library furniture — the timber cage that burned in 2014 and again, as a reconstruction in progress, in 2018 — is the other Mackintosh interior a reader cannot ignore. The chairs and tables there were for students, not for a Salon de Luxe. Billcliffe’s catalogue separates the commissions. So should this chapter. A tea-room booth and a library table share a geometry. They do not share a budget or a sitter.

Hoffmann’s Sitzmaschine for J. & J. Kohn, the “Sitting Machine,” is the Viennese object that makes the same point from the other direction: a bentwood chair that announces itself as a mechanism. The MAK has held the type next to Thonet. Loos would have granted the factory and refused the name. Both responses are period.

## Van de Velde, Guimard, and the ethical line

Henry van de Velde’s essays treat the line as an ethic. His furniture — Bloemenwerf and later Weimar work — is easier to over-read as a prelude to the Bauhaus than to see as Belgian Art Nouveau with a teacher’s voice. He did teach in Weimar. That is not the same as designing the Red Blue Chair. Keep him in Brussels and in his own chairs first.

Guimard’s Métro entrances taught the world a cast-iron plant. His furniture, when it survives with a document, should be captioned as rare. Horta’s stair at Hôtel Tassel is the better public image for the line that hits architecture before it hits the seat. A reader can walk that stair. The chair that followed it is the private cousin.

Silverman’s book remains the political frame for the French material: nature, science, the Third Republic, a luxury that thought it was modern. Nancy’s École is the place that luxury looks least like a Paris import. Gallé’s plant forms in veneer are botany as a trade skill. Majorelle’s desks are the same skill at furniture scale. Arts and Crafts would have called the veneer a lie. The lie, if it is one, is extremely well made.
