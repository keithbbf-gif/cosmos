# The Stool That Must Not Be Sat Upon

The Golden Stool of the Asante, the *Sika Dwa Kofi* (“Golden Stool born on Friday”), is not in the British Museum. It is not in the Metropolitan. It remains with the Asante nation in Ghana. Tradition places its descent from the sky in the time of Osei Tutu and the priest Anokye, at the end of the seventeenth century, as the object that holds the soul of the people. No one sits on it. It sits on its own stool. When the British, in 1900, tried to take it — Governor Hodgson’s demand that he be allowed to sit on it is the insult at the center of the story — Yaa Asantewaa raised a war. The stool stayed hidden. In the 1920s it came back into ceremonial life under an agreement that it would not be seized. Peri Klemm and others have told this in public lectures from objects that *are* in museums, because the stool itself is not available to be a gallery prop.

This chapter is about Akan stools as furniture and as political persons, and about the wider West African habit of the carved stool as a seat of office. It is not a tour of “African chairs” as a design category. It is not a place to photograph the Golden Stool. It is a place to say why a stool can be more than a seat, and why museum stools need captions that include how they left.

## Sese dwa: the everyday and the plated

Peter Sarpong, *The Sacred Stools of the Akan* (Accra-Tema, 1971), and later work by Doran Ross and Herbert Cole (*The Arts of Ghana*, 1977) describe a world in which people receive stools at coming of age, in which the form is carved from a single block (often *sese* wood), and in which the underside may carry concentric squares around a hollow core. A Harvard thesis by a student of the type (the 2010s *Asante Stools and the Matrilineage* dissertation available through DASH) uses British Museum Af 1954,23.3215 as a case: a conventional *sese dwa* in the *mmaa dwa* design, smoked color, metal plating as a status mark.

The Met’s Prestige Stool (1979.206.263), Asante, 19th–20th century, wood and metal, 14⅝ by 21 by 9¾ inches, is a museum stool: Rockefeller provenance, a label that says “prestige.” It can teach the crescent seat, the carved middle, the metal strips. It cannot teach the Golden Stool. Linguist staffs (*okyeame poma*) often carry a stool as a finial — Met 1987.452.2a–c is one — so that the idea of the seat walks in front of the speaker.

Carving a stool from one block is a different craft from framing a chair. The middle stretch — the *adwa*’s carved support — is where proverbs live: a two-headed crocodile, a chain, a geometric stand-in for a saying. To read the middle is to read a language. Cole and Ross remain the cautious iconographers.

## Other seats, other kingdoms

Dahomey, Yoruba, Bamileke, the Chokwe chief’s chair that uses a European frame and an African program of carving: West and Central Africa are not one stool. The Bamileke and other Grassfields seats, the Akan stool, the Ethiopian throne, the folding stools of the Nilotic and Ethiopian highlands (some with ancient-looking animal forms) — each is a chapter someone should write. This essay keeps the Asante object in focus because the documentary and the political record are unusually thick, and because the British Museum’s Asante gold regalia page now states, in public, how much of the gold in London arrived through war (1874 and after).

A furniture series that includes Versailles and skips Kumasi is a series about European sitting. Including Kumasi does not require treating the Golden Stool as a “design icon.” It requires treating it as a person-object that refused the category.

## How objects leave

Stools in European and American museums came through trade, mission, colonial seizure, and later art markets. Captions should not say “collected 1896” as if the year were a style. The British Museum’s own contested-objects page on Asante gold is a model of institutional unease; furniture captions can borrow the unease. The Met’s Rockefeller stools have their own path through mid-century “primitive art.” Say it.

Reproduction stools — tourist carvings, state gifts, studio copies — are part of the twentieth century. They are not fake in the sense of a forged Chippendale; they are a living type. Distinguish a stool still in ritual use, a stool decommissioned and sold, and a stool made for sale.
