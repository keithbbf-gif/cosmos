# The Stool That Must Not Be Sat Upon

The Golden Stool of the Asante, the *Sika Dwa Kofi* (“Golden Stool born on Friday”), is not in the British Museum. It is not in the Metropolitan. It remains with the Asante nation in Ghana. Tradition places its descent from the sky in the time of Osei Tutu and the priest Anokye, at the end of the seventeenth century, as the object that holds the soul of the people. No one sits on it. It sits on its own stool. When the British, in 1900, tried to take it — Governor Hodgson’s demand that he be allowed to sit on it is the insult at the center of the story — Yaa Asantewaa raised a war. The stool stayed hidden. In the 1920s it came back into ceremonial life under an agreement that it would not be seized. Peri Klemm and others have told this in public lectures from objects that *are* in museums, because the stool itself is not available to be a gallery prop.

This chapter is about Akan stools as furniture and as political persons, and about the wider West African habit of the carved stool as a seat of office. It is not a tour of “African chairs” as a design category. It is not a place to photograph the Golden Stool. It is a place to say why a stool can be more than a seat, and why museum stools need captions that include how they left.

## Sese dwa: the everyday and the plated

Peter Sarpong, *The Sacred Stools of the Akan* (Accra-Tema, 1971), and later work by Doran Ross and Herbert Cole (*The Arts of Ghana*, 1977) describe a world in which people receive stools at coming of age, in which the form is carved from a single block (often *sese* or *osese* wood), and in which the underside may carry concentric squares around a hollow core. Sarpong wrote as Archbishop of Kumasi and as a person inside the stool’s language. That is a different authority from a field collector’s. A Harvard thesis on Asante stools and the matrilineage (available through DASH) uses British Museum Af1954,23.3215 as a case: a conventional *sese dwa* in the *mmaa dwa* design, smoked color, metal plating as a status mark.

Glasgow Museums hold a *mmaa gwa* (woman’s stool) carved from *asese* (*Funtumia africana*), five supports, a slightly rounded central post (*sekyedua*) pierced with a checkerboard (*dame dame*), saw-toothed projections (*nkye kyewa*) on the outer supports. The museum’s record says it was looted from the Asante royal palace in Kumasi on 4 February 1874 and later bought at auction at Cape Coast by General Sir Archibald Alison. The incised base, the record argues, marks a queen mother’s stool; the palace find-spot makes Afua Kobi I (Asantehemaa, 1857–1884) the likely owner. That is a museum doing the work this chapter asked for: name the wood, name the type, name the war, name the woman if the evidence will bear it. The 1874 campaign — Wolseley’s sack of Kumasi, sometimes called the Sagrenti War — is the military path a stool can take to a Scottish gallery.

The Met’s Prestige Stool (1979.206.263), Asante, 19th–20th century, wood and metal, 14⅝ by 21 by 9¾ inches, is a museum stool: Michael C. Rockefeller Memorial Collection, bequest of Nelson A. Rockefeller, 1979, a label that says “prestige.” It can teach the crescent seat, the carved middle, the metal strips. It cannot teach the Golden Stool. The Rockefeller path is mid-century “primitive art,” a New York category that paid for the object and flattened the stool house into a vitrine. Say it.

Linguist staffs (*okyeame poma*) often carry a stool as a finial — Met 1987.452.2a–c is one — so that the idea of the seat walks in front of the speaker. Wood and gold foil, 63⅞ inches high, gift of Herbert F. and Teruko S. Neuwalder, 1987, dated by the museum to the 1930s. The finial combines a stool, a chain, and crossed swords. The Met’s longer note on the type is the right political grammar: regalia is stool property (*agyapadie*), stored in royal warehouses, granted and revoked. The *okyeame* speaks for the chief. The staff is the office. A stool on the staff is the office quoting the seat.

Carving a stool from one block is a different craft from framing a chair. The middle stretch — the *adwa*’s carved support — is where proverbs live: a two-headed crocodile, a chain, a geometric stand-in for a saying. To read the middle is to read a language. Cole and Ross remain the cautious iconographers. A tourist copy can carry the same crocodile without carrying the office.

## How a block becomes a seat

*Osese* or *asese* is the usual stool wood in the Akan literature: a pale, even timber, often *Funtumia* (Glasgow specifies *F. africana*; other labels say *F. elastica*). The carver works a single block. The crescent seat is scooped; the base is a rectangle or a stepped rectangle; the middle is opened into posts and a central pillar. The underside is not neglected. Concentric squares around a hollow are a common finish; they are the part that sits on the ground, and they have their own drawing. Smoke darkens some stools. Metal strips — brass, silver, later other alloys — plate a seat or a post as a rank mark. Gold leaf on the Golden Stool is a different order of plating, and not a model for photographing a museum piece as if it were that stool.

A personal stool is tied to a person. The National Museum of African Art’s 89-8-3, mid- to late nineteenth century, wood, 9 5/16 by 15½ by 8 inches, collected by Hugh Sinclair on Baden-Powell’s 1896–97 expedition, is described by the museum as probably a commoner’s everyday stool: curved rectangular seat, a cylinder on a two-step base. The label adds a use rule that Sarpong and others also record. Only the owner sat on it. When not in use it was tipped on its side. After death, a stool of a person of rank could be blackened and kept in a stool house as an ancestral seat, not a chair for the living. The Golden Stool is the state version of that idea, raised one register: not an ancestor’s seat, the nation’s. NMAFA 89-8-3 is a faint reminder, the museum says, and a collection made on a military expedition. Both facts belong in the caption.

## The 1900 insult and the stool that stayed

On 28 March 1900, in Kumasi, Sir Frederick Hodgson, governor of the Gold Coast, addressed assembled Asante leadership and demanded the Golden Stool. He wanted it brought to him. He wanted to sit on it. The demand treated a person-object as a chair and a conquest trophy. Yaa Asantewaa, queen mother of Ejisu, became the face of the war that followed: the War of the Golden Stool. The stool was hidden. It was not handed over. In the early 1920s, after damage while hidden (1921 is the year given in several public accounts) and a repair, it returned to ceremonial life under terms that it would not be seized. Smarthistory’s public essay on the *Sika Dwa Kofi* is a fair short narrative for a reader who will not see the object. Peri Klemm’s lectures do the same work from museum stools. This chapter will not invent a descent-from-the-sky scene beyond the tradition already stated. Osei Tutu and Okomfo Anokye remain the named figures of the foundation story. The foundation story is Asante political theology, not a European “legend” to be smiled at and set aside.

The British Museum’s own contested-objects page on Asante gold now states, in public, how much of the gold in London arrived through war (1874 and after). Furniture captions can borrow the unease. Gold weights, sword ornaments, and stool plaques traveled the same road as some stools. A series that includes Versailles and skips Kumasi is a series about European sitting. Including Kumasi does not require treating the Golden Stool as a “design icon.” It requires treating it as a person-object that refused the category.

## Other seats, other kingdoms

Dahomey, Yoruba, Bamileke, the Chokwe chief’s chair that uses a European frame and an African program of carving: West and Central Africa are not one stool. The Bamileke and other Grassfields seats, the Akan stool, the Ethiopian throne, the folding stools of the Nilotic and Ethiopian highlands (some with ancient-looking animal forms) — each is a chapter someone should write. This essay keeps the Asante object in focus because the documentary and the political record are unusually thick.

A linguist staff with Ananse on the finial — the Met’s other *okyeame poma*, the spider in the web, “no one goes to the house of the spider to teach him wisdom” — is speech furniture, not sitting furniture. It belongs here because the court that carved stools also carved the words that explain them. The first court linguist, in the tradition the Met repeats, was a woman who carried a cane because of her age. Staffs of office remember that cane. Stools remember a different body: the sitting that is ruling, and the sitting that is forbidden.

## Stool houses, blackened seats, and the proverb in the middle

A stool house is a room, not a metaphor. Ancestral stools, blackened in the rites Sarpong describes, are kept there as seats of the dead who still belong to the matrilineage. They are not sat upon by the living. They are the family version of the rule the Golden Stool states at the scale of a nation. A museum label that says only “ceremonial” flattens that room into a mood. Name the house if the documentation will bear it. If it will not, say that the blackened stool in a vitrine has already left its room.

The middle support is where a carver can set a proverb without writing a word. Two crocodiles sharing a stomach, a chain, a geometric stand-in Cole and Ross refuse to over-read: the cautious iconography is the one that admits a motif can be a saying, a clan mark, or a shop habit. Tourist stools repeat the popular crocodiles because visitors have learned to want them. A stool still in office may carry a quieter middle. Reading every museum stool as a full proverb is a collector’s convenience.

Gold weights and sword ornaments traveled with stools in 1874 and after. They are not furniture. They are the same seizure. The British Museum’s public unease about Asante gold is the institutional sentence a furniture caption can echo without repeating the whole gold catalog. A stool that left in the same crates is not purified by being wood.

Sarpong’s book remains the first text to hand a reader who wants the stool as a living office rather than a vitrine silhouette. Cole and Ross remain the first plates to hand a reader who wants the middle support read without a novel attached. Between them, a caption can name a type (*mmaa dwa*, *sese dwa*), a wood (*osese*), and a path (1874, 1896, 1979) without pretending the Golden Stool is in the room.

## How objects leave

Stools in European and American museums came through trade, mission, colonial seizure, and later art markets. Captions should not say “collected 1896” as if the year were a style. Glasgow’s 1874 looting date is the model. NMAFA’s Baden-Powell expedition date is the model. The Met’s Rockefeller bequest is a later, quieter path: the object had already become “art” in New York before 1979. The British Museum’s Af1954,23.3215 carries a 1954 registration that is not a carving date.

Reproduction stools — tourist carvings, state gifts, studio copies — are part of the twentieth century. They are not fake in the sense of a forged Chippendale; they are a living type. Distinguish a stool still in ritual use, a stool decommissioned and sold, and a stool made for sale. A plated museum stool can be any of the three if the paperwork is thin. When the paperwork is thin, say so.

The Golden Stool remains in Ghana. That sentence is the residual fact. Met 1979.206.263 can teach a crescent and a strip of metal. It cannot sit in for a nation. A furniture series that wanted a photograph of the *Sika Dwa Kofi* would be asking for the same category mistake Hodgson made, with better lighting. The stools that can be photographed are the ones that left. Their captions owe the leaving.


A tourist stool can carry Cole and Ross’s crocodile and still not be a stool with an office. Caption the difference. The Golden Stool remains in Ghana; this series will not pretend a gallery piece is its stand-in.
