# The Oak Chest and the Timber Hall

The Metropolitan Museum’s oak chest with iron banding (13.205.2), made in Westphalia in the fifteenth century, is later than this chapter’s core dates and still the right object to start with. Iron straps with fleur-de-lis terminals belt a box whose job is older than Gothic: hold the household’s movable wealth, lock it, survive a cart and a damp floor. Penelope Eames’s *Medieval Furniture* (1977) is the monograph English readers still use. Charles Tracy’s work on choir furniture and the V&A’s medieval galleries fill the church side. The domestic side, for the early Middle Ages, is archaeology: waterlogged pits, burnt halls, the occasional lucky chest.

A medieval hall is not a dining room with a suite. It is a timber volume with a hearth, benches, boards on trestles, and chests along the walls. The high table is a place, not a mahogany relic. When the board is taken down, the hall is a different room. Furniture is a kit that can be cleared for a court, a meal, or sleep.

## Chests before cupboards

The chest (*cista*, *hutch*, *ark*) is the medieval cupboard. It sits on the floor or on a low stand; it travels; it is sometimes a seat. Construction runs from dug-out logs (early, rural, still appearing in folk museums) to six-board chests nailed or pegged, to clamped and paneled chests that anticipate the later *cassone* and the framed cupboard. Iron is not decoration first. It is the thing that keeps the oak from bursting and the thief from lifting a board.

Met 13.205.2 keeps “decorative forms from the early gothic period” on a late gothic body, the museum says: a reminder that chests are conservative. A lock plate can be up to date; the box is a box. French and German chests in The Cloisters (16.32.20 is a sixteenth-century walnut example with iron mounts, already Renaissance in wood) show the type crossing the date line this series uses for chapter 26.

Viking and Insular chests belong also to chapter 14. The Oseberg burial packed chests and buckets; some of the metal-bound yew buckets are Insular imports. The chest as a seagoing and grave object is older than the guild chest in a parish church.

## Benches, boards, and the politics of the table

Everyone in the hall sits on a bench except the people who do not. A chair — turned, heavy, sometimes with a round seat and a triangular plan in later “thrown” chairs — is a marked seat. The word *chair* in English keeps the *cathedra*. A stool is the rest of the house. Trestle tables let a room change use. Fixed tables appear in some monastic refectories and later in halls that have given up the idea of a clear floor.

Hangings do more thermal work than any chair. A furniture history that ignores textiles in a medieval hall is a history of a cold box. The Bayeux Tapestry’s banquet scenes (Bishop Odo, the servants at the board) are cartoons of this kit: draped table, bowls, a service of people, not a sideboard.

## Church and house

Parish chests held charters and plate. They are among the best-dated wooden objects in England because the ironwork and the parish memory sometimes coincide. Monastic infirmaries and dormitories had beds that are almost entirely lost; the rule books describe them. Hospitality is a furniture demand: a guest bed, a spare chest. Eames is good on the documents — wills, inventories, accounts — that name objects the soil ate.

Turned chairs (“thrown” chairs) and the later box chair with a paneled back are the domestic *cathedra*. Surviving early examples are scarce and often over-restored. Trust a dendrochronology date more than a dealer’s “Norman chair.”

## Forests and the joiners’ names

Oak is the English and northern French sentence. Walnut appears in the south. Imported Baltic oak, in later medieval centuries, is a trade that the next chapters will need. The joiner and the carpenter are not yet the cabinetmaker. They are the people who also cut roofs. That overlap is why a hall’s furniture and a hall’s frame share a vocabulary of pegs and mortises.

Named makers are rare before the later choir-stall contracts (chapter 15). Anonymity is not a lack of skill. It is a lack of the later habit of signing a carcase.

## Inventories, wills, and the furniture that was counted

Eames built much of her book from documents: a will that leaves a “great chest,” an account that pays for a board and a pair of trestles, a royal household book that lists beds by chamber. These texts are biased toward people who had lawyers. They still beat a dealer’s “12th-century chair.” A named ark in a parish is sometimes still in the parish, ironwork and all, with a lock that has been picked and replaced.

Monastic customaries describe beds in the dormitory as a rule of length and of covering, not as design. The infirmary bed is a type we know from texts and almost never from wood. Hospitality — the guest who must be given a bed — is a furniture demand written into the rule. A monastery is a hotel with an office of prayer.

Viking-age and earlier halls in the north (the timber halls under later churches, the Yeavering-type sites) had benches as architecture: earth-fast or sill-beam seating along the walls. The high seat is a literary object (sagas, *Beowulf*’s mead-benches) and an archaeological scar. Chapter 14’s Oseberg beds are the portable version of that hall kit, packed for a grave.

By 1300 the cupboard that stands is beginning to appear in rich inventories (*dressoir*, aumbry). This chapter still belongs to the box with a lid. The standing cupboard’s victory is the next late-medieval and Renaissance sentence.

## What to carry forward

The chest is the protagonist. The chair is the exception. The table is a board on legs that can leave. Anyone who jumps from Herculaneum’s cupboards to a Renaissance *studiolo* without this chapter will not understand why a fifteenth-century room is still full of boxes. The cupboard that stands and the drawer that slides are later victories. The Middle Ages locked the lid.

## How a chest is actually built

Eames sorted medieval chests by construction, and later writers have sharpened the types. A dug-out is a log with a lid, the rural and early form that folk museums still show. A six-board chest is six planks — front, back, bottom, two ends, a lid — nailed or pegged, the ends often sitting in grooves, the whole liable to split if the iron straps do not hold it. A clamped chest (Eames’s “hutch”) is a different thought: wide stiles at the corners, the front and back boards housed in long mortises, the lid often pin-hinged. Pin hinges belong, in the older literature, to the thirteenth century; dendrochronology has confirmed and also stretched that habit.

Chris Pickvance’s work on English church chests is the recent, stubborn catalog. Four clamped chests at Westminster Abbey now have tree-ring dates: the Long Chest 1170–1202, the Hutch-type Chest 1237–1269, the Lesser Treaty Chest 1271–1287, the Deep Chest 1275–1290. A group of pin-hinged, clamped, arcaded Gothic chests in East Kent — St John’s Hospital at Canterbury, Graveney, and their cousins — are the earliest dendro-dated Gothic arcading on English clamped fronts. Pickvance argues for a Canterbury workshop with a degree of independence from the abbey’s plainer boxes. The Icklingham and Buxted chests have been called the oldest carved coffers in England; the dates have wandered from the late twelfth century to the late thirteenth as Nikolaus Pevsner, H. Munro Cautley, and the parish changed their minds. `[CITE NEEDED: the current dendro date, if any, for Buxted.]` What the argument shows is method. Ironwork style, stile width, and a romantic “Norman” label are not a date. A core is.

Parish chests held charters and plate because the parish was a legal body. They are among the best-surviving wooden objects in England for a dull reason: no one could throw away the box that held the deed. A cathedral fabric roll that pays for a lock and a pair of hinges is a furniture document even when the box is unnamed. Locks have been picked and replaced; straps have been added; the box is still a box. French and German chests follow the same construction families with different iron dialects. Met 13.205.2, the Westphalian banded chest that opened this chapter, is fifteenth-century and still wearing early Gothic fleur-de-lis terminals — conservative body, fashionable strap. The Cloisters’ 16.32.20, walnut with iron, is already on the far side of this chapter’s date line.

Oak is the English and northern French sentence. Walnut takes the south. Baltic oak — “wainscot” in the later accounts — arrives as a trade in the later Middle Ages and will matter to the stalls and the Renaissance carcase. The people who cut these boxes also cut roofs. A mortise in a hall beam and a mortise in a chest stile are the same thought at two scales. Dan Miles and Martin Bridge’s tree-ring lists, used by Pickvance and by cathedral fabric rolls, are now the dating tool a dealer’s “Norman” label cannot answer.

## The Coronation Chair and the named seat

If the chest is anonymous, the Coronation Chair at Westminster Abbey is the opposite. Edward I took the Stone of Scone from Scone Abbey in 1296 and ordered a chair to hold it. A bronze chair was priced and abandoned as too expensive. Walter of Durham, the king’s painter-carpenter, made an oak chair in the years around 1297–1301, gilded, punched with birds, foliage, and animals, a king painted on the back (Edward the Confessor or Edward I), his feet on a lion. The four gilt lions now under the chair are 1727 replacements of early sixteenth-century additions. The Stone sat enclosed under the seat until later damage opened the front. The Abbey calls it the oldest dated piece of English furniture by a known maker. It was not designed as a coronation seat; it became one, certainly by Henry IV in 1399, and it was used again in 2023. A furniture series that skipped it for being too famous would be shy of the wrong thing. It is a Gothic chair whose job is a stolen stone, made by a named man, in a year we can write.

Thrown chairs — pole-lathe, triangular plan, round seat — are the domestic *cathedra* of a later date than dealers like. Surviving “Norman” turned chairs are usually sixteenth- or seventeenth-century, or restorations. The box chair with a paneled back is the other marked seat. Dendrochronology before romance, again.

Halls that still show the volume: Penshurst Place, the great hall of the 1340s, a central hearth and a timber roof, furniture that has to work in a draft. Stokesay Castle’s hall, a late thirteenth-century timber volume with a later solar, still makes the kit obvious: benches can live along the walls; the board cannot hog the floor. Westminster Hall, which is a roof more than a room of furniture, is the extreme of the same thought. A Weald and Downland trestle reconstruction is useful only if the caption says reconstruction. The Bayeux Tapestry’s banquet (Odo at the board, servants, a draped table, no sideboard) is still the best cartoon of the kit. Trestles stack; the floor clears for a court or for sleep. Fixed tables in monastic refectories are the exception that proves the movable rule.

Eames’s sentences from wills — a “great chest,” a board and a pair of trestles, beds listed by chamber — name what the soil ate. Monastic customaries give the dormitory bed as a length and a covering. Yeavering-type timber halls in the north had benches as architecture, earth-fast seating along the walls. The high seat of the sagas is a literary object and an archaeological scar. Oseberg’s beds, in the next chapter, are that hall kit packed for a grave. By 1300 a standing cupboard (*aumbry*, *dressoir*) is beginning to appear in rich lists. Church aumbries built into walls are architecture; the wooden one that stands is furniture learning to leave the floor. This chapter still belongs to the lid. The Middle Ages locked it.
