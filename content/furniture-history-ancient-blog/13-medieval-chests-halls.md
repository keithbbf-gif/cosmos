# The Oak Chest and the Timber Hall

The Metropolitan Museum’s oak chest with iron banding (13.205.2), made in Westphalia in the fifteenth century, is later than this chapter’s core dates and still the right object to start with. Iron straps with fleur-de-lis terminals belt a box whose job is older than Gothic: hold the household’s movable wealth, lock it, survive a cart and a damp floor. Penelope Eames’s *Medieval Furniture* (1977) is the monograph English readers still use. Charles Tracy’s work on choir furniture and the V&A’s medieval galleries fill the church side. The domestic side, for the early Middle Ages, is archaeology: waterlogged pits, burnt halls, the occasional lucky chest.

A medieval hall is not a dining room with a suite. It is a timber volume with a hearth, benches, boards on trestles, and chests along the walls. The high table is a place, not a mahogany relic. When the board is taken down, the hall is a different room. Furniture is a kit that can be cleared for a court, a meal, or sleep.

## Chests before cupboards

The chest (*cista*, *hutch*, *ark*) is the medieval cupboard. It sits on the floor or on a low stand; it travels; it is sometimes a seat. Construction runs from dug-out logs (early, rural, still appearing in folk museums) to six-board chests nailed or pegged, to clamped and paneled chests that anticipate the later *cassone* and the framed cupboard. Iron is not decoration first. It is the thing that keeps the oak from bursting and the thief from lifting a board.

Met 13.205.2 keeps “decorative forms from the early gothic period” on a late gothic body, the museum says: a reminder that chests are conservative. A lock plate can be up to date; the box is a box. French and German chests in The Cloisters (16.32.20 is a sixteenth-century walnut example with iron mounts, already Renaissance in wood) show the type crossing the date line this series uses for chapter 26.

Viking and Insular chests belong also to chapter 14. The Oseberg burial packed chests and buckets; some of the metal-bound yew buckets are Insular imports. The chest as a seagoing and grave object is older than the guild chest in a parish church.

## Benches, boards, and the politics of the table

Everyone in the hall sits on a bench except the people who do not. A chair — turned, heavy, sometimes with a round seat and a triangular plan in later “thrown” chairs — is a marked seat. The word *chair* in English keeps the *cathedra*. A stool is the rest of the house. Trestle tables let a room change use. Fixed tables appear in some monastic refectories and later in halls that have given up the idea of a clear floor.

Hangings do more thermal work than any chair. A furniture history that ignores textiles in a medieval hall is a history of a cold box. The Bayeux Tapestry’s banquet scenes (Bishop Odo, the servants at the board) are cartoons of this kit: draped table, bowls, a service of people, not a sideboard.

## Church and house

Parish chests held charters and plate. They are among the best-dated wooden objects in England because the ironwork and the parish memory sometimes coincide. Monastic infirmaries and dormitories had beds that are almost entirely lost; the rule books describe them. Hospitality is a furniture demand: a guest bed, a spare chest. Eames is good on the documents — wills, inventories, accounts — that name objects the soil ate.

Turned chairs (“thrown” chairs) and the later box chair with a paneled back are the domestic *cathedra*. Surviving early examples are scarce and often over-restored. Trust a dendrochronology date more than a dealer’s “Norman chair.”

## Forests and the joiners’ names

Oak is the English and northern French sentence. Walnut appears in the south. Imported Baltic oak, in later medieval centuries, is a trade that the next chapters will need. The joiner and the carpenter are not yet the cabinetmaker. They are the people who also cut roofs. That overlap is why a hall’s furniture and a hall’s frame share a vocabulary of pegs and mortises.

Named makers are rare before the later choir-stall contracts (chapter 15). Anonymity is not a lack of skill. It is a lack of the later habit of signing a carcase.

## What to carry forward

The chest is the protagonist. The chair is the exception. The table is a board on legs that can leave. Anyone who jumps from Herculaneum’s cupboards to a Renaissance *studiolo* without this chapter will not understand why a fifteenth-century room is still full of boxes. The cupboard that stands and the drawer that slides are later victories. The Middle Ages locked the lid.
