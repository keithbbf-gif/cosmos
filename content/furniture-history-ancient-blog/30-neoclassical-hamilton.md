# Hamilton’s Vases and the Neoclassical Chair

Sir William Hamilton’s first collection of Greek vases was published in the 1760s; the British Museum bought the core of it in 1772. The Meidias hydria, already a celebrity, became a European pattern. D’Hancarville’s plates, then later Tischbein’s, taught architects and *menuisiers* the klismos, the palmette, the Greek key. The V&A’s painted klismos of about 1810 (W.21:1, 2-1958) puts a Hesperides frieze on a saber-legged chair and paints the frame black and terracotta. The museum’s own note traces the frieze to a Hamilton vase. Neoclassical furniture is, at one of its origins, a furniture of books about pots.

James Stuart and Nicholas Revett’s *Antiquities of Athens* (1762–), Piranesi’s more Roman fever, Robert Adam’s interiors, George Hepplewhite’s *Guide* (1788), Thomas Sheraton’s *Drawing-Book* (1791–94), Charles Percier and Pierre Fontaine’s *Recueil de décorations intérieures* (1801–12): the period is a war of pattern books. The chair that comes out of them can be a thin mahogany Hepplewhite shield-back or a heavy Empire *fauteuil* with gilt sphinxes. Both claim antiquity. They do not claim the same antique.

## Archaeology and the shop

Herculaneum and Pompeii, excavated in public view through the eighteenth century, gave bronze tripods, marble tables, and wall paintings to a Europe that had only had Rome’s ruins and literature. The “Etruscan” rooms of the 1770s are often Greek-vase rooms. Adam’s Etruscan closet at Osterley is a color scheme. The objects in it are Georgian.

French *goût grec* under Louis XVI — Riesener’s later work, Weisweiler, the thin ormolu line, the rectangular commode — is a rejection of Rococo swell and still a luxury trade. David’s portraits of Madame Récamier on a *méridienne* taught a posture. Jacob-Desmalter’s shop supplied the Empire. Denon’s Egypt after 1798 added a second antiquity: camel legs, lotus, the *fauteuil* that thinks it is a pylon. Caption the campaign.

## The American klismos

Benjamin Henry Latrobe designed, and John Aitken made, a set of painted klismos chairs about 1808 for William Waln’s Philadelphia drawing room. The Met’s side chair 1994.189 — yellow poplar, oak, maple, white pine, gold leaf, gesso, a Greek key on the seat rail — is among the earliest American examples. The catalog quotes George Smith (1812) on Greek outline and the rejection of “little parts.” Latrobe had already put a similar language into the interiors of the Capitol and the White House (the latter mostly lost to fire and renovation). The klismos in America is an architect’s chair before it is a cabinetmaker’s vernacular.

Duncan Phyfe’s shop (chapter 32) will turn the same antique into a New York product: saber legs, reeded seats, a harp-back, mahogany that came through the British and then the American trades. The difference between Latrobe’s painted archaeology and Phyfe’s polished mahogany is a difference of client and of shop, not of century.

## What “correct” meant

Thomas Hope’s *Household Furniture and Interior Decoration* (1807) and his Duchess Street house were sermons in accuracy. Hope had drawn in Egypt and Greece. His chairs are heavier, more archaeological, less sit-able than a Sheraton print. The argument between comfort and correctness is the period’s own argument. A klismos that copies a stele will throw a modern sitter backward. Robsjohn-Gibbings, in 1937 (Met 2001.207), will try again, in birch and vellum, and will still be solving a fifth-century problem with modern glue.
