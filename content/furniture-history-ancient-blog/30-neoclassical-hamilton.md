# Hamilton’s Vases and the Neoclassical Chair

Sir William Hamilton’s first collection of Greek vases was published in the 1760s; the British Museum bought the core of it in 1772. The Meidias hydria, already a celebrity, became a European pattern. D’Hancarville’s plates, then later Tischbein’s, taught architects and *menuisiers* the klismos, the palmette, the Greek key. The V&A’s painted klismos of about 1810 (W.21:1, 2-1958) puts a Hesperides frieze on a saber-legged chair and paints the frame black and terracotta. The museum’s own note traces the frieze to a Hamilton vase. Neoclassical furniture is, at one of its origins, a furniture of books about pots.

James Stuart and Nicholas Revett’s *Antiquities of Athens* (1762–), Piranesi’s more Roman fever, Robert Adam’s interiors, George Hepplewhite’s *Guide* (1788), Thomas Sheraton’s *Drawing-Book* (1791–94), Charles Percier and Pierre Fontaine’s *Recueil de décorations intérieures* (1801–12): the period is a war of pattern books. The chair that comes out of them can be a thin mahogany Hepplewhite shield-back or a heavy Empire *fauteuil* with gilt sphinxes. Both claim antiquity. They do not claim the same antique.

## Archaeology and the shop

Herculaneum and Pompeii, excavated in public view through the eighteenth century, gave bronze tripods, marble tables, and wall paintings to a Europe that had only had Rome’s ruins and literature. The “Etruscan” rooms of the 1770s are often Greek-vase rooms. Adam’s Etruscan closet at Osterley is a color scheme. The objects in it are Georgian.

French *goût grec* under Louis XVI — Riesener’s later work, Weisweiler, the thin ormolu line, the rectangular commode — is a rejection of Rococo swell and still a luxury trade. David’s portraits of Madame Récamier on a *méridienne* taught a posture. Jacob-Desmalter’s shop supplied the Empire. Denon’s Egypt after 1798 added a second antiquity: camel legs, lotus, the *fauteuil* that thinks it is a pylon. Caption the campaign.

## The American klismos

Benjamin Henry Latrobe designed, and John Aitken made, a set of painted klismos chairs about 1808 for William Waln’s Philadelphia drawing room. The Met’s side chair 1994.189 — yellow poplar, oak, maple, white pine, gold leaf, gesso, a Greek key on the seat rail — is among the earliest American examples. The catalog quotes George Smith (1812) on Greek outline and the rejection of “little parts.” Latrobe had already put a similar language into the interiors of the Capitol and the White House (the latter mostly lost to fire and renovation). The klismos in America is an architect’s chair before it is a cabinetmaker’s vernacular.

Duncan Phyfe’s shop (chapter 32) will turn the same antique into a New York product: saber legs, reeded seats, a harp-back, mahogany that came through the British and then the American trades. The difference between Latrobe’s painted archaeology and Phyfe’s polished mahogany is a difference of client and of shop, not of century.

## What “correct” meant

Thomas Hope’s *Household Furniture and Interior Decoration* (1807) and his Duchess Street house were sermons in accuracy. Hope had drawn in Egypt and Greece. His chairs are heavier, more archaeological, less sit-able than a Sheraton print. The argument between comfort and correctness is the period’s own argument. A klismos that copies a stele will throw a modern sitter backward. Robsjohn-Gibbings, in 1937 (Met 2001.207), will try again, in birch and vellum, and will still be solving a fifth-century problem with modern glue.

## The hydria that sat at Hamilton’s feet

The British Museum bought the core of Sir William Hamilton’s first vase collection in 1772. The celebrity of the group is the Meidias hydria, registration 1772,0320.30, catalog E224: Attic red-figure water jar, about 420–410 BCE, signed by the potter Meidias, attributed to the Meidias Painter; 52 cm high. Hamilton bought it soon after he reached Naples in 1764. He did not record a precise find-spot; he later wrote that the choicest of the vases now in the Museum came from “ancient sepulchres at Nola.” Winckelmann, seeing the drawing, called it the finest in the world. Joshua Reynolds put the pot at Hamilton’s feet in the 1777 portrait, like a pet. Pierre-François Hugues, Baron d’Hancarville, published the collection in four monumental volumes, *Collection of Etruscan, Greek, and Roman Antiquities from the Cabinet of the Hon.ble Wm. Hamilton* (Naples, 1766–76). Later Tischbein plates, and Hamilton’s second collection, kept the images moving after the first sale.

The upper register of E224 is the abduction of the Leucippides; the lower, Heracles in the Garden of the Hesperides. Gilded details catch gallery light. Architects and *menuisiers* who never stood in the British Museum learned the klismos, the palmette, and the Greek key from these plates. “Etruscan” in a 1770s room is often this palette: black, terracotta, a palmette border. The pots are Greek. The tombs were in Campania. The living rooms were Georgian.

## A vase translated into a backrest

The V&A’s painted klismos W.21:1, 2-1958 is English, about 1810: beech, terracotta on black. The wide curving back is built of twenty-one wooden blocks assembled like bricks — the museum’s own construction note, and the reason a saber curve can be a chair and not only a brushstroke. The frieze shows the gathering of the apples of the Hesperides, “probably copied from an illustration of a vase in the British Museum which belonged to” Hamilton, as the V&A still says. A label transcribed in the object history is blunter: painted with the Hesperides “from a plate in d’Hancarville’s Collection of…the Hon. W. Hamilton (Naples, 1776-7).” Neoclassical furniture is, at one of its origins, a furniture of books about pots.

Anthemion scrolls under the frieze and leaf patterns on the seat rail and legs are nineteenth-century Greek, not fifth-century joinery. Klismos chairs in this painted black-and-terracotta taste were made across Europe from the 1760s into the 1840s. The V&A’s other klismos, the James Newton chair from the Tew Park sale, is a different sentence: mahogany, a London shop, a possible Soho House history. W.21 is the pot-chair. Keep them apart.

## Two antiques, and a third from a campaign

James Stuart and Nicholas Revett’s *Antiquities of Athens* (1762–) gave measured Greece to architects. Piranesi gave Rome a fever. Robert Adam’s interiors — Eileen Harris’s *The Genius of Robert Adam* (2001) is the modern monograph — could be either, or a color scheme that called itself Etruscan. The Etruscan closet at Osterley is Georgian furniture in a vase palette. Herculaneum and Pompeii, excavated in public view, had already given bronze tripods, marble tables, and wall paintings to a Europe that had had ruins and literature. The objects in an “Etruscan” room are not Etruscan.

French *goût grec* under Louis XVI — Riesener’s later work, Weisweiler, the thin ormolu line, the rectangular commode — rejects Rococo swell and stays a luxury trade. David’s portraits of Madame Récamier on a *méridienne* taught a posture. Jacob-Desmalter’s shop supplied the Empire. Percier and Fontaine’s *Recueil de décorations intérieures* (1801–12) is the imperial pattern book: Rome after the Consulate, heavier than Hepplewhite, more architectural than a Sheraton print.

Dominique-Vivant Denon’s *Voyage dans la Basse et la Haute Égypte* (1802), after the 1798 campaign, added a second antiquity. Camel legs, lotus, the *fauteuil* that thinks it is a pylon: caption the campaign. Egyptian taste in a Paris or London drawing room is not a discovery of Karnak by a cabinetmaker. It is a book, a conquest, and a mount.

Hepplewhite’s *Guide* (1788) and Sheraton’s *Drawing-Book* (1791–94) remain the thin mahogany end of the same war of books: shield-backs, square tapering legs, a Greekness that has been through a London plate and come out polite. Hope’s 1807 plates and the Duchess Street house preached correctness over that politeness. The Fitzwilliam’s armchair M.4-1992, possibly Marsh and Tatham after Hope, plate 11, is the heavier archaeological chair a sitter can still find. It throws the body toward the pose on a stele. Comfort was not the point.

Latrobe’s Philadelphia set (Met 1994.189, Aitken, about 1808, for William Waln) is an architect’s antique in yellow poplar, oak, maple, and white pine — American woods, Greek key, gold leaf. The White House interiors in the same language are mostly lost to fire and renovation. Phyfe, in the next chapter, will turn the saber leg into a New York product without the archaeologist’s paint. Robsjohn-Gibbings, Met 2001.207, about 1937, birch and vellum, will try the fifth century again. The problem does not close.


## What “correct” cost

A thin Hepplewhite chair is cheap to look at and not cheap to sit in for three hours. An Empire *fauteuil* with gilt sphinxes is a throne for a drawing room that wanted to look like a court. Baltimore’s painted fancy furniture — a related American industry — took the Greek idea onto cheaper woods with stencils and color. Winterthur and the Met hold the type. It is Grecian for a wider street than Duchess Street.

The period’s honesty, when it has any, is in the pattern book’s title page: these are decorations for interiors, not excavations. Hamilton’s vases were real. The chairs they fathered are modern. Chapter 8 already said that almost no Greek klismos survives in wood. This chapter is the afterlife of that absence. Europe sat on a drawing.

## George Smith, Waln’s drawing room, a stele’s tilt

The Met’s note on Latrobe’s side chair 1994.189 quotes George Smith’s *A Collection of Designs for Household Furniture* (1812) on Greek outline and the rejection of “little parts.” That is the period arguing with itself. Rococo had been all little parts. The painted Philadelphia klismos — yellow poplar, oak, maple, white pine, gold leaf, gesso, a Greek key on the seat rail — is among the earliest American examples of the type. John Aitken made the set about 1808 for William Waln’s drawing room. Latrobe had already put a similar language into the Capitol and the White House; fire and renovation ate most of those interiors. What survives in New York is an architect’s chair in American woods, not a cabinetmaker’s vernacular. Phyfe will vernacularize the saber leg. Latrobe painted it first.

Hamilton’s hydria, 1772,0320.30, remains the pot at the start of that chain. He bought it after reaching Naples in 1764; Nola is the find-region he later named; d’Hancarville’s four volumes (1766–76) taught the Hesperides and the Leucippides to rooms that never saw Bloomsbury. Winckelmann praised the drawing. Reynolds sat the jar at Hamilton’s feet in 1777. The V&A chair W.21:1, 2-1958, about 1810, builds its saber back from twenty-one blocks and copies a d’Hancarville plate. A vase, a book, a chair: three objects, one frieze.

Hope’s Duchess Street house and the 1807 *Household Furniture* were sermons against the polite shield-back. A klismos that copies a grave stele throws a modern sitter backward. That is not a defect in the plate. It is the cost of “correct.” Robsjohn-Gibbings, Met 2001.207, about 1937, birch and vellum, will pay the cost again, without the terracotta. The fifth century does not get more comfortable. It gets a different glue.

Jacob-Desmalter’s Empire *fauteuils* — rolled backs, gilt sphinxes or swans — are Rome after David and, when the mounts go Egyptian, Egypt after Denon. Caption the year of the campaign. Percier and Fontaine’s *Recueil* (1801–12) is the imperial grammar book: interiors as architecture, chairs as orders. A thin Hepplewhite shield-back and a heavy gilt armchair both claim antiquity. They do not claim the same antique. Baltimore’s painted fancy furniture took the cheaper road: stencils, color, a Greek key a journeyman could repeat. Winterthur holds the type. It is Grecian for a wider street than Duchess Street or Waln’s drawing room.

The Meidias hydria is still in Bloomsbury. The chairs are everywhere. That split — a real pot, a modern seat — is the period’s invention, not its failure.


## Baltimore paint, a burned house, a book of prices

George Smith’s *A Collection of Designs for Household Furniture* (1808) and the 1812 *Cabinet-Maker and Upholsterer’s Guide* told English and American shops that Greek outline meant fewer “little parts.” Latrobe’s Waln chairs listen to that sentence. The White House interiors he designed in the same language mostly died in the 1814 fire; what we have is a paper trail and a few survivals elsewhere. Caption the loss. Do not reconstruct a room from a wish.

Baltimore’s painted fancy furniture took the same Greek idea onto cheaper woods with stencils, striping, and a gold line. Winterthur and the Met hold the type. It is Grecian for a wider street than Duchess Street or Waln’s drawing room: a chair a merchant’s clerk might own, not only a merchant. The paint is the luxury, not the mahogany. A klismos in yellow poplar and lampblack is still a modern chair pretending to be a stele. The pretense is the period.

The Fitzwilliam’s Hope armchair (M.4-1992) remains the heavy end a visitor can sit in only with permission. Smith’s plates are the lighter end a shop could steal. Between them, Europe sat on a drawing. Chapter 8’s missing Greek wood is still missing.
