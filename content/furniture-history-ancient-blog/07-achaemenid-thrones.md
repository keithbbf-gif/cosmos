# The King Under a Parasol

On the Apadana staircases at Persepolis, the king sits. The throne is a high-backed chair with turned or animal-form legs, a footstool under the feet, a canopy or parasol held by an attendant. Delegations climb toward him with jars, cloth, and animals. The reliefs were carved in the late sixth and early fifth centuries BCE, in the reigns of Darius I and Xerxes I. The wood of the throne is gone. The stone remembers the silhouette, the stool, and the fact that the king did not put his feet on the same floor as the givers.

Achaemenid furniture is a problem of representation. We have reliefs at Persepolis and Naqsh-e Rostam, glazed-brick processions at Susa, a few metal and stone fittings, Greek descriptions that are not carpenter’s notes, and tomb paintings on the empire’s edges (especially Lycia and Macedon) that may be quoting the court. What we do not have is a Persepolis equivalent of Hatnefer’s chair. Margaret Cool Root’s work on Achaemenid official art (*The King and Kingship in Achaemenid Art*, 1979) treats the throne as a political machine: the king is seated so that the world can approach in registers. Furniture is the grammar of that approach.

## The footstool is not a detail

Egyptian kings already put enemies on a footrest (JE 62046). The Achaemenid footstool is quieter and just as strict. The king’s feet do not hang. The stool is part of the throne, a two-piece seat. In some reliefs the stool’s legs rhyme with the throne’s. In the Treasury reliefs — the audience scene moved, in the scholarly story, from the Apadana — the king and the crown prince sit, and the space between stool and floor is the gap that officials do not cross.

Greek writers noticed the stool. They also noticed the canopy. Herodotus and later authors describe Persian luxury in terms that mix autopsy, rumor, and moralizing. A parasol-bearer is a job. A throne that travels — if the royal tent used a dismantled seat, as later Iranian and Islamic courts would — is a logistics problem. `[CITE NEEDED: any Achaemenid administrative tablet that lists throne parts or tent furniture by name; Persepolis Fortification Archive is mostly rations, not joinery.]` The reliefs show the assembled idea, not the packing list.

## Animal legs and the inherited Near East

The throne’s legs, in the better-preserved reliefs, include turned members and, in related court metalwork, hoofed or leonine terminals. The inheritance is Assyrian and Elamite as much as Egyptian. Assyrian kings sat on backed thrones with footstools in the ninth and seventh centuries; the Persepolis carvers knew that image, or knew objects that descended from it. Elam, whose lowland capital at Susa the Achaemenids took and rebuilt, had its own seating types still poorly reconstructed from excavation. What looks “Persian” on the Apadana is often an imperial composite: a Median dress, a Persian dress, a Babylonian gift, a Greek-looking vessel. The throne is the one object that must look like the center. Its job is to be recognizable from the bottom of the stair. High back, footstool, canopy: three signs that read at a distance.

## Tomb furniture on the western edge

Lycian tombs (Xanthos, the Nereid Monument now in the British Museum) and later Macedonian elite tombs show klinai — couches — in stone, with turned legs that art historians have compared to Achaemenid and Ionian woodwork. The comparison is real and overused. A stone couch in a tomb is a funerary type with its own rules. It may quote a wooden banquet couch from a satrapal court. It may quote a Greek symposium. In western Anatolia those were not two separate worlds.

The so-called “Persian” furniture in Athenian red-figure — stools, a king on a throne in the presence of Greeks — is Athenian theatre. It tells us what vase-painters needed a Persian king to sit on, not what a Susa joiner cut. Use it as evidence of Athenian ideas. Do not inventory Persepolis from a krater.

## Bronze, gold, and the Alexander afterlife

Court metal furniture — gold-plated wood, bronze fittings — is implied by the reliefs’ sharpness of turning and by later Hellenistic royal furniture that claims Achaemenid descent. When Alexander took Persepolis, the stories about luxury (and fire) became a European inheritance. Hellenistic kings sat on thrones that wanted to look eastern and Greek at once. The Achaemenid chair, as an idea, outlived the Achaemenid shop.

Actual bronze fittings from Achaemenid and immediately later contexts — lion-paw terminals, ring handles, cast ornaments — appear in museum catalogs as “furniture attachment” more often than as reconstructed chairs. The Louvre’s Susa collections and the Iran National Museum hold the fragments. A full reconstruction of a Darius throne in wood would be a guess. This essay will not commission one.

## Sitting as imperial work

The delegations on the Apadana do not sit. They process. The king sits. That difference is the furniture’s politics. In the chapters on Greece that follow, citizens will sit on benches in a theatre and on stools at a symposium; the democratic and oligarchic meanings of a bench are not the Apadana’s meanings. Here the chair is a monopoly. Even the crown prince’s seat, when it appears, is a copy in a smaller political font.

Parasol, stool, back, attendants: the king’s body is framed so that it does not have to stand. Standing is for givers. The history of furniture is, among other things, a history of who is allowed to stop walking.
