# Bandaji and Soban

A *bandaji* is a chest whose front drops or whose upper door opens as a flap, iron or brass fittings spread across the wood like a second architecture. The Birmingham Museum of Art’s example (1999.58), Joseon, nineteenth century, wood and iron, 37¼ by 38 by 18¾ inches, carries flower-basket and butterfly plates. It is a late piece of a long type. Korean furniture in Western museums is still too often a nineteenth-century export or a collector’s “folk” purchase. The type itself is older than those purchases. Edward Reynolds Wright and Man Sill Pai, *Korean Furniture: Elegance and Tradition* (1984), and the more recent catalogs of the National Museum of Korea and the Philadelphia Museum of Art are the places to start.

Joseon interiors, like Japanese and like many Chinese rooms, sat on the floor. The *ondol* heated floor is the Korean specific: furniture had to work on a warm, flat, papered surface. Legs are short. Chests stand in stacks. Tables are trays on low feet. A high chair is a visitor from somewhere else, not the room’s native sentence.

## Types without a throne

*Bandaji* (blanket chest, often for bedding), *jang* and *nong* (stacked chests), *sungnyungbang* kitchen chests, document boxes, book chests for a *sarangbang* (the men’s study): the vocabulary is a house plan. The *sarangbang* and *anbang* (inner, often women’s, room) had different kits. Gendered space is furniture space. A scholar’s book chest and a wedding *bandaji* are not interchangeable.

The *soban* is a small individual table, often twelve to twenty inches across, carried in with a meal and carried out. It is the opposite of the European dining table. Each sitter has a landscape of bowls at a personal height. Wood, sometimes with horsehair-like grain emphasis, sometimes with painted ox-horn (*hwagak*) on court pieces. A good *soban* is a joiner’s small theorem: tray, apron, four legs that do not rack.

## Metal as drawing

Joseon chest fittings — swallow plates, hinges, lock plates, corner guards — are the furniture’s drawing. They are not “applied decoration” in the European afterthought sense. They hold the carcase and they name the workshop’s region (Kaesong, Pakchong, the southern styles). Brass on elm or zelkova, iron on darker woods: the contrast is the design. Compare Japanese tansu hardware, which is cousinly and not the same. Korean plates spread wider and often more symmetrically.

Lacquer and mother-of-pearl (*najeon*) chests are the court and upper-luxury line, closer to a different chapter of East Asian lacquer than to a village *bandaji*. The Met and Asian museums hold both. Do not let the lacquer pieces erase the iron ones; do not let “simplicity” erase the lacquer.

## Scholars, widows, and the later market

A *sarangbang* reconstruction in a museum (the National Museum of Korea’s installations; some American period rooms) will show a low desk, a book chest, a painting. It is an elite male room. The *anbang* reconstructions, when they exist, show stacked chests and bedding.
