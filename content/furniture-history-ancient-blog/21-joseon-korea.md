# Bandaji and Soban

A *bandaji* is a chest whose front drops or whose upper door opens as a flap, iron or brass fittings spread across the wood like a second architecture. The Birmingham Museum of Art’s example (1999.58), Joseon, nineteenth century, wood and iron, 37¼ by 38 by 18¾ inches, carries flower-basket and butterfly plates. It is a late piece of a long type. Korean furniture in Western museums is still too often a nineteenth-century export or a collector’s “folk” purchase. The type itself is older than those purchases. Edward Reynolds Wright and Man Sill Pai, *Korean Furniture: Elegance and Tradition* (1984), and the more recent catalogs of the National Museum of Korea and the Philadelphia Museum of Art are the places to start.

Joseon interiors, like Japanese and like many Chinese rooms, sat on the floor. The *ondol* heated floor is the Korean specific: furniture had to work on a warm, flat, papered surface. Legs are short. Chests stand in stacks. Tables are trays on low feet. A high chair is a visitor from somewhere else, not the room’s native sentence.

## Ondol as a furniture law

An *ondol* is a masonry flue system under a stone-and-clay floor, faced with paper, heated from a firebox at one end. The sitting surface is the floor. Anything with long legs is a trip and a heat-block. Chests therefore stand on low plinths or on short bracket feet. Tables are carried in and out. Bedding is folded into a *bandaji* or stacked in a *nong* by day and spread on the warm paper at night. The house type — the wooden *hanok* with its underfloor heat — is the reason the furniture looks the way it does. A Joseon chest photographed in a white gallery, six inches off a wooden parquet, has lost its first material: warmth from below.

Wright and Pai mapped the house as a set of rooms with different kits. The *sarangbang*, the outer study associated with men of the scholarly class, held book chests, a low desk, a painting, stationery boxes. The *anbang*, the inner room associated with women of the household, held stacked clothing chests, bedding chests, cosmetic boxes. Kitchen chests (*sungnyungbang* and related types) stood nearer the firebox. Gendered space is furniture space. A scholar’s book chest and a wedding *bandaji* are not interchangeable. The National Museum of Korea’s period rooms are the public version of that map. They are reconstructions. They are still more honest than a single chest on a plinth labeled “Korean antique.”

## Types without a throne

*Bandaji* (blanket chest, often for bedding), *jang* and *nong* (stacked chests), *sungnyungbang* kitchen chests, document boxes, book chests for a *sarangbang*: the vocabulary is a house plan. A *jang* is a taller wardrobe, often in two stages; a *nong* is a stack of nearly identical chests that can be separated for moving. Height without a high chair: you stack the cupboards. The drop-front of a *bandaji* is a working door. Bedding is heavy. The flap is the width of the carcase so a rolled mattress can come out in one motion. Hinges and lock plates take the strain. That is why the metal is not a border; it is the drawing and the structure.

The *soban* is a small individual table, often twelve to twenty inches across, carried in with a meal and carried out. It is the opposite of the European dining table. Each sitter has a set of bowls at a personal height. Wood, sometimes with horsehair-like grain emphasis, sometimes with painted ox-horn (*hwagak*) on court pieces. A good *soban* is a joiner’s small theorem: tray, apron, four legs that do not rack.

National Museum of Korea types have names that are legs before they are styles. A *hojokban* is a tiger-legged table; a *gaedari* or *gujokban* is dog-legged; an *iljokban* is a simpler four-leg. Diameters run from a personal tray to a larger serving stand. One published mother-of-pearl *soban* in the museum’s orbit is 36 centimeters high and 24 across; another, with a *bok* (福) character in shell, is 24.7 centimeters high and 32 across. `[CITE NEEDED: the NMK inventory numbers for those two soban if used as figures.]` The point is the carrying. Dinner arrives. Dinner leaves. The floor is cleared for sitting, sleeping, or work.

## Metal as drawing

Joseon chest fittings — swallow plates, hinges, lock plates, corner guards — are the furniture’s drawing. They are not “applied decoration” in the European afterthought sense. They hold the carcase and they name the workshop’s region. Kaesong (Gaeseong) plates are often dense and yellow-brass, a northern court-adjacent taste. Pakch’ŏn and other northern shops have their own lock shapes. Southern and coastal work, including Tongyeong, can run thinner or more pictorial. Brass on elm or zelkova, iron on darker woods: the contrast is the design. Birmingham 1999.58’s flower-basket and butterfly plates are a readable late set: auspicious, slightly sweet, the nineteenth century’s popular language. Compare Japanese tansu hardware, which is cousinly and not the same. Korean plates spread wider and often more symmetrically. A tansu lock is a point. A *bandaji* lock is a composition.

Zelkova (*neutinamu*, related to Japanese keyaki) and pine are common carcase woods. Persimmon and other fruitwoods appear as fronts when a shop wants a dark, mottled panel. The wood is often stained or given a thin lacquer so the plates sit on a quiet ground. Front boards may be bookmatched. The back is the carpenter’s honesty: rougher, sometimes a different species. Open a *bandaji* in a museum and look at the inside of the flap. That is where the shop stopped spending.

## Lacquer, horn, and the court line

Lacquer and mother-of-pearl (*najeon*) chests are the court and upper-luxury line, closer to a different chapter of East Asian lacquer than to a village *bandaji*. The Met and Asian museums hold both. Do not let the lacquer pieces erase the iron ones; do not let “simplicity” erase the lacquer.

Met 63.121.1a–k is a warning about dates. Early twentieth century, Joseon in the museum’s period field, 34 by 27⅝ by 16¾ inches, lacquered wood inlaid with mother-of-pearl, tortoiseshell, ray skin, and brass wire; brass fittings; phoenixes, *taegeuk* roundels, flowers. Bequest of Franklin Jasper Walls, 1963. The label reads it as elite, likely a women’s-quarters object, possibly part of a set. A related two-stage piece, 63.121.2a–i, is 42¾ inches high. These are late, precious, and closer to a bridal trousseau than to Birmingham’s iron *bandaji*. Met 12.134.42a, b, a nineteenth-century box with phoenixes, floral scrolls, and insects in mother-of-pearl, Rogers Fund 1912, 6⅜ by 11 by 16⅛ inches, was in the museum’s 2021–22 *Shell and Resin* show. Met 2006.260a–g is a small early twentieth-century drawer chest, 11⅜ inches high, flowers, birds, insects, incised shell filled with black. The shape suggests writing or cosmetic tools. All four objects are Joseon-tradition lacquer in American cases. None of them is a fifteenth-century court piece. Caption the century.

*Hwagak*, the painted ox-horn veneer of court and luxury objects, is a Korean specialty: thin horn, colored from behind, applied in tiles. It appears on boxes and on some small tables. It is not a village *bandaji* technique. It is a palace and upper-house skin, bright, pictorial, easy to love in a photograph and easy to mislabel as “folk.”

## Scholars, widows, and the later market

A *sarangbang* reconstruction in a museum (the National Museum of Korea’s installations; some American period rooms) will show a low desk, a book chest, a painting. It is an elite male room. The *anbang* reconstructions, when they exist, show stacked chests and bedding. Widows’ and women’s property in chests is a legal history as much as a style history. `[CITE NEEDED: a Joseon legal or inventory study that lists furniture by room and gender.]` Until that study is on the table, the room names and the surviving kits are the evidence. A book chest with sparse fittings and a tall, thin carcase is a *sarangbang* object. A *bandaji* with butterflies and a drop front is an *anbang* object. The assignment is a habit, not a law of nature, and shops could mix.

Twentieth-century collecting, Japanese colonial-period photography (1910–45), and the Korean antique trade shaped what left the peninsula. Provenance is often thin. Missionaries, colonial officials, and later American soldiers bought chests. Dealers in Seoul and in Japanese cities supplied a taste for “simple” iron-fitted wood that mid-century modernism was ready to praise. The praise is not the making. Caption what is known. “Joseon, 19th century, southern style” is an honest label. “Ancient Korean throne” is not a type this chapter owns.

Philadelphia’s Korean furniture, San Francisco’s Asian Art Museum holdings, and the National Museum of Korea’s storage are the places to check a type before a caption invents a region. Wright and Pai remain the English handbook even when later Korean scholarship has refined the map. The Birmingham chest is a good teacher because it is ordinary in the right way: late, iron, drop-front, butterflies. The Met’s *najeon* pieces are a good teacher because they are not ordinary. A Joseon house needed both, and needed the *soban* more often than either, because people ate every day.

## Woods, wedding sets, and what a hinge admits

Zelkova fronts and pine sides are a common pairing because the show face and the unseen carcase have different jobs. Persimmon, when it appears, is a dark, speckled luxury on a small surface — a document box, a front panel — not a whole *bandaji*. Joinery is framed and pegged; the drop-front’s hinges are the place a cheap shop saves money and a good shop spends it. Open the flap and the inner face may be unfinished. That is not a defect. It is a budget.

Wedding furniture was a set: chests, boxes, a *soban* or two, sometimes a lacquered cosmetic case. The Met’s phoenix-and-*taegeuk* pieces (63.121.1, 63.121.2) sit at the late, expensive end of that habit. A village wedding used iron plates and a sturdier carcase. Japanese colonial-period photographs of interiors — studio shots as well as house surveys — froze some of those sets for an audience that was already buying. The photograph is a document of taste as much as of a room. Use it as a document. Do not treat a 1930s plate as a fifteenth-century reconstruction.

Book chests for a *sarangbang* often run taller in the leg than a bedding *bandaji*, and sparser in metal. The fittings may be limited to a lock and corner guards. The argument is the wood and the proportion. Stationery boxes and low desks complete the kit. A painting on the wall or a hanging in a small alcove is the display; Korea does not need a Japanese *tokonoma* to have a place to hang a work, and it does not need a Chinese yoke-back to have a scholar’s room. The floor is still the seat. The chest is still the wall of property.

A *soban* with *hwagak* tiles or a full *najeon* ground is a court or upper-house object. A plain wooden *hojokban* is the daily tool. Both are furniture. The series’ job is not to choose the photogenic one. It is to say that dinner, on an *ondol*, arrives one tray at a time.

Papered floors scratch. Short feet and a smooth underside are therefore a finish, not an afterthought. A chest dragged across *ondol* paper leaves a record the owner will see every day. The carpenter who rounded a foot or added a small pad knew that. Western photographs taken from above, looking down on plates, miss the underside the way they miss the heat. Turn the piece. The ground side is part of the design, and on an *ondol* it is the side that meets the heat.

The next chapter leaves the ondol for a rope bed and a howdah. The Korean lesson to take along is small: a table can be a tray with legs, and a cupboard can be a door that falls toward you. High sitting is not the only way a room ranks its people. On a warm floor, rank is which chest you own and which side of the room you are allowed to unfold it in.


## Ondol, a tray, a hinge that tells the budget

An *ondol* floor is furniture the way tatami is: a heated plane that decides the height of every table. Dinner arrives on a *soban*, one sitter at a time, and leaves again. A tall dining table would be the wrong object. Wright and Pai (1984) remain the English handbook; later Korean scholarship has refined regions without replacing the floor.

Open a *bandaji* flap and the inner face may be unfinished. That is a budget, not a defect. Hinges are where a cheap shop saves and a good shop spends. Zelkova fronts and pine sides pair because the show face and the unseen carcase have different jobs. Colonial-period photographs (1910–45) froze some wedding sets for buyers already in the market. Use the photograph as a document of taste. Do not treat a 1930s plate as a fifteenth-century room.
