# A Pulpit from Córdoba

An inscription cleaned in the 1990s says that ʿAlī ibn Yūsuf, the Almoravid amir, ordered the minbar from a Córdoba workshop in 532 AH / 1137 CE, for the congregational mosque of Marrakesh. The piece was made in parts, carried south, and assembled. When the Almohads took Marrakesh in 1147 they destroyed ʿAlī’s mosque and kept the minbar. ʿAbd al-Muʾmin installed it in the first Kutubiyya, then in the second, better-oriented Kutubiyya of the 1160s. It stayed in use until the 1950s or, in some accounts, the early 1960s, then went to the Badiʿ Palace. Jonathan Bloom, Stefano Carboni, Ahmed Toufiq, and the Metropolitan Museum conservators who worked in Marrakesh in 1996–97 published the object in *The Minbar from the Kutubiyya Mosque* (New York: Met, 1998). It did not travel to the Met’s 1992 *Al-Andalus* exhibition; it was too fragile. The book is the exhibition it could not be.

Eight steps, sided by paneled walls, a seat at the top, a ramp of geometry: a minbar is a mobile pulpit that stands to the right of the mihrab. In many Maghribi mosques it rolled out for the Friday sermon and back into a closet. Antonio Almagro has modeled the Kutubiyya’s retractable maqsura and mobile minbar from the archaeology of the site (*Arqueología de la Arquitectura* 18, 2021). Furniture here is part of a machine for assembly.

## What the wood is doing

The surfaces are a Córdoba specialty: marquetry and inlay of colored woods and bone, carved polygonal plugs in the gaps of geometric strapwork, Kufic bands with Qurʾanic text, pious formulas, and the foundation texts. Bloom and El Mostafa Hbibi describe the structure; the conservators (Jack Soultanian, Antoine Wilmering, Mark Minor, Andrew Zawacki) describe what they glued, cleaned, and supported on a new steel frame. The 1998 volume’s drawings show how the patterns grow. This is not “ornament” in the dismissive sense. It is the way a pulpit becomes readable from the floor of the mosque.

Jonathan Bloom’s larger argument about the minbar — that it is the one ubiquitous wooden furniture type of the medieval Islamic congregational mosque, and therefore the type on which woodworkers spent their greatest effort — is the right frame for this chapter. Stone and tile minbars exist later. The early and the finest are wood. A furniture history that treats Islam as a culture “without furniture” because many rooms used mats and low trays has not stood next to this pulpit.

## Rooms without chairs, rooms with a seat

Domestic interiors from al-Andalus and the Maghrib, as far as texts and later ethnographic analogy allow, used mats, cushions, low tables, and chests. The *kursī* in a Moroccan madrasa — the professorial chair studied by Nadia Erzini and Stephen Vernoit (*Al-Qantara* 34, 2013) — is a specialized seat for teaching, not a dining chair. The minbar is a specialized seat for the khutba. Both are marked woods in a visual culture that did not need a klismos in every room.

That is not a lack. It is a different assignment of carpentry. The skill that Europe put into the canopied stall, Córdoba put into the minbar’s sides. Comparing them as “equivalent pulpits” is useful only if the comparison stays at the level of a raised voice and a wooden architecture, not at the level of a shared style.

Al-Andalus also made ivory caskets (the pyxides of the Córdoban Umayyad court, now in the Louvre, the V&A, and San Isidoro) that are boxes, not chairs, and still belong in a furniture series as the luxury containers of a court. The minbar’s carved plugs, the 1998 authors note, can rival those ivories in tightness of leaf. The same city, different patrons, a shared standard of finish.

## After 1147

The Almohad choice to keep the Almoravid pulpit is a political fact about objects. A dynasty can raze a mosque and still need a Friday sermon to come from a beautiful height. The minbar’s later life — hidden from many non-Muslim visitors until the twentieth century, photographed for *Al-Andalus*, conserved by a joint Moroccan–Met team, displayed in the Badiʿ as a work of art — is a typical modern biography of a liturgical object. Toufiq’s essay in the 1998 book, “the pulpit of an empire,” tries to put it back in the days of its glory without pretending the Badiʿ is a mosque.

Other Maghribi and Andalusi minbars (the Qarawiyyin in Fez; fragments and later copies) form a family. The Kutubiyya piece is the one with a Met monograph and a cleaned date. That is why it carries this chapter. It is not the only pulpit. It is the one a reader can follow from 1137 to a palace museum without a fabricated find-story.

## Córdoba’s shop, Marrakesh’s machine, a pulpit’s afterlife

The 1137 commission is a logistics sentence: a Córdoba workshop cut panels and geometry that had to survive a journey and a reassembly. Bloom and Hbibi’s structural drawings in the 1998 Met book show a pulpit that is also a kit. Almagro’s later model of the Kutubiyya’s retractable maqsura and mobile minbar puts the object back in a Friday machine — roll out, preach, roll in. Furniture here is part of the mosque’s moving parts, not a static sculpture that happened to have steps.

Almohad ʿAbd al-Muʾmin’s choice to keep an Almoravid pulpit while razing the mosque is politics in wood. A dynasty can change the qibla and still need a beautiful height for the khutba. The minbar’s later centuries of use (into the 1950s or early 1960s, depending on the account) mean the masterpiece was not born a museum object. Toufiq’s “pulpit of an empire” essay tries to restore that use without pretending the Badiʿ display is a mosque.

Ivory pyxides of the Córdoban Umayyad court (Louvre, V&A, San Isidoro) are the same city’s other standard of finish. The 1998 authors note that the minbar’s carved plugs can rival those ivories. Different patrons, a shared intolerance for slack leaf. Domestic rooms in al-Andalus and the Maghrib, as far as texts and later houses allow, used mats, cushions, chests, and the occasional *kursī* for teaching (Erzini and Vernoit on the Moroccan professorial chair). The minbar is the marked wooden seat in a culture that did not need a klismos in every room. That is an assignment of carpentry, not a lack.

## The Córdoba shop and the pulpit that stayed home

Córdoba in 1137 was still a city that could export a pulpit. The Great Mosque’s own minbar — the lost wooden pulpit of the Umayyad congregational mosque — was still in use when ʿAlī ibn Yūsuf placed his order. Stefano Carboni, in the 1998 volume, treats the Kutubiyya piece as a shadow of that missing Córdoban original. We do not have the original. We have this, and the Qarawiyyin minbar in Fez, finished in 1144, which looks close enough in technique that a shared workshop is a reasonable guess and not a proven one. No inscription on the Fez pulpit names Córdoba. The Fez pulpit is still in liturgical use and has not had a Met winter. Comparison stays at the level of photographs and the older notes of Henri Terrasse and Jean Sauvaget.

The Kutubiyya commission is a logistics sentence. Panels, straps of geometry, inscription bands, a seat, eight steps: cut in al-Andalus, carried across the Strait, assembled in Marrakesh for the Almoravid congregational mosque. When ʿAbd al-Muʾmin’s Almohads took the city in 1147 they razed ʿAlī’s mosque and kept the pulpit. It went into the first Kutubiyya, then into the second, re-oriented mosque of the 1160s. Antonio Almagro’s 2021 study of the retractable maqsura and the mobile minbar reconstructs the Friday machine from the archaeology: a closet, a roll-out, a screen that could move. Henri Basset and Henri Terrasse had described the pulpit in *Hespéris* in 1926; Sauvaget wrote on it in 1949. Henri Terrasse later gathered “minbars anciens du Maroc” in a 1957 miscellany; the family was already visible, the drawings were not yet this tight. The 1998 book is the first time the Kutubiyya object was drawn as structure.

It remained in use until 1962 in the account a CAA review of Bloom’s volume repeats (the 1950s in others). Gaston Deverdun’s older city history had already tracked the pulpit through the two Kutubiyyas; the disagreement is about the last Friday, not about the route. Then the Badiʿ Palace. The Met wanted it for *Al-Andalus* in 1992 and the conservators said no: too old, too rickety, too big to cross a street. The joint Moroccan–Met campaign of 1996–97 was the exhibition that could not travel, done on site. Mark Minor’s treatment paper is the human record of that winter: dust enough to build a wall, a steel frame, three missing inscription sections on the proper right replaced in cedar because they were known Qurʾanic text, two lowest risers remade, losses otherwise left as losses.

## Plugs, bone, Aziz, and the Qarawiyyin cousin

The sides are the Córdoba specialty. Geometric strapwork in marquetry — colored woods, bone, ivory tooth — leaves polygonal gaps. Into the gaps go carved plugs, each a small leaf or flower, tight enough that the 1998 authors compare them with the Umayyad ivory pyxides of the same city (Louvre OA 4068, V&A 217-1865, the San Isidoro group). Kufic bands carry Qurʾan, pious formulas, and the foundation texts. Cleaning in the 1990s made the 532 AH / 1137 date readable. Another name came up in the same campaign: Aziz, a craftsman or a foreman, not a modern signature in the European sense, a word in the wood. `[CITE NEEDED: the exact inscription as transcribed in Bloom et al. 1998.]` Jonathan Bloom and El Mostafa Hbibi’s drawings show how the patterns grow from the geometry, not how they were stuck on after.

Jack Soultanian, Antoine Wilmering, Mark Minor, and Andrew Zawacki describe glue, cleaning, and the new steel spine. The pulpit had begun to rack. A liturgical object used for eight centuries has a right to be tired. The conservation choice — support, clean, replace only what the text required, do not “complete” the geometry — is why the Badiʿ display still looks like a used pulpit and not a replica. Rights to the 1998 photography sit with the Met and the Moroccan ministry. This chapter’s figures should stay with those plates until a usable CC file exists.

Nadia Erzini and Stephen Vernoit’s *kursī* — the professorial chair in Moroccan madrasas — is the other marked wooden seat in this visual culture. It is for teaching, not for dinner. Domestic rooms, as far as Andalusi texts and later Maghribi houses allow, used mats, cushions, low tables, chests. The European traveler’s sentence that “Moors sit on the floor” erased the minbar, the *kursī*, the chest, and the carpenter. Bloom’s larger argument still holds: the minbar is the one ubiquitous wooden furniture type of the medieval congregational mosque, and therefore the type on which woodworkers spent their greatest effort. Stone and tile pulpits come later. The early ones, and the finest, are wood.

Other Maghribi pulpits (the Andalusian Mosque in Fez, later copies, fragments) form a family. The Almoravid Qubba in Marrakesh is stone and water, not a pulpit, and still the architectural cousin of the Friday machine: a dynasty that could finish a dome could also order a minbar from another city. Tinmal, the Almohad mountain mosque, kept its own later furniture problem; it is not this pulpit and should not be captioned as if it were. The Kutubiyya piece carries this chapter because a reader can follow it from a cleaned inscription in 1137 to a steel frame in the Badiʿ without a fabricated find. It is not the only pulpit. It is the one that was drawn. Friday, for eight hundred years, came from its top step. The ticket now comes from a palace door. The geometry has not changed its mind.
