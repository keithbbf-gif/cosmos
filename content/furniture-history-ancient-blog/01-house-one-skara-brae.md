# House One at Skara Brae

House One still has its dresser. Two stone shelves on slab uprights sit on the wall opposite the door, exactly where a visitor’s eye would go after the body folded through the low passage. A hearth occupies the center. On either side of the fire, rectangular boxes of flagstone — V. Gordon Childe called them beds — push into the room. The floor is about forty square meters. The walls are local sandstone, packed into a mound of midden that once held heat and kept the wind off the Bay of Skaill.

A storm in 1850 stripped sand from the west coast of Mainland, Orkney, and showed the village to William Watt of Skaill House. Formal excavation came later: amateur clearing in the nineteenth century, then Childe’s campaigns for H.M. Office of Works in 1928–30, then later work that separated an earlier village (roughly 3100–2900 BCE) from the later houses visitors now walk above (roughly 2900–2500 BCE). Historic Environment Scotland dates the occupation in that range and treats the site as part of the Heart of Neolithic Orkney World Heritage listing of 1999. Caroline Wickham-Jones, in *Between the Wind and the Water* (2006), is careful about what the stone fittings were *for*. The labels — dresser, box-bed, seat of honor — are inherited. They are also the only vocabulary we have until someone finds a better one that still fits the masonry.

This chapter starts here because the furniture is still in the room. Most histories of the chair begin in Egypt, where dry air kept wood. Orkney kept stone. The difference matters. A dresser built of flagstone is not a prototype waiting to become mahogany. It is a finished answer to a house that had almost no timber large enough for a framed seat, and to a social life that wanted a rear wall to do more than hold up the roof.

## What Childe saw, and what he named

Childe published *Skara Brae, a Pictish Village in Orkney* in 1931, then revised the dating when Grooved Ware and radiocarbon made a Pictish village impossible. The furniture interpretation stuck harder than the chronology. In House 1, as in Houses 2, 4, 5, and 7, he read the rear shelves as a dresser “for storing pottery utensils” and the lateral boxes as beds that would have been lined with heather, skins, or bracken. Anna Ritchie, writing for Historic Scotland in *Prehistoric Orkney* (1995), kept the bed reading and added a block of stone sometimes set between hearth and dresser as a possible “seat of honour.” Wickham-Jones notes central hearths, stone beds softened (in later imagination) with fur or seaweed, and dressers of three shelves in two bays.

Sigurd Towrie and the Ness of Brodgar project have spent the last two decades loosening those nouns. The boxes are short. Some of them held cattle skulls and crushed pots. Sharing a “bed” with a skull is a modern joke that points at a real problem: function was assigned by analogy with a nineteenth-century croft, then repeated until it felt like evidence. The dressers, Towrie argues, sit in the same place in every late house — opposite the entrance — which is a stronger fact than “storage.” At Links of Noltland on Westray, a dresser is built into a wall recess opposite the door; the Grobust House has no dresser but a shelved recess in the same relative position. The rear of the dwelling, in late Neolithic Orkney, is marked. Furniture here is architecture that faces you.

House 8 stands apart: free-standing, a hearth, wall niches, no dresser, no boxes. Historic Environment Scotland still floats the workshop reading. The Ness team treats the nickname as a nickname. Age and role remain open. That is the right temperature for this site. A village without an obvious elite house does not mean a village without rank; it means rank, if it existed, did not take the form of a bigger dresser.

## Fitted, not portable

Egyptian and Mesopotamian furniture, in the chapters that follow, can be carried: a stool to a banquet, a bed into a tomb, a chair up the Nile. Skara Brae’s fittings were not packed. They were the room. The same is true, with mudbrick instead of flagstone, at Çatalhöyük (chapter 2). The Atlantic Neolithic and the central Anatolian Neolithic did not share a style. They shared a habit: raise some parts of the floor, face the door with a display or a shelf, put fire in the middle, bury or store along the walls.

David Clarke and Patrick Ashmore’s later work on Orkney, and Colin Richards on the Barnhouse settlement inland from Stenness, put Skara Brae back among houses that were once thought unique. Barnhouse had hearths and stone fittings; Maeshowe and the Stones of Stenness are a short walk in Neolithic terms. The dresser in House 1 is not a curiosity washed up by a Victorian storm. It belongs to a cluster of late Neolithic architecture that liked cruciform interiors, right angles in stone, and a rear wall that performed.

Childe’s early houses (Nine and Ten, and the first phases under later floors) were more circular, with boxes set into the wall. Later houses go rectangular; the boxes step into the room. That shift is one of the few sequences in European furniture that can be walked in plan. It is also a warning. “Bed” and “dresser” describe a late, standardized kit. The earlier kit used the same ideas with different geometry. Anyone who wants a single origin for “the cabinet” or “the bedstead” will not find it on the Bay of Skaill. They will find a village that rebuilt its furniture when it rebuilt its walls.

## Wood that is not there

Orkney is poor in large timber. Driftwood, coppiced shrub, whalebone, and hide do the work that oak does in later Europe. The absence is not a lack of carpentry knowledge; it is a lack of trees. Small wooden objects — pins, possible bowl fragments, the usual wet-site hints — appear in the literature with the caution they deserve `[CITE NEEDED: full organic inventory, Clarke 1976 vs later reports]`. Gaming pieces, beads, and tools in the visitor-center cases are easier to photograph than a vanished stool.

This is why the stone dresser has been asked to do too much cultural work. It stands in for a missing wooden world. Compare the slightly later (and still debated) wooden trackways and platforms of the Somerset Levels, or the alpine lake dwellings whose piles and, rarely, furniture fragments survive in waterlogged silt. Orkney chose stone because stone was the reliable sheet material. The joint is a gravity joint: slab on upright, upright on floor, packing in the gaps. No mortise is required. The sophistication is in the planning of the house, not in the lock of two members.

Historic Environment Scotland’s current public text still says “fitted stone furniture” and lists dresser, two box-beds, central hearth, and small tanks “possibly for preparing fish bait.” The tanks are as important as the dresser and get a tenth of the attention. A room that processes bait, sleeps (or stores), displays, and burns peat is a room whose furniture is a set of work stations. The later European habit of calling only the movable pieces “furniture” would leave this house empty.

## Storm, sand, and the replica

Visitors do not walk through House 1. They look down from a path, then enter a reconstructed house nearby whose dresser is dressed with pots and whose beds are piled with skins. The replica is honest about being a replica; it is less honest about uncertainty. A pot on a shelf teaches “storage.” A hide in a box teaches “bed.” Both may be right on some nights and wrong on others. The reconstruction is a nineteenth-century genre — the furnished ancient room — applied to a site that was never abandoned with its organic fittings in place.

The storm of 1850 and the later sea wall are part of the furniture’s biography. The village was inland, beside a freshwater loch, when it was lived in. Coastal erosion brought the Atlantic to the midden. Concrete and rock armor now keep House 1 from becoming a scatter of slabs on the beach. Wickham-Jones and Historic Environment Scotland have both written about that vulnerability. A dresser that has lasted five thousand years can still be lost in one winter if the wall fails. Preservation here is coastal engineering, not a desert’s accident.

## Grooved Ware, drains, and the village as a single piece of work

The later houses sit in a midden that is itself a made thing: shell, bone, ash, and the sticky matrix Historic Environment Scotland still mentions as insulation. Childe already saw that the village was not a scatter of huts on a clean beach. It was a built mound. Furniture decisions — where the dresser faces, how far the boxes project — are decisions inside that mound. A drain that may have served as a latrine (Wickham-Jones is careful with the “may”) is as much a fitting as the dresser and gets less poetry. A village that manages waste in stone is a village that thinks of the house as a machine.

Grooved Ware pots, when they appear in the houses, are the movable half of the dresser problem. A pot on a shelf is the one object that could have been taken when the village was left. Most were not, or were smashed in place. The dresser’s job, if it was display, was to hold a few of these. If it was storage, it held more. If it was a rear altar in all but name, the pots were offerings. The same sherds support all three readings. That is why this chapter will not pick a winner.

Dating has tightened since Childe. The two-phase village — earlier circular houses, later rectangular ones with projecting boxes — is now the sequence a visitor is given. Radiocarbon on the Heart of Neolithic Orkney sites clusters the late houses in the centuries around 2800–2500 BCE, contemporary with the great passage graves and the stone circles a short walk inland. Furniture at Skara Brae is therefore not a “primitive” prelude to those monuments. It is the domestic version of the same right-angle, slab-on-end habit. Maeshowe’s chamber is a dresser idea at tomb scale: a rear space you face after a low passage.

## The boxes, the cist, and the storm that did not happen

House 7 is the house Childe thought still held its life. He found it the best preserved of the late dwellings: a stone door that could be barred from the inside, incised decoration on the masonry (the same habit turns up in House 8), and a cist built under the eastern wall at the back of the eastern box. Two female skeletons lay in that cist. Childe read them as a foundation deposit, or as a burial older than the house as it stood. Historic Environment Scotland still gives the visitor the foundation-deposit sentence. What the house does not give is a last night. Popular accounts have sometimes dressed House 7 as a disaster tableau — a woman who locked herself in against a storm, a village abandoned at a run. Clarke’s 1972–73 trenches, cut with Anna Ritchie for the National Museum of Antiquities of Scotland, did not find that cinema. They found more houses (11, 12, 13), a thicker sequence of midden and sand, and a village that had been rebuilt and partly cleared on purpose. Childe’s own 1931 final report in the *Proceedings of the Society of Antiquaries of Scotland* had already admitted that some huts looked deliberately abandoned. The storm of 1850 uncovered the site. It did not empty it.

The boxes remain the harder problem. They are short. Some of them, on Towrie’s reading and on the Ness team’s comparisons, held cattle skulls and smashed Grooved Ware; at Links of Noltland the cattle-skull deposit under Structure 9 is published with photographs by Hazel Moore and Graeme Wilson. A rectangular stall of flagstone is a container. It becomes a bed if someone slept in it. It becomes a chest if someone stored in it. It becomes a grave-adjacent box if the wall behind it holds a cist. The later European habit of giving each of those jobs its own named piece of furniture is a habit this village did not share.

## Rinyo, Knap of Howar, and a kit that was already Orkney’s

Walter G. Grant and Childe dug the Braes of Rinyo on Rousay in 1938 and again in 1946. The reports in the *Proceedings of the Society of Antiquaries of Scotland* (vol. 73, 1938–39; vol. 81, 1946–47) describe cellular houses with central hearths, stone beds, dressers, and drains — the Skara Brae kit without the Bay of Skaill. Rinyo also gave Grooved Ware its first clear domestic character; Childe’s older name for the pottery, Rinyo-Clacton Ware, still sits in the footnotes. The site kills the accident theory. House 1’s dresser is not a curiosity produced by one lucky storm and one unique mason. It is one address in a late-Neolithic Orkney habit.

Knap of Howar, on Papa Westray, is earlier and differently equipped. William Traill and William Kirkness cleared the two conjoined houses in the late 1920s; Anna Ritchie re-excavated in 1973 and 1975 and published in *PSAS* 113 (1983). She put the farmstead with Unstan Ware in the centuries around 3500–3100 BCE, before the late rectangular houses on the Bay of Skaill took their standard form. House 1 has upright partitions, a hearth, a stone cupboard. House 2 is divided into three compartments; Ritchie and Historic Environment Scotland both allow that it may have been a workshop or store rather than a dwelling. There is no three-shelf dresser of the House 1 type. The idea is already there: raise a place to sit or sleep, cut a place to put things, keep fire in a known spot. What the later village adds is repetition, the dresser facing the door, and the packing of houses into their own midden.

Skaill knives — the flaked stone tools that are this culture’s everyday cutter — come from the same rooms as the dressers. A furniture history that photographs only the shelves will miss the fact that the room was also a place to work hide and meat. The dresser faced the door. The knives were in the hand.

## What this chapter is for

The series that follows will fill with named woods, named joiners, named patrons. Skara Brae has none of those. It has a plan that repeats, a rear wall that faces the door, and a set of stone fittings that later writers could not help but call furniture. That is enough to start. The first problem in this history is not the invention of the chair. It is the decision, in a particular house, to raise part of the floor and to put a shelf where a guest would look.

Childe wanted a domestic picture he recognized. Towrie and Richards want a ritual topography that still leaves room for dinner. Both are reading the same slabs. The reader who keeps this chapter should keep the disagreement, and the dresser, and the fact that House 8 has neither.
