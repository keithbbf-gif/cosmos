# The Official’s Hat Chair

The Metropolitan Museum’s yokeback armchair (1997.92) is *huanghuali* (*Dalbergia odorifera*), seventeenth century, late Ming to early Qing: 48 inches high, 23 wide, 18¾ deep. The Chinese name on the label is 官帽椅 — official’s-hat chair — because the protruding crestrail was seen to resemble the wings of a gentleman’s hat. The chair has no carving to speak of. The argument is the curve of the splat, the splay of the back legs, the grain. Yale’s high yoke-back (1997.47.1) and Brooklyn’s pair (78.246.1) say the same thing in different rooms. Wang Shixiang’s connoisseurship and Sarah Handler’s *Austere Luminosity* taught a generation of Western readers to see that plainness as a decision, not a lack.

This is not “timeless China.” It is a late imperial hardwood fashion with a specific ecology: tropical hardwoods moving north, a Jiangnan and Beijing elite that had read the wrongness of ostentation into a kind of ostentation, joiners who could cut a mitre and a pipe-joint that later cabinetmakers would treat as a moral lesson.

## Woods, names, and the later market

*Huanghuali*, *zitan* (*Pterocarpus santalinus* and related), *tieli*, *jumu* (southern elm), *nanmu*: the names in catalogs are trade and scholar names, not always Linnaean certainties. *Zitan* is darker, heavier, more court-official in later Qing taste; *huanghuali* is the honey-brown wood of the late Ming chair that American and European collectors bought in the twentieth century. The market has produced fakes, marriages, and optimistic attributions. A serious caption names the museum and the date range, not “Ming” as a brand.

Construction: members often round in section; no nails in the best work; glue as a servant of the joint, not its replacement; seats of cane or hard panel; stretchers that are part of the drawing. The yoke-back (*sichutou guanmaoyi* when the ends protrude, *nanguanmaoyi* when they do not) is one of a family: horseshoe-back armchairs (*quanyi*), painting tables, low back chairs, cabinets with round-corner and square-corner types, *kang* tables for the heated platform of the north.

## The kang and the mixed house

Northern houses had a *kang*, a heated masonry platform, with its own low furniture. Southern houses did not. A history that only photographs the yoke-back in a white gallery misses the platform and the mat that still structured many rooms. High chairs and high tables belong to a reception and studio culture. Sleeping and some family sitting stayed low. Handler is careful about this; coffee-table books are not.

Gardens and studios — the elite spaces of the late Ming — used furniture as a set of positions for looking: a chair toward a rock, a table for a handscroll. Clunas’s *Superfluous Things* puts the taste back among other luxuries (bronzes, paintings, rocks) and among anxiety about spending. The chair is a moral object in essays of the period. It is also a commodity.

## Workshops, not one genius

Named carvers are rarer than named painters. The joiner’s shop is a family and a region (Suzhou, Yangzhou, Beijing palace workshops in the Qing). Palace archives of the Qing (the *Zaobanchu* records) give the later, better-documented end: woods issued, pieces delivered, repairs. Ming shops are reconstructed from the objects and from a few ungenerous texts. Do not invent a “Leonardo of the yokeback.”

Export and the West: Chinese chairs entered European collections as curiosities long before they entered the modernist canon as “functional.” The twentieth-century American taste for Ming furniture (the Museum of Classical Chinese Furniture, later dispersals; the Met’s Dillon Fund purchases) is part of the objects’ biography. 1997.92 is a 1997 acquisition. The chair is seventeenth-century. The spotlight is not.

## What the chair refuses

It refuses upholstery. It refuses the carved skirt of a Qing court piece that wants to look like bronze. It refuses, in the best examples, a back that does not follow the spine. Whether it is comfortable is a sitter’s argument; Rietveld would later claim spiritual comfort for a different geometry. The Ming chair’s claim, in the essays around it, is about correctness and grain. This series will not baptize it as proto-modernist. It will leave it in a late Ming room with a *kang* table, a screen, and a servant who is not in the photograph.
