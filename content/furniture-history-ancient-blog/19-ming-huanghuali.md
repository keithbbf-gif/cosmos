# The Official’s Hat Chair

The Metropolitan Museum’s yokeback armchair (1997.92) is *huanghuali* (*Dalbergia odorifera*), seventeenth century, late Ming to early Qing: 48 inches high, 23 wide, 18¾ deep. The Chinese name on the label is 官帽椅 — official’s-hat chair — because the protruding crestrail was seen to resemble the wings of a gentleman’s hat. The chair has no carving to speak of. The argument is the curve of the splat, the splay of the back legs, the grain. Yale’s high yoke-back (1997.47.1) and Brooklyn’s pair (78.246.1) say the same thing in different rooms. Wang Shixiang’s connoisseurship and Sarah Handler’s *Austere Luminosity* taught a generation of Western readers to see that plainness as a decision, not a lack.

Yale’s chair came as a 1997 gift from the Wright family: Arthur F. Wright and Mary C. Wright, the historians, then their son. Brooklyn’s 78.246.1 is one of a pair, first half of the seventeenth century, 43⅝ by 24 by 23 inches, given by the dealer Alice Boney. The Met’s piece is a Dillon Fund purchase of the same year as Yale’s gift. Three chairs, three routes into American museums, one type. This is not “timeless China.” It is a late imperial hardwood fashion with a specific ecology: tropical hardwoods moving north, a Jiangnan and Beijing elite that had read the wrongness of ostentation into a kind of ostentation, joiners who could cut a mitre and a pipe-joint that later cabinetmakers would treat as a moral lesson.

## Woods, names, and the later market

*Huanghuali*, *zitan* (*Pterocarpus santalinus* and related), *tieli*, *jumu* (southern elm), *nanmu*: the names in catalogs are trade and scholar names, not always Linnaean certainties. *Huanghuali* in the strict modern sense is *Dalbergia odorifera*, a Hainan rosewood whose honey-brown figure takes a polish that looks wet. Later protection and scarcity have made the name a market weapon. *Zitan* is darker, heavier, more court-official in later Qing taste; much of it came from South and Southeast Asia. *Tieli* and *jumu* did everyday and regional work. *Nanmu* (*Phoebe* and kin) is the architectural cousin, used for pillars and for furniture that wanted a quieter brown. A serious caption names the museum and the date range, not “Ming” as a brand.

The market has produced fakes, marriages, and optimistic attributions. A splat from one chair, legs from another, a new cane seat, a wax that hides a repaired tenon: the late twentieth-century boom paid for all of it. Tian Jiaqing’s catalogs of the 1990s and after, and Curtis Evarts’s essays around the Museum of Classical Chinese Furniture, are the filters. When a chair is “Ming” in a sale title and “seventeenth century, late Ming to early Qing” on a museum label, believe the label.

Construction: members often round in section; no nails in the best work; glue as a servant of the joint, not its replacement; seats of cane or hard panel; stretchers that are part of the drawing. The pipe joint (*guanjie*) lets a round rail meet a round leg without a clumsy shoulder. The yoke-back (*sichutou guanmaoyi* when the ends protrude, *nanguanmaoyi* when they do not) is one of a family: horseshoe-back armchairs (*quanyi*), painting tables, low back chairs, cabinets with round-corner and square-corner types, *kang* tables for the heated platform of the north.

The horseshoe-back is the other drawing people remember. The crest rail and arms are a continuous curve, often assembled from five pieces with hidden scarf and tongue-and-groove work, not a single steamed wand. Portland’s early seventeenth-century example (74.25.2), huanghuali and cane, gift of Margery Hoffman Smith, is a public-domain lesson in that joinery: beadwork under the seat, a small roundel on the splat, the rail going forward to become the arms. The Met’s own horseshoe-backs should be checked on the current label before a figure list names a number; 1996.337, sometimes repeated in drafts, is not the painting table. The painting table is 1996.338.

## A table for a scroll, a cupboard that stands

Met 1996.338 is huanghuali, late sixteenth to early seventeenth century, 31½ inches high, 73¼ wide, 29⅞ deep: a painting table (*hua’an*) bought with Vincent Astor Foundation and Dillon Fund money in 1996. It is long enough for a handscroll to be unrolled by two people. The stretchers are part of the drawing. This is studio furniture, not a dining type. A scholar’s room needed a surface that would not fight the paper. The chair faced the table, or faced a rock in a garden, or faced a guest. Handler is careful about those positions. Coffee-table books are not.

Round-corner cabinets (*yuanjiaogui*) and square-corner cabinets (*fangjiaogui*) are the standing storage of the same shops. The round-corner type has hooded stiles and a soft meeting at the top; the hardware is often a face-plate and a lock in *baitong* or brass. The square-corner type is a box that admits it is a box. Pairing is common. So is later marriage of two unrelated carcases. A caption that says “a pair” should say why.

The Met also holds zitan armchairs with calligraphed splats: 1976.193.1a, b and 1976.193.4a, b, Vincent Astor Foundation purchases, seventeenth century, about 40⅛ inches high. The splats carry, or claim to carry, the writing of Suzhou and later literati — Zhu Yunming and Wen Zhengming on one pair, Zhou Tianqiu and Dong Qichang on another. The museum’s notes treat the inscriptions as a fashion: favorite objects in a study were made to hold famous hands. Whether every character is autograph is a specialist’s fight. The furniture fact is simpler. A chair could be a writing surface without being a desk.

## The kang and the mixed house

Northern houses had a *kang*, a heated masonry platform, with its own low furniture. Southern houses did not. A history that only photographs the yoke-back in a white gallery misses the platform and the mat that still structured many rooms. High chairs and high tables belong to a reception and studio culture. Sleeping and some family sitting stayed low. Handler is careful about this; coffee-table books are not.

Gardens and studios — the elite spaces of the late Ming — used furniture as a set of positions for looking: a chair toward a rock, a table for a handscroll. Clunas’s *Superfluous Things* puts the taste back among other luxuries (bronzes, paintings, rocks) and among anxiety about spending. The chair is a moral object in essays of the period. It is also a commodity.

Wen Zhenheng’s *Zhangwu zhi* (*Treatise on Superfluous Things*), compiled in the 1620s, is the period voice Clunas taught English readers to hear. It ranks objects, scolds vulgar display, and in doing so displays a new kind of expertise. A correct chair, a correct table height, a correct placement toward a view: these are furniture laws of a Jiangnan elite that had more money than the essays admit. The yoke-back’s plainness is not poverty. It is a taste that could afford *huanghuali* and still talk about restraint.

## Workshops, not one genius

Named carvers are rarer than named painters. The joiner’s shop is a family and a region (Suzhou, Yangzhou, Beijing palace workshops in the Qing). Palace archives of the Qing (the *Zaobanchu* records of the Imperial Household Department) give the later, better-documented end: woods issued, pieces delivered, repairs, the names of craftsmen who would never be treated as artists in a painting catalog. Ming shops are reconstructed from the objects and from a few ungenerous texts. Do not invent a “Leonardo of the yokeback.”

Gustav Ecke’s *Chinese Domestic Furniture*, printed in Peking in 1944 by Henri Vetch in two hundred copies, is the book that taught a Western shop to see the joints. Ecke took pieces apart, or had Yang Yue draw them as if they had been taken apart: beds, chairs, tables, wardrobes, wash stands. The measured drawings are still the clearest explanation of a pipe joint on paper. The 1962 Tuttle reprint and the later Dover edition made the wartime portfolio a handbook. Ecke’s eye was trained in a European modernism that liked uncarved geometry. That is a fact about the book, not a reason to baptize the chairs as Bauhaus ancestors. They were late Ming and early Qing seats before they were photographs in a 1944 portfolio.

Export and the West: Chinese chairs entered European collections as curiosities long before they entered the modernist canon as “functional.” The twentieth-century American taste for Ming furniture (the Museum of Classical Chinese Furniture in California, later dispersed; the Met’s Dillon Fund purchases) is part of the objects’ biography. Christie’s New York sold the Museum of Classical Chinese Furniture on 19 September 1996. 1997.92 is a 1997 acquisition. The chair is seventeenth-century. The spotlight is not. Minneapolis’s Dayton collection — Ruth and Bruce Dayton’s gifts to the Minneapolis Institute of Art — is the other American room where a reader can still stand in a reconstructed studio rather than in a single-chair spotlight. `[CITE NEEDED: a specific MIA huanghuali accession if used as a figure.]`

## How a yokeback is cut

A good yoke-back is a drawing in the round. The rear legs are one thought from floor to crest; the splat is a second thought that follows a spine without copying it; the arms step down in a sequence that is more comfortable than it looks in a photograph. Cane seats, when original, are a woven plane that gives. Hard seats are a later or a cheaper choice, or a climate choice. The front stretcher is where a sitter’s feet meet the chair; wear there is use-wear, not damage in the moral sense.

Members that look like a single curved stick are often several pieces. The horseshoe rail, as Portland’s label says, is a five-part assembly. The yoke-back’s protruding ends are cut with the grain running through the wing, or they are joined; both solutions exist, and both can be faked. Glue is allowed. A nail in a “classical” chair is a repair or a warning. Wang Shixiang’s vocabulary — the names of joints, the names of types — is the Chinese door. Ecke’s plates are the English one. Handler is the writer who put the chair back in a house with a *kang*.

Hainan’s *huanghuali* and the imported *zitan* were already long-distance woods. The joiner in Suzhou did not grow them. The timber trade that later chapters will follow into colonial teak and American hardwood towns has an earlier, quieter run here: southern and overseas trees, northern and Jiangnan rooms, a taste that made rarity look like virtue.

## Palace lists, and the wood that came south to north

Qing *Zaobanchu* registers — workshop memorials of the Imperial Household Department — list timber issued, pieces delivered, and repairs in a language that is closer to a yard account than to a poem about grain. They are the later, better-documented end of a shop culture the Ming objects ask us to infer. A palace can order a *zitan* set and a repair to a *kang* table in the same season. The registers also remind a reader that “classical hardwood furniture” was never only a Suzhou literati taste. The court bought, stored, and broke furniture. Named craftsmen appear as suppliers. They do not appear as the authors of 1997.92.

Hainan *huanghuali* and imported *zitan* were already a timber trade. The joiner did not grow the tree. Later scarcity, export bans, and a collector’s market that treats *Dalbergia odorifera* as a brand have made the wood louder than the joint. A chair of *jumu* or *nanmu* from the same shop family can be the better teacher of construction because no one has yet paid to fake it as thoroughly. Wang Shixiang looked at those woods too. The American gallery, for a generation, looked first at honey-brown rosewood and stopped.

## What the chair refuses

It refuses upholstery. It refuses the carved skirt of a Qing court piece that wants to look like bronze. It refuses, in the best examples, a back that does not follow the spine. Whether it is comfortable is a sitter’s argument; Rietveld would later claim spiritual comfort for a different geometry. The Ming chair’s claim, in the essays around it, is about correctness and grain. This series will not baptize it as proto-modernist. It will leave it in a late Ming room with a *kang* table, a screen, and a servant who is not in the photograph. Met 1997.92 is 48 inches of that argument, purchased in the year the Wright chair went to Yale. The hat is the rail. The date on the accession card is 1997.
