# Digital Craft and the Question of Wood

In 2004–06, a handful of studios were already milling chair parts on CNC routers from files that had been written, not drawn on a board. By the 2010s the same routers sat in art-school shops and in small-town cabinet shops. The joint could be a finger that no chisel had touched. The romance of the handmade and the fact of the file now share a bench. This chapter does not announce a revolution. It follows a few objects and a few arguments: studio furniture, open-source chairs, the return of solid wood as a luxury, the certified forest, the 3D-printed experiment that still sits badly, and the pandemic house that bought another desk.

## Studio furniture and the gallery

The American studio movement (Wendell Castle, Jere Osgood, the next generations at RISD and the College of the Redwoods) was already mature by 2000. Castle’s stack-laminated seats, then his later digital work, are a single career that crosses this chapter’s date. Gallery furniture is priced like sculpture. It is not a solution to sitting for a city. It is a solution to the problem of what a maker does after IKEA. The Museum of Arts and Design and the Yale Art Gallery’s furniture collections hold the types. Caption a Castle as a Castle, not as “the future of the chair.”

European and Asian studio scenes (Japan’s contemporary *sashimono*, Korean makers redoing *bandaji*, British and Scandinavian slow wood) are not a single “craft revival.” They are local answers to the same cheap-furniture flood.

## Open source, plastic, and the file

Enzo Mari’s *Autoprogettazione* (1974) is a late-century idea that the twenty-first treated as a prophecy: furniture as a set of instructions. Downloadable chairs, WikiHouse-like furniture, the Always-beta piece: some of this is generous. Some is a Silicon Valley verb on a stool. The objects that work usually look like Mari or like a Shaker table. The objects that do not work look like a 3D-printer’s first day.

Printed polymers and recycled plastics (the later Dixons and the ocean-plastic chairs of design fairs) are material essays. Comfort and longevity are still the test. A fair booth is not a ten-year kitchen.

## Wood as a question

FSC and other certifications, the CITES listing of rosewoods (including the *huanghuali* and *zitan* cousins of chapter 19), the fight over teak: the twenty-first century is the first in this series when a reader can watch a species leave the furniture trade in real time. Reclaimed wood, thermally modified wood, CLT architecture’s leftover as a table: a new vocabulary. The luxury market’s “solid walnut slab” with a live edge is a fashion that timber framers find both welcome and embarrassing. It is also, sometimes, a beautiful table.

Carbon talk around furniture is often worse than the objects. Prefer a maker who can name a forest to a brand that says “sustainable” without a species.

## The house after 2020

Dining tables became desks. The office chair entered the bedroom. A global purchase of cheap work seats will be, for a future curator, a dated stratum — like Utility, like the café No. 14. We do not yet have the monograph. This paragraph should go stale. It is still true that the pandemic was a furniture event.

## Soft close, not a product

If this series has a last object, it is not a brand. It is a joint that still has to hold: mortise, screw, dowel, a printed finger, a wedge. Chapter 41 will take the joint apart. Chapter 43 will walk through American mill towns that still cut hardwood for people who want a table to last longer than a catalog season. That is not a destiny. It is one surviving answer.
