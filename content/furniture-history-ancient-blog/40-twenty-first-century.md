# Digital Craft and the Question of Wood

In 2004–06, a handful of studios were already milling chair parts on CNC routers from files that had been written, not drawn on a board. By the 2010s the same routers sat in art-school shops and in small-town cabinet shops. The joint could be a finger that no chisel had touched. The romance of the handmade and the fact of the file now share a bench. This chapter does not announce a revolution. It follows a few objects and a few arguments: studio furniture, open-source chairs, the return of solid wood as a luxury, the certified forest, the 3D-printed experiment that still sits badly, and the pandemic house that bought another desk.

## Studio furniture and the gallery

The American studio movement (Wendell Castle, Jere Osgood, the next generations at RISD and the College of the Redwoods) was already mature by 2000. Castle’s stack-laminated seats, then his later digital work, are a single career that crosses this chapter’s date. Gallery furniture is priced like sculpture. It is not a solution to sitting for a city. It is a solution to the problem of what a maker does after IKEA. The Museum of Arts and Design and the Yale Art Gallery’s furniture collections hold the types. Caption a Castle as a Castle, not as “the future of the chair.”

European and Asian studio scenes (Japan’s contemporary *sashimono*, Korean makers redoing *bandaji*, British and Scandinavian slow wood) are not a single “craft revival.” They are local answers to the same cheap-furniture flood.

## Open source, plastic, and the file

## Mari’s instructions as a furniture type

Enzo Mari’s *Autoprogettazione* (1974) is a late-century idea that the twenty-first treated as a prophecy: furniture as a set of instructions. Pine boards, nails, a booklet. Museums now show builds from the booklet; amateurs photograph their own. The objects that work usually look like Mari or like a Shaker table. The objects that do not work look like a printer’s first day.

Downloadable chairs, WikiHouse-like furniture, the always-beta piece: some of this is generous. Some is a Silicon Valley verb on a stool. Prefer the booklet you can hold to the file that vanishes when a start-up does. Mari’s point was autonomy, not a platform.

Printed polymers and recycled plastics — the later Tom Dixon experiments, the ocean-plastic chairs of design fairs — are material essays. Comfort and longevity are still the test. A fair booth is not a ten-year kitchen. Ask, of any printed lattice in MoMA or Vitra, whether it has been sat on for a decade. Many have not. That is not a moral failure. It is a date.

## Species leaving the trade

FSC and other certifications, the CITES listing of rosewoods — *Dalbergia*, including the *huanghuali* and *zitan* cousins of chapter 19 — and the fight over teak: the twenty-first century is the first in this series when a reader can watch a species leave the furniture trade in real time. Official CITES notices, not design blogs, are the source. Reclaimed wood, thermally modified wood, CLT architecture’s leftover as a table: a new vocabulary. The luxury market’s “solid walnut slab” with a live edge is a fashion that timber framers find both welcome and embarrassing. It is also, sometimes, a beautiful table.

Chapter 39’s LÖVET already met this problem: jacaranda on a 1956 catalogue page, poplar veneer on the 2013 reissue because the species had been protected. Anderson’s mahogany book (chapter 41) is the model for writing the tree before the polish. Carbon talk around furniture is often worse than the objects. Prefer a maker who can name a forest to a caption that says “sustainable” without a species.

Jennifer Anderson’s lesson still holds: a species map of furniture is a map of power. CITES is a late instrument. Earlier instruments were sumptuary laws, naval reserves, and simply running out of a grove. The difference now is speed and a reader who can watch the listing happen.

## A joint, not a destiny

If this series has a last object, it is not a brand and not a slab with a live edge in a showroom. It is a joint that still has to hold: mortise, screw, dowel, a printed finger, a wedge, a cam lock that expects to be undone. Chapter 41 will take the joint apart. Chapter 42 will ask who sat and who carried. The last chapter is reserved for addresses that still cut hardwood as history, not as a catalog. That is not a destiny. It is one surviving answer.

The file and the bench share a week’s work. That is the residual fact. Castle crossed the router without abandoning the seat. Mari’s booklet still makes a table if someone cuts the boards. A rosewood can leave the trade while oak and walnut stay. The twenty-first century did not invent sitting. It invented a few new ways to plan a joint and a few new reasons a species disappears from the invoice.


## Cooke’s studio, Castle’s router, a forest with a name

Edward S. Cooke Jr. wrote the American studio movement as a furniture history, not as a craft fair. *New American Furniture* (MFA Boston, 1989) and the essays that follow it into the 2000s are how a reader keeps Wendell Castle, Jere Osgood, and the RISD and College of the Redwoods generations from collapsing into a single “handmade” caption. Castle’s stack lamination is a twentieth-century method. His later milled work is a twenty-first-century method. The seat is the test in both decades. MAD and the Yale University Art Gallery hold enough examples to make the point without a fair booth.

Open-source furniture after Mari splits. Some builds are pine and nails and a weekend. Some are a platform that wants a verb. The objects that last look like a Shaker table or like Mari’s booklet. The printed polymer chair in a design-museum case is an experiment. Ask the decade question. Many have not been sat on for ten years. That is a date, not a scolding.

CITES listings for *Dalbergia* — official notices — changed invoices for anyone still finishing *huanghuali* or a rosewood lounge. FSC paperwork is a later instrument and only as good as the forest it names. Chapter 39’s jacaranda table, reissued in poplar, already lived this substitution. Prefer a maker who can name a county or a concession to a caption that says nothing but “sustainable.” Anderson’s mahogany book is still the model: the tree before the polish, the labor before the shine.

The live-edge slab is a fashion timber framers find welcome and embarrassing. Sometimes it is a beautiful table. Sometimes it is a log with legs. Caption the forest if the maker did. If the maker did not, say so.


## A fair booth is not a kitchen

Design fairs after 2000 filled halls with chairs that photographed well and sat badly. Ocean plastic, printed lattices, a stool that is a verb: some of these objects entered MoMA and Vitra as experiments, which is a legitimate museum job. The kitchen test is different. Ten years of a body, a spill, a move down a stair. Mari’s booklet passes that test when someone cuts the pine. Castle’s laminated seat has already passed it in a gallery and, sometimes, in a house. A first-day printout usually has not.

The pandemic stratum — cheap desks, a secondhand Aeron, a dining table claimed by a laptop — will date faster than the fair objects, because there are more of them. Utility left a mark on the rail (CC41). The 2020 desk may leave only a sales graph. That is why this paragraph should go stale, and why it still belongs.

Cooke’s studio history, Castle’s two methods, Mari’s booklet, a CITES notice, a named forest: those are the five files this chapter trusts. A brand sentence is not among them. A joint that holds is. The router and the chisel can share a bench in the same week. That is already an ordinary fact in shops that never issued a manifesto.
