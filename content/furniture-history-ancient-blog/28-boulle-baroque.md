# Brass, Tortoiseshell, and Versailles

André-Charles Boulle (1642–1732) was sworn ébéniste to Louis XIV. The Wallace Collection’s cabinet F16, attributed to him and dated to the early 1670s, still has the floral wood marquetry of an older luxury and, on the frieze and around the door, the first serious commitment to metal marquetry — the *première partie* and *contre-partie* of brass and tortoiseshell that would take his name. Caryatid terms of Summer and Autumn, a Warin medal of the king, a pine and oak carcase under ebony: a cabinet that wants to be a garden and a state. Sir Richard Wallace bought it from the Beurdeley shop in 1872. There were small nineteenth-century alterations. There always were.

The Met’s commode 1982.60.82 (c. 1710–20, Linsky Collection) is later Boulle: walnut veneered with ebony, engraved brass and tortoiseshell, gilt-bronze mounts, a verd antique marble top. William Rieder’s Linsky catalog entry (1984) and Wolfram Koeppe’s later notes tie the type to royal and princely bedrooms. Jean-Pierre Samoyault’s *André-Charles Boulle et sa famille* (1979) is the documentary spine. The man had a workshop in the Louvre precincts, a family, a stock of objects that burned in 1720, and a reputation that the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries kept reproducing.

## What *ébéniste* means

In French guild language an *ébéniste* is not a chairmaker (*menuisier*). He is the specialist in veneered case furniture, originally in ebony. Boulle’s shop is the type of that split. Chairs at Versailles — silver furniture in the first dazzling phase, then gilded wood — belong to a different bench. The silver was melted when the wars required it. The myth of a solid-silver Versailles is a true story about a temporary room. What remains in the photographs of the Hall of Mirrors is glass, gilt, and later replacements.

Le Brun’s control of the royal look — Vaux-le-Vicomte before Versailles, then the king’s manufactories — means that a Boulle cabinet is not a private inspiration. The Wallace caryatids have been linked to Le Brun’s terms at Vaux. Furniture is part of a decorative state. Peter Thornton’s *Seventeenth-Century Interior Decoration in England, France and Holland* (1978) is still the comparative book.

## Tortoiseshell, brass, and the ethics of a surface

Hawksbill shell, brass, pewter, stained horn, ebony, imported tropical woods: the surface is a map of violence and trade as well as a map of skill. Engraving the brass, filling the lines with black, matching a *contre-partie* so that the leftover of one panel becomes the field of another — this is economy and dazzle together. Later “Boulle” is a nineteenth-century industry (the Beurdeley shop that sold F16 is part of that industry). Caption a piece as “Boulle technique” when it is not the man’s hand.

Gilt-bronze mounts (*bronzes d’ameublement*) are a second shop: the founder, the chaser, the gilder. A commode is a collaboration. The marble slab is a third. The ébéniste is the person who makes them agree.

## Beds, etiquette, and the chair of the king

The *lit de parade*, the *chaise de toilette*, the rules about who may sit in the king’s presence: Versailles is a furniture of etiquette as much as a furniture of marquetry. Saint-Simon’s memoirs are literature, not a catalog, and still the best witness to the politics of a stool. A *tabouret* can be a privilege. The duchess’s right to sit is a right named for a piece of wood.

Italian Baroque (Roman consoles, Florentine *pietre dure* tables, the baldacchino’s wood-and-bronze cousins) and English Restoration cabinets (Japanned, silver-mounted) are the contemporary other courts. This chapter stays in Paris because the documents and the surviving objects are densest there, and because “Baroque furniture” as a textbook phrase is usually French royal furniture plus an Italian table.

## F16 in the early 1670s

The Wallace Collection’s catalog entry on F16 is the paragraph to keep in front of the object. Cabinet, attributed to André-Charles Boulle, about 1670–75; 186.7 by 123 by 65 cm. Boulle had just been appointed cabinetmaker to Louis XIV. Only four similar cabinets are known. It is a descendant of earlier ebony cabinets on stands, now Baroque in character: classical ornament, a sculptural pair of carved half-figures — Summer and Autumn — whose terms echo the upper storey of the grand salon at Vaux-le-Vicomte and can be linked to a drawing by Charles Le Brun. Floral wood marquetry still dominates. Daffodils, peonies, narcissi, roses, honeysuckle; two grasshoppers, a bee, a beetle. The Wallace note ties the insects to the fashion for Dutch still-life and to a garden brought indoors. Metal marquetry is already present on the frieze drawer and around the central door — the first serious commitment to the brass-and-shell language that would take the man’s name. The medal on the front is Jean Warin’s Louis XIV. Cabinets of this kind were made to impress; the central section might also have shown prized works of art.

Sir Richard Wallace bought it from the Beurdeley shop in 1872. There appear to have been small nineteenth-century alterations, perhaps in that shop. A technical comparison published in *Technè* (the Rijksmuseum–Wallace–Getty group of floral cabinets) describes F16’s carcase as oak, veneered with purplewood, sycamore and various woods, with marquetry of ebony, brass, pewter, and copper, gilt-bronze mounts, walnut drawers, terms of painted pine. `[CITE NEEDED: confirm those woods against the Wallace’s own current materials line if it differs from the Technè table.]` The point for this series is the date. Flowers still in wood; metal already in the frieze. Boulle’s reputation is being made on this object, not recalled.

He did not stamp. Later copies and pastiches are everywhere. The Wallace is frank: attributions rest on archives plus the forms, the marquetry, and the mounts. A monumental wardrobe in the same collection is one of the few pieces that can be tied to Boulle with certainty because it was recorded, with its pair, in his workshop in 1715.

## 1982.60.82 and the Trianon model

The Met’s commode 1982.60.82 is later: about 1710–20; walnut veneered with ebony, engraved brass and tortoiseshell, gilt-bronze mounts, verd antique marble; 34 1/2 by 50 1/2 by 24 3/4 inches; Linsky Collection, 1982. William Rieder’s entry in *The Jack and Belle Linsky Collection* (1984), no. 126, is the documentary spine beside Samoyault. In 1708 Boulle executed two “bureaux” for Louis XIV’s bedroom at the Palais de Trianon (the Grand Trianon). He was paid in September 1708 and April 1709, three thousand livres. On 3 July 1708 the duc d’Antin wrote to the king that he had been to Trianon to see the second *bureau* by Boulle: as beautiful as the other, and it suited the room wonderfully. By 1729 inventories called them *commodes*. In 1790 they went to the Garde-Meuble and escaped the Revolutionary sales; later they lived at the Bibliothèque Mazarine and were nicknamed “Commodes Mazarines”; in 1933 they moved to Versailles, where they remain.

The Trianon pair invented a type: table plus the newly emerging commode, two drawers under the top, a sarcophagus body, extra tapered scroll legs. John Morley later wrote of the way the body is “slung between the eccentric legs,” a little like carriage construction. The Linsky commode is similar in construction, in the bronzes, and in the engraved ornament on the brass; Rieder calls it an early workshop version. An unidentified “3” is painted on the underside in eighteenth-century script. Other eighteenth-century versions include Petworth (ex-Hamilton Palace, said to have come from Beckford at Fonthill), a pair at Vaux-le-Vicomte stamped by Étienne Levasseur — who specialized in making and restoring Boulle-technique furniture — and several sale references (de Selle, Randon de Boisset, Marigny, Merle) that have not been nailed to surviving carcases. In the nineteenth century the model was copied hard: Blake’s pair in the Frick; Fourdinois examples in Rouen and Madrid. Caption 1982.60.82 as workshop Boulle of the 1710s, after a royal bedroom prototype, not as the Trianon pair.

## The 1720 fire and the problem of a name

Samoyault’s *André-Charles Boulle et sa famille* (1979) is the family archive: a workshop in the Louvre precincts, sons, a stock of objects, a fire in 1720 that took finished work and materials. The man lived until 1732. The name outlived the shop. Eighteenth-century *ébénistes* (Levasseur among them) restored and repeated the technique. Nineteenth-century Paris — Beurdeley, who sold F16 — made “Boulle” an industry. Caption a piece as “Boulle technique” when the hand is not the man’s. He did not stamp. Wishful attribution is the trade’s sport.

Hawksbill shell, brass, pewter, stained horn, ebony, imported tropical woods: the surface is a map of violence and trade as well as a map of skill. Engraving the brass, filling the lines with black, matching a *contre-partie* so that the leftover of one panel becomes the field of another — economy and dazzle together. Gilt-bronze mounts are a second shop: founder, chaser, gilder. Boulle, privileged in the Louvre, could flout the guild split that later *ébénistes* would trip over (Cressent, in the next chapter, will be prosecuted for casting his own bronzes). The marble slab is a third shop. The *ébéniste* is the person who makes them agree.

The silver furniture of Versailles’s first dazzling phase was melted when the wars required it. The myth of a solid-silver palace is a true story about a temporary room. What remains in photographs of the Hall of Mirrors is glass, gilt, and later replacements. Daniel Meyer’s *Versailles: Furniture of the Royal Palace* (2002) is the inventory book. Peter Thornton’s *Seventeenth-Century Interior Decoration* (1978) still holds the comparison with England and Holland. A Boulle cabinet is not a private inspiration. It is part of a decorative state that ran from Vaux to the manufactories.

The *tabouret* remains the period’s smallest political object. Saint-Simon is not a cataloger. He is still the witness to a duchess’s right to sit, named for a stool. Chairs belong to the *menuisier*. Case furniture belongs to the *ébéniste*. The split is the chapter’s grammar. Rococo will keep the split and change the curve.


## Clocks, pedestals, and the English word “Buhl”

Hawksbill shell is now a CITES problem as well as a conservation one. Museums keep the historic objects and argue about how to caption the animal. Substitutes in later “Boulle” (horn, plastic, stained wood) are not a moral improvement if the caption still says tortoiseshell. Name the material the lab named.



Boulle’s shop made clock cases and pedestals as well as cabinets. The clock is a collaboration with a movement maker; the case is what the room sees. Later English “Buhl” work — the spelling is a giveaway — is a Regency and Victorian industry that treated the technique as a surface fashion, sometimes on furniture that had never seen the Louvre. A London commode with brass in turtleshell can be 1820 and honest about it, or 1820 pretending to be 1710. Auction adjectives still blur the two.

Italian *pietre dure* tables and Roman consoles of the same century are the other court’s hardstone luxury. They do not use Boulle’s cut-brass method. They use a different map of stone. English Restoration japanning and silver-mounted cabinets are a third luxury, looking at Asia through lacquer and at France through envy. This chapter stays in Paris because the documents are densest there, and because textbook “Baroque furniture” is usually French royal work plus an Italian table. The honest reader will walk to Florence and to London afterward.

The Wallace is frank that Boulle did not stamp, and that a monumental wardrobe in the same collection is one of the few pieces tied to him with certainty because it was recorded, with its pair, in the workshop in 1715. That sentence is the antidote to a sale-room adjective. F16 is attributed; 1982.60.82 is workshop of the 1710s after a royal model. The 1715 wardrobe is a document. Three different temperatures of “Boulle,” one gallery visitor’s afternoon. Le Brun’s terms at Vaux, Warin’s medal on the door, Beurdeley’s 1872 invoice: the decorative state, the king’s face, and the nineteenth-century shop that kept the name in stock.
