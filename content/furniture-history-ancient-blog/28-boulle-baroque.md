# Brass, Tortoiseshell, and Versailles

André-Charles Boulle (1642–1732) was sworn ébéniste to Louis XIV. The Wallace Collection’s cabinet F16, attributed to him and dated to the early 1670s, still has the floral wood marquetry of an older luxury and, on the frieze and around the door, the first serious commitment to metal marquetry — the *première partie* and *contre-partie* of brass and tortoiseshell that would take his name. Caryatid terms of Summer and Autumn, a Warin medal of the king, a pine and oak carcase under ebony: a cabinet that wants to be a garden and a state. Sir Richard Wallace bought it from the Beurdeley shop in 1872. There were small nineteenth-century alterations. There always were.

The Met’s commode 1982.60.82 (c. 1710–20, Linsky Collection) is later Boulle: walnut veneered with ebony, engraved brass and tortoiseshell, gilt-bronze mounts, a verd antique marble top. William Rieder’s Linsky catalog entry (1984) and Wolfram Koeppe’s later notes tie the type to royal and princely bedrooms. Jean-Pierre Samoyault’s *André-Charles Boulle et sa famille* (1979) is the documentary spine. The man had a workshop in the Louvre precincts, a family, a stock of objects that burned in 1720, and a reputation that the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries kept reproducing.

## What *ébéniste* means

In French guild language an *ébéniste* is not a chairmaker (*menuisier*). He is the specialist in veneered case furniture, originally in ebony. Boulle’s shop is the type of that split. Chairs at Versailles — silver furniture in the first dazzling phase, then gilded wood — belong to a different bench. The silver was melted when the wars required it. The myth of a solid-silver Versailles is a true story about a temporary room. What remains in the photographs of the Hall of Mirrors is glass, gilt, and later replacements.

Le Brun’s control of the royal look — Vaux-le-Vicomte before Versailles, then the king’s manufactories — means that a Boulle cabinet is not a private inspiration. The Wallace caryatids have been linked to Le Brun’s terms at Vaux. Furniture is part of a decorative state. Peter Thornton’s *Seventeenth-Century Interior Decoration in England, France and Holland* (1978) is still the comparative book.

## Tortoiseshell, brass, and the ethics of a surface

Hawksbill shell, brass, pewter, stained horn, ebony, imported tropical woods: the surface is a map of violence and trade as well as a map of skill. Engraving the brass, filling the lines with black, matching a *contre-partie* so that the leftover of one panel becomes the field of another — this is economy and dazzle together. Later “Boulle” is a nineteenth-century industry (the Beurdeley shop that sold F16 is part of that industry). Caption a piece as “Boulle technique” when it is not the man’s hand.

Gilt-bronze mounts (*bronzes d’ameublement*) are a second shop: the founder, the chaser, the gilder. A commode is a collaboration. The marble slab is a third. The ébéniste is the person who makes them agree.

## Beds, etiquette, and the chair of the king

The *lit de parade*, the *chaise de toilette*, the rules about who may sit in the king’s presence: Versailles is a furniture of etiquette as much as a furniture of marquetry. Saint-Simon’s memoirs are literature, not a catalog, and still the best witness to the politics of a stool. A *tabouret* can be a privilege. The duchess’s right to sit is a right named for a piece of wood.

Italian Baroque (Roman consoles, Florentine *pietre dure* tables, the baldacchino’s wood-and-bronze cousins) and English Restoration cabinets (Japanned, silver-mounted) are the contemporary other courts. This chapter stays in Paris because the documents and the surviving objects are densest there, and because “Baroque furniture” as a textbook phrase is usually French royal furniture plus an Italian table.
