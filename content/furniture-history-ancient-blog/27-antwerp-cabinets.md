# Antwerp Cabinets

The V&A’s Flemish cabinet on stand (W.61-1923), given by Mrs E. Hearn, is thought to be Antwerp work of the second quarter of the seventeenth century; the stand may be a nineteenth-century addition. Ebony veneer on an oak or pine carcase, painted panels (the Prodigal Son cycle, in this case), a facade of small drawers and a central door: a collector’s architecture. The 1996–97 conservation, published in the V&A *Conservation Journal* 23, had to stabilize flaking paint before the object could travel. The cabinet had become too famous to be as fragile as it was.

Antwerp in the 1600s made paintings, prints, books, harpsichords, and these cabinets. They were sold to a Europe that was inventing the collection: coins, shells, small paintings, medals. The cabinet is a *studiolo* that shuts. Reinier Baarsen and the Rijksmuseum catalogs, and Ria Fabri’s work on Antwerp cabinets, are the scholarly doors. Ebony is the skin. Ebony is also a tropical wood arriving through Portuguese and Dutch routes — a materials fact that belongs in chapter 41 as well.

## A facade of doors

The type varies: the *Kunstschrank* in Augsburg (the Pommeranian cabinet, the one now in the Getty’s cousin-literature; the Uppsala art cabinet) is more German and more encyclopedic, with hidden drawers and a program of knowledge. The Antwerp cabinet is often more pictorial: oil panels as the “precious stones” of the facade. Both are lockable theatres. A key is part of the furniture.

Veneer technology — sawing ebony thin, gluing it to a cheaper carcase, profiling moldings in the veneer rather than in the solid — is the technical sentence. The V&A conservators noted that ebony was not used in the solid for the decorative profiles. That is a shop decision about cost and about the way ebony behaves. The nineteenth-century stand on W.61-1923 is a warning: cabinets lose their legs and gain new ones in the trade.

## Spain, the *escritorio*, and the vargueno

The Spanish *vargueno* (a fall-front writing cabinet on a stand) is a cousin, with a different hardware language and a different interior of little columns. It is sometimes a colonial object as well as a peninsular one. American *varguenos* in viceregal shops mix Iberian type and local woods. Caption the wood. Do not assume Toledo.

South German and Swiss *cabinets* with architectural interiors (the “miniature palace”) feed the same hunger. The user is a person who owns small things and wants them to have a city to live in.

## Pictorial furniture

Painted cabinets put Rubens’s city on a drawer front (not always Rubens; often a specialist in small figures). The Prodigal Son on W.61-1923 is a moral cycle on a luxury object — a tension the owner may have enjoyed. Furniture here is a patron of painting. The later European split between “fine art” and “decorative art” has to work to pull the panels out of the box. Some panels were pulled; some boxes arrived in museums already looted of their pictures.

## Ports, ebony, and a skin that cups

Antwerp’s cabinets make no sense without the port. Ebony from the Indian Ocean world — *Diospyros* and related dark hardwoods moving on Portuguese and then Dutch routes — arrived as logs and left as leaves. The V&A conservators on W.61-1923 were blunt: the decorative profiles are veneer, not solid ebony. Oak or pine takes the weight. The tropical wood is a skin. Chapter 41 will return to that geography. Here the point is a shop decision. Solid ebony is brittle, expensive, and heavy. A sawyer who can cut it thin has multiplied a log into a facade.

The 1996–97 campaign, published in the V&A *Conservation Journal* 23, had to stabilize flaking paint before the cabinet could travel. Ebony veneer cups when the glue fails; the cheap carcase moves; the oil panels, set into the facade like stones, become islands in a shifting frame. A cabinet that looks immortal in a photograph is often a patient. The stand on W.61-1923 may be a nineteenth-century addition — Mrs E. Hearn’s gift arrived already famous, and famous cabinets lose their legs in the trade. A new table is invented so the box can stand in a gallery. Read that sentence onto any ebony cabinet whose feet look too polite.

When the Scheldt’s fortunes changed, the type did not die. It moved with craftsmen and buyers toward Amsterdam, Augsburg, and later Paris. Reinier Baarsen’s Rijksmuseum catalogs and Ria Fabri’s work on Antwerp cabinets are the scholarly doors. The clientele was already international: Spanish, Dutch, German collectors who wanted coins, shells, medals, and small paintings to have a city to live in. The key is part of the furniture. A *studiolo* that shuts is a *studiolo* that can refuse the wrong guest.

## The Prodigal as a lockable sermon

The painted panels on W.61-1923 tell the Prodigal Son. They are not “decoration added to furniture.” They are why some cabinets were commissioned. Rubens’s city made small-figure specialists as well as altarpieces; a drawer front is a panel a painter could finish in a week and a collector could own without a chapel. Other cycles run to landscapes, the four continents, tiny battles. Pulling the panels out to hang them as pictures — a nineteenth-century habit, cousin to the cassone split in chapter 26 — is a theft of context even when the owner had legal title. Some boxes arrived in museums already looted of their pictures. Caption the empty frames as empty.

Furniture here is a patron of painting. The later European split between “fine art” and “decorative art” has to work to pull the panels out of the box. A moral cycle on a luxury object is a tension the owner may have enjoyed. The son wastes a fortune; the cabinet stores one.

## Augsburg’s encyclopedia, Antwerp’s picture

The German *Kunstschrank* is a cousin with a different appetite. The Pomeranian cabinet — made in Augsburg for Philip II of Pomerania, documented in a famous set of engravings, destroyed in Berlin in 1945 — was an encyclopedia in a box: hidden drawers, mineral specimens, a microcosm of knowledge. The Uppsala art cabinet (Augsburg work, now in the Gustavianum) survives and is the teaching object for that encyclopedic type. Virginie Spenlé and the Getty’s related literature are the door. Both traditions are lockable theatres. The Antwerp cabinet is usually more pictorial than encyclopedic: oil as the precious stone. The Augsburg cabinet is more mineral and mechanical. Do not mash them into one “Baroque cabinet.”

South German and Swiss cabinets with architectural interiors — the miniature palace, colonnades inside a door — feed the same hunger. The user is a person who owns small things. The furniture’s job is to give those things a street.

## A Spanish fall-front: 60.52a, b

The Metropolitan’s drop-front desk on chest, 60.52a, b, is Spanish, seventeenth century: carved, gilded, and partly gilded painted bone; wrought iron; total height 63 3/8 inches; gift of the Duchesse de Richelieu, 1960. The catalog’s useful sentence is about geometry. The patterning shows “the fondness for Islamic design that survived long after the Reconquest.” A *vargueño* (the Met’s spelling; *vargueno* and *escritorio* also circulate) is a traveling writing cabinet. The fall-front becomes a writing surface. The interior is a facade of little columns and drawers. The stand is a second object; some stands are later, as on the Antwerp cabinet.

A walnut *vargueño*, Met 31.33.57, Andrews bequest, 1931, 57 1/8 by 41 3/4 by 13 1/2 inches, is the plainer cousin. Boston’s MFA 1990.614a, early seventeenth century, walnut with iron mounts backed with velvet and bone inlay, keeps the hardware language that photographs as jewelry and is in fact a lockable architecture. Viceregal American versions — Mexican and Peruvian shops — mix the Iberian type with local woods. Caption the species when a catalog has dared to name them. Do not assume Toledo because the iron is pierced.

The *vargueño* and the Antwerp cabinet are not the same object. One writes; one collects. Both are facades you can close. The sixteenth-century Italian *studiolo* had already taught Europe that a room could be a piece of furniture. The seventeenth-century cabinet teaches that the room can shrink until it sits on a stand.

What remains, after the stands have been swapped and the panels have been stolen and the ebony has cupped, is a European hunger that started in a duke’s lining at Gubbio and went to market in a port. Paris will answer with brass and shell.


## Forchondt, St Luke, and a cabinet as an export

The Forchondt family of Antwerp — dealers as much as makers — shipped *kunstkasten* to Vienna, Lisbon, and the Spanish Americas. Ria Fabri’s two Academy volumes (1991 on type and history; 1993 on the art-historical program) remain the place a reader goes when a label says only “Flemish school.” The Guild of St Luke enrolled the painters who supplied the little panels; the cabinetmakers were a related but not identical trade. A cabinet is a contract among at least three benches: the carcase, the veneer, and the picture.

Rijksmuseum BK-NM-11906-1 is the type made public: softwood, ebony veneer, painted scenes after Rubens and his shop, Antwerp, about 1650. Baarsen, in the 2000 Rijksmuseum dossier *17th-Century Cabinets*, puts the furniture around 1640 or 1650; Fabri’s window is wider (after 1639, before 1670). The central allegory has been read as a Peace of Münster picture — 1648, the year the northern Netherlands left the war and Antwerp did not recover the Scheldt. If that reading holds, the cabinet is a political souvenir you can lock. If it does not, it is still a Rubens economy in miniature: studio inventions, copied small, sold as the city’s other export besides prints.

BK-NM-4789, also in Amsterdam, does Genesis instead of peace: Adam and Eve, Cain and Abel, a portico of Tuscan colonnettes when the doors open, mirrors behind small doors. The paintings have been passed among Frans Francken II, Hans Jordaens III, and caution. The argument is not which genius signed the drawer. It is that Antwerp could industrialize sacred history at drawer scale and still call the result furniture.

BK-NM-5669 drops the oil panels. Tortoiseshell over a red ground, bone stringing, silver mounts, Antwerp about 1655–60, a carcase of oak and softwood. The Rijksmuseum’s own note guesses a Paris-facing market: quieter facade, same inner theatre of drawers and a mirrored stage. Shell is another tropical skin, like ebony, and it will return under Boulle’s name in the next chapter. Here it is already a Flemish luxury, sawn thin and glued to a tree that grew nearer home.

## Amsterdam’s answer, and why the port still matters

Herman Doomer’s cabinet in New York (Met 2011.181, about 1640–50) is not Antwerp work. It is Amsterdam’s claim on the same ebony: oak veneered with ebony, snakewood, rosewood, kingwood, cedar; mother-of-pearl, ivory, green-stained bone; an interior “stage” lined with mirror. Rembrandt painted Doomer (Met 29.100.1). The portrait is why casual visitors find the cabinet. The cabinet is why the portrait matters to this series. After the Scheldt silted as a political fact, the tropical woods and the clients moved north. Antwerp kept making pictorial cabinets for a Catholic and Spanish world; Amsterdam made severe black boxes for a Calvinist and global one. Baarsen’s Amsterdam publications are the corrective to any map that stops at the Scheldt.

Ripple moldings — those vibrating ebony profiles that catch a gallery light — are a Dutch specialty more than a Flemish one. They are also a machine for showing that the wood is thin and expensive. A solid ebony molding of that profile would be a waste and a crack waiting. The shop saws the tropical wood like cloth.

## What a key is for

A cabinet without a lock is a cupboard. The Antwerp type is a lockable claim: medals, shells, a miniature, a relic, a packet of letters. Inventories list the contents more often than they describe the joints. When nineteenth-century owners pulled the painted panels to hang as pictures, they kept the gold and threw away the lock’s meaning. Some museums now show a cabinet and, in another room, panels that once belonged to a sibling piece. The honest caption says so.

Women used these objects. A writing cabinet with a fall front is a desk as well as a shrine. Spanish *vargueños* — the essay has already named them — traveled to New Spain with that double job. Viceregal woods (cedar, *granadillo*, later identifications that catalogs still argue) change the weight. A Toledo iron mount on a Mexican carcase is a colonial sentence, not a failure of style.

The later historicist cabinet — Napoleon III, a Rothschild house, a dealer’s “Antwerp, circa 1650” that X-rays as 1870 — is now a conservation specialty. Veneer species, nail types, and the chemistry of the black can sort some of them. A beautiful lie is still a document of the century that needed the lie. It is not a seventeenth-century shop.

Ebony’s afterlife is a forest history. Indian Ocean and African *Diospyros*, later substitutes, later stained fruitwood: chapter 41 will take the tree. This chapter’s job is the city that turned the tree into a lockable picture.
