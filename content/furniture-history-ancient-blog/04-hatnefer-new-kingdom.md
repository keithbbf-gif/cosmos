# Hatnefer’s Chair

The Metropolitan Museum’s Egyptian Expedition of 1935–36 was working the hillside below Senenmut’s tomb chapel (TT 71) at Sheikh Abd el-Qurna when it found the untouched burial of Hatnefer and Ramose. Hatnefer was Senenmut’s mother. The chair now numbered 36.3.152 was not inside the chamber. It stood outside the entrance. In the division of finds the Egyptian government gave it to New York. The museum’s own label is the sentence to trust: boxwood, cypress, ebony, linen cord; height 53 cm; seat height 24.5 cm; date in the reign of Thutmose II and the early joint reign, about 1492–1473 BCE.

The linen seat is original. That fact is rarer than gold. Most Egyptian chairs in photographs have a restored mesh. Hatnefer’s still has the cord that someone, in Thebes, wove across the rails. The joins are mortise-and-tenon, pegged; the braces to the back and seat are pegged; the whole was “reinforced with resinous glue” (Met catalog). On the back, in a row of protective signs, Bes stands at the center, with *tit* (Isis knot) and *djed* (stability) on either side. The chair is low. In banquet paintings, the Met notes, women often sit on chairs of this height; in offering scenes the tomb owner and wife take full-size chairs side by side (the museum points to 68.58).

A second Theban chair, bought in 1912 and said to come from Dra Abu el-Naga, is Met 12.182.28: tamarisk and reed, lion legs, forked-branch angle braces, a restored mesh, a modern central slat. Together the two chairs are the best introduction to New Kingdom domestic seating that does not require a ticket to Cairo.

## What a Theban joiner actually did

Killen’s chapters on stools and chairs (*Ancient Egyptian Furniture* I) remain the technical spine. The New Kingdom shop had a wider kit than Reisner could reconstruct from gold dust: boxwood for fine members, cypress, cedar, ebony inlay, ivory, leather and linen for seats. Pegs are often of a contrasting wood, left visible. The forked-branch brace on 12.182.28 is a piece of tree that still remembers being a fork. Egyptian joinery is not ashamed of the orchard.

The folding stool — Met 12.182.49, wood inlaid with ebony and ivory, Dynasty 18, about 1550–1295 BCE — is a different carpenter’s problem. Two X-frames, a leather or fabric seat (usually lost), duck-head or plain terminals, a pivot that has to work in sand and in a tent. Tutankhamun’s tomb held several. The type is military and courtly at once: a seat that travels. Rome will later call its descendant a *sella curulis* and pretend the idea is Roman.

Lion legs continue from Hetepheres. On 12.182.28 they are carved in the wood, not drawn in gold. The paw on a drum is now a Theban workshop habit. Bes on Hatnefer’s back splat is household religion in furniture form: the same god who appears on beds and on the walls of rooms where women gave birth. A chair can be an amulet you sit on.

## Carter 91, JE 62028: the chair that ate the bibliography

Howard Carter’s object 91 — “throne (or ceremonial chair)” in the Murray–Nuttall handlist — is Cairo JE 62028, exhibition number 1, now also GEM 4573 in the Grand Egyptian Museum’s numbering. The associated footrest is JE 62046. The Griffith Institute’s Carter archive and the Tutankhamun Spatial Archive both keep the paper trail: found wrapped in linen in the Antechamber; wood covered with gold and silver, inlaid with glass, faience, and semi-precious stones; height 103.5 cm, width 59 cm, length 73 cm (GEM). Side panels are winged uraei holding the king’s cartouche. The backrest shows the king and queen under a floral canopy, the Aten’s rays ending in hands. GEM’s note is dry and useful: “Although adult-sized, this armchair must date from the beginning of Tutankhamun’s reign.” The Aten on a throne that later lived in a restored Amun world is a political palimpsest, not a decorator’s inconsistency.

Carter published the piece in *The Tomb of Tut.ankh.Amen*, vol. 1 (London: Cassell, 1923), pp. 46, 99, 117–19, with Burton’s photographs (in situ, pl. xxiv; studio, pls. lxii–lxiv). Beinlich and Saleh, *Corpus der hieroglyphischen Inschriften aus dem Grab des Tutanchamun* (Oxford: Griffith Institute, 1989), pp. 35–36, handle the texts. The footrest’s nine bows and *rekhyt* birds are the standard image of peoples under the king’s feet. A chair, here, is a map.

The gold throne has starved other Tutankhamun furniture of air: the cedar chair JE 62029, the ecclesiastical thrones, the beds with Ammut and bovine heads, the white-painted boxes. Killen’s catalog is the place to put them back in a sequence of types. This chapter keeps 91 in view because it is the object a reader already knows, and because its joinery is still Egyptian joinery under the metal — rails, stiles, animal terminals, a seat that is a chair, not a platform.

## Paintings, banquets, and who sits low

Tomb paintings at Thebes are furniture catalogs with wine. Low chairs for women at banquets, stools for attendants, the tomb-owner couple on high chairs in the offering scene: the Met’s commentary on 36.3.152 and 12.182.28 is the cautious version of a gendered seating plan. It should not be hardened into “women always sat low.” Height tracks scene type as much as sex. A workman on a three-legged stool in a craft scene is not a queen.

Stools outnumber chairs in the archaeological count, as Killen’s pages make obvious. The chair is already a marked seat. To have a back is to have a little architecture behind the body. Hatnefer’s back is short; Tutankhamun’s is a chapel. Between them sits the ordinary elite chair that tomb paintings record and that termites and reuse destroyed.

## Glue, cord, and the honesty of 36.3.152

Resinous glue in Hatnefer’s joins is a laboratory fact the Met is willing to print. It matters because later mythologies of “pure wood joinery” like to skip adhesives. Egyptian carpenters were not furniture Puritans. They pegged, they glued, they webbed, they inlaid. The original linen cord on 36.3.152 is the other half of that honesty. A restored seat, however careful (12.182.28), is a modern weaver’s guess. Hatnefer’s seat is a dated textile still doing its job in a gallery.

The chair’s find-spot — outside the entrance — keeps a small mystery. Was it used in the funeral and left? Was it a threshold offering? The museum does not invent a ceremony. Neither should this essay. The object was there. The chamber was intact. The mother of Hatshepsut’s great steward sat, at some point, 24.5 cm above the floor.

## Amarna, Deir el-Medina, and the stools the gold did not eat

Amarna’s houses, abandoned in a hurry in the late fourteenth century BCE, have given up stool fragments, bed fittings, and a lot of mudbrick that once held wooden poles. The city is a better document of ordinary elite sitting than KV62, because it is a city. Barry Kemp’s excavation reports are architecture-first; the furniture is scattered through object lists. `[CITE NEEDED: a specific Amarna stool in the Ashmolean or Berlin with a published drawing.]` What can be said without a new number: the folding stool and the three-legged stool both lived in the same town as the Aten throne that Tutankhamun later took to the Valley.

Deir el-Medina, the workmen’s village of the later New Kingdom, is the other corrective. Ostraca and tomb paintings show beds, stools, and a standard of comfort for people who cut the kings’ tombs. A workman’s stool is not Hatnefer’s chair. It is the reason Hatnefer’s chair looks expensive. Killen’s catalog of stools is the typology; the village is the sociology.

Leather seats, now usually gone, were as common as linen cord. A folding stool without its leather is a pair of Xs. Museum restorations that string a modern hide are honest when they say so. The Met’s 12.182.49 is a frame waiting for a skin.

## Theban woods and the river

Boxwood for fine members, tamarisk for a cheaper chair (12.182.28), cypress, cedar, ebony in strips: the New Kingdom shop is a palette. Some of these woods are Egyptian. Some are not. The same boats that brought cedar for temples brought chair parts, or the logs they were cut from. A “Theban” chair is a Theban assembly. The tree may have stood in Lebanon or in Nubia.

Pegs of contrasting color are a joiner’s signature that does not need a name. They show on Hatnefer’s chair as dots in the rails. A modern viewer who has been taught that “good furniture hides the fasteners” is looking with a nineteenth-century eye. Dynasty 18 liked the peg to be seen. It is a rhythm as much as a lock.

## Senenmut’s hill, an intact tomb, and a chair left outside

Ambrose Lansing and William C. Hayes, writing the Metropolitan’s 1935–36 season in the *Bulletin of the Metropolitan Museum of Art* (January 1937, section II), are the first published witnesses. The tomb of Hatnefer and Ramose was found on 11 January 1936, under the terrace of Senenmut’s chapel TT 71 at Sheikh Abd el-Qurna. It was intact. Hatnefer’s coffin in New York is 36.3.1; her heart scarab is in the same accession series; Senenmut himself is represented in the museum by 36.3.252 and a thick modern bibliography. The chair, 36.3.152, was not among the coffins. It stood in front of the entrance. The Egyptian government gave it to New York in the division of finds. Rogers Fund, 1936.

The household around the chair is the social fact the gold throne in Cairo cannot supply. Hatnefer was the mother of Hatshepsut’s great steward, born late in the reign of Ahmose I if the museum’s biography is right, dead as an old woman with grey hair. Ramose, her husband, looks in the reports like a man of fifty or sixty who never rose as far as his son. Six other poorly wrapped bodies — three women, three children — shared the tomb. Lansing and Hayes floated a family tragedy. Later writers have been less sure. This essay will not invent a plague. It will keep the chair’s find-spot: outside, at the threshold of an intact burial, in the reign of Thutmose II and the early joint reign, about 1492–1473 BCE. Elite, not royal. A 53-centimeter chair with a 24.5-centimeter seat, linen still on the rails, Bes still on the splat.

Hayes’s later *Scepter of Egypt* (Harvard, 1953–59) is the museum handbook that put Hatnefer’s generation on a shelf next to Wah’s Middle Kingdom tomb and the Ramesside family of Khonsu. The comparison is useful only if it stays material. Wah’s tomb, found in 1920 under Meketre’s causeway, is another intact private burial. It does not give us a chair of this quality. Hatnefer’s does. That is why 36.3.152 carries a chapter.

## Rekhmire’s shop, duck heads, and the stools paintings still outnumber

Theban tomb TT 100, the chapel of Rekhmire, vizier under Thutmose III and Amenhotep II, is the New Kingdom’s best picture of a furniture shop that is not a reconstruction. Wall scenes show carpenters adzing, sawing, drilling, stretching webbing on a frame, assembling a chair. The tools match Killen’s catalog: adze, pull-saw, bow-drill, the copper kit that has to be sharpened often. The webbing scene is the one to keep beside Hatnefer’s linen seat. Painters knew that a chair was a frame plus a textile. Museums that restore a mesh are repeating a gesture the tomb already recorded.

Folding stools with duck-head terminals are the other Theban type a reader will meet in Cairo and in New York. Tutankhamun’s tomb held several; the Met’s 12.182.49 is the accessible cousin, wood inlaid with ebony and ivory, the leather seat gone. The X-frame is a carpenter’s problem in motion: a pivot that must work, terminals that can be carved as birds without weakening the joint, a seat that can be rolled and carried. Rome will later call a descendant *sella curulis* and write laws about who may sit on it. The Egyptian joiner was solving a tent and a boat, not a senate.

Stools still outnumber chairs in Killen’s pages and in the paintings. The three-legged stool in craft scenes, the four-legged seat for an attendant, the low chair for a woman at a banquet in TT 52 (Nakht): these are a graded kit, not a single “Egyptian chair.” Deir el-Medina’s workmen sat on the cheaper end of that kit. Amarna’s houses, abandoned in a hurry, have given up fragments that belong on the same gradient `[CITE NEEDED: a specific Amarna stool in the Ashmolean or Berlin]`. Hatnefer’s chair looks expensive because the village and the city were full of seats that were not it. The original cord is the proof that we are not only looking at a restorer’s guess. Almost no other Egyptian chair in a public collection can say that, and the Met’s label is right to say it first.

## After Thebes

Amarna will show stools and beds in a different light; the Ramesside centuries will keep the types. Then wood in Egypt becomes, for the archaeologist, a wetter and more fragmented problem. The New Kingdom is the plateau of survival. That is why these two Met chairs and Carter’s 91 can carry a whole chapter. They are not a complete sociology of sitting in Dynasty 18. They are what a joiner, a weaver, and a goldworker actually finished. A folding stool without its leather, a low chair with its original cord, a gold armchair with an Aten still on the back: three answers to sitting in one dynasty, three different budgets, one river of imported wood.

Harry Burton’s photographs of Carter 91, still under Griffith Institute control, are how most readers first met the gold armchair: linen wraps, then studio light, then a century of posters. GEM 4573 is the same object with a new number. A furniture history that only knows the poster will miss Hatnefer’s linen cord, which is the rarer survival.

The next chapters leave the Nile for ivory in the Tigris and a storeroom at Nimrud. The Egyptian lesson to take along is technical: mortise, peg, glue, a seat of cord, a leg that is an animal, a back that can be an amulet or a throne. Skara Brae’s dresser did not need those sentences. Giza’s gold remembered them. Thebes still has the wood.


## A threshold chair, and the stools the gold did not invent

Lansing and Hayes found the chair outside an intact chamber. That sentence is already in the excavation *Bulletin*. What it still does not license is a reconstructed rite. The linen cord, the pegs, the Bes row: those are the facts a reader can take home. The gold throne in Cairo (Carter 91) remains a different class of object — sheet gold, glass, a pictorial program of kingship. Putting the two in one essay is a claim about a period’s joiners, not about every household.

Killen’s stool atlas is the ordinary end of the same century: lattice, folding X, three legs. The Met’s 12.182.49 is a frame a visitor can walk around. JE 62046, the footrest under Carter 91, is the palace version of a type that appears, plainer, under officials in paint. Height tracks scene and rank as much as sex. A banquet’s low chair is not a law of gender. It is a painter’s grammar the Met’s label already treats with caution.
