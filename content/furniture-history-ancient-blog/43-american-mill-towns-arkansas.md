# Hardwood Towns

In 1878 a group of Indiana woodworkers led by William Ott arrived in Fort Smith, Arkansas, to start a furniture factory with Fred Meier, Charles Kade, and F. Schaidt. The machinery came from Ott’s old Indiana plant. Among the men was Edward Ballman, a wood turner with a public-school education. The first company did not prosper. It sold to Alex H. Reynolds about 1880, then again to Jacob C. Huff, Meier, and Ballman. In 1885 the works moved to the Arkansas River north of town when a lease ran out. Huff later drowned crossing the river. By the mid-1880s the name was E. Ballman & Co. On 6 August 1894 Ballman, Charles Cummings, and W.J. Johnston opened Ballman-Cummings Furniture Co.: chamber suites, dressers, chiffoniers, sideboards, desks. Ballman went on to help found the Fort Smith Folding Bed and Table Co. (1902), the Fort Smith Couch and Bedding Co. (1904), the Border Queen Kitchen Cabinet Co. (1906), a metal-products firm (1917), and a cooperative marketing company (1921). The Fort Smith Historical Society’s *Journal* (the March 2007 Ballman biography) is the local spine. The *Southwest Times Record* still treats 6 August 1894 as a city date.

This is not a lost civilization. It is a river town that made furniture because the hardwood was upstream and the rails were in. The chapter belongs at the end of the series because only now — after Egypt’s imported cedar, after mahogany’s Atlantic, after Thonet’s beech and IKEA’s pine — is it honest to talk about American mill towns without pretending they invented the chair.

## Forests, not a brand

The *Encyclopedia of Arkansas* entry on the timber industry maps the woods: Ozark mixed pine and hardwood; Ouachita pine on the slopes and hardwood in the valleys; southern yellow pine on sandy hills; bottomland hardwood along the rivers. Northern capital arrived with the mainline: Rittenhouse and Embree at Warren (Bradley County), the Arkansas Lumber Company, 1901; Weyerhaeuser’s Southern Lumber Company in Warren, 1902; Watzek, Gates, and Crossett at Fordyce (1892) and Crossett (1899). Corlis C. Curry’s 1952 thesis on Ashley, Bradley, and Drew Counties remains a cited local history. Bradley County’s oak and the Saline bottoms appear in Forest Service historical ecology (Bragg and Bragg on the Saline-Fifteen site) as a witness-tree world of “black oak” that is really a complex of oaks.

Furniture towns and lumber towns overlap and are not identical. Crossett became a paper and pine corporation town. Fort Smith became a factory town that bought hardwood from the interior. Warren cut and shipped. The distinction matters. A chair factory needs dry kilns, turners, finish rooms, and a catalog. A sawmill needs a river or a spur and a crew that can be hurt. Both are furniture history. Only one makes a dresser.

## What the factories made

Ballman-Cummings’s list — chamber suites, chiffoniers, sideboards — is a Victorian and early-twentieth-century list. It is the stuffed room (chapter 34) in oak and walnut, then in cheaper veneers, then in the Colonial revival that Phyfe would have half-recognized. Folding beds and kitchen cabinets (Border Queen) are types of a specific decade: the bed that pretends to be a wardrobe, the kitchen that has just become a room with branded cabinets. Couch and bedding companies mark the spring-and-textile half of the trade.

Fort Smith’s claim, in local writing, to have been a furniture center “west of the Mississippi” is a chamber-of-commerce sentence that happens to match a cluster of plants. Other clusters — Grand Rapids, High Point, the Indiana towns Ott came from, later the upholstered South — are larger in the national numbers. Arkansas’s interest for this series is the hardwood itself: oak, gum, walnut, cherry, the Southern mix, cut near the shop. That is a different geography from a Grand Rapids factory finishing mahogany that came through a port.

Labor: turners like Ballman, finishers, upholsterers, the women in sewing rooms, Black workers in a border-state industry whose shop floors were not separate from Jim Crow. The published Fort Smith pieces cited here are still thinner on race and on unions than a full history would need. `[CITE NEEDED: union records or city directories broken down by occupation and race for Fort Smith furniture, 1900–1940.]` The machines Ott brought from Indiana were already a statement: this is not a medieval bench. It is a plant.

## After the first factories

Twentieth-century Arkansas furniture follows the national line: Depression closures, war work, then a long competition with cheaper production elsewhere, then a late-century and twenty-first-century remainder of shops that still know kiln-dried oak from a catalog photograph of oak. Some plants died. Some towns kept a finishing room and a reputation. The live edge and the CNC of chapter 40 exist here too, in smaller shops that never saw Milan.

A soft bridge, and only a bridge: the same forests that fed Ballman’s turners still feed people who want a table whose species they can name. That is not a reason to end a history of Tutankhamun’s chair in a salesroom. It is a reason to remember that hardwood manufacturing in the United States had addresses — Fort Smith, Warren, a dozen river towns — and that those addresses were workshops in the same long sense as St. Martin’s Lane and a Theban hillside. The objects were plainer. The joints still had to hold.

## What the series was for

House 1 at Skara Brae kept a dresser because stone was the sheet material. Çatalhöyük raised its dead under a platform. Giza kept gold when the wood went to powder. A Fort Smith chiffonier is not their descendant in style. It is their descendant in the only way that matters to a joiner: a shop, a forest or a trade in forests, a customer, a joint. The magazine reader who began on the Bay of Skaill and ends on the Arkansas River has not been sold a destiny. They have been shown that furniture has always been local work on wood that is not always local, and that the last honest sentence is a dated workshop.

Ott’s Indiana machines in a Fort Smith brick plant already refuse the romance of a lone bench. Ballman’s path from turner to a cluster of specialized companies (folding beds, couches, kitchen cabinets, a cooperative sales arm) is how a river town tried to become a furniture center: not one genius shop, a set of plants that shared a hardwood hinterland and a catalog language with Grand Rapids and the Indiana towns the crew had left. Jim Crow on those floors, and the thinner published record of Black workers and unions, are the gaps a local history still owes. `[CITE NEEDED: Fort Smith city directories by occupation and race, 1900–1940.]`

Crossett’s pine-and-paper corporation town and Warren’s sawmill spur are not Ballman-Cummings. Lumber towns feed furniture towns; they are not the same time clock. Curry’s 1952 thesis and the *Encyclopedia of Arkansas* timber entry keep the northern investors (Watzek, Gates, Crossett, Rittenhouse, Embree, Weyerhaeuser) in the sentence. The chair factory’s dry kiln and finish room are a later building on that cut.

A late shop that still names oak from a county rather than “oak finish” from a warehouse is practicing the oldest fact in this series: a joint holds if the wood was dry and the shop was paying attention. That is the only soft bridge this chapter is allowed. No catalog number of a living maker belongs here. The series ends on a dated workshop, as it began on one.


## Indiana machines on the Arkansas

Matthew Myers’s “Edward Ballman: King of Fort Smith Furniture Manufacturing,” in the Fort Smith Historical Society *Journal* 38, no. 1 (April 2014), and the Society’s earlier March 2007 Ballman biography are the local spines. They do not need a chamber-of-commerce gloss. The facts are enough. Furniture making in Fort Smith predates Ballman: Fredrick W. Meier and C. Erhardt made furniture and caskets and repaired pieces in 1867–69. The Birnie brothers bought a shop from Meier and Erhardt in 1869. Meier joined William Ott, Charles Kade, and F. Schaidt in Ott, Meier & Co. in 1877. Ott brought machinery from an Indiana plant. In 1878 Ballman, a wood turner with a public-school education, came south from Indianapolis with Ott’s crew.

The first company did not prosper. Alex H. Reynolds bought it about 1880. About a year later Jacob C. Huff, Meier, and Ballman bought it again. In 1885 they moved north of town onto the Arkansas River when a lease ran out. Huff drowned crossing the river in a leaky boat. Meier and Ballman had to buy his interest from an Indiana estate and went into debt. About 1891 Meier retired sick and sold to Ballman. The name had already become E. Ballman & Co. in the mid-1880s. Myers notes that E. Ballman and Company made walnut chairs with wood from a large Fort Smith sawmill.

On 6 August 1894 Ballman, Charles Cummings, and W. J. Johnston opened Ballman-Cummings Furniture Co. The *Southwest Times Record* still treats that Tuesday as a city date. Chamber suites, dressers, chiffoniers, sideboards, desks. Ballman was president and owned half the stock. The *Fort Smith Elevator* reported on 8 October 1897 that employees were working past dark to ship. That is a factory sentence, not a craft romance.

## Specialties, a panic, a marketing umbrella

Ballman did not stop at chamber suites. He brought William J. Kropp from St. Louis to help open the Fort Smith Folding Bed and Table Co. in 1902. In October 1900 he and his brother Emil bought Perfection Bedding from Ed Stable for mattresses and pillows. In 1904 they invested in Fort Smith Couch and Bedding. The *Arkansas Democrat* recorded the next blow: just as the couch company filled its first mixed-car-load, a gold panic cancelled every order. In 1906 came Border Queen Kitchen Cabinet Co., with Edward Kropp from St. Louis; by 1915 a Nevada newspaper was already selling the cabinet as a kitchen with devices. Fort Smith Metal Products (1917) made bed springs, well buckets, metal seat cushions. A glass-and-mirror shop with Ennis Porter of Indiana became Porter Mirror and Glass in 1920, then Willard Mirror. In 1921 Ballman and the Kropps formed Fort Smith Furniture Manufacturers Co., a cooperative sales arm. Odie Faulk, in *Fort Smith: An Illustrated History*, wrote that Ballman served as president of these companies at one time or another. He was still president of the manufacturers’ company when he died in 1923, aged sixty-five.

Myers’s civic sentences — bank director, Speer Hardware, a story about peace talks in Indian Territory — belong to local biography. This chapter needs the plants and the list of types. Folding beds, couches, kitchen cabinets, springs: a Victorian parlor (chapter 34) taken apart into specialized factories on a river.

The 2014 *Journal* cover used an E. Ballman & Co. illustration from the Pebley Center, UAFS. The Hardwood Tree Museum Collection there holds later interviews (Flanders, Hamersley, Swofford) that run the story past Ballman’s death into mid-century plants. Use them as oral history, not as a catalog.

## Pine towns are not furniture towns

The *Encyclopedia of Arkansas* “Timber Industry” entry (2143, George W. Balogh) maps the woods and the northern money. Samuel Fordyce’s Cotton Belt opened the mainline. Watzek, Gates, and Crossett founded Fordyce Lumber (1892) and Crossett Lumber (1899). Rittenhouse and Embree founded Arkansas Lumber at Warren in 1901. Weyerhaeuser founded Southern Lumber at Warren in 1902. Crossett bought 47,000 acres from Hovey and McCracken at seven dollars an acre. No commercial timber was sold until 1902; investors had spent a million dollars. By 1905 a second pine mill helped push output toward 84 million board feet a year.

Crossett became a company town, then a paper-and-chemical complex, then a Georgia-Pacific successor after 1962. “Cut out and get out” was the method until the 1920s. In 1926 E. W. “Cap” Gates asked Yale’s H. H. Chapman about sustained yield; W. K. Williams was hired. Corlis C. Curry’s 1952 thesis on Ashley, Bradley, and Drew Counties remains the local monograph. Don C. Bragg and Hope A. Bragg’s Saline-Fifteen work is the witness-tree ecology. Kenneth L. Smith’s *Sawmill* (1986) is the book on cutting the last great virgin forest east of the Rockies.

Fort Smith bought hardwood from the interior and made dressers. Warren cut and shipped. Crossett made pine and paper. The distinction is the chapter. A chair factory needs dry kilns, turners, finish rooms, and a catalog. A sawmill needs a spur and a crew that can be hurt. Both are furniture history. Only one makes a chiffonier.

Labor on those floors is still thinner in the published Fort Smith pieces than a full history would need. Turners, finishers, upholsterers, women in sewing rooms, Black workers in a border-state industry that was not separate from Jim Crow: the machines Ott brought from Indiana were already a plant. `[CITE NEEDED: union records or city directories broken down by occupation and race for Fort Smith furniture, 1900–1940.]` Crossett’s strikes of 1940 and 1985 (Timothy J. Minchin on 1985) are paper-mill labor, not Ballman’s finish room, and should not be borrowed as if they were.

## A dated workshop, not a destiny

Twentieth-century Arkansas furniture follows the national line: Depression closures, war work, competition with cheaper production elsewhere, a remainder of shops that still know kiln-dried oak from a catalog photograph of oak. *Pratt v. Ballman-Cummings Furniture Co.* (Arkansas Supreme Court, 1973) shows the company still in court in the Ayers Furniture Industries years. That is an afterlife, not an advertisement.
