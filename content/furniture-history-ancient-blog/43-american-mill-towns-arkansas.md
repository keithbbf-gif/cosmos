# Hardwood Towns

In 1878 a group of Indiana woodworkers led by William Ott arrived in Fort Smith, Arkansas, to start a furniture factory with Fred Meier, Charles Kade, and F. Schaidt. The machinery came from Ott’s old Indiana plant. Among the men was Edward Ballman, a wood turner with a public-school education. The first company did not prosper. It sold to Alex H. Reynolds about 1880, then again to Jacob C. Huff, Meier, and Ballman. In 1885 the works moved to the Arkansas River north of town when a lease ran out. Huff later drowned crossing the river. By the mid-1880s the name was E. Ballman & Co. On 6 August 1894 Ballman, Charles Cummings, and W.J. Johnston opened Ballman-Cummings Furniture Co.: chamber suites, dressers, chiffoniers, sideboards, desks. Ballman went on to help found the Fort Smith Folding Bed and Table Co. (1902), the Fort Smith Couch and Bedding Co. (1904), the Border Queen Kitchen Cabinet Co. (1906), a metal-products firm (1917), and a cooperative marketing company (1921). The Fort Smith Historical Society’s *Journal* (the March 2007 Ballman biography) is the local spine. The *Southwest Times Record* still treats 6 August 1894 as a city date.

This is not a lost civilization. It is a river town that made furniture because the hardwood was upstream and the rails were in. The chapter belongs at the end of the series because only now — after Egypt’s imported cedar, after mahogany’s Atlantic, after Thonet’s beech and IKEA’s pine — is it honest to talk about American mill towns without pretending they invented the chair.

## Forests, not a brand

The *Encyclopedia of Arkansas* entry on the timber industry maps the woods: Ozark mixed pine and hardwood; Ouachita pine on the slopes and hardwood in the valleys; southern yellow pine on sandy hills; bottomland hardwood along the rivers. Northern capital arrived with the mainline: Rittenhouse and Embree at Warren (Bradley County), the Arkansas Lumber Company, 1901; Weyerhaeuser’s Southern Lumber Company in Warren, 1902; Watzek, Gates, and Crossett at Fordyce (1892) and Crossett (1899). Corlis C. Curry’s 1952 thesis on Ashley, Bradley, and Drew Counties remains a cited local history. Bradley County’s oak and the Saline bottoms appear in Forest Service historical ecology (Bragg and Bragg on the Saline-Fifteen site) as a witness-tree world of “black oak” that is really a complex of oaks.

Furniture towns and lumber towns overlap and are not identical. Crossett became a paper and pine corporation town. Fort Smith became a factory town that bought hardwood from the interior. Warren cut and shipped. The distinction matters. A chair factory needs dry kilns, turners, finish rooms, and a catalog. A sawmill needs a river or a spur and a crew that can be hurt. Both are furniture history. Only one makes a dresser.

## What the factories made

Ballman-Cummings’s list — chamber suites, chiffoniers, sideboards — is a Victorian and early-twentieth-century list. It is the stuffed room (chapter 34) in oak and walnut, then in cheaper veneers, then in the Colonial revival that Phyfe would have half-recognized. Folding beds and kitchen cabinets (Border Queen) are types of a specific decade: the bed that pretends to be a wardrobe, the kitchen that has just become a room with branded cabinets. Couch and bedding companies mark the spring-and-textile half of the trade.

Fort Smith’s claim, in local writing, to have been a furniture center “west of the Mississippi” is a chamber-of-commerce sentence that happens to match a cluster of plants. Other clusters — Grand Rapids, High Point, the Indiana towns Ott came from, later the upholstered South — are larger in the national numbers. Arkansas’s interest for this series is the hardwood itself: oak, gum, walnut, cherry, the Southern mix, cut near the shop. That is a different geography from a Grand Rapids factory finishing mahogany that came through a port.

Labor: turners like Ballman, finishers, upholsterers, the women in sewing rooms, Black workers in a border-state industry whose shop floors were not separate from Jim Crow. The published Fort Smith pieces cited here are still thinner on race and on unions than a full history would need. `[CITE NEEDED: union records or city directories broken down by occupation and race for Fort Smith furniture, 1900–1940.]` The machines Ott brought from Indiana were already a statement: this is not a medieval bench. It is a plant.

## After the first factories

Twentieth-century Arkansas furniture follows the national line: Depression closures, war work, then a long competition with cheaper production elsewhere, then a late-century and twenty-first-century remainder of shops that still know kiln-dried oak from a catalog photograph of oak. Some plants died. Some towns kept a finishing room and a reputation. The live edge and the CNC of chapter 40 exist here too, in smaller shops that never saw Milan.

A soft bridge, and only a bridge: the same forests that fed Ballman’s turners still feed people who want a table whose species they can name. That is not a reason to end a history of Tutankhamun’s chair in a salesroom. It is a reason to remember that hardwood manufacturing in the United States had addresses — Fort Smith, Warren, a dozen river towns — and that those addresses were workshops in the same long sense as St. Martin’s Lane and a Theban hillside. The objects were plainer. The joints still had to hold.

## What the series was for

House 1 at Skara Brae kept a dresser because stone was the sheet material. Çatalhöyük raised its dead under a platform. Giza kept gold when the wood went to powder. A Fort Smith chiffonier is not their descendant in style. It is their descendant in the only way that matters to a joiner: a shop, a forest or a trade in forests, a customer, a joint. The magazine reader who began on the Bay of Skaill and ends on the Arkansas River has not been sold a destiny. They have been shown that furniture has always been local work on wood that is not always local, and that the last honest sentence is a dated workshop.
