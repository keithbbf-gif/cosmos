## Evidence and argument

Early Islamic courts **632–900 CE** sat on textiles, mats, and portable authority objects more often than on fixed thrones. Umayyad desert palaces and Abbasid Samarra yield **architecture and stucco**; complete caliphal chairs are rarer than poetry about ceremony. The essay follows excavation reports and contemporary art historians who separate Sasanian throne memory from Umayyad audience practice.

**Khirbat al-Mafjar** (Jordan Valley, Umayyad, ca. 743–750 CE) is the best-preserved argument. R.W. Hamilton’s excavation reports and UNESCO’s tentative listing describe a bath complex with geometric mosaic “carpets,” a **bahw** apse with the famous **lion-and-gazelle** panel, and a **standing stucco caliph** above the bath porch—not a seated portrait mosaic. Court accounts of caliphs appearing behind curtains (Hamilton’s reading of mosaic “tassel” borders) address **protocol**, not furniture legs. The caliph likely received petitioners from a recess in the audience hall; reconstruction drawings show platform and textile, not an accessioned throne.

**Qusayr ‘Amra** frescoes (early eighth century) show seated and reclining figures in Byzantine-inflected paint—evidence for **low couch and cushion culture** in private princely space, published widely in Grabar and Ettinghausen surveys of Umayyad art. **Samarra** (836 onward) gives scale: Ernst Herzfeld’s 1911–13 campaigns at the **Dar al-Khilafa** (Metropolitan Museum essay on Herzfeld; Archnet site dossier) mapped cruciform audience halls, stucco revetment, and painted beams—again, fixed seating is inferential. Herzfeld’s famous “enthroned king” photographs belong to **Persepolis**, not Samarra; do not mix corpora.

Typology (Fig. 3): **portable cushion and rug**, **folding stool or enrobed seat** (Sasanian-Byzantine inheritance in text and metalwork traditions), **divan platform** in bath and private rooms. Timeline (Fig. 1) runs Rashidun through early Abbasid foundation; map (Fig. 2) orients Syria, Iraq, and Hijaz schematically.

Metal folding chairs appear in late antique and early medieval luxury trade; tie them to **documented finds and texts**, not to a caliph’s name without provenance. When in doubt, describe the floor mosaic and the standing stucco figure—artifacts you can cite without inventing numbers.
