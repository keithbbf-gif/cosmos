## Evidence and argument

Between **2600 and 1900 BCE**, the Indus tradition never produced a European-style chair typology. What survives instead is architecture that dictates posture—courtyards, staircases to upper rooms, brick bathing platforms—and a handful of stone and terracotta objects that hint at elevation without naming a joinery tradition.

The best-published house plans come from John Marshall’s Mohenjo-daro reports. Multi-room compounds such as House 13 in the VS area show porters’ lodges, wells, and staircases; Marshall notes upper-storey living and sleeping reached from the courtyard, with at least one room raised on a solid basement as flood refuge (House 8, HR area). Those raised floors are platforms in the architectural sense—sleeping and reception happen above grade—not imported “bedsteads” in wood. Bathing rooms with tightly fitted brick floors and slight fall toward drains appear repeatedly in modern summaries of the same corpus; they are wet platforms, not furniture, but they train the eye to read Indus domestic space as surfaced elevation rather than freestanding seats.

Figural evidence is sparse but not empty. Marshall’s “Seated Nobleman” from Mohenjo-daro (L Area, Chamber 75)—a headless alabaster figure with one knee raised—was found in situ above pavement and published in *Mohenjo-daro and the Indus Civilization*, vol. I, with excavation context and plates. It is sculpture, not a chair, yet it documents how an elite body occupied space: raised knee, mantle, no visible throne frame. Dholavira (R.S. Bisht, Archaeological Survey of India campaigns) adds a poorly preserved seated male stone figure comparable in spirit to Mohenjo-daro’s corpus, alongside stone architecture elsewhere built in brick at Harappa. Jonathan Mark Kenoyer’s syntheses (*Ancient Cities of the Indus Valley Civilization*, Oxford, 1998) stress perishable furnishings—wood, reed, cloth—that decayed while brick and stone survived.

Cylinder seals and tablets rarely show unambiguous chairs; typology here (Fig. 3) therefore separates **built platform**, **inferred low stool** (where terracotta legs or miniature models appear in site reports), and **courtyard bench** as a plan inference along party walls—not as a catalog of accessioned museum pieces. The timeline (Fig. 1) marks Mature Harappan urban peak and later deurbanization; phase arguments belong in footnotes, not in the schematic intervals.

The map (Fig. 2) orients Harappa, Mohenjo-daro, and Dholavira only. Comparative Near Eastern platform habits may illuminate parallel solutions to heat and rank, but they are not proof of diffusion. Where the pictorial record stops, the honest move is to stop narrating—and let house plans and a few stone bodies carry the argument.
