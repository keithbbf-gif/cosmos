## Evidence and argument

Mauryan furniture **322–185 BCE** lives in two registers that rarely touch: Greek ambassador prose about a wooden capital, and Indian administrative text about how a king moves through guarded rooms. Archaeology adds a third—polished sandstone columns without seat joinery. No secure wooden throne survives from the dynasty; the essay’s typology is therefore textual and architectural, not catalog-driven.

Megasthenes’ *Indica*, preserved in Strabo (*Geography* 15.1.35–36, Fordham and LacusCurtius translations), describes Palibothra/Pataliputra as a parallelogram city with a perforated wooden rampart, moat, and a court where the king’s name compounds with the city’s. Strabo’s adjacent ethnography lists Indian craft specialists—including carpenters—without itemizing palace beds. The famous comparison of Mauryan splendor to Persian Susa belongs to the fragment tradition summarized in modern handbooks on Megasthenes; treat it as testimony about perceived scale, not as an inventory verified find by find.

At Patna (ancient Pataliputra), Kumrahar excavations documented an **eighty-pillared hall** of polished Mauryan sandstone columns—monolithic shafts arranged in rows, implying a massive timber superstructure that decayed. That is audience architecture, not furniture, yet it bound how elites sat: under a wooden roof, on platforms or cushions lost to fire and monsoon. Kautilya’s *Arthashastra* (Shamasastry trans., 1915) is clearer on **protocol** than on joinery: Chapter 21 describes the king rising from bed through successive guarded compartments; Chapter 20 warns of assassins hidden **under a bed** in the women’s quarters—furniture as danger locus, not as typology plate. For explicit couch vocabulary (śayana, paryaṅka, mañca), later treatises such as the *Mānasāra* (English trans. on Wisdom Library, ch. 44) belong to architectural śāstra centuries after the Mauryan core—useful for lineage, not for dating Mauryan beds.

Typology (Fig. 3) holds **palace sleeping compartment**, **assembly hall seating (inferred)**, and **campaign furniture** as categories in text and ethnography, not as excavated morphotypes. The timeline (Fig. 1) runs Chandragupta through early Shunga; the map (Fig. 2) marks Pataliputra, major rock-edict regions, and nothing more precise than schematic geography.

Ashokan epigraphy documents moral kingship and landscape, not couch legs. When a gallery mounts a “Mauryan throne,” ask whether the object is Mauryan or a later stylistic echo—the honest answer here is usually the second.

## Sources to consult

- Strabo, *Geography* 15.1.35–36 (Megasthenes on Pataliputra); parallel fragments in Arrian/Diodorus via modern compilations of *Indica*.
- R. Shamasastry, *Kautilya’s Arthashastra* (1915), Books 20–21 (harem layout; rising-from-bed protocol).
- ASI Kumrahar (Patna) excavation publications on the eighty-pillared Mauryan hall; B.P. Sinha and standard Patna museum/archaeology summaries for column counts and polish.
- *Mānasāra* ch. 44 (couches/bedsteads/swing terminology)—post-Mauryan architectural text, cited for vocabulary only.
