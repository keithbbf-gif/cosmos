## Evidence and argument

For North India **1500–500 BCE**, the furniture question is mostly a question of where bodies were allowed to rest during rite and teaching—not of excavated throne joinery. Painted Grey Ware and early iron-age settlements yield hearths, postholes, and pottery; they do not yield a coherent corpus of wooden **asana** frames. This essay therefore follows text and ritual grammar first, and material culture second, without inventing museum objects to fill the gap.

Vedic and Brāhmaṇa literature is explicit about surfaces. Kuśa/darbha grass functions as pure covering and hand-held implement in the *Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa* (Eggeling’s translation in *SBE* 12, 26, 41): grass bundles on the altar, stalks for sprinkling ghee, barhis layers—ritual furniture made of plants. The same text, in a royal consecration passage (Kāṇḍa V, 2), introduces a **throne-seat made of udumbara wood** with a goat-skin cover—wooden elevation for the sacrificer, not a kuśa mat. Later smṛti and Purāṇic usage gives **kuśāsana** the narrow meaning “mat of kuśa grass” (see Wisdom Library’s aggregation of lexical and Purāṇic citations); that term should not be retrojected blindly into every Rigvedic camp scene.

The Rigveda and Atharvaveda speak of sitting on the ground, on straw, and on hides in pastoral settings; precise furniture nouns are fewer than later Sūtra descriptions. Readers must keep **textual stratigraphy** in view: a Mauryan or Kushan sculptural court with low benches belongs to other essays in this series. Ashokan pillars and later Buddhist relief programs document imperial posture centuries after the Vedic core compositions; they are comparative tail lights, not headlights.

Typology (Fig. 3) therefore encodes social gradation without pretending to archaeological morphotypes: **floor and grass** (default ritual surface), **low portable seat** (textual and later ethnographic), **teacher or king’s raised seat** (Brāhmaṇa throne episode; later tradition). The timeline (Fig. 1) crosses Early Vedic through Late Vedic and Sūtra ages as coarse bands; philologists still debate absolute dates for individual hymns—those debates stay in prose, not in Fig. 1’s ticks.

The map (Fig. 2) sketches Kuru–Pañcāla and eastern settlement zones as ritual geography, not GPS. Where archaeology is silent, the magazine voice can still be precise: cite the passage, name the translator, and refuse the glossy reconstruction.

## Sources to consult

- Julius Eggeling, trans., *The Śatapatha-Brāhmaṇa*, Sacred Books of the East, vols. 12, 26, 41 (kuśa grass ritual; udumbara throne-seat, Kāṇḍa V, adhyāya 2).
- Rigveda and Atharvaveda concordances for sitting vocabulary (consult standard translations, e.g., Griffith or Jamison–Brereton for Rigveda).
- R. Shamasastry, trans., *Kautilya’s Arthashastra* (1915) for later Mauryan-era palace protocol—compare only with period caution.
- Excavation syntheses for Painted Grey Ware / early historic transitions (ASI and university monographs); no single “Vedic throne” object.
