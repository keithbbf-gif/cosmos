## Evidence and argument

The brick **kang** of North China—sleeping platform linked to a stove flue—is a late mature form. This essay’s window (**1000 BCE–200 CE**) tracks **antecedents**: pounded-clay sleeping surfaces, stove-linked warm benches, and Han architecture that already ducted heat through walls. Calling every warm platform a “kang” would collapse two millennia of experiment; the prose keeps that distinction explicit.

Neolithic and early sites in the northeast record **direct-fired clay beds** (open fire on a pounded surface, ashes cleared before sleep). Wikipedia’s summary of Chinese scholarship cites Shenyang-area evidence and L-shaped single-flue structures in first-century building remains in Heilongjiang—architectural cousins to Roman hypocaust logic and Korean *jjokgudeul*, not identical copies. Han dynasty ruins reported in popular archaeology press (e.g., *China Daily* summaries of Qin–Han palace and household ranges) describe **kitchen ranges with discharge flues** beside bathing spaces—heat and smoke routed on purpose.

Western Han evidence tightened when archaeologists published a kang-like platform from the **Dongheishan** site, Xushui County, Hebei (Baoding region)—a multi-period mound with an early Western Han heated surface that pushed documented flue-platform history earlier than some textbook timelines. Shang-period heated structures reported from **Zhaoyao** (Hebei) appear in Chinese encyclopedic summaries; treat them as specialist literature, not as this pack’s primary anchor, unless you are writing for a Chinese-language audience with full site reports in hand.

Typology (Fig. 3) therefore contrasts **open-fired clay bed**, **single-flue bench/platform**, and **mat-on-floor summer habit**—behaviors as much as objects. Han lacquer **platform beds** in tombs (see the series essay on Han dynasty low platforms) belong to a parallel track: portable prestige furniture versus immovable heat architecture. The timeline (Fig. 1) is intentionally coarse because climate and fuel differ between Liaodong, the Yellow River plain, and the northwest.

The map (Fig. 2) marks schematic north China; it is not a thermal GIS model. Museum dioramas that show a family on a mature Qing-era kang should not be read backward into the Han without citing the excavation that proves flues under the sleeping surface.
