# Cassone and Studiolo

The Metropolitan’s Lehman cassoni — 1975.1.1940 (pair with 1975.1.1947), mid-sixteenth century, walnut, carved and partly gilded, with nineteenth-century additions; 1975.1.1944, Roman, 1575–90, rebuilt with replacements — are typical in the way that hurts: the type is real, the survival is a rebuild. A *cassone* is a marriage chest, a hall chest, a painted or carved box that sat against a wall and held the textiles that were the real wealth. Cristelle Baskins and the catalogers of painted cassoni (Schubring’s old corpus; later Getty and Met essays) have made the pictures famous: triumphs, Petrarch, the rape of a Sabine, a wedding procession. The box is furniture. The painting is a public argument about the marriage.

This chapter is about the Italian room when it acquires, besides the chest, a *lettuccio*, a *sgabello*, a table that stays up, and a *studiolo* — a small room that is itself a piece of furniture.

## From gothic box to classical order

Fifteenth-century Florence still knows the Gothic chest. By the late century the classical language — pilasters, friezes, carved acanthus, walnut instead of gilt gesso — takes the front. The *cassapanca* (chest-bench) is a chest you sit on, a civic and family object in a hall. Beds acquire testers and, in richer houses, intarsia. The *sgabello* — a stool with a carved back, often in pairs by a doorway — is a Renaissance invention that looks like a coat of arms you can sit on. It is ceremonial more than comfortable.

Intarsia is the period’s technical boast: perspective scenes in wood on the walls of the *studiolo* (Federico da Montefeltro’s rooms at Urbino and Gubbio; the Gubbio studiolo now in the Met, 39.153). The Gubbio room is furniture as architecture: benches, lattice, instruments, and books all in inlaid wood, a duke’s mind turned into a lining. Olga Raggio’s Met publications are the catalog. A reader who has only seen a cassone in a gallery has not seen what the same shops could do when given a room.

## Workshops and names

The Tasso workshop in Florence, Benedetto da Maiano’s circle, northern carvers in Venice, Lombard intarsia specialists: names appear more often than in the Gothic stall contracts, and still less often than on paintings. Vasari mentions furniture in passing. The objects themselves, when they have not been recut in the nineteenth century, are the documents. Lehman’s labels (“with 19th century additions”) should be read twice. The Victorian market liked a complete Renaissance more than a honest fragment.

Bronze mounts, *pietre dure* (a slightly later, Mannerist and Baroque luxury), and the hardstone table are the high end. The ordinary Tuscan house kept stools, boards, and chests. Inventories (the Strozzi, the Medici Magliabechiano lists, later published extracts) are the sociology. Richard Goldthwaite’s economic history of the Florentine palace is the frame for why so much walnut was needed.

## The room as a suite — almost

A Renaissance *camera* is not a nineteenth-century bedroom suite, but it is beginning to think in matched woods: bed, cassoni, chairs, a table, a *restello* or credenza. The credenza — a sideboard for plate — is a standing cupboard that Europe had been trying to remember since Herculaneum. Italy, then Antwerp (chapter 27), then the whole North, will make the cupboard a protagonist.

Chairs with arms, the *sedia*, still mark rank. Most people sit on stools. A painted *spalliera* (shoulder-height panel) above a cassone or a bench continues the picture cycle onto the wall. Furniture and painting have not yet divorced.

## What to take to Antwerp and Paris

The cassone as a decorated box; the studiolo as a room-object; intarsia as a joiner’s perspective; the honesty that many “Renaissance” pieces in America were rebuilt for J. Pierpont Morgan’s generation. The next chapter’s Antwerp cabinets will look more “baroque” and are already, in the late sixteenth century, a northern answer to the same need: a lockable architecture for collections.

## The Trebizond chest and the honesty of 14.39

The Metropolitan’s cassone 14.39 — “Cassone with painted front panel depicting the Conquest of Trebizond” — is the painted box a reader already half-knows. Attributed to the workshops of Apollonio di Giovanni di Tomaso and Marco del Buono Giamberti, Florence, after about 1461: poplar, linen, polychromed and gilded gesso, a tempera-and-gold panel; 39 1/2 by 77 by 32 7/8 inches; painted surface 15 1/4 by 49 1/2 inches. Bought in 1913 from Stefano Bardini. For a century it enjoyed the status of a rare intact fifteenth-century chest that pictured a contemporary event: Mehmed II’s taking of Trebizond, last Byzantine outpost, in 1461. A 2008 technical examination withdrew the “intact.” Parts of the chest are not integral. The painted front may come from another box. The old Palazzo Strozzi provenance, asserted by Weisbach in 1913, may not govern the picture. The end pieces, which *are* original to this carcase, carry Strozzi emblems: a hawk on a caltrop, a banderole with ME[Z]ZE. The inside of the lid and the back keep their stenciled decoration. The chest is still a Strozzi object. The battle on the front may be a different marriage’s argument, married to this box in a dealer’s generation.

That is the cassone problem in one accession. Schubring’s 1915 corpus made the pictures famous and scattered them from their boxes. Baskins’s *Cassone Painting, Humanism, and Gender* (1998) put the subjects back into the politics of a wedding: triumphs, Petrarch, a Sabine, a procession. The Lehman pair 1975.1.1940 and 1975.1.1947 — mid-sixteenth century, Rome or Siena?, walnut carved and partly gilded, coniferous secondary wood, 1975.1.1940 at 73.6 by 170 by 63.5 cm — are later, more architectural, and already marked “with 19th century additions.” 1975.1.1944, Roman, 1575–90, is “rebuilt, with extensive replacements.” 1975.1.1942, about 1550–80, is the same wound at 67.8 by 193 by 70 cm. The Victorian and Bardini markets liked a complete Renaissance. A label that says so is not a disgrace. It is the document.

## A room you walk into: 39.153

The Gubbio studiolo, Met 39.153, is about 1478–82: walnut, beech, rosewood, oak, and fruitwoods in a walnut base; 15 feet 10 15/16 inches high, 16 feet 11 15/16 wide, 12 feet 7 3/16 deep. Designed by Francesco di Giorgio Martini; executed in the Florentine workshop of Giuliano da Maiano, with Benedetto da Maiano; Rogers Fund, 1939. Olga Raggio’s two-volume *The Gubbio Studiolo and Its Conservation* (1999) is the catalog, with Martin Kemp on the perspectival system. The Urbino studiolo, a little earlier, is still in Federico da Montefeltro’s palace. This one left. It was dismantled in 1874, moved to a villa at Frascati, sold through a German dealer to Venice, and shipped to New York, where it was reassembled between 1939 and 1941. A decade of later conservation put it back into the condition a visitor now sees.

At first glance the lower walls are benches and the upper walls are cabinets with lattice doors, some open, some ajar, holding armor, an armillary sphere, musical instruments, books, a bird in a cage, the Montefeltro emblems. The cabinets cast shadows. None of it is a cabinet. It is intarsia: thousands of slivers of wood pretending to be a duke’s mind. Federico’s library and his career as a *condottiere* share the lining. A fifteenth-century chronicler called him a man “ever careful to learn some new thing every day.” The phrase is in the Met’s own audio tour. The room is the phrase made walkable.

Piero della Francesca dedicated *De prospectiva pingendi* to this patron. Luca Pacioli dedicated the *Summa* to Guidobaldo. The studiolo is not a footnote to those books. It is the books’ furniture: a lining that knows vanishing points the way a cassone knows a procession. A reader who has only seen 1975.1.1940 against a gallery wall has not seen what the same century’s shops could do when given a room.

## Sgabelli that are not Renaissance

The Lehman pair of *sgabelli*, 1975.1.2037 and 1975.1.2038, walnut, each 101.6 by 34.3 by 35.6 cm, carry the date line that should be printed larger than the adjective: “possibly 19th or early 20th century (with reused footboards, possibly 16th or 17th century).” They came from Palazzo Davanzati, through George R. Hann and the 1945 Parke-Bernet sale, and were likely assembled in Elia Volpi’s workshop — the same Florence that furnished American collectors with a Renaissance they could sit on. The backs are late-Mannerist in language, then enhanced for what a 1900 buyer thought a Renaissance chair should be. Another pair is in the Flint Institute of Arts. Heraldry at sitting height, yes. Fifteenth-century, no.

A genuine *sgabello* — a boarded stool with a carved back, often in pairs by a doorway — is a Renaissance type. It is ceremonial more than comfortable. Museum pairs without a paper trail before 1850 should be captioned as the Lehman pair is captioned. The type is real. The survival in America is often a rebuild.

## What the chest held

Inventories, not paintings, say what a cassone was for. Linens, gowns, metal thread, the textiles that were the portable wealth of a marriage. Goldthwaite’s *The Building of Renaissance Florence* (1980) is the economic frame: why so much walnut, why so many chests, why a palace needed a hall that could show boxes. The Strozzi and Medici extracts, published in pieces, are the sociology. Most people sat on stools. A *sedia* with arms marked rank. A *lettuccio* — a daybed with a paneled back — kept the Etruscan couch’s job in walnut. A *restello* or credenza stood up to hold plate: Europe remembering, without knowing it, Herculaneum’s *armarium*.

Painted *spalliere* at shoulder height continued a cassone’s cycle onto the wall. Furniture and painting had not divorced. When the nineteenth century lifted a panel to hang it as a picture, it completed a split the workshop had not intended. 14.39’s front may record that split in reverse: a picture looking for a box, a box looking for a picture.

The ordinary Tuscan house kept boards, stools, and chests. *Pietre dure* tables and bronze-mounted cabinets are a slightly later, Mannerist and Baroque luxury. Vasari mentions furniture in passing. The objects, when they have not been recut, are the documents. What to take north is the decorated box, the walk-in lining, and the habit of locking small precious things behind a facade. Antwerp will answer in ebony and oil paint.


## Urbino stays; Gubbio travels

The Urbino studiolo is still in the Palazzo Ducale: same patron, a little earlier, a lining that never became a crate. Raggio’s first volume puts Gubbio back into Francesco di Giorgio’s rebuilt palace. The intarsia was cut in Florence in the da Maiano shop and carted to Umbria. “Walk-in furniture” was already, in the 1470s, a prefabricated lining — members numbered, perspective calculated (Kemp’s essay is the place for the vanishing points), then assembled on a far wall.

Walnut, beech, rosewood, oak, fruitwoods: the Met’s materials line is a joiner’s palette. Shadows in the lattices are darker slivers. An open door is a lighter one. A book spine is a strip with an inlaid title. The bird in the cage is silly and exact at once — a pet that is also a proof. Armor in the corner is Federico’s other job. The room does not choose between the library and the helmet. It inlays both.

After 1874 the lining became an object that could be sold. Frascati, Venice, the Rogers Fund, a New York reassembly from 1939 to 1941, a later decade of conservation: 39.153’s biography is as much about the twentieth-century museum as about a duke. Seeing Urbino and Gubbio together is the only way to see what a *studiolo* was when it was still a room in a palace.

A *lettuccio* in a *camera* — paneled back, a place to sit up and receive — keeps the couch’s old Mediterranean job. Beds with testers, in richer houses, take intarsia onto the ceiling of sleep. The *cassapanca* in a hall is civic: you sit on the family’s box. None of these types requires a named genius. They require shops that could cut a mitre, a perspective, and a lid that locked. Goldthwaite’s palace economy is why the walnut was worth the cartage. Baskins is why the painted front was worth the argument. Raggio is why a visitor in New York is walking into furniture. Antwerp, next, will shrink that lining until it sits on a stand.
