# Style Guide

This series is written for a reader who keeps *Apollo*, *The Burlington Magazine*, *Crafts*, and the long Smithsonian essays. It is chronological and thematic. It is not a list of “ten chairs that changed everything.”

## Voice

Write as a person who has stood in front of the object, or who has at least read the catalog entry and the excavation report. Prefer the particular: a Carter number, a room in Fort Shalmaneser, a patent year, a workshop address. Do not open with cosmic time. Do not close with a sermon.

Banned phrasing and habits:

- delve, landscape (as metaphor), robust, leverage, unlock, cutting-edge, game-changer
- “In today’s rapidly…”
- “It’s important to note”
- stacked *Moreover* / *Furthermore*
- “Whether you’re a collector or a casual…”
- “In conclusion”
- throat-clearing first paragraphs
- symmetrical three-item filler (“X, Y, and Z”) used as a substitute for an argument
- fake omniscience: “scholars agree,” “everyone knows,” “the world was forever changed”
- COSMOS, AI, or internal process talk

Allowed: doubt, disagreement between excavators, a missing measurement, a repaired tenon, a later stand that does not belong to the cabinet. A claim without a source is marked `[CITE NEEDED]`. Invented excavation stories are not permitted.

## Openings

Start on an artifact, a dated workshop, a room, or a document. Examples of the right kind of first sentence:

- “House One at Skara Brae still has its dresser: two stone shelves on slab uprights, set on the wall opposite the door.”
- “Carter numbered it 91 before Cairo gave it JE 62028.”
- “In 1859 the Thonet factory in Vienna put Model No. 14 into production.”

Wrong: “Since the dawn of time, humans have needed a place to sit.”

## Length and shape

Target 1,800–2,800 words of body text (front matter and figure lists do not count). A piece should have:

1. A concrete opening (object, site, shop, or text)
2. A middle that moves through making, use, and argument — not a museum walk-through that never chooses
3. A close that leaves the reader with a residual fact or tension, not a recap

One essay, one problem. Adjacent chapters may overlap; they should not repeat the same paragraph in different clothes.

## Scholarship

Cite in running prose or in a short notes block: author, short title, year, and page or catalog number when known. Prefer excavation publications, museum catalogs, and standard monographs (Killen, Baker, Mols, Eames, Wilk, Handler, Clunas, Evarts, Wilk, Forty). Popular surveys may appear as orientation, not as the only source.

When a date, find-spot, or attribution is contested, say so and name the parties. When an image license or object ID cannot be confirmed, say so.

## Figures

Every essay carries a captioned figure plan of three to eight images. Prefer:

- Metropolitan Museum of Art Open Access (CC0)
- Smithsonian Open Access (CC0)
- British Museum collection pages (check current license; many are CC BY-NC-SA 4.0)
- Louvre / Grand Egyptian Museum / Topkapı pages (rights often reserved; note that)
- Wikimedia Commons public-domain files whose source museum is named

Each figure needs: suggested filename, object title, museum and accession or inventory number when known, date, material, license, suggested alt text, and a magazine caption (one or two sentences, not a label dump).

Full series documentation lives in `PHOTO_CAPTIONS.md`.

## Names and spelling

Use the form current in the holding institution’s catalog, then give a common alternative once if needed: *huanghuali* (*Dalbergia odorifera*); Klismos; *rahle*; *bandaji*; *cassone*. Transliterations follow the cited catalog. Place names: Çatalhöyük, Marrakesh, Herculaneum, Kumasi.

BCE/CE for ancient dates; CE years may drop the era once the century is clear. Do not write “BC/AD” unless quoting.

## Brand and later chapters

Ancient and early-modern chapters do not mention American mill towns, Arkansas hardwood, or any living manufacturer. A soft bridge is reserved for the last chapter and, if needed, a single closing sentence in the materials and American-shop essays. No product language. No anachronistic plugs.
