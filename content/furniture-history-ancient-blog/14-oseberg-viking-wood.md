# Beds in a Ship

In 834 two women were buried in a ship on the farm at Lille Oseberg, Vestfold. The ship itself had been built of oak in western Norway about 820; pine for deck and mast. A chamber was raised behind the mast. The dead were laid in a bed with down quilts. Tapestries, clothes, combs, kitchen gear, farm tools, three carved sledges, a work sledge, a wagon, five animal-head posts, extra beds, tents, fifteen horses, six dogs, two cows: the Museum of the Viking Age in Oslo (the former Viking Ship Museum) lists the inventory without needing to invent a queen’s name. Grave robbers had already taken the jewelry. They left the wood, because the blue clay and the turf mound had made the wood heavy, dark, and, for a thief, unrewarding.

The excavation of 1904 (Gabriel Gustafson) is a dated workshop of another kind: a modern crew lifting wet oak. About ninety percent of the ship’s wood is original. The furniture — beds, chairs, chests, the wagon, the sledges — is the largest coherent group of early medieval wooden household objects in northern Europe. It is not “Viking furniture” as a style label for a twenty-first-century chair. It is the contents of one grave, in one year, for two people whose names we do not have.

## Beds, tents, and the movable house

The Oseberg beds are frames that can be taken apart. A traveling elite needed a house that packed. Tents in the grave make the same point. The ship is a vehicle; the bed is a vehicle; the wagon is a vehicle. Furniture here is what moves with the body. That is a different lesson from Skara Brae’s fitted dresser, and closer to an Egyptian carrying chair than a tourist expects.

Chests in the grave are the hall’s chests (chapter 13) in a wetter key. Some buckets of yew with gilded bronze fittings look Insular — England or Ireland — and are among the objects the museum flags as imports. The oarsmen of a ship like this, the labels used to say, sat on their own chests. Whether that is true of Oseberg’s slender hull is a naval question; the type of the sea-chest is real enough in later sources.

A chair from the grave — the so-called Oseberg chair — is a rare early medieval backed seat in wood, reconstructed from fragments and much illustrated. Treat the reconstruction as a reconstruction. The animal-head posts, with their gripping beasts, are not chairs. They are ritual or processional objects whose exact use is still argued. They belong in a furniture series because they are carved oak of the highest skill, and because they show what a workshop could do when the object was not a box.

## The wagon and the sledges

The Oseberg wagon is a carved, four-wheeled object that looks ceremonial rather than rutted. The sledges are carved to a similar pitch. These are vehicles as display furniture: things that carry a body or a load and also carry a story in the wood. Comparisons with later medieval ceremonial wagons are tempting and thin. Keep the date. 834 is before stave churches and before the choir stall.

Wood species: oak for the ship and much of the heavy work; pine; yew in the imported buckets; other species in small objects as the conservation literature lists them. `[CITE NEEDED: latest species list from the Museum of the Viking Age conservation reports.]` The carving style — gripping beasts, interlace — is the furniture’s surface language. It is also the language of the ship’s prow. House and ship share a workshop aesthetic.

## Two women, one kit

The grave is a woman’s grave, twice. That fact has been used to write too much and too little. Too much: a whole “female Viking interior” imagined from one mound. Too little: a refusal to see that the richest wooden household we have from the ninth-century North was assembled for women. The beds, the kitchen, the textiles, the wagon: these are not a footnote to a missing sword. They are the deposit.

Who made the objects is unnamed. A royal workshop is a guess that the quality will bear and the archive will not prove. Local Vestfold work plus imported buckets is the cautious sentence.

## After clay

The furniture’s modern life is conservation science: alum treatment in the early twentieth century, later regrets, the move from the old Viking Ship Museum to the new Museum of the Viking Age. The objects are famous and fragile. Photographs in the old museum (the ship under a twenty-year roof, the wagon in a case) are already historical. This chapter should be updated when the new displays publish their catalog numbers in a stable form.

For the series, Oseberg is the northern pair to Herculaneum: wood kept by accident (clay, turf; ash, surge), a household stopped, a museum that has to decide how much to reconstruct. The women in the bed did not mean to found a chapter. The clay meant it for them.
