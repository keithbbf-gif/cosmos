# A Queen’s Bed at Giza

Shaft G 7000 X drops almost ninety feet through the limestone east of Khufu’s pyramid. In 1925 the Harvard University–Boston Museum of Fine Arts Expedition, under George Reisner, found at the bottom a sealed chamber that had been meant for Hetepheres I — wife of Snefru, mother of Khufu — and then, in some sequence still argued, emptied of her body. The alabaster sarcophagus was blank. What remained, in a jumble of gold sheet, inlay, and the ghosts of wood, was a household: a carrying chair, a bed-frame with a headrest and a canopy, an armchair, a box for curtains, silver bracelets in a box. Reisner published the first account for a wide audience in the *Bulletin of the Museum of Fine Arts* 27, no. 164 (December 1929): “The Household Furniture of Queen Hetep-heres I.”

The wood had gone to powder. What Reisner’s team reconstructed — and what Joseph Gerte of Boston copied in 1938 for the MFA — is a drawing in gold of joints that no longer exist. Cairo keeps the original reconstructions: the armchair as JE 53263, the carrying chair as JE 52372. Boston’s copies are MFA 38.957 (armchair) and MFA 38.874 (carrying chair). Digital Giza still flags that the pieces were never entered in an object register book in the field. The furniture is famous and, in the strict sense of a tree still standing inside the gold, absent.

That absence is the Old Kingdom’s lesson for this series. Egyptian furniture is not “the first furniture.” Çatalhöyük and Skara Brae have already refused that sentence. It is the first furniture that later cabinetmakers can recognize as their own: rails, stiles, animal legs, a seat at a height a living adult uses without sitting on the floor, gold used as a skin over a carpenter’s box.

## What a Dynasty 4 bed is

A bed-frame of the Hetepheres type is a rectangle of wood with leonine legs, a footboard, and a separate headrest. The sleeper’s head is lifted; the body slopes slightly. Geoffrey Killen’s *Ancient Egyptian Furniture*, volume I (Oxbow, 2nd ed. 2017), treats bed-frames as a type with a museum catalog behind the prose. The Hetepheres canopy — gold hieroglyphs on uprights, a box of wooden members that once held linen — turns the bed into a room inside the tomb. Reisner’s 1929 figures 1–3 are still the images to start from: a reconstruction that looks like furniture because the gold remembered the arrises.

Lion legs are not decoration pasted on. They are the terminal of a leg that has been carved, or built up, to end in a paw on a drum. The same idea will walk into New Kingdom chairs and, much later, into Empire sofas that think they are quoting Rome. At Giza the quote runs the other way. The animal is the support. A queen does not sit on a block. She sits on a beast that has been made to stand still.

The carrying chair is a different machine: two long poles, a seat between them, hieroglyphs naming Hetepheres, gold-plated copper at the poles’ ends (electrotypes on the Boston copy, by William Young). It is furniture and transport. The people who lifted it are not in the case. Their labor is the length of the poles.

## Cedar, ebony, and the Egyptian forest that was not there

Egypt’s valley did not grow furniture timber in the sizes a palace wanted. Killen’s materials chapter is blunt: local acacia and tamarisk for everyday frames; imported cedar of Lebanon (*Cedrus libani*) for large members and, later, for Tutankhamun’s shrines and at least one chair (Cairo JE 62029). Ebony — the word in Egyptian covers dark African hardwoods, not always *Diospyros* — comes up the Nile as a prestige skin and as small structural parts. Gold is a local boast. The joinery is Egyptian; the trees often are not.

This is already a timber trade, two thousand years before the word *deal* means a pine board in an English yard. Byblos and the Lebanese groves appear in Old Kingdom texts as the address for long beams. A furniture history that treats Egypt as a self-contained miracle of dry sand misses the boats. The sand kept what the boats brought.

Tools: copper chisels, adzes, saws, bow-drills, stone rubbers. Mortise-and-tenon is the structural sentence. Pegs lock tenons. Angled braces, sometimes a forked branch left recognizable, stiffen the back of later chairs; the Hetepheres reconstructions show the same family of thought. Resinous glue appears in New Kingdom chairs with certainty (Hatnefer, chapter 4); for Dynasty 4 the reconstructions infer it `[CITE NEEDED: Reisner on adhesive residues in G 7000 X]`.

## Why the chamber was empty

The empty sarcophagus has generated a literature that is not about furniture and will not be settled here. Reisner’s own narrative — a reburial after robbery, a secret shaft, a queen whose body was lost — has been revised, doubted, and re-revised. Mark Lehner and the Giza Plateau Mapping Project treat G 7000 X as part of the eastern cemetery’s politics, not as a detective novel. For this series the useful fact is smaller: the furniture was worth hiding with or without a corpse. A bed and a carrying chair were part of what made a queen a queen in the ground. They were not afterthoughts stacked around a coffin.

Old Kingdom tomb reliefs, from Saqqara mastabas to Giza, show beds, stools, and boxes in offering lists and in workshop scenes. The carpenter’s shop on a Fifth-Dynasty wall is a better “origin of the trade” than any modern slogan. Men adze a bed-leg; others stretch webbing. The Hetepheres find is the three-dimensional proof that those reliefs were not hieroglyphs for “comfort.” They were pictures of objects that could be weighed in gold.

## Reconstruction as a 1920s craft

Joseph Gerte’s Boston copies are furniture twice over: they are 1938 cabinetmaking that follows 1920s archaeological drawings that follow 2600 BCE gold. Digital Giza is honest about the layers. Anyone photographing MFA 38.957 as “Hetepheres’s armchair” is photographing Gerte, Reisner, and a queen, in that order. Cairo’s reconstructions have their own restorers and their own later campaigns. The gold sheet is ancient. The wood inside is a hypothesis with a grain direction.

This is not a reason to discard the pieces. It is a reason to read Reisner’s 1929 article before trusting a postcard. The article shows how little wood was left and how much the gold could still tell: the width of a rail, the place of a tenon, the profile of a lion’s muzzle. Furniture history at Giza is a history of residues.

## Tarkhan, Saqqara, and the bed before the queen

Killen’s opening pages refuse the fairy tale that furniture begins at Giza. Tarkhan, south of Cairo, yielded Early Dynastic bed-frames with bovine legs — a type Petrie published and later writers have dated around 3000 BCE. The animal is already the support. The royal bed of Hetepheres is a richer member of a family that carpenters already knew. Saqqara mastabas of the First and Second Dynasties, and the underground galleries of Djoser’s complex, have produced box fragments, stool parts, and the usual gold-and-wood ghosts. `[CITE NEEDED: a current Tarkhan bed inventory number in Manchester or Cairo.]`

What G 7000 X adds is not the invention of the frame. It is the meeting of the frame with a queen’s titles, a pyramid field, and a modern reconstruction industry. The carrying chair’s hieroglyphs name Hetepheres with the formula of a king’s mother. Furniture here is a writing surface. Gerte’s Boston copy had to reproduce those signs in electrotype because the inscription is part of the object, not a label stuck on later.

Old Kingdom offering lists put beds, headrests, and boxes among bread and beer. The objects are rations for a dead person who will still need to lie down. That is why an empty sarcophagus with a full household is not a paradox in Egyptian terms. The body could be missing. The bed was still a requirement.

## Copper, the adze, and the un-famous carpenter

The adze in a carpenter’s hand, on a Fifth-Dynasty relief, is the same tool that will still be recognizable in a Roman shop and, in changed steel, in a later turning shop. Copper in Dynasty 4 is soft by later standards. It is sharpened often. The quality of a lion’s paw on the Hetepheres armchair is therefore a quality of time spent, not of a hardened chisel. Reisner’s gold sheet keeps the time: the furrows of the mane are a chaser’s hours.

Un-famous carpenters appear in tomb scenes with names that Egyptologists know and furniture books skip. A named “overseer of carpenters” in a Giza mastaba is not a signature on JE 53263. It is evidence that the trade had ranks. The queen’s furniture was not a weekend project. It was a shop with a boss.

Dryness is the last material. Without the shaft’s sealed air and the desert’s lack of rain, this chapter would be a relief and a guess. The Old Kingdom’s gift to furniture history is accidental climate plus a gold skin that outlasted its tree.

## The canopy, the curtain box, and a second chair that stayed a drawing

The bed in G 7000 X was not a bare frame. Reisner reconstructed a canopy: gold hieroglyphs on uprights, a box of wooden members that once held linen, a room that could be assembled over the lion-legged bed and taken down again. The curtains had their own box. Cairo’s reconstruction of that box is JE 72030 (now also GEM 6426); the woodwork is modern, the gold and inlay are not. Ahmed Youssef Mustapha assembled the fragile sheets onto a new carcass. Boston’s copy, MFA 39.746, is his work as well, dated 1939. William Stevenson Smith published the box for the MFA in the *Bulletin* of June 1953; the inscriptions name Snefru, the queen’s husband, not only her son. A curtain box is easy to skip in a furniture history that wants chairs. It is the piece that proves the bed was a portable room, with textiles as structural as gold.

There was a second armchair. Reisner and Smith could restore it only on paper: an elaborately inlaid chair whose gold sheets and faience they had, whose wood they did not. Peter Der Manuelian returned to that “lost throne” in the *Journal of the American Research Center in Egypt* 53 (2017), with a visualization and a fabricated study piece. The famous armchair in the case — JE 53263, or Gerte’s MFA 38.957 — is therefore not “the” Hetepheres chair. It is the one that could be rebuilt first. The household was larger than the postcard.

The silver bracelets in their gold-covered box are the other object that trained the excavators to argue. Reisner called them anklets. Dows Dunham thought the smaller ones would never pass a foot. An inlay from the curtain box later showed the queen wearing them on her arms. Reisner did not recant. The official position of the expedition, he said, was that they were anklets. Furniture history has no need of that quarrel except as a reminder that even a sealed shaft produces interpretation, and that the man who found the gold did not always win.

## What the gold letters said, and who put the wood back

The carrying chair identified the tomb. The wood of the poles and seat had gone to dust; the inlaid gold hieroglyphs on the back had fallen in order on the chamber floor. They name Hetepheres as mother of the king of Upper and Lower Egypt. Without those signs G 7000 X would be a rich shaft with an empty alabaster sarcophagus. With them it is a queen’s household. Gerte’s Boston copy (MFA 38.874) had to reproduce the signs in electrotype — William Young’s work — because the inscription is part of the object. Cairo’s original reconstruction is JE 52372.

Reisner was in the United States when the sealed mouth of the shaft was found in 1925. A surveying instrument broke through the plaster that hid it; the news went by telegram. The full publication is not the 1929 *Bulletin* essay this chapter began with. It is *A History of the Giza Necropolis*, volume II, *The Tomb of Hetep-heres the Mother of Cheops*, Reisner’s manuscript completed by William Stevenson Smith and issued by Harvard in 1955. Twenty-five years of sorting gold sheet, inlay, and pottery sherds sit in that book. Anyone who photographs a reconstructed bed as if it had come intact from the limestone is skipping the only honest part of the story: the furniture is a drawing that gold made possible, and that Cairo’s and Boston’s cabinetmakers then had to cut.

Ahmed Youssef’s Cairo reconstructions and Gerte’s Boston copies are not forgeries. They are the form in which Dynasty 4 joinery can still be walked around. The gold is ancient. The grain direction inside it is a hypothesis. That is enough, if the caption stays honest.

## From platform to frame

Place Hetepheres next to a Çatalhöyük platform and the difference is the frame. The platform is the house. The bed is a portable house for one body, small enough to carry down a shaft, expensive enough to cover in gold, animal enough to stand on paws. Portable prestige has begun — or rather, it has begun in the surviving record. Earlier wooden frames in Egypt, from Tarkhan and other Predynastic and Early Dynastic cemeteries, already show the bed as a type (Killen’s opening catalog). G 7000 X is not the invention. It is the first time the type meets a queen, a pyramid field, and a modern museum’s need for a room to reconstruct.

Headrests — alabaster, wood, sometimes inscribed — are the bed’s other carpenter. A sleeper’s neck meets a curve that is an object in its own right, collected today as sculpture. In the tomb it was the pillow. Museums split them from bed-frames because the materials differ. A Dynasty 4 household did not.

The chapters immediately after this one stay in Egypt because the New Kingdom will multiply the evidence: Hatnefer’s chair with its original linen seat, Tutankhamun’s Carter 91 / JE 62028 throne, folding stools with duck-head terminals. The Old Kingdom chapter has to do more with less. It has a shaft, a bulletin article, two museums’ reconstructions, and a set of reliefs. That is a lot, if the gold is allowed to be a drawing. A visitor in Cairo who photographs JE 53263 as “the queen’s chair” is photographing a century of restoration plus a Dynasty 4 skin of gold. Both facts can sit in one caption. The gold is ancient. The tree inside it is a hypothesis with a grain direction chosen in the twentieth century.
