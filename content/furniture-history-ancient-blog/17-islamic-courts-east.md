# Rahle, Kursi, and the Ottoman Seat

The Metropolitan Museum’s Qurʾan stand (10.218) is signed and dated. Hasan ibn Zayn ibn Sulayman al-Isfahani made it in 761 AH / 1360 CE, of carved, painted, and inlaid teak, for endowment to a madrasa. Three layers of carving — vegetal, calligraphic — cover the folding X. The inscriptions include Allah, ʿAli, Muhammad, blessings on the Prophet and the Twelve Imams, and the maker’s information. When closed it is 51¼ inches high; open, it is a lectern 45 by 50 inches. It is a piece of furniture whose job is to hold a book at a respectful angle. It is also a Shiʿi object in wood, explicit about the Twelve Imams, made in Iran in a century Western surveys still sometimes skip between “Seljuk” and “Safavid.”

The Met’s label writes the maker as Zain(?) Hasan Sulaiman Isfahani and records a Rogers Fund purchase of 1910 from Tabbagh Frères. Sheila Canby, speaking for the museum’s educator files, notes that a name ending in al-Isfahani points to Isfahan or a family from there, and that deep carving of this kind is often associated with the wooded north of Iran. A later cataloguing of Timurid and post-Ilkhanid woodwork (the Bonn *Woodwork of the Timurid Period* project) places the stand in Dhuʾl-Hijja 761 — October–November 1360 — under the Muzaffarid Jalal al-Din Shah Shujaʿ, and follows Bernard O’Kane’s 1995 suggestion that the unnamed patron endowed it to a madrasa at Sadrabad, Anar. The upper square panels repeat Allah in four quadrants over spiral arabesques. The lower fields are cut as mihrab-shaped niches with a cypress in a vase. The inner faces, less worked, carry the date and the carpenter’s name without the title *ustad*. Two interlocking boards, no legs, a hinge that is a crossing: the type is a *rahle* (also *rahla*, *rehal*). Its theology is in the wood. Its carpentry is a pair of planks that refuse to be a chair.

This chapter stays with the eastern and central Islamic courts: Mamluk Cairo, Ilkhanid and later Iran, Ottoman Istanbul. It does not repeat the Kutubiyya minbar. It asks what else wood was asked to do when the room itself was cushions, trays, and textiles.

## Rahle and kursi

A *rahle* is a folding book-stand, usually for the Qurʾan. A *kursi* in Mamluk usage can be a hexagonal stand or cabinet for a large Qurʾan, inlaid in wood or in bronze with silver, as well as a seat. The hexagonal metal *kursi* associated with Sultan al-Nasir Muhammad ibn Qalawun exists in originals and in a crowd of nineteenth-century tourist copies (Penn Museum NEP69 is a documented copy; the Asian Civilisations Museum in Singapore holds another “Mamluk Revival” stand). The copies are a warning: not every “fourteenth-century Mamluk table” in a gallery is fourteenth-century. Read the catalog’s last paragraph.

The Cairo original is Museum of Islamic Art inv. 139: a hexagonal prism of copper alloy inlaid with silver, made by Muhammad ibn Sunqur al-Baghdadi al-Sankari in 728 AH / 1327–28. One side has a small paired door into a compartment. The inscriptions name al-Nasir Muhammad with the full Mamluk titulature; the legs carry the maker and the date. The museum’s own gallery text is honest about use. Some older writers called such objects dinner stands or chafing tables. The more careful reading is a Qurʾan cabinet, a candlestick support beside a mihrab, or a stand for a tray — a piece that served mosque, madrasa, and palace without changing its geometry. Al-Maqrizi’s *Khitat* still lets a reader walk the Suq al-Koftiyin, the market of the inlay craftsmen, between what are now al-Ghuriyya and al-Azhar. Inv. 139 is the sort of object that market could make when the patron was a sultan.

Penn NEP69 entered the museum in 1923 as a fourteenth-century kursi for “En-Nāsir Mohammed.” The 1923 *Museum Journal* described pierced plates, silver inlay, a little door, and a theory that charcoal in the compartment kept dishes warm. The present catalog has quietly rewritten the date: a later nineteenth-century copy made in Cairo for European and American visitors, the legs turned and screwed so they could be leveled on an unknown floor, the inscriptions still reciting al-Nasir’s glory. Sharjah’s SM1996-21 is another revival stand after the same Cairo prototype. A furniture history that cannot tell 139 from NEP69 will misdate a century of taste and a century of tourism.

Wood *rahle*s with square Kufic and ivory tooth inlay sit at the Mamluk–Ottoman overlap. Cevat Çulpan’s *Rahleler* (Istanbul, 1968) remains a type catalog. A late fifteenth-century Ottoman example sold in 2018 (Sotheby’s, Arts of the Islamic World, lot 132) was described as combining Mamluk carving habits with Ottoman inlay. The catalog cited radiocarbon at 1429–1470 (95 percent) and pointed to square Kufic repeating Muhammad and ʿAli, dog-tooth ivory on the edges, and cousins in the Turkish and Islamic Arts Museum: a Qurʾan cabinet, inv. 002. Topkapı’s quiver 1/10463 is not furniture; it is cited for the same square Kufic habit. Furniture and arms share a decorative shop.

## Thrones that pack

The Golden or Bayram Throne in Topkapı’s Imperial Treasury is walnut sheathed in gold, set with stones, made in ten sections so it could be taken apart. Tradition links it to a gift from Ibrahim Pasha, governor of Egypt, to Murad III, and to later use at Bayram receptions and accessions before the Gate of Felicity. It is a ceremonial machine, closer to a portable Achaemenid idea than to a dining chair. Ottoman court sitting, in the inner palace, was often on sofas and piled textiles along the wall — the *sedir* — not on a European suite. The throne is for the threshold moment when the empire must see a king.

The documentary trail is thicker than a tourist label and thinner than a maker’s contract. Palace tradition and later popular histories date the gift to the mid-1580s, sometimes to Ibrahim Pasha’s marriage into the dynasty. The walnut carcass, the ten pieces, the gold plaques, and a count of stones near 954 (tourmaline, topaz, chrysolite, and peridot appear in different modern sentences) are what the object still admits. An archive note of 1760 mentioning an imperial throne with 953 topazes is close enough to be the same piece, or a cousin; the extra stone in the present count is the kind of discrepancy a treasury clerk and a later curator can produce without either being careless. Portraits of Mustafa III, Selim III, and Mustafa IV seated on this throne hang in the palace. From 1845 the *muayede* congratulations moved toward Dolmabahçe’s audience hall. The Bayram Throne stayed a Topkapı object. After the morning prayer in the Chamber of the Holy Mantle, the sultan sat; viziers kissed the hem; others kissed the fringe of the throne. Then the chief treasurer took the machine apart and put it back in the vault.

A second Topkapı throne belongs in the same room and a different argument: the walnut seat of Ahmed I, inlaid with nacre and tortoiseshell, associated with Sedefkar Mehmed Agha, who also built the Sultan Ahmed Mosque. It is furniture as a cabinetmaker’s piety, not a gold skin over a packing case. The two thrones together refuse the idea that the Ottoman court had no seats. They also refuse the idea that those seats were the everyday furniture of the palace. Everyday sitting was the *sedir*: a built-in bench along the wall, mattresses, bolsters, a tray brought in and taken out. The throne is a portable architecture for a gate. The sofa is the room.

Safavid palaces (Ali Qapu, Chehel Sotun) show, in standing architecture and in later paintings, low seating, carpets as the real floor furniture, and wooden columns that are buildings, not chairs. Chehel Sotun, rebuilt after a fire in the seventeenth century, is a talar of wooden columns on stone bases, a porch that is a hall. Ali Qapu’s music room cuts plaster into jar-shaped niches; the furniture of sound is the wall. Surviving Safavid “furniture” in museums is often a chest, a table-stand, or a fragmentary inlay. Do not invent a Safavid klismos. Esin Atil and later catalogers of Islamic woodwork are the check. A painted picnic on a carpet, a low table for a bottle and a cup, a throne-platform under a textile: those are the seats the paintings will swear to.

## Domestic wood: chests, mashrabiyya, and the carpenter

Turned wood screens (*mashrabiyya*) are architecture, and they are also the furniture of privacy: a window that is a lattice, a gallery that is a veil. Cairo’s surviving houses (Gayer-Anderson; the Bayt al-Suhaymi) keep the screens and the built-in cupboards. A Mamluk or Ottoman chest with ivory and precious wood inlay is the portable wealth-object, cousin to the European chest and the Korean *bandaji*. The Museum of Islamic Art in Cairo and the David Collection in Copenhagen hold first-rate examples.

The David Collection’s Mosul chest, inv. 3/1993, is the rare medieval piece that is still a box: wood, carved and inlaid with ebony and bone, Iraq, about 1240, 81.2 by 80 by 50 centimeters. The lid is modern; some inlays are later. Kjeld von Folsach’s catalog note is the right temperature. Almost no furniture has survived from the Islamic Middle Ages, and it is unclear whether this chest was made for a mosque or a house. The arabesques match the tortuous thirteenth-century Mosul habit that also appears on the collection’s lyre-shaped bronze knocker (2/1993). Remnants of yellow pigment and gold leaf sit in the flat fields. A chest can be a liturgical cupboard or a household strongbox without changing its joints. The uncertainty is the evidence.

A later David chest, inv. 22/1983, is a different sentence about the same problem of sitting and storing. Wood, mother-of-pearl in black lacquer, Gujarat, early seventeenth century, made in a small group of Gujarati shops whose spiral lid ornament matches a canopy dated 1608–9 at the tomb of Nizam al-Din Awliya in Delhi. Von Folsach and, after him, Blair and Bloom read the gilded metal fittings and the geometric feet as Ottoman taste. The chest was probably intended for the Istanbul market. Indian work, Ottoman hardware language, a Sufi monument in Delhi as a dating peg: the portable cupboard of a floor-level room does not stay in one court.

The carpenter’s geometry — stars, polygons, the interpolation of plugs — is the same family as the minbar, applied to a box or a door. Craft continuity across “dynasties” is stronger than dynastic labels. A shop in Cairo in 1400 and a shop in Istanbul in 1550 can share a pattern book without sharing a court.

## The house that is a cupboard

Bayt al-Kritliyya, the pair of sixteenth- and seventeenth-century houses on the edge of the Ibn Tulun complex that Robert Grenville Gayer-Anderson was given leave to occupy and later gave to Egypt, is a furnished argument. The *maqʿad* (the loggia that catches a breeze), the *qaʿa* with its sunken marble *durqaʿa* and raised *iwans*, the built-in wall cupboards with their little wooden doors, the turned screens that let a woman see the street without being a display: none of this is a suite of chairs. It is furniture that cannot be sold separately from the house. Bayt al-Suhaymi, begun in 1648 on Darb al-Asfar and enlarged through the eighteenth century, keeps the same grammar at a grander scale. Visitors photograph the lattice. The cupboards are the furniture.

A *kursi* in a Maghribi madrasa, as Nadia Erzini and Stephen Vernoit mapped the professorial type, is a specialized teaching seat, not a dining chair. The eastern courts used the same word for stands and for seats. The overlap is linguistic and real. A hexagonal metal cabinet that holds a Qurʾan and a wooden bench that holds a teacher are both *kursi* because both raise a text. The European traveler who wrote that there were no chairs had walked through a house whose chairs were walls, trays, and a folding X for a book.

Turned lattice is a joinery of short members and a lot of time. Each bobbin is a small lathe problem; the screen is a thousand of them. The privacy it makes is not a metaphor. It is a measurable shade and a measurable view. In summer the *mashrabiyya* is a cooler; in law it is a boundary. Furniture series that only admit objects with four legs will miss the largest wooden surfaces in the Cairene house.

## What the inscriptions will still say

Hasan al-Isfahani’s stand names the Twelve Imams. Cairo’s inv. 139 names a sultan and a metalworker. The Sotheby’s rahle repeats Muhammad and ʿAli in square Kufic that a tileworker at the Safa Camii in Diyarbakır would have recognized. These are not “decorative inscriptions” in the dismissive sense. They are the reason the object was paid for. A furniture history that treats calligraphy as a surface treatment has not read the commission.

The same shops that cut a rahle cut doors, minbar sides, and the wooden caskets that held a thirty-part Qurʾan. The *juzʾ* box is a furniture type of its own: a small chest for a section of scripture, often inlaid, often part of a set. `[CITE NEEDED: a published Mamluk Qurʾan-box set with a complete inventory run in Cairo or the V&A.]` The rahle is the public face of that wood culture. The box is the storage.

Rogers’s Topkapı catalogs, Bloom and Blair’s *Grove* entries on woodwork, and Çulpan’s plates are enough to keep a writer from inventing a “typical Ottoman dining chair.” They are not enough to reconstruct the unrecorded household of a craftsman in the Suq al-Koftiyin. That household sat on what the market left over: a mat, a crate, a stool that did not get a sultan’s name. The named objects in this chapter are the expensive end of a much larger sitting.

## Against a certain European sentence

European travelers wrote that “Moors sit on the floor” and stopped. The sentence erased minbars, rahles, kursis, thrones, chests, and the whole built-in cupboard. It also erased the labor of the people who wove the mats and stuffed the cushions — furniture by any honest definition. This chapter’s objects are the wooden refusals of that sentence. The floor-level room is real. It is not empty. Met 10.218 still opens to 45 by 50 inches and still holds a book at the angle a reader of 1360 was meant to use. That is a more useful fact than a generalization about floors.
