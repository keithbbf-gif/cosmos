# The House of the Wooden Furniture

In 1932–33 Amedeo Maiuri’s team opened Insula V, no. 5 at Herculaneum, a modest house later named Casa del Mobilio Carbonizzato because of what room 8 held: a carbonized wooden couch and a small round table, found together in a room with three windows on the little courtyard. Andrew Wallace-Hadrill published photographs of the bed and table still in the corner (*Herculaneum: Past and Future*, 2011). Other writers noted a second table with greyhound legs. The house also has a triclinium (room 1) that is not the room that gave the house its name. Furniture, here, was not only for formal dining. It was for a room you looked out of.

Stephan T. A. M. Mols’s *Wooden Furniture in Herculaneum: Form, Technique and Function* (1999; Brill reissue 2020) is the monograph this chapter leans on. Mols went back to the excavation reports from 1740 onward, to the surviving pieces, and to the marks in architecture that show where a cupboard or a bed once stood. Herculaneum, unlike Pompeii, was sealed by a hot surge that carbonized wood and then kept oxygen off it. The chemistry is ugly and kind. Beds, tables, cupboards, household shrines, a cradle: a city’s wooden interior, incomplete but no longer a painting.

Pompeii has the wall pictures. Herculaneum has the beds. A furniture history that uses only the pictures will get the textiles right and the joints wrong.

## What carbonized wood will still admit

Mols’s technical chapters (materials, tools, joints) are the first time in this series, after Egypt, that a European site gives a joiner’s vocabulary in the object. Mortise-and-tenon, dowels, veneer, bronze fittings, turned legs, plywood-like layering in a few members — the Roman shop is a Mediterranean shop: local softwoods and hardwoods, imported luxury veneers, a taste for animal legs that Egypt and Assyria would have recognized. The three-legged round table with zoomorphic or canine legs is a type Mols and Ernesto De Carolis (*Il mobile a Pompei ed Ercolano*, 2007) both treat. Dionysiac and animal motifs sit on ordinary household tables. Luxury is a skin.

The couch (*lectus*) in room 8 is a frame with a raised end, veneered surfaces, a place for a mattress that has gone. It is a bed that can be a daybed. Roman vocabulary is fussy: *lectus cubicularis* for sleeping, *lectus tricliniaris* for dining, *lectus genialis* for the marriage bed. The same workshop could cut all three. What changes is height, decoration, and the room. The triclinium’s three couches in a U, later the curved *stibadium*, are arrangements, not species of tree.

Leaving the carbonized couch on display in the house, without the climate a museum would demand, has been a conservation failure. Guides and Wallace-Hadrill both record the disintegration. The object that named the house has been loved to pieces. Mols’s catalog and the later exhibition *Materia: Il legno che non bruciò ad Ercolano* are now the safer way to see it.

## Cupboards, shrines, and the closed room

Herculaneum’s wooden *armaria* — cupboards with doors, sometimes with a pediment like a small temple — are as important as the couches and get less postcard time. They held portraits, tools, food, the Lares. A cupboard is a chest that stood up. Medieval Europe will spend a long time getting back to this idea (chapters 13, 15, 26). The Roman house already had it. Wall niches and masonry *lararia* share the job; the wooden shrine is the portable, closeable version.

The cradle is a shock in the catalog: an everyday object, curved, carbonized, not a throne. Furniture history likes the ceremonial. Herculaneum keeps the child’s bed. That is Mols’s “function” chapter at its best — a typology that includes the unglamorous.

## Rome’s other seats

The *sella curulis*, the folding stool of magistrates, is ivory and politics. It appears on coins and in literature more than in carbonized wood. The *bisellium* is a double seat of honor in theatres and towns. The *cathedra* is a chair with a back, a teacher’s or a woman’s seat in some texts, later a bishop’s word. The *subsellia* are benches. None of these need Herculaneum to exist; Herculaneum reminds us that next to the political furniture was a house full of ordinary frames.

Marble tables with carved supports (*trapezophora*) — griffin, lion, Dionysiac figures — survive because they are stone. The National Archaeological Museum in Naples is full of them. They are the expensive cousins of the little wooden tripod in room 8. A Roman room mixed stone, wood, bronze, and textile. Museums sort by material and split the room.

## From Herculaneum to the Rhine

Mols’s later essay “Ancient Roman Household Furniture and Its Use: From Herculaneum to the Rhine” (2012) follows the same shapes north. Reliefs and the Simpelveld sarcophagus (Leiden) show couches and cabinets in a villa’s self-portrait. Waterlogged woods from the Rhine and dry woods from Roman Egypt and the Crimea fill gaps. The types change slowly. That slowness is a fact about the empire: a table leg in Campania and a table leg on the frontier belong to one craft koine, with local timber and local taste on the skin.

Chapter 11 takes that frontier seriously. This chapter stays in the house that named itself for a bed and a table. The eruption of 24 August 79 CE (the date is itself argued; the year is not) stopped a room in mid-use. No other European site does that for wood until a much later bog or wreck.
