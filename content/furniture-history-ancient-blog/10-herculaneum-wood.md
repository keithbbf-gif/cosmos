# The House of the Wooden Furniture

In 1932–33 Amedeo Maiuri’s team opened Insula V, no. 5 at Herculaneum, a modest house later named Casa del Mobilio Carbonizzato because of what room 8 held: a carbonized wooden couch and a small round table, found together in a room with three windows on the little courtyard. Andrew Wallace-Hadrill published photographs of the bed and table still in the corner (*Herculaneum: Past and Future*, 2011). Other writers noted a second table with greyhound legs. The house also has a triclinium (room 1) that is not the room that gave the house its name. Furniture, here, was not only for formal dining. It was for a room you looked out of.

Stephan T. A. M. Mols’s *Wooden Furniture in Herculaneum: Form, Technique and Function* (1999; Brill reissue 2020) is the monograph this chapter leans on. Mols went back to the excavation reports from 1740 onward, to the surviving pieces, and to the marks in architecture that show where a cupboard or a bed once stood. Herculaneum, unlike Pompeii, was sealed by a hot surge that carbonized wood and then kept oxygen off it. The chemistry is ugly and kind. Beds, tables, cupboards, household shrines, a cradle: a city’s wooden interior, incomplete but no longer a painting.

Pompeii has the wall pictures. Herculaneum has the beds. A furniture history that uses only the pictures will get the textiles right and the joints wrong.

## What carbonized wood will still admit

Mols’s technical chapters (materials, tools, joints) are the first time in this series, after Egypt, that a European site gives a joiner’s vocabulary in the object. Mortise-and-tenon, dowels, veneer, bronze fittings, turned legs, plywood-like layering in a few members — the Roman shop is a Mediterranean shop: local softwoods and hardwoods, imported luxury veneers, a taste for animal legs that Egypt and Assyria would have recognized. The three-legged round table with zoomorphic or canine legs is a type Mols and Ernesto De Carolis (*Il mobile a Pompei ed Ercolano*, 2007) both treat. Dionysiac and animal motifs sit on ordinary household tables. Luxury is a skin.

The couch (*lectus*) in room 8 is a frame with a raised end, veneered surfaces, a place for a mattress that has gone. It is a bed that can be a daybed. Roman vocabulary is fussy: *lectus cubicularis* for sleeping, *lectus tricliniaris* for dining, *lectus genialis* for the marriage bed. The same workshop could cut all three. What changes is height, decoration, and the room. The triclinium’s three couches in a U, later the curved *stibadium*, are arrangements, not species of tree.

Leaving the carbonized couch on display in the house, without the climate a museum would demand, has been a conservation failure. Guides and Wallace-Hadrill both record the disintegration. The object that named the house has been loved to pieces. Mols’s catalog and the later exhibition *Materia: Il legno che non bruciò ad Ercolano* are now the safer way to see it.

## Cupboards, shrines, and the closed room

Herculaneum’s wooden *armaria* — cupboards with doors, sometimes with a pediment like a small temple — are as important as the couches and get less postcard time. They held portraits, tools, food, the Lares. A cupboard is a chest that stood up. Medieval Europe will spend a long time getting back to this idea (chapters 13, 15, 26). The Roman house already had it. Wall niches and masonry *lararia* share the job; the wooden shrine is the portable, closeable version.

The cradle is a shock in the catalog: an everyday object, curved, carbonized, not a throne. Furniture history likes the ceremonial. Herculaneum keeps the child’s bed. That is Mols’s “function” chapter at its best — a typology that includes the unglamorous.

## Rome’s other seats

The *sella curulis*, the folding stool of magistrates, is ivory and politics. It appears on coins and in literature more than in carbonized wood. The *bisellium* is a double seat of honor in theatres and towns. The *cathedra* is a chair with a back, a teacher’s or a woman’s seat in some texts, later a bishop’s word. The *subsellia* are benches. None of these need Herculaneum to exist; Herculaneum reminds us that next to the political furniture was a house full of ordinary frames.

Marble tables with carved supports (*trapezophora*) — griffin, lion, Dionysiac figures — survive because they are stone. The National Archaeological Museum in Naples is full of them. They are the expensive cousins of the little wooden tripod in room 8. A Roman room mixed stone, wood, bronze, and textile. Museums sort by material and split the room.

## What the surge kept: joints, cupboards, a cradle

Mols’s monograph is the first European site after Egypt to give a joiner’s vocabulary in the object: mortise, dowel, veneer, turned legs, bronze fittings, local and imported woods. The three-legged table with animal or greyhound legs is a household type (De Carolis 2007). The couch in V.5 room 8 is a frame with a raised end, veneer, a missing mattress — a bed that can be a daybed. Roman words (*cubicularis*, *tricliniaris*, *genialis*) mark use, not species. Leaving the couch on site without museum climate has been a conservation failure; *Materia* and Mols’s plates are now safer.

*Armaria* with pediments share work with masonry *lararia*. A cupboard is a chest that stood up. Medieval Europe will spend centuries getting back to it. The cradle in the catalog is the unglamorous win: not every surviving bed was for a magistrate.

*Sella curulis*, *bisellium*, *cathedra*, *subsellia* are the political kit, better known from coins and texts than from carbonized wood. Marble *trapezophora* in Naples are the stone cousins of the little wooden tripod, split from it by museum departments. ## From Herculaneum to the Rhine

Mols’s later essay “Ancient Roman Household Furniture and Its Use: From Herculaneum to the Rhine” (2012) follows the same shapes north. Reliefs and the Simpelveld sarcophagus (Leiden) show couches and cabinets in a villa’s self-portrait. Waterlogged woods from the Rhine and dry woods from Roman Egypt and the Crimea fill gaps. The types change slowly. That slowness is a fact about the empire: a table leg in Campania and a table leg on the frontier belong to one craft koine, with local timber and local taste on the skin.

Chapter 11 takes that frontier seriously. This chapter stays in the house that named itself for a bed and a table. The eruption of 24 August 79 CE (the date is itself argued; the year is not) stopped a room in mid-use. No other European site does that for wood until a much later bog or wreck.

## Silver fir, bone hinges, and the Campanian bench

Mols sampled charred wood from twenty pieces of furniture and published the identifications in Wilhelmina Jashemski and Frederick Meyer’s *The Natural History of Pompeii* (2002). Of forty-four samples, thirty-nine were silver fir (*Abies alba*). The hardwoods are a short list: oak in a slat from the cradle, boxwood in a table leg, walnut in a tabletop, hornbeam in another leg, maple in a chest board. Beech, the northern furniture wood, is absent. The Campanian joiner built beds and cupboards in a pale, straight-grained fir that the mountains still grew, and reserved the fancy species for a leg or a top you would touch. Later work on Herculaneum’s structural timbers (Alessia D’Auria and colleagues) confirms the same bias: fir for the long members, local oak and chestnut in smaller shares. A furniture history that says “Roman wood” as if it were one tree is looking at a veneer.

Joints, on the surviving pieces, are the Mediterranean sentence already met in Egypt: mortise-and-tenon, dowels, half-laps where slats cross under a mattress. Mols describes a bed whose intersecting slats are half-lapped at the crossings and tenoned into the rails — a slatted base a modern joiner would recognize. Legs are turned and, on the better couches, cased in bronze. Stretchers tie the feet. The three-legged tables (six survive in the town) go back to Hellenistic models in the curve of the leg; the whole animal, or the head of a griffin, a hound, a young Dionysus at two-thirds height is, Mols argues, a Roman addition. The decorations point at dinner: wine, hunt, the bag on the table. The tables had to be light enough to lift between courses. Latin uses *mensa* for both the table and the course. That is how the language treated the object.

Cupboard doors are framed and paneled. Hinges are stacked cylinders, wood or bone, alternating fixed and moving — a piano hinge before the piano. Bone cylinders turn up in excavations across the empire; they are the archaeological signature of an *armarium* whose boards have gone. In Herculaneum the excavation reports also mention dark wood paired with bone. The household shrine with marble capitals and a pediment is the same construction raised to a small temple. Fifteen pieces of storage furniture survived: cupboards, racks, chests, shrines. Seating is scarce in the catalog: three benches and one stool. The stool is four straight legs, two sets of rungs, all mortise-and-tenon, the seat an eight-pointed star in wood mosaic inside a triple moulding. It is the unglamorous masterpiece of the town: no griffin, a lot of patience.

Roger B. Ulrich’s *Roman Woodworking* (2007) puts these pieces back among doors, roofs, and the toolkits from Pompeii. Shops yielded chisels, gouges, files, saws, a folding rule. The plane that would cut a moulding is easier to infer from the mouldings than to find in a tray. Mols, looking at the furniture itself, is honest: the Herculaneum cabinetmakers’ tools are mostly the traces they left.

## Partitions, biclinia, and the tools we do not have

Insula V.5 is not the only wooden interior. The Casa del Tramezzo di Legno (III.11) takes its name from a carbonized wooden partition, a screen that divided an atrium the way a cupboard divides a wall — furniture at the scale of architecture. The Casa del Bicentenario kept a wooden aedicula. Maiuri’s campaigns from 1927, when open-air excavation replaced the old mine-shaft method, are the reason any of this can be walked. Before then, as Mols’s archive chapter records, organic finds from the eighteenth-century tunnels mostly vanished. About forty pieces of furniture survive from the whole town. That number is a victory and a warning. The theatre still sits twenty-five metres down. Most of Herculaneum is still buried.

A *biclinium* — two couches joined at a right angle — has been found in the town. The word is Plautus’s; the object is Mols’s. It is the surviving cousin of the three-couch *triclinium* that texts and masonry copies describe and that no complete wooden set in Herculaneum preserves. Dining couches from Pompeii and Herculaneum more often survive as bronze *fulcra* and leg attachments. Eighteenth-century restorers sometimes built those fragments into benches that never existed; Mols reprints the caution. The middle couch of a three-couch set did not need a *fulcrum*. Reconstructions that give all three a raised end are repeating Henri Thédenat’s 1910 drawing, not the brick triclinium in the House of the Cryptoporticus at Pompeii.

Penelope Allison’s work on Pompeian house contents is the check on the “frozen day” story. People had already begun to move things as the mountain warned them. A bed in a corner may be a bed in use or a bed shoved. Wallace-Hadrill’s photographs of V.5 room 8 still matter because they show the couch and table as found, under three windows, before the climate of tourism finished what the surge began. The *Materia* exhibition — the wood that did not burn at Herculaneum — and the plates in Mols are now the responsible way to look. The house named for its furniture has been loved to carbon dust.

What the town refuses is the old handbook sentence that Roman houses were sparsely furnished. Mols is blunt: most of the wooden pieces are practical, not status objects. Status in the formal rooms is marble and bronze. Wood is the everyday kit, and there is more of it than a nineteenth-century atrium fantasy allowed. The cradle with two rockers, the star-mosaic stool, the cupboard whose bone hinge is a cylinder: these are not props in Félicien David’s opera *Herculanum*. They are a joiner’s day.
