# A Chair in Primary Colors

Gerrit Rietveld’s Red Blue Chair exists in MoMA as 487.1953. Designed 1918, painted in the De Stijl primaries plus black about 1923: standard lumber sizes, a hope of mass production that remained, for a long time, a carpenter’s object. Rietveld wrote about spiritual comfort. The chair is a spatial diagram you can bruise a hip on. Piet Mondrian’s paintings and Rietveld’s Schröder House (Utrecht, 1924) are the other two sides of the same argument: a reconstructed Europe after 1918, in planes.

This chapter is not a list of “icons.” It is about a twenty-year fight over whether a chair should be a craft object, a machine product, or a manifesto, and about the shops that actually bent the tubes: Standard-Möbel, Thonet, the residences of the Bauhaus, the Paris apartments of Le Corbusier, Pierre Jeanneret, and Charlotte Perriand.

## From De Stijl to Dessau

The Bauhaus (Weimar 1919, Dessau 1925, Berlin 1932–33) is a school, not a factory. Marcel Breuer’s B3, later “Wassily,” in tubular steel, is a 1925–26 idea from Dessau, using the bicycle-tube insight everyone repeats because Breuer himself talked about it. Thonet would manufacture related models. Mies van der Rohe’s MR chairs and the Barcelona chair (1929, German Pavilion) are court furniture pretending to be industrial: leather, chrome, a pavilion for a king. The Barcelona is closer to a throne than to No. 14. Caption it that way.

Mart Stam’s cantilever, the priority fight with Breuer, the patent thicket: Christopher Wilk’s *Marcel Breuer: Furniture and Interiors* (MoMA, 1981) and later legal-historical essays are the place. This magazine chapter will not award the cantilever. It will say the fight existed.

## Paris: equipment for living

Perriand, Le Corbusier, and Jeanneret’s *équipement* (the LC series as later marketed by Cassina) — the B306 chaise, the Grand Confort, the *siège à dossier basculant* — were shown in 1928–29 as a system. Perriand’s role was minimized in older captions and has been restored in recent catalogs (the Fondation and the 2019–20 exhibition literature). A chrome chair with pony skin is not a poor man’s Thonet. It is a luxury object with a manifesto attached. The manifesto matters. So does the pony.

Alvar Aalto’s bent plywood (the Paimio chair, 1931–32) is the Scandinavian refusal of chrome as the only modern. Wood, again, after all that steel. The war will make plywood a heroic material (chapter 38). Aalto is already there.

## What “international” hid

The International Style exhibition (MoMA, 1932) and the emigration of 1933–40 made a German-French-Dutch argument into an American faculty. Gropius at Harvard, Mies in Chicago, Breuer’s American career: the chairs become teaching tools. Meanwhile, other modernisms — Czech tubular steel, Soviet workers’ clubs, Japanese *mobo* interiors, Latin American shops — are not footnotes but they are still under-published in English. `[CITE NEEDED: a specific Czech or Soviet chair catalog to add in revision.]`

## 487.1953: lumber sizes and spiritual comfort

MoMA’s Red Blue Chair, 487.1953, is a gift of Philip Johnson: painted wood, 34⅛ × 26 × 33 in., seat height 13 in. Gerrit Rietveld designed the structure in 1918; the de Stijl primaries plus black were applied about 1923. The museum’s note is the right length. Rietveld used standard lumber sizes. He hoped for mass production. For a long time the chair remained a carpenter’s object. Gerard A. van de Groenekan came to the Utrecht workshop as an apprentice in 1919 and executed Rietveld furniture for decades; later branded examples (the V&A’s CIRC.367-1970; the Stedelijk’s KNA 1279; Centraal Museum Utrecht 12577) are part of that afterlife. The MoMA chair is the one this chapter stands on.

Rietveld wrote about spiritual comfort. The chair is a spatial diagram you can bruise a hip on. Seat height 13 in. is not a club-chair promise. Piet Mondrian’s paintings and Rietveld’s Schröder House (Utrecht, 1924) are the other two sides of the same argument: a reconstructed Europe after 1918, in planes. Marijke Küper and Ida van Zijl’s catalogue raisonné is the book behind the short museum paragraph.

De Stijl’s utopia — a harmonic human-made order after the war — is easy to mock from a later century. It was not a joke in 1923. New forms, they thought, were essential to rebuilding. The Red Blue Chair is that claim in standard stock. It is also, still, a chair a body has to negotiate.

## Tubular steel and the patent thicket

The Bauhaus is a school, not a factory: Weimar 1919, Dessau 1925, Berlin 1932–33, closed by the National Socialists. Marcel Breuer’s B3, later called “Wassily,” is a 1925–26 Dessau idea. Breuer himself talked about the bicycle tube. Christopher Wilk’s *Marcel Breuer: Furniture and Interiors* (MoMA, 1981) is the monograph that keeps the shop in view. Standard-Möbel, then Thonet, bent the tubes. The club-chair sit on leather slings is not a café No. 14. It is a new weight and a new gleam.

Mart Stam’s cantilever, the priority fight with Breuer, the patent thicket: this magazine chapter will not award the cantilever. It will say the fight existed. Legal-historical essays after Wilk are the place to take the fight when a reader wants a winner. The object lesson is simpler. A chair without back legs is a structural claim. Several people made it in the same years. Factories, not schools, put it into rooms.

Mies van der Rohe’s MR chairs and the Barcelona chair (1929, German Pavilion) are court furniture pretending to be industrial. MoMA 552.1953 is a 1953 example of a 1929 design: stainless-steel bars, leather, 31 × 29⅜ × 30 in., later manufactured by Knoll. Mies designed the chair for the German Pavilion at the Barcelona Exposition. The Spanish king was to preside. Mies said it had to be “an important chair, a very elegant chair… In those circumstances, you just couldn’t use a kitchen chair.” Only two were made for the pavilion. The type then went into production. The X-frame, seen from the side, is two curves crossing. The museum note traces the silhouette to Egyptian folding stools and nineteenth-century neoclassical seats. Caption it as a pavilion throne. Do not call it democratic.

## Perriand’s name on the equipment

Charlotte Perriand, Le Corbusier, and Pierre Jeanneret showed the *équipement de l’habitation* in 1928–29: the B306 chaise, the Grand Confort, the *siège à dossier basculant*. Cassina’s later LC numbers are a marketing afterlife, not the original names. Perriand’s role was minimized in older captions and has been restored in the Fondation Louis Vuitton / Gallimard catalogs of 2019–20 and in the Fondation’s own literature. A chrome chaise with pony skin is not a poor man’s Thonet. It is a luxury object with a manifesto attached. The manifesto matters. So does the pony.

*Équipement* means a system: storage, seating, a room as equipment for living. The system was shown in Paris apartments and at the Salons. It was not issued to workers. The International Style would later pretend that chrome was a social program. The invoices say otherwise. Keep Perriand’s name on the chaise even when a museum’s older label still leads with Le Corbusier. That is not a courtesy. It is the shop record.

## Paimio, birch, and wood’s return

Alvar Aalto’s Paimio lounge, model 41, 1931–32, is the Scandinavian refusal of chrome as the only modern. MoMA 710.1943.1 (Edgar Kaufmann, Jr. Fund) is bent plywood, bent laminated birch, and solid birch; another Kaufmann-funded example is 711.1943, laminated birch and lacquered molded plywood, made by Oy Huonekalu- ja Rakennustyötehdas Ab, Turku. Two closed loops of laminated birch form arms, legs, and floor runners. A thin plywood seat, scrolled at both ends, rides between them. The chair was designed with Aino Aalto for the patients’ lounge of the Paimio tuberculosis sanatorium. The angle of the back was meant to help sitters breathe. That medical claim is in the museum texts. It is also a furniture claim: wood can be “soft” if you bend it far enough.

Artek, founded in 1935 to produce and distribute Aalto furniture, is the shop that kept the birch in production. The Paimio chair influenced Charles and Ray Eames and Eero Saarinen — chapter 38’s plywood. Aalto is already there before the Navy splint. Wood’s revenge on chrome is not a postwar discovery. It is a Finnish sanatorium lounge in 1932.

## Weavers, emigration, and the teaching chair

Anni Albers and the Bauhaus weaving workshop made the textiles that furniture required. A chrome chair without a fabric is a frame. Magdalene Droste’s *Bauhaus 1919–1933* and the Bauhaus-Archiv’s workshop histories are the place to put the weavers back in the room. Include them when the room is the subject, not as a footnote to the tube.

The International Style exhibition (MoMA, 1932) and the emigration of 1933–40 made a German-French-Dutch argument into an American faculty. Gropius at Harvard, Mies in Chicago, Breuer’s American career: the chairs become teaching tools. Meanwhile other modernisms — Czech tubular steel, Soviet workers’ clubs, Japanese *mobo* interiors, Latin American shops — are not footnotes, but they are still under-published in English. `[CITE NEEDED: a specific Czech or Soviet chair catalog to add in revision.]`

Philip Johnson’s gift of 487.1953 is itself a historical act. The chair enters MoMA as a lesson. Students draw it. The lumber sizes remain ordinary. The paint remains a 1923 decision. Spiritual comfort remains a claim a body can test and find wanting. That tension is the chapter. The icons are a later poster. A school canteens stacking stools in Dessau and a pavilion throne in Barcelona do not belong to one politics. Call them both modern if the word still helps; do not call them both democratic. The weavers’ cloth and the chrome tube needed each other. A frame without a sling is a diagram. A sling without a frame is a rug. The 1920s joined them and then argued about who got the credit. Perriand’s name on the chaise, Stam’s name on the cantilever, a weaver’s name on the cloth: the caption is the politics. Restore the names the first posters dropped.


## Dessau rooms, not a factory floor

Historic photographs of student rooms and the canteen at Dessau show tubular chairs against a window wall. The Bauhaus-Archiv holds the images. They are lessons, not production stills. A school that taught the chair did not stamp out the chair. Thonet and Standard-Möbel did that. The confusion of school with factory is a later tourist habit.

Breuer’s American career after 1937 — Harvard, then independent practice — took the tubular idea into another country. The chairs became teaching tools. So did Mies’s Barcelona, by then a Knoll product, in architecture-school lounges that had never seen a king. The pavilion throne entered the reception area. That is a change of room, not a change of structure.

Aalto’s Artek shop, founded in 1935, kept birch in the catalog when chrome was the manifesto metal. The Paimio chair’s medical angle — breathe, cough, sit — is the one modern chair in this chapter whose comfort claim is clinical rather than spiritual or royal. Whether the angle helped is a question for a historian of the sanatorium. `[CITE NEEDED: Paimio patient records or later clinical assessments of the model 41 angle.]` The furniture fact is simpler. Wood was asked to do what the tube had just learned to do, and it did it in Finland in 1932.

Wilk’s Breuer book and Droste’s Bauhaus handbook remain the two short shelves a reader needs before the poster. The Red Blue Chair at MoMA (487.1953) is still the object to stand in front of. Standard lumber, 1923 paint, a gift from Philip Johnson, spiritual comfort claimed. A hip can still test the claim.


## A king, a hip, a sling

Mies said the Barcelona chair had to receive a king. Rietveld said the Red Blue Chair should comfort the spirit. Perriand’s chaise offered a body to a luxury apartment. Three claims, three budgets, three rooms. MoMA 487.1953, 552.1953, and the Perriand literature of 2019 are the places a reader can still test them. The hip remembers the lumber. The king’s chair remembers the pavilion. The pony skin remembers a price. None of them is a kitchen chair, and Mies was honest enough to say so.
