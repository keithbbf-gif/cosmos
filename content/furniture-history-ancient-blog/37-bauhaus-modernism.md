# A Chair in Primary Colors

Gerrit Rietveld’s Red Blue Chair exists in MoMA as 487.1953. Designed 1918, painted in the De Stijl primaries plus black about 1923: standard lumber sizes, a hope of mass production that remained, for a long time, a carpenter’s object. Rietveld wrote about spiritual comfort. The chair is a spatial diagram you can bruise a hip on. Piet Mondrian’s paintings and Rietveld’s Schröder House (Utrecht, 1924) are the other two sides of the same argument: a reconstructed Europe after 1918, in planes.

This chapter is not a list of “icons.” It is about a twenty-year fight over whether a chair should be a craft object, a machine product, or a manifesto, and about the shops that actually bent the tubes: Standard-Möbel, Thonet, the residences of the Bauhaus, the Paris apartments of Le Corbusier, Pierre Jeanneret, and Charlotte Perriand.

## From De Stijl to Dessau

The Bauhaus (Weimar 1919, Dessau 1925, Berlin 1932–33) is a school, not a factory. Marcel Breuer’s B3, later “Wassily,” in tubular steel, is a 1925–26 idea from Dessau, using the bicycle-tube insight everyone repeats because Breuer himself talked about it. Thonet would manufacture related models. Mies van der Rohe’s MR chairs and the Barcelona chair (1929, German Pavilion) are court furniture pretending to be industrial: leather, chrome, a pavilion for a king. The Barcelona is closer to a throne than to No. 14. Caption it that way.

Mart Stam’s cantilever, the priority fight with Breuer, the patent thicket: Christopher Wilk’s *Marcel Breuer: Furniture and Interiors* (MoMA, 1981) and later legal-historical essays are the place. This magazine chapter will not award the cantilever. It will say the fight existed.

## Paris: equipment for living

Perriand, Le Corbusier, and Jeanneret’s *équipement* (the LC series as later marketed by Cassina) — the B306 chaise, the Grand Confort, the *siège à dossier basculant* — were shown in 1928–29 as a system. Perriand’s role was minimized in older captions and has been restored in recent catalogs (the Fondation and the 2019–20 exhibition literature). A chrome chair with pony skin is not a poor man’s Thonet. It is a luxury object with a manifesto attached. The manifesto matters. So does the pony.

Alvar Aalto’s bent plywood (the Paimio chair, 1931–32) is the Scandinavian refusal of chrome as the only modern. Wood, again, after all that steel. The war will make plywood a heroic material (chapter 38). Aalto is already there.

## What “international” hid

The International Style exhibition (MoMA, 1932) and the emigration of 1933–40 made a German-French-Dutch argument into an American faculty. Gropius at Harvard, Mies in Chicago, Breuer’s American career: the chairs become teaching tools. Meanwhile, other modernisms — Czech tubular steel, Soviet workers’ clubs, Japanese *mobo* interiors, Latin American shops — are not footnotes but they are still under-published in English. `[CITE NEEDED: a specific Czech or Soviet chair catalog to add in revision.]`

Women in the Bauhaus weaving workshop (Anni Albers and others) made the textiles that furniture required. A chrome chair without a fabric is a frame. Include the weavers when the room is the subject.
