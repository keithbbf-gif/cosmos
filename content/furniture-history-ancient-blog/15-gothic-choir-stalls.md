# Choir Stalls and the Carpenter’s Gothic

A misericord is a small shelf on the underside of a folding choir seat, carved so that a monk or canon could perch, half-standing, through a long office. The word means mercy. The carvings mean everything else: a fox preaching, a man warming himself, a wyvern, a domestic quarrel, a green face in leaves. Dorothy and Henry Kraus, *The Hidden World of Misericords* (1975), and Charles Tracy’s *English Gothic Choir-Stalls* (1987) are the English door into a furniture type that is also a forest of images. The stall as a whole — canopy, standard, seat, desk — is the largest coordinated wooden interior of the Gothic.

This is not domestic furniture. It is a workplace for prayer, built by joiners and carvers who, in the later contracts, finally have names: William Hurley, the Disentis and Ulm shops, the Ripon and Beverley crews, the French *huchiers* who also made church doors. The choir is a room-within-a-room, a wooden architecture that can be finer than the stone around it.

## A stall is a chair that is a wall

The Gothic stall develops from simpler benches into a sequence of canopied seats, each with a tip-up misericord, a high back, sometimes a polygonal canopy that is a tiny vault. The desk in front holds a book. The whole can be thought of as a *cathedra* multiplied and standardized. Hierarchy is still there: the abbot’s or dean’s seat is marked; return stalls face the rest; the bishop, if present, may still have a separate throne.

Timber is usually oak in the north, walnut and other woods in the south. The construction is framed joinery at a building scale: mortises, moldings, applied tracery, sometimes paint and gold. Fire and fashion have destroyed more sets than survive. What remains (Chester, Lincoln, Beverley, Amiens, Ulm, Toledo, the dismantled sets in museums) is enough to see a European craft conversation. Motifs travel in pattern books and in the heads of itinerant carvers.

## Misericords: the unofficial furniture catalog

Because the underside of the seat was not the face of the church, it could take images the canopy would not. That is the old explanation and still a useful one, though “hidden” is relative — every canon saw his neighbor’s carvings. Domestic furniture appears in the iconography: a woman at a spinning wheel, a man in a chair, a barrel, a cart. The stall documents other furniture the way a Theban tomb painting did, from a different angle.

Secular misericords are not proof of a pagan choir. They are proof that the same carver who could cut a saint could cut a goose, and that the chapter paid for both. Tracy’s dating, based on documents and style, is safer than folklore about particular benches.

## Spain, Germany, and the late flowering

Iberian stalls (Toledo, León, Peti Juan and the northern carvers who worked in Castile) run late and dense, with Flemish and local hands mixed. German Hallenkirchen and collegiate churches produced stalls whose canopies are almost sacrament-houses in wood. The late Gothic is a furniture style as much as an architectural one: the stall, the cupboard, the *Schnitzaltar*. Tilman Riemenschneider’s names are altar names; the stalls beside them are often anonymous cousins.

When Renaissance taste arrives, some choirs are recut or replaced (chapter 26 will meet the *studiolo* and the classical order). Other choirs simply keep being used. A stall in daily service is a living object. The wear on a misericord is a use-wear study anyone can see.

## Domestic echoes

The box chair, the canopied bed, the cupboard with tracery doors: domestic Gothic furniture borrows the church’s vocabulary at a smaller scale and a lower budget. Surviving domestic pieces are fewer and more altered. A “Gothic revival” bed in a nineteenth-century collection is often more Pugin than 1480. For the genuine article, trust church woodwork first, then a few beds and cupboards with good provenance (V&A, Cluny, Germanisches Nationalmuseum).

The next chapter leaves the Latin choir for a minbar begun in Córdoba in 1137. The two objects — stall and pulpit — share a problem: a raised wooden place for a voice, decorated until the wood is a text. They do not share a theology. Putting them in adjacent chapters is a reader’s convenience, not a claim that one taught the other.

## Contracts, itinerants, and the stall as a building

Late medieval contracts for stalls — when they survive — name timber, dates, and sometimes a carver. Tracy’s English volumes mine them. A stall range is priced like a chapel because it is one: canopy vaults, painted bosses, a desk line that has to take books and elbows for two hundred years. Fire is the usual end. So is a later chapter’s “improvement,” when a classical taste recuts a Gothic canopy or throws the misericords into a store.

Itinerant carvers carried a bag of motifs. A green man in Yorkshire and a green man in Burgundy are not a secret cult. They are a shop repertoire. The same hand that could cut a saint’s drapery could cut a fox in a pulpit, and the chapter paid for both because the underside of the seat was a permitted zone. “Hidden” is a modern thrill. Canons saw the carvings every time a neighbor stood.

Spanish stalls at Toledo and León run late enough to pick up Renaissance portraits on the same seat that still has a Gothic canopy. Flemish and Iberian hands mix. A single “national Gothic” is a textbook convenience. The stall is a European conversation in oak and walnut, with local saints in the standards.

Domestic Gothic — a canopied bed, a cupboard with tracery — borrows this conversation at a lower budget. Survivals are few and often recut for nineteenth-century collectors who wanted a “Gothick” house. Trust a church stall’s dendrochronology before a bed with a romantic provenance. Pugin will later try to make the honest Gothic house again (chapter 34–35). He will be arguing with this chapter’s afterlife, not with its carpenters.

## How a stall is framed

A Gothic stall is framed joinery at building scale. Standards — the uprights between seats — carry the elbows, the misericord’s pivot, and the desk behind. The seat tips on wooden or iron pins; the misericord shelf is the underside of that seat, a bracket that takes a perched body during a long office. The back can be a paneled wall or a run of applied tracery. The canopy, when it arrives in the later fourteenth century, is a miniature vault: ribs, bosses, a polygonal or ogee crown that never had to keep out rain. Mortises, mouldings, and painted or gilded details are the finish. Oak in the north; walnut and mixed woods in the south. Hugh Harrison, the craftsman who has repaired and recorded stalls with Charles Tracy, is the modern witness to how the joints actually go together. Their 2004 book on Amiens is a stall monograph that includes the carpenter’s view.

Hierarchy is a plan before it is a carving. Return stalls face east toward the altar; the ranges face each other across the choir; the abbot’s or dean’s seat is taller or more canopied; a bishop, if he is in the building, may still have a separate throne. Tracy’s essay on the stalls from St Katherine’s-by-the-Tower (about 1365), the only substantial survivors from the mid-fourteenth-century royal ecclesiastical commissions, is the hinge for the English sequence. St Stephen’s Chapel at Westminster and St George’s, Windsor, are lost. Lincoln’s canopied stalls, about 1370, and Chester’s, about 1390, look less like miracles and more like a metropolitan idea that escaped the fire. Ely, Gloucester, Winchester, Beverley, Ripon, Manchester: each set has a local accent and a dating argument. Gloucester’s fourteenth-century wood sits under a stone that tourists photograph first; the stalls still have to take a day’s offices. G. L. Remnant’s 1969 catalog of British misericords is the image index; Christa Grössinger later wrote the social history of the carvings. Tracy’s two Boydell volumes (1987, 1990) remain the furniture history.

William Hurley, named in the opening of this chapter, was Edward III’s chief carpenter, the man of the Ely lantern and of St Stephen’s woodwork. He is a royal shop, not a misericord carver with a signature on a fox. The names that do attach to stalls come later and elsewhere.

Exeter’s early misericords (the 1230s–1240s, among the oldest dated sets in England) and Worcester’s later ones show the type before and after the great canopies. A visitor who only photographs Lincoln’s vaults will miss the older, lower stalls that are closer to a bench with a tip-up seat. Construction, in those earlier ranges, is still framed, still oak, still a desk line — only the skyline is quieter. Fire at York, at Chartres’s furnishings, at any number of parish choirs, is why the map of survivals is a map of luck. Tracy’s dating, document by document, is the refusal of that luck as a method. Ely’s lantern and its stalls sit in one carpenter’s century; the lantern has Hurley’s name, the stalls have a shop. Beverley’s misericords are a small encyclopedia of other furniture — a cart, a barrel, a chair — cut on the underside of a seat whose upper face is plain enough for a canon’s habit.

## Named shops: Lincoln to Toledo

Amiens Cathedral’s stalls, 1508–c. 1522, are the French late Gothic at full spend. Arnould Boulin, Alexandre Huet, and Jean Trupin are among the named *huchiers*. Tracy and Harrison walked the joinery; the canopies are flamboyant vaults in oak, the misericords a city of proverbs. Contracts survive for timber, delivery, and finish — a stall range priced like a chapel because it is one. Harrison’s repair notes on how a misericord pin wears are the use-wear the tourist can confirm by sitting (when a cathedral still allows it). Fire and revolution spared Amiens more luck than many French sets. Cologne and other German choirs lost ranges to later taste; what remains at Ulm is therefore not a typical survival but a lucky one.

Ulm Minster’s stalls, 1469–1474, are Jörg Syrlin the Elder’s oak, with busts of sibyls, philosophers, and prophets on the cheeks — a humanist program in a parish-collegiate church that was building the tallest steeple in Christendom. German Hallenkirchen liked a stall whose canopy could compete with a sacrament-house. Tilman Riemenschneider’s name attaches to altar shrines; the stalls beside those altars are often shop-anonymous. Ulm is the exception that has a name a visitor can say.

Toledo Cathedral holds two campaigns. The lower stalls, 1489–1495, are Rodrigo Alemán’s, a northern carver in Castile, dense late Gothic with a misericord wit that Spanish guides still advertise. The upper stalls, 1539–1543, are Alonso Berruguete and Felipe Bigarny (Vigarny): Renaissance portraits and classical orders on the same choir that still uses a Gothic seat. León’s stalls, and the work of Peti Juan and other northerners in Castile, belong to the same late, mixed conversation. María Paz Aguiló’s surveys of Spanish furniture are the bibliographic door; a dedicated recent Toledo monograph should replace the `[CITE NEEDED]` in the notes when the editor finds it.

English late sets — Ripon, Beverley, Manchester — keep the misericord as a local newspaper: a fox preaching, a domestic quarrel, a man warming his feet, a cart, a barrel, a woman at a wheel. Those last images are why a furniture series needs the stall. The choir documents other furniture the way a Theban tomb did, from below. Secular carvings are not a pagan survival. They are a permitted zone and a paid one.

Domestic Gothic borrows the tracery and the canopy at a lower budget: a box chair, a tester bed, a cupboard with pierced doors. Survivals at the V&A, Cluny, and the Germanisches Nationalmuseum are the ones to trust; nineteenth-century “Gothick” beds are often Pugin or a dealer. A stall still in daily use is a living object. The wear on a misericord is a use-wear study anyone can see. Fire remains the usual death. So does a later taste that recuts a canopy. The next chapter’s minbar, ordered in Córdoba in 1137, shares only the problem: a raised wooden place for a voice, worked until the wood is a text. It does not share a theology. Adjacent chapters are a reader’s convenience.
