# Splints, Plywood, and the Postwar House

Charles and Ray Eames spent the war years in Los Angeles making molded-plywood leg splints for the U.S. Navy. The same research — plywood bent in more than one plane — became, in 1945–46, the LCW (Lounge Chair Wood) and a family of chairs with separate seats and backs. The Met’s LCW 1984.566 is plywood, ash veneer, rubber shock mounts, about 1946. The V&A’s W.17-1989 came from Gregory Ain’s Tarter House in Los Feliz and was made by Evans Products’ Molded Plywood Division; Herman Miller in Zeeland took over marketing in 1946 and production in 1949. Pat Kirkham’s *Charles and Ray Eames* (1995) and the Vitra and Herman Miller archives are the paper trail. The chair’s “playful anthropomorphism” (V&A) is real. So is a rubber mount that let wood move without complaining.

This chapter is about a thirty-year attempt to put modern furniture into ordinary houses: Eames, Saarinen, Nelson, the Knoll office, Scandinavian stacking chairs, Italian cheap-luxury plastic, the Japanese and British utility stories, and the limits of “ordinary.”

## Scandinavia and the stack

Alvar Aalto’s prewar plywood becomes, after 1945, a Nordic export: Finn Juhl, Hans Wegner (the Round Chair, 1949, later “the Chair”), Børge Mogensen, the Danish teak wave that American department stores sold as taste. Arne Jacobsen’s Ant (1952) and Series 7 (1955) for Fritz Hansen are stacking plywood for canteens and universities. A stack of chairs is a new furniture fact: the room can be emptied. School halls and church basements changed.

British Utility furniture (1942–52) is the other plywood story: restrictions, standard designs, a state aesthetic. The Geffrye/Museum of the Home and the V&A hold examples. It is not glamorous. It is a furniture policy.

## Plastic, Italy, and the object that wipes clean

## Splint research and the two-shell honesty

Charles Eames (1907–1978) and Ray Eames (1912–1988) spent the war years in Los Angeles moulding plywood. The Navy leg splint — Cooper Hewitt 1986-99-43 is one museum example — is the research object. Compound curves in more than one plane: that is the “Eames process,” later patented. Gregory Ain, who would design the Tarter House, worked as chief engineer in that wartime shop. Pat Kirkham’s *Charles and Ray Eames* (1995) and the Vitra and Herman Miller archives are the paper trail.

After 1945 the office tried for a single-piece shell. The plywood cracked at the sharpest curves. They abandoned the single shell and moulded seat and back as separate pieces. Cooper Hewitt’s later note on its own LCW (2016-36-3) calls that the honest use of the material. Rubber shock mounts let the wood move without complaining. Wood-legged and metal-legged variants (LCW, DCW, LCM, DCM) are a kit. The V&A’s phrase “playful anthropomorphism” is real. So is a mount that gives.

## Met 1984.566 and V&A W.17-1989

The Met’s LCW 1984.566 is plywood, ash veneer, rubber mounts, about 1946, 27 × 22¼ × 23⅜ in., gift of Mr. and Mrs. Edward L. Stone in memory of Berry B. Tracy, 1984. The V&A’s W.17-1989 — given by Colin Sherborne — was made about 1947 by Evans Products’ Molded Plywood Division, Venice, California, plywood (the museum says probably mahogany), designed 1945–46. It came from Gregory Ain’s Albert Tarter House, 2401 St. George Street, Los Feliz, 1948. Ain had met Tarter, an animator, through the motion-picture cartoonists’ union and the failed Community Homes cooperative: a hundred acres at Victory and White Oak, eighty-eight families, an integrated plan the FHA rejected. Tarter kept Ain and built a hillside house from one of those plans. The chair in the V&A is that house’s furniture, not a showroom orphan.

Evans in Venice made the first plywood group. Herman Miller in Zeeland, Michigan, took over marketing in 1946 and production in 1949. The later lounge chair and ottoman (1956) are a different budget — rosewood, leather, a status modernism that *Playboy* and architects’ offices understood. Keep the LCW as the chapter’s object. It is closer to Thonet’s problem: a light chair for a lot of rooms.

Time magazine’s later “chair of the century” line is a magazine line. Use Kirkham and the museum records instead.

## Knoll, Saarinen, and the slum of legs

Eero Saarinen’s Womb chair (1948, Knoll) and the later Tulip pedestal (1958) try to clear the floor of what Saarinen called “a slum of legs.” Florence Knoll’s planning was as important as any single chair: the office as a furniture system. George Nelson’s storage walls and clocks are the graphic cousins. The Knoll archive and MoMA’s collection records are the paper. A pedestal table is a floor argument. So is a planning grid. The postwar office is being invented in the same years as the plywood lounge.

## Utility as a furniture policy

British Utility furniture (1942–52) is the other plywood-and-shortage story. The Board of Trade set up a Utility Furniture Advisory Committee in 1942. Gordon Russell joined, then chaired the Design Panel. Edwin Clinch and H. J. Cutler of High Wycombe designed a small range that about 700 producers could follow. The first catalogue appeared in January 1943. Every piece carried the CC41 mark — “Controlled Commodity 1941,” two cheeses in the popular name — and a manufacturer’s number. The press was lukewarm. Hardboard backs and drawer bottoms looked like cardboard. Russell thought pre-war cheap furniture had been “machine-made caricature.” There was not enough timber for bulbous legs or enough labour for carving.

The Geffrye Museum’s 1974 exhibition *Utility Furniture and Fashion, 1941–1951* (catalogue reissued 1995) remains the public history. Museum of the Home and the V&A hold examples. St Fagans shows Utility in a prefab. The scheme lasted through austerity until 1952. It is not glamorous. It is a furniture policy: the state as a specifier, a mark on the rail, a national style made from shortage. A later chapter will meet another specifying system in a Swedish warehouse. The comparison is structural, not a compliment.

## Who sat in the photograph

*Arts & Architecture*’s Case Study Houses used Eames furniture as a California promise. Julius Shulman’s photographs taught the world a glass house with plywood chairs. Most American rooms kept the Colonial revival suite. Italy’s postwar design was an export brand before it was every kitchen. The “democratic” chair is a real cheapening of price and a real narrowing of who appeared in the magazines.

The Tarter House chair in the V&A is the better emblem than the Shulman living room. It sat in a hillside house that grew out of a cooperative the federal government would not insure because the cooperative was integrated. The plywood is the same plywood. The politics of who got the house are not in the rubber mount. They belong in the caption anyway.

Herman Miller’s Zeeland plant and Evans’s Venice loft are two addresses for the same plywood idea. One is a wartime experiment; the other is a Midwestern manufacturer that already knew how to sell a modern room to a dealer network. The rubber shock mount — a small doughnut between wood and wood — is the unglamorous invention that lets the chair move without splitting. Kirkham is right to keep Ray Eames in the sentence: the splint, the chair, and the later lounge are a partnership’s research, not a lone-genius curve.

Saarinen’s “slum of legs” line is witty and a little unfair to Thonet, whose bent beech had already reduced the slum to a few rods. The pedestal table is a different wager: one stem, a harbor for knees, a floor that photographs clean. Knoll’s planning grids did more to change offices than any single womb-shaped lounge. Utility’s CC41 mark, on the other side of the Atlantic, did more to change British sitting than any magazine cover. A chapter that only loves California glass will miss both.

By 1970 the plywood lounge is already a collectible and a copy. The stack is in the church basement. The plastic S-curve is in the design museum. The Colonial suite is still in the living room. That split — photograph versus inheritance — is the postwar fact this series should not smooth away. The LCW in the Met (1984.566) and the Tarter House chair in the V&A are the same type with different biographies. Both beat a poster of a glass house that most buyers never entered. Utility’s CC41 mark and Jacobsen’s stack belong in the same thirty years as that poster.


## Zeeland, rosewood, and a different budget

Herman Miller’s takeover of the plywood group is a shop history, not a romance. Evans had the moulds and the California plant. Zeeland had the sales force and, from 1949, the production. The LCW stayed a light chair. The 1956 lounge and ottoman — rosewood (later replaced as species politics tightened), leather, a status modernism — are the objects architects’ offices understood. Do not let the lounge eat the LCW in this chapter. The Met’s 1984.566 and the V&A’s W.17-1989 are the right weight.

Florence Knoll’s planning office sold a floor, not a chair. Saarinen’s Womb (1948) is a foam-and-fabric nest on legs; the Tulip (1958) tries to make the legs disappear. Nelson’s Comprehensive Storage System is a wall that is furniture. The postwar house and the postwar office share a wish to clear clutter and a habit of photographing the result as if most rooms looked like that.

Danish teak in American department stores was taste as an import. Wegner’s Round Chair, 1949, is a joined wooden argument that does not need a rubber mount. It also became a television prop and a diplomat’s seat. The type’s afterlife is not the 1949 workshop. Keep the year. Designmuseum Danmark is the place to see the joint without the later nickname.

Panton’s S-chair and Kartell’s wipe-clean plastics close the thirty years with a material Banham called rustless. The oil in those shells is chapter 41’s problem arriving early. The stack in a church basement is this chapter’s quieter fact. A room that can be emptied is a new furniture idea. Most living rooms, in 1970 as in 1946, still could not.
