# Splints, Plywood, and the Postwar House

Charles and Ray Eames spent the war years in Los Angeles making molded-plywood leg splints for the U.S. Navy. The same research — plywood bent in more than one plane — became, in 1945–46, the LCW (Lounge Chair Wood) and a family of chairs with separate seats and backs. The Met’s LCW 1984.566 is plywood, ash veneer, rubber shock mounts, about 1946. The V&A’s W.17-1989 came from Gregory Ain’s Tarter House in Los Feliz and was made by Evans Products’ Molded Plywood Division; Herman Miller in Zeeland took over marketing in 1946 and production in 1949. Pat Kirkham’s *Charles and Ray Eames* (1995) and the Vitra and Herman Miller archives are the paper trail. The chair’s “playful anthropomorphism” (V&A) is real. So is a rubber mount that let wood move without complaining.

This chapter is about a thirty-year attempt to put modern furniture into ordinary houses: Eames, Saarinen, Nelson, the Knoll office, Scandinavian stacking chairs, Italian cheap-luxury plastic, the Japanese and British utility stories, and the limits of “ordinary.”

## From the Navy to Zeeland

Evans in Venice, California; then Herman Miller. The LCW, DCW, and the metal-legged variants are a kit. The later lounge chair and ottoman (1956) are a different budget — rosewood, leather, a status modernism that *Playboy* and architects’ offices understood. Keep the LCW as the chapter’s object. It is closer to Thonet’s problem: a light chair for a lot of rooms.

Eero Saarinen’s Womb chair (1948, Knoll) and the later Tulip pedestal (1958) try to clear the floor of “a slum of legs.” Florence Knoll’s planning was as important as any single chair: the office as a furniture system. George Nelson’s storage walls and clocks are the graphic cousins.

## Scandinavia and the stack

Alvar Aalto’s prewar plywood becomes, after 1945, a Nordic export: Finn Juhl, Hans Wegner (the Round Chair, 1949, later “the Chair”), Børge Mogensen, the Danish teak wave that American department stores sold as taste. Arne Jacobsen’s Ant (1952) and Series 7 (1955) for Fritz Hansen are stacking plywood for canteens and universities. A stack of chairs is a new furniture fact: the room can be emptied. School halls and church basements changed.

British Utility furniture (1942–52) is the other plywood story: restrictions, standard designs, a state aesthetic. The Geffrye/Museum of the Home and the V&A hold examples. It is not glamorous. It is a furniture policy.

## Plastic, Italy, and the object that wipes clean

Joe Colombo, Marco Zanuso and Richard Sapper’s child’s stack (the K1340, 1964), Anna Castelli Ferrieri’s Kartell work, Verner Panton’s S-chair (1967–68, a single plastic curve): the late 1960s make a chair that can go outside and be hosed. Reyner Banham wrote about this as a new rustlessness. The oil in the plastic is a materials fact for chapter 41. The optimism is period.

## Who got the chair

*Arts & Architecture*’s Case Study Houses used Eames furniture as a California promise. Most American rooms kept the Colonial revival suite. Italy’s postwar design was an export brand before it was every kitchen. The “democratic” chair is a real cheapening of price and a real narrowing of who appeared in the magazines. Chapter 42 will come back to labor in Zeeland and in the Danish workshops.
