# The Klismos and the Greek Room

Almost no Greek chair survives. The klismos — the light seat with splaying saber legs and a concave backrest that later Europe would treat as the Greek chair — is known from vase-paintings, grave stelai, and a handful of stone and terracotta models. Gisela Richter’s *The Furniture of the Greeks, Etruscans and Romans* (1966) is still the catalog of types: *diphros* (stool), *diphros okladias* (folding stool), *klismos*, *thronos* (heavy ceremonial seat), *kline* (couch). The words are literary and inscriptional as well as modern convenience. The objects under them are, for wood, mostly ghosts.

The Meidias hydria in the British Museum (bought from William Hamilton in 1772, made in Athens about 420 BCE) is not a furniture piece. It became one of furniture history’s most important documents because Hamilton’s publication, and d’Hancarville’s plates, taught eighteenth-century designers what a Greek seat looked like. The V&A’s painted klismos of about 1810 (W.21:1, 2-1958) copies a Hesperides scene that the museum links to that Hamilton world. The neoclassical chapter will return to this. Here the point is earlier: we see Greek furniture through painters who were not joiners, and then through modern joiners who trusted the painters.

## Types, not a suite

A *thronos* in Homer is a chair of honor, sometimes inlaid, sometimes with a footstool (*thronus* will become a European word for a different politics). In Classical Athens the heavy throne belongs to gods, to the priest of Dionysos in the theatre, to a few public seats. Citizens sat on benches (*ikria*, later stone) and on stools. The *diphros* is the workhorse: four legs, a square seat, sometimes a cushion, no back. The folding *diphros okladias* is the Egyptian traveler’s stool after a long Mediterranean life; it will become the Roman *sella curulis* when the backs of the legs grow ivory and politics.

The klismos is the odd one: a back, but not a throne’s back; legs that curve; a weight that vase-painters treat as movable. Women in indoor scenes sit on it; so do men. It is not, on the vases, a gendered ghetto. It is a comfortable chair in a culture that did not otherwise specialize in comfortable chairs. Richter collected the painted examples. Later writers have worried that the saber leg is a painter’s elegance, hard to make in wood without breaking. Surviving Roman and Hellenistic wooden furniture (Herculaneum; the Crimea) and the later revival chairs (Latrobe’s Philadelphia set, Met 1994.189; Robsjohn-Gibbings, Met 2001.207) prove the curve can be built. Whether every painted klismos was as thin as the brush is another question.

## The kline and the symposium

The furniture of the Greek men’s dining room is not a table and chairs. It is a set of *klinai* on which diners recline, two to a couch in the canonical picture, a small table in front of each couch, a crater somewhere in the room. The *andron* is designed around that kit: off-center door, space for the couches along the walls, a floor that can take a spill. Birgitta Bergquist and later symposium scholars have mapped the room. The couch is a bed used awake. Its textile is as important as its wood: mattress, pillows, covers that vase-painters love more than joiners’ details.

Women’s rooms, on the vases, show wool-working, a *klismos* or *diphros*, a chest (*larnax*, *kibotos*). The chest is the Greek *cassone* before the word: storage, display, a seat in a pinch. Tomb offerings of miniature furniture in terracotta (especially from the West and from children’s graves) give three-dimensional types that match the paintings well enough to trust the outlines.

## Materials and the shop

Greek woods: maple, oak, holm-oak, box, olive, imported ebony and citrus for luxury. Bronze fittings, bone and ivory inlay, leather seats, woven cord. The literary sources (Theophrastus on woods; later Athenaeus quoting earlier banquets) are richer than the finds. A Classical Athenian shop is known from dedications and from the occasional workshop deposit, not from a named “maker of klismoi” with a surviving oeuvre. `[CITE NEEDED: any inscribed furniture-maker’s dedication from the Agora that specifies chairs.]`

Stone furniture in sanctuaries and theatres — the prohedria seats, the thrones of priests — is public joinery in marble. It does not replace the wooden house. It shows what a city was willing to spend on sitting in public. The theatre of Dionysos in Athens, in its marble phases, is a furniture history of civic rank: who sits in the front, whose name is on the seat.

## What the stelai add

Grave reliefs of the late fifth and fourth centuries (the Hegeso stele is the famous one) show a woman in a klismos, a servant with a box. The chair is part of the domestic eternity the stele promises. It is also, because it is marble, the best “drawing” we have of the backrest’s curve and the legs’ splay. Furniture historians have traced klismos profiles from stelai more than from pots, because the chisel is less given to flourishes than the brush. Both are still representations.

South Italian vases of the fourth century exaggerate furniture into stage props: huge thrones, piled pillows, a furniture of tragedy and wedding. They are useful for types and dangerous for scale.

## Words in Homer, seats in a democracy

Homer’s *thronos* and *klismos* (the latter already a word for a reclining or easy seat in some lines) are not measured drawings. They are honors: a guest is led to a chair, a footstool is placed, a table is brought. The sequence is hospitality as furniture. Classical Athens inherits the sequence and changes the room. A citizen in the assembly sits on a bench; a juror sits on a bench; a priest of Dionysos sits on a marble throne that still stands in the theatre. The backed wooden chair of the house is, in this civic world, almost a private luxury.

Inscriptions that mention benches and the lease of theatre seating are more important for public furniture than any vase. `[CITE NEEDED: a specific IG inscription on ikria or prohedria leases.]` Wood in the early theatre was temporary and famous for collapsing. Stone seats are the later, expensive decision: sitting as a permanent civic fact.

Colonies and the west — Magna Graecia, the Black Sea — used the same types with local timber. South Italian vases of the fourth century pile pillows until the kline is a stage. Crimean tombs, later, will keep a little of the wood (chapter 11). For the Classical centuries the honest sentence is still Richter’s: we have types, not a warehouse.

Boxwood combs and small objects from the Agora wells prove the wood existed. They do not prove the klismos’s section. A conservator who builds a working klismos today (and several museums have) is doing experimental archaeology, not excavation. Caption those reconstructions as modern.

## Why Athens has almost no wood, and what Tumulus MM still holds

Egyptian shafts and Anatolian fires are accidents of preservation. Classical Attica had neither the sealed desert nor, for the houses we care about, a burned palace recorded as furniture. Wood was reused, burned as fuel, eaten by insects, and rebuilt in a climate that is not the Fayum. Gisela Richter catalogued types from paint and stone because that is what she had. The exception that makes the loss obvious sits in another kingdom. Tumulus MM at Gordion, excavated by the University of Pennsylvania Museum in 1957, held the furniture of an eighth-century Phrygian royal burial — Midas or his father, the argument still open. Elizabeth Simpson’s *The Gordion Wooden Objects*, volume I (Brill, 2011), is the publication: nine tables (one inlaid, eight plain, tray-shaped tops, three legs, height about 47–55 centimeters), two serving stands, two stools, a chair, an open log coffin. The inlaid table is in the Museum of Anatolian Civilizations, Ankara, on a modern mount. Rodney Young first called the design a horror; Simpson’s corrected drawing made it a masterpiece of joinery and inlay. The legs of the plain tables look like trained or naturally curved branches. There is no conclusive evidence for steam-bending. Eighth-century Phrygia can show a Greek historian what a banquet table actually is. Fifth-century Athens, on this point, cannot.

Olynthus, destroyed by Philip II in 348 BCE, is the Greek house plan we trust. David M. Robinson’s *Excavations at Olynthus* VIII, *The Hellenic House*, mapped andrones of the usual seven-couch size, entered through a painted anteroom, a raised platform around the dining room for the *klinai*. House A vii 4 is the type: yellow platform, red and yellow walls, and almost nothing left on the floor. Bronze decorative bosses with carbonized wood in the House of Many Colors may be a chest. The couches themselves were carried off, burned, or never left for Robinson to find. The andron is a furniture drawing in plaster and mosaic. The wood is a dotted line.

## Making a saber leg without believing the brush

The klismos that vase-painters loved is a problem in scantling. A back leg that sabers out and a backrest that cups the shoulders will snap if they are as thin as a Meidias line. Richter collected the painted examples and worried, in effect, that the elegance was a brush’s. Surviving Hellenistic and Roman wooden furniture — Herculaneum; pieces from the Crimea and the Bosporan tombs — and the modern revival chairs (Benjamin Latrobe’s Philadelphia set, Met 1994.189; T. H. Robsjohn-Gibbings, Met 2001.207) prove the curve can be built. They do not prove that every painted klismos was built as thin as the glaze.

A joiner has three honest ways to get the splay: cut from compass timber or a naturally curved member; join the seat rail to a leg whose grain can take the bend; or laminate, which Greek evidence does not give us. The concave backrest is best understood as a separate horizontal member, a splat tenoned to uprights, not as a single steam-bent hoop. Seat: woven cord or leather, like Egypt, like the *diphros*. Pegs. Bronze shoes sometimes, inferred more often than found. Theophrastus, in the *Historia Plantarum*, is richer on woods than the finds: maple, oak, holm-oak, box, olive; imported ebony and citrus for luxury. Athenaeus, quoting earlier banquet writers, is richer still and later. A Classical Athenian shop is known from dedications and from the occasional deposit, not from a named “maker of klismoi” with a surviving oeuvre `[CITE NEEDED: any inscribed furniture-maker’s dedication from the Agora that specifies chairs]`.

The folding *diphros okladias* is the clearer machine: two X-frames, a pivot, a seat that travels. Ole Wanscher’s *Sella Curulis* is the book that follows that stool into Rome. The klismos does not have an equivalent monograph of joints. It has Richter’s plates and a grave stele.

## Hegeso’s chair, the priest’s marble, and a hydria that taught Europe

The grave stele of Hegeso, National Archaeological Museum, Athens, inv. 3624, found in the Kerameikos and dated about 410–400 BCE, is the best drawing we have of a chair that almost never survives in wood. Hegeso, daughter of Proxenos — the names are on the architrave — sits in a klismos, sandalled feet on a footstool, a servant standing with an open box. The jewelry she lifts was paint and is gone. The chair’s backrest curve and the legs’ splay are marble, which is why furniture historians have trusted stelai more than pots. The chisel flourishes less than the brush. Both are still representations. A replica stands in the cemetery; the original is under a roof.

The theatre of Dionysos, on the south slope of the Acropolis, is the public opposite of Hegeso’s room. The front row of the later marble phases is a set of Pentelic *prohedria* thrones; the central seat belongs to the priest of Dionysos Eleuthereus. Many of the inscribed names are Hellenistic or Roman, cut over older assignments. The famous priest’s throne that visitors photograph is not a fifth-century wooden chair fossilized in stone. It is a later civic seat in a later theatre. It still shows what a city was willing to spend on sitting in public, and who sat in the front.

South Italian vases of the fourth century blow the types into stage props: huge thrones, piled pillows, a furniture of wedding and tragedy. Useful for outlines. Dangerous for scale. The Meidias hydria in the British Museum (Hamilton collection, acquired 1772; commonly E 224) is not a furniture piece. Pierre-François Hugues d’Hancarville’s plates after Hamilton taught eighteenth-century designers what a Greek seat looked like. The V&A’s painted klismos of about 1810 (W.21:1, 2-1958) is that lesson made sit-able. Chapter 30 will return to this. Here the point is only that we see Greek furniture through painters who were not joiners, and then through modern joiners who trusted the painters. Richter’s plates are still the place to learn the types before looking at a vase. Gordion is the place to remember that wood, when it survives, is heavier, stranger, and more jointed than a red-figure line.

## Inheritance

Etruria will take the couch and make it a couple’s banquet in terracotta (chapter 9). Rome will take the folding stool and the couch and write laws about who may sit on which (chapter 10). Neoclassicism will take the klismos and make it the chair of 1808 Philadelphia and 1937 New York. The Greek room itself — *andron*, stools, a light chair with a curved back — remains mostly paint. That is not a failure of the soil alone. It is a failure of wood in a climate that is not Egypt and a later economy that burned and reused.

Richter’s plates are still the place to learn the types before looking at a vase. The types are few. The social life around them is not.

Richter’s plates remain the first homework. A student who can tell a *diphros* from a *klismos* from a *thronos* from a *kline* has the grammar. The *andron*’s off-center door and the grave stele’s servant with a box are the sentences. Experimental reconstructions in museums are modern joinery and should be labeled as such. The Meidias hydria’s afterlife in Hamilton’s library is how this grammar leaked into 1810 painted chairs and 1808 Philadelphia. Chapter 30 will pick up that leak. This chapter leaves the leak as a warning: we see Greek furniture through painters, then through modern makers who trusted the painters.
