# A filed Florida bill would make DOE write a statewide SLP staffing plan. It is not established as law.

TALLAHASSEE — Nov. 19, 2025 — House Bill 471 for the 2026 Florida session is a five-page filing that would create section 1012.02 of the Florida Statutes and hand the Department of Education a standing assignment: write and keep a statewide strategic plan for recruiting and retaining speech-language pathologists and for the provision of what the bill calls quality services to students with speech-language disabilities.

That is the filed text on the Senate’s bill page. This pack does not establish that the bill was enacted, defeated, substituted, or signed. It does not carry vote counts. A clinician reading a recruiter summary that treats HB 471 as “the new Florida law” should open the Senate tracker, not this draft.

## What the filed draft would actually require

The plan’s first job is data. DOE would have to analyze staffing needs in districts and charter schools on an ongoing basis through the school year. The minimum list is specific: the total number of public schools, broken out by district and charter; the number of speech-language pathologists employed at each school and district, including contracted personnel; and, per district or charter, counts of doctoral-level SLPs, master’s-level SLPs, bachelor-level speech-language therapists, and speech-language pathology assistants.

The same analysis would examine caseloads and compensation for those employees and contractors, and it would compare those caseloads and that pay across Florida districts and charters and against contiguous states. The department would also track year-to-year changes in those headcounts, including resignations and retirements.

The four personnel categories are easy to blur in a district org chart. The filing does not blur them. It asks DOE to count each group separately, including contractors, so a published plan cannot hide a shortage behind a blended “speech staff” headcount.

None of that is a caseload cap. It is a mandate to count, if the filing becomes law.

## Recruitment ideas, cost estimates, and Medicaid dollars

The filed plan would have to look at strategies districts and charters already struggle to use: tuition assistance, continuing education, full-time and part-time and hybrid schedules with continued benefits, hybrid work environments, differentiated pay, and expanded mentorship for newly employed SLPs. DOE would estimate what those strategies cost and how far federal, state, and local dollars could stretch.

A separate clause would require DOE to report annual Medicaid revenue generated by SLP activity; the amount obtained through that activity and transferred into a district’s or charter’s general revenue fund; and the amount used for SLP pay supplements, stipends, continued professional training, and therapeutic materials. Those are accounting lines, not a promise that Medicaid will fund a new FTE.

## The weighted caseload method

The piece school clinicians will read twice is paragraph (e). The department would develop and implement a weighted calculation methodology for a caseload and workload plan. The plan would have to rest on an analysis of the activities and populations SLPs are responsible for, and it would have to include sufficient time for a listed set of duties: individualized therapeutic instruction and interventions; diagnostic and evaluation-specific speech, language, and literacy work; consultation and staff collaboration; attendance at IEP meetings and other staff conferences as appropriate; required documentation and reporting; Medicaid billing and related activities; and response to student interventions.

That list is the filing’s definition of work that counts. It is not ASHA’s workload policy, and it is not a district assignment letter. It is what the bill’s authors wrote into a proposed statute.

## The calendar the filing sets, if it ever binds anyone

DOE would create a survey. Each district and charter would complete it by Oct. 31 each year. By Nov. 30, the department would publish the statewide plan — integrating that survey data — on its website and update the appropriate Senate and House substantive committees on progress. The act’s own effective-date line is July 1, 2026. An effective date in a filed bill is not an enactment.

## What is not settled

Whether 1012.02 exists. Whether DOE has a survey instrument. Whether contiguous-state comparisons were ever run. Whether any district has a weighted methodology with time carved out for Medicaid billing. This desk is not reporting a floor vote, a committee substitute, or a governor’s signature, because this pack does not establish those facts.

Identical or companion language may exist under another bill number. Check the Senate and House pages for the current relationship table rather than assuming HB 471 is the only vehicle.

## What SLPs should verify

Open the filed PDF and the live bill page before you cite this in a union newsletter. If you work in a Florida district or charter, ask whether your employer already collects the headcount and Medicaid lines the filing describes. If a later session enacts a version of this plan, the Oct. 31 survey date and the Nov. 30 publication date are the clocks to watch. Until a chapter law exists, treat every “Florida now requires a weighted caseload” claim as unverified.
