# Only 12 percent of school SLPs supervised an SLPA. The shortage is larger than that.

ROCKVILLE — Sept. 14, 2026 — Assistants are the workforce idea people reach for when the job board stays full. ASHA’s 2024 Schools Survey puts a smaller number on the table. Twelve percent of school-based clinical service providers said they supervised a speech-language pathology assistant. Sixty-one percent said their district did not employ SLPAs. Another 28 percent said the district employed them and they still did not supervise. Meanwhile, 79 percent said openings outnumbered seekers. Those two facts can sit in the same building. They do not add up to a national supervision ratio, and this article will not invent one.

The 12 percent varied by setting: 4 percent among SLPs working in students’ homes, 12 percent in preschool and elementary, 16 percent in combination assignments. The survey is a weighted sample of ASHA-certified school SLPs, not a count of state-registered assistants. It does not say how many SLPAs a typical supervisor had. It does not say whether supervision time was inside the workload.

## What ASHA’s public scope actually says

ASHA’s *Scope of Practice for the Speech-Language Pathology Assistant*, a public policy page, is a minimum-recommendation document. SLPAs work only as prescribed, directed, and supervised by an ASHA-certified and/or state-credentialed SLP. They supplement; they do not take their own caseload. The supervising SLP keeps legal and ethical responsibility. Before an SLPA provides support independently, the SLP must have first contact with each person on the caseload. Direct supervision of each student, patient, or client must be documented at least every 30 to 60 days, depending on visit frequency and setting — ASHA’s floor, not a state’s. Medically fragile individuals require 100 percent direct supervision; live telesupervision counts as direct; a recording reviewed later does not.

On ratio, ASHA recommends that an SLP not supervise or be listed for more than three full-time-equivalent SLPAs, and that an SLPA not be supervised by more than three SLPs. It also says the appropriate number depends on caseload, experience, and setting, and that state requirements control. That recommendation is not a statute. Boards write the number that can get a clinician in trouble. Some states do not recognize SLPAs at all. Some use different titles. Readers who need a legal ratio should open the state practice act and the board’s current supervision rule, then the payer manual. ASHA’s page is the association’s public baseline.

Medicare is the clean negative. ASHA’s public billing pages state that federal law does not treat SLPAs as qualified providers, that assistant services are not reimbursable under Medicare until Congress changes the statute, and that “incident to” billing of those services is not allowed. A school that uses SLPAs under state education rules is in a different legal box from a Part B clinic.

## Shortage pressure is not a supervision plan

Seventy-nine percent “more openings than seekers” is a perception item, highest in the South Atlantic (92 percent) and lowest in the Middle Atlantic (59 percent) in the published table. Michigan’s Bridge story is one state’s illustration: 253 listed school SLP openings, virtual contractors filling gaps, and a superintendent on the record that face-to-face is preferred. Eastpointe’s superintendent also told Bridge she wants the state to let paraprofessionals provide speech services under therapist guidance. That is a policy ask in a newspaper. It is not ASHA scope, and it is not a ratio.

ASHA’s scope is explicit that the assistant role is not there to enlarge the SLP’s caseload. Supervision time, it says, has to be carved into the SLP’s workload. A district that adds SLPAs without adding supervisory minutes has not solved the 2024 “high workload/caseload” complaint. It has moved it.

## What this is not

It is not a staffing formula. It is not permission to treat ASHA’s “not more than three FTE” sentence as every state’s law. It is not a finding that 12 percent is too low or too high. It is not Medicare billing advice that an assistant’s minutes can ride on the SLP’s NPI. It does not describe a real student.

## What SLPs should verify

If a district offers an SLPA as the answer to a vacancy, open three documents: the state board’s assistant rule (if any), ASHA’s public scope for the professional baseline, and the payer or Medicaid school-based manual. Ask how first contact, the 30-to-60-day direct-supervision floor, and medically fragile cases will be scheduled. Ask whether Medicare or a commercial plan is in the mix — those rules diverge. Ask whether supervisory time appears in the workload analysis. If your state has no SLPA category, the 12 percent figure is someone else’s labor market.

This article does not offer clinical advice, billing guarantees, or diagnosis guidance.
