# Literacy barriers for people who use AAC are mostly outside the learner

UNIVERSITY PARK — May 21, 2025 — People who need augmentative and alternative communication can learn to read and write when instruction is adapted and someone competent delivers it. That sentence is the opening move of Janice Light, Christine Holyfield, David McNaughton, Dana Nieder, and Jamie Preece in *Augmentative and Alternative Communication* 41(3), pages 283–294. The rest of the paper is about why that instruction so often never happens.

This is a companion to Light, Fager, Gormley, Watson Hyatt, and Jakobs in the same issue, which maps societal barriers around access and participation. The literacy paper does not recanvas policy, rural access, or partner-training taxonomies. It stays on print: who is allowed into the literacy block, who knows how to teach it without requiring speech, and whether the AAC system itself ever shows text.

The design, again, is not a trial. The authors call it a narrative that starts from lived experience — Preece uses AAC; Nieder is a parent — and from the May 2024 Future of AAC Research Summit. They say they did not write a systematic review. The barrier list is not claimed to be exhaustive. Intrinsic factors (vision, hearing, motor access, working memory, language, speech, world knowledge) are named and then set aside: those needs, they argue, are reasons to adapt instruction, not reasons to skip it.

## Three external gates

First, exclusion. Some people who use AAC never receive literacy instruction, or they sit in lessons that only accept spoken answers. Low expectations do part of that work. The authors cite a 1993 parent-and-teacher survey in which 52 percent of parents and 36 percent of teachers did not expect functional literacy by age 25. They also cite more recent parent comments that treat speech as a prerequisite for reading. Curricula that require sounding out loud are, in this account, inaccessible unless someone redesigns the response.

Second, the workforce. A U.S. survey of more than 400 SLPs (Yi and Erickson, 2024) found that only 8.5 percent felt their preservice programs had prepared them well to provide literacy instruction — and the authors add that even fewer were likely prepared to adapt it for AAC. Caron and colleagues (2018) found that 87 percent of service providers named lack of training as a major barrier to literacy instruction for people who need AAC. Paraprofessionals, they note, are often assigned the work with little demonstration or feedback (Eskin and Caron, 2017). Those are survey figures, not your building’s staffing chart.

Caron, O’Brien, and Weintraub (2022), which this paper cites as a rare training study, put an online letter-sound module in front of SLPs, teachers, paras, and students. After the module, 92 percent of the practicing providers and all of the preservice students improved instructional fidelity; 89 percent of the children who used AAC gained letter-sound knowledge. That is one skill, one module, and a volunteer sample. It is also the sort of capacity-building the authors say is still missing at scale.

Third, the system on the tray. Picture-based AAC can support communication and some print awareness. The authors say there is no evidence that graphic-symbol knowledge itself teaches decoding or encoding. Transition-to-literacy (T2L) features — animation that brings text forward and speech that recodes it — are presented as a supplement. The paper is careful: T2L models sight words or letter-by-letter sounding. It does not give guided practice or feedback. It is not a replacement for instruction. Across cited T2L sight-word studies, 37 participants appear; 89 percent showed single-word reading gains, with a mean Tau-U of .80. Decoding-feature studies cover 32 participants in eight studies. Those are small, mostly single-case programs, not a national implementation.

## What the research still has not done

The authors’ own evidence review is pointed about holes. Most literacy studies with people who use AAC target early skills, one at a time, in controlled or one-to-one settings, against a no-instruction baseline. Writing and advanced comprehension are thin. Comparative tests of which program works for whom are largely missing. Samples have been mostly white and monolingual. Implementation science in schools is only beginning. That is why a four-child ALL-app study (Babb and Caron, 2026) can sit beside this paper without answering it: one is an agenda about access; the other is a small test of one parent-delivered tool.

## What this paper is not

It is not a lesson plan, a scope-and-sequence, or a bid specification for T2L software. It is not a finding that picture AAC is illegitimate. Preece’s line in the paper is that a person without literacy is not worth less. It is not a diagnosis of why any named student is not yet reading. It does not tell an IEP team to drop spoken-language goals.

## What SLPs should verify

If a school cites this article to justify more literacy minutes, ask whether those minutes use a spoken-only response. If a vendor cites T2L as “the literacy program,” the paper’s own distinction applies: model versus instruction. If a university program cites the 8.5 percent figure, read Yi and Erickson rather than treating one survey item as accreditation. Pair this piece with the societal-barriers paper for access and inequity; do not paste the two abstracts together and call it a protocol.

This article does not offer clinical advice, billing guarantees, or diagnosis guidance.
