# CMS added three remote therapeutic monitoring codes as sometimes therapy for 2026

BALTIMORE — Jan. 1, 2026 — Remote therapeutic monitoring is no longer only the 16-day, 20-minute family that therapy billers have been staring at since the codes first landed. The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services added three RTM codes to the 2026 list of codes that sometimes describe therapy services: 98979, 98984, and 98985. The same Therapy Services update says CMS revised the descriptors for 98976 and 98977. The contractor packet is Change Request 14250. The working file is the Annual Therapy Update.

That is the CMS inventory. It is not a fee table, and this desk will not invent descriptors or national rates for any of those five numbers.

## What ASHA’s public news says the expansion is for

ASHA’s November 2025 public news item on the 2026 fee schedule, in its coding-updates section for SLPs, describes the policy in English rather than in CPT punctuation: expanded RTM codes allow for shorter monitoring periods of 2 to 15 days, and new treatment-management codes now cover 10 to 19 minutes of clinician interaction.

The public 2026 SLP fee-schedule PDF, first edition November 13, 2025, is the longer version of the same story. Before 2026, ASHA writes, device-supply codes such as 98976 and 98977 could be billed only when a device transmitted therapeutic data for at least 16 days in a 30-day period, and treatment-management codes 98980 and 98981 required at least 20 minutes of interactive communication in a calendar month. Those thresholds, ASHA says, left little room for a two-week home-program check or a short post-adjustment follow-up.

CMS, ASHA reports, finalized new device-supply codes that can be billed when the device captures and transmits data for two to 15 days in a 30-day period, and kept the existing codes for the 16-to-30-day window. The PDF also describes a new treatment-management code for 10 to 19 minutes of interactive communication in a calendar month, sitting alongside the 20-minute family. Existing 98976, 98977, and 98978, ASHA writes, were revised to report 16 to 30 days of transmission per 30-day period.

This desk is not mapping those narrative buckets onto 98979, 98984, or 98985 line by line. CMS named those three as the new sometimes-therapy additions and said 98976 and 98977 were revised. ASHA described the 2-to-15-day and 10-to-19-minute policy. The Annual Therapy Update and CR 14250 are where a biller matches number to descriptor for a claim.

## Sometimes therapy is a flag, not a promise

“Sometimes therapy” is CMS’s disposition language on the therapy code list. It means the code is not automatically an always-therapy service. When a therapist bills it under a therapy plan of care, therapy rules attach. When someone else bills it under another benefit, they may not. The flag is why CR 14250 and the Annual Therapy Update matter more than a news lede.

ASHA’s public PDF adds conditions this desk will only repeat as ASHA’s, not as a coverage approval. RTM should be reported when services are provided under a specific therapy plan of care to manage a patient from monitoring results. The device must be a medical device as defined and approved by the Food and Drug Administration. Documentation still has to show the duration of monitoring, the number of transmission days, the nature of the device, and the details of interactive communication. Some of the new device-supply codes, ASHA says, will be contractor-priced, so the dollar amount may vary by Medicare Administrative Contractor.

MLN901705, CMS’s December 2025 telehealth-and-remote-monitoring booklet, describes RTM as non-physiological data related to therapeutic treatment — including musculoskeletal or respiratory system data, treatment adherence, and treatment response — transmitted by a connected medical device. The booklet also lays out general remote-monitoring construction: education and device setup, device supply, and treatment management. It is a Medicare Learning Network product, not a substitute for CR 14250.

CMS’s 2026 fee-schedule summary, MM14315, notes that the agency is using some Hospital Outpatient Prospective Payment System data to inform cost assumptions for some remote monitoring services. That is a rate-setting method for some technical services. It is another reason a national average pulled from a slide deck is the wrong number.

## What this is not

It is not a new 92507. It is not permission to bill a consumer wellness app as RTM. It is not a published national fee in this article. It is not a finding that every SLP, in every setting, will be paid for every code in the 98974–98986 range. It is not telehealth under 1834(m). Remote monitoring, as CMS has said in other FAQ language, is a non-face-to-face family with its own rules.

## What SLPs should verify

Open the current Annual Therapy Update and CR 14250 and read the actual descriptors and dispositions for 98976, 98977, 98979, 98984, and 98985. Ask the MAC whether a given RTM code is payable when billed by an SLP under a GN plan of care, and whether that code is contractor-priced. Confirm the device meets the FDA medical-device definition ASHA cites. Count transmission days and interactive minutes against the descriptor, not against a 2-to-15-day slogan. Do not pair RTM with another remote-monitoring family on the same patient unless the current CMS rule allows it. Locality still applies when a national price exists; the MAC still applies when it does not.
