# A 2023 inventory found 131 free AAC e-learning tools. It did not rank them.

AACHEN — June 26, 2025 — Students and clinicians who need AAC coursework often land on whatever a search engine surfaces first. Jessica Büchs and Christiane Neuschaefer-Rube, writing in *JMIR Medical Education* (2025;11:e63392), tried to describe what is actually posted, instead of adding another module. Their paper is an exploratory inventory of free-access AAC e-learning aimed at phoniatrics and speech-language pathology. It is not a consumer guide, and it is not a CE approval list.

The design is a 2023 systematic web search, reported against PRISMA 2020, across PubMed, peDOCS, Google Scholar, Google, the Apple App Store, and Google Play. Inclusion required free and immediate access (registration allowed), a definition of AAC plus at least one of three content areas (types of AAC, diagnostics, or therapy), and English or German. Social networks, video platforms, blogs, and forums were out. One author screened; the other checked validity. They registered the project at RWTH Aachen University. They did not rate pedagogical quality, and they did not test whether anyone learned more after using a tool.

## What the 131 items looked like

They kept 131 tools after screening a limited slice of each query (the first 25 results for broad terms, the first 10 for specific ones, for 1,616 records). Fifty-seven were coded basic — general information for learners with no AAC background. Seventy-four were coded advanced — beyond general information, expecting prior AAC knowledge or clinical experience. Those labels are the authors’ binary, not a university transcript.

Format was lopsided. One hundred five tools (80.2 percent) were websites. Twenty-one (16 percent) were online courses. Three were apps. Two were podcasts. Only two of the 21 courses were in German. Almost every AAC-related app they found was a talker or a sign-language trainer without an AAC definition, so it dropped out. That is a method choice, not a market census of clinical software.

Content and style followed the same pattern. Across overlapping codes, “types of AAC” was the most common content area. Visual text dominated. One hundred nineteen of 131 tools were receptive: read or listen. Only 12 offered performative tasks, and all 12 sat in the advanced pile. Basic tools were almost all websites and all receptive. Advanced tools more often covered diagnostics and therapy, more often used audio-visual material, and more often appeared as courses.

The authors read that split as a gap, not a ranking. Introductory material explains what AAC is. Material that asks the learner to decide, fill in, or explore a module is scarce, especially for beginners. Their own follow-on plan, stated in the paper, is to build and test a German advanced audio-visual course with a quiz.

## Limits that travel with any inventory

They list five. The web is not static, so the list is a 2023 snapshot and is not fully replicable. Search terms and engines were finite. Requiring an AAC definition may have excluded some advanced pages. The basic/advanced cut leaves no intermediate. And the study is quantitative: they did not score accuracy, currency, or conflict of interest, though they say the retained tools appeared factually sound on inspection.

Those limits are the story’s guardrail. A hospital librarian or a graduate instructor who treats the 131 as a recommended set would be doing something the paper does not do. A vendor who treats inclusion as an ASHA-equivalent endorsement would be doing the same. The authors compared their pattern — visual, receptive, website-heavy — to an earlier Lin and Neuschaefer-Rube (2021) scan of e-learning in SLP, phoniatrics, and otolaryngology. They did not compare tools head-to-head for learning outcomes.

Language is another limit. English-language courses outnumbered German ones. The search never claimed Spanish, Mandarin, or other languages. U.S. readers who want a “complete” free AAC curriculum will not find one here.

## What this paper is not

It is not proof that free web modules replace a university AAC course or supervised practice. It is not a finding that websites are better than courses. It is not permission to skip checking a source’s date, author, or commercial ties. It does not evaluate the RERC AAC Learning Center or any other named platform as superior. It does not tell a clinician which device to recommend after a weekend of reading.

## What SLPs should verify

If you use a free AAC explainer in a course or in-service, record the URL, the access date, and whether it still matches the 2023 inclusion logic — free, definition plus clinical content, not a social feed. If a student cites “the JMIR list,” ask which tool and whether it was basic or advanced under the authors’ rule. If your program needs performative practice, this inventory says you will mostly have to build it. Check whether your licensing board or employer counts any of these hours as CE; most will not.
