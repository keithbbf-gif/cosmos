# Michigan districts are filling speech vacancies on a screen. The state cannot say how many.

EASTPOINTE, Mich. — Feb. 27, 2026 — In Eastpointe Community Schools, speech-language pathology for 149 students now runs on a mixed roster: four virtual clinicians and two therapists in the building. Thirty-seven of those students are in preschool. The superintendent is not dressing it up as a model.

“This is not an ideal situation,” Christina Gibson told Bridge Michigan, in remarks Michigan News Source also carried on Feb. 27. “I think the best speech services are delivered face-to-face.”

Those two sentences are the official record of how Eastpointe’s leader described the arrangement. They are not a treatment protocol, and they are not a statewide rule. They are what one district head said when the hiring market left her with screens.

Bridge also recorded Gibson describing a national market in which speech-language pathologists can choose where they work and districts cannot. Her stated priority, in that interview, was compliance and the delivery of services to students. This desk is not adding lines she did not say. The competitive-labor-market point is hers, as Bridge reported it, and it is the reason a superintendent would accept a roster she called less than ideal.

## What Eastpointe and the task force actually counted

Michigan News Source dated the Eastpointe numbers to late February 2026: 149 students receiving speech services, 37 of them preschoolers, four virtual providers, two on-site. Bridge Michigan, which interviewed Gibson first, published the same district figures a week earlier.

The statewide vacancy count in Bridge’s reporting came from Optimise, a special-education talent task force. As of the Friday before Bridge’s Feb. 20 story, Optimise listed 253 speech-pathologist openings in Michigan public schools. Michigan News Source rounded the same problem to “more than 250.” This desk is using the Optimise figure Bridge attributed.

Neither story produced a census of virtual contracts. Districts report vacancies to the state. The Michigan Department of Education, Bridge reported, does not know how many special-education posts are being filled by virtual contractors or employees. That gap is the second fact that matters. A district can tell you what it bought. The department cannot tell you how common the buy is.

## The demand side of the ledger

Michigan News Source put more than 215,000 students on IEPs. Bridge’s more precise count, from the current school year in that story, was 215,449, up 1.8 percent from the prior year. The IEP total is not an SLP caseload. It is the pool of students who may need related services, including speech and language.

On pay, Michigan News Source reported that Michigan ranks 44th in starting teacher salary. Bridge tied that ranking to an Education Policy Innovation Collaborative analysis at Michigan State University and published EPIC’s starting figure of $41,645, with average teacher pay of $69,067 ranking 19th. Those are teacher-salary comparisons, not a published SLP schedule. They are the recruitment numbers the two newsrooms put next to the vacancy list.

Bridge also recorded EPIC associate director Tara Kilbride describing special-education turnover — including mid-year turnover — as higher than in other teaching fields. That is a workforce observation from a named researcher. It is not a claim that every vacant SLP line is a telepractice line.

## What the newsrooms said virtual work can and cannot do

Both stories said districts present virtual clinicians as Michigan-licensed and as keeping IEP goals in place. Educators in those stories also listed operational limits of a remote clinician: a person on a screen cannot walk into a classroom during a behavioral crisis the way an on-site therapist can, and building rapport in person is harder to replicate. Those are working conditions the reporters attributed to educators. They are not a finding that virtual speech is clinically inferior as a rule, and this desk is not adopting one.

Michigan News Source named Ann Arbor among districts using virtual speech-language pathologists and social workers. Bridge described a temporary virtual assignment at Dicken Elementary after an in-person therapist resigned, covering 27 students there. This pack is not independently confirming Ann Arbor’s current roster. The point for a working SLP is narrower: at least two newsrooms found districts treating virtual contracts as a continuity tool when a posting will not fill.

MDE, in Bridge’s account, would not say whether the department believes speech services should be delivered in person. A department spokesperson pointed to IEP decisions made locally against each student’s needs. That is the official posture: the state is not publishing a delivery-mode rule from Lansing.

Tina Lawson, vice president of the Michigan Association of Administrators of Special Education, told Bridge that any virtual service should be explained to the family — through an IEP discussion, a phone call, or a letter — so parents understand who is on the other side of the screen. That is a communication practice an administrator described. It is not a clinical finding, and it is not a quote invented for this draft.

## What this is not

It is not a count of virtual FTEs. MDE said it does not have that number. It is not proof that 253 Optimise openings equal 253 empty rooms on a Monday morning; postings turn over, and a task-force list is not a personnel file. It is not a clinical comparison of outcomes for the 149 Eastpointe students. Neither newsroom published an efficacy study.

Gibson’s preference for face-to-face service is on the record. A newsroom can report that sentence. It cannot turn her preference into a treatment standard for readers’ caseloads.

## What SLPs should verify

If you are interviewing in Michigan, ask the district how many speech providers are employees, how many are contractors, and how many of either sit on a screen. Ask whether the assignment is posted as temporary. Ask who is in the room with the student. Confirm that the clinician holds a current Michigan license — districts in these stories said theirs do; you still check the license, not the press clip.

If you already work in a Michigan building, ask your special-education director whether the ISD or the department has issued any local guidance since February 2026. Do not assume MDE has a virtual-staffing dashboard. Bridge said it does not.
