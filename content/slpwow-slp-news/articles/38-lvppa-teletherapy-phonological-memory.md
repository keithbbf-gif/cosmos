# Teletherapy phonological training in lvPPA helped trained items. Transfer stayed thin.

QUEBEC CITY — Dec. 19, 2025 — Most published speech-language work in primary progressive aphasia still leans on lexical-semantic drills. Guillaume Duboisdindien, Monica Lavoie, Robert Laforce, and Joël Macoir went after the mechanism that defines the logopenic variant: phonological short-term memory. Their open-access report in *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience* (DOI 10.3389/fnhum.2025.1724345), posted 19 December 2025, is a multiple-case teletherapy study. It is the first study they know of that trains pSTM itself in lvPPA. It is not a protocol a clinic can photocopy into a plan of care.

Five adults met Gorno-Tempini and colleagues’ (2011) lvPPA criteria. All had biomarker support (three CSF, two amyloid PET). Ages ranged from 54 to 68. Time since diagnosis at the first assessment was two to seven months. All used French as a long-term primary language, could read short sentences and words, and could immediately repeat at least two-syllable words. A comorbid addiction, another brain disease that would scramble the picture, or concurrent speech therapy aimed at short-term working memory kept people out.

Baseline cognition was not uniform. Montreal Cognitive Assessment scores ran from 10 to 28. Picture-naming and communication-scale scores also spread. The authors treat that spread as clinically important, not as a nuisance. Ethics approval came from the CHU de Québec–Université Laval committee. Caregivers were present for consent.

The intervention ran five weeks: 15 sessions, three a week, 45 to 50 minutes, over a secure video link. Tasks were immediate and delayed oral repetition of words and pseudowords, organized by syllable length and adjusted to each person’s starting accuracy. Delayed trials used a five-second countdown. A scaffolding ladder (syllable breakdown, then visual support, then sustained visual support) kicked in after errors. Outcome waves were two pre-tests, immediate post, four weeks, and three months. Analyses used linear mixed-effects models with time as a fixed effect and participant as a random intercept — a reasonable choice for repeated measures in a tiny sample, and one the authors warn is fragile when *n* equals five. There was no control group.

## What improved, and what stayed put

Trained words and trained pseudowords improved in both immediate and delayed repetition. Those trained-item gains were statistically detectable after treatment and, for words, held at four weeks and three months. Pseudoword delayed repetition was less durable by the last follow-up.

Generalization to untrained lists was partial and, in several contrasts, shaky once the second baseline was the comparison. Delayed repetition of untrained words was the cleaner generalization signal. Immediate repetition of untrained material looked better against the first baseline than against the second, which the authors flag as a problem for claiming a robust transfer effect.

Functional tasks were the harder test. Phonological errors on the Quebec naming test (TDQ-60) fell immediately after treatment at the margin of significance and did not hold. Cookie Theft picture description showed no significant change in phonological-error counts. Participants produced few such errors at baseline, which left little room to move. Individual plots diverged: some people stayed stable, some lost ground by three months. Mixed models attributed a large share of variance to the person, not the clock. Two participants with MoCA scores of 10 showed the weakest maintenance.

Satisfaction, collected four weeks after treatment on an 11-item Likert scale, was high. Medians sat at 3 or 4 on a 0–4 scale. That is acceptability. It is not generalization.

## How to read a first study

The authors argue that item-specific pSTM training is feasible by teletherapy and that delayed repetition may itself be an active ingredient because it forces rehearsal. They also say the effect stayed task-specific. That pairing is the honest headline. A neurodegenerative condition is not post-stroke aphasia; transfer that sometimes appears after conduction-aphasia drills did not appear here on discourse or naming.

Limits they list: five people, no control, possible habituation, naming measures that were not built to isolate phonology, no ecological communication scale, and only partial overlap with the COS-PPA core outcome set. Random-effect estimates in such a sample are descriptive. Spontaneous strategies, fatigue, and caregiver help with the computer are all in the narrative and none of them were experimentally isolated.

## What this paper is not

It is not a treatment protocol, a telehealth coverage argument, or a reason to drop semantic therapy in lvPPA. It does not establish who should start this work, at what severity, or for how many weeks. It does not diagnose lvPPA for a reader. It does not show that repetition gains change daily conversation. It does not compare Zoom to clinic.

## What SLPs should verify

If a continuing-education slide says “teletherapy fixes phonological STM in lvPPA,” the paper’s own results are the correction: trained items, limited functional transfer, heterogeneous trajectories. Read the methods for language (French), session count, and exclusion of people already in STM-focused therapy. If you are looking at Medicare or commercial telehealth rules, this Quebec research study does not speak to them. Pair any interest in remote PPA work with the feasibility literature the authors cite; do not treat five cases as a standard of care.
