# Four states are issuing ASLP-IC privileges. The other 33 members are still onboarding.

WASHINGTON — Sept. 14, 2026 — The Audiology and Speech-Language Pathology Interstate Compact is no longer a statute that exists only on paper. It is also not the 37-jurisdiction mobility system that bill hearings promised. As of the Commission’s current home page, four member states — Louisiana, West Virginia, Ohio, and Tennessee — are issuing privileges to practice through CompactConnect. Everyone else who enacted the compact is still in the onboarding queue.

That gap is the news. SLP WOW’s January 2025 note on new enactments (Alaska, Florida, Minnesota, Rhode Island, and the U.S. Virgin Islands) was accurate for its date. It is now incomplete. Enactment got the Commission a map. CompactConnect, plus each board’s data work, is what gets a clinician a privilege.

## What “issuing” means on 14 September 2026

The Commission’s public count is 37 jurisdictions: 36 states and one territory. The interactive map, fetched the same day as this draft, splits them this way: four issuing, 33 enacted but not issuing, four with active legislation, 14 with none.

ASHA’s public news desk recorded the first operational day as 28 October 2025, when Louisiana and West Virginia opened CompactConnect. Ohio’s Speech and Hearing Professionals Board told licensees it would process applications beginning 9 February 2026. The Commission posted a 28 May 2026 update that Tennessee was issuing.

Registration, the Commission says, is open for licensed practitioners in those four states. Additional members “must onboard licensees to CompactConnect and meet the compact requirements” before they can issue. Practitioners cannot create a CompactConnect account until the home state is onboarded. ASHA said the same thing in October 2025. That rule has not been relaxed in any public Commission notice we can cite.

The application URL the Commission publishes is `https://app.compactconnect.org/Dashboard`. Home-state boards, not SLP News, answer the question “when does my board go live?”

## A privilege is a license-equivalent, not a hall pass

Under the compact, an audiologist or speech-language pathologist licensed in good standing in a member home state may obtain a “compact privilege” that the Commission describes as equivalent to a license in another member state that is actually issuing. The Commission’s own explainer is blunt about geography: practice occurs in the state where the patient or client is located at the encounter.

That sentence is doing a lot of work. A telepractice session with a child sitting in Columbus is Ohio practice, even if the SLP’s kitchen table is in Baton Rouge. If Ohio is issuing and the clinician holds a qualifying home license plus an Ohio privilege, the compact is the mobility path. If the child is in a member state that has not onboarded, the compact does not invent a privilege. Ordinary licensure still applies.

School employment adds a second gate the compact does not erase. Districts and state education agencies still set educator credentials, IEP service-delivery rules, and contractor requirements. A privilege to practice is not a teaching certificate and is not an assignment letter.

## How we got a four-state footprint after half a decade

The February 2026 Commission newsletter called CompactConnect the product of more than five years of work with board staff, Commission staff, and licensees. The November 2025 newsletter dated the first operational hour to 28 October 2025 and treated that date as the moment Section 1.1’s “mutual recognition” language became more than a purpose statement.

The same February letter recorded a $50 Commission administrative fee per privilege, plus whatever state fees and jurisprudence a remote state still charges. It also recorded a rules amendment on home-state FBI criminal background checks for initial applicants, with revised timelines and a “legal impossibility” exception. Those are Commission rules, not ASHA membership perks. Applicants should read the current rule text and their board’s instructions rather than last year’s hearing testimony.

Pending legislation, as of that February letter, included carryover bills in Michigan and Pennsylvania and reintroductions in New Jersey and New Mexico. The map’s “active legislation: 4” bucket should be rechecked the week a clinician plans a move. Bills die. Maps lag.

## What this is not

It is not a national license. It is not proof that every enacted state will issue this school year. It is not authority to ignore the remote state’s practice act, supervision rules, or abuse-reporting statutes. It is not a Medicare enrollment. It is not a substitute for checking whether a school employer wants an educator certificate.

Travel and telepractice employers will keep selling the compact as if the map were already green. The Commission’s own homepage is the correction: four states issuing, more promised, none of them automatic.

## What SLPs should verify

Confirm home-state onboarding status on the Compact Map and with the licensing board, not with a recruiter slide. Confirm the remote state is issuing, not merely enacted. Budget the $50 Commission fee plus unpublished-here state fees. Ask whether a jurisprudence exam or background-check packet is still required. If the work is in schools, ask the district which credential it will accept. If the work is Medicare telehealth, treat the compact and CMS as separate clocks — a privilege does not extend a federal practitioner-eligibility date.
