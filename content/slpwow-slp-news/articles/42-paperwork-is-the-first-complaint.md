# Paperwork is the first complaint in every school setting ASHA asked

ROCKVILLE — Sept. 14, 2026 — When ASHA asked school SLPs to name their greatest challenges from a fixed list of 13, “large amount of paperwork” came in first in every facility type the association reported: special day and residential schools, preschool, elementary, secondary, students’ homes, telepractice offices, and mixed assignments. That is the lead finding in the public 2024 Schools Survey workforce PDF. It is not a slogan from a documentation vendor.

The overall share was 76.0 percent of clinical service providers (*n* at least 3,270). Elementary respondents, the largest cell, came in at 78.3 percent. Combination assignments sat at 78.8 percent. Even the telepractice-office group, which looks different on almost every other item, still put paperwork first (67.6 percent). High workload and caseload size was second in most settings (66.1 percent overall). Volume of meetings was third (54.8 percent). The rank order is the news. The percentages are survey estimates from a 25.7 percent response, not a stopwatch on anyone’s Friday afternoon.

## Why the same complaint shows up in every building type

Paperwork is the part of the job that does not shrink when the delivery model changes. A telepractice SLP still writes evaluation reports and session notes. A secondary SLP still prepares for a stack of IEP meetings. A preschool SLP still documents eligibility. ASHA’s table is useful because it undercuts the folk theory that paperwork is an elementary-school problem or a Medicaid-billing problem only. The instrument asked about a “large amount of paperwork,” not about a named form. Respondents could select every challenge that applied. Paperwork still led.

The Baltimore County record, reported by WYPR in May 2025, is one district’s version of the same clock. Seanne Herbick described IEP meetings, lengthy assessment reports, and daily notes for 20 to 30 students, plus group-session planning, against one hour of planning and documentation time. That is a public meeting comment, not a time-and-motion study. It is enough to show why a “caseload of 70” and a “paperwork problem” are not two stories.

Half of the survey’s clinical service providers put a separate salary schedule from teachers in their top two retention ideas. Thirty-three percent wanted extra pay for Medicaid billing. Those answers treat documentation as labor that the teacher scale does not price. They are preferences, not enacted pay plans.

## The medical-side rhyme is a modifier, not a template

Outpatient Medicare therapy has its own paperwork gate. For calendar year 2026, CMS set the KX modifier threshold at $2,480 for physical therapy and speech-language pathology combined, and a separate $2,480 for occupational therapy. Claims above the line need the KX modifier as the supplier’s attestation that the record can show medical necessity. Targeted medical review sits at $3,000. Those figures are on the CMS Therapy Services page and in MM14315. They are documentation rules. They are not a clinical stop, and they are not a school IEP form.

The rhyme is structural. In schools, the complaint is volume: more students, more meetings, more notes than the planning block. In Medicare Part B, the complaint is a dollar threshold that turns the chart into the thing that keeps the claim alive. Both are news about burden. Neither is a reason to buy a note-writing product, and this desk is not selling one.

ASHA’s public 2026 fee-schedule materials also remind clinicians that most SLPs are exempt from MIPS and that targeted review is not an automatic audit of every claim over $3,000. Those are payer facts. They do not shrink a school IEP stack.

Florida’s filed 2026 House Bill 471, if enacted, would force a statewide plan that budgets time for “required documentation and reporting” and for Medicaid billing as named workload items, not leftover chores. That is still a bill, not a form. It is further evidence that legislatures have noticed the same complaint ASHA ranked first.

## What this is not

It is not a finding that SLPs document too much relative to the law. Eligibility reports, session notes, and plans of care exist because statutes and payers ask for them. It is not a claim that any named template meets IDEA, Medicaid, or Medicare rules. It is not a billing guarantee that a complete note pays. It is not advice to shorten a record below what a state or a MAC requires. The 76 percent figure is not “every SLP.”

## What SLPs should verify

If an administrator says paperwork is “just part of teaching,” the 2024 rank order is the association’s own counter-data: first in every facility type asked. If a consultant promises to “eliminate documentation burden,” ask which legal requirement disappears. If you bill Medicare, read the current KX number on CMS, not on a blog, and ask your MAC how it wants the modifier and the record to look. If you work in a district, ask whether documentation time is inside the workload analysis or leftover after the last group.

This article does not offer clinical advice, billing guarantees, or diagnosis guidance.
