# ASHA’s 2024 schools survey put paperwork first in every facility type

ROCKVILLE, Md. — Sept. 14, 2026 — The American Speech-Language-Hearing Association’s public 2024 Schools Survey workforce report is still the Association’s latest even-year snapshot of how school-based SLPs described their jobs. It is a survey. It is not a census of every school clinician in the United States, and it does not bind a district.

ASHA fielded the instrument on Jan. 25, 2024, to a random, state-stratified sample of 15,000 ASHA-certified SLPs employed in U.S. school settings, half by mail and half electronically, with follow-ups on Feb. 22 and March 28 and a “be-on-the-lookout” message on Feb. 15. The postal arm’s response rate was 29.9 percent; the electronic arm’s was 21.4 percent. Combined, the methodology note reports 25.7 percent: 3,749 respondents from 14,610 eligible clinicians after removals for retirement, bad addresses, opt-outs, and other ineligibility. Weighting was used so state shares would match ASHA’s membership database. Small states were oversampled. The workforce report draws on SLPs in special day and residential schools, preschools, elementary and secondary schools, administrative offices, telepractice offices, and combinations. That is a respectable association survey. It is still people who opened the envelope. ASHA’s suggested citation for the PDF is the 2024 Schools Survey report on SLP workforce and work conditions.

## Who answered, and how they were paid

Of employed respondents, 87 percent worked full time and 13 percent part time. Facility type mattered. Administrative-office SLPs were the most likely to be full time (93 percent). Telepractice-office SLPs were the most likely to be part time (46 percent). Among those employed full or part time, 86 percent were salaried, 12 percent were contractors, and 1 percent were self-employed.

Those shares describe the people who returned the survey. They do not describe a district’s payroll unless that district’s own HR file says so.

## The ranking that did not move with the building

Clinical service providers chose from a list of 13 challenges. A large amount of paperwork ranked first in every facility type ASHA reported: special day and residential schools, preschool, elementary, secondary, students’ homes, telepractice offices, and combinations. High workload or caseload size ranked second in most of those types. Volume of meetings was third overall.

ASHA also tested four predictors — geographic area, population density, years in the schools, and years in the profession — against those challenges. Area of the country was related to 12 of the 13. This story is not reprinting the appendix tables. The pattern is enough: the paperwork rank was stubborn, and the rest of the list shifted with place and tenure.

## Burnout, retention, and what they said would help

ASHA asked clinical service providers whether burnout was prompting them to consider a change. Thirty-four percent were considering a different work setting because of burnout. Twenty-seven percent were considering leaving the profession because of burnout. Nineteen percent were considering retiring because of burnout. Twenty-four percent said they were not experiencing burnout. Seven percent were considering a change for reasons other than burnout.

Those are overlapping “considering” items, not resignations. A person can check more than one box in a career and still be in the same building in September.

On retention, respondents picked their top two ideas from a list of seven. Half — 50 percent — included placing SLPs on a separate salary schedule from teachers. The next most-chosen items in the public summary were supplementing salaries for Medicaid billing (33 percent) and reimbursing ASHA dues (32 percent). Professional-development funding, step increases, and money for materials each drew 15 percent. Reimbursing state licensure fees drew 11 percent.

## Supervision, unions, and the job-market question

Twelve percent of clinical service providers said they supervised a speech-language pathology assistant. Most — 61 percent — said their district did not employ SLPAs. Another 28 percent said the district employed SLPAs but they did not supervise one. ASHA also asked what would encourage them to supervise a clinical fellow. Seventy percent selected financial compensation for their time. That item did not vary significantly by facility type.

Sixty-three percent said union representation was available in their district. Availability varied by facility. Telepractice offices were the outlier in ASHA’s figure: few of those respondents reported a union available.

On the job market, 79 percent of clinical service providers said there were more openings than seekers in their facility type and geographic area. The range ASHA published ran from 59 percent in the Middle Atlantic states to 92 percent in the South Atlantic. Fourteen percent said openings and seekers were in balance. Eight percent said there were fewer openings than seekers. Middle Atlantic is ASHA’s label for New Jersey, New York, and Pennsylvania. South Atlantic, in the same appendix, runs from Delaware and the District of Columbia through Florida, Georgia, Maryland, the Carolinas, Virginia, and West Virginia.

## What this is not

It is not 2026 data. It is not every school SLP, only ASHA-certified clinicians in the sample frame who responded. It is not a mandate that districts split the teacher salary schedule. It is not proof that a named county has more openings than applicants. The 79 percent figure is a perception item about the local market, not a count of postings.

## What SLPs should verify

Download the public PDF before you quote a percentage in a board memo. If you need caseload size rather than a rank-order of complaints, that is a different 2024 Schools Survey report. If you work in the Middle Atlantic or the South Atlantic, use ASHA’s regional figure as a survey result, not as your HR department’s vacancy rate. And if someone tells you “ASHA says school SLPs are leaving,” the actual items are “considering,” at 34 percent for a setting change and 27 percent for leaving the profession, on a 2024 sample.
