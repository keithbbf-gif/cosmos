# What CMS says happens to Medicare telehealth on Jan. 1, 2028

BALTIMORE — Feb. 26, 2026 — The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services does not hedge the morning after the current statute dies. In a telehealth FAQ updated February 26, 2026, the agency answers the two questions that will govern January 1, 2028, if Congress does not act again: where the beneficiary may sit, and who may sit at the distant site. Speech-language pathologists are named in the second answer. They are not named as an exception.

This is a reading of a public CMS document and of ASHA’s public Medicare telehealth page. It is not a prediction that Congress will stay silent. It is not a billing protocol. It is not legal advice.

## Q1: the map comes back, with a behavioral-health hole

Question 1 asks whether Medicare beneficiaries need to be in a rural area and in a medical facility to receive Medicare telehealth services. CMS’s answer has a through-date and a start-date.

Through December 31, 2027, beneficiaries can receive Medicare telehealth services anywhere in the United States and territories. Starting January 1, 2028, except for behavioral health services, beneficiaries will generally need to be in a medical facility and in a rural area.

That is the pre-flexibility geography returning for most of the list. Behavioral health is the stated exception in Q1. The FAQ later notes that the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2021, permanently removed geographic and place-of-service restrictions for behavioral health telehealth, including in the home. Speech-language pathology treatment is not described there as behavioral health. This desk will not stretch the exception.

Audio-only narrows on the same morning. Through December 31, 2027, beneficiaries may receive audio-only telehealth in their homes. Starting January 1, 2028, Q6 limits audio-only, for physicians and practitioners, to behavioral health services at home under conditions CMS lists.

## Q2: four therapy professions leave the roster

Question 2 asks whether there are restrictions on the types of practitioners who can furnish Medicare telehealth services. CMS’s answer is the sentence SLPs should print.

Through December 31, 2027, an extended range of practitioners may bill for Medicare telehealth services. Starting January 1, 2028, physical therapists, occupational therapists, speech-language pathologists, and audiologists can no longer furnish Medicare telehealth services.

The agency does not add “unless the code is 92507.” It does not add “unless the beneficiary is rural.” It names the professions. The Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2026, section 6209 — the authority CMS’s Therapy Services page cites for PTs, OTs, and SLPs, including 98966–98968 — is written through December 31, 2027. The FAQ is the implementation voice for the day that sentence expires.

Hospitals do not get a side door in Q3. CMS says it is aligning payment for outpatient therapy, diabetes self-management training, and medical nutrition therapy furnished remotely by hospital staff to beneficiaries in their homes with Medicare telehealth policy. Through December 31, 2027, hospitals may bill for those services. Starting January 1, 2028, hospitals may no longer bill for them when furnished remotely by hospital staff to beneficiaries in their homes.

## Codes can remain listed after people drop off

ASHA’s public page is the decoder for clinicians who will otherwise hear “telehealth ended.” CMS decides which CPT codes are authorized telehealth services. Congress decides which specialties may bill them. ASHA writes that if Congress does not extend coverage for audiologists and SLPs beyond 2027, Medicare could continue to cover the services those clinicians typically provide — “as represented by CPT codes” — when a physician or practitioner furnishes them.

The list can outlive the SLP. The 2026 permanent-list decision does not repeal 1834(m)’s people rule. A permanent code is a permanent *service* category. It is not a permanent SLP enrollment.

## What ASHA says about cash-pay if the authority lapses

ASHA’s February 3, 2026, public page describes a private-pay path if Congress later fails to extend Medicare telehealth coverage for audiologists and SLPs beyond the current window. In that interval, ASHA says, clinicians can accept cash from Medicare patients for telehealth services. If Congress then extends coverage again, ASHA says clinicians must resume submitting claims to Medicare and cannot take cash from a Medicare patient for services that are on the authorized telehealth services list. For codes that are not authorized telehealth services, ASHA says audiologists and SLPs can continue to accept private payment from Medicare beneficiaries.

ASHA also warns that if a later statute restores coverage retroactively, enrolled Medicare providers would be required to refund privately paid amounts and submit claims. The association discusses Advance Beneficiary Notice practice and points to Chapter 30 of the Medicare Claims Processing Manual for statutorily excluded services. Those are ASHA’s public readings of CMS guidance and the statute. They are not this desk’s legal opinion, and they are not a template for a particular patient file.

HIPAA enforcement, ASHA notes, has already resumed. A lapse in practitioner authority would not revive the public-health-emergency platform discretion.

## What this is not

It is not a 2028 denial of in-person SLP services, not a statement that 92507 leaves the telehealth list, and not a Medicare Advantage or Medicaid rule. It is not permission to start cash-pay arrangements in 2026 while the current extension is in force. It is not a promise that Congress will, or will not, write another date.

## What SLPs should verify

Reread Q1 and Q2 of the February 26, 2026, FAQ the week a 2028 calendar is built. Watch for a new statute — and for a CMS transmittal that implements it — before treating January 1, 2028, as either a cliff or a rumor. If a cash-pay discussion is even on the table after a lapse, take it to counsel and to the MAC; ASHA’s public page is an explainer, not a representation letter. Keep traditional fee-for-service, Advantage, and Medicaid in separate folders. In-person Medicare SLP coverage is a different benefit.

This article does not offer clinical advice, billing guarantees, or diagnosis guidance.
