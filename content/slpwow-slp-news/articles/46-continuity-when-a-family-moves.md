# “Continuity of care” is a compact benefit. Issuance is still a four-state fact.

WASHINGTON — Sept. 14, 2026 — The Audiology and Speech-Language Pathology Interstate Compact Commission lists continuity of care among the reasons the compact exists, alongside military-spouse portability, access, choice, telehealth facilitation, and a simpler process. That list is a purpose statement. It is not a description of what a clinician can buy this week. As of the Commission’s current home page, privileges are issuing in four member states. Everyone else who enacted the compact is still onboarding. A family that moves does not move through one system. They move through three: licensure, school special education, and — if Medicare is in the picture — federal enrollment.

This is an analysis of those public clocks. It is not a case study, and it does not invent a household.

## What the compact can do, where it is live

A home-state licensee in good standing may obtain a privilege that the Commission calls equivalent to a license in a remote member state that is issuing. Practice is where the client is. ASHA’s 28 October 2025 news item dated the first operational day to Louisiana and West Virginia. Ohio’s board told licensees it would process applications beginning 9 February 2026. The Commission posted Tennessee as issuing as of 28 May 2026. Registration is open for licensed practitioners in those four states. Practitioners cannot create a CompactConnect account until the home state has onboarded.

The February 2026 newsletter recorded a $50 Commission administrative fee per privilege, plus remote-state fees and jurisprudence the Commission does not flatten into one chart. Continuity, in that architecture, means a clinician who already holds a qualifying home license could follow a client into another issuing state without a full second license — if both ends are live, if the fee and background-check rules are met, and if the employer accepts the privilege. Continuity does not mean the other 33 enacted members are issuing. It does not mean a school district must honor a privilege as an educator credential. It does not mean a Medicare contractor will re-enroll anyone faster.

Pending legislation noted in that February letter (Michigan and Pennsylvania carryovers; New Jersey and New Mexico reintroductions) is a map layer that changes. Bills die. This draft does not treat a filed bill as a privilege.

## What an IEP does when the school changes

Special education has its own transfer rule, and it is older than the compact. Under 34 CFR 300.323(e), if a child with an IEP transfers to a new public agency in the same state and enrolls in the same school year, the new agency must provide FAPE, including services comparable to the previous IEP, until it adopts that IEP or writes a new one. Under 300.323(f), an out-of-state transfer gets comparable services until the new agency evaluates if it decides it must and develops a new IEP as appropriate. Both agencies must take reasonable steps to move records, including the IEP.

“Comparable” is not a promise that the same SLP, the same minutes, or the same delivery model follows the child. The Department of Education has said comparable means similar or equivalent services as determined by the new IEP team, and that a team may not arbitrarily cut the level of service. A compact privilege in the receiving state can make it *possible* for a known clinician to keep seeing the child. It does not write the IEP. It does not assign the related-service provider. A receiving district that is short of SLPs — Michigan’s published virtual-coverage story is one public example of that pressure — may meet “comparable” with a different person or a different modality. That is a legal and staffing fact, not a family vignette.

## Medicare is a third enrollment

A privilege is not a PECOS file. Traditional Medicare telehealth authority for SLPs, on ASHA’s public page, runs through 31 December 2027 for listed services. Plans other than fee-for-service vary. A clinician who follows an adult client across a state line still needs to be enrolled where the payer requires it, and still needs the compact or a license where the client sits. Those are parallel gates. Missing one of them is enough.

## What this is not

It is not a moving checklist for a named family. It is not a finding that four issuing states equal national portability. It is not a guarantee that comparable IEP services will be the same SLP on a screen. It is not Medicare travel coverage. It is not advice to keep or drop a clinician after a move.

## What SLPs should verify

If continuity is the reason someone is buying a privilege, confirm both the home state and the remote state are issuing, not merely enacted, on the Compact Map and with the boards. Budget the $50 Commission fee plus unpublished-here state fees. If the client is a student, read 34 CFR 300.323 and the receiving district’s transfer procedures; ask which credential the district wants. If the client is a Medicare beneficiary, treat enrollment, the telehealth clock, and the compact as three questions. Recheck the map the week of a move. Four is not 37.

This article does not offer clinical advice, billing guarantees, or diagnosis guidance.
