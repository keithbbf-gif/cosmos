# An fNIRS-guided rTMS trial improved Chinese naming. It is adjunct research, not a standard of care.

SHANGHAI — April 22, 2026 — Choosing a stimulation target is one reason repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation studies in aphasia refuse to line up. A Huashan Hospital group tried to individualize that target with functional near-infrared spectroscopy during picture naming, then delivered neuronavigated 10 Hz rTMS on top of intensive speech-language therapy. The trial, in *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience* (DOI 10.3389/fnhum.2026.1810169), is a double-blind, randomized, sham-controlled study. It is also a single-center adjunct experiment in Mandarin-speaking subacute patients. That second sentence belongs in the lede.

Recruitment ran at Huashan Hospital, Fudan University, from 2021 to 2023, as the paper states. Eligible adults were 35 to 80 years old, right-handed native Mandarin speakers, with first-ever left-hemisphere stroke and aphasia confirmed on Chinese WAB-R and BDAE, one to six months post-onset, without prior language disorder, obvious cognitive impairment, or TMS contraindications. Of 323 inpatients screened, 28 were randomized 1:1. One withdrew before the first session because of severe COVID-19. Twenty-seven completed: 14 active, 13 sham.

Both groups received 15 weekday sessions over three weeks. Active rTMS used 10 Hz at 80 percent of resting motor threshold, 2,000 pulses a session, aimed at the left-hemisphere fNIRS channel with the strongest naming-related activation. Sham used the same schedule with the coil rotated to the edge of the scalp. Immediately afterward, both groups received 45 minutes of intensive, impairment-based SLT, therapists blinded to allocation. The primary behavioral outcome was the Chinese Boston Naming Test (30 culturally adapted items). Secondary measures included WAB-R scores, task accuracy and reaction time, and task/resting-state fNIRS.

## What the contrast showed

Both groups improved over three weeks, which is what a subacute sample plus daily SLT should do. The Time × Group interactions favored active rTMS on the Chinese BNT (F = 16.04, p < 0.01), picture-naming accuracy (F = 20.10, p < 0.01), and the WAB-R naming subscore (F = 4.44, p < 0.05). Total aphasia quotient, spontaneous speech, comprehension, and repetition did not show a group-by-time advantage. Reaction time improved in both arms without a significant interaction. An ANCOVA that adjusted for baseline scores, baseline AQ, and education left the naming effects standing. Blinding checks did not differ from chance. The authors reported no serious adverse events and no dizziness, pain, or seizure symptoms in the trial window.

Task fNIRS after active treatment showed more activation in left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and left Broca’s area, and stronger resting connectivity between those regions. Connectivity change correlated with BNT improvement (r = 0.6405, p < 0.05). Those are mechanistic associates in the same small sample, not independent proof of cause.

## Limits that keep this off a practice guideline

The authors list them. The sample is small and single-center; subgroup analyses were not powered. There was no long-term follow-up; they attribute that gap in part to COVID-related restrictions in Shanghai. Subjective improvement at discharge was not systematically scored. Targets were restricted to the left hemisphere. Language was Mandarin; the naming test was the Chinese BNT. Intensity was set for safety in a subacute cohort, not optimized. Sham that touches the scalp with a live sound is a standard control and still not a perfect one.

A reader in a U.S. outpatient clinic should add another limit the paper does not have to: this is not a coverage policy, not an FDA labeling expansion, and not a reason to market “fNIRS-guided rTMS” as routine SLP care. Both arms received intensive SLT. The contrast is stimulation on top of that therapy, in a hospital rehabilitation service that already had the hardware and a trial registry number (ChiCTR2000038515).

Descriptive means illustrate the size of the naming contrast without turning this desk into a statistics shop. Chinese BNT change was 4.64 points (SD 3.34) after active rTMS and 0.69 (SD 1.25) after sham. Picture-naming accuracy rose 8.43 percentage points versus 0.92. Those are short-term, same-examiner scores at the end of three weeks. They are not community communication outcomes, and they are not a U.S. BNT. Baseline groups did not differ on age, sex, stroke type, education, a non-language cognitive screen, or days since onset (about 104 versus 96 days). Education sat at the edge of a baseline difference (p = 0.06); the authors adjusted for it.

## What this paper is not

It is not a standard of care. It is not evidence that every person with subacute aphasia should receive rTMS. It is not a protocol for right-hemisphere or chronic patients. It does not show that comprehension or connected speech uniquely improved. It does not replace a physician-led safety screen. It does not speak to school practice.

## What SLPs should verify

If a neuromodulation course cites the 2026 Huashan RCT, ask for the sample (27 completers), the language (Mandarin), the window (1–6 months), the outcome that moved (naming), and the outcome that did not (AQ interaction). Ask whether the speaker is describing an adjunct to intensive SLT or a stand-alone “cure.” Device use, if it occurs at all in a medical setting, is a physician-and-research matter. The DOI is the document. A press-release sentence that drops “safe and effective” without those limits is not.
