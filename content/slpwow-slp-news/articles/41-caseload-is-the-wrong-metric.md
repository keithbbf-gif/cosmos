# Caseload is the number districts can count. Workload is the job.

ROCKVILLE — Sept. 14, 2026 — School speech-language pathologists have been trying to retire the word “caseload” as a complete job description for more than twenty years. ASHA’s 2002 position statement said the useful unit is workload: direct services plus evaluations, meetings, documentation, collaboration, and the rest of the legally required day. The 2024 Schools Survey shows why the argument has not landed. Among 13 listed challenges, “high workload/caseload size” ranked second in most facility types. Paperwork ranked first everywhere. The two are the same pile, counted two ways.

The survey is a spring 2024 sample of ASHA-certified school SLPs, 3,749 respondents, 25.7 percent response, weighted by state. It is not a census of every district. Seventy-nine percent of clinical service providers said there were more openings than seekers — 92 percent in the South Atlantic states, 59 percent in the Middle Atlantic. Those percentages describe perceived labor markets, not a headcount ASHA can mail to a superintendent.

## What a headcount hides

Caseload is the number of students with the SLP’s name on a service page. It is easy to put in a spreadsheet. It does not record whether those students are preschoolers who need adult facilitation, adolescents in three buildings, AAC users who need programming time, or children whose IEPs promise both direct minutes and classroom consultation. It does not record the IEP meetings, the evaluation reports, or the Medicaid notes. ASHA’s 2002 statement exists because districts that staff to a single number will always be able to say the number looks “average.”

Baltimore County made the mismatch local and on the record. At a May 2025 board meeting, covered by WYPR, a union-aided survey found that more than 62 percent of the district’s SLPs had caseloads of 61 to 100 students. The story cited ASHA’s then-most-recent national average caseload as about 50. Gail Vanderbeek, splitting two elementary schools, said students were missing legally mandated therapy and consult. Seanne Herbick called the daily load “simply unsustainable” and described evenings and weekends spent on IEP meetings, assessment reports, and daily notes for 20 to 30 students, with one hour of planning and documentation time. Superintendent Myriam Rogers said there were no SLP budget cuts in the current year or planned for FY26, that she had not seen IEP disruptions from delayed speech services, and that about 40 of 200 SLPs had booked workload meetings. Those are the public figures. They are not a national rate, and they are not a formula.

Florida’s 2026 filed House Bill 471 tries to legislate the other unit. The Department of Education would have to write a statewide recruitment-and-retention plan that includes a weighted caseload-and-workload methodology with time for individualized intervention, diagnostics, consultation, IEP meetings, documentation, Medicaid billing, and response-to-intervention work. As of this draft, that is filed bill text with a July 1, 2026, effective date if enacted — not a regulation already in force, and not a published weight table. The interesting fact is the list of duties. The legislature, or at least the bill’s authors, has noticed that “students on the list” is not the job.

## Virtual coverage as a workload symptom

Michigan’s published shortage story is the same problem seen from the vacancy side. Bridge Michigan reported in February 2026 that Optimise, a statewide special-education talent task force, listed 253 school SLP openings. Eastpointe was serving 149 students, including 37 preschoolers, with four virtual SLPs and two on-site. Superintendent Christina Gibson told Bridge, “This is not an ideal situation,” and “I think the best speech services are delivered face-to-face.” The Michigan Department of Education said it does not know how many special-education posts are filled virtually. Tara Kilbride of Michigan State’s Education Policy Innovation Collaborative pointed to turnover, including mid-year turnover, as well as vacancies. Virtual contracts keep a service page green. They do not shrink evaluations, IEP calendars, or the remaining on-site SLP’s building-to-building load. Kilbride’s on-record caution was that the alternative to a virtual clinician can be no one, or a thinner in-person staff. That is a workload statement, not an endorsement of a delivery model.

Personnel shortage ranked first among the 13 challenges for the small telepractice-office subsample in ASHA’s table, and eighth overall. The survey does not say virtual work is easier. It says the people answering from telepractice offices still named paperwork first.

## What this is not

It is not a recommended caseload cap. ASHA has not handed districts a single legal number in the 2024 workforce PDF. WYPR’s “about 50” is a cited national average from a survey, not a standard. Florida’s weights do not exist as adopted state methodology in the filed bill. No one should invent a minutes-per-disability table and hang it on this article. Burnout figures from the same survey — 34 percent considering a setting change, 27 percent considering leaving the profession — describe consideration, not next year’s resignation wave.

## What SLPs should verify

Ask whether your district staffs to a headcount or to a written workload analysis that includes meetings and documentation. If a recruiter quotes “ASHA says 50,” ask which survey year and whether that figure is a mean caseload or a recommended maximum. If a state bill promises a weighted methodology, read whether it has been enacted. If virtual coverage is the plan for a vacancy, ask who owns the evaluations, the IEP writing, and the on-site consultation — those minutes still live on someone’s workload.

This article does not offer clinical advice, billing guarantees, or diagnosis guidance.
