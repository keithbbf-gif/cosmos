# BLS projected 15 percent SLP job growth for 2024–34. That is demand, not a funded FTE.

WASHINGTON — Sept. 14, 2026 — The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics’ 2024–34 employment projections put speech-language pathologists among the faster-growing health occupations. In the vintage BLS published in its 2026 Monthly Labor Review overview — and that this pack uses for the Occupational Outlook Handbook figures — the occupation had 187,400 jobs in 2024 and was projected to reach 215,500 in 2034. That is a 15 percent increase, or about 28,200 additional jobs. BLS also projected about 13,300 openings a year, on average, over the decade, many of them replacement openings from people who leave the occupation or the labor force.

The 2024 median annual wage in that same Handbook cycle was $95,410. Median is the middle of the distribution, not the school-district step you were offered in August.

BLS revises the Handbook. Readers should reload the OOH page for the table that is live the week they cite it. The 2024–34 occupational line is the one the Monthly Labor Review overview printed for SOC 29-1127, and it is the line this story is reporting.

## What the 15 percent is

It is a national occupational projection. BLS does not publish an interim year-by-year path between the base year and 2034. The 28,200 added jobs and the 13,300 annual openings are different ideas. Net growth is the change in employment. Openings include growth plus replacements. A profession can show strong annual openings while a particular district freezes a line.

The Review’s occupational table ranked speech-language pathologists 27th of 832 occupations on percent change in that vintage, at 15.0 percent, with employment moving from 187.4 thousand to 215.5 thousand.

## The industry number next to it

A separate industry projection in the same Review is easy to confuse with the occupational one. Offices of physical, occupational, and speech therapists and audiologists — NAICS 621340 — were projected to grow 23.3 percent from 2024 to 2034, from 493,100 jobs to 607,900, a gain of 114,800. That industry is a workplace, not the SLP occupation. It employs physical therapists, occupational therapists, assistants, audiologists, and speech-language pathologists, among others.

BLS tied that industry’s growth, in the Review’s narrative, to an aging population and to demand for therapy services, and it listed the industry among the 15 fastest-growing detailed industries. The same narrative said several healthcare occupations concentrated in those settings — including speech-language pathologists — were projected to grow more than three times as fast as the all-occupation average. The all-occupation employment change in that vintage was 3.1 percent.

Do not quote 23.3 percent as “SLP job growth.” Quote it as the industry. Quote 15 percent as the occupation.

BLS’s occupational narrative, in both the Review and the Handbook cycle this pack is using, ties demand to an aging population — more stroke and dementia-related communication needs — and to continued identification of speech and language disorders in children. Those are projected drivers. They are not a diagnosis of any student, and they are not a reason to treat a projections table as a prescription.

## What the projection does not say

It does not fund a position in Baltimore County, Eastpointe, or a Florida charter. It does not set a caseload. It does not mean a school board has money for the related-service line on an IEP. Demand in a projections table is an employer-survey and demographic model. A funded FTE is a budget vote.

It also does not say where the jobs will sit. The Handbook’s work-environment discussion in the current profile still lists educational services as a large employer of the occupation, alongside therapy offices, hospitals, and nursing and residential care. Shares move when BLS updates the page. They are not a promise that school hiring will absorb the 28,200.

Replacement openings cut both ways. ASHA’s 2024 Schools Survey found school SLPs ranking paperwork and caseload at the top of their workplace problems and a minority considering a setting change or an exit. Those are survey responses, not BLS openings. They are a reminder that “13,300 openings” can be people leaving as much as people arriving.

## What SLPs should verify

If you are writing a grant, a bargaining brief, or a graduate-program flyer, cite the vintage. Use 187,400, 215,500, 15 percent, 28,200, and about 13,300 openings for the 2024–34 occupational cycle this pack recorded, and name the Monthly Labor Review overview as the printed source for that table. Recheck the live OOH URL before you lock a number in a printed handout. If a superintendent says “BLS says we have to hire,” ask where the FTE is in the adopted budget.
