# A new AAC research agenda starts with society, not the device

UNIVERSITY PARK — May 26, 2025 — The largest problem in augmentative and alternative communication, Janice Light and colleagues argue, is not a missing app. It is the set of social arrangements that decide who gets a system, who gets a turn, and who is counted at all. Their open-access paper in *Augmentative and Alternative Communication* 41(3), pages 230–244, is a narrative synthesis of lived experience and selected research, not a trial and not a treatment manual.

The design matters as much as the claim. Light, Susan K. Fager, Jessica Gormley, Glenda Watson Hyatt, and Erik Jakobs did not randomize a sample or measure a single outcome. They wrote from stakeholder discussions at the Future of AAC Research Summit in Arlington, Virginia, in May 2024, then braided those accounts with published findings. Watson Hyatt is a researcher who uses AAC. The paper is funded in part by the Rehabilitation Engineering Research Center on AAC. It is an agenda-setting essay. Sample limits are the limits of that genre: no prevalence estimate of their own, no control group, no claim that every AAC user faces the same barriers.

## Six barriers, not one gadget problem

The authors start from an estimate they attribute to Beukelman and Light (2020): more than 97 million people worldwide cannot rely on speech alone to be heard. AAC can support education, work, health care, and community life, they write, but only if tools and opportunities actually reach people. Society and communication partners, they argue, hold most of that gate.

They use the Beukelman and Light (2020) barrier types — policy, practice, attitude, knowledge, and skill — and add technology. Policy examples include disability-rights statutes that protect people generally but rarely name AAC, and the United Nations Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities, ratified widely yet not directly enforceable through the U.N. system. Practice barriers are the everyday conventions that keep services from arriving. They cite Binger and colleagues (2021): in New Mexico, 78 percent of school-age children with highly unintelligible speech had not been seen by a professional with AAC expertise. They cite Lackey and colleagues (2024) on rural access in Canada, and a U.S. post hoc analysis (Pope et al., 2022) in which Black preschoolers were reported to receive less weekly AAC intervention than white peers. Those citations describe inequity in published data. They are not a national census.

Technology barriers cover both mainstream products that still require retrofits and AAC systems that impose heavy learning costs. Attitude barriers are ableism and what CommunicationFIRST has called speechism: the habit of treating speech as proof of intelligence. Knowledge and skill barriers sit with partners who dominate turns, miss attempts, or cannot set up a device. The authors note that most AAC intervention studies still target the person who uses AAC; comparatively few target partners, and they say they found none aimed at societal change.

## What they want studied next

The research list is the news for working SLPs who already know the clinic can only do so much. The authors want studies of how policies and practices change lives; methods that actually move systems; strategies that reduce service disparities; and system-wide data that identify who needs AAC, whether they get it, and what happens afterward. They also want better preservice and inservice training research, alternative service-delivery models, inclusive training that puts AAC users in the teaching role, and work on isolation, mentoring, and partner instruction at scale.

They are explicit about the data hole. Youth who need AAC are scattered across disability categories that governments already collect. Bob Williams, quoted from 2023 remarks, said there is no uniform count of who needs AAC, who has it, or who is segregated or restrained. Watson Hyatt’s 2024 point, as they relay it, is simpler: without statistics, programs are hard to justify. Until people who need AAC are counted, they do not “count.”

Training numbers they recycle from other work are equally blunt. An Assistive Technology Industry Association survey they cite found almost 75 percent of U.S. SLPs reporting they did not feel adequately prepared to provide AAC services. Tönsing and Dada (2016) reported that 92 percent of surveyed special-school teachers in South Africa wanted more AAC training. Those figures describe self-report and setting-specific samples. They do not license a staffing formula.

## What this paper is not

It is not a how-to for assessment, device selection, or partner coaching. It is not a finding that any named student will benefit from a named system. It does not rank vendors. It does not convert a summit conversation into a practice standard. A companion paper in the same issue, by Light, Holyfield, McNaughton, Nieder, and Preece, takes literacy as its own barrier problem; that is a different story.

Readers should treat the 97-million figure, the New Mexico 78 percent, and the ATIA preparedness percentage as cited research, not as this year’s local caseload. Attitudes research, the authors themselves note, often leans on self-report and can look more positive than daily behavior.

## What SLPs should verify

Read the paper at the DOI, not a social-media summary. If a district, hospital, or vendor cites it as proof that a product “dismantles barriers,” ask which barrier, in which population, with which data. Check whether your setting even counts students or patients who need AAC as a group. Check what your preservice or employer training actually covers. If you work across states, the compact and school credentials are separate clocks from this research agenda.
