# Baltimore County school SLPs told WYPR their caseloads are unsustainable

TOWSON, Md. — May 21, 2025 — Baltimore County Public Schools speech-language pathologists went to the school board in May 2025 with a workload complaint that WYPR put in the lead: the load, they said, was not manageable. The numbers the station reported did not come from a district dashboard. They came from a survey that a BCPS speech therapist, Gail Vanderbeek, led earlier that year with help from the county teachers’ union.

That survey, as WYPR described it, found that more than 62 percent of Baltimore County school speech-language pathologists had caseloads of 61 to 100 students. The same story cited the most recent American Speech-Language-Hearing Association report available to those speakers and put the national average caseload at about 50 students. Those figures belong to the WYPR account of that survey and that ASHA citation. They are not a new count by this desk, and they are not a census of every BCPS IEP minute.

## What the station said was on the table

WYPR’s Bri Hatch reported the board meeting on a Tuesday and a superintendent press conference the next day. Clinicians at the meeting, the story said, asked for caseload caps and more in-school planning time. The audience cheered those asks. The station framed the complaint against a nationwide shortage of school speech therapists, which, Hatch wrote, made recruiting and retaining those professionals more urgent. This desk is not reconstructing testimony the station did not attribute, and it is not inventing additional board-meeting lines.

What WYPR did attribute, in paraphrase, is the shape of the job as the clinicians described it. Seanne Herbick, then in her first year as the SLP at Cockeysville Middle School after 32 years in the profession, told the station the year’s load was the hardest of her career and that evenings and weekends were going to IEP meetings, lengthy assessment reports, daily notes for 20 to 30 students, and session planning on one hour of planning and documentation time a day. Vanderbeek, who splits time between Gunpowder and Victory Villa elementary schools, told the station she was stretched across buildings with a higher caseload and that students were missing legally mandated therapy and consultative services. Superintendent Myriam Rogers, at the Wednesday briefing, said she had not seen cases in which delayed speech-language services had disrupted students’ IEPs.

Those two descriptions sit next to each other in the same story. They are not the same claim. One is a clinician’s account of missed service. The other is the superintendent’s account of what she had seen in IEP compliance. WYPR published both. It did not adjudicate them.

## What Rogers said about money and meetings

Rogers told reporters the district had made no budget cuts to speech-language pathologists in the current fiscal year and had none planned for FY26. She said a task force formed earlier in 2025 was working on workload, that the district was on track for its hiring goals for the next school year despite a national shortage, and that BCPS already offered virtual speech therapy to cover in-person gaps — a practice the station said predated the COVID-19 pandemic.

She also said every county school speech therapist could schedule an individual meeting about the next year’s workload. As of that briefing, WYPR reported, about 40 of 200 SLPs district-wide had booked one.

Two hundred is the headcount Rogers used on that day. Forty is the number who had taken the meeting offer by then. Neither number is a caseload. The survey’s “more than 62 percent” band is the caseload claim, and it is still the union-aided survey as WYPR reported it, not a personnel-office extract.

## What the survey is, and what it is not

It is a snapshot Vanderbeek and the union collected “earlier this year” relative to May 2025. WYPR did not publish a response rate, a questionnaire, or a building-by-building table. A reader cannot tell from the story how many SLPs answered, whether 61–100 was the top of the instrument, or how many sat above 100.

The ASHA “about 50” figure is the national average caseload as the WYPR story cited it. ASHA’s own 2024 Schools Survey, a separate public report, treats caseload and workload as ranked workplace problems; it is not the source WYPR named for that average, and this story is not substituting one for the other.

Rogers’s no-cut pledge is a budget statement for two fiscal years. It is not a cap. It is not a promise that assignments released around the time of the story would shrink. Vanderbeek told WYPR the recently released assignments had not shown a decrease.

## What SLPs should verify

If you work in BCPS, or you are considering an offer there, ask for the current assignment letter and the caseload definition the district is using — students, schools, and whether consults count. Ask whether the FY26 budget line Rogers described still holds. Ask how many of the 200 SLP positions are filled, how many are virtual, and whether the workload-meeting offer is still open. If you are using the “more than 62 percent / 61–100” band in a grievance or a public comment, attribute it the way WYPR did: to that union-aided survey, not to ASHA and not to this desk.
