# Shortleaf pine country

The back of the postcard does not bother with a year. “Bradley Lumber Company of Arkansas, Warren, Arkansas. General Office and Plant located amidst own timber holdings. Sawmilling Arkansas Soft Pine, mild-textured Oak, and other native hardwoods. Producers and fabricators of hardwood flooring, furniture stock, Pine millwork and construction materials. Mixed Car Headquarters.” ARGenWeb keeps the card and the August 1944 *American Builder* page that sold pre-finished hardwood flooring from the same plant. The photograph is stacks, sheds, a pale industrial weather. The language is the county’s botany turned into a bill of lading. Soft pine and mild oak in one car. That is Bradley County if you let the woods speak before the companies do.

Shortleaf pine — *Pinus echinata*, one of the southern yellow pines — is the older name for the tree the catalogs later flattened into SYP. The USDA *Wood Handbook* treats the yellow-pine group as a family of density and resin, useful to engineers and invisible to a postcard. On the ground the distinction still matters. Shortleaf takes the warmer, drier slopes of the West Gulf Coastal Plain. Loblolly, where it comes in, wants a little more moisture. Neither tree is the oak in the creek bottom. Balogh’s encyclopedia entry divides Arkansas into four rooms: Delta hardwood; an Ozark mix of slower pine and hardwood; Ouachita pine on the slopes and hardwood in the valleys; and the rolling southern hills of yellow pine. Bradley County is the southern room with a hardwood hallway along the Saline and the Ouachita. The postcard’s mixed car is not a marketing whim. It is a map.

Climate is the quiet author. Pine in south Arkansas grows faster in sandy soils and long summers than it does in the Ouachitas or the Ozarks. Hardwood takes the swampy river bottoms and does not hurry. A mill man from the Lakes, used to fifty-year recoveries, could stand on a cutover in this county and, by the mid-1920s, see a new forest where his northern cousins still saw a scar. Balogh records that recognition as the beginning of sustained-yield talk in the south Arkansas holdings. Crossett would hire the Yale men. Warren’s own conversion from cut-out-and-get-out was slower and more local: W. R. Warner of Cloquet, Minnesota, sent in 1939 to shut Southern Lumber after a fire, talking the owners into second growth instead. Bradham tells that story as a near-death. The climate had already done the persuading. The trees came back because the weather is a growing season and the sand is a pine’s argument.

“Arkansas Soft Pine” on the postcard is a trade kindness. Shortleaf is a yellow pine. It is softer than some of its cousins and harder than the white pines the Lakes had already spent. The phrase let a buyer in Ohio hear “soft” and still receive a southern framing species. “Mild-textured Oak” is the same courtesy in hardwood: not the harsh ring of a northern red, but the bottomland oaks of a peninsula that floods and dries and floods. Gum and hickory rode along. Furniture stock, the card says, and flooring. A later Warren shop — Bradley Brand Furniture among the afterlives — still dates a hardwood practice from the mill’s first years and still knows those species without a catalog photograph. The postcard is the mill’s own admission that the county was never only a pine camp.

Before anyone printed “soft pine,” the trees were the thing you removed to grow corn. The 1826–1830 plats marked fields. Bradham copied the possessors. Hugh Bradley’s generation wanted sunlight, not board feet. The Civil War left the fields weedy and the uplands unfertilized. What grew in the neglect was the forest the 1880 railroad made valuable. Isolation had kept the shortleaf honest. A tree no one could ship is a tree no one has to invent a grade for. After 5 August 1880 the grades arrived, and with them the northern habit of talking about a stand as if it were a warehouse.

The small mills of the 1880s already knew the mix. A barn wants pine. A wagon bed wants oak. A local partnership did not need a slogan to cut both. What it lacked was a car. The mixed car is a railroad object. It is the reason the postcard can boast “Headquarters.” You load the pine and the oak together because the county grew them together and because a Midwestern yard might want a little of each. Pure pine trains are a Crossett kind of sentence. Warren’s sentence is mixed because the peninsula is mixed. Do not steal the Saline River sister story here. The Saline is geography: a hardwood wall on the east county line. It is not a monograph.

Summer is the other climate. Anyone who has walked a shortleaf stand in July knows the tick of cooling bark and the way the air holds dust. The encyclopedia will not give us a mill-floor thermometer. The Gene Davis comment on the Warren page — a later memory of a man named Moree wiping his brow and saying the obvious about the heat — is the kind of weather that gets into comments and not into tables. Treat it as memory. The heat is not in dispute. Kiln-dried pine in this county is a fight with August. Green oak in this county is a fight with the same month. The mixed car is a fight with both, plus a billing clerk.

By 1907 the three large mills were posting numbers that treated the woods as a daily crop. Southern, the Weyerhaeuser-Denkman plant, 100,000 board feet and 300 men. Bradley, Fullerton’s, 100,000 and 350, and the boast of a world’s large hardwood dealer — which is the oak talking, not the pine. Arkansas Lumber, Rittenhouse and Embree, 150,000 and 400. Add them and the peninsula is asked for 350,000 feet a day. A shortleaf county can give that number for a while. It cannot give it forever. Arkansas Lumber clear-cut 85,000 acres and closed in 1928. That is the pine belt’s old math. Southern’s 1939 fire and Warner’s second-growth argument is the new math. Bradley’s hardwood identity is the hallway that never emptied as completely as the hills.

The pink tomato, introduced by the 1920s, is the agricultural twin of that mixed woods. When cotton thinned as a small-farm living, the county grew a fruit that ships if you pick it just as the top turns pink. The legislature later called it a state fruit. The festival began in 1956, the same year Potlatch bought Southern. Dual identity is not a brochure line. It is what a sandy coastal-plain county does when it has both a long growing season and a forest: it sells a tree and a tomato from the same heat. The brick streets of about 1927 still carry painted tomatoes. The postcard still carries pine and oak. Both are true. Neither cancels the whistle.

Mill-town America prefers a single species because a single species makes a clean photograph: a pine wall, a burner, a sky of smoke. Bradley County’s photograph is messier. Shortleaf on the rise. Oak in the wet. A plant that advertised flooring and furniture stock beside construction pine. The 1944 magazine page is the hardwood afterlife already at work inside the mill, years before a later shop leased space on Pennington Road. Process orientation, Balogh’s phrase for the mid-century shift toward more products from the same tree, had a Warren edition that did not require a kraft mill. Flooring, millwork, mixed cars: value added because the woods were already various.

The Ozarks and the Ouachitas can keep their slower pine. This county’s boast, if it has one, is speed and mix. Speed is the climate. Mix is the rivers. Together they explain why northern money came and why it did not find a Crossett. A company town planted on a pine tract can pretend the bottoms do not exist. A courthouse town on a peninsula cannot. The Saline will send a flood into the hardwood. The Ouachita will make a reservoir of the southern tip at Felsenthal — 15,000 acres of green-tree water the state later admitted was never only a freight problem. Ducks use the same bottoms the mills used for gum. The postcard does not mention ducks.

What the *Wood Handbook* will tell you about shortleaf, if you let it, is dry technical mercy: specific gravity, shrinkage, the way the wood takes preservative, the nail that holds. What it will not tell you is the sound of a stand on a still afternoon, or the way a cutover in this sand looks like a failed farm for ten years and a young pine woods in twenty. Warner saw the second look in 1939. The Crossett foresters had been seeing it since the mid-1920s. Bradley County’s hills agreed with both of them and did not need a degree from Yale to put a tree back on a slope. The bottoms agreed more slowly. Hardwood is a longer sentence.

A person who drives out of Warren toward Hermitage or Banks now sees pine in rows that the 1907 men would have called a crop and the 1826 men would have called a problem. The rows are the afterlife of Warner and Potlatch and every small landowner who decided a forty was worth replanting. Between the rows, in the drains, the oak still darkens in summer. The mixed car is still the honest load. The postcard, undated, is still the honest caption. Shortleaf pine country is a name that includes its own exception. The exception is the water. The water is why the furniture stock existed. The furniture stock is why a later bench in this county can still be made without lying about where the tree grew.

Leave the still on the card. Not the mill photograph. The verso. Soft pine. Mild oak. Mixed car. A clerk’s handwriting in type. The woods were never a single story. The companies that lasted longest were the ones that admitted it.
