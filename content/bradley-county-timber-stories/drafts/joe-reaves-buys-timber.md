# Joe Reaves buys timber

Every ten days, or every two weeks, the 1934 Arkansas Supreme Court record says, Joe L. Reaves left Warren for a camp in Drew County. He was manager, or supervisor. In his absence Leo Jackson kept the little office that still clung to the old commissary building: a safe, an adding machine, chairs, a desk, time lists, logging statements, a telephone that rang the main office in Warren. About twenty employees and their families still paid rent on the houses. Two engines still went out each working day to cut and load logs for the mill at home. The commissary itself had been moved to Warren in 1932. The woods had not. Reaves’s job, in that snapshot, was to appear often enough that a camp in another county remained a Bradley Lumber fact. The buying had begun thirty years earlier. The visiting was how you kept what you had bought.

Bradham’s 1951 sentence is the hinge the pack was handed. Samuel H. Fullerton of St. Louis bought a small mill from W. H. Wheeler and then engaged Joe L. Reaves, Sr., a direct descendant of Captain Hugh Bradley, to buy timber, thus establishing Bradley Lumber Company of Arkansas. The county’s first industry and its first name were already kin. Four Reaves households were on the ground by 1835, in the same arrival list as the Marks families, the Beasleys, the Scobeys. A Joseph Reaves sat on the first grand jury in April 1841. M. L. Reaves is in Bradham’s later pioneer roll. The timber buyer of 1901 did not arrive from Chicago. He was the peninsula’s own memory hired by a St. Louis payroll.

Wheeler is the other local name in the sentence. The Warren encyclopedia calls him a local man who established the Bradley Lumber mill. Major John C. Bratton, Wheeler, John T. Ederington, and Benjamin Martin “later served as the basis for business.” Fullerton’s purchase does not erase that. It converts it. A small mill large enough to attract St. Louis is already past the 1880s invoice of Shirey & Butler. Wheeler had something to sell. Fullerton had something to buy. Reaves had the woods to walk. Between the three of them they made a company that wore the county’s name and a descendant’s kinship at the same time. The documents treat the handshake as ordinary. It was the moment the founder’s peninsula learned a new math.

What Reaves bought is the part the public pack does not have. The bibliography lists his timber-buying books as still thin. `[CITE NEEDED: Joe L. Reaves, Sr., timber-buying books; acreages, prices, and grantors in the first Fullerton years.]` We are not going to invent a page. We can say what the job was. Northern capital needed a man who knew which ridges held the shortleaf and which bottoms held the oak. The local man needed a payroll that was not a farm. Kinship became acreage. That is not a fall from grace. It is the southern pine belt’s conversion of a family who had cleared fields into a family who could assemble sections. Hugh Bradley had stopped near the Saline because the land would feed people. His descendant stopped on a ridge because the trees would feed a mill.

The 1948 Supreme Court case *Burbridge v. Bradley Lumber Co.* puts Reaves on the stand in another key. Beginning in 1924, he said, it had been the company’s custom to prepare a list of property on which taxes were to be paid and to deliver it to the collector in January or February, never later than February. The list came from a Land Record Book. Reaves did not check for old forfeitures. No copy of the list was kept. The collector was trusted to work up the receipts. The company’s first knowledge that taxes had not been properly paid came when the suit was filed. A timber buyer’s afterlife is a land book. The acres he assembled had to be named every winter or they ceased, in the state’s eyes, to be the company’s. The romance of walking the shortleaf ends in a list on a collector’s counter.

Drew County, in the 1934 case, is the woods beyond the home county. Bradley Camp: houses, light plant, water plant, hostlers for the engines overnight, an agent from Warren soliciting supply orders. Reaves visited. Jackson lived there. The mill at Warren ate logs that were not Bradley County logs. A descendant of the man the county is named for was, by then, extracting timber from the next county over because the home peninsula had already been asked for too many days of 100,000 feet. Southern and Arkansas Lumber were doing the same math on their own steel. Reaves’s particular is the kinship. The captain’s line had become a route into someone else’s forty.

Fullerton did not have to live in Warren to need Reaves. St. Louis can read a cruise report. It cannot smell which stand is worth the note. The sons, Robert W. and S. Baker, were sent to operate the plant. Operation is kilns and payrolls and, later, letters to the CIO. Buying is a different education. It is fences, widows, a courthouse clerk, a man who will not sell the oak along the creek. The published record does not give us Reaves’s voice in those negotiations, and this page will not write him any. What it can write is the structure. A St. Louis owner, two sons at the mill, a local descendant in the woods. Bradley Lumber Company of Arkansas is that triangle. Remove Reaves and the company is only another northern plant. Keep him and the company is also a county story.

The Reaves Family Cemetery west of Warren is a later map fact, a Find a Grave pin, not a proof of any one grave in this pack. Treat it as geography. Families that stay long enough to sell timber to a mill also stay long enough to bury their names in the same sand. The first grand jury’s Joseph Reaves and the mill’s Joe L. Reaves, Sr., are not, on the evidence here, forced into a single identity. They are forced into a single habit. The county keeps the spelling. The mill hired the habit.

Wheeler’s sale is the other silence. We do not have the deed in this pack. `[CITE NEEDED: date and terms of S. H. Fullerton’s purchase of the W. H. Wheeler mill.]` The encyclopedia and Bradham agree on the shape: local mill, St. Louis buyer, Reaves as the timber man. Rand Wood’s later-owner history dates the plant from 1901 and should be used as a later narrative, not as the original minute book. September 1901, in Nelson’s summary, is the incorporation. Heritage shops that say 1903 are dating an early mill hour, not Reaves’s first option. The buying would have begun when Fullerton decided the Wheeler plant was useless without a woods. That decision is the job offer.

A timber buyer in the cut-out years is a kind of historian. He has to know who entered the land, who mortgaged it, who died without a will, which forty was already promised to Arkansas Lumber or Southern. Three mills in one town meant three buyers in the same courthouse. Reaves’s advantage was not only blood. It was the probability that a Pennington Township grantor had heard the name before. Hugh Bradley’s descendant asking to buy the ridge is not the same visit as a Chicago agent asking. The price may have been the same. The porch conversation was not. We do not have the conversation. We have the later Land Record Book, which is the conversation’s residue.

By 1907 the mill Reaves fed was posting 100,000 board feet a day and 350 men, and the encyclopedia was willing to call Bradley one of the world’s large hardwood dealers. The hardwood is the bottoms. The pine is the hills. Reaves had to buy both if the postcard’s mixed car was going to be more than a wish. Isolation was over; the Iron Mountain would take what he assembled. His limit was not a raft. His limit was the other companies’ cruisers and the number of families still willing to sell the shade.

Mill-town America has this figure in every county that lasted. The local buyer. The northern note. The woods that do not care who signs. The particular here is the founder’s blood on the buyer’s name. Other towns hired a good cruiser. Warren hired a grandson of the act of 1840, or near enough for Bradham to say “direct descendant” without a chart. `[CITE NEEDED: the genealogical steps from Captain Hugh Bradley to Joe L. Reaves, Sr.]` Until the chart is printed, the 1951 sentence stands as the county’s own claim. The cousinage is not guessed here; the 1951 sentence stands until the chart is printed.

What remains is not a purchase book. What remains is a court reporter’s Reaves, visiting a camp, delivering a tax list, representing the company when a lien or a forfeiture made the woods into a lawsuit. The buyer became the keeper. The keeper became a witness. The witness is how we still have him. A later hardwood bench in this county does not owe Joe Reaves a plaque. It owes the honesty that the oak was assembled by a man whose people had been here before oak was a grade. Hugh Bradley came up the Red. Joe Reaves went out the logging road. Same family habit. Different century. The trees, for a while, moved because he said they should.

Leave him on the grade between Warren and the Drew County camp, not as a statue, as a schedule. Every ten days. Two weeks at the outside. The telephone already worked. The man still went. Buying timber, after the first years, is mostly showing up so the land does not forget who claims it. The captain showed up twice, Long Prairie and then the Saline. The descendant showed up for a payroll. Both kinds of showing up made Bradley County. Only one of them required a Land Record Book and a collector who might, or might not, work up the receipts.
