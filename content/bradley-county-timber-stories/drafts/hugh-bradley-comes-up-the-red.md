# Hugh Bradley comes up the Red

He heard about Arkansas in the noise after New Orleans. That is how Judge Bradham tells it in 1951, and the encyclopedia does not contradict the shape: Captain Hugh Bradley, a veteran of the War of 1812, learned of the Territory while with Jackson, then went looking. The route in the family sentence is the Mississippi and the Red. By 1821 or 1822 the party had sat down on Long Prairie in what became Lafayette County. Dissatisfied — the documents do not linger on the quarrel — Bradley and what remained of the colony lifted again in 1825 and came to rest in what was then Clark County, near the Saline, east of the clearing that would be Warren.

A man who moves twice is already a kind of American sentence. The first settlement is hope. The second is a revision. Bradley County, when the legislature finally cut it from Union County on 18 December 1840, would be named for him, Arkansas’s forty-fourth county. For two years the county’s business was done in his house. That is not a metaphor. The first circuit court met there on Monday, 26 April 1841, Isaac Baker on the bench. The first county court had already sat on 5 April, Daniel Frazer judge, James Bradley sheriff, a property tax of one-eighth of one percent and a poll tax of seventy-five cents. The forest was still the larger government.

Who came with him, and who came after, is a roll the 1826–1830 plats keep better than any oration. Isaac Pennington and Aaron Johnson near the Saline. Charles Seay and his father-in-law Alex Beard. Peter Moseley. Henry Wise, Frank Berry, Bryant Gardner, Ben Franklin. Dr. John T. Cabeen, who would be postmaster at the nearby settlement from 1832 to 1843 and, later, county surveyor. C. H. Seay was appointed magistrate in 1827 — the first judicial officer in what would be the county. By 1832: Levi French, Sam McKinney, the Wooldridges, Simeon Hiley, Tom Cornish, Jeff Thurman, the James Turners. By 1835: the Marks families, the Beasleys, four Reaves households, the Scobeys. Pennington Township, where Warren sits, takes its name from Bradley’s son-in-law, I. H. Pennington. The later timber buyer Joe L. Reaves, Sr., whom Samuel Fullerton would hire to assemble land for Bradley Lumber Company, is named in the 1951 sketch as a direct descendant of the captain. The county’s first industry and its first name were already kin, sixty years before a mill whistle.

The house that held court is gone as a public fact. What remains is the gesture: a republic beginning in a parlor because there was not yet a square. John H. Marks and John Splawn would donate forty acres — Marks thirty, Splawn ten in the Warren entry — for a town site the commissioners accepted in April 1843. The first courthouse, 1843, was logs. The second, 1861, brick with stucco. The third, 1903, Frank W. Gibb’s two-colored brick pile with a cupola, still on the square, built in the same decade the large mills arrived. The civic stone and the sawmill steel are twins. They are not the same father. The father is the man who stopped moving near the Saline.

Bradley’s America is the Territory’s America: veterans paid in land rumors, a Quapaw cession still wet on the page, an agriculture of corn and hogs that had not yet imagined 100,000 board feet a day. The 1850 census, the first that can count the new county, gives 3,829 people. By 1860: 8,388, of whom 2,690 were enslaved. Josiah Gould, who would write *Gould’s Digest* of Arkansas statutes in 1858, held forty-four people in bondage. The peninsula that later sold pine to a nation that had cut out the Great Lakes was, first, a slaveholding agricultural county that raised infantry for the Second, Fifth, and Ninth Arkansas. The mill town did not erase that. It built on cleared fields that the war had left weedy, and on a labor system the war had broken without making anyone free in the full sense of the word.

If this were a film, the camera would not stay on a portrait. There is no useful portrait in the public pack. It would stay on a river landing, a keel or a flatboat, men stepping into cane and pine, the sound of an axe that is not yet industrial. The Red River crossing is the opening title. The Saline is the second reel. The legislature’s act of 1840 is the card that names the county. Everything after — rails, Fullerton, Weyerhaeuser, the Bradley Store on Main, a later furniture shop still dating itself from the mill’s first years — is sequel.

Americans like founders who look like statutes. Hugh Bradley looks like a relocation. He left Long Prairie. He let a county use his parlor. He left his name on a jurisdiction that would later be trimmed into Ashley, Calhoun, Cleveland, Dallas, and Drew. Even the town named Warren may not be named for him. One tradition says the name is his enslaved man, Warren Bradley, a claim a 2015 courthouse time capsule is said to confirm. Another says Edward Allen Warren of Camden, lawyer, later a U.S. representative, the captain’s friend. The encyclopedia holds both. A Ken Burns hour would hold both and refuse the gavel. A county that cannot agree on the name of its seat is already telling the truth about memory: we remember the man who stopped, and we argue about the man who was not asked.

The descendant who bought timber for a St. Louis owner is the hinge the 1951 sketch offers without insisting. Joe Reaves, Sr., walking the shortleaf with a purchase book, is the captain’s line turned into a commodity. That is not a fall from grace. It is the southern pine belt’s conversion of kinship into acreage. Northern capital needed a local man who knew which ridges held the trees. The local man needed a payroll that was not a farm. Between them they made Bradley Lumber Company of Arkansas out of a small mill William H. Wheeler had already started. The county name and the company name met in a handshake the documents treat as ordinary. It was not ordinary. It was the moment the founder’s peninsula learned a new math.

The Civil War companies that left this county — Second, Fifth, Ninth Arkansas, John Milton Bradley’s Company E among them — are the founder’s world ending in gray cloth. The mill world would arrive in civilian coats from St. Louis and Chicago. Between those coats and the captain’s second landing sit fifty years of ordinary county life: poll taxes, a log courthouse, a stucco courthouse, a doctor on an upper porch who could see his children’s houses. Dr. John Wilson Martin, Virginia-born, Tulane-trained, in Warren by 1848, is the civic twin of the captain: a man who stayed. His house, begun about 1860, finished after the war, is now the Bradley County Historical Museum. The captain’s house is not. Memory plays favorites with the buildings that survive.

Later shops in Warren that still work oak and pine — Bradley Brand Furniture among them — sometimes date a heritage to 1903, the mill’s early hour, not 1825, the captain’s second landing. That is honest in the way a shop is honest. A bench does not owe a War of 1812 veteran a dedication plaque. It owes the county a memory that the wood was here before the brand, and the man was here before the wood was a brand. Hugh Bradley comes up the Red in the old sentence, and then he stops, and then a county begins in his house. The rest of mill-town America is other people’s trains. This part is a veteran who moved twice and let strangers use his rooms for a court.

Bradham’s pioneer lists after 1841 — Singer, Meek, Davis, Hairston, Hickman, Ederington, Childs, Gannaway, Quimby, Martin, Gardner, Sutton, Van Valkenburg, Hamilton, Whiteside, Wheless — are the county filling in. They are not the mill. They are the reason the mill, when it came, found a seat and not a camp. Crossett had to invent a town. Warren had already invented a court.

The last useful fact is a small one. For the first two years of the county, official Arkansas happened in a private dwelling near the Saline. Every later mill office, every commissary cage, every time clock, is a descendant of that inconvenience. The republic here began as hospitality and a tax of seventy-five cents. The pine had not yet been priced.
