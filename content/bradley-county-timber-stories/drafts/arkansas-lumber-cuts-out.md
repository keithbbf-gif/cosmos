# Arkansas Lumber cuts out

In 1928 the largest of Warren’s three mills went quiet. Arkansas Lumber Company, Rittenhouse and Embree of Chicago, Clifford J. Mansfield of St. Louis on the ground, had posted 150,000 board feet a day and 400 men in the 1907 counts — half again Southern’s cut, more hands than Bradley’s. Bradham’s 1951 sketch gives the epitaph in a clause: it clear-cut its lands, 85,000 acres, and went out of business. Silva repeats the figure and the year. An *American Lumberman* photographer’s 1906 captions, later given to the Bradley County Chamber and then to the University of Arkansas, say 83,000. An encyclopedia photograph caption says 70,000. The museum cards on Ash Street say about 85,000, most of it going afterward to the Murphy-McKinney interests of El Dorado, oil-boom money, bought for a song. The song is the American lumber method with a name: cut out and get out.

The method is Balogh’s and the timber-industry entry’s plain speech. A company buys virgin stands, builds a mill large enough to eat them, lays rail to the trees, cuts until the parcels are stumps, and either moves the mill or dies in place. Small operators did it on forty acres. Arkansas Lumber did it on a county. The distinguishing marks, the encyclopedia says, are denuded land and the relocation — or the extinction — of production. For a town whose livelihood is the production, extinction is a kind of weather. Warren had three mills. In 1928 it had two. The west side of town learned what a peninsula looks like when the largest whistle stops.

Moses F. Rittenhouse and John W. Embree — Jesse R. Embree had been bought out of the Chicago parent in 1895 — incorporated Arkansas Lumber in March 1901. They hired Mansfield to establish the Warren plant. Charles Arthur Derby ran the mill with him; K. G. Morley sold the lumber. The 1906 *American Lumberman* pictures, taken when the plant was young, are the visual record the county still uses: office on Sturgis Street with a boardwalk toward the houses; company store, the Arkansas Trading Company, at Sturgis and Martin, bolts of cloth on one side, shoes on the other, Lee Harley a manager; clubhouse for single men, white cloths, napkins around cups, lace at the windows; dry kilns with their own boilers; a powerhouse and a log pond, an elevated tram, a tank. The powerhouse, the museum cards say, gave Warren its first electricity before the First World War. Cut-out-and-get-out still lights a town while it lasts.

Julius H. Adams, chief timber buyer, stands in the photographs in virgin pine that a handwritten date on one card will later call eighty years gone. Horses. Oxen in the western Bradley camps. A steam pump and a portable tank, because a logging camp moved and sank a new well each time. Shay engines on the Warren & Ouachita Valley, jointly owned with Southern Lumber, W. S. Hobbs general manager, Warren to Banks and the Rock Island, passenger service that will run until 1948, years after this company is dead. Engine Five, line shaft to the wheels, powerful and slow, woods only. Engine Eleven for the haul to the mill. In 1907 an excursion group rides to Banks and on toward Crossett; Derby and Zuloo Doudna Derby, their boy Lawrence with a stick, Mansfield at the right. The railroad outlives the mill that helped pay for it. Rails are harder to cut out.

The plant stood where the fairgrounds and West Side School would later stand. From the company water tank a camera looking east caught storage sheds, the store, and, in the distance, the courthouse steeple and the steeple of First Methodist South, then where the Coker Hotel would be. That is the geography that matters. Arkansas Lumber was not a camp in an unnamed township. It was a factory whose stack could be told from the cupola. Mansfield, the cards say, gave most of the money for the Warren city hall — the 1931 Art Deco municipal building on Myrtle, H. Ray Burks, fire pole and all — and left money that went into a new First Presbyterian Church. The table in his office photograph survived at Derby Insurance, a grandson’s firm. Cut-out-and-get-out, in Warren, still endowed a fire station. The contradiction is the town.

C. J. Mansfield and C. A. Derby stand in front of a workforce picture that the caption limits, carefully, to plant employees. The woods crews are another photograph, oxen, another race and another payroll the camera is less eager to name. `[CITE NEEDED: Arkansas Lumber woods-labor force, Black and white, camp names and years.]` The company houses fill the Sturgis views. The clubhouse still stood when the cards were written. The office did not; Ava Hairston and Joyce Lee’s houses took the site. A mill that lasts a little over twenty-five years can leave a street plan and erase a letterhead.

Southern Lumber Company v. Arkansas Lumber Company, 176 Ark. 906 (1928), is the other document of the last year. The two neighbors sued over pine on surveyed forties, Halfway Creek, color-coded maps, a timber deed from D. V. Stanley in 1905, tax payments, board feet measured into a chancellor’s finding. D. A. Bradham of Warren appears for a side. The year the company closes is the year the state’s high court is still parsing which tree belonged to which Chicago or Rock Island or Weyerhaeuser interest. Cut-out is not only a woods method. It is a lawsuit about the last trees.

Why 1928, exactly, the published county sketches do not linger to say. The virgin account was exhausted. That is the method’s only clock. Crossett, in the same belt and the same decade, was already hearing from Yale foresters that the woods could be a crop. E. W. “Cap” Gates would hire W. K. Williams. Second growth was visible on lands cut at the turn of the century, Balogh writes, because southern yellow pine in sandy south Arkansas does not take the fifty years of a northern stand. Arkansas Lumber did not stay to farm it. Murphy-McKinney bought the dirt. Southern, eleven years later, would almost take the same train and be talked off it by a man from Cloquet. Bradley would still be sawing hardwood when a later war wanted floors. The Chicago mill is the one that kept the old religion to the end.

Mill-town America is full of 1928. The South’s first great pine harvest is over in county after county; the paper mills and the pulp sticks have not yet fully arrived to reemploy the cutover. International Paper’s kraft mill at Camden opens that same year — a different product, a different town, a different idea of what a tree is for. Warren’s idea, for Arkansas Lumber, remained a board. When the boards were finished, the idea was finished. Four hundred men is a town inside a town. The encyclopedia’s 1907 employment figures, stacked, are a thousand and fifty on three payrolls. Losing four hundred is not a recession. It is an amputation. The two banks will hold in the next decade partly because the other two mills do not copy the amputation.

A later furniture shop in Warren that works the same species Arkansas Lumber once floated in a pond is not that company’s child. Bradley Brand Furniture dates a hardwood afterlife from the mill that stayed. Arkansas Lumber’s afterlife is a school ground, a fairground, a church gift, a city hall, a clubhouse on Sturgis, and a caption that still cannot decide whether the acreage was seventy, eighty-three, or eighty-five thousand. The acreage is gone as timber. It remains as an argument about how a peninsula should be used. Cut out and get out used it as a mine. Warner, later, would use the second story of the same mine as a farm. The rivers did not notice which theory won. They took the silt either way.

If you want a still, use the log pond on West Central, near where later churches would stand, the tram above the water, the tank to the right, the powerhouse to the left. Evening. The pond holds the last inventory. In the morning the inventory is a schedule. In 1928 the schedule ends. The electricity that was a byproduct becomes someone else’s job. The Trading Company’s cloth stays on the bolt until it doesn’t. The Shays find other woods or the torch. The courthouse steeple, which the storage-shed photograph caught in the same frame as the company store, keeps the hour for a town that is now two mills and a memory of a third. The memory is not romantic. It is 85,000 acres of stumps and a method with a name that does not bother to apologize.

Get out is the part the boosters skip. The men got out, or they walked across town and tried to get on at Bradley or Southern, or they left the county. The land got out of the lumber column and into an oil man’s speculation. The letterhead got out of the city directory. What did not get out is the method’s reputation. Every later speech about permanence — Bradham’s aura when Potlatch buys the survivors, the tomato festival’s claim on the square, a shop dating itself from 1903 — is a speech against 1928. Arkansas Lumber is the ghost those speeches require. Without a mill that left, the mills that stayed cannot be praised for staying. The ghost is a powerhouse, a pond, a Shay, and a year.
