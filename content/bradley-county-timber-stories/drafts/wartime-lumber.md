# Wartime lumber

Fifty-nine names. The encyclopedia’s county entry gives the number without a roster: fifty-nine Bradley County citizens lost their lives in the Second World War. The First World War had taken men and, the Warren entry says, put women on the mill floors under a dress rule — dress to work, slacks to saw, dress home. The Second takes fifty-nine out of the county entirely. The mills stay. The advertisement stays. In August 1944, *American Builder* carries a page for pre-finished hardwood flooring from Bradley Lumber Company of Arkansas, Warren. The war is not over. The page does not mention the fifty-nine. It mentions a floor.

A later-owner company history, Rand Wood Products, writing from the far side of Potlatch and two more letterheads, will say that by 1941 the mill had 1,100 employees, a flooring plant, a hickory division, furniture kits, construction materials, a reputation in softwood as well as the hardwood the 1907 counts had emphasized. Treat the eleven hundred as what it is: a later-owner figure, not a 1941 payroll card anyone has shown this series. The 1907 figure for Bradley was 350 men and 100,000 board feet, world’s large hardwood dealer in the encyclopedia’s phrase. An increase to something like a thousand, if the later page is even roughly right, is the difference between a big mill and a town that is a mill. Purkins, in 1949, will say the Bradley stack had been the source of power to supply labor for more than a thousand workmen. He is counting after a tornado, not after a war. The two thousands — Rand’s 1941, Purkins’s 1949 — rhyme without proving each other.

What the war proves is demand. Lumber in the thirties had been the brace that kept two banks open. Lumber in the forties is barracks, crates, ships’ interiors, floors for houses that will be built when the men the fifty-nine did not include come home. Bradley’s postcard language, undated on the ARGenWeb backs, is already a war vocabulary without a war: Arkansas Soft Pine, mild-textured oak and other native hardwoods, producers and fabricators of hardwood flooring, furniture stock, pine millwork, construction materials, mixed-car headquarters. The 1944 *American Builder* page is that vocabulary with a date. Pre-finished hardwood. A factory coat on a strip so a builder at a distance does not have to sand a town he will never see.

E. L. Bruce Co. v. Bradley Lumber Co. of Arkansas, a later equity fight over finishing compounds and licenses, will put other *American Builder* pages into a court record — October 1947, December 1947, and an *American Lumberman* of 12 October 1946 — and will remember a president of Bradley traveling to Wilmington in the 1940s to talk to du Pont about wax. The 1944 ad is earlier and simpler. It is a mill in a war year selling a finished floor to a trade that is already imagining the peace. Straight-Line oak, in a 1942 *American Builder* page, had already been pitched at defense and private housing, remodeling, oak trim, a distributor within reach of a telephone. “Bradley Brand” in that copy is a flooring line, not a later furniture shop. The words will outlive the war and change jobs.

Southern Lumber, rebuilt after the 1939 fire, modernized, kept alive by Warner’s second-growth argument, is the pine counterpart. The encyclopedia does not give Southern a 1944 advertisement. It gives the county fifty-nine dead and, in the same stretch of entry, two tornadoes. The war and the weather share a paragraph because a small county’s history is a short page. Fifty-nine is a precise number and still not a list in that entry. `[CITE NEEDED: the fifty-nine names as the county recorded them — courthouse tablet, newspaper honor roll, or adjutant’s list.]` A series that will not invent tornado victims beyond the paper’s named record will not invent a war roster either. The number stands. The names, until the tablet is copied, are a silence the mills cannot fill.

Hurley Implements built a building on Myrtle in 1944 — Haley, Claycomb, Roper & Anderson in Silva’s parenthesis — while the YMCA of 1920 burned the same year. War years are construction and ash in the same downtown. Ederington’s put on a stucco Art Deco face in the early-to-mid-1940s. Blankinship Motor Company had already opened its H. Ray Burks building at Cypress and Myrtle in September 1940. The municipal building, 1931, still housed firemen upstairs. The town that is sending fifty-nine away is remodeling as if the street will be there when the fifty-nine are not. It will be. The stack will not, not entirely, not after January 1949.

The Warren & Ouachita Valley still carries passengers to Banks through these years; the passenger end will not come until 1948, when Rock Island takes the line. Mixed cars — the postcard boast — are a war tool as much as a lumber tool. Hermitage, Banks, Johnsville, Ingalls: the county’s other names send men and, if they have a siding, sticks. Pink tomatoes keep shipping from the 1920s fields because a soldier’s mess is a market too. The dual identity is already in the soil before anyone paints a fruit on the 1927 brick. Timber and tomato, both in uniform.

Black Warren’s war is not a separate advertisement. Catfish Row’s 1931 brick — lodge, theater, bottling, grocery — is still a street. The Pastime balcony is still a balcony. Ms. O. Z. Jackson’s 1941 protest is the last dated public fact this series has for that door before the war swallows the decade. Draft boards do not publish a Jim Crow table in the encyclopedia. The fifty-nine include whom they include. `[CITE NEEDED: racial count, if any, on the county’s WWII dead.]` Dignity is leaving the blank.

The Bradley Store at 215 South Main, scrip and weekly settle, is still the company’s idea of a town in these years. Edwin Ives will be superintendent by 1949, when Purkins finds a rumpled steel I-beam in his front yard at 610 South Main and another H-beam over R. W. Fullerton’s roof into the back lot. Fullerton is the Cornell son, still in the picture a generation after S. H. Fullerton of St. Louis bought Wheeler’s mill and hired Joe L. Reaves, Sr., to buy timber. The war does not replace the founding family. It multiplies the product line. Flooring, furniture stock, hickory, pine millwork. A later shop in Warren will date hardwood furniture from 1903 as heritage. The 1944 page is the wartime version of the same claim: this county’s trees leave as a finished interior, not only as a rough board.

Mill-town America in the Second World War is a poster of women in bandanas and a caption about production. Warren’s published caption is thinner and, in one respect, older. The women-on-the-floor story the encyclopedia prints is a 1917–18 story. The 1941–45 story it prints is a death count. The mills’ own caption is a flooring ad. Between the count and the ad sits the ordinary fact that a county of 18,097 (1940) can lose fifty-nine and still have a shift. The shift is pine and oak. The oak is bottoms. The pine is the second-growth hills Warner refused to abandon. War is a market that does not care which growth you call it, if the board is straight.

Crossett, in 1940, has a paper-mill strike the literature remembers. Warren’s mill floors, in the published pack, have no union narrative this series can cite. `[CITE NEEDED: union or strike record, Bradley or Southern, 1940–45.]` The absence is not proof of peace. It is proof of what was saved and what was not. What was saved: an *American Builder* page, a later-owner headcount, a county death total, a 1944 fire at the Y, a 1944 implement house, the Fullerton name still on a house that will take a beam in 1949. What was not saved: the fifty-nine as persons in the encyclopedia’s paragraph.

If you want a still, use the magazine page. August 1944. A floor that is already finished in Warren so a builder in another state can lay it and walk away. Somewhere in the same month a telegram arrives in a Bradley County house. The page and the telegram are the same war. One is a commodity. One is a name. The mills will still be running when the war ends, which is why the 1949 tornado can be described as destroying a mill and displacing a thousand employees. You cannot displace a thousand from a plant that closed in 1944. Wartime lumber is the reason the stack is there to fall. It is also the reason a county can count fifty-nine and still have, on a Monday in January, a whistle to miss.

The peace will want houses. The houses will want yellow pine and oak strip. Potlatch will want the companies that know how to make both. The fifty-nine will want, if the living are honest, a list. Until the list is copied from stone or newsprint, the number is the honest object. Fifty-nine. A flooring ad. A later claim of eleven hundred. Three facts, three kinds of paper, none of them a conversation the record entitles anyone to invent.
