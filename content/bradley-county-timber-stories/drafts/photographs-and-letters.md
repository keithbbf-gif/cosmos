# Photographs and letters

A postcard of a mill pond is a kind of letter. It is addressed to a person who has not seen the plant, and it says, in a white caption and a crowded back, what the sender wants believed. Bradley Lumber Company of Arkansas, Warren: general office and plant amidst own timber, soft pine and mild-textured oak, flooring, furniture stock, mixed-car headquarters. Another card, from Gannaway Drug, shows the mill as a skyline a customer might buy with a soda. Southern Lumber’s cards, on the same ARGenWeb pages, do the same work for another letterhead. The photograph is a still. The back is a sales sentence. Together they are how a mill town first mailed itself to the rest of the country. Not a diary. A face for the yard.

Sanborn maps are letters to an insurance man. 1907, 1912, 1920, 1931, 1938: pink and yellow and the little marks that mean brick, frame, ironclad, a skating rink that will become a YMCA, a commissary that will become a park that will become a post office, a half block labeled “Negro Settlement,” warehouses north of the square, the Bradley Store’s footprint in 1920, Vine Street becoming Main. Silva reads them the way a person reads a family Bible — not for style, for who lived in the room. The 1907 sheet already shows a town that has outgrown Cabeens. The 1938 sheet is the last Sanborn hour. After that the town has to remember without the mapmaker’s colors. A mill town that keeps its Sanborns knows more about its risk than about its conversations. Fire was the imagined reader.

Dr. Junius Newport Bragg wrote actual letters. Confederate surgeon, 1861–65, later gathered by Helen Bragg Gaughan and printed in 1960 as *Letters of a Confederate Surgeon*. The encyclopedia cites them for a simpler civic fact: troops confiscated food in Warren; citizens went hungry. Reconstruction, the Warren entry says, hurt more than Marks’ Mills, Mount Elba, Longview. Martial law. One store in May 1865 for the Federal garrison. Night riders. Powell Clayton’s militia from Conway. The Klan popular in the way a terror is popular. Bragg’s pages are not mill pages. They are the town’s first surviving inward mail, written before a board foot was a local religion. Any honest telling of how a mill town remembers has to admit that the oldest letters are about empty larders, not about kilns. The mill remembered forward with postcards. The war remembered backward with hunger.

The *Eagle Democrat* is the weekly letter the county wrote to itself. 10 January 1949: “A Tragic Record,” the clock at Wayne’s Confectionery stopped at 5:45, Mrs. Stewart, the stack, the thousand workmen, 120 houses destroyed, 72 major, 150 minor. 13 January: thousands viewing the storm area, loot of the wind returned, I-beams in yards, a Sabbath ominously warm. 1975: millions in damage, the same path, seven names, Chief Dunaway at Dave’s café at 4:30 a.m. 15 January 1975, in another column, Maylon Rice on Banks turning seventy. 1932: clubwomen’s “Know Your County” papers on Jersey, Blue Springs, the rest. Place-name notes, Bob Newton wondering about Goat Neck and Pig Ankle, a later explanation that South Myrtle got the leftover barbecue — goat neck — because those houses did not usually work at the mills. The *Eagle* is not literature. It is the habit. A mill town remembers by putting the week on a page and letting the courthouse square sell the ads.

Jann Woodard, who wrote the encyclopedia’s Warren entry and gathered tornado pages for ARGenWeb, is a later kind of letter writer: the compiler. Billy R. Sharp typed Bradham. Angela Emery typed community histories. Rachel Silva walked the square in 2012 with Brenda Ezell, Jenelle and Johnny Lipton, Hilda Thorton, John Frazer, and turned a Saturday into a script the Historic Preservation Program still hosts. Gatewood’s 1976 bicentennial book, the 1936/1940 centennial magazine, the National Register nominations: these are the county remembering in bound form. None of them is a payroll. All of them are how a place without a university press still makes an archive.

Photographs of the 1949 damage — ARGenWeb is grateful for shared snapshots and postcards of the wreckage — are the other still. A mill town that has been hit twice learns to keep disaster pictures in a drawer and to bring them out in January and in late March. Saline River News, 2024, remembering 1949 at seventy-five and 1975 at forty-nine, is the latest version of that drawer. Later memory must be checked against the 1949 paper. The check is itself a form of respect. A photograph without a date is a rumor. A photograph with the *Eagle*’s house count is evidence.

How a mill town remembers is not how a capital remembers. There is no official historian on salary. There is a Women’s Club that moved a museum from the clerk’s office to the Martin House in 1987. There is a tomato room of menus and shirts. There is a depot kept below the grade. There is a courthouse time capsule opened in 2015 and asked to settle a naming argument it can only join. Warren Bradley, enslaved; Edward Allen Warren of Camden: the encyclopedia holds both. Memory here is an argument stored in a box, a porch, a brick street, a marquee, and a newspaper morgue.

What gets photographed is not what got punched. Postcards show plants, not filers. Sanborns show footprints, not scrip. The *Eagle* shows disasters and festivals more reliably than a Wednesday in 1924. Bragg shows hunger. The Pastime shows a façade; the balcony remains an architectural sentence. Catfish Row shows one brick remnant and a set of map labels. The Beavers brothers and John Turner enter the encyclopedia and must be held with dignity; they do not enter the Gannaway postcard. A mill town’s published memory is skewed toward owners, storms, and booster weeks. The correction is not to invent the missing album. It is to say, in the time-clock essay and in this one, that the album is missing.

Letters that would have filled it are ordinary and gone. A sawyer to a cousin in Mississippi. A woman in 1918 about the slacks. A Black grocer on Elm about a license. Joe Reaves’s timber-buying books, named in the bibliography as absent. Fullerton family papers beyond Cornell social notes, absent. We have the shape of the correspondence — mill towns ran on mail as they ran on rails — and we have almost none of the sentences. Bragg is the exception because a daughter-in-law made a book. The *Eagle* is the exception because a paper is a business. Postcards are the exception because a drugstore sold them. Memory follows what was copied.

Mill-town America has a familiar documentary grammar: sepia, a fiddle, a letter read aloud, a pan across a group photograph whose names are in the caption. Warren can furnish the grammar’s objects. It cannot furnish, in the surviving county record, the read-aloud letter from the yard. An honest telling would read Bragg on food, then cut to a Sanborn, then to the 1949 *Eagle*, then to a postcard back, then to Silva on a brick street, and would admit the cut where the sawyer’s letter should be. The admission is the scholarship. Fabricated proof would be a scripted voice in a boarding house. Do not write that voice.

The dual identity shows in the archive as two piles. Tomato: festival cookbooks, 1956–2006, Pryor Center oral history, Act 255, painted brick, a luncheon menu. Pine: Balogh, Curry’s 1952 thesis, Smith’s *Sawmill* for the larger last-virgin-forest story, Bradham, company cards, Potlatch 10-Ks, a 2008 business-page paragraph. The piles share the *Eagle* and the encyclopedia. They do not share a feeling. The fruit photographs in color. The mill photographs in smoke. A person who only sees the color has not read the letters.

What remains when the photographs fade is the habit of pointing. That is where the depot was rebuilt after the fire. That is Wayne’s, where the clock stopped. That is the cardiac wing. That is Crowtown before it was Ingalls. That is Parnell’s steps. Pointing is an oral Sanborn. Silva’s tour is pointing raised to a profession. Frazer pointing, having been ten in 1949 and mayor in 1975, is pointing as civic office. The county remembers by walking and naming. The page you are reading is a later walk.

A later hardwood shop in mill space will have its own photographs someday, and they will be tempted toward a catalog. Do not take them. Bradley Brand Furniture’s 1903 date is allowed as heritage, not as a picture of a sofa. The mill’s own postcard already showed furniture stock as a pile. That pile is the ancestor of every later picture. Keep the ancestor. Leave the catalog.

The still might be a kitchen table: a Sanborn copy, an *Eagle* clipped in 1949, a mill postcard, Bragg’s 1960 binding, and a blank space the size of a time-clock card. How a mill town remembers is that table. How it forgets is the blank. Both are the history. Hold the blank long enough that a reader wants a name. Do not supply one.
