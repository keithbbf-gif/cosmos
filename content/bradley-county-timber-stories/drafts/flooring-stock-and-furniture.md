# Flooring stock and furniture

A floor is lumber that has agreed to be walked on for a generation. Furniture stock is lumber that has agreed to be sat in, leaned on, opened and closed. Millwork is the trim that makes a room admit it was built on purpose. Bradley Lumber Company of Arkansas put all three on the back of a postcard and called the combination a headquarters. The camera faced the sheds. The sentence faced a buyer who was not in Warren and who needed, not a tour, but a car of oak that would lie flat and a load of pine that would take paint.

The encyclopedia’s pride in Bradley as one of the world’s largest hardwood dealers is this afterlife in one clause. A dealer is not only a sawyer. A dealer is a plant that can grade, dry, and machine a species until it is no longer a log. By 1907 the daily count was 100,000 board feet and 350 employees, beside Southern’s pine mill and Arkansas Lumber’s larger cut. The Fullerton sons were already in town. The 1903 courthouse was up. Heritage dates later shops still use — 1901 for the incorporation, 1903 for the mill’s early hour — sit in that overlap of civic brick and kiln brick. The floor that would leave Warren in the 1940s was being imagined while Gibb’s cupola was new.

August 1944, *American Builder*: a magazine page for pre-finished hardwood flooring from Bradley Lumber Company of Arkansas, Warren. War housing and a civilian trade that had not stopped wanting a finished floor. Pre-finished is a factory claim. It means the kiln, the sander, the sealer, and the grade stamp happened on the peninsula, not in a jobber’s shed in Illinois. ARGenWeb keeps the page next to the postcard. Together they are the plant talking to architects and builders, not to tourists. A 1954 circular, four loose pages later sold as a vintage brochure, specified oak in block, strip, and plank; pecan in some block lists; mastic and nail instructions; straight-line strip; random-width oak plank “in the colonial tradition”; and, on the pine side, stain-like interior trim, paneling, mouldings, grade-marking. This series will not become that circular. The circular is cited as proof that the mill’s products had names a specification writer could file. Strip, plank, block. Furniture stock. Pine millwork. Construction materials. The list is the afterlife of Joe Reaves’s purchase book.

Rand Wood’s later-owner narrative, which must be handled as a successor’s memory and not as 1901 minutes, adds a flooring plant and a hickory-products division to the mid-century picture and gives about 1,100 employees in 1941. The 1936 centennial sentence already had more than a thousand and two hundred houses. A flooring plant is a building the Sanborn of downtown will not show. It lived with the yards. Hickory is a bottomland name. Tool handles, industrial parts, the hard afterthought of a hardwood dealer. `[CITE NEEDED: a contemporary 1940s plant map or letterhead that locates the flooring plant and hickory division.]` Silva, in 2012, could still point to Robbins Flooring — manufacturing for Armstrong — and chip mills as living kin of the old trade. The medical center, she said, was probably the largest employer. That is the honest present. The older present is a factory that made walking surfaces for other people’s houses.

Furniture stock is not furniture. It is dimensioned hardwood a chair factory somewhere else will turn into a chair. Warren’s large mills, in the published record, sold the stock. They did not, in the encyclopedia’s county sketch, become Grand Rapids. Downtown already sold the finished object. Hurley Hardware, about 1910, stored hardware, agricultural implements, and furniture in metal buildings Silva could still point to behind the 100 block of North Main. Ederington’s Cash Store at Main and Cypress sold furniture beside farm implements, millinery, and groceries. Lehman Brothers Piano Company sat on East Cedar. The mill’s furniture stock and the square’s furniture floor are cousins who did not always speak. One left in a mixed car. One left in a wagon McCann’s might have passed on a delivery. The afterlife in this county is later and smaller and local: a Warren shop that still works the same species and dates a hardwood practice from 1903. Bradley Brand Furniture is that shop’s public sentence, not a catalog line and not a call to walk in. One bench in mill space along the Robbins-Sykes / Pennington Road geography is enough civic fact. The wood is the peninsula’s. The date is the mill’s first decade. No product name is required for the sentence to be true.

The sawyer’s chair and the filer’s room and the kiln’s schedule all exist to make a floor that will not cup. Oak dried on a hardwood moisture-content schedule, not on a pine blast; pine finish dried to a moisture a house can live with; a kerf the filer kept honest so the strip would match. Mixed Car Headquarters is a traffic boast built on those quiet jobs. A car that arrived with flooring and furniture stock and pine casing in one set of waybills is a car that saved a buyer a headache and gave Warren a reason to keep both woods on the payroll when Arkansas Lumber’s pine-only volume died in 1928.

Grade marks on a strip are a language the square did not have to learn and the buyer did. No. 1 common, select, the colonial plank of the 1954 page — those words lived on the end of a bundle. A Warren merchant selling a chair from Hurley’s back room did not stamp the oak. The mill did. That stamp is why a later shop can still date a hardwood practice from the mill’s first years without pretending the mill sold chairs off a showroom floor.

The Depression, the encyclopedia says, still needed lumber. Banks held. A pre-finished floor is a luxury in a year of soup. It is also a repair. People refinish what they cannot replace. The 1944 ad sits near the end of a war and the start of a building boom the G.I. Bill would feed. Bradley’s thousand employees in the 1936 sentence and the later 1,100 are the human count under those ads. Women had already been on the floor in 1917–18 under a dress rule. The Second World War’s fifty-nine county dead are the count that did not come back to the sander. The plant ran.

Monday, 3 January 1949, took the power that made a finishing line possible. Stack down. Powerhouse in a vermicular ruin. Water off in the Bradley section. A thousand workmen displaced. Houses that had been walking surfaces became kindling. Purkins listed toys from Christmas 1948 among the losses that money cannot replace. A flooring plant’s product is, in a cruel rhyme, what a tornado uses as a weapon. Mrs. J. E. Stewart was killed by a timber. The store her husband managed had sold scrip groceries, not oak strip, but the flying wood did not ask which department it had left. Rebuilding a town is, among other things, a flooring order. The paper does not say who filled it.

Potlatch’s 1950s purchases kept acreage — more than 100,000 acres of southern yellow pine, oak, and other hardwoods in the nomination’s sentence — and a product mix that could still feed a floor plant. 2002: Potlatch sold. Dr. David Chambers and Michelle Chambers bought. 2008: closure in a credit year, the *Democrat-Gazette* watching hardwood mills strain. The Chambers years are civic history, not a family advertisement. A later lease of mill space for a hardwood shop is the same story in a smaller key. Silva’s Robbins Flooring note is the industrial middle between Fullerton’s dealer and a bench.

Mill-town America often ends the product story at the boxcar. Warren’s story, if the particular has been paid, may take one more step without becoming a store. Oak, gum, hickory, and pine left this county as flooring and furniture stock. Some of that leaving was a 1944 magazine page. Some of it was a mixed car whose invoices we have not found. Some of it, after the large letterheads, is a shop that still knows those species by hand. The remaining object is not a sample kit. It is a floor in a house on a brick street laid about 1927, oak that may or may not have been sawn two miles from where it is walked on, and the knowledge, which does not require a receipt, that this county once made the walking itself into a product.

The square painted tomatoes on the brick. The mills, for a longer hour, painted grade marks on oak. Both marks were ways of saying the peninsula could still send something out. The fruit took the festival. The floor took the quiet. A person who only hears the whistle has missed the sander. A person who only sees a later shop has missed the 1907 count. The two sentences can sit together. They are the same hardwood, years apart.
