# Hardwood and pine

The back of the postcard is the truer letterhead. ARGenWeb still keeps the verso, date unknown, under a photograph of mill and factories: “Bradley Lumber Company of Arkansas, Warren, Arkansas. General Office and Plant located amidst own timber holdings. Sawmilling Arkansas Soft Pine, mild-textured Oak, and other native hardwoods. Producers and fabricators of hardwood flooring, furniture stock, Pine millwork and construction materials. Mixed Car Headquarters.” The capital letters are the argument. Not pine or oak. Both. Not a single commodity on a single track. A mixed car: several species, several products, one freight bill, a buyer in some other state who wanted a floor and a casing and a load of framing from a county that could cut them in the same week.

The 1880s small mills — Shirey & Butler, Crandall & Leavitt, Edmondson, Glasgow & Temple, Lanark, Whittington, Parker & Kinard, Lowry & Taylor, J. E. Walker — cut whatever stood nearest the skid. They did not need a verso. Thomas A. Carpenter’s 1891 plant was the first large mill in Warren. W. H. Wheeler started the mill Fullerton bought. The mixed identity is a large-mill fact. A small mill sells a local order. A large mill sells a reputation to a buyer who will never see the Saline.

The encyclopedia’s county entry makes the same argument in a different register. Bradley Lumber, founded by Samuel H. Fullerton, “became one of the world’s largest dealers in hardwood lumber.” By 1907 it also produced 100,000 board feet a day and employed 350. The *also* is the hinge. A world’s large hardwood dealer that is also a pine mill of Southern’s size is not a specialty shop. It is a peninsula translated into a catalog. Southern, 100,000 and 300 men, was the Weyerhaeuser-Denkman pine plant grown from Lindsay & Ainsworth 1882. Arkansas Lumber, 150,000 and 400, was Chicago capital on the largest daily count. Bradley sat between them with a reputation that looked north toward furniture cities and a production number that looked like yellow pine. The postcard is how the company said it when the camera was on the sheds.

Jay Gould’s Iron Mountain had already written Arkansas into a larger ledger. Bradley County’s service line, the Little Rock, Mississippi River and Texas, made Warren a western terminus on 5 August 1880 and linked the seat toward Dermott. Without that iron, a mixed car is a wagon fantasy. With it, oak from the bottoms and shortleaf from the hills can share a waybill. The Warren & Ouachita Valley after 1899 added Banks and the Rock Island. Passenger service to 1948 meant the same rails that hauled the mix also hauled the men who graded it. A depot with white and colored waiting rooms, Silva notes of the later MoPac pair, is the human version of a mixed shipment the company did not print: two woods, two doors.

Balogh’s map of Arkansas timber is four rooms. Delta hardwood in the east. Ozark mix in the north. Ouachita pine-and-hardwood to the west. Rolling southern hills of yellow pine. Bradley County is the southern room with a hardwood hallway along the Saline and the Ouachita. Shortleaf — southern yellow pine in the later language — on the warmer sandy slopes, faster than the mountain pines. Oak, gum, hickory in the wet. The 1826–1830 plats called some of those bottoms fields. The mills called them holdings. Joe L. Reaves, Sr., hired by Fullerton to buy timber, walked both. A purchase book that only understood pine would have left money in the swamps. A dealer who only understood oak would have left the hills to Weyerhaeuser. The mixed car begins in that walk.

“Arkansas Soft Pine” on the card is a trade name for a feeling, not a separate species. The feeling is mild texture, paint that takes, interior trim that does not fight the brush. A 1954 architectural circular from the same company, later sold as a vintage brochure, still talked about soft-pine stain-like interior trim, paneling, finish, grades and grade-marking, mouldings — beside oak block, strip, and plank flooring. The brochure is not a shop list for these essays. It is evidence that the mixed identity lasted into the decade Potlatch arrived. The August 1944 *American Builder* page, also on the ARGenWeb plant page, advertised pre-finished hardwood flooring from Warren while the plant still claimed the pine. War demand did not force a choice. It used both.

A mixed car is a railroad idea. The Ouachita Division reached Warren on 5 August 1880. The Warren & Ouachita Valley, 1899, gave Southern and Arkansas Lumber a short line to the Rock Island at Banks. Iron Mountain, later Missouri Pacific, took the older route. A buyer in St. Louis or Chicago who wanted a car of nothing but No. 1 common oak could have it. A buyer who wanted oak flooring, a little gum, and a stack of pine finish in one car saved a switch. Headquarters, on the card, is a boast that the plant could make up that car without begging a neighbor. Whether the boast was always true is a sales question the invoices would answer. `[CITE NEEDED: a surviving Bradley mixed-car invoice or traffic book.]` The postcard is not an invoice. It is the face the company wanted on a drugstore rack. Gannaway Drug, Silva notes, sold a mill postcard of its own. The square sold the image of the yards. The yards sold the square’s growth: 954 people in 1900, 2,057 in 1910.

Hardwood’s afterlife is furniture and floors. Pine’s afterlife is houses and boxes and the everyday. The county’s dual identity later added a fruit. Pink tomatoes from the 1920s, a festival in 1956, a state emblem in 1987. Timber and tomato are not a contradiction. They are what a peninsula does when it has sand, water, and a rail. The tomato did not replace the mixed car. It shared the banks that did not fail in the 1930s. Depression demand for lumber, the encyclopedia says, held. Neither Warren bank failed. A hardwood dealer with a pine mill is a better Depression animal than a single-species camp. Someone, somewhere, still needed a floor. Someone still needed a 2×4. The car could carry either.

Race and species are not analogies. They are facts that shared a town. The Sanborn’s “Negro Settlement” at Walnut and Ash, Catfish Row on Elm, the Pastime balcony, the Beavers lynchings — those are the human mixed car the company did not advertise. Black labor cut and stacked both woods. White letterheads claimed both woods. The postcard’s courtesy is a photograph of sheds. The sheds sat in a Jim Crow county. A hardwood-and-pine essay that only praises versatility has told the salesman’s truth and called it history. The history is that versatility was built on a segregated payroll the directories have not yet given us by job and race. `[CITE NEEDED: 1900–1940 occupational tables for Warren by race.]`

Arkansas Lumber’s 1928 exit — 85,000 acres gone — subtracted the largest daily pine number. Southern’s 1939 fire and Warner’s second-growth argument kept a pine future. Bradley’s hardwood reputation was the name that could have left with the virgin oak and did not, because the bottoms were not empty and because flooring and furniture stock are what you do when the first cut is no longer a bonanza. Process orientation, Balogh’s mid-century theme, is the mixed car grown up: less waste, more products, smaller trees. A flakeboard plant and a kraft mill happened in other Arkansas towns. Warren’s published products stayed closer to the card: boards, flooring, millwork, furniture parts. Potlatch, buying both remaining plants and more than 100,000 acres of pine, oak, and other hardwoods, inherited the mix as acreage.

The 1949 tornado did not choose a species. It took the powerhouse, the stack, houses, and Mrs. J. E. Stewart on South Main. Steel from the mill yards landed in superintendent and president yards. A mixed plant makes mixed debris. Oak timbers and pine boards in the same wind. The *Eagle Democrat* counted houses, not board feet. One thousand workmen displaced is a species-blind number. Rebuilding, when it came, used whatever the surviving sheds could dry. The festival and the tomato market, already moving in the 1940s and 1950s, gave the county a second crop to stand on while the mills learned second growth.

After Potlatch, after the 2002 sale, after Dr. David Chambers and Michelle Chambers and the 2008 closure, the mixed car is a postcard in a genealogical attic. What remains in the county is the woods’ own mix and a later shop that still names oak and hickory without a catalog photograph. Bradley Brand Furniture, dating a hardwood practice from 1903, is one afterlife of the dealer half of the old sentence. The pine half went to other letterheads, other planers. The peninsula did not choose. It grew both. The company, for a long American hour, was honest enough to print both on the back of a card and to call the combination a headquarters.

The remaining object is the verso. Not the smokestacks in the photograph, which a storm could take. The sentence that refused a single wood. Mild-textured oak. Arkansas soft pine. Other native hardwoods. A reader who only knows mill-town America from a pine-camp monograph will look for a single species and a single fence. Warren’s fence was leaky and its woods were mixed. The card knew. The rivers had known first.
