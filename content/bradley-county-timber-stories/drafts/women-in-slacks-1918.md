# Women in slacks, 1918

The dress rule, as the encyclopedia still writes it, was simple. Wear a dress to work. Change into slacks. Wear a dress home. Jann Woodard’s Warren entry and the 2016 commercial-district nomination use the same three clauses. When registration for the First World War began, the mills experienced a shortage of manpower and began hiring women. That is the whole published mechanism. No wage. No named woman on a time card. No letter home about the change of clothes. `[CITE NEEDED: a 1917–1919 payroll, photograph caption, or Eagle Democrat item that names a woman in a Warren mill job.]` The rule will have to carry the essay. The town will have to carry the rule.

A dress on the street and slacks on the floor is not a fashion note. It is a boundary the company and the town wrote on a woman’s body. Main Street, 1918, still had the older merchants — Ederington, Hurley, Gannaway, McCann — and not yet the 1920 Bradley Store at 215. Church Street already had its name from the Methodist house west of Main, the Baptist house farther south, the Presbyterian house that would stand on the Citgo corner until at least 1938. A woman in a dress could walk those blocks as a churchgoer, a clerk, a mother. A woman in slacks, on those blocks, would have been a different sentence. The mill gate was the place the sentence was allowed to change. The gate, then, was a dressing room the paper does not describe. Dignity is not an invented scene of laughter in a locker aisle. Dignity is leaving the aisle empty because the aisle was not filed.

Warren in that year was a town that had already doubled. 954 people in 1900, 2,057 in 1910, 2,145 in 1920 — the war decade is a plateau after a leap, not another leap. The three mills of 1907 had posted 1,050 jobs on the daily counts. Some of those jobs went into uniform. Fifty-nine county dead would be the Second World War’s number; the First’s county list is not in the brief. `[CITE NEEDED: Bradley County’s World War I death roll, comparable to the encyclopedia’s WWII figure of 59.]` Registration is the encyclopedia’s verb. Registration is a draft apparatus, a board, a name. The mills’ reply was to hire the names that were not taken. Women had been in Warren’s wage world before 1917 — Josephine Horne’s academy in 1851, clerks, cooks, the four women who conceived Trinity Baptist in 1868 and were joined by three husbands and a member from Ebenezer to make the required eight. The mill floor was a different room. Sawdust, belt, carriage, the smell of the kiln. Slacks are what you wear when a skirt is a hazard.

The Council of 22 formed in 1918. Citizens and businessmen, the nomination says, saw a need for youth and recreational programs with an emphasis on faith. Two years later the YMCA took a charter and a building on the old skating-rink corner: pool, baths, reading room, gymnasium, stage. The Council and the dress rule are the same year’s answers to a town that had become larger than its older rooms. One answer is a Christian recreation for young men. The other is a female labor the mill needed and the street was not to see. The 1920 YMCA would burn in 1944. The dress rule, as far as the published pack knows, did not need a fire. It needed the Armistice and the return of men. Whether every woman left the floor in 1919 is a question the directories have not answered. `[CITE NEEDED: how long women remained in mill jobs after 1918, and in which departments.]`

Black women are in the county. They are not in the dress-rule sentence. The Sanborn’s “Negro Settlement” at Walnut and Ash, twelve frames by 1912, was already on the map when the war began. Catfish Row on Elm had restaurants and a boarding house by 1907. The Pastime’s balcony was still seven years away. A labor shortage that hired “women” in a Jim Crow mill town hired along the same lines the housing already used. The paper’s silence is not proof of absence. It is proof of who got a clause. The Beavers brothers, 1890 and 1892, are the older violence. Crane Green, John Turner, 1903. A war-year hiring story that pretends the floor was only white has borrowed the encyclopedia’s brevity and made it a lie. The brevity should stay marked as brevity.

What jobs did the slacks do? The pack will not say. A sawyer’s chair is a skilled station the essay on that chair refused to populate with a fiction. A filing room is quiet and unnamed. Kilns, yards, sorting chains, the planer, the shipping shed — any of these can take a body that is not a story. Off-bearing is a job a photograph from other southern mills sometimes gives to women in the war years. Warren’s photograph, if it exists, has not been placed in the bibliography. `[CITE NEEDED: any wartime image of the Bradley, Southern, or Arkansas Lumber floor that includes women.]` The 1930s group portrait of Bradley employees on ARGenWeb is later and mixed only in the way a crowd is mixed: hats, coats, a camera too far away for a slack to read.

Scrip and housing framed the new hire as they framed the old. If she lived in a company residence at half rent, the 1936 sentence’s world in an earlier key, the whistle owned her morning. If she lived on a private street, she still heard the blast. The commissary on Main at Church was still standing in 1918; the 1920 store at 215 was about to replace a part of that system with a brick transom. Weekly settle does not care about the sex of the ticket. It cares about the week. A woman paid in scrip was a woman whose Saturday was the company’s. The independent grocers on the square — McCann’s, delivering — were a walk away. The walk, in a dress, was the walk home.

The churches had already used women’s organizing. Trinity Baptist, 1868, four devout women, first building on the old West Brothers site after three years in the courthouse, first organ played by Miss Mollie Wheeles, the congregation renamed First Baptist in 1930. First Presbyterian, organized 1 August 1858, bricks interrupted by the Civil War, hosted by the Methodists until an 1880 building at the northeast corner of Main and Church. The Methodist house that gave Church Street part of its name. Pentecostal work in D. H. Durham’s home in 1918, Fannie Corbett preaching, Flora Cargile assisting — the same war year as the dress rule, a different floor, sawdust of a tabernacle to come in 1919. Sister Cargile and a Model A are in the museum’s caption. The mill’s women did not get a caption. The contrast is not a moral. It is a filing difference. A preacher can be remembered as a preacher. A mill hand in slacks is remembered as a rule.

After the war the town’s heyday, the nomination says, was the 1920s and 1930s. Brick streets about 1927. Pastime circa 1925. Bradley Store 1920. YMCA 1920. The dress rule belongs to the hinge between explosive growth and that heyday. It is easy for a later century to make the hinge a triumph. The paper does not. It says shortage, hiring, slacks. Shortage is the employer’s word. Hiring is a door that can close. Slacks are a garment that had to come off before the sidewalk. The three clauses are a control as much as an opening. A series that will not invent a heroine will not invent a liberation either.

The Second World War would take fifty-nine. The mills would run again with whatever labor the draft left. The encyclopedia does not repeat the dress rule for 1941–45. Rand Wood’s later 1,100 in 1941 is a headcount, not a sex ratio. The 1949 tornado killed without asking who had worn slacks thirty years before. Mrs. J. E. Stewart died on the way to pick up a husband at a store. Other women died in houses Purkins named. Choirs of women, almost certainly, took turns in the aisles. That is a different slack, a different year, the same town.

Mill-town America has a wartime photograph it likes: women at machines, a flag, a caption about the home front. Warren’s published caption is thinner and, in its thinness, more exact. Dress. Slacks. Dress. The remaining object is the rule itself, still sitting in an encyclopedia entry as if it were simple. It was simple the way a whistle is simple. Everyone heard it. Not everyone was meant to be seen obeying it. The women of 1918 date from a shortage. The mills date from 1901. Both dates are true. Only one of them required a change of clothes at the gate.
