# Before the rail, the small mills

The county paper, when it bothered to list them, sounded like a dry-goods invoice. Shirey & Butler. Crandall & Leavitt. Edmondson. Glasgow & Temple. Lanark. Whittington. Parker & Kinard. Lowry & Taylor. J. E. Walker’s mill. The *Encyclopedia of Arkansas* still recites that roll under Warren, the way a clerk recites who is owed. No daily capacities. No head-counts. No surviving letterheads in the public pack. Only the fact that, long before three northern companies taught the town to speak in hundred-thousand-foot days, Bradley County already knew how to turn a tree into a board for a neighbor.

Isolation was the tax on every one of those names. Until a railroad made Warren a terminus, a log that could not reach a raft or a wagon road stayed a tree. The Saline would take what the Saline could float. The Ouachita, farther off, would take what a landing could hold. Everything else — the shortleaf on the sandy rises, the oak too far from a wet bank — was local lumber or it was scenery. George Balogh’s timber-industry entry puts the problem without romance. After the Civil War, powered machinery let a man set up a small sawmill. Those mills served only the communities that could walk or wagon to them. The means to reach the Northeast and the Midwest did not exist until railroad builders decided Arkansas was a ledger they wanted.

Warren in 1880 counted 301 people. That is not a market. That is a courthouse, a few stores, and a ring of farms that needed framing, flooring, and fence. The small mills of the 1880s were the county answering itself. A church needed sills. A cotton shed needed boards. A new house on the square needed something better than logs. The circular saw, by then ordinary in the South, could be run by a boiler a local partnership could afford. The partnership’s name went on the cut. The cut went as far as a mule could pull it.

Lanark is the name on the list that is also a place. A mill that becomes a community is the small-mill story in miniature: enough families to need a designation, not enough capital to need a New York office. Hermitage, settled before 1850, sat at the county’s center and remained a farm town with timber on its edges. Johnsville was a crossroads famous, the county entry says, because so many of its men were named John. Banks and Ingalls came later, Ingalls first Crowtown, then a Kansas senator’s name at a railroad’s insistence. Parnell Springs, northwest of Warren, sold mineral water and cabins into the early twentieth century and failed by the 1930s. None of these were company towns. They were the older county the small mills served, a peninsula of farms that happened to be standing in virgin pine.

What a small mill was, mechanically, is not mysterious. Balogh describes the hour. Contractors or company men — in the small case, the partners themselves — selected a tree, trimmed it, cut it, rough-sawed it into log sections, and skidded it with mules. At the mill the logs were sorted, sawn, and, if anyone had the patience, stacked to dry. There was no logging railroad into a 40,000-acre block. There was a road, a team, and a pond if the site was lucky. The chief difference between a large operation and a small one, Balogh says, was parcel size and the plant left behind. The small mill bought a little timber, cut it, and either moved the rig or sold the leftover. Cut-out-and-get-out, practiced in miniature, does not make the papers. It makes a clearing and a debt.

Shirey & Butler, Crandall & Leavitt, Glasgow & Temple, Parker & Kinard, Lowry & Taylor: the paired names are the period’s credit instrument. Two families, or a family and a machinist, split the boiler and the risk. Edmondson, Whittington, J. E. Walker: single names, which may mean a stronger owner or only a shorter line in the later list. `[CITE NEEDED: locations, capacities, and years of operation for the 1880s Warren-area mills named in the encyclopedia.]` The thinness is the point. These firms did not endow a YMCA. They did not lay brick streets. They did not hire a St. Louis timber buyer. They cut enough lumber to keep a county of 6,285 people (1880) in boards while the Great Lakes cut, which had been the nation’s lumber, began to fail the westward towns.

Jay Gould’s Iron Mountain was already writing Arkansas into a larger book. Bradley County’s own service line, when it finally arrived, ran toward Dermott. The encyclopedia’s sentence after that arrival is the one the small mills could not write: the stage was set for the exploitation of the shortleaf yellow pine lands. Exploitation is the accurate verb. The small mills had been using the woods. They had not been emptying them for Chicago. A local saw is a neighbor. A mill that posts 100,000 board feet a day is a climate.

Thomas A. Carpenter established the first large lumber mill in Warren in 1891. The encyclopedia gives him that sentence and almost nothing else. Eleven years after the railroad, someone finally built to the scale the rail could feed. Carpenter is the hinge between the invoice of partnerships and the letterheads that followed. He is not, in the published pack, a biography. `[CITE NEEDED: Carpenter mill location, daily cut, and disposition of the plant after 1891.]` What the date does is end an era without a funeral. The small names did not all vanish on a Monday. They were out-capitaled. A large mill buys stumpage in sections. A small mill buys a forty and hopes the boiler lasts.

William H. Wheeler is the next local name the papers keep. The Warren entry calls him a local man who established the Bradley Lumber mill. Bradham’s 1951 sketch says Samuel H. Fullerton of St. Louis bought a small mill from W. H. Wheeler and then hired Joe L. Reaves, Sr., to buy timber. Both sentences can sit on the same plank. Wheeler started something the town could still call local. Fullerton bought it and made it a St. Louis company with a county’s name. The small-mill generation ends, in that telling, not with a shutdown notice but with a sale. A man who has built a mill large enough to attract Fullerton has already left the 1880s invoice behind. He has become the last local owner the later story will bother to spell.

The 1880s were not a prelude that knew it was a prelude. Reconstruction had left the fields weedy. Cotton was still the language of credit. The pink tomato was not yet a festival. Major John C. Bratton, Wheeler, John T. Ederington, and Benjamin Martin, the Warren entry says, later served as the basis for business. Merchants and Planters Bank would not arrive until the 1890s. Warren Bank not until 1901. The small mills ran in a town that still looked like a farm seat: a stucco courthouse from 1861, a clerk’s office not yet built (1890), Gibb’s cupola not yet imagined (1903). Silva’s Sanborn walk through the later brick is a different century. The 1880s mill sat nearer the trees than the square, or it sat on a creek that could turn a wheel, and its smoke was a weather a farmer understood.

Isolation had a sound. No crossing bell. No logging engine in Drew County. A wagon on a sand road in a wet month, the bed sucking at the wheels, a load of green oak that would not pay the haul if the next town already had a mill. The peninsula between the rivers is generous in timber and stingy in exits. That is why the small mills existed. That is why they could not become the industry. An industry needs a door. August 1880 opened one. Carpenter walked through it in 1891. The northern companies walked through it after 1900 and did not bother to learn the 1880s names.

Mill-town America likes to begin with the large stack. The type is familiar: a camp, a commissary, a whistle, a census that doubles. Warren’s type is slower. The county had been sawing for neighbors while it was still a cotton county that happened to be standing in shortleaf. Balogh’s distinction among Arkansas towns is useful here and should not be stolen by Crossett. Camden and Pine Bluff were river towns before they were timber towns. Fordyce and Warren owed their existence to farms and then to rails. Crossett was invented out of virgin pine. The small mills are the proof that Warren was the farm-and-rail kind. You cannot list nine local firms from the 1880s in a town that was only a camp.

The woods themselves were mixed, which is why a small mill could live. Shortleaf pine on the warmer slopes, the southern yellow pine of the later catalogs. Bottomland hardwood along the water: oak, gum, hickory. A partnership without a planing mill or a dry kiln still had customers. A farmer wanted pine for a barn and oak for a wagon bed. The later Bradley Lumber postcard would call that mix a virtue and sell it as “Mixed Car Headquarters.” In the 1880s the mix was only the county. Nobody needed a slogan to ship two species to the next settlement.

Women are missing from the mill list. So are Black owners. The published roll is a white partnership roll, which is not the same as a complete labor roll. The men who fired the boilers and stacked the lumber in that decade are not named in the encyclopedia’s sentence. `[CITE NEEDED: labor, race, and wage evidence for Bradley County’s 1880s mills.]` When the large mills later hired women in the First World War — dress to work, slacks on the floor, dress home — they were inventing a wartime rule, not a first female presence in a saw shed. The small-mill decade is a silence that later payrolls do not repair.

Carpenter’s 1891 mill is the last still in this hour. A large plant in a town of a few hundred, the Iron Mountain already willing to take a car, the shortleaf still mostly uncut. Ten years later Fullerton, Rittenhouse, Embree, Weyerhaeuser, and Denkman would treat that uncut as a holding. The 1880s names would become a paragraph. They deserve more than a paragraph and less than a myth. They were firms. They cut wood. They kept a county in boards while the county was still a county and not yet a mill town.

What remains of them is the list. No stack on the skyline. No National Register depot. No commissary at 215 South Main. A later shop in Warren that works oak and pine is the afterlife of the large-mill hardwood hour, not of Shirey & Butler. The small mills’ afterlife is quieter. It is every outbuilding in the county whose sills were sawn before the northern letterheads arrived, and every place-name — Lanark especially — that still means a mill if you know how to hear it. Before the rail, that was enough. After the rail, it was a sentence the big companies inherited and did not cite.
