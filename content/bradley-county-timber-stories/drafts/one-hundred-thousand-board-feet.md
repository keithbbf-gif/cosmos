# One hundred thousand board feet

A town that can say one hundred thousand board feet before supper has decided what it is. 1907. Southern Lumber Company: 100,000 a day, 300 men. Bradley Lumber Company: 100,000 a day, 350 men. The *Encyclopedia of Arkansas* prints the figures in the county entry without a drumroll, which is the correct music. Arkansas Lumber, the same year, posted 150,000 and 400 men, and that larger number is a sibling fact, not this page’s title. The title is the number two mills shared, the number a farm seat learned to treat as civic weather, the number that meant a peninsula could be asked, every working day, for a small forest.

Board foot is a clerk’s unit: a foot long, a foot wide, an inch thick, or the cubic equivalent. One hundred thousand of them is a pile a child cannot imagine and a mill superintendent can. In 1900, Balogh says, the method was still straightforward. Extend the logging railroad. Select, trim, cut, rough-saw, skid with mules, load the company cars. At the mill, sort, saw, plane, dry, season, ship. By 1907 the straightforward method, multiplied by three plants, was a town’s identity. Warren did not need a slogan. It had a daily cut.

The number is not a mood and must not be rounded into one. It is attached to a year and to named companies. Southern, the Weyerhaeuser-Denkman plant grown from Lindsay and Ainsworth’s 1882 operation, 100,000 and 300. Bradley, Fullerton’s, Wheeler’s old mill underneath, 100,000 and 350, and the encyclopedia’s additional claim: one of the world’s large hardwood dealers. The second claim is why Bradley’s 100,000 is not a copy of Southern’s. Southern’s hundred thousand is the shortleaf hill. Bradley’s is the hill plus the hallway — oak, gum, hickory — counted in the same unit because a clerk has only one unit. A hardwood dealer’s day and a pine mill’s day can look like the same digit and not be the same woods.

How long a day was, the published pack does not say. `[CITE NEEDED: shift length and actual operating days per year at the three Warren mills, 1907.]` Whistle time is not invented here. We can say that a daily capacity is an advertisement as well as a measurement. Mills posted what they could do when the logs came and the belts held. A wet month, a broken engine, a camp fever, and the civic number became a hope. The encyclopedia still treats 1907 as a year the hope and the fact were close enough to print. That is the civic use. The town said the number the way another town said the height of a courthouse.

Population is the other 1907 number, arriving a little late. 954 in 1900. 2,057 in 1910. Doubled, the county entry says, as a result of the lumber business. One hundred thousand board feet, twice, plus Arkansas Lumber’s one hundred fifty, is the cause the census is answering. A thousand men on three payrolls, plus the camps, the short lines, the boarding houses, the women not counted in the 1907 head-counts, will double a town of a thousand. The number of feet and the number of people are the same event in two units.

What did a day look like on the square? Silva’s 1907 Sanborn is the closest still. Warehouses north of the courthouse. A skating rink. Frame shops on Elm. The Iron Mountain’s combination depot near Chestnut and Elm. Gibb’s cupola, four years old, looking down on a labor force the law had not yet given a union hall. The boards themselves did not stay on the square. They went into cars. Mixed cars, if you believe the later Bradley postcard — pine and mild oak together, furniture stock and construction stock, a headquarters for a load that admitted the county’s botany. One hundred thousand feet of that mix is a different civic boast than one hundred thousand feet of pure pine. Warren’s boast, at Bradley’s gate, was the mix. Southern’s was the Weyerhaeuser name. Arkansas Lumber’s was the extra fifty thousand.

A board foot is also a death of a tree, and the civic use of the number has always preferred not to say so. Cut-out-and-get-out, Balogh’s phrase, is the method that makes a daily 100,000 possible for a decade and impossible for a century. Arkansas Lumber would prove the impossibility on 85,000 acres by 1928. Southern would burn in 1939 and be talked into the other method. Bradley would ride hardwood and second thoughts into the Potlatch years. 1907 does not know those endings. 1907 knows a good day. The civic fact is the good day’s publication, not the ending.

Mill-town America posted numbers like these from Bogalusa to Crossett. The particular here is that a courthouse town used them as if they were its own. Warren did not become Southern City or Fullerton. It remained Warren, two names, and said 100,000 the way it said the date of the Pink Tomato Festival later. Dual identity begins in the unit. The town could be a seat of justice and a daily cut at the same time. Gibb’s brick and the stacks went up in the same generation. Citizens posed on the steps of both. The number is the pose in digits.

Women enter the later mill under a wartime dress rule. They are not in the 1907 head-count. Black workers are in the town and not in the company sentence. The 1912 Sanborn’s “Negro Settlement” of twelve frame houses, Catfish Row’s first wood-frame shops by 1907 — restaurant, barbers, a boarding house — are the labor the 100,000 required and did not print. A civic fact can be true and incomplete. The number of feet is true. The 300 and the 350 are true as company figures. The town that doubled included people the figures treated as atmosphere.

The First World War would test the number by subtracting men. The Depression would test it by subtracting buyers; lumber demand, the encyclopedia says, held, and neither bank failed. The 1949 tornado would test it by subtracting the powerhouse and the stack and a thousand jobs in an afternoon. 1907 is before those tests. It is the year the test had not yet been written. A town allowed itself to believe that a hundred thousand feet was a climate. Climates change. The belief is still a fact about 1907.

Furniture stock is inside Bradley’s hundred thousand, not after it. The postcard and the 1944 *American Builder* page are later, but the hardwood dealer claim is 1907. Oak and gum leaving Warren as flooring and chair parts is the mill hour that later shops still date from. Bradley Brand Furniture, naming a heritage from 1903, is the long afterlife of that particular 100,000, the part that was never only framing. One sentence is enough. The number is the subject. The bench is the echo.

Arkansas Lumber’s 150,000 sits on the edge of this page the way a louder sibling sits at a table. The title refuses that extra fifty thousand on purpose. Two mills saying the same number is a rhyme. Three mills saying three numbers is a list. The civic fact in question is the rhyme. Southern and Bradley, pine fortune and hardwood dealer, Lakes name and St. Louis name, 100,000 and 100,000. A town that could hear both whistles in the same noon and add them without becoming two towns. The third whistle was louder. It also stopped first.

What remains of a day’s cut is not a pile. The pile was loaded. What remains is the habit of treating a unit of lumber as a unit of civic health. When Potlatch was still a large employer, the habit held. When the Chambers mill closed in 2008 amid a credit suit and a housing market that had stopped asking for boards, the habit broke in public. The *Democrat-Gazette* wrote the strain as a state story. Warren had already learned, in 1928 and 1939 and 1949, that a number can leave. 1907 is the year it arrived loudly enough to name a town’s decade.

Leave the still on the digit itself, printed in the county encyclopedia as if it were a river or a date of incorporation. One hundred thousand. Board feet. A day. Two mills, then a third. A doubled census. No invented whistle. No invented wager on the floor. Only the clerk’s unit, the company’s head-count, and a farm seat that learned to say both before dark. The cupola was new. The shortleaf was older. For one year that the paper is willing to freeze, they agreed on a number. The agreement was the town.

A person who walks the square now cannot see the 100,000. The stacks that posted it are gone or renamed or remembered as Potlatch. The brick streets of about 1927 are still there, tomatoes painted on them, as if the civic unit had changed species. It did, and it did not. The tomato is a later crop. The board foot is the crop that doubled the population. Both are Bradley County. Only one of them required a mule, a logging car, a dry kiln, and a clerk who could add to a hundred thousand without looking at the woods. The woods, on a good day in 1907, were the part that did not need to be told.
