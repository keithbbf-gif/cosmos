# Dry kilns and the smell

Green pine smells of sap and river weather. Kiln-dried pine smells of heat and resin cooked until it will hold still. Oak, wet, smells of tannin and bottomland. Oak, dried too fast, smells like a mistake — a sharp, sour note that is really a check you cannot yet see. A person who has stood on Warren’s square on a damp evening when the mills were running did not need a tour script to know which way the wind sat. The smell was the town’s industrial weather. It came from sheds that Balogh, in 1900, reduced to two verbs at the end of a list: dried, seasoned.

Those two verbs are not twins. Seasoning, in the older yard, was air and time: stickered piles, a roof if you had one, months in which the peninsula’s humidity argued with the pile. Drying, once the large mills built kilns, was a schedule. The Forest Products Laboratory’s *Wood Handbook* is not a Warren document. It is the physics the Warren kilns had to obey whether or not a superintendent owned a copy. Hardwood generally takes a moisture-content schedule: start gentle, 120 degrees Fahrenheit and a high relative humidity when the lumber is green, then hotter and drier as the boards lose water, perhaps to 180 degrees by the time the load sits near 15 percent. Southern pine structural stock is the exception the handbook names. It can take a hard, steady heat — the later industrial practice ran dry-bulbs that would ruin an oak load — and be brought to a construction moisture, often spoken as a 19 percent maximum for dimension, while furniture and millwork want 6 to 8. Heavy lowland oak, the Laboratory warned in a separate note, may need a long air-dry to about 25 percent before the kiln, or the surface will check and the inside will honeycomb. Bradley County grows both problems. Shortleaf on the sandy hills. Oak, gum, hickory in the wet. A kiln that served a mixed-car mill was not one recipe. It was a kitchen with two religions.

Bradley’s postcard verso made the religions into advertising. Sawmilling Arkansas soft pine, mild-textured oak, and other native hardwoods. Hardwood flooring, furniture stock, pine millwork, construction materials. Mixed Car Headquarters. The kiln is how a mixed car is possible. Green oak in a boxcar to St. Louis is a lawsuit. Green pine to a Midwestern yard is weight you paid to haul and warp you will hear about. Fullerton’s plant, which the encyclopedia still calls one of the world’s large hardwood dealers while posting pine numbers beside Southern’s, had to smell like both species or it was not the plant on the card.

The 1907 counts — Southern 100,000 board feet and 300 men, Bradley 100,000 and 350, Arkansas 150,000 and 400 — are sawmill numbers. They imply kiln numbers the paper does not break out. `[CITE NEEDED: kiln capacity, number of charges, or a Sanborn outline of dry kilns at Bradley, Southern, or Arkansas Lumber.]` Sanborn maps of mill towns usually draw the kilns as long brick or frame boxes near the sheds, a fire risk the insurer cares about more than the tourist. Warren’s commercial-district nomination is a downtown document. The kilns lived with the stacks, south of the square, in the section Purkins would call the Bradley plant after the 1949 storm. A kiln without steam is a shed full of expensive firewood. When the powerhouse went down at 5:45 p.m. on 3 January, the charges already in brick were on their own weather. The paper does not say whether those charges were saved. The smell that week was rain, wet plaster, and pine that had been houses an hour earlier.

Air-drying never left. Even a mill with kilns keeps a yard. Stickered piles — thin strips between courses so air can move — are the older technology the kiln never retired. A person walking the mill section in a dry autumn would have seen a city of stacks before he saw the brick boxes. Oak, especially, spends time in stickers because kiln weeks are money and yard weeks are cheaper if the degrade can be borne. OSU’s later extension language — green 4/4 white oak more than a month in the kiln, air-dried oak a week or two — is not a 1910 Warren invoice. It is the same arithmetic. Arkansas Lumber, cutting 150,000 a day toward an 85,000-acre cut-out, lived on volume. Volume argues for pine schedules and a yard that looks like a city of stacks. Bradley, selling hardwood reputation and pine convenience from the same spur, had to keep the oak pretty. Pretty oak is slow. The peninsula’s summers are not slow. A filer can ruin a day. A kiln man can ruin a month.

The smell is chemistry the town experienced as mood. Southern yellow pine carries oleoresin — turpentine and related fractions — that heat drives into the air. Crossett would later build a chemical company on that fact and sell turpentine, acetic acid, wood alcohol. Warren’s large mills, in the published county record, stayed with lumber, flooring, millwork, furniture stock. They still cooked the same molecules. Oak’s tannins and the sour of a too-hot charge are a different kitchen. On a still August night the two smells layered. A child on a company street did not say terpene. The child said the mill. Tomato fields, after the 1920s, added a third smell in season, acid and vine, and the county learned to be proud of the fruit because the trees were finite. Dual identity: timber and tomato. The kiln did not care about the festival. The festival, after 1956, was what merchants did when they wanted the square to smell like supper instead of steam.

Southern’s 1939 fire and modernization sit inside the kiln story. A fire at a dry mill is a feared sentence. Kilns are designed to be hot; the rest of the plant is not supposed to join them. Modernization after 1939, the encyclopedia says, made Southern one of the more up-to-date mills in the country. Warner, arriving to shut it, kept it for second growth. Second-growth pine dries differently from old-growth. The rings are wider, the resin sometimes more, the pieces smaller. A schedule written for a virgin cant is a starting rumor. The kiln operator, like the filer, had to learn a new forest without a new title. Process orientation — less waste, smaller trees, more products — made drying more, not less, central. You cannot make flooring stock from a tree you have not first persuaded to stop moving.

Sample boards — kiln samples weighed by hand, later sometimes by load cell — are how a moisture-content schedule is not a guess. The handbook describes the method. Warren’s published pack does not name the man who walked the charges with a scale. `[CITE NEEDED: a Bradley or Southern kiln log, 1910–1950.]` The nose was the unofficial instrument. The scale was the official one. A mixed-car mill that shipped oak flooring and pine finish from the same week needed both.

Women on the floor in the First World War, slacks under the dress rule, worked in a plant that already smelled like this. The paper does not assign them to the kiln crew. `[CITE NEEDED: occupational list for kiln or yard labor by sex, either war.]` The Second World War’s fifty-nine county dead are a different absence. The kilns ran. Someone pulled charges and recorded moisture. The someone is not in the pack.

Monday’s storm in 1949 threw the mill’s iron into yards and living rooms. A bale of cotton plastered a fence like down, the later memory says; steel traveled three-quarters of a mile. Kiln brick is heavy and low. It can survive a wind that takes a stack. It cannot run without the boiler the stack belonged to. One thousand employees displaced includes the men who knew, by nose, when a charge was ready. Housing wrecked — 120 destroyed, 72 major, 150 minor on the incomplete 1949 check — includes the porches that had smelled the night shift. Good Friday 1975 repeated the path. Seven dead. The kilns, by then, wore Potlatch’s name.

Potlatch Forests, Inc., of Idaho bought Southern in 1956 and Bradley in 1958 and, the nomination notes, more than 100,000 acres of southern yellow pine, oak, and other hardwoods. A West Coast letterhead knows kilns. What it inherited in Warren was a peninsula that had been drying both species since the Fullerton sons arrived. After 2002, after the Chambers purchase, after the 2008 closure, the smell left the south side of town the way a weather leaves. The remaining object is not a kiln door. It is the habit, in a later shop, of asking whether a board is dry enough to trust. Bradley Brand Furniture, dating hardwood work from the mill’s first years, still has to answer that question. Oak that grew in the same bottoms the 1826 plats called fields will still move if the board is rushed. The handbook and the nose agree. The town, when it had three mills, agreed every day at quitting time, when the heat stayed in the brick and the square took the smell and kept it overnight.
