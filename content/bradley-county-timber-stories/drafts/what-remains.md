# What remains

The depot still sits below the grade. 325 West Cedar. Split-level wood, a building that looks, if you squint, like a foursquare house that learned to accept freight. The Warren and Ouachita Valley Railway put it up in 1909 and rebuilt it on the same dirt in 1911 after a fire. Twin entrances with transoms and sidelights, a shingled upper story, wide eaves, shed dormers, a warehouse roof that runs out over the loading. Passenger trains used it until 1948. The National Register took it in 1977, still in railroad use then. Potlatch had bought the depot in the 1950s and, around 1970, restored it for the Warren and Saline River short line. Silva’s tour notes that the W&SR later bought two miles of W&OV track through town and, in 2010, sold to a Pinsley company out of McGehee. The building remains. That is the first still. A mill town is supposed to lose its depot. Warren kept a wooden one, which is rarer in Arkansas than a brick one, and rarer still when the mills that paid for the line have changed names four times.

Walk east and the square is Gibb’s 1903 courthouse, the third on the site. Logs in 1843. Brick and stucco in 1861. Then yellow and light-brown brick, rusticated first floor, towers of unequal height, a cupola, Neoclassical body, Romanesque nerves. Frank Wooster Gibb of Little Rock, the same architect the commercial-district nomination still lists with state pride. The county clerk’s office, 1890, pressed-tin roof and cresting, windows later bricked, post office on the 1907 Sanborn, city then county library, Women’s Club museum before the Martin House, sheriff’s office until the courthouse grew an addition. National Register, 1976, for the pair. A county that once met in Hugh Bradley’s house now meets under a cupola that has outlived Arkansas Lumber, the Fullerton letterhead, the 1949 stack, and the 2008 hardwood gate. The law is the object that did not require a log to stay.

The Dr. John Wilson Martin House on Ash Street is the oldest known residence left in the county. Construction began about 1860; the house was finished after the war. Vernacular Greek Revival: symmetrical front, multi-pane transom and half-sidelights, six-over-six windows, a two-story porch on square columns. An I-house first — central hall, two rooms up, two down — with a servants’ stair still in the wall and later rear additions, the kitchen ell interior coming in the 1920s or 1930s. At least ten outbuildings once: stables, smokehouse, detached kitchen, privy. Martin, born 1819 in Virginia, studied at Tulane about 1843, was in Warren by 1848, married Mary Elizabeth Franklin that year, and was the only doctor, traveling to houses. Federal troops took food and stock. Eight children, seven living to adulthood. It is said he could stand on the upper porch and see all their homes; one house remains near Martin and Pine. He died in 1910, past ninety. Descendants stayed until 1987, when the Warren Women’s Club bought the place for a museum. Historic Preservation Restoration Grants came in 2001, 2004, 2005, 2008. National Register, 1990. The captain’s house is gone. The doctor’s house remains. Memory plays favorites with the buildings that survive.

Brick streets, about 1927, running bond, diagonal at the intersections, listed in 2007. Cedar, Myrtle, Chestnut, First, Walnut, Elm, Cypress: the nomination names the grid. Cities paved this way and then paved over. Warren did not, not here. Painted tomatoes appeared later, the dual identity in traffic paint. A person who walks the square in summer hears the brick before seeing the fruit. Horses heard it first. Then Model T’s. Then the festival weekend. The brick is the mill town’s floor after the mill floors closed. It does not flex. It remembers wagon width.

The Pastime Theater, 110 West Cedar, about 1925 — *The Reel Journal*, 22 March 1924, already had W. A. Halley of Warren drawing a $20,000 house. Mission-style parapet in stucco, original marquee, a slab foundation, one and a half stories. A separate entrance for Black patrons; a designated balcony. It ran into the late 1970s. Silva still found the marquee. A 1971 photograph says Pastime; a 1986 photograph says Bryant’s Inc.; later street views still show the old lettering. Paul Theroux’s *Deep South* put the façade on a cover, Steve McCurry’s picture. The building is what remains of a Saturday night that was never only white. The 1941 protest, a policeman kicking Ms. O. Z. Jackson, belongs to the balcony’s essay. This one only has to say the balcony is still implied by the architecture even when the door is a later storefront. Jim Crow was a floor plan. The plan remains in the parapet’s shadow.

What remains is also what is almost gone. Catfish Row on East Elm: Black-owned businesses, a painted fish on a window if the *Eagle*’s later note is right, a block that by 1931 was full of one- and two-story brick — restaurants, a theater, two bottling works, a lodge. What stands is a one-story brick of four storefronts, Hammons Funeral Home, G. W. H. in the eastern bay for George W. Hammons. The YMCA of 1920 burned in 1944; a later Reynolds building took the corner that had been a skating rink on the 1907 Sanborn. The Sutherland Hotel — seventy-five rooms, the only commercial elevator in town — burned in 1964. The Iron Mountain / MoPac passenger and freight depots at Chestnut and Elm are not extant. The commissary and warehouse of Bradley Lumber, Main at Church, were gone by the mid-1920s; a park and a miniature golf sat on the lot; Louis A. Simon’s Colonial Revival post office rose in 1935–36 and served until 1998. Remains are a selection. Fire and taste and the 1949 wind did the editing.

Eleven buildings on the National Register, the Warren encyclopedia said in its older sentence; Silva in 2012 counted thirteen city listings and one on the Arkansas Register. The commercial historic district gathers the square: courthouse, clerk’s office, Warren Bank of 1927 with an eagle on the pediment, the 1935 post office, the Pastime, Blankenship’s 1940 Art Moderne on Cypress and Myrtle, the 1931 Art Deco municipal building with a fire pole in living memory. Period of significance, 1890 to 1966: from the clerk’s office to the last years before the square thinned toward the bypass. Tornadoes in 1949 and 1975 cut through and left the upper façades more intact than the mill houses. The district is not innocence. It is brick that outlasted wood.

A person who wants the mill itself will be disappointed by the square. The mills sat off it — powerhouse, pond, kilns, the geography later called Pennington and Pine and the bypass. What remains of that industrial body is a set of later names: Robbins, Armstrong, chip mills, a lease in mill space for a smaller hardwood shop. Bradley Brand Furniture, dating a practice from 1903, is an afterlife, not a ruin exhibit. The depot is closer to the old verb. It was built to move lumber to Banks and people to the Rock Island. It still knows the grade.

The Martin House museum keeps a tomato room: menus, shirts, the festival’s paper trail. It also keeps the doctor’s century. Civil War taking, children’s houses in sight, a porch that is a documentary’s natural still. The Women’s Club’s move from the clerk’s office in 1986–87 is the kind of civic sequence this county repeats. A small room holds a temporary use. Then a house is purchased. Then grants arrive. Then a school group stands in the hall and is told that Warren had a doctor before it had Fullerton. That lesson is what remains when the whistle is a memory.

First person is almost not needed. The depot still sits below the grade. That is a standing observation, not a diary. From the platform line a person can see how the town used the railroad as a basement: commercial streets up, freight down, race and class arranged by which door you were handed. White and colored waiting rooms were on the later MoPac passenger depot that is gone. The W&OV wood depot keeps the domestic face — fanlights, sidelights — as if a station could pretend to be a house. Hugh Bradley’s house pretended to be a court. The rhyme is not cute. It is how this county makes official life in buildings that look like they might also be lived in.

After the big mill, after the hardwood close, after two tornadoes, the remaining objects do the work the payroll no longer does. They tell a visitor the town was a seat, a railhead, a Jim Crow balcony, a brick experiment, a doctor’s porch, a festival. They do not tell the visitor the names on the time clock. That is a different remaining, thinner, paper, often lost. What remains in three dimensions is the civic set. What remains in one dimension is the *Eagle Democrat* and the Sanborns and a postcard of a mill pond. The next essay is that paper. This one is the walk.

Mill-town America photographs a gate and a stack. Warren’s remaining photograph is a wooden depot, a cupola, a Greek Revival porch, a brick street with a painted tomato, and a Mission marquee that has outlived its projector. The type is the preserved downtown. The particular is the split-level wood and the two-colored courthouse and the doctor who could see his children’s roofs. Crossett’s remains are company-town remains. Warren’s are seat-of-government remains that the mills filled and did not found.

The still, if this were a film, would begin on the depot step, hold long enough to hear no train, then tilt to the cupola, then cut to the Martin porch, then drop to the brick, then end on the Pastime’s unlit marquee. Five objects. One town. No sermon. The mills made the money that paved the brick and filled the balcony. The objects remain after the money changed clothes. A person can walk them in an hour. The hour is the history that did not burn.
