# Pink tomato and pine

They picked it when the shoulders had barely gone pink. Not red. Not ripe in the kitchen sense. Pink enough to ship, green enough to travel, a fruit that would finish its color in a crate on a wagon or a truck headed out of Bradley County. Silva’s 2012 downtown script still says the reason in one dry sentence: farmers chose a variety that would ship if it left the vine at that first blush. The encyclopedia is prouder. It calls the Bradley Pink a standard for the American tomato market and, later, Arkansas’s gift to the nation. The mill town had already given the nation pine. The fruit was the second letterhead.

Cotton had been the older field crop. By the 1920s the small cotton farm in this county was losing its argument with price and soil. Local growers turned to tomatoes, especially the pink, introduced here as early as that decade. The plant lacks a yellow pigment. The color is the name. Bradley County, which the *Encyclopedia of Arkansas* still introduces as a prime production area, learned to speak of itself in two commodities at once. Pine on the hills. Pink in the rows. A later booster line — land of tall pines and pink tomatoes — is advertising language, and the county story does not need the slogan. The dual identity is older than the slogan. It sat in the same two banks that, the encyclopedia is careful to say, did not fail in the 1930s. Mill money and tomato money used the same tellers.

Warren had already been a mill town for a generation when the first commercial pinks went out. Southern Lumber, Bradley Lumber Company of Arkansas, Arkansas Lumber Company: by 1907 the three of them posted 350,000 board feet a day and employed a thousand men. Warren’s population had doubled between 1900 and 1910. The square filled. Brick streets were laid about 1927. The Bradley Store on South Main took scrip. The fruit arrived into that noise, not into a vacuum. A county that had learned to count trees in board feet learned to count tomatoes in lugs. The two counts did not cancel. They shared a courthouse and a rail.

June 1956. A group of Warren merchants, and Loran Johnson, then director of the Chamber of Commerce, put on a one-day event to celebrate the tomato industry and to help the stores. Musicians. A carnival. Exhibits. The Bradley County Pink Tomato Festival was born in that ordinary civic hour, the kind of hour mill towns invent when they need a camera that is not a smokestack. A chairperson was chosen each year. In 1957 a parade and a beauty pageant were added. The festival site later claimed that every Arkansas governor serving since 1956 has attended. `[CITE NEEDED: a year-by-year attendance record, if one exists outside festival memory.]` What the encyclopedia will stand on is simpler. The day became a week. About 30,000 people come now. Warren calls itself the Pink Tomato Capital of the World. The title is self-proclaimed. The crop is not.

The All Tomato Luncheon has a founding weather. Jean Frisby, the University of Arkansas Cooperative Extension home economist for the county, and Loran Johnson, by then serving as county extension agent, found themselves in the Southern Hotel on the square with the young women who were running for Tomato Queen, crowded into rooms that were not air-conditioned. Frisby wanted a more proper event. The next year the luncheon was held in the YMCA building. About twenty-five guests came. The year after that the extension homemakers planned for the same number and more than a hundred arrived. Fresh tomato juice was served to people waiting in the lobby. Speakers often came from Cooperative Extension, in thanks for the organization’s part in teaching the crop. Everything on the menu, including dessert, was made with tomatoes. Silva notes a tomato room in the Martin House museum: old menus, old shirts. Memory prefers the luncheon to the packing shed. The shed did the work.

House Bill 1480, Regular Session, 1987. Representative Johnny Lipton of this county carried the fruit to the legislature. Act 255, approved 16 March 1987 by Governor Bill Clinton, designated the South Arkansas Vine Ripe Pink Tomato as both the official state fruit and the official state vegetable. The act’s finding is a small poem of civic nerve. The tomato has a taste, texture, appearance, and aroma second to none. It is botanically a fruit and used as a vegetable. Arkansas had neither a state fruit nor a state vegetable. Therefore this one would be both. The law is still on the books as Arkansas Code 1-4-115. A mill county that had never been asked to furnish a state tree of its own furnished the state’s salad and its dessert in a single paragraph.

The festival’s later hours are public and ordinary. A street dance on the square. Live music on the weekend nights. A Saturday 5K, then a parade of pageant winners, riders, politicians, firefighters. A tomato-eating contest that often includes the same class of men who pass acts. An auction. Arts and a race. The chamber’s later page still lists 104 North Myrtle as the office. None of that is the essay. The essay is the coincidence of dates. 1956 is also the year Potlatch Forests, Inc., of Idaho bought Southern Lumber Company. Two years later Potlatch bought Bradley Lumber. The festival and the West Coast owner arrived in the same civic season. One was a week in June. One was a letterhead change on the mill. A county that was already two things became two things more loudly: a tomato town for the camera, a timber town for the payroll.

Hermitage, nearer the county’s geographic center, kept more of the field work. Clanton Farms, Harrod & Hensley Tomato Company, Morris Hatchery, a packing facility that outlasted an office move to Arizona: the encyclopedia’s Hermitage entry is a farm list, not a mill list. Warren kept the festival and the mills. The dual identity is not a metaphor painted on brick, though the brick did receive painted tomatoes. It is a division of labor. The seat of government threw the parade. The ridge in the middle of the county grew the fruit. Banks, Johnsville, Ingalls, the old rows between them — those places never needed a capital-of-the-world sign. They needed a crop that would ship.

Silva, walking the square in 2012, said people had been switching to watermelons and bell peppers because they are easier to grow. Potlatch was still logging, not in great financial shape. Robbins Flooring and chip mills were still in operation. The Bradley County Medical Center was probably the largest employer in Warren. That is a later still. In 1956 the dual identity was not yet a hospital story. It was a crate and a whistle. The crate was new enough to celebrate. The whistle was old enough to be assumed.

A reader who knows only the festival will miss the older wage. Shortleaf pine on the sandy hills, oak and gum in the bottoms, three mills posting numbers in 1907 as if a peninsula could be emptied on a schedule: that was the county’s industrial century. The tomato was the agricultural revision after cotton. Dairy later supplied a Kraft cheese plant in Warren. Jack Winter’s women’s clothing shop opened in 1958, the same year Potlatch took Bradley. La Donna Garment Company began a line in 1975. The encyclopedia lists those plants as postwar industry. They are footnotes to pine. The pink tomato is not a footnote. It is the other title on the same map.

Mill-town America often has a second crop. A peach in one county, a rice field in another, a festival that photographs better than a dry kiln. The particular here is the color and the law. A fruit without yellow pigment, picked early to travel, written into the statutes as both fruit and vegetable because a legislature could not choose. The type is the booster week. The particular is Act 255 and a luncheon that outgrew its guest list. Warren did not stop being a mill town when it learned to be a tomato town. It learned to hold two letterheads without dropping either.

The square in June still smells like cut fruit and warm brick. The depot below the grade does not. The depot smells like creosote and dust, the older perfume. A bench owes the pine and the oak. It does not owe a pageant. The fruit and the wood shared the banks, the rail, and the habit of leaving town in a box. One left pink. One left kiln-dried. Both left because isolation had ended in 1880, when Warren became a western terminus and a tree, or a crate, could finally move.

What remains of the dual identity on an ordinary Tuesday is quieter than the second full weekend in June. A packing shed in the county’s middle. A painted tomato a visitor photographs and mistakes for the whole story. A mill site whose owners have changed names more often than the fruit has changed color. The encyclopedia’s 2020 census still gives the county 10,545 people. That is not a festival crowd. It is a peninsula that learned, for one long American century, to sell two things the rest of the country wanted: yellow pine, and a tomato that was willing to travel while it was still almost green.

The still might not be the parade. It might be a lug of fruit at first light, shoulders just pink, and, somewhere offscreen, a mill whistle that has not yet decided whether it is history or a workday. The county did not choose. It carried both.
