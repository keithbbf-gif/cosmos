# After the big mill

The big mill, in this county, was never one stack. It was three letterheads that taught Warren to count in board feet. Southern Lumber Company, 100,000 a day, 300 men, by 1907. Bradley Lumber Company of Arkansas, 100,000, 350, already a world’s large hardwood dealer. Arkansas Lumber Company, 150,000, 400. Then Arkansas Lumber clear-cut 85,000 acres and closed in 1928. Southern burned in 1939; W. R. Warner of Cloquet, Minnesota, came south to shut it and talked the owners into second growth instead. Potlatch Forests, Inc., bought Southern in 1956 and Bradley in 1958. After that, “the mill” in ordinary speech meant Potlatch. After Potlatch’s hardwood hour ended, the sentence changed again. After the big mill, in this town, is not an empty lot. It is a sequence of smaller wages that had to learn to stand without a thousand-man whistle.

Silva, walking downtown on 14 July 2012, gave the later still in four clauses. People had been switching from tomatoes to watermelons and bell peppers because they are easier to grow. Potlatch was still logging, not in great financial shape. Other lumber-related industries — Robbins Flooring, which manufactured Armstrong flooring, and chip mills — were still in operation. The Bradley County Medical Center was probably the largest employer in Warren. That is not a lament. It is a payroll map. The hospital took the number the mills used to take. The flooring plants took the species. The chip mills took what a saw no longer wanted. The fruit took the festival. None of them took the 1907 count.

Population is the other clock. The county peaked in 1940 at 18,097 and then declined as mills modernized and farms needed fewer hands: 15,987 in 1950, 14,029 in 1960, 12,778 in 1970, a brief lift to 13,803 in 1980, then 11,793, 12,600, 11,508, and 10,545 in 2020. Warren itself is a stranger curve. 301 in 1880, 954 in 1900, 2,057 in 1910, then a long plateau near 2,500 until the 1950 census, 2,615. In 1960 the town jumped to 6,752. Annexation and mill-town housing will explain some of that; the published town entry does not pause to footnote the jump. `[CITE NEEDED: the 1950–1960 Warren annexation or count-method change.]` The peak is 1980: 7,646. Then 6,455, 6,442, 6,003, 5,453 in 2020. After the big mill, the seat is still the seat. It is not the 1910 doubling. It is not 1907.

Postwar industry tried on other clothes. Dairy farms supplied a Kraft cheese plant in Warren. In 1958 — the Potlatch-Bradley year — Jack Winter opened a women’s clothing manufacture. In 1975, the year of the Good Friday tornado, La Donna Garment Company began a line of women’s clothes in the county. The encyclopedia lists them the way a county history lists them: established, produced, implied gone. They are not the afterlife of hardwood. They are the American habit of putting a sewing room where a saw has thinned. Tomato packing at Hermitage proved more durable. Clanton Farms, Harrod & Hensley, a hatchery, a packing shed that outlasted an office’s move to Arizona: the ridge in the middle of the county kept a field wage when the square kept a hospital wage.

Robbins Flooring is the mill’s next of kin. The plants sat on mill geography: 501 Pennington Road, the Sykes name still on later environmental filings; 688 Highway 278 Bypass, the Witt plant, later Armstrong Flooring in the same public lists. Hardwood flooring is what Bradley Lumber had already advertised in an *American Builder* page in August 1944 — pre-finished stock from Warren — and what a 1940s postcard called mild-textured oak alongside Arkansas Soft Pine. Furniture stock, pine millwork, mixed-car headquarters: the postcard back is a catalog of afterlives that were already the mill’s present. Robbins and Armstrong did not invent the floor. They inherited a county that knew how to dry oak and mill a tongue. A later Warren shop, Bradley Brand Furniture, still dates a hardwood practice from 1903 for the same reason. The species did not leave when the letterhead did.

Chip mills are a less photogenic sequel. They take pulpwood and residual, the trees that will not make a floor, and they turn a cutover county into fiber. The UAM forest-business profile for the county, 2021, still found forests on 88 percent of the land, 94 percent privately owned, and forest industry at about a quarter of county economic activity — far above the state share. PotlatchDeltic, Armstrong Hardwood Products, Ouachita Hardwood Flooring, Oasis Trading, Price Fibers, sixteen logging firms: the list is a later directory, not a 1907 whistle. After the big mill means the woods did not retire. The single employer did.

The medical center is the civic surprise only if a person thinks a mill town must die when the stack cools. Warren had doctors before it had Fullerton. Dr. John Wilson Martin, Virginia-born, Tulane-trained, in town by 1848, walked the county to houses. The Hunt Hospital occupied part of the top floor of Ederington’s Cash Store. The 1975 tornado wrecked a cardiac wing. The later Bradley County Medical Center is the institutional descendant of those rooms: not a mill, not a commissary, a payroll that does not depend on a log. Silva’s “probably the largest” is the 2012 sentence. School districts compete with it. Tomato sheds compete in season. The point is not a ranking for a chamber brochure. The point is that the largest wage in a mill town can become care.

Potlatch’s own afterlife is a split. Pine stayed longer than hardwood. On 3 June 2002 the company announced it would close the Bradley hardwood mill and exit that business in Arkansas, citing scarce hardwood logs and the cost of buying them off the market. Softwood — southern yellow pine — dominated the company’s timberland math. Thirty-five jobs would be added at the Warren pine mill; seventy would go at hardwood. A buyer was sought. In August, Dr. David Chambers and Michelle Chambers bought the hardwood plant. That purchase is the next essay. The 2008 closure is the one after that. After the big mill, even the afterlife had an afterlife.

What the town kept in the interval is ordinary and specific. Brick streets, about 1927, still under the painted tomatoes. The W&OV depot, 1909, rebuilt 1911, National Register 1977, later a Potlatch short-line office. The 1903 Gibb courthouse. The Martin House museum after 1987. The Pastime’s marquee. Catfish Row reduced to a brick stretch with Hammons on the eastern storefront. The Bradley Store building on South Main, scrip gone, later a dollar store. These are not employers. They are what a person walks through on the way to an employer that is no longer a mill.

Mill-town America has a standard third act. The company sells or closes. A flooring plant or a pallet mill or a chipper takes a corner of the yard. A hospital or a prison or a chicken plant takes the head count. A festival takes the postcard. Warren’s third act is that sequence without a prison and with a fruit the legislature already named. Crossett, south, was invented as a company town and had to invent a civic life afterward. Fordyce and Warren, Balogh notes, already owed their existence to farms and rails. After the big mill, Warren could remember being a seat of justice in 1841. The hospital is built on that older civic habit as much as on the mill’s departure. A county that once met in a captain’s house knows how to make an institution out of a need.

The woods came back in the way Warner hoped in 1939. Shortleaf and loblolly on the hills, hardwood still in the bottoms. The 2020 density is about sixteen people to the square mile. That is enough to staff a hospital, a school system, a few plants, a packing season, and a June weekend. It is not enough to staff 1907. After the big mill is therefore not a ruin photograph. It is a thinner payroll and the same peninsula. The rivers did not retire. Felsenthal’s green-tree reservoir at the southern tip, Moro Bay where three counties touch the Ouachita: the state admitted the water was never only a freight problem. The mill men of 1901 wanted the trees. The later employers wanted shifts. The peninsula wanted, as it always had, a way for whatever was cut or picked to leave.

A person who drives Pennington Road past the old Sykes geography, or the 278 bypass past the Witt name, is driving the afterlife in the literal sense. Flooring left those doors. Chips left others. Ambulances left the medical center. None of those vehicles is a 1907 log train on the W&OV. All of them are the county’s later sentence: we still make something, and we still take care of the people who used to make more.

The still, if this were a film, would be the hospital lot at shift change, pine on the horizon, no stack required for the skyline, and a flooring plant’s metal wall catching the same late light that used to hit a burner. After the big mill, the light did not need a new county. It needed a new clock.
