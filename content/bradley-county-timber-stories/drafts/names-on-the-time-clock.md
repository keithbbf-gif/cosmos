# Names on the time clock

The time clock does not keep the names. Payroll ledgers for Bradley, Southern, and Arkansas Lumber have not come into this pack. Union cards from the Warren floors, if they existed in the early mill decades, have not been quoted. City directories by occupation and race, 1900–1940, remain a thin file. `[CITE NEEDED]` What the clock would have held is therefore a set of jobs, dates, and counts. Sawyers. Filers. Stackers. Kiln tenders. Firemen at the powerhouse. Teamsters, then truck men. Graders who knew oak from gum by the end grain. Women, in 1918, who wore a dress to the gate, slacks on the floor, a dress home. More than a thousand workmen displaced when the 1949 stack fell across the power. Those are the names we are allowed. Invented ones would be a plausible lie. This series does not do that.

By 1907 the three large mills posted a combined thousand jobs if you add the encyclopedia’s round figures: 300 at Southern, 350 at Bradley, 400 at Arkansas Lumber. Warren had 954 people in 1900 and 2,057 in 1910. The difference is not only births. It is men, and then families, who came because a whistle paid better than a weedy field. The small mills of the 1880s — Shirey & Butler, Crandall & Leavitt, Edmondson, Glasgow & Temple, Lanark, Whittington, Parker & Kinard, Lowry & Taylor, J. E. Walker — had already taught the county the occupations. Carpenter’s 1891 mill, Wheeler’s Bradley start, Fullerton’s purchase, Reaves walking timber: those are owner names. The clock did not punch them. It punched the people who made 100,000 board feet a day look like weather.

A sawyer’s work is a judgment repeated until the ear rings. Head saw, carriage, the log turning to show the face that will waste least. A filer is the mill’s other priest. Bands and circulars go dull; the filer restores the tooth; production is a function of his patience. Those two jobs are in every southern pine-belt memoir. They are in Warren whether or not a signed card survives. The bibliography’s thin list is honest. We do not have the Wednesday of a particular sawyer in 1912. We have the job, the year of the count, and the town’s sudden doubling.

When registration for the First World War began, the mills ran short of men and hired women. The dress rule, the Warren encyclopedia says, was simple. Wear a dress to work, change into slacks, wear a dress home. The sentence is almost all the published labor history we have for women on those floors. No wage. No shift length. No list of departments that would take them. The Council of 22 in 1918 and the YMCA charter in 1920 are men’s civic furniture around the same hour. The women are a dress rule. That thinness is the record, not a reason to invent a conversation at a locker. A Ken Burns hour would hold the rule and the silence after it. Slacks on the floor meant the mill needed their hands. A dress at the gate meant the town still needed the old silhouette. Both needs are historical. Neither is a name.

Race is on the clock even when the card is lost. Sanborns label a half block at Walnut and Ash, by 1912, “Negro Settlement”: twelve small frame dwellings, still there on the 1938 sheet. Catfish Row on East Elm filled with Black-owned businesses — barber, boarding house, confectionery, pool, cobbler, grocery, later brick, a theater, bottling, a lodge. The Pastime’s separate door and balcony were a floor plan for the same people who also punched mills. The mill yards themselves, in southern practice, were mixed and ranked. `[CITE NEEDED: Warren-specific occupation-by-race tables; do not import Crossett’s strike file as a substitute.]` Lynching is in the town’s other register: William and Henry Beavers, 1890 and 1892, ages estimated fourteen and sixteen; John Turner, 1903; two white men, Hamilton and Ludberry, in 1887. A time-clock essay that pretends the yard was only a wage is incomplete. The yard was a wage inside a Jim Crow county. Dignity here is naming the record and not turning it into weather.

Scrip at the Bradley Store, 215 South Main after 1920, is the clock’s partner. Shop in company paper, settle at the week’s end. The store is an owner’s building. The people who used the scrip are the missing names. McCann’s Grocery delivered; independent merchants on the square sold what the commissary also sold. Tomato money and mill money later shared the two banks that did not fail. The clock’s people were not sealed inside a company town. Warren’s fence, as an earlier essay said, was leaky. That leak is the closest thing we have to a labor freedom in the published civic record. It is not a union contract.

1941, in a later-owner history, puts about 1,100 employees at the Bradley mill. Treat the figure as a later-owner figure, the bibliography says, not as a 1901 proof. Even as a later figure it is a crowd. 1949 turned the crowd into a displacement number. The *Eagle Democrat*’s tragic record: around the powerhouse, the stack crumpled across the source of power for more than a thousand workmen. Water off in the Bradley section. Telephones dead in the south of town. Darkness. Rain in sheets. Mrs. J. E. Stewart, wife of the Bradley Store manager, killed by a timber on South Main. She has a name because the paper gave her one, and because she was a manager’s wife. The thousand do not. They have a count and a ruined powerhouse. The clock, that Monday, had nothing to punch.

World War II took fifty-nine county dead. Some of those names were mill names first. The published list in this pack is a county number, not a plant roster. `[CITE NEEDED: which of the fifty-nine had mill occupations.]` The mills ran. The women of 1918 had already shown the method. The 1940s floor is another thin place. We can say the plant advertised flooring in 1944. We cannot say who stacked it.

Filers and sawyers are skilled. Much of the thousand was not called skilled in the payroll sense and was skilled in the body sense: knowing when a cant would kick, when a kiln was wrong by smell, when a wagon was loaded past sense. Chip mill and flooring-plant jobs later required some of the same body knowledge. The medical center required a different one. After the big mill, the clock’s descendants punched hospital badges and school badges and, for a time, Robbins. The occupations changed. The habit of a shift did not.

Do not invent a letter home. Do not invent a wage in 1910 dollars. Do not invent a conversation between a woman in slacks and a filer at noon. If a story is too good and the paper is thin, say so. The paper is thin. What can be stood on is this: a town doubled because of jobs; the jobs had names even when the people did not reach the encyclopedia; 1918 changed the dress code; 1949 stopped the clock for a thousand at once; the ledgers that would have listed them are not here.

Mill-town America’s documentary habit is to find one talker — an old sawyer in a kitchen, a woman who remembers the slacks — and let that voice stand for the yard. This series does not have that talker on tape. It has Bradham, Silva, the encyclopedia, the *Eagle*, a postcard, a Sanborn. The honest substitute for an oral history is a refusal to fake one. Jobs, dates, counts. The time clock as an object: a machine that converted a face into a number of hours. The faces are why the machine existed. The hours are what the company kept. We have the company’s board-feet more reliably than we have the faces. That imbalance is the scar.

A later Warren bench, made of the same species the stock men graded, does not restore the names. It is not asked to. Bradley Brand Furniture’s 1903 date is a mill hour, not a payroll. The people who punched 1903, if the date is close, are dead. Their grandchildren may or may not still live on Myrtle or in Hermitage or out of state. We will not invent them at a reunion. We will say the clock was full, and that fullness is the town’s first industrial fact.

If this were a film, the camera would hold on a rack of blank cards, or on a clock face without a surviving photograph of this particular clock, and the voice-over would read jobs the way Bradham read a grand jury: sawyer, filer, stacker, kiln man, fireman, grader, the woman in slacks, the thousand in the rain. Then it would stop. A list is not an oration. It is the record we have.

The still is a gate at shift change, 1907 or 1918 or 1941, faces we cannot lawfully draw, a whistle, and a clock that did its work and left us the jobs instead of the names. The names were real. They are simply not ours to mint.
