# Two names for Warren

Moscow Cemetery, near Prescott, holds a congressman under a name the county seat sixty miles of pine away may or may not wear. Edward Allen Warren, late of Camden, late of the Thirty-Third and Thirty-Fifth Congresses, died on 2 July 1875 and was buried in Nevada County. The town of Warren, Arkansas, kept working. The argument about its name did not go into the ground with him.

Before the argument there were other names, and they were not arguments. They were directions. The clearing that became the county seat was called Pennington Settlement, for the captain’s son-in-law I. H. Pennington, whose township still carries the family into the tax books. It was called Cabeens, for Dr. John Thomas Cabeen, postmaster from 1832 until 1843, later county surveyor, the first civic pulse that was not a court. It was called Saline Settlement, which is only honest: the river is the reason anyone stopped. The *Encyclopedia of Arkansas* keeps all three. Rachel Silva, walking downtown in 2012 for the Arkansas Historic Preservation Program, kept them too. A settlement that answers to three names is not confused. It is unfinished.

John Harvie Marks gave thirty acres. John Splawn gave ten. In 1841 the pair sat on the commission to locate a seat, then resigned because a donor should not also be the judge of his own gift. E. B. Owen stayed. Nathaniel Barnett and A. S. Franklin took the empty chairs. In April 1843 they reported the site of the present courthouse and filed the deeds to forty acres. Warren officially became the seat in 1842, Silva says. The town’s incorporation is a split date of the kind this county collects: 26 December 1850 in the encyclopedia’s Warren table; 1851 in Silva’s tour script. Both papers are trying to say the same thing. A farm hamlet on the Chicot Trace — also the Gaines Landing Road, the Fort Towson Road, the Washita Road, depending on which wagon you ask — agreed to be a municipality. The mills were not yet a rumor. The name was already a problem.

Judge David A. Bradham, writing in 1951, called the Warren Bradley story “the most popular belief.” The town, in that telling, was named for Captain Hugh Bradley’s most trusted enslaved servant, a man called Warren. Goodspeed’s 1890 county biography of the surviving son, Hugh Bradley the younger, had already put the same claim in print: the seat “was named for the Captain’s colored body servant.” Local family tradition, the Warren encyclopedia entry says, held that line for a long time. In 2015 a time capsule came out of the courthouse wall with a letter attributed to Hugh Bradley, Jr., and the encyclopedia, revising, treated the find as confirmation. A lawyer’s blog from the square called the mystery solved. A film about the captain had finished shooting two days before. The timing is the kind of coincidence a documentary loves. It is not a gavel.

The style of this series is to hold both names in one sentence and refuse the gavel. The 2015 capsule is evidence. It is not a verdict the other paper has agreed to vanish. The same encyclopedia paragraph that cites the capsule still says there is sufficient evidence the town was named for Bradley’s close friend Edward Allen Warren, who practiced law in Camden and served the state as a U.S. representative. The county entry, revised 2022, is blunter: “a difference of opinion exists.” Silva, three years before the capsule, taught the walking tour both theories and did not pick. A county that cannot agree on the name of its seat is already telling the truth about memory. We remember the man who stopped. We argue about the man who was not asked.

Warren Bradley himself is almost not in the paper. No wage. No age in the published pack. No recorded children, no deed, no burial that the encyclopedia bothers to locate. `[CITE NEEDED: any primary record of Warren Bradley beyond later family tradition and the 2015 capsule letter, whose full text is not in this pack.]` That absence is not a reason to drop him. It is the reason to say his name carefully. An enslaved man used as a town’s namesake is not a mascot. He is a person the official century found convenient to mention and inconvenient to document. The courthouse that stored the letter is the same courthouse that stored the rest of the county’s silences.

Edward Allen Warren is the opposite kind of thin: over-documented in the places that print congressmen, under-documented in the town that may wear him. He was born near Eutaw, Alabama, on 2 May 1818, to Robert H. Warren and Lydia A. Minter Warren. He read law on his own, married Mary Elizabeth Warren in October 1838, was admitted to the bar in 1843, and practiced in Clinton, Mississippi. The Mississippi House had him in 1845 and 1846. In 1847 he moved to Camden, opened a practice, and, as a newcomer Democrat, won a seat in the Arkansas House in 1848. He was speaker in 1849. He sat as a judge on the Sixth District circuit. He went to Congress in 1853, served on the Committee on Public Lands, declined to run, watched Albert Rust, and came back for 1857–1859. When the secession convention of 1861 needed a man for Virginia’s proposed border-state meeting at Frankfort, Kentucky, Warren was selected. The meeting was empty. Arkansas was already in. He spent the rest of his life at the law and died in Prescott. None of that biography requires him to have given a town his first name. Friendship with Hugh Bradley is the hinge the Warren entry offers. The friendship is asserted more often than it is footnoted. `[CITE NEEDED: a contemporary letter or deed tying Edward Allen Warren of Camden to the 1840s naming of the Bradley County seat.]`

Both men can be true as traditions. Only one can be the baptism. The county has never held the christening. What it held instead was a sequence of rooms. Court in the captain’s house, 1841. A log courthouse, 1843. Brick with stucco, 1861. Frank W. Gibb’s two-colored pile with a cupola, 1903, the year the large-mill decade was teaching the square a new weight. The 2015 box came out of that third building. A naming dispute stored in a wall is a civic object. So is a cupola that looks down on a town whose water tower says one word.

Incorporation, whichever December you prefer, did not settle the older names. Cabeens remained a memory of a post office. Pennington remained a township. Saline remained the river. The first school, 1847, met in the Methodist church near Cabeens. Josephine Horne opened an academy for young ladies in 1851 at thirty-five dollars for five months — a fee the encyclopedia records because someone wrote it down, which is more than we have for Warren Bradley’s life. Thomas Henry Mathis organized a private school in 1856 and later helped found towns in Texas. The Baptist Centennial Institute came in 1874. The Presbyterian Training School in 1906. A mill town is often accused of inventing its institutions. Warren invented them as a farm seat and then enlarged them when the northern mill men arrived. The name was already on the incorporation paper, disputed, before a circular saw had a payroll large enough to double a census.

The roads into the place are a catalog of other people’s destinations. Chicot Trace. Gaines Landing Road. Fort Towson Road. Washita Road. Warren sat on the way to somewhere else and then, because Marks and Splawn laid forty acres under a courthouse, became a somewhere. Reconstruction hurt more than the nearby engagements at Marks’ Mills, Mount Elba, and Longview. Martial law. A single store in May 1865 for the Federal garrison. Night riders. Governor Powell Clayton sending militia from Conway. The fields went to weeds. The name on the map did not change. Names rarely do the work of repair.

A series that cares about mill-town America has to care about who gets written on the water tower and who does not. The county is Hugh Bradley. The township is Pennington. The town is Warren, and Warren is two men, one of them enslaved, one of them a congressman who never had to live here. The lynching record is the other list of names the square did not put on a sign. In 1887, the Warren entry says, two white men reported as Hamilton and Ludberry were lynched for the alleged murder of brothers. In 1890 and 1892 the Beavers brothers, William and Henry, African American, estimated fourteen and sixteen, were lynched for alleged assaults on white women who survived. In 1903, John Turner, also Black, was lynched for an alleged “attempted assault.” Crane Green, the county entry adds, in 1903. Those names are not a sidebar to a naming essay. They are what a courthouse town does with names when the law is a crowd. Dignity is the only tone that will hold. The 2015 capsule did not mention them. The encyclopedia does. So does this page.

By 1880 the census could count 301 people in Warren. By 1900, 954. By 1910, 2,057. The doubling that the lumber decade is proud of happened under a name that was already old. Southern, Bradley, and Arkansas Lumber did not christen the place. They filled it. Major John C. Bratton, W. H. Wheeler, John T. Ederington, and Benjamin Martin, the Warren entry says, later served as the basis for business. Merchants and Planters Bank in the 1890s. Warren Bank in 1901. Two banks that would not fail in the 1930s. The letterheads were new. The argument on the masthead was not.

Hugh Bradley, Jr., the son who never left the homestead, is the author the 2015 story wants. Goodspeed had already interviewed that kind of memory in 1890: a man on the old place, 200 acres, 150 in cultivation, a veteran of Company D, Ninth Arkansas, married in 1854 to Amelia V. Blankenship. If his letter in the wall says the town is Warren Bradley, then a family said so twice, sixty years apart, once to a subscription biography and once to a box. If Edward Allen Warren’s friends in Camden said otherwise, they said it in the kind of paper that elects speakers of the House. The town used the name either way. Usage is older than proof.

What remains is the word itself, one syllable, on the water tower and the high-school scoreboard and the depot that is no longer a depot. You can stand on the square and hear it spoken as if it had always been simple. It was never simple. Pennington, Cabeens, Saline Settlement — those were descriptions. Warren is a choice that the choosers did not write down in a single hand. The capsule is a later hand insisting. The congressman’s grave is a later silence. Between them the town goes on answering to a name that will not resolve, which is the most honest thing a courthouse town can do with its first word.

The mills would later prefer a single story: production, payroll, the whistle. A later hardwood shop in this county may date a practice from the mill’s first years. The town’s name dates from an argument. Both dates are true. Only one of them required a saw. The other required two men, a river settlement with extra names, and a county willing to live with a caption it cannot finish.
