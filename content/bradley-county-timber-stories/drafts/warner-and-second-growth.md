# Warner and second growth

A man from Cloquet, Minnesota, arrived in Warren in 1939 with orders to shut a mill. W. R. Warner. Bradham writes the sentence eleven years later as if it were the hinge of the county’s second century: Southern’s owners engaged him to come down and close Southern Lumber Company; he saw the possibilities of second-growth timber and persuaded them to let the facility keep operating. The encyclopedia’s county entry, which does not name Warner, gives the other half of the year. A fire at Southern led to repairs and modernization. The mill became, the entry says, one of the nation’s most up to date. Fire, a funeral order, a refusal. The pine that had been a mine became, in one man’s argument, a crop.

Cloquet is not a romantic origin. It is a Great Lakes mill town that had already lived the story Warren was being asked to repeat. The Cloquet–Moose Lake fires of 1918 had run through cutover. The Hinckley fire of 1894 was the older scar. Northern companies had taken the white and Norway pine, left stumps, and watched flame finish the work. By the time Warner is sent south, Cloquet’s district is a lesson the South is only beginning to sit for: if you treat a forest as a last harvest, you get a last harvest, and then you get a ticket home. Second growth is the northern phrase for the trees that came back anyway. In south Arkansas they came back faster. Sandy hills. Long summers. Shortleaf and other southern yellow pines that do not take fifty years to make a log.

Southern Lumber Company was the Weyerhaeuser venture in Warren — Bradham says the only venture the staunchly patriotic Weyerhaeuser ever had in the South — grown from Lindsay and Ainsworth’s 1882 operation, incorporated 1902, principals Frederick E. Weyerhaeuser and E. P. Denkman. By 1907: 100,000 board feet a day, 300 men. It shared the Warren & Ouachita Valley with Arkansas Lumber, Warren to Banks, passengers until 1948. It survived 1928, when Arkansas Lumber cut out 85,000 acres and died. It survived the thirties, when the encyclopedia says lumber demand held and neither Warren bank failed. In 1939 it burned. The owners’ first thought, if Bradham is right, was not rebuilding. It was Warner, and a closing.

Warner’s given names, employer of record, and any 1939 report or minute that records the shutdown order and the reversal.]` Bradham is a county judge writing a family-book sketch, not a forester writing a monograph. The sketch is still the best sentence we have. It has the shape of a hundred southern mill stories from the same decade, when Yale-trained men and company foresters were walking cutover and doing arithmetic. E. W. “Cap” Gates at Crossett had called H. H. Chapman at Yale in 1926 and then hired W. K. Williams. Gifford Pinchot’s old gospel — sustained yield, the forest as a crop — had taken a generation to reach the Gulf Coastal Plain in operational form. Warner’s argument, as Bradham renders it, is that gospel without the school name. The trees were coming back. A mill that burned could be a mill that stayed.

Modernization after a fire is a brutal kind of luck. You do not have to persuade a board to scrap a working plant. The plant is already scrap. The encyclopedia’s “most up to date” is a 1939–40 claim, not a tour of the new machines. `[CITE NEEDED: the 1939 fire date to the day, the acres of plant lost, and the equipment installed in the rebuild.]` What matters for the county is the decision that followed the ashes: not Arkansas Lumber’s decision, not Cloquet’s old decision, but a decision to remain in Warren and cut the second story of the same woods. Bradley Lumber, the hardwood house, was still running. A town of two mills could imagine a future. A town of one mill, or none, would have been a courthouse and a tomato.

Second growth is not wilderness. It is a smaller tree, a different mix, a woods that remembers a railroad grade and a camp well. The 1906 photographs of Arkansas Lumber’s virgin pine — Julius Adams standing in stems that look like columns — are the first growth. The second is the stand that came up in the stumps after 1901, after 1907, after the Shays finished a parcel and moved the well. By 1939 those stems were approaching merchantable size in a climate that hurries pine. Warner did not invent the trees. He noticed them in time to cancel a funeral. Noticing is the whole of industrial forestry when the noticing is done by a man with the authority to keep a boiler.

Potlatch Forests, Inc., of Idaho would buy Southern in 1956, if you follow Bradham and Silva, and Bradley two years later. The encyclopedia’s county entry collapses both purchases into 1958 and calls the buyer a West Coast firm, Potlatch Inc. Wikipedia’s corporate chronology also prefers 1958 for both. The disagreement is a dating quarrel, not a quarrel about what the purchase meant. Bradham, who had already written Warner’s refusal, wrote the meaning: the Potlatch purchases seemed to give some aura of permanency to Warren, where community leaders had thought Southern and Bradley might one day follow Arkansas Lumber and go out of business. But they didn’t, as a matter of fact. The “as a matter of fact” is doing a lot of work. Warner is the first matter of fact. Potlatch is the second. The aura is a feeling leaders had about a letterhead from Idaho. Feelings are not deeds. The deed was 1939, when a Cloquet man declined to obey the first half of cut-out-and-get-out.

Mill-town America likes a savior with a hat. The hat here is Midwestern and unnamed. The series will not dress him beyond Bradham’s clause, or invent a walk through smoking timbers, or a speech to a crew. The fire happened. The order was to shut. The counter-order was to stay. The stay required owners who still had cash after a Depression they had survived because lumber was still needed. Continual need, the encyclopedia’s thirties phrase, becomes, after 1939, a need that can be supplied from trees that were saplings when Arkansas Lumber was dying. The peninsula had been waiting, Balogh says in another connection, with the patience of a crop no one had yet learned to count. Warner learned to count it.

The W&OV’s passenger trains have nine years left when the mill is rebuilt. The depot of 1909, rebuilt 1911 after its own fire, will be listed on the National Register in 1977. Fire and rebuild is a Warren rhyme: bank, depot, YMCA, Southern. The rhyme is not destiny. Arkansas Lumber burned through its woods without needing a plant fire to finish the thought. Southern’s plant fire became an excuse to think again. The difference is a man, a climate, and a decade of professional forestry talk that had finally reached the directors who signed tickets from Cloquet.

A later Warren shop that still works pine and oak lives in the permission Warner’s decade created. Bradley Brand Furniture dates a hardwood practice from 1903, which is Bradley’s hour, not Southern’s. The permission is larger than a brand. If Southern had closed in 1939, the town that Potlatch later bought would have been a smaller town. The county that peaked at 18,097 in 1940 would have started its slide from a steeper stair. Second growth is demography. It is also a smell — kiln-dried pine again, after a year when the smell was wet ash.

What Warner could not do was make permanence. Potlatch would run the Warren operation as a southern yellow-pine fact into a new century, build a new $27 million sawmill in the early 1990s, close a hardwood mill in 2002 and sell it, keep a pine mill, and eventually leave the old aura to whoever next leased the sheds. The Chambers years, the credit-suit closure of 2008, the later remanufacturing names on Martin Street — these are other essays. This one ends earlier, on a decision that still has dirt under it. The second stand was already in the ground. Someone had to say it was enough to keep a boiler.

If you want a still, do not use a hero’s portrait. There is none in the public pack. Use a blackened boiler wall and, behind it, a ridge of twelve- and sixteen-year-old shortleaf that does not yet look like wealth. A man from a burned northern county points. The owners, who had bought a ticket to close a southern county’s mill, buy different equipment instead. The stack goes back up. The W&OV still answers. Bradley’s hardwood mill keeps its own weather on the other side of town. In 1939 the woods were not finished. That is the whole revelation. It was enough to cancel a shutdown, and it was late enough that a county could feel, for the first time since 1928, that the method had a rival.
