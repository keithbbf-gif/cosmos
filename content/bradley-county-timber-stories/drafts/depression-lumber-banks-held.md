# Depression lumber, the banks held

Two bank doors on Main stayed open through the thirties. That is the sentence the encyclopedia wants a reader to take home, and it is not a small sentence. Merchants and Planters, organized in the 1890s. Warren Bank, organized in 1901. The Warren entry says both are still in existence, which is the civic way of saying the Depression, in this county, did not eat the ledgers. Across Arkansas, and across the country, doors closed. These two did not. The reason the county entry gives is lumber. Continual need. The 1930s still wanted boards.

Need is not the same as boom. Arkansas Lumber Company had already clear-cut its 85,000 acres and gone out of business in 1928. The largest of the three 1907 mills — 150,000 board feet a day, 400 men — was a silence on the west side of town before the crash had a name. Southern and Bradley remained. A town that had learned to live on three stacks learned to live on two. The learning is the Depression here, more than a breadline the published record has not yet put in the street. `[CITE NEEDED: Warren relief rolls, CCC camps, or mill short-time notices, 1930–35.]` What the record puts in the street is brick, circa 1927, still new enough to look like a promise, and a Warren Bank building that had burned about 1927 and been replaced by the Neoclassical pile with the eagle, the battlements, the date, the offices of Derby Insurance and Drs. Ripley, Bond, and Hugh Moseley, Jr., upstairs. A bank that burns and rebuilds two years before the nation falls is already practicing a kind of stubbornness.

Judge Bradham, writing in 1951, remembered an explosive growth before the First World War and a steady one after. The census agrees in its blunt columns. County population: 15,970 in 1920; 17,494 in 1930; 18,097 in 1940 — the peak. Warren itself: 2,145; 2,523; 2,516. The town does not swell in the thirties. It holds. The county adds people while the nation sheds them from payrolls. Lumber is the encyclopedia’s explanation. Tomatoes are the quieter one. Bradley County farmers have been raising a pink shipping tomato since the 1920s. Cotton is still a cash crop and will be into the late 1940s. A mixed peninsula — shortleaf on the hills, hardwood on the bottoms, a fruit that will travel — does not starve in one key.

The banks’ survival is not a parable about virtue. It is a fact about deposits that kept coming because the mills kept sawing. Samuel Fullerton’s Bradley Lumber, run on the ground by the sons Robert W., Cornell, and S. Baker, had been, by 1907, one of the world’s largest hardwood dealers at 100,000 board feet and 350 men. Southern, Weyerhaeuser and Denkman, same board-foot figure, 300 men. Those 1907 numbers are not 1932 numbers. The published pack does not give the Depression-year daily cut. `[CITE NEEDED: Bradley and Southern production and employment, 1932–38.]` The encyclopedia will only say the citizens fared as well as most Arkansans, which is a careful phrase, and that lumber’s continual need was a factor. As well as most is not well. It is not collapse.

Carroll Charles Hollensworth of Warren spent nearly two decades in the Arkansas House beginning in 1930 and finished as floor leader after a turn as Speaker. William Shields Goodwin had already represented the Seventh District in Congress from 1911 to 1921. The county’s political class in the Depression is the class that could still afford a train to Little Rock. The mill class is the class that could still afford a Saturday on Main because a two-bank town had not frozen the Saturday. Scrip at the Bradley Store, 215 South Main, weekly settle, is a Depression instrument even when it predates the Depression. A company that pays in paper it will redeem at its own counter is a company that does not entirely trust the street, or does not wish the street to have the last word. In the thirties the street’s last word, in many American towns, was a chain on a bank door. Warren’s street did not get to say it.

The Centennial Magazine of 1936, celebrating the county’s first hundred years, lists the modern comforts as if they were a harvest: good roads, electric lights, natural gas, water, sewerage, automobiles, electric and gas ranges, refrigerators. The list is booster copy. It is also an inventory of what had not been repossessed. Arkansas Lumber had once given the town its first electricity, before the First World War, from a powerhouse beside a log pond. By 1936 the lights have other fathers. The magazine does not linger on the empty Arkansas Lumber office on Sturgis, or on the clubhouse for single men that still stood, or on the acres that had gone to Murphy-McKinney of El Dorado, oil money buying cutover land “for a song,” as a later museum caption would put it. Booster copy leaves the song out. The banks remain in the picture because they are open.

Fire, not bankruptcy, is Southern’s thirties crisis at the end of the decade. In 1939 the Southern Lumber plant burns. The encyclopedia says the repairs and modernization made the mill one of the nation’s most up to date. Bradham’s later sentence is stranger and better: the owners engaged W. R. Warner of Cloquet, Minnesota, to come to Warren and shut Southern down; Warner saw second growth and talked them out of the funeral. A Depression that ends, for one company, in a fire and a man from the cutover North who refuses the cut-out script is a Depression with a sequel. The sequel is not this essay. This essay is the decade when the two remaining mills, and the two remaining banks, and the pink tomato, and the new brick, kept a county seat from becoming a monument.

Mill-town America in the thirties is usually a photograph of a closed gate and a soup kitchen. Warren’s photograph, if the papers are to be believed, is a teller’s window and a lumber stack. The type is not cancelled. Black Warren on East Elm is still Jim Crow Warren. The Pastime balcony is still a balcony. The lynching decade is earlier, but the caste does not take a holiday because deposits hold. Women who had worn slacks under dresses in the First World War are not, in the published record, a 1930s mill story. The floors remain a men’s fact until the next war. What holds is narrower than a legend and wider than a press release: two institutions with vaults, two mills with whistles, a fruit that ships pink, a population that peaks in 1940 at 18,097 and will not see that number again.

The 1940 peak is the hinge. After it, modernization cuts the farm workforce and then the mill workforce. The encyclopedia is plain. Warren’s own count will jump in the late-century tables when annexation and definition change — 2,615 in 1950, then 6,752 in 1960 — but the county slides: 15,987 in 1950, 14,029 in 1960, 12,778 in 1970, 10,545 in 2020. The Depression, locally, is not the slide. The slide is the long after. The thirties are the last decade in which a person could stand on the new brick, hear two whistles, and believe the banks and the woods were a single organism.

A later hardwood shop that dates itself from 1903 is living on the far side of that organism. Bradley Brand Furniture can name oak and gum because Bradley Lumber, in the thirties, did not follow Arkansas Lumber into silence. The banks’ refusal to fail is the civic twin of that refusal. Neither fact is a guarantee. Potlatch will buy the survivors in the 1950s and, much later, sell a hardwood mill that will close again in a credit year. The thirties do not know those names. The thirties know Merchants and Planters, Warren Bank, a rebuilt eagle on a parapet, and the sound of a planer that has not been sold for scrap.

If you want a still, do not use a breadline no one has documented. Use the eagle. Use a teller’s grate at four o’clock on a Thursday in 1933, a mill hand with a weekly envelope, a farmer with tomato money, a clerk who can still open the drawer. Shortleaf going out in boxcars. Hardwood going out as flooring stock. The nation arguing with itself about banks. This county, for once, not arguing. The doors held. The need for lumber was the brace. The brace was not kindness. It was a market that had not yet learned to do without southern yellow pine and mild-textured oak. When the market learned, the doors would have other tests. In the thirties they had this one, and they passed it in the only way a bank can pass: by being open Monday morning.
