# The YMCA, nineteen twenty

The Sanborn map of 1907 puts a one-story roller skating rink on a Warren corner that later generations will call the Y. The ink is ordinary. The town is not. Southern Lumber, Bradley Lumber Company of Arkansas, and Arkansas Lumber are already posting the board-foot numbers of that year — 100,000 and 100,000 and 150,000 a day, three hundred and three hundred fifty and four hundred men — and the farm seat that had 301 people in 1880 has become a mill town of 2,057. A rink is what a mill town builds when the whistle has taught it leisure and the dirt streets have not yet taught it brick. Boys circle. The floor remembers wheels.

By 1912 the same building has a stage and a new name on the map: Rink Theater. The conversion is almost comic in its thrift. Add a proscenium to a skating floor and you have culture. In that year a moving-picture house also sits at the northwest corner of Cedar and Mulberry. By 1920 the movie trade has shifted to 120 Cedar. Warren is learning the American habit of stacking amusements on the same lots, as if recreation were another species of lumber that could be recut. The rink is the first still. The YMCA is the second cut from the same timber.

In 1918 a group of citizens and businessmen called themselves the Council of 22. The National Register narrative for the commercial district is the published sentence: they saw a need for youth and recreational programs with an emphasis on faith. The war is not over when they meet. Registration has already thinned the mill floors. The Warren entry in the encyclopedia records the dress rule the companies invented for the women they hired — a dress to work, slacks on the floor, a dress home — which is a civic fact about gender and a quieter fact about how a mill town treats a shortage as a costume change. The Council of 22 is the other costume change. Twenty-two men decide that the town which can saw 350,000 board feet a day should also have a reading room.

The Warren chapter of the Young Men’s Christian Association received its charter in 1920. The encyclopedia lists the date as one of two things a reader is supposed to know about the town: that it has a YMCA from that year, and that many of its antebellum houses still stand. The pairing is not accidental. A mill town that keeps old houses and founds a Y in the same generation is advertising permanence. The 1920 building, on the rink’s corner, rose 3½ stories with a 2½-story rear wing. Silva’s 2012 downtown script, walking from the Martin House museum, names the program inside: swimming pool, bathing facilities, reading room, gymnasium, and stage. The rink’s floor has become a tank of water. The stage remains. A boy who learned to skate in 1907 could, if he lived, have learned to swim in the same air.

The YMCA as an American type is a northern export, born in London in 1844 and naturalized in mill towns and railroad towns that needed a Protestant answer to the saloon. Warren’s version arrives after the three large mills, after the Warren & Ouachita Valley’s passenger trains have been running to Banks since 1899, after the Bradley Store’s scrip logic is about to take a brick body at 215 South Main. It is not a company Y in the Crossett sense. The published record does not give a mill letterhead on the 1920 charter. `[CITE NEEDED: whether Bradley, Southern, or Arkansas Lumber subscribed the 1920 building, and in what sums.]` What the record gives is a civic committee with a number for a name, and a building that looks like a hotel for the sober.

Main Street in those years is still Vine on older tongues. The 1931 Sanborn will print both. North of the square, Ash runs toward the museum that is not yet a museum. Southeast of Walnut and Ash, the 1912 map labels a half-block of twelve small frame dwellings “Negro Settlement.” East Elm, just north of the tracks, is already assembling the Black-owned row the town will call Catfish Row — restaurant, barbers, boarding house by 1907; bottling works, confectionery, pool hall, dry cleaners, cobbler, grocery by 1920. The YMCA of 1920 sits in the white civic weather of the same decade. The published tour scripts do not give a parallel Black Y for that year. The silence is a fact. A reading room is not a democracy.

The Pastime Theater, planned in 1924 by W. A. Halley at a figure of about $20,000, opened circa 1925 at 110 West Cedar with a Mission parapet, a marquee, a separate rear entrance, and a balcony reserved for Black patrons. The Y and the Pastime are the same town’s two answers to an evening. One answers with a pool and a Bible. One answers with a reel and a rope across the stairs. In 1941 a policeman kicked Ms. O. Z. Jackson because she refused to wait in an alley until white patrons had bought their tickets. There was a protest. The National Register keeps the sentence. The YMCA’s 1920 charter does not cancel that kick. It shares a downtown with it.

The 1920 building burned in 1944. Fire is Warren’s other weather. The Warren Bank had already burned about 1927 and risen again as a Neoclassical pile with an eagle and battlements. The W&OV depot had burned in 1911 and been rebuilt on the same site. Four buildings on the west side of the square would burn in 1980. The Sutherland, later called the Southern Hotel, would burn in 1964. The Y’s fire is one more in that ledger, and it falls in a war year. Fifty-nine county citizens will be counted among the dead of the Second World War. A civic building that had been a rink, then a theater, then a Christian gymnasium, becomes a vacant lot’s argument. The replacement went up on the same corner. The Register says it continues to serve, remodeled many times. Silva, walking in 2012, already has to mention a later Donald W. Reynolds facility whose new cost she gives as four million dollars. The corner keeps the use. The walls do not keep the 1920 brick.

On the night of 3 January 1949 the replacement building became something the founders had not named. DuVal Purkins, writing in the *Eagle Democrat*, records that ambulances ran all night from Griffin-Leggett, Healey & Roth, Drummond, Reubel, and from Monticello, Hamburg, Lake Village, Dermott, McGehee, Pine Bluff, Rison, Fordyce, Camden, El Dorado, Crossett. Doctors and nurses came from Little Rock and the southeastern towns. Before midnight, S. M. Dixon, Arkansas Power & Light, and the U.S. Engineers of Pine Bluff had set emergency current for Hunt Hospital, the Crow Clinic, Frazer Funeral Home, the YMCA building, and the American Legion Hut. The Hut, on Main, dated from the 1920s. The Y and the Legion — two postwar rooms of male civic life — took the broken bodies the stack’s fall had made. By three in the morning Frazer’s held forty dead under the care of eighteen licensed embalmers. The electric clock at Wayne’s Confectionery on the square had stopped at 5:45 p.m. The Y’s lights, when they came back, were not for basketball.

The Y is an afterlife of the rink: mill first, then the rooms a mill town builds so the men will not only drink. That is what a peninsula does with surplus time. It is not a product.

Mill-town America repeats the YMCA as a photograph: brick, a painted triangle, boys in wool, a pool that smells of chlorine and sawdust because the mill is never far. Warren’s particular is the stack of uses on one corner, and the speed of the stack. 1907 rink. 1912 stage. 1918 council. 1920 charter and a 3½-story house of water and books. 1944 fire. A rebuilt hall that, five years later, is an emergency ward while rain falls in sheets and the Bradley powerhouse lies under its own chimney. The type is Protestant recreation. The particular is a map that will not leave the lot alone.

The Council of 22 is the name that most needs a list and does not have one in the tour scripts. Twenty-two is a number that wants surnames — Ederington, Fullerton, Mansfield, Derby, Hurley, Gannaway, Martin — and the published record, as of this writing, does not print them. `[CITE NEEDED: the twenty-two names on the 1918 council, if a minute book or newspaper survives.]` A Ken Burns hour would hold the number and refuse to invent the roll. The county’s first grand jury, in 1841, is a neighbor poem because Bradham wrote the names down. The Council of 22 is a neighbor poem with the names torn off.

What remains on the ground is a habit. The corner still works as a Y. Children still learn to swim in a town that learned, first, to skate, then to watch a stage, then to charter a Christian association because twenty-two men in a war year decided the mill’s sons needed a room that was not a balcony and not a commissary. The 1920 charter is the dated object. The fire of 1944 is the dated absence. Between them a mill town practiced the American faith that a pool can civilize a payroll. It can. It cannot desegregate a theater. It cannot hold a smokestack up in a January wind. It can keep a corner from becoming only a memory of wheels.

If you stand there now, the still is not the Reynolds glass. It is the older idea of a floor. First wood for skates. Then boards for a play. Then water. Then smoke. Then cots. A county that once held court in a captain’s house is entitled to hold recreation in a recycled rink. The mills made the population that needed the room. The Council of 22 named the room. Nineteen twenty is the year the ink dries.
