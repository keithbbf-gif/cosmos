# Northern money, southern trees

They did not come from the Saline. They came from letterheads. Davenport. Moline. Rock Island. Chicago. St. Louis. Cloquet, later, when a mill needed a man to shut it. Bradham’s 1951 sketch still reads like a hotel register from about 1900: Frederick Weyerhaeuser and his associates from the northern Midwest, operating as Weyerhaeuser and Denkmann — Denkmann the brother-in-law; Moses Rittenhouse and his partner from Chicago; Samuel Holmes Fullerton of St. Louis. The encyclopedia’s timber entry adds the rest of that migrating class: Dr. John Wenzel Watzek, Charles Warner Gates, Edward Savage Crossett. South Arkansas, in their decade, was what the Lakes had been in their fathers’: a virgin stand and a railroad that had finally arrived.

Warren was not a blank plat. That is the first distinction, and it must be paid before Crossett is named. Balogh’s type-list is a short, useful thing. Camden and Pine Bluff were river towns before they were timber towns. Fordyce and Warren owed their existence to farms and then to rails. Crossett developed out of virgin pine, first as a sawmill camp, then as a company town. The northern money that came to Bradley County bought into a courthouse seat with a name argument, two future banks, and a memory of Cabeens. The northern money that went to Ashley County invented a town and named it for one of the partners. Comparison is not a twin essay. It is a fence. Warren stays on this side of the fence.

James E. Lindsay of Davenport, Iowa, and Charles Ainsworth of Moline, Illinois, started an operation in 1882 that the later Southern Lumber Company would grow from. Twenty years of Iowa and Illinois practice in someone else’s woods: that is the small northern story that predates the famous names. In 1902 the Weyerhaeuser group founded Southern. The principals, the county encyclopedia says, were Frederick E. Weyerhaeuser and E. P. Denkman. Bradham adds a claim that should be kept as Bradham’s: this was “the only venture the staunchly-patriotic Weyerhaeuser ever had in the south.” By 1969 the same surname would buy Dierks Forests in the Ouachitas. A 1951 sentence is about a generation, not a forever. In that generation, Southern at Warren was the southern exception on a northern map.

Moses Franklin Rittenhouse and John William Embree — John in the timber encyclopedia, James in Bradham; the Chicago pair anyway — established the Arkansas Lumber Company at Warren in 1901. Rex Nelson, writing much later from the same sources, gives March 1901 for the incorporation. Bradham says they engaged Clifford J. Mansfield of St. Louis to establish the plant. Chicago money, St. Louis management, south Arkansas trees: the triangle is the period. Arkansas Lumber would become the largest of the three Warren cuts by 1907, 150,000 board feet a day, 400 men, and the one that most completely lived the old math. Eighty-five thousand acres clear-cut. Closed 1928. Cut-out-and-get-out, practiced at the scale only Chicago could fund. The town that remained after the company left is the proof that Warren was never only a camp. Camps die when the mill moves. County seats have a square.

Samuel H. Fullerton of St. Louis took the third door. September 1901, Nelson says, for Bradley Lumber’s incorporation. He bought a small mill from William H. Wheeler, a local man, and hired Joe L. Reaves, Sr., a Hugh Bradley descendant, to assemble timber. The company name is the county name. That is not a courtesy. It is a St. Louis decision to wear the place. Fullerton’s sons, Robert W. of Cornell and S. Baker of the Midwest, were sent to operate it. Bradley became, the county entry says, one of the world’s largest dealers in hardwood lumber — the mixed-woods identity that a pure-pine camp would not have needed. Northern money, in this case, bought a southern hallway.

The Lakes had been cut. Westward towns still needed boards. That is the encyclopedia’s Gilded Age paragraph, and it is enough motive without a speech. What the northern men brought was not a discovery of trees. The small mills had been cutting neighbors’ orders for a decade. What they brought was the ability to buy in sections, to lay logging steel, to post a daily cut that a farm town had no language for, and to wait out a market from an office that was not on the square. Weyerhaeuser’s fortune was already a Lakes fortune. Rittenhouse and Embree were already Chicago lumber. Fullerton was already St. Louis. Warren offered stumpage and a rail to Dermott. The deal was not equal. It was ordinary for its hour.

Crossett, founded 1899 by Watzek, Gates, and Crossett — the same class, sometimes the same drawing rooms — is the comparison that tempts a writer into a second town. Mention what is needed and come home. Crossett was the company town: houses, store, the later Yale forestry, the kraft and chemical afterlives, the 1940 and 1985 paper-mill strikes that Warren’s published record cannot match. `[CITE NEEDED: union records on the Warren mill floors; Crossett paper strikes are not a substitute.]` Warren was the leaky-fence town. Independent merchants on the square sold what the commissary also sold. Tomato money and mill money sat in the same two banks. The northern owners did not plat the courthouse. They filled the streets around it. That difference is why a walking tour of downtown Warren is a tour of Hurley Hardware and Ederington’s and Wayne’s, not a tour of a single company fence.

Mansfield of St. Louis, Reaves of the county, Warner of Cloquet: the northern money always needed a man who would stay. Capital does not live in a dry kiln. It visits. The visitor who became a resident — Warner talking Southern’s owners out of a shutdown in 1939 — is the hinge between cut-out and second growth. Bradham treats the Potlatch purchases of 1956 and 1958 as an “aura of permanency” after community leaders had watched Arkansas Lumber leave and wondered whether Southern and Bradley would follow. Idaho money replacing Lakes and Chicago and St. Louis money is still northern money. It is a later latitude. The trees were still southern. The second-growth argument was a southern climate’s argument, finally believed by men from Minnesota and Idaho.

The 1907 numbers are the invoice of that first northern decade. Southern: 100,000 a day, 300 men. Bradley: 100,000, 350. Arkansas Lumber: 150,000, 400. Warren’s population doubled between 1900 and 1910 — 954 to 2,057. The county went from 9,651 to 14,518. Those are not moods. They are the census answering a letterhead. A town that had counted 301 the year the railroad arrived now counted mill hands who had not been born in Pennington Township. Northern money moved people as well as pine. Some of the people were Black workers the Sanborn maps would later gather into a “Negro Settlement” of frame houses at Walnut and Ash. Some opened on Catfish Row. The letterheads did not print those names. The town did, in brick, by 1931.

Cut-out-and-get-out is Balogh’s phrase for the method, large and small. Buy virgin parcels. Build a plant. Cut the marketable trees. Ship. Move the plant, or, if you are large, leave the plant and extend the logging road. The distinguishing marks are denuded land and, for towns that are only mills, death. Arkansas Lumber performed the method and left a hole in Warren’s employment without taking the courthouse with it. Southern and Bradley, by surviving the 1930s demand and the 1939 fire, became the other method: stay, modernize, treat the hill as a crop. Northern money was capable of both. The trees did not get a vote. The climate, which grows shortleaf faster here than in the Ozarks, quietly favored the men who stayed.

A later civic hour belongs on this page because it is the end of the same purchase. In 2002 Potlatch sold the mill. Dr. David Chambers and Michelle Chambers bought it. In 2008, amid a credit suit and a housing market that had stopped asking for boards, the plant closed. The *Arkansas Democrat-Gazette* wrote the hardwood-sawmill strain as a state story. That is not a family advertisement. It is the last northern-to-local transfer the large-mill century could arrange, and it ended in the same national weather that had once filled the yards. The trees were still here. The money, again, was not.

What the northern men did not buy was the name of the town. They bought Southern, Bradley, Arkansas Lumber. They bought 85,000 acres and sold them as boards. They bought a short line to Banks, jointly, because Chicago and the Weyerhaeuser group needed the Rock Island as well as the Iron Mountain. They did not buy Cabeens. They did not settle the argument between Warren Bradley and Edward Allen Warren. They did not have to. A letterhead only needs a post office.

The still is the Sutherland register about 1910 — seventy-five rooms, a restaurant, a Western Union, the only commercial elevator in town. St. Louis. Chicago. A man from Cloquet on a later page. A local timber buyer in the lobby because the visitors do not know which ridge holds the oak. Northern money, southern trees. The phrase is not a slogan. It is a lobby. The trees were older. The money was louder. The town, which had been a farm seat, learned to live with both, and then learned, when Arkansas Lumber’s acres were gone, that loud money can leave on a timetable. The square did not leave. The shortleaf, given a decade, came back. That is the comparison with Crossett that matters, and it is a comparison of roots, not of stacks. Warren had roots the letterheads did not plant. The letterheads cut anyway.
