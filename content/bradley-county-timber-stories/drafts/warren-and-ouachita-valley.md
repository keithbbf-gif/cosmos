# The Warren and Ouachita Valley

The depot still sits below the grade. 325 West Cedar. Cream clapboard on the first story, rectangular shingles on the second, dark red trim, a hip roof with shed dormers, twin front doors with sidelights and transoms, a warehouse running back under a gable that covers the docks. It wants to be a foursquare house. Cyrus Sutherland, writing the building for the Society of Architectural Historians, said so. The National Register nomination, prepared in 1975, said the same with more measurements: arched fanlights, a three-sided bay, secondary doors in semicircular arches, a one-story porch on brackets. Built 1909. Burned. Rebuilt on the same site by the end of 1911 for just under six thousand dollars. Listed 3 August 1977. A short-line station that outlived the short line’s name.

The Warren and Ouachita Valley was incorporated in 1899 by the two mills that needed a second door. Arkansas Lumber Company and Southern Lumber Company laid about sixteen miles of track from Warren to Banks, where the Rock Island ran. Silva’s tour script gives the partnership and the passenger dates: 1899 until 1948, when the Rock Island bought the line. The National Register text agrees on the passenger span and adds the older ghost under the ties. On 12 August 1852 the Arkansas General Assembly chartered the Mississippi, Ouachita and Red River, the first railroad chartered in the state, to cross from Ferguson’s Point on the Mississippi to Fulton near Texas. The route was cleared. The roadbed was graded. No usable track, in that company’s hands, was ever laid through this county. Engineering, summer fever, mosquitoes, money. In 1899 a portion of that graded dream was finally put under rails. Fifty years after the charter, a lumber short line finished a piece of Arkansas’s first railroad argument.

They had wanted Camden. The nomination is plain. Difficulty in the marsh stopped the line at Banks. A town named for a river valley that never reached the Ouachita at Camden is already a lesson in naming. The valley is real. The destination is a junction in Bradley County, a community that exists because a grade could not be pushed through the wet. Banks became the place you changed for Memphis and Chicago. Daily passenger service, the architectural historians say, offered those connections. Lumber was the reason. People were the extra.

The Iron Mountain already sat at Warren. 5 August 1880 had made the town a western terminus of another company’s Ouachita Division. By 1907 a combination depot stood near Chestnut and Elm; after the 1917 Missouri Pacific merger, a passenger house with white and colored rooms and a freight house replaced it. Those buildings are gone. The W&OV station is not. Two railroads, two architectures of waiting. The main line’s Jim Crow rooms have vanished with the brick. The short line’s wooden house remains, which is not innocence. It is survival. A frame depot that looks like a dwelling can be loved into a listing. A segregated brick pair can be demolished without a tour.

Bradley Lumber, the third mill, built its own steel — the Warren, Johnsville and Saline River, later the Warren and Saline River — toward Hermitage and a Rock Island connection of its own. American-Rails and the later operating history put that road at about fifteen miles, logs in, finished cars out. The W&OV was the other two companies’ answer to the same geometry. Three mills, two short lines, one Iron Mountain, one Rock Island at Banks. Warren in the mill decade was not a town on a railroad. It was a town that had become a railroad argument. Cars were classified by company as well as by species.

The depot’s upstairs held offices. The waiting room, by the 1975 interview with resident manager L. C. Hedger, held an arts and crafts shop. Two fireproof vaults on the ground floor held records dating to 1899. The nomination lists those vaults the way another listing would list a mural. Paper from the first year of the company, still in the building that was rebuilt after the fire. `[CITE NEEDED: present location of the 1899–1948 W&OV vault records, if they left Cedar Street.]` A short line that keeps its first year in a vault is a short line that knew it was also a memory. Hedger was still running trains from the same address when the nomination went to Washington. Commercial service had outlived passengers. The Rock Island’s name was on the upstairs door. The house was still the house.

1909 and 1911 are the years the Register freezes. Construction, then fire, then a rebuild cheap enough to list as a figure. Just under $6,000 is not a grand union station. It is a lumber town replacing a wooden tool because the tool still earned. The cream and dark red, the shingled second story, the domestic eaves: someone wanted the station to look as if a superintendent could live in it. Many did, in other towns, above the waiting room. Whether anyone slept at 325 West Cedar the pack does not say. The building’s manners say the thought occurred.

Passenger service to 1948 is a civic span. Children who saw the first trains could have ridden the last. Two world wars, a doubled town, a depression that did not break the banks, a 1939 mill fire, a 1949 tornado the short line’s calendar just missed. The Rock Island purchase ended the passenger habit and not the freight. Silva notes that the Warren and Saline River later bought two miles of W&OV track through town (1978) and sold in 2010 to a McGehee company. Potlatch, Sutherland writes, restored the depot around 1970 for that other short line. The house has been willing to change railroad surnames. It has not been willing to leave Cedar Street.

Banks is the other still. A junction in the western county, pine and wet weather, the Rock Island’s main-line impatience. Ingalls, first Crowtown, took a Kansas senator’s name at a railroad’s insistence. Hermitage sat in the middle of the county as a farm town. The W&OV did not invent those places. It tied two of them to Warren’s mills and let the third remain a name on a road. A sixteen-mile railroad is a local fact that national systems use. The Rock Island wanted the lumber. The lumber wanted the Rock Island. Banks is the handshake. The marsh that stopped Camden is the handshake’s limit.

The 1852 grade under the 1899 ties is the kind of buried line a Ken Burns hour would hold on. Arkansas’s first chartered railroad, a Camden convention, Lloyd Tilghman’s air-line through Warren, a groundbreaking that did not produce a train. Reconstruction hopes of reaching Warren by 1872. Failure. Gould’s later main line coming in from Dermott instead. Then, at the century’s end, two lumber companies picking up a piece of the old survey because a marsh would let them go that far and no farther. The W&OV is not a failed trans-Arkansas road. It is a successful sixteen-mile admission. The valley in the name is an ambition. The timetable is a mill.

Race, again, is a door. The main-line passenger depot’s paired waiting rooms are in Silva. The W&OV nomination does not linger on who sat where in the wooden house. A short line that carried mill hands and traveling men in the Jim Crow years did not escape the years. `[CITE NEEDED: segregated waiting arrangements, if any, in the W&OV depot.]` Catfish Row sat a few blocks toward the Iron Mountain tracks, Black-owned, thickening into brick by 1931. Two stations, two kinds of waiting, one town. The house on Cedar is the one a preservation program could photograph as architecture. Architecture is not the whole of a depot.

What the line carried, besides people, was the 100,000-and-150,000-foot days of Southern and Arkansas Lumber. Logs in from the woods, boards out to Banks, connections toward Chicago — which is to say, toward Rittenhouse and Embree’s home paper — and toward Memphis. Bradley’s hardwood used the other short line and the Iron Mountain. The W&OV is therefore not the whole of Warren’s steel. It is the pair-of-mills steel. When Arkansas Lumber cut out in 1928, the partnership’s original logic lost a partner. Southern kept the road alive long enough for the Rock Island to buy the name in 1948. A short line can survive a company death if another company still needs the junction.

Mill-town America is made of tap lines. The particular here is the house that does not look like a tap line. It looks like a dwelling that learned to issue tickets. Wide eaves. Fanlights. A porch. You could miss the warehouse if you came at it from Cedar and thought of a doctor’s place. Dr. Martin’s house, the oldest residence, is the other wooden civic object, Greek Revival, now a museum. The depot is the mill decade’s answer to that older wood: still a house, already a machine. Both listed. Both still in town. The captain’s house, where court first sat, is the absence between them.

Leave the still on the platform side, if the grade still lets you stand there. Cream and red. A hip roof. The idea of a train to Banks that no longer takes your ticket. The vaults that once held 1899. The National Register plaque that holds 1977. The Warren and Ouachita Valley never reached the Ouachita at the city it aimed for. It reached a junction and a listing and a wooden building that refused, after a fire, to leave. Sixteen miles. Forty-nine years of passengers. A marsh. A first-charter ghost. Two mills’ money. One town’s remaining depot. That is enough steel for a county that already had a main line and still needed a door the Rock Island would open.
