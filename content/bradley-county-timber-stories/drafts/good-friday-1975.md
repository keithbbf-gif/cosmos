# Good Friday, 1975

The clock on the square had already learned 5:45. That was a Monday, 3 January 1949, Wayne’s Confectionery, an electric hand that stopped when the first storm took the mill section. Twenty-six years later the second storm did not need a confectionery clock. It had a liturgical calendar. Good Friday, 28 March 1975. The *Eagle Democrat* put the hour at 7:55 in the evening. The *Arkansas Gazette* of 30 March said 7:50. The difference is five minutes and a typesetter. The path was not in dispute. Farmville first, southwest of town, then the same corridor the 1949 wind had used, into the heart of Warren.

Spring, the *Gazette* wrote, is usually a gentle friend to this Saline River valley town famous for its pink tomatoes. That Friday it was not. The paper called the night a hellish nightmare and used the word ironically for the route: the twister selected basically the same route as the 1949 tornado that killed fifty-five. People who had lived through the first one were still in town. Mayor John Frazer, thirty-six, remembered being ten and riding a bicycle through the 1949 wreckage. A retired Potlatch man named Caplinger told the *Eagle* the Friday-night damage seemed about the same. The death counts were not. 1949 took fifty-five or fifty-six, depending on the list. 1975 took seven.

Farmville was the first room. Houses went down. Three people died there. Large trees blocked the roads into Warren. The injured had trouble reaching the hospital because the roads were the hospital’s problem before the hospital was. Danny Richard Boyd, twenty-two, a student at the University of Arkansas at Monticello, husband of Barbara Ross Boyd, member of First Baptist. Marilyn Jeanette Robertson, thirty, his aunt. Vertie Lou Stoddard, seventy-three, their companion. The *Eagle* set those three in the Farmville yards. The other four died in Warren.

Ellis Clanton, sixty-one, was at work at Potlatch. The *Gazette*’s typesetting called the plant the Polach Corporation and said the huge structure was virtually destroyed. The name is Potlatch. The death is Clanton’s. Dorothy Fay McKinstry, forty-one, of Wilmar, and John Albert Frey, seventy-five, a retired carpenter, member of Westside Baptist, died at their homes in the southwest section. Gentenval Morgan, eighty-five — the papers also spell Genteval — was hurt in the west end and died the next day. The Storm Prediction Center later counted fifty-one injuries. Local papers said more than sixty. The series does not reconcile the ledgers by invention. It names the dead the papers named, and it stops.

A quarter of the town was damaged or destroyed. The *Eagle*’s Saturday tally, gathered after Chief Tommy Dunaway asked breakfast eaters at Dave’s café on East Cypress, at 4:30 in the morning, to form search squads: 151 homes totally destroyed, 238 damaged. Cost, over ten million dollars, probably much more. Frame houses in the southwest were gone. A pool hall left one table on a slab. Sligh Egg Company went down; green styrofoam cartons littered the east side of town, outside the path, a kind of bright trash the wind used as a signature. The Warren hospital took a hard blow. A nurse, Mrs. Shelton Clark, told the papers the cardiac wing virtually exploded in the wind. Industrial buildings, commercial buildings, the mill again. 1949 had crumpled the Bradley powerhouse and dropped the stack across the source of power for more than a thousand workmen. 1975 found Potlatch on the same ground and treated it as if the company name had not changed.

The path was not a tracing. The *Eagle* said the Friday night wind touched down with ferocity at Farmville, moved west of the 1949 track, ripped into the Potlatch plant, then perhaps a hundred yards north of the old wind, doing major damage on the Wheeler side — the paper still said Bob Fullerton’s — on to Myrtle, Powers, and then up and through. Same town. Same mill neighborhood. A small northing. People who had rebuilt after 1949 rebuilt in the corridor the second storm already knew.

January 3, 1949, remains the louder American number. F4, later estimates; fifty-five dead, the deadliest January outbreak piece in the state’s later summaries; 120 homes destroyed, 72 major, 150 minor in the 1949 incomplete check; I-beams carried three-quarters of a mile; a clock on the square; Mrs. J. E. Stewart, wife of the Bradley Store manager, killed by a timber on South Main across from the Ederington house. Choirs taking turns because the funerals would not fit a single day. More than a thousand mill employees displaced when the powerhouse went. That Monday is its own essay. This one is the Friday that rhymed with it and killed fewer people because, the survivors said without comfort, warning and building had changed, and because luck is not a method.

Good Friday is a church word. Warren had churches enough to use it. First Baptist buried Boyd on Monday afternoon, 31 March, James Walker and Forrest Bynum officiating, Oakland Cemetery, Frazer’s Funeral Home. Frey went that same Monday from Frazer’s Chapel of the Chimes, Paul Huskey and Jimmy Howard, Freehouse Cemetery at New Edinburg. The other names had their hours. The *Eagle* printed the obituaries the way a county paper prints them: born, parents, church, survivors, pallbearers. That is the dignity available. It is enough. A mill-town disaster essay that turned the seven into weather poetry would be the wrong essay.

Representative Johnny Lipton, Mayor Frazer, assistant Marvin Parnell, Chief Dunaway: the civic list stayed up all night. Families at daybreak loaded pickup trucks and station wagons with what could be salvaged. The National Guard and the usual neighboring towns did what neighboring towns do. None of that is unique to Warren. The unique fact is the double path. Two violent storms, twenty-six years apart, chose the mill houses and the mill itself as if the town had only one corridor worth ruining. Farmville to the southwest section to the plant to Myrtle. Tomato country and timber country in the same wreckage. The *Gazette*’s opening sentence already knew the dual identity. Famous for pink tomatoes. Killed on the mill’s street.

Potlatch in 1975 was still the large employer. Southern had become Potlatch in 1956; Bradley in 1958. The hardwood and pine floors that later shops would inherit as species knowledge were, that Friday, a plant that could kill a man who had come in to work. Clanton’s death is a labor death and a weather death at once. The time clock does not record the distinction. The paper does, by placing him at the plant and the others at houses and at Farmville.

Sixty-two injured, the Warren encyclopedia entry says; seven dead. County peak population had already passed — 18,097 in 1940, 12,778 by 1970. Warren itself would peak in 1980 at 7,646, after this storm, a number that surprises anyone who thinks a tornado ends a town. It does not. It subtracts houses and adds a second date that must be taught to children who will otherwise only learn 1949. The Pink Tomato Festival was nineteen years old that June. The All Tomato Luncheon had already outgrown the YMCA guest list. The festival went on. The dual identity went on. The corridor remained.

A county that could put a fruit and a vegetable into one statute could also put two tornadoes into one street and still open the square in June. That is not a poster word. It is a calendar that does not ask permission of the wind.

If this were a film, the camera would not linger on the styrofoam cartons, though they are the kind of detail a documentary loves. It would hold on the hospital wing, or on the one pool table, or on Frazer at ten on a bicycle in 1949 and Frazer at thirty-six in a mayor’s car in 1975. Two ages. One path. The voice-over would refuse to say the town was tested and found strong. The town was hit. Seven people died. The mill was hit again. The papers disagreed by five minutes and agreed on the route.

What remains is a habit of looking southwest when the radio talks. Farmville is not a metaphor. It is a community that lost three people before Warren lost four. The county beyond the square paid first. Then the plant. Then the cardiac wing. Then the Saturday search from a café on East Cypress. Dave’s is not on the National Register. It is in the *Eagle* of that week, which is the better listing for a breakfast that became a roster of the ruined.

A later hardwood shop in this county does not owe this Friday a product line. It owes the county the memory that the same species, the same mill ground, and the same streets have been entered by weather as well as by capital. 1949 took the stack. 1975 took the renamed plant. The wood, when it grew back, did not know either date. The people did.

The still is a Good Friday kitchen at 7:50 or 7:55, a radio, a sky that had already done this, and a town that had already learned the sound. The clock on the square did not need to stop a second time. Everyone who had been there in 1949 already knew what time it was.
