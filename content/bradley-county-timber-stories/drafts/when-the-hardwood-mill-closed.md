# When the hardwood mill closed

Credit papers closed a hardwood mill that a tornado had not. The Bradley plant had lost a powerhouse in 1949 and a renamed roof in 1975 and had still cut oak. In 2008 the gate closed on a lawsuit and a housing market. Nancy Cole, writing in the *Arkansas Democrat-Gazette* on 30 November of that year, put Warren in a statewide paragraph about hardwood sawmills feeling the strain as new-home demand collapsed. Also shuttered since January, she wrote, was Bradley Lumber Company in Warren. The mill, which opened in 1903, had been purchased six years earlier from Potlatch by Dr. David Chambers and Michelle Chambers. Last month, Webster Business Credit Corporation of New York had sued in the Western District of Arkansas, seeking more than $2.88 million. Chambers had counterclaimed that Webster cut off a $6 million line at a crucial moment. The Arkansas Development Finance Authority, which had loaned Bradley Lumber $4.4 million between 2002 and 2006, had intervened. Those are the public numbers. They are enough. This is civic history, not a prospectus.

The housing slump is the weather of the year. Orders sagged. Foreclosures accelerated. Home values tumbled. Oak that had left Warren for furniture, flooring, pallets, and ties found fewer houses to enter. Potlatch had already said, in 2002, that hardwood logs were scarce and dear. Chambers had bought the half of the business the corporation no longer wanted. Six years is not a century. It is long enough for a town to believe the whistle might stay, and short enough for a credit committee in New York to decide it would not.

The later Eighth Circuit opinion in *Bradley Timberland Resources v. Bradley Lumber Company*, 712 F.3d 401 (2013), walks the same file without romance. Chambers owned companies in the southern Arkansas lumber trade: president and 90 percent of Bradley Lumber, which milled oak and pine; chairman and 70 percent of Bradley Timberland, which had held about twenty-five square miles of woodland. In October 2006 he sought a revolving line from Webster. Webster agreed to $6 million, secured in part by the timberland. In the fall of 2007 the bank grew concerned about liquidity. The timberland company later alleged that Webster demanded a recapitalization by selling woodland, and that the land sold at a loss of $700 to $800 an acre. Webster did not renew the revolver after the sale. In August 2008 it wrote that financing was permanently discontinued. The next month the mill stopped paying. In October, Webster sued. After three years a district court entered judgment for the bank of about $2 million, in December 2011. The 2013 appeal is a limitations fight, not a mill story. The mill story ended when the line ended.

ADFA’s intervention is the state in the room. Between 2002 and 2006 the authority put $4.4 million into the plant. That is public money with a public theory: a hardwood mill in a county that had already watched Potlatch choose pine was worth a development bet. The bet sat in a federal file beside a New York credit corporation. When the paper uses those names it is describing a civic hope that had to speak the language of liens. It is not describing a triumph. The mill closed anyway.

January 2008, in Cole’s sentence, is the shutter month. August 2008 is Webster’s letter. October is the suit. November is the statewide feature. The sequence is a calendar, not a thriller. Workers who had struck in 2002, gone silent two days early, come back under a local owner, and run oak into a falling housing market, learned a second closure in the same decade. The 1949 paper said more than a thousand workmen lost their power when the stack fell. The 2008 paper does not give a head count for the Chambers mill. `[CITE NEEDED: Warren paper’s employment figure at the 2008 close.]` The series will not invent one. Absence of a number is not absence of people. Sawyers, filers, kiln men, lift operators, office clerks: the jobs are the names we have.

What closed was not the county’s entire timber trade. Potlatch pine continued. Robbins Flooring and chip mills were still in Silva’s 2012 present. The medical center was probably the largest employer. Tomato packing still ran in season at Hermitage. The hardwood mill’s death is a specific death. Oak for furniture stock, the very species a 1940s postcard had already listed with pine millwork, stopped leaving that particular gate. A town can lose a specialty and keep a woods. Bradley County did.

Mill-town America has a literature of last shifts. A whistle, a line of cars, a union hall, a camera. Warren’s 2008 close is quieter in the public pack. No recovered *Eagle Democrat* extra has been quoted here with a last-board photograph. `[CITE NEEDED: local last-day coverage, if any, beyond the *Democrat-Gazette* statewide frame.]` What we have is a Little Rock byline, a docket, and an appeals court later cleaning a fraud clock. That thinness is itself a fact. 1949 was a tragic record on the square. 1975 was a Good Friday extra. 2008 was a business-page paragraph. The mill had become, to the rest of the state, one more hardwood plant in a slump. To Warren it was the Bradley name on a gate that had said Fullerton, then Potlatch, then Chambers.

Do not turn the loans into a morality play. ADFA is not a villain for lending and not a savior for intervening. Webster is not a northern conspiracy; it is a credit corporation that did what credit corporations do when they dislike a borrower’s liquidity. Chambers is not a failed hero and not a cautionary advertisement. He is a physician who said, in the same 2008 piece, that he had put a life savings and a retirement into jobs and lost millions. The quote belongs to the purchase essay and to this one, because the loss is the close. Civic motive does not set aside a judgment. It keeps the close from being narrated as if only interest rates lived here.

The courthouse that Gibb built in 1903 was still on the square when the mill of that same year stopped. Cupola, rusticated brick, unequal towers. The depot of 1909/1911 was still below the grade. The Martin House had been a museum since 1987. Brick streets still ran in their 1927 bond. The Pastime’s marquee still named a pastime. None of those objects closed in 2008. They are what a town keeps when a specialty wage leaves. After the hardwood mill closed, Warren was still a county seat. It was less a hardwood town.

A later shop in mill space — Bradley Brand Furniture, leasing on the Robbins-Sykes / Pennington Road geography — is not a reopening of Bradley Lumber Company. It is a smaller afterlife of the same species, and the 2008 close is not a prologue to a catalog. It is an ending with a docket number.

Compare the three closings the county already knew. Arkansas Lumber in 1928: cut-out-and-get-out, 85,000 acres, a Chicago letterhead finished with the trees. Southern in 1939: a fire, a man from Minnesota sent to shut it, a decision to stay for second growth, then Potlatch. Bradley hardwood in 2002: a corporation’s raw-material chart, then a local buy, then 2008. The first close was exhaustion. The second was almost a close and became a modernization. The third was a market and a line of credit. All three changed the sound of the town. Only 2008 happened after the hospital had already begun to out-hire the stack.

If this were a film, the camera would not pan a mahogany boardroom. It would hold on a locked pedestrian gate, a stack of unused stickers, a parking lot with fewer trucks, and, across town, the pine mill’s kiln vapor, which had not been asked to stop. The voice-over would read Cole’s paragraph once and not again. Numbers: $2.88 million sought, $6 million line, $4.4 million public, about $2 million later judged, twenty-five square miles sold, $700 to $800 an acre alleged. Then it would leave the money and stay with the gate. The money is how the gate closed. The gate is what the town saw.

What remains after a hardwood close is a vocabulary. People still say mill when they mean the pine side, or the old Bradley yard, or the flooring plant, or the place a relative used to work. The word outlives the shift. County conversation in 2009, 2012, 2020 still had to decide whether “the mill” was dead or only changed clothes. Silva’s tour said Potlatch was still logging, not in great shape, flooring and chips still running, hospital probably largest. That is the sentence after the close. Not silence. Reassignment.

The still is January 2008, a gate, no extra edition we can yet quote, and a courthouse from the mill’s first year still doing county business as if oak were not a party in a Western District file. When the hardwood mill closed, the county did not. It filed the loss with the rest of its losses — 1928, 1949, 1975, 2002 — and went on counting tomatoes, pine, and hospital badges. The docket is in the record. The jobs are not named. Both facts are the history.
