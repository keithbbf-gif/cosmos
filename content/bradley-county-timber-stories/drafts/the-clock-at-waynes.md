# The clock at Wayne's

On the court square, at Wayne’s Confectionery, the electric clock stopped at 5:45 p.m. Monday, 3 January 1949. DuVal Purkins wrote that sentence for the *Eagle Democrat* while the mud was still wet, and there is no better first still. Life is close to death and death is close to life, he said, and then he refused to pretty the rest. About the time the clock stopped, Mrs. J. E. Stewart, wife of the manager of the Bradley Store, was driving from her home to pick up her husband at the end of the day’s work. Her automobile had reached the curb across from the Louis Ederington house, 326 South Main. A friend found her unconscious from the blow of a timber hurled from the air. About 1:00 a.m. on the 4th she died in a Monticello hospital.

The storm was a late-afternoon tornado on a January day warm enough to be wrong. It came up from the Farmville Road beyond the W. C. Hargis place, ran north perhaps a mile, then turned northeasterly across the southeast mill section and out beyond the Louis Wilson Ederington home two miles east of town. At points, Purkins wrote, the path was three-quarters of a mile wide; at others, not over a quarter. It zigzagged. It toppled a tomb and a cedar on the Ederington lot near the center of Oakland Cemetery, well off the main wake. It destroyed homes and stores on South Main and skipped the C. K. Adams house, breaking one window. On all sides of that house, a piled mass. The National Weather Service later compilations call the Warren storm an F4 in the outbreak of 3 January 1949. The local paper, in the first week, was too busy counting to rate a scale.

Wayne’s, on Silva’s later walking tour, is the Sandwich Shop building on the south side of the square: Bailey’s Drug first, Dr. Herring’s clinic upstairs, then Frank’s restaurant and pool hall, then Wayne’s restaurant and pool hall, Wayne’s Sporting Goods on the upper floor, cast-iron columns at the walk. Purkins’s word is confectionery. The clock is electric. When the mill’s powerhouse went down, the square’s current went with it. 5:45 is therefore not only a time of death. It is a time of a town’s electricity ending in a single gesture, the hands frozen the way a kiln is frozen when the steam stops.

Around the powerhouse at the center of the Bradley Lumber Company’s plant — the county’s largest industry, Purkins called it — there was a vermicular scene of ruin. The mighty smokestack had crumpled and fallen across that which had been the source of power to supply labor for more than a thousand workmen. The city and its suburban area were in complete darkness. Water in the Bradley section was cut off. Telephone service for the south part of the city ended. Rain was falling in sheets. Bewilderment, agony, despair — the paper’s words, not a later documentary’s. The encyclopedia will say the destruction of the Bradley mill displaced 1,000 employees, and that the storm killed fifty-five and injured more than 250. Purkins, writing as the week closed, said the death toll reached 54 when William F. West, 81, died on 9 January, and that the list was then accounted for. The ARGenWeb compilation of that first list still prints 54 names through 13 January. Later memory and the Warren encyclopedia entry prefer 55. Some outbreak summaries say 55–56. The paper, on its own deadline, said the exact number will never be known, and that five hundred or more were wounded, 102 placed in hospitals through the night. When the counts disagree, the disagreement stays in the sentence.

An incomplete check, Purkins reported: 120 homes destroyed, 72 major damage, 150 minor. More than two hundred blown away, wrecked, or substantially damaged. In the struck area, at least 1,500 citizens had lived. By 3:00 a.m. Frazer’s Funeral Home held 40 dead under the care of 18 licensed embalmers. The Arkansas National Guard and a large number of State Police had the streets. Ambulances from Little Rock — Griffin-Leggett, Healey & Roth, R. F. Drummond, P. H. Reubel — and from Monticello, Hamburg, Lake Village, Dermott, McGehee, Pine Bluff, Rison, Fordyce, Camden, El Dorado, Crossett. Doctors and nurses from Little Rock and southeastern Arkansas. Before midnight, S. M. Dixon, Arkansas Power & Light, and the U.S. Engineers of Pine Bluff had emergency current on Hunt Hospital, the Crow Clinic, Frazer’s, the YMCA building, and the American Legion Hut. The Y that had burned in 1944 and been replaced was a ward. The Hut on Main was a ward. Sirens all night.

No victim is named beyond Purkins’s record. The record, in Purkins’s first walk, names the dead where he is standing. On South Myrtle, near the smashed garage of the Laurence Hurley house and the roofless Manning Taylor house: Mr. and Mrs. Clint Eason, Mrs. Mary Bartram, Mrs. Blanche Enos, Sam Baker and Mrs. Baker. Further northeast: Mrs. Josephine Harding, Mr. and Mrs. H. E. Green, Mrs. Jesse Hamilton. Mrs. J. E. Stewart on South Main, as above. William F. West on the 9th. The compiled list of 54 adds, among others, the Derryberrys — Mrs. R. C., Richard, Carolyn Rose — the Hendersons, the Johns children Betty Jo and Shirley Ann, the Lovells, the McKinneys, the Moores, the Reeps, Melvin White and Mrs. Melvin White. Entire families, Purkins said, in some of the southwest houses. He listed destroyed homes there without making a second death roll out of the architecture: Ruth Byrne, Fay Clemons, J. B. Hartsell, the Atkins house, Horace Moore, H. A. Hattaway, E. T. Johnson, Fred Williams, Melvin White, Cyril Derryberry, Lammon Turner, Rufus Sanders, Jim Sanders, Cecil Sinks, J. A. Frey, R. B. Robbins, Mrs. Henry Peirson, Mrs. Frank Boykin, J. J. Johnson. The stores of J. B. Hartsell and Robbins Grocery were leveled. A list of houses is not a list of the dead. The distinction is the ethic.

Steel traveled. In Edwin Ives’s front yard — Ives, superintendent of the Bradley, 610 South Main — a rumpled I-beam that would weigh a ton. No one killed at that place. Over R. W. Fullerton’s house, a steel H-beam, more than a ton, into the back yard; the house Purkins called palatial was not damaged. Out of the wake, near B. S. Hargis, a panorama of the path. A Frank Pumphrey bus wrapped around a tree as if climbing, where Max Anderson’s auto body works had been. V. O. Buck, trying to close a door on Main, a leg injury that would take months. The O. W. Petway house lifted from its blocks and set down. A piece of steel through the west window of D. C. Harris’s dining room. The 13 January paper will add the bale of cotton blown in from the southwest, plastered on a mill-yard fence like a million down feathers; a child’s toy left beside Skeeter Hughes’s car when the garage walked away; a bed’s headboard still locked to vanished rails on Gannaway Street.

The commercial square, the Register later notes, escaped most of the path. Wayne’s clock is therefore a witness at the edge. The hands stopped because the current died, not because the marquee of the Pastime peeled off. Gibb’s courthouse kept its cupola. The brick streets of 1927 held. The mill section, the southwest houses, Powers Street, East Central, the Monticello highway — those were the rooms the wind took. Seventy-five state convicts, Purkins wrote on the Saturday after, were removing debris under guards with rifles. The image is southern and specific. Reconstruction is not the rhyme. The rhyme is a January cleanup that required the state’s prisoners because the town’s men were burying the town’s men.

The Warren entry says that in one day the funerals were so numerous that choirs took turns singing as the coffins rolled down church aisles. That is the encyclopedia’s sentence, and it belongs with the clock. 5:45 is the civil time. The aisles are the religious time. Between them Mrs. Stewart’s timber, the stack’s fall, the forty bodies at Frazer’s by three, the fifty-four or fifty-five, the thousand off a payroll. On 28 March 1975, Good Friday, another tornado will take the same path from Farmville through the city and kill seven. The second storm is a cruel proof that the first had a geography, not a grudge.

The mill, on this Monday, is a powerhouse under a fallen stack. The furniture stock that had been wartime flooring in the 1944 ads is, tonight, splinter. The afterlife comes later. Tonight the still is a confectionery clock and a woman who was only going to pick up a man at a store that took scrip.

On the square at a winter dusk, the still is the hands. 5:45. Wayne’s. Electric. The mills have made the current and the current has failed in the mills’ own weather. Purkins wanted the record to be poor and careful. Language is inadequate, he said, and then he used language anyway: names, curbs, a timber in the air, a stack, a clock. His list is not improved by guessing. The clock is enough. It stopped. The rest of Warren, in the rain, kept the hour by siren.
