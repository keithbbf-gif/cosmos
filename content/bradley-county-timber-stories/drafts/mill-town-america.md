# Mill-town America

Crossett was invented as a camp and then a company town. Bogalusa was a mill that made a city say its name. Fordyce and Warren, Balogh notes, already owed their existence to farms and then to rails. Camden and Pine Bluff were river towns before they were timber towns. The type is American and late: a whistle, a commissary, a boarding row, a courthouse that learns to share the skyline with a stack. Warren is that type. Warren is also a parlor in 1841, a dual name, a pink tomato written into the statutes, a wooden depot below the grade, and a hospital that out-hired the mill. The particular has to be paid before the type is allowed to speak. This series has been the payment. The type may now sit down, briefly, and then leave the room.

The particular, once more, without a recap’s grammar. A peninsula between the Saline and the Ouachita. Captain Hugh Bradley up the Red, Long Prairie, then the Saline in 1825. County cut from Union on 18 December 1840, named for him. Court in his house, 5 April and 26 April 1841. Marks thirty acres, Splawn ten. Warren Bradley, enslaved, in a 2015 capsule; Edward Allen Warren of Camden in the next sentence. Incorporated 26 December 1850, or 1851 if you hold Silva. Rail on 5 August 1880. Small mills, then Carpenter, then Wheeler, then Fullerton and Reaves, then Southern and Arkansas Lumber. 301 people in 1880, 954 in 1900, 2,057 in 1910. Three mills, 350,000 board feet, a thousand jobs, 1907. Scrip, a YMCA, a Pastime balcony, Catfish Row, brick about 1927. Women in slacks, 1918. Banks that did not fail. A clock at 5:45, 3 January 1949. Fifty-five dead, a thousand displaced. Potlatch 1956 and 1958. A festival, 1956. A fruit that is also a vegetable, 1987. Good Friday, 1975, seven dead, same path. A physician’s bill of sale, 2002. A hardwood gate, 2008. A medical center as the likely largest employer, 2012. County peak 18,097 in 1940; 10,545 in 2020. Those are not a list for a brochure. They are the years a type would skip in order to say “mill town” faster.

What the type gets right is the sequence. Farm hamlet. Railhead. Large capital from a wetter, colder forest that had already been cut — Great Lakes money, St. Louis money, Chicago money, Cloquet, Idaho. A short line to a junction. A store that takes company paper. A population spike. A cut-out or a second-growth argument. A corporate sale. A smaller afterlife. Warren hits every mark. Arkansas Lumber is the cut-out, 85,000 acres, 1928. Southern is the second-growth argument, Warner, 1939. Potlatch is the corporate sale. Robbins and chips and a later bench are the smaller afterlife. If you need Warren to be mill-town America, you may stop there. You would be accurate and unfinished.

What the type misses is the older civic layer. This was already a seat of justice for a territory of counties not yet cut away — Ashley, Calhoun, Cleveland, Dallas, Drew walking off with pieces. The mills did not found the square. They filled the streets around it. Gibb’s 1903 courthouse and the large-mill decade are twins. They are not the same father. The father is a veteran who stopped moving near the river and let strangers use his rooms. A company town cannot say that. A camp that became Crossett cannot say that. Warren can. The type, applied without that sentence, turns a county seat into a plant with a government annex. The annex was here first.

The type also likes a single race story or none. Warren’s other street is in the maps. Negro Settlement on the Sanborn. Catfish Row. A balcony door. Lynching dates in the encyclopedia, including boys. A 1941 protest and a policeman’s kick. Tomato crews later, a Hermitage census that is partly Hispanic. The mill town as a white postcard is a Gannaway card. The mill town as it was is the card plus the map label plus the balcony plus the names the time clock did not keep. Mill-town America, if the phrase is going to be any use at the end of a series, has to mean that whole street, not the booster half.

Dual identity is the local revision of the type. Other mill towns have a second crop. Few have a legislature declaring the second crop both fruit and vegetable, and a festival as old as the West Coast owner. Pine and pink are not a slogan in this last hour. They are the reason the same two banks could hold two kinds of money, and the reason a June weekend photographs better than a dry kiln. After the big mill, the fruit did more of the remembering than the stack. The hospital did more of the hiring. That is not unique in America. The particular is the color of the fruit and the wooden depot that did not get torn down.

Hermitage, Banks, Johnsville, Ingalls, Parnell Springs: the type rarely leaves the company gate. The county is the correction. A junction that lost a bank to whiskey sales, a crossroads named for Johns, a Crowtown renamed for a Kansas senator, a pavilion that collapsed after Southern Lumber took the note — these are mill-town America too, the part that furnished the log and did not get the commissary. A series that closed only on Warren’s square would have accepted the type’s geography. The peninsula is larger than the type.

Two tornadoes on one path are not in the textbook chapter. Weather is supposed to be incidental. Here it is a second author. 1949 wrote the tragic record. 1975 wrote the rhyme. The mill was in both sentences. So were houses that were never on a company plat. Mill-town America as industrial history undercounts wind. Warren cannot.

The long afterlife is where the type and the particular meet without a sale. Oak, gum, hickory, and pine still known in a later Warren room; a 1903 date chosen because the courthouse and the mill grew up together; no product name, no invitation. Bradley Brand Furniture is one sentence in a closing essay because the hardwood afterlife is true and because a closing essay that pretended the species ended in 2008 would be another kind of cut-out. The shop is not the series. The series is the county. The shop is proof that the type’s third act can be small and local instead of only a prison or a plaque.

The two banks are the type’s other miss. Merchants and Planters in the 1890s, Warren Bank in 1901, neither failed in the 1930s, while Hermitage’s bank did, in 1934, and had to be bought and reopened. A textbook mill town has a company office that is also the bank. Warren had independent tellers and a leaky fence: McCann delivering groceries the commissary also sold, tomato checks and mill checks in the same drawer. The Depression chapter in mill-town America is often a padlock. Here it is two surviving ledgers and a third that died on the ridge and came back under Warren’s name.

Warren’s use as a type is simpler than a lesson. It is a place a person from another mill town can recognize without being lied to. Another town had a rail; this one had 5 August 1880. Another had a store; this one had scrip at 215 South Main. Another had a Saturday theater; this one had a Mission parapet and a separate door. Another had a last owner; this one had a physician who said he spent a retirement on jobs. Another replaced a whistle with a festival; this one had tomatoes that travel pink. Recognition is not identity. The particular remains the peninsula, the parlor, the two names, the two storms, the two woods, the three mills, the seven dead, the thousand in the rain, the ledgers we do not have.

The first essay in this file began on water and a low rise. The last one ends on an object that is not water. The camera in this imagined film holds the depot step, because a mill town is a town that learned to send things away, and the step is where that learning still has a body. No train. No sermon. A wooden building rebuilt after a fire in 1911, listed in 1977, too domestic in its fanlights, too industrial in its warehouse eave. Type and particular in one frame. America, and this county only.

A reader who has walked the brick does not need a moral hung on the cupola. The mills made a town loud and left it thinner. The seat of government outlasted the loudness. The woods came back in Warner’s sense. The fruit kept a week in June. The photographs and letters are a table with a blank the size of a card. The names on the clock were real. The peninsula is still a peninsula. Mill-town America can find another example tomorrow. It will not find this parlor, this cupola, this pink, this path from Farmville, this physician’s quote, this wooden depot. That is why these pages were not a chapter in a textbook. They were a walk through one county’s dated rooms.

The still is the step. Hold it. The whistle, if you hear one, is memory. The grade is not.
