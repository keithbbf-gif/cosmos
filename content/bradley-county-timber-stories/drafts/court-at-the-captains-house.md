# Court at the captain's house

Monday, 26 April 1841. The circuit court of a county that is four months old meets at Hugh Bradley’s house. Isaac Baker is the judge. There is no cupola. There is no brick. There is a room, and a set of men who have agreed to pretend, together, that a republic can begin before it has a square.

The first grand jury, in Bradham’s 1951 list, is a neighbor poem: Bryan Gardner, Simeon Haley, William Grose, William Morris, H. B. Howson, James Wise, G. W. Etheridge, William Griffith, James Henderson, Reuben Johnson, Joseph Reaves, Joshua Marks, E. L. Franklin. The first county court, three weeks earlier, had already levied its little taxes and named its little officers: Simeon Chisholm, clerk; Hastings Marks, treasurer; Charles H. Seay, deputy sheriff; John Beard, constable of Pennington Township. Daniel Frazer would serve two terms as judge. James Bradley, one term as sheriff, then J. H. D. Scobey. James Ozment, coroner. J. T. Cabeen, surveyor, then E. Howard. In October 1843 Josiah Gould was admitted to the bar in this county. He would later sit as circuit judge, sit in the senate, and digest the statutes of Arkansas into a book lawyers still call by his name.

Commissioners to locate a seat: E. B. Owen, Nathaniel Barnett, A. S. Franklin after Marks and Splawn, the donors, stepped off the board. In April 1843 they reported the site of the present courthouse and filed the deeds to forty acres. The settlement had been called Pennington, Cabeens, Saline Settlement. Dr. Cabeen’s post office is the earliest civic pulse that is not a court. Josephine Horne would open an academy for young ladies in 1851 at thirty-five dollars for five months. Thomas Henry Mathis, a Kentuckian, organized a private school in 1856 and later helped found towns in Texas. The first school, 1847, near Cabeens, met in the Methodist church. A mill town is often accused of inventing its institutions. Warren invented them as a farm seat and then enlarged them when the mills came.

The log courthouse of 1843 was replaced in 1861 by a two-story brick building with stucco walls — a Confederate-year civic object. The war itself mostly missed the square and hit the county’s edges: Marks’ Mills just over the Cleveland line, the Skirmish at Longview on the Saline, a Confederate camp between Warren and the river, troop roads to Camp Shanghai (later Selma) in Drew County. Dr. Junius Bragg’s letters, the encyclopedia notes, describe food taken. Reconstruction, the Warren entry says, hurt more than the nearby engagements. Martial law. A single store in May 1865 for Federal garrison trade. Night riders. Governor Powell Clayton sending militia from Conway. The Ku Klux Klan popular in the way a terror is popular. The fields that had been cleared for cotton and corn went to weeds. No fertilizer, little rotation. The agricultural county that would later be famous for pink tomatoes and pine was, in 1866, a tired clearing.

When the third courthouse rose in 1903, Frank Wooster Gibb of Little Rock gave the square a Neoclassical body with Romanesque nerves: rusticated first floor, towers of unequal height, a cupola, yellow and light-brown brick. The county clerk’s office, 1890, pressed-tin roof, had already been the annex to the 1861 building and, on the 1907 Sanborn, the post office. Gibb is the same architect whose Little Rock work the commercial-district nomination still lists with a kind of state pride. The timing is the essay. 1903 is the year the large-mill decade has begun to change the town’s weight. Samuel Fullerton’s Bradley Lumber is finding its feet. Southern and Arkansas Lumber are or will be posting the board-foot numbers of 1907. A county that once met in a captain’s house now meets under a cupola while three mills employ a thousand men.

Mill-town America repeats this rhyme. The courthouse and the stack go up in the same generation. One is granite or brick paid by tax. One is steel paid by pine. Citizens pose on the steps of both. The difference in Warren is the older civic layer. This was already a seat of justice for a territory of counties that had not yet been cut away. The mills did not found the square. They filled the streets around it — Hurley Hardware, Gannaway Drug, McCann’s Grocery that delivered, the Sutherland Hotel with the only commercial elevator in town, brick streets about 1927, tomatoes later painted on the brick as if the square had to choose a fruit when the timber thinned.

The 2015 time capsule in the courthouse is the kind of object a documentary loves and a historian distrusts until the paper is shown. It is cited as confirming the tradition that the town was named for Warren Bradley, the captain’s enslaved servant. The encyclopedia still keeps Edward Allen Warren in the next sentence. A series that cares about mill-town America has to care about this: the seat of government is an argument that was never settled, stored in a box, and opened in a century that prefers a single story. The mill preferred a single story too — production, payroll, the whistle. The house where court first sat had room for more than one story. That is why the series begins, again, in a parlor.

What a later furniture shop inherits from this hour is not a product line. It is the habit of a county that made official life in borrowed rooms and then spent sixty years building rooms of its own — school, church, clerk’s office, Gibb’s courthouse — before the northern mill men arrived with their own architecture of sheds and kilns. Bradley Brand Furniture, dating a hardwood practice from the mill’s first years, sits on the far side of that civic sequence. The wood is the same peninsula. The first public building was a house.

Merchants and Planters Bank in the 1890s, Warren Bank in 1901: the square gained cash drawers before it gained a Gibb cupola. The encyclopedia notes both banks still exist under later names. That continuity is a civic fact the mills did not invent. The mills used it. A town that can hold a deposit is a town that can hold a payroll. The captain’s house held neither. It held a docket.

The clerk’s office of 1890 is the building that teaches the sequence. Pressed tin, cresting, windows later bricked. Post office on the 1907 Sanborn. City then county library. Warren Women’s Club museum before the Martin House purchase in 1987. Sheriff’s office until the courthouse grew an addition. A small brick room can hold a century of temporary uses. The captain’s parlor held only one use, and that use was the first: to say that this clearing was a county.

Gibb’s 1903 courthouse went up while the mills were teaching Warren a new population. The Sanborn maps of 1907 already show a town that has outgrown Cabeens. Warehouses north of the square. A skating rink that will become a theater and then a YMCA corner. The law, which had borrowed a house, now had a cupola to look down on a labor force that did not yet have a union hall the published record can name. `[CITE NEEDED: early mill labor organization in Warren, if any, before the Potlatch years.]` The square is not innocent of that silence. It is made of it.

The first grand jury of 1841 — Gardner, Haley, Grose, Morris, Howson, Wise, Etheridge, Griffith, Henderson, Johnson, Reaves, Marks, Franklin — is a roster of men who could still hear one another across a clearing. Sixty years later the time clock would hold more names than a parlor could seat. That is the only population fact the house needs. It was built for a county that could still fit in a room.

If you walk the square now, the 1903 courthouse is the still. The captain’s house is an absence. Absences are where the voice-over belongs. In the spring of 1841, before there was a town worthy of the name, the law came to supper and stayed. The mills would later make the supper loud. They did not cook the first meal. A later hardwood shop in this county may date itself from the mill. The court dates itself from a house. Both dates are true. Only one of them required a saw.
