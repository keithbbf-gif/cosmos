# Mill housing, company street

By 1912 the Sanborn Fire Insurance map had already chosen a phrase for the southeast half of the block at Walnut and Ash. Twelve small frame dwellings. The label: “Negro Settlement.” Rachel Silva, standing at what is now a baseball field, told a Saturday tour in 2012 that those houses were still on the lot in 1938, the last Sanborn. The map’s language is the town’s language made official and thin. It does not name a family. It names a geography. A mill village essay that will not invent a tenant has to start with that ink, because the ink is what the company and the city agreed to file with an insurer.

Bradley Lumber Company of Arkansas did not invent the southern company street. It imported a form that Crossett, Bogalusa, and a hundred pine towns already knew: houses the mill owned, rents the mill set, a walk to a whistle the mill blew. The Warren version is particular because Warren was already a seat. Hugh Bradley’s house had held court in 1841. Marks and Splawn had given forty acres. Gibb’s courthouse was up in 1903, the same decade Samuel Fullerton bought W. H. Wheeler’s small mill and sent his sons to run a hardwood-and-pine plant. The company street here is not a camp dropped on a blank plat. It is a second street system laid beside a square that had merchants, two banks, and churches older than the first large mill.

The 1936 *Bradley County Centennial Magazine*, as the commercial-district nomination quotes it, is the closest the surviving county record comes to a housing census. Bradley Lumber’s employees “average in number more than one thousand, and the labor turnover is at the minimum. It owns more than two hundred residences, moderately equipped and furnished to employees at about half the regular rental.” Half rent is a number that does the work of a paragraph. It says the house is a benefit. It says the house is a hold. Turnover at a minimum is the company’s boast. The boast and the hold are the same fact. `[CITE NEEDED: a plat or rent roll that locates those two hundred houses by street, race, and room count.]` The encyclopedia’s media file still keeps a photograph: “Warren Mill Worker Housing,” houses provided for lumber mill workers, circa 1930s, Arkansas State Archives. The camera, like the Sanborn, prefers the type. Rows. Porches. A dirt or cinder walk. No name on a mailbox the caption will print.

Southern Lumber and Arkansas Lumber kept camps in the woods as well as streets in town. Horses and oxen first, then the Warren & Ouachita Valley after 1899, Warren to Banks, passenger service to 1948. A logging camp is housing that moves when the steel moves. A company street in Warren is housing that stays when the cut moves farther out. The difference matters to children. A camp child changes schools. A Warren child may walk from a mill house to a school the Methodists had already begun in 1847, or to the Baptist Centennial Institute of 1874, or to the Presbyterian Training School the congregation funded in 1906. The mill did not found those rooms. It filled them.

Segregation was not a side note to the fill. It was the street plan. Walnut and Ash, twelve frames, a label that used the word the white mapmaker preferred. Catfish Row on East Elm: Black-owned restaurant, barber, boarding house by 1907; a full brick block by 1931, lodge hall and two bottling works, George W. Hammons’s initials still on a storefront. The Pastime on West Cedar, circa 1925, separate entrance, balcony not chosen. In 1941 a policeman kicked Ms. O. Z. Jackson for refusing the alley. The YMCA of 1920, born of a 1918 Council of 22, was a Christian recreation the charter did not have to say was white for the town to know it. The 1920 building burned in 1944; the replacement still sits near the old skating-rink corner. Company housing sat inside that map. A Black mill hand’s house and a white mill hand’s house might share a whistle and a scrip ticket at 215 South Main. They did not share a Sanborn noun.

The lynching record is the older street under the new ones. William and Henry Beavers, 1890 and 1892, brothers, the encyclopedia’s ages estimated at fourteen and sixteen, accused of assaults the surviving white women lived through. Crane Green, 1903; John Turner, 1903, the Warren entry’s name. Hamilton and Ludberry, two white men, 1887. Dignity is not a soft focus. It is the refusal to make a spectacle of a boy’s death and the refusal to skip the names when the subject is the town’s other street. Company housing did not cause those murders. It inherited the map they wrote. A porch at half rent does not make a citizen. It makes an occupant.

White mill housing had its own grades. Superintendents and owners lived on named streets the tornado of 1949 would treat as addresses in a disaster list. Edwin Ives, superintendent of Bradley, at 610 South Main: a steel I-beam in the front yard, the palatial house not damaged, no one killed there. R. W. Fullerton’s place: an H-beam over a ton in the back yard, house spared. The Louis Ederington Tudor at Main and Central, H. Ray Burks, 1926–27, National Register 1984 — not a mill cottage. The two hundred residences of the 1936 sentence were “moderately equipped.” The adjective is doing class work. A company street is not one street. It is a set of streets the payroll can tell apart.

Monday, 3 January 1949, made the set visible by wrecking it. The *Eagle Democrat*’s incomplete check: 120 homes destroyed, 72 major damage, 150 minor. More than two hundred blown away, wrecked, or substantially damaged, Purkins wrote, and then gave the incomplete numbers. Around the Bradley powerhouse the stack was down. Water off in the Bradley section. A thousand workmen displaced — the encyclopedia says the destruction of the Bradley mill displaced 1,000 employees. South of the cemetery, whole families gone from houses whose owners Purkins listed because a county paper lists neighbors: Hartsell, Clemons, Hattaway, Sanders, Robbins. On Powers Street, not a house left intact. The storm’s path from Farmville through the mill section and out past the Ederington house east of town is the same path the 1975 Good Friday tornado would take. Housing is what a path is measured in.

Rebuilding began before the funerals finished. Purkins, on a Saturday afternoon five days later, watched a little house going up on the street north of the cemetery, beyond the wreck of the H. E. Green home, both Greens dead, and thought it might have a cover by nightfall. Seventy-five state convicts cleared debris on South Main under guards with rifles. The YMCA and the American Legion Hut took the injured. Frazer’s Funeral Home held forty bodies by 3:00 a.m. Choirs, the encyclopedia says, took turns as coffins rolled down church aisles. A company street after a tornado is no longer a rent question. It is a question of who still has a floor.

Warren’s population figures are the long housing story the Sanborn cannot tell. Town: 301 in 1880, 954 in 1900, 2,057 in 1910, 2,145 in 1920, 2,523 in 1930, 2,516 in 1940, 2,615 in 1950 — then 6,752 in 1960, a jump that records annexation and a different civic math more than a sudden mill baby boom. `[CITE NEEDED: the ordinance or census note that explains Warren’s 1950–1960 leap.]` County peak 18,097 in 1940; 10,545 in 2020. The houses that held the peak were mill houses, farm houses, and the antebellum places the Warren entry likes to mention. Potlatch bought Southern in 1956 and Bradley in 1958. Half-rent cottages become, in time, private sales, vacancies, or lots. The baseball field at Walnut and Ash is one ending. The field is not an insult to the twelve dwellings. It is what a town does when the last Sanborn is thirty years old and the label has become unbearable to paint on a sign and too true to forget.

Mill-town America photographs the company street as a single vanishing point: identical eaves, a water tower, a child in a yard of packed dirt. Warren’s photograph, if it is to be this county’s, must hold three planes. The square and Gibb’s cupola. The mill yards and the two hundred moderately equipped houses. The twelve frames the insurer named in a language the occupants did not choose. After those three, the type may be said. Southern yellow pine towns from Arkansas to east Texas built labor as real estate. They rented the sleep that made the next day’s board feet. They segregated the sleep. They called the arrangement a village. The remaining object in Warren is a corner that is now a game. Walnut and Ash. The map’s phrase is gone from the ground and not gone from the file. The people who stacked the lumber slept on streets the mill priced, and on streets the mill refused to name as its own.
