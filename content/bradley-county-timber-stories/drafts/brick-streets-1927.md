# Brick streets, 1927

After rain, the running bond holds water in little troughs between stretchers, and the square looks briefly like a mill pond no company owns. The bricks are four by eight and a half inches, red to reddish-black, probably two inches thick. At the intersections they turn forty-five degrees and make an X. The X is not decoration first. The National Register nomination, written in 2006 and listed on 24 January 2007, says the diagonal lock was engineered to keep the roadway from walking. Warren’s streets had been walking for a long time. Mud in wet weather. Dust in dry. The Sanborn of November 1920 still calls them unpaved.

The commercial-district papers put the paving of the downtown between 1924 and 1927, the brick itself circa 1927. By July 1931 the town could tell the mapmakers it had invested in some seven miles of brick and concrete streets. What the 2007 nomination saves is the largest contiguous remnant: portions of Cedar, Myrtle, Chestnut, First, Walnut, Elm, and Cypress — about 4,950 feet, just under a mile, on roughly 4.5 acres. Sixty-foot rights-of-way on most blocks; eighty around the west, south, and east sides of the courthouse. Roadways thirty-six feet wide, two-foot curb and gutter; sixty-seven feet of brick around three sides of Gibb’s 1903 pile. Main Street, where the bricks cross the old Vine, is asphalt now. The rest, for the most part, is still the 1927 floor.

A mill town paves when the automobile has arrived and the wagon has not quite left. Bradley County’s pink tomatoes are already a farm fact in the 1920s — a variety chosen to ship if you pick it when the shoulders have only turned pink. The mills are in their heyday, the encyclopedia’s word for the 1920s and 1930s. Arkansas Lumber is one year from cutting out. Southern and Bradley are not. The Warren Bank burns about 1927 and rises as a Neoclassical temple with an eagle and a date in the parapet. The YMCA has been chartered seven years. The Pastime has been showing pictures for two. Brick is what a town lays down when it believes the decade will last.

Paving brick is not house brick. The nomination walks the chemistry without romance: clay, shale, sand, flux; vitrification until the pores disappear; a crushing strength the text puts at eight to ten thousand pounds per square inch against concrete’s thirty-five hundred. Charleston, West Virginia, in 1870, is the American sermon on the subject — Dr. Nathan Hale paving a street at his own expense because the council thought brick ludicrous, then the city converting after the wheels failed to ruin him. Warren does not need the sermon. It needs a supplier. Acme at Malvern and Perla in Hot Spring County are the likely Arkansas kilns. The nomination will not swear which plant made these stretchers. `[CITE NEEDED: manufacturer and contract date for Warren’s c. 1927 paving brick.]` The color range, red to near black, is the kiln’s signature and the street’s weather.

The pattern is a language. Running bond, stretchers perpendicular to the curb, course after course, except a short modified herringbone along the north curb of First Street northeast of the courthouse, and except the X at every crossing, and except a fifteen-course skew at West Cedar and Walnut where Cedar bends. Someone stood in the street and solved a curve with a chisel count. That is engineering. It is also the closest thing the square has to a handmade date. You can still put a hand on it.

Tomatoes came later, as paint. Silva, stopping the 2012 walking tour on the brick, says the obvious thing: note the tomatoes painted on the street. Rex Nelson, writing years after, still uses the big tomato on the courthouse square as the image that tells a stranger what Warren decided to be when the timber thinned. The Pink Tomato Festival begins in 1956 — the same year Potlatch Forests, Inc., of Idaho buys Southern Lumber, if you follow Bradham and Silva; the encyclopedia’s county entry prefers to date both mill purchases 1958. The fruit becomes the state fruit and vegetable in 1987, south Arkansas vine-ripe pink, at the urging of John Lipton of Warren. The paintings are not 1927. They are a later argument stenciled onto an older floor: this square grows more than board feet. Dual identity. Timber and tomato. The brick is willing. It will take paint the way it took wagon grease and oil drips from Blankinship’s Fords.

The streets that still show brick are not a nostalgic loop. They are a traffic plan. Cedar between Chestnut and Elm. Myrtle from Cypress to the railroad. Chestnut from Cedar to the railroad. First between Main and Myrtle. Walnut between Cypress and Cedar. Elm between Seminary and Martin — which means the brick runs toward Catfish Row, toward the G W H storefronts, toward the tracks that divided the town’s two commercial languages. Cypress between Walnut and Myrtle. A person walking those names in order is walking the mill town’s indoor weather: hardware, drug, grocery, hotel, bank, theater, and, if the walker turns the right corner, a Black lodge hall the 1931 map still bothers to draw.

Asphalt is the American default because it is easier to patch and quieter under a radio. Many towns poured it over their brick and called the result modern. Warren kept a mile. Mayor Bryan Martin, in a 12 July 2006 letter the nomination quotes, says he is proud of the city’s effort to maintain the brick streets as they are, and that they draw people downtown. The sentence is civic boosterism and also a conservation report. The substrate is believed to be concrete, not sand; the nomination infers this from the lack of the wave a sand bed would have taken after eighty years of trucks. Where the bed cracked, the brick shifted. Where Main was overlaid, the brick disappears like a creek under a culvert. First Street, north of the courthouse, was narrowed for a circa-2000 addition. Integrity, in the Register’s language, remains remarkable. Remarkable is a bureaucratic word for: you can still hear a heel.

The 1949 tornado, which treated the mill section as a second river, left the commercial district mostly aside. The Register’s district nomination is careful to say so. Fires did more to the square than wind: bank, hotel, the 1980 block, a later store. The brick under those fires is the survivor that does not get a headline. A street cannot burn the way a Sutherland Hotel can. It can only be covered. The decision not to cover it is the town’s longest committee meeting, renewed every time a public-works superintendent looks at a pothole and orders a brick instead of a truck of mix.

Mill-town America paves in the same decade it founds a YMCA and a picture house, because all three are declarations that the camp has become a town. Crossett was a company plat. Warren was a courthouse that suddenly had somewhere to send a tree. The brick is the courthouse’s answer to the stack: tax money, or bond money, made into a floor that automobiles can trust. `[CITE NEEDED: ordinance, bond issue, or contractor for the 1924–27 paving.]` The mills paid the wages that bought the Fords that required the brick. The tomatoes, later, paid for the paint.

Hardwood left Warren over rails and, after 1927, over a street that would not vanish in a February thaw. Flooring stock and street brick are both fired or dried objects meant to be walked on. One you ship. One you leave.

The listing of 24 January 2007, NRIS 06001277, under the multiple-property cover “Arkansas Highway and Transportation Era Architecture, 1910–1965,” is the hour the federal government agreed the X at the intersection was history. Period of significance in that paper: circa 1927 to 1957. Significant years: 1927 and 1957. The second date is a cutoff, not a parade. In 2016 the same streets become contributing structure inside the Warren Commercial Historic District. Two nominations for one floor. Paper is cheaper than brick and less useful in the rain.

If you come to the square on a late summer afternoon, the still is not the cupola. It is a stained tomato on vitrified clay, the fruit a little too pink, the mortar dark, a pickup ticking as it waits the light on Main where the asphalt has covered the older idea. Under the asphalt the idea continues. Under the paint the 1927 bond continues. A county that once met in a parlor paved its argument in clay because mud was no longer a sufficient government. The mills had made the population. The automobiles had made the mess. The brick made a surface that could hold both, and later, without being asked, a festival.
