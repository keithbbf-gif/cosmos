# The Fullerton sons

Cornell is a cold river and a clock tower. Warren is a dry kiln and a whistle. Bradham’s 1951 sketch sends both weather systems into one sentence. Samuel Holmes Fullerton of St. Louis bought Wheeler’s mill, hired Joe Reaves to buy timber, and then sent the sons — Robert W., a graduate of Cornell, and S. Baker, educated in the Midwest — to Warren to operate Bradley Lumber Company. Operation is a different verb from founding. The father could stay on letterhead in St. Louis. The sons had to learn the smell of a kiln that had not dried evenly, and the sound of a town that had not asked for them and then could not do without their payroll.

Robert Wilson Fullerton, 1880–1950, is the son the later paper can hold. By 1947 he was signing as president. Two letters from that winter sit in a university digital collection and do not need invention. On 4 January he wrote Walter H. Harris of the CIO Organizing Committee that the company was under contract with the United Brotherhood of Carpenters and Joiners (AFL) and therefore could not legally negotiate with the International Woodworkers of America. On 31 March he wrote the employees that, under Arkansas law, they were not required to sign a dues check-off to keep their jobs. The workers had just voted, in an NLRB election, for the IWA-CIO. The letters are labor history, not dialogue. They are also a portrait. A Cornell man, forty-seven years into a life, sitting in a Warren office, using the state’s right-to-work weather against a check-off. The mill floor had become a politics. The son’s job was to keep the politics from becoming a second payroll.

S. Baker is thinner in the surviving county record, which is not the same as absent. Bradham gives him the Midwest education and the joint assignment to operate. A later National Register nomination for the Crocketts Bluff Hunting Lodge, far north on the Grand Prairie, says a Sam Fullerton who owned Bradley Lumber built a lodge there in 1938 to entertain lumber customers, that the lodge burned in 1955, and that a grandson, S. Baker Fullerton, built the present house in 1956 of native wood. Potlatch took the property with the mill in 1958. Do not collapse the generations. S. H. is the St. Louis founder. S. Baker the son is Bradham’s operator. S. Baker the grandson is a 1956 builder on a duck marsh. The family liked the initial. The paper, outside Bradham, has not yet given the middle son a Warren decade-by-decade. `[CITE NEEDED: S. Baker Fullerton’s dates, offices, and residence in Warren.]` Thin is honest.

The encyclopedia’s comment page under Warren holds another Fullerton who must be handled as memory, not as proof. Gene Davis, writing from a Phoenix retirement about summers in the 1950s, remembers “Bob Fullerton, with the Bradley County Lumber Mill, and his daughter Roberta” as party hosts, Roberta “the most fun girlfriend I ever had,” Warren his town in the summer, Little Rock Junior College his town in the winter. The comment also remembers a tornado, crossties in a dining-room table, a lawn man named Moree, Jack Scobey’s Chevrolet. It is a splendid comment. It is not a minute book. Bob may be Robert W., or a later Bob, or a household name that attached to the mill the way “Southern” attached to the Sutherland Hotel. Roberta is not in Bradham. Do not promote a social memory into a biography. It will say that a mill-owning family was remembered as hospitable, and that hospitality is not a production figure.

What the sons inherited was already a hybrid. Wheeler’s local mill. Reaves’s county timber. St. Louis money. A hardwood boast on a shortleaf peninsula. By 1907 the plant posted 100,000 board feet a day and 350 men, and the county encyclopedia was willing to call Bradley one of the world’s large dealers in hardwood. The sons’ work, if Bradham is right about the sending, was to make that boast a routine: oak and gum and hickory as well as the Arkansas soft pine the postcard would later advertise, mixed cars, flooring, furniture stock, millwork. Cornell, in that decade, was a school that also knew forestry; the first American college forestry programs were new enough to be news. Whether Robert W. read those courses or read engineering or read something else, the record does not say. `[CITE NEEDED: Robert W. Fullerton’s Cornell degree year and field.]` The useful fact is the sending. St. Louis did not trust a hired superintendent alone. It sent blood.

Blood in a mill town is a public fact. The sons would have been visible on the square, in the hotel dining room, in whatever pew a St. Louis Presbyterian or a borrowed Methodist seat offered. We will not invent the pew. We can point at the civic objects their payroll helped stand up. The Bradley Store, 215 South Main, 1920, scrip, weekly settle. The commissary and warehouse on the post-office corner from at least 1910. The YMCA, 1920. Brick streets about 1927. A company that employs 350 in 1907 and, in a later-owner figure, something like 1,100 in 1941 (Rand Wood — treat as later-owner, not as 1901 proof) is a company whose sons are part of the town’s weather whether or not they wanted to be. The 1949 tornado would kill Mrs. J. E. Stewart, wife of the Bradley Store manager, and take the mill powerhouse and stack down. The sons’ generation had already made the mill a place that could displace a thousand workers when the wind was wrong.

The 1947 letters sit near the end of Robert W.’s life. He died in 1950. Potlatch bought Southern in 1956 and Bradley in 1958. The sons’ era is the long middle: from the doubling of the town to the Idaho purchase, including a depression in which lumber demand held and neither Warren bank failed, including a world war that put women on the floor under a dress rule, including the 1939 fire at the other mill that Warner turned into a modernization. Bradley’s own survival through those years is the sons’ operating record, even when the paper names only the president. S. Baker’s share of that record is the part we have to leave as a joint verb. They were sent to operate. They operated. The town doubled and then steadied. The hardwood claim remained.

A hunting lodge on the Grand Prairie is a footnote that teaches the class. Lumber customers were entertained with ducks. The 1938 camp, the 1956 rebuild, the Potlatch inheritance, the later Frank Lyon purchase: northern and local money using the same woods for a different harvest. The lodge is not a Warren building. It is a Fullerton habit. Customers do not buy mixed cars only from a catalog. They buy them from a man who has fed them wild duck. The NR nomination is willing to say so. This page is willing to say so once. Then it comes back to the kiln.

Gene Davis’s Roberta, if we let her stay in the comment where she belongs, is the mill daughter as a summer fact. College boys from Little Rock, tomato fights, a radio dispatcher, a tornado. The comment’s date-sense is 1957, which is after Robert W.’s death and inside the Potlatch approach. Memory folds decades. That is why it is memory. A writer who needed a scene could misuse it. The reader needs a warning. The Fullerton sons are Bradham’s operators and the 1947 letterhead. They are not a party the comment can prove.

What St. Louis wanted from Cornell and the Midwest was a pair of men who could talk to both rooms: the Lakes-and-river-city room where credit lived, and the Warren room where the whistle lived. Robert W.’s 1947 sentences are fluent in the first room’s law. His presidency is evidence he also survived the second. S. Baker’s Midwest education is the same bet without the later archive. The father, S. H., remains the man who wrote the check to Wheeler. Founders get the incorporation date. Sons get the decades when the incorporation either becomes a town or becomes a cutover. Bradley Lumber became a town fact. The sons are why the verb is “became” and not “visited.”

Mill-town America is full of sons sent down. The particular here is the company name. They were not sent to operate Fullerton Lumber. They were sent to operate Bradley, in Bradley County, with a Bradley descendant buying the timber. St. Louis wore the place. The sons had to live inside the wearing. A man from Cornell can leave. A man whose payroll holds 350 families leaves differently. The leaky fence of Warren — independent stores beside the commissary, tomato money beside mill money — meant the sons could not own the town even if the mill owned the week. They could host. They could write letters. They could not make Main Street into a company street. Silva’s walking tour is the proof. Ederington’s, Hurley, Wayne’s, the Pastime: other surnames, same sidewalk.

Leave them on the 1947 letterhead, not on a remembered dance floor. President. Bradley Lumber Company of Arkansas. Warren. A sentence about a dues check-off. The Cornell clock is far away. The whistle is not. S. Baker’s name sits in Bradham as the other half of a sending. The comment page’s Bob and Roberta sit as a summer the encyclopedia did not edit into the entry. Three kinds of evidence. Only one of them can carry a production figure. The production figure is the town’s. The sons’ job was to keep the figure from stopping. For half a century, in the middle of a peninsula that had learned to count trees, they did.
