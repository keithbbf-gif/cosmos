# Sunday in a mill town

The street is called Church because the churches got there first. Silva, at the corner of Main and Church, said it without a sermon: Methodist just west of Main, Baptist a little farther south on Main, and, historically, a Presbyterian house on the lot that would become a Citgo, standing until at least 1938. Filling stations later took the other corners — Lion, Esso, American, Mobil — as if the Sabbath and the automobile had agreed to split an intersection. The name of the street is older than the pumps. It is older than the whistle. Monday would belong to steam. Sunday belonged to a calendar the mills did not write and could not quite ignore.

Charles Hinton Seay, circuit rider, came into the country after Hugh Bradley, his brother-in-law — they had married Beard sisters — and rode the Territory with saddlebags the museum still shows. Born 1799, ordained in Tennessee, he sat in the last Territorial legislature in 1834 and served as county judge while the land was still Union County. The first Methodist house in Bradley County was hewn logs, two and a half miles east of Warren, preaching twice a month. The first school, 1847, near Cabeens, met in a Methodist building. Josephine Horne’s academy for young ladies opened in 1851. A mill town is often accused of inventing its institutions. Warren invented church and school as a farm seat and then enlarged both when the mills came.

First Presbyterian organized on 1 August 1858. Bricks were being made when the Civil War stopped the work. The Methodists, who only had preaching two Sundays in four, invited the Presbyterians to use their house. In 1880 — the year the Ouachita Division made Warren a railhead — the Presbyterians raised a building at the northeast corner of Main and Church. The Baptist story in town begins with four women in 1868. Trinity needed eight members; three husbands and a member from Ebenezer completed the number. They met in the courthouse three years, then built on the old West Brothers site. Miss Mollie Wheeles played the first organ. In 1930 the congregation took the name First Baptist. The Baptist Centennial Institute of 1874 and the Presbyterian Training School of 1906 are the educational cousins of those houses. Sunday was already a civic fact when Thomas A. Carpenter’s 1891 mill was still a rumor.

Shady Grove at Jersey, organized 2 October 1847, had been the county’s first Baptist church and had practiced a sharing the museum sign still likes: Baptist one week, Methodist the next, whichever preacher arrived. Calvary Presbyterian at Johnsville, 1857, Rev. A. L. Crawford, Georgians and Carolinians. The county’s Sabbath was never only Warren’s. Hermitage, Banks, Johnsville, Ingalls — Crowtown before a senator’s name — had their own bells. The mill town’s Sunday is the one the whistle had to wait for.

Whether the large mills ran on Sunday is a thinner file. `[CITE NEEDED: a 1907–1940 notice of Sunday shutdown or Sunday sawing at Bradley, Southern, or Arkansas Lumber.]` Mill-town America, after the particular of these three plants, often treated Sunday as a boiler watch and a quiet yard, not a full carriage shift. Steam still wanted a man. The whistle, if it blew, blew fewer times. The published Warren pack will not let us invent a blast schedule. What it will let us say is that Monday had a sound Sunday did not, and that Church Street’s name is the civic record of that difference.

The Council of 22, 1918, wanted youth programs with an emphasis on faith. The YMCA charter of 1920 put a pool and a reading room on the old rink corner. Faith, in that project, was recreation with a locker. The 1920 building burned in 1944; the replacement is still a Warren fact the encyclopedia lists beside antebellum houses. Pentecostal work had already begun in the county at Blue Springs in 1908, brush arbors; in Warren, in D. H. Durham’s home in 1918, Fannie Corbett preaching, Flora Cargile assisting. First Assembly was set in order in the late fall of 1919 by Rev. W. W. Walthall. A tabernacle with a sawdust floor went up a year later. Nazarenes joined; Sister Cargile’s Model A is in the caption. Holy Cross Episcopal — St. Mary’s, then St. Catherine, then the 1909 name — organized in 1907, dwindled in the early 1930s, sold in 1947 to Bishop Albert L. Fletcher. St. Luke’s Catholic mission on Meek Street, 1916, Rev. Matthew Saettle, later Pine Street in 1948, a hundred seats, about twenty families. Sunday in this county was not one steeple. It was a set of steeples that did not all hear the same sermon and did not all share a pew.

Jim Crow kept the set divided. White Methodist, white Baptist, white Presbyterian on the named street. Black congregations the downtown tour script does not stop to number. `[CITE NEEDED: names and locations of Warren’s Black churches in the mill decades.]` Catfish Row on Elm was a Saturday night as well as a Monday ledger. The Pastime, circa 1925, had a Sunday question the paper does not settle for us — whether the balcony filled after church or whether the Sabbath closed the marquee. `[CITE NEEDED: Pastime Sunday hours, 1925–1940.]` What the paper does settle is the 1941 protest: Ms. O. Z. Jackson kicked in an alley for not waiting until white tickets were sold. A Sunday essay that mentions churches and not that kick has used the street’s name as a courtesy and not as a map.

Scrip settled at the week’s end. Saturday, in a commissary town, is the day the ticket becomes a debate. Sunday is the day the debate is supposed to pause. The Bradley Store at 215, after 1920, sat a short walk from the Baptist and Methodist houses. A man could settle on Saturday, sit on Sunday, and hear the Monday whistle as a kind of liturgy the preacher did not write. Half-rent company housing put some of those sitters in pews the mill had priced. The leaky fence put others in houses the mill did not own. Both sets of shoes could use the same brick after 1927. The brick does not record the sermon.

Tomato season, after the 1920s, gave Sunday a second calendar: fields that wanted picking whether or not the mill was quiet. Dual identity, timber and fruit. The Pink Tomato Festival, 1956, would make a June weekend into a civic Sabbath of a different kind — luncheon, contests, the square as a parish of merchants. The older Sunday was quieter and stricter and split by race. The festival Sunday is loud and invented and still split in ways a visitor can miss. Both belong to the same county.

Monday, 3 January 1949, was not a Sunday. It was a workday ending at 5:45 p.m. when the clock at Wayne’s Confectionery stopped and the stack went down. The Sabbath that followed is in the paper. 13 January 1949, *Eagle Democrat*: “All roads led to tornado torn Warren Sunday as hundreds of cars carrying thousands of people drove in to see for themselves the havoc… The Sabbath day, ominously warm and windfree, found most of Warren’s harassed and tired citizens in their church pews, seeking solace and guidance for the days of readjustment.” Sightseers on the roads. Neighbors in the pews. The encyclopedia adds that funerals were so numerous choirs took turns as coffins rolled down the aisles. Frazer’s had held forty bodies by 3:00 a.m. on the night of the storm. A mill-town Sunday, that week, was a funeral factory. The whistle had no authority. The churches had more work than the yards.

Good Friday, 28 March 1975, was already a church day when the second tornado took the same path and killed seven. The rhyme is almost too neat for a historian and too true for a novelist. The mills, by then, said Potlatch. The pews said the older names. Seven dead on a Friday that names a crucifixion is a fact the county still tells without needing a metaphor.

What mill-town America understands, once Warren’s corners have been paid, is that Sunday is the only clock the company did not invent. It could invent a whistle, a scrip week, a half rent, a dress rule. It could not invent the first day. It could only decide whether to honor it with silence. The published silence is incomplete. Boilers, watches, the woods, a tomato field — someone always worked. The street’s name is the wish. The wish is not nothing. A child who walked Church Street in a dress, 1918, past a mill that had just taught her mother to change into slacks, already knew the two calendars. One smelled of kiln-dried pine. One smelled of whatever the ladies had cooked because the whistle had not blown.

The remaining object is the corner. Main and Church. Post office on a commissary’s grave, Louis A. Simon, 1935–36. A gas station where the Presbyterians were. A Methodist house still west. A Baptist house still south. The cupola on the square to the north. No stack to the south that a Sunday morning can hear. The churches date from a log house two and a half miles east and a set of women who needed eight names in 1868. Monday will come. The street has already said what the first day is for.
