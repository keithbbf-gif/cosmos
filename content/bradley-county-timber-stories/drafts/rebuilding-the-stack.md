# Rebuilding the stack

On the Sabbath after the storm, 9 January 1949, the *Eagle Democrat* would remember a day ominously warm and wind-free, most of Warren’s harassed and tired citizens in their church pews, seeking solace and guidance for the days of readjustment. The paper printed that sentence in the 13 January issue, under a headline about thousands viewing the storm area and the loot of the wind coming home. All roads led to tornado-torn Warren. Hundreds of cars. Relatives who had not been able to get news. Sightseers. The pews were the town’s first rebuild. The stack would take longer.

Purkins, walking the wreck on Saturday the 8th, had already seen the other rebuild: a little house going up on the street just north of Oakland Cemetery, beyond where Mr. and Mrs. H. E. Green’s home had been, both of them dead. Before nightfall, he thought, it would have a cover again. Fortitude and will, he called it, and he was not wrong, and he was a man writing booster scripture over fresh graves. The convicts were still in the southwest wreckage with rifles behind them. Forty bodies had been at Frazer’s by three in the morning of the 4th. William F. West had died on the 9th, the 54th in Purkins’s count. Choirs, the encyclopedia says, took turns in the aisles because one choir could not do all the coffins. Rebuilding begins in a hymn and a tarp.

The mill is the civic rebuild the county cannot skip. The Bradley powerhouse was the center of the plant. The stack had fallen across the source of power for more than a thousand workmen. Water off in the Bradley section. Phones off in the south. Darkness complete. You do not reopen a hardwood mill, or a pine mill, by wishing. You reopen it by putting a chimney back in the air and steam back in the lines. The encyclopedia’s short clause — the destruction of the Bradley mill displaced 1,000 employees — is a January unemployment figure disguised as weather. Southern, Warner’s second-growth mill, is not the plant Purkins describes under the fallen brick. Bradley is. Fullerton’s house took a beam and kept its roof. Ives’s yard took a beam and kept its people. The men on the payroll did not have yards that could make that boast. A thousand off the clock is a town inside the town, sitting in pews, waiting for a stack.

The 13 January paper is a catalog of return. A letter addressed to H. E. Green — killed with his wife when the fury hit — came back from Stuttgart, seventy-five miles north of east. A coat identified as Leonard Henderson’s, another of the dead, was found in the churchyard at Newton Chapel between Cornerville and Star City, thirty miles northeast, and Mrs. L. A. Fich brought it to a relative, M. C. Jenkins. Peter Vining was said to have found another Henderson coat. A service picture of Mr. and Mrs. Ray Chatham, taken while he was in uniform, turned up in a yard at Rock Springs, twenty miles as the crow flies; a friend recognized it. Their house at 604 East Church was gone to one foundation block. She had run to Georgia Hoffman’s and been less hurt. He had been driving for “44” Taxi. The wind gave back paper and wool. It did not give back the Greens.

Will Hargis’s barn on the Prospect road, where the storm first hit the Warren community before its dip into the mill, had a roof cleanly split and laid open, the hay left in the mow. From there the wind took I-beams and girders of several tons and put them in Ives’s yard, in D. C. Harris’s living room on Wheeler Street, over Fullerton’s roof, and — one piece of a ton or more — twirled three-quarters of a mile and dropped it on Cliff Morgan’s lot on South Myrtle. Electric machines and refrigerators lay out in vacant air. A bale of cotton, origin southwest, caught in broken timbers back of the sawmill; the flying lint stuck to the fine wire fence and made a drapery of down. Heavier timbers from houses or the lumber yard had driven two or three feet into lots between Gannaway and Hankins, vertically, as if the ground were a rack. Skeeter Hughes’s garage walked off; his car and a child’s toy stayed. At the Pirtle house on Gannaway the head of a bed remained with the locks still in the sockets; the rails, springs, mattress, and footboard did not. Next door, friends went to see whether the Curtis Walkers were all right. No house. A blank corner. The Walkers were not at home.

These are the 13 January objects. They are how a paper rebuilds a week: by listing what came back and what stood still when the walls left. Scores of people, the same issue said, told of running the last distance to reach their homes and then looking out on a leveled swath of the ground they had just covered. It would take a book, the writer said, and then did not write the book. The book would have been the mill’s reopening ledger, the church contribution lists, the Red Cross correspondence, the insurance adjustments, the order for a new stack. `[CITE NEEDED: Bradley Lumber’s own 1949 rebuild schedule — stack contract, first whistle after 3 January, days of lost production.]` The published pack has the pews and the returned coat. It does not have the invoice for the chimney.

Churches are not a metaphor here. They are addresses. Newton Chapel held a dead man’s coat. Oakland held a toppled tomb on the Ederington lot and, just north, a new roof going on a neighbor’s walls. First Methodist, First Presbyterian, the Baptist house on Main, the Methodist on Church Street — Church Street named for that density, Silva notes, with a Presbyterian on the later Citgo corner until at least 1938 — are the buildings that had to do the funerals the encyclopedia compresses into one day of rotating choirs. The 20 January paper, on the ARGenWeb index, still has a services-and-funerals column. A mill town that can saw a thousand men’s work can also bury fifty-four or fifty-five in a fortnight if the pews hold. The pews held. That is the first stack.

The second stack is brick and steel on the Bradley yard. Powerhouse first, because nothing else turns. Then the sheds. Then the payroll. The wartime flooring ads of 1944, the later-owner claim of 1,100 in 1941, Purkins’s “more than a thousand” — these are the numbers that make the rebuild non-negotiable. A county that had kept two banks open in the Depression because lumber was still needed cannot, in 1949, decide that lumber is optional. Southern is already the modernized pine mill. Bradley is the hardwood house, furniture stock and strip floors, the Fullerton name. If Bradley stays down, Warner’s 1939 refusal becomes a one-mill town. The aura Bradham will later assign to Potlatch has to be preceded by a chimney in 1949. Without that chimney there is nothing for Idaho to buy.

Seventy-five state convicts in the residential wreckage are the rebuild’s other labor. The image is ugly and documented. Guards, rifles, South Main’s crumpled mass. National Guard on the streets the first night. State Police holding the curious back so ambulances could move. By the 13th the curious were the story: thousands in cars, a Sunday spectacle. The paper’s headline treats the returned loot and the viewers as a single phenomenon. People came to see. Things came back. The stack, in that issue, is already an assumption — of course the mill will rise — because the alternative is unprintable in a town whose banks and grocers live on the mill envelope.

A later shop that still works oak in this county works it because the 1949 rebuild happened. Bradley Brand Furniture’s 1903 heritage date skips the January when the heritage was on its side in the mud. The honest civic date between 1903 and Potlatch is 1949: the year the powerhouse was a vermicular ruin and then was not. Chambers, much later, will buy a hardwood mill Potlatch is finished with. That purchase is a grandchild of the January decision to put the stack back. Grandchildren do not always know the weather of the birth.

Mill-town America rebuilds a stack the way it rebuilds a steeple: because the skyline is the payroll’s signature. Warren’s particular is the speed of the two rebuilds in one week — a house north of the cemetery under a roof by dark on the 8th, churches full on the 9th, a paper on the 13th already talking about readjustment in the past tense of pews — and the slowness, only partly documented, of steam. The tornado of 28 March 1975 will follow the same path and kill seven. The 1949 stack will be there to be threatened again. That is the definition of a rebuild: it lasts long enough to face the next weather.

If you want a still, use Sunday morning, 9 January. Warm air that should not be warm. A choir that has already sung this week. A coat from Newton Chapel folded on a relative’s table. Out the Prospect road, a barn with its roof laid open and the hay decently in the mow. At the mill, a fallen chimney that has not yet been replaced, only surrounded by men who know that the next hymn is brick. The clock at Wayne’s still says 5:45 if no one has moved the hands. Someone will. The stack will go up because a peninsula that has already refused 1928 and refused 1939 does not, in 1949, accept a January wind as a company policy. The paper of the 13th is not a policy. It is a crowd on a road, and a town in a pew, and the first proof that the living intended to stay.
