# The mill whistle

A mill town keeps two clocks. One is on the square — later an electric face in a confectionery window, the kind of civic object a county paper will remember when it stops. The other is a throat of steam on a stack. It does not ask permission to speak. It speaks over Church Street and over Catfish Row, over the brick that will be laid about 1927 and over the dirt that came before the brick. It speaks to a house the company owns and to a house it does not. Warren learned the second clock first, because the first large mill arrived before the town had finished inventing a skyline worthy of a postcard.

The published record for Bradley County does not pin the exact hours of the blast. `[CITE NEEDED: Bradley, Southern, or Arkansas Lumber whistle schedules — start, noon, quit — in a mill notice, time-clock card, or Eagle Democrat item.]` Mill-town America, once the particular has been paid, is consistent on the grammar. A morning call. A noon call. A quitting call. Sometimes a short blast for fire or breakdown. The sound is steam until it is electricity, and then it is still called a whistle because the ear refuses the upgrade. Diboll, Texas, later made a folklore of an 11:15 “cornbread” blast. Newport, Tennessee, set kitchen clocks by seven, noon, and five. Those are other stacks. Warren’s stack is this one: Bradley Lumber Company’s powerhouse in the mill section south of the square, the “mighty smokestack” DuVal Purkins would watch fall on a Monday in January 1949, and, a few blocks and a few ownerships away, the Southern and Arkansas plants that had already taught the same air to carry three companies’ hours.

Thomas A. Carpenter put the first large mill in Warren in 1891. The 1880s had already been loud with smaller names — Shirey & Butler; Crandall & Leavitt; Edmondson; Glasgow & Temple; Lanark; Whittington; Parker & Kinard; Lowry & Taylor; J. E. Walker — but those were neighborhood machines. They did not yet own the sky. W. H. Wheeler started the mill Samuel H. Fullerton of St. Louis would buy; Joe L. Reaves, Sr., a descendant of Captain Hugh Bradley, walked the shortleaf with a purchase book; the Fullerton sons, Robert W. of Cornell and S. Baker, came to run Bradley Lumber Company of Arkansas. Southern Lumber Company, 1902, grew from Lindsay & Ainsworth’s 1882 start and carried Weyerhaeuser and Denkman on the letterhead. Arkansas Lumber Company, Rittenhouse and Embree of Chicago, with Clifford J. Mansfield of St. Louis to establish it, posted the largest of the 1907 numbers. By that year Southern cut 100,000 board feet a day with 300 men; Bradley 100,000 with 350; Arkansas 150,000 with 400. Warren’s population had been 301 in 1880 and 954 in 1900. In 1910 it was 2,057. The whistle is what a doubled town sounds like when it has not yet laid brick.

George W. Balogh’s 1900 description of Arkansas timber work is not a romance. Contractors or company men ran a logging railroad into the woods, chose a tree, trimmed it, dropped it, rough-sawed it into sections, and skidded the sections to the railhead with mules. Cars took the logs to the mill. There they were sorted by size, cut, planed, dried, seasoned, and shipped. Every one of those verbs had a clock attached. The woods crew left in the dark. The carriage crew met the first blast. The planer and the kiln had their own later hours. A child on Ash Street did not need to see a time card to know which verb was running. The air told it.

The Warren & Ouachita Valley, built in 1899 for Arkansas Lumber and Southern Lumber, added a second grammar: the crossing bell, the passenger run that lasted until 1948, the split-level depot of 1909 rebuilt in 1911 after fire. Iron Mountain, later Missouri Pacific, had already made Warren a western terminus on 5 August 1880. Rails and whistles are cousins. One is iron on iron. One is steam given a mouth. Both teach a farm seat that isolation is over. Both can be heard from a porch that does not belong to the company.

Civic Warren tried to keep its own time. Frank W. Gibb’s 1903 courthouse put a cupola over a county that had once met in Hugh Bradley’s house. The clerk’s office of 1890 had already been, on the 1907 Sanborn, a post office. Merchants & Planters Bank in the 1890s, Warren Bank in 1901 — two ledgers that, the encyclopedia is proud to say, did not fail in the 1930s. The Council of 22 formed in 1918, the same war year the mills hired women and wrote a dress rule. The YMCA took a charter in 1920 and a building that would burn in 1944. Brick streets about 1927. Pastime Theater circa 1925, with a separate entrance and a balcony that was not a choice. None of those institutions blew a whistle. They lived under one. That is the difference between a county seat that acquired mills and a camp that was only a mill. Crossett was invented as a town to house a cut. Warren already had a square. The whistle did not found the square. It colonized the hours around it.

Company housing made the colonization intimate. A circa 1936 county publication said Bradley Lumber averaged more than a thousand employees, with labor turnover at a minimum, and owned more than two hundred residences, “moderately equipped and furnished to employees at about half the regular rental.” Half rent is not a metaphor. It is a leash that sounds like a kindness. When the morning blast came, it came through walls the company had priced. When the noon blast came, it came into kitchens that might send a pail back toward the yard or a walk toward the Bradley Store at 215 South Main, the 1920 building where scrip still had to be settled at the week’s end. The whistle and the scrip are the same authority in two registers: one audible, one paper.

The sound did not fall equally. By 1912 the Sanborn map had labeled twelve small frame dwellings at the southeast corner of Walnut and Ash a “Negro Settlement.” Those houses were still on the last map, 1938. East Elm, one block of Black-owned businesses the town called Catfish Row, had its own restaurant and barber and, later, brick. The Pastime’s balcony had its own door. The whistle did not change pitch for any of that. It is a democratic instrument in the worst sense: it treats every ear as if the town were one ear. Jim Crow is the reply that the ear is not one. A series that cares about mill-town America has to keep both facts in the same paragraph. The blast called everyone. The streets did not.

Monday, 3 January 1949, is the day the civic clock and the mill clock failed together. On the square, at Wayne’s Confectionery, the electric clock stopped at 5:45 p.m. About that hour Mrs. J. E. Stewart, wife of the Bradley Store’s manager, was driving toward the end of her husband’s day. A timber out of the air found her car across from the Louis Ederington house at 326 South Main. She died about one in the morning in a Monticello hospital. Around the Bradley powerhouse Purkins saw “a vermicular scene of ruin.” The stack had crumpled across the source of power for more than a thousand workmen. Water failed in the Bradley section. Telephones failed in the south of the city. Rain fell in sheets. The encyclopedia and the later outbreak summaries give the dead as fifty-five or fifty-six; Purkins, writing in the week, watched the count move and said the exact number would never be known. One thousand employees displaced. One hundred twenty homes destroyed, seventy-two with major damage, one hundred fifty with minor — the 1949 paper’s incomplete check. Steel I-beams carried three-quarters of a mile. The superintendent’s yard held a rumpled beam of a ton. Robert W. Fullerton’s back yard held another. The whistle’s house had been the powerhouse. When the stack went down, the town did not need a blast to know the shift was over.

Good Friday, 28 March 1975, took the same path and killed seven. The second storm is a rhyme the county did not ask for. Between the two dates the ownership of the remaining mills changed its letterhead. Arkansas Lumber had already clear-cut some 85,000 acres and closed in 1928. Southern burned in 1939 and was modernized; W. R. Warner of Cloquet, Minnesota, sent to shut it, talked the owners into second growth instead. Potlatch Forests, Inc., bought Southern in 1956 and Bradley in 1958. A corporate purchase can keep a whistle. It cannot keep the meaning. The blast that once meant virgin shortleaf and a thousand time cards later meant second growth, then a smaller payroll, then, after 2002, a sale, and after 2008 a silence the credit markets finished.

What mill-town America understands, after Warren has been paid, is that a whistle is not nostalgia. It is a labor agreement the workers did not write. It tells a body when it belongs to the carriage and when it belongs to supper. It tells a merchant when the sidewalk will fill. It tells a teacher when the children of the yard will be late. It tells a preacher that Monday will arrive whether or not Sunday was kept. The published union record for these Warren floors is thin. `[CITE NEEDED: early mill labor organization in Warren; time-clock cards; the first year a whistle was electrified.]` In March 1947, R. W. Fullerton wrote employees about an NLRB vote and a dues check-off the company did not wish to treat as a condition of work. That letter is paper. The whistle was older than the letter and louder than the vote.

A person who stands on the square now can still see Gibb’s cupola. The stack that taught the hours is an absence, as the captain’s house is an absence. Absences are where the voice-over belongs. The remaining object is not a whistle. It is the habit of listening for one — the way a county still tells time by a mill that is not there, and still knows, without a catalog, that the day once began in steam. The wood is the same peninsula. The clock was a sound.
