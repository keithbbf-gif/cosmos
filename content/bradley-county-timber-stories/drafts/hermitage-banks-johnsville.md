# Hermitage, Banks, Johnsville

The county is larger than its seat. Warren took the courthouse, the three large mills, the festival, the hospital. The rest of Bradley County took the ridge, the crossing, the springs, and the names that did not fit on a letterhead. Hermitage sits near the geographic center. Banks sits where two railroads once argued about freight. Johnsville is a crossroads that named itself for a surplus of Johns. Ingalls was Crowtown until a Kansas senator, a Warren judge, and the Rock Island townsite company finished the argument. Parnell Springs sold water and then failed. Any mill-town history that never leaves the square has not yet reached the county.

About 1849, Wiley and Louisa Powell took title to the land that is now Hermitage. James and Susan Thompson, Louisa’s parents, came that year, as did Robert Pulley, pastor of Holly Springs Baptist, and the Jarrett family. When Wiley Powell died in June 1850 he left his widow sixteen enslaved people, twenty-eight head of livestock, 700 bushels of corn, and a cotton gin. Jefferson Singer, in the county since 1840, later acquired the farm. A post office named Hermitage opened in 1854. The name honors Andrew Jackson’s house in Nashville. Until 1906 the place was one house, one church, and one store that held the mail. In 1875 the Singer family ran a steam cotton gin that could turn out 200 bales a day. Then the Rock Island came across southern Arkansas, a depot went up, J. P. Lansdale opened a second store, a blacksmith arrived, and on 30 August 1907 Hermitage incorporated as a second-class city. The census gave it 247 people in 1910. In 2010 it had 830. In 2020, 525.

The Bradley County Bank was operating in Hermitage by 1930 and failed in 1934. Merchants and Planters of Warren bought it and reopened the doors in 1937. Warren’s two banks had not failed. Hermitage’s did. The Depression did not treat the center of the county as it treated the mill seat. Around 1945 the city built a hall that was also a jail; the building served both until a new hall went up on the bypass in 2000. The old one entered the National Register on 19 September 2007. School consolidation closed other county schools and left Hermitage’s system standing: elementary, middle, high, 624 students in the encyclopedia’s later count. Baptist churches, a Methodist church, an AME church. A grocery, two gas stations. Clanton Farms. Harrod & Hensley Tomato Company. Morris Hatchery. Arkansas Tomato Shippers, founded here in 1997, moved its offices to Arizona in 1999 and kept a packing facility. The 2010 census counted 290 white residents, 237 African American, 349 Hispanic. The county’s later Spanish-speaking workforce is not a Warren-only fact. It is a Hermitage fact. Tomato and poultry, not a hardwood letterhead, keep the ridge employed.

Banks is one of only three incorporated towns in the county. Caddo lived in that western reach long before a depot. John McFarlin took a land grant in 1856; John Evans and Pleasant D. Morgan in 1860. Before the Civil War some ground was cleared for the Mississippi, Ouachita and Red River Railroad, chartered 1852, never finished here. After the war James E. Lindsay of Davenport, Iowa — the same Lindsay who, with Charles Ainsworth, started what became Southern Lumber — acquired property in 1883 at the place that would be Banks. The Choctaw, Oklahoma and Gulf laid track later taken by the Chicago, Rock Island and Pacific. A depot made a post office. The office was first called Morgan, probably for Pleasant D. Morgan. In 1899 the Warren and Ouachita Valley Railway, built by Arkansas Lumber Company and Southern Lumber Company, ran sixteen miles from Warren to that junction so pine could meet a main line. Passenger service on the W&OV lasted until 1948. In 1906 the post office changed its name to Banks. The *Encyclopedia of Arkansas* says the reason is no longer remembered. A student paper reprinted in the *Eagle Democrat* says the name is A. B. Banks, an insurance man from Fordyce. Hold both traditions and do not gavel. The town incorporated 31 August 1907, one day after Hermitage. By 1910 it had 244 people. In 1920, 347, the high. In 2020, 87.

Maylon Rice, writing in the *Eagle Democrat* on 15 January 1975, walked the early ledger. Banks was platted in 1905 by the Rock Island Town-site Company, thirteen miles west of Warren by the later highway, sixteen by rail. First store: J. D. McFarland and Company — McFarland, J. M. Spraggins, A. E. Blythe. Then S. L. Kyser Grocer, T. J. Bratton General Store, J. H. Splawn Hardware. Wash McLeod and John Childs opened in 1906. W. F. Spraggins built the Banks Hotel that year. Citizens Bank opened in 1907 with $10,000 in assets and $1,000 surplus; McFarland president, Roy Wood cashier. Three men took whiskey licenses in 1913. Combined sales that year, Rice says, ran to $85,000, about the working capital of the community. Within fifteen months almost every business failed, including the bank. The bank reopened in 1916 with A. B. Banks as president and L. K. Taylor cashier, and liquidated again in 1928. A $12,000 brick school went up in 1915. The *Eagle* is doing civic history, not romance. A junction town can drink itself out of a bank.

When the timber was cut out, Banks shrank. Rock Island traffic lasted until the railroad’s bankruptcy in the 1970s and the shutdown in 1980. Highways 4 and 275 remained. In 1987 the town began a Buck Fever Festival on the second weekend of November — parade, talent, horseshoes, an ecumenical service — to mark the opening of deer season. The encyclopedia now calls Banks chiefly a hunters’ town. That is a late identity. The first identity was Lindsay’s trees and a short line built so two Warren mills could reach Chicago without asking Warren’s permission to be the only town.

Johnsville never incorporated. The encyclopedia’s sentence is the whole civic poem: the crossroads was named because so many of its men were John — John R. Hampton, John Cabeen, John Martin, John Brady, to cite a few. A later *Eagle Democrat* recollection, signed as memory of the place ninety years on, puts a date under the poem. On 20 September 1856, James Saddler Martin arrived from Mississippi and crossed the Saline. In 1857 John Brady came out from Warren and built a store at the crossing of the Moro and Longview road and the Warren and Moore’s Mill road. Men standing at Henry Rawls’s house discussed a name and took Johnsville for the Johns they could already count. A Presbyterian church went up in 1858–59; Rev. Crawford preached. Baptists built about two miles north. When the war came the community sent men, as the rest of the county sent men to the Second, Fifth, and Ninth Arkansas. Afterward it remained a crossroads. The Warren, Johnsville and Saline River Railroad — later the Warren and Saline River — is mill geography, not a sister-town monograph. Logs moved. The Johns stayed.

Ingalls was Crowtown first. The post office rejected that name. Other names failed the department. Judge Hughey of Warren, a Republican, offered Ingalls for John James Ingalls, the Kansas senator. The railroad wanted the honor too. The town was first laid out in 1906 about a half mile from its present site. In June 1906 the Rock Island came through and refused to establish a station so close to Hermitage — four and a half miles, the company thought, was too near for both to prosper. In 1907 the townsite company laid off Ingalls where it now sits, six miles south of Hermitage on the same rails. By the 1930s and 1940s the place had stores, a church, a school, a cotton gin, and a sawmill. It was never Warren. It was never supposed to be. It was a railroad’s idea of spacing.

Parnell Springs lay ten miles northwest of Warren, seven south of New Edinburg, two miles off Highway 8 on gravel. By 1880 it was a community and a health resort, named for Joseph M. Parnell, who bought the land in 1881, built a log house that became a hotel, and bottled the water in five-gallon containers. He shipped by buggy to Warren, then by train. Overworked, he sold to W. L. Kennedy. In 1893 Kennedy, after a dining room and other improvements, sold to Robert Wells, who tore down the log building, raised the Wells Hotel, and put a pavilion over the springs between 1893 and 1898. A post office at the springs itself did not take. The Moseley Cemetery had held reunions since 1840. Methodist camp meetings under a brush arbor drew more than a thousand. Families came in wagons, cooked on the ground, and bathed in water thought to heal, especially in cholera summers when the rivers were not to be trusted. Anna Moseley Parnell, who had come from Alabama in the 1840s, was the great-grandmother of Governor Harvey Parnell. Kinship and water were the first industries.

J. A. Smith, the next owner, built an 8,000-gallon water tower. Cabins, stables, a restaurant, a sawmill, two cotton gins, a boarding house, two stores, grist and corn mills, a blacksmith, a post office, two schools, a barber: most of it around the hotel and the springs. In 1906 the financial strain took the property from Smith and gave it to Southern Lumber Company of Warren. The mill company acquired a resort the way it acquired timber — because someone could not hold the note. Interest faded in the 1920s and 1930s. By the decade the encyclopedia names, the venture had failed. In this century the hotel is gone, the pavilion collapsed, the springs broken, the concrete steps still there for a person who knows which gravel road to take.

Warren is the type Americans mean when they say mill town: stack, commissary, a square that filled when the payroll filled. The county is the correction. Hermitage grew tomatoes and a school system after a cotton gin. Banks grew at a junction and shrank when the trees and the Rock Island left. Johnsville kept a name that is a roll call. Ingalls is a senator and a spacing rule. Parnell Springs is a mill company’s leftover hotel. None of these places invented Bradley Lumber Company. They invented the county that the company had to cross to get a log. A later hardwood shop in Warren may date a practice from 1903. The post office at Hermitage dates from 1854. Both dates are true. Only one of them required a cupola.

Drive the county now and the incorporated map is three dots. Warren, Hermitage, Banks. Everything else is a sign, a church, a packing shed, a hunting camp, a collapsed pavilion. The 2020 census still gives the whole county 10,545 people on 649 square miles. Sixteen to the mile. That density is not a city. It is a peninsula of named places that refused to become the seat. Mill-town America photographs the stack. This county’s other photograph is a depot at the center of the map, a junction that lost its bank twice, and a gravel road that once led to water people paid to drink.

The still might be the concrete steps at Parnell Springs, wet in winter, no hotel, no mill letterhead, the woods already the larger government again. Warren is twenty minutes of highway. The county is the rest of the hour.
