# Commissary and scrip

The windows at 215 South Main once had a multi-pane transom that let the street see the goods. Rachel Silva, walking the block for the Arkansas Historic Preservation Program on 14 July 2012, still called the transom great, and then said the metal cover. The building went up in 1920 to be the Bradley Store, owned and operated by Bradley Lumber Company of Arkansas. Workers shopped there with company scrip. They had to settle up at the end of each week. That is the whole mechanism in two sentences. The rest of the essay is what a county seat does when a mill puts a store on the same street as the independent grocers.

Next door, 213 South Main, was built the same year as a little extension of the same store — same transom language, same later slipcover. The National Register commercial-district list still carries 215 as BR0093, contributing. In the 1950s J. J. Neal bought the building and turned it into a General Dollar Store, later United Dollar. The company hour ended in discount paint. The brick did not apologize.

Before 215 there was already a commissary. From at least 1910 until the mid-1920s, Bradley Lumber had a warehouse and commissary on the northwest corner of Main and Church — the corner the street names as if commerce and Sabbath had agreed to share a curb. After the commissary came down, the empty lot was a park, then a miniature golf course. The United States Treasury, needing a post office, took the site. Construction was awarded to James I. Barnes of Logansport, Indiana, in October 1935. The building that rose in 1935–36 is Colonial Revival, Louis A. Simon the supervising architect, brick and a concrete cornice, square in plan, lobby on the first floor. It served as a post office until 1998. The city bought it in 2002. A federal lobby on a commissary footprint is the American sequence in one lot: company store, civic play, government mail. Existence is not identity. The empty-dir scar of a demolished commissary is still a corner.

Scrip is not a local invention. Southern mill towns from the Gulf to the Ouachitas used paper or metal stand-ins that were good at the company counter and awkward everywhere else. The published Warren record, as Silva has it, is weekly settle. It does not, in the tour script or the encyclopedia, print a wage, a scrip discount, or a sample ticket. `[CITE NEEDED: a surviving Bradley scrip piece, a ledger page, or an Eagle Democrat notice that states the rate of exchange.]` A responsible sentence stops there. The temptation is to invent a conversation over a counter. The paper will not support a conversation. What it will support is geography. The Bradley Store sat on Main, not behind a fence. McCann’s Grocery, which delivered, sat on the square at 101 Main. Hurley Hardware, about 1910, stored implements in metal buildings that Silva could still point to. Ederington’s Cash Store & Implement Company sold furniture, farm implements, hardware, millinery, and groceries in a two-story block at Main and Cypress; Hunt Hospital used part of the top floor. Independent merchants and a company store on the same numbered street is the leaky fence this county never quite built.

Crossett, a few counties south, was a camp that became a town because a mill needed houses and a counter. Fordyce and Warren, Balogh notes, already owed their existence to farms and then to rails. Camden and Pine Bluff were river towns before they were timber towns. Bradley County’s version of the commissary is therefore a hybrid. The mill wanted the capture of a payroll. The square already had men who had been selling dry goods since Major John C. Bratton, W. H. Wheeler, John T. Ederington, and Benjamin Martin “later served as the basis for business,” as the Warren entry puts it. Merchants & Planters Bank in the 1890s, Warren Bank in 1901 — two institutions that held tomato money and mill money in the same vaults and, unlike many Arkansas banks, did not fail in the Depression. A worker who settled scrip on Saturday could, in principle, walk a block and spend cash on Monday. Principle is not the same as a full purse. The weekly settle is designed to make the walk shorter.

The Coker Hotel, on the 1912 Sanborn, sat on South Main near the future Bradley Store — a long-term residential hotel with a restaurant and a garden, later an annex people still lived in when Silva walked. Hotels and commissaries are cousins. Both feed men who are not yet sure they will stay. The Sutherland, later called the Southern, on the south side of the square, had seventy-five rooms, a restaurant, Western Union, a barber, a millinery, and the only commercial elevator in town. It burned in 1964. Traveling lumber buyers, Fullerton men, Rittenhouse men, a drummer with a sample case of hardware — the hotel took their cash. The commissary took the weekly ticket. Two economies, one town.

A circa 1936 county publication, quoted in the commercial-district nomination, said Bradley Lumber averaged more than a thousand employees, turnover at a minimum, and owned more than two hundred residences rented at about half the regular rate. The store on Main is named in the same breath. Housing and scrip are one system. Half rent on a company street, a charge account at 215, a settle on Saturday: the mill did not need a wall. It needed a week. Arkansas Lumber, which posted 150,000 board feet a day and 400 men in 1907, closed after clear-cutting some 85,000 acres in 1928. Southern modernized after the 1939 fire. Bradley remained the large name on Main. Potlatch would buy the plants in 1956 and 1958. The store building outlived the scrip because brick outlives a ticket.

The other Main Street — the one the Sanborn did not have to label because the town already had a phrase — was East Elm, Catfish Row, a block of Black-owned businesses north of the tracks: restaurant, barber, boarding house by 1907; bottling, confectionery, pool hall, dry cleaners, cobbler, grocery by 1920; brick by 1931, including a Dr. Pepper plant and a lodge hall. George W. Hammons left initials on a storefront that later held a funeral home. Scrip did not found Catfish Row. Cash and credit among neighbors did. A commissary essay that stops at 215 has told only the company’s sentence. The row is the reply: a commercial street the mill did not own.

Jim Crow made the reply incomplete. The Pastime, circa 1925, kept a separate entrance and a balcony. In 1941 a policeman kicked Ms. O. Z. Jackson when she would not wait in an alley until white patrons had bought their tickets. The protest that followed is in the nomination. It is not a scrip story except in this way: the same week that could settle a mill ticket could also enforce a door. The Beavers brothers, lynched in 1890 and 1892, and the later names the encyclopedia keeps — Crane Green, John Turner — are the older violence under the new commerce. A store with a transom does not cancel a record. It sits on top of one.

Monday, 3 January 1949, tied the store to the weather. Mrs. J. E. Stewart, the manager’s wife, left home to pick up her husband after his shift. The clock at Wayne’s Confectionery stopped at 5:45 p.m. A timber from the air struck her car at the curb across from 326 South Main, the Louis Ederington house. She died about 1:00 a.m. in Monticello. Purkins wrote it in the *Eagle Democrat* as if the town needed the sentence to believe the hour. The mill’s powerhouse and stack were down. Water off in the Bradley section. A thousand workmen without a shift. The store’s week had no Friday settle that the paper bothers to describe. `[CITE NEEDED: whether the Bradley Store opened, closed, or issued emergency credit in the week after 3 January 1949.]` What the paper describes is a manager’s household entering the death list through a piece of flying wood — the product, even in disaster, still timber.

Independent Warren kept selling. Silva’s block-by-block is a poem of counters: Cunningham’s Grocery, Oscar Kohler’s barber, Botts Clothing, Gannaway Drug, Bailey’s then Wayne’s, Ederington’s department store with an Art Deco skin in the 1940s, Kroger by the late 1930s at 222, Imogene’s Fashion Center — Imogene French, married to Bill French of the Ben Franklin — a Jewish couple on South Main in a mill town that also had a Presbyterian church on the Citgo corner until at least 1938. Tomato money, after the 1920s, joined mill money on those sidewalks. The Pink Tomato Festival would wait until 1956. The fruit was already in the ledger. A commissary that must settle weekly is, among other things, a competitor with a festival the merchants would later invent to keep the square busy when the cut thinned.

Mill-town America likes to photograph the company store as a closed system: one counter, one ticket, one debt. Warren’s photograph, if the camera is honest, includes the transom, the metal that later hid it, the post office on the commissary’s grave, the dollar store in the mill store’s body, and the grocery on the square that delivered. The particular is a 1920 brick with two addresses. The type is the southern payroll captured for a week at a time. The remaining object is the address. 215 South Main. You can still stand under the covered transom and know that a ticket once had to become a Saturday before it became a Sunday.
