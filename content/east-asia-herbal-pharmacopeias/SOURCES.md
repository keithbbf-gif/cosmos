# Sources behind the drafts

This is a working shelf, not a complete bibliography. Drafts cite what they actually leaned on. If a fact in a draft cannot be walked back to a row here or to a source named in that draft, treat the fact as unsecured.

## Handbooks and surveys

- Unschuld, Paul U. *Medicine in China: A History of Pharmaceutics*. Berkeley: University of California Press, 1986. The spine for Chinese *bencao* as a literature, including the warning that a single Han original titled *Shennong bencao jing* is not proven.
- Unschuld, Paul U., and Zhang Zhibin, eds. *Dictionary of the Ben cao gang mu*. Berkeley: University of California Press, 2015–. Especially the project introductions on compilation dates, family labor, and citation mess.
- Needham, Joseph, and collaborators. *Science and Civilisation in China*, Vol. 6 (biology and botanical technology). Cambridge: Cambridge University Press. Use for the long *bencao* series, not as a last word on editions.
- Métailié, Georges. Work on East Asian botanical knowledge and transmission into Japan (see especially later handbook chapters).
- Shin Dongwon and related Korean medical historiography; Soyoung Suh, *Naming the Local: Medicine, Language, and Identity in Korea since the Fifteenth Century* (Harvard University Asia Center, 2017), and Suh’s article “Herbs of Our Own Kingdom,” *Asian Medicine* 5 (2009).
- Trambaiolo, Daniel. Essays on Tokugawa pharmacy and the limits of “Sinification.”
- Marcon, Federico. *The Knowledge of Nature and the Nature of Knowledge in Early Modern Japan*. Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 2015. For *honzōgaku* as a textual institution.

## China — texts and editions

- Tao Hongjing’s role in *Bencao jing jizhu* and the red/black (later large/small character) distinction: Unschuld 1986; later reconstructions.
- *Xinxiu bencao* (659): *Tang huiyao* dating; Su Jing and the twenty-two-name team; Japanese transmission and Dunhuang fragments. See also Mayanagi Makoto’s work on *Ishinpō* quotations, and Toyo Bunko notes on NLC fragment 臨.2371.
- Chen Cangqi, *Bencao shiyi* (often dated Kaiyuan 27 / 739): lost as a book; recovered in quotations. Shang Zhijun’s modern collation is the usual working text.
- *Kaibao bencao* (973/974): first printed Song official *bencao* in the standard narrative; original Song copies not identified in ordinary catalogs.
- Su Song, *Bencao tujing* (presented 1061): illustrations gathered from the prefectures; text now mainly through later *Zhenglei* recensions.
- Tang Shenwei, *Jingshi zhenglei beiji bencao* (and the Daguan / Zhenghe / Shaoxing lines): the private encyclopedia the Ming still ate.
- Liu Wentai et al., *Bencao pinhui jingyao* (1505): Ming official color herbal; not printed; later court copies. Library of Congress and Wellcome catalog notes.
- Li Shizhen, *Bencao gangmu*: manuscript work mid-sixteenth century; Wang Shizhen preface 1590; Jinling blocks associated with Hu Chenglong, often dated Wanli 21 (1593); Jiangxi official recut 1603. UNESCO Memory of the World files treat the Jinling print as the ancestor. Popular “1596” should not be used without a note.
- Zhao Xuemin, *Bencao gangmu shiyi*: often given as finished 1765; He Bian, “An Ever-Expanding Pharmacy,” *Late Imperial China* / *JAS* 2017 on the social conditions of the supplement.
- *Zhonghua yaodian* (1930, promulgated 1931): Republican code, not a *bencao*. Unschuld’s later remarks; Bernard E. Read, “The Chinese Pharmacopoeia,” *CMAJ* 23.4 (1930).
- *Zhonghua renmin gongheguo yaodian*, 1953 edition: 531 monographs in the official Chinese Pharmacopoeia Commission history (chemical, botanical, animal, antibiotic, biologic, preparations). 1957 addendum. 1963 as the later herbal-processing landmark.

## Japan

- Fukane no Sukehito, *Honzō wamyō*: Engi-era (901–923), commonly ~918. Oldest surviving Japanese pharmaceutical name-book in the usual account; 1796 Taki Motoyasu (Tanba Motoyasu) print from a shogunal library copy.
- Tanba Yasuyori, *Ishinpō*, 984: oldest surviving Japanese medical encyclopedia; National Treasure; Nakarai-family Heian copy at Tokyo National Museum. Vol. 30 is the *honzō* volume in Mayanagi’s reading.
- Hayashi Razan and the 1607 introduction of *Bencao gangmu*; Kyoto reprints from 1637 (Noda), later *kundoku* editions.
- Kaibara Ekiken, *Yamato honzō*: text 1708/1709, plates 1715. Counts of “Japanese” versus Chinese items vary by how one reads the categories; do not freeze a single integer as if it were a census.
- Ono Ranzan, *Honzō kōmoku keimō*, 1803–1806, later expanded by his grandson.
- *Nihon yakkyokuhō*, first edition: Naimushō Ordinance No. 10, 25 June 1886, in force 1 July 1887; 468 monographs in the PMDA historical pamphlet. Foreign teachers (Geerts, Eijkman, Langgaard) in the drafting story. Kampo’s later official return is a twentieth-century regulatory event, often dated in secondary literature to the 1960s–1970s listing process — treat exact “1974” claims as needing a statute cite before they harden.

## Korea

- *Hyangyak gugeupbang*: Goryeo compilation commonly placed around 1236 at the Tripitaka office on Ganghwa; 1417 Joseon reprint. Surviving copy reported at the Archives and Mausolea of the Imperial Household Agency, Tokyo. Encyclopedia of Korean Culture (AKS) is the ordinary Korean-language entry.
- *Hyangyak jesaeng jipseongbang*, 1399: early Joseon local-herb formulary in the standard narrative.
- *Hyangyak jipseongbang*, 1433: Yu Hyotong, No Jungnye, Park Yundeok; 85 *gwon* in the usual count; reprint 1478, later 1633.
- *Uibang yuchwi*: compilation finished 1445; printed 1477 after a long, punitive collation. Volume counts move in the literature (the 365-to-266 story is widely repeated; quote it as a scholarly account, not as a page audit).
- Heo Jun, *Donguibogam*: royal commission under Seonjo; often described as completed 1610 and printed 1613 by the Naeuiwon in 25 volumes / five parts. UNESCO Memory of the World, 2009.
- *Korean Pharmacopoeia* (KP): first edition 1958; later numbered revisions (KP 10, 2012; KP 11, 2014 in MFDS-facing literature). Separate *Korean Herbal Pharmacopoeia* (KHP) for items not in KP.
- Colonial period: Japanese rule marginalized *hanui* in the official system; wartime shortage (1930s–early 1940s) produced a partial, instrumental rehabilitation and local herbal-standard work. See *Korean Journal of Medical History* essays on traditional medicine after the 1930s. Do not narrate this as a simple revival.

## Pictures, trade, poison, modern codes

- Prefectural illustration as a Song administrative practice: *Bencao tujing* prefaces and later studies of *Zhenglei* images.
- Tribute and budget-bureau drug supply in Tang codes: reconstructions from *Tiansheng ling* medical statutes (use as a window, not as a complete Tang code).
- Aconite / *fuzi* as the textbook case of a “lower” or toxic drug that official books still keep: any responsible history must talk about processing and danger without writing a protocol.
- Conservation: later readings of rhino horn, tiger bone, pangolin, and other listed species are ethical and legal problems. Historical presence in a *bencao* is not a warrant.

## Catalogs and memory programs

- UNESCO Memory of the World nomination files for *Bencao gangmu* and *Donguibogam*. These are advocacy documents. Use them for holdings and dates they photograph, not for superlatives.
- Library of Congress records for *Dong yi bao jian* and *Ben cao pin hui jing yao*.
- Tokyo National Museum / e-Museum notes on the Nakarai *Ishinpō*.
- National Library of China and British Library Dunhuang *bencao* fragments.
- PMDA, “日本薬局方の歴史.”
- Chinese Pharmacopoeia Commission historical pages for 1953/1963.
