# East Asian herbal pharmacopeias — draft collection

Status: **draft, staged, not a book.**  
Scope: the history of herbal *materia medica* books and later national pharmacopeias in **China, Japan, and Korea**.  
Voice: a person reading, not a brochure.  
Rule: **claims-guarded.** These files describe texts, offices, editions, and arguments. They do not recommend herbs, doses, or treatments.

This folder is a working pile, not a finished history. Forty-five numbered drafts sit under `drafts/`, grouped by **stage**. Read them in sequence if you want the argument to accumulate. Skip around if you already know which book you came for. Either way, start with stage 0. It says what the pile will not do.

## What this is

A pharmacopeia, in the modern legal sense, is a book a state can point at when a bottle is out of spec. East Asia wrote something older and stranger first: classified memories of substances — plants, animals, minerals, manufactured things — kept by courts, clans, temples, printers, and later ministries. Chinese *bencao*, Japanese *honzō*, Korean *hyangyak* compilations, and the twentieth-century *yaodian* / *yakkyokuhō* / *yakjeon* books are not one genre with three national costumes. They share a script world and they keep refusing to be the same object.

The drafts treat that refusal as the subject.

## Claims guard, short form

- Legendary attribution is not authorship.
- A reconstructed text is not the lost original.
- A date of completion is not always a date of print, and a date of print is not always a date of use.
- “First” is almost always a first *in a defined class*, not a first in the world.
- Historical use is not efficacy. Historical use is also not innocence: some of these books describe poisons, abortifacients, and animal parts now illegal or extinct.
- National pride sentences (“the world’s first,” “the greatest physician”) are treated as claims to inspect, not as facts to repeat.

If a draft slips, the slip is a defect. Mark it. Do not sand it into confidence.

## What is not here

No recipes meant for use. No “top ten herbs.” No clinical advice. No attempt to settle whether traditional systems “work.” No Vietnam, Ryukyu, or Tibetan chapters — they appear only when a Chinese, Japanese, or Korean book is already talking about them. No primary-text edition. These are secondary essays written from published scholarship and catalog records, with the uncertainties left visible.
