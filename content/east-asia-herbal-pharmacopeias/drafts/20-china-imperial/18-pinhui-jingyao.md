In 1505, the last year of the Hongzhi emperor, a team at the Imperial Academy of Medicine finished a book the court had ordered. The title is *Bencao pinhui jingyao* 本草品彙精要 — essentials of materia medica, arranged. The lead name is Liu Wentai 劉文泰. The painters include Wang Shichang and a small group of court hands. The book is large: forty-two *juan* in the usual description, on the order of 1,800 substances, well over a thousand color pictures. It is the only official Ming *bencao* in the ordinary telling, and the last official one of the imperial series before the modern codes. And then it does not print.

The story that travels with the book is almost too shapely. The emperor dies. Liu is blamed for a failed treatment. The manuscript stays in the palace. For centuries the public *bencao* world runs on *Zhenglei* and then on Li Shizhen, while the official color album lives as a thing you copy if you can get near it. Qing court recopying in the Kangxi period; a French Jesuit (Pierre d’Incarville, 湯執中) later associated with a recopy that goes to France; more than twenty manuscript families now scattered through libraries. I have read this in catalog notes (Library of Congress, Wellcome, Chinese Academy holdings) and in Chinese handbook essays. I have not followed the stemma of the copies. “Over twenty” is a reported count, not my finding-aid.

What the book was trying to be is clearer than the scandal.

It wants **a grid**. Later descriptions list twenty-four fields for each substance: name, seedling, place, time, harvest, use, substance, color, taste, nature, qi, smell, and so on through contraindications and fakes. That is an administrative fantasy of completeness. It is also, if you squint, closer to a monograph than Tao’s page or Tang Shenwei’s chorus. I will not call it a modern monograph. I will say the palace wanted every drug to answer the same questions in the same order.

It wants **color**. Not Su Song’s travelable line. Court pigment. Some pictures, scholars now say, are *Zhenglei* lines dressed in imagined color. Some look like they have met a plant or a pharmacy specimen in Beijing. The mix is the point. A court painter is not a field botanist. A color that was guessed from a sentence is a claim about the sentence, not about the leaf.

It wants **to be official**, and then officialness becomes privacy. This is the file’s lesson for the whole collection. Imperial sponsorship does not equal public standard. The 659 book at least entered a copy world we can still trip over in Dunhuang and Japan. The 1505 book entered a vault. Li Shizhen, working decades later as a failed examination candidate with a family workshop, will out-public the palace without meaning to. The *Gangmu* is unofficial and everywhere. The *Pinhui* is official and nowhere you can buy it — until modern facsimiles.

I am cautious about the medical-failure story. It is widely repeated. It may be true. It is also the kind of story a later court tells about a book it has decided not to love. A second pass should read the Hongzhi and Zhengde veritable records, not the catalog blurbs.

For Japan and Korea this title is not a main character. I include it because a history of “official pharmacopeias” that jumps from Song print to Li Shizhen has skipped the Ming state’s actual official attempt — and skipped the fact that the attempt failed as a public book. Failure is part of the genre.

## Open questions

- What do the veritable records actually say about Liu Wentai and the Hongzhi death?
- Which of the surviving copies are Ming traces and which are Qing court remakes, and how much *Gangmu* material did the Kangxi recopy absorb? Handbook notes mention a continuation of some 480 additions; that needs a file of its own if true.

## Claims I am not making

- That the color pictures are reliable identifications.
- That the disgrace story is settled history.
- That “last official feudal *bencao*” is a useful phrase. It is a later periodization.
