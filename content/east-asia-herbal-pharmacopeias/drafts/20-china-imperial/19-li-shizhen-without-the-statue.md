# Li Shizhen without the statue

There is a statue, and it has done damage. The man on it is Li Shizhen 李時珍, “the greatest physician-pharmacologist,” the one who walked the mountains and wrote the encyclopedia and died just as the blocks were cut. Needham’s praise sits behind some of the plaques. UNESCO’s file sits behind others. I am going to write about a person who is smaller and more interesting than the statue.

**Dates.** You will see 1518–1593 on a great many Chinese notices and 1508–1593 in Unschuld’s *Gangmu* apparatus. I will not pick the birth year in a caption. The death year is the more stable end of the stick, and even that is a biographical tradition, not a death certificate I have read.

**Life, in the unspectacular version.** A family of physicians in Qizhou (Qichun), Huguang. Failed civil-service examinations — more than once, in the usual story. Turns back to the family craft. A period at the Imperial Medical Academy that does not become a career. Decades of compiling. A preface by the literary man Wang Shizhen dated 1590, in which Li is already a visitor with a fat manuscript and no extra luggage. Sons and grandsons in the editorial and picture credits of the first print. Death in 1593, the same Wanli year the Jinling blocks are associated with. That is enough life for this collection. The rest is film.

**Labor.** Li names family help. Unschuld points at the obvious: 1.6 million characters, fifty-odd *juan*, a workshop problem. We do not have the payroll. “Sole author” is a later convenience. I will say *Li Shizhen and his workshop* when I am talking about the making, and *Li* when I am talking about the name the tradition chose to keep.

**Method, without the halo.** He reads an enormous pile, especially the *Zhenglei*. He travels and talks to practitioners, in his own account. He rearranges the house (draft 20). He argues — *bianyi*, *zhengwu* — with earlier sentences. He also misattributes, shortens names, and quotes at second hand. The dictionary project that now sits beside the *Gangmu* exists because the statue’s book is a messy library, not because it is a scripture.

**What he was not.** He was not the Ming official compiler. That was Liu Wentai, and Liu’s book stayed in the palace (draft 18). He was not a modern botanist. He did not “anticipate Darwin” except in the sense that later people like to make him do so; Darwin’s “ancient Chinese encyclopedia” compliment, if you meet it, is a compliment, not a research collaboration. He was not a nationalist monument until later centuries needed one.

**What he was.** A late-Ming literate physician who believed the drug literature had become a tangle of errors and omissions, and who had the stubbornness to restack it. The 1590 preface’s own numbers — 1,518 inherited items, 374 added, sixteen departments, fifty-two *juan*, three drafts, eight hundred-odd books consulted — are Li’s (or Li’s workshop’s) self-description, spoken through Wang Shizhen. I treat them as a self-description. They are the right scale. They are not an audit.

Japan will take this man as an institution (drafts 27–29). Korea will meet the book as one more Chinese encyclopedia in a market already full of them, not as a founder. The statue is mostly a Chinese modern object. I am not sneering at the need for monuments. I am keeping them out of the colophon.

If you want a sentence to carry: **Li Shizhen is the name we give to a family-and-workshop encyclopedia that out-circulated the Ming state’s official herbal.** That sentence is already long enough to prevent a statue.

## Open questions

- Can the birth-year split be resolved in a biographical study that is not monument-literature?
- How much of the “thirty years / twenty-seven years / three drafts” story is Li’s own rhetoric of labor, a genre I respect and do not take as a timesheet?

## Sources used

Unschuld/Zhang *Dictionary* introductions (1508 dating, workshop inference, 1547–1580 compilation window as their hedge). Wang Shizhen preface 1590 as printed in later *Gangmu* editions. UNESCO Memory of the World file (1518 dating, 1578 first-draft story, 1593 Jinling, Needham quotation) — used as a prestige document, not as a referee. Chinese bibliographic work on Jinling/Jiangxi (draft 21).

## Claims I am not making

- That Li personally verified every item in the field.
- That the *Gangmu* is “the most complete medical book ever written.” That sentence is a trumpet.
