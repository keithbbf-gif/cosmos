# Zhao Xuemin and the leftover drugs

Li Shizhen’s machine was never going to be the last page. The title that admits this is again *shiyi* — a picking-up of what was left. Zhao Xuemin 趙學敏, a Qing figure usually dated by his activity in the later eighteenth century rather than by a tidy birth year, writes *Bencao gangmu shiyi* 本草綱目拾遺. The year you will see on the finished work is 1765. He Bian’s 2017 article is the reason I will not treat Zhao as a lone rustic who “added 900 drugs.”

Bian’s argument, as I read it: Zhao is a sojourning private secretary, not a court physician and not a famous literatus. That social room — *muyou*, the hired brush in someone else’s office — is exactly a room where elite and lowbrow knowledge can meet without either wearing a statue. He can hear a shop, read a rare book, meet a maritime good, and write it down as if writing it down were a way to outlast Li. The “ever-expanding pharmacy” is not a march of discovery. It is a late-imperial information world: more print, more travel, more things on ships, more people who are literate enough to keep a note.

Counts, as always, wander. You will see “over nine hundred” additions in one scholarly sentence and “716” in a popular one, depending on whether someone is counting new items, corrections, or a particular edition’s furniture. I will not freeze a number. The direction is enough: **a lot**, and enough to mean the *Gangmu* had not closed the set.

What kinds of leftovers? The ones I am willing to name as *types*, not as a tour of miracles: substances that came through coastal and long-distance trade; local things Li’s workshop did not meet; corrections of identification; categories Li’s sixteen rooms had squeezed. Bian warns against taking Zhao’s classical framing at face value. The framing is a charter. The practice is contemporary documentation.

This matters for Japan and Korea in a sideways way. Edo *honzōgaku* is, in the same centuries, also living past the *Gangmu* without leaving it — Ono Ranzan’s *keimō* is a clarification, Zhao’s *shiyi* is a supplement. Different verbs, same refusal of closure. Joseon medicine after *Donguibogam* is not waiting for Zhao; it has its own reprint and vernacular problems (draft 37). I do not want to make an East Asian “eighteenth-century turn.” I want to say that **the leftover became a respectable object** in more than one place.

Zhao also writes other medical books; I am not opening his collected works here. For this collection he is the man who makes Li’s machine look like what it was: a late-Ming stack, not the end of stuff.

The modern pharmacopeias will deal with leftovers by revision cycles and by supplementary herbal codes. That is a bureaucratic *shiyi*. I do not think the MFDS or the Chinese Pharmacopoeia Commission would thank me for the comparison. I still like it, as a comparison, not as a lineage.

## Open questions

- Which edition of the *Shiyi* should a second pass treat as working text, and how much of the “900” is editorial accumulation after 1765?
- How much of Zhao’s new material is genuinely new to *writing*, and how much is new to the *Gangmu*-centered page?

## Sources used

He Bian, “An Ever-Expanding Pharmacy” (2017), for social location and the argument about documentation versus classical framing. Handbook dates (1765) and fluctuating addition-counts. Chen Cangqi as the earlier *shiyi* (draft 14).

## Claims I am not making

- That Zhao “completed” Li.
- That new entries are newly discovered species.
- That I have audited either 716 or 900.
