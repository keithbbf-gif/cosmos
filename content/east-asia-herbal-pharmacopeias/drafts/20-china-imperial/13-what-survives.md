# What survives: Dunhuang, Japan, reconstruction

The *Xinxiu bencao* is famous, and it is mostly not there. That is not a tragedy to perform. It is the ordinary condition of Tang books. What is less ordinary is *how* it is not there: gone as a circulating complete work in China by the Song, present as fragments in a cave, present as a copy tradition in Japan, present as quotation in books that were trying to be about something else.

**Dunhuang.** Among the manuscripts taken from the sealed library are pieces of *bencao* text that scholars have identified with the *Xinxiu* or with its neighborhood. I say “neighborhood” because fragment identification is a specialist fight. A piece in the British Library, a piece in the National Library of China (the 臨.2371 discussion), comparisons of hand and wording — this is work I have read *about*, not work I have done. What I take from it: the Tang official book, or something wearing its clothes, was copied far from the capital, in a manuscript culture that also copied Buddhist sutras and private letters. That gives the “promulgation” story a little body. A little. One cave is not a distribution map.

**Japan.** The line that matters for reconstruction is a copy tradition associated with an eighth-century transmission (721 is the date that keeps being printed) and with later Japanese physicians who still had a book Chinese printers had replaced. Fukane no Sukehito’s *Honzō wamyō* is arranged on the *Xinxiu*’s bones (draft 24). *Ishinpō* quotes a “Bencao” that Mayanagi thinks is, in important stretches, the *Xinxiu* large-character layer (draft 25). In the Edo period, Taki Motoyasu prints old pharmaceutical books from shogunal library manuscripts. In the twentieth century, Fukane’s book is used, again, as a way back into a Tang table of contents. The Japanese copy is not a neutral fridge. It is a book that has been read, annotated, and put to Japanese office use for a thousand years. Reconstruction that forgets those readings will mistake a Heian gloss for a Tang sentence.

**Quotation tanks.** Chen Cangqi, the Song *Zhenglei* line, the *Gangmu* — they all ate Tang wording. Sometimes they label it. Sometimes they do not. A modern editor who “restores the *Xinxiu*” is choosing among these witnesses and among Japanese ones, and the choices should be visible. I have seen popular reprints that are not visible. Those reprints can be useful as reading texts. They are not the Tang book.

**What survival does to the story.** It makes Japan a co-owner of the first Chinese state *bencao*, whether nationalist histories like that or not. It makes a desert Buddhist library a co-owner. It makes every confident sentence about the 659 pictures a sentence about a lost object. It also makes later official Chinese books look less like a relay race. The Song did not hand the Tang baton forward. The Song built a new official book because the old one had become a ghost, and then the ghost kept turning up in other countries’ houses.

I have not seen the NLC fragment in person. I have not compared the Japanese manuscript line *juan* by *juan*. This file is a map of kinds of survival, not a catalog of every folio. A second pass should be a table: witness, holding, what *juan*, what identification, who disagrees.

Until that table exists, be suspicious of any English paragraph that begins “The *Xinxiu bencao* states…” and does not say *according to which witness*.

## Open questions

- Which Dunhuang fragments are securely *Xinxiu* and which are neighboring *bencao* or student copies?
- Is the 721 transmission date a colophon fact or a later Japanese bibliographic tradition? I have seen it repeated; I have not seen the colophon.

## Sources used

Toyo Bunko paper on NLC 臨.2371. British Library / Dunhuang bibliography as reported in handbook literature. Mayanagi on *Ishinpō* quotations. Kotobank / *Kokushi daijiten*-style entries on *Honzō wamyō* as a witness to *Xinxiu* order. Unschuld on Song reconstruction of lost official books.

## Claims I am not making

- That a complete Tang-period copy now exists in Japan in the 659 state. “A copy tradition exists” is the claim.
- That Dunhuang fragments represent typical provincial copies.
