# Zhenglei bencao: a private encyclopedia the state later ate

Tang Shenwei 唐慎微 was not, in the story that has come down, a court compiler. He was a physician, usually placed in Sichuan, who gathered other people’s books until his own book became the book. The title that later people shorten to *Zhenglei bencao* 證類本草 has longer official-looking skins — *Jingshi zhenglei beiji bencao* and the Daguan, Zhenghe, Shaoxing names that mark recensions and reigns. I will say *Zhenglei* for the family and name a recension when I am talking about a particular print.

The working picture: Tang takes the printed official bones (Kaibao / Jiayou) and Su Song’s illustrated classic, then pours in quotations from a couple of hundred other titles — medical, literary, anecdotal. The result is a page that looks like a conversation caught in wood. Handbook counts sit around 1,700-odd substances. A date you will see for a first public life is the early twelfth century (1108 is printed in some surveys). I treat the early-1100s as the moment the book is *out*, not as the moment Tang finished a manuscript in a single winter.

Why this object is a hinge:

**It preserves what official books were.** Because later people copied *Zhenglei*, we still meet Kaibao wording and *Tujing* pictures. The private encyclopedia becomes the fridge of the official series. That is an irony worth keeping in the mouth.

**It makes quotation the method.** The *Gangmu* will do this even harder. Once a *bencao* is a weaving of named sources, the editor is a librarian of drugs as much as a physician. Unschuld’s team spends volumes just trying to say who is being named. The mess starts here in a serious way.

**It is unofficial, then it is not.** Reign-title recensions, commercial reprints, a 1185 reprint that people still meet — the state and the market both decide this is the book to run. Liu Wentai’s 1505 palace album will still be digesting *Zhenglei* (draft 18). Li Shizhen will still be living in it (drafts 19–20). A private Sichuan compilation becomes the default memory of “what the Song knew.”

I do not want to turn Tang Shenwei into a rustic genius. We have him through a success so large it erased the working table. The “more than 240 books” figure is a later boast-shape. Maybe he had that many. Maybe a recension’s editors did. The practice is what we can see: **attach the source to the sentence.** That practice is a claims-guard. It is also a way to be wrong with a name attached, which is at least a better wrong.

The *Zhenglei* page is hard to read if you want a single voice. That is a virtue. A single voice is what the Tang court wanted. Tang Shenwei’s book says the court was never the only speaker. Song print is what lets that chorus travel.

When this collection talks about “the Chinese tradition” arriving in Japan or Korea in the late imperial period, it is often this family of pages that is actually in the luggage — or the *Gangmu* that ate this family. Not the 659 book. Not the farmer. A printed conversation.

## Open questions

- What can be securely attributed to Tang Shenwei’s own notes as opposed to Daguan/Zhenghe/Shaoxing editorial layers?
- How did Sichuan medical culture, specifically, shape the quotation diet? I have seen the Sichuan placement repeated; I have not seen a study I trust on his library.

## Sources used

Handbook accounts of Tang Shenwei and the *Zhenglei* recension names. Unschuld on the *Zhenglei* as Li Shizhen’s main meal. Image-and-text survival of *Tujing* through this line. 1108 as a commonly printed publication-date; not independently confirmed by me as a first-block date.

## Claims I am not making

- That the *Zhenglei* is a transparent window onto pre-Song texts. It is a Song (and later) editorial window.
- That official recension names mean Tang Shenwei became a court official.
