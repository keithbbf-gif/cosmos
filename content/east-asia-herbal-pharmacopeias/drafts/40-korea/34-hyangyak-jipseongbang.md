Sejong’s court likes a big official book. The medical one that belongs in this collection is *Hyangyak jipseongbang* 鄕藥集成方 — collected and standardized prescriptions of local botanicals. The compilation is ordered in 1431, in the usual account, through Jiphyeonjeon names: Yu Hyotong 兪孝通, No Jungnye 盧重禮, Park Yundeok 朴允德. The print is 1433. Kwon Chae writes a preface. Jeolla and Gangwon are told to cut it. Reprints in 1478 and, later, 1633 (Hullyeon Dogam small type) appear in the encyclopedia record. I am following the Encyclopedia of Korean Culture. I have not collated the reprints.

The book is described as an expansion of the 1399 *jesaeng* compilation: old symptom-categories growing from a few hundred to 959, formulas from a couple of thousand to 10,706, plus acupuncture items and a *hyangyak bencao* with processing notes, the whole in 85 *gwon*. Those numbers are the book’s (or the encyclopedia’s) self-description. They are the right scale for a state project. They are not my count of a copy.

Two sibling projects sit next to it and tell you what kind of object this is.

**A gathering calendar.** *Hyangyak chaechwi wollyeong*, ordered of the same men around 1428, is a monthly ordinance: when to take the local thing, under what vernacular name, how the taste and the drying go. That is procurement language. It belongs with Tang tribute geography and with Su Song’s prefectural pictures. A court that writes a calendar for gathering has decided that identity is seasonal labor, not only a graph.

**A geography.** The *Sejong sillok* geographic treatises are being finished in the same years. Local products, including drugs, become part of knowing the provinces. The herbal and the map are roommates.

Suh’s article is again the caution: “local” here is a court category. The structure of the book, she notes, still lives in older organizational habits — including, in her analysis, a debt to Tao Hongjing’s kind of arrangement and to Chinese emergency titles. A Joseon official *hyangyak* book is not a rejection of the Chinese page. It is a Korean official use of the Chinese page to house Korean supply.

Is it a pharmacopeia? Closer than the 1236 emergency book, because it includes a *bencao* and processing, and because it wants to be the standard of a kingdom’s local drugs. Still not a legal assay-book. It is a collected formulary-plus-identity project. I will call it **the fifteenth-century Joseon official local materia medica**, and I will not call it the Korean *Xinxiu*. The comparison flatters both books in the wrong way.

What it does for the rest of the stage: it makes Heo Jun possible without making him first. A seventeenth-century royal encyclopedia that cares about native herbs and vernacular names is working in a country that already spent Sejong’s reign staffing that care. The UNESCO prose around *Donguibogam* sometimes writes as if 1613 invented public herbal knowledge. The 1433 book is the polite cough from the previous century.

I have not read the 85 *gwon*. A second pass should pick one local-drug monograph and one formula and show how the vernacular name, the Chinese graph, and the gathering note sit on the same page. That would be worth more than another summary of the counts.

## Open questions

- How much of the 10,706 formulas is duplication, recategorization, or genuine addition to 1399? Encyclopedia numbers do not say.
- What survives of the *wollyeong*, and has anyone mapped it onto the *sillok* geography?

## Sources used

Encyclopedia of Korean Culture, “향약집성방” (1431 order, 1433 print, editors, 1399 base, counts, 1478 and 1633 reprints, sibling *wollyeong*). Suh 2009 on structure and the layered local. Draft 10’s procurement theme as a comparison, not a lineage.

## Claims I am not making

- That 85 *gwon* of official paper produced uniform provincial practice.
- That the formulas are clinically validated.
- That I have handled a 1433 printing.
