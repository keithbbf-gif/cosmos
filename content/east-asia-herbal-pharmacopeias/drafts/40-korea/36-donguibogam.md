# Donguibogam: encyclopedia, not just a drug list

*Donguibogam* 東醫寶鑑 is the Korean book the world has been asked to remember. UNESCO put it on the Memory of the World Register in 2009. The National Library of Korea holds a first printing. The Library of Congress has a record that says Seoul, Royal Hospital, 1613. The nomination file says Heo Jun 許浚 finished the editorial work in 1610 and the Naeuiwon printed it in 1613 with movable wooden type, twenty-five volumes, five parts after two contents volumes. I will use **compiled under royal order, completed about 1610, printed 1613**. I will treat UNESCO’s adjectives as adjectives.

Heo Jun (you will see 1539 or 1546 to 1615) is a court physician. The commission comes from Seonjo, in a kingdom that has been through the Imjin war and through famine. Later prose likes to say the book was for the people. The book is a royal medical encyclopedia. Those can both be true. They are not the same statement. A court can want a public-facing medical order for reasons that include legitimacy, administration, and, yes, a wish that fewer people die. I will not pick the nicest reason and call it history.

The five parts, as every introduction repeats: *Naegyeong* (inner body / the body’s treasures), *Oehyeong* (outer form), *Japbyeong* (various diseases), *Tangaek* (decoctions — the herbal-pharmacy section), *Chimgu* (acupuncture and moxibustion). Only one of those is a drug list in the *bencao* sense, and even *Tangaek* is a formulary world as much as a monograph world. **This is not a pharmacopeia.** It is an encyclopedia that contains a pharmacopeia-sized argument: what to gather, when, how long a prepared thing is said to keep, what the vernacular name is, how a Korean reader might find a Korean plant for a problem a Chinese monograph once described.

The title’s *Dongui* — Eastern medicine — is a positioning. Not “Korean medicine” as a modern brand, not “Chinese medicine” as a humble import. East, from a court that has to live with Ming (and then Qing) as the other literate medical pole. I will not over-read a two-graph direction into a nationalist manifesto. I will not under-read it into a neutral synonym for “medicine.”

UNESCO and the later Korean state like two further claims: that the book invented, or at least developed, preventive medicine and state public health “unknown until the nineteenth century.” Those are nomination sentences. What I will keep: the encyclopedia spends real space on maintenance, food, and the body’s inner stores, and it was printed by a royal medical office for use beyond a single palace body. What I will not keep: a world-historical first in “public health.” Public health is a later professional and legal object. Joseon had medical administration. That is already a serious fact.

Heo Jun did not write alone. The nomination file says “collective support of medical experts and literati.” Good. Draft 19’s workshop point applies. The face is Heo. The labor is an office.

China and Japan received the book. That is not a vague compliment; there are reprints and citations. I have not mapped them. A second pass should. Reception is not the same as “informed the evolution of medicine in East Asia and beyond,” which is the UNESCO trumpet.

For this collection, *Donguibogam* is the Korean file that most tempts a statue. The statue is already built. I am more interested in the *Tangaek* pages as a seventeenth-century attempt to make a kingdom’s drugs findable, and in the fact that this attempt sits on a fifteenth-century *hyangyak* basement the statue sometimes forgets.

I will not quote a formula. I will not say the book heals. I will say it is the most famous Joseon medical encyclopedia, that its drug section is one chapter of a five-part machine, and that its 1613 print is a dated object we can still hold — unlike the *Xinxiu*, unlike the Kaibao, unlike Chen Cangqi. Sometimes the humble virtue is survival in the place that made the book.

## Open questions

- Which 1613 copies are true first print and how do later resets differ in the *Tangaek* names?
- How does Heo Jun cite the 1433 *jipseongbang* and the *Gangmu* — as ancestors, as rivals, as silent furniture?

## Claims I am not making

- That this is “the first medical text” on the Memory of the World list as a scholarly ranking. It is a 2009 inscription fact that UNESCO’s own magazine repeated.
- That preventive-medicine firsts are demonstrated.
- That I have read the twenty-five volumes.
