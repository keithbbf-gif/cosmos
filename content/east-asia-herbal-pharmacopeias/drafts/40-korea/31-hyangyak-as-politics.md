# Hyangyak: the local as a political category

If you take one Korean word from this collection, take *hyangyak* 鄕藥. It does not mean “herb.” It means the drug that is *of the district* — local, as against the article that comes from China under a Tang or later prestige name. Soyoung Suh has already done the work I am not going to redo: the local is layered. It is made in lists, in vernacular names, in fiscal arguments, in the shame and pride of using a substitute. I am going to keep the word untranslated after this page so it cannot collapse into “Korean folk medicine.”

Why a polity needs the word:

Imported Chinese materia medica are expensive. They move through tribute, trade, and war. They are also, in the literate medical imagination, often the *right* thing — the monograph was written about a Sichuan or Jiangnan article, not about the plant on a Korean hillside. A kingdom that cannot always buy the right thing has three choices: do without, pretend the local is the same, or build a literature that says the local is good *as local*. Goryeo and early Joseon spend a remarkable amount of official paper on the third.

That is not a folk revival. The books I will walk through — *Hyangyak gugeupbang*, the 1399 *jesaeng* compilation, the 1433 *jipseongbang* — are office products. They have editors with posts. They have print. They have, in the fifteenth century, a companion *monthly ordinance* for gathering. They sit next to the *Sejong sillok* geography project. Suh’s “layers of the local” is the right suspicion: some “local” items are Chinese drugs that happen to be grown or substituted in Korea; some are Korean things given Chinese graphs; some are names that hide a chain of look-alikes.

Two refusals.

**I will not say *hyangyak* is the essence of Korean medicine.** Later nationalist histories like that sentence. The same centuries that compile local herbals also compile *Uibang yuchwi*, a giant that swallows Chinese formularies, and later *Donguibogam*, which is an “Eastern” synthesis, not a village list. The local and the oceanic live in the same court.

**I will not say *hyangyak* is only poverty.** It is also a claim of adequacy. A state that can name, gather, process, and prescribe from its own ground is a state that has a medical policy. Bang Saryang’s late-Goryeo / early-Joseon argument, as later historians reconstruct it, ties local herbs to a ban on luxury imports and to the finances of a new dynasty. That is not romance. That is a budget.

Japan has a cousin problem (*wamyō*, “does it grow here?”) and does not, in the surviving early titles, make the cousin into a dynasty-founding compilation series. China has local *bencao* too — regional monographs, later Qing leftovers — but the official series pretends to the empire. Korea’s official fifteenth century is unusually willing to put *hyang* in the title of the big book. That willingness is the Korean fact this stage is about.

When a modern Korean pharmacopeia and a separate herbal specification book (draft 38) still have to decide what a name points at, they are still inside the *hyangyak* problem: identity, substitute, import, law. The graphs have changed. The problem has not retired.

## Open questions

- How much of the *hyang-* in Goryeo titles is already a literate fashion rather than a supply crisis? Fashion and crisis can travel together.
- Where do island and northern-border substances sit in “the local”? A peninsula-word can hide a lot of edges.

## Sources used

Soyoung Suh, “Herbs of Our Own Kingdom” (2009) and *Naming the Local* (2017). Shin Dongwon and handbook chapters on Goryeo–Joseon medical policy. Encyclopedia of Korean Culture entries on the titled *hyangyak* books (drafts 32–34). Bang Saryang as reported in later syntheses (draft 33).

## Claims I am not making

- That local drugs were “just as good” in a clinical sense. That is a claim the books sometimes want. I do not adjudicate it.
- That *hyangyak* is independent of Chinese textual models. It is written *in relation* to them.
