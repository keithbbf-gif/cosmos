The oldest surviving Korean medical book, in the usual account, is a small emergency volume with a long title: *Hyangyak gugeupbang* 鄕藥救急方 — emergency prescriptions with local drugs. The compilation is placed in Goryeo, around 1236, at the Tripitaka office on Ganghwa, in the years when the court is also cutting a canon against the Mongol war. The Joseon reprint of 1417, done in Uiheung (today’s Gunwi), is the printing later people can still point at. The Goryeo and the 1417 Korean copies are both described as lost on the peninsula. A 1417 copy sits, we are told, in the Archives and Mausolea Department of the Imperial Household Agency in Tokyo. I have not handled it. I am working from the Encyclopedia of Korean Culture and from scholars who have.

That custody sentence should stay loud. **The oldest Korean medical book survives as a Joseon reprint in a Japanese imperial archive.** A nationalist story has to swallow that or lie. This collection will swallow it.

What the book is, as described:

Three *gwon* in one volume, movable type in the 1417 story. Emergency prescriptions arranged by problem, including women’s and children’s sections, swellings, wind, madness, fevers, headache, and then — this is why it is in a pharmacopeia folder — notes on how to take drugs, on incompatibilities, and an appended list of the local botanicals used in the formulas, with vernacular names. The *hyangmyeong* are written in the *idu* / loan-graph practices of the time, which makes the book a language witness as well as a medical one. Linguists have been here. I will not pretend to be one. I will say: **the vernacular name is doing the same work Fukane’s *wamyō* did, in a poorer, more urgent genre.** Not a court dictionary. A book for when you cannot get the Chinese article and someone is already sick.

1236 is a “around” date. You will also see 1245 in some English pages. I will stay with the Korean encyclopedia’s *circa* 1236 (Gojong 23) and mark the other year as a reminder that Goryeo dating in secondary English is sloppy.

The Tripitaka office as publisher is a fact I like and do not want to over-read. A state that is carving a Buddhist canon can also carve or set an emergency herbal. That does not make the herbal Buddhist. It makes the office a place that can print.

Is it a pharmacopeia? No. It is a formulary with a local-drug list attached. It belongs here because the list is one of the first times Korean writing *stabilizes local identities in public*, and because later Joseon compilations treat this Goryeo work as an ancestor they can still eat.

I will not excerpt a prescription. Emergency books are easy to misuse. The claims-guard is not only about efficacy. It is about not turning a 1417 reprint into a first-aid manual.

## Open questions

- Has the Tokyo copy been fully digitized and collated against later quotations in Joseon books? A second pass should start there, not in a summary encyclopedia.
- How many of the *hyangmyeong* can be securely mapped onto modern Korean plant names, and who did that mapping?

## Sources used

Encyclopedia of Korean Culture, “향약구급방” (1236 Tripitaka office, 1417 Uiheung reprint, Tokyo custody, vernacular names, appended local-drug list). Suh 2009 on the local as a category. English 1245-date noted as a conflicting secondary figure.

## Claims I am not making

- That I have seen the Tokyo copy.
- That 1236 is a colophon fact I have read.
- That the book proves an independent Korean pharmacy in the modern national sense. It proves a Goryeo office printed emergencies with local names.
