# Food as drug, and the edge of the list

A *bencao* is never only herbs. Grain, fruit, meat, salt, water, a distilled liquor, a fermented paste — they keep walking into the list as if the list had no door. That is not a confusion. It is a theory of stuff. If the world is made of things with taste and qi, then supper and medicine share a metaphysics. The upper-grade fantasy in draft 07 (the thing you can live on) is a food fantasy wearing a pharmaceutical hat.

The Tang century, which gives us the first state *bencao*, also gives us — in quotation and in later recovery — a literature that makes the overlap explicit. *Shiliao bencao* 食療本草, “materia medica for food therapy,” is the title most often attached to Meng Shen and to a later thickening associated with Zhang Ding. The original book is not sitting on my desk. Pieces of it live in the same quotation tanks as Chen Cangqi and the official Tang book. *Ishinpō* is one of the places those pieces still have a body. I mention it when I talk about Japanese archives (draft 25). I mention it here to say: **the edge was already a genre.**

Why guard this? Because English “food as medicine” has become a wellness slogan, and the slogan will eat this file if I let it. Tang *shiliao* is not a smoothie recipe. It is a literate attempt to classify everyday substances in the same voice as the drug books, for readers who already live inside taste, season, and contraindication. I will not translate its claimed effects into nutrients. I will not say it anticipated dietetics. Dietetics is a later profession. This is a neighboring shelf.

The edge matters for the rest of the collection in four ways.

**Counts.** When a later encyclopedia boasts a larger number of “drugs,” some of the growth is the kitchen walking in. Compare lists before you compare genius.

**Class.** Li Shizhen’s sixteen *bu* will have grains, vegetables, fruits, as their own rooms. That is a decision about the house, not a discovery that rice exists.

**Korea.** *Donguibogam* spends serious space on food and on the body as something you maintain, not only something you rescue. UNESCO prose likes to call this “preventive medicine.” The phrase is modern. The editorial choice is real: a royal medical encyclopedia that refuses to let the kitchen be beneath the clinic.

**Law.** A modern pharmacopeia often has to decide whether a thing is a food, a supplement, or a drug. The old lists do not help that decision, because they were not written to help it. People who cite a *bencao* entry to say “this was always a food in China” or “this was always a drug” are picking a later legal fight with an earlier mixed page.

There are other edge-books I am not giving their own drafts: the *Yinshan zhengyao* (the Yuan dietary classic for the court), the various *bencao* of tea, the specialized perfume and incense lists. They belong in a larger history of substances. They appear here only to keep the main series from pretending it had a clean object.

If I am guilty of scope-creep by including this file in a “pharmacopeia” collection, the guilt is intentional. A collection that only followed official drug codes would miss why official drug codes keep having chapters that smell like lunch.

## Open questions

- Where should a second pass put Meng Shen / *Shiliao* — as a Tang *bencao* variant, as a dietary genre, or as a quotation-cloud that should not have a single author?
- How much of *Ishinpō*’s food-and-drug material is *Shiliao* and how much is other Tang titles? Mayanagi’s work is the door; I have not walked the table.

## Claims I am not making

- That historical food-*bencao* entries are nutritional advice.
- That “food as medicine” is an ancient slogan we are entitled to reuse in a clinic.
