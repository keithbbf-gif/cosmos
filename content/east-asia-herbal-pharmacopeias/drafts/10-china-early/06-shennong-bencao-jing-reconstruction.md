There is no Han-period copy of the *Shennong bencao jing* 神農本草經 on anyone’s desk. What we have is a classic that later people assembled from other people’s quotations, and then printed, annotated, and taught as if the assembly were a recovery. I do not say that to be clever. I say it because almost every popular sentence about “the oldest Chinese pharmacopeia” is a sentence about this reconstructed object, and the reconstruction has a history of its own.

The usual shape, the one you will meet in handbooks, is this. An old text in three *juan*, terse, almost aphoristic, listing substances in three grades, conventionally 365 items to match a year. Tao Hongjing, around 500, says he has versions, and he embeds the old words in a larger work, distinguishing them — in the story of the book — by color of ink. Song printers cannot do red and black the same way, so they invent a typographic substitute: large characters and small, white and black, “new attachments” marked as such. From those layered Song books, especially the *Zhenglei* line, Qing and modern scholars pull “the *Benjing* text” back out and publish it as a book. You can buy that book. It is a book. It is not a tomb text.

What can be said with a straight face:

- By the late Han or the Three Kingdoms, writers are referring to a *Bencao* or a *Shennong bencao* as if a reader might know what they meant. The exact title is not stable. Chinese bibliographic work (I am thinking of recent essays that comb *Han shu*, *Sui shu*, and early *Erya* commentaries) keeps pushing the first firm citations around. I will not freeze a year.
- Tao Hongjing is the first person whose editorial practice we can describe in any density. Without him, the reconstruction has no spine. See draft 08.
- The *Taiping yulan* and the *Zhenglei* series are the great quotation tanks. A sentence that exists only in one late encyclopedia is a weaker witness than a sentence that sits in the same form in several independent streams. Most popular editions do not show you the stemma.
- Japanese witnesses matter. *Ishinpō* quotes “Bencao” material that Mayanagi and others have tried to align with the *Xinxiu* large-character text, which itself claims to carry Tao’s old layer. That is a witness of a witness. Still better than a modern typeset *Jing* with no apparatus.

What cannot be said with a straight face:

- That we know the original table of contents.
- That 365 is a Han fact rather than a cosmological edit. The number is perfect. Perfect numbers in this literature are suspects.
- That every item in a modern *Shennong bencao jing* was in every early recension.
- That botanical identifications printed next to those items are Han identifications. They are later guesses, some responsible, some patriotic, some commercial.

Unschuld’s warning stays in force: we may be dealing with a title that several Han collections could wear. If that is right, Tao was already doing the thing later scholars do — making one book out of a noise of books. The *Jing* in our libraries would then be a reconstruction of a reconstruction. I am comfortable leaving that possibility open. I am not comfortable narrating it away.

Why does this file sit so early? Because China, Japan, and Korea all inherit a gesture: *the old classic said*. Official books mark the old words as a privileged layer. Private encyclopedias quote them to disagree. A Heian name-list is arranged on a Tang official book that was itself arranged on Tao who was himself arranged on “Shennong.” If we treat the bottom layer as solid earth, the whole stack looks like transmission. If we treat it as a working surface, the stack looks like what it is — people arguing with a moving text.

I have not made my own recension. This draft is a warning label to glue onto every later mention. When a subsequent file says “the *Benjing* classifies ginseng as an upper drug,” read: *in the reconstructed, Tao-and-after text that later printers marked as the old layer, this item sits in the upper grade.* Longer. Bearable.

## Open questions

- Which modern critical edition of the *Jing* should a second pass standardize on, and what does its apparatus actually show about variant quotation?
- How much of the three-*juan* structure is Tao’s, how much is bibliographic convention, and how much is cosmology?

## Claims I am not making

- That the reconstructed *Jing* is useless. It is one of the most worked objects in the field. Worked is not original.
- That I have collapsed the quotation stemma. I have not.
