The reconstructed *Shennong* text, and the books that grow out of it, do not open with chemistry. They open with a social metaphysic of stuff. Substances are *shangpin* 上品, *zhongpin* 中品, *xiapin* 下品 — upper, middle, lower. The usual gloss, the one that gets printed on museum cards, is roughly this: upper drugs nourish life and can be taken for a long time; middle drugs treat illness and have a double edge; lower drugs attack the disease hard and are not to be taken long. I am paraphrasing a later teaching voice, not quoting a Han manuscript. Even as paraphrase it is already a machine for ranking the world.

Notice what the machine does. It makes longevity and toxicity into a single scale. It makes “food-like” substances look morally higher than “attacking” ones. It makes a poison useful and, at the same time, low. Aconite can be indispensable in a later clinic’s imagination and still sit in the basement of the list. Ginseng can sit in the attic without anyone having to prove, in a modern sense, what it does. The attic and the basement are claims about how a body and a cosmos are supposed to work. They are not assays.

They are also political. An upper drug is the kind of thing a culture-hero might taste and live. A lower drug is the kind of thing a state still needs in the storehouse because people get the diseases the sage-kings did not abolish. Courts that later sponsor *bencao* books keep the three grades long after they have other ways to classify — by mineral/plant/animal, by unctuousness, by channel, by prefecture. The grades survive because they do something a botanical key does not: they tell a reader what kind of person they become by taking the thing.

I want to be careful not to freeze the contents of each grade. Items move. Editors argue. Song books mark new attachments. Li Shizhen will eventually throw the whole furniture out and build sixteen *bu* on another principle (draft 20). The Ming palace album of 1505 still uses upper/middle/lower in one of its axes while also using a twenty-four-field descriptive grid. Classification in this literature is not a single tree. It is a set of overlays. The three grades are the overlay that most often gets mistaken for nature.

Japan and Korea inherit the graphs. They do not always inherit the moral heat. A Heian name-book may follow the Tang official order because that is the textbook the office already has. A Joseon local-herb compilation may care more about whether the thing grows in the province than whether it is “upper.” When a later nationalist history says the three grades prove an ancient ecological wisdom, I hear a modern moral being poured back into a medieval machine. Possible. Not shown.

There is a clinical rumor that still travels: upper means safe. The old texts do not give you that warranty, and I will not either. “Taken for a long time” in a cosmological dietetics is not a toxicology report. Some substances that sat high are biologically active in ways a modern lab would not call gentle. Some that sat low are mild in one preparation and lethal in another. The grade is a claim in a book. The plant does not read the book.

If this collection has a recurring suspicion, it starts here: **the order of a list is an argument.** Alphabetical order is an argument (it pretends names are the key). Prefectural order is an argument (it pretends the empire’s map is the key). Grade order is an argument about virtue, time, and risk. When I describe a book’s structure in later drafts, I am describing its argument, not its discovery of reality.

## Open questions

- When does the three-grade scheme first appear as an explicit preface-doctrine rather than as a later teaching about the *Jing*? I have not isolated the first preface that theorizes it in full.
- How do Korean and Japanese textbooks of the seventeenth century teach the grades — as living clinic, as classical furniture, or as both?

## Sources used

Handbook accounts of the three-grade scheme in the reconstructed *Jing* and in Tao’s arrangement. Unschuld on pharmaceutical literature as a distinct therapeutic ideology from correspondence medicine. Later overlay systems: *Zhenglei* structure; Li Shizhen’s sixteen *bu* (draft 20); *Pinhui jingyao*’s twenty-four fields (draft 18).

## Claims I am not making

- That upper drugs are safe or that lower drugs are simply poisons.
- That 365 items in three grades is a Han inventory of the natural world.
