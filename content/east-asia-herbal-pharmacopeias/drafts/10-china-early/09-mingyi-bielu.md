Beside the short classic, the early *bencao* world had a second pile: extra records, later names, things “famous physicians” had used that the old list had not bothered to keep. The title that gathers that pile is *Mingyi bielu* 名醫別錄 — supplementary records of famous physicians. It is usually tied to Tao Hongjing’s workshop, sometimes described as a work he compiled, sometimes as a body of material he used. I am not going to pretend those are the same statement.

What the title is doing is clearer than who owned it. *Bielu* says: this is the other register. Not the canon. The annex. In a manuscript culture, the annex is where a living clinic leaks into a book. It is also where a later editor can park material he wants without having to claim the farmer wrote it. That is a claims-guard *inside* the tradition, and I want to give it credit. Someone already knew that not every drug sentence should wear the *Jing*’s hat.

Later official books keep the distinction even as they bind the piles together. Song printers mark old-classic items one way and later attachments another. The Kaibao editors, in the preface story, talk about white characters and black characters and “newly attached” drugs. They are still living in a page-image of layers. The *Bielu* is one of the names they give the middle time — after the farmer, before us.

I do not have an independent *Bielu* on my desk any more than I have an independent *Jing*. What I have is a category that reconstructions and citation dictionaries still need, because Li Shizhen and his predecessors keep saying *Bielu*. When the *Gangmu* attributes a sentence that way, Unschuld’s team will tell you the attribution is sometimes wrong. Secondary transmission: a later book says the *Bielu* said, and the *Gangmu* copies the later book. That is ordinary, and it is lethal to anyone who wants to write “the Six Dynasties clinic used X because the *Bielu* lists it.”

Why keep a whole draft for a book we cannot hold? Because **the extra drugs are the first place this literature admits growth**. A closed 365 is a cosmology. An annex is a history. Once you have an annex, a Tang court can add tribute items, a Tang private scholar can add folk names (draft 14), a Song bureau can add what the prefectures sent, a Ming family can add what they saw on the road, a Qing secretary can add what came off a ship (draft 22). The *Bielu* is not the cause of that. It is the first named permission.

Japan’s *Ishinpō* and the Heian name-book will quote “Bencao” without always meaning our reconstructed *Jing*. Some of what they quote may be *Bielu* material, or Tao’s combined page, or the Tang official layer. I will not sort those sentences here. I will only say that “the extra records” traveled as part of the same ocean of quotation, and that Korean and Japanese readers met a layered Chinese page, not a pure classic plus a later national extra.

If you take one habit from this file: when a later list is longer, do not call the extra items “discoveries.” Call them **admissions** — of trade, of local practice, of a compiler’s curiosity, of a state’s new border. Discovery is a prize word. Admission is closer to how a *bielu* works.

## Open questions

- Is there a defensible way to edit a stand-alone *Mingyi bielu*, or should the name remain a layer-tag inside Tao and the Song books?
- How often does *Gangmu* attribution to *Bielu* survive a check against earlier quotation tanks? Unschuld’s dictionary is the place a second pass should live for a month.
