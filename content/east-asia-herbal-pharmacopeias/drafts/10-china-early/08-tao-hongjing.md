# Tao Hongjing and the collected commentaries

Tao Hongjing 陶弘景 (456–536) is the first person in this collection I am willing to treat as an editor we can almost see. That is not a compliment in the modern professional sense. He was a Daoist polymath at the Liang court and in the mountains, a man who wrote on alchemy, maps, and the old drug books. The work that matters here is the *Bencao jing jizhu* 本草經集注 — collected commentaries on the *Bencao jing* — usually placed around the year 500. The original as a complete book is not sitting in a modern library in Tao’s hand. What we have, again, is a work that later official books swallowed and that scholars now pick back out.

What later prefaces and reconstructions agree on, enough that I will use it as a working picture: Tao took a short old classic and a mass of additional material — including what gets called the *Mingyi bielu*, the extra records of famous physicians (draft 09) — and made a single arranged text. He distinguished layers. The famous distinction is color: red for the old classic, black for the later notes, or some version of that practice. Whether every early copy actually used red pigment, or whether “red and black” is already a way later people remembered a visual hierarchy, I am not in a position to swear. Song editors, facing print, say the colors have drifted and they will mark the layers another way. That complaint is itself evidence: by the tenth century, people thought Tao had left a visible archaeology in the page.

Tao also enlarges. The number-stories vary, as number-stories do, but the direction is not in doubt. More substances. More words about place, appearance, processing. More of the world the old aphorisms did not bother to describe. He is not a conduit. He is a thickening.

I care about him for three reasons that are not hagiography.

First, **he makes philology a pharmaceutical act**. He talks about place-names that belong to Han administration. He talks about bad copies. He talks about more than one *Shennong* text. That means the “classic” arrives in our history already as a problem, not as a tablet.

Second, **he is the hinge the Tang court will use**. The memorial that starts the *Xinxiu bencao* project, in the usual telling, is a request to copy and repair Tao. The first state *bencao* is not a new idea of a book. It is a state deciding that Tao’s private (or at least non-imperial-print) arrangement is too important, and too corrupt, to leave alone. Draft 12 is that decision.

Third, **his geography is already an empire of supply**. When a later Japanese name-book wants to know whether a Tang drug grows at home, it is asking a question Tao’s kind of entry made normal: *where is this from, and what does it look like when it is the right one?* Identity and logistics are the same sentence.

Tao is easy to romanticize — mountain, alchemist, last man who knew. I will not. We have him through later books that needed an ancestor. Some of what is called “Tao’s note” in a Song print may be a Song guess about which unattributed sentence sounds like him. That is an ordinary problem in layered texts. It is also why I will not write “Tao says” about a pharmacological effect. I will write “the layer later marked as Tao’s commentary, in this recension, says the text claims…” — or I will shorten that, and you will remember this paragraph.

His dates I have given as the common ones. His authorship of every alchemical and medical title attached to him is a separate tangle I am not solving here. For this collection he is the editor who made the *bencao* look like a book a court could later staff.

## Open questions

- What is the best current reconstruction of *Jizhu*, and how much of it is actually shared wording versus editorial inference?
- How should we talk about Tao’s Daoist and alchemical work without letting it become a personality cult that then “explains” the drug book?

## Sources used

Unschuld 1986 on Tao as the first thick editor. Later official prefaces (Tang and Kaibao, as transmitted) that describe the red/black problem. Standard biographical dates 456–536 as handbook consensus. The Tang project’s dependence on Tao: draft 12.

## Claims I am not making

- That we possess Tao’s manuscript.
- That Tao “saved” a Han original we can now read cleanly.
