If you walk through a Chinese pharmacy museum you will meet a man with a horned brow and a handful of grasses. Labels will say he tasted a hundred herbs and gave medicine to the people, and that he lived in some round-numbered millennium BCE. The man is Shennong 神農, the Divine Farmer. He is one of the culture-heroes who make agriculture, fire, markets, or writing look like gifts instead of arguments. He is not a historical author. I need that sentence early, because a book titled *Shennong bencao jing* will otherwise start this collection as if a Bronze Age farmer had a desk.

What we can say without swelling: later Chinese writers, certainly by the time Tao Hongjing is arranging pharmaceutical texts in the late fifth and early sixth centuries, treat “Shennong” as the proper name of an old drug classic, or of a family of such texts. Tao is already doing philology. He notices administrative place-names that smell like the Han. He talks as if he has more than one recension in the room. That is our first solid air. Everything before it — the tasting, the year 2800 BCE, the personal authorship — is story, and the story has work to do. It makes a drug list older than empire. It makes experiment look like sacrifice. It makes a later editor look humble.

Paul Unschuld put the uncomfortable version in print decades ago: we cannot presently prove that there was a single original work titled *Shennong bencao jing* in the Han, as opposed to several pharmaceutical collections that wore that title or one like it. I am not going to “split the difference” and say “compiled in the Han by followers of Shennong.” Followers of a figure are still a legend with a committee attached.

The word *bencao* itself is older than the surviving book. It shows up in Han bibliographic and historical writing as a category of knowledge and as a title-element. That matters. It means a Han reader could already point at a kind of text — a literature of roots and herbs — without our being able to open the copy. Absence from one catalog is not absence from the world; presence of a word is not a table of contents. Draft 06 lives in that gap.

Why attach the farmer at all? Because pharmaceutical knowledge, in the early Chinese medical field Unschuld describes, sat awkwardly beside the other great invention of the late Warring States and Han: a medicine of systematic correspondence, the *Huangdi neijing* world, where health is a matter of living inside natural law. Drug lists are messier. They assume that bodies break and that stuff from outside can be put in. They collect. They smell of markets, mountains, and hearsay. Putting Shennong on the door does two things at once. It gives the messy list a sage. And it keeps the list from having to pretend it is the Inner Classic.

I do not know when the pairing became inevitable. I know it became useful. Every later official *bencao* that opens by bowing to Shennong is borrowing a charter. The Tang court book does it. The Song printed books do it. Li Shizhen, who is otherwise willing to argue with the dead, still works in a title-world the farmer underwrites. Japan and Korea inherit the graphs and the bow. They do not inherit a man.

There is a modern temptation to rescue Shennong as a “symbol of empirical method” — he tasted, therefore proto-science. I will not do that. Tasting as a story is a story about risk and kingship. It is not a lab notebook. If we want empiricism in this literature we will find it later, in people who go to a prefecture and ask for a picture, or who walk a mountain and say the old book has the wrong leaf. Those people sometimes invoke Shennong. They do not need him to be real.

When a draft after this one says “the *Shennong* text,” it means the reconstructed, layered classic described in draft 06. It does not mean a person. If I slip and write “Shennong says,” catch it.

## Open questions

- What is the earliest securely dated use of the two-graph title *Shennong bencao*, as opposed to *bencao* alone? I have seen Han-and-after claims; I want a table of first attestations before this file hardens.
- How much of the popular tasting legend is medieval and how much is modern illustrated-book culture? I have not sorted the iconography.

## Claims I am not making

- That Shennong lived, tasted, or wrote.
- That the Han “must have” possessed one canonical drug classic of 365 entries. That number is a later shape. See draft 06.
