This collection will fail in a familiar way if it is not watched: a cautious paragraph will end with a clean date, the clean date will be repeated, and by the third file the caution will have fallen off. I have seen it in my own notes. The guard is a set of habits against that gravity.

**Legendary people are not authors.** Shennong does not write. The Yellow Emperor does not write. When a title puts them in the byline, the title is doing political and cosmological work. The historical question is when someone started writing under that protection, and what later editors thought they were recovering.

**A lost book is not available for paraphrase.** If the *Xinxiu bencao* is gone in China and partly recoverable from Japanese copies, Dunhuang fragments, and quotations, then every sentence about “what the Tang book said” is a sentence about a reconstruction. I will try to name the witness. When I fail, treat the sentence as soft.

**Counts are artifacts of editions.** “1,892 drugs” in the *Gangmu*, “468 monographs” in JP 1, “531” in ChP 1953, “85 *gwon*” in the 1433 Korean compilation — these numbers come from particular prefaces, catalogs, or commission histories. They move when someone recounts appendices, synonyms, or illustrations. I use them as order-of-magnitude facts and as claims about how a book presented itself. I do not use them as a census of nature.

**“First” needs a class.** First *state-sponsored* Chinese *bencao* in the usual Tang story. First *printed* official *bencao* in the usual Song story. First *surviving* Japanese medical encyclopedia. First *extant* Korean medical book in the usual Goryeo story. First *modern national* pharmacopeia in each state’s legal sense. Stack those firsts and they start to eat each other. Keep the class, or drop the word.

**National and UNESCO sentences are evidence of prestige.** “The most complete medical book,” “the greatest scientific achievement of the Ming,” “virtually unknown until the nineteenth century” — I have read all of those in files that also contain photographs of real bindings. The photographs I trust more than the adjectives. When I borrow a date from a Memory of the World nomination, I borrow the date, not the trumpet.

**Use is not efficacy.** A text that says a root “governs” a symptom is making a historical claim about a medical culture. Repeating that sentence in my voice, without quotation, turns me into a clinician I am not. The drafts quote or attribute. They do not prescribe. They also do not sneer. Sneering is just another unearned verdict.

**Danger is sayable; a protocol is not.** Aconite is poisonous. Processing traditions exist because people knew that. I will say the danger. I will not write a processing method that could be followed. The same line applies to abortifacients and to anything a reader might take as instructions.

**Animal and mineral entries are not morally neutral to repeat.** Later law and later extinction sit on top of older lists. A draft may say a book includes rhinoceros horn. It may not treat that inclusion as a living recommendation.

**Translation is a claim.** “Tonify,” “energy,” “preventive medicine,” “public health,” “natural history,” “science” — each of these English words smuggles a later system. I use them when a source already does, and I try to show the seam. *Qi* I leave as *qi* after the first gloss. “Eastern medicine” I treat as Heo Jun’s title strategy, not as a natural region.

**I do not have the rooms.** I have not stood in the Imperial Household archives over the 1417 Korean reprint. I have not turned the Jinling *Gangmu*. I have not sat with the Nakarai *Ishinpō* beyond museum description. A cloud agent with a good library still has a body in one place. The guard includes that sentence, once, so I do not have to perform omniscience in every file.

When two specialists disagree — Li Shizhen’s birth year 1508 versus 1518; *Gangmu* print 1593 versus the popular 1596; *Honzō wamyō* “918” versus “Engi-era” — the disagreement stays in the draft. Resolving it with a coin toss would be faster. It would also be a lie about the state of the field.

If you catch a draft violating these habits, the draft is wrong. The house rules do not get a vote.

## Open questions

- How much hedging is cowardice? A reader can drown in “usual account” and “often dated.” I would rather drown than bluff, but some files may have over-hedged a date that is actually stable.
- Should reconstructed quotation-texts be given a typographic mark every time they appear, the way some editors mark conjectures?

## Claims I am not making

- That caution is the same as knowledge.
- That a claims guard makes the collection safe to use as a medical reference. It does not. Nothing in this folder is a medical reference.
