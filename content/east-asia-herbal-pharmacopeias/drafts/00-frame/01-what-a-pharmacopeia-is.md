A modern pharmacopeia is a legal instrument. It tells a manufacturer, an inspector, and sometimes a court what a named article must be: identity, purity, assay, storage, a method that can fail. The Japanese *Nihon yakkyokuhō* of 1886 was built to be that kind of book. So were the Republican *Zhonghua yaodian* of 1930 and the later national codes in Beijing and Seoul. If you come to this collection from USP or Ph. Eur., those are the objects you already know.

They are not the oldest objects here.

For most of the period these drafts cover, the governing word is not *yaodian* (drug code) but *bencao* — literally “roots and herbs,” and in practice a classified literature of substances. A *bencao* entry may give a name, aliases, a taste and a qi, a toxicity grade, a place, a season to gather, a way to process, a set of claimed indications, a recipe neighbor, a warning about a look-alike. It may include a picture. It may quote six earlier books and then argue with all of them. What it usually does *not* include is a modern assay. That absence is not a failure to be European. It is a different job.

Japanese *honzō* is the same graph read in Japanese, and then, in the Edo period, something that starts to behave like natural history while still living inside Li Shizhen’s table of contents. Korean *hyangyak* is not a synonym for “herb.” It is a policy word: the local, as against the imported Chinese article. A book that puts *hyangyak* in the title is already making an argument about supply, money, and what a kingdom can afford to call medicine.

So we have at least four objects sharing a filing cabinet:

1. **The classified substance book** (*bencao* / *honzō*), official or private.
2. **The formulary** (方 *fang* / 方書), which is about combinations and emergencies more than about monographs.
3. **The medical encyclopedia** that swallows both — *Ishinpō*, *Uibang yuchwi*, *Donguibogam* — and happens to contain the best surviving drug lists of their place and century.
4. **The modern legal pharmacopeia**, which may or may not still house crude drugs, and which may keep a *separate* herbal supplement (Korea’s KHP is the clean example).

I will keep using “pharmacopeia” in titles when the draft is about official standards or when English scholarship already does. In the body I will prefer the native genre word. If that makes the prose slower, good. Speed is how a Tang court book and a Meiji ministry book get treated as the same ancestor.

There is a fifth object, and it causes trouble: **the reconstructed classic**. The *Shennong bencao jing* as we meet it is a later reconstruction from quotations, layered through Tao Hongjing and then through Song print. To call that reconstruction “the oldest Chinese pharmacopeia” is a sentence with three soft spots (oldest, Chinese, pharmacopeia). Drafts 05–07 live in those soft spots.

Official versus unofficial is another trap. The Tang *Xinxiu bencao* was commissioned, staffed, and — in the usual account — promulgated as a standard for drug use. How far that standard reached, in a manuscript empire without print, is a separate question, and I do not have a census of provincial copies that would let me answer it. The Song *Kaibao* book is official *and* printed. Tang Shenwei’s *Zhenglei* is a private compilation that later official and commercial editions ran on. Li Shizhen’s *Gangmu* is a family-and-workshop encyclopedia that became, after print, more official than some official books. Liu Wentai’s 1505 color herbal *was* official and then was not printed, which is a useful reminder that “imperial” and “public” are not synonyms.

Japan and Korea add their own officialities. A Heian name-book compiled under an Engi-era order is official in one sense. A Joseon publication from the Jiphyeonjeon / royal medical office is official in another. A colonial-era standard written in a Japanese university laboratory in Keijō is official in a third, and it is not a Korean national pharmacopeia. Draft 38 will not let those collapse.

If you need a working test for this collection: **a file belongs here if it is about how a polity or a scholarly community tried to stabilize the names and identities of medicinal substances in writing.** Recipes appear when they are the vehicle of that stabilization. Clinics appear when a book claims to serve them. Efficacy does not appear as a verdict.

That test excludes a lot of good history — pulse, acupuncture, epidemic administration, the social world of the pharmacy shop. It also stops the pile from becoming “traditional medicine, complete.” Completeness is how claims get smuggled.

## Open questions

- Should later food canons (*Shiliao bencao*, dietary sections of *Donguibogam*) count as pharmacopeia history or as a bordering genre? I included one draft on the edge and may have been greedy.
- How should Taiwan and Hong Kong modern codes sit? Stage 5 mentions them; they do not get their own files. That is a scope choice, not a judgment of importance.

## Sources used

Unschuld, *History of Pharmaceutics* (1986), on the *bencao* series as a literature distinct from the correspondence-medicine classics. PMDA historical notes on the 1886 JP. Suh (2009, 2017) on *hyangyak* as a local/political term. See `SOURCES.md`.

## Claims I am not making

- That *bencao* is “just” an early pharmacopeia, or that the modern *yaodian* is “just” a late *bencao*.
- That official promulgation equals widespread use.
