# Tradition is not a pipeline

The sentence I am trying to kill is this one: *Chinese medicine spread to Korea and Japan, where it was preserved and developed.* You can find a version of it in tourist museums and in papers that should know better. It has a direction, a substance, and a moral. Knowledge originates, travels, is kept, is improved. The books in this collection do not behave like that.

They behave like **textual institutions**. I am stealing Federico Marcon’s phrase for Edo *honzōgaku* and using it more widely than he might like. A textual institution is a stack of titles that later writers have to live inside. You comment, you abridge, you add a “supplement,” you reprint with reading marks, you go into the field with the table of contents already in your hand. Li Shizhen’s encyclopedia becomes, in Japan, a building. Kaibara Ekiken walks out a side door and writes in Japanese about “Yamato” things; Ono Ranzan walks back in and calls his life’s work a *keimō*, a clarification, of the same building. That is not a pipeline. That is occupancy.

Korea’s *hyangyak* books make the occupancy problem explicit. The point of writing “local botanicals” into a title in the thirteenth and fifteenth centuries is not a folk revival. It is a fiscal and logistical argument: imported Tang and later Chinese articles are expensive, politically entangled, and sometimes unavailable. A state that can name, gather, and process a local substitute has a different budget and a different medicine. Soyoung Suh’s work is the reason I will not tell this as a story of Korean essence expressing itself. The local is layered. It is made.

China is not a still point either. The *bencao* series is a conversation that keeps changing its own past. Tao Hongjing does not “transmit Shennong.” He arranges, expands, and color-codes a set of texts he already thinks are corrupt. The Tang court does not “preserve Tao.” It restaffs the book with tribute geography and illustrations, then loses the result at home. The Song does not “print the Tang.” It collates, adds Chen Cangqi’s extras, and — this matters — makes a book that can be reproduced without a scriptorium. Li Shizhen does not “sum up the tradition.” He reclassifies it, argues with it, and leaves a citation practice that later dictionaries have to mop. Zhao Xuemin does not “complete Li.” He writes from a different social room, with different goods coming in on ships.

Even the modern codes, which look like a clean break, are occupancy. Japan’s 1886 book is a European-style legal instrument drafted with Dutch and German ghosts in the room. It does not erase Kampo; it exiles it from the official list for a long time, which is a relation, not an absence. The Republican *yaodian* of 1930 refuses the word *bencao* on purpose. The PRC book of 1953 inherits a commission process and a monograph count; the 1963 book puts processing back in a way herbalists still point to. Korea’s 1958 pharmacopeia arrives after a colonial period in which “official” had meant Japanese, and after a wartime moment when the colonial state suddenly wanted Korean herbs because the war had eaten the other drugs. None of that is a pipeline from the *Shennong* text to a blister pack.

Two more refusals.

**I will not use “East Asia” as an author.** East Asia did not write a pharmacopeia. People in offices, families, temples, and print shops did. The region name is a scope, the brief I was given. When a practice is shared, I will say what is shared: graphs, tribute routes, a reprint, a quotation. When a practice is local, I will leave it local.

**I will not use “science” as a prize.** Needham can call the *Gangmu* the great Ming achievement in his series; that is Needham’s argument. Edo scholars can be written into a story that leads to Linnaeus; that story has been told, and it has been criticized for making *Yamato honzō* valuable only when it stops being medicine. I am interested in what the books were for in their rooms. If a later laboratory method appears, it appears as a later laboratory method.

The pipeline image is attractive because it lets a collection have a plot: origin, spread, climax, modern science. This collection has stages, which is a weaker thing. Stages are a reading order. They are not a destiny. If you finish stage 5 and feel that the story resolved, go back to the open questions. They are there to keep the plot from winning.

## Open questions

- Is “textual institution” too Japanese a tool to carry into Song print culture and Joseon compilation? I am using it anyway, and I may be stretching it.
- How to talk about Indian and Buddhist medical streams that Korean and Japanese sources sometimes name, without opening a South Asian collection I am not writing.

## Sources used

Marcon, *The Knowledge of Nature* (2015) and his essay on the book as fieldwork. Suh, “Herbs of Our Own Kingdom” (2009) and *Naming the Local* (2017). Unschuld on the long split between correspondence medicine and pharmaceutical literature. Trambaiolo on the limits of Sinification in Tokugawa pharmacy.

## Claims I am not making

- That there was no transmission. There was constant transmission. Transmission is not a pipeline.
- That local books are “really Western science in embryo” or “really Chinese medicine in costume.”
