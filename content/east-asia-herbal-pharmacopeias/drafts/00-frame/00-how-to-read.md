I would rather you put this file down after four pages than treat the folder as a closed history. The forty-five essays that follow are staged drafts. They argue. They guess in public. They leave joints visible. That is the point of staging them instead of polishing them into a single voice that sounds sure.

The subject is a family of books. Some were issued by a court and meant to travel with an order. Some were private encyclopedias that later officials ate. Some are name-lists. Some are medical encyclopedias with a pharmacopeia hiding in one chapter. Some, after 1886, really are pharmacopeias in the modern legal sense: a ministry can fail a bottle against them. Calling all of this “East Asian herbal pharmacopeias” is a filing decision. It is useful. It is also already a claim. Stage 0 exists so that claim does not sneak past you.

Read for three things and refuse a fourth.

The first is **the book as an object**. Who paid. Who signed. Whether the thing in our hands is a print, a manuscript, a reconstruction from quotations, or a nineteenth-century recut wearing an older title. A lot of famous dates in this field are dates of a preface, or of a memorial, or of a later cataloger. I try to say which.

The second is **the name as a problem**. The same graph can mean a plant in Sichuan, a substitute in Kyushu, and a Korean local that a fifteenth-century clerk insisted was “just as good.” Romanization piles on top of that. If a draft spends a paragraph on spelling, it is not decoration.

The third is **the state**. Courts wanted tribute drugs they could find, gardens they could staff, and a list their medical office could examine people against. Later nation-states wanted something a customs house could use. Those are different hungers that happen to produce books with overlapping titles.

The fourth thing, the one to refuse, is **the sentence that heals**. These drafts will tell you that a text *claims* a use. They will not tell you the use is true. They will not give a dose. They will not rank herbs. If you came for a clinic, you are in the wrong folder.

A note on “I.” I use it when I am marking a limit: I have not handled the 1417 *Hyangyak gugeupbang* reprint; I have not collated Jinling against Jiangxi line by line; I am working from published scholarship, catalogs, and the nomination files that institutions write when they want a book onto a register. Those files are useful and they are also advocacy. UNESCO prose loves a superlative. I do not.

A note on nations. China, Japan, and Korea are the brief. They were not always those states, and their book worlds leaked. A Tang official *bencao* survives in Japan in a way it does not in China. A Goryeo emergency herbal is known from a copy in Tokyo. Li Shizhen’s encyclopedia became, in Edo, a textual institution that Japanese naturalists lived inside for two centuries. I will not tell a story in which knowledge “originates” in one place and is “received” in another as if the receivers were shelves. I will also not flatten three literatures into one “Oriental herbalism.” The shared script is real. So is the refusal to be the same book.

Staging, in this folder, means two practical things. The files are grouped so an argument can accumulate: method, early China, imperial China, Japan, Korea, then the comparative problems (trade, pictures, poison, omission, modern codes). And each file keeps `status: draft` until a later reader has checked it against the primary book or a diplomatic edition. Promotion is a human act. If someone merges this pile and calls it done, they have misread the header.

If you only have an hour, read this file, then `01`, `02`, `12`, `20`, `25`, `36`, `43`, `44`. If you have a week, walk the manifest in order and argue with the open questions at the end of each draft. Those questions are not homework. They are the parts I could not honestly close.

## Open questions

- Is “pharmacopeia” even the right English filing word, or should the collection have been titled *bencao / honzō / hyangyak / yaodian* and left the translation for last?
- How much of what I treat as “scholarly consensus” is still a Chinese-language conversation that English surveys flatten?
