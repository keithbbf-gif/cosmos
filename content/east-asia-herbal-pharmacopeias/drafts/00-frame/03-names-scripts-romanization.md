# Names, scripts, romanization

The books in this collection were written in a world that shared graphs and did not share speech. That is the first fact about their names. A clerk in Chang’an, a physician in Heian-kyō, and a compiler in Hanyang could look at 甘草 and know they were in the same written conversation. They did not say the same word. They did not necessarily mean the same plant. The graph is a treaty. Treaties get broken.

I am going to be slightly pedantic, because the alternative is a mess that looks like English fluency.

**Chinese.** I use Hanyu Pinyin without tone marks in running prose, and I keep the source graphs at first mention of a book or a person when the graph is the identity. *Bencao gangmu* 本草綱目, Li Shizhen 李時珍. I do not write “Pen-ts’ao kang-mu” unless I am quoting an older catalog. I do not write “Ben Cao Gang Mu” with those capitals except in a title that already does. Wade–Giles will appear in quotations and in Needham. I will not silently modernize a quotation.

**Japanese.** Modified Hepburn: *Honzō wamyō*, *Ishinpō* (the long *ō* stays; you will also see *Ishinhō*). *Honzōgaku* for the Edo field. Personal names in the form the draft first commits to — Tanba Yasuyori, Fukane no Sukehito, Kaibara Ekiken, Ono Ranzan, Hayashi Razan — with the warning that court-style and art-names multiply. Fukane is also Fukae; Tanba is also Tamba. I pick one and put the other in parentheses once.

**Korean.** Revised romanization in the file titles and in my voice: *Hyangyak gugeupbang*, *Hyangyak jipseongbang*, *Uibang yuchwi*, *Donguibogam*, Heo Jun. McCune–Reischauer lives in catalogs (*Tongŭi pogam*, *Hyangyak kugŭppang*) and in older articles. When I cite those, I do not pretend they are typos. Soyoung Suh’s work will pull you toward yet another set of hyphens. The graphs are the crosswalk: 鄕藥救急方, 鄕藥集成方, 醫方類聚, 東醫寶鑑.

**The word *bencao* / *honzō* / *boncho*.** Same two graphs, three lives. I do not translate it as “pharmacopeia” after this file except when I am talking about a legal code. “Materia medica” is the usual scholarly English and I will use it, knowing it is Dioscorides’ ghost.

**Drug names.** I give the historical Chinese name, a common English plant or substance name *only when the identification is boringly stable* (licorice, ginger, salt), and I refuse a Linnaean binomial unless a cited paper has done the identification work for that passage. Old *bencao* names are not species. One name can cover several species, a processed form, a geographic grade, and a substitute. Writing *Panax ginseng* every time a text says 人參 is how a later botany colonizes an earlier list. Sometimes the colonization is correct. I still want to see the labor.

**Local names.** Korean *hyangmyeong* and Japanese *wamyō* are not colorful extras. They are the point of several books in this pile. A thirteenth-century Korean emergency text that writes a vernacular name next to a Chinese graph is doing literacy politics. A Heian name-book that says whether a Tang drug grows in Japan is doing supply politics. I will quote those names when I have a source that transcribes them. I will not invent a cute English equivalent.

**People.** Li Shizhen’s dates are written 1518–1593 on statues and 1508–1593 in Unschuld’s *Gangmu* apparatus. I flag the split where he first appears as a person (draft 19) and do not resolve it. Heo Jun’s dates are commonly 1539 or 1546 to 1615; I will not pick a birth year in a caption and then forget the other. Su Jing is also Su Gong in later polite reference. Liu Wentai is the Ming medical official on the 1505 album; the stories about his disgrace after the Hongzhi emperor’s death are widely repeated and still need a court-source check.

**Places.** I use historical names when the book does (Jinling, not “Nanjing,” for the first *Gangmu* blocks; Keijō when I am talking about the colonial university). I gloss once. Modern map-words after that are allowed if they help a reader who is not a specialist. They are not allowed if they erase a border the book was written on.

If this file feels like bureaucracy, it is. Names are where silent claims hide. A collection that wants to be claims-guarded and then writes “ginseng, the famous Korean cure” has already failed twice.

## Open questions

- Should later drafts include a short name-index (graph, pinyin, Hepburn, RR, common English) as a separate staged file? Probably yes. Not in this pass.
- How to handle Sino-Vietnamese and classical Japanese readings without opening a fourth country. I mostly do not.

## Sources used

Catalog practice from LOC, TNM, AKS Encyclopedia of Korean Culture, and the romanization habits of Unschuld, Suh, and Marcon. This is a convention file, not a linguistic study.

## Claims I am not making

- That one romanization is “correct.” It is a house choice for this folder.
- That a stable English plant name means the historical identification is settled.
