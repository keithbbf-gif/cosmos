The three-grade machine (draft 07) wants a world in which the highest things are almost food and the lowest things are almost weapons. Clinics, if the later literature is any guide, do not live in that world. They live in a world where a “low” drug is the one you need and a “high” drug can still ruin a body. I am not going to prove that with case records. I am going to say that **the books know about danger and they do not always put danger where the moral scale puts it**.

Aconite (*fuzi* and its kin) is the example everyone uses, so I will use it and then get off it. Official and unofficial *bencao* keep it. They talk about processing. They talk about contraindication. They talk, sometimes, about deaths. They do not, in any sentence I will write, give you a method. Processing traditions exist because the raw object is dangerous. That sentence is allowed. A protocol is not.

The upper-drug problem is the inverse. A substance parked in the attic — ginseng is the celebrity, but the attic is crowded — inherits a reputation for long use and gentle life-nourishing. Museum cards still say this as if it were a finding. It is a cosmological claim. Modern toxicology and modern pharmacology, which I am not practicing here, have their own ways of talking about the same objects. Those ways do not have to win for the historical point to stand: **grade is not a safety certificate**.

Dose is the missing legal piece in most of the premodern books. There are amounts in formulas. There are “do not take long” warnings. There is not, usually, a monograph in the JP sense with a numbered assay and a content limit. When the modern codes arrive, one of the things they are for is to make dose and impurity into numbers a court can hear. That is not the old books becoming honest at last. It is a new official object (draft 01). The old books’ honesty, when they have it, is a story: this thing killed, this thing was faked, this thing looks like that thing.

Fakes belong in this file. The *Pinhui* grid includes a slot for counterfeits. Li’s machine argues with look-alikes. Modern KP/KHP reviews still worry about origin and Latin names. A poison that is the wrong poison is a cargo problem (draft 39). Identity is a safety practice even when no one has a spectrometer.

I want a comparative note on **official silence**. Some dangerous or sexually or abortifacient substances sit in the lists with a straight face. Some sit in *Ishinpō*’s rooms that later readers find scandalous. Some are left to women and to unlicensed specialists (draft 42). A state’s drug book is not a complete map of danger. It is a map of dangers the state is willing to spell.

Japan’s 1886 book, by starting with a European chemical and preparation list, changes which dangers count as official. Aconite may still appear as a crude drug in later JP editions; Kampo formulas that use it will have a different legal life than they had in an Edo shop. I have not traced that legal life. The point is the shift of *which danger is a monograph*.

The claims-guard, restated for this file:

I will not say a historical processing method makes aconite safe.

I will not say an upper drug is safe.

I will not list “toxic herbs of East Asia” as if that were a public service. Lists like that become cookbooks or panic.

I will say: the literature is aware of harm; its classifications of harm are moral and cosmological as well as practical; the modern pharmacopeia’s way of numbering harm is a later official dialect.

If you came to this collection to learn how to use a dangerous plant, you have misread every header. Go away.

## Open questions

- When do official Chinese *bencao* first give systematic incompatibility tables (*shibachi* / *shijiu* and the later *paozhi* warnings) as a closed list, and how do Korean and Japanese books carry those tables?
- How should a second pass talk about historical poisoning cases without turning them into folklore?

## Claims I am not making

- Any clinical claim about any substance.
- That modern numbers have made the old moral scale obsolete as *history*. They have made it obsolete as *law*, in countries that adopted the numbers.
