A *bencao* entry likes to sound like a plant. Often it is a cargo.

I have been circling this since the Tang procurement loop (draft 10) and the Korean word *hyangyak* (draft 31). It is time to say it as a comparative claim: **the books in this collection are, among other things, technologies for moving objects with names on them.** Tribute, tax, market, ship, smuggle, substitute — the page is a way to say that the object in the box is the object in the sentence.

Three circuits, too clean, but useful.

**Tribute and prefecture.** The Tang official book, if the statute reconstructions are even half right, is a finding-aid for the palace: look up the place, send the order, receive the thing. Su Song’s *Tujing* asks the prefecture to send a picture when the thing itself cannot come. Joseon’s gathering calendar and *sillok* geography do the same work on a peninsula. The object here is a *product of a place*. Place is a grade. “Sichuan” on a honey pot (the Wellcome *Pinhui* note) is already a brand.

**Import and substitute.** Japan’s *wamyō* notes on domestic production, Korea’s whole *hyangyak* policy, Chen Cangqi’s leftovers, Zhao Xuemin’s ships — these are books written because the right object did not arrive, or cost too much, or had a cousin growing over the hill. The substitute is the most honest object in the literature. It admits that the name and the body can part.

**Long-distance and colonial cargo.** Ginseng as a political good; spices and resins that were always already from somewhere else; New World and maritime things that show up in Qing leftovers; Japanese crude drugs that, in modern JP practice, are often grown in China and Southeast Asia and then tested in Japanese monographs. The 1886 JP was written partly because imported Western drugs needed a standard. The colonial Korean herbal standards of the late 1930s were written because the other imports had failed. Modern ChP, JP, and KP still live in customs houses. The object is a *lot*, with a certificate.

I will not write a history of the ginseng trade. I will say that a collection which only followed ideas — yin and yang, three grades, Linnaeus — would miss the box. Ideas travel in boxes. Boxes need names that survive a dock.

Pictures are part of this (draft 40). So is poison (draft 41): a dangerous object is an object a state especially wants to name, because the wrong box kills the wrong person. So is omission (draft 42): women’s drugs, abortifacients, unlicensed animal parts, the things a court list is embarrassed by or a later law forbids.

A claims-guard for cargo:

Do not assume a place-name in a monograph is a botanical range. It may be a market, a tribute stop, or a prestige adjective.

Do not assume “imported” means “better.” Some books think so. Some *hyangyak* books exist to deny it. Neither verdict is mine.

Do not assume a modern DNA barcode has ended the cargo problem. It has changed the test. The box is still a box.

If I have a comparative thesis for the whole pile, it is here more than in “China-Japan-Korea shared a tradition.” They shared, sometimes, **a problem of objects that needed names across a dock**. The books are different answers. The dock keeps showing up.

## Open questions

- Where would a cargo-history of one substance (not ginseng — too famous; maybe licorice, maybe aconite) sit as a sequel file?
- How much of Tang “tribute” language is actually market purchase dressed as tribute? The budget-bureau reconstructions should be read with a colder eye.
