# Three modern books and a shared ancestor they do not admit the same way

By the late twentieth century a customs officer in Kobe, a tester in Shanghai, and a reviewer in Osong can all open a book that looks, to a USP reader, like the same genre: monographs, general chapters, a legal voice. The books are the Japanese Pharmacopoeia, the Pharmacopoeia of the People’s Republic of China, and the Korean Pharmacopoeia (with KHP beside it). They are not the same ancestor in a costume. They are three state decisions about what a drug-name is allowed to mean. This file is about those decisions, and about the older pages they sometimes house and sometimes refuse.

**Japan, 1886/1887.** The first mover in the modern legal sense. Drafted with European teachers, aimed at a club of national codes, leaving Kampo outside the law for a long time (draft 30). Later editions grow crude-drug and Kampo-extract articles. The ancestor *honzō* is admitted as *material*, when it is admitted, not as the book’s form.

**China, 1930 and 1953.** The Republican *Zhonghua yaodian* (printed 1930, promulgated 1931 in the account I used) drops the word *bencao* on purpose. Unschuld reads it as a conversion to a Western therapeutic-and-standard world. Bernard Read is already explaining “the Chinese Pharmacopoeia” to a Canadian journal in 1930. Reprints get called “editions” in a way that later bibliographers find sloppy. The PRC’s 1953 book is a new commission (Li Dequan as chair in the official history; 531 monographs; 1957 addendum). The 1963 book is the one herbalists point to for *paozhi*. Later ChP volumes split traditional and chemical worlds into different tomes. The ancestor *bencao* is admitted as a *national heritage and a still-living industry*, in a legal dialect that is not Li’s machine. The 1953 preface, I am told, distances itself from the Republican effort. I have not sat with that preface. The distancing is plausible. New states like a new year-one.

**Korea, 1958.** After the colonial JP-world and the wartime herbal interlude (draft 38). KP plus, later, a separate herbal specification book. The ancestor *hyangyak* / *Donguibogam* is a cultural object the state can celebrate (UNESCO 2009) while the legal identity of a root sits in a different genre. That split is not hypocrisy. It is two jobs.

Taiwan’s continued *Zhonghua yaodian* line and Hong Kong’s own lists are the reminder that “Chinese modern pharmacopeia” was never only Beijing. I will not give them files in this pass. I will not pretend they are footnotes to the PRC.

What the three books share:

A nation-state. A ministry. A revision cycle. A need to speak to trade. A monograph style that can fail a sample. An awkward hospitality toward crude drugs that older books once housed in another voice.

What they do not share:

The same year-one. The same colonial scar. The same way of splitting “chemical” and “herbal.” The same story about the farmer, the *Gangmu*, and the royal encyclopedia.

The shared ancestor they do not admit the same way is not Shennong. It is **the idea that a polity may stabilize substance-names in writing**. Tang Gaozong’s team, Sejong’s two projects, the Meiji Hygiene Bureau, the 1950 Shanghai-and-Beijing commission — those people would not recognize one another’s pages. They would recognize the impulse. I am willing to say that. I am not willing to say they are one tradition fulfilling itself.

A comparative table a second pass owes the reader:

| | Year-one (modern legal) | Herbal hospitality | Older book it cannot be |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| JP | 1886/87 | later, managed | *Honzō kōmoku* as law |
| ChP / *yaodian* | 1930; 1953 | built-in, especially after 1963 | *Bencao gangmu* as law |
| KP (+KHP) | 1958 | split-book | *Donguibogam* as law |

The last column is the point. A modern pharmacopeia can *cite* an old encyclopedia. It cannot *be* one, not without ceasing to be a modern pharmacopeia.

## Open questions

- How do the English editions of ChP and JP handle older Chinese names — as synonyms, as heritage decoration, or as identity?
- What would a fourth row for the 1937–42 colonial Korean herbal standards do to this table if their legal force were ever pinned down?

## Claims I am not making

- That the three books are harmonized.
- That “admission of herbs” equals continuity of traditional medicine as a system.
- That I have compared current editions article by article.
