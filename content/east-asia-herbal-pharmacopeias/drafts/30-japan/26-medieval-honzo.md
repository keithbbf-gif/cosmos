# Medieval honzō, temples, and the court

Between *Ishinpō* and the Edo *Gangmu* factory there is a long stretch that popular timelines skip. I almost skipped it too. That would have been a pipeline trick: Heian classics, then Tokugawa science. The medieval centuries are thinner in the sources I had time to read, and I will not fill the thinness with atmosphere. I will name the problem of the stretch.

The court medical house — the Tanba line and its cousins, the later Wake and other lineages — keeps Chinese text as capital. A name-book and an encyclopedia are already on the shelf. What a Kamakura or Muromachi physician actually gathers, imports, or substitutes is a different file, and much of it is in diaries, temple records, and pharmacy-shop evidence I have not worked. So this draft is a **placeholder with teeth**: do not write “medieval Japan had no *honzō*.” Write “this pass does not yet have a medieval *honzō* corpus it trusts.”

Teeth, then, four of them.

**Temples are not the court.** Monastery medicine, Kōyasan and other mountain worlds, the Buddhist pharmacopeia of incense and simple — these are substance-cultures with their own lists. They share graphs with the court book and they do not share a payroll. A history that only follows the Ten’yakuryō will miss the garden that actually grew.

**War and disruption change supply, not only doctrine.** When the capital’s import routes wobble, a literary drug becomes a local problem again. I do not have a medieval Japanese *hyangyak* movement to parade. I have the logical pressure. If someone in a second pass finds a Kamakura text that does the Korean thing — official local substitutes — I want that text in this folder immediately.

**Song and Yuan books arrive when they arrive.** Print in China means, eventually, print on ships. The *Zhenglei* family and the Song formularies (*Hejiju fang* and the rest) become East Asian objects. Japan’s later word *yakkyokuhō* will even calque a Song formulary title (draft 30). Medieval reading of those imports is a study I have not done. The arrival is not Edo-only.

**Lineage secrecy is a kind of anti-pharmacopeia.** A family that lives on exclusive recipes has a reason not to write a public standard. Official *bencao* want one voice. Lineage medicine wants a voice you cannot steal. Both can exist in one city. If the medieval paper trail looks thin, some of the thinness may be intentional.

The Nakarai *Ishinpō* itself has a medieval chapter: a Kamakura replacement scroll, a later story about an emperor giving the set to a Bureau of Medicine head. Museum notes mention that the set left the family only once, on loan to the Tokugawa in 1854. That is a fact about custody, which is a medieval-and-after fact. Books that matter sit in houses.

I am aware that this file is shorter on names than the Heian and Edo files. That is not because the centuries were empty. It is because I will not invent a medieval survey from a couple of encyclopedia sentences. Staging means a thin draft can stay thin and still block a false smoothness.

If you are a reader with the temple and Gozan print corpus in your head, this is the file to attack.

## Open questions

- What is the first Japanese printed *honzō* or medical book that is not a Chinese reprint? I will not guess a title I have not sourced.
- How should Shintō, Buddhist, and court substance-lists be typed so they do not collapse into “religion versus science”?

## Claims I am not making

- That medieval Japan lacked pharmaceutical literature.
- That I have surveyed that literature.
- That temples were “more empirical” or “more superstitious” than the court. Those are prize words.
