# Hayashi Razan and the arrival of the Gangmu

In 1607, in the ordinary telling, Hayashi Razan 林羅山 — Neo-Confucian fixer of the early Tokugawa world — is the name attached to the *Bencao gangmu*’s official introduction into Japan. I was not at the dock. Marcon’s account, which I trust more than a plaque, treats that year as the start of a **textual institution**: demand immediately outruns Ming imports, Kyoto printers begin to recut (Noda Yajiemon in 1637 is the first local reprint Marcon names), and then a cascade — 1653 with reading marks, 1659 with new pictures, Matsushita Kenrin 1669/1672, Kaibara associated with an edit, Inō Jakusui’s annotated version 1714, and a long tail of abridgements and glossaries, including Razan’s own *Tashikihen*.

The *Gangmu* does not “arrive” like a guest. It becomes a building you work in.

What the building does to Japanese *honzō*:

**It sets the table of contents.** Sixteen *bu* and the inner monograph sequence become the default rooms. Even when Kaibara writes *Yamato honzō* in Japanese and puts “Yamato” things in (draft 28), the Chinese encyclopedia is still the thing he is walking beside. Even when Ono Ranzan spends a life in the field (draft 29), he titles the life a *keimō* of Li’s book.

**It creates a market for reading marks.** *Kundoku* is not translation in the modern sense. It is a way to make a Chinese page speak in Japanese order. A reprint with marks is a claim about who the reader is: not a Chinese literatus, a Japanese scholar who still wants the graphs. The drug name sits in that tension.

**It multiplies pictures**, which is not the same as improving them. New illustrations can be new observations or new mistakes. Draft 40 will not let “more pictures” mean “more truth.”

**It makes natural history possible without requiring a break.** This is where I side with the critics of the old modernization story. The story says: Kaibara leaves medicine, invents *hakubutsugaku*, Japan joins science. The counter-story, which Marcon and others have been telling, says: people keep living in Li’s rooms and also go outside. The book is fieldwork’s partner, not its enemy. I like the counter-story because it matches the titles. If they had left the building, they would have stopped putting *Honzō kōmoku* on the door.

Razan himself is not a pharmacist-hero. He is the kind of man who makes a Chinese encyclopedia look like part of a Tokugawa intellectual order. That is enough. The printers and the later naturalists do the occupancy.

Korea is next door and not in this building the same way. Joseon has *Donguibogam* already in the 1610s, and a different official compilation history. Chinese books still matter in Seoul; they do not, on the evidence I have, produce a two-century *Gangmu* reprint industry of the Kyoto kind. I will not turn that difference into a national character. I will say: **different textual institutions, different official needs, different printers.**

If 1607 is wrong by a few years, the cascade of reprints is still the fact. A second pass can tighten the first dock-date. It should not loosen the claim that Edo *honzō* is, among other things, a *Gangmu* culture.

## Open questions

- What exactly did “official introduction” in 1607 mean — a presentation copy, a shogunal library accession, a Razan preface? Marcon’s footnote trail is the next read.
- How many of the early Kyoto reprints were complete *Gangmu* and how many were commercial abridgements pretending to be the building?

## Sources used

Marcon, *The Knowledge of Nature* (2015) and “The ‘book’ as fieldwork” (*BJHS Themes*), for 1607, the Noda 1637 start, and the reprint cascade. Yabe/Ueno modernization narrative as the story being argued with (via the *SJLC* essay on “naturalizing” the *Gangmu*). Drafts 20 and 28–29.

## Claims I am not making

- That Japanese scholars did no *honzō* before 1607. They did. This is the *Gangmu* chapter, not the origin of Japanese plant knowledge.
- That occupancy equals slavishness. Occupancy includes argument.
