# Honzō wamyō: naming as local knowledge

The oldest surviving Japanese pharmaceutical book, in the usual account, is not a recipe collection. It is a name-book. *Honzō wamyō* 本草和名, Japanese names for the *honzō*. The compiler is Fukane no Sukehito 深根輔仁 (also Fukae; also, in later nicknames, the author of “Sukehito honzō”). The order is an Engi-era (901–923) court order in the ordinary telling. The year you will see pinned is 918, Engi 18. I will say **Engi-era, conventionally 918**, because I have not read a colophon that forces the year.

What the book does, if we follow the standard descriptions and the fact that its arrangement copies the *Xinxiu*:

It takes the Chinese drug names of the Tang official textbook and writes Japanese names beside them. It notes aliases. It notes, crucially, whether a thing is produced in Japan and sometimes where. It is a dictionary aimed at an office that already has to live in two languages and two landscapes.

That is local knowledge in a dry sense. Not folklore. Not “the Japanese people knew their plants.” A court physician-scholar making a crosswalk so that a Tang graph can be said, found, or marked as absent. The identifications are not safe. Later Japanese scholars, and modern botanists, have said some of the matches are wrong. Of course they are. A wrong match is still evidence of a tenth-century attempt.

The book we open is not a tenth-century manuscript I can photograph for you. The print that made it public again is Taki Motoyasu’s 1796 edition, from a manuscript in the shogunal library, with his collation. Edo philology is the reason a Heian name-book is a book and not a rumor. That means every time we use *Honzō wamyō* to reconstruct the *Xinxiu*’s table of contents — and people do, because the order follows the lost official book — we are also using an eighteenth-century editor’s care. Draft 13 already said this. It is worth saying again next to the title.

Counts of items wander by category (minerals, plants, animals) depending on who is summarizing. I have seen 81 / 509 / 182-style breakdowns in encyclopedia entries. I will not pretend I recounted.

*Ishinpō*, a few decades later, uses this name-work. The first *juan*’s Japanese drug names are described as quotations from Fukane. So the name-book is not a dead end. It becomes part of the next archive.

Why this file is more than a “first surviving” plaque:

Because **naming is the Japanese official act** in a way compiling a new three-grade classic is not. The Heian court does not, on the surviving evidence, staff a twenty-two-man team to rewrite Tao. It staffs a name-book so the Tang rewrite can be lived with. That is a different theory of what a state owes a drug list. Korea, in the same broad era and after, will go further and compile local *prescriptions*. Japan’s early surviving move is lexical. I do not rank those moves. I distinguish them.

Because the vernacular name, written beside the graph, is a political object everywhere in this collection. *Hyangmyeong* in the Korean emergency book, *wamyō* here, later *kundoku* marks on a *Gangmu* reprint. The graph stays. The mouth changes. The plant may or may not.

If you only remember one caution: **do not use a *wamyō* as a modern botanical determination.** Use it as evidence that someone in the Engi-era office thought this Japanese word and this Chinese graph should share a line.

## Open questions

- Is 918 a dated fact or an encyclopedia convenience? A second pass should sit with the *Kokushi daijiten* apparatus and the 1796 preface.
- How many of Fukane’s “produced in Japan” notes can be mapped onto Heian estates and gardens rather than onto a vague “the country”?

## Claims I am not making

- That I have handled a Heian manuscript.
- That Fukane’s identifications are botanically correct.
- That this is “the first Japanese pharmacopeia” in the modern legal sense. It is a name-book.
