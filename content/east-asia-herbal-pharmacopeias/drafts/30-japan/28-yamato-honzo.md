# Kaibara Ekiken's Yamato honzō

Kaibara Ekiken 貝原益軒 (1630–1714), Fukuoka domain, Zhu Xi Confucian, writer of too many books, is the name on *Yamato honzō* 大和本草. The text is finished, in the usual account, in Hōei 5 (1708) and printed in 1709; the picture *juan* wait until 1715. Kyoto University’s copy notes those years on the prefaces and on the plates. I will use **1709/1715** as the public life of the book.

What the book is: a Japanese-language encyclopedia of stuff, sixteen volumes plus appendices and pictures in the descriptions I have, that walks beside the *Gangmu* and also beside other Chinese *bencao*, and then puts in things Kaibara thinks are Japanese or otherwise unaccounted for. A *Dictionnaire historique du Japon* entry gives a breakdown — 772 from the *Gangmu*, 203 from other Chinese books, 358 “proper to Japan,” 29 from neither China nor Japan, total 1,362. Other summaries say 1,366. I will not pick a digit. The gesture is the fact: **a count that admits the Chinese rooms and then adds rooms with Japanese names.**

The modernization story wants this book to be the birth of Japanese natural history: Kaibara observes, Kaibara writes in Japanese, Kaibara detaches stuff from medicine, therefore science. Ueno and Yabe have been quoted that way. The *SJLC* essay I used exists to make that story uncomfortable, because the same historians admit the *Gangmu* is still the must-read a century later. You cannot have a birth that leaves the parent running the house.

My working reading, claims-guarded:

Kaibara is doing *honzō* in the vernacular, for a reader who lives in “Yamato,” which is already a charged word. He includes uses, including pharmaceutical ones, when he wants to. He also includes stuff that is not for a clinic. That is not a conversion to science. That is a Confucian polymath writing a classified book of the visible world, in a century when classified books of the visible world are a way to be serious. Medicine is one of the uses of stuff. It is not the only use.

He is not replacing Li. He is writing a companion that can be read without Li’s Chinese, and that can shame Li when the Japanese plant does not match. Shame is a kind of intimacy.

The pictures arriving in 1715 matter because they are late and local. I have not studied them. A second pass should. Until then I will not call them accurate.

For this collection, *Yamato honzō* is the Japanese file that most tempts a prize (“first native pharmacopeia”). It is not a pharmacopeia in the legal sense. It is not official in the Tang sense. It is a domain scholar’s public book. That is enough, and it is already a lot. It shows that **the local can be written without a state compilation project** — unlike the Korean fifteenth-century official *hyangyak* books, which are state compilation projects. Different tools for a related problem (drafts 31–34).

Kaibara’s other fame, the *Yōjōkun* and the popular Confucian voice, I leave on the shelf. It would be easy to make him a lifestyle brand. He has been made one. Not here.

## Open questions

- How does Kaibara mark uncertainty — does he have a slot like Li’s *bianyi*, or does the Japanese prose swallow the doubt?
- Which of the “358 Japanese” items are genuinely unlisted in Chinese books, and which are Chinese items with Japanese names and a patriotic count?

## Sources used

Kyoto University Rare Materials note (1709–1715). *Dictionnaire historique du Japon* entry (counts, 1708/1709, 1715 plates). *SJLC* essay “Naturalizing Li Shizhen’s *Bencao gangmu*” (the modernization story and its contradiction). Marcon on the *Gangmu* as still-the-building.

## Claims I am not making

- That *Yamato honzō* is the origin of Japanese science.
- That writing in Japanese equals writing from “pure observation.” Kaibara is a book-man too.
- That the 1,362-count is a census of Japanese nature.
