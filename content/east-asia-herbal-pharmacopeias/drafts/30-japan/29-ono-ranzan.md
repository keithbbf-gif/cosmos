# Ono Ranzan and the field as commentary

Ono Ranzan 小野蘭山 (1729–1810), born Ono Motohiro, art-name Ranzan, is the Edo naturalist later people called “the Japanese Linnaeus.” I will use the comparison once, to retire it. Linnaeus is a Swedish professor with a naming system that wants to travel without a Chinese encyclopedia. Ranzan is a Kyoto-trained *honzō* man who ends up at the shogunate’s medical school in Edo and titles his life’s book *Honzō kōmoku keimō* 本草綱目啓蒙 — a clarification, dictated, of the *Gangmu*. The field, in his practice, is a way of commenting on a book. That is stranger and more interesting than a nickname.

The book appears in 1803–1806, edited with his botanist grandson Mototaka, and later expands (a *jūshū*, a re-compilation, in the mid-nineteenth century). I have not sat with a first printing. The title is already the thesis.

Marcon’s “book as fieldwork” is the frame I want. Ranzan’s generation does not throw Li away. It treats Li’s monograph as a hypothesis about a plant or an animal you might actually meet on a Japanese road. You walk. You ask. You bring the thing back to the page. You say the Chinese sentence is right, or wrong, or about a different thing that happens to share a graph. The *keimō* is a record of that argument. It is not a lab notebook in the modern sense. It is not a commentary in the Song scholastic sense only. It is both, which is why the modernization writers get confused and start handing out Linnaeus medals.

Ranzan also knew European herbals. He worked on Dodoens. He had Weinmann in the room. That matters, and it does not turn the *keimō* into a Western book. The Western pages are more tools for the same occupancy: how to make a name stick to a thing. A second pass should read the Dodoens work *next to* the *keimō*, not as a conversion narrative.

I will not write a biography of the servant and the son and the students. Kimura Kenkadō as a student, the 1799 Seijūkan post — those are handbook facts. They place him in a public teaching role. Teaching is how a textual institution reproduces.

Why he closes the Edo run of this collection:

Because after him you can see the Meiji cut coming (draft 30). A country that has spent two centuries making Li’s rooms livable in Japanese fields is about to adopt a legal pharmacopeia whose first edition is mostly not those rooms. The exile of Kampo is not the death of *honzōgaku*. It is a change of official genre. Ranzan’s students will still know plants. The 1886 book will not, at first, want their plants as law.

Because he is the last person in the Japanese stage for whom I will say: **the commentary is the science.** Not science as prize. Science as a public, correctable, named argument about identity. The *keimō* is closer to that than a statue of Li, and closer than a museum label that says “Japanese Linnaeus.”

## Open questions

- How does Ranzan handle substances that never lived in Japan — does the commentary become purely philological, and does he mark the switch?
- What did Savatier actually translate, and how did that French afterlife reshape Ranzan’s reputation in Europe?

## Sources used

Marcon, “The ‘book’ as fieldwork.” Standard biographical dates and the 1803–1806 *keimō*. Encyclopedia notes on Dodoens/Weinmann and the Seijūkan post — used lightly. The Linnaeus nickname treated as a later label, not a description.

## Claims I am not making

- That Ranzan invented experiment in Japan.
- That the *keimō* replaces the *Gangmu*. It explains it.
- That European herbals are the “real” source of his accuracy. That would be another pipeline.
