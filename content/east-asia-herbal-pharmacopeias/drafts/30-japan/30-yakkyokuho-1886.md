# The Japanese Pharmacopoeia of 1886 and the long exile of Kampo

On 25 June 1886 the Naimushō issued Ordinance No. 10 and published, as an official gazette extra, the first *Nihon yakkyokuhō* 日本薬局方. It took effect on 1 July 1887. The PMDA’s historical pamphlet and the older *Kotobank* legal-history entries agree on those bureaucratic dates. I will keep them. This is, at last, a pharmacopeia in the sense draft 01 started with: a legal standard a state can point at.

It is not a *bencao*.

The making, in the official memory: after the 1874 medical regulations hint that a Japanese pharmacopeia should exist, Dutch and other foreign teachers at the drug-testing stations (Geerts, Eijkman, Langgaard) draft in conversation with Japanese naval and army medical men and Hygiene Bureau staff. Dutch and German books sit on the table. A German-language draft gets translated. In 1885 a Japanese-German-Latin set is submitted. 468 monographs in the first edition’s count — chemicals, inorganics, some crude drugs, oils, preparations. A Latin edition in 1888. A commentary in 1890. The pamphlet likes to say Japan was the twenty-first country to have a national pharmacopeia. I treat that ranking as official self-location in a European club, not as a research result of this collection.

The word *yakkyokuhō* itself is a calque with a Song ghost. Edo rangaku men had already used *kyokuhō* for a Dutch apothecary book; the graphs remember the *Hejiju fang* 和劑局方, the Song official formulary. A European genre enters through a Chinese-graph memory of a different Chinese genre. That is occupancy at the level of a title. I like that more than I like “Japan modernizes.”

What the first JP does *not* do is take Kampo as law. Crude drugs appear; the inherited prescription-world of *honzō* and later Kampo schools does not become the official list. Secondary literature, including a 2017 *Scientific Reports* paper I do not otherwise need, repeats that traditional Japanese herbal medicines were not “recognized as official medicines” until a twentieth-century regulatory moment, often given as 1974. I will not harden 1974 until a statute or *yakuji* notice is sitting next to this file. The direction is not in doubt: **a long official exile, then a managed return as listed crude drugs and extracts inside a modern code.**

Exile is a relation. Kampo does not vanish in 1886. It loses the ministry’s voice. Practitioners, printers, and later academic revivalists keep another voice. When the JP eventually houses Kampo articles, it houses them as *articles*: identity tests, limits, a monograph style that is not Li’s machine and not Ranzan’s *keimō*. A reader who calls that “the tradition recognized at last” has missed the genre change. A reader who calls it “Western science erasing Asia” has missed the crude-drug pages and the later extract lists. The claims-guard wants both misses refused.

Colonial aftershocks belong in draft 38. The JP and Japanese drug law become, in Korea under rule, the official. That is not this file’s story to finish. It is this file’s warning: **an 1886 book written to join a European club becomes, a generation later, someone else’s imposed standard.**

I have not compared JP 1’s 468 titles to a Kampo prescription list. A useful second-pass table would do exactly that, and would also count how many of the 468 are plant drugs with Edo *honzō* names. Seventy-seven “European herbs” appears in one paper; I want a better count before I repeat it as fact.

This is the end of the Japan stage’s arc: from a Tang textbook problem to a legal book that, for a while, does not want the textbook. The plants did not leave. The official genre did.

## Claims I am not making

- That Kampo is ineffective or effective.
- That 1886 erased Japanese herbal practice.
- That the JP is the destiny of *honzōgaku*. It is a different official object.
