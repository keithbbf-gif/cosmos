# How a Tang book became a Japanese problem

The first Japanese problem in this collection is not “how to invent a pharmacopeia.” It is **how to live with a Chinese official book that names drugs the islands may not grow**. That problem is older than the surviving Japanese titles. It is already there when Tang medical culture — people, texts, Buddhist networks, Korean intermediaries — becomes something a Japanese court office has to staff.

I am not going to tell a century-by-century story of “medicine enters Japan in 562” or whichever date a textbook has chosen this year. Those dates are real as *claims in chronicles*. They are not a pipeline (draft 04). What I need is the shape of the problem:

- The set text in a court medical office is, for a long time, a Chinese *bencao* — in the Heian period, the *Xinxiu* in particular, if we believe the way *Honzō wamyō* is built (draft 24).
- The plants and animals on the ground are not a Tang prefecture. Some names will match a thing you can pick. Some will match a thing you can import. Some will match nothing, and then you have a choice: drop the name, force a local onto it, or keep the name as a literary drug.
- Import is politics. A drug that must come by ship is a drug a court can fail to have in a war or a bad season. Korea knows this in its bones and makes *hyangyak* a policy word. Japan knows it too, in a different register: *wamyō*, Japanese names, notes on whether a thing is produced at home.

So the early Japanese *honzō* is not a cute local flora. It is a **bilingual and logistical labor** imposed by a textbook.

Two offices sit near this labor, and I will not pretend I have their daily papers. The court medical bureaucracy (the names change; *Ten’yakuryō* is the one English readers meet) trains people who must pass through Chinese text. Temples keep other streams — Buddhist healing, monastery gardens, a different reason to care what a plant is called. Draft 26 will refuse to make those one “Japanese medicine.”

There is a third stream I will name and not develop: the Korean peninsula as a route, not only China-direct. Métailié and others have insisted on this. A collection that writes Japan as China’s student and Korea as China’s other student has already forgotten geography.

What I will not do is say that Japan “preserved the *Xinxiu* for China.” That sentence shows up, sometimes, as a compliment. It makes the Japanese copy a fridge. The copy was a textbook. It was annotated. It was used to make a name-book and to feed *Ishinpō*. Preservation happened, in the sense that wording survived. The purpose was Japanese office life.

When, centuries later, Edo scholars treat the *Gangmu* as a building they live in, they are repeating the same problem at a higher resolution: a Chinese encyclopedia of stuff, a Japanese landscape, a need to name. The Tang book is the first time the problem has a surviving Japanese paper trail. That is why this file sits at the front of the Japan stage, before the famous titles.

If you want a single image: a clerk in Heian-kyō with a Tang monograph in his head, a market name in his ear, and a plant in someone’s hand that might be neither. The rest of the Japanese drafts are versions of that clerk.

## Open questions

- What is the earliest Japanese source that *names* the *Xinxiu* as the office textbook, as opposed to our inference from *Honzō wamyō*’s structure?
- How much of the early drug supply was peninsula-routed, and how would we know from Japanese texts that prefer to cite Tang titles?

## Sources used

Kotobank / *Kokushi daijiten*-style descriptions of *Honzō wamyō* as built on *Xinxiu*. Métailié on peninsula transmission (as cited in later handbook chapters). Drafts 13, 24, 25 for the documentary trail. Trambaiolo on later limits of Sinification — used here only as a warning not to make early Japan a clean Chinese transplant.

## Claims I am not making

- That I have a complete history of Asuka/Nara medical institutions.
- That Japanese *honzō* is “just” translated Tang *bencao*.
- That preservation was the Japanese intention.
