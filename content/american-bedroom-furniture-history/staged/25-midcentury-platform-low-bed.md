# The Midcentury Platform and the Low Bed

George Nelson and Henry Wright’s *Tomorrow’s House* (1945) asks the reader to look at the bedroom as a place where more than sleep happens, not as a room that exists to hold a standard suite. Nelson, from 1946 design director at Herman Miller, had the factory to try the thought. The Thin Edge bed of 1954 is a low platform with a slim headboard, a horizontal line, no posts, no footboard theater. It sits close to the floor by the standard of a 1938 waterfall. It is an American industrial object, designed in Michigan’s orbit, not a futon and not a Copenhagen export. If you want the postwar American bedroom’s argument in one piece, start here rather than with a teak headboard that arrived in a department-store container.

The posts had been in trouble for a long time. Eastlake and the sanitary writers of the 1880s already hated hangings. Brass beds of 1900 had no testers. Stickley cut the height. Hollywood took the legs off for the camera. The platform is the thought completed: the bed is a surface, not a piece of architecture. What is new after 1945 is the advertising, which will mention Japan, and the competition, which will mention Denmark, and the tract house, whose ceiling is low enough that a tall-post tester looks like a mistake.

## Nelson’s floor and Herman Miller’s bedroom

Nelson’s 1946 Platform bench for Herman Miller is a cousin: a slab on legs that is a table, a seat, a TV stand, a place to put a suitcase. The bedroom takes the same idea and puts a mattress on it. Thin Edge (1954) is named for the rim. Storage pieces in the same family have a similar edge, a wooden or metal thinness that photographs as expensive. The bed recedes. Nelson wanted the room to remain the subject. *Tomorrow’s House* is full of that editor’s impatience with “standard furniture.” A four-poster in a small postwar bedroom is a standard. A platform is a decision.

Herman Miller’s bedroom was never the company’s loudest room — the office and the lounge ate the famous names, Eames and Nelson again — but the bed existed, in walnut or a specified finish, with a mattress spec that assumed a thinner pad than later pillow-tops. Rails are part of a low box. You do not lace rope. You do not hide a waterfall radius. You see the floor. American wall-to-wall carpet, the other postwar surface, runs under the bed or stops at a plinth. Either way the posts are gone.

The Eames office did not give America a canonical bed. That absence is useful. The famous plywood chair is a living-room object; the bedroom had to be solved by case goods and by mattress companies. A couple who owned DCM chairs might still sleep on a Hollywood frame with a quilted headboard from a department store. The photographs that mix Eames and a low walnut platform are decorator photographs. Zeeland could ship the platform. It did not ship the majority of American sleep.

Joinery on a Thin Edge case is a rim, a biscuit or a spline, screws in a pattern, a finish that shows a dent. The bed’s platform is a frame with slats or a deck, knock-down hardware that a dealer’s man could assemble in a tract house without a Hall brother’s patience. Legs, if they exist, are short. Some platforms are a box that sits on the floor like a Japanese bed and is not one: no tatami, a Western mattress, a sheet. The American object is taller than the drawing because the mattress industry won.

Knoll’s bedroom furniture in the same years — Florence Knoll’s case goods, the rational storage wall — treats sleep as an item among items. A headboard that is a padded rectangle on a steel frame. Mies and Barcelona in the sitting end of a loft; a mattress on a low base behind a divider. This is not how most Americans slept. It is how the magazines slept. The distinction is the chapter’s job.

## “Japanese” as a copy line

House Beautiful under Elizabeth Gordon spent 1960 on *shibui*, a Japanese word the magazine used for a quiet, understated beauty it wanted Americans to want. The summer issues are documents of a taste campaign, not of a Tokyo carpenter in a Levittown bedroom. Low furniture, natural wood, a mattress closer to the floor, paper lanterns, an absence of dust-catching posts: the pictures look Japanese to a reader who has not been in a Japanese house. They also look like Nelson, like Craftsman without oak’s fumed moral, like a Hollywood bed that got honest about wood.

Advertisers took the hint. “Oriental modern,” “Japanese influence,” a low headboard with a cane panel, a shoji-like screen used as a room divider in a small ranch. The objects are American factory: maple, walnut veneer, a brass ferrule, a paper label in a drawer. A genuine Japanese bed — a futon on tatami — is not what was in the carton. The carton held a platform with a headboard a few inches high and a copywriter’s Japan.

The Greenes had already done a more serious Japan in Pasadena, with pegs and cloudlifts, and they had not sold it as a bedroom suite. 1960 *shibui* is a magazine Japan: pillows on a platform, a paper lamp, a vase. Arts & Architecture’s Case Study Houses sometimes showed low beds in glass rooms; those beds are closer to Nelson than to Kyoto. A Case Study bedroom with a mattress on a plinth is architecture using furniture as a line. The tract ranch using a cane headboard is a store using Japan as a spice. Both are American. Only one had a copywriter.

Gordon had also run Scandinavia. The July 1959 Scandinavia issue and the shibui issues sit next to each other in the bound year. American modern bedrooms were asked to choose, or to blend: Danish teak height, Japanese low, American walnut. Most rooms blended badly and happily. A platform bed, two nightstands with tapered legs, a paper lantern from a import shop, a highboy still in the parents’ house. The posts were what you did not buy.

## Tract ceilings, Hollywood frames, and the mattress

The ranch house and the Cape that the GI Bill filled have eight-foot ceilings, or less under a slope. A pencil-post tester is a museum object in that room. The low bed is also a practical answer: a mattress you can sit on to put on socks, a headboard that does not fight a picture window. Kids’ rooms took bunks and then took platforms. The “Hollywood frame” — a metal angle-iron support that takes a headboard and no footboard — is the cheap structure under a million American mattresses. It is not a designer platform. It is the platform’s vernacular. A headboard bolts on. The dresser does not match unless you bought a suite.

Simmons, Sealy, the inner-spring as a hygiene product: the mattress thickens through the 1950s. A low frame plus a thick mattress is not as low as the drawing. By 1965 the platform look often needs a thinner pad than the customer wants. Waterbeds will solve this later by being a thick object that is still “on the floor.” The 1950s platform is a wooden or metal idea fighting a mattress industry that sells height as comfort.

Joinery on a designer platform is screws, metal brackets, a knock-down that is proud of being engineered. Compare Stickley’s pinned tenon. The honesty has moved from the peg to the specification sheet. A Herman Miller bed that still has its original rails is a document. Most do not. The headboard survives on a later frame. Caption that.

## Not Danish, not yet the McCobb chapter

Danish imports — the next but one chapter — bring teak, a sculpted foot, a headboard that is a board with a lift. American copies, Selig among them, will put that silhouette in living rooms first. Bedrooms follow. The platform as Nelson drew it is flatter, more architectural, less “crafted” in the teak sense. American modern after the war is several bedrooms:

- Herman Miller / Knoll: specified, expensive, a headboard as a line.
- McCobb Planner: maple, iron or tapered wood legs, open stock, a headboard you can afford (chapter 26).
- Heywood-Wakefield: blond birch, a suite that still matches, Streamline leftover (chapter 27).
- Department-store “modern”: walnut veneer, brass ferrules, a suite in the old sense with the new legs.
- The Hollywood frame and a single padded headboard: majority practice.

Japanese influence as advertised sits on all of these except the last, which has no copy, only a truck. Do not let a museum platform stand for the ranch house. Do not let a Wegner chair in a bedroom photograph stand for the bed. The bed is lower than the chair. That is the whole formal event.

Labor in a Zeeland or a Grand Rapids modern plant in 1954 is not a Hall brother cutting ebony. It is a line, a jig, a woman in finish, a man in the machine room, a designer’s name on the catalog because names now sell modern the way Mission once sold oak. Nelson’s name on a bed is a warranty of taste. The mill still cuts the parts.

Adrian Pearsall’s Craft Associates platforms and walnut headboards — Pennsylvania, a different mill culture — give a more sculptural American low bed, a walnut slab with a lift, sometimes mistaken for Danish in an estate sale. Lane, Drexel Declaration (Kipp Stewart and Stewart MacDougall), Broyhill’s later Brasilia: American factories putting a low headboard on a suite that still wants to be complete. The platform idea leaks into the suite instead of killing it. You get a low bed and a triple dresser on tapered legs. Completeness learns to crouch.

## Residual

The posts do not return as a necessity. They return as Colonial Revival, as a four-poster a couple buys because the magazine said heritage, as a brass revival in the 1970s. The platform remains the modern default: a mattress, a low support, a headboard that is a wall or a board. Nelson’s Thin Edge is an expensive sentence in that paragraph. The Hollywood frame is the prose. Japan in the advertisement was a way to tell a customer that low was not poverty. It was a look. The floor, suddenly visible around the bed, is the American bedroom’s new wood.

A last particular: storage headboards — a shelf, a sliding door, a cubby for a clock radio — are the platform’s American cousin, a mill trying to sell one more case where the posts used to be. They are homely. They are everywhere. They are not Japanese. They are the suite logic glued to the wall behind a mattress. Nakashima will do this with a slab and sliding doors and make it wood as argument. The department store will do it with walnut print and a plastic track.

Box-spring height is the silent enemy of the drawing. A 1954 Thin Edge under a 1962 extra-firm innerspring is already a different silhouette. Photographers for Herman Miller used thin pads. The ranch house used what the mattress store financed. The low bed, in practice, is a negotiation with Simmons.
