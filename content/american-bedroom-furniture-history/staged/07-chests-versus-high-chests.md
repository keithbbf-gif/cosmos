# Chests versus High Chests

Met 18.110.4 stands ninety-one and three-quarter inches in the American Wing: mahogany and mahogany veneer over yellow pine, tulip poplar, and northern white cedar, Philadelphia, 1762–65. A scrolled pediment, a figural finial, a serpent-and-swan carving on the bottom drawer that Morrison Heckscher’s catalog traces to Thomas Johnson’s *A New Book of Ornaments* (London, 1762). Collectors call it a highboy. The shop that made it called it a high chest of drawers. The legs are the argument. This case does not sit on the floor. It stands on cabriole legs the way a dressing table stands, and that is why Philadelphia could sell it with a matching low chest — the dressing table — as a pair without ever inventing a suite.

Three rooms away, or three galleries later depending on the hang, Met 64.249.3 tells the other American story. Chest-on-chest, New York, 1760–90, mahogany over yellow poplar, yellow pine, and white pine. Seventy-eight and three-quarter inches. Wide cornice. Canted and fluted corners. Front claw feet with carved knees, ogee-bracket rear feet. Until the Met acquired it in 1964 it had stayed in the Van Rensselaer family of Albany. No cabriole undercroft. No space to sweep under. Two stacked cases on feet that behave like architecture. New York liked this form. Philadelphia, in the same decades, spent its carving budget on the high chest with legs.

The question this chapter has to answer is not which is prettier. It is why American shops kept both, and why one of them — the high chest on legs — drops out of fashionable production around 1800 while the other keeps going, under a Federal skin, into the 1820s.

## Two stacks, two stances

A high chest of drawers is a chest on a frame, or a chest on a lower case that still has legs. The upper case is a box of drawers. The lower case is either a frame with one long drawer and cabriole or turned legs, or a short stack of drawers on those same legs. A waist molding hides the joint. You can take the upper case off to move the piece up a stair. That is not a museum convenience. It is how the thing entered a chamber in 1760.

A chest-on-chest is two cases of drawers, the lower one sitting on bracket, ogee, or claw-and-ball feet. The waist is still there. The removable upper case is still there. What is gone is the undercroft: the air, the legs, the dressing-table kinship. You gain cubic storage. You lose the silhouette that later Americans will put on a postage stamp.

William Macpherson Hornor’s *Blue Book of Philadelphia Furniture* and Heckscher’s Met Chippendale volume keep the vocabulary honest. Shops billed high chests, high cases of drawers, chests of drawers. *Highboy* and *tallboy* are later English-derived collector words. *Tallboy* in London often means what Americans mean by chest-on-chest. The American *highboy* is the legged form. Mixing the two in a caption is how a dressing table gets called a lowboy and then, worse, a “bedroom set.”

Boston had already stacked drawers on turned legs with stretchers before 1730. That is the previous chapter. What happens after mid-century is regional divergence, not a single American type.

## Philadelphia’s pair

Philadelphia’s high chests of the 1750s–70s are the most theatrical wooden objects American shops had yet put in a chamber. Scrolled pediments. Rocaille carving on the skirt. Ball-and-claw feet with undercut talons. A dressing table that repeats the skirt, the knee carving, and the brass pattern so the two pieces read as kin when they stand in the same room. They are a pair. They are not a five-piece suite. The bed in that chamber is still a textile machine. The looking glass may not match. The nightstand does not exist as a type.

Winterthur’s holdings, catalogued in Joseph Downs’s *American Furniture: Queen Anne and Chippendale Periods*, are the place to see the pair as a habit rather than a unique. Du Pont bought high chests and dressing tables that share carving vocabularies even when a paper trail does not prove they left the shop the same week. The honest label says “companion form,” not “original suite.” `[CITE NEEDED: a specific Winterthur high chest / dressing table pair with accession numbers once the collection page is locked for figures.]`

Joinery is how you date when the pediment is lying. Drawer bottoms in Philadelphia work often run front to back, the grain oriented so the bottom can shrink toward the back without splitting the front. Dustboards between drawers are more common in better shops. Secondary woods: tulip poplar, yellow pine, cedar — the Met’s 18.110.4 mix is typical, not exotic. Dovetails on drawer sides are relatively fine. The upper case sits in a rabbet or on a molding; a repaired waist is as common as replaced brasses. Conservators trust the unseen box.

The carving on 18.110.4’s bottom drawer — serpent and swan after Johnson 1762 — is the kind of London citation that made Philadelphia furniture look metropolitan. The Chippendale *Director* supplied the pediment logic. Neither book told the shop to put the case on cabriole legs. That stance was already American Queen Anne practice, carried forward and dressed rococo. The high chest is a Baroque body in a Chippendale coat.

Labor in a Philadelphia shop of this grade is divided. A joiner builds the cases. A carver — sometimes a specialist, sometimes the same man on a different day — does the knees, skirt, and pediment. A turner may not be needed if the legs are cabriole, sawn and shaped. Enslaved and free Black workmen are documented in Philadelphia trades in this period more thickly than in Boston of 1720; named cabinet-shop attributions remain sparse and should be marked as such rather than filled with a convenient biography. `[CITE NEEDED: a named workman of color in a Philadelphia cabinet shop connected to high-chest production, if bills yield one.]`

Brasses are a dating problem. Original rococo plates have a shadow when they are gone. A Colonial Revival replacement row is the most common lie on an otherwise honest case. Heckscher treats hardware as evidence. So should a caption.

## New York’s floor-standing stack

New York’s chest-on-chest, the Van Rensselaer Met example being the public one, prefers mass and a cornice that would not look wrong on a secretary. Canted corners with flutes. Claw feet in front, ogee brackets behind — a mixed stance that is typical rather than indecisive. The form follows English tallboy practice more closely than Philadelphia’s high chest does. New York patrons with Loyalist or London-facing taste could buy a case that looked like a London export without paying the freight on a London carcass.

The Met’s other New York chest-on-chest citations in the Chippendale TOAH essay (47.35 among them) keep the type from being a unique. `[CITE NEEDED: confirm 47.35 as chest-on-chest and its date range before the figure caption is locked.]` The Van Rensselaer provenance matters because it ties the form to a landholding family who will, two generations later, also buy Lannuier beds. The chamber’s storage and the chamber’s bed do not have to come from the same aesthetic generation. Houses accumulate.

Construction: two full cases. The lower case is deeper in the drawer count. The upper case often has a secret or shallow top drawer in the frieze, or a bonnet that is a box rather than an open scroll. New York bonnets tend to be denser, less airy than Philadelphia’s pierced pediments. Secondary woods overlap — yellow poplar, pine — but the mix is not a fingerprint by itself. The fingerprint is the canted corner, the cornice weight, the refusal of cabriole undercroft.

Why would a shop offer this instead of a high chest? Storage. A chest-on-chest packs more linen into the same wall. It sits flush to a baseboard. It does not require the floor to be seen underneath, which matters when the chamber is crowded with a hung bed, a dressing glass, and a trunk. It is also cheaper in carving hours if you skip the knees and skirt show. A New York claw foot on the front is still hours. It is fewer hours than a Philadelphia high chest with carved skirt, knees, and pediment rosettes.

## New England and Newport keep both

Boston and the North Shore never abandon the high chest; they just stop competing with Philadelphia on carving. Blockfront and later bombé cases in Massachusetts put the theater in the facade, not in a rococo skirt. Newport’s Townsend and Goddard shops put block-and-shell on high chests *and* on chests-on-chests and on dressing tables. The Met’s block-and-shell citations (15.21.2 and kin) are the public proof that the shell is a Newport sentence, not a single form. You can say the same thing on legs or on brackets.

That is the regional answer to “why both.” Newport’s prestige language was the shell and the block, not the stance. A patron could order the language on whatever stack fit the chamber. A high chest still paired visually with a dressing table. A chest-on-chest still stored more. Both were Newport if the shells were right.

Rural New England shops kept making simpler maple and cherry high chests after the cities had moved on, because the form was known, the joinery was teachable, and a chamber still needed vertical storage in the footprint of a table. Those country high chests are not failed Philadelphias. They are the form doing its original job without the *Director*.

## What the legs were for

The high chest’s undercroft is not empty for elegance alone. You can sweep under it. You can set a dressing box, a pair of shoes, a child’s stool. In a chamber without a closet, the floor at the base of the case is working space. The dressing table — lower, with a kneehole or a stack of small drawers — is where you sit with a basin and a glass. The high chest is where folded linen lives. Together they are a storage-and-toilet pair that a later suite will try to absorb into a dresser-plus-vanity. They are not yet that.

The chest-on-chest cannot offer the undercroft. It offers a wall. In a narrow townhouse chamber the wall of drawers is the point. In a New York house with English pretensions the architectural case is the point. Neither shop is confused. They are answering different rooms.

## When the highboy falls out of fashion

Fashionable American shops stop making the cabriole high chest in the last two decades of the eighteenth century. The date is not a law. It is a thinning. Federal taste — Hepplewhite and Sheraton plates, French-derived legs, light veneers, inlay instead of rococo carving — does not want a Baroque stack on hairy-paw feet. What it wants in the chamber is a bureau (a chest of drawers of moderate height), sometimes a secretary, sometimes a chest-on-chest with French feet, inlay stringing, and a straight cornice. Charles F. Montgomery’s *American Furniture: The Federal Period* at Winterthur is the catalog of that replacement, not of a “highboy in Hepplewhite dress.” A few shops will veneer an old form. Most will not bother.

Philadelphia, which had made the high chest its masterpiece, makes the turn with particular visibility. The rococo high chest of 1765 is already conservative by 1785. By 1800 a new chest for a new house is a veneered bureau with oval brasses. The old high chest stays in the family. It is not reordered from the shop.

New York’s chest-on-chest survives the turn more easily because its stance was already architectural. Put French feet under it, lighten the cornice, inlay an urn, and you have a Federal tall case. Shops keep making those into the 1810s and 1820s. Collectors who call every tall American case a highboy erase this survival.

The high chest on legs does not vanish from rooms. It vanishes from *orders*. That distinction is the whole decline. An object can stand in a chamber for eighty years after the shop has stopped billing it. Inventories still name high chests in 1810. The bills for new ones have dried up in the cities.

Country shops run later. A Vermont or western Massachusetts maple high chest of 1800–20 is not a city fashion statement. It is a useful stack. Those pieces confuse dating if you only read silhouette.

Then the form dies a second death and gets a second life. By the 1840s a rococo high chest is old furniture. By the 1876 Centennial, and then in the Wallace Nutting decades, it is the American antique par excellence. Colonial Revival bedrooms will put a highboy in a room with a pencil-post bed and call the pairing colonial. It is a pairing the eighteenth century sometimes made and the nineteenth-century salesman never sold as a numbered set. The Revival loves the legs. It loves the pediment. It does not need the dressing table to match, and it has forgotten that the bed was cloth.

## Contested attributions, ordinary lies

Unlabeled high chests get famous names because the names raise the case. A “Goddard” without a bill, a label, or a construction quirk isolated in the literature is a wish. Michael Moses’s Newport studies and the later Townsend-Goddard scholarship are the check: shop habits in drawer bottoms, glue-block shapes, shell carving, and secondary-wood mixes. Heckscher and the Met object files are the check for Philadelphia. Downs is the check for Winterthur’s purchases, which include things du Pont wanted to be pairs.

Replaced finials, replaced cartouches, replaced brasses, a pediment rebuilt after a ceiling scrape — these are ordinary. A high chest that has been “improved” in 1920 with extra carving is a known Winterthur and market problem. The honest catalog says so. A magazine caption that does not is selling.

The chest-on-chest has its own lies: a lower case from one shop under an upper case from another, married in an antiques store because both were mahogany and both were seventy-eight inches. Look at the waist. Look at the drawer-lip profiles. Look at the secondary woods. A marriage is not a type.

## Residual

Americans kept both forms because one of them was a piece of furniture on legs that could converse with a dressing table, and the other was a wall of drawers that could converse with a secretary. Philadelphia bet on the first. New York bet on the second. Newport wrote the same shell sentence on both. When Federal taste arrived, the legged high chest had nowhere to go that veneer and inlay wanted to follow, so the shops stopped making it. The chest-on-chest put on French feet and continued.

The highboy that Americans now treat as the bedroom antique is a form the fashionable shop abandoned. That is why it could be rediscovered. A living product does not need a Revival. Met 18.110.4 is ninety-one inches of a discontinued idea, still better at holding linen than at explaining a suite.
