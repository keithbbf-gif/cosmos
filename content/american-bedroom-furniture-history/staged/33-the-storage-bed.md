# The Storage Bed

A captain’s bed, in the language of American furniture ads, is a platform with drawers in the box the mattress sits on. Sometimes a bookcase headboard. Sometimes a cubby at the foot. The name wants a ship: a cabin so tight that the only place for shirts is under the berth, the captain’s privilege a few extra drawers. Maritime museums hold shipboard furniture — folding desks, berths built into bulkheads, chests lashed to the deck — but the retail “captain’s bed” of a 1970s apartment is not a documented descendant of a named naval type the way a Boston high chest is a Boston high chest. It is a nickname that stuck because it sounds like thrift and authority at once. `[CITE NEEDED: a dated ship-chandler or naval-furniture catalog that uses “captain’s bed” for a drawer-berth, if one exists; until then treat the nautical origin as folklore with a plausible cabin logic.]`

What the object actually is, in American houses, is easier. The floor under a bed is dead space if the bed is posts and a rail. Fill that space with drawers on slides, and the high chest’s work — folded clothes, a vertical address — moves into the sleeper’s footprint. The chamber can lose a case piece. In a room that never had a high chest, the storage bed is the first case piece, horizontal, with a person on top.

## The job that used to stand up

Chapter 3’s Boston high chest stacked drawers because a chamber’s floor was small and a lid-chest made you move the looking glass. Vertical storage was an answer, not a style. Two centuries later the American bedroom is often smaller again, or feels smaller: a mattress that has grown from a rope-grid tick to a queen, a room that also holds a desk because the house has no study, a closet that the developer already claimed as the wardrobe. The high chest does not fit the ceiling, or the budget, or the taste for a low platform. The drawers go under.

This is not evolution. It is a relocation of cubic feet. A six-drawer MALM chest and a MALM storage bed in the same room are the suite admitting it needs both the wall and the floor. A storage bed alone, in a studio, is the suite reduced to a single rectangle. The photograph still wants a nightstand. The dresser can go.

Shaker communities built beds that rolled on wooden wheels so a room could be cleared. That is a different use of the under-bed: access to the floor, not occupancy of it. Trundle beds (chapter 44) slide a second sleeper into the same cavity. The storage bed fills the cavity with goods instead of a child. All three inventions treat the volume under the mattress as too valuable to leave to dust.

## How the drawers are made

A storage bed is a carcass. Sides, a deck for the mattress, partitions, drawers. If the mattress is to sit low, the drawers are shallow and the count goes up. If the bed is to sit high — twenty-four to thirty inches to the deck is a common sales claim for “captain” height — the drawers can be deeper, like a chest on its back. Clearance to open them is a room problem: you cannot push the bed to the wall on the drawer side. The object dictates a plan.

Joints follow the shop. A custom hardwood version from a small American shop in the 1970s — teak and oak captains’ beds were a known special-order in cities where apartments were tight — can have dovetailed drawers, web frames, a knockdown bed rail so the box ships in panels. A forum memory of a 1977 ten-drawer teak bed that still looked new decades later is not a museum accession, but it names the type’s promise: the piece is furniture, not a box spring with bins. `[CITE NEEDED: a dated catalog from a named U.S. maker of solid-wood captain’s beds, 1965–1985.]` Factory versions use the same shortcuts as other case goods: dowels, screws into particleboard, metal slides. The slides matter. A drawer under a mattress takes weight from above and a hip against the side. Cheap slides rack. Then the drawer binds, and the owner stops using the storage, and the bed is only tall.

IKEA’s contribution was to make the type a warehouse SKU. MALM beds with under-bed drawers, BRIMNES, HEMNES daybeds with three drawers, later ottoman-style lifts in other markets — the names shift, the logic does not. The deck is particleboard or slats. The drawers are foil-wrapped. You assemble the box on the floor. You are warned, if the bed is also a chest of drawers at the head, that the whole thing is a tip-over cousin; a tall bookcase headboard is a case piece that happens to have a mattress attached. Anchor it. The 2016 dresser recall (chapter 32) is about free-standing chests, but the physics of a loaded upper drawer does not care whether the marketing department called the object a bed.

American copies filled the middle price: solid-pine captains’ beds from the import wave, “mission” oak storage beds with stained veneer, upholstered storage beds whose drawers hide behind a skirt. The lift-up ottoman, more common in British retail, keeps the mattress on a gas-strut lid and uses the whole cavity as a trunk. That is a chest. The sleeper is the lid. A high chest never asked you to move the bedding to get to the sheets. The ottoman bed does. Convenience always has a motion.

## What the mattress does to the box

Nineteenth-century American chambers sometimes kept a low chest or a valise under a high-post bed. That is storage under a bed, not a storage bed. The distinction is a carcass. Until the mattress sits on a deck that is also the top of a case, you have a hiding place, not a type. English and later American *divan* bases — an upholstered box that holds the spring and, in some trade, a drawer — sit on the same line. The word *divan* in British retail still means a bed whose base is a stuffed box. Drawers in that box are the captain’s idea in another dialect.

A rope-grid bed could not easily be a drawer case. The sleeping surface was a lacing that needed to be reached. A slat bed can be a lid. A platform of plywood or particleboard is a tabletop with drawers under it. The twentieth-century mattress — innerspring, then foam — wants a flat, even support (chapter 34). The storage bed and the foam mattress are allies. The box spring, which was a second upholstered box, is the enemy of under-bed drawers because it occupies the cavity. When the boxed foam mattress killed the box spring’s necessity, the cavity came back on the market.

The bed rail, in a post-and-rail frame, is a tension member: it keeps the posts apart and carries the slats. In a storage bed the rail is a case side. It has to stay square or the drawers bind. I have squared a storage-bed carcass the way I square a dresser: diagonal measures, a clamp across the long way, screws into a corner block that should have been a proper glue block. The joint that fails is usually the front corner where a hip sits to put on shoes. That is not a diary. It is where the rack is.

Height is the hidden cost. A high chest put storage in the air and left the bed low. A storage bed puts storage in the air the sleeper must climb. Older sleepers, hospital-style needs, a child’s first climb — the captain’s bed is not always kind. A trundle is kinder to the person who has to get in. The catalog does not always say so.

Dust is the other cost. Drawers under a bed are on the floor’s weather: grit, pet hair, the leak from a bottle. A chest on legs at least keeps the bottom drawer off the mop. The storage bed’s lowest drawer is a dustpan. Anyone who has opened one in August already knows. The high chest’s bottom drawer had the same problem, which is why bun feet and brackets exist. The storage bed has no bun foot. It has a kick.

## Labor and the small room

Who needed this object? Sailors, if the folklore is allowed, and then anyone whose room was a cabin by another name: boardinghouse, dormitory, studio apartment, child’s room, the second bedroom that is also the office. Katherine Grier’s work on parlor and comfort is about display rooms; the storage bed is the opposite display. It hides the goods. The Victorian chamber stuffed with a suite wanted you to see the walnut. The captain’s bed wants you to see a made mattress and nothing else.

Children’s rooms took the type early because toys and clothes fight for the same floor. A twin captain’s bed with three drawers is a dresser the child cannot knock over as easily as a tall MALM — though a bookcase headboard can still come down, and bunks have their own CPSC life (chapter 44). The adult version in a 1980s condo is the same calculation with sweaters.

Labor to make one, in a small shop: a carcass is many square feet of panel, many slides, many drawer boxes. It is more work than a simple rail-and-post bed, less work than a bonnet-top high chest. The drawer count is the bill. Each box needs sides, a bottom in a groove, a face that matches the bed’s show wood, a pull that will not catch a passing shin. Factories like it because it is a box. Boxes nest in production even when they do not nest in the room. The American furniture towns that lost case-goods plants in the late twentieth century — Bassett in Virginia, the cheaper High Point lines, the Grand Rapids afterlife — still knew how to make a drawer. The storage bed kept that skill employed while the highboy went to the auction room.

Imported pine versions of the 1990s and 2000s often used a honeycomb or paperboard deck, a staple, a cam. They photographed as “solid wood” because the rails were pine. The drawers were not. A buyer who wanted the high chest’s cubic feet at a platform-bed price got the cubic feet. The secondary-wood honesty of an eighteenth-century case — white pine interiors named as pine — was replaced by a hang-tag that said “solid wood construction” and meant the parts you see from the doorway. That is a sales sentence with a long American life. It is not unique to this type. The storage bed just makes the unseen volume larger, so the lie, when it is a lie, has more cubic feet to occupy.

## What is not a storage bed

A few objects nearby should not be collapsed into the type. A Murphy or wall bed hides the sleeper, not the shirts. A sleigh bed with a dust ruffle and plastic bins on wheels is a makeshift, as Wikipedia’s distinction says: dedicated slides and a carcass are the furniture; bins are an admission. A hospital bed with a crank is a machine for a body, with storage if any in a separate locker. A berth on a ship may be a drawer-bed in fact without ever having been sold as a “captain’s bed” in a mall.

The highboy pair is not a storage bed. That needs saying only because both objects store clothes in a chamber. One is a facade you face. The other is a volume you climb. When a collector puts a high chest in a front hall (chapter 37), the clothes have already moved: to closets, to a MALM, to the drawers under the guest mattress. The afterlife of the high chest and the birth of the storage bed are the same household decision.

The residual fact is architectural. The American chamber once put linen in a stack that faced the bed. The late small room puts linen in a stack the bed is. You sleep on the dresser. The high chest’s job did not disappear. It lay down.
