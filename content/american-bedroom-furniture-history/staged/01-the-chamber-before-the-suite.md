# The Chamber Before the Suite

Inventories from seventeenth-century Massachusetts do not open on a “bedroom set.” They open on a bedstead and what hung on it. A 1653 Essex County listing that names a “feather bed, boulster, and two pillows” before it bothers with the frame is not being poetic. The feathers were money. The posts were a carpenter’s problem. The room that later Americans would call a bedroom was still, in most houses, a chamber: a place to sleep, store, receive, and, when the house was small, work.

The word *suite* is a nineteenth-century sales idea. It does not belong here except as a warning. What this chapter has to do is see the colonial chamber without that later grid — bed, dresser, chest, nightstand, all in one finish — laid over it.

## The bed as the expensive object

Robert Blair St. George, Abbott Lowell Cummings, and the probate work that sits behind *Rural Household Inventories* keep returning to the same imbalance: bedding outvalues the bedstead. A turned maple frame with a simple tester can be priced in shillings. The tick, the flock or feathers, the curtains, the valance, and the rugs that went over the coverlet run into pounds. Benno Forman’s essays on New England joinery and Peter Follansbee’s later shop reconstructions of the same joints make the wood side countable: mortised rails, turned posts, a headboard that is often a single board, slats or a rope grid. The textile side is a different trade — spinning, weaving, imported stuffs — and a different ledger.

A chamber without hangings is not an incomplete suite. It is a colder room. In a house that has not yet learned central heat, the bed is a small room inside the room. Tester rails exist so cloth can make a box. The later four-poster that Americans photograph as “colonial” is often this machine with the cloth removed, which is like photographing a loom without the warp.

Winterthur and the Met hold enough early bedsteads to make the type visible. Many are later in date than the inventories that first name them; wood survives when cloth does not. The honest label says so. A “seventeenth-century bed” in a period room is frequently an eighteenth-century frame dressed to a seventeenth-century idea. Cummings’s *Framed Houses of Massachusetts Bay* is still the architectural half of that caution: the chamber is a framed room with a chimney bay, not a showroom.

## Chests, not dressers

The other object in the room is a chest. Six-board, joined, carved, painted. The Hadley chests of the Connecticut Valley — the type Robert Trent and Philip Zea have kept in the literature — are storage with a front that announces a family. They are not “bedroom storage” in the later sense. They hold linens, clothing, and the household’s portable wealth. A chest of drawers, when it arrives in the late seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries, is a New England and London idea stacked: drawers in a case, still often in the hall or chamber without a matching bed.

Boston and Salem shops in the first decades of the eighteenth century begin to make the high chest of drawers that later collectors call a highboy. That form gets its own chapters. Here the point is only that the chamber accumulated case pieces one at a time, by marriage, inheritance, and a cabinetmaker’s bill, not by a catalog page that showed all five pieces in walnut.

A dressing table — the lowboy — is even later as a named partner. Early chambers had a looking glass if they were lucky, a basin if they were ordinary, and a chest whose top served. The vanity as a type is a twentieth-century word laid on an eighteenth-century table.

## Who slept in the chamber

The “master bedroom” is not a colonial category. Parents, children, servants, and visitors share rooms in ways that later privacy language cannot describe. In a one- or two-chamber house, the best bed is in the parlor chamber or the hall chamber; lesser beds are trundles, truckle beds that slide under, or ticks on the floor. Enslaved people in the Chesapeake and in northern port towns slept in lofts, kitchens, outbuildings, and, less often than white family myth prefers, in the same chamber. The furniture record is thinner there because the objects were cheaper and the inventories were written by the people who owned the labor. `[CITE NEEDED: a specific Chesapeake inventory that prices enslaved people’s bedding separately from the parlor chamber.]`

John Michael Vlach and the archaeological work on quartering are the corrective, not a decorative inclusion. A history of American bedroom furniture that begins only with mahogany high chests has already decided who counted as a sleeper.

## Regional wood, imported cloth

New England used maple, oak, pine, and, when the port allowed, walnut and then mahogany. The Mid-Atlantic had walnut earlier and in quantity. The Chesapeake used walnut, yellow pine, and cypress in ways that MESDA’s southern furniture surveys have made ordinary. The cloth in all three regions was often imported: English woolens, later printed cottons, linens from Ireland and the Continent. Jennifer Anderson’s *Mahogany* is a later-century book; the principle is already here. The American chamber is local joinery dressed in an Atlantic textile market.

That is why a surviving bedstead looks naked. The shop made posts. London and the Indies made the surface you saw when the candles were lit.

## What is not yet a suite

A matching finish across a room is possible in a wealthy house by the 1740s — same cabinetmaker, same wood, same brass. It is still not a suite in the Grand Rapids sense. No one is selling “the chamber set, no. 4, in ash.” The high chest and dressing table that travel together in Philadelphia after 1750 are a pair, not a five-piece package. The bed remains a textile architecture with a wooden skeleton. The nightstand does not exist as a type. The wardrobe is a later, European-derived answer to a closet that American houses often lacked.

If you want a single residual fact from this opening chapter, take it from the inventories: count the pounds on the feathers before you count the inches on the posts. The American bedroom will spend the next two centuries trying to make the wood as visible, and as sellable, as the cloth once was.

The later suite does not evolve out of this chamber the way a species evolves. It replaces a logic. A salesman in Grand Rapids will need a room that can be photographed as a set. A colonial chamber cannot be photographed that way without lying. This series starts on the lie’s opposite: a bed, a chest, a bill for stuffs, and a house that has not yet decided the bedroom is a showroom.

## A room that is still a workplace

The later American bedroom is a room you leave in the morning. The colonial chamber is often a room you work in. Spinning, sewing, the keeping of accounts, a sickbed that lasts a month — the furniture has to stand that use. A chest at the foot of the bed is a work surface. A chair by the fire is as much a chamber object as the bedstead, and the inventories say so. This series is about bedroom furniture, not a full household. The warning still belongs: if you photograph only the bed and the high chest, you have already modernized the room.

Urban chambers in Boston and Philadelphia by 1720 can look closer to the later idea: a best bed, a case of drawers, a looking glass, fewer tools in sight. Rural chambers keep the mixed use longer. The difference is money and the number of rooms, not a sudden invention of privacy. Privacy is a count of doors.

## What the later photograph erases

A Colonial Revival bedroom of 1925 will put a highboy on one wall, a four-poster without hangings on another, a lowboy under a glass, a Windsor by the window, and call the result “early American.” Every object may be old. The room is not. The old chamber did not match. It accumulated. It priced feathers above posts. It hung cloth that the revival was afraid of because cloth is dust and work.

This chapter’s job is to make that accumulation visible so the high chests in chapters 3–5 do not look like they invented the bedroom. They invented a vertical wooden face. The bedroom was already there, expensive in stuffing, cheap in maple, shared, and unfinished by any catalog.

Cummings’s inventories, Forman’s joints, and the Hadley chests are enough to start. The Chesapeake gap on enslaved bedding is enough to keep the start from being a New England comfort. The rest of the series can get specific about shops. It should not get nostalgic about chambers.


## Six-board, joined, and a till in the dark

A six-board chest is a nailed box. Bottom, two sides, front, back, lid. The ends of the front and back usually overlap the sides; sometimes the shop reverses it. A till — a small lidded compartment at one end — sits in the dark when the lid is down and holds a deed, a pair of gloves, a little silver. Pintle hinges, or battens nailed across the lid so the board does not cup. A lock if the household had anything a lock was worth. Peter Follansbee’s reconstructions of this object are useful because they count the hours: a competent man can nail a board chest in a day. Oak, pine, or a mix. The earlier boards may be riven, not sawn. You can still see the split in the grain if you look at the end.

A joined chest is a frame. Stiles and rails, mortised and tenoned, pinned through the tenon so the joint can be read from the face. Panels float in grooves so the wood can move through a New England year. The front may be carved — lunettes, palmettes, the trailing vine that Connecticut Valley shops laid on oak until the type got the collector’s name Hadley. Robert Trent and Philip Zea’s work on those chests is not a tourist caption. The initials on a Hadley front are often a woman’s, a marriage or a portion, the chest traveling with her into a chamber that already had a bedstead. Historic Deerfield and the Wadsworth Atheneum hold enough of the type to see that the carving is a vocabulary, not a unique. `[CITE NEEDED: one Hadley chest accession — Deerfield or Wadsworth — locked to the figure caption.]`

Thomas Dennis of Ipswich, and the joined oak associated with his shop and that town, is the other named New England joinery of this date. Chests, cupboards, the wainscot chairs that inventories put in the same rooms as beds. The Met’s Sage bequest and MFA Boston have published examples; the accession for a caption should be read off the collection page, not remembered from a lecture. `[CITE NEEDED: a Thomas Dennis / Ipswich joined chest accession for Fig. 7.]` What matters for the chamber is the labor split. Joined work is a specialist’s week. Nailed chests were what most floors held. If a museum shows only the carved front, it has selected the wedding object and skipped the box that actually took the blankets.

The court or press cupboard — two stages, often more expensive than the bedstead — stored plate and linens and made a public face in a room that was not yet a dining room. Some households kept a cupboard in the parlor and a chest in the chamber. Some had only the chest. The later dresser did not replace the cupboard in a clean line. The cupboard left. The chest of drawers arrived. The names in the inventories are the only map.

Wethersfield’s so-called sunflower chests, often tied to Peter Blin’s shop, put a different carving on the same joined logic: sunflowers, tulips, a paneled front that is a Connecticut sentence rather than a Connecticut Valley Hadley vine. The two types get collapsed in caption writing. They should not be. Different towns, different irons, the same job: a lid you lift, a lock you key, a chamber that has not yet learned the stacked drawer.

## Stuffs, ticks, and a price in pounds

A Massachusetts inventory that gives a feather bed at four pounds and a bedstead at eight shillings is doing economics, not folklore. `[CITE NEEDED: exact Essex or Suffolk docket and printed page in Cummings for a four-pound / eight-shilling pairing if used as a caption.]` Feathers were imported or saved from the household’s own birds. Ticks were sewn. Flock — chopped wool, sometimes worse — was the cheaper fill. A household that owned two feather beds and a flock bed had a ranking of sleepers without writing the word rank. The frame under them might be maple posts and a rope grid, or a truckle that the inventory prices in shillings and forgets to describe.

The mercer’s or shopkeeper’s line for cloth can outrun every wooden line on the same page. Imported moreen, a cheap woolen, a striped dornick, later a printed cotton. Local linen for sheets if the household had flax and a spinner. The woman who spun and sewed is rarely a named vendor. She is the reason the textile total is high and the cabinetmaker’s name is absent. The bill names a stuff. It does not name her.

Enslaved labor in northern port towns and in the Chesapeake handled feathers, ticks, and the weekly airing. The inventory still belongs to the owner. Chesapeake runs — York County, Virginia, among them — sometimes list “Negro bedding” as a separate, cheap line: a tick, a rug, no species of timber, or a pine frame that did not earn a joiner’s vocabulary. `[CITE NEEDED: a named Chesapeake inventory with a Negro-bedding line and a parlor-chamber feather bed on the same document.]` That is the start this chapter already asked for. It belongs next to the Hadley initials, not in a paragraph the reader can skip.

A carved stile is a week. A feather bed is a year’s birds and a tick that can be emptied and sewn again. Count the till pins if you like. Then count the pounds.
