# Heywood-Wakefield Blonde

Heywood-Wakefield’s Streamline Modern bedroom — solid birch, a wheat or champagne finish, a dresser whose corners never learned a sharp corner — sat in American houses that could not afford Herman Miller and did not want another waterfall. The look launched in the mid-1930s, while the Depression suite was still on the floor, and it lasted, with styling changes, into the 1960s. Collectors now say “Heywood” and mean this blond. The company was older than the blond. Gardner, Massachusetts, had been a chair town since the Heywood brothers began in a barn in 1826. Wakefield Rattan was the other name. The 1897 merger, and the later Heywood-Wakefield Company (the 1921 form of the name is the one the labels use), made wicker, rattan, school desks, railway seating, and, when the century turned, the kind of furniture a mill town makes when it already knows steam and a finishing room.

Chicago appears in the firm’s geography as a showroom city and as the 1933–34 Century of Progress, where Streamline design had a public. `[CITE NEEDED: exact Heywood-Wakefield exhibit presence at the Century of Progress; secondary accounts associate the line with that fair’s moment.]` Gardner remains the bedroom’s hometown. The eagle stamp, after 1949, goes in the top left drawer, under a table, or on a headboard stretcher. Before 1949 you hunt a paper tag that often failed.

## From rattan to a birch radius

Levi Heywood’s machinery argument in the 1840s is a company story: brothers skeptical, Levi right, a national chair business by the time he died in 1882, offices as far as San Francisco. Wicker and rattan were the nineteenth-century fame. Theater seats and school furniture were the volume. Early American suites were in the catalog when every mill had a colonial line. Gilbert Rohde worked with the company until 1934. The next year Streamline Maple appears; in 1937 the name is Streamline Modern. Maple first, then birch as the primary wood by the late 1930s, exclusive birch in much of the modern line by about 1940. Steve and Roger Rouland’s *Heywood-Wakefield Modern Furniture* (1995) is the collector’s book. Use it as orientation and check a label.

Count Alexis de Sakhnoffsky is sometimes credited as the Streamline designer. Other accounts treat the line as in-house after Rohde. Mark the attribution as contested. `[CITE NEEDED: a period trade credit — catalog title page or *Furniture Record* — naming a designer for Streamline Maple 1935.]` What is not contested is the radius. Streamline is trains, radios, a fender. Furniture takes the curve without waterfall’s veneer trick: this is solid wood, edges eased, a bullnose you can feel. No bookmatched walnut picture. The luxury is the thickness and the finish.

Wheat is a warm, medium blond lacquer that darkens a little in a sunny bedroom. Champagne is paler, a hint of rose in some descriptions. Amber and bleached are early stencil words on the backs of pieces. If a dresser back says Bleached or Amber, you may have a pre-war example whose paper tag is gone. Construction clues: slotted screws on early work, solid drawer sides, solid tops. The Roulands and the company’s later identification notes are firm on this: modern Heywood-Wakefield is solid birch or maple, not a gum core with a photograph of wood. That is the difference from chapter 23. A waterfall is a picture of walnut. A wheat dresser is birch all the way through the drawer side.

Open a Heywood drawer. The sides are birch, the bottom a thin panel in a groove, the dovetails or lock joints a mill’s consistent cut. Runners wear. A 1948 vanity that still slides has been lucky in humidity. The radius on the top is a hand-and-machine event: a shaper, then sandpaper, then the lacquer that will later chip on the high point of the curve — the same physics as waterfall, except the wood under the chip is birch, not a sliced face over gum. You can sand a Heywood radius and stay in the species. You cannot sand a waterfall chip without meeting plywood.

## The bedroom suite returns, blond

Heywood-Wakefield sold groups. A Streamline bedroom is a suite in the old sense: bed, dresser, vanity or a dressing chest, nightstands, a tall chest, sometimes a vanity bench that later gets lost. The M 776 deluxe vanity in the Sculptura line is the kind of named SKU dealers hunt — a round or shaped mirror, a birch body, the wheat glow. Sculptura is a later styling inside the modern family, more 1950s in the cut, still blond. Early Streamline (1936–41) has the more dramatic Deco curve; postwar pieces slim down and pick up a mid-century shoulder. The wheat does not change enough for a civilian to date by color alone. Look at the hardware, the stamp, the shipping date on the back. Small numbers, a month and a day and a year, a factory habit.

The bed is a panel headboard, sometimes a pair of twins, a footboard that is a smaller panel, legs that are part of the radius rather than McCobb’s thin taper. It is higher than Nelson’s Thin Edge and lower than a Victorian tester. It matches the dresser. We are back to completeness, but the completeness is modern and blond and middle-class, not Depression gum. You could still buy it on time. The difference is the mill’s pride: solid wood, a name that had been in the house as a wicker chair for a grandmother. Heywood-Wakefield is a respectable modern. McCobb is a sharper modern. Danish is a foreign modern. Waterfall is the marriage furniture you hide when the Heywood arrives.

Chicago’s merchandising — a big-city showroom, a fair, a department-store presence — moved Gardner’s birch into rooms that thought of themselves as up to date. The firm had seven plants and thousands of employees in the pre-Depression peak; the modern line is a survival strategy as much as a style. `[CITE NEEDED: 1930s plant count and employment, from a company report rather than a newspaper’s “heyday” sentence.]` A bedroom suite that used solid birch at that scale is a forest and a finish room, not a designer’s one-off.

Wicker had taught the company to think in curves. A rattan arm is a radius. Streamline is that habit in hardwood. Railway seating taught durability and a finish that can be wiped. The blond bedroom inherits both: a curve you are not afraid to touch, a lacquer that belongs in a house with children. Catalogs show the suite in a room with a scatter rug, not a museum plinth. That is the class position. Not poor. Not Gibbons. Gardner.

## Middle-class modern, and what that means

Middle-class here is a price and a store, not an insult. Heywood-Wakefield modern cost more than Sears waterfall and less than Knoll. It went into the houses of teachers, small-business owners, the professional couple who read *House Beautiful* and did not hire Haines. The wheat bedroom photographs as calm. It does not photograph as Craftsman moral or as Hollywood gilt. It is the American compromise: a suite (old sales idea) in a modern silhouette (new) in a light wood (neither oak fumed nor walnut stained).

Labor in Gardner is mill labor. Birch from the northern forest, kiln, machine, hand sanding on the radius where a machine would chatter, a finish that is a lacquer schedule. Women in the sewing rooms had done wicker and upholstery for a century; the modern bedroom still needed a bench cushion. The eagle stamp after 1949 is an inspector’s cousin: a brand in the wood, a date on the back. Together they make Heywood easier to identify than unsigned waterfall and harder than a Nutting paper label. Paper tags on 1936–48 pieces died in the move. Dealers now “authenticate” by the radius and the solid drawer. They are often right. They are sometimes looking at a later blond from another mill. The eagle ends the argument on postwar cases.

Hardware on wheat pieces is often champagne-toned metal, a wooden knob, or a simple metal pull that does not fight the radius. A later replacement in black iron “Craftsman” taste is a common injury. The bed’s rails are birch, with the usual American knock-down hooks. Twins sold as pairs for the respectable bedroom; a double is the marriage bed. Headboards survive on Hollywood frames after the original rails split. The dresser is the piece that still says Heywood when the bed has become a padded rectangle from 1998.

School furniture and railway seating paid the overhead that let a bedroom line exist. That is not romantic. It is why a Massachusetts company could tool Streamline while Grand Rapids was still shipping gum. Diversified mills survive style changes. A mill that only made chamber suites in dark walnut had a worse 1935.

## What the blond is not

It is not Danish teak. Birch is a different color temperature, a different hardness, a different scratch. Oil on teak is a maintenance culture. Lacquer on wheat is a wipe. It is not McCobb’s Planner, though both are Massachusetts maple-family woods; Planner is open stock and a ring pull, Heywood is a suite and a radius. It is not Greene & Greene, though both love a eased edge; the Greenes’ edge is a pegged joint, Heywood’s is a production bullnose. It is not a waterbed frame, though 1970s owners sometimes parked blond dressers next to a vinyl bladder and created a room that historians will have to unsort.

When the Gardner modern line winds down in the 1960s, the company still has a name. Later revivals and the South Beach reissues in yellow birch with an amber finish are another product. They are lighter than historic champagne and wheat, by the current maker’s own description. A caption that confuses a 1955 wheat dresser with a twenty-first-century reissue is a caption that has not looked at the drawer stamp.

## Residual

A wheat-finish nightstand with a shipping date in the back, July something, 1955, and an eagle in the left drawer, is a Gardner document. The bed it matched may now have a mattress that is too thick, rails that were replaced, a room that also holds a teak chair. The suite idea survived in blond after it died in the magazines. That is Heywood-Wakefield’s bedroom: modern enough to be new, complete enough to be sold as a set, solid enough that the drawer side tells the truth. The forest was birch. The finish was wheat. The posts were already gone.

The wishbone or “bullet” dresser — a long case with a curved, pointed stance in some Streamline groups — is the living-room famous piece that sometimes stood in a bedroom as a low chest. Dealers over-name it. Use the catalog letter-and-number. The bedroom’s ordinary hero is a three-drawer chest on a radius plinth, wheat, a pair of them as nightstands, a bed whose headboard repeats the radius. Ordinary is the point. Gardner made a lot of them.

A wheat bench at a vanity is upholstered in a period fabric that rarely survives. Replacement Naugahyde is a 1970s injury on a 1948 object. The birch underneath the cushion is the document. Look at the webbing, then look at the radius on the apron. If the apron matches the dresser, you still have a suite, even if the stool is later.

## Notes

- Steve and Roger Rouland, *Heywood-Wakefield Modern Furniture* (1995).
- Company history: Heywood brothers, Gardner, 1826; merger with Wakefield Rattan, 1897; Heywood-Wakefield Co. name form 1921; Rohde until 1934; Streamline Maple 1935, Streamline Modern 1937; birch by c. 1940; eagle stamp 1949; modern line into the 1960s.

- Later reissues: different birch, amber described as lighter than historic wheat/champagne.
