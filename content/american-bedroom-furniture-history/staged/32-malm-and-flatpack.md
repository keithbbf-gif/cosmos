# MALM and the Flat Pack

The IKEA Museum in Älmhult files the MALM chest of drawers as a 2002 object: function, chest of drawers; design, IKEA of Sweden; price, EUR 75. The series, the museum says, is a modern bedroom classic — beds, wardrobes, chests in three, four, five, or six drawers, beech, birch, oak. Tomas Lundin, a former product developer for bedroom storage, remembered the chest this way: the team developed it with a furniture factory in Poland. The idea was sketched on a napkin. An engineer made a simple drawing. The factory built a prototype. The team went to Poland to finish it on site. “It was a quick and efficient process.”

Ingvar Kamprad, in September 2002: “Bravo! Especially the version with the wooden knob!” The museum’s gloss is that he liked the many combinations of knobs and finish, and that leaving the knobs off at the start cut cost. The drawer profile was meant to run smoothly anyway. KOMPLEMENT interiors from the 1995 catalog could go in; later, zippered polyester inserts now sold as SKUBB. The chest is a system. So is the bed that shares the name.

This is the late suite. Not Grand Rapids oak with a catalog number in Gothic type, not Ethan Allen maple with a patriot’s name. Particleboard, foil or a thin veneer, a cardboard carton, an Allen key, a wall-anchor packet that too many households did not use. The American bedroom met it in the millions. The high chest’s job — a vertical address for shirts and sheets — is here a low or medium stack you assemble on the floor of a rental. The bed is a platform with a headboard panel, sold in the same series. The nightstand is another SKU in the same finish. If chapter 31’s traditional suite taught a suburb to match maple, MALM taught a later apartment to match foil.

## What the board is

Particleboard is wood reduced to chips, bound with resin, pressed into a panel. The faces that photograph as oak or birch are veneer or printed foil. Drawer sides are often foil-wrapped particleboard; bottoms, fiberboard. The back is a thin sheet. This is not a moral category. It is a material with properties. It is heavy for its stiffness. It does not like water. Its edges must be banded or they swell. A screw in the edge has little long grain to hold; cam locks and dowels are the joint because the panel is the shop. A solid-wood drawer side can take a dovetail. A particleboard drawer side takes a groove, a dowel, a bit of plastic.

Flat-pack is older than MALM. Gillis Lundgren’s BILLY bookcase, napkin sketch in the late 1970s, is the company myth of the same method. The point of the carton is not a lifestyle. It is freight: more product per truck, the customer as the last assembler, the warehouse as a stack of rectangles. A Philadelphia high chest travels as two cases and a set of legs, if it travels at all. A MALM chest travels as a box that fits a hatchback. That difference is the furniture history. The American chamber that once accumulated objects one bill at a time now takes a Saturday and a hex key.

The bed in the series is a platform: side panels, a headboard, slats, sometimes drawers underneath (chapter 33). It is not a tester. It is not a maple poster. It is a low wall around a mattress that may itself have arrived in a box (chapter 34). Joinery, in the eighteenth-century sense of a rail tenon into a post, is not the argument. The argument is a metal bracket, a cam, a wooden dowel, and a page of line drawings. A shop that still cuts shoulders on a bed rail can look at this and see a different trade. Both trades put people to bed.

## Independent, said IKEA

Philipp Mainzer designed a solid-wood bed, SL02 Mo, for the Frankfurt firm e15 in 1999. The type is a low, thick-rimmed platform with a simple headboard, oak, the corners a cabinetmaker’s thickness. e15 showed it; a German design registration followed in 2002, with a claimed exhibition priority from the Cologne furniture fair on 14 January 2002. IKEA sold the MALM bed in Germany from 2003. The two objects, in photographs, are cousins in silhouette: a shallow tray for a mattress, a panel at the head. One is solid timber at a designer price. The other is veneered board at a warehouse price. Mainzer told the German press that customers did not understand the price difference; what the bed cost at IKEA, e15 paid for the wood alone. `[CITE NEEDED: confirm the exact Manager Magazin wording if a caption quotes it; the paraphrase here follows the December 2016 Dezeen report.]`

e15 sued. IKEA’s position, as reported, was independent development: a parallel creation, without knowledge of the Frankfurt design, with a predecessor model called BERGEN already in preparation. Court records of the German litigation (BGH, I ZR 9/16, 29 June 2017) describe IKEA of Sweden’s December 2001 work on BERGEN — a null series in Poland, assembly instructions released — and advertising of BERGEN from August 2002, with MALM following in the German stores in 2003. The beds, the courts noted, differed little in the height of the headboard. IKEA has said it does not deliberately copy other companies’ or designers’ products. That is a corporate sentence. The legal question was not morality. It was whether a prior-use right in Germany could rest on preparations made abroad, and whether MALM infringed a registered design.

Lower courts favored IKEA. The Bundesgerichtshof in 2017 vacated and remanded: serious preparations for use, like use itself, had to have taken place in Germany. e15 called the remand a partial victory. The case is a furniture-history fact because it shows what a “suite” now is: a silhouette so reduced that two shops, one in solid oak and one in board, can land on the same outline and spend years in court over who owned a rectangle. Do not flatten the outcome into a winner if the file is still a remand. `[CITE NEEDED: final disposition after the 2017 remand to the Düsseldorf court.]` The historical point does not need a winner. The American bedroom bought the cheaper rectangle.

## The chest that would not stay up

This is a safety fact, not a joke, not a design punch line, not an irony to smile at.

On 28 June 2016, the U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission, with IKEA North America of Conshohocken, Pennsylvania, announced a recall of chests and dressers that did not meet the voluntary industry standard then in force, ASTM F2057-14. Children’s chests taller than 23.5 inches, adult chests taller than 29.5 inches. About 29 million units, including MALM three-, four-, five-, and six-drawer models and other IKEA chests. Unstable if not anchored to the wall; tip-over and entrapment that can kill a child. A later IKEA revision put U.S. units at about 17.3 million. The 2017 reannouncement listed eight child deaths associated with the recalled chests. MALM incidents named in the CPSC notices include a two-year-old in Woodbridge, Virginia (September 2011, unanchored MALM three-drawer); a two-year-old in West Chester, Pennsylvania (February 2014, unanchored MALM six-drawer); a twenty-three-month-old in Snohomish, Washington (June 2014, MALM three-drawer); a twenty-two-month-old in Apple Valley, Minnesota (February 2016, MALM six-drawer). None of those chests, the notices say, had been anchored. IKEA also reported dozens, then hundreds, of non-fatal tip-overs.

The physics is not mysterious. A tall, shallow case with drawers that extend is a lever. Open the upper drawers, climb, pull, and the center of gravity walks forward of the feet. A high chest of 1760 is also a lever, but it is dense hardwood, often with a deeper lower case, and it was not sold by the million to households that put it on carpet in a child’s room without a French cleat. Particleboard’s mass is real — the chests are heavy when they fall — and the back is a thin panel that does not triangulate the case the way a solid backboard does. The instruction leaflet said to attach the chest to the wall. Households did not always. A leaflet is not a joint.

ASTM F2057 was voluntary when the first deaths occurred. After years of advocacy, Congress passed the STURDY Act (Stop Tip-overs of Unstable, Risky Dressers on Youth) in December 2022. CPSC later adopted ASTM F2057-23 as a mandatory standard for clothing storage units: tests on carpet, tests with loaded and multiple open drawers, tests that simulate a child’s weight. Units manufactured after 1 September 2023 must meet it. Anchoring remains good practice. The statute is the government admitting that a dresser is a hazard class, not a style.

IKEA’s later MALM chests changed the toe-kick and the stability hardware; the company still prints “please attach to the wall.” A recalled chest with a recessed toe-kick is not a collectible. It is a product that should be repaired, refunded, or anchored. If this series has a duty in a chapter about a popular chest, it is to say the names of the towns in the CPSC list and to refuse the cheap sentence that “IKEA furniture always falls down.” Plenty of dressers tip. The MALM recall is large because the production run was large. The furniture problem is a case that is tall, light in its structure, and sold into rooms with children. That problem is older than 2002. The scale is not.

## The late suite

MALM is a suite in the Grand Rapids sense. The bed, the chest, the dressing table, the mirror, the nightstand, sometimes a wardrobe, share a finish and a name. You can fill a room from one aisle. The photograph in the catalog is the room. That is the same agreement Baumritter demanded in 1939, executed in particleboard and a much lower price. The difference is the assembler. The factory does not send a delivery crew to set the posts. The customer is the last millwright.

The American copies followed, as they always do: warehouse clubs and e-commerce copies of the low veneered chest, the platform bed with a panel headboard, the two-drawer nightstand. `[CITE NEEDED: a named U.S. big-box analog contemporary with MALM’s first decade, if a catalog page is used as a figure.]` Target, Walmart, and the online twins did not need to copy a registered German design. They needed a low rectangular bed and a chest that shipped. The point is not to police a silhouette. It is to see that “modern bedroom” in 2005 often meant this family of objects, as “traditional bedroom” in 1965 often meant Ethan Allen maple. Both are catalog suites. Both replace the accumulated chamber.

Particleboard itself is older than the bedroom series. German and American mills pressed waste wood into panels for the building trade; furniture factories learned that a foil face could stand in for a lumberyard species. The American bedroom had already met the material in 1970s wall units and waterbed pedestals. MALM’s achievement was not the invention of chipboard. It was the reduction of a chamber suite to a set of panels a customer would finish with a key, at a price that made the Grand Rapids suite look like a ceremony.

What happens to the high chest’s work? It goes short. Six drawers in a MALM are a vertical address with a lower center of gravity than a Philadelphia case, until the drawers come out. The rest of the storage slides under the mattress (chapter 33) or into a closet the developer built. The nightstand (chapter 45) remains because the lamp and the phone need a shelf. The suite is complete when the photograph has a plant.

The residual fact is double, and both halves are true. A napkin in a product-development meeting became, through a Polish factory, one of the most repeated bedroom cases in the world. Kamprad liked the wooden knob. The same series, as a dresser, killed children when it was not fastened to a wall. A history that only loves the napkin is selling. A history that only loves the recall is preaching. The object is a particleboard chest with a foil face, a cam lock, and a hole for an anchor. The American bedroom is full of them. Some of them are still not attached.
