# Newport Block and Shell

Yale’s Furniture Study holds a Newport high chest that left the 1929 Girl Scouts loan exhibition, sat in Richard de Wolfe Brixey’s and then Doris Brixey’s hands, and came to New Haven in 1984. Michael Moses, whose *Master Craftsmen of Newport* was nearly on press, came to look at it and found what he had been hunting: a high chest he believed John Townsend had made between 1756 and 1762. The drawer backs were labeled A to E. On one surface, the searchers read a signature and a date — “Made by John Townsend Newport 1759” — that the published object-lesson later treated as the chest’s voice. `[CITE NEEDED: confirm the present Yale accession and the exact inscription transcription on the collection page before a caption quotes it.]`

That is enough of an opening. Newport is not Philadelphia with less carving. The Townsend and Goddard shops — two families married together, a Quaker port on the way to the West Indies — developed a facade that blocks in and out and caps the blocks with convex and concave shells. The shells radiate from cross-hatched centers. The rays can be serpentine. The knees of the cabriole legs carry abstract shells instead of Philadelphia’s acanthus. The mahogany is thick enough to block without veneer theater. Morrison Heckscher’s “John Townsend’s Block-and-Shell Furniture” (*Antiques*, May 1982) is still the essay that taught a generation to see the hand.

## Blocking as joinery, not appliqué

A blocked drawer front is not a applied molding. The front is cut from a thick board, or built up, so the surface advances and recedes in vertical bays. The shell sits on the blocking like a capital on a pilaster. Do this on a bureau table — Yale 1957.37, attributed to John Townsend, Garvan gift — and you have the desk form that made the school famous. Do it on a high chest and the chamber gets a vertical street of bays.

John Goddard (1723–1785) married a Townsend. The Art Institute of Chicago’s high chest 1989.158, attributed to Goddard, 1755–85, mahogany with chestnut and white pine, is the Chicago sentence of the same language. RISD’s Pendleton House and MFA Boston hold others. Documented pieces are rare. Labels and inscriptions are rarer. Moses spent a career separating Townsend from Goddard from the cousins and the journeymen. Later writers have loosened some of his certainties. Mark a tight attribution as a belief unless the inscription is on the board.

Secondary woods are chestnut and white pine more often than Philadelphia’s tulip poplar mix. That is a Rhode Island timber map, not a moral. The port’s wealth was mercantile and, in part, the slave trade. A history of Newport furniture that stops at the shell’s cross-hatching has stopped too early. The mahogany and the money have addresses in the Caribbean. Anderson again; also the Newport historical work that has stopped pretending the cabinet shops sat outside the trade.

## What the shells are not

They are not Chippendale’s rococo C-scrolls. They are not Johnson’s swan. A reader who comes from 18.110.4 will look for a carved narrative and find a geometry. That is the point of putting this chapter after Philadelphia. The two cities were reading some of the same London books and making different American furniture. Newport’s best work is quieter at ten feet and louder at ten inches. You have to put a thumb in the shell.

The feet — open talon, tight ball, the particular Newport claw — are another dating fight. Do not settle it in a magazine sentence. Point at the foot and say the argument exists.

## The chamber

A Newport high chest stood in a merchant chamber that also hung a bed and, if the house was rich enough, held a bureau table in another room or the same one. It is still not a suite. The matching dressing table exists in the school; it is a pair when it is a pair, a separate bill when it is not. The Revolution hurt Newport. The shops that had made the great blocked cases did not resume at the same pitch. Federal Newport is a smaller story. The high chests that survive are colonial objects that outlived their city’s boom.

Colonial Revival and the Girl Scouts exhibition put those objects back into public looking. Brixey’s chest is a collection history as much as a shop history: 1929, a private house, a university study, Moses on the train to New Haven. The signature, if the present label still reads it that way, is the rare thing American furniture usually lacks. Most of this series is “workshop of” and “Philadelphia.” Here a man wrote his name and his town and a year on the wood.

## Residual

Block-and-shell is a way of making a drawer front that cannot be faked cheaply with a thin veneer and a pressed ornament. That is why later reproductions look tired and why collectors still pay. It is also why a shop in a later hardwood county, looking at a photograph, can steal the lesson without stealing the pediment: thickness, a shell that is cut, a secondary wood that matches the forest you have. The gilt bust on 18.110.4 does not travel. The blocked bay does, if you have the board.

Do not end on that shop. End on the inscription. A cabinetmaker in a slave-trading port signed a high chest in 1759. The shells are still sharp enough to cut a thumb. The drawers still run. That is the object. The rest is the city we have to keep in the sentence.

## Quaker shops, Atlantic money

The Townsends and Goddards were Friends. Newport’s meeting and Newport’s rum and slave trade sat in the same streets. Cabinetmaking wealth in that town is not separable from the cargoes that paid for mahogany and for the houses the high chests stood in. Later museum labels have begun to say so. Older catalogs did not. This chapter will not pretend a shell is a political statement. It will not pretend a shell is only wood.

Job Townsend, John Townsend, John Goddard, the next generation that worked into the Federal years — the family tree is in Moses and in the Newport historical literature. A magazine essay cannot replay it. What matters for the chamber is that a small city produced a consistent geometry for a few decades and then, after occupation and a lost war economy, did not. The objects outlived the boom because they were too good to burn and too heavy to throw away.

## How blocking fails

If the board is too thin, the blocked bay becomes a veneer and a hope. If the shell is applied, the glue line telegraphs. Nineteenth-century and Colonial Revival copies fail in those two places first. A thumb in a 1759 shell finds no glue line at the rays. That is the test the photograph cannot give. It is why this form still belongs in a furniture study, not only on a pedestal.

Drawer runners and dustboards in the Yale chest — the A-to-E marks — are a shop’s assembly language. Townsend labeled parts so the case went back together after transport or after the finishing room. A high chest is a knockdown object before it is a monument. Chapter 39 will say the same of bed rails. The American chamber’s best furniture was already thinking about stairs and carts.


## A board thick enough to block, and a glue line you can feel

Blocking is hours in a thick board. The raised bay is often the original mahogany surface. The recessed bays are cut down with rabbet planes and chisels until the facade advances and recedes in vertical streets. A Newport high chest or bureau table that does this in the solid is spending timber the Caribbean trade had already made expensive. Thin stock cannot do it. That is why later copies go to veneer and applied shells, and why a thumb still works as a test. A glue line around a shell’s circumference is the tell. Some period shells were built up with an extra pad of mahogany before the rays were cut — a legitimate shop shortcut, not automatically a fake. The fake is the pressed ornament and the 1920s shallow block. Moses and Heckscher both spent pages on this. Patricia Kane’s later Yale show, *Art and Industry in Early America: Rhode Island Furniture, 1650–1830* (2016), is the book that loosened some of Moses’s named-hand certainties without loosening the geometry.

Chestnut in the drawer sides and backs is a Rhode Island timber map. American chestnut — *Castanea dentata* — turns up as secondary wood in Newport case work more often than tulip poplar does. After the blight, restorers who replaced a runner or a side in poplar left a different forest in the dark. Conservators look for chestnut the way they look for white pine in Boston. It is not a moral. It is a port’s woodshed. White pine still appears. The mix is the fingerprint, not a single species.

Stop-fluted quarter columns — flutes that end in a carved stop before the necking — are another Newport habit. Philadelphia often runs the flute. A reader who can see that difference in a hallway has the connoisseurship this series actually needs. The open-talon foot, the paper that will slide behind a claw, is the argument from the floor. Do not settle it in a sentence. Point.

A blocked drawer front that still has its original thickness at the raised bay will measure on the order of an inch and a half to two inches of mahogany before anyone starts talking about veneer. `[CITE NEEDED: a measured thickness on Yale 1957.37 or the Brixey high chest from the object file.]` That number is the shop’s expense. The inscription “Made by John Townsend Newport 1759,” if the present Yale page still reads it that way, is the rare American case that also speaks a year. Confirm the transcription on the collection page before a caption quotes it. A signature does not make the cousins disappear. Goddard’s Chicago high chest, 1989.158, is still attributed. Belief and inscription are not the same word.

## Occupation, rum, and the mahogany that is not Rhode Island

Newport’s cabinet shops sat in a town that distilled rum and traded in people. The mahogany in a blocked facade grew in the Caribbean. Jennifer Anderson’s book is the forest and the enslaved cutting. The houses the high chests stood in were paid for in the same Atlantic. Later museum labels have begun to say so. Older catalogs stopped at the shell. This chapter will not pretend the cross-hatching is a political statement. It will not pretend the board was innocent timber from Conanicut.

Named enslaved workmen in the Townsend and Goddard shops remain thinner in the published bills than the family tree. The gap is not a proof of absence in a slave-trading port. `[CITE NEEDED: a named enslaved or free Black workman in a Newport cabinet shop, if Kane, Moses, or the city records yield one.]` Quaker membership and the meeting’s discipline sat on the same streets as the wharves. Occupation, 1776–79, took the market. John Townsend kept a bench. The volume dropped. The objects that survive are colonial work that outlived the boom because they were too heavy to throw away and too good to burn.

RISD’s Pendleton House and the Hunter House in Newport are where a reader sees the furniture in a room instead of a pedestal. The chamber still hung a bed. The bureau table — Yale 1957.37 — is the school’s other famous facade, a kneehole and a prospect, dressing and writing in one blocked body. Chapter 6 will have to keep that type from being called a lowboy. Here it is the same geometry at a different height. The shells are closer to the hands.

## Rays, centers, and the copy that tires

Townsend’s rays can serpentine. The centers are cross-hatched. Heckscher’s 1982 *Antiques* essay taught a generation to see that hand in the shell rather than in a silhouette. Goddard’s blocking, in the literature, is sometimes read as a different weight. Later writers have warned against treating every ray as a signature. Mark the tight call as a belief unless the board is inscribed. The Girl Scouts loan exhibition of 1929 put these cases back into public looking and into the Brixey living room. Collection history is part of the object. So is the train to New Haven.

A Colonial Revival copy fails first in thickness and in the shell’s glue line. A factory “blockfront” from Grand Rapids is a different chapter and a different honesty. 1759. Newport. The drawers still run. The chestnut is still in the dark if no one has replaced it.
