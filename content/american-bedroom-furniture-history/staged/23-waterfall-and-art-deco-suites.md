# Waterfall and Art Deco Suites

A Sears, Roebuck bedroom “outfit” of the 1930s is a photograph of a bed, a vanity with a stool, a dresser, a chest, sometimes a nightstand, all with the same rounded front. The top of the dresser does not end in a sharp arris. It pours. Veneer — walnut, a figured stripe, a blond — runs over the curve so the grain looks like water going over a lip. Buyers called it waterfall. The factories called it modern. It was the American bedroom’s Art Deco: not the 1925 Paris of Jacques-Émile Ruhlmann’s macassar and ivory, not Süe et Mare, not an ébéniste’s file. It was lumber-core plywood, a mold, a glue pot, and a price that a newlywed could carry on time.

That last clause is the next chapter’s problem. This one is the object: how the suite is made, why the curve exists, and why calling it French is a dealer’s convenience.

## Plywood, the curve, and a cheap luxury

Until the First World War, a respectable American case was solid wood or a thin veneer over a solid core. Conservation talk and the cost of hardwood pushed mills toward lumber-core plywood: cheap inner plies, faces at right angles for strength, a show veneer. Curved work had been expensive — coopered doors, steam-bent solids, many clamps. Molded plywood made a radius repeatable. Portland Manufacturing and other plywood pioneers are in the industrial literature; furniture men in Grand Rapids and in the southern mills took the sheet and made bedrooms. `[CITE NEEDED: the specific Grand Rapids firm first identified with American waterfall production after the 1931 Paris Colonial Exposition, if the trade press names it.]`

A true waterfall edge is the show veneer carried over a molded radius so there is no separate banding at the front of the top. Better pieces do this on the case sides as well. Cheap pieces fake it with a quarter-round molding nailed on, a stripe of veneer that chips at the first move. Bookmatching on a drawer face — two leaves opened like a butterfly — is the mill showing off for a few extra cents of figure. Crossbanding around the bookmatch is a little more money. Bakelite pulls, orange or butterscotch, with a brass ring or a backplate, are the hardware of the style. Some vanities have frosted glass, an illuminated sphere, a round mirror etched at the rim. The bed is a panel headboard and footboard, the same radius, rails that knock down.

Later vintage writers and the thin scholarly literature on waterfall are working without a single designer’s name. This is factory furniture. A few plants — Sligh, parts of the Grand Rapids cluster, unnamed southern veneer shops — made better carcasses than others. Widdicomb in these years is a different, more designed modern and should not be dumped into the waterfall bin. The Sears four-piece set that later dealers call “signed Sears, Roebuck & Co.,” walnut and mahogany and cherry figures on a 1939 date, Bakelite and brass, dovetailed drawers, is the upper end of mail-order, not a unique studio. It is still a suite.

How the radius is made: a core is bandsawn or stacked to a rough curve, or a molded plywood skin is laid over a form. Hide glue or the newer synthetic resins, depending on the year and the plant. The show veneer is a flitch, sliced, not rotary-cut if the mill is proud — rotary rotary-cut walnut looks like a cheap plywood door. The veneer is jointed, taped, glued face-down or face-up depending on the press. A caul takes the curve. Too much pressure and the face splits at the tightest radius. Too little and a bubble becomes a blister in the second summer. Finish is sprayed lacquer, often toned, then rubbed. The Bakelite pull is a bought part from a hardware specialist, not a foundry in the furniture plant. Brass ferrules and backplates come in barrels.

Rail hardware on the bed is stamped steel: hooks, bolts, a wooden slat ledge. The headboard is a frame-and-panel or a solid-looking slab that is a veneered panel in a lumber-core. Posts are short. No tester. The Craftsman sanitary argument has won, even here, in the absence of oak. You can wipe a lacquered waterfall. You cannot wipe a dimity tester.

## What a suite contained

Three-piece: bed, dresser, chest. Five-piece: add vanity and bench. Seven-piece: nightstands, a cedar chest, a chifforobe. The chifforobe — chiffonier plus wardrobe — is the American closet problem in one object: a house without a closet gets a tall case with drawers and a hanging well. Sears advertised chifforobes as a category of their own in 1933, seven styles on a page, because the small house and the small apartment needed a place for a coat that was not a hook. Combination wardrobe-dresser-chests in 1938 ads are the same logic, denser.

Cedar chests sat at the foot of the bed in the advertising photograph, a hope chest that had already done its pre-marriage work and now stored wool. Nightstands were the easy piece to lose or mix. Surviving “sets” in the wild are often a vanity from plant A, a bed from plant B, nightstands from a later marriage. The veneer pattern will not match. The Bakelite will. Collectors now treat completeness as a virtue. In 1936, completeness was a payment book.

Swing-era catalog work that tracks Sears from 1930 to 1939 shows “modern” concentrated in the bedroom first. Only late in the decade does the copywriter expect a mass market for modern in other rooms. The bedroom was allowed to be new because it was the room a couple might own when they owned nothing else — a rented room in the bride’s parents’ house, the suite the one property. Sentiment stuck to it. That is why so much waterfall survived: not because it was precious, but because it was the marriage.

Prices in secondary literature run from about twenty dollars to forty for a basic 1930 outfit, more for “luxurious” sets, a 1939 figure around fifty for a better group. Treat those numbers as order-of-magnitude until a specific catalog page is in the caption. `[CITE NEEDED: exact Sears or Wards page, year, and printed price for one named outfit.]` Even at fifty dollars, this is not Ruhlmann. Ruhlmann’s bed for a Paris client was a commission in exotic wood with a cabinetmaker’s name. The waterfall bed is a SKU.

## Not French

The 1925 Paris exposition gave American mill designers a vocabulary: sunbursts, ziggurats, a sun-ray mirror, a stepped plinth. The 1931 Paris Colonial Exposition is sometimes cited as the splash that moved waterfall specifically. Streamline Moderne — trains, radios, a horizontal speed — is the closer cousin. American waterfall is Deco the way a 1936 Chevrolet is Deco: a radius, a highlight, a mass product. Ivory inlay, shagreen, amboyna, a silvered bronze mount by Ruhlmann’s *atelier* — those are another country and another bill.

Ébénisterie means a trade with a guild memory. Grand Rapids means a plant with a veneer slicer. The American suite borrows the French word Art Deco after the fact; in the 1930s the ads said modern, modernistic, sometimes “new.” Blond waterfall is not Swedish Modern, though a wheat finish will later confuse the two in an estate sale. Dark walnut waterfall is not a colonial highboy. The round mirror is the Hollywood vanity’s cheap brother. All of these borrowings sit on the same plywood box.

Open a drawer. You want to know if the mill cut dovetails or nailed a drawer box. Better waterfall has dovetails and a plywood bottom in a groove. Worse has a stapled box that smells of old glue. Veneer checks. Cigarette burns on the nightstand are a period injury; the style lived with ashtrays. Water damage on a vanity top is the other injury; the mirrored glass held a glass of water for teeth. Restoration is often not worth the veneer. Families kept the pieces anyway.

Sunburst veneer on a door — a radial match — is the mill’s most expensive parlor trick, more common on radio cabinets than on bedroom cases, but it turns up on chifforobe doors. A ziggurat plinth, stepped like a setback skyscraper, is the other Deco loan. Most waterfall sits on a simple recessed base instead. The bed’s footboard may carry a small cartouche of lighter wood, a cartouche that is a slice, not a carving. Compare a Philadelphia shell, cut with a gouge. The waterfall cartouche is a picture of luxury in 1/28-inch wood.

## Labor in the veneer mill

A waterfall dresser is a sequence: core, glue, mold, veneer, trim, finish, hardware. The skilled job is matching the figure so two drawers read as one tree. The unskilled job is stacking. Women in some plants did sanding and packing; the trade press is patchy on who stood where. `[CITE NEEDED: gendered labor in a named 1930s bedroom-suite plant.]` The designer, if there was one, was a plant man with a French magazine, not a Paris name on the catalog.

Southern hardwood — gum, poplar, tupelo — made cores. Walnut and mahogany figures were sliced thin. “Walnut finish” on gum is a stain, not a lie the mill was ashamed of. The customer was buying a look. The complete bedroom was the product. Chapter 24 will follow the payment book. Here the residual making fact is the radius: a machine could do in minutes what a cabinetmaker’s coopered door did in hours. That is why there are so many of these suites and so few Ruhlmanns. It is also why a waterfall edge chips. Thin veneer over a tight curve is a condition problem from day one.

A Grand Rapids finish room in 1935 is lacquer over filler, a schedule timed to the conveyor. Fuming oak is a Stickley story; here the color is in the gun. Blond waterfall uses a light veneer — maple, birch, sometimes primavera or a stained gum — and a clear coat that yellows. The yellow is now part of the object. Strip it and you have a pale, thirsty curve that never existed in the showroom.

Hardware failures are predictable. Bakelite shrinks and cracks around the screw. Brass plate wears through to white metal. A replaced pull from a later kitchen ruins the drawer’s face more than a missing chip of veneer. Original orange butterscotch is worth leaving.

After the war the style dies in the showroom. Blond Heywood-Wakefield and maple Planner and Danish teak will take the modern argument and make it about legs and honesty again. Waterfall looks suddenly like what it was: a Depression curve. Houses keep sleeping in it for thirty more years because the payment book is paid and the marriage is in the grain.

A last shop note: the bed rails on a waterfall suite are often the first pieces replaced. Original rails are hardwood, sometimes veneered on the show face, with hooks whose spacing is mill-specific. A later metal Hollywood frame under a surviving headboard is the common frankenstein. The headboard still matches the vanity. The sleeping structure does not. Caption that honestly. The suite was always a visual contract more than a structural one.

## Residual

Ruhlmann did not design the Sears outfit. A Grand Rapids or Indiana mill did, with a plywood radius and a Bakelite pull. The American Art Deco bedroom is that mill’s bedroom. If you want Paris, look at a museum mount. If you want to know what a 1936 couple slept beside, look at the chipped waterfall on a vanity that still holds a round mirror. The curve is the expensive effect. The suite is the sales idea. Neither required a French word to work.
