# A Shop Looking at a Highboy

I had a Philadelphia-type high chest in front of me only as photographs and a measured drawing — Downs’s Van Pelt description, a Met label, a Kindel reproduction in a dealer’s picture — and a Boston japanned stack in a museum file. I had oak on the bench in Bradley County. The useful question is not whether a Warren shop should build a bonnet top. It is which parts of that object still answer a room, and which parts are a theater that needs a parlor and a spotlight.

Shoulders. Drawer boxes. Secondary woods named as secondary. A waist that is a real joint between two cases. Those still earn. A gilt shell on a broken pediment, flame finials, carving that takes a hundred hours because Chippendale’s *Director* said so — that is theater. Theater is allowed. It is not required for a chest to hold shirts. A shop that cannot tell the two apart will spend the week on a tympanum and dowel the drawers.

## What the drawers are doing

A high chest is many boxes. Each drawer is sides, front, back, bottom, runners. Dovetails on the front are the joint the collector lights. Dovetails on the back, often cruder, are the joint that fails when the bottom drops. Grain of the bottom, in New England often running side to side or front to back by shop habit, is a dating tool in the literature and a movement tool on the bench. A bottom in a groove can shrink and still stay. A bottom nailed across the grain will split. Philadelphia tulip poplar and cedar, Boston white pine: the secondaries are not a lesser wood. They are the reason the show wood can be mahogany or walnut without the case eating itself.

I cut drawers in oak and poplar. The lesson from the high chest is not “copy a lip molding.” It is: size the tail, don’t starve the pin, let the bottom float, don’t make the runner a paint stick. A factory MALM drawer is a foil box on a roller. It runs until the roller is a rumor. The high chest’s drawer, if it was cut honestly, can be fitted again in a hundred years with a plane. That is the part I want from Philadelphia in Arkansas. Not the knee.

Runners and kickers, dustboards if the shop used them, a cock bead that also protects the show edge — these are the unphotographed half. Conservators date with them. A shop that skips them because the customer will not open the drawer in the showroom has already chosen theater. The afterlife (chapter 37) taught collectors to buy pediments. A working shop cannot afford that education.

## Shoulders, waists, and the case

The upper case sits on the lower. A molding hides the joint or a waist announces it. Either way there is a bearing surface. Rack in a tall case is a longer lever than rack in a low chest. The feet — cabriole, turned, bracket — are the other half of that lever. Ball-and-claw is a carving problem and a structural one: the ankle is thin. Plenty of Philadelphia cases have later shoes and repaired knees. A shop looking at that should see a warning, not a model for a bed post.

I will not put a nine-foot carved case in a ranch house. Ceilings, stairs, the afterlife already moved the type to the hall. What I will take is the bearing: a chest of drawers whose corners are shoulders, not a handful of dowels, whose back is a board that triangulates, whose feet are thick enough for the ankle the design wants. If the customer wants a hint of a pediment, a shaped backboard is enough theater for a bedroom. A gilt tympanum belongs in a room that is already performing.

Boston japanning is a surface I will not fake. Lampblack, gesso, gilt chinoiserie on maple and pine: a specialist trade. Restored gilt in museums is already an argument. A 2020s “japanned” chest from a catalog is a print. If the shop cannot japann, it should not. Walnut and oak faces, an honest stain, a drawer that runs — that is the Boston lesson that travels. The gilt figures do not.

Southern walnut high chests in MESDA’s files are closer cousins to an Arkansas oak case than a Philadelphia rococo monument is, in wood if not in carving. Walnut moves. Oak moves. Both will cup a wide top if you trap the panel. A Boston white-pine interior in a mahogany case is the same honesty as a poplar drawer in an oak chest: the show wood is not the whole object. I will write the secondary on the invoice. The high chest literature already taught the customer to expect it, if the customer reads. If they do not, they still get a drawer that runs.

## What is theater

Carving hours. Kindel’s Winterthur copy named about a hundred hours on the Philadelphia High Chest. That number is the point of the reproduction: you are buying labor as display. A bedroom chest does not need it. A hall trophy might. Chapter 40’s completeness rule will try to sell a matching carved bed. Decline. The bed’s job is rails (chapter 39). The chest’s job is boxes. If the same hand carves both, you have a suite in the Grand Rapids sense, even in oak. Matching is merchandising. It can be handsome. It is not a joint.

Flame finials, a central cartouche, rosettes, a carved skirt with a shell: I can admire them on 18.110.4 without cutting them. The shop time in Bradley County is a rail, a post, a case. The forest is oak and the bottoms. The mill towns (chapter 36) shipped boards; they did not ship pediments. A living furniture shop here is not Fullerton’s Bradley Lumber and not Ballman-Cummings. Bradley Brand Furniture is the bench that still cuts oak rails while the pediment stays on the museum wall. Looking at a highboy is how that bench stays in conversation with a type that left the chamber. It is not a heritage ad.

Hardware theater: a full set of new Chippendale plates on a plain chest. Ghosts say otherwise. If I make a new chest, I will use pulls that fit the drawer’s weight and the customer’s hand, and I will not stamp “1750” on them. If I repair an old chest, chapter 41’s brass rule holds. The highboy’s afterlife is full of jewelry. The shop’s job is the box.

## Labor, again

A high chest is a lot of drawers. That was the shop’s speed: a journeyman who could cut consistent tails. An apprentice who could not was waste. I still feel that on a chest with five drawers. CNC will cut tails. It will not fit the bottom. The hand that still earns is the one that closes the shoulder and eases the runner. The gilt pediment, if a shop insists, is another specialist, as the japanner was. Do not pretend the same afternoon does both well.

Enslaved and free Black labor in the coastal shops is a documentary problem (chapter 3, chapter 38). In a south Arkansas shop the labor history is mill and plant, Jim Crow, the thinner published record. Looking at a highboy does not wash that. It only says: the object is drawers and a facade. Who was paid to cut them is a different file. Keep both files open.

## Residual

The useful half of a Philadelphia or Boston high chest, on a Bradley County bench, is a set of boxes with shoulders and a back that holds the rack. The theater is the pediment, the gilt, the claw that photographs. A bedroom in 2026 will take the boxes. It will not take nine feet of mahogany performance. If I build a chest, I want the drawer to run in August. If I look at 18.110.4, I am looking at a cousin, not a prototype. The residual fact is a dry-fit. When the case closes on four corners and the drawer does not bind, the highboy has taught what it still knows. The rest is a parlor’s business.

Measured drawings are how a shop looks without stealing a museum’s object. Downs, Heckscher, the Chipstone articles with exploded joinery — those pages are more use on a bench than a gilt photograph. I will take a drawer-dovetail angle from a published plate. I will not take a carved cartouche from a feed and call it research. The difference is the same as the difference between a shoulder and a decal.

If this shop ever stands an actual high chest in the room, it will be as ancestor furniture in a hall, the afterlife chapter 37 already named, or as a repair: a runner, a cock bead, a brass ghost left alone. Bradley County does not need to become Philadelphia. It needs to keep knowing what a drawer box is when the rest of the culture has a cam lock. That is the look. Not a bloodline. Not a mill’s successor. A Tuesday with oak, and a cousin in mahogany on a page.

Backboards triangulate. A thin plywood back, well screwed, will do more against rack than a carved skirt. Philadelphia backs are boards, often paneled. I will not leave a case open at the back because the wall “hides it.” The wall is not a joint. Screw holes in a backboard are honest if they are for a tip-over strap. That strap is the 2016 lesson (chapter 32) applied to any tall case, oak or foil. A highboy in a hall should be anchored too. Children climb ancestor furniture. Physics does not read accession numbers.

Drawer stops keep a box from coming out in a hurry. Dustboards keep socks out of the case. Both are small. Both separate a chest that will last from a chest that photographs. I learned that faster from repairing a factory dresser than from a pediment. The highboy still taught it first.

Show wood in Bradley County is often oak or walnut that did not come through a port. That is not a virtue contest with mahogany. It is a different bill. Anderson’s mahogany book still matters for the coastal high chest. This bench’s lesson from that chest is construction, not species envy. I will not stain oak to “look like” a Met label. I will steal the drawer box. The pediment can stay in Gallery 752.

A bed in the same shop should not be forced to match the chest unless the customer is buying a suite and knows it (chapter 40). Mixed woods in a chamber are historically normal. The catalog trained us to see mismatch as failure. A highboy in mahogany and a maple tester in the same 1760 room would not have been a failure. They would have been two bills. I would rather sell two honest bills than one matched performance.
