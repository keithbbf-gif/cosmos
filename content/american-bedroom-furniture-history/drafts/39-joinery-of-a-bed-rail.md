# Joinery of a Bed Rail

The rail is the member people forget until it rattles. Posts get photographed. Headboards get fabric. The rail carries the slats or the hooks for the rope, keeps the posts from walking, takes a hip when someone sits to tie a shoe, and has to come apart because the house has a turn in the stairs. I have fitted a hook plate on a white oak post and felt the plate sit proud of the mortise, a line of light that would become a click at 2 a.m. Chalk, a paring cut, dry-fit again. That is not a diary. It is the joint.

Beds knock down. Chests of drawers, as a rule, do not. The difference is the object’s length and the building’s openings. A high chest travels as two cases. A bed is a rectangle the size of a sleeper, plus posts that may be six feet before you add a tester. Eighteenth-century shops already knew this. The knockdown joint is the bed’s native intelligence, not a modern convenience.

## Rope, slats, and what the rail is for

On a tester bedstead the side rail is a tension member with a second job: it holds the sleeping surface. Holes for rope, or a rabbet for a sacking bottom, or later a ledger for slats. A bed wrench tightened the rope until the tick sat. The rail had to take that inward pull without bowing into a banana. Maple and oak do that if they are thick enough and the grain is straight. A thin pine rail will creep.

When rope gave way to slats, the rail became a ledge. The load is downward and the rack is still there: two people, a mattress, a child jumping, the rectangle wanting to become a parallelogram. The joint at the post has to answer rack, not only gravity. That is why a dowel in end grain is a short conversation, and why a hook that only hangs the rail like a picture will clatter.

The foot rail and the head rail are shorter and often stiffer. They may be tenoned for real, because they do not have to come out as often, or they may hook too, for a move. Tester rails at the top of the posts are another knockdown: pegged or bolted, the cloth’s frame. Take the cloth away and those upper rails look mysterious. They are not decoration. They are a box’s ridgepole.

## Hooks

The hook-on rail is a pair of iron plates: hooks on the rail, slots or pins on the post, or the reverse. Nineteenth-century and later factory beds use it because it is fast in the house. You lift the rail, drop it on, the bed stands. No wrench. No nut that rolls under the dresser. The plate must be let into the wood so the show face is fair, and the screws must find something besides end grain. A hook that pulls out of a post is a bed that has become two nightstands and a rumor.

There are better and worse hooks. A long plate with several screws into long grain; a pin that is a proper diameter; a depth of slot that will not let the rail jump when the mattress is thrown on. Cheap hooks are stampings that bend. I have seen a rail walk off a shallow hook because the mattress was foam and heavy and the installer bounced it. The joint is a mechanical lock. It should look like one.

Hook plates are cousins of knocked-down bed hardware sold as restoration parts: the same idea, a better or worse steel. Ball and Ball and the other hardware houses still make period patterns. A 1760 bed may have used a wooden pin through a tenon instead. Do not put a 1900 hook on a 1740 post and call it original. The honest repair is a plate that works, disclosed.

## Bolts

The bed bolt is a long threaded rod through the post into a nut in the rail, or through the rail into the post, with a decorative cap on the show face. You see it on Empire and later beds, on some Federal posts, on farm beds that wanted a joint a farmer could tighten in the spring. A bolt in tension pulls the shoulder of the rail against the post. If there is a shoulder. If the rail is cut square and the post is square, the bolt is a clamp. If the meeting is a gap, the bolt is a wish.

The cap is theater that earns its keep. It hides the hex and tells the room this is a bed that comes apart on purpose. Inside, a square nut in a mortise in the rail is the older way; a captured nut, a barrel nut, a cross-dowel in later shops. The wrench is a household tool, like the rope wrench before it. Lose it and the next move is a fight.

Through-bolts can split a thin post. The hole is a lot of nothing in a 2-inch square. Leave meat. A washer under a cap saves the show wood from a dent. None of this is mysterious. It is why a revival bed (chapter 35) that photographs as a slab and bolts like a shipping crate will rack: the bolt cannot invent a shoulder.

## Through-tenons and the Craftsman argument

A through-tenon on a rail, wedged on the far side of the post, is the joint Stickley and the Oak shops loved because you can see it. It answers rack by cheeks and shoulders, and the wedge is the mechanical lock when hide glue fails. It is also a knockdown if you knock the wedges out. Some Craftsman beds do; some are glued for the ages and then the ages move house.

Greene and Greene pegs are a related honesty: you see the decision. They are not all through-tenons. A bed that quotes Pasadena with ebony plugs in dowel holes is quoting the photograph. Look at the cheek.

I will still cut a through-tenon on a trestle or a heavy bed if the customer will live with a proud peg and a post thick enough to take the mortise. I will not through-tenon a 1¼-inch rail into a 1¾-inch post and then talk about generations. The old third-thickness rule is a starting point. On a bed the post also has to take a headboard groove or a bolt from another direction. Stacked hardware in a skinny post is how splits start.

## Iron angles and the cheap rectangle

Angle irons — L-brackets, screws into rail and post — are the joint of the Hollywood frame and of a thousand factory beds that needed to be a price. They work in shear if the screws are many and the wood is not particleboard. In particleboard they work until they do not (chapter 32). The bracket is honest when it is visible and sized. It is a lie when it is a pair of ½-inch screws in foil-wrapped chip pretending to be a rail tenon.

Cam locks in a MALM bed are the flat-pack cousin: a steel cam, a dowel, a drawing that assumes you will not rack the case while you attach the fourth corner. They are knockdown by religion. They are also why a move to a new apartment produces a bag of leftover cams and a rail with a stripped hole. Repair is a longer screw or a dowel reset, not a dovetail.

Wooden pins in a drawbored mortise-and-tenon are the older mechanical lock. A bed can drawbore if someone will knock it apart with a drift and not a wrecking bar. That is a shop conversation with the customer, not a default.

Eighteenth-century rails, in the better shops, often meet posts in a locked joint that later hardware copied in iron: a tenon with a pin, or a hook that is still wood. Southern testers with tall posts add a problem of leverage; a long post is a long lever if the rail joint is weak. Charleston and the Chesapeake shops knew that. MESDA’s files are the check on whether a given tall post is a good joint or only a good silhouette. New England pencil posts, thinner, need a lighter tester and a rail that does not overpower the shaft. The joint follows the post, not the other way around.

Iron bedsteads of the 1880s–1910s weld or bolt. The rail is a tube. The “joint” is a bracket or a casting. Sanitation is the point; knockdown is still required. Brass beds with porcelain casters roll, which is another way to move a rectangle through a door without taking it apart. The wooden trade kept the hook and the bolt because the posts were still lumber.

A tester rail at the top is a different load: mostly the cloth’s weight and a little rack from someone tugging a curtain. Pegs and small bolts. Lose one and the valance sags. Museums that show naked testers have often removed a joint’s reason. The honest label says the upper rails are there to carry fabric.

## Why knock down

Stairs, landings, a door that is 30 inches, a tester post that is 90, a mattress that now comes in a box but a walnut rail that does not. Freight: a bed that ships in a carton of rails will be made. A bed that ships as a welded idea will be local. The American household moves. Census data are the sociologist’s version; the furniture maker’s version is a rail on a blanket in a pickup.

The residual fact is a click in the night. If the rail can talk, the joint is loose. Tighten the bolt, drop the hook home, knock the wedge, replace the stripped cam. The bed is a knockdown object because architecture is smaller than sleep. A joint that only works once — glued for the photograph, hopeless on the landing — is not a bed joint. It is a crate that got promoted.

A last shop note, because slats are part of the rail’s job. Slats that sit in a ledger should not be able to walk off the end; a retaining rail or a pin at the foot saves a 3 a.m. collapse. Foam mattresses (chapter 34) make this more urgent: they are heavy, they do not grab the slats the way a tufted innerspring can, and they will fall through a gap the manufacturer called “platform.” Spacing is a warranty number now. It used to be a joiner’s eye. The rail is still the thing that holds the ledger. If you only photograph the post, you are looking at the wrong half of the object — the same mistake as photographing a tester without cloth, except this time the missing half is a 1×4. Tighten what you can name.

## Notes

- Period hardware: bed bolts and caps in Empire and later American beds; restoration suppliers (Ball and Ball and others) as pattern sources, not as original parts.
- Shaker and Craftsman beds: wheeled frames, through-tenons; Cathers; museum collections at Craftsman Farms and Hancock.
- On rope grids and bed wrenches: chapter 2; Historic New England examples.
- Flat-pack cams: IKEA assembly logic, chapter 32.
- Shop practice: dry-fit, shoulders, the third-thickness rule as a starting point, not a religion.
