Met 2007.475a–i is a bedstead, not a four-poster. Forty-two and a half inches high, fifty-eight wide, ninety-two and a half long. Mahogany, ebony, and rosewood; secondary woods ash and yellow poplar; gilded gesso; die-cut and stamped brass; iron. New York, about 1805–8. Charles-Honoré Lannuier and his Parisian cousin Jean-Charles Cochois. The American Wing’s label calls it the only signed piece the two men produced together. Cochois was in New York only from 1804 to 1808. That window is the date.

The frame sweeps. The Met’s text compares it to a Roman lectus — a daybed — which is a curatorial analogy, not a shop word. A bronze plaque at the foot shows a profile the museum reads as a Roman magistrate or military officer. Reproduction upholstery and hangings, not original cloth, follow models associated with Pierre de La Mésangère and with Charles Percier and Pierre François Léonard Fontaine. The hangings are an argument about what a Federal French bed wore. They are not evidence of what this particular bed wore in 1806. Say so in the caption.

This is the early Lannuier chamber object the Met can hang a chapter on. It is not the only Lannuier bed that survives. It is the one that carries both names.

## Paris on Broad Street

Lannuier trained in Paris as an *ébéniste* and reached New York in 1803. From 1804 until his death in 1819 he kept a shop at 60 Broad Street. Peter M. Kenny’s 1998 Metropolitan Museum catalog — *Honoré Lannuier, Cabinetmaker from Paris: The Life and Work of a French Ébéniste in Federal New York*, with Frances F. Bretter and Ulrich Leben — is the book this chapter stands on. Leben on the French family and training. Kenny on the New York years and on connoisseurship of labeled and stamped work. Bretter on patrons. The exhibition ran 17 March–14 June 1998. The catalog’s documentary appendix transcribes invoices, a marriage contract, a will. Use those. Do not invent a voice for the cabinetmaker.

The bilingual trade label is the ordinary proof of later Lannuier, not of this early bed. The label used in the mature period names him *Cabinet Maker from Paris* / *Ebéniste de Paris*, Broad Street, No. 60, and advertises new-fashion furniture in both languages. The Bartow-Pell bedstead carries that label. The Met’s 1805–8 bedstead is the signed collaboration with Cochois, which is a different kind of document: two hands, one object, a short overlapping residency.

Phyfe’s shop, a few streets and a whole aesthetic away, was already the Anglo-American address for New York’s mercantile chamber. Lannuier’s address was the French one. Patrons used both. That is not a rivalry anecdote. It is a buying pattern, and Bretter’s essay is where the pattern is documented.

## Percier, Fontaine, La Mésangère

Percier and Fontaine’s *Recueil de décorations intérieures* (Paris, from 1801) and La Mésangère’s *Collection de meubles et objets de goût* are the print sources the Met names for the reproduction hangings on 2007.475. They are also the sources Lannuier’s shop could actually have seen. An *ébéniste* from Paris did not need a New York translator to read a French plate. The *lit en bateau*, the French bedstead with headboard and footboard of equal height, the crown (*ciel de lit*) from which cloth falls, the Grecian couch that is a daybed in a parlor and a cousin to the chamber bed — these are plate types before they are American antiques.

The Met bed is a sweeping frame meant to be dressed. Without cloth it is a lectus in a gallery. With reproduction hangings it is a period-room argument. Either way the original textiles are gone. Federal beds lose their first stuffs the same way colonial testers do: wear, wash, moth, and the day someone decides the wood is the antique. Chapter 2 already made that point for the eighteenth century. Here the cloth language is French Empire, not moreen and dimity, but the split in the bill is the same. Frame from the cabinetmaker. Hangings from the upholsterer. Two trades, two ledgers.

Gilded gesso on this bed is Paris practice in New York humidity. Die-cut and stamped brass is shop jewelry that can be ordered, applied, lost, and replaced. Iron is the hidden armature — rails, fastenings — not the look. Secondary ash and yellow poplar are the American box inside the mahogany, ebony, and rosewood show. Jennifer Anderson’s *Mahogany* is the Atlantic cost of the show wood. The ebony and rosewood are the extra claim: this is not a maple bed stained dark.

## Bartow-Pell: the crown that survived

The French bedstead at Bartow-Pell Mansion Museum, Pelham Bay, is the later object. Kenny dates it 1812–19. Family tradition wanted it closer to Isaac Bell and Mary Ellis Bell’s marriage in 1810. The date range follows the mature antique style — monumentality, rich classical ornament — and the bilingual engraved label of that period. Margaret Highland’s 2016 Bartow-Pell note remains the public object history: figured mahogany veneer; secondary mahogany, yellow poplar, white pine; casters; *vert antique* lion’s-paw feet; gilded acanthus; columns ending in gilded foliate scrolls. Gift of Henry S. Peltz and Mary Nevius.

What makes the bed rare is not the paws. It is the original crown. Gilded-brass classical faces around a lion’s head at the center. The crown is the machine for hangings. A French bedstead is designed to sit with one long side against the wall and to be seen from the other. Head and foot the same height, often scrolling out. Cloth falls from the crown, not from a four-post tester. Most American examples lost that crown. Bartow-Pell did not. The present hangings are restorations after period types, as at the Met. The crown is not.

The label on this bed — eagle from the Great Seal in the pediment of a cheval glass, bilingual text — is the War of 1812-era document Kenny uses among other reasons for the 1812–19 bracket. Patriotic engraving on a French cabinetmaker’s ticket is New York, not Paris. Lannuier sold Frenchness to Americans who also wanted the eagle.

Do not collapse Bartow-Pell into the Met bed. One is 1805–8, signed with Cochois, lectus-like, no surviving original crown. The other is 1812–19, labeled, with the crown still there. They are two moments in a sixteen-year shop.

## The Van Rensselaer bed

The Albany Institute of History & Art holds a French bedstead by Lannuier, accession 1951.61, gift of Constance Van Rensselaer Thayer Dexter, great-granddaughter of Stephen Van Rensselaer IV. The Institute dates it 1816–19. The marriage that explains the commission is 1817: Stephen Van Rensselaer IV and Harriet Bayard. Paper labels inside the headboard and footboard give the Broad Street bilingual ticket. Materials: mahogany, burl elm veneer, ash, gilt and *vert antique* (the green now read as black from color change), eastern white pine, soft and hard maple, cherry, rosewood veneers, cut-brass inlays, ormolu mounts. Dolphin feet. The Institute’s note is the place to stand, not a recycled Met caption.

Originally the bed stood at the Van Rensselaer Manor House in Watervliet. Costly hangings would have fallen from a wall-mounted crown and enclosed the bed. The crown is not claimed as original in the same breath as Bartow-Pell’s. The labels are. The dolphins are. The burl elm is the flash: a veneer that photographs as wealth even when the gilt has gone over.

Kenny’s catalog treats this bed among the documented late works. Use the catalog entry for construction and provenance; do not paraphrase a sentence as if it were a quotation.

## Bayard buys both shops

William Bayard is the patron who prevents this chapter from becoming a Lannuier hero card. The parlor furniture he ordered from Duncan Phyfe — some of it now at Winterthur — is the Anglo-New York interior people already knew. Bretter’s essay in the 1998 catalog, using the Bayard-Campbell-Pearsall Papers at the New York Public Library, is the demonstration that Bayard and kin also went to Lannuier for the showiest French pieces: winged caryatid furniture in the late Empire taste.

The 1817 weddings of Bayard’s daughters organize the surviving bills. Harriet married Stephen Van Rensselaer IV. Maria married Duncan Pearsall Campbell. The Met’s card table 1995.377.1 — mahogany veneer, white pine, yellow poplar, gilded gesso, *vert antique*, gilded brass, dated 1817 — is one of a pair descended in the Van Rensselaer line, commissioned in the larger Bayard purchase. A nearly identical pair of card tables and two pier tables with gilded swan supports went as wedding furniture. The invoice for the Campbell pieces survives. The pair of card tables was priced at $250. The pier table at $300. A journeyman cabinetmaker’s wage was about a dollar a day. Those numbers are the Met’s, on the card-table object page. They belong in this chapter because they price the shop that also made beds.

The Van Rensselaer bed at Albany is the chamber half of that wedding world. The card tables are the parlor half. Bayard did not have to choose Phyfe or Lannuier. He used Phyfe where Phyfe’s Grecian parlor language was wanted and Lannuier where the French *ébéniste* label was the point. Brinkerhoff, in Bretter’s account, repeats the pattern: large Phyfe orders, Lannuier for the flash. `[CITE NEEDED: Bretter page cites for Brinkerhoff if used in a caption.]`

Southern patronage and French émigré clients (James Leray de Chaumont or his son Vincent, 1816, northern New York) are in the same essay. They matter because they keep Lannuier from being a single Manhattan neighborhood story. James Bosley’s Baltimore seating suite is the rare domestic seating set Bretter discusses; the Savannah–Baltimore possible reuse (A. S. Bulloch) is a footnote speculation in the catalog literature, not a fact to flatten. Chipstone’s 1998 review of the catalog is the place that flags the speculation as speculation.

## What a Federal French bed is

It is not a colonial tester with the hangings taken off. Head and foot tend toward equal height. The long side is the show face. Placement is against a wall. Cloth comes from a crown or a wall fitting, not from four tall posts. Casters are common because you roll the bed out to change linen. The mattress still sits on sacking, slats, or a later grid; the Empire silhouette does not modernize the sleeping surface.

American shops that were not Lannuier’s made versions. Phyfe’s shop made high-post bedsteads as well as Grecian couches; the Met’s Phyfe bedstead 2021.109a–vv, 1815–25, mahogany and mahogany veneer with *vert antique* and gilded cast brass, is a high-post frame with rope-twist and waterleaf posts, miniature Doric columns on the footboard, possibly the “Mahogany High Post Bedstead” valued at $2 in the open garret of Phyfe’s house at 193 Fulton Street in the September 1854 inventory. That object is Phyfe’s chamber language: still posts, already classical. It is not a sleigh. The next chapter takes the sleigh. This chapter’s job is to keep the French bedstead and the high-post Federal bed from collapsing into one “neoclassical bed.”

Lannuier died in 1819, age forty. The shop’s New York run is sixteen years. Phyfe lives until 1854 and the name becomes a style word that later German cabinetmakers in New York, Ernest Hagen among them, will both document and reproduce. Lannuier does not become a style word in the same way. He becomes a connoisseur’s labeled object. Kenny’s phrase in the literature is that the near-obsessive labeling and stamping make him a connoisseur’s problem with more handles than Phyfe. Handle is not the same as typical. Most New York beds of 1810 were not signed.

## Joinery and the hidden American box

Veneer is the Federal show. Figured mahogany on a shaped headboard is a skin. The box is ash, yellow poplar, pine — woods that take glue and screws and do not have to be imported. Cut-brass inlay is sawn metal set into a routed line; it is not ormolu, which is cast and gilt. *Vert antique* is a greenish bronze finish on carved wood, imitating excavated bronze. It darkens. Albany’s note that the *vert antique* now reads black is a conservation fact, not a design change.

Gilded gesso on carved elements is a layered claim: glue, gesso, bole, gold. New York’s climate is unkind to it. Losses are expected. A perfect gilt surface on a Lannuier bed is often a later campaign.

Rails meet the head and foot in hardware that is meant to knock down. Iron in the Met’s medium list is that hardware world. A Federal bed that cannot come apart did not go up a New York stair.

## Residual

The only signed Lannuier–Cochois piece is a bed. That is an accident of survival and of two men overlapping for four years, not a proof that beds were the shop’s center. The Bartow-Pell bed kept its crown. The Van Rensselaer bed kept its labels and its dolphins. Bayard’s invoices kept the prices. Percier, Fontaine, and La Mésangère kept the plates the upholsterer still uses when a museum puts cloth back on a frame.

What did not survive, again, is the first textile. The Federal French bed is a side-facing machine for hangings. Galleries that show the machine without saying so are repeating the colonial tester problem in Empire veneer. The Broad Street label knew the furniture was “new fashion.” The fashion included cloth.
