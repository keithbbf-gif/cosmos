# Nakashima Slab Headboard

A Conoid headboard and platform bed from George Nakashima’s shop in New Hope, Pennsylvania, dated 1976 in one auction file, is French olive ash burl and American ash: a headboard about twenty-four inches high and 108 inches wide, an overhang with sap grain and a free edge, four sliding doors, a shelf behind, a platform about ten inches high for a sixty-inch mattress. Another, 1974, for a client named Schull, is signed on the underside of the platform. Order cards exist. Drawings exist. This is not a suite number in a Winchendon folder. It is a tree, a client, a date, and a shop on Aquetong Road.

Nakashima (1905–1990) was born in Spokane, trained as an architect (University of Washington, MIT, time in France, Tokyo in Antonin Raymond’s office), built at Sri Aurobindo’s ashram in Pondicherry, came back to Seattle, and was sent with Marion Okajima Nakashima and infant Mira to an internment camp in Idaho after Pearl Harbor. At Minidoka he worked wood with Gentaro Hikogawa, a trained Japanese carpenter. In 1943 Raymond pulled the family to his farm in New Hope. By 1945 Nakashima had a furniture shop. The compound grew to some fifteen buildings, most of his design, including the Conoid Studio of 1957–59, a shell of concrete and wood that named a base: the Conoid, a curved, truncated cone in the supports of tables and, here, in the spirit of a bed that is one board thick at the wall.

The board is a slab with a free edge, bark or sapwood left as the tree’s decision, knots and checks accepted, butterfly keys in walnut across a split. *The Soul of a Tree* (1981) is Nakashima’s own book. He writes as a man who has rescued timber the mill would have wasted. The headboard is that argument stood on edge behind a mattress.

## One-off against the factory suite

A Grand Rapids or North Carolina suite is a set of drawings for a species, a finish, a pull, a price. Nakashima’s shop made named forms — Conoid, Minguren, Trestle, Pyramid, the Mira chair (1950), Frenchman’s Cove tables — and then made each example from a particular plank. The client’s name on the platform is the opposite of a Sears inspector stamp, and also its cousin: both are documents of a making. One names a household. The other names a plant.

Beds were never the shop’s loudest product. Tables were. Chairs were. The Conoid headboard with storage — sliding doors, a shelf, a place for books and a clock — is a bedroom case attached to a slab. It solves the American closet-and-nightstand problem without a matching triple dresser. The platform is low, ten inches in the 1976 example, which is Nelson’s height with a different religion. No aluminum ring. No wheat lacquer. Oil, or a finish that lets the ash read as ash. The sapwood stripe on the overhang is the decoration. A mill would have trimmed it off.

Knoll had a brief Nakashima relationship in the 1940s. Widdicomb-Mueller showed a Nakashima collection in 1958, *House Beautiful* in October of that year. Those are the factory experiments. They are not the New Hope bed. The Widdicomb pieces are Nakashima thinking inside a mill’s constraints: proportion, a more repeatable edge. The slab headboard is the shop unconstrained, except by the tree and the client’s mattress size. Double, queen, a 108-inch headboard for a wide wall: the width is the room’s.

Mira Nakashima, born 1942, raised in the shop, took the bench after 1990. The Mira chair was named for her in 1950, a small Shaker-related form. She has made her own lines (Keisho, Shoki, the Concordia chair for musicians) while keeping classic forms in production with a crew — chair makers, finishers, woodworkers — on the same acres. A Conoid headboard ordered now is a Mira-shop object with a George form. The one-off continues as a practice, not as a dead man’s relic. Caption the date and the hand.

## Internment, Hikogawa, and the joint

The camp is not a sentimental preface. It is where Nakashima’s American joinery met a Japanese carpenter’s. Hikogawa’s skill is named in the auction biographies because the shop named it. Butterfly keys across a check are a visible joint that is also a repair, a thing Stickley would have understood as honesty and a Greene would have understood as a peg’s cousin. The free edge is not Japanese in the *House Beautiful* shibui sense. It is a mill-reject board promoted. American hardwood — walnut, ash, cherry, walnut burl, the imported exotics when a client paid — comes through New Hope as lumber that still looks like lumber.

A slab headboard’s making: select the board, flatten, leave the live edge, cut sliding-door tracks, hang doors that must run true in a board that will move with the seasons, fit a shelf, hang the whole thing on a wall or on a platform structure, oil. The platform is a separate object: a box or a frame, signed, sometimes with storage, sized to a 1970s mattress that is thinner than a 2020 pillow-top. The 1976 Peachey bed’s provenance — acquired from the artist, then by descent — is the shop’s preferred history. No hire-purchase book. A wait. Nakashima clients waited for wood. That wait is the opposite of the Depression outfit’s truck on Monday.

Seasonal movement in a 108-inch board is not a theory. Ash and walnut take on moisture in a Pennsylvania summer and drop it in a heated winter. Tracks that bind in August are a shop conversation. Mira’s crew still fits doors with that in mind. A factory suite cannot wait for August. It ships a printed grain that does not move because it is not a tree anymore. The Conoid headboard’s sliding doors are therefore a labor cost: plane, fit, wait, fit again. The adjustable shelf behind is a grooved standard, a shop solution, not a Knoll specification sheet.

French olive ash burl on the 1976 example is an exotic the shop used when the client’s order and the lumber pile met. American ash is the quieter cousin, a pale, tough hardwood, not teak, not walnut. A bedroom in ash reads as light even when the form is heavy. Burl is the wild figure waterfall mills would have sliced into a drawer face. Here the burl is allowed to be the whole weather of the overhang. Sap grain on the edge is a stripe of biology the mill would have called wane.

Labor in the shop is named more often than in Grand Rapids, and still not enough. Nakashima did not make every piece with his own chisel; the legend said he did. Mira’s later interviews are dry on the crew. Three chair makers, finishers, a rate of some hundreds of pieces a year in the later shop: that is a workshop, not a factory, and not a lone genius. The bedroom headboard is a crew object. The slab is the signature.

## Wood as the argument

The American bedroom had made wood visible before: Stickley’s flake, Greene’s teak and walnut, Heywood’s birch through the drawer. Nakashima’s claim is stronger and stranger. The tree is the designer. Checks are not defects. A burl that would have been sliced for waterfall veneer becomes the whole face of a headboard. The mattress is almost an embarrassment, a manufactured rectangle against an unmanufactured edge. That tension is the object. A fitted bedspread that hides the platform is a choice against the design. A quilt that stops at the sapwood is a choice with it.

Compare a Greene cloudlift, which is a cut in a rail, a human geometry. The free edge is not a cloudlift. It is the refusal of that geometry. Compare a McCobb headboard, a thickness with a small radius. Nakashima’s overhang is a roof. You can put a book on it. The sliding doors are Japanese in a more literal sense than *House Beautiful*’s lanterns: fusuma as furniture, a track, a recess. The internment carpenter is in that track.

The Mira chair’s Shaker kinship is a Pennsylvania fact: New Hope is not far from the folklore of Shaker rooms, and Nakashima’s small chair is a reduction, a seat and a back, named for a child. A bedroom that holds a Mira chair and a Conoid headboard is a shop’s family, not a suite. The chair does not match the slab. It is allowed not to. That permission is the one-off’s gift. Grand Rapids would have stained them the same.

The Conoid name comes from the studio’s roof and from a base used under tables: a curved understructure. On a bed, Conoid may mean the storage headboard type more than a conical leg. Dealers will say Conoid for anything Nakashima in a bedroom. Look at the order card. The shop wrote what it built.

## What a one-off does to the series

This magazine pack has lived in suites, catalogs, and reproductions. Nakashima is the chapter that says a bed can still be a commission in the United States after 1945. It is not a return to the Greenes. The Greenes had Halls and a winter house. Nakashima had clients from all over, a shop that shipped, a price that selected the room. Most Americans did not sleep under a slab. They slept under Declaration slats or a padded Hollywood headboard. The slab is the argument that the mill’s honesty — even Planner’s maple honesty — is still a product. The tree is not a product until the mill makes it one. Nakashima stopped before that sentence finished.

There is a piety around this work that the objects do not need. The platform is ten inches high. The doors stick in August. The oil wants a cloth. Mira’s crew still cuts butterflies. The American bedroom’s factory path did not end because a man in New Hope loved ash. It ended, when it ended, because tastes and plants closed. The slab is a parallel path, narrow, still open.

## Residual

The Schull name on the underside of a platform is a better caption than “organic modernism.” A family slept there. The headboard was a board with doors. Mira can still make the form. The factory suite cannot make that board, because the mill requires the edge to be predicted. Nakashima’s sleeping furniture is the prediction refused. The mattress, American, rectangular, sits on the ash and does not win. The tree holds the wall.

A last shop measurement: the 1976 headboard’s nineteen-inch depth, in the Rago file, is a piece of casework, not a thin board. Sliding doors need a box. The slab is the face of a cabinet. People who know Nakashima only as a coffee table miss that the bedroom object is storage. The Greenes put a night table beside the bed. Nakashima put the night table in the headboard and let the live edge be the cornice.

The Peachey order’s digital copy of the original card, sold with the 1976 bed, is the kind of paper this series prefers to a later auction adjective. Wood, width, doors, a date. A Grand Rapids suite has a catalog number instead. Both papers are true. Only one of them required a particular ash tree.
