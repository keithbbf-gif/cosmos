# Vanity and Dressing, 1900

An American dressing table of 1760 is a low chest with cabriole legs, a companion to the high chest, no glass attached. You set a looking glass on it or hung a glass on the wall. An American dresser of 1880 is a tall chest with a mirror bolted to a splash, marble on top, part of a chamber suite. Between them, and then beside them, a third object takes a new word: vanity. A table, often with drawers in the apron, a kneehole or an open knee space, and a mirror that is the point — one glass or three, the triptych that lets a woman see the back of her head. Mail-order catalogs and furniture ads around 1900–1920 sell it as the woman’s piece in the bedroom. Hollywood, a decade and more later, will photograph it as a horseshoe of light.

The word *vanity* as furniture is a twentieth-century preference. Eighteenth-century bills say dressing table. Victorian catalogs say dressing case, dressing bureau, lady’s dresser. *Vanity table* and *vanity* ride with cosmetics sold in sets, with plumbing that removes the washstand’s basin from the marble, and with advertising that knows who is supposed to sit there.

## From lowboy to bolted glass

Chapter 7’s dressing table was storage and a surface for a basin. The glass was a separate, expensive, easily broken object. Attaching the glass to the case — first on Victorian dressers, then on lighter tables — is a hardware decision that changes the room. The piece must now stand away from nothing; it is a facade. It needs a wall. It needs to be heavy enough not to fall when the glass is tall. Factory dressers solved this with mass. The vanity solves it with a wider stance, a lower profile, and a mirror designed as furniture, not as an accessory.

By 1900 the attached mirror is ordinary. What is new is the table that is all dressing and little linen storage. Drawers for gloves, pins, later cosmetics. A shelf. Sometimes a bench that matches — the vanity bench, a short upholstered stool, sold as part of the piece. Grand Rapids and its competitors can include this in a suite: bed, dresser (tall, for shirts), vanity (low, for her), chiffonier or chifforobe, nightstands. The gendered split is the ad copy. His tall chest. Her glass.

Grier’s parlor book is still the method: comfort, display, gender as a marketed arrangement. Women’s magazines — *Ladies’ Home Journal*, *Good Housekeeping* — run furniture ads that show a woman at a vanity whether or not the house has a maid. The chamber is advertised as hers even when the deed is not.

## Plumbing, cosmetics, and the washstand’s death

Indoor bathrooms, spreading in American middle-class houses from the late nineteenth century into the 1920s, steal the washstand’s job. The marble splash and the slop jar leave the bedroom. What remains of “dressing” is not washing so much as arranging: hair, face, jewelry. Cosmetics as a mass-market, packaged, advertised industry — powder, later lipstick — want a place to sit that is not a basin stand. The vanity is that place. A glass top, sometimes, because perfume etches wood. A little tray. Electric light later, around the mirror, or a pair of lamps.

This is not Hollywood yet. It is 1910 catalog copy. A three-piece mirror, center glass plus hinged wings, is already a furniture type before studio makeup tables. The woman in the illustration leans in. The copy names mahogany, bird’s-eye maple, enamel white, gold. White enamel suites, overlapping the white metal bed, make the chamber look washable even when the vanity is wood.

The dresser with a bolted glass does not disappear. Many rooms have both: a tall dresser for clothes, a vanity for the face. Small rooms choose. A mirrored chifforobe door can steal the vanity’s job, as the last chapter said. Catalogs offer all three and let the floor plan decide.

## The woman’s chamber as advertised

Advertisers did not invent the idea that a bedroom might be a woman’s workplace of appearance. They industrialized it. A 1915 suite ad that shows a woman seated at the vanity, husband absent, children absent, is selling privacy and leisure that many buyers do not have. The furniture still ships. The stool still matches.

Class: a maid’s room does not get a vanity in the ad, though a servant may dress a mistress at this table. Race: the illustrated woman in national ads is almost always white. Southern mail-order buyers still order the table. The object is wider than the picture.

Mail-order vanities in oak, then in cheaper gum painted to look like mahogany, are how the type leaves the city. Freight, knockdown mirror, a bag of screws. The triptych is fragile. Surviving catalog vanities often have replaced glass. Original bevels are a dating pleasure when they survive.

Design languages: Colonial Revival vanities with cabriole legs, pretending to be lowboys with attached glasses the eighteenth century did not bolt on. “Sheraton” vanities with reeded legs. Painted “Adam” enamel. Mission oak vanities that almost contradict the Craftsman brief (Stickley’s daughters will get inlaid case pieces, not a Hollywood horseshoe — next chapter). Art Nouveau in custom shops, rare in Grand Rapids. Each style is a skin on the same brief: a place to sit with a face.

Electric light around a glass is the Hollywood tell, but a pair of dresser lamps on a 1912 vanity is already a lighting plan. Outlets in the apron come later. Early sitters use a window. Ads that show a vanity under a window are not being poetic; they are showing the only reliable lamp the furniture can assume.

Bird’s-eye maple and white enamel are the two show woods of the 1910s vanity, overlapping the white metal bed. A maple vanity with a triptych is a factory flex: figured wood, fragile wings, a bench in matching veneer. Gum stained as mahogany is the cheap line. The same mill may have made Eastlake washstands thirty years earlier. The suite grammar holds; the washstand has been replaced by a table that does not hold water.

Men’s “dressing stands” and chiffoniers in the same catalogs are tall, with a small glass or none. Shaving happens more and more at the bathroom sink. The vanity as a seated, mirrored, cosmetic table is marketed female even when a man in the house might have used a glass. The ad decides who sits.

## Hollywood later

Movie dressing rooms — bulbs around a glass, a low table, makeup in ranks — become a public image in the 1920s–30s. Fan magazines photograph actresses at tables that look like furniture-store vanities with more light. Hollywood Regency, later still, will chrome and gloss the type. The suburban vanity of 1935 with a curved glass and blond wood is already quoting the studio.

Do not date the furniture type to the movies. Date the movies as a loud user of a type the catalogs had. What Hollywood changes is the lighting and the idea that the table is a workstation of celebrity. Ordinary vanities pick up round mirrors, then kidney-shaped tables, then blond suites after the war. The word *vanity* by then has stuck. *Dressing table* sounds like an antique.

Men’s dressing, meanwhile, is a high chest, a chiffonier, a closet when there is one, a bathroom mirror. Ads rarely sell a “man’s vanity.” A gentleman’s dressing case in the eighteenth century was a box of bottles. The twentieth-century gendered bedroom puts the glass on her side of the room. That is a sales decision with a long afterlife in how Americans still name the furniture.

Kidney-shaped and horseshoe vanities of the 1930s–50s, blond or walnut, are the type after Hollywood has been seen. They are not the origin. They are the afterimage. A suburban “waterfall” suite with a vanity is Grand Rapids or its North Carolina successors still selling a matched room, now with a curved drawer front. Chapter 12’s photograph has learned chrome.

Perfume and nail polish wrecked more veneer tops than water did. Glass tops, then later plastics, are a material answer. A vanished glass top leaves an outline of unfaded wood. Caption that as use, not as a missing marble.

## Joinery, glass, and the bench

A vanity’s carcass is a shallow table: legs, apron drawers, sometimes a center kneehole drawer that the sitter’s knees must clear. Drawer sides pine or poplar, machine dovetails on factory pieces. The mirror posts are the structural anxiety — racking, a glass that wants to be a sail. Factory vanities add a back rail. Custom ones may look lighter than they should.

Triptych hinges are furniture hinges, not picture-frame afterthoughts. They loosen. The wings droop. A conservator’s problem that looks like a design one.

The matching bench is upholstered in the same years as parlor comfort: a pad, later vinyl, a storage lid on some cheap versions. Grier’s comfort argument in miniature. The sitter is part of the object. A dressing table without a seat is a standing desk for hair.

Marble, which ruled the washstand, is less common on vanities (perfume, weight, cost). Wood, glass, later Bakelite trays. Electric outlets in the table are a late gift. Early vanities assume window light or a lamp you supply.

## What the vanity is not

It is not a colonial lowboy, even when the legs are cabriole. The bolted glass is disqualifying. It is not a dresser. The storage is too small; the sitting is the point. It is not a bathroom vanity in the later plumber’s sense — a sink in a cabinet — though the word will migrate there and confuse search engines. This chapter’s vanity has no tap. It has a glass.

It is not inevitable. A room with a chifforobe mirror and a bathroom down the hall can skip it. After the Second World War, many American bedrooms do skip it: more closets, more bathrooms, cosmetics at a medicine cabinet. The vanity becomes optional, then nostalgic, then a “makeup table” in a teenager’s room. Hollywood keeps the image longer than the average floor plan keeps the furniture.

Department-store windows, 1910s–20s, dressed a vanity with bottles the store also sold. The furniture and the cosmetics were one ticket. That is why the object wants a glass top and a photographer. A missing original stool is as common as a replaced triptych wing; the table without a seat is only half the type.

## Residual

The dressing table learned to hold a mirror, then learned to be sold as a woman’s face. Advertisers in 1900 needed a piece that was not the washstand (plumbing was taking that) and not the tall dresser (linen). They needed a photograph of someone looking at herself. The vanity is that photograph in oak or enamel. Hollywood only turned up the lights. The colonial pair never asked anyone to sit and look. It asked them to fold. The difference is the glass, bolted down, and a stool that matches.
