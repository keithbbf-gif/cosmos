In 1991, Fagerdala World Foams in Sweden sold a viscoelastic mattress under the name TEMPUR. In 1992, Bob Trussell, a Kentucky horseman who had met a Fagerdala executive, brought the product into the United States as Tempur-Pedic. NASA had already, in the late 1960s, paid for the foam’s ancestor. Charles Yost, working under contract on energy-absorbing seating, made an open-cell polyurethane with slow spring-back — “temper foam” — for crash and vibration work. NASA later let the formula into wider commercial use. Swedish labs spent the 1980s making a version that could be slept on. Hospitals tried it. Then the American bedroom did.

The object that matters for furniture history is not the brand. It is a mattress that does not want a box spring, does not need a rail to hold a rope grid, and can arrive compressed in a carton. The bedstead, for two centuries the wooden half of a textile machine, becomes optional. A platform of slats will do. A floor will do, against warranty advice. A headboard ordered from a different aisle will do. Joinery, in the sense this series has been using the word, has nowhere to go.

## What the foam asked of the frame

Innerspring mattresses of the twentieth century were partners of the box spring: two upholstered boxes, coils on coils, a metal or wood frame around them, a headboard bolted to brackets. Simmons Beautyrest, Sealy, Serta — the names on the tag — assumed a flexible base. The bedstead’s job was to keep those boxes off the floor and to give the room a wooden face. Posts, rails, a footboard. Even a cheap metal frame was a perimeter.

Tempur-Pedic’s own support notes are blunt. A traditional coil box spring is not compatible; it will void the warranty. The foam wants a solid, elevated, relatively rigid surface. Approved: the company’s flat foundation, a power base, a platform whose slats are at least three inches wide and not more than four inches apart. Plywood and untreated wood are warned against. The numbers shift slightly across help-center pages; the logic does not. Foam sags into gaps. A box spring’s “give,” which once helped a spring mattress, is now a defect.

So the wooden bed loses a job. If the mattress does not need a deep frame, the frame can be a low platform or a metal grid from the same company that sold the foam. The expensive object in the room becomes the cushion, as it was in the seventeenth-century inventory — feathers first, then the posts — except the cushion is now petrochemical and branded. Chapter 1’s residual fact comes back wearing a NASA sticker.

Charles Yost’s foam was not designed for a bedroom. It was seating and impact. The transfer into sleep is a classic spinoff story, and NASA has been willing to tell it. A furniture historian can let the agency have the paragraph and still notice what the bedroom lost: a reason for four posts, a reason for a tester, a reason for a rail stout enough to take rope. What remains is a reason for a headboard, and that reason is mostly pictorial.

## The box on the porch

Casper launched in April 2014 with a single mattress, sold online, compressed into a box, a hundred-night trial. First-year revenue was reported in the nine figures. The company did not invent compression shipping; BedInABox and others had already mailed foam. Casper’s achievement was to make the carton ordinary: FedEx up the stairs, plastic, expand, wait. Within a few years the American trade was full of twins — Purple, Leesa, all the vowel brands — and the old mattress stores had to answer a customer who had already slept on a wrapped rectangle.

Queen and king sizes, which Simmons and others pushed in the 1960s, had already strained the wooden bed. A colonial frame is a twin or a generous full by later measure. A king rail is a long span; it wants a center support or it will bow. Foam kings are heavier in a different way — dead weight, no coil spring’s air — and they still want that center support. The wooden shop that survived did so by adding a middle leg the catalog called a feature. The foam companies added a foundation SKU. Either way the old four-poster logic, two rails and two ends, is undersized for the mattress the culture now buys.

Waterbeds, in the 1970s and 1980s, were a previous theft of the frame. A vinyl bladder in a pedestal, often particleboard, often with drawers, made the “bed” a tank stand. Some households put a captain’s pedestal under a water mattress and called it furniture. The foam carton is quieter, lighter, easier to dump. It learned the pedestal’s lesson: the mattress is the product; the wood is a box.

The boxed mattress is an attack on the bedstead as a delivery problem. A queen innerspring on a box spring needs two people and a truck. A foam queen needs a doorway. Apartments, walk-ups, the college move, the divorce — the carton wins. The frame, if any, is a separate click: a platform from a warehouse, a headboard from a catalog, legs that screw into a metal rail. West Elm, Pottery Barn, IKEA, the apartment-assembly startups: headboard as SKU, “wall-mounted” or “freestanding,” often upholstered, often with no footboard. The bed is a cushion, a panel, and a pair of nightstands if the photograph still wants them.

The “Hollywood frame” — angle iron, wheels, brackets for a headboard — had already taught department stores that the wooden bedstead was optional. Mid-century apartments used it under a box spring because it was cheap and it rolled. Foam did not invent the iron rectangle. It made the rectangle sufficient. Once the box spring left, the Hollywood frame could shrink to a platform of slats with the same brackets. The headboard from the suite, if any, still bolts on. The posts are gone.

Joinery does not vanish because people stop needing it. It becomes ornamental or absent. A “solid wood bed” in this market may be a headboard with posts that do not take a rail tenon, because there is no rail, only a metal hook-on frame from a different factory. The wooden object is a facade. That is not new — Victorian beds had applied carving on factory posts — but the facade used to hide a working joint. Now it may hide the fact that there is no wooden joint at all.

Retail photography completed the reduction. A lifestyle catalog shows a foam slab, white sheets, a plant, a phone, a headboard in linen. No footboard. No rails visible. The nightstands (chapter 45) are the remaining wood, or metal, or a stacking crate. If there is a case piece, it is a MALM or a dresser from a different country with the same foil language. The suite is a mood board. Grand Rapids needed five numbered pieces. The carton needs one SKU and a click for the panel.

## What happens to the posts

A Federal pencil post without hangings was already a sculpture (chapter 2). A 2018 upholstered headboard with no posts is the next reduction. The sleeping surface does not attach to it. Some headboards bolt to the metal frame; some sit on the floor and lean; some fasten to the wall like a picture. The wall becomes a piece of furniture. A high chest faced the bed as a case. The headboard faces the bed as a cushion. Storage has left the bedstead (or crawled under it, chapter 33). The frame’s last public job is to be photographed.

Adjustable power bases — motors, massage, USB — complete the theft. The wooden rail cannot hinge in three places. The base is a machine the mattress sits on. Wooden “sleigh” shells exist to dress the machine. They are hats. A shop that cuts a through-tenon on a bed rail is making an object the dominant mattress companies treat as optional equipment.

There is a class split. Custom hardwood beds still get ordered: white oak, walnut, a footboard, a joint you can name (chapter 35). Those beds often still require a platform or closely spaced slats because the customer also bought foam. The joinery is real and the mattress does not care. The foam would lie as still on a $200 metal grid. The wood is for the room, not for the back. That is an honest division if the shop says it. It is a confusion if the catalog says the oak is what makes you sleep.

## The old expensive object, again

Inventories priced feathers above frames because feathers were imported skill and the posts were local wood. Memory foam reverses the geography: the petrochemical cushion is the engineered import (even when poured in North America, the recipe is a lab’s), and the frame is the thing you might skip. Price tags follow. A Tempur-Pedic king can cost more than a decent hardwood bed. The boxed foam competitors raced the price down; the premium foam stayed high. Either way, the customer’s money moved off the cabinetmaker’s ticket.

Upholstery shops noticed. Headboards became a padded trade: foam, fabric, a plywood back, buttons. The upholsterer, who in the eighteenth century made the bed furniture — the cloth box — returns as the person who makes the only wooden-looking object in the room, except it is not wood, it is cloth over wood. The vocabulary word *bed furniture* meant textiles. It fits again, accidentally.

Hospitals had already taught the lesson. A modern hospital bed is a steel mechanism with a vinyl mattress. No one asks for a tester. The domestic bedroom delayed the same conclusion until foam and a carton made the ceremonial frame look like extra. Sanitation writers of the 1880s who wanted iron beds without drapery would recognize the foam slab on a metal base. They would not recognize the price.

Labor moved with the object. Innerspring plants — coilers, tufters, tape-edge operators — were American factory jobs with a geography (the old mattress cities, then the Sun Belt). Foam pours in different plants, often with different unions, different noses. The boxed brands contracted the pour and the knit cover and the marketing. Cabinetmakers did not get the work. Upholsterers got a fraction of it, in headboards. The bed-rail hook, as a trade, waited for the households that still wanted wood.

A note on smell and heat, because they are furniture facts. Viscoelastic foam sleeps warm. Later gels and coils-in-foam are answers to that complaint. A wooden tester with dimity, in a Chesapeake summer, was an answer to mosquitoes and air. Different climates, different envelopes. The foam slab does not make a room. It occupies a room that air-conditioning already made possible. Take away the HVAC and the seventeenth-century inventory looks less quaint. The tick and the hangings were climate control. The carton assumes a compressor in the window.

## Residual weight

The mattress does not destroy joinery. It unemploys it. A rail still has to keep a wooden bed square if you insist on a wooden bed. Hooks, bolts, through-tenons (chapter 39) still work. They work for people who want a bedstead. The mass market does not need them to sleep.

The residual fact is a warranty line. Use a box spring and we will not stand behind the foam. The sentence looks like a product note. It is a historical claim: the partnership between a wooden or metal frame and a spring mattress is over for this material. What you see in the apartment — a wrapped cushion, a panel, a phone charger — is the chamber before the suite, minus the hangings and minus the chest, unless the chest is a MALM. The feathers have become foam. The posts have become a SKU you can decline.
