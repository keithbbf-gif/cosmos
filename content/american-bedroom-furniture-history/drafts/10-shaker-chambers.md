# Shaker Chambers

Met 1981.226 is a bedstead from New Lebanon, New York, 1830–60, maple and pine, seventy-one by thirty by thirty-one inches. Purchase, anonymous gift, 1981. Wooden wheels that do not pivot. You pull the bed straight out from the wall to change the tick and sweep. The 1845 Millennial Laws said bedsteads should be painted green. This one still has enough early paint that the Met’s retiring-room text treats the survival as the point. Toward the twentieth century, old Shaker finishes were often stripped and varnished. A green bed that is still green is evidence, not a decorator’s choice.

The bed is narrow. Thirty-one inches is not a double. Retiring rooms were shared by same-sex members. Several beds in a room was typical. The Met’s installed retiring room, architectural elements 1972.187.1 from the North Family Dwelling at Mount Lebanon, about 1830–40, is shown with one green bed because a gallery has to walk around it. Originally the room would have held more. The pegboard around the walls is not a motif. It is how chairs, clothing, and tools leave the floor so the floor can be cleaned.

No tester. No crown. No sleigh scroll. No theater. That is the chapter’s argument, and it has to be made against a century of “Shaker style” bedrooms that hang a peg rail over a four-poster and call the mixture colonial.

## Mount Lebanon, Hancock

Mount Lebanon — New Lebanon in the object records — was the central ministry community in upstate New York. Hancock, near Pittsfield, Massachusetts, was the Bishopric neighbor. The two villages are the places this chapter can stand without pretending to cover Kentucky, Ohio, and Maine in one breath. Eighteen communities at the mid-nineteenth-century height, on the order of six thousand members, from Maine to Kentucky: the Met’s room text uses those figures. The furniture in a Maine dwelling house and a Kentucky one will share laws and habits, not identical benches.

The North Family Dwelling at Mount Lebanon was a five-story building for one of the community’s families, the work-and-faith units: kitchens, dining rooms, chapel, retiring rooms. A retiring room was a bedroom and, under the Millennial Laws, a place to retire in silence before meeting. The Met bought the architectural fabric of one such room in fall 1972: woodwork, pegboards, built-in cupboards. Emily Crane Chadbourne Bequest. Object 1972.187.1. The furniture inside is assembled from Shaker-made pieces, not a single documented original room-load. Period rooms lie that way. The pegs and the cupboards are the Mount Lebanon sentence. The green bed is a New Lebanon type in the same dialect.

Hancock’s dwelling-house chambers, as June Sprigg described them when she was curator there, are the other public version: pine floors, white plaster, a pegboard circling the room, a closet and a tall built-in cupboard and case of drawers in butternut and pine, stained yellow and orange, drawers diminishing in height as they rise. Pegs threaded into the board so they can be unscrewed when a tall cupboard needs to sit flush. Sprigg’s *Inner Light: The Shaker Legacy* (1984) is a curator’s look, not a catalog raisonné. It is still better than a furniture-shop “Shaker bedroom” page.

Hancock’s round stone barn is what tourists remember. The retiring room is the furniture history. Built-ins mean the chamber storage is architecture. A high chest is portable wealth. A Shaker cupboard of drawers is the wall. You cannot take it when you leave because leaving, in theory, is not the plan, and property is common.

## Beds on wheels

The nonpivoting wooden wheel is a design brief. Castors that swivel would let the bed wander on a scrubbed floor. Fixed wheels let it track out and back in the same line, like a cart in a vised groove. Trundle logic without the trundle: the bed itself moves. The Met’s object text calls them trundle bedsteads with nonpivoting wooden wheels. The word *trundle* here is the rolling, not a second bed under a first.

Green paint: the 1845 revision of the Millennial Laws is the cited source. Comfortables — bed coverings — should be of a modest color, not checked, striped, or flowered. The bed is painted; the coverings are quiet. That is the opposite of the colonial inventory in which the textiles outpriced the frame. Here the frame is a painted machine and the cloth is forbidden to perform.

Why green? The Met’s room essay notes that Shaker furniture and architecture rarely used green otherwise, and that period accounts associate green with vegetation and the health of a community. Mark the interpretation as the museum’s, not as a law of optics. What is solid is the instruction to paint bedsteads green and the rarity of surviving early green.

Maple and pine, as on 1981.226, are the ordinary woods. No mahogany show. No veneer bookmatch. The headboard is a board. The posts are low. A mattress on slats or sacking, narrow. Shared rooms, so the bed is a person’s small floor, not a family’s theater. Enslaved labor is not the making system here; Shaker communities were gathered, celibate, communitarian, and on the record as opposing slavery, with Black members in some communities. The labor is Believers’ labor, including women’s work in textiles and men’s in wood, with plenty of overlap that the gendered tourist script flattens. `[CITE NEEDED: a named Hancock or Mount Lebanon maker for a documented bed, if a community journal yields one.]`

## Peg rails and built-ins

A peg rail is a board with turned pegs, run around the room at a height that takes a chair hung by its ladder-back. Chairs off the floor. Bonnets off the floor. A broom off the floor. Cleaning is theology you can see. The plentitude of pegs in the Met room, the museum text says, signals how many women would have shared the space. One bed on view, many pegs: the installation undercounts sleepers and still overexplains storage.

Built-in cupboards next to the door, drawers graduated, a closet if the dwelling’s plan allows, are the high chest’s opposite. They do not have cabriole legs. They do not have a waist molding you can take apart for a stair. They were made for this wall. Take them out, as the Met did, and they become “architectural elements,” which is to say furniture that lost its building and then got a new one on Fifth Avenue.

Threaded pegs, the Hancock detail Sprigg loved, are extra work. A stick glued in would hold a hat. A threaded peg can be removed. That is joinery in the service of a future rearrangement, which is a very un-highboy idea. The high chest assumes it may move house. The peg assumes the cupboard may move an inch.

Painted woodwork — yellows, oranges, reds, blues — against whitewash is the actual chromatic range. The later stripped-and-varnished “natural Shaker” interior is often a twentieth-century correction. Edward Deming Andrews and Faith Andrews, who did more than anyone to put Shaker furniture into American museums and shops, also helped create a taste for the scrubbed, unpainted, “honest wood” object. Their books remain essential and are not neutral. June Sprigg, Charles Hummel at Winterthur, and later community-based curators at Hancock and Mount Lebanon have spent decades putting color and use back.

## No theater

Compare the Shaker bed to everything this series has already put in a chamber. The colonial tester is a cloth box. The Philadelphia high chest is a carved facade. The Lannuier French bed is a side-facing veneer machine with a crown. The Empire sleigh is a scroll for a wall. The Shaker bed is a painted cart for a tick.

It is not “simple” in the brochure sense. Fixed wheels, specified color, pegs at a working height, built-ins with graduated drawers, chairs designed to hang — these are constraints. Constraint is not absence of design. It is design with the audience removed. There is no guest to impress. There is a ministry to satisfy and a floor to wash.

The looking glass, if present, is small. The dressing table as a type does not belong. A built-in or a small stand takes the basin. The retiring room is not a woman’s chamber in the Godey’s-Lady’s-Book sense. It is a shared cell in a gathered house.

## Colonial Revival “Shaker style”

Twentieth-century taste wanted Shaker as a cousin of Colonial: peg rails in suburban bedrooms, “Shaker” chairs made in factories, unpainted maple four-posters marketed as Shaker, museum period rooms that under-furnish and over-whiten. The Colonial Revival already loved the naked tester post. It could love the peg rail too. What it could not love without translation was communal sleeping, celibacy, and a law that told you what color to paint the bed.

“Shaker style” in a furniture catalog means tapered legs, tape seats, a mushroom finial, maybe a pegboard headboard. It does not mean 1845 Millennial Laws. It does not mean a thirty-inch-wide green bed on rigid wheels. It does not mean three women in a room with twenty-four pegs. The Revival product is not a lie about joinery — tape seats and ladder-backs are real Shaker types — but it is a lie about the chamber. A couple’s bedroom with a king-size “Shaker” pencil post and a peg rail used for hats is an American private room wearing a communal village’s hardware.

Hancock and Mount Lebanon as museums have had to fight their own gift shops. The honest installation is crowded, colored, and slightly inconvenient to a visitor who wants to photograph emptiness. The Met room, with one bed, is already a compromise. Read the label that says several beds were typical. Then look at the pegs and count.

Andrews-era collecting also pulled objects out of dwelling houses into a connoisseur’s sequence: the “best” chair, the “classic” sewing desk. Beds, being painted and humble, were slower to become trophies. 1981.226 entering the Met in 1981 is late for a masterpiece culture that had already canonized the chair. The bed is the harder object to luxury-collect. It is also the object that makes the retiring room a retiring room.

## Labor, laws, and the unnamed maker

Shaker furniture is often unlabeled. Community production, not a Broad Street ticket. Attributions to a named brother — Isaac Youngs, or a Hancock woodworker known from journals — should be specific or omitted. A “Hancock bed, anonymous Believer, 1840s” is better than a celebrity.

Women’s textile work in the same room — sheets, comfortables, the modest undyed or quietly dyed coverings — is the other half of the bed, again. The Laws police the cloth’s pattern. The wood is the men’s shop only in stereotype; communities varied. What the furniture historian can say without a journal in hand is that the bed was made to be washed around, painted to a rule, and slept in without display.

The later commercial Shaker chair industry, Mount Lebanon’s production for outside sale, is a different economy: objects made to leave. Beds were less an export form. A chair can hang on a New York apartment pegboard. A green wheeled bed wants the room it was sized for. That is why the Met had to buy the room.

## Residual

The wheels do not swivel. That single refusal is the difference between a castered Empire sleigh, which wanders, and a Shaker bed, which tracks. Someone in a dwelling house decided that the floor would be cleaned on a schedule that required the bed to come out in a straight line. The Colonial Revival bedroom that borrows the peg and not the wheel has borrowed the photograph and not the work. Met 1981.226 is still, inconveniently, green.
