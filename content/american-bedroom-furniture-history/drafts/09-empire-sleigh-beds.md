# Empire Sleigh Beds

The Codman House sleigh bed in Lincoln, Massachusetts — Historic New England 1969.3260, bequest of Dorothy S. F. M. Codman — is a French bedstead that has lost its French name. Mahogany and mahogany veneer. French brass or ormolu mounts. Original maple casters. Cherry supports screwed to the side rails to hold the sleeping frame. Rectangular headboard and footboard flanked by half-columns with gilded capitals. One long side concave, one straight. Turned feet. About 1820–35. Forty-one inches high, sixty-nine wide. It was meant to sit against a wall. The concave long side is the show face. The straight side went to the plaster.

That geometry is the type. Head and foot the same height, or near enough that you do not climb into a tester box. Scrolls at the corners that, from the side, read as a sleigh’s runners. American dealers will call it a sleigh bed. The shop bills, when they survive, are more likely to say French bedstead, Grecian bed, or just bedstead. The nickname is later. The form is after 1815, when Empire taste in New York is no longer only Lannuier’s labeled specialty and has become a look other shops can veneer.

## The French bed after the ébéniste

Lannuier died in 1819. The French bedstead did not. New York shops — Phyfe’s among them, Meeks’s, and a long list of unlabeled veneer shops — kept making equal-height chamber beds with scrolled ends and mahogany faces. The crown for hangings becomes optional as stoves spread and as the taste for visible wood grows. Some beds still take a wall-mounted *ciel*. Many do not. The sleigh that museums photograph as a naked scroll is often a textile machine that has been allowed to forget its cloth, same problem as the colonial tester, different silhouette.

Placement stays continental. You do not center this bed on a wall like a four-poster with a footboard toward the door. You run it along the wall. You look at it in elevation, like a sofa. That is why the show veneer is on one long side and why the Codman example bothers to be concave there. A bed that will be seen in plan, from the foot, is a different object. High-post Federal beds still exist; the Met’s Phyfe 2021.109a–vv of 1815–25 is one, posts up, Doric minis on the footboard, possibly the cheap “Mahogany High Post Bedstead” in Phyfe’s 1854 garret inventory. Two bed types, same city, same decades. The sleigh is the one that has accepted the wall.

## Phyfe later Grecian

Duncan Phyfe’s shop does not freeze in 1810. The early New York Grecian furniture — light, reeding, lyre, saber legs — thickens after the mid-1810s. Later Phyfe, and furniture in his manner, takes on more Empire mass: heavier rails, more veneer, gilt brass capitals, carved paws, the “Grecian” couch that is a parlor daybed with one scrolling end. Attributions are a mess. John Hewitt and other contemporaries could supply similar chairs. Early twentieth-century reproductions, Hagen’s “Phyfe’s Antique” among them, confuse the market. The Met TOAH essay on Phyfe and Lannuier is the warning label: a Grecian couch “attributed to Phyfe” is often a New York type.

For beds, the documented Phyfe high-post of 1815–25 shows the shop still willing to carve posts. The later Grecian chamber taste in the Phyfe orbit is the equal-height veneered bed with columns or scrolls, closer to the Codman geometry than to a tester. Conclusive shop labels on sleighs are scarce. Say “New York, Phyfe circle or competitor,” unless a bill or a construction quirk isolated in Peter Kenny’s Phyfe catalog work (the 2011 Met Phyfe exhibition literature) pins it. Do not promote a dealer’s “Phyfe sleigh” without that pin.

Montgomery’s Winterthur Federal catalog still helps for the earlier end of this taste. The later, heavier end — 1825–40 — is less well served by “Federal” as a word. American Empire is the ordinary magazine term. It means mahogany veneer, columns, gilt, scroll, and a willingness to let the wood be the show.

## Veneer as the shop’s speed

The circular veneer saw, which the Met’s Meeks pier-table entry notes as a technology that slices imported wood thinner and wraps it around curves, is the industrial fact under the sleigh’s pretty face. Figured mahogany is expensive. A thin skin of it over pine or tulip poplar is how a New York shop puts flash on a chamber bed without building the whole carcass in board-foot mahogany. Bookmatching on a headboard is a veneer trick: open the flitch so the grain mirrors. Losses at the ears of a scroll are expected. A sleigh with perfect veneer at every curl has often been recut.

Secondary woods: pine, tulip poplar, ash, cherry for slat supports as on the Codman bed. Maple casters. Gilt brass capitals that unscrew. Ormolu mounts that were ordered, not carved. The joinery of the rails is knockdown hardware, same as Lannuier’s, because the bed still has to go up a stair and come apart for a move. Screwed cherry supports on the Codman rails are a particular solution for holding the inner frame; they are not universal. Look for them. Their presence is a construction note, not a date.

Labor in these shops is veneer-room labor as much as bench labor. A man who can lay a curved face without bubbles is as valuable as a carver. The carver still does paws and capitals when the price allows. On cheaper beds the “carving” is a turned half-column glued to a flat board. On still cheaper beds the scroll is a bandsawed profile with a veneer face and no column at all.

## Pillar-and-scroll

Joseph Meeks & Sons’ 1833 broadside — Met 43.15.8, hand-colored lithograph, Endicott & Swett, twenty-five and three-quarter by eighteen and three-eighth inches — is the print that makes the late classical shop visible. Forty-one furniture designs and two drapery sets. Pillars in the French manner. S- and C-scrolls until the scroll is the style. The Met’s Meeks pier table 2001.640a, b, 1829–35, mahogany veneer over pine and ash, marble, glass, is the three-dimensional cousin: exuberant scrolled supports, the look the broadside is selling.

This is not Lannuier. It is what happens when Empire has been in New York long enough to be wholesale. Projecting columns. Scroll profiles a bandsaw can cut. Mahogany or rosewood veneer on a cheap carcass. Stenciled gilt sometimes substituting for mounts. John Hall’s *The Cabinet Maker’s Assistant* (Baltimore, 1840), 198 plates, pushes the same grammar into a how-to for shops that will never see Broad Street. Hall’s ellipse-and-scroll religion is explicit in the plates: S- and C-scrolls on everything, including forms meant to be inexpensive. Pine or tulip poplar, mahogany veneer, bandsaw. Regional shops from Baltimore to Cincinnati can now make a “New York” sleigh without a New York journeyman. The pillar-and-scroll chamber bed is how Empire becomes middle-class.

Cincinnati, Philadelphia, Boston, and later Midwest shops all produce versions. The differences are veneer quality, the plumpness of the scroll, whether the columns are half-round or flat pilasters, whether the feet are turned, paw, or a simple bun. Labels are uncommon. Construction and secondary woods are the regional clues, and they are often inconclusive. A Cincinnati Empire bed can look like a New York one because both shops read Hall or a Meeks-style advertisement.

## What happens to the hangings

Early French bedsteads, Lannuier’s included, assume cloth. The Codman sleigh, twenty years later, may have been dressed or may have been proud of its mounts and veneer. Inventories of the 1820s–40s still list bed furniture — textiles — but the vocabulary is shifting toward the wooden object as the named value. Stoves reduce the need for a cloth box. Reform taste has not yet arrived; Eastlake is a generation away. What arrives first is a visual preference: mahogany grain as the chamber’s luxury, not a festoon.

Mosquito netting in the South will still want height. The sleigh is not a Charleston answer. Southern testers keep their posts (next chapter but one). The sleigh is a northern and mid-Atlantic, heated-house, veneer-shop answer. When it travels south it travels as fashion, not as climate.

## Baltimore, Philadelphia, Cincinnati

New York did not own the Grecian couch, and it did not own the sleigh. Baltimore’s painted and caned furniture of the 1810s–20s — the Finlay shops and their circle — is a different show: paint and gilt rather than a mahogany slab, often lighter in rail. Philadelphia Empire beds can be heavier in the paw and more architectural in the column, as the city’s cabinetmaking still remembered carving hours. Cincinnati, by the 1830s–40s, is a factory river town that can veneer a Hall profile as fast as Grand Rapids will later incise an Eastlake one. Mitchell & Rammelsberg, before they become a Victorian-suite name, sit in a city already shipping western furniture. A Cincinnati sleigh in cherry with mahogany veneer and stenciled gilt is not a failed Phyfe. It is Hall plus a steamboat.

The Currier Museum’s Classical Grecian couch, New York 1820s, unidentified maker, is the attribution warning in three dimensions: restrained veneer, lion-paw and eagle feet, gilt metal paterae on the scrolls, sometimes given to Phyfe without a bill. The couch is not a bed. It is the parlor cousin that trains the eye to read a scroll as Grecian. Chamber sleighs borrow that reading. Dealers then call the bed a couch or the couch a daybed. In a small house the same object may have been both. Inventories that say “grecian couch” in a back chamber are not confused. They are using the shop’s word for a side-facing frame with a tick.

## Regional shops, ordinary beds

Most American Empire sleighs were never in a museum. They are mahogany-veneered twins in county houses, often cut down, often with replaced rails to take a modern mattress, often stripped of mounts. The inner width of 1969.3260 is a period mattress problem: sixty-nine inches overall is not a modern queen. Dealers stretch rails. The honest object keeps the rail length and admits a shorter mattress.

Ash, maple, and cherry sleighs exist where mahogany veneer was too dear. Painted and grained versions exist in the same years as fancy chairs. They are not failures of Empire. They are Empire without the import invoice. A grained sleigh with stenciled gilt is a shop using Hall’s profiles and a painter’s kit.

The sleeping surface remains pre-modern: sacking, slats, a tick. Metal rails come later. A sleigh that feels “comfortable” in a historic house has a later mattress on a later support. Fine. Caption it.

## Contested names

*Sleigh bed* is a collector’s word that also covers later Revival sleighs of 1890–1930, which are not Empire. *Boat bed* and *lit en bateau* are closer to the French type. *Grecian bed* in a New York bill may mean this form or a couch. *French bedstead* is the safest period term when a bill uses it. Magazines that call every scrolled American bed 1820–40 a “Phyfe sleigh” are doing the Hagen trick: one famous shop standing in for a city.

The 1854 Phyfe inventory’s two-dollar garret high-post is a useful insult to our eyes. What a shopkeeper keeps upstairs at the end of a life is not what the Met hangs. The sleigh that survives in a Brahmin house like Codman’s is the other end of the same decades: kept, mounted, photographed, bequeathed. Both are American Empire. Only one is a donor’s bed.

## Residual

After 1815 the American chamber can buy a bed that behaves like a sofa: side to the room, veneer to the eye, casters to the floor. Phyfe’s later Grecian is the expensive New York version of that idea, and even there the high-post is not dead. Pillar-and-scroll, Meeks in 1833 and Hall in 1840, is how the idea gets out of Manhattan in a bandsaw profile. The Codman sleigh still has its maple casters and its one concave show side. That concavity is a map of the room. Someone planned which wall would take the straight edge. The suite has not been invented yet. The photograph that will invent it is still a generation of factories away. The sleigh is already posing.
