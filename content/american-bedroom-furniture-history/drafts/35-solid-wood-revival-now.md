A white oak bed in a small American shop in this century looks, if the shop is paying attention, like a problem of rails and posts. Through-tenons or hooked iron, a headboard that is a panel or a set of slats, a footboard that will take a knee when someone sits to put on socks. The species is often *Quercus alba* or a related commercial white oak, sometimes walnut, sometimes cherry. The mattress is probably foam on slats. The joinery is the part the foam does not buy.

After a long run of particleboard suites and upholstered headboard SKUs, a fraction of the American bedroom trade went back to boards you can name. That return is not the same thing as a live-edge slab on hairpin legs, and not the same thing as a Colonial Revival maple suite with a patriot’s name. It is closer to a Craftsman argument that got a CNC and a website.

## What “solid” is doing

Solid wood, in honest use, means the structural parts are lumber, not a foil face on chips. It does not mean there is no plywood in the drawer bottoms. An eighteenth-century high chest used white pine as secondary and nobody called it a fraud. The fraud is a hang-tag that says solid oak when the rails are oak and the deck is honeycomb. A revival that matters will say which parts are which.

Why go back? Some of it is taste after a generation of identical platforms. Some of it is a distrust of furniture that cannot be repaired. A cam lock in swollen particleboard is not a repair; it is a replacement. A wedged through-tenon can be knocked apart and reassembled. Some of it is the same impulse that sent Gustav Stickley to oak: a wish to see how the thing is made. Bankruptcy still happens. Honesty still does not guarantee a balance sheet. The wish persists.

The woods are regional when the shop is regional. Appalachian and southern oak, walnut from the same hills, cherry, maple. Pacific Northwest shops use different firs and maples. A bed is a good place for quartersawn oak because the posts and rails show figure without needing a carved knee. It is a bad place for a board that has not been dried. A rail is a long member; it will bow. Kiln schedules are the unphotographed half of the revival. A shop that talks species and does not talk moisture is selling a picture.

Amish and Mennonite shops in Pennsylvania, Ohio, Indiana, and elsewhere kept making hardwood bedroom groups through the particleboard decades, often in oak and cherry, often in a suite logic that would not surprise a 1910 catalog: bed, dresser, chest, nightstands. Some of that work is excellent. Some of it is factory habits in a plain coat — dowels, heavy stain, a catalog number by another name. The revival that matters here is not “plain people make real wood.” It is the smaller, mixed shops — secular, church, immigrant, whatever — that treat a bed as a frame first. Credit the benches that kept oak moving. Do not romanticize a sales channel.

Reclaimed timber is another caution. A barn beam can make a handsome post if the powder-post beetle is gone, the checks are pinned, and the metal is out of the saw. It can also make a post that splits along a hidden check the first heating season. A revival that sells “history in the grain” without a metal detector is selling a nail. Furniture-grade reclaim exists. So does fencing.

## Live edge, with a caution

George Nakashima’s slabs — the Conoid line, walnut with a free edge, the spider joinery, the English oak he wrestled into tables — taught a later public to love a board that still remembers the tree. Nakashima was a maker with a philosophy of the cut and a shop in New Hope, Pennsylvania. His beds exist; they are not the internet’s bed. The internet’s bed, after about 2010, is often a slab headboard with bark, an epoxy river, metal legs, a mattress from a box. The live edge is the show. The joint is a bracket.

Bark is not a joint. A live edge on a headboard can be beautiful and still be theater if the rails behind it are steel tubes with no shoulder. The caution is not moral. It is structural. A bed racking under two people is a parallelogram problem, the same one a table sees when a child sits on the corner. Shoulders take that rack. An epoxy vein does not. A shop that puts a live-edge headboard on a well-tenoned frame has used the slab as a panel, which is a legitimate use of a panel. A shop that lets the slab’s irregularity excuse a sloppy rail meeting has used the tree as a distraction.

There is also a materials caution. A waney edge holds pith, sapwood, insect gallery, bark that will detach in a heated room. Nakashima’s better pieces are not unconsidered; the free edge is chosen and stabilized. The 2010s commodity slab is often a kiln afterthought with a router-smoothed high spot and a finish that cannot decide whether it is food-safe oil or a plastic skin. In a bedroom, that finish will take skin oil and a glass ring. Say so before the photograph.

Tropical slabs raise a different paper trail: Lacey Act, CITES when the species is listed, a customs declaration. A revival that only means “thick and brown” will import a problem this series has no duty to launder. Domestic oak and walnut already do the job of a bed.

## CNC and the hand

Computer-numeric cutters cut tenons, mortises, headboard slats, a curved rail if the file says so. They are faster than a backsaw and, when the bits are sharp and the fence is true, repeatable. They do not close a shoulder. Dry-fit is still the inspection. I have had a CNC tenon come off the machine a hair fat on one cheek, the same as a tablesaw tenon, and the same shoulder plane finished the argument. The machine is a millwright. It is not a conscience.

Handwork that still earns its keep on a bed: paring shoulders, fitting a drawer if the bed has storage, scraping a finish, chopping a mortise that a hollow-chisel left with a rounded end, seating a hook plate so the rail does not rattle. Dovetails on a drawer are a choice. They are not required for the bed to stand. They are required if the bed is also a case and you want the drawer to last. A revival that puts hand-cut tails on a headboard panel and dowels in the rail has spent the theater budget in the wrong place.

The honest hybrid is common and fine: CNC for the repeatable cheeks, hand for the fit, a machine mortiser, a human with a mallet. Greene and Greene’s cloud lifts were not CNC; they were a shop’s time. A 2020s shop that CNC-cuts a cloud lift and then pegs it with ebony is quoting Pasadena, not continuing it. Quotation is allowed if the joint still works. Pretending the file is a 1909 hand is not.

Internet custom, from the 1990s bulletin boards through Etsy into whatever storefront follows, changed who could find a shop. A household in a city without a furniture plant could order a walnut bed from a maker two states away. Freight then became the design constraint: knockdown rails, a headboard that ships on edge, a weight limit. The revival is partly a shipping revival. Chapter 39’s hooks and bolts are not antiquarian in this trade. They are how a 250-pound oak frame goes up a walk-up. Shops that refuse to knock down are shops that sell to ranch houses with a garage.

Finish fashions followed the tabletops: hardwax oils, conversion varnish, a wipe of linseed that will never last on a footboard. A bed takes skin, a vacuum bump, a suitcase. Oil that looks alive in a photograph will look thirsty in March. A film finish that looks plastic in the same photograph will still be there when the mattress is replaced. Pick. Do not tell the customer both are maintenance-free.

## What the revival is not

It is not a return to the tester. Heat is still in the house. Hangings, if they appear, are hotel-style drapery behind a platform, not a cloth box. It is not a return to the Grand Rapids suite, though some shops will match a bed to a dresser in the same oak. A matching finish is still a sales idea (chapter 40). Solid wood does not purify it.

It is not Arts and Crafts except where it copies Arts and Crafts. Stickley’s oak, the Roycroft, Greene and Greene, the Cotswold shops — those were arguments about factory sham, some of them also catalogs. A twenty-first-century bed with a through-tenon is in conversation with that oak. A twenty-first-century bed with a dark stain and a mission hardware pack from a catalog is a costume. Both exist. The critic’s job is to look at the shoulder, not the copy.

It is not, except at the luxury end, a replacement for MALM. Price and freight still rule most rooms. The revival is a parallel trade: fewer pieces, heavier, sold to households that already have a foam mattress and want the wooden half to outlast the foam. That is a reasonable wish. It will be tested when the mattress is the third replacement and the rails have been knocked down for a move. Beds that come apart on purpose (chapter 39) belong to this revival if the hardware is sized. Beds that can only be moved as a welded idea do not belong in a country that moves.

## Shop facts that are not a brand

A rail should be thick enough that a tenon can be a third of it and still leave meat. A post should be thick enough that a mortise does not make a box of veneer. Bed bolts should have a clean hole and a cap if you want the nineteenth-century look; hooks should have a plate that cannot tear out of end grain. Glue is a guest. A pin is an admission that glue is a guest. Southern humidity will swell a tenon in August and shrink it in January; the mortise walls need to survive both. None of this is new. It is why a revival that only photographs grain is incomplete.

Secondary woods still tell the truth. A walnut bed with poplar slats and a plywood deck is a reasonable bed. A walnut-stained poplar bed with a walnut-looking headboard of veneered MDF is the old sham in a new font. Look at the end grain of a rail. If there is no end grain, you are not looking at a rail. You are looking at a wrapped edge. The revival’s useful habit, borrowed from the conservators and the old shops, is to name the secondary. Pine, poplar, maple for slats. Say it on the invoice. The customer who cares already knows from the high chest literature that interiors can be a different species. The customer who does not care will at least have been told.

The residual fact is a fingernail. If you can feel a gap at the shoulder on day one, the CNC file was not the joint. The live edge will not hide it in ten years. The American bedroom can have a named hardwood bed again. The test is the same as it was for a table apron: the shoulders close, the rack is answered, the mattress — foam or feather — is a load, not the object. A slab is a board. A bed is a frame.

- USDA Forest Products Laboratory, *Wood Handbook* (FPL-GTR-190 / GTR-282), for oak, walnut, cherry movement and moisture — shop practice, not a brand.
- Mira Nakashima and the New Hope literature on George Nakashima’s slabs; do not treat commodity “live edge” beds as the same shop.
