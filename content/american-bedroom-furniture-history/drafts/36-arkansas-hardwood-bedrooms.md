# Arkansas Hardwood Bedrooms

On 6 August 1894, Edward Ballman, Charles Cummings, and W. J. Johnston opened Ballman-Cummings Furniture Co. in Fort Smith, Arkansas. Chamber suites, dressers, chiffoniers, sideboards, desks. The *Southwest Times Record* still treats that Tuesday as a city date. The Fort Smith Historical Society’s *Journal* — the March 2007 Ballman biography, and Matthew Myers’s later piece in the April 2014 issue — is the local spine. This is not a lost civilization. It is a river town that made bedroom furniture because the hardwood was inland and the rails were in.

The American chamber suite, by 1894, was already a Grand Rapids idea: a numbered set, a finish, a photograph. Fort Smith did not invent it. Fort Smith made it west of the clusters that dominate the national numbers, in oak and walnut and the southern mix, for a market that included the border and the territories. A history of American bedroom furniture that only runs Boston–Philadelphia–Grand Rapids–High Point has skipped a hardwood hinterland that shipped both lumber and finished cases.

## Indiana machines on the Arkansas

Furniture making in Fort Smith predates Ballman. Fredrick W. Meier and C. Erhardt made furniture and caskets and repaired pieces in 1867–69. The Birnie brothers bought a shop from Meier and Erhardt in 1869. Meier joined William Ott, Charles Kade, and F. Schaidt in Ott, Meier & Co. in 1877. Ott brought machinery from an Indiana plant. In 1878 Ballman, a wood turner with a public-school education, came south from Indianapolis with Ott’s crew.

The first company did not prosper. Alex H. Reynolds bought it about 1880. About a year later Jacob C. Huff, Meier, and Ballman bought it again. In 1885 they moved north of town onto the Arkansas River when a lease ran out. Huff drowned crossing the river in a leaky boat. Meier and Ballman had to buy his interest from an Indiana estate and went into debt. About 1891 Meier retired sick and sold to Ballman. The name had already become E. Ballman & Co. in the mid-1880s. Myers notes walnut chairs from wood cut at a large Fort Smith sawmill.

Ballman-Cummings in 1894 is the bedroom sentence. Chamber suites: bed, dresser, chiffonier — the tall chest of the factory era — maybe a commode or a dressing table, the finish matched, the posts factory-turned. The *Fort Smith Elevator* reported on 8 October 1897 that employees were working past dark to ship. That is a plant, not a bench. Ballman was president and owned half the stock. He did not stop at suites. William J. Kropp came from St. Louis to help open the Fort Smith Folding Bed and Table Co. in 1902. Folding beds are a bedroom type of a specific decade: the bed that pretends to be a wardrobe, the chamber that has to be a parlor by day. In October 1900 Ballman and his brother Emil bought Perfection Bedding from Ed Stable for mattresses and pillows. In 1904, Fort Smith Couch and Bedding. The *Arkansas Democrat* recorded a gold panic that cancelled orders just as the couch company filled its first mixed-car-load. Border Queen Kitchen Cabinet Co., 1906. Fort Smith Metal Products, 1917, among other things bed springs. A glass-and-mirror shop became Porter Mirror and Glass, then Willard Mirror. In 1921, a cooperative sales arm, Fort Smith Furniture Manufacturers Co. Odie Faulk wrote that Ballman served as president of these companies at one time or another. He was still president of the manufacturers’ company when he died in 1923, aged sixty-five.

The list is a Victorian and early-twentieth-century bedroom taken apart into specialized factories on a river: the wood half, the spring half, the mattress half, the mirror, the folding type that denies the bed by day. Grand Rapids had the same split at a larger scale. Fort Smith’s claim, in local writing, to have been a furniture center “west of the Mississippi” is a chamber-of-commerce sentence that happens to match a cluster of plants. Other clusters were larger. Arkansas’s interest for this series is the hardwood itself and the fact that chamber suites were a local product, not only an import from Michigan.

Labor on those floors is still thinner in the published Fort Smith pieces than a full history would need. Turners like Ballman, finishers, upholsterers, women in sewing rooms, Black workers in a border-state industry that was not separate from Jim Crow. The machines Ott brought from Indiana were already a statement: this is a plant. `[CITE NEEDED: union records or city directories broken down by occupation and race for Fort Smith furniture, 1900–1940.]`

*Pratt v. Ballman-Cummings Furniture Co.*, Arkansas Supreme Court, 1973, shows the company still in court in the Ayers Furniture Industries years. That is an afterlife, not an advertisement.

## Warren cut and shipped

The *Encyclopedia of Arkansas* “Timber Industry” entry (George W. Balogh) maps the woods and the northern money. Samuel Fordyce’s Cotton Belt opened the mainline. Once the rails were in, Illinois and Iowa capital acquired south Arkansas timber. Moses Franklin Rittenhouse and John William Embree established the Arkansas Lumber Company at Warren, Bradley County, in 1901. Frederick E. Weyerhaeuser founded the Southern Lumber Company in Warren in 1902. Watzek, Gates, and Crossett founded Fordyce Lumber (1892) and Crossett Lumber (1899). Corlis C. Curry’s 1952 University of Arkansas thesis on the timber industry in Ashley, Bradley, and Drew Counties remains the local monograph. Kenneth L. Smith’s *Sawmill* (1986) is the book on cutting the last great virgin forest east of the Rockies. Don C. Bragg and Hope A. Bragg’s Saline-Fifteen work is the witness-tree ecology: a bottomland world of oaks that a later shop will still want to name.

Warren’s mills were not Ballman-Cummings. They cut and shipped. Arkansas Lumber, the Rittenhouse and Embree concern, owned tens of thousands of acres near Warren and operated a little over twenty-five years, going out in 1928; local accounts put much of the land afterward with Murphy-McKinney interests from the El Dorado oil money. The Warren and Ouachita Valley Railway was a logging line, jointly owned by Southern and Arkansas lumber companies, Warren to Banks and a Rock Island connection. That is how a bedroom in St. Louis or Dallas could be oak that had been a tree in Bradley County without ever having been a Fort Smith suite. Boards travel farther than chiffoniers. Furniture history that only follows finished cases will miss the county that dried the lumber.

Potlatch later had a large lumber operation in Warren and kraft mills elsewhere in the state. The date of a mid-century purchase, and the 2008 date sometimes attached to a later furniture company in this geography, stay marked until a primary document is cited: `[CITE NEEDED: Potlatch purchase of Warren-area operations; 2008 furniture-company date.]` Do not tidy those years with a story.

Hardwood towns and pine towns overlap on a map and are not the same time clock. Crossett became a company town in pine, then a paper-and-chemical complex, then a Georgia-Pacific successor after 1962. “Cut out and get out” until the 1920s; in 1926 E. W. “Cap” Gates asked Yale’s H. H. Chapman about sustained yield. Crossett’s strikes of 1940 and 1985 (Timothy J. Minchin on 1985) are paper-mill labor. They should not be borrowed as if they were Ballman’s finish room. A chair factory needs dry kilns, turners, finish rooms, and a catalog. A sawmill needs a spur and a crew that can be hurt. Both are furniture history. Only one makes a chiffonier.

The *Encyclopedia*’s forest map still holds: Ozark mixed pine and hardwood; Ouachita pine on the slopes and hardwood in the valleys; southern yellow pine on sandy hills; bottomland hardwood along the rivers. A bedroom in oak from the Saline bottoms is a different object from a bedroom in pine from a paper town, even if both are “Arkansas wood” on a hang-tag. Species is a joint’s business. Pine makes a good secondary and a good crate. Oak takes a shoulder.

What did a Fort Smith chamber suite look like on the floor? The published illustrations are factory pictures: a bed with turned or square posts, a dresser with a framed mirror, a tall chest, sometimes a commode. Oak in the first decades, then the cheaper woods and veneers the whole American trade learned. Colonial revival would arrive here as it arrived in Grand Rapids, a later finish on a factory post. The customer was not buying a Winterthur argument. They were buying a room that matched, at a price a railroad town could pay, in wood that had not crossed an ocean. Jennifer Anderson’s *Mahogany* is the Atlantic luxury story. This chapter is the inland answer: not no luxury, a different luxury, local hardwood dressed as a national type.

## What a chamber suite meant in Fort Smith

A Ballman-Cummings chamber suite in oak or walnut was the stuffed room of chapter 31’s ancestor: matched finish, factory posts, a dresser with a mirror that the glass shop might have supplied, a tall chest for the clothes the closet had not yet eaten. It was not a highboy. It was not a tester with hangings. It was a sales idea with Arkansas lumber in it. Customers in the border towns and the territories bought a photograph they already knew from the national catalogs, executed closer to the trees.

Folding beds and kitchen cabinets in the same entrepreneurial cluster show the house rearranging. The bedroom had to vanish by day in a small dwelling; the kitchen was becoming a room with branded cabinets. Springs and mattresses in separate companies show the textile-and-metal half pulling away from the cabinetmaker, the same split that would later let a foam carton ignore the frame (chapter 34). Fort Smith ran that split in miniature, with named plants.

The 2014 *Journal* cover used an E. Ballman & Co. illustration from the Pebley Center, UAFS. The Hardwood Tree Museum Collection there holds later interviews (Flanders, Hamersley, Swofford) that run the story past Ballman’s death into mid-century plants. Use them as oral history, not as a catalog.

## A shop that still names oak

Twentieth-century Arkansas furniture follows the national line: Depression closures, war work, competition with cheaper production elsewhere, a remainder of shops that still know kiln-dried oak from a catalog photograph of oak. Some plants died. Some towns kept a finishing room. The live edge and the CNC of chapter 35 exist here too, in smaller shops that never saw Milan.

Fullerton’s Bradley Lumber is a mill story, not a furniture line. Warren cut boards; Crossett made pine and paper; Fort Smith made chamber suites. Keep those clocks separate. The hang-tag temptation is to blur them into one “heritage” sentence when the core is chips.

Fort Smith made chamber suites. Warren cut timber. Crossett made pine and paper. A shop that still names oak is practicing the oldest fact in this series: a joint holds if the wood was dry and the shop was paying attention. Bradley Brand’s Arkansas bench is one shop in that continuity: hardwood, a climate, a bed rail that should admit the year it was cut. One sentence is enough. The rest is chamber history.
