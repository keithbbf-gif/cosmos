# Boston and the Early High Chest

Met 40.37.3 stands sixty-two and a half inches in Gallery 713: japanned soft maple, poplar, and white pine, thirty-nine and a half by twenty-one and a quarter inches in plan, Boston, 1710–30. Purchase, Joseph Pulitzer Bequest, 1940. It descended in the Pickman family of Boston and Salem. The ground is a solid black. Raised, gilded figures — people, animals, oversize flowers, tiny pavilions — sit on it in a relaxed undulating pattern that never left a New England shop. The legs are turned. Stretchers keep the stance from racking. Collectors say *highboy*. The shop said high chest of drawers, or billed the case and let the japanner finish the sentence.

A high chest of drawers is a chest on a frame, or a chest on a chest, depending on how the shop built the lower case. The form is English. The American reading that matters first is Boston’s, because Boston had the port, the japanners, the walnut, and then the mahogany, and because the surviving early examples cluster there and in Salem before Philadelphia makes the type theatrical.

40.37.3 is one of the earliest American japanned high chests the Met will stand in a gallery. The motifs are a Western imitation of lacquer, fashionable in the colonies after 1700. When the surface was new it must have been dazzling. What you see now is lampblack, gesso, and gilt that have already been through a century of conservation. Call it japanned. Do not call it Chinese.

Brock Jobe and Myrna Kaye’s *New England Furniture: The Colonial Era* and Benno Forman’s work on the chest of drawers as a London-derived stack remain the shelf. Morrison Heckscher’s Met catalogs treat the later Boston japanned cases — 40.37.1 among them, 1730–40, also Pickman — as the Queen Anne continuation of the same trade.

## Why stack

A joined chest has a lid. A chest of drawers has a face you can open without moving the looking glass. Stack the drawers high enough and you store a household’s linen in the footprint of a table. In a chamber that is still sharing space with sitting and receiving, vertical storage is not a style. It is an answer to a small floor.

The lower case can be a frame with one long drawer and turned or cabriole legs — the form that later pairs with a dressing table — or a second stack of drawers. Early Boston examples often sit on turned legs with stretchers, a William and Mary stance. Queen Anne cabriole legs arrive as the century’s second quarter. The change is not a clean year. Shops kept both.

Brasses are maps. Early drop handles and engraved plates give way to bails. A replaced brass row is the most common lie on an otherwise honest case. Heckscher’s Met catalogs and the Winterthur furniture files treat hardware as a dating problem, not as jewelry.

## Japanning as a Boston surface

Boston japanners — the shops associated with names like William Randle and the later literature around *Boston Furniture of the Eighteenth Century* (Brock Jobe, ed.) — covered pine and maple cases with lampblack, gesso, and gilt chinoiserie because walnut veneer was not the only way to look expensive. The figures chip. The ground craze. What survives in museums is a fraction, and it has usually been restored.

Met 10.125.58 is the later Boston sentence in the same wing: high chest, ca. 1747, japanned maple and white pine, seventy and a quarter by thirty-nine and five-eighths by twenty-one inches, Sage gift, 1909. Tortoiseshell ground — lampblack streaked over vermillion — with a dragon and winged swans pulling a chariot. Its matching dressing table is 10.125.68. The Met’s object page is willing to call the pair well preserved and to distinguish the decoration from the Pickman suite (40.37.1, .2, .4). A pair. Still not a five-piece suite. The bed in that chamber was still cloth.

Do not collapse 40.37.3 and 10.125.58 into “the Met japanned highboy.” One is a black-ground William and Mary stack on turned legs. The other is a tortoiseshell Queen Anne case that already thinks it has a mate.

## Salem and the North Shore

Salem shops in the same decades made walnut and maple cases with a quieter face: less gilt, more banding, a provincial Boston. The distinction is real and also overdrawn. Journeymen moved. Timber moved. A “Boston” high chest can have been finished in a town that used Boston brasses and Boston habits. Attribution to a named maker on an unlabeled early case is a wish unless a bill, a label, or a construction quirk that Moses and others have isolated is present.

Construction quirks that do travel in the literature: drawer bottoms with the grain running side to side or front to back by shop habit; secondary woods (white pine in New England, almost a rule); dovetail size and angle; the way the upper case sits on the lower — a molding that hides the joint, or a visible waist. Conservators date with those, not with the silhouette alone.

## What the form is not yet

It is not yet the Philadelphia rococo monument of Met 18.110.4. It is not yet Newport’s block-and-shell. It is not a suite. A matching dressing table may exist in the same house; it is not sold as a boxed set. The bed in the same chamber is still a textile machine. The high chest is the wooden object that later Americans will remember as “the bedroom antique” when they have forgotten the hangings.

That memory is already forming in the nineteenth century, when Colonial Revival taste pulls high chests out of farm attics and into front halls. Wallace Nutting and the collectors of the 1920s will treat the Boston japanned case as a prize and the ordinary maple stack as a lesser cousin. The ordinary maple stack is closer to how most people stored.

## Labor in the early shop

A high chest is many drawers. Each drawer is a box: sides, front, back, bottom, runners. A journeyman who can cut consistent dovetails is the shop’s speed. An apprentice who cannot is the shop’s waste. Enslaved and free Black workmen in northern ports are documented in other trades more thickly than in Boston cabinet shops of 1720; the gap is not proof of absence. `[CITE NEEDED: named workmen of color in Boston or Salem cabinet shops before 1750, if the directories or bills yield any.]` The japanner is a specialist. The turner who made the early legs may be another shop.

Before the high chest, the chamber’s storage was a lid you lifted. After it, the chamber has a facade: a wall of drawers that faces the bed. The suite will come later and try to make the whole room match that facade. Boston only needed the stack.

## Walnut, maple, and the first mahogany

New England walnut is not Philadelphia walnut in quantity. Boston shops used what the coast and the coastal trade gave: local walnut where it existed, maple that could be stained, pine that could be japanned, and then, as the century moved, mahogany that had already been a Boston merchant’s cargo. The high chest is one of the first American case forms to show that shift on a single tall body: an early walnut example and a later mahogany example can share a drawer stack and not share a forest.

Veneer appears early on the better cases — herringbone borders, crossbanding, a flat top that is a field for figure. Solid maple stacks for a lesser market keep going. The later collector’s eye that only wants mahogany has erased most of the maple rooms. Those rooms were the ordinary chamber.

## How a high chest enters a house

Marriage, a father’s gift, a cabinetmaker’s debt paid in kind, a used piece from a deceased neighbor. The high chest is expensive enough to be mentioned and portable enough to move when a family leaves a house. Unlike a tester bed, it does not depend on a textile crew to make sense. That is one reason it survives in museums at a higher rate than hung beds. Wood outlasts stuffs. The survival rate is not a measure of how the chamber looked. It is a measure of what did not rot.

When the form reaches Philadelphia and Newport, the stack is already understood. Those cities will add carving and blocking. They will not invent vertical drawers. Boston did the American translation of a London chest of drawers into a tall chamber object. The rest is dialect.

## Coit’s label, Randle’s ground, and what gilt costs

A paper label is the rare Boston voice. Job Coit and Job Coit Jr. labeled a chest of drawers in 1738 — a case, not always a high chest, and the distinction matters — and the Boston literature has treated that label as a stake in a town that usually did not sign. Jobe and Kaye, and the Colonial Society’s Boston furniture volume, are the place to confirm where the labeled Coit case sits now before a caption names a museum. `[CITE NEEDED: present location and accession of the 1738 Coit labeled chest of drawers.]` What the label is good for is a date and a shop practice: Boston case work in the 1730s was already a stack of drawers with a written name on the inside, not a folk object waiting for Nutting.

William Randle advertised japanning in Boston in the early decades of the century: chests of drawers, looking glasses, the London taste in lampblack and gilt. Robert Davis, another Boston japanner, left tools in an estate. The high chest was one of the bodies they covered. The process is not mysterious. Size the pine or maple. A black ground. Raised figures in gesso. Gold leaf and bronze powders. Varnish. Robert Dossie’s *Handmaid to the Arts* is the English recipe book American shops could read. What survives in a gallery has usually been re-gilt. Conservators at MFA and Winterthur write the percentages; a caption that says “original japanning” without that file is guessing. Chipstone’s later reading of 40.37.1 treats that Pickman case as one of the better-preserved grounds. 40.37.3 is earlier and more worn. Both are Boston looking toward a fantasy East, in a port that already knew the real Indies as cargo.

The gilt does not change the drawers. It changes who the case is performing for. A maple stack in the same years held the same shirts. The japanned case is expensive surface, cheap carcass, Atlantic taste, local wood.

## Trumpet legs, stretchers, and the waist you can take apart

Early Boston high chests stand on turned legs — trumpet or cup turnings, sometimes a Spanish foot — with stretchers that keep the stance from racking. That is a William and Mary sentence. The turner may be a different shop from the case maker. You can see it in the way a leg’s shoulder meets the case: a joint, not a carved cabriole growing out of a stile. When the cabriole arrives in the second quarter, the stretcher often goes. The pad foot and then the slipper foot are dating arguments, not a clean calendar. Shops kept cutting both for a while.

The waist is how the object climbs a stair. The upper case is a box. The lower case is a frame or a short stack. A molding hides the meeting. Take the drawers out, lift the upper case, walk it up. This is not a museum trick. It is the delivery. A high chest that has had the waist nailed shut by a later owner is a piece that stopped traveling.

Drawer construction is the other Boston sentence. White pine almost as a rule for bottoms, sides, backs. Dovetails that a journeyman could repeat all day. Bottoms whose grain runs side to side or front to back by shop habit — the literature argues, and a single chest is not a law. Runners glued to the case. Early cases may lack the full dustboard that later Philadelphia work likes. You date with that interior, not with a replaced bail. A new brass row is the common lie. The shadow of an old plate, if it is there, is the evidence.

Veneer on the better walnut cases — herringbone borders, crossbanding, a burl field on a drawer — is London practice read in a Boston yard. Solid maple for a lesser market kept going and has been dumped from the collector’s room. Those maple rooms were the ordinary stack. The japanned and walnut survivors are the loud minority.

## A bonnet before Philadelphia’s scroll

Boston cut a bonnet top on high chests while Philadelphia was still, in many shops, a flat cornice or a quieter arch. A tympanum, two or three finials, a shell or palmette that is Queen Anne rather than Johnson’s swan. Do not call it Chippendale. It is not 18.110.4. It is a Boston answer to a tall problem: the stack wanted a head. Salem and the North Shore followed, sometimes plainer. Journeymen moved with the habit.

A 1730s Boston case can still be standing in a 1790s chamber next to a newer bed. The high chest did not need an upholsterer to make sense. Wood outlasts stuffs. The survival rate is a rot report, not a picture of the room.

Named workmen of color in Boston cabinet shops before 1750 remain a gap. The japanner’s shop, the turner’s shop, and the case shop were not always one door. Labor is split even when the label is a single English name.

## Residual

Boston built the facade early, in pine and gilt and walnut, and left Philadelphia and Newport to argue about carving and blocking on a type the port had already understood. Met 40.37.3 is sixty-two inches of that first American stack: turned legs, stretchers, a black ground that never saw Asia, drawers that still hold the idea of a chamber with a vertical address. The suite will try to make a whole room match that facade. Boston only needed the stack.
