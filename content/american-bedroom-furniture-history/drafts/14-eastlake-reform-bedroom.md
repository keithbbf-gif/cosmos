# The Eastlake Reform Bedroom

Charles Locke Eastlake’s *Hints on Household Taste in Furniture, Upholstery, and Other Details* appeared in London in 1868. American editions followed from 1872 and went through six printings in eleven years. Eastlake was an architect and a taste writer, not a cabinetmaker. He did not have a factory in Grand Rapids. He had opinions about bedrooms, dust, honest construction, and the fashionable upholsterer’s habit of rounding every corner that construction wanted square.

In the bed-room pages he tells readers not to buy “shaped” chests of drawers — those that bulge in front — or veneered work if they can help it. Find a place where mahogany furniture is made in quantity, he says, and order it in solid wood, rubbed with linseed oil, or, if it must be stained, stained black before it is polished. White-metal drawer rings from a “mediaeval ironmonger,” attached to ebonised wood. The hanging-press — the wardrobe — should not have its corners rounded off, its panels forced into extravagant curves, strips of paltry scroll-work glued on, a heavy uninteresting cornice, the whole lacquered in French polish. The money spent on that sham would pay for a sound oak or mahogany wardrobe whose proportions and a few mouldings might be actually good. Increase of cost, he says, can enrich furniture; it cannot invent good design. Paraphrase him that closely and you are still in his argument. The American factory bedroom that took his name often did the opposite: veneer, machine-incised “honesty,” glued-on brackets, a suite photograph.

He noticed. American tradesmen, he wrote later, continually advertised what they were pleased to call Eastlake furniture, “the production of which I have had nothing whatever to do, and for the taste of which I should be very sorry to be considered responsible.” `[CITE NEEDED: exact edition and page of this disclaimer — widely quoted in the Eastlake-movement literature; lock it to a first-hand page before treating the wording as courtroom evidence.]` The disclaimer is the residual of this chapter whether or not we have the page in this draft. The name sold. The author walked away from the goods.

## What the English book wanted in a chamber

Eastlake’s bedroom is a hygiene and construction brief before it is a style. Dust and “blacks” collect on shaped surfaces and on needless cavities. Hangings that pool on the floor are dirt traps. A bed that is a textile palace is hard to clean. The reform interior of the 1860s–70s, overlapping with early sanitation writing and with the English Arts and Crafts moral tone (Ruskin, Morris — Eastlake is not Morris, but American readers stacked them), wants furniture you can wipe.

Angular, notched, honestly joined. Incised ornament rather than applied rococo. Oak and cherry grain shown, not buried in black varnish — though he allows ebonising as a deliberate stain on solid wood, which is not the same as a piano finish on cheap walnut. Metals: simple rings and plates, medievalizing, brass or white metal, not rococo bail handles. The bed: lower, wipeable, not a tester box. The wardrobe: square, serviceable, a “hanging-press” with a right to look like a box.

This is a middle-class argument. He wanted the prices “as cheap as that which is ugly,” a thought the later literature repeats. `[CITE NEEDED: page for the cheap-as-ugly remark.]` English readers could still hire a joiner. American readers, in bulk, would buy Grand Rapids.

American publishers sold *Hints* the way they sold any English taste book: as a manual for wives who were being told they had a new professional duty, the home. Catharine Beecher and Harriet Beecher Stowe’s *The American Woman’s Home* (1869) is the domestic-science cousin, harder on ventilation and plumbing than on incised lines, but in the same decade’s argument that a bedroom should not be a drapery warehouse. Eastlake supplies the furniture grammar. The Beechers supply the air. Factories supply the set.

English Eastlake and Morris interiors still assume a joiner you can instruct. American readers, past a certain income, assume a retailer. That is why the disclaimer was inevitable. A man who wrote for people who ordered from shops cannot control shops that order from Grand Rapids.

## What American factories made

Herter Brothers in New York could read Eastlake and the related Anglo-Japanism and produce ebonized, incised, inlaid chamber and parlor furniture at a custom price. That is one American Eastlake. It is not the one that furnished most bedrooms.

The other is factory Eastlake, roughly 1875–1885, Grand Rapids and its peers: rectilinear cases, incised linear decoration, turned galleries, brackets, trestles, burl panels still in play because the plants already had veneer presses, brass pulls, sometimes ebonized finishes and gilt incising, sometimes simulated bamboo. Marvin D. Schwartz’s line, often quoted, is that Eastlake forms were strongly rectilinear, with geometric ornaments, turnings, brackets, trestles, and incised linear decoration — all easily executed with machines. The machine is the tell. English reform wanted the look of a proud workman. American “Eastlake” wanted a cutter that could repeat a groove down a dresser splash and a headboard in the same shift.

Berkey & Gay, Phoenix, and the rest added Eastlake to the suite grammar of the previous chapters. You did not have to believe in dust theory. You had to believe in a numbered set with incised lines. Witherell’s Berkey & Gay plates include the style among Gothic and Renaissance. A chamber suite in “Eastlake” is still a suite: bed, dresser, washstand, matching brass, matching galleries. Eastlake the writer had attacked matching fashion as the upholsterer’s tyranny. Eastlake the trade word became a way to match.

Ebonizing on American factory pieces is often a black stain over walnut or maple, not Eastlake’s solid mahogany dyed and oil-rubbed. Brass is stamped. Incising is routed. “Oriental” touches — fret, gilt, simulated bamboo — are Anglo-Japanism at wholesale, the same years as the 1876 Centennial’s Japanese displays. Eastlake’s book had plates; manufacturers used the plates as a motif source and ignored the paragraphs about not gluing on paltry scroll-work. They glued on brackets.

Cottage furniture, the cheaper painted and incised bedroom sets, rides alongside. Not every incised American dresser is a Grand Rapids walnut suite. Some are spare pine, factory-painted, sold through mail order. They may be closer to Eastlake’s price argument than the burl-and-brass sets that kept the Victorian mass.

Drawers in an Eastlake suite still have to run. Factory runners, pine sides, a face of walnut with an incised border that does not wrap into the interior. Open the drawer: the reform stops at the lip. Secondary woods are the same Grand Rapids mix as a Renaissance Revival case from the same mill — because it is the same mill. A shop can change a cutter and a catalog name without changing a dovetail machine.

Hardware catalogs of the 1870s–80s sell “Eastlake” pulls as a separate product. A small-town joiner can order the brass and incise a pine dresser and meet the look without Berkey. That is how a style becomes a hardware finish. Replaced pulls on a surviving suite are as common as replaced Chippendale plates. The shadow of a rectangular Eastlake plate is a dating friend.

## Dust, sanitation, and the death of hangings

The reform bedroom and the hospital metal bed (next chapter) share an enemy: the textile box. Dust, vermin, laundry. Eastlake’s shaped-chest warning is a finger-run-along-the-molding argument. Sanitation writers in the 1870s–90s will say the same about canopy drapery. A tester that cannot be boiled is a medical problem. Brass and iron beds will claim washability. Eastlake’s wooden bed claims wipeability and less drapery.

American households did not throw away their suites. They bought Eastlake suites with less cloth. Headboards stayed high — factories like a vertical photograph — but testers became rarer in the catalog page. Washstands still matched, marble still caught water, the chamber was still a set. Reform as a trade style is always partial. The dust argument sold a look. It did not empty the factories.

Women’s labor is the unspoken hygiene. Someone still dusts the incised lines. Incising holds dirt in a groove if you do not attend to it; the “easy to clean” claim of later Eastlake-movement summaries is relative to rococo undercut carving, not relative to a Shaker painted rail. A gallery of turned spindles on a dresser top is a dust trap with a reform name. Say so. Eastlake’s own hanging-press complaint was about sham curves, not about whether a housekeeper had time.

## Brass, ebonizing, incised lines — the toolkit

Incised lines: shallow grooves, often in geometric borders, sometimes filled with gilt. A router or a carving machine. On better pieces, a bit of actual drawing; on worse, a repeating border that does not resolve at the miter.

Ebonizing: black stain, then polish. American factories liked it for “Japanese” and for contrast with gilt and brass. Eastlake allowed black stain on solid wood. He did not ask for a black piano case with machine gold.

Brass: pulls, escutcheons, sometimes galleries. Medievalizing rings if the maker read the book; stamped rococo-Eastlake hybrids if not. Original brass on an Eastlake suite is as much a dating and honesty problem as Chippendale plates. Replaced Colonial Revival knobs on an 1880 dresser are a common mess.

Woods: walnut still dominates the better factory suites. Oak, which Eastlake liked, comes on stronger toward the 1890s golden-oak wave, which is not exactly Eastlake. Cherry. Maple, ebonized. Ash, cheaper. Mahogany, when they can stain it into the price.

Joinery: machine dovetails appear in this period depending on grade. Dustboards sometimes. Backboards often unfinished pine, as on any factory case. The reform is in the face. The box is still a Grand Rapids box.

Kimbel and Cabus showed ebonized, incised, gilt-highlighted furniture at the same 1876 Centennial where Berkey & Gay showed Neo-Grec chamber suites. Two reform languages under one roof: New York custom Anglo-Japanism, and Michigan wholesale classicism. American “Eastlake” in the following decade borrows more from the Kimbel look — ebonizing, incising, a little gilt — than from Eastlake’s oil-rubbed mahogany paragraph, and then puts it on Berkey’s suite skeleton. The fair did not make anyone choose.

A factory Eastlake headboard is often a rectangular frame with a burl or incised panel, a gallery of turned spindles, and posts that stop at a height a mattress photograph likes. It is wipeable compared with a full tester. It is not wipeable compared with Lawson’s iron hospital bed. Reform is a sliding scale sold as a name.

## Herter, and the custom exception

A Herter ebonized and inlaid bed or cabinet, 1870s–80s, is American reform furniture that can stand next to the English argument without embarrassment. Incising, Anglo-Japanism, quality. The Met’s Herter holdings are parlor and cabinet more than a standard chamber suite; do not force a bedroom set. `[CITE NEEDED: a specific Herter bed or chamber piece with accession if used as a figure.]` The point of naming Herter is only to keep “American Eastlake” from meaning exclusively the factory. Two trades ran at once. The factory won on count.

Kimbel and Cabus in New York, the 1876 Centennial, ebonized furniture with incised gilt — another custom-or-small-shop pole. Grand Rapids looked at the same fair and showed Neo-Grec chamber suites. Both are 1876. Only one is Eastlake’s nightmare of his name on a trade label.

## Residual

The English book wanted a bedroom you could wipe, a wardrobe that admitted it was a box, and metal rings on wood that had not been French-polished into a lie. The American factory wanted a new groove for the same suite it was already photographing. Incised lines, ebonizing, and brass became the look of reform without the inconvenience of unmatching the set. Eastlake’s disclaimer — if we honor the widely printed wording — is a man watching his surname become a SKU. The dust did not lose. It moved into the incising.
