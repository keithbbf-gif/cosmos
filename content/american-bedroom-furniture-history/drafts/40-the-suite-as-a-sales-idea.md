# The Suite as a Sales Idea

Berkey & Gay’s chamber suites for the 1876 Centennial, and the Grand Rapids plates that followed, are a sales idea with carved posts. A numbered set, a finish, a photograph of a room that does not yet exist in the customer’s house. The word *suite* in American furniture English does not mean “things that ended up in the same chamber.” It means a manufacturer has decided these SKUs belong together and has printed them on one page. That is the argument. The rest of this series has been walking toward it.

A Philadelphia high chest and dressing table that left the same shop in 1765 are a pair. They share wood, brass, a carving hand, a bill. They are not a suite. No one sold “the chamber set, no. 4, in mahogany.” The bed in that chamber was a textile machine on a different ticket. The looking glass might have been English. The nightstand did not exist as a type. Matching finish across a wealthy room was possible. A catalog number was not.

## What the photograph required

Grand Rapids learned, as the trade catalogs in that public library still show, that a buyer would take five pieces if five pieces were shown as a room. Bed, dresser with mirror, chiffonier, dressing table, chairs or a bench. Later the nightstands in a pair, because electric lamps want a table and symmetry wants two. The finish room made the unity. The shop could use different secondary woods, different hardware vendors, a bed from one line and a case from another, as long as the varnish matched in the plate.

That is merchandising, not a type in the eighteenth-century sense. A type is a high chest, a tester, a six-board chest. A suite is a marketing envelope around several types. You can dissolve it. Sell the bed alone. Leave the chiffonier. The remaining objects are still a dresser, still a bed. They are no longer a suite. The idea lives in the invoice.

Ethan Allen (chapter 31) perfected a later version: correlated groupings you could add to over years, a franchise that required the store to display the group as a room. Nathan Ancell said they sold thoughts, not things. The sentence is accurate about suites. The maple was a vehicle for a picture the suburb already wanted: traditional, complete, photographable.

MALM is a suite. Bed, chests in several heights, dressing table, mirror, wardrobe, nightstand, the same foil or veneer, one name. You fill a room from one aisle. Tomas Lundin’s napkin and the Polish factory (chapter 32) made the cheapest widely distributed version of the Grand Rapids idea. Particleboard did not change the logic. It lowered the price of matching. The e15 Mo bed, a single expensive object, is not a suite. It is a bed. IKEA’s MALM is a suite because the catalog says the SKUs combine.

## What is not a suite

A highboy pair is not a suite. Repeat it until the caption writers hear it. The pairing is real: dressing table and high chest, sometimes documented together, sometimes married by a dealer who needed a lot. Heckscher’s Met catalogs and the Winterthur files treat true pairs as a research problem. Even a true pair is two objects. The bed is elsewhere. The nightstand is later. Calling a high chest and lowboy a “bedroom suite” is Colonial Revival English. It is how Nutting’s public talked. It is not how the shops billed.

A Craftsman oak bed and a separate chest from the same catalog year are not automatically a suite. Stickley’s lots had numbers. Customers mixed them. The Craftsman Farms house is the test case that keeps the argument honest.

## Suites without a number

On the second floor of the Log House at Craftsman Farms, the Stickley girls’ bedroom was furnished with a matched inlaid oak group: copper and stained-wood designs, a bed, a dresser, later a writing desk on loan from Crab Tree Farm to complete the 1911 photograph. That room is a suite in the visual sense. It did not need a Grand Rapids plate. A father who ran a factory furnished his daughters’ room from the factory’s language. The matching is a household decision, the same way a wealthy Philadelphia chamber might have several pieces from one cabinetmaker without a “set number.”

Gustav Stickley also made, about 1915–17, a five-piece Chromewald bedroom suite for his second daughter, Mildred Cruess: bed, chest of drawers, nightstand, dressing table, bench. Sausage-turned legs, Colonial Revival idioms he had spent *The Craftsman* years arguing against. Object numbers in the Stickley Museum records (the bed L2012.11.5; the dressing table L2012.11.2a-b; the nightstand in the same loan) are museum numbers, not catalog numbers. Provenance: made for Mildred, used until her death, then her granddaughter Cynthia McGinn, last used by a great-granddaughter. The set is original to the Farms. It is a suite. It never got a catalog number in the Grand Rapids sense. Stickley, after bankruptcy, was making furniture so excellent it would “set a standard,” in Mary Fanton Roberts’s report of his aim. He was also making a matched bedroom for a daughter, in a historicist language, with a nightstand in the group. The nightstand’s presence is the tell. By 1916 the small table by the bed is part of what a suite means (chapter 45). A 1760 chamber did not need it to be complete. A 1916 family suite does.

That is the argument’s hinge. Suite is not “matching wood.” It is a completeness rule that a seller or a household applies. Grand Rapids printed the rule. IKEA printed it. Stickley applied it at home without a SKU. A collector who buys a high chest and a bed from different centuries and stains them alike has made a suite in the weakest sense and a lie in the stronger. A shop that builds a bed and a dresser in the same oak, sold together, is in the Grand Rapids business even if the joints are handmade. Matching is a choice with a history. It is not a virtue.

## How the suite ate the chamber

The colonial chamber accumulated. Marriage, inheritance, a cabinetmaker’s bill, a mercer’s bill for hangings. The room was complete when the household said so, which might mean a bed and a chest. The suite replaces that logic with a picture. Incomplete means you have not bought the chiffonier yet. Ethan Allen’s correlated groupings made the incompleteness a feature: come back in three years, the finish will still match. The photograph is patient.

What gets lost is the bed’s old job as a textile machine, the chest’s old job as a lid, the high chest’s job as a facade that did not need a matching footboard. What gets gained is a room a salesperson can stand in. The American bedroom, as a retail category, is that standing. Without the suite idea there is still furniture in chambers. There is not a “bedroom department.”

Waterfall walnut of the 1930s, Hollywood blonde of the 1940s, the Danish-modern teak groups, the Mediterranean pecan of the 1970s — each is a suite fashion. The objects change. The completeness rule does not. MALM is the late, cheap, honest version: we will not pretend this is mahogany, we will pretend this is a room. It is.

Department stores made the idea walkable. A buyer in 1928 could stand in a Marshall Field or Macy’s “bedroom” that was three walls and a rug, every piece tagged, a salesperson talking correlated groupings without using Ancell’s words. Gilbert Rohde’s Bedroom Group 3317 for Herman Miller, shown at the Century of Progress in Chicago in 1933, is the modern-design version of the same envelope: a numbered group, a fair, a photograph, a manufacturer stepping away from historical suites that were not selling. Norman Bel Geddes’s metal bedroom for Simmons is the machine-age version. The suite survived modernism. It just changed species.

What the completeness rule cannot hold is labor. A suite photograph has no servant’s iron bed, no trundle, no pallet. It is chapter 38’s class fact in commercial form. The sales idea is not only matching wood. It is a matching life, implied: two sleepers, no one else in the room, a door, a lamp on each side. The highboy pair never claimed that. It claimed storage and a place to dress.

## Residual

If you need a test, use three objects.

A Philadelphia high chest and its dressing table, if they are a pair: not a suite. Two case pieces, a chamber that still needs a bed on another bill.

A MALM bed, chest, and nightstand in the same foil: a suite. The catalog says they combine. The price says the completeness rule is now for renters.

Mildred Cruess’s Chromewald five-piece at Craftsman Farms: a suite without a catalog number. A factory man’s household applying the completeness rule as a gift.

The residual fact is the invoice. If the paper lists a set, you have a suite. If the paper lists a high chest, you have a high chest. The wood does not care. The camera does.

A last distinction, because restoration chapters will need it. You can break a suite without breaking the furniture. Sell the nightstands. Paint the dresser. The remaining bed is a bed. Collectors of “complete original suites” are collecting the sales idea, which is a legitimate collection. It is not the same as collecting a type. A complete MALM room in original foil is a historical document of 2002 merchandising. A complete Kindel Winterthur highboy is a reproduction of a type. A complete Ethan Allen maple chamber from 1964 is a document of suburban traditional. None of these is a 1760 chamber. The invoice keeps them straight if the caption does.

The high chest will keep being called a suite in dealer ads. MALM will keep being sold as separate SKUs that photograph as a room. Craftsman Farms will keep showing a daughter’s room that matches. The argument does not ask those practices to stop. It asks the caption to say which completeness rule is in force. A pair is a pair. A catalog page is a suite. A father’s gift can be either. The American bedroom learned to want the photograph. That want is the type.

Rohde’s 3317 and Bel Geddes’s Simmons group prove that “modern” did not kill the suite; it redesigned the envelope. A numbered bedroom group at a world’s fair is Grand Rapids logic in chrome. The highboy pair still is not a suite. The daughter’s room at Craftsman Farms still is, without a number. Keep the three tests. The rest is fashion in a matched finish.

Department-store “model rooms” after 1945 — Levittown adjacent, a maple group or a blonde oak group, a price on a card — taught the GI household the completeness rule at walking speed. You could buy the bed this year and the chest on the allotment. Ethan Allen’s correlated groupings were the franchise version of that walk. MALM is the warehouse version. The highboy pair never had a card that said “collect all five.” If a dealer writes “suite” on a high chest and lowboy, the caption should correct it. The argument chapter’s whole job is that correction, repeated until it sticks.
