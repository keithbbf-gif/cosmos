# McCobb Planner Bedroom

Winchendon Furniture Company, Winchendon, Massachusetts, had been making colonial-style maple since the 1920s when Paul McCobb and B. G. Mesberg redesigned the line for a market they thought was coming. Planner Group went to retailers in 1949 and to consumers in 1950. It ran to about 1964. Solid selected maple, the catalogs say, with birch in the same New England habit; finishes named natural, tobacco, walnut, and black; colored door panels; hardware that includes the aluminum ring pull you can find in the dark. Beds are the quiet pieces: a headboard, a footboard or none, tapered wood legs or iron, a mattress on rails that knock down. Open stock. Any room. The chest that sat in a bedroom on Monday could be a sideboard on Tuesday in the copy. That is the design. It is also the sales method.

McCobb (1917–1969) had come to notice in 1948 as a consultant at Martin Feinman’s Modernage Furniture in New York. Mesberg met him there. The Milwaukee *Journal* of 30 September 1956, cited in later McCobb literature, describes the split: Mesberg handled production and distribution, McCobb design. They did not buy a modern factory. They turned a colonial maple mill. That fact is the bedroom’s grain. Planner looks Scandinavian at a glance — tapered legs, a low case, a refusal of waterfall’s curve — and is Massachusetts maple with an aluminum ring.

## What the mill already knew

A Winchendon colonial chest is maple, turned legs or a bracket foot, a finish that wants to be “Early American.” The machines cut maple. The men knew maple. McCobb’s cases keep the species and change the stance: thinner legs, a flush drawer, a pull that is a ring or a simple plate, no colonial scallop. The 1955 Planner folder — “Planner Group Designs by Paul McCobb,” the trade sheet that still circulates among dealers — calls it America’s foremost coordinated contemporary group, open stock, chests on attached legs or platform bases, smaller cases on benches, beds, desks, chairs. The prose is trade-boast. The list of parts is the truth.

Bedroom pieces in that system are not a numbered “outfit” in the Sears 1938 sense. You buy a bed and two chests, or a bed and a chest-on-bench, or a headboard and a pair of night tables that are the same case as the living-room end tables. The aluminum ring pull is the family resemblance. So is the maple. Tobacco finish reads darker, closer to walnut from across the room; natural is the blond cousin of Heywood-Wakefield’s wheat without the Streamline radius. Black is the rare one in bedrooms, a lacquered or stained maple that photographs as sophisticated and shows every nick.

Iron legs on some Planner beds and benches are a different silhouette: thin, dark, a little more “Italian” or “Italianate modern” than the wood taper. The headboard stays simple — a panel, sometimes cane in related groups, no carved shell, no cloudlift, no Bakelite. Rails are modest. The bed is a place to put a mattress, not a piece of architecture. Nelson would approve; McCobb’s price did the democratic work Nelson’s Herman Miller bed could not.

Winchendon, in Worcester County, is a mill town with a furniture habit, not a design school. The colonial line had taught the shop to finish maple so it looked like “pine” or “maple antique.” Planner asked for a clearer finish, a grain that would be read as modern honesty. That request is labor: more sanding, a different stain schedule, a black finish that is unforgiving. Tobacco is the bedroom favorite because it hides. Natural is the one that shows a water ring from a glass that should have been on a tray. The mill’s men knew that. The catalog still showed natural in the living room.

## Mesberg, stores, and a fifth-floor shop

Current Biography (1958) is the source later writers lean on for the commercial numbers: Planner bought by 250 stores in the first year; manufactured in Sweden for European distribution as well as retailed in North and South America; Bloomingdale’s in 1957 putting a Paul McCobb shop on the fifth floor with fifteen room arrangements. Treat those as *Current Biography*’s claims, which is to say as a standard reference interview, not as a factory ledger. The Bloomingdale’s shop is the public bedroom. Fifteen rooms means the open-stock argument, staged: a bedroom next to a dining group next to a study, maple repeating, pulls repeating, a customer walking a loop instead of staring at one suite under a hanging card.

The *New York Times* of 12 March 1969, McCobb’s obituary, is cited in McCobb Museum and dealer literature as calling Planner the best-selling collection of modern furniture for the 1950s decade. Quote it as the *Times*’s sentence, not as an audited ranking. A newspaper obituary is a claim. It is a better claim than a website’s adjective. It is not a unit count from Winchendon.

Chon Gregory, McCobb’s associate for seventeen years, told the *Times* on 6 August 1996 that Planner was “the furniture of the people. It was basic, easy to understand and easy to use.” That is a named source, late. It is not a 1952 customer. Do not decorate this chapter with invented letters from brides. The 250 stores and the Bloomingdale’s floor are the evidence of reach. So are the pieces still in American bedrooms with the ring pulls intact.

Directional, Calvin, Predictor, Connoisseur: McCobb’s other lines, other mills (Calvin Furniture, O’Hearn, H. Sacks and Sons). They matter to a career. This chapter’s bed is Planner. Calvin Group’s more luxurious walnut is a different price and a different room. Keep them apart. The Winchendon maple bed is the one that went into the small house that had just paid off a waterfall suite, or into the apartment that had never had a suite.

## How the bed is made

Maple, hard, from New England forests that had been furniture wood since the joined chests of chapter 1. Kiln-dried. Legs turned or tapered on a machine, then sanded. Cases doweled and glued, not pinned with ebony. Drawer sides maple or a compatible hardwood, bottoms plywood. Dovetails if the mill is doing what the price allows; otherwise a locking joint that is still better than a Depression staple box. `[CITE NEEDED: Winchendon drawer-joint spec on Planner cases, from a catalog or a shop manual.]` The aluminum pull is a stamping, anodized or left bright, a circle you hook with a finger. It does not bark shins the way a Bakelite protrusion does. It also loosens. Original screws are small. Replacements from a big-box store are wrong in the slot.

A Planner bed with tapered wood legs is a knockdown: legs off, rails in a carton, headboard a panel. Iron-leg versions use a different bracket. Mattresses of 1952 sit lower than a 2020 pillow-top; a surviving bed will look “too low” or “too high” depending on what you put on it. Original rails have a length for a period full or twin. Queen conversions are later iron. The honest photograph shows the maple headboard and the pulls on the chests, not a padded hotel headboard behind them.

The aluminum ring is a stamping from a hardware maker, not a Winchendon foundry. It is peened or screwed through a hole. A missing ring is a 3/8-inch problem that dealers solve with a wrong pull. Original rings have a specific section — a tube, a slight flattening, a color that is not kitchen-chrome. Black-finish Planner sometimes gets brass rings in later “upgrades.” Leave them if they are period options from the folder; the 1955 sheet says several styles of hardware. Do not invent a rule that every Planner must wear aluminum. Do say that aluminum is the tell on the maple bedroom cases that went everywhere.

Cane appears on some Planner chairs and on related case doors; it is not the bed’s main event. A simple wooden headboard is the event. Compare Greene’s inlaid walnut footboard. McCobb’s board is a thickness, a radius at the corner so small you could miss it, a refusal to compete with the window. That is the modern bedroom’s courtesy.

## Open stock against the suite

The Depression outfit sold completeness as a matching set you could not break. Planner sold completeness as a system you could add to. The difference is credit and the difference is taste. You could still buy Planner on time through a department store. You were not forced to take the vanity to get the bed. A young household in 1952 might take two chests and a bed, then a dining table the next year, same pull, same maple. The bedroom ceases to be a sealed photograph. It becomes a room that shares a language with the rest of the house.

That is why Bloomingdale’s fifteen rooms mattered. They taught the eye to stop needing a suite. The highboy had been an ancestor. The waterfall had been a marriage. Planner wanted to be a plan — the name is a pun that the trade folder leans on — for living that did not require a full outfit. It is the closest American factory furniture comes, in the bedroom, to the Craftsman idea of a numbered form you could buy one of, without Hubbard’s orb or Stickley’s fumed moral.

MoMA’s Good Design exhibitions showed McCobb. That is a museum fact, a stamp of the taste bureaucracy. The Winchendon mill did not need it to sell a bed in Ohio. It helped the fifth floor. The two publics — Good Design and the 250 stores — are the point of Planner. Rarely does a maple bed serve both.

Contract orders by the mid-fifties — offices, institutions — pulled Planner out of the house. A dormitory or a clinic that bought maple cases is not a bedroom history except when the same SKU was a chest at home. Open stock was always a double life. The bed, though, stayed domestic. Institutions bought metal beds. Families bought the maple headboard. That split kept Planner in the sleeping room even when the chests went to work.

## Residual

The ring pull is still the way you identify the chest in a dark guest room. McCobb died in 1969. Planner had already stopped. Winchendon’s colonial machines could go back to scallops, or sit still. The beds that remain are not rare in the Nakashima sense. They are rare in the sense that an honest American modern bedroom is rare in the photographs: not teak, not a suite, not a platform from Zeeland, but a Massachusetts maple headboard on tapered legs, bought open stock, paid for at Bloomingdale’s or at a store that had never heard of the Hall brothers. The *Times* called the collection the decade’s best-seller. Mark that as the *Times*. The maple does not need the sentence. It needs the pull left original.

A Planner night table is often a small case on a bench, the bench a separate SKU. That separation is the open-stock idea in one stack. Lose the bench, and dealers invent a new leg. The original bench has a stretcher and the same maple. Look for screw holes in the case bottom. The bedroom that still has the bench still has the plan.
