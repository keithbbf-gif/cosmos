A 1653 Essex County inventory that names a feather bed before the frame (chapter 1) is already choosing whose sleep counts. The best tick in the best chamber is a line in a probate that a court could understand as wealth. A servant’s straw, a child’s trundle, a pallet in a kitchen loft, a bunk in a barracks, a hospital iron cot — these enter the furniture record late, cheap, or not at all. Privacy, in the American house, is not a human constant. It is a room count, a labor system, and a lock.

The “master bedroom” is a twentieth-century developer’s phrase laid on a colonial chamber that did not exist as a type. Parents, children, visitors, and hired or bound labor shared heat and square feet in ways later taste cannot describe without lying. A history of bedroom furniture that begins only with mahogany high chests has already decided who counted as a sleeper.

## The chamber and the other beds

In a one- or two-chamber house the best bed is in the parlor chamber or the hall chamber. Lesser beds are trundles — truckle beds that slide under — or ticks on the floor. Abbott Lowell Cummings’s inventories keep returning to multiple bedsteads in a house that later Americans would call small. Children slept with parents, then in trundles, then in cold rooms as the house added bays. A cradle (chapter 44) might stand in the chamber for a season and then go to the attic. The furniture is a sequence, not a suite.

Hired girls in New England slept in attics, kitchens, and back chambers on cheaper bedsteads: maple, pine, a rope grid, a thin tick. Urban boardinghouses stacked iron or cheap wood beds in rooms that were not chambers in the inventory sense; they were rent. The boardinghouse bed is American bedroom furniture. It has no poster to collect. Grier’s work on parlor comfort is the display half of the nineteenth-century house. The boarding half is a bedstead you could move when the rent failed.

Servants in the merchant house after 1850 might have a defined room — still often a leftover, north-facing, a iron bed the sanitation writers approved of because it washed. The brass and iron beds of the 1880s–1910s (chapter 2’s after-the-stove) are a public-health object as well as a taste. They show up in servant rooms and hospitals first, then in fashionable chambers that wanted to look clean. The photograph of a matching walnut suite is a different household.

## Enslaved quarters, and the thin record

Enslaved people in the Chesapeake, the lowcountry, and northern port towns slept in lofts, kitchens, outbuildings, and, less often than white family myth prefers, in the same chamber as the people who claimed them. John Michael Vlach’s *Back of the Big House* and the archaeological work on quartering are the corrective. The furniture record is thin because the objects were cheaper, the inventories were written by the owners of the labor, and a pallet does not become an antique.

When inventories do price enslaved people’s bedding separately, the lines are worth more than a dozen high chests to the bedroom record. `[CITE NEEDED: a specific Chesapeake or lowcountry inventory that prices bedding in quarters separately from the parlor chamber — carry forward from chapter 1.]` A rope bedstead of pine, a tick of husks, a blanket: these are bed furniture in the eighteenth-century sense, textiles and a frame, without the mahogany and without the name of a joiner. Archaeology finds postholes and hearth locations. It rarely finds a labeled chest.

Northern slavery and bound labor in port towns had chambers too: garrets, kitchens, the back of a shop. The gap in Boston cabinet-shop documentation for workmen of color (chapter 3) is not proof of absence. The sleep of those workmen is even less documented. A history that only follows labeled case pieces will never find it.

After emancipation the record does not suddenly thicken. Tenancy, shotgun houses, company housing in mill towns — including Arkansas mill towns (chapter 36) — used iron beds, pine, what the commissary sold. Jim Crow structured rooms as it structured plants. The published furniture literature is still catching up. This paragraph is a marker, not a finished account.

Hotels and Pullman cars are the commercial version of the barracks. A hotel bedstead in 1890 is often brass or iron, a mattress the house launders, a room that is private for a night if you can pay. Pullman berths fold; they are storage beds for people, not shirts. The porter’s labor is the hangings: he makes the cloth box each evening. Florence Montgomery’s textile history does not usually include a Pullman blanket. It should, in a history of who slept where. The object is still a bed. The class fact is the ticket.

YMCA dormitories, lodging houses, the “flophouse” iron bed photographed by journalists who wanted reform — these are American bedrooms. Jacob Riis’s New York rooms, whatever one thinks of his politics, show furniture: a bed, sometimes two, a stove, not a suite. The later single-room occupancy hotel with a metal bed and a hot plate is the same chamber without a high chest and without a myth.

## Children

Children’s sleep is not cute filler. Inventories name cradles, trundle beds, “small bedsteads,” later youth beds. A trundle is a second sleeper in the parents’ chamber, a fact of heat and watching, not a style. As houses added rooms, children were pushed out — a historical change in privacy that looks like a furniture change. The youth bed, the half-size poster, the painted “cottage” suites of the nineteenth century, the 1930s enamel metal cribs, the drop-side cribs that CPSC would ban in 2011 (chapter 44) — these are a sequence of answers to a body that grows and a fear that the bed will kill.

Privacy for a child is a class fact. A house with a nursery, a night nursery, a governess, and a hall is not the house with a trundle. Both are American. Only one photographs as a bedroom.

Orphanages and later “homes” for children institutionalized the youth bed: iron, numbered, a room of many sleepers. The CPSC crib is a domestic afterlife of that fear — the state in the nursery — without the ward’s rows. Chapter 44 takes the objects. Here the point is only that children’s sleep was often public to the household or the institution, and private childhood is a room the family had to be able to spare.

## Hospitals and barracks

Hospital bedsteads, by the late nineteenth century, are iron, washable, often with a crank. Florence Nightingale’s writing on wards, American sanitary commissions in the Civil War, the later professional hospital — they wanted a bed that would not hold infection in hangings. The domestic reform bedroom that followed Eastlake and the sanitation writers borrowed that morality: less drapery, more metal, the open air. The Craftsman bed is a cousin of the hospital argument even when it is oak.

Barracks and ship berths are the state’s bedroom furniture. Iron bunks, stacked, a footlocker the only case piece. Civil War camps, the New Deal CCCs, the World Wars, later bases: the bed is a unit of logistics. It knocks down. It stacks. It has no suite. A soldier’s privacy is a blanket, not a room. The folding cot that came home as surplus and went into the guest room or the child’s room is barracks furniture in a domestic afterlife. So is the metal bed in a boardinghouse.

Prisons, asylums, orphanages: institutional sleep as a field of its own. The objects are beds. The occupants did not choose them. A magazine series about the American bedroom that never mentions a ward has accepted the real-estate photograph as the type.

## Privacy as a class fact

Lock the door. That sentence is a late luxury. A hall-and-parlor house has no corridor; rooms open into rooms. You pass through sleep to get to sleep. The center-hall plan and then the corridor plan of the later house make the chamber a dead end. That architectural change is the furniture’s partner. A high chest against the wall of a through-room is storage in traffic. A high chest in a dead-end chamber is storage in a private room. The suite of the Grand Rapids photograph assumes the dead end: a room used for sleeping and dressing, photographed with the door implied shut.

Servants’ bells, speaking tubes, later intercoms: the wealthy chamber was private from the street and open to labor. Enslaved and hired people entered to make the fire, empty the slop, bring water. The nightstand’s ancestor is partly that traffic — a place to put a candle so the person who came in did not grope. Privacy was vis-à-vis neighbors, not vis-à-vis labor. When the labor left the house — the long twentieth-century story of domestic service’s decline — the bedroom could become the sealed room the photographs always pretended it was. A foam mattress, a laptop, a door: the current chamber (chapter 42) is that seal plus screens. It is not more ancient. It is cheaper labor living somewhere else.

Who slept where, then, is a map of who could close a door on whom. The high chest and the tester belong to the side of the map that left inventories. The trundle, the pallet, the iron bunk, the hospital crank belong to the same history. They are bedroom furniture. They are harder to accession. The residual fact is the photograph’s lie: a matched suite in a quiet room is not the American bedroom. It is one class’s bedroom, at the moment the house had surplus rooms and the catalog had a camera. Everyone else slept on what was left.

Women’s labor made most of the beds that were not bought as suites. They sewed ticks, filled them, remade hangings from worn gowns, emptied slops, carried water. Enslaved seamstresses did that work without a name on the line that says “bed furniture.” The phrase still means the cloth. A furniture history that only follows cabinetmakers will miss the people who made the expensive half of the eighteenth-century bed and the cheap half of the later one. The upholsterer in a merchant city is the named exception. The unnamed rule is the household.

Birth happened in these rooms. Death happened in these rooms. The “funeral parlor” later took one of those jobs away; the hospital took the other, for those who could reach it. The bedroom as a sealed private place for two healthy adults is a late, narrow use of a room that had been a theater of the whole life. Furniture for that older use includes the cradle, the sickbed’s extra pillow, a chair for the watcher. The suite photograph cannot hold them. It does not try.

Mid-century developer houses finally gave the “master” a bath. That lock-and-bath is the furniture’s partner as much as the corridor was. A nightstand on each side, a door that closes, a suite in maple or walnut: the class fact became a floor plan you could buy with a VA loan. Everyone else still slept in smaller rooms, on bunks, on the fold-out, in the grandmother’s iron bed. The photograph in the catalog was never a census.

Pullman porters, hospital night staff, and barracks corporals made other people’s beds as work. That labor is furniture history too: hospital corners, a numbered bunk, a sheet count. The photographed suite assumes no one makes the bed except the sleeper. That assumption is the class fact in linen.
