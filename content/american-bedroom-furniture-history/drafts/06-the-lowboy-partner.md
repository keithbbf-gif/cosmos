# The Lowboy Partner

Met 32.93 stands thirty-one and a quarter inches in the American Wing: mahogany and mahogany veneer over tulip poplar, yellow pine, and white cedar, thirty-five and seven-eighths by twenty-one and three-eighths inches in plan, Philadelphia, 1762–90. Morris K. Jesup Fund, 1932. The object page is willing to say it was made en suite with high chest 18.110.4, and then the useful caution: same shop, different carving hands. The table’s cut is deeper and livelier than the chest’s. Collectors say *lowboy* the way they say *highboy*: a nickname that got into the house and stayed. The shops said dressing table, low chest, or billed the piece by the job.

A matching pair is a real Philadelphia and Newport habit. It is still two bills, or one bill with two lines. It is not Berkey & Gay’s chamber suite. 32.93 is the shorter voice of a ninety-one-inch argument, not a SKU.

## Height, glass, and the basin

A dressing table’s first job is a surface: basin, bottles, a glass, the morning’s small work. Drawers hold what a chest of drawers would hold in miniature — small clothes, ribbons, razors, later a man’s collar box. The looking glass is usually a separate object, often English, hung on the wall. Integrated vanity mirrors are a later, factory idea. If you see a colonial dressing table with a tall attached glass in a period room, ask whether the glass is a marriage.

Boston examples of the second quarter of the eighteenth century follow the high chest’s stance: walnut or japanned, cabriole legs, a skirt with a carved shell or a simple cyma. They do not automatically match a high chest in the same house. Probate lists a “dressing table” and a “case of drawers” two rooms apart. The pair is a possibility, not a rule.

Met 10.125.68 is the Boston pair you can walk around: dressing table, 1747, japanned maple and white pine, thirty and five-eighths by thirty-three and seven-eighths by twenty-one and an eighth inches, Sage gift, 1909. Tortoiseshell ground, dragon, winged swans pulling a chariot. Its high chest is 10.125.58. The Met distinguishes that decoration from the Pickman suite. Accumulated in the same shop, photographed together now. Still not a numbered set. The bed in that chamber was still cloth.

## Philadelphia pairs

The Hollingsworth furniture associated with Thomas Affleck — high chests and dressing tables ordered around a 1768 marriage — is the documented pair that sale catalogs have worn smooth. Use it as a documented extreme, not as the type’s everyday life. Most Philadelphia dressing tables are orphans. Their high chests burned, were sold, or never existed. Carving on the central drawer and the knees will rhyme with a high chest in the same shop’s vocabulary without proving a commission.

32.93 is the public pair that is not an orphan. Construction: the top is often a single board or two, with a molded edge. The case is shallow compared with a high chest’s lower story, even when the facade copies it. Legs are the same cabriole conversation. Secondary woods follow the city’s habit — tulip poplar, yellow pine, cedar. A dressing table is quicker than a high chest and cheaper. That is why more households could own one without owning 18.110.4.

Met 30.120.61 is the other Philadelphia dressing table in the same wing: mahogany, yellow pine, tulip poplar, white cedar, thirty and five-eighths by thirty-five and seven-eighths by twenty and five-eighths inches, 1755–90, Sylmaris Collection, gift of George Coe Graves, 1930. No mate on the label. An orphan that still does the morning’s work.

## Newport

Block-and-shell dressing tables exist. They are among the objects Moses and Heckscher used to isolate Townsend’s rays and Goddard’s blocking. A Newport dressing table with three carved shells on a blocked facade is not a high chest sawn off. The proportions are designed at table height. The top matters more because you see it. The shells are closer to the hands that use the drawers.

Met 1994.449 is the quieter Newport table: mahogany, 1740–50, thirty and three-eighths by thirty-four and a quarter by twenty-two and a half inches, slipper feet, a shell fully integrated into the scrolls of the skirt. Gift of Yvette and Thomas Cole, 1994. Queen Anne, not yet the full blocked street. Met 2016.797.8 is the later Newport dressing table: mahogany, 1760–90, thirty-one by thirty-four and three-quarter by twenty-three and a quarter inches, Blair / Lorillard bequest, 2016. The school could say the same thing at basin height that it said at eighty-eight inches.

A reproduction lowboy from the 1920s — Nutting and the factory Colonial Revival — often gets the height wrong, the blocking shallow, the shells applied. The nickname *lowboy* helped the revival. It sounds like a mate. It sold as a mate. The colonial shop was less sentimental.

## What the form becomes

Federal dressing tables slim down: square tapering legs, satinwood inlay, a lighter skirt. Empire tables get pillars and marble. The Victorian suite replaces the dressing table with a dresser that has a built-in glass and a marble top — a different object that ate the lowboy’s job. The vanity of 1900–1940 is the dressing table after electricity and Hollywood. The mid-century low dresser is the job again, without cabriole legs.

So the lowboy is a colonial and early Federal answer to a basin and a glass. It is the high chest’s partner when a shop and a client wanted a pair. It is not the origin of the matching suite. The suite will need a factory, a catalog page, and a room that can be photographed as a set. The dressing table only needed a morning.

## A word on the nickname

*Highboy* and *lowboy* are American collector words with English roots (*boy* as a piece of furniture, not a child). They are useful in a caption. They are sloppy in a shop history if they make the pair sound inevitable. Keep *dressing table* in the running text and let *lowboy* into the title because that is how a reader will search. The residual stain is the basin ring you can still find on a top that has not been refinished into oblivion. Someone used this. It was not a pedestal for a high chest.

## Skirts, knees, and the dropped central drawer

The dressing table’s skirt is where a shop shows off without a pediment. A carved shell at the center, C-scrolls, a cyma that repeats the high chest’s lower edge — these are the rhymes that make a pair visible from across a room. Knees may carry leaves or, in Newport, small shells. Feet match the taller case when the commission was a pair; they wander when it was not.

The dropped central drawer — a small drawer hung lower than its neighbors — is a Philadelphia habit that also appears elsewhere. It breaks the rectangle and makes a place for the carving. It is not a rule. Boston tables can be a straight row of drawers over a quieter skirt. Do not date a table by the drop alone. 32.93 is the dropped-drawer conversation standing next to 18.110.4’s carved lower drawer. Same shop, the page says. Different chisels.

## Brasses and the looking-glass problem

Hardware follows the high chest: drops, then bails, then later replacements. Because dressing tables were used hard — water, powder, spilled spirits — tops were replaced more often than high-chest tops. A replaced top with original legs is a common frankenpiece. A looking glass bolted to a backboard is usually a later marriage. The honest restoration leaves the glass on the wall and the stain on the top.

## Dressing table, bureau table, toilet glass

The collector’s *lowboy* hides three objects. An American dressing table is usually a case of drawers on cabriole or turned legs, table height, no kneehole, a basin on the top, a looking glass on the wall. A Newport bureau table — Yale 1957.37 is the public one — is a kneehole piece with a prospect and a blocked facade, writing and dressing in the same body. An English toilet table may have a kneehole the American dressing table refuses. If a caption calls all three a lowboy, the pair with a high chest becomes destiny and the kneehole becomes a sawn-off desk. Keep the words apart.

The toilet glass, or dressing glass, is the fourth object in the morning and the one period rooms most often omit. A small swinging mirror on a box, sometimes with tiny drawers, sits on the table. It is not bolted to a backboard. English plate shows up in inventories as a separate line from the table. American shops made the boxes; the glass was often imported. When a Colonial Revival dealer screws a tall glass to a colonial dressing table, the marriage is visible in the screw pattern and in a crest that does not know the skirt. The later vanity type is that marriage made into a factory SKU. Here the honest restoration is the stain on the top, the glass on the wall or on its own box, the basin where the ring still is.

Boston japanned dressing tables exist and are rarer than the japanned high chests. 10.125.68 is the one the Met will stand next to its high chest. Probate already said the pair was a possibility, not a law: two rooms apart is as common as a matching skirt.

## Table joinery under a case of drawers

A dressing table is closer to a table than a high chest’s lower story is. Four legs. Side rails and a back rail. Glue blocks in the corners where the cabriole meets the rail — if those blocks are gone, the legs splay and the skirt splits. The skirt is a structural rail with a shaped lower edge, not an apron glued onto a box. Treat it as furniture jewelry and the piece fails on the next dry winter. The drawer pack sits on that frame: one long drawer over three small, or a row of four, or the dropped central drawer that Philadelphia likes because it makes a place for the shell. Boston tables can be a straight row over a quieter cyma. Newport blocked tables carry the three shells at hand height.

The top is one board or two, glued, a molded or thumbnail edge. It is fastened from below through pocket blocks or nails. Water, powder, spilled spirits: this is the American case form that gets used hardest at the surface. Replaced tops are common. A later screw map, a board that does not continue the edge-moulding logic, a finish that does not match the legs — frankenpiece. The legs may be original. The morning’s work ate the top.

Cabriole legs on a dressing table are the same conversation as the high chest: pad feet in Queen Anne Boston, ball-and-claw in Philadelphia, open talon in Newport when the shop is speaking that language. They are often tenoned into the case rather than run as a continuous stile. That is a different joint from a chair, and a different joint from a high chest whose lower stiles sometimes think they are a frame. Conservators photograph from below. The glue blocks are the truth.

Secondary woods follow the city: white pine in New England, tulip poplar and yellow pine and cedar in Philadelphia, chestnut and pine in Newport. A dressing table is quicker than a high chest. Fewer drawers, no pediment, no upper case, no stair in two lifts. That is why more households could own one. It is also why dealers can “pair” an orphan table with any high chest of the same wood. The rhyme of a skirt is real. It is not a bill. 32.93 has the bill the Met is willing to write: en suite with 18.110.4. Most tables do not.

## What the drawers held, and who stood there

Inventories that bother to open the dressing table list small linen, ribbons, razors, combs, later a collar box — the morning’s kit, not the household’s sheets. The sheets lived in the high chest or the plain chest. The dressing table is toilet furniture in the old sense: washing, dressing, the looking glass. Enslaved and servant labor in richer houses carried the basin, emptied it, waxed the legs. The stain on the top is their work as well as the owner’s face. The furniture literature has been slow to put those hands next to the brass. `[CITE NEEDED: a chamber inventory that lists basin, dressing table, and a named servant or enslaved person in the same room.]`

Height is particular. Twenty-eight to thirty-two inches is the ordinary band. 32.93 is thirty-one and a quarter. A Victorian dresser with a marble top and a bolted glass is a different object that ate this job. Federal dressing tables slim the leg to a taper and inlay a stringing; the basin still sits. Empire tables pick up pillars and sometimes marble. The job remains a surface until the factory vanity swallows glass, electric light, and a row of small drawers in one carcass.

The Hollingsworth pair associated with Affleck is still the documented extreme, a 1768 marriage order that sale catalogs have worn smooth. Most mornings did not have it. Most dressing tables lived without a ninety-inch mate. The nickname *lowboy* helped the twentieth century forget that. A basin ring on an unrestored top is the correction. Someone stood there. The high chest, if there was one, was behind them, holding linen they were not yet wearing.

## Residual

Most dressing tables lived without a mate, and most mornings did not require a ninety-inch case to make sense of a basin. Met 32.93 is the exception the gallery likes to photograph: the shorter voice of 18.110.4, carved by someone else in the same shop. The nickname *lowboy* makes that photograph sound inevitable. The colonial shop billed a dressing table. The suite will need a factory. The table only needed a morning.
