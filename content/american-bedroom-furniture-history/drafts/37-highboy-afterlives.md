# Highboy Afterlives

Met 18.110.4 stands in the American Wing as a Philadelphia high chest of drawers: mahogany, the secondary woods the museum names, a carved pediment, the type Morrison Heckscher’s catalogs taught a generation to see. Exact measurements belong on the label; the fact that matters here is the room it no longer occupies. The object was built as chamber storage, a vertical address for linen, a facade facing a hung bed. It now stands in a gallery or, in houses that still own its cousins, in a front hall beside a looking glass that never saw a basin. The highboy’s afterlife is to be ancestor furniture. The clothes went to closets and to MALM.

Joseph Downs, in *American Furniture: Queen Anne and Chippendale Periods* (1952), called the Winterthur high chest made for William and Mary Turner — later the Van Pelt family, acquired by Henry Francis du Pont in 1929, shown in the Port Royal Parlor — the “highest development of the Philadelphia Chippendale school of furniture.” Kindel Furniture of Grand Rapids took a license in 1983 to reproduce Winterthur examples; the Philadelphia High Chest in that program required, Kindel’s own copy said, extraordinary hand carving, motifs from Chippendale’s 1762 *Director*, on the order of a hundred hours of carving per chest. Serial numbers turn up on the used market. A Kindel Winterthur highboy is not a fake. It is a licensed descendant that auction houses will catalog as a reproduction even when it brings mid-five figures, and whose original retail, in dealer memory, ran into six. The afterlife has a price list.

## What collectors still chase

Wallace Nutting photographed and sold “colonial” furniture to a 1920s public that wanted the high chest more than the hangings. Israel Sack and the other dealers who built the canonical American collections taught buyers to read brasses, secondary woods, and the difference between a Boston japanned case and a Philadelphia carved one. Heckscher, Hornor, Montgomery, the Chipstone *American Furniture* annuals — the shelf is the chase’s bibliography. The objects that still move serious money are the ones that can carry a paragraph: a named shop or a tight attribution, a family descent, carving that can be grouped with a small cohort of knees and shells.

Auction English is a dialect. *Attributed to the shop of* is not *labeled by*. *Descended in the family of* is a provenance claim that may be a letter, a faint chalk inscription, or a dealer’s wish. *The finest of the form* is a comparative that Downs was willing to print and that a catalog still borrows. *Restorations, replacements, and later brasses* are the sentences that actually determine the bid. A replaced row of Chippendale plates, the ghost of an earlier drop handle still in the wood, will not always be in the headline. It will be in the condition report if the house is doing its job. Chapter 3 already called replaced brasses the common lie. The afterlife lives on that lie’s detection.

Christie’s catalog for the Benjamin Marshall high chest — attributed to Henry Clifton and Thomas Carteret, Philadelphia, 1750–1765 — printed a family document about a British officer’s sword and a drawer, then said the sword could not be confirmed and the descent could. That is the dialect at its best: the story, then the check. Collectors chase the carving and the paper. They also chase the story. A history of the bedroom has to notice that the story has left the bedroom.

## Reproductions, licensed and otherwise

Colonial Revival factories had been making “highboys” for decades before Kindel’s Winterthur license: maple and mahogany-stained gum, broken pediments, ball-and-claw feet machine-carved, a size that would fit a 1928 dining room because the high chest had already migrated toward display. Ethan Allen’s traditional line (chapter 31) offered the recognition without the Philadelphia knee. Eldred Wheeler and other late-century revival shops made stricter copies for a smaller public. `[CITE NEEDED: a dated Eldred Wheeler high chest catalog entry if used as a figure.]` Kindel’s license is the institutional version: Winterthur’s scholars and conservators as a quality gate, Grand Rapids as the plant, a serial number as a new kind of label.

A licensed reproduction is a useful object. It lets a household have the silhouette without taking a 1760 case out of a museum or off a market that already concentrates the best examples in a few zip codes. It is also a confusion. Visitors to Winterthur see the Van Pelt chest in a parlor, not a chamber. Visitors to a 1990s dining room see a Kindel and think they have the type. Both rooms have already accepted the afterlife: this is not where you keep shirts.

Chest-on-chest is a sister form, not a nickname. The lower case is a second stack, not a frame with one long drawer and cabriole legs. Collectors and auction houses still blur the words. A Boston William and Mary high chest on turned stretchers is not a Philadelphia rococo monument. A Newport block-and-shell chest-on-chest is not a highboy except in a headline. The afterlife flattens types because the public word *highboy* won. Shops did not need that word. They needed a bill.

Fakes and marriages are the other reproduction. A bonnet top from one case on a base from another, a new pediment on a decent lower case, carving added in 1930 to a plain 1780 stack. The literature is full of them because the money was in the theater — gilt, flame finials, a scrolled tympanum — not in the drawer boxes. Chapter 43 will look at that split from a shop floor. Here the point is only that collectors’ afterlives created a market for theater, and theater can be nailed on.

## The hall, not the chamber

Why did the form leave the chamber? Ceilings in developer houses. Closets. The matching suite that wanted a low dresser and a pair of nightstands, not a nine-foot stack. Central heat, which made the hung bed optional and the bedroom a showplace of bedspreads rather than a cloth box. The high chest is tall. It needs a stairwell or a hall to be seen. Du Pont put masterpieces in parlors at Winterthur because he was building a sequence of furnished rooms, not a lesson in where linen lived. Period rooms at the Met and MFA do the same kindness and the same distortion. A high chest photographed beside a tester with reproduction hangings is closer to use. A high chest alone under a spotlight is a sculpture. Both displays are arguments.

In living houses the pattern is consistent enough to be a fact of taste. The “good” case — the inherited maple Queen Anne, the walnut chest-on-chest, the mahogany highboy with replaced brasses — stands where guests pass. The MALM or the closet holds the T-shirts. Privacy and storage split. The ancestor is public. The work is hidden. That is the opposite of the eighteenth-century chamber, where the high chest’s facade *was* the public face of private goods, in a room that was not yet only for sleeping.

Nutting’s photographs and the Colonial Revival bedroom of the 1920s wanted posts and a highboy together, hangings feared as dust. They assembled a “colonial bedroom” that no inventory would have recognized as a suite. The afterlife began as interior decoration. Collectors later professionalized it into connoisseurship. The hall is where those two afterlives meet: the decorator’s statement and the bidder’s prize.

Magazine decorating in the same decades — *House Beautiful*, *Antiques*, later shelter monthlies — taught the high chest as a trophy of American-ness. A broken pediment in a hall said the household had a past, even if the case was a 1925 Grand Rapids reading of Philadelphia. The pairing with a lowboy, when both survive, is the one afterlife that still remembers use: a dressing table and a stack, a pair from one shop. Heckscher and others have been careful about true pairs versus later marriages. Auction English is less careful. “Matching lowboy” in a lot description should send a reader to the construction, not to the finish color.

Du Pont’s Winterthur is itself an afterlife machine: masterworks arranged as rooms that are truer than most houses and false to most lives. The Port Royal Parlor is not a bedroom. The high chest in it is doing civic work for a collection. That is a legitimate museum job. It is not a chamber.

## What the form still teaches

A high chest is many drawers. Each drawer is a box: dovetails, a bottom whose grain direction is a shop habit, runners, a cock bead or a lip. Secondary woods (white pine in New England, tulip poplar and cedar in Philadelphia, the southern mix in MESDA’s territory) date and locate when the label is gone. The waist molding hides or announces the joint between upper and lower case. The pediment is joinery plus carving plus, sometimes, a gilt shell that is theater. Collectors who only see the pediment are buying the afterlife. Conservators who open a drawer are still in the chamber.

The market has narrowed. Not every high chest is a Downs masterpiece. Plenty of plain maple stacks survive in New England and are worth less than a Kindel, which tells you that the afterlife values carving and a story more than the type’s original job. Those plain stacks are closer to how most people stored. They are harder to photograph for a catalog cover. They are also, still, furniture that could go back in a bedroom if the ceiling allowed and the owner needed drawers. Few owners need them. Closets won.

Regional afterlives differ. Boston japanned cases are conservation problems: the gilt chips, the ground craze, restorations that look original in a dim hall. Philadelphia carving is a specialist’s market. Southern walnut high chests in MESDA’s files never got Nutting’s full attention and still live closer to use in some houses, which is another way of saying they were worth less to the coastal chase. A form’s afterlife is a map of money as much as of taste.

Construction analysis, the kind Michael Moses and others brought to unlabeled case work, is the afterlife’s best habit. Drawer bottoms, dovetail angles, the way the upper case sits on the lower, a scribe line the finisher did not quite bury — these return the object to a shop. Auction English that skips them is selling a silhouette. A collector who opens a drawer is closer to the chamber than a collector who only lights the pediment. That is the one way the form still works as furniture history rather than as a trophy.

The residual fact is a room assignment. The form that organized the chamber’s linen is now, in the houses that can still afford it, a guest in the hall. Auction English will go on calling it a highboy, attributed, descended, the finest of. The shirts are in a particleboard stack that has to be anchored to the wall. Both objects are American bedroom furniture. Only one still lives where it was built to work.
