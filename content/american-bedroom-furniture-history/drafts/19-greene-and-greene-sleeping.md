The bed in Bedroom No. 1 of the Gamble House, 4 Westmoreland Place, Pasadena, is black walnut. Not teak. Not mahogany. Those woods belong to other rooms in the same 1908 winter house: Burma teak in the entry, Honduras mahogany in the dining room, California redwood in the living-room panels, Douglas fir in the rafter tails that tourists photograph from the lawn. The USC Greene and Greene Digital Archive records the master-bedroom bedframe as unmarked black walnut, accession 01-66-205 in their numbering, with a footboard whose inlaid panel is the thing you actually stop for. A matching single-drawer night table, 01-66-207, is the same walnut, twenty-six inches high. The shop that cut them was not Grand Rapids. It was Peter and John Hall, working to drawings by Charles Sumner Greene and Henry Mather Greene, for David Berry Gamble and Mary Huggins Gamble, who already had a house in Cincinnati and wanted a Pasadena season that did not look like Ohio.

That is the opening fact because the later market will try to make “Greene & Greene” a finish. It is not a finish. It is a house, a set of rooms, and furniture that refuses to travel as a boxed suite.

## What the Gambles asked for

David Gamble was a Procter & Gamble principal. Mary Gamble came from a Cincinnati family that knew how a formal house worked. They were not Arts and Crafts converts shopping a Craftsman catalog. They hired the Greenes in 1907 for a winter residence on a private court west of Orange Grove, in a neighborhood that was already filling with timber houses whose roofs hung farther than their walls. Construction ran 1907–09. Edward R. Bosley, long the house’s director and author of the Phaidon monograph *Greene & Greene* (2000), has spent decades telling visitors that the brothers were building in 1908, not reconstructing Japan. Randell L. Makinson’s *Greene & Greene: Furniture and Related Designs* (1979) remains the furniture half of that sentence: the furniture is architecture at chair scale.

The Greenes had been in practice in Pasadena since the 1890s. The early work is competent, sometimes dull. After 1904 the Hall brothers — John (1864–1940) and Peter (1867–1939), Swedish-born builders who could hold a complex timber joint without simplifying it for the mill — became the hands. Bosley and the Gamble House’s own “Design Without Compromise” notes make the collaboration structural, not romantic. The Greenes had been unable to find craftsmen who would spend the hours the drawings demanded. The Halls would. That is why the master-bedroom chair’s ebony pegs follow the arch of the crest rail instead of sitting as a row of identical plugs. A factory would have standardized the peg. The Halls cut the top of the peg to the curve.

Mary and David slept in Bedroom No. 1. Guest rooms, including furniture in maple with silver inlay, sit along the same floor. Aunt Julia’s room and its sleeping porch are part of the second-floor circuit that the Greenes treated as a climate machine: air, shade, a porch that is not a decoration. The “master bedroom” as a phrase is later American real-estate language. The Greenes drew Bedroom No. 1.

## Walnut, teak, and the lie of the matching set

A Grand Rapids chamber suite of 1908 is a sales unit: bed, dresser, chiffonier, dressing table, often a cheval glass, all in one wood and one hardware. The Gamble House does the opposite. Public rooms take imported hardwoods that announce the house. Private rooms take woods that behave in a bedroom: walnut that does not glare, maple that takes silver, Port Orford cedar and oak where the Greenes needed them. More than a dozen species are in the building. Twisted Preservation’s later walk-through with Bosley lists the split: teak entry, mahogany dining, redwood living-room carving, black-walnut bedroom furniture, maple guest furniture. Makinson’s plates let you check the claim against the objects.

The bed is a frame with a low, inlaid footboard, not a Victorian high-post tester and not a Stickley slab of fumed oak. Headboard and footboard are assembled as casework: rails, posts, a panel that is a picture in wood. Measurements in the USC record for the footboard detail run about 31½ by 40½ inches for the photographed element; the full bed is a double of the period, not a later queen stretched by a dealer. `[CITE NEEDED: published overall bed dimensions from Makinson or the Gamble House furniture inventory, not the cropped footboard photograph.]` Side rails meet the posts in hardware the Greenes did not hide. The mattress sits on a wood grid, not on a brass coil advertised as hygienic. There is no matching “suite number.” If you want a second walnut case, you walk to the night table. If you want a dresser, it is the dresser designed for that wall, with the same pegs and the same cloudlift in the stretcher, not a factory twin shipped in the same crate.

Cloudlifts are the Greenes’ most stolen detail. The word describes a shallow, stepped curve in a rail or apron, related to Japanese timber and to Chinese furniture the brothers knew from books and from the 1894 California Midwinter International Exposition’s Japanese display, which both Greenes saw. `[CITE NEEDED: the precise exposition visit is in the Greene literature; confirm against Bosley rather than tour-script memory.]` On a living-room rocker at the Gamble House the cloudlift is bold, a crest you can read from across the rug. On dining-room built-ins at the Thorsen House in Berkeley (1908–10), it is thinner, a cabinet-maker’s lift. In Bedroom No. 1 the lifts sit in chair crests and table stretchers at a scale that belongs to a room you occupy in a dressing gown, not a hall you process through. A factory “Greene-style” bed of the 1990s will stamp a cloudlift onto a rail and call it a tribute. The original is a change of thickness in a specific stick of walnut, cut so the shadow line moves when the morning comes in from the porch.

Ebony pegs are the other signature, and they are not decoration first. They pin joints. Square ebony, sometimes slightly proud, sometimes flush, sometimes — on that master-bedroom chair — sculpted to the crest. They read as a rhythm down a stile the way a Philadelphia high chest’s brass row reads as a rhythm, except the peg is the fastener. Lose one and you have a hole that is a conservation problem, not a missing jewel. The Greenes also used splines, breadboard ends on tabletops, and finger joints that announce the end grain. Makinson’s furniture volume is a catalog of those decisions. A reader who only knows the living-room settle has not yet seen how the same grammar behaves when the object has to stand next to a bed.

## The Hall shop, not a mill town

Peter and John Hall ran a shop that could take a Greene drawing and not “value-engineer” it. That phrase is later. What they refused to do was simplify a through-tenon because a round-end tenon would have been faster on the machine. The Greenes drew overlapping timbers, revealed pegs, and corners that required the woodworker to think in three directions at once. James Ipekjian’s later Details and Joinery tours at the Gamble House exist because the joints are still the argument. You can stand in Bedroom No. 1 and see that the furniture and the architecture share a hand: the same cloudlift family, the same peg family, the same refusal to cap a post with a cheap finial.

Labor is not a footnote. A Greene bed is many hours of fitting after the mill has done what a mill can do. The Halls employed men. The names on the surviving shop records are thinner than the architecture files. `[CITE NEEDED: named Hall-shop workmen other than the brothers for 1907–09 bedroom furniture, if the Greene and Greene archive yields them.]` What is not in doubt is the division of work: Greenes designed, Halls built, Gambles paid for a winter house that was never meant to be reproduced as SKU 4 in walnut.

Compare a Gustav Stickley bed of the same years — Craftsman Workshops, Eastwood, New York, a numbered form in a catalog, fumed quartersawn white oak, a price. Stickley wanted a middle-class ethic that could be sold through retailers. The Greenes wanted a room. Both are Arts and Crafts. They are not the same industry. A Roycroft twin bed from the Inn at East Aurora, model 0106 in oak, is closer to Stickley than to Pasadena: a catalog object with an orb-and-cross, heavy, medieval in the shoulders. The Gamble walnut bed has no brand burned into a rail. It has a house.

## Sleeping porches and the medical air

The second floor of the Gamble House is famous for its sleeping porches. Outdoor sleeping was not a Greene invention. It was a health fashion with a literature: tuberculosis, “fresh air,” the belief that a night in moving air was a treatment. American architects of the 1900s put sleeping porches on bungalows from Pasadena to the Midwest. The Greenes made them into timber cages with views, rafter tails, and a relationship to the garden that is Japanese in the sense Bosley names — wood-building cultures, not a pagoda appliqué.

Aunt Julia’s sleeping porch is the one later guests remember, because the house has used it as a teaching bed: a simple frame, a table, the Arroyo and the mountains framed by the balcony rail. Bedroom No. 1 has its own access to air. The bed inside the room and the bed on the porch are two different objects doing two different jobs. The walnut frame is for the winter nights when Pasadena is cold enough that you want walls. The porch is for the nights when the argument is air. A factory suite cannot sell that distinction. It can only sell a matching dresser.

The porch also explains why the interior furniture stays relatively low. You do not put a tall-post tester in a Greene bedroom. The ceiling is timber, the lights are Tiffany or Greene-designed, the Japanese prints and the stained glass already occupy the vertical. The bed is a platform of joinery, not a textile machine. Chapter 2’s hung colonial bed is the opposite problem. Here the wood is the cloth. You see every peg.

## What collectors do to the type

Greene & Greene furniture that left other houses — Blacker, Pratt, Thorsen — now lives in LACMA, the Huntington, private collections, and auction rooms where a cloudlift armchair can outprice a Philadelphia chair that took the same hours in 1770. The Gamble House is the intact exception: architecture and furniture still in the rooms they were cut for. That is why this chapter stands on Bedroom No. 1 instead of on a gallery grouping. A gallery grouping is already a suite. The Greenes did not design a suite. They designed a sleeping floor.

Reproductions are a separate industry. Competent makers copy cloudlifts and ebony pegs; some of them, Ipekjian among them, have the skill to make the joint true. A reproduction bed in a suburban bedroom is still not a Gamble bed, because it does not have the porch, the teak hall, or Mary Gamble’s closet wall. It is a style. The original is a commission.

The Greenes’ later work thins out. Charles moved north. Henry stayed. The Halls kept building. The 1907–09 cluster — Gamble, Blacker, Pratt, Thorsen overlapping — is the moment when the bedroom furniture and the house are one drawing. After that, American bedrooms go back to catalogs: Mission oak by the carload, then Colonial Revival highboys, then the waterfall veneer that will pretend to be Art Deco. Pasadena in 1908 is a dead end in the best sense. You cannot scale it. You can only visit it, or lie about it on a furniture-mall tag.

## Residual

The walnut night table is fifteen inches deep. It holds what a 1908 dressing routine needed within arm’s reach of a bed that does not match a factory number. Downstairs, teak and mahogany perform for guests. Upstairs, the Greenes let the sleeping room be walnut, pegged, cloudlifted, and open to a porch. The American bedroom industry spent the next twenty years selling the opposite: a set you could photograph as complete. The Gamble House is complete in a different way. It is finished because the Halls stopped when the drawings were built, not because a catalog page ran out of pieces.
