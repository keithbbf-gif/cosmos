Charles Hall, a design student at San Francisco State, showed an eight-foot-square heated water mattress called the Pleasure Pit at a 1968 gallery event, “The Happy Happening,” on Leavenworth Street (some later accounts say the Cannery). He had tried starch and Jell-O. They stank and weighed too much. Water in a vinyl bladder, with heat, became the object. Innerspace Environments manufactured. U.S. Patent 3,585,356, “Liquid Support for Human Bodies,” issued 15 June 1971. Hugh Hefner ordered a king covered in Tasmanian opossum for the Playboy Mansion. Jefferson Airplane and a Smothers Brother appear in the early press. Bloomingdale’s later stocked waterbeds. By 1978 the *New York Times* could run “The Waterbed Has Come Out of the Closet”: Abraham Herrar of the Waterbed Factory in San Francisco selling 1,500 a year, frames in Early American and French Provincial, a $139 pine single and a $1,900 king with a mirrored canopy. Ike Fogel of the waterbed association remembered the 1960s as a joke that had become a product.

The Craftsman bed — oak, slats, a pinned tenon — did not have a good decade in the showroom. It was attic furniture, or a dealer’s “mission,” or a brown rectangle a Mediterranean suite was designed to replace. Stickley had gone bankrupt long before. L. & J.G.’s successor would revive Craftsman later. In 1974 the American bedroom wanted either a bag of water, a brass post, or a carved oak cathedral panel with antiqued brass bail pulls.

## Vinyl, heat, and a frame that had to look like furniture

Hall’s first public mattress was not a bedroom suite. It was a pit, a happening, a Haight-Ashbury object in a listing Victorian that rented for sixty-seven dollars in one later telling. `[CITE NEEDED: Hall’s 1968 rent figure is in the 2018 *Times* profile; confirm before a caption.]` The patent is a liquid support, not a highboy. To enter the American house the bladder needed a box: plywood, pine, a liner, a heater that did not electrocute, a thermostat, a fill kit, a fear of leaks that never quite left. Two thousand pounds of water on a second floor is an engineering problem the Craftsman slat never posed. Landlords banned them. Leases said so. The waterbed store became a specialist retail type, a 1970s Main Street with posters and a demo bed you were invited to lie on.

The heater is a furniture part. It sits between platform and bladder, a grid of wires in a sheath, a thermostat on a cord. Failures are fires and cold nights. Safety standards arrived after the happening, as they always do. Vinyl seams are radio-frequency welds, a factory skill, not a cabinetmaker’s. A patch kit in the nightstand drawer is the 1970s equivalent of a bed wrench. The Craftsman wrench tightened rope. The waterbed patch stopped a marriage from flooding the downstairs ceiling.

Frames did the cultural work. A pine box is honest and looks cheap. A “Mediterranean” carved frame, a “Colonial” cannonball, a brass four-poster around a vinyl bag: the 1978 *Times* list is the chapter. The water is the mattress. The wood or brass is the furniture. Hire-purchase logic returns — specialist stores, terms, a complete “waterbed system” that is heater plus liner plus frame plus optional canopy. Completeness is a kit, not a Sears veneer outfit, but the sales psychology is kin. Newlyweds, apartments, the couple who want the bed to be a topic. Hall’s patent was widely ignored; he said the early industry was too small and too fly-by-night to sue. By 1985, trade figures in later reporting put annual sales in the millions of units. `[CITE NEEDED: the 4 million figure in 1985 reporting — trade association vs. journalism.]`

Mirrored canopies over waterbeds are Hollywood Regency’s late, cheap grandchild: glass, chrome, a ceiling you see yourself in. French Provincial waterbed frames are a Depression memory in new pine. Early American waterbeds are Nutting’s posts without Nutting’s mill. The bladder does not care. It wants a rectangular cavity and a heater. All styles are cavities.

## Brass revival

Victorian brass beds had been a sanitary modernism around 1900: metal, washable, no tester required. They went to the attic or the junk man when oak and then walnut veneer took the room. The 1970s brought them back as nostalgia, sex, and a photographable post. Hollow brass tubing, often imported, polished, with ceramic or brass knobs, a high head, a footboard, sometimes a canopy rail for a plant or a piece of macramé. The American mill could make them; so could factories in the brass belt; so, increasingly, could overseas shops. A 1970s brass bed is rarely a nineteenth-century bed. The joints are different. The tubing is thinner. The “antique brass” is a finish.

Next to the waterbed, brass is dry. Next to Mediterranean oak, brass is light. A house might put brass in the guest room and a waterbed in the master, or brass around the water. The Craftsman oak bed, if it appeared, was a flea-market find for a different taste — the beginning of the Arts and Crafts revival that would explode in the 1980s–90s collecting boom (Cathers, Clark’s 1972 show as a scholarly start). In 1975 that revival was not the floor at Levitz. Brass was.

Joinery on a 1970s brass bed is mechanical: bolts, a knuckle, a rail that is steel painted or brass-sleeved. Compare a Stickley pinned tenon. The honesty has moved to shine. You polish brass. You oil teak. You ignore Mediterranean pecan until it chips. Three maintenance cultures, three bedrooms.

A nineteenth-century brass bed, if an original survived, has cast fittings, heavier gauge, a different screw. Dealers in the 1970s sold both, and mixed parts. A “Victorian brass” in a 1976 classified ad is a bet. Look at the rail iron. Look at the knob’s seam. The revival mill made a lot of them because the posts photographed in a shag-carpet room next to a macramé owl. Hollywood had put white on posts. The 1970s put shine. The highboy stayed in the dining room if it had not already gone to a museum.

## Mediterranean suites

Bassett, Stanley, Drexel Heritage, Thomasville, the southern plants: Spanish Revival, Mediterranean, “Mediterranean oak,” pecan, fruitwood, flyspeck distressing, cathedral-arch panels, antiqued brass bails that look like stirrups. A 1970s Bassett triple dresser with a wagon label, nine drawers, arched center doors, is a common surviving object. Stanley’s 4213 Fruitwood, carved panels, pull-out trays. Drexel’s Mediterranean Heritage bedroom: double dresser, headboard with openwork, nightstands, a “masculine” copywriter’s word. The suite is back, heavier than waterfall, darker than wheat, carved where McCobb had refused carving.

This is not Spanish colonial furniture. It is a 1960s–70s American mill style, overlapping “Spanish Modern” and a suburban idea of a hacienda. Distressed finishes hide gum and particle board. Drawer boxes may still be oak or pine with dovetails on better lines; cheaper lines are stapled. The headboard is a wall of carving, tall again — the platform’s low line rejected. Posts return as finials. The Craftsman slat is the opposite of this panel. The customer who bought Mediterranean was often the child of waterfall or the buyer who had never liked teak. Dark, carved, complete. Time payments still applied.

Open a Bassett Mediterranean drawer. The wagon label is gold paper. The interior may be oak, a better surprise than the distressed pecan face. Flyspeck — black flecks in the finish — is a gun’s work, a factory distressing that imitates age the Nutting mill would have been ashamed to fake this way and the 1920s collector would have called dishonest. The 1970s wanted the look of an heirloom without the Girl Scouts’ highboy. Applied moldings make cathedral arches on a flat panel. They come off in a move. Glue residue is a dating tool.

Lane’s cedar chests continued. Ethan Allen sold Early American to a parallel public. The bedroom floor of a 1974 furniture store was a war of ancestries: Mediterranean, Colonial, “Modern” leftover, brass, and a waterbed in a separate alcove because of the demo’s slosh. Stickley oak, if present, was in antiques, not in the new-goods warehouse.

## What happened to the Craftsman bed

It waited. 1972’s Princeton Arts and Crafts exhibition (Robert Judson Clark) is a scholarly date. Dealers in oak existed. The mass bedroom did not go fumed. The Craftsman bed’s decade is a negative: not in the ads, not in the waterbed store, not in the Mediterranean suite. A few architects and a few collectors kept oak. The rest of America put the Stickley, if they had one, in a den or sold it. Reproduction Craftsman would come later, a different mill, a different customer, sometimes the same last name in Manlius.

The waterbed, ironically, could sit in a pine frame that looked more “Craftsman” than the Mediterranean oak: simple posts, a slat, a box. Honesty of a kind — you could see it was a box for water — without Stickley’s tenon. The heater was the modern part. The pine was the moral part. Neither was Eastwood, New York.

Hall, later, made money on other patents (the Sun Shower among them) more reliably than on a royalty for every bladder. The industry outgrew the happening. Middle America, the 1978 *Times* said, had discovered waterbeds. Families bought them because they came in styles. That sentence is the American bedroom in one move: an invention from a design class, absorbed by the suite idea, offered as Early American. The posts that Nelson removed came back as brass. The oak Stickley had fumed came back as Mediterranean carving that Stickley would have hated. The vinyl bladder was new. The sales idea was 1934’s completeness, heated.

## Residual

A 1971 patent number on a heater tag, a Bassett wagon label in a carved drawer, and a brass bed whose tubing is too thin to be Victorian: three documents from the same decade, often from the same house at different years. The Craftsman bed’s absence is the fourth document. It will return as a collectible, a reproduction, a moral rectangle for a later buyer tired of brass and vinyl. In the 1970s it was not the argument. The argument was whether the mattress should slosh, shine, or look like a Spanish door. Hall’s water, the brass mill’s shine, the southern plant’s cathedral panel. Sleep, as usual, happened anyway.

Fill kits, hoses, a waterbed store’s loan of a pump: the labor of owning a bladder is closer to a garden than to a chest of drawers. You empty it to move. You treat the water. You do not drag a two-thousand-pound mattress down a stair. The Craftsman bed knocked down into posts and rails a man could carry. The waterbed’s furniture — the pine box — is what you carry. The mattress is a plumbing event. That is the decade’s new joint: a hose clamp where a tenon used to be.

Waveless mattresses — baffles inside the vinyl — are a later product, a mill’s answer to the slosh joke. They still need the frame. The 1970s argument this chapter cares about is the first decade, when the kit was heater, liner, bag, and a style shell. Brass, Mediterranean, Early American: three shells, one bladder.
