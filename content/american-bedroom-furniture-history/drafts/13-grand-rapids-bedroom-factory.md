In 1859 Julius Berkey and James Eggleston started making sash, doors, and blinds in a small shop on Erie Street in Grand Rapids. Door trim. Not beds. Julius, a mechanic, also made quartette stands — small tables — on the side. William A. Berkey had a similar millwork factory on Mill Street. In 1860 Julius and Alphonso Ham were using part of that building’s second floor for furniture. The winter after, Julius sold his interest in Berkey and Eggleston to his partner, who wound the sash shop up. In April 1861 Julius started again at the same place, furniture this time, alone until November 1862, when Elias Matter came in and the firm became Berkey & Matter. Julius put in about five dollars in cash and a few hundred dollars’ worth of machinery and materials. Matter inventoried his tool chest at about six dollars. Those figures come from a Grand Rapids institutional memory (Robert W. Merrill is named as the source in the later company sketches) and should be treated as lore with a price tag, not as a audited ledger. `[CITE NEEDED: the 1891 or 1926 company history page that quotes the five-dollar / six-dollar start, before a caption uses the numbers as fact.]`

Wholesale furniture from Grand Rapids is the thing Julius is credited with seeing. Ham, in the same reminiscence, prophesied a furniture city. Whether the prophecy was that neat does not matter as much as the sequence: millwork, small tables, a partnership, then a factory that would sell chamber suites to retailers who had never met a cabinetmaker named Berkey.

## Incorporation, the six-story plant, the Centennial

William A. Berkey bought a half interest on 5 October 1863; the name became Berkey Bros. & Co.; capital stock is given as $17,215.33 in the same institutional narratives. `[CITE NEEDED: confirm capital-stock figure against a primary incorporation or the 1891 sketch.]` In 1866 George W. Gay bought William’s half. Berkey Bros. & Gay. Elias Matter withdrew 28 February 1870. William A. Berkey withdrew in January 1873. In August 1873 the Berkey & Gay Furniture Company was incorporated, Julius Berkey president, George W. Gay treasurer, John A. Covode secretary. Capital stock $500,000 in the company histories and in Time’s later gloss. Time also says “twenty years after 1853,” which does not match the 1859 Erie Street start; the magazine was rounding a hero tale in 1932. Prefer 1859 for trim, 1873 for incorporation.

In 1874 the new company opened a six-story factory in Grand Rapids, wholesale and retail showrooms in the same building. Additional showrooms in New York City in 1875. By 1882, nomination histories say, the plant covered three city blocks. `[CITE NEEDED: 1882 three-block claim against a Sanborn or a period description.]`

The 1876 Philadelphia Centennial is the sales event. Two Neo-Grec chamber suites by Berkey & Gay won recognition of high merit. Brian L. Witherell’s *Late 19th Century Furniture by Berkey & Gay* (Schiffer, 1998) — the catalog book this chapter should keep on the table — uses that award as the firm’s public arrival. Time, in 1932, called it a gold medal for a suite by Berkey and claimed the Eastern market opened there. “Gold medal” and “high merit” are not always the same sentence in Centennial paperwork. Witherell’s “recognition of high merit” for two Neo-Grec chamber suites is the version to prefer unless the official awards list is in hand. `[CITE NEEDED: official Centennial awards listing, exact wording.]` What is not in doubt is the product: chamber suites, Neo-Grec, shown as a set, in Philadelphia, in the year America put its manufactures on display. Chapter 12’s photograph logic had a world’s-fair stage.

Neo-Grec for a bedroom meant classical grooving, palmettes, angularity, a French-archaeological fashion that factories could incise and parcel-gilt. It sat beside Renaissance Revival walnut in the same plant. Grand Rapids did not owe allegiance to one revival. It owed allegiance to the suite.

## What they sold, and to whom

The main market was wholesale to retailers: whole suites of bedroom, dining, and office furniture. In the last quarter of the nineteenth century the company produced Gothic, Rococo, Renaissance, Neo-Grec, Eastlake, Empire — the Victorian stack. Higher-end pieces in walnut and mahogany with burled veneer panels. Lower-end suites in ash. Some early drawers used red cedar on sides and ends against moths. Marks: 1870s black stencil under; “B&G” sometimes on marble undersides; 1880s–90s paper labels, “From Berkey & Gay Furniture Co. Grand Rapids, Mich.”; around 1905 a branded disc of concentric rings or a brass badge in drawers. After 1929, “DIVISION OF SIMMONS CO.” on some marks, not always.

Witherell’s book reproduces more than three hundred photographs from company catalogs. That is the visual archive for the chamber suites: walnut, mahogany, ash; beds with high headboards; dressers with marble and mirrors; washstands that match. Value ranges in a 1998 Schiffer book are dealer apparatus, not this magazine’s concern. The plates are.

By 1885, company histories say, sales were over half a million dollars, worldwide. `[CITE NEEDED: 1885 sales figure against an annual report or a period newspaper; the $500,000 is widely repeated and may be rounding the incorporation capital together with sales lore.]` Until a primary appears, treat “over a half million” as the standard secondary claim, marked.

Grand Rapids as a furniture city is not a Berkey monopoly. Phoenix, Nelson Matter, the Grand Rapids Chair Company (which by the late 1880s advertised medium-grade chamber suites in maple, birch, cherry, walnut, and ash), later dozens of plants. The 1876 Centennial and the semi-annual furniture markets made the town a destination for buyers. A retailer from the Plains could walk a showroom and order in lots. Berkey & Gay’s New York showroom (17 Elizabeth Street appears in later decorative-arts notes) was the Eastern counter. `[CITE NEEDED: confirm Elizabeth Street address and dates.]`

The semi-annual Grand Rapids furniture markets — buyers in town, showrooms dressed, hotels full — are the other half of wholesale. A retailer who will never own a six-story plant can walk Neo-Grec and Eastlake and golden oak in a week and place orders that keep the mill on night shifts. Time’s 1932 story still remembers those shows as something Simmons wanted to kill in favor of warehouse distribution. The show is a live version of Bayne’s photograph: the suite as a room you can stand in. Julius’s early “quartette stands,” small tables made beside sash, are the seed of a mind that thought in pieces that could multiply. Gay’s capital, when he bought in, is what let pieces become floors.

## Labor and the six-story stack

A six-story furniture factory is a vertical mill: rough mill, machine room, cabinet room, carving, veneer, finish, packing. Steam power. Later electricity. The chamber suite is a routing problem: a headboard and a dresser splash that share a carved panel must come from the same pattern shop. Marble from outside contractors. Glass for mirrors. Brass. Cedar lining as a selling point against moths.

Grand Rapids’s workforce was immigrant and native, male on the machines, with women in some finish and upholstery tasks depending on the decade. Exact Berkey & Gay headcounts fluctuate in secondary sources; do not invent a payroll. `[CITE NEEDED: a year-specific employment figure from a city directory, census of manufactures, or company report.]` Injuries, dust, finish fumes — the ordinary furniture-plant costs — rarely enter the catalog photographs Bayne and his peers made.

The factory is also a showroom. Wholesale and retail on site, 1874, means a buyer can walk past the noise into a room of suites. That walk is part of the product. Cincinnati’s Mitchell & Rammelsberg had already shown that the Midwest could factory-make revival furniture. Grand Rapids added the furniture city as a brand: not one firm, a place.

## 1920s, Simmons, bankruptcy, after

In 1920, some corporate histories say, Berkey & Gay merged with Wallace Furniture and Grand Rapids Upholstery as Consolidated Furniture Companies, still using the Berkey & Gay trademark. `[CITE NEEDED: 1920 consolidation details.]` Sales in the 1920s were strong; the firm expanded. Period reproductions — “fine” historical styles — were a Grand Rapids specialty. Time in 1932 said Berkey & Gay was doing nearly $10 million a year when Simmons bought it, and making most of Grand Rapids’s share (33 percent) of fine period reproductions in the United States. Those Time figures are journalism in a Depression story, useful and not audited here. `[CITE NEEDED: 1929 sales; Time’s $10 million vs. other sketches that say $8 million and five plants totaling 1.5 million square feet.]` Secondary dealer histories give five plants, 1.5 million square feet, $8 million annual sales in 1929. Mark both.

Simmons Company of Chicago bought Berkey & Gay in 1929. Bedman Simmons wanted to abolish exclusive dealerships, let the semi-annual shows lapse, and push Berkey & Gay through Simmons warehouses with national advertising — a furniture General Motors, Time sneered, aimed at $20 million a year. Simmons stock fell from $188 a share in September 1929 to $11 in 1930. The furniture industry’s insolvency list ballooned. Berkey & Gay closed plants in 1931, went into receivership in February 1932 (Time). Other sketches say bankruptcy in 1931. The chronology that fits the surviving marks and local histories: purchase 1929; shutdown/receivership 1931–32; local stockholders reopen 1935; World War II conversion; postwar attempt; bankruptcy and close 1948. The name later sold to Harvest Furniture of Louisville. `[CITE NEEDED: Harvest sale documents.]`

Marks after 1929 may read DIVISION OF SIMMONS CO. A 1 January 1930 price list, “Berkey & Gay Furniture Co., division of Simmons Co.,” dining room, bedroom, living room, 97 pages, sits in the Grand Rapids History Center. That pamphlet is the suite idea at the edge of the cliff: still listing bedrooms, already a division.

The 1935 revival produced Traditional, Art Deco, and Modern. It did not recover the Centennial plant’s authority. 1948 is the end of the factory, not of the suites still in American bedrooms.

## Witherell, and what a catalog book can do

Brian L. Witherell, *Late 19th Century Furniture by Berkey & Gay* (Atglen, PA: Schiffer, 1998), 320 pages, 300-plus catalog photographs, is a dealer’s reference that happens to be the easiest public corpus of the chamber suites. Gothic Revival and Eastlake as the styles the jacket mentions; Neo-Grec Centennial suites as the prize. Use the plates as plates. Use the biography as secondary, checkable against the 1891 city history and the factory nomination. Do not let 1998 price ranges into a magazine caption.

The National Register nomination for the Berkey and Gay Furniture Company Factory is the architectural file: mill construction, the physical plant that outlived the firm. The building is not the suite. It is where the suite was a routing problem.

## Residual

Door trim in 1859. A six-story factory in 1874. Neo-Grec chamber suites under glass in Philadelphia in 1876. Ash and walnut wholesale rooms for retailers who would never meet Julius. A Chicago bedding company that thought a Grand Rapids name could be a national warehouse line. Receivership. A 1935 second life. Silence in 1948.

The matching suite did not die with the plant. It had already taught the country what a bedroom looked like in a photograph. Walk an American classified ad for a “vintage bedroom set” and you are still in Berkey & Gay’s 1876 idea, even when the labels say otherwise. The factory closed. The set did not.
