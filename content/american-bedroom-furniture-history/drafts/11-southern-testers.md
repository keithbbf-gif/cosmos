A Charleston tall-post bed of about 1800, mahogany, the footposts turned, reeded, and carved with a swelling that collectors will call rice whether the carver intended grain or not, is the object this chapter has to slow down. Middleton Place still shows one of the type: square, relatively plain headposts, show carving on the footposts, no full tester rail required in summer if netting can drape from a lighter frame or from the posts themselves. E. Milby Burton, in *Charleston Furniture, 1700–1825*, noted that so much mahogany arrived from the Caribbean and South America in bed-post sizes that cabinetmakers used “bed post” as a timber measure. The expensive wood came in as posts. The climate asked for height. The mosquitoes asked for netting. The nickname asked for a sheaf of rice.

MESDA — the Museum of Early Southern Decorative Arts, Winston-Salem — is the check on the nickname and on the map. Southern furniture is not one Charleston shop. The Chesapeake, the Lowcountry, the backcountry, the Gulf, each has woods, joinery, and labor that a single “rice bed” caption erases. This chapter stays with Charleston and the Chesapeake, where testers and tall posts are thickest in the record, and with the people who made them, many of whom do not have surnames on the bills.

## Height as climate

A New England pencil-post bed can be tall. A Charleston or Annapolis bed is often taller still, or feels taller, because the posts are stout, the ceilings are high, and the hangings in summer are net, not wool. Heat is the first fact. A cloth box that made sense in a Massachusetts January is a punishment in an August chamber on the Cooper River. So the southern tester is a seasonal machine. Winter: a wooden frame, sometimes added, with moreen or a heavier stuff. Summer: netting against mosquitoes that carried malaria and yellow fever, the “summer fevers.” Charleston Magazine’s later popular account is right on the netting even when it is loose on Thomas Elfe. The posts exist to get cloth off the sleeper and into the air.

Rails at the top of the posts are not mandatory on every surviving “rice bed.” Some never had a full tester. Some had one that was removed when the wood became the antique. The honest object file says whether there are mortises or iron fittings at the post tops. A bed photographed without a tester is not proof it never had one. Chapter 2 already warned about naked posts. The southern version of the warning is: naked posts may mean summer netting, lost winter furniture, or a dealer who wanted the carving to show.

Mosquito netting is bed furniture in the eighteenth-century sense — textiles — even when it is thin. It wears out faster than moreen. Survival bias therefore favors the posts. Museums in the South as in the North show the lesser half of the phrase unless they reconstruct the net.

## Woods: cypress, walnut, pine, mahogany

Mahogany was the prestige timber in Charleston, imported in diameters that could turn a post. Cherry appears. Walnut is the Chesapeake’s ordinary fine wood, as it is in the Mid-Atlantic. Yellow pine is structural and secondary, and sometimes primary in plainer beds. Bald cypress — *Taxodium*, the Lowcountry’s swamp wood — is the regional sentence: resistant to rot and insects, used for built-in woodwork, sometimes for furniture, always for the houses whose chambers held the beds. MESDA’s White Hall drawing-room woodwork, black cypress cut on the place, is architecture, not a bed, but it is the same labor and the same tree. A cypress bedstead in a damp climate is a material choice, not a rustic aesthetic.

Secondary woods in Charleston cabinetwork often include yellow pine and cypress. Drawer bottoms, bed rails’ inner faces, glue blocks: local. Show surfaces: import when the patron can pay. Jennifer Anderson’s mahogany story is Atlantic and enslaved at the cutting end; the Charleston end is a shop that turns that timber into posts while enslaved joiners, hired out or held by the cabinetmaker, do a share of the work that the account book prices as the master’s.

A pine or walnut Chesapeake bed with turned posts and a tester is not a failed Charleston mahogany object. It is a different market: Williamsburg, Annapolis, rural tidewater. MESDA’s object database is where those beds should be compared, not a Charleston nickname laid north onto the Bay.

## The “rice bed” caution

Plantation owners liked footposts carved with stalks. The Middleton Place example, probably a local cabinetmaker around 1800, has low-relief bunched stalks, blades, seeded stems, on the swelling of the footposts above the waist. *American Heritage*’s 1990 note on that bed is careful on a point popular writing forgets: there is no evidence that rice-post beds originated in Charleston, and the carving looks like a local variant of the English wheat-sheaf, a fertility motif already at home on London furniture. Rice was the Lowcountry’s gold. Of course a Charleston carver might mean rice. A reeded post with a leafy swelling is not automatically a botanical illustration.

Thomas Elfe (1719–1775) is often credited as the originator of the rice bed. It cannot be substantiated. Elfe’s account book shows a large Charleston shop — hundreds of chairs, more than a thousand pieces over the years the book covers — and it shows enslaved labor as part of the workforce. That is the Elfe fact that matters. “Elfe invented the rice bed” is a tourist sentence.

Not every reeded southern post is rice. Reeding is a Federal turning habit from Boston to Savannah. Not every tall post is Charleston. The Chesapeake made tall posts. New England made tall posts. The nickname does collector work: it makes a bed a souvenir of Carolina Gold. MESDA’s files, and Bradford Rauschenberg and John Bivins’s *The Furniture of Charleston, 1680–1820*, are the literature that can say when a carving is a sheaf and when it is a reed. If the object file does not say rice, the caption should not.

Headposts are often plain, square or lightly tapered, because the bed stood with the head to the wall. Footposts are the show. That is the same logic as the French bed’s long-side veneer, solved with posts instead of a sleigh. Carving budget follows the eye.

## Enslaved labor in the shop and on the place

Charleston cabinetmaking in the decades around 1800 employed white masters, journeymen, apprentices, and enslaved craftsmen, including men hired out by their owners. Newspaper advertisements, account books, and court records are the surviving voices, and they are the owners’ voices. A VCU thesis that mines this material for 1760–1800 Charleston shops — “‘An Amazing Aptness for Learning Trades’” — is one path through the ads and Elfe’s book. The South Carolina Encyclopedia’s summary of Elfe’s labor (cabinetmakers, journeymen, slaves; hiring-out charges that imply skill) is the short version. Named enslaved cabinetmakers appear in ads when they run or when they are offered for sale. That is a hideous documentation standard, and it is the one the archive has.

On plantations, the bed in the big house and the tick in the quarter are not the same object class. John Michael Vlach’s *Back of the Big House* remains the architectural corrective. MESDA’s work on White Hall’s woodwork names enslaved carpenters in the Porcher orbit — Jacky, Cyrus, Old Dick, and earlier Sharloe, Jemmy, Jupiter — because wills bothered to identify skill. A “slave called Black Washington” appears in a later reminiscence as the man who did interior decorations at a cypress house built in 1812. Those names are carpenters and house joiners, not necessarily bedstead makers. They belong here anyway. The same trained hands that could run a molding on a drawing-room door could turn or assemble a post. The inventory line that says “bedstead” in the parlor chamber does not name them.

A history of southern testers that lists only Elfe, Hepplewhite plates, and mahogany is a history of who paid. The people who slept in the house’s lesser beds — enslaved people in rooms, lofts, kitchens, and purpose-built quarters — had furniture that inventories undervalue or skip. Truckle beds, pine frames, ticks on the floor. Netting, if they had it, was not a carved post. The mosquito does not respect the inventory.

Free Black cabinetmakers in southern ports exist in the later record and should not be collapsed into enslavement. When a name and a shop can be documented, use them. When they cannot, do not invent a representative man to stand in.

## Elfe’s book, and what a post costs

Elfe’s surviving account book is the Charleston document people cite without opening. Over the years it covers he produces on the order of 537 chairs and, in one tally used in later theses, something like 1,502 pieces of furniture before his death in November 1775. Those counts are chairs-and-all, not bedsteads. What the book is good for is the shop as a mixed labor system: apprentices, journeymen, enslaved men whose hire is a line item. A bedstead in such a book can look modest next to the upholsterer’s or mercer’s bill for the pavilion — the mosquito furniture — just as a Massachusetts tester looked modest next to feathers. Burton’s “bed post” mahogany is the import measure. The turning of that post, in a shop that also ran chairs by the dozen, is piecework even when the footpost gets a sheaf.

If a caption needs a price, take it from a named page of the account book or not at all. Invented Elfe quotes are how the rice bed got an inventor.

## Chesapeake beside Charleston

Williamsburg ranked after Charleston as a southern cabinetmaking center in the older surveys. The Chesapeake’s beds are walnut and pine as often as mahogany, with London and Philadelphia patterns arriving by print and by immigrant journeymen. Tall posts, testers, and netting for the same insects, in a climate that is only relatively milder. MESDA’s Virginia and Maryland beds are the comparison set: less likely to be called rice, more likely to be called just high-post.

Joinery: posts lathe-turned or octagonal, rails hooked or bolted, rope or sacking bottoms, casters on better beds. Southern posts are often assembled in sections — a square plinth, a turned vase, a reeded shaft, a block for a tester iron — so a broken top can be replaced without turning a new eight-foot stick. Look for the iron pin or the wooden dowel at those joints. A “married” rice bed is sometimes two good footposts and two later headposts. The sheaf, if it is a sheaf, will not match the head. Cypress in the Lowcountry; yellow pine almost everywhere as secondary. A Charleston bed with mahogany posts and pine rails is ordinary, not a marriage. A marriage is two posts from one bed and two from another, a dealer’s height.

Bills: Elfe’s account book is the famous Charleston document. Individual Chesapeake shop books are thinner in publication. Williamsburg reconstructions at Colonial Williamsburg show hung beds with reproduction textiles; they are arguments, as in chapter 2. Use them as climate demonstrations — netting, height, summer and winter — not as specific cabinetmaker attributions.

## After the posts

By the 1830s–50s a southern chamber can also hold an Empire sleigh if the house is fashionable and heated enough to care. The tester does not vanish. Rural and conservative houses keep tall posts into the late nineteenth century, and the Colonial Revival will love them again, especially the “rice” ones. Reproduction rice-post beds are a Charleston industry of their own. They photograph. They sell a grain economy as a bedroom style. The enslaved knowledge that made Carolina Gold possible is not in the carving. It is in the field, and in the shops where the posts were turned.

## Residual

Call a post rice when the carving is a sheaf and the object file will stand behind the word. Call it reeded when it is reeded. Call the height climate. Call the netting the expensive, missing half. And when the account book prices a bedstead under a white cabinetmaker’s name, leave room on the line for the unnamed joiner who could be hired out by the week. MESDA’s named carpenters at White Hall — Jacky, Cyrus, Old Dick — are not bed labels. They are what a southern furniture history looks like when the will bothers to write a skill down.
