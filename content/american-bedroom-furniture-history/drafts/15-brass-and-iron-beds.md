# Brass and Iron Beds

In 1837 Thomas Lawson, Surgeon General of the United States Army, wanted better hospital equipment. Wooden bedsteads in wards were hard to clean and easy for vermin. He had one single and one double metal bedstead made and sent to the War Department that fall. Fifteen dollars each. The experiment was good enough that Congress appropriated money for more, and the Army began buying metal bedsteads for hospitals and barracks. In December 1842 Charles P. Curtis of New Haven received a U.S. patent for an improved iron bedstead, aimed in part at institutional use, prisons included. The patent talked about durability, cleanliness, and resistance to vermin. Iron beds, the patent implied, had already been around long enough to improve.

That is the American institutional start, earlier than the parlor brass bed of the 1880s–1910s that this chapter also has to cover. The hospital wanted a frame you could wash. The later middle-class bedroom wanted the same argument in nickel and brass, with knobs, without a tester. Eastlake had tried to reform the wooden chamber. Metal tried to leave wood.

## Washable, on purpose

Cast iron and later tubular brass and iron do not harbor bedbugs the way a rope-rail maple frame with a tick does — or that was the claim. You can enamel iron. You can wipe brass. You can take the bedstead apart, boil what will boil, and move it on casters (porcelain or wooden; later dealers will cut the casters off because they mark parquet). Compared with a walnut suite headboard full of grooves, the metal bed is a diagram of sanitation.

Victorian England’s brass and iron beds — often called French beds in the English trade, confusingly for readers of the Lannuier chapter — were a Birmingham specialty. Hoskins and Sewell and their peers filled catalogs. White enamel and black enamel. Brass knobs. Cheaper iron with transfers that pretended to more brass than the bed had. The middle class in the 1890s–1900s liked white enamel. Institutions liked plain iron. The English story matters because American bedrooms imported it and then made it.

Kenrick — Archibald Kenrick and Sons, West Bromwich — is ironfounding, hollow-ware, builders’ ironmongery, enamelling (Timothy Kenrick’s 1846 patent for glazing and enamelling cast iron). In 1889 they acquired the bedstead-castor business of Parry and Wythes. That is the documented Kenrick bed connection in the company chronology: casters, not a famous American hospital contract. Do not promote Kenrick as an American bed factory. They are English hardware in an Atlantic metal bedroom: enamel knowledge, casters, the ironmonger’s side of a bed that is no longer a cabinetmaker’s object. `[CITE NEEDED: a specific Kenrick-marked bedstead in an American collection, if one is to be illustrated; otherwise keep Kenrick at casters and enamel.]`

American makers and importers: Charles P. Rogers & Co., New York, from 1855, manufacturing and importing brass and iron bedsteads — the firm’s own later histories call Rogers a pioneer in that dual role. Other New York and later Pennsylvania shops. Pittsburgh’s steel world, later in the century, sits behind cheaper iron. Catalog houses by 1900 will sell a metal bed the way Grand Rapids sold a walnut suite: a page, a number, freight.

A Science Museum (London) cast-iron hospital bed, second half of the nineteenth century, Wellcome collection, short, low, no springs, is the institutional aesthetic: not adjustable, not pretty, better than what some patients had at home. American ward beds follow the same refusal of theater.

## No tester

The metal bed of the 1880s–1910s, in a private house, usually has a head and a foot of metal tubing or castings, brass joints, no canopy. Hangings are the enemy. If cloth appears it is a light spread, not a box. The sanitation writers and the Eastlake dust argument have won the top of the bed. What they have not won is the rest of the room: the matching walnut dresser can still stand six feet away, marble-topped, unwashable, full of dustboards that do not get dusted.

So the metal bed is often a break in the suite. You can buy a brass bed from Rogers and a chiffonier from Grand Rapids. Catalogs will also sell painted metal chamber sets — bed plus side tables — but the classic American image is a brass bed in a room of wood. The photograph likes the shine. The suite logic of chapter 12 is dented, not destroyed.

Colonial Revival will sometimes put a metal bed in a “colonial” room, which is nonsense historically and common photographically. More often the Revival wants the four-poster back, cloth optional, sanitation ignored. The brass bed’s decades are a window when modernity in the chamber meant metal, not a pilgrim finial.

## Springs, mesh, and the mattress company

Once the frame is metal, the sleeping surface wants to be metal too. Woven-wire mesh, then coil springs, then the named mattress firms. Simmons is a bedding company before it is a Grand Rapids owner. The brass bed and the coil mattress are a pair in catalogs of 1900 even when the rest of the room is oak. Rope holes in a maple rail, chapter 2’s household tool, do not belong here. A bed wrench is useless on a socket.

Height changes. Metal beds with springs sit differently from a sacking tick in a four-post frame. Later hospital beds will add cranks; the nineteenth-century ward bed in the Science Museum still does not. Domestic brass beds add brass, not medicine.

Children’s iron cribs, white enamel, are the sanitation argument at small scale. They survive in attics with peeling paint that is now a lead problem. Period ads boast washability. They do not boast of what is in the enamel.

## Joinery that is not joinery

A cabinetmaker’s bed is mortise, tenon, peg, bolt. A metal bed is threaded fittings, cast sockets, iron lugs. Rails hook. The sleeping surface becomes wire mesh, then coil springs — the other sanitation and comfort revolution, Simmons and their competitors, overlapping the later brass-bed years. Simmons will buy Berkey & Gay in 1929; the bedding company wanted wooden suites as well as mattresses. In the 1890s the metal bed and the mattress trade are already kin: both sell against the old tick on rope.

Porcelain casters, brass knobs that unscrew (and get lost), iron that rusts if the enamel chips, brass that gets lacquered and then ugly when the lacquer fails — the conservation problems are not worm and shrinkage. They are plating and rust. A “brass” bed that is brass-plated iron is ordinary. Magnets tell. Honest catalogs said iron with brass. Dealers say brass.

Widths are period: three-foot, four-foot, four-foot-six. Modern mattresses do not fit. The same rail-extension problem as the Codman sleigh, in metal.

## Hospitals, barracks, steamships, hotels

Once the Army had shown the purchase, institutions followed. Hospitals, almshouses, prisons (Curtis’s patent), later hotels that wanted beds they could wipe between guests, steamships — Hoskins and Sewell’s later fame includes ship contracts in the English literature. American hotels of the 1890s–1910s full of brass beds are a type. The private bedroom borrows institutional cleanliness as a fashion, the way kitchens later borrow restaurant stainless.

Military and medical beds stay plain. Domestic beds put money into the knobs and the height of the head. Children’s metal cribs take the same logic: wash the frame, paint it white.

Vermin is the unpretty word on the 1842 patent. Bedbugs in wooden joints are a real nineteenth-century complaint. Metal is not magic — ticks and mattresses still harbor — but a wipeable frame is a better war than a carved walnut headboard. Reform, here, is literally a change of material.

## Kenrick, Birmingham, and the Atlantic hardware chain

Do not Americanize Kenrick. West Bromwich ironfounders, 1,200 employees claimed in 1914, hollow-ware and builders’ iron. Enamel from the 1846 patent forward. Bedstead casters from 1889. Brassfounding added with Thomas Pemberton in 1898. If an American metal bed has Kenrick casters, that is imported feet under a New York or Birmingham frame. Hardware travels. The cabinetmaker’s secondary-wood map (pine in New England, cypress in the Lowcountry) becomes, in this chapter, a foundry map: Birmingham, West Bromwich, New Haven, New York, later Pittsburgh.

American brass beds were not all imports. They were not all domestic. Rogers’s import-and-manufacture model is the honest dual. A magazine that calls every 1900 brass bed “American” or every one “English” is guessing.

Hotels and Pullman cars, steamships and boarding houses, buy metal because turnover is the hygiene. A wooden suite in a tourist hotel of 1880 is already tired; a brass bed can be wiped at noon. The private buyer who wants a brass bed in a walnut room is borrowing that hotel argument as status: we are as clean as an institution. It is a strange boast and a successful one.

Charles P. Rogers’s New York business — making and importing — means a catalog can mix Birmingham tubes and American assembly. Marks on rails and on caster sockets are the only honest nationalities. A “New York” brass bed may have English tubes and Kenrick feet and an American slat. The cabinetmaker’s secondary-wood map does not apply. Read stamps.

## What the metal bed refuses

It refuses the tester. It refuses to match the walnut dresser except by sitting in the same room. It refuses the cabinetmaker. It refuses, in the hospital version, to be pretty. The domestic brass version wants to be pretty *and* washable, which is why the knobs get so large.

It does not refuse the mattress trade. If anything it invites a modern mattress, springs, a different height. The sleeper’s body in 1910 on a brass bed with a coil mattress is not the sleeper of 1810 on sacking in a Lannuier frame. The chamber’s wood may still be Eastlake. The bed has changed species.

By the 1920s–30s painted metal and then cheaper tubular looks go institutional again, or juvenile. Wooden Revival beds take the middle-class chamber back. The brass bed waits in attics for a 1980s taste that will strip the enamel, cut the casters, and sell “Victorian romance,” which is almost the opposite of Lawson’s 1837 brief.

A “French” iron bed in an English catalog of 1880 is not Lannuier. It is a low metal bed with equal ends, sometimes a knob, sometimes a small brass rail. The word has drifted from ébéniste to foundry. American ads pick it up and confuse two centuries of bedroom French. Caption the metal one as metal.

## Residual

Fifteen dollars each, a single and a double, 1837, for the War Department. The American metal bed begins as a hospital purchase, not as a bedroom fashion. Kenrick’s name in this story is English enamel and, after 1889, casters. Charles P. Rogers is the New York importer-manufacturer to put on a catalog caption when the label supports it. The tester is gone because cloth was the dirt. What remains in a good surviving example is a frame you could wash and a knob someone polished. The suite, in the same room, still could not go in the tub.
