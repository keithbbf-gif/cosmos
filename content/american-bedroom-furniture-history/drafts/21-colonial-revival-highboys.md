# Colonial Revival Highboys

A Wallace Nutting Philadelphia Chippendale-style highboy, branded, with a paper label in the lower right drawer dated 1927 and numbered 992, stands about ninety-six inches high, forty-six wide, twenty-two deep. It is Massachusetts furniture pretending, with skill, to be 1770 Walnut Street. The bonnet top, the carved drawers, the cabriole legs and claw-and-ball feet are a lesson Nutting had already published in photographs. *Furniture Treasury* (1928–33) taught Americans what a highboy was supposed to look like. Then the Nutting shop in the Framingham–Ashland–Saugus orbit sold them a wooden copy they could put in a bedroom that had never seen a colonial chamber.

Thomas Andrew Denenberg’s *Wallace Nutting and the Invention of Old America* (Yale, 2003) is the book that keeps this from being a joke about fakes. Nutting (1861–1941) was a Congregational minister, a photographer of plows and sun-dappled lanes, a restorer of houses, a collector of seventeenth-century oak, and a manufacturer. He named the product Old America. The highboy is the eighteenth-century half of that invention. The oak joint stool is the seventeenth. The bedroom of 1925 was asked to hold both, plus a reproduction Windsor, and to feel like a genealogy.

## What Nutting made

The reproduction shop is documented in catalogs: *Colonial Reproductions by Wallace Nutting* (Ashland, Mass., 1921) with a price list; later general catalogs, the “Supreme Edition” among them, whose plates rhyme with *Furniture Treasury*. Windsor chairs were the volume business. Highboys were the monument. A ninety-six-inch case is not a hobby. It is a factory that can carve a Philadelphia shell, stack drawers on a waist, and brand the back or the drawer so the customer knows they bought Nutting and not a Grand Rapids “colonial.”

Construction is the tell if you have handled period Philadelphia work. Period high chests of the 1760s–70s — the type Morrison Heckscher catalogued at the Met, the type chapter 3’s Boston stack grew up to become in another city — have secondary woods (poplar, cedar, pine by region), hand-cut dovetails of a certain size, drawer bottoms whose grain direction is a shop habit, original brass outlines that leave a shadow when replaced. Met 18.110.4, the Pompadour high chest in Gallery 752, ninety-one and three-quarter inches of mahogany over yellow pine, tulip poplar, and northern white cedar, is the object the 1920s wanted to live next to. It is not a Nutting. It is the mountain. Nutting’s men were good. They were not 1770. Machine marks, a 1927 paper label, a brand, a mahogany that does not quite match the oxidation of a two-hundred-year-old surface: the object is honest as a Nutting and false as a Cadwalader.

A period Philadelphia upper case sits on the lower with a waist molding that is a joint, not a stripe of trim. Drawers in the best shops have bottoms whose grain runs as the bench taught — side to side is common — and dustboards between drawers in the better chests. Knee blocks on the cabriole are glued and carved as a family of curves; a revival knee block can look applied, a lump under varnish. Claw-and-ball feet on a 1770 chest have toes that were carved as anatomy; 1927 toes can be pattern-shop anatomy, regular as a catalog. Brasses: Nutting could buy good reproduction plates. Period brasses, if original, have a pin pattern and a backplate outline that a replaced set will not match. Heckscher’s catalogs treat hardware as dating, not jewelry. The same eye works on a Nutting, except the date is on the paper.

He also sold the idea that the highboy *was* the American ancestor. Not the hung bed. Not the six-board chest. The vertical case with a broken pediment became, in the 1920s living hall and then in the bedroom, the piece that proved a family had a past. It is a strange proof. Most colonial Americans did not own a Philadelphia high chest. They owned a chest, a bedstead, and a textile bill. Nutting’s camera and Nutting’s mill both preferred the spectacular.

Helen Comstock, writing in *Antiques* in 1961, still had to survey “The American Highboy” as if the type needed an introduction, and then, the same year, “Wallace Nutting and the Furniture Treasury in Retrospect.” The magazine had been the revival’s parish newsletter. By 1961 the parish was arguing with itself about reproductions.

## Collecting in the 1920s

The decade that made the highboy a national ancestor was a decade of auctions, *Antiques* (founded 1922), and private collections that later became museum wings. Howard Reifsnyder’s 1929 sale is a landmark in the trade. Francis P. Garvan gave Yale. Henry Francis du Pont was already absorbing Winterthur. J. Insley Blair, Luke Vincent Lockwood, Fiske Kimball — the same names recur as lenders and as taste. They wanted labeled Philadelphia, Newport block-and-shell, Boston japanned, Newport Townsend-Goddard if they could get it. They wanted the high chest as a peak. The Reifsnyder sale’s prices taught the newspapers that American furniture was a market like pictures. `[CITE NEEDED: a specific Reifsnyder lot — high chest hammer price — from the 1929 catalogue if used in a caption.]` Garvan’s gifts to Yale came with a civic sentence: this is national art. Du Pont’s rooms at Winterthur came with another: this is a house, reconstructed, with the high chest in a chamber that is still a period room’s idea of sleep. Both sentences are more serious than Nutting’s mail-order highboy. They share the highboy.

What they did not want, as a rule, was the chamber as chapter 1 described it: feathers first, a tester with hangings, a room that was not a shrine. They installed highboys in paneled rooms with Oriental rugs and a bare four-poster, the cloth gone, the wood victorious. Colonial Revival interiors — McKim, Mead & White’s earlier colonial, then the 1920s house with reproduction brasses — taught a generation to read American identity off case furniture. The bed became a sketch. The highboy became the parent.

Nutting’s own collection of seventeenth-century furniture, much of it now in museums, is better than his eighteenth-century reproductions as evidence. He cared about turned oak. He also cared about a product line. The two cares do not cancel. They sit in the same career, which is why Denenberg’s title says *invention*.

## The Girl Scouts Loan Exhibition, 1929

From 25 September to 9 October 1929, at the American Art Galleries in New York, the National Council of Girl Scouts put up a loan exhibition of eighteenth- and early nineteenth-century furniture and glass, lustre ware, “Lowestoft,” toile de Jouy, portraits by Stuart, Peale, and others. More than 925 objects, chiefly furniture, almost all from private collections. The catalogue — *Loan Exhibition of Eighteenth and Early Nineteenth Century Furniture & Glass* — is a brick. The 1977 reprint is how most scholars now hold it. Lenders and sponsors included Lockwood, Garvan, Blair, du Pont, Kimball.

Wendy A. Cooper’s *In Praise of America: American Decorative Arts, 1650–1830 / Fifty Years of Discovery Since the Girl Scouts Loan Exhibition* (Knopf, 1980) is the anniversary argument: the 1929 show was a starting gun for a field that then spent fifty years correcting itself. The Girl Scouts did not invent collecting. They staged it as a civic benefit, which made American furniture look like a public good rather than a rich man’s sport. High chests stood in that light. So did dressing tables, secretaries, and the glass the subtitle promised. Beds were not the stars. How could they be? The surviving wood was the casework. The hangings were gone. “Lowestoft” in the subtitle is itself a 1920s tell: Chinese export porcelain misnamed for an English factory, the same hunger to Anglicize an Atlantic object that made a Massachusetts highboy into Philadelphia. Toile de Jouy on the wall taught visitors that cloth had been in the story, then parked it as a textile, not as bed furniture. The exhibition’s furniture list, if you read it as a sleeping-room inventory, is a list of case pieces. That is the argument it could not help making.

Two weeks later the stock market broke. The exhibition’s afterlife is a catalog and a generation of curators. The highboy’s afterlife is a bedroom corner in houses that could still afford Old America after 1929, and a reproduction industry that did not stop because brokers jumped.

## Karolik and Kaufman

Maxim Karolik and Martha Codman Karolik gave the Museum of Fine Arts, Boston, a collection that Edwin J. Hipkiss published as *Eighteenth-Century American Arts: The M. and M. Karolik Collection* (1941). High chests in that gift are among the objects through which a museum public learned the type as art, not as attic salvage. The Karoliks bought with a program: American eighteenth century at a level that would force the MFA to treat it as it treated European painting. The highboy in a marble gallery is a different animal from the highboy in a Nutting photograph of a cottage interior. Both were 1920s–30s operations. One went to a museum as an original. The other went to a customer as a branded copy.

George and Linda Kaufman’s collection, published by J. Michael Flanigan as *American Furniture from the Kaufman Collection* (National Gallery of Art, 1986), is a later, still more refined version of the same desire: William and Mary through Federal, regional peaks, the high chest as a lesson in “national and regional tastes.” The National Gallery’s loan shows, and Cooper’s 1980 *In Praise of America* which included Kaufman objects, kept the 1929 flame. The Kaufman high chests are not Nuttings. They are the thing Nutting was selling a memory of. Standing in front of them, you see why the 1920s lost its mind. You also see, if you have read chapter 1, what is missing: the bed as a textile box, the chamber as a mixed-use room, the people who did not own a high chest.

## What they got wrong about colonial chambers

The errors are specific, not a smear.

They treated the high chest as a bedroom essential. In many houses it stood in a parlor chamber or a best room that was not only for sleep. Vertical storage is not a nightstand.

They treated matching woods as a period taste. A high chest and a dressing table from the same shop are a pair. A bed in maple with hangings, a looking glass, and a walnut case in the same room are a household, not a failure of taste.

They stripped testers. Colonial Revival bedrooms love posts and fear cloth. The inventories priced the stuffs. A Nutting bedroom photograph will show a coverlet and a rag rug before it shows a dimity tester in working order.

They over-Philadelphia’d the country. Boston’s early stack (chapter 3), Newport’s shells, Charleston’s cypress and rice-carved posts, rural maple — the revival flattened these into “the highboy” as if one silhouette were the United States.

They undercounted labor beyond the famous white shops. Enslaved joiners in the South, anonymous journeymen, the japanner as a specialist: the 1920s label wanted a maker’s name, preferably a white Protestant ancestor. The Girl Scouts catalog is a list of owners more than makers.

They believed reproduction could be a moral equivalent. Nutting thought a good copy taught the eye. Sometimes it does. It also taught a generation to furnish a 1920s sleeping room as a museum period room, which is a way of not sleeping in the eighteenth century at all.

They also got the labor wrong in the shop they did celebrate. A Philadelphia high chest is weeks of drawer-making. Each drawer is a box: sides, front, back, bottom, runners, maybe cockbeading. A journeyman’s speed was the shop’s profit. An apprentice who wasted a mahogany board was a cost. Carving on a pompadour or a leafy drawer is a specialist’s day, not a weekend. Nutting’s mill divided labor too — turners, carvers, finish men — and then branded the result with one minister’s name. The colonial shop had branded less often. The 1920s wanted a genius. It found Nutting, or Goddard, or a label. It did not find the unnamed bench.

The highboy was never the bed. The revival made it the ancestor because it survived, photographed well, and could be priced as art. The chamber’s expensive object had been feathers. The 1920s could not auction feathers. It auctioned walnut.

## Residual

Label 992, 1927, in a drawer of a Massachusetts highboy dressed as Philadelphia, is a better document of the American bedroom than a dozen undated “colonial” suites from a later mill. It tells you what a customer would pay to have an ancestor in the room. The Girl Scouts packed 925 objects into two weeks at the American Art Galleries and taught the eye that followed. Karolik and Kaufman, in museum time, kept the lesson expensive and, slowly, more accurate about regions and shops. What they could not put back on the highboy was the room it did not come from: a hung bed, a chest that was not yet a shrine, a house that had not hired Nutting to invent Old America in mahogany.
