# Testers and Hangings

Met 49.91a–e stands ninety-six and a half inches in the American Wing: birch and white pine, fifty-six and a quarter by seventy-seven and a quarter inches in plan, 1760–1800, a New England bedstead with a Wethersfield history. Purchase, Joseph Pulitzer Bequest, 1949. The foot-posts are diminutive versions of work Eliphalet Chapin was making in East Windsor. That is not why the object is the one to stand in front of. The cornices are.

This bedstead kept a set of original eighteenth-century fabric-covered cornices — the sole surviving American ones the Met’s object page is willing to call unique. The wood was carved and pierced first. Then a blue-resist cotton, indigo-dyed in steps to a large floral, was glued to that carving. The cloth was almost certainly woven in India, shipped to England, and sold into the Hudson Valley, Long Island, and Connecticut. Small pulley wheels sit in the three cornices, one narrow at the foot and two longer at the sides. Rings on the back of the curtains, a line of twine, a pull: festoons by day, a closed box at night. The head is a plain board full of nail holes. Someone took the head cloth down to wash it and nailed it back.

Stand in a gallery and look at an American four-post bed with the hangings off. You are looking at a rack. The posts are too tall for the mattress logic of the twentieth century. The headboard is a board. The rails have holes or hooks whose job is not obvious until you put rope or sacking in them. 49.91 still has the fittings that make the rack a room. Most American bedsteads lost that half.

A tester is the canopy frame. Full tester, half tester, tent, field. The English words traveled. American shops turned maple and mahogany posts and then waited for the upholsterer — sometimes the same shop, often a different bill — to make the room. Florence Montgomery’s *Textiles in America* and the Winterthur textile files are the catalog half. The wood half is smaller than it looks in photographs.

## The bed as a room

In a house without a furnace, the hung bed is the one room whose walls you can close. Valances cut the draft from the ceiling. Side curtains close against the window wall. A head cloth takes the plaster’s cold. The coverlet is not decoration; it is the last lid. Anyone who has slept in an unheated chamber in January already knows why the inventories priced the stuffs.

The later American habit of leaving posts bare — “pencil posts,” “rice-carved” Charleston posts, a Federal tester reduced to a sketch — is a taste that arrives when stoves and then radiators make the box optional. Colonial Revival bedrooms of the 1920s love the posts and fear the cloth. They photograph well. They sleep colder than the original argument.

## Upholstery as a trade

Thomas Chippendale’s *Director* and the London upholsterers’ price books treat the bed as a textile commission that happens to need a frame. American shops in Boston, Newport, New York, Philadelphia, and Charleston knew that split. A cabinetmaker’s bill for a bedstead can look modest next to the mercer’s bill for dimity, moreen, or copperplate cotton. Linda Baumgarten’s Williamsburg work on clothing and household textiles, and the Colonial Williamsburg reconstructions of hung beds, are the public version of a fact the bills already knew.

Reproduction hangings in period rooms are arguments. They choose a stripe, a festoon, a color temperature. They are not the original cloth. The honest caption says “reproduction, after a documented type.” A caption that says “original hangings” is rare enough to stop a reader. Most American bedsteads lost their first textiles to wear, wash, moth, and the day someone decided the wood was the antique. 49.91’s cornices are the exception that proves the filing habit.

## Posts, rails, and the hidden grid

The joinery is not romantic. Posts are turned or square, often with a removable tester rail pegged or bolted at the top. Side rails meet the posts in hooks, bolts, or through-tenons, depending on date and shop. The sleeping surface is rope laced through holes, or slats, or a sacking bottom tacked to a rabbet. Later, metal rails and angle irons. The mattress — tick stuffed with feathers, hair, husks, or wool — sits on that grid. A bed wrench for tightening rope is a household tool, not a curiosity.

Met 10.125.336 is the other public New England machine in the same wing: Massachusetts, 1760–90, mahogany, maple, and white pine, eighty-eight and an eighth by sixty-one and a quarter by seventy-seven inches, Sage gift, 1909. Fluted columnar foot-posts, claw-and-ball feet, removable knees carved with acanthus and rosettes. One of a small group attributable to the same shop. The raked-back talons and the secondary woods are Massachusetts sentences. The hangings are gone. The posts are what a later photograph wants.

Southern testers run taller. Charleston and the Chesapeake needed air and mosquito netting as much as they needed wool. The “rice bed” of the Carolina lowcountry — posts carved with rice stalks — is a Federal and later type that collectors have over-named; not every carved reed is rice, and not every tall post is Charleston. MESDA’s object files are the check on the nickname. Charleston’s tall posts and rice carving belong to a warmer climate. Here the shared fact is the rack.

New England pencil posts are a thinner, later taste: octagonal shafts, a tester that can be light, hangings that can be summer dimity. The form photographs as “Shaker-simple” and is not Shaker. Shaker beds have wheels and almost no theater.

## Who hung the bed

In a merchant house the upholsterer came. In a farmhouse the women of the house made and remade the hangings from cloth that had already been a gown or a curtain. Enslaved seamstresses in the South and in northern towns did that work without their names on the inventory line that says “bed furniture.” The phrase *bed furniture* in the eighteenth century means the textiles, not the posts. Readers who come from a furniture-shop vocabulary have to relearn the word.

A history of American bedroom furniture that treats the bedstead as the object and the hangings as optional trim has accepted a nineteenth-century photograph as an eighteenth-century room.

## After the stove

Cast-iron stoves, then central heat, then the open bedroom of the late nineteenth century, make the full tester a choice instead of a necessity. Eastlake and the reform bedroom will argue against dust-catching drapery. Hospitals and sanitation writers will call hanging beds unhealthy. The brass bed of the 1880s–1910s often has no tester at all: a metal frame proud of being washable. The Craftsman bed will go further — oak, low, no cloth box.

None of that erases the earlier machine. If you restore an eighteenth-century bedstead and leave it naked in a heated apartment, you have made a sculpture. Fine. Say so. Do not call it complete.

## Colors, stuffs, and the wash

Moreen, dimity, calamanco, copperplate cotton, later chintz — the names are a mercer’s list, not a decorator’s mood board. Florence Montgomery’s glossary is the place to look a stuff up before you caption it. Washability mattered. A wool hanging in a closed box held smell and moth. A cotton that could go to the tub was a different politics of the chamber, and a different Atlantic market (Indian cottons, then English prints that imitated them). The American shop did not weave most of this. It hung it.

Color in period rooms is a guess disciplined by fragments and by the mercer’s bill. Deep greens and reds show in some documented houses; pale “colonial” pastels are often a revival taste. Do not trust a 1930s restoration for a 1760 palette. Winterthur’s textile study collection is safer than a painted postcard. 49.91’s blue resist is not a guess. It is glued to the cornice.

## Field beds and the traveling frame

A field bed is a collapsible or light-posted bed with an arched tester, associated with campaign furniture and with smaller chambers. American examples exist in maple and mahogany. They are not all military. They are a way to have hangings without a full architectural box. The later “pencil-post field bed” of the reproduction trade mixes types. Keep the words apart: field is a tester shape and a knockdown habit; pencil is a post section.

Knockdown is already here, before the iron hook of the factory bed. A tall-post bed that cannot come apart does not go up a New England stair. The rail joint on a factory bed is the long afterlife of that problem. The hangings come off first. Then the tester. Then the rails. The posts go last, like a small timber frame. 49.91’s cornices themselves are a knockdown: mortise-and-tenon at the corners, four pieces that make a lid.

## Grant’s books, Fleeson’s ads, and thirty-odd yards

Samuel Grant’s Boston upholstery accounts — decades of them, the 1730s into the 1760s — are the particular American bill this object can stand beside. Grant sold and fitted bed furniture: harateen, cheney, later printed stuffs, the small hardware of rings and tape. He was not a cabinetmaker. The posts came from somewhere else. His line is the cloth and the making-up. Brock Jobe’s work on the Boston upholstery trade, and the excerpts from Grant that furniture historians keep citing, are the place to confirm a yardage before a caption quotes one. `[CITE NEEDED: one Grant invoice with a named client, a stuff, and a yardage, from the published excerpts or the manuscript.]`

Thirty to forty yards is not an unusual full furniture for a hung bed in the London and colonial bills. Valance, head cloth, four side curtains, sometimes a tester cloth, the coverlet counted separately. A farmhouse reused a gown. A merchant house ordered the set. The difference is visible in the mercer’s unit, not in the post turning.

Plunket Fleeson advertised in Philadelphia — beds, upholstery, the sea-bed for a cabin — in the decades when Affleck and Randolph were making the wooden half of richer chambers. Fleeson’s newspaper lines are not a shop drawing. They are proof that the upholsterer’s name could be public while the joiner’s was not. Charleston had its own men; Edward Weyman’s world belongs with the southern testers. A cabinetmaker’s modest bedstead next to an upholsterer’s furniture is the colonial normal. The museum that shows only the maple has filed the cheap half.

Reproduction hangings at Colonial Williamsburg — the Peyton Randolph House among the public rooms — are arguments you can walk around. They choose a stripe, a festoon, a depth of fold. They are better than a naked post. They are still not the first cloth. The bills are how you know what the first cloth cost.

## Rope, sacking, bolts, and the tester that comes apart

The sleeping surface is a grid before it is a mattress. Side rails and end rails bored for rope, the holes spaced so a hemp line can be laced in a pattern the house already knew. Forty to sixty feet of rope is a common household length for a full bed; the number moves with the hole count. Tightening is two people and a wooden wrench, not a proverb. The folk etymology that turns the wrench into “sleep tight” is a caption writer’s toy. Leave it. The wrench is a crank. The rope stretches through a humid week and goes slack. Someone crawls under and takes another turn.

A sacking bottom — coarse linen or hemp tacked into a rabbet, sometimes laced — is the English upholsterer’s other answer. American inventories in New England say “cords” more often than they say sacking. You date the surface by the rail, not by a later slat someone nailed in when the rope holes looked dirty. A replaced rail with no holes in a “colonial” bed is a later sleeping habit wearing an old post.

The tester itself is a small timber frame. Rails pegged or bolted into the post tops, sometimes iron straps, sometimes a wooden pin you can knock out. Iron bed bolts with square nuts show up as the century moves; early New England work is more often a hooked rail or a through-tenon pinned at the post. The cornice is a separate upholsterer’s frame, sometimes with a cabinetmaker’s small molding, and it is the piece that loses its tack holes first. A surviving cornice is rarer than a post with a nice turning. 49.91 kept the rare piece.

Chippendale’s *Director* plates for beds are cornice theater. American shops rarely built that full London machine. They turned posts and let the upholsterer make a simpler box. 49.91 is already a Connecticut reading of that split: Chapin-related posts, an imported cotton, pulley hardware that is neither cabinetmaker’s nor a later museum invention.

Southern shops needed height for air and net as much as for wool. A Charleston post and a Salem pencil post are both racks. The climate writes the cloth. The joinery of the rail is the shared sentence.

## Who sewed, who aired, who is not on the bill

In a merchant house the upholsterer’s men tacked the valance and hung the rings. In a farmhouse the same work was unpaid and unnamed: turning a worn curtain, patching a moth hole, taking the wool down in May. Enslaved seamstresses in southern chambers and in northern towns did that work on cloth the inventory calls the master’s. The line “bed furniture” does not name them. A history that treats the wrench as a quaint accessory and the seam as invisible has chosen the iron because the iron survived.

Wash day is part of the object. A cotton that could go to the tub changed the smell of the box and the Atlantic market that fed it. A wool moreen that could not go to the tub held smoke and the last winter. Montgomery’s glossary still saves a caption from calling every stuff “damask.” Look the word up. Then look at the rail holes. The object is both. 49.91’s head board, with its nail holes, is the wash day you can still count.

## Residual

*Bed furniture* meant the cloth. The posts were the lesser half of the phrase. Museums that show the lesser half as the whole object are not lying about the wood. They are lying about the room. Met 49.91a–e is the rare American bedstead that kept enough of the first half — cornice, resist, pulley — to make the lie expensive. If a shop builds a four-poster for a heated house, the client is buying a rack. The colonial chamber bought a box.
