# Restoration and the Antique Bedroom

A Philadelphia high chest with a row of Chippendale plates that do not quite sit in the old scars is already a restored room, even if the bed is original and the hangings are new. The brass is the first lie most owners buy, because hardware catalogs still sell the pattern and because a shiny plate photographs as care. Conservators at Winterthur and the Met taught a different care: keep the surface that is evidence, add as little as will make the object stable and readable, say what you did. The antique bedroom, as a project, is where those ethics collide with a household that wants to sleep.

Households and museums are not the same client. A Winterthur period room does not take a nightly hip on the rail. A private tester that is slept in will loosen, stain, and pick up a suitcase scrape. The ethic still applies. Stabilize the joint (chapter 39) with a disclosed plate if the original hook is tired. Do not glue a knockdown bed so it cannot take the stairs. Do not plane a drawer front to “freshen” it until the lip is gone. The object’s job, if it is in a bedroom, includes use. Conservation that forbids sleep has made a gallery. Say that, and then choose. Many honest restorations are use-restorations: a working bolt, a new rope, hangings that can be washed. They are not the same as a stripping-and-poly campaign. They are closer to what the shop originally sold — a bed that came apart and a cloth that wore.

This is not a manual. It is a set of decisions that keep coming back: finish, paint, brasses, hangings, rails, mattresses that do not belong to the frame. Mark Anderson and Michael Podmaniczky’s Winterthur papers on minimally intrusive treatment, Gregory Landrey on surfaces, the WAG essays on hardware coatings — these are the shop half of the ethic, written for colleagues. The magazine reader needs the ethic without the solvent list.

## What to keep

Original finish, even tired, is information. It is also often ugly by a 1980s decorator’s light. The stripping wheel that took eighteenth-century surfaces down to “natural wood” and then laid on polyurethane is a historical event. It cannot be undone. Landrey’s account of a Philadelphia pillar-and-claw table that had received six coats of Zar on the top after a 1974 stripping is a parable. The later campaign had to invent a shellac that would look like age without claiming to be the 1760s film. The honest caption says “finish restored after later stripping.” A caption that says “original finish” on a glassy table is the lie.

Keep later finish if it is itself a history. A grain-painted chest with a nineteenth-century overpaint may be more true as a painted object than as a stripped “mahogany.” Winterthur’s work on a Mahantongo Valley chest — cleaning, local varnish on blanched paint, new feet based on other examples, the bad replacement feet retained as a record, a cast escutcheon in the old holes — is the ethic in a paragraph. Completeness is not the same as newness. The previous wrong feet were kept. That sentence should travel.

Japanned high chests (chapter 3) are a special case. The gilt is almost always restored. The ground is cracked. What you see in a gallery is a painted argument that has been argued several times. Do not treat restored gilt as original thickness. The conservation file is the document.

## Later paint

Paint on a bedstead or a case may be original, early, later “colonial,” or last summer. Cross-section microscopy, when a museum can afford it, is how you stop guessing. A household cannot always afford it. What a household can do is not strip on a hunch. Cream paint on a cottage suite may be the object. “Colonial” blue-grey on a maple bed may be 1928 Nutting taste. White paint on a tester that once had red wash is a fashion. The ethic is: find out, then decide whether you are conserving a surface or making a new object that quotes an old one. Both are allowed if you say so. Passing a 1928 paint job as 1740 is not.

Beds were painted more often than collectors like. Southern and New England painted testers, “fancy” beds, later factory enamel on metal. A stripped maple poster that “looks original” may have lost its only original surface. Keep a sample, at least, if you must strip for a reason that is not a photograph.

## Replaced brasses

Ghosts in the wood: a smaller plate, a drop-handle pin, a steel screw where a wrought nail was. Heckscher’s catalogs treat hardware as dating, not jewelry. Ball and Ball will sell you a Chippendale plate that fits a 1760 idea. Installing a full new row because the old ones were “wrong” or dull is the commonest way to spend money on a lie. Polish is not conservation. Thomson’s WAG paper on coatings on brass is the specialist version: historic lacquer, later polish, the difficulty of telling an original coating from an early one. The household version: if the plates sit in old scars and the color of the brass matches the wear on the lock, leave them. If you must replace a missing pull, copy the survivors, and do not “improve” the whole facade.

A bed’s hardware is easier to lose: bolt caps, casters, a hook plate swapped in 1910. Disclose. A working joint (chapter 39) may require a new plate. That is a repair. A matched set of reproduction caps on a bed that never had them is costume.

## Reproduction hangings

Most American bedsteads lost their first textiles to wear, wash, moth, and the day someone decided the wood was the antique. Period rooms that show full testers are almost always wearing reproductions. Florence Montgomery’s textile work and the Winterthur and Williamsburg reconstructions are arguments: a stripe, a festoon, a color temperature. The honest caption says “reproduction, after a documented type.” A caption that says “original hangings” is rare enough to stop a reader.

Should a private bedroom hang a tester? If the rails are there and the room is the reason for the bed, yes, with the same honesty: this cloth is ours, this pattern is a type, we did not find grandmother’s moreen in the attic. Museums fear light and dust on old textiles; a household will fear the vacuum. Neither fear is a reason to leave the machine naked and call it complete. Chapter 2 already said that. Restoration is the chapter that has to live with the sewing bill.

Upholstery on a later bed — a 1930s padded headboard, a sleigh with a damask — has its own files. Original show cloth is rare. A muslin under-upholstery, tacks in old holes, a webbing pattern, are evidence. Reupholstering in a fabric that “looks colonial” on a Federal tester is a taste, not a recovery. Keep a scrap of the last cloth in a bag in the drawer. The next conservator will thank you. So will you, when you forget what you took off.

Do not nail new hangings into original testers in a way that chews the arris. Upholstery conservation’s non-invasive wrapping is the museum version. A household can use Velcro on a removable rib. The point is reversibility — a word from the ethic that earns its keep. The next owner may want the rails bare. They should not have to fill a row of new holes.

## Rails, mattresses, and the object’s job

Do not force a king foam slab onto a full-size tester and call it a restoration. The rails will tell you. A bunkie board on a rope grid is a compromise with a date. A box spring on a frame that never saw one is a 1950s intervention. None of these is a crime. The crime is the label “original condition” on a bed that has a 2020 mattress, 1974 polyurethane, and 1990 brasses.

Winterthur’s ethic, reduced for a house: stabilize, do not improve; keep evidence; make additions readable and reversible; write down what you did. The Met’s American Wing labels, at their best, already do this in public. Jargon soup — “inpaint,” “B-72,” “reversibility” stacked without an object — does not help a reader who is looking at a dresser. The brass ghost does. The extra screw hole does. The hangings’ newness does.

The residual fact is a caption. If the bedroom is antique, the sentence under the photograph should name the later parts. Later paint. Replaced brasses. Reproduction hangings. A mattress that does not belong to the rails. The object can still be slept in. It cannot be a time machine. The ethic is not to freeze a room in 1760. It is to stop lying about 1985.

A last practical list, because the bedroom gathers later objects the way a ship gathers barnacles. A foam mattress on a rope grid: disclose, and consider a proper tick if the rails can take it. Reproduction hangings in a documented pattern: yes, labeled. A “colonial” canopy from a 1980s department store on an 1810 tester: costume, unless you like it and say the date. Casters added to a bed that never had them: a use-repair, not original. A high chest in the same room with replaced brasses and a Kindel pediment on a different case: two afterlives, two captions. The suite idea (chapter 40) will try to make the room match. Restoration ethics will try to make each object readable. Those aims fight. Let them. A matched antique bedroom is often a matched set of later decisions.

Rope, if you restore it, is not a craft-fair trick. It is the sleeping surface the rail was bored for. Use a cord that can take the load; document the pattern if a historic lacing is known. A plywood platform laid on the rails of a tester is a twentieth-century decision with a date. It may be how the household can sleep. It is not how the bed was a machine. The caption can hold both sentences.

Finishes on beds take a different abuse than tabletops. Feet, a vacuum wand, a suitcase, skin. A French-polished tester rail will fail at the hand-hold. A farm bed’s milk paint may be more honest to keep, chips and all, than a new oil that looks “period” in a listing. Wax over a stable old film is often enough. The ethic is not a single product. It is a refusal to make 1760 and 1985 the same sentence.

Hardware on a bed that must still knock down should be serviceable. A seized bolt is not original virtue; it is a frozen joint. Penetrating oil, a correct wrench, a new nut in the old mortise if the old nut is round: use-conservation. Then write it on a card in the drawer so the next owner does not drill a second hole.
