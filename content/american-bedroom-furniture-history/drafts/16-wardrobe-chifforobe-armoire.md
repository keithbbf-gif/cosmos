The 1908 Sears, Roebuck catalog put chifforobes on a page and called them “a modern invention, having been in use only a short time.” The *Oxford English Dictionary* takes that catalog as the first recorded use of the American word: a piece of furniture incorporating a wardrobe and a chest of drawers. Chiffonier plus wardrobe. Drawers on one side, a hanging well on the other, doors or an exposed chest face, depending on the design. Mail-order. Freight to a town whose houses were built without closets.

That is the late, named hybrid. The problem it solves is older. Walk an American house of 1800, 1850, even 1910 outside the better suburban plans: bedchambers with plaster walls, a chimney breast, maybe a tiny press beside a fireplace, often no dedicated hanging closet. Clothing lived in chests, in high chests, on pegs, in a wardrobe if you had one. English and French houses of the same classes had more built-in cupboards and more wardrobes as furniture. American timber framing and later balloon framing did not automatically include a closet. Closets cost space. Chests were furniture you could take to the next rental.

## Houses without closets

Seventeenth- and eighteenth-century New England inventories, chapter 1’s ground, list chests and boxes, not closets. A *press* in a Chesapeake house may be a shallow built-in. A *wardrobe* as a movable cupboard for apparel is, in English, late eighteenth century as furniture (the *OED*’s furniture sense from the 1790s; the room-sense is medieval). American shops make wardrobes in the Federal and Empire years — tall boxes, often cedar-lined or with a cedar pole later, paneled doors, sometimes a drawer in the plinth. They are not standard in every chamber. They are a purchase.

Nineteenth-century pattern books and reform writers treat the hanging-press as a known need. Eastlake’s wardrobe paragraph (chapter 14) assumes the object and hates the fashionable curves. Grand Rapids suites can include a wardrobe as a tall matching piece. Mail-order houses will sell one without a suite. The closet, as a planned closet in a balloon-frame bedroom, becomes ordinary in better American plans toward the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries — and still not in every farmhouse, shotgun, or mill house. The South, in particular, keeps the furniture word after the North has begun to say *closet*.

Pegs, in Shaker dwelling houses and in ordinary back rooms, are another no-closet technology. A chifforobe is a bourgeois peg rail with doors.

## Wardrobe, armoire, chiffonier

*Wardrobe*: English, the clothes-cupboard. In American catalogs, a tall case, hanging space, maybe a shelf, maybe a drawer. Cedar is a selling point against moths, as it was in Berkey & Gay drawer sides.

*Armoire*: French word, American use for a more architectural wardrobe — often with a cornice that wants to be a room’s built-in, sometimes with a mirrored door. Dealers like it because it sounds like an antique. A Mississippi pine wardrobe is not an *armoire* until the ad. In Louisiana and among Creole households the French word has a real history; elsewhere it is varnish on a catalog.

*Chiffonier* (French *chiffonnier*, originally a rag-gatherer): in furniture, a narrow chest of drawers, sometimes with a small cupboard, a side piece. Early nineteenth century in English. The American *chifforobe* eats the first half of that word and the whole of wardrobe.

Do not call a high chest a wardrobe. The high chest is folded storage. The wardrobe is hanging storage, a response to clothing that is no longer only folded — coats, dresses on hooks, later hangers. The hanger itself is a nineteenth-century object whose spread changes what a wardrobe’s interior looks like: a pole instead of hooks, width instead of a stack of shelves.

## The chifforobe as hybrid

Typical layout: a vertical hanging compartment, a stack of drawers beside it, overall the width of a generous dresser and the height of a wardrobe. Two doors hiding both, or drawers exposed and a single door on the hanging side. A hat compartment or a small cupboard above. Mirrors on some doors, because the dressing table and the wardrobe are competing for the chamber’s glass.

The hybrid is a floor-plan solution. A small southern bedroom cannot take a full wardrobe *and* a full chest of drawers. One carcass, two jobs. Sears in 1908 is selling that economy to people who order from a book. Grand Rapids and other factories make finer versions in oak and walnut, sometimes as part of a suite, sometimes as a standalone.

Spelling is a mess: chifforobe, chifferobe, chiffarobe, chiffrobe. Carson McCullers, *The Ballad of the Sad Café* (1951; the novella is often dated 1951 in book form, 1943 as magazine — use the book’s “chiffen robe” as McCullers’s ear, not as a dictionary). Alice Walker, *The Color Purple* (1982), “chifferobe.” Thomas Harris, *The Silence of the Lambs* (1988), Clarice Starling knowing deputies from houses “that had chifforobes instead of closets.” The literary afterlife is southern and classed: the word means a house that stores clothes in furniture. Do not use those novels as furniture history. Use them as proof the word stuck in speech after Sears had moved on.

Knockdown is the mail-order wardrobe’s other invention. Cornice lifts off. Doors lift off pins. A hanging pole unscrews. Backboards are pine slats or a thin plywood later. The 1908 Sears page is not only a new word; it is a crate diagram waiting to happen. A farmer with a freight depot and a Sunday can have hanging space without a carpenter building a closet into a wall that has no depth. Balloon-frame closets need a stud bay. A chifforobe needs a floor.

Cedar is the moth story again. A cedar wardrobe or a cedar lining is priced extra. Red cedar in Berkey & Gay drawer sides and a cedar chifforobe interior are the same insect. Metal beds claimed vermin at the frame; cedar claimed moths at the wool. A household might buy both and still keep a walnut dresser that fed beetles in the backboards.

Southern yellow-pine wardrobes, painted, local, 1880–1920, are the unlabeled majority. Raised panels, a simple cornice, hooks instead of a pole if hangers have not arrived in that house. The clothes hanger as a wire or wooden shoulder is a late-nineteenth-century object whose household spread is uneven. A wardrobe full of hooks is older-fashioned than a pole. A chifforobe with a pole is already assuming dresses that hang. Catalog copy sells the pole as modern, the same adjective Sears put on the word itself.

## Mail-order, cedar, freight

A wardrobe is awkward freight: tall, heavy, often knockdown. Factory wardrobes and chifforobes are designed to come apart — cornice off, doors off, a backboard of pine. Mail-order instructions assume a husband or a hired man and a Sunday. Cedar lining, cedar poles, moth claims — the same insect war as metal beds and cedar drawer sides. A chifforobe in oak grain, golden, 1910, with a mirror door, is a chamber’s largest object after the bed. It photographs like a wardrobe and works like a dresser.

Sears, Montgomery Ward, Spiegel: the chifforobe page is a southern and western rural page as much as an urban one. Closets were spreading in new bungalows. Older houses, rental houses, and warm-climate houses with less wall depth kept buying the furniture. The South’s attachment to the word tracks that housing stock. Puerto Rico and Cuba appear in dictionary notes on the word’s range; this chapter is not competent to write those rooms without objects. Stay with the U.S. catalog and the southern speech.

Grand Rapids factories, including firms that had made chamber suites, sold chiffoniers and wardrobes in the same woods. A “chiffonier” in a 1900 ad may be a tall chest of drawers, not the hybrid. Read the picture. The hybrid needs the hanging well.

## Armoire as revival and as French

Colonial Revival and European Revival taste in the early twentieth century brings in “armoires” that are new oak with old cornices, and actual European cupboards imported by dealers. A Norman armoire in a New York apartment is not an American closet-substitute of the Sears type, though it does the same hanging job. French beds in chapter 8 needed crowns; French cupboards need height. American manufacturers put a French name on a Kansas oak box when the catalog wanted more money.

Mirrored-door wardrobes double as dressing glass. The vanity chapter is next; the competition is already here. If the wardrobe carries the room’s big mirror, the dressing table can be smaller. If the woman of the 1910 ad is given a vanity with a triptych glass, the chifforobe can lose its mirror and keep drawers. Catalogs mix the options. Houses mix them worse, over decades.

Shotgun houses, mill houses, and rental bungalows without a closet bay are the architectural demand. A built-in closet in a 1920s suburban plan is the beginning of the word’s decline in those rooms — not in the older stock. Building culture does not flip in a decade. The chifforobe is furniture for a housing lag.

## Labor and the unnamed joiner, again

A wardrobe is a lot of panel work: frames, raised panels or veneered slabs, a hanging interior that must be snag-free. Factory versions are machine-planed pine or poplar inside, show wood out. A southern yellow-pine wardrobe, painted, local shop, 1890, may be as good at hanging a dress as a Grand Rapids oak chifforobe and will never see a catalog number. Enslaved labor is earlier; by the chifforobe’s named years the southern shop is Jim Crow wage labor, Black and white, poorly documented on the object. Do not paste 1770 Charleston hiring-out onto a 1908 Sears crate. Do say that the closet-poor house is not only a white middle-class story. Harris’s deputies and Walker’s Celie are using a word that furniture history should not leave to novels, even if the surviving labeled objects are mail-order oak.

A mirrored wardrobe door that swings past a bed in a nine-foot room is a floor-plan fight the catalog drawing never shows. Hinges fail. The chifforobe, with drawers that do not swing, often wins in that room.

## Residual

The closet, when it finally arrives as a standard in American plans, kills the wardrobe’s necessity and not quite the chifforobe’s name. People still say the word where the house still needs the object, and in families that had the object when the house did not. Sears in 1908 needed a sentence — modern invention, only a short time in use — to sell a hybrid the floor plan had been asking for all century. A high chest folded shirts. A wardrobe hung a coat. A chifforobe did both in a room that had no closet to lie about.
