MGM’s publicity for *Our Dancing Daughters* (1928) bragged that Cedric Gibbons had designed “modernistic” effects, including “weird beds” without conventional legs. Joan Crawford moved through rooms that were white solids and black voids, stepped recesses, mirrors, a bed that did not look like Nutting’s ancestor and did not look like a Stickley oak rectangle. Gibbons had been to the 1925 Exposition Internationale des Arts Décoratifs in Paris. He ran MGM’s art department from the studio’s start in 1924 until he retired in 1956. Howard Gutner’s *MGM Style* is the career book. The bedroom this chapter needs is not a surviving suite in a museum. It is a set that existed to be filmed, then a house that tried to live like the set, then a decorator’s trade that later got named Hollywood Regency.

The term is partly retrospective. Designers in the 1930s said Moderne, modernistic, sometimes Regency when they meant English gilt and a sabre leg. Later decorators bundled mirrored vanities, white-and-gilt, animal print, and Dorothy Draper’s baroque into one phrase. Use it as a magazine convenience. Then look at the objects.

Other pictures did the same work. *Grand Hotel* (1932) and *Dinner at Eight* (1933) are inventories of satin and telephone stands; *The Women* (1939) is a dressing-room picture, a cycle of vanities. Adrian’s gowns get the monographs. The beds under them were art-department problems: a frame that would read, a spread that would not flare under carbon lights, a night table at the height of a star’s elbow. Union carpenters in Culver City built “Louis” that had never seen Paris, then painted it white because white separated from a dark tuxedo. Composition ornaments — plaster and glue, not carved mahogany — took gilt size and bronze powder. The labor is closer to a theater shop than to a Philadelphia bench. A breakaway rail lets a camera through. A real Cadwalader would not.

## The Big White Set and a bed without legs

Gibbons understood that incandescent light on white plaster would photograph as luxury in black and white. High-contrast geometry read. A mahogany highboy’s carving did not, not at twenty feet on a Saturday screen. “Weird beds” without legs are platform thinking before the postwar platform: a mattress on a plinth, a headboard as a wall, no foot posts to snag a camera move. *Our Modern Maidens* and *Our Blushing Brides* kept the Crawford cycle in rooms that were already an advertisement for a life. As Cari Beauchamp and others have noted in studio histories, the penthouses in those pictures did not yet exist in that quantity in New York. The set invented the taste, then the houses tried to catch up. Architectural Digest’s later account of Gibbons’s own 1930 house for himself and Dolores Del Rio — brushed-steel stair, glass wall, a dressing room of mirrors — is the private version of the Big White Set. A star of Del Rio’s wattage required mirrors. The vanity is not a colonial lowboy. It is a piece of lighting.

William Haines, MGM leading man until the studio punished his life, opened an antique shop in 1930 and became the decorator of the people he had acted with. Joan Crawford, Carole Lombard: the literature names them as early clients. Haines’s interiors mixed English Regency, Chinese export, mirrored screens, and a Hollywood sense of the camera even when no camera was present. A bedroom he touched is a place to be seen waking. The furniture is often antique or antique-shaped, not a Grand Rapids waterfall. That distinction matters. Hollywood Regency at this altitude is not the factory suite. It is a decorator’s room. The factory will steal the mirror.

Los Angeles also had a modern that was not gilt. Kem Weber, Paul László, the imported Bauhaus of the houses on the hills: low beds, cork, a different glamour. Those rooms belong in the platform chapter as much as here. The Regency half of the Hollywood pair is the English-and-mirror half, the sabre leg, the convex mirror, the bleached wood. Haines could do both in a career. A single bedroom rarely did. The camera wanted a decision: white Moderne or Regency sparkle. Brown Craftsman was for the character who was about to die.

## Dorothy Draper’s American baroque

Dorothy Draper established Dorothy Draper & Company in 1923, among the first American firms to sell interior design as a business with her name on the door. Hampshire House on Central Park South, the 1930s, is the urban monument: black and white, plaster, overscaled baroque, a hotel corridor that refuses to whisper. Later, Arrowhead Springs. Later still, the Greenbrier in White Sulphur Springs, reopened after the war (1946–48) in a shock of color that made “Draper” a palette: cabbage rose, lime, the opposite of a fumed-oak ethic. She called some of it Modern Baroque. The España chests that later decorators lust after are a Draper language of painted and gilt casework — the exact genealogy of a given chest to a 1930s commission versus a later revival reproduction is a caption problem, not a vibe. `[CITE NEEDED: original España chest commission, maker, and date versus later Kindel or other reproductions.]`

Draper’s bedrooms, when she got to sleep at all in the public work, use the bed as a upholstered object: a headboard in fabric, a corona, white paint, gilt. *Decorating Is Fun!* (1939) is her own voice, brisk, anti-brown, a professional talking to women who will not hire a firm. She argues for courage in color and scale. She does not provide a shop bill. The Greenbrier’s postwar bedrooms, when they are photographed, show what a resort thinks sleep is: a headboard as a piece of upholstery architecture, curtains that are almost testers, the colonial canopy returned as fabric theater without the inventory’s feathers. The highboy is not invited, or it is painted until it stops being a highboy. That is a real American choice in these decades. One house could have a Colonial Revival dining room and a Draper guest room. The attic held the brown furniture. The sleeping room that wanted to be in the pictures chose white.

Custom casework for a Draper hotel is a mill working to paint schedules: gesso, enamel, gilt lines, a hardware suite in lacquered brass. The carcass underneath can be poplar. It does not matter. The object is a colored solid. Compare a Nutting highboy, where the mahogany is the argument. Draper’s argument is the coat.

Mirrored vanities are the portable version. A dressing table with a glass top, a round or shaped mirror, sometimes lights at the sides — the “Hollywood vanity” that later beauty culture will claim — shows up in film dressing rooms and then in department stores. Silvered glass, gilt metal, a stool in satin. Bakelite appears on cheaper cousins. The object is a place to sit and be lit. Compare chapter 1’s looking glass on a chest top. The vanity as a type is twentieth-century language. Hollywood made it a shrine with bulbs.

## What the audience could buy

Most Americans who watched Crawford did not hire Haines. They bought a suite. The 1930s factory bedroom — waterfall veneer, the next chapter — is the mass echo of Moderne, not of Draper’s plaster. A mirrored vanity in a Sears catalog is a thin, silvered sheet on plywood, not a Gibbons dressing room. White enamel bedroom suites existed: iron and brass painted, or wood painted, a “French” or “Hollywood” finish in the copy. Gilt is a highlight, a decal, a plastic molding. The glamour is a stain.

That is not a sneer. It is the American pattern. Greene & Greene could not be scaled; Hollywood could be, badly. A Depression household that put a round mirror over a waterfall vanity was in conversation with MGM whether the mill in Indiana knew Gibbons’s name. The film bedroom is the type. The hire-purchase suite is the token.

Elsie de Wolfe is earlier — *The House in Good Taste* (1913), painted furniture, a lighter paler taste — and Hollywood borrows her permission to paint the brown ancestor. Syrie Maugham’s all-white rooms in London are in the same family. The American difference is the studio system: a salaried art department, a camera, a million people on Saturday. Draper’s hotels and Gibbons’s sets are public machines. Haines’s houses are private. Together they make a bedroom that is a stage.

## Glamour versus the highboy attic

The Colonial Revival did not die in 1928. It went upstairs, or into the dining room, or into Wallace Nutting’s remaining customers. A 1930s American house can be a split brain: reproduction Windsor in the breakfast nook, a white leatherette headboard in the sleeping room. The highboy becomes “the antique,” which means the thing you do not use. Drawers that once held linen hold tax papers. The bed you sleep in wants to photograph. The ancestor wants to be furniture.

There is a class line. Du Pont’s Winterthur chambers and the Girl Scouts’ high chests are one America. Gibbons’s white set is another. Draper’s Greenbrier is a third, a resort America. All three are expensive. The shared enemy is the Victorian clutter the Craftsman already killed, and the brown suite the mill still ships. Hollywood Regency, if the phrase is allowed, is the decision that the bedroom may be theatrical. Stickley said the bedroom should be honest. Nutting said it should be old. Gibbons said it should read at thirty feet in nitrate.

Labor in a film bedroom is not cabinetmaking first. It is plasterers, painters, a property department that can fake ormolu overnight, union grips who move a bed between setups. The “furniture” may be a breakaway. When Haines specified an actual Regency chair, that was a different invoice. When Draper ordered custom casework for a hotel, that was a mill, but a mill working to a decorator’s paint schedule, not to a Philadelphia shell. The gilt is often composition, not gold. The mirror is often plate, not an eighteenth-century mercury glass. Say so in the caption.

Retail mirrored furniture — dressing tables with glass aprons, chests with silvered fronts — came from New York and Chicago specialty shops as well as from the picture studios’ leftovers. A plate-glass top is a maintenance object: it scratches, it chips at the corners, it shows every powder spill. Hardware on the better vanities is chrome or gilt brass with a backplate; on the worse, a stamped pot metal that blooms green. The stool hides a wood frame in satin or leatherette. Sit on enough of them and you know which mill blocked the seat. None of this is in the Girl Scouts catalog. It is the other 1930s.

Jean Harlow’s white on-screen bedrooms, Carole Lombard’s later houses, the black-and-white stills that made satin look like a building material: these are documents of desire. They are terrible documents of how a mattress sat on a frame. Do not trust a still for joinery. Trust it for the argument: sleep as spectacle.

Pickfair is earlier and more Tudor-Revival than Regency; it belongs in the colonial-glamour split, a star house that still wanted ancestors. The 1930s decision, on the MGM lot, was to put the ancestor in storage. White enamel iron beds — hospital cousins, washable, a Craftsman sanitary hangover — still sold in catalogs beside gilt-wood “French” suites. A reader who knows only the Crawford still will miss the iron bed in the furnished rooming house. Hollywood Regency is a minority taste with a majority audience. The audience went home to gum veneer and, if they were lucky, a round mirror.

## Residual

Gibbons’s “weird beds” of 1928 have no accession number. They had a shot list. The American bedroom that took them seriously bought a vanity with a round mirror and painted something white. The highboy in the attic kept the Girl Scouts’ lesson warm for a later curator. Both objects are in this history. Only one of them was designed to be slept in under a key light. The other was designed to prove you had a past. A past is a difficult thing to film. White plaster is easy.
