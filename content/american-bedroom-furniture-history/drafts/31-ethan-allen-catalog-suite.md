In 1939, at the Chicago Housewares Show, two New York jobbers who had bought a bankrupt Vermont plant showed a twenty-eight-piece group of Early American furniture and called it Ethan Allen. The name belonged to the leader of the Green Mountain Boys. The furniture belonged to a sales idea: correlated pieces in maple, meant to be displayed together, added to over years, and recognized from a catalog page the way Grand Rapids had taught America to recognize a chamber suite. Selected buyers only, and only if they would put the group on the floor as a room. That franchise condition is the chapter’s object as much as any bedstead. The suite was not complete until a store agreed to photograph it with its own lighting.

Theodore Baumritter and his brother-in-law Nathan S. Ancell had founded the Baumritter Corporation in 1932 as a housewares agency. Plaster gnomes, trellises, garden swings — other people’s goods, drop-shipped. A 1965 *Home Furnishings Daily* profile later put them three thousand dollars in the red at the end of that first year, more than they had put in. Ancell told Earl Lifshey that 1935 was the crucial year: no future in being a middleman; if they were going to build a destiny they would have to control the elements. In the mid-1930s they bought a bankrupt plant at Beecher Falls, Vermont. Trade histories date the furniture factory to 1936 and the price to $25,000 from a federal agency. The company’s later public timeline sometimes gives 1935 for a sawmill. `[CITE NEEDED: reconcile the 1935 mill date on ethanallen.com with the 1936 Beecher Falls factory purchase in the trade biographies.]` The machinery, Ancell said, dated from 1917 and was belt-driven. They fell in love with Vermont, or with what New England represented historically, which is not the same love. The line they put together was named for a Revolutionary hero because the style they wanted to sell was “Early American,” a market they judged mostly untapped.

Early American, in 1939, was not a colonial chamber. It was a twentieth-century reading of turned maple, a low poster or a simple tester sketch, a dresser with a framed mirror, a chest of drawers, and, soon enough, the small tables that a photograph needed at the bedside. The wood was often rock maple, or maple with a finish that said maple. The joints were factory joints: dowels, screws, glue blocks, a bed rail that hooked or bolted so the suite could be trucked. Nothing here is a Boston high chest. The ancestor is the Grand Rapids catalog, not Met 18.110.4.

## Grand Rapids, minus the city

Grand Rapids in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries had already invented the thing Ancell and Baumritter were selling: a numbered chamber suite, photographed as a set, finish matched across bed, dresser, chiffonier, dressing table. Berkey & Gay’s Centennial suites, Nelson-Matter’s bedroom plates, the trade catalogs now in the Grand Rapids Public Library — those are the documents. What Baumritter added was a brand that was also a place-name and a patriot, a Vermont factory story, and a retail rule that the group had to be shown as a room. The furniture wholesale buyer, Ancell and Baumritter observed, focused on price. The typical customer, in their account a woman, wanted decorating advice and a way to add pieces later without the room falling apart as a picture. The solution was correlated groupings. By the mid-1960s the Ethan Allen traditional Early American line ran to about 1,600 pieces, which Lifshey’s company history called the biggest single group of furniture made by any manufacturer in the world. That number is a sales claim. Treat it as a claim. The point is the logic: not one bed, a vocabulary.

Ancell told Lifshey they were selling “thoughts,” not “things,” an “environment — not furniture.” The sentence is merchandising, and it is also accurate about what a suburb was being asked to buy. A Philadelphia high chest and dressing table that traveled together in the 1760s were a pair from one shop. They were not a five-piece package with a catalog number and a finishing schedule that would still match in 1968. Ethan Allen’s contribution was to make “traditional” a renewable order. You could buy the bed in 1952 and the matching chest when the baby came. The suite was a time-payment of taste.

The word *traditional* in this use does not mean historically accurate. It means recognizable. Sausage turnings, a broken pediment on a bookcase that never saw Philadelphia, a maple poster bed whose posts are too thin to hang a tester and were never meant to. Colonial Revival had already done this work in architecture and in Wallace Nutting’s photographs. Ethan Allen industrialized the bedroom half. A 1950s split-level in New Jersey or Ohio could have a “Colonial” chamber without a single object that would pass a Winterthur seminar, and that was not a failure of the customer. It was the product.

## Maple, and the finish that said maple

Beecher Falls sits up in the Northeast Kingdom, near the Connecticut River and the Canadian line. Maple is a New England furniture wood with a long shop life: hard, pale, a tight grain that takes a stain toward amber or toward the reddish “nutmeg” and “heirloom” colors later catalogs loved. A factory that owns the saw and the dry kiln can talk about solid wood without lying. A factory that veneers maple over a cheaper core can still photograph as maple. Vintage pieces carry stamps: Baumritter, Ethan Allen by Baumritter, a maple-tree motif, later “Ethan Allen Inc.” after the 1969 rename. Identification guides for the used market treat those marks as dating tools. They are. They are also the brand doing what a cabinetmaker’s label did in 1760, which is to attach a name to a case so the next owner knows whom to blame.

Construction is the honest difference from the eighteenth-century stack. Drawer sides in a Grand Rapids or Vermont factory of 1948 are often pine or poplar, machine dovetails or even a lock joint, bottoms of plywood. Bed rails meet posts with hooks or bolts, not a through-tenon you can see. A mirror attaches with brackets. The suite’s unity is the finish room, not the secondary-wood habit of a single shop. That is not a moral failure. It is how you make 1,600 correlated pieces. A reader who stands a 1960 Ethan Allen dresser next to a 1730 Boston high chest and asks which has better dovetails has asked a real question and the wrong comparison. One object is a portable wall of linen storage cut by journeymen. The other is a product line.

Kling Factories of Jamestown, New York, bought in 1962, added a Colonial group of some 350 pieces and a Viko line of steel furniture. Steel in the same company that sold maple posters is a useful crack in the nostalgia. Baumritter also made lamps, painted wooden accessories, wall hangings. The gallery needed props. A chamber suite in the eighteenth century accumulated a looking glass and a basin. A chamber suite in 1964 shipped the lamp.

## The gallery and the suburb

By the mid-1960s there were around 700 Ethan Allen dealers in the United States, with others abroad. Average store about 16,000 square feet. Sites chosen by population and income, studied from the ground and, the company histories say, from the air. In 1970 Ancell became chair; Baumritter retired. In 1972 the Baumritter Corporation became Ethan Allen Industries and moved headquarters from New York to Danbury, Connecticut. That year the firm began to phase regular franchised dealers toward owner-managed “showcase galleries,” concentrated in suburban shopping centers. Owner-managers invested tens of thousands to build the stores, owned their inventory, and were not required to sign the old franchise contracts. By 1982 some 350 such galleries existed, a handful company-owned. Sales volume, in the figures the trade biographies repeat, rose from $43 million in 1965 to $70 million in 1971.

This is the suburb as a furniture problem. The house has a “master bedroom,” a phrase the colonial chamber did not have. It has a mattress on a box spring, a dresser that matches the headboard, nightstands in a pair because the photograph has two lamps. Privacy is assumed. The high chest, if the family owns one, is in the hall or the living room, because a nine-foot carved case does not fit the ranch’s ceiling and because “antique” and “bedroom set” have become different purchases. Traditional, in the Ethan Allen sense, is the set you can still buy. The antique is the object you cannot.

Ancell’s language about female buyers — protectiveness, warmth, orderliness, a need for someone to guide her — is period merchandising and should be read as such. The industry was talking to the person it believed signed the check. The furniture does not care who paid. What the gallery actually sold was a way to keep adding without the room becoming a jumble. That is a real domestic problem. It is also how a manufacturer locks a household to a finish.

National print advertising from about 1951; television later. In 1972 Ancell announced expansion beyond Early American into other styles, plus carpeting, wallpaper, draperies. The suite wanted the whole envelope. Interco bought the company in 1980. M. Farooq Kathwari, who had formed a product joint venture in 1973, became president in 1985 and led a 1989 buyout. The 1990s stores, in the accounts of people who had to renovate them, looked like museums; customers got lost. Kathwari’s answer was to drop the colonial monopoly. American Impressions, 1991, solid cherry, Shaker and Wright in the press releases. By mid-1992 only an eighth of displayed furniture was Early American. Classical storefronts replaced white columns. The tag line “Everyone’s at home with Ethan Allen” was an invitation to stop being your parents’ maple.

That later history matters because it shows what the 1939–1980 product had been. If the company could shed Early American once research said the style was no longer popular, then Early American had been a market, not a creed. The maple suite in the split-level was doing the same work the Grand Rapids oak suite had done in 1890 and the waterfall walnut suite had done in 1930: make the bedroom a package. The patriot’s name and the Vermont mill were the costume.

## What the wood was doing in the room

A typical Ethan Allen bedroom of the long colonial run — exact collection names shift, and used-market lists should be checked against catalogs before anyone writes a number on a dresser `[CITE NEEDED: a specific 1950s–60s Ethan Allen bedroom collection name and catalog page for the figure plan]` — puts a bed on the long wall, dresser with mirror opposite or on the window wall, tall chest if the room has the width, nightstands flanking. The bed may be a poster, a sleigh, or a simple headboard-and-frame. Hangings are optional and usually absent. The tester machine of chapter 2 is gone. Heat is in the house. The expensive object is no longer the cloth. It is the matched wood, or the idea of matched wood.

Compare a Craftsman Farms chamber. Gustav Stickley’s daughters had a matched inlaid oak group, and Mildred Cruess later received a Chromewald suite from the shop (chapter 40). Those rooms match because a maker furnished a house. Ethan Allen matches because a catalog says the SKUs belong together. The visual result can be similar. The economic result is not. One is a family shop’s gift and experiment. The other is a national inventory.

Labor in Beecher Falls and the later plants is factory labor: machine rooms, finish rooms, a delivery fleet. By 2000 the company claimed most products made in its own U.S. plants. That claim is a later chapter’s problem. For this chapter the point is only that “Vermont maple” in a suburban bedroom was often a real mill town’s output, not a sticker. The men and women on those belts are as much the history as Ancell’s interviews. Their names are thinner in the published company story than the founders’ names. `[CITE NEEDED: workforce numbers and union or wage record for Beecher Falls, 1940–1970.]`

## Traditional as a suburb’s word

Walk through a 1960s developer house. The living room may have a maple stand or a print of a clipper. The bedroom is where the brand concentrated. Traditional meant: not modern, not Danish teak, not the brass bed of the grandmother who kept the farmhouse, not the mahogany suite from the city apartment. It meant a finish you could live with and a style the neighbors would not have to ask about. Colonial Revival architecture had already put broken pediments on doorways of houses that had never seen a Chippendale case. Ethan Allen put the same recognition into the chamber.

That is why the highboy is the wrong ancestor and the right ghost. Collectors in the same decades were paying for Philadelphia carving (chapter 37). The suburb was paying for a dresser that looked “old-fashioned” at a price a mortgage could absorb. Both purchases used the word American. Only one needed a patriot’s first name on the headboard.

The residual fact is a 1939 condition of sale. The twenty-eight-piece group would be shown only to buyers willing to display it properly. The suite is not the bed. The suite is the agreement to make a room that can be photographed. Grand Rapids wrote that agreement in trade catalogs. Baumritter wrote it onto a Vermont hero and a franchise map. When Kathwari’s stores took the white columns down, the maple beds did not vanish. They went to the used market, where they are still “traditional,” still not colonial, and still a better document of how a suburb slept than a high chest in a front hall.
