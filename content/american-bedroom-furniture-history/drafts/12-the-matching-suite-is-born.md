# The Matching Suite Is Born

James Bayne had been a portrait photographer in Grand Rapids when he noticed that furniture salesmen had two bad options. They could ship sample pieces by freight to the cities on their routes, slow and costly. Or they could talk. Many buyers could not see a walnut dresser in a spoken sentence. Bayne photographed the furniture instead, sold the manufacturers on sets of prints for the road, and built a business that was still printing on Front Avenue in the 1950s. He was not the first in town to point a camera at a dresser — Elias Matter of Nelson, Matter had a skylight studio on the factory’s top floor; Phoenix had Lon M. Neely on the 1878 catalog; Berkey & Gay had photographs in theirs the same year — but Bayne made furniture photography a trade. The Grand Rapids History Center’s James Bayne Company negatives are what remain: glass plates of sofas, desks, dressers, sideboards. A salesman could open a case of pictures and sell a room.

That is the birth this chapter means. Not the first time two pieces matched. Philadelphia had been matching a high chest to a dressing table for a century. The Victorian suite is something else: a numbered set, wholesale, in one wood and one ornament language, designed to be pictured together and bought together. Bed, dresser (or bureau), washstand, often a nightstand, sometimes a wardrobe or chiffonier, sometimes a towel rack and a commode. The chamber becomes a catalog page.

## Not a colonial pair

Chapter 7’s pair is two case pieces that share carving and brasses. The bed in that room was a separate textile commission. The looking glass might not match. No one billed “Chamber Suite No. 4, walnut, seven pieces.” Hornor’s Philadelphia bills do not read like a Grand Rapids price list.

The Victorian idea needs factories, railroads, and a middle class that furnishes a dedicated bedroom rather than a chamber that also receives. It needs a finish that can be repeated: walnut, then ash, then oak, then mahogany stain. It needs ornament that a carving machine or a skilled team can repeat across a headboard, a dresser splash, and a washstand door — Renaissance Revival panels, burl veneers, incised Eastlake lines, marble tops from the same quarry run. It needs a word. *Suite* and *suit* both appear; Grand Rapids men said “suit,” Time magazine later felt obliged to gloss it. *Chamber suite* is the trade term. *Bedroom set* is what the twentieth century will say.

Kenneth Ames’s work on Victorian halls and dining rooms, and Katherine Grier’s *Culture and Comfort* on parlor upholstery, are parlor-first books. The chamber follows the same logic: matching as respectability, the room as a coordinated moral surface. Grier’s parlor is plush; the bedroom’s coordination is wood and marble more than fringes, until the hanging-bed fights the sanitation writers. The suite can absorb an Eastlake argument in one decade and a Colonial Revival argument in the next without ceasing to be a suite. The constant is not style. It is the set.

## What the set contains

A full chamber suite in a Grand Rapids wholesale list of the 1870s–90s is more furniture than a colonial chamber had names for.

The bed: high headboard, often a lower footboard, no tester required. Walnut Renaissance Revival in the 1870s, with burl panels and carved crests. Eastlake in the late 1870s–80s, incised, galleried, ebonized details. Golden oak later. The mattress is still a separate purchase. Rails are a problem for later owners; they get replaced.

The dresser: a chest of drawers with a looking glass attached, often marble-topped. The attached mirror is the nineteenth century’s gift and burden. An eighteenth-century dressing table had a separate glass. Once the mirror is screwed to the dresser, the piece is a facade for a wall, heavy, hard to move, perfect in a photograph.

The washstand: marble top, splash, a cupboard for the slops, a drawer for cloths. Until houses have bathrooms, this is the chamber’s wet furniture. It matches the dresser in marble and carving because it has to stand in the same picture. When indoor plumbing arrives, the washstand becomes the most disposable piece of the suite. Surviving sets often lack it. Dealers call a bed-and-dresser a “suite” anyway.

The nightstand or commode: small, one drawer, a door. Optional in cheaper sets.

The wardrobe or chiffonier: hanging space or a tall chest, for houses without closets. The next chapter but one takes the chifforobe. Here it is enough that the suite can include a tall case if the catalog says so.

Towel racks, benches, a lady’s desk in expensive sets. Berkey & Gay’s Centennial display will show how far the idea can be pushed as a prize (next chapter). The ordinary wholesale suite is three to five pieces. The photograph makes them a room.

Children’s chamber suites exist by the 1870s–80s in the same factories: smaller beds, a dwarf dresser, sometimes a crib that pretends to match. They are the suite idea taught early. Hotels buy metal beds (chapter 15) and wooden suites both; a commercial traveler’s hotel room in 1890 may be a cheap ash set, headboard to the wall, washstand with a common pitcher. The photograph in the factory catalog is always the best bedroom. The crates that leave Grand Rapids are every bedroom.

Marble is a separate trade. Tops for dressers and washstands come from American and imported yards, cut to templates the furniture plant sends. A suite whose marble does not match in thickness or in the profile of the edge is a remarriage. White marble photographs as luxury in monochrome. Spills stain. The washstand top is the first to go when a bathroom arrives and someone wants a garden bench.

## How the salesman photographs a room

Early factory photographs are often a piece on a plain studio floor, not a lifestyle tableau. Bayne’s plates, and the 1878 catalogs, sell the object: a dresser isolated, a bed isolated, then a page where the pieces of Suite No. 12 stand near one another in the same wood. The “room” is implied. Later advertising — 1890s into the twentieth century — builds fake chambers in the studio: a rug, a window backdrop, the suite arranged as if someone slept there. The Grand Rapids furniture shows, semi-annual, let retailers walk actual rooms. The traveling man with a case of photographs is the rest of the year.

Shipping samples by freight, Bayne’s original problem, never entirely dies. Large retailers and the shows still want the thing. But the photograph lets a small-town merchant in Iowa buy a walnut suite from a Grand Rapids catalog without standing in the factory. Mail-order houses will take the logic further: a drawing, then a halftone, then a numbered set you can order by freight to a depot that has no cabinetmaker.

The photograph also lies in the usual ways. Scale. A headboard looks shorter than it will in a nine-foot parlor chamber’s leftover bedroom. Color. Walnut and ash separate in the room and merge in a black-and-white plate. Completeness. The washstand in the picture may not be in the crate. Marble is extra. The mirror may ship with a different glass. The honest catalog says “marble tops extra” and no one reads it.

Elias Matter’s top-floor studio, skylight, factory dust: the photograph is an industrial object. It is not an artist’s impression of home. Home is what the buyer is asked to project onto the plate. That projection is the suite. Without it, you have a bed, a dresser, and a washstand that happen to share a finish. With it, you have a bedroom.

## Styles the suite can wear

Renaissance Revival, the 1860s–70s: walnut, burl, carved pediments, bun feet, marble. Mitchell & Rammelsberg in Cincinnati, Berkey & Gay and Nelson, Matter in Grand Rapids, New York shops still making custom. The suite idea is already wholesale in the Midwest factories.

Eastlake, 1870s–80s: the next chapter’s problem. Factories put incised lines and brass pulls on the same suite skeleton. The photograph does not change.

Golden oak, 1890s–1910s: cheaper timber, heavier machine carving, the suite democratized. Mail-order oak three-piece sets.

Colonial Revival, overlapping: a “colonial” suite with a highboy-ish dresser and a pencil-post bed, matching finish, sold as a set. This is the lie chapter 1 warned about, now as a product. The colonial chamber could not be photographed as a set without lying. The Colonial Revival chamber is designed to be photographed as a set. That is what makes it Revival.

The suite is therefore not a style. It is a commercial grammar that styles pass through.

## Labor in the matching

To match five pieces you need a shop that can repeat. Hand shops could match a pair. Factories match a line. Carving machines, later, and teams of carvers on the same pattern, earlier. Veneer presses for burl panels. Marble cut to templates. Mirror plates from a glassworks. A finish room that can stain a run of ash to look like walnut, or walnut to look even.

The men and women on those floors are not the named ébéniste of Broad Street. Grand Rapids employment included immigrants, children in some plants (the Grand Rapids Chair Company’s caning at the State Reform School in Lansing is a documented horror in the chair trade; chamber-suite plants had their own divisions of cheap labor). `[CITE NEEDED: a specific Berkey & Gay or Phoenix payroll fact for bedroom-suite workers, as opposed to the Chair Company caning.]` Piecework on drawers. A journeyman’s dovetail on a dresser side, or a machine dovetail, depending on grade. Higher-end walnut suites still show hand-cut pins. Ash suites for the trade may not.

Repeating a carved crest from bed to dresser is the visual match. Repeating drawer-lip profiles and secondary woods is the actual match. A “married” Victorian suite — bed from one factory, dresser from another, similar walnut — is a market commonplace. Look at the backboards. Look at the unexposed finish. Look at the marble hardware. The photograph married them first.

## What the suite does to the bed

The bed in a matching set has to look like the dresser. That sounds trivial. It is a change in what a bed is. A tester bed was a textile architecture. A Lannuier bed was a French side-facing machine. A Shaker bed was a green cart. A suite bed is a wooden headboard with a family resemblance to a case piece. The headboard grows. The tester dies in this market (it will survive in the South and in Revival). Brass and iron beds, a later chapter, will refuse the match on purpose. As long as the suite rules, the bed is the tallest member of a wooden family, not a separate trade.

Upholstery of hangings becomes optional decoration, then a dust problem, then a target for Eastlake and for hospitals. The suite photograph prefers bare walnut and a mattress. Cloth confuses the match.

## Residual

The colonial pair was two objects that could share a carver. The Victorian suite is a room that can share a negative. Bayne’s plates, and the factory catalogs of 1878, taught a salesman to sell the chamber as a set the way a later man would sell a kitchen. The washstand would vanish when the bathroom arrived. The idea would not. Every “five-piece bedroom set” in a twentieth-century warehouse is still Bayne’s answer to the freight problem: if the buyer can see the room in advance, the room can be shipped in crates. The eighteenth century could not photograph a chamber as a suite without lying. The nineteenth century built a chamber that was already a picture.
