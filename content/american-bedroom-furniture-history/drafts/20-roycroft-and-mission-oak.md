The Roycroft Inn at East Aurora, New York, put paneled twin beds in its rooms. Model 0106 in the 1912 *Roycroft Handmade Furniture* catalog is oak, about forty-eight inches at the headboard, thirty-nine wide, seventy-eight long, a pair of closed panel beds with an impressed inventory number on a lower leg — R 106 on surviving Inn examples that later went through Chicago auction rooms. The orb-and-cross is carved or branded. The shoulders are heavier than a Gustav Stickley bed of the same years. The Gothic is not a joke. Elbert Hubbard wanted a medieval shop in Erie County, and the bed looks like it.

Hubbard founded the Roycroft in 1895 after a visit to William Morris’s world, or after a salesman’s version of that visit, depending on which biography you trust. He printed books, then copper, then furniture. He died on the *Lusitania* in 1915. The campus kept making. The beds did not become a national suite the way a Grand Rapids mill could flood a market. Roycroft furniture was never manufactured in large quantities. That is the first distinction this chapter needs. Mission oak as Americans bought it in 1905 was mostly not Roycroft. It was Stickley, and then it was everyone who could quarter-saw white oak and fume it.

## Hubbard’s shop and a bed that stays in the inn

Roycroft furniture carries the device in the wood. You do not hunt a paper label in a drawer. The 1906 catalog, reprinted often, shows a line that is more monastic than Craftsman: corbels, thick posts, copper overlay on some case pieces, a language closer to Hubbard’s magazine *The Philistine* than to Irene Sargent’s essays for Gustav Stickley. A bedroom at the Inn is a room in a hotel that was also a brand. Guests slept in 0106. They ate downstairs. They bought a book. The bed is hospitality furniture with a philosophy attached, which is a dangerous combination. It can make the oak look more handmade than the shop hours justify, and it can make a later collector pay for the orb rather than for the joint.

Construction is oak or, on some twins, chestnut. Through-tenons and pinned joints appear, but the proportions are the tell: Roycroft posts are thicker in the shoulder, the panel is more castle than bungalow. Allison Eckardt published a pair — one oak, one chestnut, carved Roycroft on both — in *The Magazine Antiques*, October 1983. Rails go missing. A bed without rails is a headboard and a footboard and a story. The Inn provenance is the story that holds.

Joinery on a Roycroft bed is visible because Hubbard wanted it visible. Posts are through-tenoned or pinned at the rails; the panel sits in a groove; the headboard is a field, not a carved rococo. Compared with Stickley’s 923, the Inn twin is more closed — panels instead of a lifted, thinned crest — and more hotel: two matching twins, not a double that dominates a bungalow. A chestnut example next to an oak one is a shop using what it had. Chestnut in 1905 is still an American furniture wood; the blight that will empty the forests is already moving. `[CITE NEEDED: Roycroft chestnut sources and dates against the blight’s local arrival.]` Rails bolt or hook; Inn housekeeping knocked beds down. Missing rails on later auction lots are not a mystery. They are what hotels lose.

What Roycroft did not do is invent the American Arts and Crafts bed. Hubbard was a salesman of a life. The furniture shop was one of the products. Compare the hours in a Greene & Greene walnut bed in Pasadena: Hall-shop fitting, ebony pegs cut to a crest. Roycroft is faster in the mill and louder in the brand. Both refuse Victorian varnish. Only one of them is a factory in all but name.

## Stickley, and which Stickley

Gustav Stickley (1858–1942) is the brother the later bedroom remembers. Eastwood, New York, now inside Syracuse. The Craftsman Workshops. *The Craftsman* magazine. Quartersawn white oak, ammonia-fumed so the tannins darken and the ray fleck reads as a stripe. Mortise-and-tenon left visible. Hammered copper or iron hardware. A bed is a numbered form.

Bed no. 923 in the Stickley Museum at Craftsman Farms, about 1908–15, is the object to stand in front of if you can. The museum’s file traces it from earlier Bedstead no. 917, in *Cabinet Work from the Craftsman Workshops: Catalogue D*, as early as 1904. 917 is more rectilinear. 923 eases the crest of headboard and footboard, thins the posts, tapers them, lifts them, and cuts arched interior corners and a shaped bottom rail. A line drawing in *What is Wrought in the Craftsman Workshops* (1904) shares features; the later production is a restatement, not a new religion. The Craftsman Farms example passed with the Log House contents to George and Sylvia Farny in 1917, then down the family, then to the museum in 1997. It is original to the house Stickley built for a life he advertised.

Stickley hated the word Mission. He said so. The furniture was Craftsman. Retailers and the public said Mission anyway. The 1909 *Catalogue of Craftsman Furniture* from Eastwood is the document: beds among settles and tables, a system you could mail-order through retailers — over a hundred of them at the height. That is already a different economy from Roycroft’s campus and from the Greenes’ one house.

Then the brothers. Leopold and John George opened L. & J.G. Stickley in Fayetteville, down the road, in 1905. Their decals — “The Work of L. & J.G. Stickley,” later “Handcraft,” later “Onondaga Shops” depending on the year — are the collector’s headache. The furniture is often as good as Gustav’s in the oak and the joint, sometimes better in the production discipline. Stephen Gray and Donald Davidoff and Robert Edwards have done the L. & J.G. catalogs. A bedroom from L. & J.G. is still a numbered oak bed, not a Pasadena commission.

Stickley Brothers in Grand Rapids is another firm. Albert Stickley, with J.G. for a time, from 1891. They stamped “Quaint Furniture.” Quaint is a Grand Rapids word. It means a line you can sell next to other lines in a city that already knew how to make a chamber suite. The Grand Rapids company kept producing into the mid-twentieth century. `[CITE NEEDED: last year of Stickley Brothers Grand Rapids production; accounts say 1950s.]` Charles Stickley stayed closer to Binghamton and sold competing oak. Five brothers, several factories, one last name. A “Stickley bed” in a classified ad is not an identification.

## Mission as a market word

The style’s origin story, as the textbooks now tell it, is not Stickley at all. A. J. Forbes made a chair about 1894 for San Francisco’s Swedenborgian Church. Joseph P. McHugh of New York copied related chairs and, by 1898, was selling a line he called Mission, nodding at California’s Spanish missions. The furniture of those missions was not this furniture. The word was a window display. The 1901 Pan-American Exposition in Buffalo helped. By the time Stickley’s New Furniture appeared at the Grand Rapids trade show in July 1900, and Tobey of Chicago agreed to distribute, the rectangle in oak already had a name the shops could print.

David Cathers’s work on Gustav Stickley, Robert Judson Clark’s 1972 Princeton exhibition *The Arts and Crafts Movement in America*, and Wendy Kaplan’s later surveys are the shelf. They all have to spend a page unteaching Mission. The word sold. It sold beds. It sold a moral: simple, honest, handmade-looking. The factory was still a factory. Stickley used machines. He also used hands. The exposed tenon is a true joint and a brand.

What a Mission bed is, in oak: posts square or slightly tapered, headboard a field of vertical slats or a panel, footboard to match, side rails in hooks or bolts, a mattress on slats or a spring. No tester. No hangings. The Craftsman argument against the Victorian chamber is partly sanitary — fewer dust-catching textiles — and partly moral. The bed becomes wood again. Chapter 2’s *bed furniture* meant cloth. Stickley’s bed furniture means the frame.

## Limbert, Lifetime, and the Grand Rapids problem

Charles P. Limbert’s company sat in Grand Rapids and then in Holland, Michigan. Limbert oak is the one with the cutouts: squares, hearts, a lighter, more Dutch silhouette than Stickley’s gravitas. Some Limbert is genuinely odd in the best way — a table that looks as if a Glasgow designer had a Midwestern mill. Bedroom pieces exist. They are not the famous objects. Limbert’s chairs and tables carry the literature. The beds follow the same cutout grammar when they appear.

Grand Rapids as a whole is the problem the ethic cannot survive. The city could make anything. Once Mission sold, Grand Rapids could make Mission: Lifetime (Grand Rapids Bookcase and Chair Company), Harden, Stickley & Brandt, Ford Johnson, unsigned plant stands that still look like the style if you do not open a drawer. Quartersawn oak veneer over a cheaper core. Dowels where a tenon should be. Red oak stained to imitate fumed white oak. A bed that photographs as Craftsman and is a suite piece with a vanity in the same stain.

That is not a moral failing of a mill worker in 1910. It is a market. The Arts and Crafts ethic said the maker’s hand should remain visible. Grand Rapids Mission said the customer’s eye should remain satisfied at the price. Both produced beds people slept in. The later collector market pretends only the first was real. Most American bedrooms that “went Mission” between 1905 and 1916 went the second way. They bought a bed and a dresser from a retailer who had a Grand Rapids catalog, not a subscription to *The Craftsman*.

Kenneth Ames’s work on Victorian hall furniture is a reminder that moral furniture is still furniture. Ames is not writing about Mission, but the method travels: do not take the advertisement as the room. Grier’s work on the parlor is the same caution. A Mission bedroom in a small American house was often three pieces in oak finish, a brass bed leftover, and a quilt. The catalog page is cleaner than the room.

## How the bed is made when the mill is honest

When the mill is Stickley or L. & J.G. or a careful Limbert, the oak is quartersawn so the medullary rays flake on the panel. Fuming in ammonia — a tent or a chamber, not a brush-on stain, if the shop is doing what the literature says — changes color without burying grain. `[CITE NEEDED: shop-level confirmation of fuming versus stain on a specific bed model; fuming is documented for Stickley production in general, not always for a numbered bed.]` Pins in the tenon are walnut or oak. The rail-to-post joint has to knock down for shipping. A Craftsman bed that never came apart is a local delivery. Most came apart. Look for the hardware holes.

Slats are hardwood, often thinner than the posts, nailed or dropped into ledges. Later owners put box springs on frames that were never sized for them. A 1908 bed for a seventy-six-inch mattress looks “wrong” under a 2020 queen. Dealers stretch rails. The honest rail has a length that matches a period tick.

Fuming is chemistry, not a stain bottle, when the shop is doing what Stickley’s publicity described: ammonia reacting with tannin in white oak, a wait, a color that still shows the ray. Grand Rapids could skip the tent and brush a brown. The eye at six feet may not care. The drawer side will. A fumed Stickley drawer side is oak through the thickness; a stained Mission suite may be gum or poplar with an oak face. Open the drawer. The American bedroom of 1910 did not always open the drawer.

McHugh’s Mission, meanwhile, kept selling in New York as a line among lines. The Swedenborgian chair was a craft object in a church. McHugh’s copies were a retail idea. Stickley’s Craftsman beds had to live in that word-weather whether he liked it or not. A customer in 1908 who asked for a Mission bed might be sent Gustav, L. & J.G., Quaint, Limbert, or a crate from a mill that had never read Ruskin. The oak did not confess.

Roycroft 0106, Stickley 923, a Limbert panel bed, and a Grand Rapids unsigned “Mission” suite bed can stand in a row and teach the whole chapter. Same rectangle. Four economies. Only two of them wanted you to know the shop’s name.

## Residual

Hubbard’s orb is easy to love because it is a picture. Stickley’s red decal is a paper ghost in a drawer. Grand Rapids often left nothing. The American bedroom that went oak in those years was usually the third. The ethic lived in Eastwood and, in a different temperature, in East Aurora. The word that sold the bed was Mission, which named a California the furniture had not seen. A Roycroft Inn twin is a real object. It is not the typical bed. It is the hotel version of an argument Grand Rapids had already priced by the suite.
