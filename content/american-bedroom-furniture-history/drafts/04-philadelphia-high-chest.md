# The Philadelphia High Chest

Met 18.110.4 stands ninety-one and three-quarter inches in Gallery 752: mahogany and mahogany veneer over yellow pine, tulip poplar, and northern white cedar, brass, 44 5/8 by 24 5/8 inches in plan, John Stewart Kennedy Fund, 1918. The scrolled pediment carries a figural finial bust that the American Wing’s label ties to plates in Chippendale’s *Director* (1754). The serpent-and-swan carving on the bottom drawer goes to Thomas Johnson’s *A New Book of Ornaments* (1762). The chest is dated 1762–65 and made in Philadelphia. It is not attributed to a named shop on the current label. That refusal is the first honest sentence.

Philadelphia made the American high chest into a monument. Boston had stacked drawers and, sometimes, gilt. Newport blocked the facade and carved shells that belong to a different chapter. Philadelphia carved the case until the chamber had a piece of architecture that did not need a tester to look expensive. William Macpherson Hornor’s *Blue Book of Philadelphia Furniture* (1935) is still the rumored map. Morrison Heckscher’s Met catalogs and the Winterthur high chests — 1957.0506, 1958.0592, and their kin — are the objects you can walk around.

## A port that read London

Thomas Affleck arrived from Aberdeen by way of London in 1763 with a letter from Thomas Fisher: a Friend, a man David Barclay would own. He advertised the cabinetmaking business “in all its branches” after he moved to Second Street. John and Lambert Cadwalader, Michael Gratz, Levi Hollingsworth, Thomas Mifflin, James Pemberton, Governor John Penn — Hornor and the later Christie's literature on the Hollingsworth pair of high chests and dressing tables keep those names in the bills. Affleck is documented. Most high chests are not. William Savery labeled more often than the rococo carvers did; his surviving labels have made him a magnet for wishes. Benjamin Randolph’s shop is another magnet. The carving on a Philadelphia case is often a specialist’s work bought in, not a single genius’s hand from pediment to claw.

The woods are an Atlantic sentence. Mahogany for the show faces. Tulip poplar and yellow pine in the hidden members — the same secondary-wood logic Duncan Phyfe’s shop will still be using on Fulton Street two generations later. Northern white cedar in 18.110.4 is a drawer or dust-board choice that smells of the Delaware, not of Honduras. Jennifer Anderson’s *Mahogany* is the forest at the other end of the ship. A Philadelphia high chest is not innocent because the shop was on Second Street.

## Pediment, cartouche, claw

The broken scroll pediment is the type’s theater. A cartouche or a bust or a flame finial occupies the gap. Rosettes terminate the scrolls. The waist molding hides the joint between upper and lower case. Fluted quarter columns turn the corners into pilasters. Cabriole legs end in ball-and-claw feet whose webbing and knuckle are the dating arguments conservators still have. The central drawer of the lower case is often carved — a shell, a rocaille, Johnson’s swan — and set a little differently from the flanking drawers. That is the facade talking.

A dressing table — the lowboy — repeats the lower case in a shorter voice. Chapter 6 is that partner. Here the point is only that Philadelphia thought in pairs more consistently than Boston had. Hollingsworth’s matching high chests and dressing tables, associated with Affleck, 1765–75, are the documented extreme: not a five-piece suite, a pair that could furnish a chamber with one argument in walnut or mahogany.

Met 18.110.6, also Kennedy Fund 1918, is the other Philadelphia high chest in the same gift: 99 by 45 1/2 by 25 inches, mahogany, tulip poplar, yellow pine, dated 1755–90, “powerful stance and ambitious ornamentation.” The wider date range on the label is a curator admitting that silhouette is not a year. Use both objects. Do not collapse them into “the Met highboy.”

## Who slept under it

The high chest stood in a chamber that still hung a bed. The drawers held linens and clothing, not display porcelain. A piece this tall is a servant’s problem as well as a merchant’s pride: dusting the pediment, waxing the legs, keeping children off the carved drawer. Enslaved laborers in Philadelphia households — the city that would pass gradual abolition in 1780 and then take decades to mean it — opened those drawers. The furniture literature has been slower than the social history to put their hands on the brasses. `[CITE NEEDED: a Philadelphia chamber inventory that names an enslaved person in the same room as a high chest.]`

Quaker plainness is a myth if you only look at meetinghouse benches. The same Friends who dressed down in the street paid Affleck and Randolph for carving that would have looked at home in a London wareroom. The contradiction is the city’s. The high chest is its furniture.

## After the Revolution

The high chest does not survive the Federal period as a fashionable type. Chests-on-chests, smaller drawers, and then the Grecian bedroom of Lannuier and Phyfe take the wall. Philadelphia shops keep making case furniture; they stop building ninety-inch temples for the chamber. The form waits in farmhouses and then in collectors’ halls. Colonial Revival will drag it back as an ancestor (chapter 21) and put it where no colonial sleeper would have: a front hall, under a hunting print, without a bed.

18.110.4 remains a 1760s sentence. Chippendale’s plate and Johnson’s ornament have been read by a carver who knew how to make a serpent lie on a drawer without looking like a print glued on. The finial bust is a person you cannot name. The label does not pretend to name the shop. That is the residual fact. The most famous American highboy in the most famous American museum is still, correctly, “Philadelphia.”

## Carvers for hire

Philadelphia’s rococo furniture is a city of specialists. A cabinet shop framed the case. A carver — sometimes in the same building, often not — cut the pediment, the drawer, the knees, the feet. The Cadwalader commissions that scholars have tied to Randolph’s shop and to carvers in his orbit are the documented version of a habit that unlabeled high chests only imply. Heckscher’s *American Rococo* is the book that keeps the print sources (Chippendale, Johnson, Lock) next to the Philadelphia wood. A serpent that looks like a plate is not a failure. It is a shop doing what a port city paid for: London, on time, in mahogany.

The finial bust on 18.110.4 is the place a reader wants a name. There is none. It may be a generic classical head. It may be a portrait type. The label does not guess. Neither should a caption.

## Humidity, glue, and the tall case

A ninety-inch case in a Delaware summer moves. Hide glue lets go and takes hold again. Drawer runners wear. Pediment scrolls check. The secondary woods — poplar, pine, cedar — move differently from the mahogany faces. A high chest that still sits quiet in Gallery 752 has been conserved. The 1918 Kennedy Fund pieces came through a market that had already repaired and refinished. What you see is not 1763 untouched. It is 1763 argued through a century of taste. The swan may be sharper now than it was in 1900, or duller. Conservation reports, not the gallery glance, settle that. `[CITE NEEDED: Met conservation summary for 18.110.4 if cited in a caption as “original surface.”]`

## Why this chapter sits here

Chapters 3 and 5 are other American dialects of the same tall problem. Boston stacked. Newport blocked. Philadelphia carved. The three cities knew one another’s work through the coast. They did not merge. A reader who can tell them apart in a hallway has learned the only connoisseurship this series needs. The suite will try to erase that difference with a matched finish. The high chest, at its best, is a single object that refuses to be a set.


## Bernard, Jugiez, and the quarter column that is not the corner

Philadelphia’s carving is a purchase. Nicholas Bernard and Martin Jugiez advertised as carvers and gilders; their trade card is rococo theater in ink, trophies and scrolls, a London-facing sheet printed for a Delaware city. They sold work to more than one cabinet shop. James Reynolds and Hercules Courtenay occupy the same orbit in the Cadwalader paper trail — fire screens, tables, the documented carving that unlabeled high chests only rhyme with. Heckscher’s *American Rococo* keeps those names next to Chippendale and Johnson. A caption that gives 18.110.4 to Randolph or Affleck without a bill has chosen a magnet. The label’s “Philadelphia” is still the better sentence.

The quarter columns at the corners of a better case are a particular joint. They are not the case corner carved into a flute. A separate mahogany quarter is glued on, fluted, headed with a small capital. You can see the glue line if you are allowed to look. An applied column that has taken a knock tells the truth. Flutes that run through, versus stop-flutes, are a regional argument Newport will win on other objects. Philadelphia likes the pilaster that turns the case into a small building.

The pediment is a construction, not a single board. Scrolls laminated or sawn, rosettes applied, the central cartouche or bust pegged so it can come off when the case goes up a stair. Finials get lost. That is why so many museum high chests wear later flames or honest emptiness. The figural bust on 18.110.4 has been called Pompadour in dealer slang for a century. The Met’s label has not made her a sitter. Heckscher is cautious. A resemblance is not an identification. Leave the woman unnamed. The carving still has to sit in the gap of a broken scroll without looking like a print glued on. That is the shop problem Johnson’s plate did not solve in wood.

Ball-and-claw feet are the same timber as the leg, not a ball glued under a stick. The carver shapes the cabriole, then the toes, then undercuts the talons so air shows behind them. Philadelphia undercutting is a quality boast and a breakage risk. Webbing between the toes, the knuckle count, the tightness of the grip: conservators still argue from those. A restored claw that is sharper than the skirt is a later chisel. The 1918 Kennedy Fund pieces had already been through that market.

## Bills, pairs, and the hours you cannot put on a label

Thomas Affleck’s documented clients — Cadwalader, Hollingsworth, the Friends who paid for carving their meetinghouse taste would not have worn in the street — are the bills this city actually has. John Cadwalader’s papers are richer on other forms than on a named high chest; do not drag a commode invoice onto 18.110.4. The Hollingsworth high chests and dressing tables, 1765–75, associated with Affleck, remain the documented extreme of a pair. Christie's literature has worn those objects smooth. Use them as a pair that existed, not as proof that every carved skirt had a mate in the same order.

Joseph Downs’s Winterthur catalog — the Van Pelt high chest among the public ones — is how a reader sees the type off Fifth Avenue. Winterthur 1957.0506 and 1958.0592, already in this chapter’s figure plan, are the walk-around. Downs is 1952. He names woods and carving with a confidence later files sometimes narrow. Read the current object page.

Labor hours do not sit on the Met label. A case of this grade is weeks of joinery before the carver starts: drawers, dustboards, the waist, the backboards that are yellow pine and not meant to be seen. The carving on pediment, skirt, knees, and feet can outrun the case hours. Kindel’s later Winterthur reproductions, and the hour-counts that shop-talk likes to quote, belong to a later chapter. For 1763 the honest sentence is cruder: the carver was a specialist, the joiner framed, the brass was bought, the mahogany had already crossed. Enslaved and free Black labor in Philadelphia trades is documented in the city; a named high-chest hand is still the cite this chapter asked for and has not filled.

The dustboards between drawers, the front-to-back grain on many Philadelphia bottoms, the tulip-poplar sides, the cedar that shows up in 18.110.4 — those are the Delaware in the hidden members. Hide glue in a humid August lets go. Drawer runners wear to a hollow. The quiet stance in Gallery 752 is conservation as well as joinery. A reader who can see the quarter-column glue line has learned more than a reader who can recite Chippendale’s plate number.

The high chest still faced a hung bed. The drawers still held linen. The pair, when there was a pair, still was not a suite. 18.110.4 is that order, unsigned.
