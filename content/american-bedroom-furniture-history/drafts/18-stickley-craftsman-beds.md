The Craftsman Farms daughters’ beds never got a catalog number. Object 1997.2 in the Stickley Museum at Craftsman Farms, Morris Plains, New Jersey, is oak and oak veneer, inlaid with copper and tinted woods, manufacture of the inlay attributed to George H. Jones. Thirty-eight and five-eighths by fifty-seven and a half by seventy-eight and a half inches. About 1907–11. A shop-mark decal on the inside of the headboard rail. Anonymous gift. Gustav Stickley had the bed by 1911; it sold with the contents of Craftsman Farms in 1917 to George and Sylvia Farny; by descent to Cyril Farny; purchased by an anonymous donor for the Craftsman Farms Foundation.

The museum’s description is the one to stay inside. United by inlaid motifs and by the shape of the headboards and footboards — a shape that looks back to Stickley’s earliest work and recalls the stretchers of the “Tokio” plant stand published in 1900 — the bed is part of the original suite for his daughters’ bedroom in the Log House. The head- and footboards echo the lower apron of the dressers. That echo is the suite’s grammar. The dressers are an inlaid variation of chest of drawers no. 913. They are chests of drawers. They are not beds. The bed is not 913. Catalog 913 is a dresser form Stickley altered — inlays, a subtle taper in the legs, the apron shape — so that the central carcass still matched a standard offering while the room matched itself.

This form of bed does not appear in any of the catalogs Stickley issued. A glass-plate negative of the model without inlay is at the Winterthur Museum Library. That plate is the ghost: the same boards, no copper, no tinted woods, a product that could have been sold and, on this evidence, was photographed as if it might be. The daughters got the inlaid one. The firm did not print it.

## The room in the Log House

Craftsman Farms, Stickley’s New Jersey property, Log House, family home until the money ended. On the second floor, the girls’ bedroom was the only room furnished with a matched suite featuring inlaid designs in copper and stained woods. Notes from the museum (spring 2017) are blunt about that uniqueness. Crab Tree Farm later lent the original inlaid oak writing desk so the room could be seen as intended. The suite also involved nightstands; the museum’s linked objects treat the inlaid chest of drawers (variation of no. 913) as the bed’s relation, not its identity.

Two beds, daughters, a room that had to read as one statement. Each bed veneered on both the inlaid side and the plain side of the footboard — more work than a catalog bed, because a footboard has two faces and a child’s room has circulation. The relative simplicity of the outline hides that labor. Oak, not mahogany. Copper and tinted-wood inlay, not ormolu. No tester. No brass knobs of the sanitary bed. No Eastlake gallery. Low: thirty-eight and five-eighths inches high. A person sitting on it is in a different relation to the floor than a person climbing into a Renaissance Revival walnut suite.

The dressers’ original finish, the museum says, was what Stickley called “gray oak,” a gray wash related to his driftwood effect of about 1902 or to “Silver Grey Maple” adapted to oak, a matte shellac-based coating with “varying tones of silver gray, like those found in a hornet’s nest.” That hornet’s-nest sentence is Stickley’s, as quoted in the dresser object record; it is not a caption to invent around the bed. The dressers also had, originally, bright robin’s-egg-blue circles setting off wooden drawer pulls — now faded. Inlay of metal and dyed woods would have caught firelight. The bed shares the inlay language. Do not dump the dresser’s blue circles onto the bed unless the bed’s record says so. The record in hand describes the bed’s inlay and veneer, not blue rings on the headboard.

Harvey Ellis, who designed inlaid furniture for Stickley in 1903–4, is the name people want to hang on any inlay. Ellis died in 1904. These beds are circa 1907–11. The inlay here is attributed to George H. Jones, a specialist, on the museum’s object page. Ellis is the earlier inlaid catalog furniture. Jones is the attribution for this room. Mixing them is how a daughters’ bed becomes an Ellis relic. It is not.

## What the catalogs did sell

Gustav Stickley’s Craftsman Workshops issued catalogs of numbered furniture: chairs, tables, chests, beds in oak, sometimes willow, the mission rectangle Americans still call “Stickley.” David M. Cathers, *Furniture of the American Arts and Crafts Movement* (first published 1981; the standard furniture text), is where those numbers and the firm’s production logic live. Cathers’s later *Gustav Stickley* (2003) is the biography, including the bankruptcy. Use Cathers for what was standard. Use the Craftsman Farms object files for what was not.

Catalog beds tend to be oak plank head- and footboards, through-tenons, wooden knobs or none, sometimes a slight arch. They photograph as a type. They were meant to. The daughters’ bed uses the firm’s oak religion and then does private work: inlay, an apron rhyme with a modified 913, veneer on both faces of the footboard, no number. A factory that preached honesty of construction still had a back room for family.

Through-tenons and pinned joints on standard Craftsman beds are the visible joinery Eastlake had wanted and Grand Rapids had generally faked with incising. Stickley’s early United Crafts rhetoric, and Irene Sargent’s essays in *The Craftsman*, put that joinery in print. The magazine ran from 1901 to 1916. Sargent edited the early years. Arts and Crafts as a moral interior: Morris, Ruskin, then American houses, gardens, a political range Stickley was willing to host without becoming a socialist in any party sense. Beds in the magazine are often in drawings of Craftsman houses, oak, low, coverlets, no canopy. The daughters’ inlaid suite is more dressed than the magazine’s typical bed and less dressed than any Victorian suite. Copper in the oak is a glitter Stickley usually reserved for hardware.

L. & J. G. Stickley, the brothers’ firm, made related oak furniture; later they would make Colonial reproductions after Gustav’s collapse. Do not put 1997.2 on a Leopold label. The decal is Craftsman Workshops, inside the headboard rail.

## 1915, 1917

On 23–24 March 1915 Gustav Stickley was declared bankrupt. He owed on the order of a quarter of a million dollars. `[CITE NEEDED: the petition’s exact figure if a caption will use it; museum history text says nearly a quarter of a million.]` *The Craftsman* continued into December 1916, then died (a 1917 merger with *Art World* appears in some accounts). Craftsman Farms was sold in 1917 to the Farnys to stave off foreclosure; Winterthur’s Stickley finding aid also records 1918 for a farm sale in one sentence, 1917 in the museum’s object provenance. Prefer the object file for the furniture: 1917, George and Sylvia Farny, contents of the Log House. The Winterthur business papers (Col. 60) hold the ugly part: creditors, Craftsman, Inc., the Craftsman Building in New York, the magazine’s dissolution.

Taste had shifted toward Colonial Revival and other historic styles — the same Revival that put highboys back in bedrooms and made “Shaker style” a product. Oak mission looked dated to buyers who had liked it in 1902. Stickley tried late ornament and painted elements. It did not save the firm. Cathers, *Gustav Stickley*, pages 201–202 in the 2003 edition, is cited as the best short account of the bankruptcy. Read those pages; do not quote them from memory.

The daughters’ furniture left with the house sale. An antique dealer in Massachusetts, decades later, coordinated a private sale from the Farnys to an anonymous donor; twenty pieces of original Log House furniture came back, including an inlaid oak bed and dresser from the girls’ bedroom. Press, then visitors seeing furniture as it had been in 1911 photographs. Object 1997.2 is that return. A second daughters’ or related bed in the museum’s orbit (L2012.11.5, a later suite associated with Mildred) is a different object, 1915–17, not the inlaid 1997.2. Do not conflate. This chapter’s bed is 1997.2.

## Construction, inlay, the Winterthur plate

Oak and oak veneer: even here, in the honesty shop, veneer. Both faces of the footboard. Inlay: copper and tinted woods, a technique that is not Stickley’s standard quarter-sawn oak show. George H. Jones as inlay attribution — a specialist brought to the work, the way a Philadelphia high chest needed a carver. The decal inside the headboard rail is where Craftsman marks often live, out of the show face.

Dimensions: 38 5/8 inches high, 57 1/2 wide, 78 1/2 long. Width is a three-quarter or generous twin in later talk; it is a period bed for a girl, not a modern queen. Rails that still take that length are original logic. A “Stickley bed” cut down for a queen is a different, usually catalog, object.

The Winterthur glass plate of the uninlaid version is the firm caught thinking this outline might be a product. Without inlay the rhyme with dresser 913’s apron would still hold if the dressers were plain; with inlay the room becomes a closed set. Catalogs did not get the plate’s bed, on present evidence. The archive did. Cathers and the Winterthur Stickley papers are where to chase whether an uninlaid example was sold. Until a numbered catalog page appears, the daughters’ inlaid bed is extra-catalog.

Do not call dresser no. 913 a bed. No. 913 is a chest of drawers in the Craftsman list. The Craftsman Farms dressers are a variation of that chest: same central carcass, altered legs and apron, added inlay. The bed echoes the apron. That is influence inside a room, not a shared catalog number.

## *The Craftsman*, 1901–16, and what a bed was supposed to be

Stickley founded the magazine in 1901 to extend United Crafts. Offices to New York in 1905. Houses, gardens, furniture, politics, conservation. A Craftsman bed in those pages is a moral oak rectangle in a bungalow. The Farms’ daughters’ room is more personal than the bungalow ideal: inlay, color (on the dressers, blue and gray oak), a suite in a movement that often pretended not to sell suites. It *is* a suite. Chapter 12’s matching idea, stripped of marble and Renaissance crests, still organizing a room so that bed and case pieces rhyme. Stickley needed that rhyme for his children. He did not need it enough to print the bed.

United Crafts and the Craftsman Workshops still used machinery. The through-tenon is not a medieval miracle. It is a chosen visible joint. Veneer on 1997.2 is a chosen hidden efficiency. Both can be true. Eastlake’s American nightmare was machine incising pretending to be craft. Stickley’s better oak is machine-aided craft admitting the joint and, in this bedroom, admitting decoration he usually preached against in quantity.

Mildred Stickley’s later five-piece suite (museum object L2012.11.5 and relations, ca. 1915–17) is original to the Farms and a different story: not the inlaid 1907–11 daughters’ bed, but a subsequent family set that stayed in descent into the twenty-first century. Keep the inventory straight. This chapter’s critical object is 1997.2.

## Residual

A decal inside a headboard rail, a number the catalogs never used, a Winterthur plate of the same outline without copper, a dresser numbered 913 that remains a dresser, a bankruptcy in 1915, a farm sale in 1917, a return gift that let the bed stand again in the Log House. The Craftsman daughters’ bed is the Arts and Crafts movement doing what Victorian Grand Rapids did — matching a room — without agreeing to sell the match. Cathers can number the chairs. He cannot number this. The Farny household slept in it after Stickley could not keep the roof. The inlay is still copper in oak, low to the floor, no tester, no brass knobs, a child’s width. The suite photograph, for once, was a family photograph.
