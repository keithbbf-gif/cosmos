A four-legged stand beside the bed, the top about mattress height, one or two drawers for what the night required, is not a colonial type. Candle stands of the eighteenth century were light, often tripod, easy to tip, parked where light was needed, not dedicated to sleep. The Kovel column that traced the four-legged lamp table to the early 1800s — safer than a three-leg, a drawer for nighttime things, maple or mahogany, later drop leaves and glass knobs — is a popular version of a real shift. Whale-oil lamps wanted a stable deck. A glass of water wanted the same. The object that furniture English would eventually call a nightstand is a small case or a table that has agreed to live next to the sleeper.

By the time Gustav Stickley made a nightstand for Mildred Cruess’s Chromewald suite about 1915–17 (Stickley Museum, with the bed L2012.11.5 and the dressing table L2012.11.2), the small table was part of what a bedroom group meant. Sausage-turned legs, a shelf, a drawer, Colonial Revival without accuracy. The museum’s note on that nightstand is frank about the historicism. It is also frank that the set is five pieces, original to the Farms. The nightstand is the tell. A 1760 chamber could be complete without it. A 1916 suite could not photograph as complete without it. That is the invention: not the first table that ever stood by a bed, the first time the suite required one.

## Lamp, glass, book

Light is the first job. A candle stand could be moved to a chair. A lamp table by the bed is for reading in the cloth box, then for reading after the tester came off, then for the electric bulb that did not smell. Four legs because three tipped when you reached from the mattress. Drawers because the night has small objects: spectacles, a watch, later pills, a gun in some households, a prayer book, a handkerchief. The chamber pot is a different object — a commode, a close stool, a pot under the bed — and should not be collapsed into the nightstand, though some nineteenth-century “night commodes” are cabinets that did both. The type this chapter means is the small table, not the pot cupboard, even when catalogs mixed them.

Electric light made the type unavoidable. A candle could be pinched. A bulb stays on a deck all night, a shade, a cord to a socket that might be across the room until houses were wired for the bed wall. The nightstand is partly an electrical object: a place that is not the floor for a hot lamp. Hotels bolted them down so the lamp and the table left together. The domestic suite copied the pair of lamps and left the bolts off.

Ralph Kovel’s 1998 sketch is orientation, not the monograph. Museum examples of work tables, candlestands, and “night tables” in Winterthur and the Met need object-by-object checking before a caption names an accession. `[CITE NEEDED: a specific Met or Winterthur night table accession used in the figure plan.]` What is not in doubt is the nineteenth-century American bedroom’s growing clutter of small wood by the mattress as lamps improved and books cheapened.

A glass of water is a serious object. It stains. A marble top on a hotel nightstand is a later answer. A wooden top wants a finish that can take a ring. Shop problem, not a catalog sentence: a drawer that swells in August and will not open when you need the aspirin is a failed nightstand. I have eased nightstand drawers that were cut too proud on a humid week, the same as any small case. The joint is a runner. The load is a pull at 2 a.m. with a sleepy hand. If the drawer binds, the type has failed even if the lamp still stands.

## Telephone

The telephone did not begin on the nightstand. It began in a hall, on a wall, on a gossip bench — a seat plus a shelf for a directory, a piece Dwell and the telephone-furniture writers have treated as a type of its own. By the 1930s a large minority of American households had service; the instrument was still often public to the house. The Princess phone, 1959, compact, a light in the dial, marketed for bedrooms, is a date the nightstand had been waiting for. A second line, a long cord, a teenage door: privacy as a class fact (chapter 38) now with a ringer.

Not every nightstand held a phone. Many held a lamp and a clock. The clock-radio of the later twentieth century is another small machine that needed a deck. The alarm clock is a nightstand object. So is the baby monitor. So is the phone brick of chapter 42. The type’s genius is to be a parking place for whatever the culture will not put on the floor. When the culture puts it on the floor anyway, the nightstand is unemployed, like the bedstead under foam.

Gilbert Rohde’s 3317 nightstand for Herman Miller, chestnut and sequoia burl, matte chrome, shown with the Bedroom Group at the 1933 Century of Progress, is the modernist admission: the small table belongs to the numbered group. Waterfall suites from Huntingburg and a dozen Indiana and North Carolina plants put a pair of them beside a bed because the photograph has two lamps. Pairing is the suite’s last symmetry. A single nightstand is a household. A pair is a page.

Open shelves on a nightstand — a cube, a Holmdel-style teak box, a MALM open cube — are the type without a drawer. They photograph. They collect dust and a glass ring. The drawer is the hard problem because it must run after a season. A shop in a county that still names oak can treat that small case as a test: if the nightstand drawer binds, the chest drawers will. I would rather fit a small runner honestly than veneer a cube. No product name is required for that sentence. The bedside table is a shop problem the size of a stack of books.

## The last piece of the photographed suite

Grand Rapids could sell a chamber without nightstands when the type was still a lamp stand the customer might already own. By the mid-twentieth century the suite photograph is incomplete without them. Ethan Allen maple, the waterfall walnut, the blonde oak, MALM: two small cases or two open tables, a lamp on each, a plant if the art director is anxious. The nightstand is cheaper than the dresser and more visible in the image than the chest. It is the last SKU because it sits in the foreground.

Take it away and the suite still functions as storage and a bed. The photograph does not. That is why the object survived the high chest’s departure for the hall, survived the tester’s loss of cloth, survived the foam carton. A boxed mattress on a platform still wants a place to put a phone. IKEA sells the small table in the same series. The American copies do too. The type is now often a cube, a drawer, foil, a cam lock. It is still the invention: a dedicated small case for the sleeper’s edge.

Stickley’s Chromewald nightstand, with sausage turnings that would not have met a William and Mary shelf in any accurate shop, is the Arts and Crafts man conceding the suite. He had argued against historicism and then built a historicist small table so a daughter’s room would be complete. The drawer bottom of that piece, the museum notes, carries test patches of finish — a maker still working after bankruptcy, using the unseen wood as a trial. That is a shop observation the object itself holds. A nightstand is small enough to experiment on. It is also small enough to be the piece a factory adds when the completeness rule demands another SKU.

## Residual

The colonial chamber put a basin on a chest and a candle where it would not light the hangings on fire. The nightstand is what happened when light became a lamp, water became a glass, time became an alarm, and conversation became a phone by the pillow. The last piece of the photographed suite is a small table because the camera needs a lamp and the sleeper needs a deck. A shop that still cuts a drawer that does not bind is practicing the type’s only hard problem. In a county that still names oak, that drawer is a Tuesday, not a product line. The rest of the room may be a carton and a screen. The small table, if it is there, is the suite’s last honest requirement: a place to put the glass.

A pair of nightstands in a suite photograph is symmetry, not anthropology. Plenty of households had one table, the lamp on the other side a windowsill. The pair is a sales idea (chapter 40) that the camera loved. Hotels doubled it because two occupants paid. The type can be singular. The invention still holds: a dedicated small deck at mattress height, not a chest top across the room, not a candlestand you fetch. Once electric light and a clock and a phone agreed to live there, the object was a type. MALM’s small chest is the late, cheap version. Stickley’s sausage-turned stand is the family-suite version. The colonial chamber did without it because the candle moved and the basin sat on the chest. Completeness changed. The wood did not become more virtuous. It became more local to the pillow.

Cup holders, USB ports, a wireless pad: the type now swallows the machine. That is still a nightstand. The drawer that does not bind is still the joint. A shop can refuse the USB and still make the table. A factory will put the port in because the photograph needs a glow. Neither choice is ancient. Both are the invention continuing: the sleeper’s edge as a dock.

Waterfall nightstands with a single drawer and a waterfall pull are easy to find used. They are the 1930s suite’s foreground, often the last pieces left when the bed was dumped. Collect them as documents of the sales idea, not as colonial. The small table outlasts the suite because it is cheap to keep and because the lamp still needs a deck. That is the afterlife of chapter 45’s object: not a hall trophy, a useful leftover.

Princess phones went to the nightstand; smartphones went to the bed. The table did not die. It lost the cord and gained a brick. If the brick is on the floor, the invention has been declined, like the bedstead under foam. Plenty of rooms decline it. The type remains the last piece a photographer will add back when the suite needs to look like a room.

A glass still rings. That is enough work for a small table.
