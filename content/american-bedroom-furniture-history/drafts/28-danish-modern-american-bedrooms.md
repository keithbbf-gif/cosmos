# Danish Modern in American Bedrooms

Hans Wegner’s Round Chair — “The Chair” — landed on the cover of *House Beautiful* in November 1954. Marion Gough’s line about it being stolen-from is the later reputation; the 1954 fact is a Danish dining chair on an American shelter magazine, not a bed. That is where this chapter has to stay honest. What stood in United States bedrooms under the word Danish was often a teak or walnut-finish headboard from a department store, a pair of nightstands with turned, tapered legs, and a mattress on a Hollywood frame. The Wegner stayed in the dining room if it came at all. Finn Juhl’s collaboration with Baker Furniture in Grand Rapids put a Danish name on American production for a short, famous moment. The Chieftain did not become a headboard. The bedroom took the idea of teak, oil, and a sculpted foot, and ran it through mills that already knew how to make a suite.

Maggie Taft’s work on Danish design in America — the Marshall Plan, Thai teak, subsidies, the fact that between 1952 and 1957 teak became the wood most used in Danish furniture exported to the United States — is the trade history. American rooms consumed a large share of that export. Den Permanente in Copenhagen sold to tourists and to correspondence-order clients. Department stores in New York, Chicago, San Francisco, and a hundred smaller cities opened “Danish” corners. Edgar Kaufmann Jr. at MoMA liked the organic parallel between Danish and American design. Elizabeth Gordon enlisted both in a Cold War domestic argument. The bed, as usual, was slower than the chair.

## What actually stood in the room

A documented American bedroom of 1958 might hold:

- A Drexel Declaration headboard in walnut, Kipp Stewart and Stewart MacDougall, slats or a sculpted stretcher, a “Hollywood” mount.
- A Lane chest — Acclaim’s faux dovetail inlay, or a quieter walnut group — because Lane was in the stores and the cedar-chest habit had trained the customer.
- A Selig chair in the corner, imported from Denmark or made to a Lawrence Peabody “Danish Line” that the vintage market still misattributes to Ib Kofod-Larsen.
- A mattress on metal rails.
- No Wegner.

Selig, founded 1931, Massachusetts, later Monroe, Indiana in the lore, imported and branded Scandinavian modern for Americans. Peabody’s Danish Line of the mid-1950s was designed for Selig; after he left, Kofod-Larsen and others entered the ads. The living room took the chairs. Bedrooms took a headboard if Selig or a competitor offered one, or they took American walnut that “looked Danish.” The tell is the oil: true teak likes oil; American walnut veneer often wears a lacquer that imitates oil. Wipe it. The cloth will say.

Broyhill Brasilia (Chet Beardsley, 1962) is not Danish. It is American walnut with a starburst carving, a suite, a low bed. It stood in the same rooms as the teak chair. So did Heywood wheat, so did Planner maple. The American bedroom in these years is a mixed dialect. Magazine photographs purify it into one country. Estate sales tell the truth: a teak nightstand that is actually walnut, a cane headboard, a brass lamp, a Colonial highboy in the hall.

Department stores — Bloomingdale’s, Macy’s, Marshall Field’s, Gump’s, the local “fine furniture” floor — staged Danish rooms with a bed if they could get a bed. Many Danish factories were small, under ten employees, Taft notes, and could not fill American demand. Copies filled it. “Danish modern” on a hangtag in 1961 may mean:

1. Imported teak, oil finish, a maker’s name in a drawer.
2. Made in Denmark for an American retailer (Selig’s model).
3. Made in the United States in walnut or pecan, “inspired by.”
4. Printed grain on particle board, a later cheapening, already possible at the end of the decade.

Only (1) and (2) are Danish in the factory sense. The bedroom did not always know the difference. The customer knew the color: a warm brown that was not waterfall walnut and not fumed oak.

Gump’s in San Francisco and the West Coast import shops had an easier time with actual teak because of Pacific shipping. A New York store might mix Juhl at Baker with a Danish chair and an American headboard and call the room Scandinavian. Kaufmann Jr.’s MoMA organic-design language gave the salesman a script that was not a species. Organic could mean a sculpted walnut stretcher from High Point. It could mean a teak plank from a Copenhagen cabinetmaker. The American sleeping room took the cheaper organic. The chair, if there was money left, was the expensive Dane. That split — sit Danish, sleep American — is the typical floor plan.

## Teak, oil, and a sculpted rail

Teak in a Danish bed — when a bed was exported — is a panel headboard, often a slight lift or a scooped edge, turned legs, a footboard omitted or kept low. The joinery is dowels, a proud craft of rounding, a finish that is oil or a very thin varnish. American hands that oil a teak headboard every year are doing maintenance the mill expected. American hands that never oil it own a gray, thirsty board. The wood was often Thai, the work Danish, the money American. That triangle is the Marshall Plan’s furniture.

Wegner-adjacent, in an American bedroom, means a round, softened geometry, a wishbone in the living room, a headboard that wants to be a single gesture. Keep Wegner in the adjacent. He did not furnish Levittown. The stolen-from chair taught American mills to round a stretcher. Drexel Declaration’s cutout slats are that lesson in walnut. They are also American: a suite name, a catalog, a matching nightstand with a sculpted handle.

Baker’s Juhl pieces are a Grand Rapids experiment in making Danish under an American roof. Short-lived. The lesson for the bedroom is that even a famous name did not convert the American mill to one-off cabinetmaking. Baker still had to ship. Juhl still had to be adapted. The Chieftain’s scale is a living-room problem. A bed adapted from that thinking would still have to take a Simmons mattress and a bedspread from a bridal registry.

## Department-store rooms and the copy mill

A “Danish” bedroom on the floor of a 1960 department store is a stage: teak-colored cases, a low bed, a paper lantern, a rug that is not an Oriental in the 1920s sense. Salesmen had a script about oil and honesty. They also had a suite to move. American copies from Bassett, Thomasville, the southern plants that had made waterfall, put tapered legs on a triple dresser and called it modern Scandinavian. Pecan and pecan-finish gum did a lot of that work. The legs are the giveaway: too thin, a ferrule that is brass rather than teak, a drawer interior of plywood that never saw Funen.

Lawrence Peabody’s Selig chairs were sometimes manufactured in Denmark for both markets. That fact scrambles the import/copy binary. An American designer, a Danish factory, an American store: the chair is Danish-made and American-conceived. A bedroom that holds that chair and a North Carolina headboard is a typical 1962 room. Do not call the room Danish. Call the chair by its label.

Lane’s Perception and other sculpted-leg groups, Drexel, Heritage, Founders: the American “good modern” floor. Founders’ walnut, a little more architectural, sometimes closer to Nelson than to Wegner. The bed is a headboard SKU. Footboards vanish. The platform chapter and this chapter overlap in the object and disagree in the wood. Danish is a color and a rounding. Platform is a height. A room can have both. Many did.

## What the Craftsman bed looks like from here

Stickley’s oak rectangle and the Danish teak headboard share a moral vocabulary: honesty, wood, no gilt. They do not share a mill, a century, or a posture. Stickley is vertical slats and a fumed flake. Teak is oil and a taper. The 1960s American who bought “Danish” as a rejection of mother’s waterfall was repeating the 1905 rejection of Victorian walnut, with better heating. The highboy remained in the attic or went to the auction that would feed Kaufman and the next museum. The Danish bedroom did not want ancestors. It wanted a clean floor and a chair that *House Beautiful* had blessed.

Labor in a Danish shop of ten people is not Winchendon’s line. Labor in a North Carolina copy mill is Winchendon’s cousins. The American bedroom, statistically, got the cousin. Teak oil in the garage, a can of Watco, is a 1960s smell that belongs to both the import and the copy, because the copy wanted to be oiled too. The grain under the oil will confess if you look at the end of a rail. Teak is teak. Walnut is walnut. Pecan-stain gum is a third thing.

A teak bed rail, when you do find an imported one, is a turned or tapered stick with a bolt into a headboard that is a solid plank or a framed panel. The Danish shop rounded the arris so a shin would not bark. American copies used a router and a varnish that built up in the hollow of the round. Hardware is often unseen: a bed bolt with a cap, or a metal bracket painted brown. The mattress still sits on slats or a metal frame. Danish honesty never extended to inventing a new sleeping surface. Simmons did that.

Import invoices and store labels: look for a paper in the drawer with a Copenhagen or a Zealand town, or for Selig’s American tag on a Danish-made chair. A headboard with no label and a pecan interior is a southern mill. The 1960s customer who saved the receipt did us a favor. Most did not. Identification now is species, finish, and the shape of the leg. Teak’s pore and color are not walnut’s. A blacklight will not help. A knife on an unseen edge will. Do not vandalize a museum. Do it on a flea-market rail.

## Residual

Wegner’s chair on a 1954 cover did more for American dining rooms than for American sleep. What stood beside the bed was a headboard that had learned to be low and brown and a little round, made in Denmark if you were lucky and in a southern plant if you were typical. The word Danish did the work Mission had done: it sold a moral rectangle. The wood was teak when the invoice was serious. The mattress was American. The posts were still gone. A highboy in the hall, if it survived, was the ancestor the teak room refused to sleep with.

Den Permanente correspondence, when it survives in archives, is a customer in Ohio asking about a chair, not a bed. `[CITE NEEDED: a published Den Permanente letter about bedroom furniture, if Taft or a museum file has one.]` The American sleeping room was never the export’s center. Chairs and tables paid the Danish shops. The bed remained a domestic mill product with a foreign accent on the headboard. That is the residual, restated as a floor plan: teak in the corner, walnut at the pillow.
