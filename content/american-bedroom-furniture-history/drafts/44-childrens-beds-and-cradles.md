# Children's Beds and Cradles

Inventories name cradles the way they name feather beds: as value, not as sentiment. A cradle is a box that rocks, a body that cannot leave it unassisted, a textile lining, sometimes a hood. When the child can climb, the object is a different problem — a trundle in the parents’ chamber, then a small bedstead, then, much later, a crib whose slats the state will measure. The cute photograph is the enemy of this chapter. The objects are furniture. Some of them kill.

CPSC’s crib rule, effective 28 June 2011, banned the manufacture and sale (including resale) of traditional drop-side cribs and tightened slats, mattress supports, and hardware. Full-size cribs, 16 C.F.R. part 1219; non-full-size, part 1220. Child-care facilities had until December 2012 to comply. Cradles, as rocker or pendulum types, sit outside those crib definitions and under other infant-sleep rules. The federal sentence is long. The furniture fact is short: the infant bed became a regulated product because the household bed was injuring infants. That is not a design story first. It is a body count, then a standard.

## Cradles in the record

A colonial cradle may be boarded, turned, hooded, painted. New England examples in Historic New England and Winterthur are maple, oak, pine, a hood that is a small tester. The cloth again outruns the wood: a lining, a quilt, a tick. Rockers are the joint that matters. They have to be long enough not to dump, short enough to live under a chair rail. A hooded cradle is a room inside the chamber, as the hung bed is a room for the adult. Heat, dark, a mother’s reach.

Who made them? Joiners, turners, the same shops that made the adult bedstead, sometimes a specialist. Painted “fancy” cradles of the early nineteenth century are a type collectors like. Enslaved children in quarters: the record thins the way chapter 38’s pallets thin. A basket, a box, a drawer — archaeology and scattered documents, not a labeled maple hood. `[CITE NEEDED: a specific inventory or archaeological report that names a cradle or infant bed in quarters.]`

Wicker and later millwork made cheaper cradles for a broader public. The object remained a box that moved. When it stopped moving, it was a bassinet on a stand, then a crib that did not rock because rocking became a medical argument. `[CITE NEEDED: dated pediatric advice against cradle rocking, if a caption needs a year.]` The furniture followed the advice as it followed sanitation.

Shaker communities made cradles and small beds with the same wheeled, untheatrical intelligence as their adult frames. A Shaker cradle is not a folk toy. It is a box with rockers, a textile, a community’s child. Collectors have over-loved some of them. The ordinary painted pine cradle in a farm inventory is closer to most American infant sleep. Museums show the handsome hood. The chapter should remember the cheap box.

## Trundles

A truckle or trundle bed is a low frame on wheels that slides under a taller bedstead. Cummings’s houses and the inventories that stocked them treat it as ordinary: another sleeper in the chamber, a child, a servant, a visitor. Historic New England’s examples are maple, a rope grid, no theater. The joint is the wheel — wood, then iron — and a rail low enough to pass under the parent’s rail.

The trundle is not a youth bed. It is a second chamber in the same floor plan. Privacy is not the point; watching and heat are. When houses grew corridors and extra rooms, the trundle became quaint. Colonial Revival put it back as a guest trick. The original was not a trick. It was a body in the room.

A modern “trundle” under a captain’s bed or a daybed (HEMNES, the American copies) is storage or a guest mattress. The child’s version with a pop-up is a hotel idea. CPSC cares about the crib, not the trundle, until the object is marketed for infants. Definitions matter. A youth bed is not a crib. A crib used past the intended age is a climbing problem.

## Youth beds and the drop side

Nineteenth-century “youth” or “child’s” bedsteads are small posters, painted cottage suites, sometimes a half-size of the adult chamber set. They are suites in the Grand Rapids sense when they match a dresser. They are also a commercial admission that children had rooms. Metal enamel cribs of the early twentieth century are cousins of the hospital bed: washable, bright, a drop side so an adult back would not have to bend. The drop side is the joint that failed.

Hardware on a drop side takes repeated load. Pins walk. Plastic brackets, in later cheap cribs, crack. Infants suffocate in the gap. The 2011 rule did not come from taste. It came from incidents. Resale of drop-side cribs — garage sales, the used market that also moves Ethan Allen maple — became illegal for that type. A household that kept grandmother’s drop side as a laundry hamper is still holding a regulated object if it could be used as a crib. The law is about the stream of commerce. The ethic is not to put an infant in a joint that walks.

Bunks and loft beds are youth furniture with a different death: falls, the upper rail, the ladder. ASTM and CPSC have had bunk standards; they are not the 2011 crib rule. A captain’s bed in a child’s room (chapter 33) trades tip-over of a dresser for height. Bookcase headboards still want a wall strap. None of this is cute.

Youth “chamber suites” in the Grand Rapids catalogs — a small bed, a dresser, a chair, a finish that matched the parents’ room in miniature — are the sales idea (chapter 40) aimed at a new room count. They are documents of families that had a room to spare. They are also how a child got a drop-side later, when the youth bed was swapped for a crib language that had come from the hospital. The sequence is not linear in every house. A trundle in 1900 and a youth suite in 1925 and a 1970s bunk in the same family line can all be true.

## Safety as a furniture fact

ASTM F2057 and the STURDY Act (chapter 32) are about dressers, not cribs, but the child’s room is where they meet. A MALM in a nursery, unanchored, is the high chest’s lever at a height a toddler can pull. A crib that meets 2011 can still sit next to a chest that does not. The room is a system. Furniture history that only follows the cradle’s hood will miss the chest that falls.

Inclined sleepers, inclined “nests,” the later infant-sleep product rule — these are not bedsteads in the cabinetmaker’s sense. They are products that occupied the cradle’s job with foam and a marketing department. The CPSC’s 2021 infant sleep product work pulled some of them toward the bassinet standard. A magazine chapter cannot litigate every SKU. It can say: the cradle’s descendants include objects that are not wood, and the state is in the nursery because the market was not.

## Residual

The most regulated bed in the American house is the infant’s, not the tester and not the king foam. That fact should sit next to the inventory line that priced a cradle as wealth. The object was always serious. Sentiment made it look otherwise. A trundle in a 1740 chamber and a 2011 crib in an apartment are both answers to a small body in a household that has other sleepers. The residual fact is a gap: between a drop side and a mattress, between a chest and a wall, between a hooded cradle and a quarter where no cradle was listed. Fill the gap with a standard or with a name in an inventory. Do not fill it with a pastel caption.

A last inventory habit: count the small bedsteads. If the house has one “best bed” and three cheap ones, you are looking at labor and children, not at a master suite. The 2011 crib is the state’s version of that count: we will specify the infant’s box because the household’s other beds were not built for a body that cannot roll over with intent. The high chest never had to pass ASTM. The infant bed did. That inequality of regulation is the chapter’s map of whose sleep the law can see.

Mesh play yards, bassinets, and “nests” took market share from the wooden cradle because they fold and they advertise. They are furniture in the sense that they occupy the infant’s night. They are not joinery. A history of bedroom furniture that pretends the only infant bed is a hooded maple box has accepted a museum highlight as a census. The CPSC’s other parts — 1218 bassinets, the infant sleep product work — are the state’s attempt to catch the non-wood. Woodworkers do not own this chapter. Inventories and the federal register do.

A youth bed with a guardrail is a joint problem: the rail must take a climbing load. Cheap rails snap. That is not a metaphor. It is a fracture. The same shop eye that looks at a bed-rail hook (chapter 39) should look at a guardrail’s fasteners. Children’s furniture is not a lesser scale of adult furniture. It is adult furniture plus a body that will test every edge.

Cradles as heirlooms get carved dates and family names. That is furniture as genealogy, closer to a Hadley chest than to a CPSC crib. Keep the genealogy. Do not let it erase the other infant beds that never got a name. A drawer used as a bassinet, a suitcase, a box from the store — those are in the history even when they are not in the collection. The 2011 crib is visible because it is a product. The unnamed box is the older majority.

Trundle wheels fail. Replacement wheels are a use-repair, like a bed bolt. A Colonial Revival trundle that never slides because the carpet is thick is a decoration. The original had a floor it could take. Restoration ethics (chapter 41) apply: do not call it complete if it cannot do the job the inventory assumed.

CPSC’s 2011 FAQ was aimed at retailers and day cares as much as at parents: you may not sell the drop side, including at a yard sale. Furniture history rarely has a date when a type becomes illegal to resell. This one does. Treat it as a date like 1939’s Ethan Allen line or 2002’s MALM: a year the bedroom changed by statute, not by taste.

A cradle that rocks on a stone hearth and a crib that must not rock are the same assignment: keep a small body through the night. The furniture changed because the medicine did, and because the market did, and because the law did. Inventories, then CPSC. Not a cute chapter.
