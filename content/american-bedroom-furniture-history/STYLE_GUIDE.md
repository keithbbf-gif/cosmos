# Style Guide

This series talks to people who sleep in rooms, restore chamber furniture, buy suites, or cut bed rails in a shop. Bradley Brand works Arkansas hardwoods. The series teaches how American bedroom furniture was made and used. It does not sell a line.

## Voice

Magazine voice, not catalog voice. Write as a person who has stood at a high chest, lifted an upper case off a waist, or read a factory stencil under a drawer. Prefer names, dates, accession numbers, and joint names over general claims.

Speak to the reader as a peer. No coaching. No cheerleading. No closing moral.

First person is allowed for shop or room observation. It is not a diary.

## Banned phrasing

Do not use: delve, landscape (metaphorical), robust, leverage, unlock, cutting-edge, game-changer, “In today’s,” “It’s important to note,” Moreover, “Whether you’re,” “In conclusion,” “At the end of the day,” “rich tapestry,” “journey,” “elevate,” “empower,” “seamless,” “holistic,” “unpack,” “nuanced” as filler.

Do not open with a dictionary definition. Do not close with recap “key takeaways.”

No COSMOS. No AI. No process talk about how the draft was written.

## Facts and citations

Pin museum objects, pattern books, factory marks, and local histories. Mark `[CITE NEEDED]` when the fact is traditional but unpinned.

Do not invent accession numbers, prices, or voyages.

## Structure

1. Open on an object, joint, room, or failure the reader can picture.
2. Name the form and woods.
3. Explain joinery and how the piece moves in a house.
4. Walk history including labor where it is documented.
5. End with sources and cross-links, not a sermon.

Target 1,400–2,200 words of body text.
