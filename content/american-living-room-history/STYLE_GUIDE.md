# Style Guide

This series talks to people who sit in living rooms, buy sofas, restore parlors, or specify seating for a house that still has a front room. Bradley Brand works Arkansas hardwoods. The series teaches how American living-room furniture was made and used. It does not sell a line.

## Voice

Magazine voice, not catalog voice. Write as a person who has stood in a period parlor and then gone home and looked under a sofa. Prefer names, dates, ports, accession numbers, and tool marks over general claims. A sentence may be short. A sentence may run if it is carrying a fact.

Speak to the reader as a peer. Do not coach. Do not cheerlead. Do not close with a moral.

First person is allowed when it is a shop or room observation. It is not a diary.

## Banned phrasing

Do not use: delve, landscape (metaphorical), robust, leverage, unlock, cutting-edge, game-changer, “In today’s,” “It’s important to note,” Moreover, “Whether you’re,” “In conclusion,” “At the end of the day,” “rich tapestry,” “journey,” “elevate,” “empower,” “seamless,” “holistic,” “unpack,” “nuanced” as filler.

After an editor pass, set `voice_check: edited` in frontmatter (read-aloud, grammar, magazine voice; no sell copy).

Do not open with a dictionary definition. Do not close with a recap list of “key takeaways.” If a list is a working table (dates, patents, museum objects), keep it. Decorative bullet stacks are out.

No COSMOS. No AI. No process talk about how the draft was written.

## Facts and citations

Furniture history needs a named source: a museum object record, a pattern book, a price book, a builder’s guide, a catalog, a monograph. Preferred anchors include Winterthur, the Met American Wing, MESDA, Chipstone *American Furniture*, Fitzgerald’s *Three Centuries of American Furniture*, Clark’s *The American Family Home, 1800–1960*, Downing’s *Architecture of Country Houses* (1850), Lillian Hart Tyron (1915), Henry L. Wilson’s *Bungalow Book* (1910).

If the fact is traditional shop knowledge and not pinned, mark `[CITE NEEDED]`.

Do not invent a voyage, a price, a royal commission, or an accession number. If a story is too good and the paper is thin, say so.

Do not conflate the 1910 *Ladies’ Home Journal* living-room language with the 1918 influenza. The parlor-as-death-room / funeral-parlor story is older and separate. The 1918 flu accelerated funerals leaving the house; it did not invent the living-room name.

Hunt board, Colonial Revival “Pilgrim,” and dealer romance are named as later stories when they are later stories. This series is living rooms, not dining rooms. Do not steal dining-room slugs or ledes.

## Structure of an article

1. Open on an object, a room, a plate, or a failure — something a reader can see.
2. Name the form (sofa, easy chair, center table, sectional, recliner) and the woods or upholstery.
3. Say how the piece is joined or stuffed and how it moves (casters, recline, modular clips, knockdown).
4. Walk the furniture history, including enslaved labor, factory labor, and servants where they are true.
5. Place the piece in a house: hall, parlor, sitting room, living room, family room, great room.
6. Leave the reader with one way to look at a piece in the room.
7. End with sources and cross-links, not a sermon.

One unified essay. No recap that restates the lede.

Target 1,400–2,200 words of body text. Frontmatter and source lists do not count toward the band.

## Brand and SEO staging

These drafts are staged for BBF / Bradley Brand furniture-history SEO. They teach. They do not sell sofas.

Bradley Brand / BBF may appear where Arkansas or southern hardwoods are the actual subject. One or two sentences. No product names, no “our craftsmen,” no call to action.

Primary keywords must not cannibalize. One slug owns one query. Cross-link siblings; do not repeat the same lede in two files.

Pillar map (intended, when `content/_seo/furniture-pillars.md` is present): History hub. Craft and woods pages may be linked later. No therapy, no supplements, no AI, no figs except a single winter-barn mention if a later editor adds it.

Do not cannibalize `content/american-dining-room-history/`. A sofa is not a dining chair. A parlor is not a dining parlor unless the house actually used one room for both and you say so.

## Names and spelling

American spelling in the running text. Historical British terms stay British when they are terms: parlour only in a quoted British source; sofa, settee, couch as the period used them. Use the furniture-trade name first.

Quartersawn, flatsawn, riftsawn: one word each after first explanation.

## Numbers

Dates: 1721, not “the early eighteenth century,” unless the year is honestly unknown. Dimensions from museum labels in inches as published. Do not convert a label you have not seen.

## Photos

Caption in `PHOTO_CAPTIONS.md`. Prefer public-domain plates, museum collection images with rights stated, and (as fills) BBF oak and walnut shop shots. Do not paste copyrighted magazine interiors.
