# The parlor as death room

The blinds are down. A badge of crape hangs at the front door. Inside, the best room has been rearranged: chairs pulled from the walls, a coffin on trestles or a cooling board where the center table usually stands, the matched parlor set doing the only work it was always willing to do — hold still while the household performs. The American Heritage essay “The Parlor” (October 1963) lists the occasions that room was built for. Among them, without romance, is “the hushed and lugubrious conversation when a member of the family had died.” That hour is this page. It is older than the influenza of 1918 and older than the magazine habit of calling the front room a living room. It is also the hour the undertaker eventually takes out of the house, along with the name *parlor*.

Do not stitch *Ladies’ Home Journal* in 1910 to the flu of 1918. The dates do not work. 1910 is eight years before the pandemic. Henry L. Wilson’s *Bungalow Book* (1910) and Lillian Hart Tyron’s *Speaking of Home* (1915) are arguing for a room you may sit in. They are not renaming a death-room after a plague that has not yet arrived. The flu accelerated funerals leaving the house. It did not invent the living-room name, and it did not invent home viewing. Those are separate histories. A later website that staples them — Rancho Los Cerritos, among others, repeating the stitch — is useful for Fitzgerald’s seven-piece set and Downing’s center table. It is not useful for 1910 and 1918.

## The public room takes the body

Before there is a funeral home, the dead stay in the house. Washing and dressing the body is women’s work for most of the period this essay covers: an extension of nursing, of laying out linen, of the same hands that sewed in the sitting room. Men order the coffin, dig or pay for the grave, and manage the street. Sidney George Fisher, the Philadelphia diarist, recorded in the 1830s and 1840s funerals that were already becoming male and private in their street arrangements. The room that holds the viewing is still the parlor, because the parlor is the room the household has already declared public.

The furniture does not change species for a death. It changes arrangement. The center table — Downing’s “emblem of the family circle” in 1850 — is moved if the coffin needs the floor. Chairs that spent their lives against the wall come into a circle or a line. Casters, which the parlor set has so the room can be cleared for a reception or a dance, now clear space for mourners. Horsehair does not care why it is slick. A sofa that was never meant to be slept on is not a bier. The body is in the coffin or, earlier in the process, on a cooling board: a portable plank the undertaker or the family uses to lay the dead out, sometimes in a bedroom, before the parlor viewing.

Oscar Fitzgerald’s *Three Centuries of American Furniture* is a furniture book, not a mortuary manual. It still belongs here because the seven-piece set is what many households owned when the viewing happened. Armchair, lady’s chair, sofa, four chairs. The set is rank furniture. Death does not suspend rank. The husband’s chair and the wife’s chair are still in the room. Guests sit on the side chairs. The sofa may hold family. None of that is a living room. It is the face of the house doing the last face work.

In a house too small for a separate sitting room, the same front room has been the sewing room and the call room. The death-room nickname is later popular language for a fact inventories already knew: the parlor is where the household receives, including this reception.

## Cabinetmakers become undertakers

The profession that takes the dead out of the house begins in the shop that already knows how to make a box. Mid-nineteenth-century city directories list cabinetmakers who are also undertakers. The Encyclopedia of Cleveland History, working from that city’s directories, is unusually plain. By 1857 Cleveland listed sixteen cabinetmakers, ten of them also undertakers. Fabric and furniture on one side of the store; laying-out rooms and ware rooms on the other. Ready-made coffins from a pine box to a mahogany or rosewood case. The early undertaker’s establishment seldom included a funeral parlor. Funerals were still held in private homes. The shop sold the coffin, the door badge, sometimes rental chairs. The house still supplied the room.

That dual trade lasts a long time. Brewster & Stroud in Chagrin Falls, founded as a furniture-and-undertaking business in 1870, did not split the two trades until 1937. `[CITE NEEDED: a New England or Mid-Atlantic city-directory sample, 1850–1880, counting cabinetmaker-undertakers the way the Cleveland encyclopedia does.]` The joint is visible in the object. A man who can dovetail a drawer can miter a coffin. A man who can stuff a parlor sofa knows how to line a case. Upholstery and undertaking share horsehair, tacks, and a willingness to work with a body that is not going to complain about the fit.

The Civil War changes the chemistry, not the parlor. Embalming, practiced in the field so bodies could travel, becomes a civilian service. Thomas Holmes is the name later trade histories repeat as the war’s most visible embalmer; treat the biography as trade memory unless you have the army contract in front of you. `[CITE NEEDED: a primary wartime advertisement or contract pinning Holmes’s civilian parlor practice, as opposed to later professional folklore.]` What can be said without folklore is that arterial embalming lengthens the time between death and burial. A longer interval makes a longer viewing possible. It also makes a specialist more useful than a neighbor with a cooling board. After the war, embalmers attach themselves to furniture shops and livery stables. “Furniture and undertaking parlors” appear as a storefront phrase. The parlor is still, in most towns, the family’s front room. The undertaker comes to the house with a portable table, instruments, and later formaldehyde. St. Louis genealogical notes describe the pattern into the early twentieth century: embalm in the bedroom, dress the body, carry it to the front parlor for visitation, then provide the hearse.

Women lose the laying-out as the male trade professionalizes. That is not a metaphor. It is a labor transfer. The sitting-room sewing table does not prepare the body once the undertaker’s bag is in the hall.

## The funeral parlor takes the name

Professional associations arrive before most households give up the home viewing. The Ohio State Undertakers Association formed in 1881. The National Funeral Directors Association formed in 1882. Embalming schools in Cleveland, Cincinnati, Pittsburgh, and other cities teach a trade that now includes marketing. Neighborhood funeral homes begin to consolidate three functions: the preparation room, the family parlor, and something like a chapel. The word *parlor* moves with the furniture idea — a room for receiving — and leaves the house.

Dean Lampros’s dissertation on the residential funeral home (1910–1960) tracks the next architectural shift. Undertakers leave downtown storefronts for large houses in declining nineteenth-century neighborhoods, especially around the First World War and after. The building looks like a house because the ritual still wants a parlor. Zoning fights follow. The industry advertises privacy and “homelike” comfort as reasons not to keep the body in the actual home. By about 1930, in many cities, visitation at the funeral home is ordinary. Home funerals persist in some communities into the 1940s and later. The furniture history is the transfer of a room-type. The matched set stays in the family’s parlor, which is now embarrassed by the old job. The undertaker buys his own sofas.

Crape, door badges, and rental chairs are the small objects that show the trade entering the house before the house is abandoned. A furniture store that sold parlor suites already stocked chairs. Renting them for a funeral is the same inventory on a different invoice. `[CITE NEEDED: a dated undertaker’s or furniture dealer’s price list for rental parlor chairs and a door badge.]` I will not invent the dollar figure.

## What the 1918 flu did and did not do

The influenza pandemic of 1918–1919 killed quickly and in volume. Cities limited public gatherings. Some limited the size of funerals or the time a body could remain unburied. Undertakers were already offering a place that was not the family’s front room. A pandemic makes that offer look like sanitation instead of fashion. The flu accelerated a move that the NFDA, the embalming school, and the residential funeral home had already begun.

It did not rename the parlor. *Ladies’ Home Journal* in 1910 is a women’s magazine issue from eight years earlier. Wilson’s bungalow copy of 1910 wants a cheerful living room. Tyron in 1915 can already write that we are becoming a parlorless nation because of space, service, expense, and a new social feeling — “few friends and good ones.” None of those sentences require a virus. A later internet habit pins the living-room name on the flu and on a 1910 magazine as if the magazine had waited out the pandemic and then issued instructions. That is a chronological impossibility. The living-room essay in this series owns the name change. This essay owns the viewing.

The two histories touch without merging. A household that has stopped using the front room except for calls and corpses is a household ready to hear Wilson. A household that has sent the corpse to a funeral parlor is a household whose front room has one less reason to stay sacred. The furniture does not become honest overnight. A 1920 living room can still hold a parlor suite with the arms cut down. Colonial Revival can put Windsors back in and call them Early American. The job changed faster than the stock.

## How to look at the room without turning it into a ghost story

If you are in a house whose front room still has a center table at parlor height, casters on the chairs, and a door to the street, you are looking at a room that could have taken a coffin. That is not a reason to stage one. It is a reason to notice the clearance. Parlor sets roll because the room had to open — for a reception, a dance, a viewing. A living-room sofa pointed at a hearth or a screen does not need that clearance. The death-room is a use, not a style.

Look at photographs. A coffin in a parlor is a documented American image from the wet-plate decades through the early postcard. The wallpaper, the suite, and the trestles are the furniture history. Post-mortem portraits that pose the dead in a chair are a different, smaller practice; do not read every stuffed chair as a prop. `[CITE NEEDED: a museum-catalogued parlor viewing photograph, 1860–1910, with a named city and a visible suite — cite the archive, not a viral scan.]`

Labor stays visible if you look for who is missing. The woman who would have washed the body in 1850 is not in the 1910 undertaker’s advertisement. The cabinetmaker’s name has become a firm. The parlor set in the family’s house is now only for calls, and then not even for those. Tyron’s two chairs and a palm are what is left when the public work has gone down the street to a house that is not a home.

This series is living rooms, not mortuary science. The reason the death-room belongs here is the furniture: the same seven-piece set, the same casters, the same face of the house. The funeral parlor is a professional who borrowed the room-word and then took the body. The living room is a household that decided the best seats may be used. Keep those sentences apart. The flu belongs to the first as an accelerant. It does not belong to the second as a christening.
