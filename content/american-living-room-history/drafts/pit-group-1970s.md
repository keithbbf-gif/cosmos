# The 1970s pit group

Selig Furniture advertised a modular seating group in 1973 under a name that told the truth and then ducked it: The Playpen. “It’s not for everyone.” The photograph is a living room without a sofa in the old sense. Low square modules, velvet or velour, arranged as a U or a closed square, a well of cushions you step down into even though the floor is flat. A 1977 Van Nuys newspaper advertisement sold the same idea by the piece: build a playpen, or a sofa, or a pair of lounges; velveteen cushioning; a price that put the type on a tract-house budget. Kroehler’s Galaxy — dealers still use the name for a related pit sectional — is the factory cousin. The living room has become a piece of upholstery large enough to be architecture.

This is not the conversation pit. The conversation-pit essay in this series owns the sunken floor: Miller House, the built-in banquettes, the architect’s hole in the slab. The pit group is the furniture answer for a household that did not pour a pit. It sits on the existing floor and fakes the drop with the thickness of the foam and the absence of legs you can see. Shag carpet runs up to the bases and sometimes under them. You take your shoes off. You do not perch. You sprawl.

## Modules, clips, and velvet

Name the form. A pit group is a set of armless and corner sections, sometimes ottomans, sometimes a wedge, that fasten with hidden brackets, pins, or interlocking bases. The frame is usually hardwood or plywood, the deck a sinuous spring or a foam slab on webbing, the cushions thick polyurethane wrapped in polyester fiber. The cover is the period: crushed velvet, cotton velour, sculptured nylon, later Herculon if the household feared children. Colors run rust, chocolate, gold, avocado, the palette of a 1970s kitchen extended onto the floor. Buttons tuft the backs. Welt cords outline the squares.

Selig had been a modern manufacturer for a generation — Monroe, Michigan, among other places, a town that also made recliners — and knew how to sell a low, clean sectional. The Playpen is that sectional allowed to go feral. Dealer listings now attach Milo Baughman’s name to almost any cube modular from the decade. Baughman designed for Thayer Coggin; Selig made its own line. Unless a label or a dated catalog page ties a specific group to a designer, treat “Baughman style” as a later sales adjective. `[CITE NEEDED: a 1973 Selig catalog page that names a designer for The Playpen, if one exists.]` The object is the module, not the signature.

Kroehler was the other factory voice: a large American upholstery house that could ship a Galaxy pit in enough sections to fill a ranch living room and still leave a path only if you sacrificed a section. Eleven-piece groups turn up in the used market. That count is a floor-plan fact. A Victorian seven-piece parlor set left walking space because the pieces were upright. A ten-piece pit group is a carpet with backs. The coffee table, if it survives, becomes a low island in the well or disappears in favor of a tray. The cocktail-table essay in this series owns the drink height. Here the drink sits on the floor or on an ottoman.

Knockdown is the point. Modules come off a truck. Two people can reconfigure a Saturday. The living room that hosted a Super Bowl can become a conversation square on Sunday and a straight sofa against the wall when the parents want the room to look like a room again. That flexibility is real and limited. The brackets scar the frames. The corner sections only work as corners. A missing ottoman leaves a bite. Unlike a true conversation pit, you can take this one with you when you move — if the next living room is large enough, which it often is not.

## Shag, sunken rooms, and the echo

The pit group needs a floor that forgives. Shag — long nylon or polyester pile, sometimes wool in the better houses — is the textile that made sprawling sanitary enough to try. Dirt hides. Toes disappear. Cigarette ash, in a decade that still smoked indoors, becomes a archaeology. The carpet is not furniture, but it behaves like upholstery. A pit group on a bare oak floor looks stranded, like a Belter sofa in a barn.

Ranch houses of the 1950s and 1960s had already dropped a step or two into the living room in some plans. The ranch-house essay owns that slab. A sunken living room plus a pit group is a double drop: architecture and foam agreeing. A flat living room plus a pit group is the cheaper echo. Either way the sitter’s eye-line falls. Conversation happens across a well, faces closer to the same height, knees toward the center. That is the conversation-pit idea without the concrete saw.

Television sits on the remaining wall or on a low stand at the open side of the U. The television essay owns the box. The pit group owns the audience. A recliner at the lip of the pit is a compromise some households made — one person still wanted a lever — and it breaks the democracy of the foam. Children claim the ottomans. Guests who do not want to climb in sit on the rim and look like they missed the point. The furniture ranks people by willingness to get down.

Labor is factory cutting rooms and a household on its knees with a vacuum head that cannot reach the crevice between two modules. There are no servants in this living room. There is a teenager told to pull the sections apart and vacuum the crumbs. Velvet holds pet hair. Herculon holds a grudge in a different way. Reupholstery of a ten-piece group is a decision against moving house.

## What the pit group is not

It is not a sectional in the 1950s sense. The sectional-sofa essay in this series owns the earlier angled pieces that still read as a sofa and a half. The pit group wants to erase the sofa outline. If you can still say “that is the sofa” without pointing at a constellation, you may have a modular sectional that is not yet a pit.

It is not an inglenook. Built-in benches beside a bungalow fireplace keep the back vertical and the feet on a hearth. The pit group has no hearth requirement. It can face a fireplace, a television, or a blank stucco wall. When it faces the television, the ceremonial wall has already moved. When it faces nothing, the ceremony is the group itself.

It is not Danish modern, though both are “low.” Wegner’s armchair leaves air. The pit group fills the air. A teak chair pulled up to the edge of a velvet pit is a collision you can date to a real living room. Households mixed. Taste did not stay in lanes.

Sexual advertising clung to the Playpen in later retellings — key parties, the “adult” joke in the name. Period ads are flirtier than a parlor catalog and tamer than the internet’s memory. Sell comfort, sell imagination, sell a room that is not for everyone. The living-room fact is more ordinary: a young household, a new ranch or a split-level, a wish to sit on the floor without sitting on the floor.

## How to look at a surviving group

Count the pieces and draw the floor. A true pit needs corners. If the corners have been sold off, you have a low sofa and a story. Look under a module for a Selig or Kroehler label, a style number, a fabric lot. Unlabeled cube sectionals from the 1970s are a type; do not invent a designer. Check the brackets. Bent hardware and stripped screw holes mean the group has been rearranged until the wood gave up.

Push a cushion down. Early polyurethane crumbles. A group that sits on the webbing is tired, not “original sag.” Velvet that has gone bald on the front edges of the platforms is the living-room wear this series treats as evidence. A replacement cover in a 1990s khaki twill is an alteration — the catalog decade putting a slipcover morality on a pit. Name it.

If the group still occupies a sunken living room, stand on the upper floor and look down. The furniture should explain the step. If it sits on a flat slab in a great room, you are looking at a revival or a holdout. The great-room essay owns the later, taller space that made pits look like leftover toys. Between about 1968 and 1982 the toy was the living room. You got in, and you stayed in, and the front rail of a parlor sofa had nothing to say about it.
