# The overstuffed sofa of the 1920s

A Washington newspaper in August 1926 sold a Karpen overstuffed suite the way a grocer sells flour: by the covering. Blue, mulberry, or beaver velour. Jacquard taupe. Tête brocaded mohair. Taupe mohair with frieze seat tops. The frames are barely in the copy. That is the point of the form. Between about 1915 and 1935 the American living-room sofa stops presenting carved wood and starts presenting a stuffed volume. Legs shorten, then disappear under a skirt or a deep apron. Springs go deeper. Cushions come loose. The word the trade prefers, in a great many towns, is not sofa. It is davenport.

The parlor seven-piece set had already taught households to buy seating as a matched kit. The overstuffed living-room suite keeps the kit and changes the construction. A gentleman’s chair and a lady’s chair still appear in the ads, but they are now deep club shapes with the same pile as the long seat. The room they serve is the room Lillian Hart Tyron said had replaced the parlor. People sit in it on a Wednesday. The furniture has to take that sitting.

## What overstuffed means in the shop

Overstuffed is a trade word for a frame that does not show. The hardwood skeleton — maple, birch, gum, sometimes oak — is webbed, then sprung, then padded, then covered so that the silhouette is upholstery. Seat springs are coil springs, often hand-tied in eight-way patterns on better work, clipped or machine-tied on cheaper work. Back springs appear on many 1920s pieces, which is why a surviving sofa can sag in two planes. Burlap, moss, curled hair, and cotton sit over the ties. Karpen advertised that it did not use excelsior, the shredded-wood filler that cheaper shops stuffed into voids. Curled hair in Chicago came from the stockyards. The copy said “thoroughly purified.” The labor did not need the adjective.

Loose cushions are the second half of the type. A Victorian parlor sofa is often tight-upholstered: the seat is stitched to the frame. The 1920s living-room sofa wants reversible or at least removable seat cushions, spring-filled or down-wrapped, so a household can turn them and so a factory can ship a deep seat without a single hard pad. Spring-filled cushions show up in Hecht Company copy in January 1927 on a bed davenport suite. The same ad sells a mahogany cane-back suite with spring-filled cushions in damask. Two markets, one interior idea: the sitting surface is a separate object from the box.

Mohair is the covering that dates the decade in the hand. The National Museum of American History holds a 1928 length of cut mohair pile from the Massachusetts Mohair Plush Company — blue-green pile on a cotton back, sold largely to railroads and furniture companies because the pile did not crush easily. Living-room ads of 1926 already treat mohair as a grade above velour and tapestry. Frieze, a uncut or patterned pile, appears on seat tops where bodies slide. The fabric is animal: Angora goat hair, spun and woven in New England mills, then cut in Chicago and Grand Rapids upholstery rooms. Women did much of the sewing. Men pulled the springs and set the frames. A suite that looks like one designer’s idea is a stack of trades.

## The davenport, and the legs that vanish

A. H. Davenport and Company of Cambridge, Massachusetts, made high-style interiors and sofas for architects, including work connected to H. H. Richardson and the 1902 White House renovation. The company’s boxy upholstered sofa was popular enough that “davenport” became a generic American word for a substantial couch, often one that could sleep a body. Do not confuse it with the English davenport desk. The living-room davenport of the 1920s may be a fixed sofa or a bed. The Hecht ad is blunt: a living room by day and a bedroom by night, with storage for a mattress. Apartments and small houses needed that honesty. A parlor sofa did not admit it.

Legs are the tell. A Federal or Empire sofa shows turned or carved supports. A Renaissance Revival parlor piece shows them as part of the display. The overstuffed sofa shortens those supports until they are blocks, bun feet, or nothing a visitor sees. Skirts, deep welts, and T-cushions that hang over the front rail complete the collapse. Tuxedo arms — straight, even with the back — and club arms that roll once and stop are the new silhouettes. Cane-back suites keep a little wood visible and were sold as lighter in a small room. They are the exception that proves the rule. The decade’s ambition is a stuffed horizon.

S. Karpen & Bros. of Chicago is the firm that made this look national before the 1920s and then rode it. Solomon Karpen, a German-Jewish immigrant from West Prussia, opened the shop in 1880 with his brothers. By 1899 the company claimed to be the world’s largest upholstered-furniture maker, with a vertical plant that kiln-dried lumber, built frames, and upholstered on a railroad spur. Karpen advertised in mass magazines, not only trade papers, and put a metal tag with a guarantee on every piece. In 1906 Isaac Karpen received U.S. Patent 826,170 for a spring-bottom construction. That patent is a shop fact, not a fairy tale about comfort. The 1920s ads still sell “Karpen construction” the way they sell mohair. Kroehler, grown from the Naperville Lounge Company, was the other giant. Its 1926 catalog of davenport beds and living-room furniture talks about “hidden qualities”: the spring steel you cannot see. The overstuffed sofa is an argument that the important work is inside.

Pueblo/Deco and other carved-frame davenports exist. William Penhallow Henderson’s pine sofa of about 1925, in the New Mexico Museum of Art, is 79 3/4 inches long, mortise-and-tenon, with removable box springs under cotton. It still has a wooden face. Most American living rooms did not get a Henderson. They got a factory box with a pile cover and a skirt.

## How the room changed around it

The living room that takes this sofa is already losing the parlor center table as a standing-height emblem. A deep sofa wants a low table in front of it — the coffee-table essay owns that form — and a radio cabinet somewhere the sitters can face. The seating group becomes a U or an L: sofa on the long wall, two chairs angled, a lamp table at each arm. Downing’s family-circle table does not survive that geometry. The family circle is now a stuffed horseshoe aimed at a hearth or, by the late 1920s, at a speaker cloth.

Clark’s *American Family Home* is still the book that keeps this from becoming costume. Middle-class houses after the First World War have fewer servants and more evenings at home. The overstuffed suite is comfort as a purchase. It is also labor transferred. Someone still has to beat the cushions and rotate them so the mohair does not shine in one stripe. In houses with a maid, that is her Thursday. In houses without, it is the same woman who chose the beaver velour from the newspaper. Factory furniture made the suite available in towns that had never seen Belter. It did not make the suite weightless.

Bed davenports add a different labor. The mechanism — folding, sliding, or pivoting — is iron under the seat. You do not see it until a guest needs it. The mattress stored in the base is a claim that the living room can become a chamber. That is an old American habit. The hall-and-parlor house slept in the best room. The 1920s apartment does it with a hinge. Do not romanticize the hinge. It pinches fingers and ruins the seat if it is cheap.

Colonial Revival living rooms of the same years sometimes refused the overstuffed look and kept a wooden sofa with printed linen. Those rooms are a parallel taste, not a refutation. Department stores sold both on the same floor. The Hecht page that offers the bed davenport also offers the cane-back mahogany suite. A household could choose visible wood or choose pile. After about 1915, more households chose pile.

## What the covering hides

Lift a skirt and you are in the 1920s. The legs, if they exist, are stained hardwood stubs, often with casters so the heavy box can be walked out for cleaning. Casters on an overstuffed sofa are not parlor dance casters. They are janitorial. The front rail may be a simple plank. Corner blocks and dowels hold the frame; better shops still use mortise-and-tenon at the arm posts. Glue failure shows as a lean toward the end a sitter preferred.

The inside of a loose cushion is a small industry. Spring units in muslin, down jackets, cotton cores. A household that replaced only the covers in 1948 or 1972 has kept the 1920s interior and changed the face. That is why a “1920s sofa” in a shop can be wearing rayon that did not exist when the frame was built. Read the springs, not the floral.

Mohair lasts. That is why so many of these sofas survive in a crushed, shiny state rather than in tatters. The pile lays down in the sit. Railroad coaches used the same goods for the same reason. A living room that kept mohair through the Depression was keeping a fabric that did not need to be replaced when money stopped. The collapse of visible legs and the survival of goat hair are the same decision: fewer show surfaces, more wear surfaces.

## How to look at the sofa you have

Sit first. If you sink past the front rail and the back takes your shoulders without a carved crest rail hitting your neck, you are in the overstuffed decade or its echo. Look for a skirt or a deep upholstered apron. Look for T-cushions that step around the arms. Look for a metal tag — Karpen’s is the famous one — or a paper label under the deck. Look at the arms. If they are the same height as the back and boxed, think tuxedo. If they roll once and are almost as wide as a head, think club. If a wooden show-wood border still outlines the whole piece, you may have a transitional suite that has not yet given up the parlor.

Measure the height of the seat deck, not the cushion top. Deep springs sit in a box that is already a piece of case construction. If the piece opens to a bed, you are in davenport territory even if the family always said sofa. If the legs are tall and carved and the seat is tight, you have walked backward into the seven-piece set.

This page owns the stuffed horizon of 1915–1935. It does not own the Victorian parlor set, the later sectional, or the 1970s pit group. A mohair davenport facing a cathedral radio is a living room. A rosewood Belter sofa facing a center table is a parlor. The difference is not morality. It is how much wood you are allowed to see, and whether the household expected the best seat to be used every night.
