# The ranch-house living room

Maynard L. Parker’s September 1954 negatives of Cliff May’s Experimental Ranch — the Skylight House, Los Angeles — show a living room that has already given up the parlor’s closed box. Floor-to-ceiling glass, a hearth, movable cabinets instead of walls, a ceiling that wants you to look up at trees. Christian Choate shared the design. Thomas Church did the garden. The house is a custom experiment, later published in *Western Ranch Houses by Cliff May* (Lane, 1958). Most Americans never lived in it. They lived in a one-story tract with a picture window on the street, a chimney somewhere on the long wall, and a sofa that could not satisfy both.

That sofa problem is the furniture history of the ranch living room. The picture window wants the seating to face the glass. The hearth wants the seating to face the fire. The dining table, often in the same volume, wants a path that neither arrangement wants to block. California invented the glamour of the type. The rest of the country copied the plan and cheapened the glass. The living room is where you can see the copy.

## Cliff May, then the tracts

Cliff May (1908–1989) built his first ranch house in Talmadge Park, San Diego, in 1932, on land and capital from his future father-in-law, Roy C. Litchty, with a master carpenter on the job. Colonel Arthur J. O’Leary bought it for $9,500. A second house, backed by the grading contractor O. U. Miracle, sold in 1933 to Captain William Lindstrom for the same price. *Architectural Digest* published the Lindstrom house in 1934. By 1936 May was being called a father of the California ranch, a title that sticks because *Sunset* later made it national. He moved to Los Angeles in 1938. *Sunset* ran him during the war years and in 1946 issued *Sunset Western Ranch Houses*, a collaboration in which May dominated a large share of the designs. Kevin Starr later wrote that no other Sunset book had so shaped the Far West’s built environment. Four printings and 50,000 copies are the figures the California histories repeat.

May’s better houses turn their backs to the street. The entrance is understated. Living space opens to a patio, a court, or a wing of garden. Sliding glass and a wide eave do the work a Victorian porch once did. Furniture in those rooms is low, often a mix of his own Monterey-and-Craftsman pieces in the early San Diego houses and, later, whatever a decorator such as Peggy Galloway put in front of Parker’s camera. The hearth can sit on an interior wall while the glass takes the view. That is a solvable problem if you have fifty feet of living room and a designer. It is not solvable in 1,200 square feet with one picture window facing the builder’s curb.

Postwar tracts took the one-story footprint, the attached garage or carport, and the picture window as a street face. Laura Kviklys, reporting a University of Georgia sample of 467 published 1950s ranch plans for the National Park Service, gives the volume. Housing starts in 1955 ran about 1.65 million, then about 1.5 million a year for the rest of the decade. Nine of ten, in that account, were ranch houses. Average interior area in the sample grows from 1,114 square feet in 1950 to 1,272 in 1955 and 1,356 in 1959. Sixty-eight percent of the plans are open: living, dining, and often kitchen in one flow. Thirty-four percent enter directly into the living room; 35 percent use a vestibule. A 1956 housing study found women wanted a place to shed wet coats before the front room. Builders who skipped the vestibule saved money and soaked the rug.

Clark’s *American Family Home* remains the book that keeps the ranch from becoming a cartoon. The house is a middle-class machine for a family with a car, fewer servants, and a new idea of casualness. Gwendolyn Wright’s *Building the Dream* sits beside it. Neither is a furniture catalog. Both explain why the living room had to do more jobs in less space.

## Picture window, hearth, dining

The picture window is a single large pane, or a large pane flanked by sashes, placed on the street in most tracts because curb appeal sells houses. From the sidewalk you see a lamp, a plant, and the back of a sofa. From the sofa you see the street, the neighbor’s driveway, and, at night, your own reflection. Fabric fades. Conversation faces the glass and therefore the public. Households that turn the sofa toward the room put its back to the window and give the street a upholstered horizon. That is the picture-window sofa problem. There is no elegant factory solution. There are only choices, each of which wastes something May’s patio-facing rooms did not have to waste.

The hearth is still the older organizer. A ranch chimney can sit on the long wall, on an end wall, or as a partition between living and dining. Furniture wants to face it. If the picture window is on the opposite wall, the sofa can do both jobs: fire on one side, glass behind. If the window and the chimney share a wall, the seating group breaks. Club chairs angle. A sectional — the previous essay — turns an L so one leg sees fire and one sees glass. A coffee table sits on the rug as a raft between the two loyalties.

Dining is in the same air. Kviklys found formal dining rooms declining across the decade. The living-dining L is cheaper and matches the informal script. Iron trellises, half-walls, and changes of flooring mark a border without a door. The dining table is not a dining-room suite in the old sense; the dining-room series next door can take the sideboard. Here the table is a living-room problem: it must not block the path from the front door to the hall, and it must not sit so close to the sofa that a pulled chair hits a shin. Breakfast happens in the kitchen nook (59 percent of Kviklys’s plans have some snack or alcove space). Dinner guests happen in the L. The living room’s furniture has to look acceptable from the table.

Floors are often hardwood with a single rug under the seating, or asphalt tile at the edges. Ceilings are lower than a Victorian parlor’s. The 8-foot plate is common. Overstuffed high-back davenports look wrong under it. The low, buttoned sofa of the 1950s, the open-end Kroehler, the first sectionals — those are ranch living-room seats whether or not the store used the word ranch.

## What stood in the room

A typical tract living room of 1955 holds a sofa, two chairs, a pair of lamp tables, a coffee table, and a case for a radio or, increasingly, a television. The television essay will take the screen. Until it arrives, the hearth and the window compete without a third monarch. After it arrives, the sofa often turns away from both and faces a box on a low bench. That turn is the great furniture event of the late ranch. It is also why a picture window ends up behind the sitters, a display for the street and a glare on the tube.

Woods are pale: limed oak, maple, walnut in better custom houses. Blonde suites from furniture factories ride the same boxcars that brought mohair a generation earlier. Wrought-iron and glass tables echo patio furniture indoors. A planter, sometimes built into a half-wall, is architecture pretending to be a console. Sliding glass doors want draperies that can clear the track. Someone has to pull those draperies every evening if the family does not want to eat in a fishbowl. That someone is usually the woman the open plan was supposed to free. The open plan lets her see children in the yard from the kitchen. It also lets every guest see the kitchen.

Custom California rooms — May, and later Eichler atriums in a different modernist key — can take a pair of lounge chairs, a low table, and a view. Tract rooms take what the credit office allowed. Both are ranch living rooms if the plan is one story, zoned, and open to the weather through a big pane.

Labor in the house is family labor. The live-in servant of the parlor decades is gone from these square footages. A cleaning woman may come. The daily move of school books off the coffee table, the rotation of the sofa cushions, the fight over who faces the fire, are unpaid. Builders’ carpenters put up the picture window in a day. The glass is the expensive piece. The furniture is whatever arrived on a Saturday.

## How to look at a ranch living room

Stand in the front door. If you are already in the sitting space, you are in the 34 percent. Look for the wet-weather scar on the rug. Then find the chimney and the largest glass. Draw a line from each to the sofa. If those lines agree, the builder or the household solved the problem. If they disagree, look at the compromise: a sectional, a pair of chairs that do not match the sofa’s facing, a television that has stolen both views.

Look at the dining L. If a trellis or a half-wall still stands, it is a period tell. If the sofa’s back is the only divider, the room has been “opened” again in a later renovation. Look at the window covering. Drapery hardware on a picture-window wall is part of the furniture plan. A bare pane with a sofa against it is either courage or a listing photograph.

Do not read this room as a failed Craftsman bungalow. The bungalow essay owns the inglenook and the 1910 living room. The ranch keeps the hearth and adds a sheet of glass the bungalow could not afford. Do not read it as a conversation pit. A sunken floor is a later, rarer luxury. Most ranch living rooms are on one level, with a rug that tries to do what architecture did not.

Cliff May’s experimental house is the dream of the type: furniture on wheels, glass, a hearth, no street display. The American tract living room is the type as built: a picture window, a sofa that cannot face everything, and a dining table in the same air. The furniture history is the history of that cannot.
