# Cocktail tables in Prohibition rooms

A Paul T. Frankl occasional table from about 1927, sold through Frankl Galleries in New York, stands 26 inches high on a 16 1/4-inch square. Another, about 1928, in paint and silver leaf with reverse-painted glass, stands 22 1/4 inches. Those are not the 14-to-18-inch slabs that later Americans call coffee tables. They are drinks-height, or near it: a standing highball, a seated cocktail, a lamp and an ashtray in a small apartment. The trade would learn to say cocktail table for objects in this family — smaller in plan than the sofa-front coffee table, often taller, often lacquered or mirrored, at home in a hotel suite and in a living room that wanted to look like one.

The Eighteenth Amendment took effect in 1920. Repeal arrived in December 1933. Furniture catalogs did not wait for Congress to invent a low table, and they did not stop at Repeal. What changed is the name on the price ticket and the permission to put glasses on it in public copy. This page owns that naming and that smaller, sharper object. The coffee-table essay owns the broader American sofa-front form. Do not staple them into one inventor story.

## A table for a glass, not a tea service

Prohibition did not invent mixed drinks, and it did not invent the occasional table. It did push drinking into private rooms. Speakeasies had their own furniture. Apartments and houses had to serve friends without a legal bar. A small table that could hold a shaker, two glasses, and a dish of something salty is a different brief from a parlor center table that held an album and a lamp at standing height. It is also a different brief from the later, longer coffee table that holds magazines, remotes, and a child’s homework.

Height is the first way to keep the types apart. Fayen’s 1934 living-room coffee table is 18 inches. Frankl’s 1945 cork coffee table is 14 1/2. The 1920s cocktail or occasional tables in his skyscraper line sit in the low twenties and mid-twenties. Hotel lounge drinks tables can be taller still; a British “Halls patent” mahogany hotel cocktail table that dealers date around 1900 is 34 inches, which is standing-bar height and not a living-room coffee table at all. American living rooms after 1920 wanted something a seated guest could reach without standing and without stretching into a deep sofa. That compromise often landed higher and smaller than the postwar coffee table.

Plan is the second way. A cocktail table is frequently square or round and not much wider than a tray. A coffee table is frequently oblong and as long as the sofa’s middle third. When a 1930s catalog uses the two names for the same drawing, the industry is already confusing them. When a 1920s hotel photograph shows a small lacquered stand between two club chairs, you are looking at the cocktail type even if the caption says occasional.

## Lacquer, glass, and hotel moderne

Art Deco — a historian’s name, not the 1920s shop name — liked surfaces that took a wet glass and showed it. Lacquer, black or Chinese red, is the finish dealers still reach for. Blue mirrored glass on a low top is a distinctively American cheap luxury; Buffalo’s architectural historians note it as a coffee-or-cocktail treatment in moderne rooms. Chrome banding, eggshell inlay in the French manner, and silver leaf on stepped bases all appear. Jean Dunand’s 1923 Paris occasional table, 15 inches high and eggshell-inlaid, is not an American factory object. It is the luxury temperature the American copies wanted. Henri Kahn’s Paris dry-bar cocktail table of about 1935, 19 3/4 inches high, opens to bottles. That is a bar pretending to be a table. American department stores sold milder versions: a drawer for cards, a shelf for bottles that were not supposed to exist.

Paul Frankl, born in Austria, working in New York by the 1920s, is the American designer who made the city’s new architecture into furniture. *Good Furniture* in 1927 called his skyscraper pieces “as American and as New Yorkish as Fifth Avenue itself.” The Art Institute of Chicago’s Skyscraper Cabinet, about 1927, is a bookcase, not a cocktail table. The Brooklyn Museum step table is 38 3/4 inches of painted wood. The small square occasional tables are the ones that could stand beside a sofa in a one-bedroom and hold a drink. California redwood with black lacquer, as on a desk-bookcase later shown at Cooper Hewitt, shows how far the finish traveled. Frankl later moved to Los Angeles and softened. The 1920s work is stepped, sharp, and urban.

Hotel moderne is a room type as much as a finish. Joseph Urban’s hotel interiors and, a little later, Donald Deskey’s Radio City rooms taught a public what a modern lounge looked like: low light, reflective surfaces, small tables that do not interrupt the carpet. Guests went home and asked a store for something like the table between the chairs. Grand Rapids and New York shops answered with lacquered maple, gum, and plywood. The living room of a small apartment cannot take a seven-piece parlor set. It can take two chairs, a sofa, and a cocktail table that does not hog the floor.

Donald Deskey’s own tables and the broader American moderne of the early 1930s — tubular metal, bakelite, cork — belong here when they are living-room drinks tables. They belong in the coffee-table essay when they drop below about 18 inches and stretch out in front of a sofa. The same designer can make both. The room decides the name.

## Prohibition, Repeal, and catalog language

From 1920 to 1933, a household that served alcohol in the living room was breaking federal law. Furniture copy in those years often says coffee, tea, or “occasional.” That is not evidence that no one drank. It is evidence that a manufacturer selling through a department store had a lawyer. After Repeal, “cocktail table” becomes easier to print. Dealer histories like to say the coffee table was simply renamed. Fayen and the Chipstone exhibition do not need that joke. They already have a 1920s–30s low table whose name follows fashionable drinks. Coffee stays in the copy because coffee stayed in the cup. Cocktail joins it because the glass is legal again and because the word sounds like a hotel.

Do not claim that bathtub gin invented the form. Poor liquor made people mix fruit into spirits. Mixing can happen on a pantry board. What the living room needed was a place to set the result down without walking back to the kitchen. The small table is that place. Repeal made the place advertiseable. Sales of low tables in the Depression, when they rose, rose because entertaining at home was cheaper than going out, not because a constitutional amendment redesigned the apron.

Department-store rooms of the mid-1930s already show the later confusion: a long low table called cocktail in one ad and coffee in the next. By 1945 the long low table has mostly won the living room. The word cocktail survives on smaller, dressier tables and on hotel purchase orders. A ranch-house family will say coffee table even if the object is a leftover lacquered square from an aunt’s apartment.

## Who stands, who pours

A cocktail table is a piece of service furniture with the servant designed out of the drawing. The host pours. In a household that still had a maid in 1928, she might bring the tray and leave. In most of the apartments that bought these tables, there was no maid. The labor is the host’s, often the wife’s in the magazine, often anyone’s in fact. Ice is a kitchen problem. The living room only receives the wet glass. Rings on lacquer are the period’s scar. Rings on mirrored glass are worse. A glass top that lifts off for cleaning is a shop trying to save the finish.

Factory labor is finishing-room labor: spraying lacquer, rubbing, polishing edges so a sleeve does not catch. Veneer on a 16-inch top is a luxury of matching, not of timber volume. Silver leaf is a specialist’s work. Cheap sets substitute metallic paint. You can see the difference at a raking light.

## How to look at a small table from these years

Measure height and plan before you accept the dealer’s word. If the top is in the low twenties and the footprint is near a tray, you are closer to a cocktail or occasional drinks table. If the top is under 18 inches and as long as a sofa cushion, you are in coffee-table territory, even if a 1934 tag says cocktail. Look at the finish. Lacquer and mirror want a party. Worn cherry with a piecrust wants a Colonial Revival living room that never admitted a shaker. Look for bottle stains on a lower shelf, a tiny drawer that still smells of tobacco, and casters that were meant to roll the table to a group of chairs rather than park it for a decade under a stack of books.

Do not call Frankl’s tall skyscraper cabinet a cocktail table. Do not call a 34-inch hotel drinks stand a living-room coffee table. Do not say Repeal invented the object. Say Repeal let the catalog tell the truth about the glass.

A Prohibition living room with a small lacquered table between two chairs is a room that has already left the parlor center table behind and has not yet settled on the long, low, magazine-bearing coffee table. That in-between object is this slug’s property. The drink is in the name. The furniture history is in the height.
