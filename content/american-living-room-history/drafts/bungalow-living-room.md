# Bungalow living room furniture

Henry L. Wilson, a Los Angeles architect who advertised himself as “The Bungalow Man,” put the new room in the front of *The Bungalow Book* (fifth edition, Chicago, 1910):

> The Bungalow is a radical departure from the older styles of cottage, not only in outward appearance, but in inside arrangement. The straight, cold entrance hall and the stiff, prim, usually darkened parlor have no place in it. Entrance is usually into a large living room—the room where the family gathers, and in which the visitor feels at once the warm, homelike hospitality. Everything in this room should suggest comfort and restfulness. The open fireplace and low, broad mantel, a cozy nook or corner, or a broad window seat, are all means to the desired end. Bookcases or shelves may be fitted into convenient places, and ceiling beams add an air of homely quaintness which never grows tiresome.

That is not a furniture catalog. It is a plan that makes certain furniture inevitable: a fire, a seat that belongs to the wall, shelves that do not stand free like a whatnot, wood left dark. Wilson’s book is 112 houses, exteriors, floor plans, interiors, cozy corners. California invents the type for a national market. The Midwest builds it in oak and in kit pine. This page is the living room those houses required. The parlor-to-living-room name change has its own essay. Here the objects are built-in and stained.

## Built-ins are furniture

A window seat with a lid is a settle that cannot be sold separately. Bookcases flanking a fireplace, the same oak or fir as the mantel, are an étagère that has joined the wall. A cozy corner — a bench, a post, a shelf — is an inglenook without the English word. Wilson’s plates show these again and again because they photograph well and because they reduce the number of purchased chairs a young household must buy. The carpenter and the millwork catalog do the work the Grand Rapids parlor set used to do.

Stained wood, not painted, is the bungalow tell. California redwood and Douglas fir take stain and show grain. Midwest oak, sometimes quartersawn if the owner has been reading Stickley, sometimes flatsawn and dark, does the same job in a colder climate. Ceiling beams, even false ones, pull the eye down. The furniture that then stands free — a Morris chair, a oak rocker, a table that is lower than Downing’s center table — has to live with that darkness. A gilt étagère looks wrong. A reed organ can still stand on the wall; an upright piano fits an alcove. The square piano’s middle-of-the-floor claim is finished.

Sears, Roebuck sold Modern Homes by mail. Aladdin, in Bay City, Michigan, sold kit houses that arrived in a boxcar: numbered lumber, a book of instructions, millwork for the mantel and the bookcase if the plan included them. The living room in a Sears or Aladdin bungalow is Wilson’s diagram at a price. Built-in benches and colonnades between living and dining rooms are millwork, not Belter. The dining side of that colonnade is a dining-room problem. The living side is a bench, a plant stand, a place to drop a hat. Do not steal the dining series’ buffet. Do look at the living-room face of the same millwork: often a bookcase or a seat.

Kit-house furniture was not in the crate. Households bought Mission oak from the same catalogs that sold the house, or they dragged a parlor suite from the old address. Photographs of 1915–1925 bungalow interiors are mixed: a Craftsman armchair and a brass bowl, a leftover marble-top table cut down, a porch rocker that has come inside. The plan is consistent. The movable objects are a compromise.

## Fireplace first

Wilson’s “open fireplace and low, broad mantel” is the organizer. Place the fire. Place the seats toward it. Place the table where a lamp can serve the seats without claiming the whole floor. The later essay on the fireplace as organizer will take the long history. The bungalow’s contribution is to make the hearth the first thing a visitor sees, because the visitor walks into the living room, not into a hall that protects a darkened parlor.

Brick, clinker brick in some California houses, cobblestone in others, glazed tile in a more expensive plan. The mantel is wood, low, a shelf for a clock and two candlesticks, not a marble pile of Rococo fruit. Built-in benches may flank it. If they do not, a settle or a pair of chairs does the same work and can be replaced when the upholstery dies.

Heat is not only symbolic. A bungalow living room with a real fire and a real chimney is still a hall-and-parlor idea: the best room is the warm room. Furnaces complicate that. Many kit houses have a furnace and a fireplace that is lit on Sunday. The furniture still faces the brick. Facing the brick is the living-room habit that television will later steal.

## California and the Midwest

The California bungalow — low, wide eaves, a porch, a living room that opens to a dining room through a wide opening or a colonnade — is the type Wilson sold from Los Angeles. Earthquake stories attach to the low profile in later copy; treat them as later copy unless you have Wilson saying so. `[CITE NEEDED: Wilson’s own words, if any, on earthquakes; Dover’s jacket language is not Wilson.]` What Wilson does say is hospitality and the death of the darkened parlor.

Midwest bungalows — Chicago bungalows with their brick and their leading, Minneapolis 1-1/2 stories, the kit house on a Midwestern lot — keep the living-room idea and change the weather. Storm windows. Darker oak. A radiator under the window seat. The built-in bookcase still fits because the plan was published nationally. Aladdin and Sears made California a Midwest product without moving the owner to Pasadena.

Greene and Greene’s Gamble House in Pasadena (1908) is the custom-end of the same appetite: teak and mahogany, built-in seating, a living room as a complete interior. Do not inventory that house from memory. The Gamble House has its own literature. Mention it as the expensive cousin, then go back to Wilson’s plans and the kit millwork. Most American bungalow living rooms never saw a Greene and Greene drawing. They saw a catalog and a carpenter. The expensive cousin is useful as a ceiling, not as a shopping list.

Labor: carpenters and millworkers, not enslaved labor, not a Belter carver. Women still chose fabrics — portieres in the colonnade, a table scarf, the window-seat cushion. Children were allowed in. That is the point of Wilson’s “family gathers.” Tyron in 1915 will say we could not get our parlors back if we tried. The bungalow is why.

## What stands free

A Morris chair. A rocker. A table about twenty-four to twenty-eight inches high — on the way to a coffee table, not yet the low cocktail slab. A tabouret. A magazine stand. Lamps with mica or glass, because the fireplace is not the only light. Wicker on the porch comes inside in some photographs; it is cheap and it photographs as casual. Overstuffed chairs arrive in the 1920s and begin to replace Mission oak in the same floor plan. The overstuffed-sofa essay will take that stuffing. The bungalow plan can hold either.

Do not put a seven-piece rosewood set in this room unless the photograph shows it. Some households did. The furniture then looks like a guest that missed the train. Casters on those chairs still work. The built-in bench does not need casters. The room has chosen sides.

An upright piano against the wall is common. A parlor organ lasts in smaller towns. The stool is still a screw stool. Music is no longer the excuse for the room, only one of its jobs. Radio cabinets will claim a corner in the next decade.

## How to look at a bungalow living room

Walk in the front door. If you are in the living room without a formal hall, Wilson has already won. Face the fireplace. If bookcases or a window seat are the same wood as the mantel, you are looking at furniture that was ordered with the house. If every seat is a movable upholstered piece and the mantel is bare brick, later owners stripped the plan or the kit never included the millwork.

Look at the stain. Painted woodwork in a 1910 bungalow is often a later “Colonial” or a later white-out. Look at the colonnade. Living-room benches on this side, dining-room plate on that side: keep the jobs straight. Look at the chair arms. A Morris chair and a leftover ladies chair can sit in the same photograph. The house is in transition. The plan is not.

This page owns bungalow living-room furniture: Wilson’s 1910 language, built-ins, stain, kit houses, California and the Midwest. Stickley owns the Eastwood oak that many of these rooms bought. Prairie owns the custom hearth with the architect’s furniture. Colonial Revival owns the painted-floor reaction. If you find a low mantel, a window seat with a broken hinge, and a Morris chair whose leather has gone to dust, you are in the room Tyron said we could not get the parlor back from. She was right about the feeling. The built-in is why the feeling stayed.
