# The best bed in the parlor

The expensive thing is not the posts. It is the cloth. A four-post bedstead of oak or maple, pinned at the rails, can be a competent joiner’s week. The hangings — tester cloth, inner and outer valances, head cloth, side and foot curtains, counterpane or coverlet, the featherbed, bolster, and blankets — are import, labor, and display. Abbott Lowell Cummings’s treatise on bed hangings, 1650–1850, and Nina Fletcher Little’s essay in that volume, are the New England place to start: the complete set closes the bed into a room inside the room. Heat stays in. Eyes stay out. Appraisers knew it. In house after house the best bed, not the cupboard and not the table, is the highest line in the inventory.

That bed often stands in the parlor. The word will mislead anyone who hears Godey’s “face of the house.” In a hall-and-parlor house of 1650–1750 the parlor is the inner room, the parlor-chamber, sometimes called the best chamber. It is not a Victorian showplace. It is the second heated room, and it holds the household’s best sleeping furniture. The hall-and-parlor essay in this series owns the plan. This page owns the bed that made the parlor worth having.

## Parlor-chamber, not showroom

Clark’s *American Family Home* opens later, but he is right to start with the mixed Chesapeake room. Inventories there put hoes in a parlor. They also put the best bed there. Ronald L. Hurst and Jonathan Prown, *Southern Furniture 1680–1830*, keep Chesapeake seating and case pieces in working rooms instead of in a later decorator’s “colonial parlor.” A Virginia or Maryland planter’s parlor-chamber can hold the hung bed, chairs or benches, a table, weapons, and the traffic of bound labor. Enslaved workers move through a room the inventory treats as the planter’s. A history that names only the carved tester is a history of the lock.

New England gives the same diagram with different weather. Cummings, *The Framed Houses of Massachusetts Bay, 1625–1725*, is the house book: two rooms on the ground floor, chimney between them or in a pile, chambers above if the house has a second story. The hall takes the larger fire and the everyday work. The parlor takes the better bed. Massachusetts Bay and Connecticut Valley inventories list joined chests, cupboards, forms, joint stools, a great chair, and the bed with its furniture — “furniture” here meaning the textiles, not a suite of chairs.

Chester County, Pennsylvania, sits between the two regions this essay is written for, and it is useful because a thesis has counted the rooms. Of room-by-room inventories, 1683–1751, the room named parlor most frequently contained the house’s best bed — best defined as most expensive. Some inventories call that room the best chamber. William Brinton’s house near Dilworthtown (the brick house of about 1704) is the standing example the thesis uses: at Brinton’s death in 1751 the best bed was a “feather bed and all ye furniture belonging.” The surviving tester bed now shown in that parlor is not documented to the family. Treat it as the type, not the object. The type is an uncarved four-post stead, tester frame, hangings that make a box.

## Hangings as wealth

Samuel Sewall, ordering from London for a daughter’s bedchamber in 1719/20, wanted a fully hung bedstead: tester and valances, head cloth and curtains that closed the whole bed, and possibly a base. He specified a watered worsted that would read as moiré, matching trim, and more of the same to coordinate cane chairs. He asked for a “Chintz Quilt well-made” — painted cotton from the Coromandel Coast, fashionable in London, a global textile in a Boston room. The Colonial Society’s essay on Boston case furniture, 1690–1725, uses that order as evidence. The bedstead is the most expensive piece of furniture in the house because the textiles are the furniture.

Chesapeake evidence runs to silk in a way rural New England often does not. Cummings, compiling rural New England inventories, found no silk bed rugs. Gloria Seaman Allen’s work on silk bedcoverings in the early Chesapeake, using the Gunston Hall probate database among other sources, finds silk rugs, quilts, and counterpanes concentrated among the well-to-do. John Carlyle of Alexandria, dying in 1780, left a silk quilt, a silk rug, a pair of silk blankets, and four pair of woolen blankets. `[CITE NEEDED: the full Carlyle inventory line as published, to keep the count from memory.]` Eleven percent of decedents in one Gunston Hall slice, 1751–1770, owned silk quilts or counterpanes. Those numbers are not a price list. They are a reminder that the wealth is in the cloth, and that the Chesapeake’s cloth is sometimes silk when New England’s is worsted, linsey-woolsey, or a woolen rug.

A complete set of hangings is a list, and the list is the object: head and foot curtains, counterpane or coverlet, inner and outer valances, head cloth, tester. Add the featherbed, the under mattress or straw tick, bolsters, pillows, blankets. The joiner’s bill for the frame is the smaller invoice. The Longfellow House’s later parlor-chamber bed — Radford Brothers of Portland, January 1808, painted oak-leaf cornice, Pompeian red-and-yellow curtains — is outside this essay’s closing date as a fashionable Federal object, but the house’s own interpretation is the right sentence: combined with featherbeds and mattresses, the textiles far exceeded the cabinetmaker’s bill for the frame. That sentence is true in 1680 and still true in 1808.

Woods for the stead are local first. Oak in the seventeenth century. Maple, pine, walnut as the shops and the ports allow. Mortise-and-tenon rails, holes or pins for the cord or sacking that holds the tick. A tester frame of light wood, sometimes barely more than sticks, because the cloth is the architecture. Carving on posts is display when the hangings are open. When the curtains are drawn you are looking at wool.

## Trundle and the shared heat

A trundle is a low bed that lives under the high bed and pulls out. It is a child’s claim, a servant’s claim, or a second sleeper’s claim on the same chimney. The hall-and-parlor essay already flagged the citation problem: later catalog language loves the word. Period inventories sometimes say truckle, trundle, or “a little bed.” `[CITE NEEDED: a dated Chesapeake or New England inventory that lists a trundle or truckle in the parlor-chamber by name.]` Until that line is on the table, treat the trundle as a known form whose parlor placement is likely and not yet pinned in a sentence you can quote.

The point of the form is heat. You do not waste a fire on a child in an unheated loft if the parlor-chamber already has the chimney and the hangings. You also do not give the child the featherbed. The trundle gets the thinner tick. In a Chesapeake house the person on the trundle may be enslaved or indentured; the inventory will not always say so. In a New England house it may be a child or a hired girl. The high bed remains the best bed. Rank sleeps on feathers. The rest sleep lower and closer to the draft.

Castors are rare. You do not roll this furniture. You cord the bed, you air the feathers, you take the hangings down for vermin and for wash day. Joint stools move by hand. A settle at the fire — the settle essay owns that form — is the other timber that argues with the cold. The bed argues by closing.

## When the bed leaves the front room

The bed leaves when the house can spare a chamber that is only for sleeping and a parlor that is only for sitting. That is not a year. It is a wealth line and a plan line.

A second story with heated chambers lets the best bed go upstairs. The ground-floor parlor can then keep chairs, a table, and, by the late eighteenth century, a sofa if the household has reached that form. Genteel houses in Boston, Newport, Philadelphia, and the Chesapeake capitals make that split in the eighteenth century. The parlor becomes a receiving room. The best bed becomes chamber furniture. Easy chairs — medical, winged, expensive — follow the bed upstairs. The wing-chair essay takes that object. Inventories that still list a bed in a “parlor” after 1750 may be using the old room-name for a room that has not yet been socially rebranded, or they may be describing a house that has not yet specialized.

By 1800 a fashionable Federal parlor in Salem or New York is ashamed of a bed. McIntire’s carved sofas and the Grecian couch want visible legs and daylight. Scenic paper and marble baseboards — the Williams House parlor at the Met is the later architectural example — assume no tester. The hangings have moved upstairs with the privacy claim. A reader who finds a bed in the front room of an old house and feels embarrassed for the owners is reading with that Federal, and then Victorian, eye. The seventeenth-century household was keeping the heat and showing the cloth.

Colonial Revival will put a four-post bed back into a “parlor” and call it Pilgrim. That is a twentieth-century room. Dealer romance around Brewster chairs and “first period” bedrooms is a later story. If the bed has a history, it has a place, a wood, and an inventory line. If it has a romance, put the romance in the caption.

## How to look at a parlor that still has a bed

Stand at the foot and look up. If there is no tester and no evidence of one, you may be looking at a later low-post bed shoved into an old room. If there is a tester, look at the cloth before the carving. Reproduction hangings are honest when the house says so — Brinton, Longfellow — and dishonest when a decorator’s toile is asked to do the work of an appraisal.

Look at the chimney. The bed wants the inner wall or a corner where curtains can close without blocking the fire or the door to the hall. A bed under a window is a later, warmer house. Look for the gap that would take a trundle. Look at the floor for the polish of a pulled frame.

In a Chesapeake house, ask who was not listed next to the featherbed. The parlor-chamber is a planter’s room in the document and a labor room in the week. In a New England house, ask whether the parlor is still the inner room or has already become the face. The living room, much later, is permission to sit on the best seating. The colonial parlor-chamber is permission to sleep in the best cloth, in the room that can be shut, next to the fire that cost the wood. If you take the bed out to make the room look like a living room, you have made a different house.
