# Sitting room versus parlor

The arm of a sitting-room easy chair tells the truth before the inventory does. The mahogany or maple shows through the horsehair at the front roll. The loose cushion has a dent that does not spring back. Next to it, if the household can pay for the form, stands a work table — Sheraton or Empire, two shallow drawers, a silk bag hanging from a slide — with the lid a little proud from years of being lifted for thread. That is a room people occupied on a Tuesday. Across the hall, or behind a pair of folding doors, the parlor keeps its finish. The sofa’s front rail is still sharp. The rosewood or walnut has been waxed more often than sat on. Godey’s Lady’s Book called that room “the face of the house — the most noticeable part — and that from which visitors take their impressions of the whole.” The sitting room was the rest of the face: the part that chewed, sewed, read aloud, and wore out.

This page owns the difference. The living-room essay owns the later collapse of the pair into one name. Do not read a 1910 bungalow as if it invented family sitting, or a hall-and-parlor house of 1700 as if it already had a Victorian parlor. Between about 1800 and 1915, in New England and Mid-Atlantic houses large enough to specialize, the furniture mix is the evidence.

## Two rooms, two jobs

Clifford Edward Clark’s *The American Family Home, 1800–1960* keeps the parlor from becoming costume and the sitting room from becoming a slogan. A house that could afford a second heated sitting space kept the front room for callers and the back or side room for the family. The front room performed gentility. The sitting room absorbed work.

The American Heritage essay “The Parlor” (October 1963) is later magazine writing, not a primary inventory, but it names the occasions the front room was built to hold: the morning call (any time between eleven and five), the tea, the ten minutes before dinner, the proposal, and — a separate essay takes the hour — the hushed conversation when someone in the house had died. Those events need furniture that looks unused. Oscar Fitzgerald’s *Three Centuries of American Furniture* is blunt about the mid-century kit: armchair, lady’s chair, sofa, four parlor chairs. That kit is the parlor’s job.

In a New England farmhouse of 1840 the sitting room may be the old hall under a new name: everyday fire, drop-leaf table, rocker, lamp stand. In a Philadelphia or New York row house the plan is more rigid. Front parlor on the street. Back parlor or sitting room behind it, sometimes with a dining use this series will not steal. Folding doors can make one long room for a reception and two rooms again on Wednesday. The show half has casters so the chairs can be pulled into a circle and put back against the wall. The family half has wear.

## What sits in the sitting room

Start with the work table. In the Federal and early Empire years a lady’s work table is a cabinetmaker’s form, not a later craft-table romance. Lightweight, finished on all four sides, often mahogany with maple or satinwood veneer, reeded or turned legs, two drawers, sometimes a writing slide, and a pouch of gathered silk to take fancywork and mending. The Huntington’s Federal example of about 1810 is the type: coin-cornered top, stringing on the drawer fronts, a bag slide, Eastern white pine as the secondary wood. `[CITE NEEDED: a named New England or Mid-Atlantic cabinetmaker’s bill that places a work table specifically in a sitting room rather than a parlor or chamber.]` You pull it to the fire. You push it aside when a child needs the floor.

Margaret Quincy, daughter of the president of Harvard, wrote the scene down on 20 August 1824. She and her mother “descended to the parlour, opened windows, dropped blinds, placed the little table in the middle of the room with work, books, etc., etc., and seating ourselves on the sopha, Mama held some work and I read aloud *Red Gauntlet*.” The word she uses is parlor. The furniture mix is sitting-room furniture: a little table, work, books, a sofa you sit on while someone sews. In houses with only one front room the names collapse. In houses with two, the little table and the worn sofa migrate to the sitting room and the rosewood stays forward.

The easy chair is the other sitting-room tell, and it is a dangerous one. The wing-chair essay in this series owns the eighteenth-century medical form: fully upholstered, winged against drafts, often a chamber piece for age, illness, or lying-in. By 1800–1850 a household that still owns such a chair may keep it upstairs. A household that has bought a later stuffed chair — barrel-back, square, or a rocker with upholstered pads — parks it where someone actually sits. The front of the arm wears. The loose cushion compresses. If you find an easy chair in a parlor inventory with no wear story, ask whether the appraiser used the word for any stuffed seat.

Children’s stools, a sewing bird clamped to a table edge, a stand for a kerosene lamp after mid-century, a bookcase that is not an étagère: those are sitting-room objects. A sewing machine after the 1850s and 1860s is a sitting-room claim so loud that it can out-appraise the parlor. Southern Spaces reprints the late-nineteenth-century inventory of Harriet Evins of Spartanburg County, South Carolina — outside this essay’s regions, useful as a control. Her parlor held a square rosewood piano, marble-topped mahogany, two large upholstered rosewood sofas, matching chairs. Her sitting room held a walnut writing table, bookcases, about a hundred books, a mahogany sofa, three wooden rocking chairs, and a sewing machine. The sitting-room furniture, machine included, appraised higher than the parlor. That is not a Southern exception. It is what happens when a machine that works sits next to chairs that are used.

New England inventories after 1850 tell the same split in colder language. `[CITE NEEDED: a published Massachusetts or Connecticut inventory, 1850–1890, that rooms a sewing machine or work table in a sitting room and a matched set in a parlor.]` Mid-Atlantic city directories and household advice assume the lady of the house receives in one room and sews in another when the plan allows it. When the plan does not allow it, she sews in the parlor and then has to restore the face of the house before a call. That labor is the point.

## What the parlor refuses

The parlor refuses abrasion, and it refuses certain woods if the household is performing. Rosewood and mahogany for the show set. Walnut as the respectable substitute after mid-century, once Grand Rapids and Cincinnati can ship a matched suite. The sitting room can take maple, painted pine, a Windsor left over from the last generation, a chair whose slip seat has been recovered twice. The parlor’s chairs have casters. The sitting room’s rocker stays by the fire.

Andrew Jackson Downing, *The Architecture of Country Houses* (1850), called the center table “the emblem of the family circle.” Downing was writing for villa clients. In a house with two rooms the emblematic table often sits in the parlor and the actual circle — lamp, work, newspaper — sits in the sitting room on a cheaper stand. In a house with one room the same table has to do both jobs and the finish suffers.

Sereno E. Todd, writing for farmers, asked why a country household should keep “a costly parlor or two” and “go look into the—almost sacred—apartment about once a week.” The American Heritage parlor essay quotes him and quotes the Stocktons of the 1870s: was there ever an American woman who, furnishing a house, did not first lay aside the money for the parlor? The sitting room is what is left after that money is spent. It is also, in honest houses, the room that works.

## Wear is a document

Look at the front rail of the best sofa. A parlor that was only a face does not wear there. A sitting room does. The bag slide of the work table polishes its own runners. The rocker cuts the pine floor. Parlor casters can roll to the wall and back for forty years without anyone sitting long enough to crush the seat.

Horsehair on a parlor sofa is meant to stay slick and dark. A sitting-room chair gets a slipcover, then a cheap plush that pills. Springs, when they arrive in mid-century factory seats, break first in the room that is used. A work table’s drawer sides are often pine, dovetailed, running on the case. A parlor side chair may have a carved crest doweled to a rosewood back, the joint hidden by upholstery and by the fact that no child climbed it. Windsor chairs — a sibling essay owns the form — take the abuse a parlor suite is not allowed to take. They stack, go to the yard, and come back with paint worn to the pine seat.

## Who carries, who sews, who is not in the portrait

A parlor that exists to display gentility needs a person who is not displayed. In New England and Mid-Atlantic houses of 1800–1840 that person may be a hired girl, a free Black servant, or, in households that still held people as property into the early nineteenth century in parts of the Mid-Atlantic, an enslaved worker. Someone carries the best chairs from the wall to the fire for a call and carries them back. Someone dusts the rosewood when no one is expected. The sitting room’s labor is the family’s, or it is the same servant on a different shift: mending, lamp-filling, watching a child.

The lady of the house who sews in the sitting room is not a symbol. She is doing the work that ready-made clothing has not yet taken away. After the sewing machine, she is still finishing, altering, and teaching. The work table is her bench. The easy chair is her father’s or her mother-in-law’s. Rank in the sitting room is not gentleman’s chair versus lady’s chair. It is who gets the stuffed seat and who gets the Windsor.

Lillian Hart Tyron, *Speaking of Home* (1915), writes after the two rooms have begun to fail as a pair:

> We are fast becoming a parlorless nation… The parlor now is relegated to the cold and viewless side [of the house], or is crowded into a corner of the hall, with two chairs and a palm. We could not get our parlors back if we tried, because we ourselves have change. The living room answers to a new social feeling.

Tyron’s spelling of “change” is hers. The social feeling is fewer friends, better ones, and no patience for a room that punished the people who paid for it. Henry L. Wilson’s *Bungalow Book* (1910) gives the replacement a temperature: warmth, comfort, a room you are glad to turn to. Those sentences belong to the living-room essay. They matter here because they describe the sitting room winning. The parlor does not become honest overnight. It is absorbed. The worn chair stays. The rosewood is cut down, recovered, or sent to the attic.

## How to look at the two rooms you still have

If you are in a New England or Mid-Atlantic house that still has a front room and a back room, stand in the doorway and ask which furniture you would apologize for. The parlor is the room you apologize from. The sitting room is the room you apologize for: the mending, the newspaper, the chair that sags. Finish that has never been sat on is a parlor finish. A bag slide that still runs is a sitting-room joint.

Do not confuse this pair with the hall-and-parlor plan — working hall, parlor-chamber, often a best bed. This later pair is a claim to civilization made with factory furniture. Viewing happened in the parlor because the parlor was the public room; that hour has its own essay. Do not staple *Ladies’ Home Journal* in 1910 to the influenza of 1918. The name “living room” and the undertaker’s parlor are different histories.

Lift the work-table drawer. If the dividers are worn where scissors and a paper of pins rode for a decade, you are in the sitting room, whatever the listing called it. If the drawer is lined with unused paper and the bag is a decorator’s invention, you are looking at a face. The furniture history in this series is which face a household was willing to wear.
