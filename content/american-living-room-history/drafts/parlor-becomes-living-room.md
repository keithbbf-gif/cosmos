# The parlor becomes a living room

The Metropolitan Museum’s Rococo Revival parlor is a room that has already been edited. The woodwork came from Horace Whittemore’s villa in Astoria, Queens, built about 1850. The furniture is John Henry Belter’s laminated rosewood, the height of mid-century fashion, set as if a prosperous household still received callers there. The Museum is honest about the splice: architecture from one house, seating from a shop that sold across the country. What you see is not one family’s Tuesday. It is the idea of a parlor — the most expensive sitting furniture a household could show — held still for visitors who will never sit.

That is the first fact of American living-room furniture. The sofa is older than the name. For most of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries a prosperous household received in a parlor, sat in a sitting room, and slept a best bed in a front chamber that inventories still called a parlor. The living room, as a word and as a job, is late. It gathers two Victorian rooms — the show parlor and the family sitting room — and asks one set of furniture to do both. By 1910 the bungalow books prefer the new name. By 1915 Lillian Hart Tyron can write that we are becoming a parlorless nation. The furniture does not disappear. It changes jobs.

## The face of the house

Godey’s Lady’s Book called the parlor “the face of the house — the most noticeable part — and that from which visitors take their impressions of the whole.” The sentence is mid-nineteenth-century and it is still the right way to start. A parlor is a performance. Guests enter. The family performs gentility. Chairs have ranks. The center table is, in Andrew Jackson Downing’s *Architecture of Country Houses* (1850), “the emblem of the family circle.” Downing was writing for villa clients who could afford a room that did one thing. Most American houses could not. They kept a front room they were afraid to sit in and a back room they actually used.

Clifford Edward Clark’s *The American Family Home, 1800–1960* is the book that keeps the parlor from becoming a costume. Clark follows the middle-class house as it adds rooms, loses servants, and argues with itself about comfort. The parlor is not a European leftover that Americans failed to shake. It is an American claim to civilization, made with furniture that industrialization had begun to cheapen. A household that could not own land in the old way could own a matched set. Magazines told the lady of the house what the set should look like. Oscar Fitzgerald’s *Three Centuries of American Furniture* is blunt about the kit by mid-century: an armchair, a lady’s chair, a sofa, and four parlor chairs. The husband’s chair has arms. The wife’s chair does not, or has little ones, because her dress and her rank both needed the space.

None of that is a living room. A living room is a room you are allowed to occupy on a Wednesday. The parlor’s job was to look occupied even when no one was in it.

## Two rooms, then one

A house large enough to specialize kept a parlor for callers and a sitting room for the family. The sitting room could take a sewing table, a worn easy chair, a child’s stool. The parlor kept the rosewood, the étagère, the album. Between 1850 and 1890, the Cincinnati thesis that tracks parlor-to-living-room language finds the formal room loosening. Children are allowed in. The furniture count drops. The floor area shrinks. *House Beautiful* in 1898 went further than a name change. It called the old drawing room an “old gold-and-brocaded over-decorated horror of yesterday” and a show-room that violated the idea of home. The aesthetic is Arts and Crafts talking: stay in the room, wear the furniture in, stop striving for effect. Henry L. Wilson’s *Bungalow Book* (1910) gives the replacement a temperature. The living room is warmth and comfort, the heart of the house, “just the sort of cheerful, cozy, sunny, restful place you will be glad to turn to.” If the parlor was a face, the living room is a hearth with chairs that may be sat in.

Lillian Hart Tyron, *Speaking of Home* (1915), writes the funeral of the old room without pretending the furniture vanished:

> We are fast becoming a parlorless nation… The parlor now is relegated to the cold and viewless side [of the house], or is crowded into a corner of the hall, with two chairs and a palm. We could not get our parlors back if we tried, because we ourselves have change. The living room answers to a new social feeling.

Tyron’s spelling of “change” is hers. The social feeling is fewer friends, better ones, and no patience for a room that punished the people who paid for it.

## What the name is not

A later internet habit pins the living-room name on the 1918 influenza and a *Ladies’ Home Journal* issue of 1910, as if the magazine renamed the death-room after the flu. The dates do not work. 1910 is eight years before the pandemic. Funeral viewing in the front room is real — the parlor-as-death-room essay in this series takes that hour — and undertakers did take the viewing out of the house. That is a separate history from Wilson’s bungalow copy and Tyron’s parlorless nation. Do not staple them.

The name also does not mean the furniture became honest overnight. A 1915 living room could still hold a parlor suite with the arms cut down and the center table lowered in the story if not yet in the shop. Colonial Revival would put Windsor chairs and a gateleg back into a “living room” and call it Early American. The job changed faster than the stock.

## Who sits, who stands

Specialization of the front room is a labor claim. Someone polished the rosewood when no callers were expected. Someone carried the best chairs from the wall to the fire. In Southern houses, and in some Northern houses into the early nineteenth century, enslaved workers did that carrying. Free Black and white servants did it in others. A parlor that exists to display gentility needs a person who is not displayed. When the living room absorbs the sitting room, some of that labor collapses into the family. The lady of the house who no longer has time to keep a death-room perfect is also a woman with suffrage coming, with wage work opening during the First World War, with less staff. Tyron is writing that change as comfort. It is also a staff count.

Factory furniture makes the collapse visible in wood. Grand Rapids could ship a parlor set to a town that had never had a Belter. The set still performed rank — gentleman’s chair, lady’s chair — but it was a catalog rank. The living room that follows is often the same factory, less carving, more spring, a davenport instead of a sofa. The word *davenport* is a trade word for an upholstered seat that wants to be slept on. A parlor sofa did not admit that job.

## How to look at the room you have

If the front room has a center table at dining height, casters on the chairs, and a wall that is too blank for a painting and too low for a secretary, you may still be in a parlor, whatever the listing says. If the sofa faces a hearth and the chairs can turn, you are closer to Wilson. If the sofa faces a black rectangle and the hearth is a mantel for photographs, you are in the television hour — later essays take the screen, the recliner, and the great room.

This page owns the name change. Other slugs own the seven-piece set, the center table, the bungalow, the family room. Do not read a ranch-house pit group as a failed Belter parlor. Do not read a hall-and-parlor house of 1700 as a house that forgot to build a living room. The living room is invented when the household decides the best seating may be used. The furniture history in this series is the history of that permission.

Biddle’s dining-room recess is a carpenter’s tell for a different room. The living-room tell is simpler and easier to miss: abrasion on the front rail of the best sofa. A parlor that was only a face does not wear there. A living room does.
