# The Victorian ladies chair and the gentleman's chair

Oscar P. Fitzgerald, *Three Centuries of American Furniture*, gives the mid-century parlor kit without romance: an armchair, a lady’s chair, a sofa, and four parlor chairs. The husband’s chair has arms. The wife’s chair does not, or has little ones, because her dress and her rank both needed the space. Rancho Los Cerritos, citing Fitzgerald, repeats the diagram for an 1870s parlor: the armchair reads as “throne-like”; the lady’s chair is less imposing; the sofa is for guest and host together. That is a social drawing. It is also a set of joints, springs, and seat widths. This page takes the two named chairs. The seven-piece set as a commercial unit belongs to another slug. Do not sneer at the lady’s chair for lacking arms. Measure the skirt, then look at the mortise.

A ladies chair in the trade is a fully upholstered parlor chair with a shaped back — balloon, spoon, or shield — and either no arms or short, low arms that stop before they fight a crinoline or a bustle. A gentleman’s chair is the same suite’s armchair: full arms, often a wider seat, often a taller or more carved crest. Side chairs in the set are smaller versions of the ladies chair, four of them, for “lesser people,” as Fitzgerald put it in a later lecture when he walked chairs through four centuries. The language is rank. The wood is the same rosewood or walnut. The difference is the arm and the width.

## Dress is a dimension

A woman in a cage crinoline of the 1850s or a bustle of the 1870s occupies more floor than a man in trousers. Arms on a chair catch silk. They also catch the cage. An armless parlor chair lets the skirt fall and lets the sitter rise without lifting the chair. Short arms — a carved stump, a padded rest that does not reach the front of the seat — are a compromise that appears on some ladies chairs and on some “half-arm” side chairs. Historic New England’s Renaissance Revival side chairs from Roseland Cottage, Woodstock, Connecticut, 1870–1880, are described in the file as having “carved and incised half arms” (1970.495.3–.5). The armchair in the same set has rounded hand rests and full arm supports (1970.495.1–.2). Walnut, leather, a slanted pediment crest, burl lozenges, turnip feet. The gender is in the arm, not in a different species of furniture.

Crinoline years and bustle years are not the same silhouette, and shops did not issue a new patent every time fashion moved. A ladies chair of 1848 and a ladies chair of 1880 can share a balloon back and differ in seat depth and in how far a carved support projects. Look at the upholstery line. A chair reupholstered in the 1920s for a Colonial Revival living room may have lost the seat pitch that a hoop required. The frame still tells.

Men’s coats of the 1840s–70s are not narrow. A gentleman’s armchair is still wider because rank wanted width and because a man was expected to sit with elbows out — reading, smoking if the room allowed it, occupying. The armchair is not more comfortable by some eternal law. It is the chair that announces the head of the household to a caller who has been standing in the hall.

Etiquette books assign seats. A Debrett’s compilation of Victorian visiting rules — British, but read in America — has the host offer an armchair to a young man, insist on the armchair for an elderly man, and beg a lady to take the sofa. The most honourable winter places are the corners of the fireplace. American parlor practice is less formula in the documents we have and more diagram in the furniture: two named chairs, a sofa, four lesser chairs, a center table. The lady of the house may sit in the ladies chair or on the sofa with a guest. The furniture does not lock her there. It proposes.

## Joinery and stuffing

The parlor chair of 1840–1870 is a hardwood frame — rosewood, mahogany, walnut — with a show surface that may be laminated and carved and a hidden structure of ash, oak, maple, or chestnut. Belter’s shop and its competitors steam-bent laminated backs in two planes (U.S. Patent 19,405, 23 February 1858). A factory in Grand Rapids a generation later saws a balloon back from a board and glues on a carved crest. Both are parlor chairs. The first can take piercing that would split solid wood. The second can be priced for a town that never saw Broadway.

Seat frames are mortised and tenoned, later dowelled. The front rail is the rail that wears if the room becomes a living room. Webbing, then a layer of coil springs in better work after mid-century, then tow or horsehair, then canvas, then the show cover. Horsehair cloth — woven with a weft of horse tail — is the shiny black or brown stuff that still sits on the McLoughlin House sofa. It is durable and it is slippery. Damask, brocatelle, and needlework covers are for households that can clean them or recover them. The Met’s Rococo Revival parlor uses a reproduction blue-and-gold silk brocatelle copied from Camden, Port Royal, Virginia, 1856–59. That is a museum decision. A middling parlor used what the upholsterer had.

Cabriole legs on Rococo Revival chairs end in a scroll or a caster. Renaissance Revival chairs of the 1860s–80s prefer turned front legs and a more architectural crest — pediment, incised line, burl panel. The ladies chair follows the suite’s style. It is not a separate fashion. If the gentleman’s chair is laminated rosewood with a floral crest, the ladies chair is the same carving without the arms. If the gentleman’s chair is walnut with an incised pediment, so is she.

Slipper chairs — low-seated, armless, often for a bedroom — are not ladies parlor chairs. Belter’s slipper chair at the Met (51.79.9, gift 1951) is a showpiece of pierced grapes and vines, 44 1/4 inches high, 17 1/2 wide. It can stand in a parlor. It is still a different form. Do not collapse every armless chair into the ladies chair of the match set.

## Rank without a joke

The diagram is patriarchal. Saying so is not the same as treating the furniture as a cartoon. A woman who received callers in that room ran a labor system: servants, children, the tea tray, the album, the conversation that made the household look civilized. Her chair’s missing arms are a tool for her dress and a sign of her rank relative to her husband’s chair, not a joke about her importance. The parlor was, in period language, her face of the house. Godey’s said the parlor was the face from which visitors took their impressions of the whole. She sat in the lesser chair and ran the greater room.

Factories made the diagram cheaper. Grand Rapids could ship a “Turkish” or a “French” parlor set to a prairie town with the same arm logic printed on the catalog page. The carving thins. The springs stay. The names stay: gent’s chair, lady’s chair. A catalog rank is still a rank. When the living room absorbs the parlor, the pair often survives as “his and hers” lounge chairs with no relation to a skirt. The later pair is comfort. The Victorian pair is a receiving diagram.

Enslaved workers and free servants moved these chairs from the wall to the fire and back. A chair on casters is a chair meant to be rearranged for each call. The lady’s chair is lighter than the gentleman’s if the arms are gone; that is physics, and it is also who was expected to help rearrange. In a staffed house the chairs move without the sitters touching them. In an unstaffed house the diagram is a daily nuisance. That is one reason the living room drops the match set.

Southern parlors before 1865 sit the same gendered pair on floors maintained by enslaved labor. The chair does not record that. The house does. After emancipation the same rosewood is dusted by wage workers or by the family. The joinery does not change. The labor does.

## How to look at a pair

If you have two chairs from a parlor set, put them side by side. Same crest, same leg, same seat rail profile: they are a pair. Arms on one, none or half on the other: you are looking at gentleman and lady, not at a random armchair and a random side chair. Side chairs are smaller in seat and usually in back. A ladies chair is closer to the armchair in scale. Dealers sometimes sell a side chair as a ladies chair. Measure the seat width and look at the back height against the sofa, if the sofa survives.

Look under the seat. Webbing patterns, spring counts, and webbing tacks tell you whether the upholstery is original, old, or last Tuesday. Look at the arm stumps on a ladies chair that “should” be armless — sometimes arms were added to make the chair useful after dresses narrowed. Look at an armchair whose arms have been cut down to fit a later room. The saw cut is a living-room decision imposed on parlor furniture.

Sit, if the museum or the owner allows it. A ladies chair with a surviving pitch will feel upright and slightly forward, a receiving chair, not a reading chair. A gentleman’s chair may let you slouch a little because the arms take your weight. Neither is a Morris chair. The Morris chair is a later living-room argument about ease. These two are arguments about who sits where when the bell rings.

This page owns the gendered pair. The seven-piece set owns the commercial suite. Belter owns laminated rosewood. Renaissance Revival owns the pedimented walnut generation. Eastlake owns the reform that got tired of the stuffed pair. If you find one armchair and one armless chair in matching walnut and you smile at the names, look again at the seat rails. Someone planed those shoulders for a dress you have never worn.
