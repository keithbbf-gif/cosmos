# Federal parlor furniture

The Metropolitan Museum’s Richmond Room is a parlor taken from a house built in 1810–11 for the Richmond lawyer William Clayton Williams (1768–1817). The woodwork is mahogany. The baseboards are blue-and-gray King of Prussia marble from Pennsylvania. Two doors flank the fireplace; the left led to the dining room, the right was a false door, symmetry over traffic. Theophilus Nash (died 1854) is the maker the Museum names for the room. The gift is Joe Kindig Jr.’s, 1968. The object number for the architecture is 68.137. That is a published Met number for a room, not a sofa. I will not invent furniture accession numbers to fill the floor.

The scenic paper you see now is not Williams’s paper. It is a modern silk-screen of *Les Monuments de Paris*, the Dufour panorama of 1814 designed after Jean Broc, the sort of French paper American merchants sold in the 1810s. The plaster ceiling medallion copies one at Hampstead in New Kent County, Virginia, about 1825. The marble mantel is not original to the house. The furniture the Museum places here — Phyfe and Lannuier, New York Empire at a high level — is not Williams’s suite. The Museum says so. The room is an Anglo-French setting for the collection, not a frozen Tuesday in Richmond. Use it as architecture: visible expensive wood, marble at the floor, a parlor that expects chairs with legs you can see.

Charles F. Montgomery, *American Furniture: The Federal Period, in the Henry Francis du Pont Winterthur Museum* (1966), is the catalogue that still organizes this furniture. Federal, in his sense and in this page’s, is the American neoclassical of roughly 1790–1825: lightened after Chippendale, before the full weight of Empire. Legs show. Mahogany takes over the good parlor. Inlay — satinwood, ebony, holly, stringing — draws the lines a carver used to cut. A “suite” in these years is not Fitzgerald’s later seven-piece factory match. It is a set of chairs, a sofa or Grecian couch, maybe window seats, pier tables that belong to the wall. This series stays in the sitting furniture. The dining-room series may have the sideboard.

## Visible legs, mahogany, the end of the skirt

A late Chippendale parlor chair hides behind a splat and a heavy seat rail. A Federal parlor chair stands on tapered square legs or turned reeded legs, often with spade feet or small brass casters. The seat rail is thinner. The back is a shield, an oval, a square with a tablet, a splat cut into drapery or a urn. Hepplewhite and Sheraton, London books, are the paper sources American shops used without becoming English shops. George Hepplewhite’s *Guide* (1788; 3rd ed. 1794) and Thomas Sheraton’s *Drawing-Book* (1791–94) and *Cabinet Dictionary* (1803) circulate. American chairs come out lighter in the splat and thicker in the seat than the plates if the shop is Boston, more carved if the shop is Salem, more reeded if the shop is New York.

Mahogany is the primary wood for the show surface, imported from the Caribbean and Central America, the same trade that paneled Williams’s parlor in solid mahogany rather than paint. Secondary woods are the American ones: Eastern white pine, tulip poplar, ash, maple for turning. Veneer lets a Boston or New York shop book-match a shield back or a sofa rail. Inlay is not “decoration” in the Victorian sense. It is the drawing.

Upholstery on parlor seating is horsehair, moreen, silk, later a fine hay or hair stuffing over webbing. Seats are often slip seats, upholstered off the frame, so a household can change the cloth. Sofas and Grecian couches are fixed upholstery with loose cushions. Casters on the front legs of chairs and on sofa feet let the parlor rearrange for a call. The sitting-room essay owns the worn easy chair. This parlor does not want that wear. It wants the legs to remain visible after the maid has gone around them with a mop. In Southern and some Northern houses into the early nineteenth century, the person with the mop may be enslaved. In Boston and Philadelphia after gradual emancipation, the labor is hired, still not in the portrait.

## Boston, New York, Philadelphia, Salem

Boston’s Federal parlor seating is the Seymour shop if you are lucky and a good anonymous shop if you are honest. John Seymour and Thomas Seymour, immigrant English cabinetmakers, are documented for case pieces and for seating of a particular neatness: satinwood, barber-pole stringing, reeded legs that do not shout. A parlor in a Boston townhouse of 1805 wants a set of square-back chairs and a sofa whose crest is a gentle swell, not a cornucopia. New York is Phyfe’s city and Lannuier’s city. This page will not take their biographies. The Phyfe essay owns parlor sofas and window seats. The Empire essay owns the Grecian couch and the recamier as a form. What belongs here is the Federal New York parlor before the arms turn into horns of plenty: reeded legs, thunderbolt and bowknot carving on a crest if the shop is ambitious, cane sometimes, a lightness Montgomery’s Winterthur sofas still show.

Philadelphia’s Federal chairs can keep a little of the old weight in the seat while the back goes oval or shield. Henry Connelly and Ephraim Haines are the names the literature attaches to reeded, painted, and cane-seated seating around 1805–10, including work associated with Latrobe’s circle. Painted fancy chairs also sell in Philadelphia; Baltimore will out-paint them. A Philadelphia parlor of 1800 may still hold a Chippendale easy chair upstairs and a new sofa downstairs. Inventories in Trent’s easy-chair sample put the sofa in the ground-floor parlor. That placement is the Federal claim: the expensive stuffed seat for guests is now a sofa with legs, not a winged invalid chair.

Salem is carving. Samuel McIntire (1757–1811), architect and carver, supplies furniture carving and architectural carving for the same houses. Motifs the auction literature and Montgomery repeat: bowknot, grapes, star-punched ground. Seating associated with Elias Hasket Derby and with Elizabeth Derby West is at the Museum of Fine Arts, Boston, in the Karolik Collection — shield-back chairs and a sofa — as published by Edwin Hipkiss and cited by Montgomery (his cat. no. 15 among related objects). The Peirce-Nichols House parlor, remodeled by McIntire in 1801 for a daughter’s marriage to George Nichols, had matching sofas and window stools. A Grecian or recamier sofa with McIntire-tradition carving, derived from a plate in Sheraton’s *Cabinet Dictionary* (1803), is a Salem parlor object of about 1805. Use the type. Do not invent a Met number for a Salem sofa that lives in Boston or in a private collection.

## The Grecian couch in a Federal parlor

Sheraton’s *Cabinet Dictionary* illustrates the Grecian taste that American shops turn into a couch with one high scrolled end, or two, a long seat for reclining, visible legs or a plinth. Madame Récamier’s name attaches later, from Jacques-Louis David’s 1800 portrait; American shops say Grecian couch more often than recamier in the first years. Montgomery catalogues Winterthur examples. The Empire essay takes the heavier New York and Philadelphia couches of 1815–40 — cornucopia arms, paw feet, dolphin supports. The Federal parlor’s couch is still a light thing: mahogany, maybe cane, a silk mattress, a place to sit along a wall between windows, not yet a sculpture.

Window seats belong to the same wall logic. They fit the pier. Phyfe’s later Grecian Plain window seats (the Foot house suite, New York, about 1837) are an Empire end of a Federal idea: furniture that admits the window. A Salem window stool carved by McIntire’s shop is the earlier, smaller version.

## How the suite sits in the house

A Federal parlor is a receiving room. The bed has gone upstairs if the house can afford the split the best-bed essay describes. The easy chair has gone with it. What remains is chairs in a set, a sofa or couch, a center or pier table, paper or paint, and, in a house like Williams’s, woodwork expensive enough to be the furniture. Callers stay a quarter of an hour. Casters let the chairs come off the wall and go back. The sitting room, if there is one, takes the work table and the worn chair.

Do not read the Richmond Room as a document of Williams’s seating. Read it as a document of what a parlor built in 1810–11 thought wood could do. Solid mahogany paneling is a cabinetmaker’s wall. Marble baseboards are a stone version of a chair rail’s job: take the mop, take the kick. Scenic paper, even as a Museum reproduction, tells you the walls were meant to be a view, not a place to hang a bed. The false door tells you symmetry outranked a second passage. The real door to the dining room tells you the parlor was one of a pair of public rooms. This series stops at the parlor side of that door.

Factory furniture has not yet made a matched seven-piece set the middle-class kit. A Federal parlor can be a set of twelve chairs from one shop and a sofa from another. Inlay can mismatch and still be of the year. The later Victorian hunger for a single carved crest on seven frames is a different economy.

## How to look at Federal parlor seating

Look at the legs first. If they disappear into a heavy apron and a skirt of carving, you may have slipped into Empire or into a Revival piece. If they are tapered, reeded, or slightly splayed, and you can see air under the seat, you are in the right decades. Look at the secondary woods. Pine and poplar are not defects. Look at the slip seat. A frame that never had one may be a sofa or a later recover. Look at the casters. Brass rollers on a delicate taper are period options, not always later adds.

If someone offers you a “Williams House sofa,” ask which house and which museum. The Richmond Room’s seating is a curatorial sentence, not a provenance. If someone offers you a McIntire sofa, ask for the grapes, the punch ground, and a paper trail to Derby or Peirce-Nichols or an equal document. Montgomery is still the book to walk through a collection with. The living room that wants a Federal sofa is asking for visible legs and a lighter seat than the seven-piece set. That request is historically right for 1810. It is not a reason to strip the paint from a Windsor and call the result Federal. Different chairs. Different rooms. Same permission, later, to sit on them.
