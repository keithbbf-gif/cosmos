# Sectional sofas before the pit

A living-room corner in the Broadway Department Store’s exhibit at the 1935 National Housing Exposition in Los Angeles is already thinking in pieces. The *Los Angeles Times* photograph caption calls it “just a corner.” Seating turns the angle. Tables fill the gaps. Nothing in that picture needs to be a single eight-foot sofa. The American sectional — modules that make a longer seat, an L, or a U — is older than the sunken pit and older than the wrapped-foam groups of the 1970s. Department stores sold the idea as a group. Harvey Probber, after 1945, named it modular and designed the units so a household could recombine them. Kroehler and its peers sold open-end sofas that were sectionals in all but the word.

This page stops before the pit group. The conversation-pit essay and the 1970s pit-group essay own the sunken and the wrapped-foam afterlives. Do not read a 1954 Bermuda Group as a failed Miller House. Do not read Probber as the first person who ever put two seats at a right angle.

## Unit furniture, then a living-room kit

What Probber’s obituaries and the 2020 New York School of Interior Design exhibition call his invention is a system, not the first corner. “Unit furniture” in the first decades of the twentieth century already meant cases and seats that stacked or sat in series. Bookcases in sections, dining chairs in sets, porch furniture in addable lengths. Living-room upholstery was slower because a sprung sofa wants a continuous rail. The 1930s solution in stores was the group: a sofa, a matching chair, and a corner chair or wedge that let the suite turn a wall. Broadway’s 1934 showrooms, photographed for the store, show customers sitting in staged living rooms that are already L-shaped. The National Housing Exposition corner of 1935 is the same idea under fair lighting.

Those groups often did not clip. You shoved them tight and hoped the front rail lined up. Casters and uneven floors produced a gap that collected crumbs. The sectional as a hardware problem — hooks, pins, alligator clips, overlapping plates — is a later factory refinement. `[CITE NEEDED: a dated American trade catalog, 1930–1945, that shows a named connecting device between upholstered units.]` Until that hardware is common, the sectional is a composition, not a mechanism.

Frames stay hardwood: maple, birch, gum. Legs are tapered wood or metal rods after the war. Arms may exist on both ends of a sofa, on one end only (the “open end” that Kroehler advertised), or on neither. A corner unit is the piece that makes the L: a square seat, sometimes with a low table attached, sometimes only a wrapped angle. Cushions are loose, in the 1920s habit, or tight foam by the 1960s. Mohair gives way to textured synthetics and to printed barkcloth in the tracts.

## Harvey Probber’s modules

Harvey Probber was born in Brooklyn in 1922. The *New York Times* obituary (2003) is the careful source for the biography. He sold a sofa design at sixteen. After high school he designed at Trade Upholstery on West 17th Street and learned how a small factory actually builds a seat. In 1945 he founded Harvey Probber, Inc. Around that year he introduced the Sert Group: standalone upholstered modules that worked in combination. He called the idea modular furniture. In 1947, when Merchandise Mart space in Chicago was not available, he took the line to Grand Rapids. In 1948 he opened a New York showroom at 136 Fifth Avenue. A sling chair of his appeared in MoMA’s Good Design orbit; later writers sometimes collapse dates, so treat exhibition labels as the authority when an editor has them. `[CITE NEEDED: the exact MoMA Good Design year and object for Probber’s Sling chair versus later citations that say 1948.]`

Probber also talked about “nuclear” furniture: tables and seats that clustered. The Nuclear and Sert groups, and later the Architectural Series and Cubo, are the pieces the NYSID show put back in a room. M2L now licenses Deep Tuft, Nuclear Sert, Cubo, Mayan, and the rest. Those names are midcentury shop names, not 1930s department-store names. Probber’s modules could sit on legs or on bases. They could be rearranged when a household moved from an apartment to a ranch. That is the living-room claim: the seating is not a fixed suite. It is a set of parts.

He was not the only person in the room. Edward Wormley at Dunbar, Tommi Parzinger, and other “elegant modern” designers sold sectionals to people who did not want a ranch-house maple group. Probber’s distinction, if the NYSID thesis holds, is that he treated the module as the product rather than as a leftover corner chair. The *Times* called the 1940s sectional a furniture-industry phenomenon. Phenomena have many parents. Give Probber the system and the name. Give the stores the earlier corner.

## What the stores sold

Kroehler, by the 1940s the second-largest American furniture maker if you believe the company’s own later lore, pushed upholstered groups into the postwar living room. A May 1954 *McCall’s* advertisement for the Bermuda Group sells a red open-end sofa — right or left — with a companion chair and a regular sofa as alternatives. “Cushionized” is Kroehler’s comfort word. The price in that ad for a full-size sofa is $165. The open end is a chaise that attaches in the photograph’s grammar even when the hardware is only adjacency. A three-piece salmon sectional of about 1955, the kind dealers still drag out of basements, is the same kit in a different cover.

Life magazine’s 28 January 1957 issue carried a Carmel Group ad. The point of printing the date is not nostalgia. It is to show that by the mid-1950s a reader of a general magazine was expected to know what a living-room group was. You bought a configuration, not a Belter. Department stores from Broadway in Los Angeles to Marshall Field in Chicago staged the L against a picture window or a fireplace. The ranch-house essay will take the window. Here the furniture is what makes the window usable: seats that turn the corner so someone can see glass and someone can see the hearth.

Labor in these factories is the same gendered split as in Karpen’s earlier plants: frame mills, springers, sewing rooms. Naperville and Stratford (Ontario) Kroehler shops left catalogs that talk about hidden steel. Modular work adds a new shop problem, the matching of welts across a joint. A bad sectional shows a dogleg in the front rail. A good one reads as one horizon.

## Before the pit, after the parlor

A sectional is a living-room answer to a room that is no longer a parlor. The seven-piece set faced inward toward a center table. The sectional faces a focus: fire, window, radio, then television. It can also face itself, an L in which relatives talk across a corner. That conversation geometry is why later designers will sink the whole thing. The 1930–1970 sectional still has legs. You can sweep under it. You can pull a module out and fail to put it back straight. Children make forts from the corner unit. None of that requires a sunken floor.

Apartments liked sectionals because an L uses a corner that a long sofa wastes. Ranch houses liked them because the living room opens to the dining area and the seating has to stop without a wall. Family rooms will take the tougher cover and the television. The front living room, once a family room exists, may keep a more careful sectional that no one sits on — the parlor habit in modular clothing.

Do not steal the dining room. A banquette in a breakfast nook is not a sectional sofa. A built-in banquette is architecture. This slug is loose furniture, delivered in pieces, meant to be rearranged at least once.

## How to look at the pieces in the room

Count the seams on the floor. Two seams and a corner make a three-piece L. A row of armless middles is a midcentury modular. If every piece has two arms, you have a suite shoved together, not a sectional. Look for clips or plates at the back legs. Look at the open end: is it a chaise, a wedge, or just a missing arm? Look at the cover. A 1930s group in mohair or tapestry will have taller backs. A 1950s Kroehler will sit lower, with buttoned or plain backs and tapered legs. A Probber module will look like a designed unit even when it stands alone — the welt, the base, the proportion.

If the seating is sunken or the foam wraps the floor, you have left this essay. If the pieces clip into a pit-shaped square but still stand on a level floor, you are in the late 1960s approaching the 1970s group. Stop there and send the reader forward.

The sectional’s American history is a history of the living room admitting that one sofa is not enough wall and not enough conversation. Stores sold the admission as a corner. Probber sold it as a system. The pit will try to make it architecture. Until then, the living room’s modular seat is still furniture you can pick up, if you have two people and a clear path to the door.
