# Hall-and-parlor house furniture

A Virginia planter’s house of the late seventeenth century does not have a living room. It has a hall and a parlor, and both words will mislead a modern buyer. The hall is not a corridor. It is the working room: eat, work, receive, sleep if the weather or the household requires it. The parlor — sometimes called the chamber or the inner room — is not a Victorian showplace. It is the second heated room, often with a bed in it, benches, a table, chairs, and, in Clark’s quotation of the Chesapeake inventories, hoes. The hoe in the parlor is the sentence that should stop anyone from hanging a reproduction portrait and calling the room done.

Clifford Edward Clark, *The American Family Home, 1800–1960*, is writing a later house, but he opens with this earlier plan because the later parlor is an argument with it. Mark Girouard’s English country-house hall is a ceremonial ancestor. The American hall-and-parlor house is smaller, colder, and more mixed. Furniture has to earn its floor. Nothing is resident in the twentieth-century sense. A table that does not fold is a claim on heat.

## Two rooms, many jobs

Abbott Lowell Cummings and the New England house literature give the same diagram with different weather. A hall-and-parlor plan is two rooms on the ground floor, chimney in the wall between them or in a pile at the center, chambers above if the house has a second story. The hall takes the larger fire. The parlor takes the better bed. Inventories in Massachusetts Bay and the Connecticut valley list joined chests, cupboards, forms (backless benches), joint stools, a great chair for the head of the household, and turned chairs that later dealers will call Brewster or Carver without the documents always supporting the names.

The furniture is oak first, then maple, pine, walnut as the woods and the ports allow. Mortise-and-tenon frames, wooden pins, chests with till boxes, tables with stretcher bases you can rest a foot on because the floor is cold. Upholstery, when it exists, is a cushion you can move. A fully upholstered easy chair is a later, expensive, often medical object — the wing-chair essay takes that form. In the hall-and-parlor house the soft thing is textile: bed hangings that keep a body warm, table carpets that are too good to be walked on and are therefore also display.

Southern inventories complicate the Northern picture. A Chesapeake hall can be the planter’s public room and the place where bound labor is directed. The parlor-chamber can hold the best bed and the weapons. Enslaved workers move between rooms that the inventory treats as the planter’s. A history of this furniture that names only the joined chest is a history of the lock, not the house.

## What is not here

There is no sofa. There is no center table in Downing’s emblematic sense. There is no seven-piece set. A settle — a high-backed bench that can face the fire and keep a draft off the neck — appears where the household can pay for the timber and the joinery. The settle essay in this series owns that form. In many houses the settle is a chest-settle or a bench that later taste will call primitive and price as if age were a finish.

There is also no dedicated dining room. Meals happen in the hall. The dining-room series next door is a later invention; do not drag a sideboard into this plan. A cupboard or a court cupboard can hold plate. A dresser can hold pewter. Those are storage with a public face, not a dining-room suite.

A joined chest is the case piece that inventories list most often and dealers later romanticize most freely. It holds linen, sometimes a till for papers or coin, and it can sit at the foot of the parlor bed as a bench. The show face may be carved or paneled; the ends and back are often plainer oak or pine. Open the lid. If the interior is dirty in a way a refinisher would have cleaned, you may be looking at use. If every surface is the same orange varnish, you are looking at a twentieth-century finish story. A court cupboard, where it appears, is taller and more public: plate on the open tier, a closed cupboard below. It faces the hall, not the chamber. Neither object is a living-room console. They are storage that people also sat on.

Windsor chairs, when they arrive in the eighteenth century, are sitting-room and tavern furniture that will later look “Early American” in a Colonial Revival living room. In a 1740 hall they are light, cheap relative to joined great chairs, and stackable in a way a wainscot chair is not. The Windsor essay owns the form. Here they are a reminder that the hall-and-parlor house is not frozen in 1680. It absorbs new chairs the way it absorbs a second story.

## Heat is the furniture plan

Place the fire first. Then place the settle or the great chair. Then place the table so a meal can happen without blocking the door to the stair or the yard. Beds in the parlor sit against the inner wall or in a corner where hangings can make a room inside the room. A trundle is a child’s or a servant’s claim on the same heat. `[CITE NEEDED: a dated Chesapeake or New England inventory that lists a trundle in the parlor-chamber by name, not by later catalog language.]`

Windows are few and expensive. Furniture that needs to be seen — a cupboard with applied ornament, a chest with a carved façade — faces the room, not the glass. The Victorian parlor will reverse this: objects face the visitor who entered from the hall. The hall-and-parlor house faces the fire.

Castors are rare. You move a joint stool by hand. You fold a table if the table was built to fold. You do not roll a rosewood suite to the walls for a dance. When the McLoughlin House, much later, keeps mid-nineteenth-century parlor furniture on castors so the room can clear, that is a different century’s problem. Do not read it backward.

## Names that arrived later

Brewster and Carver chairs are useful museum terms and dangerous sales terms. A great chair with spindles is not automatically a relic of a *Mayflower* passenger. Pilgrim Century as a dealer category is a twentieth-century romance that Colonial Revival living rooms will swallow whole. If the chair has a history, it has a place, a wood, and a joint. If it has a romance, put the romance in the caption as a later story.

The word *parlor* in a 1690 inventory is not Godey’s face of the house. It is the inner room. A reader who hears parlor and sees Belter has jumped two centuries. This series will make that jump on purpose, in order. This page stays on the early plan.

Secondary woods matter as much as the show face. A New England joined chest may be oak on the front and pine on the back boards. A Chesapeake table may be walnut or cypress where a Boston shop would have used maple. Hurst and Prown’s Colonial Williamsburg catalog is the Southern check: yellow pine as a structural wood, not a defect. If a restorer has replaced every secondary board with clean poplar, the object still has a shape and has lost a document.

## How to look at a house that still has two rooms

If you are in a surviving hall-and-parlor house — Tidewater, the Connecticut valley, a restored New England saltbox — stand in the door between the two rooms and look at the chimney. The furniture should explain the brick. A sofa pointed at a television in the hall is a living-room solution to a hall problem. It will block the work path and make the parlor-chamber into a junk room. A bench or a pair of chairs at the fire, a table that can be moved, a chest that still holds linen: those are the jobs the plan was written for.

Southern hardwoods as a later shop language belong to the woods essay at the end of this series. Here the wood is oak, pine, walnut, maple, and whatever the creek and the coast provided. A white-oak settle made in Arkansas in 2020 can rhyme with this plan. It is not this plan.

The living room is a later permission: sit on the best furniture. The hall-and-parlor house does not grant that permission because it does not have best furniture in the Victorian sense. It has furniture that works, and a parlor-chamber that is slightly more private than the hall. If you find a bed in the front room of an old house and feel embarrassed for the owners, you are reading with a living-room eye. They were keeping the heat.
