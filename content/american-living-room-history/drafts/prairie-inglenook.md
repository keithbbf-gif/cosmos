# Prairie School living room

The Frederick C. Robie House, Hyde Park, Chicago, was commissioned in 1908 and completed in 1910. On the main floor a Roman-brick fireplace stands between the living room and the dining room. The flues are pulled into side piers so the space above the fire stays open. Seating and cabinetry at the hearth are stepped down into the floor. The Frank Lloyd Wright Trust and the Society of Architectural Historians both describe that brick mass as the organizer: living and dining share a ceiling and a south window wall, and the hearth is the permeable wall between them. You do not need a parlor suite to know where to sit. The architecture has already assigned the seats.

This page is that assignment — built-in seating, the hearth as furniture, the free-standing pieces we can document — in Chicago and the Midwest between about 1900 and 1920. It is not a catalog of every chair Wright ever drew. Where the furniture has been scattered, sold, or replaced, the essay will say so. Invented inventories are how Prairie rooms become stage sets.

## Hearth first

Wright’s own Oak Park house and studio (from 1889, altered through the next two decades) is the workshop where the hearth-centered plan was practiced before Robie. The Prairie houses that follow — Ward Willits, Darwin D. Martin, Susan Lawrence Dana, Robie — treat the fireplace as the center of the family plan, a point Wright and his interpreters have not been shy about. Inglenook language comes from English and American Arts and Crafts: a recessed fire with seats. Wright’s version is often less a nook than a monument. At Robie the fire is a pier you walk around. The seats are part of the brick and oak, not a Stickley settle pulled up after the fact.

Built-in seating is furniture that does not go to a dance. There are no casters. The cushion can be recovered; the box is the house. A window seat in a prow-shaped bay — Robie’s living and dining ends terminate in prows — is the same idea turned toward glass. The sitter is in the architecture. A Victorian ladies chair could be carried to the fire. A Prairie built-in is the fire.

George Mann Niedecken of Milwaukee executed much of the Robie furniture from Wright’s drawings: red oak, in the Trust’s conservation literature. The living room’s original free-standing pieces, as summarized from house histories and museum files, included a sofa with extended armrests, a bench with side tables, a smoker’s cabinet, and movable chairs. A small study with a desk and lamp sat in the living-room space. The Smart Museum of Art holds original Robie furniture; a sofa from that group has been on loan to the Met. Replicas now stand in the house for the pieces that live elsewhere. That is a documented scatter, not a full inventory. Do not add a tabouret or a lamp because a photograph of another Prairie house had one.

Dining furniture at Robie — high-backed chairs, a table with lampposts at the corners — is dining furniture. The dining series can have it. The living room’s job is the hearth seats, the sofa with the long arms, the chairs that could turn toward the fire or toward the south glass. Wright wanted a total interior. He did not want a seven-piece parlor set. He also did not want the family eating in the living room. The open plan is not one room. It is two rooms that share air.

## Dana-Thomas, Springfield

Susan Lawrence Dana’s house in Springfield, Illinois, 1902–1904, is the Prairie house that kept its furniture. The State of Illinois bought it in 1981. Site interpretation notes original Wright-designed furniture, a large central fireplace, art glass in windows, doors, bookcases, and lamps, and a library with bookcases and built-in seating on a lower level. The living room, in the site’s tour script, is the room where Dana studied and held meetings; the chairs there are described as designed to lean back. That is the site’s language. It is not a La-Z-Boy patent. It is a reclining pitch in a custom chair. Do not turn it into a recliner history.

The library’s built-in seating is the form this essay came to see: oak, glass, a garden light, a bench that is the wall. A musician’s balcony and a bowling alley are other rooms. They tell you the house is a large custom job, not a bungalow. Use Dana-Thomas for what the state has published. Do not inventory the living room chair by chair from a tourist memory.

Purcell & Elmslie, Walter Burley Griffin and Marion Mahony Griffin, George Washington Maher: Prairie School is not only Wright. A Minneapolis or Iowa living room with a Roman-brick fire, art glass, and a built-in bench can be their drawing. The furniture, when it is original, follows the same rule — oak, geometry, the hearth as the boss — without a Wright signature. Attribute what you can. “Prairie” is a school, not a brand.

## What the room refuses

It refuses the marble-top center table as emblem. Light comes from glass and from fixtures Wright designed as part of the ceiling and the sconces. A table, when it exists, is oak and low enough, or high enough, to match the sills. It refuses the étagère of bibelots. Display, if any, is in a glassed cabinet that continues the woodwork. It refuses gendered match chairs. Seats are built for the wall’s dimension. A movable chair is a cube or a high-backed geometry, not a balloon back.

It refuses casters. Moving the furniture would break the plan. Servants at Robie and at Dana — both houses had staff — still cleaned, still served, still kept children from the art glass. They did not roll a suite to the wall. The labor is maintenance of a fixed interior, not rearrangement for callers. Callers walk into a living room that is already arranged. That is closer to Wilson’s bungalow hospitality than to Godey’s face, even when the house is a millionaire’s.

Enslaved labor is not the workforce of these 1900–1920 Chicago houses. Domestic service is. The Robie House had servants’ quarters on the main floor’s service side. The plan hides that labor the way a Victorian house hid it, only the hiding is now in a horizontal service zone rather than in a basement kitchen. The hearth faces the family. The work faces the alley.

## Kit Prairie and the ordinary house

Most Midwestern living rooms of 1910 are not Robie. They are bungalows and American Foursquares with a brick fireplace and a bought Morris chair. Some have art-glass transoms from a lumberyard. Some have a built-in bench a carpenter copied from a magazine. Clark’s family home is still the book for that majority. Wright’s living room matters because it made the hearth-and-built-in diagram a high art that catalogs could cheapen. Sears did not sell a Robie sofa. It sold houses with fireplaces and a blank for your oak chair.

Do not call every oak bench Prairie. Stickley’s settle is a cousin with different joints. A Colonial Revival window seat with a Windsor chair parked in it is a different revival. Prairie seating is geometric, oak or mahogany, tied to the brick, often with a cap rail that continues a picture molding. If the bench can leave the room without a carpenter, you may be in Craftsman, not Prairie.

## How to look at a Prairie living room

Stand at the fire and look both ways. If you can see dining furniture through or over the hearth, you are in a Robie-type plan. If the fire is in a nook with two fixed benches and a low beam, you are closer to an inglenook in the English sense, which Wright used and also outgrew. Count what is built in. Count what can move. The ratio is the design.

Ask where the original furniture is. A house museum that has the benches and has replaced the chairs is telling you the truth. A house museum that has filled the room from a dealer’s Prairie lot is telling you a story. The Smart Museum and the Wright Trust have been explicit about Robie’s scatter. Follow that example.

Look at the seat height against the sills. Wright’s chairs and benches often sit in a relationship to the windows that a Victorian parlor chair, designed for a blank wall, does not. Look at the arm of a sofa. An extended arm that becomes a table is a documented Robie idea. A wraparound pit is a 1970s idea. Do not read one as the other.

This page owns the Prairie School living room: Wright, the hearth as organizer, built-in seating, Robie and Dana-Thomas as documented cases. It does not own the bungalow kit, the Stickley settle, or the conversation pit. If you find Roman brick, a dropped seat at the fire, and a file that says where the sofa went, you are doing this work correctly. If you find a list of fourteen Wright chairs with no museum number and no house history, someone invented the parlor.
