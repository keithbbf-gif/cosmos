# Windsor chairs in the sitting room

Pick up a sack-back Windsor by the bow. The hoop is steam-bent oak or ash, sprung into holes in a thick pine seat. Hickory spindles, rived not sawn if the shop is working in the old way, run up into that bow. The legs are turned maple or oak, tapered, often with a vase or ball turning, through-tenoned into the seat and wedged from above so they cannot pull out. The whole chair is light enough to carry in one hand and tough enough to take a tavern night. It was painted. Green, black, red, a Spanish brown, later a mustard or a fake bamboo. It was not stained to show “honest wood.” A stripped Windsor in a living room is a twentieth-century taste laid on an eighteenth-century chair.

Nancy Goyne Evans wrote the book that stops the romance. *American Windsor Chairs* (Hudson Hills, in association with Winterthur, 1996) is 744 pages, more than a thousand illustrations, maps, and a checklist of about 2,400 American Windsor craftsmen, 1745–1850. She had been on the Winterthur staff. The Chipstone review of 1997 called it the first of three related volumes and the regional study the field had been waiting for. Evans’s argument, for this page, is simple enough to sit on: the form comes from England in the 1730s; Americans innovate; by the 1790s Windsor sales surpass those of all other chairs combined; the industry runs from handcraft toward mass production around 1850. Philadelphia first. Then New York, Rhode Island, Connecticut, the rest of New England, the South, the Midwest.

This series is living rooms. The Windsor is sitting-room, tavern, and porch furniture that Colonial Revival will later install in a “living room” and call Early American. It is not a dining-room slug. A Windsor side chair at a table is a real use. This page stays with the chair as a place to sit in the room that works.

## Stick construction, not a joined great chair

A wainscot chair is a joiner’s object: mortise-and-tenon frame, paneled back, oak, heavy. A Windsor is a turner’s and a bender’s object built on a plank seat. The seat is the chassis. Everything else sockets into it. That is why the woods are mixed and why the chair is light. Pine or poplar for the seat because it saddles well with a gutter adze and a travisher. Hard maple or oak for legs that take a lathe. Elastic hardwoods — hickory, oak, ash — for spindles and bows that must flex and then hold. You do not make a Windsor out of one board of mahogany and you do not dovetail it.

The joints you can see if you turn the chair over are the through tenons of the legs, wedged, and the stretchers — H-stretcher or a bulbous medial stretcher — tenoned into the legs. The joints you can see if you look down on the seat are the wedge ends, sometimes covered by later paint. The bow is a bent member in compression and tension at once. When it splits, the chair is in trouble. When the seat splits along the grain, a man with a cleat can save it. Glue is not the original religion. The wedge is.

Paint is the finish. Period chairs were coated, often in layers, because paint hides the mixed woods and because a chair that goes outdoors or into a taproom needs a skin. Scraping to bare pine and calling the result “original” is a Colonial Revival and dealer habit. Evans documents decoration as part of production, not as an apology. Fancy painting — striping, freehand, later stencil — belongs especially to the nineteenth century and to the Baltimore fancy chair, which is a cousin the painted-parlor essay will take. A Philadelphia sack-back of 1765 wants a single color, worn at the handrail and the front of the seat.

## Philadelphia, then the coast

Philadelphia is the American starting shop. Evans’s regional chapters put chairmaking there from the 1740s. Comb-backs — a tall crest like a rake, spindles rising through a shoe or into a carved crest rail — are an early high form, a little ceremonial, the closest a Windsor comes to a great chair. Sack-backs — the hoop, the “sack” of spindles — become the useful armchair. Fan-backs, bow-backs, and, later, the continuous-arm: each is a pattern with a shop geography.

Rhode Island, especially the continuous-arm, is a New England claim Evans’s New England chapters support. The arm rail and the back are one bent member. No separate arm-to-stile joint to loosen. Newport and Providence shops sell chairs that look simple and are not. Connecticut — Hartford, Norwich, the valley towns — produces in volume by the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries. Brace-backs, bamboo turnings, and, after 1800, the step-down and rod-back chairs that fill farmhouse sitting rooms. Massachusetts and the rest of northern New England follow. New York City has its own shops from the 1740s and a nineteenth-century factory story Evans takes separately.

I will not invent a maker’s label. Branded seats exist. “J. HITCHCOCK, HITCHCOCK-VILLE, CONN. WARRANTED” is a later, painted, rush-seat factory chair that people call Windsor when they should not. Lambert Hitchcock’s chair, 1820s–1830s, is a stencil-painted cousin. The sitting room takes both. The historian should keep the names apart.

## Where the chair actually sat

In a 1760 Philadelphia house a set of Windsors can stand in a back sitting room, a piazza, or a shop. They are cheaper than a suite of mahogany Chippendale chairs and more movable. They go to the yard for a wedding. They come back. In a New England sitting room of 1800 they are the everyday chairs. The parlor, if the house has one, may keep a few mahogany seats for calls. The Windsor takes the sewing, the newspaper, the child.

Robert F. Trent’s Philadelphia inventory work, in the easy-chair literature, catches Windsors doing lounge work: a “Green Windsor Sophia With Cushin” in Mary Brown’s estate, 1799; a “Windsor easy Chair” in Samuel Tatem’s, 1806; even a “Close-Stool windsor Chair & Aparatus” in John Roberts, 1805. Those lines matter because they keep the Windsor from being only a kitchen chair. A high raked back and a cushion can be a cheap easy chair. A settee with a hair mattress can be a cheap sofa. The upholstered parlor sofa still outranks them. The Windsor is what you sit on when the sofa is for show.

Taverns and public rooms used them because they stack and clean. That public life is why so many survive and why so many are worn to the seat. A parlor suite that never left the carpet does not have a saddle worn through the paint to the pine.

Labor is shop labor: turners, benchmen, painters, often in a subdivided trade by 1800. Enslaved labor in Mid-Atlantic shops is a documented fact in some crafts; do not pin it to a named Windsor maker without a paper. `[CITE NEEDED: a signed or branded Philadelphia or Rhode Island Windsor with a documented enslaved or indentured workman in the shop.]` Apprentices and journeymen are the ordinary names. Women appear in the nineteenth-century fancy-painting shops — the Finlay warehouse in Baltimore employed women as ornamenters — more than in the 1760 Philadelphia turning shop.

## Paint, not stain, and the Revival mistake

Wallace Nutting and the Colonial Revival living room of 1900–1930 put Windsors in front of hearths that had never seen them as “Early American” relics. Nutting photographed, collected, and manufactured. Some period chairs were stripped and waxed to match a taste for brown wood. Some new chairs were made to look old. A living room with a gateleg table, a Windsor armchair, and a hooked rug is a Revival room. It can be a good room. It is not a 1760 sitting room.

The 1790s high-water mark Evans names — Windsor sales ahead of all other chairs — is a production fact, not a parlor fact. Households bought them because they were light, cheap relative to joined mahogany, and fashionable in a republican way: no carving you had to explain. By 1850 factory methods, painted sets, and Boston and Midwestern chairs have changed the labor. The sitting room of 1880 may still hold a grandfather’s sack-back in the corner while the parlor holds a seven-piece walnut set from Grand Rapids. The living room of 1915, Tyron’s parlorless nation, is happy to inherit the Windsor. It looks honest. It sits. It does not demand a call.

Stickley’s Craftsman living room will want an oak settle, not a hoop-back. That is a different rhyme. The settle essay takes it. The Windsor that survives into the bungalow is often the painted farmhouse chair, not the Philadelphia comb-back. Dealers will later reverse the prestige. Museums already have.

## How to look at the chair in the room you have

Turn it over. Through-tenoned legs with wedges are the right religion. A seat that is a flat board with legs doweled from below and no saddle is a later cheap chair. Look at the bow: one piece, or a repair with a scarf? Look at the paint in the spindle holes and under the seat. If the only color is a 1920 varnish, someone has been “honest” in the Revival sense.

Sit. A comb-back should catch your head. A sack-back should catch your shoulders. A continuous-arm should feel like one gesture. If the chair is at a coffee table, you are in a living room using sitting-room furniture. That is allowed. It is the job the form always had — move, sit, take wear — now performed under a name Wilson and Tyron preferred.

Do not call every spindle chair a Windsor. Do not call a Hitchcock a Philadelphia sack-back. Do not hang a romance on a *Mayflower* passenger. Evans’s checklist is 2,400 craftsmen with dates and towns. If your chair has a brand, start there. If it has only paint and a good saddle, that is already a history. The living room that wants a Windsor is asking for a chair that was never too good to use. That request is the opposite of the parlor suite. It is why the chair belongs in this series.
