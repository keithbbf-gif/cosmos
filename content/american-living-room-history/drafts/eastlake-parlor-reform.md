# Eastlake parlor furniture

Charles Locke Eastlake’s *Hints on Household Taste in Furniture, Upholstery, and Other Details* was assembled from London magazine pieces and published in 1868. The first American edition appeared in Boston in 1872. Buffalo architectural historians, working from the reprint history, note six American editions within eleven years. Eastlake was an architect and a critic, not a factory. He did not stamp a chair in Grand Rapids. American factories stamped his name on chairs he would not have signed. That gap is the subject. The stuffed parlor of rosewood and bounce is the thing the book was written against. The ebonized, incised parlor set that still turns up at estate sales is the thing America made instead.

Eastlake wanted furniture whose structure you could see: rectilinear frames, honest joints, ornament that was incised or turned rather than applied as a plaster of grapes. He liked oak and cherry, and rosewood or walnut if the varnish did not hide the grain. He disliked the curved, stuffed, French-revival suite that Downing had already admitted was the ladies’ favorite in 1850. American readers took the plates and the moral and ordered a suite from a catalog that said “Eastlake” on the leaf. The suite often had more brackets, more gilt in the incised lines, and more upholstery than the book’s argument allowed. Eastlake later disclaimed responsibility for commercial “Eastlake” furniture. The disclaimer did not unsell the chairs.

## The book and the shop

*Hints* is a house book: wallpaper, curtains, pottery, jewelry, and furniture. The furniture drawings are angular, notched, deliberately against Louis-the-whatever curves. Bruce J. Talbert’s Gothic Reform drawings sit in the same English air. American shops that read both men could produce what the trade called Modern Gothic: revealed construction, strap hinges, incised gilt, a tile. Kimbel & Cabus of New York — Anthony Kimbel and Joseph Cabus, partnership from 1862 until 1882 — became the leading American interpreters. Their ebonized parlor furniture, cherry stained black, incised and gilt, inlaid tiles, won notice at the 1876 Centennial Exhibition in Philadelphia. A rare photographic trade catalog of their Modern Gothic line survives at the Cooper Hewitt library. The Smithsonian’s note is the right inventory of traits: ebonized wood, incised gilt, medieval pattern, strap-like hinges, forms that show the structure.

That is Eastlake as a serious New York shop could do it. Mitchell & Rammelsberg of Cincinnati made dining-room and hall pieces in a related language; those rooms belong to other series when the object is a sideboard. In the parlor the same factory language appears as chairs with turned spindles, incised crests, and a settee that is not a Belter sofa.

Grand Rapids and the other factory cities learned that an incised line is a machine line. Routers and engraving setups could put “Eastlake” ornament on walnut at a price a county seat could pay. The results are the platform rockers and parlor sets Fred Taylor has spent a career describing: rectilinear, notched, sometimes ebonized maple with gilt scrolling, sometimes black walnut with burl, a factory interpretation that Eastlake’s doctrine of simplicity did not survive. Japanese influence after the Centennial — fake bamboo, ebonized “Oriental” panels — got glued onto the same frames. Eastlake had not asked for a pagoda on a rocker. The rocker sold.

## Ebonized and incised

Ebonizing is a stain and a finish, not a wood. Cherry, maple, and sometimes walnut go black to recall lacquer and to make gilt lines flash. The incised line is cut into the rail, the stile, the crest, then filled or left as a cut. Geometric bands, sunflowers, a little Gothic capital. Compared with Belter’s piercing, the work is shallow. Compared with a plain Stickley stretcher, it is busy. It is reform that still wants to be seen from the doorway.

Upholstery on an Eastlake parlor chair is often flatter than the coil-sprung balloon of 1855. Some chairs are wood seats with a pad. Some keep springs and a stuffed back but square the outline. The ladies chair and gentleman’s chair persist in factory sets — the diagram is older than the reform — but the arms may be turned spindles instead of padded scrolls. A platform rocker in this taste is a parlor chair that moves, a mechanized cousin of the easy chair, and it is everywhere in the 1880s photographs. The mechanism is a factory product. The moral is still Eastlake’s: less grape, more line.

Parlor tables go rectangular. Lower shelves. Incised aprons. Marble appears less often than on the Rococo center table; wood tops with a cloth, or a square of marble on a very architectural base, remain. The center-table essay owns the emblem. Here the emblem gets a rectilinear stand and, sometimes, loses the cloth because the wood is the point.

Whatnots and étagères in ebonized cherry — Bancroft and Dyer at the Currier, Kimbel & Sons at the Met (1991.230) — are the display furniture of the same years. The Aesthetic parlor is a little museum with better geometry than 1855 and just as many objects. Reform of structure is not the same as reform of appetite.

## Reform of the stuffed parlor

*House Beautiful* in 1898 called the old drawing room an “old gold-and-brocaded over-decorated horror of yesterday.” That sentence is Arts and Crafts talking, a generation after Eastlake’s first edition, and it is aimed at the stuffed parlor this series has been describing: rosewood, bounce, étagère, the face of the house. Eastlake is an English preface to that American complaint. He is not William Morris, and he is not Gustav Stickley. He is the critic who made it respectable to say the suite was bad taste.

The living room, as a name, is just over the horizon. Wilson’s *Bungalow Book* is 1910. Tyron’s parlorless nation is 1915. Eastlake parlors of 1875–1890 are still parlors: callers, a best room, a match set. What has changed is the carving budget and the moral language. A household that bought ebonized incised chairs could believe it had reformed. The chairs might still be too many and too formal for a Wednesday. Clark’s family home is the book that keeps that argument from becoming a style-sheet.

Labor in the Eastlake factory is machine labor plus hand finishing. Incising can be cut by a skilled hand or by a setup that repeats. Ebonizing is finishing-room work, bad for lungs if the shop is careless. Upholstery is still the upholsterer’s bench. The reform did not return furniture to a medieval guild. It gave American factories a new ornament vocabulary after rosewood lamination had peaked.

Women still furnished the parlor. Eastlake’s reader, in England and in America, is often the person who orders wallpaper and chairs. The book’s tone is instructive. It is not a shop manual. A lady who followed it might still have needed a husband to sign the factory check. The furniture does not record who paid.

## The set, the rocker, the joints

A factory “Eastlake” parlor set of about 1880 is still Fitzgerald’s kit in outline: sofa or settee, gentleman’s chair, ladies chair, four small chairs. The sofa’s back is a rectangle or a triple panel, not a pierced rose. Arms are turned or bracketed. The crest is a row of incised stops and a sunflower if the year is Aesthetic. Casters remain. You can still clear the floor for a dance. The reform did not take the wheels off. It took the grapes off.

The platform rocker is the chair that tells you the decade without a catalog. A platform — a low stand with springs or a patented linkage — lets the chair rock without chewing the carpet. Factories in the 1870s and 1880s issued these by the tens of thousands, many in the incised-and-ebonized taste. The sitter is in motion in the best room. That is a small crack in the parlor’s stillness. A Belter armchair does not rock. A platform rocker does, and it still has a carved or incised crest so the room does not have to admit it has become a sitting room.

Joints on a Kimbel & Cabus chair can be pegged and honest. Joints on a Grand Rapids chair of the same silhouette are often dowels and glue, the incised molding a separate strip. Look at a broken crest. If the “incising” is a glued-on ribbon of decorated wood, you are in the factory that read the book as ornament. If the line is cut into the stile and the tenon shows, you are closer to Talbert. Stretchers are turned, sometimes with a flattened central block for a line of gilt. Seat frames stay hardwood — oak, maple, ash — even when the show wood is ebonized cherry.

Upholstery shops had to learn a squarer pad. A balloon back hides sloppy webbing. A rectangular back shows every lump. Some Eastlake chairs give up the coil spring and go to a webbed seat with a loose cushion, which is closer to the English reform and closer to the Morris chair that will follow. Others keep eight springs in a square box and only change the show wood. Both left the factory on the same freight car. The buyer who wanted bounce could still have it. The buyer who wanted to believe the book could point at the incised line and ignore the springs.

Servants still dusted gilt lines. Ebonized work shows every scratch; the black is a stage for damage. A household that reformed the parlor’s carving did not reform the weekly labor until the objects themselves thinned out. When the living room arrives, it will prefer oak that hides a bruise. Eastlake black does not.

## How to look at “Eastlake”

If a tag says Eastlake, look for a rectilinear frame, incised lines, turned work, brackets, and either walnut or an ebonized finish. If the piece is a curved laminated rosewood sofa, the tag is wrong. If the piece is quartersawn oak with through-tenons and no incising, you are in Craftsman country and a later decade. If the piece is walnut with a pediment and burl lozenges and only a little incising, you may still be in Renaissance Revival. The line between late Renaissance Revival and factory Eastlake is a catalog line. Incising and ebonizing push you toward Eastlake. Architectural pediments without the black finish push you toward Renaissance.

Look at the joints. A revealed mortise or a peg is closer to the book. A dowelled factory frame with applied incised moldings is closer to Grand Rapids. Neither is a fraud. One is closer to Eastlake’s argument.

Look at the seat. A thin pad on a webbed frame is reform. A full coil-sprung balloon in an incised walnut frame is the stuffed parlor in a new coat. Both exist. The second sold because people still wanted bounce.

This page owns Eastlake parlor furniture: the book, the New York Modern Gothic shop, the factory afterlife, the ebonized line. It does not own the Stickley living room or the bungalow built-in. Those are later permissions to sit in the best room without performing a suite. Eastlake is the moment the suite was told to straighten up. It straightened the ornament first. The room’s name changed later.
