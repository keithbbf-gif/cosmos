# The Colonial Revival living room

Wallace Nutting attended the Philadelphia Centennial Exhibition in 1876, the year the “New England Kitchen” and other nostalgic tableaux taught a national crowd what early America was supposed to look like. He was a young man then, not yet the minister, photographer, and furniture manufacturer who would turn that lesson into a business. In 1917 he published a guidebook to American Windsor furniture. In 1918 Wallace Nutting, Inc., of Saugus, Massachusetts, issued *Wallace Nutting Windsors, correct Windsor furniture*, a forty-eight-page catalog with prices. “Those who know the pictures, want the chairs,” he wrote in the integrated-marketing sentence historians of his firm still quote. The pictures were hand-tinted photographs of staged interiors: painted floors, hooked rugs, a Windsor by the hearth, a woman in a cap who never lived in that room. The chairs were new. The living room they furnished was not a seventeenth-century room. It was a twentieth-century front room pretending to be a Pilgrim parlor. This series has already said that a 1690 parlor was a chamber with a bed. Colonial Revival invented a Pilgrim living room anyway.

The revival begins, for furniture history, at the 1876 fair and runs through the 1930s, with a long tail in suburban “Early American” after that. It overlaps Eastlake, Craftsman, and the bungalow. A household could own a Morris chair and a Nutting Windsor in the same year. The living room is the room that absorbed the romance: painted floorboards, a gateleg or a tavern table, Windsors, a wing chair, a highboy against a wall that never saw a highboy in 1700. Hunt-board stories and dealer Pilgrim Century labels belong in this light. They are later stories. Name them as later.

## What 1876 put on the floor

The Centennial’s colonial displays were not measured rooms. They were lessons in national origin, staged with spinning wheels and hearths for a country that had just finished a civil war and was filling its parlors with Renaissance walnut. The effect on shops was not immediate in every town. By the 1880s and 1890s, architects and clients were specifying “Colonial” halls and sitting rooms: white woodwork, a mantel copied from an eighteenth-century house, Windsor chairs that might be old and more often were not. Wallace Nutting is the name that made the living-room version a mail-order product.

Nutting collected antiques, photographed them, and used the photographs as both art and prototype. He measured. He made drawings. He put reproduction chairs into department stores and into his “Chain of Houses,” historic buildings you could enter for a twenty-five-cent admission and then want the furniture. Framingham, Massachusetts, later held the shop; the 1928 seventh-edition catalog, 46 Park Street, offered three to four hundred reproductions. The 1930 Supreme Edition illustrated a still larger line. In 1921 he published *Furniture of the Pilgrim Century*. In 1928, *Furniture Treasury*. In 1924 his collection went to the Wadsworth Atheneum in Hartford under an arrangement with J. P. Morgan Jr. The books taught two generations of collectors to see. The factory taught them to buy.

Windsor reproductions were the opening line because Windsors had been ordinary eighteenth-century sitting furniture — light, cheap relative to joined great chairs, stackable — and because they photograph as “early.” The Windsor essay in this series owns the eighteenth-century form. Here the chair is a 1918 product with a Nutting brand, or a Nichols & Stone, or an unsigned factory “Colonial.” Comb-backs, bow-backs, writing-arm chairs: the catalog offered the types the guidebook had just made intelligible. A living room of 1925 could put six around a gateleg and call the arrangement Pilgrim. No Pilgrim sat that way. A 1720 hall had a form (a bench), a great chair, and a table that might fold. It did not have a matched set of reproduction Windsors on a painted checkerboard floor.

## Painted floors and the stage

Nutting’s photographs, and the museum rooms that learned from them, like painted floors. Eighteenth-century houses sometimes painted floors; sometimes they left them bare; sometimes they laid floorcloths. Colonial Revival made the painted floor a style: solid color, spatter, a faux rug, a checkerboard in two paints. Hooked rugs then sit on the paint. The furniture reads against the color. A dark parlor suite looks wrong. A yellow Windsor looks right. The floor is doing what the étagère used to do — declaring the room’s taste — with less dusting.

White or off-white woodwork, a paneled chimney breast, printed wallpapers in “colonial” patterns that are 1920s patterns: the envelope is new. Into it go objects with mixed dates. A real eighteenth-century candlestand, a new gateleg, a wing chair whose upholstery is chintz from a department store. The wing-chair essay owns the medical and parlor history of the easy chair. In a Colonial Revival living room the wing chair is “Grandfather’s chair,” whether or not any grandfather sat in it. The hunt board, a Southern serving form that dealers inflated into a colonial essential, sometimes wanders into the living room as a hall table. Leave it to the dining series when it is a dining object. When it is in the living room it is usually a dealer’s idea of atmosphere.

The invented Pilgrim living room is the extreme. “Pilgrim Century” as a dealer and collector category — Robert F. Trent and others have spent careers separating the joined oak from the romance — stuffed 1680 chests and Carver chairs into rooms named living rooms, a word the seventeenth century did not use for a sitting space. Brewster and Carver are useful museum terms. They are dangerous sales terms. A spindle-backed great chair is not a *Mayflower* ticket. The hall-and-parlor essay already said this. Colonial Revival living rooms swallowed the romance whole because the photographs were good.

## Who made the chairs, who sat in them

Nutting’s factory employed shop labor in Massachusetts, not seventeenth-century joiners. The chairs are machine-turned and hand-finished, branded, sold with a story. Quality is real; so is the story. Other factories — Grand Rapids could make “Colonial” as easily as it had made “Eastlake” — filled the middle of the market. Department stores put a Windsor living-room group in the window next to a colonial-print sofa.

Women appear in Nutting’s photographs as costumed sitters, the “Pilgrim” wife at the hearth. Women in actual 1920s houses bought the chairs and chose the paint. The living room is still their charge, now with a different script: not Godey’s face, not Wilson’s cozy bungalow only, but a national origin story performed with seating. A Black middle-class household that bought Windsors in 1928 was buying the same catalog. The romance of Pilgrim New England did not include them. The furniture did not refuse their money.

Servants in a Colonial Revival house still cleaned. There is less piercing to dust than in a Belter parlor and more turned stretchers to catch a rag. Painted floors show every footprint. The look is simple. The maintenance is not always.

## Against the oak, against the stuff

Colonial Revival living rooms exist in argument with two neighbors. Against the Craftsman living room they put turned spindles and paint where Stickley put quartersawn flake and fume. Against the leftover Victorian parlor they put a gateleg where the marble center table was. Some houses did all three in thirty years: a Renaissance parlor in 1880, oak Mission in 1910, Windsors and a painted floor in 1928. The architecture might be a bungalow the whole time. Wilson’s plan can hold Nutting’s chairs. The hearth does not care whether the settle is oak or the chairs are hoop-backs.

By the 1930s *The Index of American Design* and the first generation of academic furniture history were already correcting Nutting’s more cheerful attributions. Winterthur, under Henry Francis du Pont, was building a better-documented version of the same appetite: period rooms with real objects, still arranged as living rooms and parlors that the objects’ first owners might not have recognized. The Colonial Revival living room is not a lie you throw out. It is a twentieth-century room with a seventeenth- and eighteenth-century accent. The accent can be good furniture. The room is still 1920.

## How to look at one

If the front room has painted floorboards, a hooked rug, Windsors, and a gateleg, you are in Colonial Revival whether the listing says 1740 or 1930. Look under the Windsor seat. A Nutting brand, a factory stencil, or an eighteenth-century saddle with tool marks: three different objects. Look at the floor. Paint in the corners and bare traffic lanes can be old paint. A perfect checkerboard is often a restoration of a revival, not of 1710.

Look at the hearth. A settle and a kettle may be props. A radiator under the Windsor is the truth of the house. Look at the wall. A highboy in a bungalow living room is Revival placement; a highboy’s original job was a chamber. If a bed is gone and a sofa is in, you are not in a hall-and-parlor house. You are in a living room that borrowed the hall-and-parlor’s furniture names.

Do not sneer at the invention. The invention seated people. It also erased the hoe in the Chesapeake parlor and the enslaved labor that moved early Southern furniture. Correction is part of looking. So is admitting that a 1918 Windsor is a well-made chair.

This page owns the Colonial Revival living room: 1876 to about 1940, Nutting, Windsor reproductions, painted floors, the Pilgrim living room as a later story. The Windsor essay owns the original form. The hall-and-parlor essay owns the two-room house. The living-room name-change essay owns Tyron and Wilson. If you find a painted floor and six hoop-backs around a table that would not have stood in a 1690 chamber, you are looking at Nutting’s success. The chairs are real. The Pilgrims are not coming to sit in them.
