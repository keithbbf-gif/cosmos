# The La-Z-Boy recliner

The Henry Ford keeps a wood-slat porch chair from 1928 that does not look like a throne. It looks like an Adirondack cousin that learned a trick. Lean back and the seat slides forward. The slats follow the body. The chair folds for winter. Object 2015.78.6 — digital image THF162922 — is cataloged as a Kna-Shoe Manufacturing Company Reclining Porch Chair, Monroe, Michigan, gift of La-Z-Boy, Incorporated. Height 38.5 inches, width 23.75, length 25.5. Cream and light blue paint over wood and metal. It is a seasonal object. That is the whole first problem.

Edward M. Knabusch and Edwin J. Shoemaker were cousins in Monroe. On March 24, 1927, they left steady work — Knabusch had been a woodworker at Weis Manufacturing; Shoemaker had been farming — and opened a shop in Knabusch’s father’s garage under the name Kna-Shoe Manufacturing Company. Knabusch sold. Shoemaker engineered. Monroe’s nickname was Floral City; they took that name when the shop outgrew the garage and borrowed to put a plant on US 24 north of town. Novelty pieces came first, including a telephone stand with a seat. Then the porch recliner. Lemelson’s account and the Monroe News both keep the orange-crate prototype: slats mocked up on crate wood, shaped for “nature’s way of relaxing.” The Henry Ford’s label is more cautious and better: loosely based on an Adirondack chair, the back reclines as the user leans.

A porch chair sells in warm months. A furniture buyer — company histories name Arthur Richardson; Lemelson says only “a customer” — asked them to upholster it for year-round use. They did. The indoor chair of 1929 hides the mechanism inside a frame that could pass, at a glance, for late-1920s overstuffed seating. The Henry Ford has that one too: Floral City Furniture Company La-Z-Boy Reclining Chair with Ottoman, 1929, object 2015.78.24, image THF162692. Chair 37 inches high, 27.5 wide, 28.5 deep; a separate ottoman, because the footrest was not yet built in. Dark and olive cloth, wood, metal. The living room could take it. The porch did not have to.

## The patent, the name, the plant

Monroe attorney Faust F. Crampton filed the reclining-chair application on January 24, 1929. United States Patent 1,789,337 issued January 20, 1931, inventors Edward M. Knabusch and Edwin J. Shoemaker, assigned to Floral City Furniture Company. The specification is a linkage problem. The seat moves horizontally relative to the frame. The back slides on a hinged back frame. Links keep clothes from dragging as the sitter changes angle. Crampton’s drawing includes a figure of a person reclined, feet on the floor. The Henry Ford holds a copy of that patent paper as object 2015.78.1.81 (image THF290342). You do not need the accession number to read the claim. You need it if you want to stop a dealer from calling every later rocker-recliner “the original patent chair.”

The chair needed a name. The cousins ran a contest. Surviving lists — Lemelson, the company’s 2002 seventy-fifth-anniversary release, Tom Adamich in the Monroe News — agree on Sit-N-Snooze and Slack Back (sometimes Slack-Back). The anniversary release adds Comfort Carrier. The winner was La-Z-Boy. The Henry Ford’s note on the 1929 upholstered chair says the cousins had settled on the name by 1929. Trademark histories put the mark in 1931. `[CITE NEEDED: the Patent and Trademark Office registration number and the exact contest winner’s name, which company copy repeats without a filed entry list.]` The hyphen is part of the object. Lazy Boy, without the carpenter’s joke, is a later mouth.

Floral City Furniture ran through the Depression as a store-and-factory. In 1941 the cousins separated the chair into the La-Z-Boy Chair Company. Pearl Harbor stopped recliner production; the plant took war work. In 1946 the chairs came back. The living room that received them was already changing jobs — radio cabinet against one wall, then a television console against the same wall. The recliner essay and the television essay in this series are the same floor plan seen from two objects. A parlor easy chair faces the fire and the company. A recliner faces the screen and the sitter’s own legs.

## Mechanism before upholstery

Do not start with the fabric. Start with the links. Early chairs hide a wood-and-metal geometry descendant of the 1928 slats. The sitter’s weight is the motor. There is no electric motor in this era. Later handles and levers lock a footrest. The Ottomatic — La-Z-Boy’s name for the built-in adjustable ottoman — is the postwar change that erased the separate footstool. The Henry Ford calls it one of the important innovations after the war and does not print a year on the label; treat the year as unpinned if you need a single digit. `[CITE NEEDED: first advertised year of the Ottomatic footrest.]` What the object does is clear. The chair now occupies one footprint and fully reclines. A small living room can keep the path to the kitchen.

The Reclina-Rocker of 1961 is the chair that made the living-room type. Platform rocker plus recline plus the built-in footrest, a lever to lock the ottoman while the sitter rocks. The Henry Ford’s sales-model example (image THF162404) traveled with salesman William M. Clary of Atlanta. The paper tag on the bottom lists patents, and the first number on that tag is 1,789,337. Lemelson repeats the company’s claim that this model lifted sales from $1.1 million to $52.7 million between 1961 and 1971. That is a corporate figure, not a ledger you can audit from the sofa. What you can see is the type: a large upholstered body, a handle, a chair that does not match the sofa and does not apologize.

Upholstery is factory work. Hardwood or plywood frames, sinuous or coil springs, foam that replaces the horsehair a parlor shop would have known. Scotchgard appears in later advertising as a stain story. The Washington Post’s 1998 obituary of Shoemaker quotes a 1963 line: “The king of the house should have a throne, here’s a Reclina-Rocker all your own!” The gendered chair is not an accident. A 1960s living room often assigned the recliner to the husband and left the sofa to everyone else. Servants are gone. The labor is now the sitter’s: pull the lever, face the set, do not get up when the commercial ends.

Competitors filled the same job — Barcalounger is the name a living room used when it did not say La-Z-Boy — but the Monroe plant owned the American word. In 1972 the company went public. This page stops at 1980, before leather motion sofas and motorized lift chairs rewrite the form again. The living-room fact is already complete: an adjustable chair that is not a Morris chair, not a wing chair, and not ashamed to be the most used seat in the house.

## What the recliner is not

It is not a Morris chair. The Morris essay in this series owns the spindle-sided recliner with loose cushions and a visible rod. Knabusch and Shoemaker hid the motion. A buyer in 1929 could take the chair for ordinary upholstery until someone sat and went back. That concealment is the American preference. Show the comfort; do not show the shop.

It is not a medical easy chair, though the old wing chair had been, in part, a sickroom object. The recliner’s alibi is leisure. Television gave it a job. Lynn Spigel’s numbers for set ownership — two-thirds of households by 1955 — are the demand curve under Monroe’s postwar shipping. A chair that points at a hearth can turn. A chair that points at a screen wants to stay pointed.

It is not Danish modern. A Wegner armchair and a Reclina-Rocker can share a 1962 ranch living room; they do not share a shop. One is a joint you can see. The other is a linkage you are not invited to see. Taste arguments that treat the recliner as a failure of design are arguing with the household. The household voted with the lever.

## How to look at the chair in the room

Find the patent tag or the paper label on the underside. Monroe, Michigan, is the town. Floral City Furniture is the early assignee. La-Z-Boy Chair Company is the later name. A porch slat chair with a sliding seat is the 1928 type; do not upholster a reproduction and call it the indoor original. A 1929 indoor chair still wants a separate ottoman. A postwar chair with a hidden footrest is already the living-room machine. A 1961 rocker-recliner will have a lock for the ottoman.

Look at the front rail and the arm noses. The living room wears those first, the same way a parlor sofa wears the front rail when it is allowed to be used. A vinyl or Naugahyde skin that has split at the flex points is not a moral failure. It is a map of the mechanism. If the chair has been dragged to face a black rectangle and the fireplace is a shelf of photographs, you are in the television hour. If it sits at an angle to both, someone in the house still admits conversation.

The Henry Ford has the porch chair. That is the object to stand in front of if you want the story without the logo. Monroe made a seasonal slat chair, a customer asked for cloth, Crampton filed on January 24, 1929, and a contest beat Sit-N-Snooze. Everything after that — Ottomatic, Reclina-Rocker, the chair that outlives the sitcom — is the living room agreeing that the best seat may recline.
