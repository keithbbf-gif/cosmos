# John Henry Belter and the laminated parlor

John Henry Belter’s 1860 advertisement in the *New York Times* told buyers what the shop wanted believed: goods “composed of from 7 to 21 thicknesses of veneers laid with the grain at right angles to each other,” lighter, more graceful, and “five hundred percent stronger” than furniture made in the ordinary manner, warranted to stand furnace heat or any climate. The Met’s slipper chair (51.79.9) is that claim in rosewood and ash, 44 1/4 inches high, grapes and vines pierced through a back that would split if it were a single board. Belter did not invent lamination or steam-bending. Early scholarship said he did; the American Wing has walked that back. He did take out patents on furniture-making methods, and the one that matters for parlor chairs is U.S. Patent 19,405, 23 February 1858: laminating and bending across two planes so a chair back can be dished, not merely bowed.

The furniture is New York work, 1840s into the mid-1860s. The rooms it stood in were everywhere the firm could ship. This page is the shop, the laminate, the competitor, and one honest museum splice. It is not a second history of the living-room name.

## The shop

Johann Heinrich Belter was born in 1804 in Hilter, Germany. He reached New York in 1833, was naturalized by 1844, and had his own cabinetmaking workshop in lower Manhattan by then. In 1854 the firm incorporated as J. H. Belter & Co., showroom on Broadway — 547 Broadway from 1853 to 1861, the address on a Heppenheimer lithograph in the Museum of the City of New York (39.253.8) — and a steam-powered manufactory of two buildings at East 76th Street and Third Avenue. An 1858 R. G. Dun credit report said they made the best of furniture for a good class of customer. Clients are documented from New York to Chicago to Natchez. Belter died in 1863. His brothers-in-law, the Springmeyers, ran the shop two more years, then as Springmeyer Brothers until 1867.

Four patents, the Museum says, at least. The 1856 bedstead (U.S. 15,552, 19 August 1856) is a bed problem: assembly, bugs in recesses, thick posts wasting space. The 1858 furniture-method patent is the parlor problem. A patent model for that method is in the National Museum of American History. There was earlier work on machinery for sawing arabesque chair work; treat the 1847 date as shop history that needs the patent text in front of you before you quote the claims. `[CITE NEEDED: full claim language and number for Belter’s 1840s sawing-machinery patent.]`

Pieces are rarely labeled. Attribution is construction: number of veneers, rosewood show faces, the particular energy of the piercing, the way a crest is carved. That is why so much laminated rosewood was called Belter in the 1950s and 1960s that was not. J. & J.W. Meeks made parlor furniture in the same city, in the same taste, with fewer layers as a rule and a silhouette that can read more pointed than Belter’s rounded crests. The Chrysler Museum’s Meeks center table, about 1850, laminated rosewood, 31 by 45 by 29 inches (71.2293), was once attributed to Belter. The file has been corrected. A parlor history that says “Belter” every time the wood is layered is a dealer history.

## How the wood is made

Rosewood veneers, seven to twenty-one thicknesses in the firm’s own advertisement, grain reversed each layer, glue, heat, a mold. The laminate is then carved and pierced. Solid rosewood of that thinness and that height would fail. The laminate can take a sofa crest at 48 1/2 inches (Met sofa 57.130.1, 1850–60, rosewood, ash, pine, 89 3/4 inches long) and a floral piercing that is more air than wood. Secondary woods — ash, pine, chestnut, walnut — make seat frames and hidden blocks. Show surfaces are rosewood. Modern upholstery sits on old webbing more often than not.

Cabriole legs, C- and S-scrolls, cornucopias, roses, grapes, acorns. Castors on the feet so the suite can be moved. The Met’s assembled parlor suite — sofa, two armchairs, two side chairs, a tête-à-tête — is attributed to Belter or J. H. Belter & Co., gift of Mrs. Charles Reginald Leonard, 1957 (57.130.1, .2, .7 among them). The tête-à-tête, also called a confident, puts two sitters facing opposite ways for talk that need not be public. Smooth uncarved rosewood sets off the carved fruit. That form is parlor furniture, not dining furniture. A dining room of the same years wanted a sideboard. This series does not.

The Manney gift of 1980 brought a center table and console tables attributed to the firm (1980.510.1, .2): marble tops, dolphins on the center-table legs, lace-like aprons on the consoles. Marble is the weight the laminate was sold as being able to carry. The advertisement’s “furnace heat” is New York steam heat, which splits ordinary furniture. The warranty is a climate claim.

Carvers in the shop were not all named Belter. A manufactory with steam power is a factory with a famous proprietor. German-trained labor is the usual story for this shop and for its neighbors; the payrolls that would let you name the carvers are thin. Do not invent a master carver. The piercing is team work.

## Meeks

Joseph Meeks had been making furniture in New York since the late 1790s. The firm’s third phase, J. and J.W. Meeks (John and Joseph W.), 1836–1859, is the Rococo parlor phase. The house had been selling what the public wanted for two generations before Belter’s naturalization. Laminated rosewood parlor suites, including patterns dealers now call “Stanton Hall” after the Natchez house that holds related furniture, came from that shop. Meeks pieces typically show fewer laminate layers than Belter’s advertised twenty-one. The carving can be excellent. The name on the market brings less money than Belter, which is a market fact and not a quality scale.

Misattribution ran both ways and mostly toward Belter, because Belter had the patent story and the later fame. A responsible label now says Meeks when the construction and the documented patterns say Meeks. This page will not assign a suite to Belter because the roses look energetic. If a museum file says attributed, write attributed.

Hunzinger is another German New York name of the same decades, more often chairs with patent mechanisms than full laminated parlor suites. He is a neighbor in the city, not a twin of Belter’s suite.

## The Whittemore splice

The Richard and Gloria Manney John Henry Belter Rococo Revival Parlor at the Met is a room that has already been edited. The woodwork — columnar screen, windows, doorway, doors, cornice, rosette — came from the double parlor of a Classical Revival villa built about 1850 in Astoria, Queens, for Horace Whittemore (1813–1871), a wholesale fur and hat merchant. Josiah Whiney is the builder the Museum believes designed and executed the house. Whittemore lived there with his wife, Catherine, five children, and as many as six Irish-born servants. Census names include John Delaney, Elizabeth Flynn, Ellen Manning, Sarah Templeton, Catherine McQuade, Ellen Flood, and others. Before he built in Astoria, circuit records in Saint Louis show he emancipated a thirty-two-year-old woman named Letty and her children in 1848. The parlor’s brass and porcelain bell-pull knobs, near the mantel in the Museum’s installation, are the hardware of that labor system.

The Whittemore furniture is not known to survive. No detailed descriptions have been found. Curators chose a Belter-attributed rosewood suite as the type a man of Whittemore’s means might have owned. The house was demolished in 1965. Sirio Molteni and Rita Molteni Pooler, of the last family to live there, gave the parlor architecture to the Museum. The room opened in the renovated American Wing in 1983. The Museum widened the room slightly, removed a window, and added a chimney breast and a carved white marble mantel from an 1853 house in North Attleborough, Massachusetts (64.36.1a–c), because photographs of the Astoria parlors at demolition showed no fireplaces, while a 1920s plan and period practice argued for one. Paint analysis put the original woodwork in a white semi-gloss zinc oxide over lead primer. The wallpaper, the brocatelle, the carpet are curatorial assemblies from other houses and other documents.

That is a splice: Astoria woodwork, Belter-attributed seating, a Massachusetts mantel, a Virginia upholstery document, a British carpet. The series opener already used this room as the idea of a parlor. Here the room is evidence for Belter’s furniture standing in a New York parlor of the 1850s — as a type, not as Whittemore’s inventory. Downing, in 1850, admitted that “Modern French furniture, and especially that in the style of Louis Quatorze, stands much higher in general estimation in this country than any other,” favorite of ladies, even though Downing himself was not fond of it. The Met’s room follows his advice that the parlor should be lighter and gayer than any other room.

## How to look at laminated rosewood

Count layers at a break if a break exists. Seven to twenty-one is the firm’s range; Meeks often fewer. Look at whether the back is dished in two planes or only bowed. Look for a label; do not expect one. Look at casters, seat rails, and whether the piercing is crisp or later-cut. A “Belter” with a Grand Rapids seat frame is a marriage.

Sit only if invited. The chairs are receiving chairs, upright, carved crests at the height of a standing conversation. They are not living-room slouch. When this furniture enters a twentieth-century living room it is usually recovered in a fabric that would have looked poor in 1855, or it is put behind a rope. Abrasion on the front rail still tells you whether anyone was allowed to sit.

This page owns Belter’s parlor furniture and the New York laminated problem. Meeks is the competitor, not a footnote. The ladies chair and the center table own their forms whether or not the laminate is Belter’s. The Met room is a splice and should be read as one. If a dealer’s tag says Belter and the layers are three, you are in the 1960s confusion, not in Third Avenue’s steam shop.
