# Television as living-room furniture

In 1950, on a former potato field in Hempstead, Long Island, Levitt and Sons offered a new built-in. An Admiral television sat in the living-room wall of the Levittown house. Lynn Spigel opens *Make Room for TV* on that fixture, and on the speed of the purchase: between 1948 and 1955 nearly two-thirds of American families bought a set. By 1960 almost ninety percent of households had at least one receiver. The number is not a broadcasting statistic first. It is a furniture statistic. A new object, heavy, warm, and expensive, had to be given a wall, a height, a set of chairs, and a story about where the family would look.

The first successful story was that the television was furniture. Not a machine on a table. A cabinet. Blonde and mahogany consoles with doors, sometimes a radio and a phonograph in the same carcass, veneer matched to the rest of the living-room suite. The Henry Ford’s RCA Victor 7T143, about 1950, is the type in one object: television, AM/FM radio, 45 and 78 phonograph, mahogany and plywood and plastic, gold lettering for VERTICAL, HORIZONTAL, BRIGHTNESS, PICTURE. A “Golden Throat” certificate inside the door. The set is a sideboard that happens to light up. When the doors close, the living room can still pretend it is a parlor.

This page is about that object and about what happened when the object stopped pretending. It is not a history of programming. It is not advice to put a screen on a mantel. People did put screens on mantels. The reasons are historical. The results are a bad height, a dead fireplace, and a sofa that no longer knows which way to face.

## Blonde consoles and the furniture alibi

Postwar living rooms had already learned blonde. Bleached mahogany, limed oak, the light suites that sat against the ranch-house drywall. Television makers followed the finish. Hoffman’s Easy-Vision consoles of the mid-1950s — History San Jose holds a blonde example, about 1954, yellow-green tinted screen, turntable, AM/FM — are furniture first in the photograph. Round pulls. A long low box. The screen is a dark rectangle in a field of pale wood. Philco’s Predicta line, 1958 to 1960, begins to break the alibi: a tube that swivels, a pedestal like a pump, blonde or mahogany, the Holiday and the Pedestal and the Penthouse. Catherine Winkler and Richard Whipple’s Pedestal is patented as a design, D183,782. The living room could see the machine again.

Doors matter. A 1952 RCA console with mahogany doors lets the household hide the tube when company comes, the way a parlor closed the best chairs away from daily dust. Combination units — TV, radio, phonograph — occupy the same mental slot the radio cabinet had held in the 1930s. The radio-cabinet essay in this series owns that earlier box. The television inherits its wall and its status as the expensive new thing that must look like it belongs next to a sofa.

Weight and heat are construction facts. Early chassis are full of tubes. The cabinet is plywood and veneer over a void that vents warm air. You do not slide a 1950 console the way you slide a later plastic portable. Two people move it. The floor takes a dent. Placement is a decision you live with for years. That is why the first generation of advice columns spends so much ink on *where*, not only on *what to watch*. Spigel reads those columns in *Better Homes and Gardens*, *Ladies’ Home Journal*, *House Beautiful*. The set is a family problem: who faces it, who controls the dial, whether children sit too close, whether the living room is still a room for talk.

*House Beautiful* of October 1954 printed “Rooms for a Lively Life,” including a small television room with seating aimed at the set. Maynard Parker photographed it. Sam Maloof is credited as furniture designer on that Huntington record — a reminder that studio chairs and factory consoles could share a published room. The living room was already splitting. Some households gave the tube its own alcove. Most put it in the only front room they had.

## The box that ate the mantel

Before 1948 the fireplace organized the living-room furniture. The hall-and-parlor house had put the settle at the fire. The bungalow had put the inglenook and the built-in bench there. The ranch house still drew a hearth wall, brick or stone, a raised slab, a copper hood. Chairs faced that wall because heat and ceremony agreed. The television needed the same agreement. It needed a wall the chairs already knew how to face. The mantel was the candidate.

People put the set on the hearth wall for reasons that are not stupid. The fireplace was already the visual center. The sofa already pointed that way. The mantel was a shelf at about the height a small early tube could sit — or, later, the height a household thought a screen *should* sit, because pictures hang high and the mantel is where pictures go. When the fire went out for the season, or when a gas log made the fireplace a decoration, the shelf looked empty. Cable boxes and VCRs in the 1970s and 1980s wanted a surface. The mantel was a surface. A later flat screen could be hung on the chimney breast the way a portrait had been hung. The household was continuing a layout, not inventing a folly.

The folly is the viewing geometry. A mantel is too high for a seated eye if the sofa is at normal living-room distance. Necks tip. The fire, if it is ever lit, sends heat and soot at electronics and finishes. A tube or a later set on a narrow shelf is a falling hazard. The sofa, once it faces the chimney, turns its back on the rest of the room and on any second conversation. The fireplace becomes a pedestal. This series will not tell you to repeat the arrangement. It will tell you why the arrangement felt inevitable: the living room had only one ceremonial wall, and the new object demanded ceremony.

By the late 1960s the furniture alibi was wearing thin. Portables sat on carts and on the new low tables. The coffee-table essay owns the table; here the table is only a landing. Wall units and “entertainment centers” of the 1970s and 1980s — particleboard towers, glass doors, a slot for the VCR — are the console’s grandchildren, less veneer, more storage. The screen grows. The doors go away. By 1990 a large box can dominate a wall the way a Belter sofa once dominated a parlor, without any of Belter’s carving to justify the bulk. The living room has a new center of gravity, and it is not a hearth.

## Who sits where

Specialization returns in a new key. The family room essay in this series owns the basement and the den. The living room, if the house still has both, sometimes keeps the better sofa and loses the set — a late parlor, unused except on holidays. More often the set stays in the living room and the better sofa learns to slouch. Recliners point at the tube. The La-Z-Boy essay is the chair half of this sentence. Children sit on the floor, then on the pit group. The pit-group essay is the floor half.

Labor is the household’s. Someone dusts the vents. Someone works the rabbit ears, then the rooftop antenna, then the cable box. Someone decides whether the doors stay closed when guests arrive. In the early years that someone is often the woman of the house, whom Spigel finds in the magazines negotiating the machine during the day’s work. The living room is her workplace and the children’s theater. The furniture has to serve both.

Factory labor is Camden and Chicago and the other chassis towns. Cabinet shops veneer the boxes. A living-room history that names only the program is skipping the plywood.

## How to look at the wall you have

Stand in the door and see what the sofa faces. If it faces a chimney and the chimney holds a screen, you are looking at a transferred hearth. Measure the height from the seated eye to the center of the picture. If you have to tip your head, the mantel won the argument and the neck lost. If the fireplace is intact and cold, with photographs on the shelf and a sofa turned away from it, you are in the later hour this series calls the dead fireplace — the fireplace-layout essay takes that room.

A blonde console with doors is 1950s furniture. Name the maker on the back label — RCA, Philco, Hoffman, Admiral — and the wood is likely veneer over plywood, not a solid mahogany case. A Predicta is already a machine that wants to be seen. A 1970s tower of fake oak and smoked glass is storage pretending to be architecture. A set that still hides behind doors is a household that remembers the parlor. A set that sits on the mantel is a household that remembered the fireplace and forgot the sitting angle.

Do not put the television on the mantel to complete a “historic” look. The historic look was a console on the floor, doors closed, chairs in a shallow arc, the fire still a fire. The mantel placement is a later compromise. Understand it. Do not restore it as if it were a period rule.
