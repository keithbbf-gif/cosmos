# The fireplace organizes the living room

Stand in the door of a hall-and-parlor house and find the chimney. The furniture, if it is honest, will explain the brick. A settle or a great chair takes the heat. A table stays out of the path from yard to stair. Beds in the parlor-chamber keep to the inner wall. There is no sofa. There is no living room. There is a fire, and every object that wants to be warm has to negotiate with it. That negotiation is the oldest furniture plan this series has.

Three centuries later the same chimney breast can sit behind a sofa, cold, a television on the mantel, a great-room vault overhead. The fire no longer organizes. It is scenery. This page is the layout history between those two rooms. It will not tell you how to “style a hearth.” It will tell you where the chairs went, and why they left.

## Hall fire, parlor fire

The hall-and-parlor essay owns the seventeenth- and eighteenth-century plan. Repeat only the furniture fact: the larger fire is in the hall, the working room; the parlor-chamber may have the second fire and the better bed. Inventories mix hoes and weapons with chairs because the rooms mix jobs. A settle — high back, sometimes a box seat — is a hearth instrument. It faces the fire and keeps draft off the neck. Forms and joint stools move. A great chair for the head of the household claims the best heat. You do not float a seat in the middle of a 1700 hall. You would block the work and miss the warmth.

When the house specializes in the nineteenth century, the parlor fire becomes a face. Godey’s parlor is for visitors; Downing’s center table is the emblem of the family circle, but the fireplace is still the wall that ranks the chairs. Gentleman’s chair and lady’s chair take positions that can see the fire and the door. Castors appear so the suite can be rolled to the walls for a dance or a funeral — the death-room essay owns the second of those clearances. Heat is now partly a stove in another room, partly coal in a grate, partly a performance of warmth. The rosewood does not want to sit as close as the settle sat. Soot is an enemy of show wood. The layout loosens: chairs in a shallow arc, a fire screen, a fender. The fire still wins the wall.

Servants and, in Southern houses, enslaved workers carried fuel and ashes and the best chairs. A parlor fire that burns for callers is labor. When the living room absorbs the parlor, some of that labor becomes the household’s, and some of it becomes a gas log.

## Inglenook and bungalow hearth

Arts and Crafts put the fire back at the center on purpose. An inglenook is a recess with a fireplace and seating built into the recess — benches, often with backs, sometimes with storage under the lids. The word is older than the bungalow; the American living room meets it in shingle-style, Prairie, and Craftsman houses around 1900. Henry L. Wilson’s *Bungalow Book* (1910) writes the living room as the room you enter, no cold hall, no prim parlor, and then specifies the instruments of rest: “The open fireplace and low, broad mantel, a cozy nook or corner, or a broad window seat.” Ceiling beams, bookcases fitted into convenient places. Wilson is selling plans, not poetry. The furniture is built in because loose chairs would fail the photograph and the draft.

Stickley’s Craftsman living rooms and the Prairie inglenooks — the Prairie essay in this series owns Wright’s hearth — make the fire a masonry argument. Flanking bookcases, a settle or two facing each other across the slab, a table that is not Downing’s tall center table. You sit in the nook and the rest of the living room can do other work. The settle-bench essay owns the loose settle. The inglenook is the settle that became architecture.

Not every bungalow has a true nook. Many have a chimney breast, a mantel, two chairs, a sofa opposite. The plan is still fire-first. Mission oak and a leather cushion take the heat better than Belter’s laminate. The living room of 1915 that Tyron called parlorless is, at its best, a hearth room. At its worst it is a small fireplace and a suite that still wants to be a parlor.

## Ranch hearth, then the other wall

The ranch house pulls the living room long and low and puts a hearth on a stone or brick wall, sometimes a raised slab, sometimes a copper hood, sometimes a corner fireplace that tries to serve two seating groups at once. Clark’s family home is already suburbanizing. Postwar ranches add the television. For a decade the two ceremonial objects compete. Some plans put the fire on the patio wall and the tube on the interior wall. Some stack them. Some give the fire to the living room and the tube to the family room — the family-room essay owns that split.

Furniture follows the winner. A sofa opposite the hearth, chairs at 45 degrees, a coffee table in the gap: that is the ranch living room the catalogs sold. A pit group on the shag, facing the fire or the tube: that is the 1970s variant. A recliner at an angle that can see both: that is the household refusing to choose. The recliner essay and the television essay are the object histories. The layout history is simpler. There is only so much facing a room can do. When the screen won, the hearth became a nicety.

People put the television on the mantel because the mantel was already the shelf at the center of the facing. The television essay states why that is a bad height and a bad heat. This page states why it felt like obedience rather than revolt. The chairs already knew that wall.

## The dead fireplace behind the sofa

The great room of the 1980s–2000s often keeps a fireplace because elevations sell stone. Gas logs, a switch, a two-story veneer. The sofa floats in the middle of the volume, its back to the kitchen, its face to a media wall — or its back to the fireplace, if the only solid wall was the chimney wall and the screen took it. A fireplace behind a sofa is a fire no one can sit at without turning around. It is the opposite of the hall settle. It is also common. Walk a subdivision and count the hearths that heat the upholstery’s spine.

Some households reverse the sofa when guests come, a ghost of the castered parlor. Most do not. The dead fireplace collects photographs, a clock, a candle the catalog living room taught. Inglenook benches, if the builder applied the word to a pair of stock cabinets, have cushions no one uses. The ceremonial wall is now a texture.

Open plan makes a second failure: the fire that is in the room but not in the seating group, a corner unit aimed at no one’s knees. Heat, when the gas is on, stratifies in the vault. The furniture does not move closer because the rug is sized to the sofa, not to the slab. Wilson’s “cozy nook” cannot survive a twenty-foot ceiling. Susanka’s complaint about unused formal rooms has a hearth version: used great rooms with unused fires.

## How to read the wall you have

Stand in the main door of the sitting space and see what the primary seat faces. Fire only: you are in the old plan, or in a house that has kept it on purpose. Screen only: you are in the television hour. Fire below and screen above: you are in the stacked compromise, and the seated eye will tell you which object won. Sofa back to the fireplace: you are in the dead-hearth plan. Chairs that can turn — a pair of easy chairs on a diagonal — are the cheapest restoration of choice. A settle or a bench at a working fire is the oldest restoration.

Look at the floor. Hearth brick that stops under a sofa means the sofa invaded the fire’s territory. Casters on older chairs mean the household once cleared the room. Built-in benches with lids are inglenook memory even when the recess is shallow. A floating sofa with a console at its back is a great-room object; it will not warm at a fire without turning the plan inside out.

If you light the fire, move a chair. That is the whole lesson the hall already knew. A living room that cannot move a chair to the heat is a living room the fireplace no longer organizes. The furniture history in this series began at that heat. It can end with a cold chimney used as a shelf, so long as you can still see the difference.
