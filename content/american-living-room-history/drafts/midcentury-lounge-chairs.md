# Midcentury lounge chairs in America

On NBC’s *Home* in 1956, a new lounge chair and ottoman appeared as a pair: molded plywood shells, leather pads, a swiveling metal base. Charles and Ray Eames had wanted, they said, the warm look of a well-used first baseman’s mitt. Herman Miller, in Zeeland, Michigan, put the chair into production that year. It is still in production. The living room that could buy one in 1956 was not the living room that bought a Karpen mohair suite in 1926. The seat is a single person’s claim, like the Morris chair, but the wood is a veneer shell, not an oak arm, and the recline is in the shape, not in a rod.

This page stays in the living room. It will not take Saarinen’s pedestal dining chairs, a Knoll dining table, or the Eames molded-plywood dining side chair except as cousins. The objects here are lounge chairs: deep, low, meant for a lamp and a drink, facing a hearth, a window, or another chair. Three designs did the most to set the American type between 1946 and 1965 — the Womb, the Eames Lounge, and the Barcelona as an imported luxury that Knoll then made here. Other chairs sat in the same rooms. These three organized the wish.

## Womb, 1948

Florence Knoll asked Eero Saarinen for a chair she could sit in sideways, “like a basket full of pillows,” something she could curl in. She was tired, she later said, of the long, narrow lounge. Saarinen had already worked with Charles Eames on molded plywood, including the Organic Design in Home Furnishings prize at MoMA in 1941. For Knoll he went to a fiberglass shell, foam over the shell, and loose seat and back cushions. The chair was released in 1948 as Model 70. The nickname Womb stuck and became official. Knoll’s later story says Saarinen filed a patent for a “shaped chair.” Do not invent the number if the certificate is not on the desk. `[CITE NEEDED: U.S. patent number and issue date for Saarinen’s Womb / Model 70 shell.]`

The Womb is a living-room chair because of the posture it allows. You can sit across it. You can pull a leg up. A parlor lady’s chair did not permit that, and a Barcelona will not either. The steel rod base is a four-legged cradle, not a hidden box. Upholstery is a stretch fabric or a leather that shows the shell’s bowl. Herbert Matter’s photographs and Knoll ads put the chair in rooms with a low table and a lamp — living rooms, apartments, the occasional office lobby that wanted to look like a living room. A 1958 Coca-Cola campaign and a Norman Rockwell *Saturday Evening Post* cover later made the silhouette public even to households that would never buy Knoll.

Factory work is in two places: the shell, which is a plastics problem, and the cover, which is an upholstery problem. Knoll’s East Greenville, Pennsylvania, plant and its contractors had to keep the bowl consistent. A bad Womb is a shell that telegraphs a ridge through the foam. A good one disappears under the sit.

## Eames Lounge, 1956

The Eames Lounge (Herman Miller’s later shorthand is ELO) is three rosewood or walnut-faced plywood shells — head, back, seat — with leather cushions and an aluminum swivel base, plus an ottoman on a similar base. The 1956 *Home* broadcast is the launch most company histories cite. MoMA later acquired examples. The chair is assembled by hand in the sense that the shells and cushions are fitted; it is not a Morris shop chair. Herman Miller’s Zeeland production is a factory with a design office in California. Charles and Ray Eames had already given the company molded-plywood seating in 1946. The lounge is the luxury end of that research: thicker veneers, hide, a chair that reads as a club chair without the club’s skirted box.

Rosewood became the famous face and then a conservation problem as tropical hardwoods grew scarce. Walnut and other veneers are period-correct alternatives, not always later cheapening. The leather pads attach with zippers and laces; they can be replaced without throwing away the shells. That is the Morris chair’s loose-cushion idea in a modern material language. The swivel is the new motion. There is no rod. You turn to the lamp, the fire, or the person on the sofa.

Where it sat: a living room with a low table, often a walnut slab or a Noguchi glass, and a sofa that was no longer overstuffed to the floor. The Eames Lounge is a reader’s or a talker’s chair, usually one, sometimes a pair. It is a terrible sofa substitute. Households that bought only the chair and the ottoman were buying a rank seat — the new gentleman’s chair — in a room that otherwise tried to look equal. The ottoman is not optional in the original idea. Without it the rake is a different sit.

## Barcelona, imported, then Knoll

Ludwig Mies van der Rohe, with Lilly Reich, designed the Barcelona chair for the German Pavilion at the 1929 International Exposition in Barcelona. Two chairs with ottomans, chrome-plated flat-bar frames, ivory pigskin, horsehair, stood in the pavilion as monumental objects Mies did not want moved. Commercial production followed in the early 1930s (Bamberg, then a short Thonet period) and stopped with the European catastrophe. Mies came to the United States in 1938 to head the architecture program at IIT in Chicago. Florence Knoll had been his student. Knoll production of the chair in America begins in the late 1940s; Knoll’s own pages disagree with themselves on 1947 versus a formal grant of rights in 1953. Treitel-Gratz (Titlegratz in some spellings) made chairs in New York about 1945–47. Use a labeled example. Do not invent a single “American introduction” year.

In an American living room the Barcelona is a luxury import even when it is welded in Pennsylvania. The sit is upright compared with a Womb. The leather cushions are tufted squares. The X-frame is the show. Pairing two Barcelonas with a glass table in front of a fireplace is a 1950s–60s architect’s living-room diagram, closer to a Mies interior than to a ranch. The chair does not recline. It does not forgive a slump. It photographs as modern even when no one has sat in it since Sunday. That is its parlor quality. It is still a living-room chair, not a dining chair. The dining-room series can take the Brno if it must.

Pigskin and later cowhide, foam replacing horsehair, stainless replacing chrome in the 1960s — those are production changes. The living-room fact is price and posture. A household that owns a Barcelona and a recliner owns two ideas of lounge. They rarely sit in the same corner.

## Other chairs in the same rooms

Harry Bertoia’s Diamond chair (Knoll) is a lounge if you add a pad and treat it as a living-room seat; it is also a dining and a lobby chair. Jens Risom’s early Knoll webbing sits closer to wartime. George Nelson’s coconut and marshamallow pieces for Herman Miller are living-room objects with a sense of humor the Barcelona refuses. The Danish chairs that fill American rooms after 1950 — Wegner, Finn Juhl, the imports the next essay will take — share the lounge job at a different price. This page does not steal that slug. It only notes that an American living room of 1960 could mix an Eames Lounge with a Danish teak sofa and a factory coffee table and think of the mix as one style.

The Morris chair is the ancestor in function: a single deep seat for reading. The 1920s club chair is the ancestor in volume. Midcentury lounge chairs show their structure again — shell, strap, X — after the overstuffed decade hid it. That showing is the Craftsman lesson returned in fiberglass and chrome.

Labor is factory labor in Michigan and Pennsylvania, plus the California studio that designed the Eames. Upholsterers still sew the leather. Finishers still rub the walnut. The romantic story is two designers and a mitt. The invoice is Herman Miller or Knoll.

## How to look at a lounge chair in the living room

Sit. If you can turn sideways and the chair still holds you, think Womb. If your head meets a separate rosewood or walnut shell and your feet want an ottoman, think Eames Lounge. If you sit up on tufted leather and see an X of steel, think Barcelona. If the arms are oak and a rod sets the back, you have walked back to 1904. If the chair swallows you in a hidden mechanism, you have walked forward to a recliner.

Look at the living room, not the catalog. A lounge chair that faces the sofa is in conversation. A lounge chair that faces the television has been demoted to a family-room job. A lounge chair that faces a picture window has the ranch problem. A pair of Barcelonas that face each other across a glass table with no other seat in the room is a show parlor in modern clothes. Abrasion on the front of the leather is the same tell as abrasion on a Victorian sofa rail. A living room uses the chair. A parlor photograph does not.

Do not put a Womb at a dining table in this series. Do not call every molded chair Eames. Do not call every X-frame Barcelona if the welts and the steel do not match a labeled Knoll or a documented prewar example. The American living room after 1946 learned these three silhouettes whether it owned them or not. The ownership was luxury. The lesson was that a lounge chair could be a piece of modern case-and-shell furniture and still do the Morris chair’s work: keep one person in the room, at an angle, with a book.
