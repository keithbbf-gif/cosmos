# The Empire sofa in America

One end is high and scrolled. The other is low, or it is only a bolster, or it is a second scroll that does not match the first. The seat is a long mahogany box with a deep rail. The arm, if the shop is working in the 1820s, may be a cornucopia: a carved horn of fruit and flowers that is also a place to put your elbow. The feet are carved paws, or dolphins, or splayed sabers with brass paw sockets. The upholstery is a mattress and bolsters, silk or haircloth, sometimes cane under a loose cushion. You do not sit on this object the way you sit on a Federal square sofa. You recline, or you pretend you might. Jacques-Louis David painted Juliette Récamier on a similar long chair in 1800. American shops, reading Sheraton’s *Cabinet Dictionary* (1803) and French plates after Percier, Fontaine, and Pierre de La Mésangère, called it a Grecian couch more often than they called it a recamier. The form is the subject. Duncan Phyfe’s shop made versions. Charles-Honoré Lannuier’s shop made versions. So did anonymous men in New York, Philadelphia, and Baltimore. This page owns the form. The next page owns Phyfe’s parlor seating as a shop’s output.

## What the object is

A Grecian couch is a recumbent seat with an unequal silhouette. A double-end sofa of the same years can look almost Federal until you notice the rail has thickened, the legs have turned to cornucopias or animal feet, and the crest has become a carved tablet. The recamier in later dealer language is the single-end couch. Period bills are sloppier. Read the shape, not the tag.

Woods: mahogany first, often as veneer over pine, ash, or tulip poplar so a wide rail can show a single flaming sheet. Rosewood appears as the taste darkens. Gilded brass — ormolu in the French sentence, stamped brass in the New York one — marks terminals and feet. Gilded gesso on carved wood stands in for bronze when the shop cannot or will not mount French metal. Cane shows on some New York settees; Montgomery published a Winterthur cane sofa (his cat. no. 278) in the Federal volume that already leans toward this weight. The Diplomatic Reception Rooms’ caned Grecian sofa, attributed to Phyfe’s orbit, is the type with a warning attached: too many sofas with cornucopia, thunderbolt, bowknot, and acanthus have been given to Phyfe. Attribution is not the form.

Joints are frame joints: mortise-and-tenon seat rails, dowels in the scrolls, veneer that will later lift at the edges if a radiator sits under the rail. The carved cornucopia arm is often laminated or built up, then carved through the layers. Saber legs continue a Federal turning and then flatten into a heavier section. Paw feet are carved from the solid or applied. Castors hide in the paw or in a brass cup. You roll the couch for cleaning and for the rare rearrangement. You do not stack it. You do not take it to the yard.

Upholstery is a specialist’s job. A hair mattress on webbing, stitched edges, bolsters that complete the scroll. Yellow silk is a museum favorite because some period accounts name yellow. Black horsehair is the practical parlor cloth: slick, dark, a little hostile, the same finish the Victorian seven-piece set will still be using. A cane couch without its cushion is an incomplete sentence.

## New York, Philadelphia, Baltimore

New York is the heavy city. Currier Museum’s Classical Grecian couch, thought to be New York of the 1820s, is the type described without a forced Phyfe stamp: sinuous arms and crest, a straight heavy seat rail, mahogany veneer allowed to do the talking, feet that combine lion paws with eagles turning back to hold swags. Gilt metal paterae at the terminals of the scrolls are a New York habit. The Currier text notes that New York couches tend to be more substantial than Baltimore or Philadelphia ones. That comparison is useful if you do not turn it into a contest. New York had the shops, the mahogany, and the clients who had seen French furniture or wanted to have seen it.

Lannuier (1779–1819) arrived from Paris in 1803, shop at 60 Broad Street, trained as an *ébéniste*. He sold Directory, then Consulate and Empire. Peter M. Kenny’s Met catalogue *Honoré Lannuier, Cabinetmaker from Paris* (1998) is the book. His sofas and couches use columns, caryatids, dolphins, paw feet, gilt. He died in 1819. The form did not die with him. Phyfe’s workshop, larger and longer-lived, made Grecian couches and then the plainer Grecian Plain seating of the 1830s. Those objects belong to the Phyfe page when they are his or his shop’s. They belong here when they are the form: one high end, a cornucopia, a dolphin, a rail like a piece of architecture.

Philadelphia’s Empire seating is easier to lose among the city’s case furniture — Antoine-Gabriel Quervelle’s marble-topped tables get the textbooks. Parlor couches were made. They often read a little lighter than New York’s, a little closer to the Federal sofa, then suddenly a paw foot appears and you are in the 1820s. `[CITE NEEDED: a published, firmly attributed Philadelphia Grecian couch, 1815–35, with a shop name and a house history.]` Until that citation sits on the table, say Philadelphia made the form and New York signed more of the ones we can argue about.

Baltimore’s Empire parlor is painted as often as it is mahogany. The Finlay page owns the fancy chair and the landscape tablet. A Baltimore Grecian couch may be japanned, gilt, and caned, or it may be mahogany with a restraint the Currier comparison already named. The city sells south. A couch on a packet to Charleston or New Orleans is parlor furniture for a house this series will not turn into a dining room. Painted settees with three or four seats are cousins: more chair than recamier, same wall.

## Cornucopia, dolphin, paw — the carved vocabulary

The cornucopia arm is the tell a child can learn. It is a classical horn, fruit tumbling, sometimes wheat, sometimes flowers, the scroll of the recamier rewritten as abundance. It is also a structural arm: the horn’s curve is the place your elbow lands. When the carving is crisp and the veneer of the rail is quiet, the shop knew which surface was the drawing. When every inch is carved, you may have slipped toward a later Revival piece or a shop that sold by the pound.

Dolphin supports and ketos monsters appear on New York sofas. Chipstone’s Lannuier essay (2004) illustrates the type and warns against assuming Lannuier invented the sea-creature sofa; other New York makers used dolphins, and Lannuier’s documented dolphins in that article are on tables for Trinidad. Do not steal a table to decorate a couch. Note the motif, keep the form.

Paw feet are Roman and then Empire and then everywhere. Eagle feet are an American inflection. Brass animal-paw casters are hardware you can still buy, which is why a later replacement is common. Look at the pin and the wear, not the catalog.

Sheraton’s 1803 plates and La Mésangère’s *Collection de Meubles et Objets de Goût* (issued 1802–35, the 1820s numbers matter for Grecian Plain) are the paper. American shops do not copy plate-for-plate. They copy the silhouette and the permission to make the rail thick.

## Who lies on it, who stands beside it

A Grecian couch in a parlor is a display of leisure. The person on it is meant to be seen. The person who beats the cushion and oils the paw feet is not. In New York and Philadelphia houses of 1810–1840 that labor is hired; in slaveholding cities, including Baltimore, it may be enslaved. The couch does not fold. It does not become a bed in the honest sense, though a guest could sleep on it. The best bed has already left this room. The easy chair is upstairs with the invalid. The couch is the parlor’s idea of comfort: recumbent, dressed, temporary.

Phyfe and Lannuier sold south as well as to New York names. The Met’s Richmond Room, architecture of 1810–11, is staged with their furniture to make that Anglo-French point. The furniture is not original to William C. Williams. The staging is still a true sentence about the market: a Virginia parlor wanted New York seating, or a museum believes it did. This page will not turn that room into a Phyfe biography.

By 1840–45 the form is everywhere and thinner in the shops that cannot carve. Rosewood, heavier scrolls, the first hints of the Rococo Revival sofa that the seven-piece set will require. A factory in the 1850s can make a “Grecian” sofa that is only a silhouette. The carved cornucopia of 1825 is a different object.

## How to look at the couch

Walk the silhouette. One high end? Call it a recamier or a Grecian couch and then look at the feet. Two equal scrolls and a thick rail? Call it an Empire sofa. Cornucopia arms? You are in the 1820s–30s vocabulary even if a dealer says Federal. Visible delicate reeded legs and a thin rail? Walk back to the Federal page.

Look under the rail. Ash or pine with tool marks is right. A plywood web is a recover or a reproduction. Look at the veneer match on the rail: a single book-matched sheet is the New York luxury; pieced veneer is a later economy. Sit if the museum allows it, which it will not. The seat is deeper than a Federal sofa and less honest about being a chair. You slide. Haircloth helps you slide. That slickness is period.

Do not ask this object to be a dining bench. Do not ask it to be Phyfe unless Kenny and Brown, or an equal paper, will stand behind the sentence. The form is bigger than one shop. The living room that wants a recamier now is asking for a silhouette. The parlor that bought one in 1825 was asking for a classical body in the room where callers could see that someone in the house had time to lie down.
