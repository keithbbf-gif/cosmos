# The great room and the open plan

A production house of 1994 often greets you with a two-story volume the builder’s elevation labeled Great Room. Vaulted drywall, a ceiling fan on a long rod, a wall of windows facing the backyard, a kitchen island in the same air, a fireplace on the remaining solid wall or — just as often — a media niche. The sofa does not touch a wall. It floats on a rectangle of carpet or, later, of wide-plank wood, its back a low hedge between the sink and the television. Behind that back, a traffic path. In front, a coffee table too far from anyone’s knees. The parlor is gone. Sometimes the living room is gone too, its name retired to a small formal room off the foyer that no one sits in.

This page owns that builder room and the furniture problem it created. It does not own the Victorian parlor’s death — that is the first essay in the series — and it does not own the 1970s pit. The great room is later, larger, and more vertical. It asks one set of seating to serve kitchen talk, homework, guests, and the screen, in a volume designed to photograph as square footage.

## What a great room is

The phrase is realtor English before it is architectural English. By the late 1980s and through the 1990s, subdivision plans sold a combined family room, informal dining space, and kitchen as the heart of the house. The older ranch had already opened a pass-through or a half-wall. The great room knocks the rest down. Sightlines run from the front door, in the brasher plans, all the way to the patio slider. Sound does too. A blender is a living-room event.

Sarah Susanka’s *The Not So Big House* (Taunton, 1998) is the book that named the waste without rejecting the wish to live in one space. She had been driving through Des Moines subdivisions, looking at what she later called starter castles: unused formal living rooms, unused dining rooms, cathedral ceilings over furniture that could not furnish the air. The book was in a seventh printing by May 1999; *Life* put her on a cover; she went on *Oprah*. The argument is not “close the plan.” It is: stop paying for a parlor you will not sit in, and make the room you use every day worth sitting in. Builder practice heard the first half more clearly than the second. Formal living rooms shrank into unused “parlors” off the foyer, or vanished. Great rooms grew. The sofa still looked small.

Clifford Clark’s family-home history stops in 1960, but his plot remains the right one: Americans add rooms, then collapse them, then argue about comfort. The living room of 1910 collapsed parlor and sitting room. The great room of 1990 collapsed living room, family room, and kitchen. Each collapse is sold as honesty. Each one leaves a furniture type unemployed. The seven-piece parlor set was unemployed by 1915. The formal living-room sofa — matched chairs, a window behind, no television — was unemployed by 2000 in a great many new houses. It sat in the unused front room with the unused piano, or it was never purchased.

## The floating sofa

A parlor sofa has a wall. A bungalow sofa has a wall or an inglenook. A ranch sofa often has a low wall or a window. A great-room sofa has a back that must be finished, because people will walk behind it with a plate. That is a construction fact. The upholsterer now makes a sofa with a tailored back, not a back meant to hit plaster. Legs show all the way around. A console table gets pushed to the sofa’s spine to catch lamps and mail — a piece of furniture invented by the plan. The coffee-table essay owns the low table; here the table is stranded in a sea of flooring, scaled for the photograph, wrong for conversation.

Sectionals try to make a room inside the room. An L or a U pointed at the media wall is a pit group that stood up and lost the velvet. The pit-group essay is the ancestor. The great-room sectional is taller, often leather or microfiber, sometimes a recliner at one end. Motion sofas — the La-Z-Boy idea extended into a three-seat frame — appear because the volume is large enough to hide the mechanism and the household has given up on a separate den. Two sofas facing each other, the old parlor argument, almost never appear. There is no second wall to back the second sofa. There is a kitchen.

Fireplaces in this plan are optional scenery. A gas unit on a two-story stone veneer, or a corner stove, or nothing. The television takes the ceremonial job even when the fire is lit. Households mount the screen above the mantel because the great room has only one solid wall that photographs well. The television essay explains why that happened and why the neck pays. The fireplace-layout essay takes the longer history. In the great room the two objects stack: fire below, screen above, sofa floating in homage to both and comfortable for neither.

Scale is the other failure. A parlor sofa was sized to a twelve- or fourteen-foot room. A great-room sofa is sized to a photograph of a twenty-by-twenty-four volume and then looks like a rowboat. Builders sometimes dropped a carpet “island” on the slab to invent a room the walls had refused to make. The rug becomes the parlor. Chairs that sit with two legs on oak and two on wool are telling you the island is too small. Ottomans multiply because the coffee table cannot serve a U-shaped sectional. None of this is a new form. It is the old sofa asked to furnish air.

## Labor, noise, and the lost parlor

Open plan is a servantless plan that still wants a servant’s invisibility. Cooking, homework, and guests share air. Someone — still, in most households, the person who cooks — is on view. The Victorian parlor hid work. The great room displays it and calls the display togetherness. Children are in the room because there is nowhere else that counts. The family-room essay owns the older basement rec room; the great room pulls that function upstairs and removes the door.

Factory furniture fills the volume because the volume is cheap to drywall and expensive to furnish. Catalog sofas of the 1990s — the next essay — are scaled for this photograph: big roll arms, slipcovers that read as casual, a khaki that does not fight the oak cabinets. Builder-grade coffee tables arrive in boxes. The woods are often veneer over engineered cores. A floating sofa in cherry-stained maple is doing a social job, not a cabinetmaker’s job.

By the 2010s some buyers asked for walls again. Noise, cooking smell, the wish for a door during a video call. The plan did not universally reverse. Many houses kept the great room and added a “flex” room where the formal living room had been. The flex room is a parlor that is allowed to become an office. The sofa in the great room stays where the builder’s furniture sticker sheet put it: in the middle, facing the only wall that can hold a screen.

## How to look at the room you were sold

Stand at the island and look at the back of the sofa. If you can read a zipper, a rough deck, or a fabric that was never meant to be seen, the piece was bought for a wall and then floated. That is a mismatch between stock and plan. If the back is finished and a console table sits behind it, the household understood the plan. Measure the path. A great room that cannot take a person with a tray behind the sofa is a living room pretending to be a thoroughfare.

Look for the unused front room. A sofa no one sits on, a window treatment that is still the builder’s, a piano that is furniture — that is the parlor coming back as square-footage theater. Susanka’s complaint was this room. Do not call it a living room in the Wilson sense. Wilson’s bungalow living room was the room you were glad to turn to. The unused front room is a room you walk past.

If the only seating faces a stacked fireplace and television, you are in the late open plan, not in a bungalow. A pair of chairs that can turn toward the island restores a little of the old sitting-room liberty. A sofa that touches a wall, even a short wall, will feel older than the house. That feeling is information. The great room invented a lot of air. It did not invent a new way to sit. It borrowed the sofa, floated it, and hoped the conversation would fill the vault.
