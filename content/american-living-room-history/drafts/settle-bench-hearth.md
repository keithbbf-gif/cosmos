# Settle bench and hearth

Put your back to the planks and your shins to the coals. A New England settle of the late seventeenth or early eighteenth century is a bench with a high boarded or paneled back, arms if you are lucky, and often a box under the seat. The back is a wall. It keeps the draft that comes from the door and the unglazed or lightly glazed window off your neck. The front, on many pine examples, drops nearly to the floor so the fire has a room of its own. Luke Vincent Lockwood’s generation already knew the form as something between a chest and a chair. Wallace Nutting and the Colonial Revival living room later photographed it as “Pilgrim” furniture. The object in the hall-and-parlor house is simpler than the photograph: oak or pine, nailed or pinned, heavy, meant to be dragged a few feet when the fire is built up and left alone when it is not.

Gustav Stickley’s oak settle of 1901–1910 is not that object. It rhymes. It has a high back, it faces a hearth or an inglenook, it is called a settle in *The Craftsman*. It is a living-room bench from a Syracuse factory and a magazine, quartersawn oak, visible tenons, drop-in cushions. Treat the rhyme as a rhyme. This page owns the hearth bench first and the Craftsman cousin second, so a reader does not buy a 1910 settle and think it explains 1690.

## Chest, bench, wall

English furniture history likes to derive the settle from the chest: a coffer that grew a back and arms because people already sat on lids. American examples that still look like chests exist. A paneled oak piece found at Great Barrington, Massachusetts, published in the older colonial-furniture surveys as later seventeenth century, has a lifting seat, three panels above and below, and stiles that rise into arms. The carving matches chest vocabulary. The story that it spent time in a stable holding salt for cattle is dealer-adjacent color; the construction is the evidence. Handsome carved oak settles were rare in New England. What was common, the same surveys said, was pine: high back, front nearly to the floor, a screen against kitchen and hall wind. A candle shelf cut into the back appears on some family pieces. I have not stood in front of that Great Barrington object. I have stood in front of pine settles whose backs are a single wide board, nailed to stiles, the seat a lid over a well for boots or kindling.

Joints on the common pine settle are not cabinetmaker’s dovetails. They are nailed rabbets, pinned through-tenons if a joiner had time, butt joints that shrink and open. Oak paneled examples use mortise-and-tenon frames the way a wainscot chair or a chest does. The chest-settle’s lid needs cleats so a wide pine board does not cup. Iron strap hinges, or wooden pintles, or a lid that simply lifts off. A settle that does not store anything is still a wall. A settle that stores is a chest that refused to stay against the cold side of the room.

Hooded settles — a canopy over the head — are more an English and later Irish story than a common American one. Winged ends that cup the sitter appear. Do not invent a hooded American masterpiece to make the draft more picturesque. `[CITE NEEDED: a documented seventeenth- or eighteenth-century New England settle with an original hood, if one is published.]`

## Where it stands

Heat is the furniture plan. The hall-and-parlor essay said it: place the fire, then the settle or the great chair, then the table. The settle faces the hearth in the hall, which is the working room, or in a later kitchen that has inherited the hall’s job. It is not parlor-chamber furniture in the sense of the best bed. You do not hang silk on a settle. You may throw a loose cushion on the seat. You sit to warm, to mend, to watch a pot. In a house with one fire, the settle is the evening sofa. In a house that has already specialized a parlor, the settle stays with the work.

New England is the region that kept the form longest in ordinary houses, because New England kept the cold. Mid-Atlantic houses have them; Chesapeake houses have benches. The word *settle* in an inventory should be read next to *form* (a backless bench) and *great chair*. Claudia Kinmonth, writing on Irish furniture, notes that bench and settle were sometimes interchangeable, but a settle was generally understood to have arms. American appraisers are not always so careful. `[CITE NEEDED: a run of New England inventories, 1650–1750, that distinguishes settle, form, and bench by room.]`

Castors do not belong here. You drag a settle by an arm. Two people lift it if the box is empty. A later Victorian “settle” in oak with machine-carved panels and casters is a hall piece for umbrellas, not a hearth wall.

Labor: the joiner or the carpenter, not the upholsterer. Enslaved labor in New England is thinner in the documents than in the Chesapeake; hired and family labor move the settle and fill the box. A woman sewing on a settle is using it as a sitting-room chair. A man sleeping on it is using it as a bed the best-bed essay does not cover. Neither use makes it a sofa.

## The Craftsman rhyme, 1900–1915

Gustav Stickley, born in Wisconsin in 1858, German-immigrant household, shop with his brothers in Binghamton in 1884, own factory in Syracuse by 1899, then the Crouse stable across from Fayette Park, *The Craftsman* magazine, Eastwood workshops. He wanted what he called sturdy and primitive forms, against Victorian carving and against the parlor suite. The settle was a medieval word he could print. Thomas Guiler of Winterthur, quoted when Craftsman Farms lent an inglenook settle to the Onondaga Historical Association, defined the type Stickley meant: a large bench with high, solid back, arms, and wings, to seat three or four. Stickley placed them in entryways, halls, and living rooms — the new room-word — and in actual inglenooks after the fire at his Columbus Avenue house in 1901, a renovation later called an early Craftsman interior.

*The Craftsman* for August 1902 showed a settle in a living room designed to be “convenient and beautiful,” the simplicity meant to have a “soothing influence upon the brain.” A rare model no. 196, about 1902, Eastwood, is published in that issue and again when one of the two known examples was sold; I name the number because the catalogue did, not because every oak bench is a 196. Quartersawn white oak, through-tenons with exposed pins, a seat that takes a leather or canvas cushion. No chest under the seat. No need to stop a kitchen draft. The house has storm windows. The settle is an ethic.

Midwest Craftsman shops — Grand Rapids makers who picked up the Mission line, Charles Limbert in Michigan, the Stickley brothers in various combinations — ship settles to bungalows from Illinois to California. Henry L. Wilson’s living room of 1910 can take one. Lillian Hart Tyron’s parlorless nation of 1915 can take one. The seven-piece walnut set is what they are arguing with. The pine hearth settle of 1700 is what the copy is pretending to remember.

Do not collapse the two. The 1700 settle is a heating device with a bench attached. The 1905 settle is a living-room sofa that refuses springs and fringe. Woods differ: wide pine and local oak, then standardized quartersawn oak. Joints differ: nails and farm joinery, then advertised handcraft in a factory. The fire differs: open hearth that eats four-foot logs, then a Craftsman fireplace with a copper hood and a tiled face. Sitting differs: you perch and lean into the heat, or you lounge with a cushion and a magazine.

Colonial Revival complicates the rhyme. A 1910 living room may hold a Nutting-style pine settle *and* a Stickley oak settle and think they are the same American past. They are not. One is a revival of the draft-stopper. The other is an Arts and Crafts bench named for the draft-stopper. A dealer who calls both “early American” is selling a feeling.

## How to look at the bench by the fire

If the back is higher than your head and the front falls to the floor, look for soot bloom on the lower boards and a slight crook from heat. If the seat lifts, empty the box before you decide it is a reproduction: old wells collect the wrong century’s hardware. If the oak is quartersawn and the tenons are proud, you are after 1900. If there is a paper Craftsman label or a joiner’s compass mark, start there; do not invent one.

Stand the piece as the chimney requires. A settle parallel to a television is a living-room solution that abandons the form’s job. A settle facing a sealed fireplace is a rhyme with nothing to rhyme. In a New England house that still has an open hearth, the old plan still works: fire, settle, table that can move. A later southern shop asked to make a bench for a house that wants the old plan without the hoe is making a rhyme, not a 1690 object. Here the wood is pine, oak, and whatever the lot and the mill provided.

The living room inherits the hearth as a symbol. The settle is how you can tell whether the symbol still heats. If you need a cushion and a throw to stay on the bench, you are in Stickley’s room. If you need the back to keep from freezing, you are in the other one. Keep the names if you want. Keep the objects apart.
