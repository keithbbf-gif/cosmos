# Studio furniture in the living room

The Smithsonian American Art Museum’s Conoid Bench by George Nakashima is a seat before it is a sculpture. Black walnut slab, hickory spindles, 1977, object 1991.121, gift of Dr. and Mrs. Warren D. Brill: 31 1/8 by 84 1/2 by 35 5/8 inches. The right end of the seat keeps the tree’s free edge and does not take the backrest; twenty spindles rise into a crest that stops short, leaving a wide place to put a body or a tray. A butterfly key, in the type if not always on this example, would arrest a split without hiding it. The Renwick’s first exhibition, *Woodenworks*, in 1972, had already put Nakashima in a federal gallery. The bench is living-room furniture. You sit on the slab. You do not dine at it.

This page stays on seating. Nakashima’s Conoid tables are famous and they belong to another series if they belong here at all. Wharton Esherick’s chairs, Sam Maloof’s rockers, Wendell Castle’s laminated and fiberglass seats — those are the living-room objects. Studio furniture is a shop of one, or of a few, making pieces that a factory living room might own one of, the way a parlor once owned one Belter sofa among lesser chairs.

## New Hope, Paoli, Alta Loma, Rochester

George Nakashima (1905–1990) trained as an architect at the University of Washington and MIT. The war interned him at Minidoka in Idaho, where he worked wood with Gentaro Hikogawa. Released in 1943, he opened in New Hope, Pennsylvania. The philosophy he repeated is simple enough to misuse: keep the goodness of the tree. The shop practice is specific. Slabs are dried, read, and assigned. Butterfly keys of contrasting wood stabilize checks. Legs are shoulder-tenoned into the underside. The Conoid bench, introduced in 1960 to mark the Conoid Studio and a run of crotch logs, is designed board by board. The shop’s own range is roughly 72 to 96 inches long, 18 to 34 wide, seat height 14 inches. Toledo’s Conoid Bench with Back Rest describes the construction cleanly: walnut legs, free-edge plank, twenty-one hickory spindles, rosewood butterfly at a crotch split, end spindles through-tenoned and wedged at the crest. No two benches share a tree. Mira Nakashima’s *Nature, Form & Spirit* is the family monograph. The living-room fact is the low seat: guests sit in a line, conversation goes down the slab, a backrest that does not run the full length admits a sprawl at the wide end. It is a settle that learned the free edge.

Wharton Esherick (1887–1970) worked on a hillside in Paoli, Pennsylvania. The hammer-handle chairs are the living-room type this essay can pin. About 1938 he bought two barrels of ash and hickory hammer handles at a turning-shop auction — short carpenter’s handles and long smithing handles — and did not at first know the job. Hedgerow Theater needed chairs for the rehearsal room, the large living room of the house where the actors lived. Esherick made a run of related chairs, handles for legs, stretchers, rails, and backs, a shaped crest, rawhide or webbing seats. He traded thirty-six to Hedgerow for an apprenticeship for his daughter Ruth. The Museum of Arts and Design’s example, object 2000.13, is cataloged c. 1950, hickory with rawhide, 30 3/4 by 20 by 19 1/2 inches. Moderne’s Hedgerow example keeps the 1938 date and the Mansfield Bascom biography as the narrative source. The chair is a tool reused as a seat. It belongs in a living room that can take a rough honesty. It is not a dining chair that wandered in.

Sam Maloof (1916–2009) is the California rocker. Self-taught, a bandsaw man, walnut his usual wood, joints sculpted so the arm seems to grow from the post. The Smithsonian’s Rocker of 1980, object 1997.88, gift of Roger and Frances Kennedy, is 44 3/4 by 45 3/4 by 26 3/4 inches, walnut. The Museum of Fine Arts, Boston, describes the type: concave crest, seven slats, stiles that rise through the crest, band-sawn arms, laminated rockers, exposed pins, a seat carved to the body. Maloof refined rather than reinvented. Clients sat. The rocker is a living-room chair that moves without a La-Z-Boy linkage. You see every decision. *House Beautiful* had already used Maloof seating in a 1954 television room; the Huntington’s Parker photographs credit him on that published set. Studio furniture and the tube shared a magazine spread. The rocker outlasted the chassis.

Wendell Castle (1932–2018) took seating off the stick chair altogether. Stack lamination — boards glued into a mass, then carved — let him make a chair that is a single thickened gesture, no stretcher logic. The Molar chairs of 1969 are the other pole: gel-coated fiberglass, open edition, a tooth-shaped seat you do not mistake for a Windsor. Wright’s example of Molar chair II is 27 by 20 by 17 1/2 inches, signed and numbered. Castle taught, ran a school in Scottsville, New York, in the 1980s, and put furniture into MoMA and the Renwick. In a living room a Castle seat is a pause in the conversation. It does not make a suite. It makes an argument that the chair can be an object you walk around.

## One piece in a factory room

Studio furniture rarely furnished a whole American living room. The cost and the time forbade it. A household bought a Maloof rocker and sat it next to a catalog sofa. A Nakashima bench faced a factory pair of lounge chairs. That mix is the historical room. Dealers now stage pure studio interiors the way museums stage pure parlors. Both are edits.

*Woodenworks* (Renwick, 1972) is the public date when this seating entered the federal eye: Nakashima and others, wood as a craft claim. The American Craft Council and the later studio-furniture surveys — including the work of Edward S. Cooke Jr. — give the movement a bibliography. This essay does not need the whole movement. It needs the living-room sit: bench, rocker, side chair, lounge. Enslaved labor is not the labor here; the labor is the maker in the shop and, in Nakashima’s wartime beginning, the coerced camp workshop that taught a vocabulary. Name Minidoka. Do not sentimentalize it as a craft school.

Joints are the identification. Nakashima: butterfly, shoulder tenon, spindle tenon, sometimes a client name inscribed under a seat. Maloof: exposed pins, sculpted transitions, a branded or signed underside. Esherick: the handle’s shoulder used as a joint swelling, no two chairs quite alike. Castle: no traditional joint at all on a laminated chair; a mold seam on a Molar. A factory “Nakashima style” slab bench with a butterfly glued into a sound board is a souvenir. Look at the split. If there was no split, the butterfly is a decal.

These pieces do not knock down. A Conoid bench is a slab and a back; you move it with two people and a blanket, not a carton of legs. A Maloof rocker is one object; the laminated runners are not a mechanism you service. Castle’s fiberglass molar is light enough to slide and cold enough to need a cushion someone will lose. Shop labor is the maker and a small bench crew — Mira Nakashima’s continuation of the New Hope shop after 1990 is the living sequel, still seating, still slabs. Alta Loma, where Maloof worked, is a California house-shop: clients came, sat, and ordered a rocker the way a parlor client once ordered a suite, except they left with one chair. That single-chair economy is why studio furniture lives in mixed rooms. The household could afford a signature seat. It sat on factory foam for the rest of the evening.

## How to sit, and how to look

Sit on the bench before you photograph it. A Conoid at fourteen inches of seat height is lower than a parlor sofa. Old knees will tell you. A Maloof rocker that does not rock because it has been parked on a thick rug is a chair fighting the great-room floor. An Esherick handle chair with a replaced rawhide seat is an alteration; name the new hide. A Castle fiberglass molar with kitchen wear on the gel coat has been used as furniture, which is the right insult.

If the living room also holds a Danish armchair, you are in a house that bought modern more than once. Wegner’s joint and Nakashima’s slab are not the same religion. If the room holds a khaki slipcover and one rocker, you are in the 1990s catalog hour with a studio object used as the “collected” piece the photograph always implied. The catalog essay owns the rest of that room.

Do not turn the bench into a display table and then complain that studio furniture is unused. The permission this series tracks — sit on the best furniture — applies here. A slab that cannot take a body is a relic. Nakashima’s shop made a bench. The living room’s job is to wear the front edge.
