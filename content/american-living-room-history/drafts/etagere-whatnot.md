# The Victorian étagère and the whatnot

A photograph made about 1903 in the Arnold house at Rushville, Indiana, shows a five-tier whatnot against the wall. Spool-turned uprights. Doilies on every shelf. Cabinet cards, baskets, seashells, small figures. The photographer was John C. Arnold, an amateur with a camera in his own rooms. The furniture is not a masterpiece and does not need to be. It is the form that made a parlor look inhabited when no one was sitting: open shelves, four or five of them as a rule, sometimes six, holding the household’s portable proof that it had been somewhere and could afford to keep the evidence.

Étagère is the French trade word — a stand of shelves. Whatnot is the English and American word, already in use in Britain by the early nineteenth century for a light stand of open shelves, and common in American inventories and catalogs by mid-century. Dealers now use the two as if they were grades. They were not always. A New York rosewood piece with a mirrored back and marble shelves is an étagère in the French sense and a whatnot in the household sense. A Cincinnati factory stand with bobbin turnings is a whatnot that a clerk might also invoice as an etagere. Oscar P. Fitzgerald, *Three Centuries of American Furniture*, puts the form in the parlor kit beside the sofa and the match chairs. The historiography of the parlor — Godey’s “face of the house,” Clark’s middle-class claim to civilization, the seven-piece set — needs this piece because the set seats people and the étagère seats objects.

## Four or five shelves

Count the shelves before you count the carving. A typical parlor whatnot of 1850–1880 has four or five graduated tiers, the widest at the bottom, the smallest at the top, so the pile does not look top-heavy and so the best small object can stand where the eye meets it standing. Corner whatnots cut the footprint to a triangle and put the display in the dead space beside a chimney breast. Wall étagères stand flat against the plaster, sometimes with a drawer in the lowest case, sometimes with a mirrored back that doubles the porcelain and throws lamp light.

The Illinois State Museum holds a Rococo Revival walnut étagère made by Mitchell & Rammelsberg of Cincinnati, dated 1858–1880 in the file, transferred as 1984.64.3. The museum’s label is the right sentence: open shelves held “the prized mementoes and objects of art that decorated mid-Victorian homes,” and the form was “needed in every ‘proper’ parlor,” manufactured at many price levels. Mitchell & Rammelsberg had a St. Louis branch. The whatnot is a railroad object. It does not require a New York carver.

Bancroft and Dyer of Boston labeled an ebonized cherry étagère now at the Currier Museum, galleries and brackets and carved sunflowers, the Aesthetic Movement’s version of the same job. The Currier’s essay calls the étagère “perhaps the most emblematic form of its period” and the home’s “little museum.” By 1880 Americans collected coins, butterflies, exotic porcelains, and, already, colonial furniture. The parlor shelf is where that habit became furniture. A. Kimbel and Sons’ Chinese-inspired cherry étagère of about 1885–90 is at the Met (1991.230), 69 3/4 inches high, a paper label with style numbers on the back. Kimbel & Cabus had been the Modern Gothic firm; after 1882 the sons sold historical revival and the shelf form stayed.

Allison Lockwood Leopold, *Victorian Splendor* (1986), is often quoted for the wall étagère as “the primary means of displaying the family’s burgeoning collection of treasures.” The sentence is later than the furniture. It is still a fair description of the job.

## What the shelves held

Bibelots: the French diminutive that American writers used for small decorative objects. Souvenirs of travel — a Swiss carving, a bit of lava from Vesuvius, a transfer-printed cup from Niagara. Shells from a coast holiday or from a cabinet-maker’s supplier. Parian figures. Bohemian glass. A hair wreath under a glass dome if the family had had a death and the dome had not gone to the center table. Photographs in standing frames, then cabinet cards, then more photographs. The Arnold whatnot is already a photograph of photographs. The display is recursive.

The wife’s display is not a sneer. Period advice put the parlor in the lady’s charge. Downing wanted the drawing room lighter and more elegant than any other apartment. Catherine Whittemore, in the Astoria villa whose woodwork is now at the Met, would have been the person who decided what stood on the shelves, even if Irish-born servants dusted them. A husband’s office might hold a specimen cabinet. The parlor étagère is the household’s public cabinet, arranged for callers. Fitzgerald’s parlor historiography treats the match set as a social diagram of sitters. The whatnot is the diagram of things: we have been abroad, we read, we own a bit of the sea, we keep grandmother’s cup.

Do not invent a Grand Tour for a family that took the cars to Saratoga. A shell from Atlantic City and a shell from Palermo can look the same from the doorway. The furniture does not certify the voyage. If a diary or a label pins the object, say so. If it does not, call it a souvenir and stop.

Natural history belongs here. The Met’s Rococo Revival parlor essay notes parlor domes of taxidermy, hair, feathers, shells, wax, and wool, set on a mantel, a center table, an étagère, or a piano. The glass kept dust off and made a conversation piece. Ferns in Wardian cases are cousins. The whatnot is the cheaper, more vertical version of that appetite.

## Woods, turnings, and how it stands

High-style New York and Philadelphia étagères of the 1840s–60s are rosewood or mahogany, sometimes with marble shelves, pierced galleries, carved supports in the Rococo taste — grapes, roses, C-scrolls. A mirrored back is a luxury and a trick: it hides the wall and multiplies the goods. The frame is mortise-and-tenon, often with glued blocks at the shelf corners. The whole thing is tall and thin. It wants a wall and it wants not to be bumped by a hoop skirt. Casters appear on some; many stand on turned feet and stay put. This is not a dance piece. The center table rolls. The whatnot is scenery.

Factory whatnots of the 1860s–90s are walnut, cherry, or maple, spool-turned or bobbin-turned uprights, sawn brackets, a little incised line if the year is Eastlake. The shelves are thin boards with a bead on the front edge. The cheapest are shipped knocked down, uprights through the shelves, a nut under the bottom. `[CITE NEEDED: a dated Grand Rapids or Cincinnati catalog plate that prices a four-shelf whatnot and states knockdown hardware.]` Spool turning is lathe work a boy can learn. It is also why so many survive: there is not much carving to snap, and when a shelf splits you replace a board.

Corner whatnots use two faces of turnings and a diagonal back. They are the form that still turns up in farmhouse attics because they fit the leftover corner after the stove and the organ took the walls. Five shelves is a common count in photographs. Three is a plant stand pretending. Seven is a shop showing off.

Ebonized finishes arrive with the Aesthetic 1870s: cherry or maple stained black, gilt in the incised lines, a tile or two if the maker has been to the Centennial. That is the Eastlake essay’s wood. The form is older than the finish.

## Parlor historiography

The étagère is how the stuffed parlor got its reputation for clutter. A room with a seven-piece set, a center table, a piano, and a five-shelf whatnot groaning with china will photograph as excess. *House Beautiful* in 1898 called the old drawing room an “old gold-and-brocaded over-decorated horror of yesterday.” The horror is partly textile and partly this furniture: open storage that rewards accumulation. Arts and Crafts living rooms will build the books into the wall and call the whatnot a relic. Colonial Revival will put three pewter plates on a hanging shelf and claim simplicity. The Victorian household was not confused. It was using the form that magazines and factories had sold as necessary to a proper parlor.

Fitzgerald’s kit — armchair, lady’s chair, sofa, four parlor chairs — is seating. The étagère is the extra that made the seating look like a collection rather than a waiting room. Clark’s *American Family Home* follows the middle-class house as it adds rooms and arguments. The whatnot is one of those arguments in wood: we have things worth showing, and we have a room whose job is to show them. When the living room absorbs the parlor, the whatnot often moves to a hall or an upstairs sitting room, or it is broken up for kindling. Built-in bookcases in a bungalow are the same appetite with the dusting reduced.

Servants dusted every object. A five-shelf whatnot with forty objects is a weekly labor claim. When staff counts drop, the shelves look like work. That is one reason the form dies in the living room and survives in the antique shop.

## How to look at one

Stand to the side and count shelves. Four or five is the parlor norm. Look at the uprights: hand-carved Rococo, factory spools, ebonized incising. Look at the back: mirror, board, or open. Look at the shelf edges for doily tan-lines and wax drips. Look at the wall behind for the shadow of a taller piece. If the shelves are empty in a restored parlor, the room is lying about how it was used. If they are stuffed with gift-shop porcelain, the room is lying the other way.

A corner whatnot that still fits a chimney breast is in its old job. A five-tier stand used as a plant rack in a sunroom is a demotion that the casters, if any, will forgive. Do not call a modern etagere bookcase a Victorian whatnot because it has open shelves. The period object is thin, tall, and built for display, not for novels in rows.

This page owns the parlor étagère and the whatnot. The center table owns the horizontal display. The ladies’ chair owns the sitter. The bungalow essay owns the built-in that replaced both. If you find seashells and cabinet cards on five doilies, you are in Arnold’s Rushville, not in a museum’s idea of taste. That is the better evidence.
