# Stickley and the Craftsman living room

Samuel Howe visited Gustav Stickley’s house on Columbus Avenue in Syracuse and wrote “A Visit to the House of Mr. Stickley” for *The Craftsman* of December 1902. After a fire in 1901 Stickley had rebuilt the interior in the language he was selling: oak, settle, hearth, an inglenook. Winterthur holds what the Gustav Stickley House Foundation calls the only known period photograph of that dining room and inglenook. The settle in the nook is close to catalog form 196, with butterfly joints; it later went on loan from the Stickley Museum at Craftsman Farms to the Onondaga Historical Association. Thomas Guiler, speaking from Winterthur, defined the settle as a large bench with a high solid back, arms, and wings, medieval on purpose, three or four sitters. That settle is living-room furniture even when the photograph is of a dining inglenook. The dining-room series owns Stickley’s trestle tables — catalog 631 among them. This page stays on the room the magazine taught America to sit in: Morris chairs, settles, the nook, quartersawn oak.

Gustav Stickley was born in Wisconsin in 1858 to German immigrants. He worked with his brothers in Binghamton from 1884, opened his own Syracuse factory in 1899, and moved the work toward what he called sturdy and primitive forms after looking hard at English Arts and Crafts. United Crafts became the Craftsman Workshops at Eastwood, a Syracuse suburb. *The Craftsman* ran from October 1901 to December 1916. Catalogs from Eastwood — the January 1909 *Catalogue of Craftsman Furniture* is on the Winterthur library shelf — priced the living-room kit: armchair, Morris chair, settle, tabouret, a table that is not a dining trestle. Craftsman Farms, the log-house estate at Morris Plains, New Jersey, was his later stage set, begun about 1908, now the Stickley Museum. Eastwood made the furniture. The Farms tried to live the copy.

## Quartersawn oak

Quartersawn means the log is cut in quarters, then sawn so the blade runs roughly radially. The board shows medullary rays — the flake that collectors learned to want — and it moves less across the width than a flatsawn board. Stickley’s shops used white oak, fumed or stained to a range of browns, a finish that is a chemistry of ammonia and time as much as pigment. Riftsawn and flatsawn appear where the shop could hide them. The show surfaces want the flake.

Joints are the advertisement. Through-tenons with visible pins, keyed tenons, butterfly keys across a split or across two boards, chamfered posts. A Morris chair’s side is a frame you can read from the doorway. The cushion is leather, or a canvas that was meant to be leather. Weaving on some backs is a later taste or a different maker. Stickley’s better chairs are wood first, pad second.

He did not invent the Morris chair. The adjustable-back easy chair associated with William Morris’s firm is an English form of the 1860s; American factories had been making “Morris chairs” before Eastwood’s catalog made one a Craftsman badge. The Morris-chair essay in this series owns the mechanism: rod or peg, a back that drops, a man in shirtsleeves. Here the chair is a living-room citizen, the replacement for the gentleman’s parlor armchair. It has arms because a body wants arms when the chair’s job is ease, not a crinoline. There is no ladies Morris chair in the Victorian sense. There is a smaller armchair and a larger one. The diagram has changed.

## Settles and inglenooks

A settle at a hearth is an old form — this series already gave the seventeenth-century settle its page. Stickley put the form back in the living room as a sofa that does not bounce. High back, wings if the catalog says so, a seat that takes three, a depth that keeps a draft off. Catalog numbers move between editions; 173, 206, and 196 are among the living-room settles that dealers and museums still use as shorthand. Quote a catalog you have in your hand. The 1909 Eastwood catalog is a safe place to start.

The inglenook is architecture: a recessed hearth with seating built in or with a settle pulled in, a low beam, sometimes cabinets. Medieval halls and English country houses are the romance. American bungalows and Craftsman houses made it a plan you could order. Stickley’s Columbus Avenue nook, post-fire, is called in Syracuse local history the first Craftsman interior in the country. That is a local claim with a photograph behind it. Craftsman Farms has hearths and oak enough to make the same point without a catalog number.

*The Craftsman* published house designs and furnished rooms. A 1904 plate of a Craftsman house living room — armchair, wood, a fire — circulated as the magazine’s argument. The living room is not a parlor with the carving planed off. It is a room you are supposed to occupy. Wilson’s bungalow copy of 1910 will say cheerful, cozy, sunny, restful. Stickley’s copy says sturdy, primitive, honest. Both are selling permission to sit on the best furniture.

## What is not in this room

A seven-piece rosewood set is not in this room. A marble-top center table at dining height is not the hero; a lower oak table, or a tabouret, starts the long slide toward the coffee table. An étagère of bibelots is a clutter Stickley’s plates avoid. Books go in a stand or in a glassed case with the same tenons. The piano, if there is one, is an upright against a board-and-batten wall, not a square in the middle of the floor.

Dining trestle no. 631 is not in this room. Neither is the #935 directors table that the Columbus Avenue photograph shows in the dining space. Those forms, and the #355 leather dining chairs around them, are dining-room furniture even when the house plan lets you see them from the settle. The living room’s table is for a lamp and a book, not for a roast. If a house has one great room, say so and still name the objects by job.

Mission is the broader trade word. Stickley disliked some of what sold as Mission. Other Stickleys — Leopold and J. G. in particular — and shops from Grand Rapids to Michigan Chair made oak furniture that photographs as Craftsman and is not Eastwood. Limbert, Roycroft, the unnamed factory “mission settle” in a 1912 Sears house: cousins. This page is Gustav’s workshops and magazine. A through-tenon is not a signature.

## Labor, bankruptcy, the magazine’s end

Eastwood was a factory. Men cut oak, fumed it, fitted tenons, nailed leather. The Arts and Crafts moral — joy in labor, the medieval maker — sits on top of a payroll. Stickley published the moral and ran the factory. That is not a hypocrisy unique to him. It is the American pattern: a reform interior produced at scale.

Craftsman Farms was supposed to be a school and a farm and a house. It became a costly stage. Stickley’s business failed in 1915. *The Craftsman* ended in 1916. The furniture kept circulating. Other factories filled the orders. Living rooms from Syracuse to California kept the Morris chair and the settle after the proprietor had lost the shops. The 1915 living room that Tyron called the answer to a new social feeling could be oak and leather without ever having seen Eastwood. It could also be a parlor suite with the arms cut down. Both happened.

Women furnished these rooms too, though the magazine’s voice is often male and workshop-minded. A Morris chair is not gendered like a ladies chair, but the household still had a person who chose the curtains and paid the upholsterer to recover the leather. Servants, where they existed, now dusted oak instead of rosewood piercing. There is less to dust. That is part of the reform’s success.

## How to look at a Craftsman living room

Stand at the hearth. If a settle or a pair of built-in benches faces the fire, and a Morris chair can turn toward the same brick, you are in Stickley’s diagram even if the chairs are factory Mission. If the sofa faces a television and the settle is a hall piece for shoes, the house has moved on and kept the oak.

Look at the ray flake. Quartersawn show surfaces are the Eastwood pitch. Look at the tenons. Through and pinned is the advertisement. Look at the finish. A thick shiny varnish is often later; fumed oak is quieter. Look at the underside of a cushion. Leather that has been replaced five times can still sit on an honest frame.

Do not call every oak armchair a Stickley. Do not put a dining trestle in the middle of this paragraph. Do not read a 1905 living room as a failed parlor. The parlor’s job was to look occupied. The Craftsman living room’s job is to be occupied, with furniture heavy enough that you do not roll it to the wall for a dance. Casters are rare. You live around the settle.
