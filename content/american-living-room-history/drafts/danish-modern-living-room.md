# Danish modern in the American living room

On September 26, 1960, John F. Kennedy and Richard Nixon sat down for the first televised presidential debate. The chairs were Hans J. Wegner’s Round Chair of 1949, the oak armchair Americans had already begun to call, simply, The Chair. Interiors magazine had published it in 1950 — the first substantial American report on a Danish cabinetmaker’s chair — and had praised the top rail as “a complicated collection of twisted curves and joints… wrested into quiet obedience.” Ten years later the same rail was on camera in millions of living rooms. The debate is a political story. It is also a furniture story: a Danish armchair had become the American picture of a serious seat.

Danish modern in the United States is not a mood board. It is a set of chairs, a few sofas, a low table, and a storefront that taught a suburban household how to sit without a parlor suite. The woods are teak first, then oak, walnut, and the maple and harewood Baker Furniture used when it put Finn Juhl into Grand Rapids production. The joinery is mortise-and-tenon, often with a visible wedge or a through-tenon you can read with a thumb. Seats are paper cord, cane, or a thin upholstered pad. The chair does not recline. It does not hide a mechanism. It asks the sitter to meet the wood halfway.

## The Chair, and the other chairs

Wegner designed the Round Chair in 1949 for Johannes Hansen, after Hansen told him the Copenhagen Cabinetmakers’ Guild exhibition already had enough plywood. Wegner made it in two days. The arm-and-crest is three pieces of solid wood, carved and joined so the circle reads as one gesture. Early production was slow. Johannes Hansen’s shop could not flood a market; a Chicago club’s order for four hundred chairs took years. `[CITE NEEDED: the club name and the two-year fulfillment claim, which circulate in Wegner trade histories without a published invoice file.]` Americans who wanted The Chair waited, or they bought something adjacent.

The same year Wegner designed the Wishbone — CH24 — for Carl Hansen & Søn. The Met’s example, object 2017.454, is beech and paper cord, 28 1/2 by 22 1/2 by 20 1/8 inches, designed 1949 and made in the long run that began about 1950. The Y-back is a Ming memory reduced to a steam-bent bow and a forked splat. It stacks in the mind with every teak dining chair that later leaked into the living room. This page stays on living-room seating: the Wishbone beside a sofa, the Round Chair at a desk that faces the hearth, the Papa Bear and the other lounge forms that actually hold a body for an evening.

Finn Juhl’s American hour is 1951. Edgar Kaufmann Jr. had written him up in Interiors in 1949. Hollis Baker of Baker Furniture, Grand Rapids, commissioned a modern line. Cooper Hewitt’s account of Baker Modern is the one to trust: introduced June 1951, twenty-four pieces, desks and coffee tables and sofas and armchairs, woods that include English harewood, American walnut, and white rock maple, finished to keep the grain and take family wear. The Baker Sofa — two-piece back, oak or walnut frame, spring cushion — is living-room furniture that a Michigan factory could ship. Juhl’s Copenhagen cabinetmaker, Niels Vodder, remains the hand-shop story. Baker is the American living-room story. The Chieftain and the NV-45 stay in the mind; the Baker armchair is what a younger household could order without a passport.

Teak is the American nickname for the whole style, and it is only partly fair. Danish shops used teak from Southeast Asia — Thailand and Burma are the usual trade names in the period literature — because it oiled well, moved less than oak in a heated house, and photographed as a warm brown against a white wall. Juhl’s Baker line often refused teak. Wegner’s Round Chair is not a teak chair in the famous pictures. A living room that bought “Danish” in 1958 might be sitting on plantation teak, on oak soaped to a pale gray, or on walnut that a Grand Rapids finisher had been taught to leave alone. Smell the oil. Look at the end grain. Do not let the word teak do the identification.

## Storefronts, not catalogs of mood

The American living room met this furniture in a store, not in a Danish farmhouse. Georg Jensen Inc. — Frederik Lunning’s New York house, official importer of Jensen silver from the 1920s — held the Fifth Avenue floor that mattered. The firm sat at 667 Fifth Avenue, at 53rd Street, from 1935 to 1968. Cooper Hewitt’s biography of the shop is dry and useful: silver, ceramics, lighting, and, after the war, furniture. In 1952 Finn Juhl redesigned that Fifth Avenue store. In 1954 Georg Jensen mailed a folder of furniture inserts — Wegner, Folke Ohlsson, Poul Volther, Helge Sibast — in the same year the touring exhibition Design in Scandinavia opened. That exhibition, organized by the Nordic craft societies, ran 1954 to 1963 through twenty-seven cities in the United States and Canada. House Beautiful’s July 1963 issue put “The Scandinavian Look in U.S. Homes” on the American coffee table. The look had a mailing address.

Just Lunning took the presidency after Frederik’s death in 1952 and ran the store until his own death in 1965. A Wegner retrospective at Georg Jensen in 1959 is the date the New York trade histories agree on. Late in the 1960s Salesco, the Danish consortium that handled much of Wegner’s factory work, gave Georg Jensen exclusive United States distribution. Designers who had bought in Copenhagen balked at the New York multiplier. `[CITE NEEDED: the “three to four times Danish retail” figure, which is repeated in Wegner biographies and has not been checked here against a 1966 invoice pair.]` When the Lunning estate sold the store in 1968, the Fifth Avenue pipe closed. Johannes Hansen, outside Salesco, kept a separate channel. Everyone else scrambled.

Other floors carried the weight Georg Jensen could not. John Stuart Inc. and George Tanier are the importer names that keep turning up in the same 1950s advertisements. Dunbar sold adjacent modernism from Berne, Indiana. A ranch-house living room in 1962 might own one Wegner armchair bought on a New York trip and a sofa from a Grand Rapids catalog that had learned to taper a leg. The mix is the American fact. Pure Copenhagen interiors existed in photographs. Most households sat in a translation.

Design Within Reach is a later store. Rob Forbes incorporated the company in California in November 1998 and began selling in 1999, first by catalog and web, later from “studios.” DWR’s early story is Herman Miller and the Eames lounge as much as it is teak. It belongs in this essay only as a warning about dates. A 2004 catalog that puts a Wishbone under a lamp is revival retail, twenty-five years after the import boom cooled. Do not hang a 1958 living room on a 1999 shopping bag.

## How the chair sits in the house

Clifford Edward Clark’s *The American Family Home* ends before this furniture lands, but the house it describes is the house that received it. The parlor was already a living room. The bungalow hearth was already a permission to sit on the best chair. Danish modern did not invent the informal front room. It gave that room a lighter object. A Belter sofa claims the wall. A Wegner armchair can stand in the middle of a ranch living room on a wool rug and still leave a path to the kitchen.

The coffee-table essay in this series owns the low table. Here the table is only the height problem Danish seating created: a lounge chair with a low seat wants a table you do not dine at. Americans already had the cocktail table. Teak trays and tile-top cubes filled the catalogs. The sectional essay owns the sofa that tried to be as low as the chair. A pit group will try to go lower still. Danish modern, at its strictest, is a few discrete objects with air around them. That is why a 1970s velvet pit and a 1955 Wegner armchair can share a decade on a timeline and still be different rooms.

Labor is factory and shop, not servants in this hour. Johannes Hansen’s bench made three Round Chairs in a day at one cited mid-1960s count. Carl Hansen could make Wishbones by the many. Baker’s Grand Rapids line is American factory labor on a Danish drawing. Teak logs are someone else’s forest. A history that names only Wegner’s hand is a history of the signature, not the crate.

## How to look at the chair you were left

Turn it over. A Johannes Hansen or Carl Hansen stamp, a paper label, a Salesco tag, a Baker stencil — those are the names that pin a date. An unmarked teak armchair with paper cord and a wishbone back is a type, not a Wegner. Paper cord darkens and fuzzes on the front rail first; that abrasion is the living-room tell, the same wear this series uses on parlor sofas. A glossy film finish on teak is often a later “restoration.” Oil or soap is the period hand. Through-tenons that were never wedged, stretchers that were dowelled in a hurry, a seat recaned in plastic — those are alterations. Name them.

If the living room still has a fireplace, the Danish chair can face it. If the television has already eaten the mantel — the television essay takes that theft — the same chair will be dragged into a row. Wegner did not design for a screen. He designed for a body and a conversation. The American living room after 1949 used him for both, and then used cheaper cousins for the second. Look at the joint before you look at the brown.
