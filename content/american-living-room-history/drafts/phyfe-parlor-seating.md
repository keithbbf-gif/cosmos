# Duncan Phyfe parlor seating

The sofa in the Metropolitan Museum that once belonged to Thomas Cornell Pearsall is a parlor object: mahogany, tulip poplar, cane, gilded brass, 34 by 84¾ by 23¾ inches as the Museum publishes the tape. The date is about 1810–20. The credit line is Gift of C. Ruxton Love Jr., 1960. The object number is 60.4.1. I use that number because it is on the Met label, not because a sentence needs a digit. The suite around it — a pair of armchairs, ten side chairs, two footstools; more chairs at the Museum of the City of New York — is seating for a room where people sit and are seen. The curule bases come from Greco-Roman stools as redrawn in the 1808 supplement to the London *Chair-Makers’ and Cabinet-Makers’ Book of Prices*. An inscription stamped inside the seat rails, plus the ownership history, is why the Museum says “attributed to Duncan Phyfe” instead of “New York, anonymous.” Skill in the details is the other reason. This page stays with sofas, window seats, and parlor chairs. It will not walk Phyfe’s dining tables.

Peter M. Kenny and Michael K. Brown, with Frances F. Bretter and Matthew A. Thurlow, *Duncan Phyfe: Master Cabinetmaker in New York* (Metropolitan Museum of Art / Yale, 2011), is the catalogue of the exhibition that reset the shop. More than sixty works, some newly documented, from the early Sheraton-and-Hope years to the late Grecian Plain. Phyfe (born near Lock Fannich, Ross-shire, 1768 or 1770; died New York, 1854) was called, in his working life, the “United States Rage.” The rage was a workshop: journeymen, apprentices, a Fulton Street address that moved with the city’s numbering, a roster of rich New York and Southern clients. Biography is in Kenny and Brown. The furniture problem here is what a parlor got when it paid that shop.

## What the shop put in a parlor

A Phyfe parlor sofa of the 1805–20 years is a long seat with a visible mahogany frame, reeded or carved legs, and a crest that may carry thunderbolts, a bowknot, drapery, or a quiet tablet. The Met’s scroll-back sofa, gift of Mrs. Harry Horton Benkard, 1942 (object 42.16), 1805–15, is the type the Museum calls, in quality and condition, among the finest known of the classic New York form. Mahogany, white pine, tulip poplar. 37 by 80¼ by 25 inches. The black horsehair and gilded tacks you see are a modern recover over the original foundation, meant to look like the day it left the shop. That sentence is the right way to talk about upholstery: the haircloth is a look, the foundation is the document.

Window seats are parlor furniture that admit the window. The Met’s window seat from the Samuel A. Foot townhouse, 678 Broadway, completed 1837 (object 66.221.9; part of suite 66.221.1–.10 and related 1972.264.1–.2), is Grecian Plain: figural mahogany veneer, S-scroll trusses after La Mésangère, 16¼ by 44¾ by 14 inches, ash and pine under the show wood, modern upholstery. The Museum allows “Workshop of Duncan Phyfe” or “Duncan Phyfe & Sons (1837–1840).” The Foots later moved to Geneva, New York, to a Grecian villa named Mullrose; the suite likely went with them. A window seat is not a stool you kick aside. It is a low sofa for a pier, a place to sit in the light without blocking the drape. The Empire essay owns the recamier silhouette. This seat is the shop’s later, flatter version of wall seating.

Parlor chairs from the same suites — klismos profiles, saber legs, curule bases on the Pearsall set — are the chairs a caller actually used. Caned seats with cushions appear. So do slip seats. Footstools belong to the same order: not dining-room furniture, not a church hassock, a parlor finish for the person who has already been given a chair.

## Frame, cane, haircloth

Phyfe’s show wood is mahogany, sometimes rosewood in the later years, veneered when a rail needs a flame. Secondary woods are the New York set: white pine, tulip poplar, ash. Reeding is a shop signature that other shops stole. Carved wheat, thunderbolts, and acanthus are not fingerprints. The State Department’s caned Grecian sofa note is the right caution: a plethora of sofas with cornucopia and bowknot cannot all have come from one cellar. Kenny and Brown’s documented works are the ones that teach the hand. The rest is New York.

Joints: mortise-and-tenon on the seat rails, glue blocks, dowels in the scrolls, through-tenons rarely advertised. Cane is woven through holes in the seat frame; a later machine cane in a groove is a repair. Brass is stamped and gilded, not always French ormolu. Castors are brass, often in a cup, because a parlor sofa moves for cleaning and for the reception that needs a different circle.

The upholsterer is a second shop even when the invoice is Phyfe’s. Curled hair, stitched edges, a linen or canvas foundation, then horsehair or silk. The Benkard sofa’s surviving foundation is why the Museum could recover it honestly. A sofa that has been sprung in the 1880s and foamed in the 1980s has lost that document. You can still read the frame.

## The parlor, not the dining room

Phyfe’s shop made tables. Other slugs and the dining-room series may have them. A pillar-and-claw dining table is a different permission: sit to eat, leaves that come out, casters for the dance of a meal. A parlor sofa does not grow leaves. A window seat does not take a cloth. The confusion is dealer convenience. A “Phyfe dining parlor” is a sentence that collapses two rooms. In a New York townhouse the front parlor and the dining room may share a hall and a maker. They do not share a form.

Pearsall was a merchant and shipowner. Foot was a lawyer. Those are parlor clients. Southern buyers ordered from New York and received goods in Charleston and other ports; the Met’s Richmond Room stages Phyfe and Lannuier seating in a Virginia interior to say so. The seating is not original to William C. Williams. The market is real. Enslaved labor in the houses that received the crates is real. Phyfe’s own shop, in a New York that had already begun gradual emancipation, ran on wage labor, apprentices, and journeymen. Do not invent an enslaved craftsman at the Fulton Street bench to make the labor sentence more symmetrical. Do not omit the enslaved housemen who moved the sofa in Richmond or Charleston.

Lannuier is the other name on Broad Street. French, shorter career, gilt and caryatids. The Empire essay takes the shared Grecian form. Competition is a period fact; conflation is a later museum problem that the 1998 Lannuier show and the 2011 Phyfe show were meant to end. If the sofa has a stamped Phyfe surname and a document, say Phyfe. If it has a Lannuier label, say Lannuier. If it has a cornucopia and a wish, say New York.

## Grecian Plain, 1830s

The late shop — Duncan Phyfe & Sons, 1837–1840, then the aging man — simplifies. Veneer does more. Carving does less. S-scroll trusses appear on tables and on the Foot window seat alike. La Mésangère’s plates matter more than Sheraton’s. A parlor of 1837 that buys this suite is already looking at a heavier, quieter classicism than Pearsall’s curule chairs. The living room is not yet the name. The furniture is already willing to be sat on for longer than a morning call. Haircloth still says do not put your feet up. The window seat says the light is part of the room.

Colonial Revival and the twentieth-century “Phyfe style” will thin the legs, gild every reed, and sell a tea room. Nutting is not the only sinner. A 1920s living room with a reproduction Phyfe sofa is a Revival room. Kenny and Brown are the correction.

## How to look at a parlor sofa that might be his

Measure. An eight-foot sofa with a thin seat and reeded legs is in the conversation. A nine-foot cornucopia couch may be the Empire form, shop unknown. Turn it over. Pine and poplar with animal-glue ghosts are right. A branded or inked inscription inside a rail is worth a photograph and a letter to someone who has the 2011 catalogue open. Look at the cane: hand-woven through holes, or a spline? Look at the crest. Thunderbolts are a vocabulary, not a signature.

If a dealer offers a Met accession that is not 60.4.1, 42.16, or 66.221.9 (and its published suite-mates), ask to see the object page. If the number is recited from memory and the sofa is in a barn, walk away. If the sofa is in the American Wing, read the label twice. Attributed, workshop, and Phyfe & Sons are three different sentences.

Sit, if you are ever allowed, and notice that the Pearsall cane wants a cushion. The Benkard haircloth wants a formal spine. The Foot window seat wants you lower than the windowsill. Three parlor jobs, one shop tradition, forty years. The living room that later inherits a Phyfe sofa is inheriting a call-room object. Wear on the front rail means the permission this series keeps tracking has finally reached Fulton Street goods. A parlor that was only a face does not wear there. Pearsall’s sofa, under museum light, still looks unused. That is not proof it was. It is proof the Museum is doing the parlor’s old job: hold still while visitors look.
