# How to read an American sofa

Turn the sofa on its back, or get on the floor with a flashlight. The living room will forgive you. The front is a performance — show wood, fabric, a skirt, a nailhead story. The underside is the shop. Rails, corner blocks, webbing or springs, a paper label, a later staple gun, a patch where a caster tore out. This page is a method, not a recap of the series. It will not tell you what a parlor “means.” It will tell you how to look at the object that has been asked, for two centuries, to be the best seat in the front room.

A sofa is a long upholstered seat for two or more, with a back, usually with arms. Settee, couch, davenport are period words with their own jobs; use the trade name on the label when you have one. The frame is a box: front rail, back rail, two side rails, sometimes a third longitudinal rail under the deck. Legs are integral or doweled or bolted. Everything above that box is stuffing and a cover. Everything that fails starts in the box.

## Rails and secondary woods

Pick up a corner, if the piece is small enough, and sight the front rail. A true hardwood rail — maple, oak, ash — holds screws and corner blocks. A rail of yellow-poplar or sweetgum is a secondary wood doing a primary job, common and respectable in American shops that saved walnut and mahogany for the legs you see. Pine appears in cheap frames and in some honest country frames. Plywood and later particleboard appear in factory sofas after the mid-twentieth century, especially in the back rail no one was expected to photograph. None of those materials is a moral grade by itself. A poplar rail with glued-and-screwed corner blocks will outlast a mahogany show-leg glued to a crumbly core.

Show wood is the species on the exposed leg, arm scroll, or crest. Secondary wood is the species in the hidden frame. Winterthur and the shop manuals agree on the split: it is not a trick unless a dealer calls the whole sofa “solid mahogany.” Look at an unstained underside or a break. Mahogany legs and poplar rails are normal for a nineteenth-century parlor sofa. Maple legs and plywood rails are normal for a 1990s catalog sofa. Gum and poplar in a Grand Rapids frame are normal for 1900. Name what you see.

Corner blocks are triangular or trapezoidal pieces glued and screwed into the rail corners. Their absence on a “fine” sofa is a warning. Their presence, with fresh screws in stripped holes, is a repair. A mid-rail that has cracked under the sitters is the living-room wear this series treats as evidence. A parlor that was never used may have a clean mid-rail and dusty show surfaces. Abrasion on the front rail’s top edge, under the fabric, means bodies.

## Webbing, coils, and the eight-way tie

The deck is how the sofa holds you up. Three systems matter in American work.

Webbing alone — linen first, then jute by the 1840s in the wider three-to-five-inch bands — is a lattice stapled or tacked to the rails, sometimes with a thin pad and no springs. Eighteenth-century easy chairs and some Federal sofas sit on webbing and stuffing. If you see only jute, do not invent coils.

Coil springs on webbing are the nineteenth-century luxury that became the trade standard. Sherman Blair of New Haven patented an “elastic spring sofa” in 1822 and an “elastic spring cushion” in 1823. William Hancock advertised elastic spring-seat chairs and sofas within months and added rocking chairs in 1825. Samuel Pratt’s English patent of 1828 is the other ancestor shops mention. Early American spring seats were not all webbed; the Hancocks stapled helical iron-wire springs to a plank deck, and as late as 1867 George Henkels noted board bottoms on cheaper chairs and sofas. Kathleen M. Johnston’s Winterthur/University of Delaware work on the Hancocks is the monograph to read before you date a spring by wish. Webbing under coils becomes common as jute widths arrive. The later refinement is the eight-way hand-tie: each coil tied with twine to its neighbors and to the frame in eight directions so the tops stay level and work as a field. Drop-in units that imitate the look from below are a later factory shortcut. Look for knots at the coil rims and twine that runs to the rails. If the “coils” sit in a prefabricated wire cage dropped into the box, you are not looking at a tied deck.

Sinuous springs — zigzag no-sag wire — are a 1930s invention (Kaden’s reissue patent Re. 21,263 is the type cited in later engineering patents) clipped to the front and back rails. They are fast, cheap, and legitimate. A 1990s slipcovered sofa with sinuous springs is not a fraud. It is a factory deck. Marshall units — pocketed coils in a cloth jacket — turn up in cushions more than in decks. Foam slabs on webbing or on a board appear in pit groups and in cheap moderns. Crumbled foam is a date and a condition, not a style.

Horsehair, tow, and cotton batting are the old stuffing; polyurethane is the new. A horsehair sofa reupholstered in foam will sit higher and deader. Name the stuffing if you can see it at a tear.

## Labels, stamps, and the paper trail

The underside is an archive. Paper labels: manufacturer, city, style number. Grand Rapids names, High Point names, a union bug from the Upholsterers International Union, a later fire-law tag (CA TB 117 and its successors) that dates a recovver. Stencils and burned brands on the back rail. Pencil shop notes. A retailer tag stapled over a maker tag. Photograph them before anyone “cleans” the frame.

A missing label is not a mystery to solve with a romance. It is a missing label. Federal sofas with Phyfe attributions need more than a reeded leg; the Phyfe essay in this series owns that problem. A Victorian parlor sofa with a Belter claim needs laminated rosewood and the right carving, not a black finish. The Belter essay owns that problem. This page owns the habit of looking.

Cushion labels lie last and least. They tell you the foam house or the down processor, often from the last recover. Use them to date the cover, not the frame.

## Alterations: the sofa’s second life

Reupholstery is the commonest alteration and not a crime. A living-room sofa that has never been recovered has either been unused or used hard for a short life. Look for extra tack holes, a forest of staples over older tacks, webbing that does not match the stain of the rails, springs tied with nylon mason line instead of jute twine. Skirts added to hide damaged legs or cheap feet. Casters removed and the holes plugged. Arms recut — a gentleman’s chair or sofa arm lowered to modern height. Show wood stripped and stained a Colonial Revival maple over an older mahogany. A mid-century sofa given a 1990s khaki slipcover is a catalog hour laid over a ranch hour. Keep both dates.

Cut-down sofas — backs lowered, legs shortened — are real. Measure. A Federal sofa that has lost two inches of leg will sit in a wrong relation to a center table. The center-table essay owns the table height. Here the tell is a foot that is too blunt, a caster plate with nowhere to go, a show-wood ankle that has been sanded into a stump.

Repairs that use drywall screws from above, sistered pine under a cracked rail, or a steel angle across a corner are shop-truth. They keep a living-room sofa in the living room. Record them. Do not strip them to “restore original” if original was a break.

## How to look, then stand the sofa up

Work in this order. (1) Overall type and date range from the silhouette — Federal, Empire, Rococo Revival, overstuffed, sectional, pit, catalog slipcover — without forcing a maker. (2) Show wood versus secondary wood. (3) Deck system. (4) Labels. (5) Alterations. (6) Wear: front rail, arm noses, cushion fronts. Then set the piece up and see how it faces the room. A sofa that faces a hearth is in one history. A sofa that faces a screen is in another. A sofa that floats in a great room with an unfinished back was bought for a wall. The great-room essay owns that mismatch.

If you cannot turn the sofa, take a phone photograph of the underside from the front, then from each end. The flashlight will find the twine. Your hand will find the corner block. The living room is a place you are allowed to sit. It is also a place you are allowed to look. The best American sofas, from a Hancock spring seat to a slipcovered khaki, will survive the inspection. The ones that will not survive it were already lying.
