# The Victorian parlor center table

The High Museum’s Gothic Revival center table, attributed to Alexander Jackson Davis and dated about 1850, stands thirty inches high. The top is white marble, hexagonal. The base is rosewood and cherry, six clustered columns, brass, a triangular foot with trefoil terminals. Accession 1983.190. That height is the first fact. You do not put your feet on it. You do not set a tray of drinks at shin level. A Victorian parlor center table is a standing-height gathering point for a lamp, a Bible, a sewing basket, and the people who pulled chairs in at dusk. The coffee table is a later, lower object. This series gives that later form its own page. Do not read one as the other.

Andrew Jackson Downing, *The Architecture of Country Houses* (1850), wrote that center tables “have long been popular pieces of parlor furniture” and that “the centre-table is to us the emblem of the family circle.” He illustrated one (fig. 223). He also told villa clients that the table’s good effect depended mainly on the cloth spread over it, “concealing all but the lower part of the legs.” Marble was expensive and cold. Wool was winter. Green baize and broadcloth show up in the trade press of the 1850s as the ordinary winter cover. The emblem is not the stone. It is the circle of chairs that the stone organizes.

## Height, stone, and the lamp

A parlor center table of the 1830s–1870s is commonly twenty-eight to thirty-one inches high. That is dining-table height, or near it, because the job is the same job a hall table once did in a smaller house: hold light and work at the height of a seated adult’s hands. The Virginia Museum of Fine Arts holds another Davis-attributed Gothic table, about 1845–50, rosewood, oak, and walnut under white marble, given as 30 3/4 inches high (object 2009.3). The Chrysler Museum’s laminated rosewood center table by J. & J.W. Meeks, about 1850, is 31 inches (object 71.2293). Those numbers are labels, not conversions. They tell you the form was never meant to sit in front of a sofa the way a 1920s cocktail table would.

Marble tops are Italian or domestic white, sometimes gray-veined, sometimes black. The slab is heavy. The frame that carries it is a pedestal, a tripod, four cabriole legs, or a cluster of colonnettes. Stretchers meet under a carved basket of fruit or a turned boss. Secondary woods — pine, chestnut, oak — take the unseen work. The marble is the public face. It does not stain as quickly as a polished rosewood top when a kerosene font sweats, and it reads as money in a room that exists to be read.

The lamp is the reason the table sits in the middle of the floor. A sinumbra lamp — circular oil font, glass shade, “shadowless” in the trade language — throws light around a circle of readers. Argand burners, then kerosene, then gas if the house has it, then electricity if the house lasts that long. *Three Experiments of Living* (1837) has a young wife remind her husband that they have been talking about getting a centre-table: “I thought it would be very convenient; and then it gives a room such a sociable look; besides, as we had a centre-lamp.” The lamp needs the table. The table needs the lamp. Wall outlets will later drag both to the perimeter. That is a twentieth-century problem.

## Casters and the dance

The McLoughlin House in Oregon City, a unit of Fort Vancouver National Historic Site, was built in 1845 for Dr. John McLoughlin, retired chief factor of the Hudson’s Bay Company at Fort Vancouver. National Park Service interpreters describe the 1850s parlor as the room for reading, cards, parties, and dances. “To accommodate their guests, much of the furniture on the first floor of the home has parts that fold away or roll on wheels to create a larger entertaining space.” That sentence is the shop fact. Casters are not decoration. They are how a marble-topped table leaves the center of the floor when the same room must become a dance floor.

A brass or iron caster under a cabriole foot is a small machine: a cup, a horn, a wheel, sometimes a leather roller that dies first. You tip the table, you roll it to the wall, you fold a drop-leaf if the table has one, you stack balloon-back chairs. The McLoughlin parlor still holds a family sofa upholstered in horsehair — the NPS offers visitors a touch sample — and a Caribbean mahogany pivoting game table treated by conservators. A marble-top table from the house was among the pieces Jon Brandon conserved on site in 2010. The house also held a Prince & Co. melodeon. Music and the cleared floor belong to the same evening. The piano essay in this series takes the instrument. Here the point is the table’s willingness to leave.

Bartow-Pell’s Bronx parlors keep center tables on casters for the same reason. The mansion’s historian, writing from the objects, notes that today’s concert setup is not unlike what the Bartows would have done for an evening party. A table that cannot roll is a table that forbids the room its second job.

## What sat on it

*Godey’s Lady’s Book* ran a column called “Centre-Table Gossip.” The name is the job. The table is where the household performs literacy and taste. Mrs. J. H. Hanaford, “The Centre-Table versus the Dining-Table,” *Water-Cure Journal* (1853), lists a large Bible, one or two annuals in gay attire, the daguerreotype of an absent son, and a cologne bottle. A poem in *The Monthly Miscellany of Religion and Letters* (1840) piles Bible, hymn book, annuals, the daily paper, the last review, tracts, pamphlets, inkstand, wafers, sand-box, paper, knife, “in rich confusion there.” Formal parlors were tidier than sitting rooms. The best room might hold a floral arrangement, a parlor dome of wool birds or wax fruit under glass, a shell, a stereoscope. The back parlor or family sitting room took the sewing and the ink.

An 1850 short story in the *Gazette of the Union* opens with a wife who thinks they must have a center-table because “our parlor is quite singular without it, they are so common now.” The husband tells her the house is her domain. That is the gender of the object. The lady of the house furnishes the emblem. Someone else — a servant, an enslaved worker in a Southern house before the war, a daughter — dusts the marble and replaces the cloth. The table’s moral language in the *Family Miscellany and Monthly School-Reader* (1854) is thicker still: wheel the table to the center, bring the lights, mother sews, daughter knits, father or son reads aloud. The furniture is a civics lesson. It is also a lamp stand.

Publishers sold “centre-table” books the way later decades sold coffee-table books. The phrase is period. Illustrated Bibles and gift annuals were sized and bound to be seen from a standing height, not from a sofa cushion. Do not confuse that trade with the low table of the 1920s.

## How it is made

New York shops of the 1840s and 1850s — Belter, Meeks, Alexander Roux, the unnamed makers who filled orders for Davis’s Gothic designs — built center tables as showpieces of the same parlor suite that included the sofa and the ladies’ chair. Laminated rosewood, steam-bent and carved, can carry a marble slab that would crack a thin solid board. The Met’s Belter-attributed center table in the Rococo Revival parlor (gift of Gloria and Richard Manney, 1980.510.1) puts two carved dolphins on each of four legs and a bouquet under the stretchers. Casters under the feet. The marble is the weight; the lamination is why the legs can be that thin.

A cheaper table from Cincinnati or Grand Rapids a generation later is walnut, incised, factory-turned, a pedestal that unscrews for the freight car. The marble may be thinner, or it may be a painted slate pretending. The casters are still there. The emblem idea survives the shop that made it.

Joints: the pedestal meets the top frame with a large wooden screw or a bolted iron plate. Legs dovetail or mortise into a block. Stretchers are tenoned. The marble sits in a rabbet or on felted blocks; it is not glued. If you lift a period table and the top is screwed through the marble, someone in the twentieth century got nervous. Look at the underside. Pine dust and a New York shop stencil are better evidence than a dealer’s “Belter.”

Thomas Hope’s 1807 *Household Furniture and Interior Decoration* published a “round monopodium or table in mahogany” that scholars have linked to the American form. Charles-Honoré Lannuier’s New York guéridons of the 1810s are marble-topped ancestors. The parlor center table of 1830–1870 is that lineage made moral and middle-class. Empire bases go Gothic, then Rococo, then Renaissance Revival pedestals with incised walnut and a porcelain caster. The job does not change until the lamp cord and the sofa-facing room change it.

## Who moves it

A marble slab on rosewood, rolled to the wall for a dance, is labor. In the Whittemore villa in Astoria — the house whose woodwork now frames the Met parlor — census records list four to six Irish-born servants at a time. Someone carried chairs. Someone rolled the table. Someone trimmed the lamp. In a house without staff the family did it, which is why casters and fold-away leaves matter more in a middling parlor than in a house that can ring a bell. The McLoughlin House, far from New York, still needed the same hardware. Oregon City in the 1850s was not Grand Rapids, but the furniture language had already traveled: fold, roll, clear the floor, put the lamp back when the guests have gone.

Enslaved labor belongs in this paragraph where the house is Southern and the date is before emancipation. A Natchez or Charleston parlor that owned a Meeks or a Belter table owned the people who moved it. After the war the same table is moved by wage staff or by the household. The caster does not record who pushed it. The inventory sometimes does.

## How to look at one now

If a listing calls a thirty-inch marble-top pedestal a coffee table, the listing is selling a parlor object to a living-room job. You can use it that way. You will bruise shins and you will put the lamp too high for a sofa conversation. If the piece has casters, test whether they still roll under load; a locked caster is how marble tops get cracked in doorways. If the cloth is gone and the marble is the show, you are looking at the table the way a visitor saw it between evenings. Downing wanted the cloth.

Look at the height against a chair seat. Look at the wear on the caster horns. Look at whether the underside is finished or is raw pine with shipping instructions. A table that lived in the center of a room has scuffs on all four feet. A table that lived against a wall for fifty years of Colonial Revival rearrangement has a dirty back and a polished front.

This page owns the parlor center table. The seven-piece set, the ladies’ chair, the étagère, and the coffee table are other slugs. Do not flatten this object into a low modern table with a marble top. Downing’s emblem is a circle at standing height, a lamp, and permission to sit close. The living room will later lower the table and point the seats at a screen. That is a different diagram.
