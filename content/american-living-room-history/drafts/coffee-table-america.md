# The American coffee table

The Chipstone Foundation and the Milwaukee Art Museum once put an eighteenth-century Philadelphia tea table in the same argument as a 1934 glass-and-chrome disc. Sarah Fayen’s essay for that exhibition is the cleanest way to start. The tea table stands 28 1/2 inches high. The later coffee table in her Figure 5 stands 18 inches. One is a standing or chair-height ritual around a tilt top. The other is a knee-height plane in front of an upholstered seat. Americans who call every low table in a front room a coffee table are collapsing two centuries and two rooms. This page owns the twentieth-century living-room form. The parlor center table — Downing’s emblem of the family circle, dining height, often marble, often at the middle of a formal set — belongs to its own slug.

No sourced patent makes one person the inventor of the coffee table. Treat any story that names a single father as advertising until a document appears.

## What the form is

A coffee table is a low occasional table meant to sit in front of a sofa or a group of easy chairs, within reach of a seated arm. Height is the first fact. Fayen’s House of Tomorrow–derived example is 18 inches. Paul T. Frankl’s 1945 lacquered-cork and mahogany table in the Milwaukee Art Museum is 14 1/2 inches high, 47 1/2 inches wide, and 35 1/2 inches deep. Later American trade practice settles near the mid-teens to high teens. A parlor center table at 28 or 30 inches is a different job even if a later owner has cut the legs. Cutting the legs is how some households made the new form. It is not proof of a factory origin.

The top can be rectangle, oval, circle, or a biomorphic slab. Materials follow the decade more than a single shop: walnut and mahogany veneer in the 1920s, glass and plated steel in the 1930s, cherry and plywood in Grand Rapids “colonial” lines, cork and lacquer after the war. Legs may be four turned posts, a pair of trestles, a pedestal, or a chrome frame. Drawers are uncommon. A lower shelf is common. Magazines, ashtrays, and a tray live there because the living room has nowhere else to put them once the center table is gone.

The coffee table does not fold as a rule. The eighteenth-century tilt-top tea table folded to the wall. The coffee table is resident. It stays in the middle of the seating group the way the radio console stays on the wall. Casters appear on some factory tables so a cleaner can roll them off the rug. They are not parlor dance casters.

Fayen is right about the name. Coffee and tea tables were never only for their drinks. The names sold a kind of entertaining. Coffee had overtaken tea as the American hot drink across the nineteenth century. By the 1920s, housekeeping copy told women to brew it at home in the evening as well as the morning. Furniture makers attached that drink to a new low table because the sofa had already dropped the sitting height. You cannot use a parlor table comfortably from a deep mohair cushion. You lean, you rattle the cup, you give up. The low table is a mechanical answer to the overstuffed sofa.

## The inventor story that does not hold

F. Stuart Foote founded the Imperial Furniture Company in Grand Rapids in 1903. He had learned the trade at his father’s Grand Rapids Chair Company. Imperial made tables — dining tables at first, then occasional furniture at a scale the Grand Rapids Public Museum’s catalog notes as a company specialty. Foote told a story that he invented the coffee table by shortening a dining table while helping his wife prepare for a party. Christian Carron’s *Grand Rapids Furniture* repeats the claim as Foote’s own. Fayen calls it a story that reeks of a marketing ploy and still uses it for what it reveals: the table is a centerpiece for entertaining, not a shrine to a beverage.

British trade already knew objects called coffee tables in the late nineteenth century. Makers such as William Watt and Collinson & Lock appear in later dealer summaries. `[CITE NEEDED: a dated British catalog or pattern-book plate that uses “coffee table” for a low living-room form before Foote’s birth year of 1872, with a published height.]` Even without that plate, Foote cannot be the inventor of a name that predates his factory. What Imperial did do, and what the Midwest industry did with it, was to make a low, often historicist occasional table in volume for American living rooms. An Imperial cherry-and-plywood butler table, dated 1929–53 in Fayen’s caption, has a low working surface and leaves that fold up with handles, like a tray a servant would have carried. The servant count was falling. The table keeps the tray and loses the person.

Japanese and other East Asian low tables matter as a taste, not as a patent. The 1876 Philadelphia Centennial’s Japanese exhibit moved American designers toward lower, lighter furniture. Aesthetic Movement rooms already knew the low stand. The 1920s coffee table is not a copy of a specific Japanese form in most factories. It is an American sofa-front table that can wear a Chinese red lacquer, a colonial cherry, or a chrome hoop as the catalog requires.

## 1915 to 1960, without a single hero

Before 1920 the living room still often keeps a tall center table or a nest of chair-height stands. Magazine rooms of the late 1910s begin to show a lower oblong in front of the davenport. `[CITE NEEDED: a named American shelter-magazine plate, 1915–1922, that captions a low sofa-front table as a coffee table.]` By the mid-1920s the object is ordinary enough that department stores do not have to explain it. By 1934 the Chicago House of Tomorrow at the Century of Progress has a professionally designed glass-and-chrome prototype; a store then issues a version. Fayen’s circular example is 18 inches high and 24 1/4 inches across. Glass reduces visual mass in a small, open-plan room. Depression houses were small. A table you can see through is a table that does not crowd the sofa.

Colonial Revival coffee tables of the same years wear Queen Anne cabriole legs and piecrust language on a height no eighteenth-century tea table used. That is why so many of them fool later buyers. A 17-inch “Chippendale” table is a twentieth-century living-room object. The parlor-center-table essay will keep the tall marble. This essay keeps the short fake antique.

Hotel moderne and apartment moderne use the same low plane with different skins: black lacquer, mirrored glass, stepped bases. Paul Frankl’s skyscraper furniture of the late 1920s is often too tall to be a coffee table — his Brooklyn Museum step table is 38 3/4 inches — but his 1945 Milwaukee table is fully the postwar living-room form: thick cork edge, sharp mahogany legs, a sculpture that also holds a cup. Isamu Noguchi’s glass-and-wood table for Herman Miller, designed in the 1940s, is another designer-name version of the same job. Most American rooms got a factory oval from Grand Rapids or High Point.

After 1945 the ranch-house living room locks the table in place. The sofa faces a picture window or a hearth; the table sits on the rug like a raft. Sectionals will later wrap it. The conversation pit will try to abolish it. Until then the coffee table is how a living room admits that people sit low and put things down.

## Labor, woods, and who carries the tray

Grand Rapids table plants were machine rooms: veneer presses, turning lathes, finishing conveyors. Occasional tables were a way to keep those machines busy between dining suites. Plywood cores with cherry or mahogany faces are not a defect. They are how a low, wide top stays flat. The butler table’s folding leaves are a hardware problem: hinges and a catch. Someone in the shop fitted them. Someone in the house pinched a finger.

The missing servant is the social fact Fayen names. A tea table in 1760 implied a person who boiled water and a person who carried the tray. A coffee table in 1935 implies a hostess who sets cups down herself, or a husband who is told the coffee will keep him at home. Advertising is not evidence of happiness. It is evidence of who was expected to serve. In Black households and in white households with domestic workers, the low table did not erase carrying. It changed the height of the set-down.

Southern and midwestern hardwoods — oak, cherry, walnut, maple — all appear. A later shop asked to make a coffee table for a hall-and-parlor house that wants the old plan without the hoe is making a twentieth-century object for a seventeenth-century diagram. The woods essay takes that hour. Here the wood is whatever the catalog finished to match the davenport.

## How to look at the table in front of the sofa

Measure the height before you name it. If the top is near dining height and stands in the middle of the room away from the sofa, you may still have a parlor center table, whatever the listing says. If the top is at the sitter’s knee and the sofa’s knees are already at the apron, you have the American coffee table. Look for magazine wear on the lower shelf, cup rings that do not match a lace doily pattern, and a finish that is paler on the side toward the window.

Do not credit Foote. Do not credit a Japanese “first” unless you have a specific imported object with a date. Do not credit Prohibition with inventing the low table; the cocktail-table essay takes the drink name and the higher, smaller cousin. If a dealer says the table is colonial because the knees are carved, ask for the height. Carving is cheap. Height is the form.

A living room without a coffee table after about 1930 is either a room that kept a tall center table too long or a room that has already gone to a pit, a tray on an ottoman, or a television stand. The coffee table is the ordinary American solution: a low, resident plane that lets the overstuffed sofa function as a place to stay.
