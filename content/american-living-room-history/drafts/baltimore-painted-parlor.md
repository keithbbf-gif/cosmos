# Baltimore painted parlor furniture

The tablet on the crest is a small oil. A house, a lawn, a fence, a tree that knows which way the wind comes off the Patapsco. Around it the chair is black or yellow or a dark green, gilt striping, maybe a gothic arch in the splat, a cane seat, saber legs that have already read Sheraton. You are not looking at mahogany waiting to be shown. You are looking at paint that is the furniture. John and Hugh Finlay, Baltimore, called themselves fancy furniture manufacturers, painters, and coach makers. On 24 October 1803 they advertised in the *Federal Gazette & Baltimore Daily Advertiser* a side chair whose splat is two gothic-arched panels flanking a central tablet — the same chair form as the armchairs in the Morris suite. Through 1804 they offered furniture “with or without views adjacent to the city.” On 8 November 1805 they boasted work “of every description and all colors, gilt, ornamented and varnished in a stile not equalled on the continent.” The spelling is theirs. The claim is trade talk. The chairs survive in enough numbers, and in enough museums, to make the talk checkable.

This page is parlor seating: fancy chairs, settees, the occasional pair of window seats. A pier table with a painted view is a wall object that sat in the same room; it will appear as suite furniture, not as a dining-room sideboard. The Windsor essay owns stick construction and the painted farmhouse chair. Baltimore fancy work is a cousin that went to the front room.

## Fancy, japanned, caned

*Fancy* is the period word for painted, gilt, and ornamented seating that is not show-wood furniture. *Japanned* is the shop word for a varnished, often dark ground that imitates a harder lacquer. Seats are commonly cane. Cushions were loose and are mostly lost. Frames are maple or beech or another turning wood, light enough to gild, not meant to be stripped. The person who scrapes a Finlay chair to “good maple” has erased the object.

Construction is closer to a fancy Windsor and a klismos than to a Phyfe mahogany sofa. Turned and saber legs, a crest rail that is a tablet or a tablet-and-arches, stretchers when the saber curve needs help. The Finlays trained as coach painters. Coach painting is layers, varnish, striping, and a tolerance for weather. A parlor chair gets the same skin indoors. Ornamenters — including, in the shop’s busy years, women and boys as well as men — do the striping and the freehand. The Baltimore Museum of Art’s teacher materials repeat a high employment figure of sixty-eight artisans (thirty men, twenty-five women, thirteen boys) at one time. `[CITE NEEDED: the directory, census, or shop record behind that 68-person count, as opposed to a later museum round number.]` Even if the count is soft, the mix is the point. This is not a one-man carved signature. It is a warehouse.

Hugh Finlay sketched country seats. William Russell Birch’s *The Country Seats of the United States* (1808–09) printed some of the same Maryland houses, including Montebello, that appear on furniture. Francis Guy, English-born landscape painter in Baltimore, is the name Lance Humphries and the BMA attach to the best tablets on the Morris suite: oil vignettes of houses around the city, close to Guy’s canvases of Mount Deposit, Bolton, Druid Hill, Willow Brook. Guy exhibited a Druid Hill view in 1811, which may be later than the suite. The tablets are still his kind of picture.

## The Morris suite and the houses on the chairs

The Morris suite, about 1803–1805, is the document Humphries published in *American Furniture* 2003. Ten armchairs, two settees, a pier table; perhaps more chairs once. Seventeen medallions of country houses and other buildings in and around Baltimore. Attribution to the Finlays is by form (the 1803 advertisement) and by the offer of “views adjacent to the city,” not by a signed invoice Humphries could print. He is honest about that. The suite is still the set to learn from.

The Baltimore Museum of Art holds chairs from this painted culture with named views: Belvidere, Bolton, Rose Hill, Mount Deposit, Oakley, Willow Brook. Willow Brook was built in 1799 for Thorowgood Smith, merchant and mayor; it stood west of the city with a view to the Patapsco. The BMA’s Willow Brook chair is the one they walk students through. I will not invent the BMA accession numbers. The titles are on the museum’s object list. Other suites exist. Humphries notes a Buchanan-family group: ten side chairs, window seats, two card tables, a pier table, the tablets mixing houses with trophies. Suites come in tens and in sixes. A parlor that ordered ten chairs and two settees was not ordering kitchen seating.

Thomas Renshaw and John Barnhart are the other Baltimore names that painted furniture scholarship keeps in the room so the Finlays do not swallow the city. `[CITE NEEDED: a published Renshaw or Barnhart parlor settee with a dated label or bill.]` Christian M. Nestell’s drawing book, which Nancy Goyne Evans published in *American Furniture* 1998, is the ornamenter’s paper: how a painter, not a cabinetmaker, thought about pattern. Evans belongs on the Windsor page for chairs and here for the paint trade.

## Latrobe, the White House, and what burned

Benjamin Henry Latrobe hired the Finlays to make painted seating after his own drawings. The Magazine Antiques essay on Latrobe and the Finlays tracks two related jobs: furniture for the Waln family and, in 1809, seating for James and Dolley Madison’s White House Oval Room. Samuel Smith of Baltimore recommended the brothers. The Madison chairs in Latrobe’s drawings are severe klismos forms — sweeping saber legs, horizontal rails, tablet crests — more extreme in their inward curve than the Waln chairs. The sofa drawings show S-shaped ends; the settees, four of them, have upholstered bolster ends. The White House furniture burned in 1814 when the British burned the house. What survives are the drawings. That is enough to put Baltimore painted parlor seating at the highest political address in the country, and not enough to describe the paint. Do not invent the colors. Do not invent a surviving Oval Room side chair in a private collection.

The Finlays also advertised temporary architecture and furniture for parades, assemblies, funerals, and civic events. Coach painters who can do a funeral are in the same town as the undertaker-cabinetmakers the death-room essay follows. A fancy chair can stand at a viewing. It is still a parlor chair.

## How a painted suite sits in a Baltimore parlor

A Federal mahogany parlor in New York wants visible grain. A Baltimore parlor of 1805 may want a black-and-gilt set that reads as French and as local at once: klismos air, Maryland houses on the tablets. The views are not metaphorical. They are portraits of seats a guest might have ridden past. Displaying Willow Brook on a chair in a townhouse is a civic and a social sentence. Displaying your own house would be ruder and is not what the Morris tablets appear to do. They show the city’s estates as a shared picturesque.

Cane seats are cool and light. They take a cushion for a winter call. They click. They break at the cane holes if a sitter is heavy and the cane is old. Replacement cane is common; replacement tablets are a crime. Look at the crest under raking light. If the house is a decal or a later oil on a scraped rail, the chair has been improved in the wrong century.

Export matters. Baltimore fancy furniture went south. Ads in New Orleans announced arrivals. A painted settee in a Charleston or Savannah parlor is still Baltimore work if the tablet and the saber agree. Enslaved labor in those receiving houses is part of the furniture’s life. The Finlay warehouse’s own labor, as far as the ads and the later counts show, is wage and family labor in a free-labor city that still sold goods into slave states. Keep those sentences next to each other.

By 1820–30 the fancy chair is everywhere — Hitchcock in Connecticut, New York shops, Philadelphia — and the Baltimore tablet of a specific house is no longer the only luxury. Stencils replace some freehand. The Finlays are listed as a fancy furniture warehouse. The parlor can now buy painted sets that have no view at all, only gilt and a rose. Those sets are still fancy chairs. They are not the Morris suite.

## How to look at the chair

Do not strip it. That is the first instruction and the last. Look at the tablet as a picture: is it a house with a documented name, or a generic mill? Compare it to Guy’s canvases and to Birch’s prints if the house is Maryland. Look at the splat. Gothic arches flanking a tablet is the 1803 Finlay advertisement. A solid tablet on a klismos is closer to Latrobe’s drawings. Look at the seat. Cane through holes. Look at the ground color in the protected places — under the seat, inside the rear rail. Yellow that has gone brown, black that has gone olive, gilt that is size and powder.

A settee is a chair multiplied: three or four seats, a longer tablet or a run of tablets, the same paint. It is the sofa of this parlor. It is not a Grecian couch and it is not a seven-piece walnut set. If you find one in a living room now, you have a painted bench that was always willing to be sat on, unlike the rosewood suite, and always willing to be looked at, unlike the pine settle. Wear shows on the front rail and on the cane. A parlor that was only a face still used these chairs; paint hides abrasion until it doesn’t.

If a label says Finlay, photograph it. If a dealer says Finlay because the chair is painted and from Maryland, open Humphries. The living room that wants a Baltimore chair is asking for a picture and a cane seat. That is a historically right request for 1810. It is not a reason to hang a hunt board in the same sentence. Different room. Different series.
