# Family room versus living room

A ranch house with two sitting rooms is performing an old American argument with new names. The front room has the better sofa, the picture window, and the carpet no one is supposed to spill on. The room off the kitchen has the television, the tougher weave, and the door to the yard. Magazines in the 1950s called the second room new. James A. Jacobs, whose *Detached America* is the first national study of postwar house plans as a furniture problem, treats the family room as the spatial expression of “casual living.” The living room, in that pairing, is reserved again. The parlor had been abolished in name. The job of a reserved best room returns through the garage.

This page owns the split. It does not own the 1910 living-room name; that is the first essay in the series. It does not own the ranch plan’s glass and hearth; that is the ranch essay. It owns the moment a household decides that everyday sitting and company sitting are once more two rooms.

## Rec, rumpus, play, then family

The words arrive in a pile. Recreation room and rec room are basement and club language from earlier in the century: a ping-pong table, a bar, a linoleum floor, chairs that can take a wet glass. Rumpus room is a livelier synonym, in print by the 1930s and common in builder ads after the war. Japanese researchers hunting the first “family room” in American magazines found rumpus and play rooms in *Parents’ Magazine* in 1947 where later writers had remembered a family room, and then found the words Family Room on a *Better Homes and Gardens* plan in 1948. Play room and multipurpose room already existed in 1920s and 1930s plan books. Dual living — a living room plus a second informal sitting room — is, in their account, settled by the early 1960s.

Jacobs’s public lectures and the JSTOR Daily summary of his work give the social claim. After 1945, shelter magazines sold the family room as a new idea for suburban households: parents and children in one easy space, a living center to match the kitchen’s work center. At first the room’s program is vague. It can take sewing, homework, a guest bed. As the 1950s go on, it becomes leisure-only. A leisure-only room is a class marker. So is a living room that can be kept for display. Housewives, Jacobs notes, also asked for a door between the uproar and the work of the kitchen — the open ranch’s promise colliding with noise. The two-zone house is the architectural result: public leisure in the living room, family leisure in the family room, bedrooms apart.

Ryan Homes, the Pittsburgh builder Jacobs used as a case, photographed recreation rooms with asphalt-tile shuffleboard floors, paneled walls, a bar, and upholstered furniture. That is not a front living room. It is a basement or a ground-floor extra that can look like a tavern. San Fernando Valley tracts enclosed patios and called the new rooms rustic or rumpus: a step down, asbestos tile or cork, imitation wood paneling, a former exterior door still in the wall. By the late 1970s those rooms are being advertised as family rooms or dens. The paneling is the tell. The living room kept plaster or drywall and a smoother paint.

## What furniture went where

The reserved living room takes the better suite. A sofa that still has its show-wood or its new synthetic brocade, a pair of chairs that match, a coffee table that is not allowed to collect school papers, lamps with intact shades. The television may stand here in early 1950s houses because there is only one, and guests are invited to watch. As sets cheapen and a second room exists, the television migrates. Wear follows it. The living room’s sofa then fails to shine at the front edge. The family-room sofa shines, sags, and gets a throw.

Family-room seating is the tougher, lower, more modular stuff: an open-end Kroehler, a sectional that can take a nap, recliners once they become ordinary, a pit group at the end of this period. Tables are lower and meaner — a laminate cocktail table, a trunk, a stacking set. The bar, if there is one, is furniture and joinery: knotty pine, a linoleum top, stools that leave heel marks. Built-in benches around a hearth in a 1960s family room are already halfway to a conversation pit.

The ranch essay’s picture-window problem is partly solved by the split. Put the formal sofa in the front window for the street. Put the everyday seating toward the back glass and the patio. Children go out the sliding door without crossing the pale carpet. That is the plan as drawn. In small ranches the family room is only a widened breakfast area with a different rug. In split-levels it is the downstairs. In two-story colonials of the 1960s it is the back room the realtor will later call a den.

Clark’s long view still helps. The nineteenth-century house specialized parlor and sitting room. The early twentieth century collapsed them into a living room. The postwar house, once it can afford the square footage, specializes again. The names change so the collapse can be denied. No one wants to say they have rebuilt a parlor. They say they have a living room for company and a family room for living. Tyron’s parlorless nation grows a parlor in the front and refuses the word.

## Who cleans which room

The family room is a labor claim dressed as informality. Someone still vacuums two carpets. Someone still decides that the good lamps stay in front. In the magazine fantasy, the mother watches children from the kitchen while the family room does the noise. In the house as built, the open pass-through means she hears everything and the living room becomes a museum she is also expected to keep ready. Teenagers colonize the rec room and close a door. That door is the first piece of furniture the family room wants and the open-plan ranch does not want to give.

Domestic workers, where they still come by the day, are told which room to do first. The living room is for the listing and for Sunday. The family room is where the ashtrays actually live. Cigarette burns on a laminate table are a family-room accession mark. A plastic runner on a living-room carpet is the reserved room admitting it cannot stay reserved.

Builder labor is cheap paneling, cheap tile, a sliding glass door that sticks. Furniture labor is the same factories as the living-room suite, with a different cover. Kroehler could sell Bermuda for the front and a tweed group for the back from the same catalog year.

The television is the piece that decides the split in most houses after about 1955. Early sets are furniture: a mahogany console in the living room, guests invited to watch a coronation or a debate. Once a second, cheaper set exists, or once the first set is wheeled down the hall, the living room’s job returns to conversation and display. The family room takes the glow, the snack table, and the recliner when that form arrives. A house that kept the only set in the front room never completed the reserved-living-room idea. A house that put the set in the basement rec room completed it early, at the cost of making everyday life a downstairs exile.

Do not staple this split to the 1918 influenza or to *Ladies’ Home Journal* in 1910. Those dates belong to the parlor-name essay. The family room is a postwar square-footage purchase. GI Bill houses of 1948 often had one sitting room. Ten years later a builder could sell a “family room” as an upgrade the way he sold a second bath. The furniture followed the wall. If the upgrade was only a name change on a kitchen alcove, the furniture tells on it: a dinette set is not a family-room sofa.

## How to look at a house that has both

Ask which sofa faces the television. That room is the family room, whatever the realtor’s card says. Ask which sofa faces the street or the unused hearth. That room is the living room in the reserved sense. Look at the floors. Tile, linoleum, indoor-outdoor, or worn oak toward the yard: family. Carpet with a pad and a vacant small table: living. Look at the walls. Wood-grain paneling, a bar, a trophy shelf: rec or rumpus ancestry. Look at the door. A room you can close is already more sitting room than the 1950s open-plan sermon allowed.

If there is only one sitting room, you are in an early or small ranch, an apartment, or a house that never bought the second zone. Do not invent a family room in a 1920s bungalow because a later owner paneled the basement. The basement rec room is an ancestor, not the same invention. If the only sitting room is sunken, you have jumped to the pit essay.

The great-room essay, later in the series, will collapse the split again: kitchen, dining, and sitting in one volume with a taller ceiling. That collapse is a 1980s–2000s answer to the two-room house. This page stays with the years when the American living room, having absorbed the parlor, gave everyday life away in order to become a parlor once more.
