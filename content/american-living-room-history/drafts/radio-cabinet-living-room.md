# Radio cabinets in the living room

A Philco “Baby Grand” does not look like a machine first. It looks like a small Gothic clock that has grown a grille. Collectors now call that arched table set a cathedral. Philco called the type a mantel radio and kept the Baby Grand name on midget sets into 1940. The cathedral belongs on a shelf, a mantel, or a side table. The object that reorganized the living room is taller. It is a console: a floor case in walnut veneer, highboy or lowboy, with a chassis behind doors or a tapestry grille and a speaker large enough to fill the room. Between 1922 and 1940 that case is furniture. The tubes are why you bought it. The cabinet is why it was allowed to stand where a secretary or a music cabinet had stood.

Broadcasting made the purchase urgent. Station KDKA in Pittsburgh went on the air in 1920. The Radio Act of 1922 tried to order the rush of transmitters. Households that had listened on crystal sets and headphones in a den or a bedroom moved the sound into the front room once amplifiers and loudspeakers could share it. A living room is a room in which more than one person sits. Radio, once it left the headset, needed a piece of casework that could face those people.

## Chassis and cabinet are different trades

Early 1920s sets were still radio furniture in pieces. A receiver in a box, a horn or a separate speaker, batteries on the floor or in a lower compartment. Wet batteries leaked. Rugs stained. The living room accepted the set only when the mess could be hidden and the current came from the wall. AC sets in the mid-1920s made that possible. The cabinet then became a single object: tuner, tubes, speaker, sometimes a phonograph, in a wooden envelope a furniture factory knew how to make.

Those factories were often the same shops that made suites. Walnut and walnut veneer over secondary woods — gum, poplar, basswood — dominate the surviving consoles. Carved aprons, turned legs, and broken pediments quote the period revival language already on sale as “Spanish,” “Italian,” or Chippendale. A radio highboy is a highboy that happens to hold a chassis. The legs are longer than a lowboy’s; Philco’s own highboys, the company’s identification literature says, still kept the legs shorter than some competitors’. After the early 1930s, legs shorten and the case sits closer to the floor. Ornament thins. The late console is a plain box with a grille, closer to modern case goods than to a linen press.

The California Historical Radio Society’s older journal is blunt about the cathedral’s dates. The arched table sets appear about 1930 and run only a few years, into 1933–34. They frightened the industry because they were cheap. Depression households could not always buy a floor model. A four-tube TRF in a thin cabinet sold near twenty dollars. A complex superheterodyne with shortwave in a better cathedral could reach seventy or eighty. The speaker sat above the chassis, which kept the box compact and threw sound forward. The mantel nickname was not only romance. A fireplace mantel already had lamps and an outlet. The little arched set could stand where a clock had stood and still be the second radio in a house that kept a console in the living room.

Tombstone sets — flat-topped table radios taller than they are wide — follow and overlap. They are still furniture, but they do not command a wall. The console does. Zenith’s large 1930s floor models used the cabinet as a sounding board. Philco’s Inclined Sounding Board consoles tilted the speaker. The furniture decision and the acoustic decision are the same piece of plywood.

Metal is rare. The Atwater Kent “Little Stove,” a floor cabinet in metal, is the exception radio historians mention because almost every other console is wood. World War II stops civilian radio-cabinet production. After the war the living room’s electronic case will be a television or a hi-fi, not a radio-only monument. That is a later essay.

## Opposite the sofa

Placement is not a mystery if you think like a speaker. The console stands on a long wall or in a corner where the grille can see the seating. The overstuffed sofa of the same years faces it the way an earlier sofa faced the hearth. Lamps sit on the case or on tables beside it. A clock, a doily, a framed photograph — the cabinet collects the small objects a center table once held, but at the height of a sideboard. Children are told not to turn the knobs. Someone who knows the stations does the tuning. In houses with a maid, she dusts the veneer and does not adjust the set. In houses without, the same hands that rotate the mohair cushions learn the dial.

The arrangement is social. Headphones had been a solitary sport. A console makes a group. Evening programs, prizefights, fireside talks, and Sunday music organize the week the way a piano once did. The parlor-piano essay in this series owns the older instrument. Radio does not replace every piano. It replaces the idea that the living room’s sound has to be produced in the room. The cabinet is a piece of furniture that listens to another city.

Corner placement appears in photographs and in dealer floor plans. A highboy on the bias uses the corner the way a whatnot did, but the whatnot did not require a lead-in or an outlet. Aerial wires and later power cords are part of the furniture plan. A household that hides the cord behind the sofa is already treating the radio as permanent architecture. A household that keeps the set on a tea wagon is not. Casters exist on some consoles. They are for cleaning and for the rare move to a porch, not for nightly rearrangement.

Do not confuse the cathedral’s mobility with the console’s job. A mantel set can migrate to a bedroom or a kitchen. The living-room console stays. It is too large to be casual. That is why furniture makers, not only radio engineers, owned the look. A buyer choosing between two chassis with similar tubes often chose the better veneer. Good Furniture and the trade press treated radio cabinetry as a design problem: how to put a modern machine into a historicist case so the living room would not have to become a laboratory.

Raymond Loewy’s Westinghouse Columnaire of 1931, a tall clock-like floor radio, is the designer-name exception. Most American rooms got a factory console without a famous designer. The exception matters because it shows the industry knew the object was furniture. It does not mean every living room owned a skyscraper clock.

## Labor in the case

Cabinet shops laminated the doors, cut the fretwork for grilles, and stained walnut to match a suite the customer already owned. Upholstery shops supplied the speaker cloth — tapestry, silk, or a printed fabric that imitated brocade. The cloth is the one soft surface on a hard object. It wears at the height of a child’s hand. Behind it, a paper cone. The living room never sees the cone unless the cloth splits.

Radio assembly plants put chassis on benches. Women often did repetitive wiring and tube-socket work; men did heavier assembly and testing. `[CITE NEEDED: a dated plant study or photograph caption that states the gendered division for a named maker, 1925–1935.]` Furniture factories and radio factories were not always the same company. A chassis maker could buy empty cases. A furniture maker could sell a cabinet to a radio brand. The surviving object may have a paper label from both. Read both labels. The furniture history is in the case construction: dowels, hide glue, veneer matching, a backboard that is plywood or composition, never show-wood.

Servants and children share the dusting. The dangerous work of the early 1920s — acid batteries, high-voltage eliminators — is why the set wanted a closed compartment. Fail-closed is not a metaphor here. A cabinet door that does not latch leaves tubes and fingers in the same space. Later AC consoles still run hot. The veneer checks near the tubes. That checking is how you date a life of use.

## How to look at a radio that is still in the room

Decide first whether it is a table or a floor. An arched top on a table is a cathedral or a later nostalgia copy. A flat-topped tall table set is a tombstone. A floor case with legs is a highboy or lowboy console. A floor case without much leg is a later console. If the object has no chassis, it is still furniture. Empty cabinets were sold, and many lost their works when a repairman took the chassis and did not return.

Stand where the sofa is. If the grille does not face you, the room has been rearranged for a television or a hearth and the radio has been demoted to a side wall. That demotion is already underway by 1940 in houses that bought a set early and a sofa later. The television essay will take the next black rectangle. This page stays with the wooden one.

Look at the veneer for a furniture shop’s habits: book-matched doors, a carved apron that matches no historical highboy exactly, secondary wood on the unseen sides. Look at the grille cloth for a living room’s habits: nicotine, sun-fade, a hole at the height of a broom. Look at the back. A factory stencil, a model number, a list of tubes. Do not invent a patent from a blurry plate. If the plate is gone, say so.

A living room organized by a console is a room that has accepted a machine as a peer of the davenport. The cathedral on the mantel is a smaller, cheaper, often second voice. Both are cabinets. Only the console is a piece of architecture in the plan. When you find one still opposite the sofa, you are looking at a seating group that has not yet been told to face the television. Keep that orientation in your head when you date the rest of the furniture. Mohair and a walnut grille belong to the same decade’s idea of comfort: sit, face the sound, do not see the works.
