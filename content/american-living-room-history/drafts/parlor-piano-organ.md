# Parlor piano and parlor organ

The Metropolitan Museum’s Stewart & Chickering square piano of about 1824 (1984.610.1) is a mahogany case with rosewood striping, four carved turned and reeded legs, a folding music rack, and — the furniture fact — an upholstered octagonal revolving stool on a tripod rosewood base (1984.610.2). Jonas Chickering’s name is already on the nameboard. The instrument is a table you cannot eat at. It takes the floor like a center table and then more so: 178 centimeters long in the Met’s metric label, a rectangular box of strings, wrestplank, and an English double action. A parlor that owns this object is a room that has decided music is part of receiving. The stool screws up and down. That is the first joint.

A square piano is not a baby grand and not an upright. It is a horizontal piano in a rectangular case, the form that dominated American parlors from the late eighteenth century into the 1870s and lingered in catalogs after the fashionable shops had moved on. A parlor organ — reed organ, cabinet organ, pump organ, earlier the smaller melodeon — is a different machine with a similar social job: hymns, parlor songs, a daughter’s accomplishment, a Sunday that stays in the house. Steinway and Chickering belong here as industry, not as a brochure. The stool, the case, and the floor plan are the furniture history.

## The square as furniture

Chickering opened in Boston in 1823. By the late 1830s Jonas Chickering was working on a one-piece cast-iron frame for the square piano; he patented the iron frame in 1840. American weather splits wooden frames. Iron lets the strings take more tension. The National Museum of American History’s 1850 Chickering square (serial 10683) has an iron frame, a rosewood case, compass CC to c5, leather hammers, two pedals. That is a Boston factory object sitting in a parlor anywhere the railroad and the dealer could place it.

Steinway & Sons began in New York in 1853 — Heinrich Engelhard Steinweg and his sons, German shop practice on a Manhattan floor. By the 1860s and 1870s the firm was winning the public fights that piano historians still narrate: exhibitions, testimonials, a move uptown. Arthur Loesser, *Men, Women and Pianos*, and later Cynthia Adams Hoover’s work for the Smithsonian are the places to pin production numbers, not a sales floor. Chickering built a huge Boston factory and won at London in 1851. Steinway pulled ahead in output and earnings by about 1870, in Loesser’s account. Both firms made squares and then made fewer of them as the grand and the upright took the money. A square piano after 1880 is often a household that bought earlier or a factory clearing a form. `[CITE NEEDED: the year Steinway discontinued the square in regular production; secondary accounts differ.]`

The case is cabinetwork. Rosewood veneer, walnut, carved legs that match a parlor suite or ignore it. The lid is a table when closed — lamps have burned circles in more than one. The action is a machine the household does not service. A tuner comes. A daughter practices. The room smells of felt and oil. In the Whittemore house in Astoria, the 1871 inventory put a piano in the southeast, river-facing parlor with a sofa, chairs, tables, and a music stand. Horace Whittemore’s 1865 tax list included a piano forte among the taxable luxuries. The Met’s period room is a splice and does not claim his instrument. The inventory is enough: a double parlor could specialize, one room for sitting, one for music, or the piano could stand in the only parlor a smaller house had.

McLoughlin House, Oregon City, kept a Prince & Co. melodeon — a reed instrument, smaller than a later cabinet organ, the sort of thing that traveled west more easily than a Chickering square. MPF Conservation treated it. The NPS parlor essay is about folding and rolling furniture for dances. A melodeon or a square piano is the object you do not roll if you can help it. You roll the chairs and the center table. The instrument stays and the floor clears around it. That is a plan.

## The parlor organ

Estey Organ Company of Brattleboro, Vermont, made parlor reed organs by the tens of thousands after the Civil War. The Smithsonian’s Estey of about 1870–1880 is a single-manual pump organ, compass FF to f4, stops that read like a church in miniature: Melodia, Dolce, Viola, Diapason, Vox Humana, Vox Jubilante, an octave coupler, two knee levers. The case is wood, 60 1/4 inches high, 60 1/2 wide, 21 3/4 deep — a vertical cabinet against a wall, not a table in the middle of the room. Estey’s Style 2000 of 1892 (serial 256717, Estey Organ Museum) is a walnut piano-cased reed organ with brass candle holders particular to the style. The bench in that museum is a reproduction from Estey plans. Benches split. The organ stays.

Mason & Hamlin in Boston, Story & Clark, and a long list of Chicago and Midwest makers sold the same idea: a parlor organ is cheaper than a good piano, it does not go out of tune the same way, and it will do hymns. Foot pumps, a reservoir, free reeds. The sitter works. A piano sitter does not pump. That labor difference is why organs feel like furniture you operate and pianos feel like furniture that answers. Both are parlor furniture. The organ’s case often carries more Eastlake or Renaissance Revival ornament because the factory that made the reeds also made a show cabinet: ebonized panels, incised gilt, a music rack, a lamp stand.

A parlor organ against the wall leaves the center table in the middle of the floor. A square piano fights the center table for the same real estate. Houses chose. Inventories sometimes list both. A large double parlor can take a piano in one half and an organ in a sitting room. A bungalow of 1910 will prefer an upright against a wall, or a piano in the alcove Wilson’s book would call a cozy corner. The square is already old-fashioned by then. The reed organ lasts longer in rural parlors because it was paid for and because hymns do not require Steinway.

## The stool

A piano stool is a small, vicious piece of joinery. The seat revolves on a screw — wood or metal — so the sitter can match the keyboard. Tripod bases, four-legged bases, a skirt of upholstery, later a metal claw with glass balls. The Met’s 1824 Chickering stool is rosewood, octagonal, upholstered, a tripod. That is cabinet work matching the instrument. Factory stools of the 1870s–90s are walnut or ebonized, a cast-iron screw, a stuffed round that loses its horsehair and is recovered in needlepoint by a granddaughter. Organ benches are rectangular, often with a lid and a music store inside. They rack at the joints if children stand on them. Estey knew that; restorers still sister the rails.

The stool is the only parlor seating that exists solely for the instrument. It is not in Fitzgerald’s seven-piece set. It does not have a gender in the catalog the way the ladies chair does, though the player often did. Girls were taught. Boys might be. The stool’s height range is the household’s range of bodies. A stool that no longer screws is a stool someone forced.

Castors on piano legs are for shifting the instrument a few inches to clean or to find a wall that sweats less, not for dances. Moving a square piano is a four-person job and a good way to crack a leg. The McLoughlin rule — furniture that folds or rolls — applies to chairs and tables. The piano is the exception that organizes the room around a heavy rectangle.

## Industry without a brochure

Chickering and Steinway were not the only shops. They were the names that made a parlor piano a national product. Boston and New York are the ports. German-trained workers are the labor in both cities: Steinway’s shop culture is immigrant; Chickering’s later workforce is mixed. Actions were made, felt was cut, cases were veneered by people who did not play in the parlor. Enslaved labor does not build these Northern factory instruments, but enslaved households in the South owned pianos bought from the same trade, and enslaved musicians played in some of those rooms. After the war a piano in a Black middle-class parlor is the same form doing the same gentility work. The instrument does not care. The inventory does.

By 1900 the upright has won the small parlor and the grand has won the large one. Player pianos will make the stool’s skill optional. Radios will make the whole argument optional; that is a later slug. The parlor organ becomes church surplus and then antique-mall furniture. Square pianos are so many sideboards in the wrong room — dealers have been known to cut them down. Do not do that and call it history.

## How to look at the room

If the front room has a rectangular case on four legs in the middle of the floor, you are looking at a square piano’s claim, even if the strings are dead. If a tall walnut cabinet with a pump pedal stands on the wall opposite the fireplace, you are looking at a parlor organ’s claim. If there is a revolving stool with a worn screw, someone played. If the center table and the piano both want the middle of the room, the household had to choose every time company came. Casters on the table, not on the piano, tell you which object yielded.

Open the lid, if you are allowed. Rosewood dust and a cracked soundboard are shop facts. A nameboard is a nameboard: Chickering, Steinway, Estey, Prince, a stenciled “New York” that may be a stencil. Serial numbers can be checked against factory lists when the lists survive. Do not invent a concert career from a serial number.

This page owns music furniture in the parlor: square piano, parlor organ, stool. The center table owns the lamp circle. The ladies chair owns the sitter who is not playing. The bungalow living room owns the upright against a stained-wood wall. Steinway Hall is a different building. The parlor is the room where the industry became a piece of furniture with a stool.
