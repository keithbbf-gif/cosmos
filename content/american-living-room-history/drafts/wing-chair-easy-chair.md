# The American easy chair

The wings are not a flourish. They are a wall. Thomas Sheraton, *The Cabinet Dictionary* (1803), describing a stuffed tub chair for sick persons, said the side wings coming forward “keep out the cold air, which may be totally excluded from the person asleep, by laying some kind of covering over the whole chair.” George Hepplewhite, in *The Cabinet-Maker and Upholsterer’s Guide* (1794), plate 15, offered a “Saddle Check, or easy chair” whose use he called apparent, and a gouty stool that raised and lowered at both ends. He recommended leather, horsehair, or a linen case over the canvas stuffing — covers you can wash. An American easy chair of 1740–1800 is that idea in a Boston, Newport, or Philadelphia frame: a fully upholstered chair with cheeks, a loose cushion, and a price that put it in a chamber, not on the parlor carpet as a “wing chair” for guests.

Morrison H. Heckscher’s *In Quest of Comfort: The Easy Chair in America* (Metropolitan Museum of Art, 1971) is the exhibition and catalogue that made the form a subject. It opened 24 November 1971 as the first of the American Wing’s small didactic shows. Heckscher traced the evolution of the form and the framing methods. His *Antiques* essay the same December, “Form and Frame: New Thoughts on the American Easy Chair,” is the shop paper. Winterthur’s chairs and the Chipstone catalogue work of Mark Anderson and Robert F. Trent (1993) are what you look at when the upholstery is off. The modern living room that parks a reproduction wing chair by the hearth is using a chamber object as parlor furniture. That later use is real. It is not the eighteenth-century job.

## Frame first, then hair

The wood you see is often only the legs. Mahogany for a Philadelphia cabriole or a Federal taper. Walnut in some New England frames. The rest — rear posts, wing-and-arm posts, seat rails — is maple, oak, yellow pine, tulip poplar, sometimes beech, chosen to hold tacks and to stay hidden. Heckscher’s point about framing is that the upholsterer and the chairmaker have to agree. The outside of a Philadelphia chair with outsprung C-scroll arms is concave. Stuffing has to follow the scroll, not fight it. Anderson and Trent, looking at stripped frames, describe successive vertical members that tilt a little more to the outside, a gradual splay that makes the arms inviting and the outer panels hard to cover.

Joints are mortise-and-tenon in the seat frame, dowels and nails where the upholsterer will hide them, linen or sackcloth webbing, then curled horsehair stitched into edge rolls. The loose cushion is a boxed pad, often feathers in ticking, sitting on the deck. A tight formal cover — worsted, moreen, silk damask, later haircloth — and, almost as often, a loose case of check, chintz, or linen that can come off. Elisabeth Donaghy Garrett, *At Home: The American Family 1750–1870*, stressed slipcovers for washability and to match a bedchamber’s textiles. Jonathan Dickinson Sergeant’s Philadelphia bedroom inventory of 1793 listed chintz bed curtains, window curtains, chair bottoms, and an easy-chair cover as one expensive textile line, separate from the mahogany easy chair and wash stand.

Brass casters appear as an option in the Philadelphia price books. An invalid chair that must move from fire to window wants them. A ceremonial parlor chair does not need them yet. When you see original leather-wheeled casters on an easy chair, you are looking at a piece that was meant to travel a few feet in a chamber, not to roll into a reception line.

## Boston, Newport, Philadelphia

Boston makes early easy chairs in the Queen Anne years: compass seats, cabriole legs, Spanish feet or pad feet, wings that read as part of a continuous upholstered mass. The wood under the cloth is New England maple more often than the leg wood admits. Newport’s shops — the Townsend-Goddard orbit is a case-furniture fame — also produce seating; an easy chair from that town is a luxury object in a merchant house, the frame as disciplined as a high chest’s cabriole. Philadelphia is the late-colonial climax. Heckscher and the Met labels are blunt about the type. A chair of 1760–90 with a curved front seat rail and flared arms ending in C-scrolls is the classic Philadelphia form. The Met’s example, gift of Mrs. C. F. Dickson, 1963 (object 63.114), is mahogany, walnut, tulip poplar, and yellow pine: 46 by 37 by 28¼ inches as published. A later Federal Philadelphia chair, 1790–1800 (object 1993.31), follows Hepplewhite’s saddle-cheek design and, widened by a padded hole in the seat deck, originally functioned as a commode for an elderly or infirm user. It keeps its original horsehair and muslin foundation. That is the object that teaches how the upholsterer followed the C-scroll instead of burying it.

The Cadwalader chair of 1770 — John Cadwalader’s Philadelphia house, a rush of orders to leading makers — is the earliest datable easy chair Anderson and Trent cite with single-scroll arms and a trapezoidal seat. Thomas Affleck’s name attaches to other Cadwalader seating. Treat shop attributions as the literature states them, not as a blanket. Winterthur’s Philadelphia chairs (including acc. 92.31, 1770–90, and acc. 91.37, 1795–1805) are the frames to read in the Chipstone plates.

## Invalid, aged, lying-in — and the argument

William Macpherson Hornor, Jr., argued in 1931 that easy chairs lived in second-story chambers and never in ground-floor parlors, most of them fitted with close stools. Heckscher endorsed parts of that and added the aged and the ill, citing Copley portraits of older women in easy chairs and the Nicholsons’ “Chair in which the late Duke of York died.” Myrna Kaye, in Brock Jobe and Kaye, *New England Furniture, the Colonial Era* (1984), said the correction had gone too far: not every cabriole easy chair had a pot, and men of means bought prestigious chairs before they needed to doze. Trent’s 1993 Chipstone essay answers her on the evidence. A Charleston easy chair at Winterthur (acc. 60.1058) has mortises for a pot-board. Close stools become more common after 1790, not universal before. Kaye is “factually incorrect but conceptually on target” if the claim is that every easy chair was a sickbed. The chairs were still not parlor lounge furniture for the healthy young.

Philadelphia price books are shop documents. *The Journeymen Cabinet and Chair-Makers Philadelphia Book of Prices* (1795) lists a standard easy chair with plain feet, no low rails, straight front seat rail, and close-stool options: a plank with a hole, a framed seat, or a loose seat. The 1811 Pennsylvania book adds a slider to draw out behind with the pan, and “no lower back rail” so the pan does not hit a stretcher. Norwich’s 1801 price book describes a back that falls with racks and a footboard hinged to the front rail — a full invalid chair. Charles White’s *Treatise on the Management of Pregnant and Lying-In Women* (London 1773; Worcester reprint 1793) illustrates an easy chair with a footboard for women after delivery. Elizabeth Drinker’s diary, 1799, puts her daughter Sally “most of the night in the Easy Chair as it is called” during a hard labor. Garrett’s chamber references sit with those medical ones.

Trent’s inventory sample, Philadelphia 1780–1810, found upholstered sofas almost invariably in ground-floor parlors and easy chairs confined to bedchambers. One hard exception: James Bringhurst, 1810, an easy chair in the south front parlor. One doubtful pair of “easy” chairs in John Keble’s front parlor, 1807, may be a lolling chair and an old Windsor. The sitting-room essay in this series should not steal the easy chair as everyday family seating. A worn stuffed chair in a nineteenth-century sitting room is often a later object. The eighteenth-century easy chair is chamber furniture that later living rooms will misunderstand on purpose.

## How the chair sits in the house

Place the fire, then the bed, then the easy chair. The best-bed essay owns the hangings. The easy chair is the daytime half of the same nursing complex: bed, bed-chair, close stool, gout stool, sometimes a couch. Enslaved or hired labor in a Philadelphia or Newport house carries the slops, changes the case, moves the chair on its casters. The occupant is the person who cannot walk to another room. That is why you put a pot in an expensive stuffed seat you would rather not soil. Trent’s explanation is the practical one: lift the person, remove the cushion, use the pan, sit them down. A slider after 1811 lets you empty the vessel without the lift.

Boston winter makes the wings make sense. Newport damp does too. Philadelphia’s brick chambers still draft. Sheraton’s covering over the whole chair is a tent. I have seen stripped frames in museum storage with the tack holes so dense on the wing’s leading edge that you can read the upholsterer’s nervousness. The living-room reproduction, glued up in maple and covered in a floral that will never be washed, has no such holes. It has a job the old chair was not hired for: look settled by a fireplace in a room where nobody is ill.

## How to look at a wing chair now

Ask where it lived. If the dealer says parlor, ask for the inventory. If the frame has a pothole or mortises for a board, you are not in a reception room. If the legs are fully carved mahogany and the secondary woods are poplar and pine, you may be in Philadelphia. If the cushion is nailed on, someone has lost the loose pad. If the outside back is a different cloth from the wings, you may be looking at a later recover that ignored the case.

Do not confuse this chair with a lolling chair (a New England upholstered open armchair, often parlor or sitting-room), a Morris chair (a later, adjustable living-room form this series will take separately), or a barrel-back Federal easy chair that is already walking toward the nineteenth-century lounge. The 1720–1820 object is a stuffed enclosure. The wings are cheeks. The comfort is medical and geriatric and obstetrical before it is sociable.

A living room may still want one. Put it where a draft is, not where a television is, and do not pretend a caller was meant to perch there in 1770. The permission this series keeps describing — sit on the best furniture — arrives after the easy chair has come downstairs and lost its pot. Until then the best stuffed seat in the house is upstairs, next to the best bed, doing work the parlor suite will never be asked to do.
