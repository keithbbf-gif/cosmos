# Morris chair America

The Victoria and Albert Museum keeps an oak armchair whose back is not a fixed plane. A horizontal rod sits in a pair of racks. Move the rod, and the back falls to a new angle. The cushions on that example are covered in William Morris’s *Violet and Colombine*, a wool-and-mohair cloth first shown in 1883 on the Morris & Co. stand at the Foreign Fair in Boston. The chair itself is older than the cloth. Philip Speakman Webb designed the first version in the 1860s for Morris, Marshall, Faulkner & Co. The firm later sold it as Morris & Co. stock. Americans who met the chair in a catalog after 1900 rarely saw this English object. They saw a factory copy with a different silhouette, a different cushion, and the same promise: a living-room chair that would let a reader tip back without calling a servant to fetch a stool.

That promise is why the Morris chair belongs in this series and not only in an Arts and Crafts monograph. A parlor gentleman’s chair has arms and rank. A Morris chair has a hinge. The difference is mechanical and social. Once the back moves, the best seat in the room is no longer a pose. It is a tool for sitting through a book, a paper, or an evening.

## A Sussex chair, rewritten in London

Warrington Taylor, business manager of Morris, Marshall, Faulkner & Co., saw the idea in 1866 in the workshop of Ephraim Colman, a carpenter at Herstmonceux, Sussex. Taylor sent a sketch to Webb. The V&A still holds that letter in the National Art Library. Webb did not invent the adjustable back. He took a vernacular late-Georgian armchair and made it fit the firm’s oak, turning, and upholstery. The first chairs went into production soon after. William and Jane Morris used one in the drawing room at Kelmscott House. Edward Burne-Jones had another. The design stayed in the catalog into the 1910s. Mahogany versions existed beside the oak.

The English chair is not the American Craftsman block that later dealers photograph first. Webb’s arms and stretchers still speak Victorian turning. The cushions are thick and often buttoned. The adjustment is a rod in hooked racks, not always a row of pegs. What traveled was the diagram: two un-upholstered arms, a hinged back between them, a seat deep enough for a loose cushion, a way to lock the rake. American factories could cut that diagram in oak, birch, or cheap stained hardwood and ship it by the carload.

The Museum of Fine Arts, Houston, holds a related Webb adjustable chair dated about 1870, made by Morris & Co. in London, with a Morris textile on the cushions. The label is honest about the nickname. Webb designed it. The trade called it a Morris chair because the firm sold it. That naming scar never healed. In the United States after 1900, “Morris chair” means the type, not a chair Morris sat in and not, in most cases, a chair Morris & Co. built.

## What the American chair is made of

A factory Morris chair is a frame first. The arms are the thickest members. They have to take the thrust of a sitter who leans and the wear of a rod or a set of pegs. The back is a separate frame, hinged at the seat rail or just above it, with slats or a solid panel behind a cushion. The seat is a drop-in frame, a webbed platform, or, later, a spring box. Early high-end Craftsman chairs used rope or cane in a drop-in seat. Later production wanted a spring cushion that could be shipped and replaced.

Woods follow the market. Gustav Stickley’s Craftsman Workshops at Eastwood, a suburb of Syracuse, preferred quartersawn white oak, ammonia-fumed, with the medullary rays left visible. Chestnut, mahogany, and maple appear in the same catalogs. Grand Rapids factories used oak when the customer paid for oak and birch or gum when the customer paid for a stain. Arms may be flat, bowed in a laminated or steam-bent curve, or sloped. The bow-arm is the American signature that English Webb chairs do not share. A three-board bow, reverse-tapered legs, and pyramidal back pegs mark the earliest Stickley bow-arms, including model 2340, made about 1901. Model 336, a later bow-arm, is in the 1904 catalog. Auction houses publish those numbers because collectors need them. A living-room historian needs the joint more than the number: through-tenons pinned, or doweled frames that only look pinned.

The back moves in two common ways. One is a wooden rod that drops into a series of notches or saddles cut into the inner faces of the arms. The other is a metal bar that catches in hooked racks, closer to the English hardware. Pegs through the arms, some pyramidal on early Stickley work, hold the back’s pivot. You can read use on those parts. The notch that is polished darker than its neighbors is the household’s preferred rake. A cracked arm at the last notch is a sitter who wanted more angle than the timber would give.

Cushions are not upholstery in the parlor-suite sense. They are loose. Leather, green or brown cloth, and later tapestry sit on the seat and hang over the back. The frame stays visible. That is the Craftsman argument against the overstuffed sofa that will occupy the next essay: show the wood, keep the stuffing movable, let the chair be cleaned and recovered without ripping a suite apart.

## Eastwood, then the rest of the map

Gustav Stickley founded his company at Eastwood in 1898. In 1901 he published the first issue of *The Craftsman*. The issue is an homage to William Morris, written largely by Irene Sargent. Stickley took Morris’s motto *Als ik kan* and a joiner’s compass as a trademark. The chair that Americans now call a Stickley Morris chair is an homage in oak, not a licensed Morris & Co. product. David Cathers and the Dallas Museum of Art’s Stickley exhibition literature treat the early bow-arms as experimental shop work that then settled into catalog production. The Craftsman Workshops made flat-arm and bent-arm versions as well. Leopold Stickley and J. George Stickley, working as L. & J. G. Stickley, made their own adjustable chairs that borrow a bow from one brother and a slat from another. Albert Stickley’s Stickley Brothers Company in Grand Rapids sold “Quaint” furniture that included Morris chairs for a different price and a different finish.

The Grand Rapids story is volume. Christian Carron’s *Grand Rapids Furniture* is the civic monograph for a city that shipped case goods and chairs to every railroad town. Morris chairs were in that stream by the 1890s. They were carved, serpentine, and often more Victorian than Craftsman. Horner and other high-end names made expensive versions. Sears, Roebuck and Montgomery Ward sold cheap ones. The Larkin Soap Company of Buffalo put Morris chairs into premium catalogs as premiums — a chair for soap wrappers — which is as American a distribution method as a Stickley showroom. `[CITE NEEDED: a dated Larkin premium catalog page that prices or pictures a Morris chair by that name.]` Charles Limbert in Grand Rapids and Holland, Michigan, ran Jugendstil curves through the same adjustable diagram. Prairie offices — Frank Lloyd Wright’s and Purcell & Elmslie’s boxier chairs — used the high arm and the deep seat without always keeping the rod. The living-room reader did not require a trademark. The reader required a back that moved.

Factory labor is not the Morris shop romance. Eastwood was a factory with a magazine. Grand Rapids was piecework, finishing rooms, and immigrant upholsterers who sewed the loose cushions that the catalog called “leather” or “art fabric.” Women in those shops did a large share of the sewing. Men cut and framed. The adjustable chair looks handmade because the arms are thick. The thickness is also a jig: the same arm pattern, repeated, with a rod hole drilled in the same place. A change that adds comfort while leaving the shop larger and faster is still a factory change.

## Where it sat

Henry L. Wilson’s *Bungalow Book* (1910) wants a living room that is warm, used, and glad to be turned to. The Morris chair is the piece that makes that sentence physical. It stands near a lamp, a window, or a hearth, often at an angle to the sofa rather than in a matched pair. In a Craftsman living room the chair faces the fire the way a settle once did, but it is a single person’s claim, not a bench. In a Colonial Revival living room the same chair can look like an interloper next to a Windsor and a gateleg. Households kept it anyway because the Windsor does not recline.

The chair is not a medical wing chair. The wing-chair essay in this series owns the sickroom and the draft. The Morris chair is a well person’s tool. It is also not a later recliner. La-Z-Boy’s patent furniture is a different mechanism and a different century’s living room. Do not read a 1970s recliner backward onto a 1904 Stickley. The Morris chair still shows its wood. The recliner will swallow it.

Servants, where they still existed after 1900, did not carry this chair to the fire. It is too heavy, and it is meant to stay. What they did was plump the loose cushions and move the rod for a guest who did not know the notches. In houses without staff, the reader does that work. Lillian Hart Tyron’s parlorless nation is partly a nation that stopped keeping a show chair no one sat in and started keeping a chair that wore at the arm.

## How to look at one in the room you have

Stand beside the arm, not in front of the cushion. Count the adjustment stops. A row of four or five clean notches is catalog work. A single deep groove and two unused notches is a household that found its angle and stayed. Look at the hinge pegs. Early Stickley pyramidal pegs are a dating tell if the rest of the chair agrees; a reproduction can fake a pyramid. Look under the seat. Rope or cane in a drop-in frame is early or careful. A spring box with a factory stencil is later and more common. Look at the wood. Quartersawn oak with flake is the Craftsman claim. Flatsawn oak with a dark stain is Grand Rapids or mail-order. Neither is a moral category. Both sat in living rooms.

If the arms are fully upholstered and the back is a hidden ratchet, you have left the Morris type. If the back is fixed, you have a settle or an easy chair that borrowed the name. Dealers will call a wide oak armchair a Morris chair because the word sells. The word, in this series, means the hinge.

A later woods essay can take a reader’s chair with an adjustable back and no hidden mechanism. The historical woods here are English oak and mahogany first, then American oak, chestnut, and whatever the factory could stain.

The living-room reader’s chair is the Morris chair’s American job. Webb’s rod made the job possible. Eastwood and Grand Rapids made it ordinary. A parlor that would not let you tip back has ended when this chair is the one that faces the lamp.
