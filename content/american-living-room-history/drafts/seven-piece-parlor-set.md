# The Victorian parlor set

Seven frames, one carving. An armchair with full arms for the husband. A smaller chair with no arms, or short ones, for the wife, because the dress and the rank both needed the space. A sofa for the guest to sit beside the host. Four side chairs for everyone else. Oscar P. Fitzgerald, *Three Centuries of American Furniture*, is blunt about the mid-century kit, and Rancho Los Cerritos, citing him, put the sentence in a parlor interpretation that is still useful if you ignore its later 1910/1918 stitch. By the 1870s a household that wanted a parlor wanted those seven pieces to match: the same crest, the same cabriole or turned leg, the same haircloth or plush. Factory furniture made the match possible in towns that had never seen John Henry Belter’s laminated rosewood. Belter is another slug. This page owns the set as a social object and as a shipping crate.

The Illinois State Museum’s Hill family set, Bloomington, about 1865 — walnut, Rococo and Renaissance Revival mixed, reproduction upholstery — is the type in a documented house. William Hill was a physician. The Museum’s old label said even the simplest sets cost about $100, out of reach of most wage earners, with price varying by cloth and tufting. I repeat that figure as the museum’s figure, not as a price book I have in my hand. `[CITE NEEDED: a Grand Rapids or Cincinnati catalogue page, 1860–1880, listing a seven-piece parlor suite with the published dollar amounts.]`

## What matches, and how it is made

The match is the point. A Federal parlor could mix a Seymour chair and a New York sofa. A Victorian parlor set is one design cut into seven silhouettes. The gentleman’s chair is the sofa’s arm end turned into a throne. The lady’s chair is the gentleman’s chair without the arms, or with a low pad that will not crush a skirt. The side chairs are the lady’s chair reduced again. Sit in them in order and you can feel rank in the width of the seat.

Woods: rosewood when the household is performing at the New York level; walnut when Grand Rapids and Cincinnati have their way; mahogany as a respectable middle; oak later and less often for this particular kit. Secondary woods are pine and chestnut and whatever the factory had in the yard. Veneer on the rails, carving on the crest — grapes, roses, a cartouche, a Renaissance shield as the 1860s turn. Rococo Revival (about 1840–65) wants C- and S-scrolls, cabriole legs, flowers. Renaissance Revival (about 1860–80) wants turned legs, architectural crests, more walnut than rosewood. Transitional sets exist because factories did not change knives on a historian’s schedule. John Mark Power’s working notes on a set of about 1860–80 are typical: Rococo outline, Renaissance crest, machine-simplified carving with some hand work still required.

Joints are factory joints. Dowels begin to replace the mortise-and-tenon that a Phyfe rail took for granted. Glue blocks, screws, and a carved crest that is doweled or bolted to the back. Laminated rosewood — Belter, Meeks, the New York shops that steamed veneers in a mold — is a special case this series will take on its own page. Most seven-piece sets are not laminated. They are carved from the solid or from glued-up blocks, then the same pattern is repeated. Casters on every front foot: the parlor must clear for a reception, a dance, a viewing. The death-room essay owns the last of those uses. The casters are why a set that looks immovable will roll.

Upholstery is horsehair first, black or dark, tufted or plain, slick so a sitter slides forward into posture. Springs enter mid-century factory seats and change the sit. Plush, needlepoint, and a later velvet that pills are the sitting-room contaminations. A parlor set that still has its haircloth has been protected. A parlor set recovered in a floral chintz has been loved in a living room. Webbing, stitching, and a muslin foundation are the same trade that stuffed the easy chair, now working at piece rates.

## New York, Grand Rapids, Cincinnati

New York is the fashion city and, for a while, the shop city. Rosewood suites with high-relief carving come out of Manhattan and Brooklyn workshops in the 1840s and 1850s. Some are one-off. Some are already multiples. The name on the crest is less often a cabinetmaker’s stamp than a retailer’s. I will not invent a suite for a named Astor parlor. The Met’s Rococo Revival parlor — Whittemore House woodwork from Astoria, about 1850, Belter furniture from elsewhere — is the living-room essay’s opening room. It shows the idea of a parlor, not a factory packing list.

Cincinnati industrializes the set earlier than the East likes to admit. Mitchell & Rammelsberg, founded in 1847, became one of the large Midwestern factories, steam power, division of labor, catalogues, river and rail. A walnut suite from that city can be as “Victorian parlor” as a New York rosewood one, and cheaper to land in Indiana. `[CITE NEEDED: a Mitchell & Rammelsberg catalogue plate of a numbered parlor suite, 1850s–1870s.]` The factory floor is men at saws and carvers at benches, not a romantic shop of twelve. Women’s labor in upholstery shops is real and poorly signed.

Grand Rapids after the Civil War is the name that swallows the others. Berkey & Gay, Phoenix, Nelson-Matter, and a dozen firms that the city directories sort out and the later collector collapses into “Grand Rapids.” Railroad cars take matched sets west and south. The carving is competent and repeated. The walnut is American. The gentleman’s chair is still a throne. A town that never had a Belter can have a parlor. That is Fitzgerald’s industrial point even when he is writing about style. Clark’s *American Family Home* is the house-side of the same fact: a middle-class claim to civilization made with furniture that factories had cheapened just enough.

Do not flatten every set into Grand Rapids. A New York laminated suite and a Cincinnati walnut suite and a Grand Rapids catalogue suite are three economies. They share the seven-part kit. They do not share a carver.

## Rank in the front room

Godey’s face of the house still applies. The set is the face’s teeth. The American Heritage parlor essay (1963) is later and snide and useful: you entered rarely, you were not meant to be comfortable, every house had to have the room. Formal calls, tea, the ten minutes before dinner, the proposal, the death. The gentleman’s chair is where the husband is placed if the family is performing the diagram. The lady’s chair is where the wife is placed. Guests on the sofa. Children on the side chairs, or not in the room. Frances Trollope in Cincinnati in the late 1820s saw men and women herd to opposite parts of the room — a little early for the full seven-piece kit, already right about the sexes. The set hardens that geography in walnut.

Servants move the chairs. In 1840s New York that may still include free Black labor and Irish hired girls. In a Southern house receiving a Grand Rapids shipment, enslaved workers may still be doing the carrying into the 1860s. After the war the labor is hired or family. Someone polishes the rosewood when no one is coming. Someone rolls the sofa to the wall when someone has died. The sitting-room essay owns the worn easy chair and the sewing table that never got a matching crest. The parlor set is what that sitting room is not allowed to be.

Downing’s center table, 1850, sits in the middle of the seven pieces as the emblem of the family circle. The circle is theoretical. The table holds the album. The chairs hold the ranks. A household that actually sits in a circle at that table on a Wednesday has already begun to turn the parlor into a sitting room. Wear on the sofa’s front rail is the tell the living-room essay ended on. A set that was only a face does not wear there.

## The kit loosens, 1880–1890

Eastlake and the “artistic” parlor attack the set without immediately abolishing the count. Furniture becomes, in the American Heritage essay’s words, solid, dowelled, and “sincere.” The spirit of the room stays formal. *House Beautiful* in 1898 will call the old drawing room an over-decorated horror. Stickley’s settle, the Craftsman living room, Wilson’s bungalow, Tyron’s parlorless nation — those are the next slugs. What happens to the seven pieces is ordinary and brutal. They are split. The sofa goes to a later living room and gets new springs. The gentleman’s chair becomes a desk chair. The lady’s chair is sold. The four side chairs turn up around a card table. Dealers now offer “a three-piece parlor set” and mean they lost the chairs.

Colonial Revival will replace the kit with Windsors and a gateleg and call the result Early American. That is a different face. The factory that made the walnut suite will make the overstuffed davenport the 1920s living room wants. Same city, sometimes the same firm, a different permission: sit, slump, stay.

## How to look at seven pieces, or fewer

Count first. Sofa, gentleman, lady, four small. If you have six, you are missing a side chair; if you have five, you may have a “parlor set” that was never the full kit — some factories sold five-piece and three-piece groups. Look at the crests. The same carving in seven sizes is the factory’s pride. Look at the arms. Full on one chair, absent on the next: that is gender in walnut, not a repair. Look at the casters. Brass or porcelain, all present, all matching: the set was meant to move as a set.

Turn a side chair over. Dowels and a pine seat frame are right for 1870. A fully hand-cut mortise in rosewood may be New York and earlier. Laminated layers in the crest send you to the Belter page. Walnut with a Grand Rapids brand or a stenciled number is the Midwest story; photograph the stencil.

Sit in the lady’s chair if the museum or the owner will let you. You will feel the missing arms as a freedom for a skirt and as a demotion. Sit in the gentleman’s chair. You will feel the arms as a fence. That fence is the parlor. The living room that inherits one chair from the set and puts it by a television has broken the kit, which is historically correct. Tyron said we ourselves have changed. The change is visible in the empty space where the other six pieces were.

This series is living rooms. The seven-piece set is the last furniture that refuses the name. It wants a face, a call, a rank. When the household decides the best seats may be used, the set has to be broken, recovered, or banished. Look at the front rail. If it is sharp, you are in the parlor. If it is dull, someone finally sat down.
