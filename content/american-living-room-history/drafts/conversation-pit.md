# Conversation-pit furniture

The living room of the Miller House in Columbus, Indiana, is 30 by 40 feet, under a band of skylights, with a cylindrical fireplace shaft and a fifty-foot storage wall. In the western part of that room the floor drops. The drop is a square, 15 feet on a side, lined with seating and marble coping. The ceiling stays at 8 1/2 feet; the pit floor is 1 1/2 feet below the travertine. You go down on steps — five in the house as documented for the National Historic Landmark file, four in some later magazine counts. There is no railing in the original. The cushions and the colored pillows are the furniture. The architecture is the sofa.

Eero Saarinen was the architect. Alexander Girard was the interior designer. Dan Kiley did the garden. Kevin Roche was Saarinen’s associate. J. Irwin Miller, who led Cummins, and Xenia Simons Miller commissioned the house after they bought the Highland Way site in September 1953. Taylor Brothers began construction in 1955. The family moved in during March 1957. Those are the dates this page will keep. The house is a National Historic Landmark, given with most of its furnishings to the Indianapolis Museum of Art (now Newfields) in 2009 and opened to tours in May 2011. It is the object a later American “conversation pit” wants to be, and almost never is.

## A room within a room

Girard is the name most careful accounts attach to the pit. He had used a related sunken seat in his own house. He built a mockup and put it in a New York storefront so strangers could sit in it. Architectural drawings for Columbus credit Saarinen and Girard together. Roche later said the Millers wanted a house that could take family gatherings and that a conversation pit was among the features they wanted. Do not embroider that into parlor gossip. The documents disagree on who spoke first. The pit exists. The mockup existed. The house was a year-round residence for five children, not a weekend glass box, which is why Saarinen could not simply copy Farnsworth or the Glass House.

The point, in the designers’ terms, was to sit without a slum of legs. Saarinen used that phrase for the undercarriage of ordinary chairs and tables when he talked about his pedestal furniture. In Columbus the phrase becomes architecture. Freestanding sofas would have cluttered the 30-by-40 room and blocked the view to Kiley’s allées. The pit makes a second, smaller room whose horizon is marble and textile. Blair Kamin later called it a room within a room. *Architectural Forum* published the house in 1958 and described the lounge pit. *House & Garden* made it a Hallmark House in 1959. After that, the form was available to anyone who could afford to change a floor.

Cushions are loose, in Girard’s seasonal logic. Accounts differ on whether the house ran two palettes or four; they agree that slipcovers and pillows changed with the year. Summer could go to a grass mat and lighter cloth. Winter could go deep red. The living-room carpet Girard designed carries emblems of family interests. A grand piano, the shaft of the fire, and the storage wall were the standing objects. From the pit you saw the scarlet underside of the piano. That is a designer’s joke you can still verify on a tour. It is not a story about the clients’ private lives.

Steps and marble coping are the joinery. The seating is built-in upholstery, sprung or padded on a carpenter’s frame, covered in cloth that can be removed. No casters. No clips. You do not rearrange a pit. You rearrange pillows. Elderly guests later had trouble getting out. In 1994 Roche added a handrail. The museum took the rail off again to restore the original section. Those two acts are the pit’s whole mechanical history: it does not move, so people must.

Servants kept the house working. The Millers employed a cook, a handyman, a personal assistant for Xenia, and a gardening crew. The plan includes a servant’s bedroom and a kitchen with two counters. A conversation pit in a house with staff is not a democratic hole. It is a luxury of unused floor and of people who clean around a drop. Tract sunken living rooms will copy the drop and skip the staff.

## What the pit is not

It is not the first sunken seat in America. Bruce Goff’s Adah Robinson House in Tulsa is often listed among earlier or parallel experiments. Treat that as a separate object with its own citations if an editor wants a prehistory. It is not Saarinen’s TWA terminal lounge, which is a public building. It is not a 1970s foam pit group that sits on a level floor and wraps a conversation by upholstery alone. The pit-group essay owns that portable cousin.

It is not a family room. The Miller House has zones — parents, children, guests, service — pinwheeling off the central living room. Children slept in small rooms around a playroom. They also, the children’s later interviews say, used the pit for sleepovers. That is a use, not a redesign. The living room remains the ceremonial center. A suburban sunken living room of 1968 that sits three steps below the foyer is trying to buy this ceremony with a change in slab elevation.

Do not invent what the Millers said to each other about guests, politics, or taste. Irwin wrote Girard in 1957 that he would allow one press event only if location, cost, and owners stayed secret. *House & Garden* published pictures anyway; the local paper then named the house. Those are published facts. Lady Bird Johnson stayed overnight in 1967. That is a published fact. Leave the rest.

## Sunken living rooms, 1958–1975

Once the Miller House was in the magazines, builders and custom architects had a name and a picture. A conversation pit in a 1960s custom house might be a square of built-in benches around a metal fire hood, upholstered in orange nylon, with shag on the steps. A developer’s sunken living room might be a whole rectangle dropped 12 or 16 inches, with ordinary sofas carried down the steps and a railing a lawyer liked. The first is furniture built as architecture. The second is a ranch living room with a broken ankle.

California and the Southwest took the idea easily because indoor-outdoor floors were already in play. Midwestern and northeastern split-levels sometimes treated the lower front room as a sunken living room even when it was only the split. The word pit should be reserved for a seating well inside a larger room, not for any room on a lower level. Family rooms with built-in benches around a brick hearth are a vernacular pit. They share the Miller House’s wish to talk without a coffee table in the way. They do not share Girard’s pillows or Kiley’s allée.

By the mid-1970s the custom pit is already a period cliché: hard to vacuum, hard to leave, hard to sell. Households fill them with a flat floor and a sectional. That fill is the most common last act of the form. The 1970s pit group — modular foam on the old level — is a way to keep the conversation and lose the hole.

## How to look at a drop in the floor

Measure first. A 15-foot square with seating on all four sides is a true pit in the Miller sense. A living room three steps down from a foyer, with a conventional sofa and a coffee table, is a sunken room, not a pit. Look at the coping. Marble or wood that you can sit on is designed as furniture. A drywall knee-wall is a later enclosure. Look at the steps. Worn carpet on the nosing is use. A rail that does not match the rest of the ironwork is an aging household or a code officer.

If the cushions are the only furniture and the piano is the only leg in the room, you are very close to Columbus. If there is a coffee table in the hole, the household did not trust the idea. If the hole has been floored over, look in the basement for the original drop. Builders rarely erase a pit without leaving a joist story.

The Miller House pit is a living-room furniture type made of slate, marble, cloth, and a change in slab. Saarinen and Girard built it for a specific family between 1953 and 1957. American rooms that followed took the photograph and the name. Most of them kept too many legs. The ones that did not are the ones this slug is for.
