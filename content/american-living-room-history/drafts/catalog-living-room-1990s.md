# The catalog living room of the 1990s

Open a Pottery Barn catalog from the middle 1990s and the living room has already been cleaned for you. A slipcovered sofa in a color the trade joked was furniture khaki. A sisal rug. A coffee table that looks like a recovered factory door or a simple farm plank. White walls, or walls the color of clay. A throw that implies a body without showing one. No Victorian clutter, no pit-group velvet, no Danish teak unless it has been paled into a “natural” stain. The room is a photograph of ease. The sofa is a cotton duck envelope over a frame you are not invited to see.

This page is about that photograph as a furniture type, not as a store to visit. Pottery Barn, Crate & Barrel, and Restoration Hardware — three catalogs with different biographies — taught a generation of living rooms the same lesson: look collected, buy matched, wash the cover. The lesson is a period style, as dated as Belter once it is named. It furnished the great room and the last unused parlor alike.

## Three floors, one pale room

Pottery Barn began in 1949, when Paul and Morris Secon bought second-run Glidden pottery stored in barns in Alfred, New York, and sold it from a Chelsea storefront. The name is literal. Gap owned the chain briefly; Williams-Sonoma bought it in 1986. The mail-order catalog that matters here was first published in 1987. That date is the start of this essay’s living room. Hilary Billings, director of the Pottery Barn catalog, told the *Chicago Tribune* in May 1996 why slipcovers had become the line: a trendy look; a way to change a room without buying a new frame; and wine. “When you spill red wine on the couch, you pull the slipcover off and wash it.” The paper reported a sofa-size slipcover package in the $999 to $1,199 range, cover included with the sofa, and a one-size-fits-all elastic cover at $275. The *Spokesman-Review* in January 1997 found polar-fleece slipcovers in gray or white on the Dakota line — $549 for a sofa cover, $1,399 for cover and sleeper. Those figures are period prices, not invitations. They locate the object in a middle-class living room that could write a check after a catalog evening.

Crate & Barrel is older as a store and adjacent as a look. Gordon and Carole Segal opened on December 7, 1962, in 1,700 square feet of a former elevator factory at 1516 North Wells Street, Chicago, Old Town. Crating lumber on the walls, barrels as display — the name, again, is literal. European glass and copper first; furniture later. The 1990s catalogs sold a cleaner modernism than Pottery Barn’s farmhouse khaki: tighter sofas, more chrome and pale wood, a living room that still admitted a Danish cousin. The two books landed on the same coffee table. Households mixed them.

Restoration Hardware is the nostalgic third. Stephen Gordon, restoring a Victorian in Eureka, California, in 1979, could not find hardware he trusted. He opened a store in 1980. David Brooks’s *New Yorker* piece of January 25, 1999, is the period portrait: a shop organized around a life, not a category, Louis Armstrong on the system, objects that ransack childhood tactile memory. Brooks notes that hardware was only seventeen percent of sales; furniture was thirty. The living-room version is a club chair that wants to be a memory of a club chair, a table that wants to be older than it is, a finish that photographs as “found.” By 1998 the company was public, with dozens of stores. The catalog living room absorbed the nostalgia without absorbing Eureka.

A *SmartMoney* piece of the early 2000s — the scanned pages that still circulate among retailers — called the mid-1990s slipcovered sofa “the furniture of khakis.” Twentysomethings, the copy said, did not want stodgy Drexel. They wanted denim slipcovers. That sentence is rude and accurate. The living room dressed the way the owners dressed: cotton, khaki, a belt off. Restoration Hardware, in the same comparison, is the more expensive cousin — eight-way talk, Thai silk, a price that leaves Pottery Barn’s two-thousand-dollar sofa looking like the sensible choice. Do not take the magazine’s prices as a catalog. Take the ranking. Three stores, one pale room, different pretenses of age.

## Slipcovers, frames, and the staged room

The slipcover is the form. A separate textile skin, washable or dry-cleanable, over a simple upholstered body. The frame underneath is often hardwood or engineered wood with sinuous springs — the how-to-read-a-sofa essay will teach the underside. The 1990s catalog rarely taught that underside. It taught the envelope: white, natural, denim, sage, the khaki that hid dust in a sunlit great room. Welting is optional. Skirts return, not the Victorian fringe skirt but a tailored drop that hides cheap legs. When the legs show, they are maple or a maple stain, square, a Craftsman memory without Stickley’s joints.

Construction is factory, often North Carolina or a contractor plant, knockdown legs in the carton. The catalog photograph conceals the carton. What you see is a room that appears to have been assembled over years: a basket, a wooden bowl, a fake botanical, a book whose spine is turned for color. That staging is the other form this essay names. Department-store rooms of the 1950s had staged too. The 1990s catalog made the staging portable. You could order the bowl. The living room became a set you could restock after a spill and after a move.

Sisal and sea-grass rugs sand the floor. Pine and “distressed” coffee tables sand the history. Iron candlesticks sand the fireplace. The Colonial Revival living room of 1920 had also invented age. This is a later invention: age without carving, comfort without a parlor set, class without gilt. Oscar Fitzgerald’s parlor kit — armchair, lady’s chair, sofa, four chairs — is unthinkable here. The kit is a sofa, a chair that does not match too obviously, an ottoman that is also a tray.

Labor is warehouse and call center as much as sewing room. Someone in a plant boxed the frame. Someone in a photograph studio steamed the slipcover. Someone in the house pulled the cover off after the wine, or did not, and lived with the stain the catalog had promised to erase. Servants are not in this picture. The washable cover is their ghost.

## What the look is not

It is not Arts and Crafts, though it borrowed the square leg and the mission stain. Stickley’s living room was a joint and a settle. The catalog living room is a cover. The Craftsman essay in this series owns the earlier oak.

It is not a pit group. The body sits up. You do not take your shoes off as a rule, though the sisal will punish you if you do not. Children are allowed; that is the point of the washable skin. The formality is informal. That paradox is the style.

It is not Danish modern, even when a Wishbone appears in a later photograph. Design Within Reach (1999) is the revival store that sold modern by catalog after this khaki hour was already running. A 2004 living room might add a Wegner chair to the slipcovered sofa. That mix is a date. Do not read it back onto 1992.

It is not a sales floor in this series. The stores still exist, renamed or not. This page stops at about 2010, when the pale room began to darken toward gray sectionals and restoration-as-luxury. The object to study is the 1990s catalog spread and the living rooms that copied it without buying every object on the page — a white slipcover from one book, a table from a garage, a great-room vault the builder had already painted builder-white.

## How to look at the khaki sofa

Pull the cover if it will come. Look at the frame the catalog hid. Sinuous springs and a stapled deck are honest factory work; they are not eight-way hand-tie just because the chair is expensive. A Pottery Barn or Crate label, a lot number, a care tag that mentions dry-clean only on a “washable” cover — those are the documents. A skirt that hides particleboard feet is an alteration of the photograph’s intent or a cheaper tier of the same look.

Wear shows on the front of the seat cushion and on the arm where a body leaves a chair. Slipcovers wear at the welts and at the wash. A cover that no longer fits the frame has been washed in heat; that is a history, not a defect to scold. If the living room still has a sisal rug bleached by a slider and a coffee table with ring marks in the “distress,” you are in the period. If a gray sectional has replaced the khaki and the catalog is in a recycling stack, the hour ended. The great room is still there. The envelope changed.

Stand where the photographer stood: from the corner, sofa three-quarter, window light, no television in the frame. Then turn around and look at the actual wall. The catalog omitted the screen. The household did not. The living room of the 1990s is the staged room plus the object the staging would not admit. Read both.
