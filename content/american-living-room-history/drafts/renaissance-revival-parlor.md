# Renaissance Revival parlor furniture

Historic New England’s Renaissance Revival armchairs from Roseland Cottage, Woodstock, Connecticut, dated 1870–1880 in the file, are walnut, leather-seated, 39 3/4 inches high. The crest is a slanted pediment with a carved shell, two rectangular burl lozenges, and two turned button medallions. Stiles carry incised geometric work and exterior corner brackets. The splat is vase-shaped with an applied burl shield. Front legs are turned and end in turnip feet. Stretchers are vase-and-ring. Accession 1970.495.1 and .2. The matching side chairs (1970.495.3–.5) keep the pediment and take half arms. That is not Belter’s rose. It is architecture reduced to a chair: pediment, panel, incised line, a burl that stands in for marble.

Renaissance Revival in American parlor furniture runs roughly 1860–1885, overlapping the last Rococo suites and the first Eastlake reforms. The dining-room series next door owns the big walnut sideboards with broken pediments and portrait medallions. This page stays on parlor tables and chairs. A sideboard in a sitting room is a house that has lost a wall, not a parlor form.

## After the curve

Rococo Revival had made the parlor a garden of C-scrolls. Renaissance Revival makes it a small palazzo. The references are sixteenth-century — or what New York and Grand Rapids understood as sixteenth-century after looking at pattern books and at the French and German work on the exhibition circuit. Straight stiles replace cabriole legs on many chairs. Crests become pediments, scrolls become carved heads or shields, fruit becomes a controlled swag. Incising — a V-cut line, sometimes filled with gilt — is cheaper than piercing twenty-one layers of rosewood and reads as “designed” in a factory catalog.

Walnut is the wood. Black walnut, often with burl walnut panels and overlays. Rosewood still appears at the top of the market. Ebonized details — a turned drop, a line of black — anticipate Eastlake without being Eastlake. Marble tops stay on center tables and on some parlor cabinets. The marble is now a slab on a more architectural pedestal: square dies, incised panels, a carved urn.

Herter Brothers in New York could make a Renaissance Revival parlor that was furniture as interior architecture. Pottier & Stymus the same. Those shops also worked in other revival languages in the same years; a house might have a Renaissance library and a lighter parlor, or the reverse. Downing’s 1850 rule that each room could be a discrete style is still in force. The Met’s Whittemore notes quote him preferring Gothic for libraries and dining rooms and Modern French for parlors. By 1870 the parlor itself could go Renaissance and still be the best room. `[CITE NEEDED: a dated American advice manual of the 1870s that explicitly prefers Renaissance Revival for the parlor over Modern French.]`

Factory versions from Cincinnati and Grand Rapids — Mitchell & Rammelsberg, the later Michigan plants — shipped walnut parlor sets with the same pediment idea sawn and glued. The Roseland Cottage chairs are the form a prosperous New England house actually sat on: leather, incising, burl, not a Herter invoice. Rancho Los Cerritos’s 1870s parlor mixes revivals on purpose. The parlor table and a maroon shield-back armchair, original to the site and dated 1865–1880 in their essay, are Renaissance Revival. The sofa and lady’s chair in the same room run Rococo. Households did not change suites overnight. They added a table, recovered a chair, bought four new side chairs. The style name is a historian’s convenience.

## Tables that are not sideboards

A Renaissance Revival parlor table is still a center table in job: lamp height, often marble, often casters, a place for the album. The difference is the base. Four turned or square legs with stretchers and a shelf; a pedestal with incised panels; carved heads at the knees instead of dolphins. The Chrysler Meeks table is still Rococo. A walnut table of 1870 with a square marble and a lower shelf for books is Renaissance in the parlor sense. The lower shelf is useful and it is a show of walnut.

Parlor cabinets and étagères in this taste grow architectural frames: columns, pediments, a glazed door if the objects are better than dust. The whatnot essay owns the open-shelf form. Here the point is that the same appetite for display now wants an aedicule — a little building — rather than a Rococo vine. Kimbel & Cabus will soon make that building Modern Gothic and ebonized. Renaissance Revival is the walnut middle.

Do not drag a dining-room sideboard into this paragraph to pad the style. Sideboards of these years are the most spectacular Renaissance Revival objects many museums own. They are also dining furniture: height for plate, a surface for service, a room with a table you eat at. A parlor cabinet that looks like a small sideboard is still a parlor cabinet if it stood against the sitting-room wall and held figurines. Inventories decide. If the inventory is gone, say so.

## Chairs, sets, and the social diagram

The ladies chair and the gentleman’s chair continue. The arms still mark rank. The difference is the silhouette. A Renaissance ladies chair may have a rectangular back and a pediment instead of a balloon. Half arms, as at Roseland, are a documented compromise. Leather seats — the Roseland set — are more durable than silk and less “French.” They also read as masculine in later taste, which is a later problem. In 1875 leather on a parlor chair is a practical show cover.

Coil springs remain in the better seats. Frames are dowelled more often as factory practice spreads. Turnip feet and porcelain casters. A suite might include a sofa with a triple-arch back, each arch a small pediment, the arms padded, the crest carved with a head or a shield. Transitional sets of about 1860 keep cabriole front legs and add a Renaissance crest. Conservators see those marriages constantly. John Mark Power’s notes on a ca. 1860 set are typical shop talk: Rococo lines, later crest, machine-simplified carving. Call them transitional if both languages are in the wood. Do not call them confused.

Grand Rapids could print a parlor set in “Renaissance” the way it had printed “French.” The names in the catalog are sales names. The chairs still arrive as gent’s, lady’s, sofa, four small. Fitzgerald’s kit survives the change of ornament. What changes is the carving budget. Incising is a router’s cousin. A boy can learn the line. Pierced laminated rosewood is a specialist’s day.

## Labor and the walnut years

Walnut logging and veneer-cutting are Midwest and Appalachian facts as much as New York shop facts. Burl walnut is scarce enough to be reserved for panels. A parlor set with four burl lozenges per chair is a claim on that scarcity. Factory towns — Grand Rapids above all — employed immigrant labor, much of it from the same German-speaking world that had staffed Belter’s New York. The proprietor’s name on a Renaissance parlor chair is less often a Belter and more often a company. The carver is still there, on the crest if nowhere else.

Servants still move the chairs. The pedimented armchair is no lighter than the Rococo one. Casters still matter for a dance if the house still dances in the parlor. By the 1870s some households have a separate sitting room that takes the daily wear, and the Renaissance parlor is already on the way to being a room you shut. Clark’s family home is adding rooms and losing patience. The furniture gets more architectural as the room gets less used. That is not a law. It is a tendency you can see when an 1880 parlor has perfect walnut and un-worn front rails.

Southern houses after the war buy this furniture with different money and different staff. The style is national. The labor is local.

## How to look at a parlor in walnut

Look for the pediment. If the crest is a triangular or scrolled architectural cap with incising and a burl panel, you are in this language even if a dealer says Victorian and stops. Look at the legs. Turned or square and architectural: Renaissance. Cabriole: Rococo or a hybrid. Look at the wood. Walnut and burl are the tell; rosewood piercing is the older shop.

Measure the table height before you call it a library table or a coffee table. Thirty inches of walnut and marble in the middle of a room is still Downing’s emblem, dressed as a palazzo. If the table has a lower shelf with a stain the shape of a kerosene base, the lamp sat there. If the chairs have half arms and a matching armchair, Fitzgerald’s diagram is intact.

Do not use this page to identify dining chairs with leather seats and stretchers. Dining chairs can share the ornament. They stood at a table with crumbs. Parlor chairs stood in a circle around a center table or against a wall. The wear is different. The dining series will take the sideboard and the extension table. Leave them there.

This page owns Renaissance Revival parlor seating and parlor tables. Belter owns the laminated years just before. Eastlake owns the reform just after, when the incised line becomes a moral. If you find a walnut pediment in a room that still has a marble-top center table, you are in the twenty years when the parlor could not decide whether it was a garden or a civic building. It sat people either way.
