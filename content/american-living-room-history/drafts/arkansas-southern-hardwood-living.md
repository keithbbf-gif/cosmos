# Arkansas hardwoods in the living room

A quartersawn white-oak settle arm in a Southern living room shows its business on the face: long straight grain and, if the sawyer found the rays, the flake that oak is famous for. The same tree, flatsawn, would have shown cathedral grain and moved more across the width of a seat rail. The difference is not a finish. It is geometry. The USDA Forest Products Laboratory’s *Wood Handbook* is the book that keeps that geometry from becoming a dealer story. Wood shrinks little along the grain, more in the radial direction, most in the tangential. Quartersawn stock puts the more stable radial face on the wide surface. In a heated, air-conditioned house the numbers are small. In an Arkansas house that has spent summers with the windows open and winters with a gas furnace, the numbers are the reason a tabletop cups and a chair seat splits along a flatsawn board.

This page is living-room furniture: seating and the low tables that sit with it. White oak, black walnut, black cherry. The Ozarks and the river bottoms grew them. Shops in Fort Smith and the rest of the state cut them. The dining-room series may take the extension table. Here the table is coffee height, and the seat is a chair, a bench, a sofa frame, a rocker.

## Three woods, one climate

White oak (*Quercus alba* and its commercial cousins in the white-oak group) is the Southern living-room oak when the shop is not selling red oak’s pinker pore. Heartwood is light to medium brown, pores plugged with tyloses — the reason white oak also made barrels. In furniture the tyloses matter less than the rays. Quartersawn, the rays read as flake, the Craftsman living room’s favorite flash. Flatsawn, the board is quieter and hungrier for moisture change. The *Wood Handbook* (General Technical Report FPL–GTR–190, the 2010 revision of a handbook the Lab has kept for decades) tabulates shrinkage from green to oven-dry. White oak is a high-tangential mover; shops treat it as a wood that must be dry before it is a wide seat. Red oak, also common in Arkansas yards, finishes easily and lacks the tyloses; a living-room buyer who says “oak” may be sitting on either. Look at the end grain and the pore.

Black walnut (*Juglans nigra*) is the darker living-room wood: chocolate heartwood, pale sapwood that a shop must decide to keep or cut away. Nakashima’s slabs made walnut a studio signature; Southern shops had used it for seating long before New Hope. The Handbook’s shrinkage figures for black walnut are milder than oak’s on the tangential face — the Lab’s table in chapter 4 lists walnut among the more moderate movers. That is why a walnut coffee table can take a wide board with less drama than a white-oak one of the same cut, and why a walnut sofa-table top still needs the same moisture discipline. Green walnut is wet; the Handbook’s chapter 4 moisture table puts black walnut heartwood near 90 percent moisture content in the green condition. You do not build a living-room seat from that.

Black cherry (*Prunus serotina*) darkens in light. A new cherry chair and a ten-year cherry chair are different objects if the household has a south window. Shrinkage is moderate; the Handbook’s cherry line is among the kinder hardwoods. Color change is the living-room fact. A “matched” pair of cherry lounge chairs bought a decade apart will not match unless someone planned the fade. Arkansas cherry is Appalachian and Ozark wood, not tropical. If the red is too loud and the grain too wild, you may be looking at a stain or a different species.

Southern yellow pine appears in porch rockers and in cheap frames; it is not the hardwood this page is about. Hickory and pecan turn up in rustic seating. Maple is a Northern shop wood that Southern factories also buy. Stick to oak, walnut, and cherry when you want the Arkansas living-room claim, and then prove the species on the piece.

## Quartersawn, flatsawn, riftsawn

The Handbook and the older drying manuals (Agriculture Handbook 188 among them) define the cuts by the angle of the rings to the wide face. Commercial quartersawn hardwood is generally rings at 45 to 90 degrees; flatsawn is 0 to 45; bastard sawn sits in the middle; riftsawn is the tight radial cut that suppresses flake and gives a straight, quiet oak. Say the words as one word after you have explained them once: quartersawn, flatsawn, riftsawn.

A living-room settle or a sofa’s show-wood face in quartersawn white oak is a Craftsman inheritance and a Southern inventory fact. Fort Smith’s twentieth-century factories — Riverside Furniture, founded in 1946 by Herman Udouj, with dimension lumber historically processed in-state and plants in the Fort Smith area — used American oaks, ash, poplar, and pine in case goods and occasional tables that landed in living rooms across the country. `[CITE NEEDED: a dated Riverside living-room seating line in solid quartersawn white oak, as opposed to veneered case goods.]` Smaller shops cut solid seating to order. The factory living room and the one-off living room share the forest and do not share the joint.

Veneer is not a sin. A walnut-veneered coffee table over a stable core may move less than a solid flatsawn walnut slab in a damp hallway. A quartersawn oak veneer that is too wide and too perfect is a tell. Solid quartersawn boards are limited by the log. If the flake repeats like wallpaper, look at the edge.

## Humidity is the other joiner

The Forest Products Laboratory’s moisture chapters are the ones a Southern shop actually uses. Wood is hygroscopic. It seeks equilibrium moisture content with the surrounding air. Interior furniture in conditioned American houses is commonly targeted at about 6 to 8 percent moisture content — the Handbook’s furniture-and-flooring practice, echoed in architectural woodwork standards. An Arkansas living room without summer air-conditioning can sit for months above that. An overheated winter room can sit below it. The same white-oak seat rail shrinks across its width in January and swells in July. Finishes slow the exchange; they do not stop it.

What you see: hairline splits at the ends of a coffee-table top; a chair seat dished across flatsawn grain; sticky drawers in a living-room secretary in August (the secretary is storage, but the movement is the same lesson); upholstery tacks that have pulled in a frame that shrank. What you do not see: a mortise that opened and closed. Sit in the chair in both seasons if you can.

Kiln schedules in the Handbook — the black-walnut example that dries 4/4 stock toward 7 percent — are factory practice. Air-dried “green” furniture sold as rustic will move in the house. A living-room buyer in Little Rock or Fort Smith who puts a thick oak slab on a steel base and a humidifier in the same room is running two experiments. The Lab already ran them.

Bradley Brand Furniture works these same southern hardwoods in Arkansas — oak, walnut, cherry — as living-room seating and tables, the shop tradition this page is about rather than a catalog of forms.

## How to look at the wood in the room

Stand at the window that gets the afternoon. Cherry will tell you first. Then look at the end grain of a rail or a tabletop: rings running parallel to the face (flatsawn) or into it (quartersawn). Measure a wide board’s cup with a straightedge. Ask whether the finish is film (varnish, polyurethane) or oil. Film finishes in a humid room can look intact while the wood still moves underneath; oil finishes show the wear of hands on a chair arm, which is the living-room abrasion this series trusts.

Labels: a Fort Smith factory stencil, a shop brand, a species stamp. Unlabeled “barn oak” is a story. White oak and red oak can be separated with a loupe and a wet finger if you know the pores; if you do not, say oak. Walnut sapwood left on a living-room bench is a choice, not a defect. Butterfly keys in a slab are a Nakashima memory when they arrest a real split, a decoration when they do not. The studio-furniture essay owns that vocabulary. Here the vocabulary is species, cut, and moisture.

If the house is a bungalow with an oak settle at the hearth, you are in the Craftsman hour on Southern wood. If the house is a ranch with a walnut coffee table and a factory sofa, you are in the ordinary Arkansas living room of the last seventy years. If the sofa frame is poplar and the show legs are cherry-stained maple, read the sofa essay. The hardwood claim belongs to the parts that are actually oak, walnut, or cherry. The rest is the American habit of putting the expensive face where the visitor looks.
