# After the Knife

*Educational history for SLPWOW. Not a treatment plan and not a substitute for evaluation by a licensed clinician.*

Gustav Killian, in Freiburg, taught the twentieth century to hang a patient and look past the glottis. Chevalier Jackson, in Pittsburgh and Philadelphia, taught America to pull a peanut out of a bronchus and to keep a card file of the objects. Those are airway stories. They belong here because the people who learned to go *through* the larynx also learned, slowly, not to wreck it on the way.

Phonosurgery is a later word. Hans von Leden and Godfrey Arnold used it in the early 1960s for operations meant to *change* the voice, not only to save a life. Arnold’s Teflon injection era — he described Teflon paste into a fold in 1962 — is the American quick fix of that decade: a paralyzed fold pushed toward the midline with a material the next generation would spend years regretting and removing. History is allowed to say “regretting.” It is not allowed to turn this paragraph into a materials lecture.

## Isshiki’s frame

Nobuhiko Isshiki (1930–14 August 2022) changed the job. Laryngeal framework surgery moves the cartilage house instead of cutting the traveling cover. The community named the operations Isshiki types I through IV; the British Laryngological Association’s memorial is careful to say *he* did not hand out the numbers like a catalog. *Phonosurgery: Theory and Practice* (1989) is the book. The 1970s papers are the work.

For an SLP the meaning is practical and historical at once. Postoperative voice is no longer only “the cancer is out.” It is a fold that has been *repositioned*, and a person who has to learn the new instrument. The hallway outside the OR is a therapy room whether the hospital admits it or not.

## What the older knife left

Hemilaryngectomy, cordectomy, the rough mid-century operations for cancer, the radiotherapy voice, the total laryngectomy and its electrolarynx and tracheoesophageal puncture — each is a literature. This essay will not survey them. It will say the obvious thing training programs sometimes skip: voice-disorders heritage includes the people who no longer have the organ the rest of this pack is romantic about. Jackson’s bronchoscopy children grew up. Some of the adults on the cancer service did not.

Janina Casper’s Syracuse work included laryngectomy groups as well as the voice-center book she wrote with Raymond Colton. She belongs in that sentence so the “professional voice” gala does not eat the page.

## Residue

A word, phonosurgery, that still sounds like a promise. A set of cartilage operations named for a man in Kyoto. A leftover caution about injectables. And a staffing fact: the SLP who sees the person on day three is part of the operation’s history, not an add-on.

## A card file and a hanging chair

Jackson’s bronchoscopy is easy to turn into Americana: the peanut, the coin, the child who breathed again. The useful historical object is the card file — a running list of foreign bodies and the instruments that got them out. That is a laboratory habit in an OR. Killian’s suspension laryngoscopy is the other object: a patient positioned so the examiner’s hands are free. Later microlaryngoscopy lives in that freedom. Neither man set out to found “voice therapy.” Both left the SLP a person whose airway had been entered and whose voice might never be a side issue again.

What the mid-century cancer operations left, besides scars, was a vocabulary of absence: the person who speaks with a device, the person who mouths, the person who is still on the ward when the gala committee has gone home. A heritage series that only remembers framework surgery is doing Isshiki’s elegant work and forgetting the ruder knife that made the hallway necessary.
