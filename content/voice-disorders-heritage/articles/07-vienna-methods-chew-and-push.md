# Chewing, Pushing, and the Viennese Methods

*Educational history for SLPWOW. Not a treatment plan and not a substitute for evaluation by a licensed clinician.*

Emil Fröschels liked a homely picture. If the voice was tight, you could ask a person to chew — ordinary chewing, exaggerated, voiced — and see whether the larynx would borrow the jaw’s ease. The *Kaumethode*, the chewing method, is the piece of his work American clinicians still recognize by name. The pushing exercises are the piece they argue about. Neither is a homework sheet in this article.

Fröschels (1884–1972) was a Viennese physician who wanted a word for the therapy side of Gutzmann’s medical claim. *Logopädie* — logopedics — is the word he put on the 1913 textbook and on the 1924 international meeting that became IALP. The sibling pack tells the association story. This pack needs the voice methods, because they crossed the Atlantic in his suitcase and in Friedrich Brodnitz’s, and they sat in New York rooms into the 1950s.

## A method is a theory of the larynx

Chewing, as Fröschels wrote it, was not a party trick. It assumed that a hyperfunctional voice was a misuse of a swallowing-adjacent mechanism, and that a vegetative movement might loosen a skilled one. Later resonant-voice and “easy onset” work would say this in other dialects. The family resemblance is real. So is the gap. Fröschels did not have Hirano’s layers, Titze’s models, or a stroboscope in every closet. He had a consulting room, a war behind him, and a city that still believed a physician could prescribe a movement.

Pushing — effortful closure gestures used, in his circle, for certain weak or paretic voices — is easier to misuse on the page. Mid-century American reprints sometimes flattened it into “have the patient push.” That flattening is why this essay exists and why it will not list steps. A historical method that can injure a fold if copied from a blog is exactly the kind of thing `CLAIMS_GUARDRAILS.md` exists to keep out of a brand site.

## Who sat in the Vienna rooms

Not only singers. Children who stammered. Soldiers with head wounds. Adults with “nervous” aphonia — the diagnosis that Paul Moses would later take to Stanford and dress in psychoanalytic language. Vienna before 1938 was a city where psychoanalysis, phoniatrics, and music lived in the same coffeehouses and did not always admit it.

Deso Weiss, Auguste Jellinek, and others in the Fröschels orbit would also land in the United States. The chewing paper Brodnitz and Fröschels published together in 1954 (*Folia* / the American journals — see the bibliography) is an exile document as much as a technique paper: two Berlin-Vienna men, in a new language, still arguing that a mundane movement could treat a nodule.

## What later people kept

They kept the *idea* that voice therapy is allowed to use the rest of the head: jaw, breath, a task that is not “say /a/.” They discarded, or should have discarded, the permission to copy a 1930s gesture onto a 2020s lesion without imaging. They argued, correctly, that Fröschels’s name on a method is not an efficacy trial.

Svend Smith’s accent method, from Denmark, is a cousin from another city: rhythmic, breath-led, studio-adjacent. It belongs in essay 16 with Boone and Verdolini, not as a Vienna sequel. The Viennese residue is specific: a physician who thought like a teacher, a teacher-movement that thought it was medicine, and a word — logopedics — that still names the European therapy profession.

Fröschels fled in 1938. He died in New York in 1972. The chewing method’s American afterlife is Brodnitz’s clinic and a few stubborn textbook paragraphs. If your training program still mentions it, mention the date and the exile. If it does not, do not resurrect it from this page as a protocol.

## After 1938 the method became English

A technique that lives in German in a Vienna room is one thing. A technique explained to a New York singer in 1951 is another. Exile translated the chewing method the way it translated the men. Something is always lost. Something is also advertised. Brodnitz’s paperback readers met a homely picture that had already been through a language and a sea.

Who was left out of the official method story: the logopedists who never emigrated, the children in municipal courses, the people for whom “chew” was not a metaphor they could use. A named method is always smaller than the rooms that used unnamed ones.
