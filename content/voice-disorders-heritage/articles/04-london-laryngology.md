# Golden Square and a Crown Prince

*Educational history for SLPWOW. Not a treatment plan and not a substitute for evaluation by a licensed clinician.*

In 1865 Morell Mackenzie opened, in Golden Square, the Hospital for Diseases of the Throat. London already had great general hospitals. It did not have a house whose door said the larynx was enough. Mackenzie (1837–1892) had gone to Vienna and Budapest to learn the new mirror work; he came home and made it a British institution. *The Hygiene of the Vocal Organs* (1886) is the book singing teachers still find in used shops. The hospital is the fact clinicians forget when they treat “voice center” as an American invention of the 1970s.

Dates for the founding wobble in secondary lists between 1863 and 1865. This pack uses **1865** for the Golden Square hospital as the date the specialty later cited; if a hospital minute book prints an earlier outpatient start, the article should absorb it rather than pretend the door never moved.

## Hygiene as a genre

Mackenzie’s hygiene book sits in a nineteenth-century genre that will not die: advice to speakers and singers that sounds like medicine and reads like manners. Rest. Weather. Alcohol. The “wrong” use of the voice. Later American paperbacks — Brodnitz’s *Keep Your Voice Healthy* is the famous mid-century one — are grandchildren of this tone. They are also, at their worst, a way to blame the patient for a lesion the mirror has not yet found.

Read Mackenzie’s hygiene as a document of its decade, not as a protocol. The useful historical point is that laryngology claimed the *speaking and singing* voice, not only the cancerous one, almost as soon as it had a hospital.

Felix Semon (1849–1921), German-born and London-based, is the other name on this street. “Semon’s law,” about the posterior cricoarytenoid and the order of paralysis, is still taught, still qualified, still argued. A law that needs a century of footnotes is doing real work.

## The case that ate the man

In 1887 Crown Prince Frederick of Prussia — husband of Queen Victoria’s daughter Vicky, heir to the German Empire — developed a laryngeal illness. German physicians (including Ernst von Bergmann and Carl Gerhardt) and Mackenzie did not agree. Mackenzie’s examinations and his reluctance to treat the lesion as frankly malignant became, after Frederick’s brief reign in 1888 and his death of laryngeal cancer that June, a European pamphlet war. Mackenzie wrote *The Fatal Illness of Frederick the Noble* (1888). German colleagues wrote him as a foreigner who had killed an emperor with English caution.

This pack will not retry the case. The slides are not in our cabinet. What belongs here is the shape of the disaster: a new specialty, a royal neck, nationalism, and a method still young enough that two competent men could look at the same larynx and name it differently. Later textbook sentences that say “Mackenzie missed the cancer” are doing Bismarck’s work for him. Later sentences that say “the Germans were butchers” are doing Mackenzie’s.

Frederick III’s death is a public fact. It is not a reason to put a clinic’s branding on a dead emperor.

## What London left

A hospital door. A journal culture (Mackenzie is tied to the early *Journal of Laryngology*). A hygiene genre. A scandal that taught every later voice physician the cost of a famous patient.

Chevalier Jackson would make American bronchoscopy a different kind of fame — coins, peanuts, children. Killian in Freiburg would teach suspension laryngoscopy. Those are essay 15. The London residue in a modern SLP’s week is quieter: the idea that the throat can be a *service line*, with hours and a name on the lintel, not a corridor you pass through on the way to the chest.

## Golden Square after the pamphlets

The hospital outlived the emperor. That is the sentence British institutional histories prefer, and they are not wrong. A door with hours is a sturdier invention than a royal consult. What the door also invented, for better and worse, was the idea that a city might have a *throat address* the way it had a fever hospital. American “voice centers” a century later are that idea with better parking.

Mackenzie’s enemies in Berlin were not cartoon militarists and his defenders in London were not cartoon liberals. They were physicians inside nations that were about to be worse to each other. A 2026 reader who wants a moral can have a smaller one: do not let a famous neck write your specialty’s origin story.
