# The Voice Laboratory

*Educational history for SLPWOW. Not a treatment plan and not a substitute for evaluation by a licensed clinician.*

On 1 September 1958 the new *Journal of Speech and Hearing Research* printed a paper whose title is a theory: Janwillem van den Berg, “Myoelastic-Aerodynamic Theory of Voice Production.” Five years earlier he had defended, *cum laude*, a Groningen dissertation in Dutch — *Physica van de stemvorming, met toepassingen* — that Bell Labs was said to have put into English almost at once. The 1958 paper is the version American training programs can cite without a translator.

The claim, against older “neurochronaxic” stories that tried to fire each cycle from a nerve, is that the fold is a myoelastic valve in an airstream. Muscle sets the posture and the stiffness. Air does the rest of the honoring. Later work would complicate every noun in that sentence. The sentence still organizes a lab.

## What a mid-century lab actually held

Not a cloud dashboard. A pneumotachograph. A microphone. Sometimes a glottograph. A man who could machine a model larynx in wax — Ron Scherer later told the NCVS hall-of-fame page that van den Berg’s paper sent him to the bench. The Gould Award for 1960 went to van den Berg for laryngeal physiology. The cash prize was $350. The plaque was the point: a Dutch physiologist had become, in American ENT print, a person you were supposed to have read.

Ingo Titze would take that bench and build an institution around it. *Principles of Voice Production* (1994) is the book students still fear and need. The National Center for Voice and Speech, and the Denver recording-and-research center Gould funded (later the Wilbur James Gould Voice Research Center), are the American addresses. Titze’s living profile is 37. What belongs here is the *genre*: voice as a problem in physics that clinicians are allowed to flunk.

## Spectrum, air, and the temptation of the number

Once you can print a spectrum, you will be asked to treat the printout as a diagnosis. Mid-century and late-century labs learned this the hard way. A jitter number without a task is a fidget. An airflow without a story is a leak. The honest laboratories — Titze’s, Hirano’s, the Wisconsin and Iowa rooms, the later European houses — kept the task in the sentence: this vowel, this loudness, this person, this day.

The dishonest ones sold “voice analysis” as a box. History is allowed to say that. It is not allowed to name a vendor.

## Who was not in the lab

Most school SLPs. Most singing teachers. Most of the world’s phoniatricians, who had patients at 2 p.m. The laboratory and the clinic are cousins who visit. Van den Berg already wanted “practical applications” in a dissertation title. The applications that stuck were conceptual: why a fold starts, why it fails to start, why a louder voice is not only more muscle. The applications that did not stick were the ones that treated a patient as a poor edition of a model.

## Residue

A theory with a hyphen. A book with equations. A habit of asking “what is the air doing?” before “what is the person doing wrong?” The last habit is the one worth keeping. The rest is furniture.

## Bell Labs and a Dutch title

If the NCVS page is right, a Groningen dissertation in 1953 was English almost immediately because an American industrial lab wanted the physics. That is a different patronage than a medical faculty. It means voice science’s mid-century English canon is, in one of its founding cases, a translation commissioned by a telephone company. Clinicians who treat “the 1958 *JSHR* paper” as a prairie invention are skipping a hiding-place in wartime Amsterdam and a physiology bench in Groningen.

The later temptation was to put a spectrum on a waiting-room wall. Titze’s generation had the equations to resist that, and the institutions to fund the resistance. The resistance is incomplete. Every decade invents a new box that promises a number instead of a task.
