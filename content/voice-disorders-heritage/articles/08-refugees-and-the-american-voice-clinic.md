# After 1933: Refugees and the American Voice Clinic

*Educational history for SLPWOW. Not a treatment plan and not a substitute for evaluation by a licensed clinician.*

The American mid-century voice clinic did not grow only from Iowa and Wisconsin. It grew from people who left.

Friedrich Brodnitz left Berlin. Paul Moses left. Emil Fröschels left Vienna in 1938. Deso Weiss left. Godfrey Arnold — still Gottfried then — would later make an American career out of a Viennese ear clinic. Hans von Leden, born in Breslau in 1918, followed a father who had already read the 1930s correctly. Each of those sentences is a life. Together they are a staffing pattern. Phoniatrics, as a German-speaking medical trade, was smashed and packed into New York, St. Louis, San Francisco, and Chicago. What Americans later called “the interdisciplinary voice clinic” is, in part, an émigré hallway.

## New York: a dean without a throne

Brodnitz (1899–1995) took the American Board of Otolaryngology in 1942, worked at Mount Sinai’s phoniatric clinic and at Beth David, taught at Hunter College, and saw singers in a private office until he was past ninety. *Keep Your Voice Healthy* (first edition 1951; later reprints 1973 and after) is the book civilians found. *Vocal Rehabilitation* is the book students found. The *Journal of Voice* memorial in 2001 called him the dean of American phoniatrics. Deans in exile do not get departments named for them. They get patients and a nickname.

Duchan’s history page says he studied phoniatrics with Hermann Gutzmann Sr. The Union of European Phoniatricians says Hermann Gutzmann Jr. and Theodor Flatau, after ENT training with Carl von Eicken. Gutzmann Sr. died in 1922; Brodnitz’s Berlin medical year is 1924. This pack follows the UEP line and prints the disagreement. A clean “student of Gutzmann” makes a better poster. It is not better history.

## San Francisco: the voice as a psyche

Paul J. Moses (1 April 1897 – 7 June 1965) ran the speech and voice section in Stanford’s otolaryngology division in San Francisco. *The Voice of Neurosis* (Grune & Stratton, 1954) is a short book with a large claim: that pitch, register, rhythm, and timbre carry a psyche, independent of the words. Reviewers in the medical journals treated him as a rare person who could hear like a laryngologist and think like a psychiatrist. Later clinicians would wince at “neurosis” and keep the useful half — that a normal-looking larynx can still host a disordered voice, and that the disorder is not always a nodule you missed.

Moses is easy to parody. He is harder to replace. Every later chapter on “psychogenic” voice — Aronson’s Mayo work is the American textbook version — owes him a debt and an argument.

## What the host country already had

American speech clinics existed. Blanton’s Wisconsin door (1914), the 1925 academy, Van Riper’s textbook (1939), the VA wards — the sibling pack has them. What the host country did *not* have in density was a physician whose whole job was the speaking and singing voice. ENT existed. It was busy with tonsils, ears, and cancer. The émigrés arrived as a missing middle: too medical for the speech department, too vocal for the average ear-nose-throat list.

They also arrived as Jews, or as men who had worked with Jews, into a profession that did not always want to say so. This essay will not turn that into a sermon. It will not leave it out. The American voice clinic’s European accent is a fact of persecution, not a gourmet import.

## Residue

A joint clinic. A hygiene paperback. A suspicion that the larynx is not the whole story. A later generation — Moore and von Leden with a camera, Gould with a foundation, Sataloff with a double degree — would Americanize the hallway and forget, sometimes, who had furnished it.

If your training program’s history slide jumps from García to Hirano, insert 1938.

## St. Louis, Hunter College, a board exam

Fröschels’s American stations are easy to flatten into “then he came to New York.” The intermediate rooms matter. So does the 1942 board exam in Brodnitz’s CV. An émigré physician who wants to keep seeing singers has to pass someone else’s test in someone else’s language. The test is not the scandal. The scandal is the reason the test was taken in English.

American speech departments, meanwhile, were already granting Ph.D.s and writing undergraduate textbooks. The two guilds looked at each other across a war and a credential. Joint clinics are the later peace treaty. This essay is the refugee clause in that treaty.
