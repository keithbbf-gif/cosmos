# Cover and Body

*Educational history for SLPWOW. Not a treatment plan and not a substitute for evaluation by a licensed clinician.*

In 1974 *Folia Phoniatrica* printed five pages by Minoru Hirano: “Morphological Structure of the Vocal Cord as a Vibrator and its Variations.” The paper had been received in February 1973 and accepted in August. Kurume, not Vienna, not Iowa. A layered fold, described so a surgeon and a teacher could share a cartoon.

Hirano split the vibrator. A **body** — thyroarytenoid muscle and the ligamentous deep stuff that can stiffen with it. A **cover** — epithelium and the loose superficial lamina propria that can travel. The traveling wave clinicians thought they were seeing on strobe finally had a tissue to be a wave *in*. Later teaching added the intermediate layer and talked about a transition. The 1974 move is the one that remade Western sentences.

## Why Western teaching needed a Japanese paper

Van den Berg had given the air and the muscle a theory. He had not given the fold a decent histology for the clinic. American and European textbooks still talked as if a “cord” were a string with a disease on it. Hirano’s atlas work — the later *Histological Color Atlas of the Human Larynx* is the luxury edition — made the string into a sandwich. Nodules, cysts, scars, and the reason a microflap tries to stay in one layer all sit on that sandwich.

He also gave the West a rating scale it could argue about: GRBAS — grade, roughness, breathiness, asthenia, strain. The letters are easy to mock. They are also the first shared perceptual form many students meet. Every later CAPE-V is a cousin who went to a different school.

## What the paper did not do

It did not tell you how to do therapy. It did not tell you when to operate. It did not settle singing-register wars, though singers now use “cover” in a way that would make a histologist wince. Hirano was trying to understand how one pair of folds makes many colors. The four typical postures later writers extract from the thyroarytenoid/cricothyroid story are interpretations. The 1974 text is shorter and more modest than the industry that grew on it.

## Kurume as an address

Hirano spent his academic life at Kurume University and trained a generation of Japanese laryngeal surgeons, including his son Shigeru. The American Laryngological Association’s notice says he died suddenly on 19 December 2017. Western obituaries treat him as a corresponding fellow who had already become a chapter heading. The chapter heading is earned. So is the reminder that the “Western” voice clinic’s most-used tissue model is not Western.

Slide decks that list Hirano after a row of European beards treat a Kurume paper as late illustration; chronology and geography both argue for an earlier, central place.

## The word “cord”

Hirano’s English title still says *vocal cord*. So did almost everyone in 1974. Later American teaching preferred *fold* because a fold is a layered shelf and a cord is a string. The paper that killed the string kept the older noun. That is not irony for its own sake. It is a reminder that a five-page *Folia* article cannot also be a style guide. Students who correct a 1974 title are doing branding.

Western citation practice also flattened the atlas into a single cartoon. Hirano’s later color plates are the thing surgeons actually used. The 1974 diagram is the thing slide decks use. Courses that teach only the 1974 diagram should say so; surgeons worked from the color atlas. The plates are why a cyst and a nodule are not the same afternoon.

Hirano’s singing question — how one pair of folds makes many colors — is why pedagogues grabbed the paper. The grab was sometimes a mistranslation. A cover that travels is not a “covered” tone in the studio sense. Keep the words in their guilds. The 1974 text is short enough that a teacher can read it on a lunch break. That is part of why it won.
