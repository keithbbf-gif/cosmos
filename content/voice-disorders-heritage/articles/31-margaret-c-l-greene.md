# Margaret C. L. Greene

*Educational history for SLPWOW. Not a treatment plan and not a substitute for evaluation by a licensed clinician.*

> **Portrait placeholder.** No public-domain or clearly licensed portrait located as of the pack date. See `PORTRAIT_SOURCES.md` for hunt status. Do not invent or generate a substitute likeness.

Margaret C. L. Greene put a British therapist’s book on a subject London laryngologists had treated as theirs.

She was born in 1913 and died in 2007. *The Voice and Its Disorders* appeared in 1957. Later editions kept the title alive; Lesley Mathieson carried the sixth (2001) after Greene’s working life. The sibling SLP pack treats her in the British-speech-therapy essay and notes AFASIC (1968), the parents’ organization for children with speech and language impairments. This profile is the 1957 spine. A voice-disorders heritage series that only lists Boone as “the textbook” is an American series pretending to be the world.

## What the 1957 book did

It gave speech therapists in the National Health Service a single volume that was not a laryngologist’s hygiene paperback and not an American undergraduate menu. The tone is medical. The reader is a clinician. The disorders are named as if they belonged on a ward list. That combination — therapist as reader, larynx as object — is the British mid-century settlement. Elsie Fogerty had opened a speech clinic at St Thomas’ in 1913; Greene, two generations later, gave the voice caseload a book.

1957 is not current protocol; it is the year British training could point to a *voice* text the way American training would point to Boone in 1971.

## Residue

A title that survived its author. A sixth edition that is already itself historical. A reminder, next to Fogerty and Morley in the sibling pack, that British speech therapy’s voice chapter is not a colony of ASHA. ## AFASIC and the other door

The 1968 parents’ organization is the sibling pack’s Greene. It belongs in one sentence here so she is not only a 1957 title. A clinician who writes for therapists and a citizen who helps found a charity for children’s language are the same British mid-century type: the NHS professional who does not stop at the ward door.

Mathieson’s sixth edition is already a historical object. Cite 1957 when you mean Greene’s voice; cite 2001 when you mean the book as later teaching found it.
