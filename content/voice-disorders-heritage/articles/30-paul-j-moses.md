# Paul J. Moses

*Educational history for SLPWOW. Not a treatment plan and not a substitute for evaluation by a licensed clinician.*

> **Portrait placeholder.** No public-domain or clearly licensed portrait located as of the pack date. See `PORTRAIT_SOURCES.md` for hunt status. Do not invent or generate a substitute likeness.

Paul Josef Moses wanted a psychiatrist’s ear in a laryngologist’s chair.

He was born 1 April 1897 and died 7 June 1965. At Stanford’s medical school in San Francisco he ran the speech and voice section of the otolaryngology division. *The Voice of Neurosis* (Grune & Stratton, 1954) is 131 pages and a claim: that range, register, resonance, rhythm, melody, intensity, and the rest of the acoustic furniture carry a psyche, independent of the words. Reviewers in *Archives of Pediatrics* and *Physical Therapy* treated him as a rare double — a man whose training in both directions was, they thought, actual. They also repeated his own complaint: psychiatrists were not taught to hear a voice, and laryngologists were not taught to hear a life.

## “Creative hearing”

He named the skill he wanted taught. The phrase is dated. The problem is not. A normal-looking larynx and a disordered voice still share a waiting room. Aronson’s later Mayo textbook would give the American differential a colder tone. Moses gave it a psychoanalytic one. Both can overreach. Both are why “just a nodule” is not a complete sentence.

He sat in a circle that included Brodnitz, Deso Weiss, Auguste Jellinek, Gottfried Arnold — émigré and European names, the same hallway as essay 08. He cited singing-teacher experiments (Alfred Wolfsohn appears in the later encyclopedic summaries) as if the studio were a laboratory of range. That habit can become mysticism. In 1954 it was also a way to take singers seriously as data.

## How to read him

“Neurosis” is a period word, not a billing label; the book is not a diagnostic instrument. The 1954 introduction’s jurisdictional sentence — two professions, each half-deaf — is why he belongs in a voice-disorders pack and not only in a history of psychiatry.

## 131 pages, a mixed review

The 1954 book is short enough to assign and large enough to misuse. Pediatric and physical-therapy reviewers praised the double training and repeated the jurisdictional complaint. Later voice textbooks kept the complaint and dropped the word *neurosis* when they could. Aronson is the colder American heir. The émigré circle — Brodnitz, Weiss, Jellinek, Arnold — is the social warrant that Moses was not a lone mystic.

Wolfsohn in the secondary summaries is a singing-teacher cameo, not a protocol. Leave him a cameo.
