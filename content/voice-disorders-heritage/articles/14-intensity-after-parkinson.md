# Intensity After Parkinson

*Educational history for SLPWOW. Not a treatment plan and not a substitute for evaluation by a licensed clinician.*

Daniel Boone noticed, in VA rooms in the 1950s, that people with Parkinson disease could be louder when they were asked to be loud on purpose. The automatic voice had gone thin. The intentional one was still, sometimes, there. Decades later a Texas program would turn “speak with intent” into a branded line and thank him for it. The historical fact is older than the brand: intensity is a lever in a hypokinetic voice.

Lorraine Olson Ramig and colleagues built, in the late 1980s and 1990s, the research line American clinicians know as Lee Silverman Voice Treatment. The name is a patient’s name. That is unusual and worth keeping visible. The early papers sit in the ordinary journals of the field; the later ones are a literature about dosage, follow-up, and what happens when a method becomes a product.

This essay will not print the cues, the daily schedule, or the certification ladder. Those are clinic and company work. The heritage question is different: why did *loud* become the American answer to a Parkinson voice, and what did that answer crowd out?

## Why loud

A quiet, monotone, rushed voice is easy for families to call “not trying.” It is also easy for a well-meaning clinician to treat with a dozen targets at once: breath, rate, articulation, pragmatics. The Ramig line bet on a single, effortful target and on the idea that calibration — the person’s sense of what “normal” loudness is — had drifted. You can believe that bet and still want better evidence. You can doubt the bet and still owe the literature a read.

Boone’s earlier observation sits underneath: the voice is not only a larynx. It is a motor plan that can be invited out of automaticity. That sentence is why this essay lives in a voice-disorders pack and not only in a neurology one.

## What got crowded

Quiet approaches. Singing-based programs that did not want to shout. Non-English clinics with their own names. The unglamorous work of swallowing, cognition, and the day the person is too tired for effort. A history that treats LSVT as the only Parkinson-voice event is an American training-program history. Write it as such.

Competing and complementary programs — SPEAK OUT! is the one that hosted Boone the week he died — belong in a later editor’s update, not as a product comparison table here.

## The week in Richardson

Boone died in the early morning of 27 October 2018 (Parkinson Voice Project; the University of Arizona notice also prints the 26th) after traveling to Richardson, Texas, to teach. He was three days short of ninety-one. The death is not a parable. It is a date. The parable people want — the old man still in the room — is true enough to be cheap. Keep the date. Leave the halo.

## Residue

A literature about effort. A certification economy. A generation of clinicians who can say “calibration” without blushing. And a debt to a VA clinician who, before the acronyms, noticed that intention still had a volume knob.

## What “calibration” was for

The word sounds like a lab. In the Ramig literature it is closer to a quarrel with a person’s own ear: the voice that feels like shouting to the speaker may sound like a reasonable conversation to everyone else. Boone had already met that quarrel in a VA corridor. The later papers tried to measure it, follow it, and see whether it stayed followed. That is why this is a heritage essay and not a brochure. Measurement is a date. A cue card is a product.

International clinics that never bought the American acronym still had to decide what to do with a fading Parkinson voice. Some used singing groups. Some used speech tasks that did not want to be loud. A pack written for an American brand can admit that the slide deck is provincial without pretending the provincial work did not help people.
