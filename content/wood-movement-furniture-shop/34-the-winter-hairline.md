# The winter hairline

Raking light on a chest lid in
January. A pale line at the
ends of an applied molding, or
at a rail where it meets a
solid side, or at a miter that
was tight in August. Someone
wants it filled and rubbed
out. Fill it and you have a
wedge for May, and a wider
line next January.

## The line is a witness

Finish sits on two parts. When
the parts disagree about
width, the film tears at the
meeting or the glue lets go
at the ends. The middle often
holds. The ends are where
travel accumulates. That is
why hairlines start at ends
and miters and not in the
center of a long glue.

A finish that was too brittle,
or a glue that starved, can
look the same in a photograph.
Look at the year. If the line
closes in June, you are not
in a bottle problem.

## Moldings

Nail or glue the middle. Let
the ends slide — a slot in
the molding, a finish nail
that can walk in an elongated
hole, a coping instead of a
dead miter on a moving field.
A full-length glue line under
a molding on a solid lid is
a scheduled hairline.

Painted work hides the line
until the paint bridges and
then cracks. The crack is
still the width. Caulk on
furniture is a kitchen habit
I do not want on a lid. If
you must have a sealed look,
use a construction that does
not move, or a molding that
is allowed to be a shadow
line.

## Color change

A line that shows raw wood is
uglier than a line that shows
finished wood. Finish the
meeting faces before you
close them when you know a
stripe will come — panel
edges, field ends under
breadboards. The winter
hairline then looks like a
shadow, not a tan.

## What not to do in January

Do not smear wax into a
seasonal line and call it a
restoration. Do not belt the
proud edge of a breadboard
flush. Do not add a screw at
the end of a molding "to
close it." You have locked
the next year.

A light color-matched wax
that you expect to refresh
is a client conversation, not
a repair. A rebuild of the
fastening is a repair.

## On the care card

"A small winter line at a
molding or panel is the
piece working. Call if the
line becomes a crack or
stays open in summer." You
have named the hairline
before it photographs as a
betrayal.

## A lid molding that taught the rule

A painted pine chest, lid
grain running long, a
quiet maple molding glued
full-length around the
perimeter for a
"furniture" edge. August
was tight miters. January
opened the two ends of
the front molding by a
line you could catch a
nail in. The paint
bridged, then cracked, so
the photograph looked
like a finish failure.
The finish was a witness.
The save was to cut the
end glue free, pin the
middle, and leave the
miters as a shadow. The
client wanted caulk. Caulk
on a lid is a kitchen
habit. We did not caulk.

## When a hairline is a crack

If the line goes to the
core, if it runs with a
glue line that has glossy
cheeks, if it stays open
in June — you are not in
this draft's seasonal
stripe. You are in the
split draft. Do not use
the care-card sentence
as a cloak. A crack that
takes a playing card is
not a hairline. A
hairline that takes a
thumbnail in January and
nothing in July is.

## Light and the tan

Raking light sells the
line. Overhead light
hides it. Clients send
the raking shot. That is
fair. Your job is to
know whether the raking
shot is a treaty working
or a lock failing. The
back of the piece,
again, is how you know.
A hairline over a row of
tight screws is a lock.
A hairline at a floated
panel edge is a stripe
you should have
finished.

The winter hairline is
honest. It is also optional
in the sense that you can
design it out — plywood,
floating meetings, no
applied quiet length on a
live width. If you keep the
look, keep the honesty. Fill
is how shops turn a witness
into a problem.
