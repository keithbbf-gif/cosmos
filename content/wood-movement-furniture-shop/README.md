# Wood movement for the furniture shop

These are **drafts**. They are staged on purpose. Read them in order and
you get a working model: wood takes on water from the air, it changes
width, and the joint that pretends otherwise fails on a calendar, not
on a clamp.

This is shop writing for furniture makers. The numbers come from the
USDA Forest Products Laboratory *Wood Handbook* and from ordinary
bench practice. The weather in the sentences is South Arkansas — hot
wet summers, heated winter rooms that go dry, metal buildings that
lie about both — because that is the climate that taught the
examples. The physics does not care what county you are in. The
delivery does.

## What this series is

Wood is hygroscopic. Below the fiber saturation point it shrinks when
it loses bound water and swells when it gains it. Almost all of that
change is **across** the grain. Almost none of it is along the grain.
Furniture that lasts is furniture that left room for the across.

Eight stages:

1. **Shop talk** — the crack, the proud breadboard, the call in
   August, the shop that only exists in April.
2. **Physics** — three directions, flatsawn vs quarter, EMC, the
   scrap-paper formula, cup.
3. **Measuring** — pin, pinless, wet cores, a protocol, hygrometers
   that flatter.
4. **Shop climate** — a number you can defend, seasonal swing,
   machines, stickering, the house the piece is going to.
5. **Design** — grooves, breadboards, tops, drawers, doors,
   cross-grain, when to refuse the drawing.
6. **Species and stock** — oak, walnut, cherry, maple, pine,
   plywood, the one wide board.
7. **Finish, glue, time** — films slow moisture; they do not stop
   it. Glue and mill schedules have weather in them.
8. **Failures and the house** — warp names, splits you can save,
   the seasonal phone call, the cut-list checklist.

## Shop-safe fence

This series stays inside **legal furniture-shop practice**.

It will talk about:

- how to read moisture content and relative humidity
- how to size gaps, slots, and fasteners for seasonal change
- how to hold a shop in a range a piece can survive
- what to tell a client when a top pulls or a drawer swells

It will **not** teach:

- how to run a dry kiln as a commercial operation, or how to
  improvise a kiln that becomes a fire
- how to treat wood with restricted pesticides or vapor barriers
  sold as magic
- how to hide a structural crack and call it a finish problem
- how to write a warranty that pretends indoor climate does not
  exist

A moisture meter is a tool. It is not a verdict you owe a lawyer
without the species setting, the core, and the room.

## How to read (staged)

| Stage | Name | Drafts | What you should be able to say after |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Shop talk | 01–05 | The failure is weather wearing a glue costume |
| 2 | Physics | 06–13 | Width, not length. Formula on a scrap. |
| 3 | Measuring | 14–18 | A number you can repeat next Tuesday |
| 4 | Shop climate | 19–25 | The shop and the house are two climates |
| 5 | Design | 26–35 | The joint left room, or it will open |
| 6 | Species | 36–42 | Oak is not walnut. Plywood is not still. |
| 7 | Finish, glue, time | 43–46 | Slow is not stop. Both faces. Wait. |
| 8 | Failures and the house | 47–50 | Name the warp. Call the client. Check the list. |

Read a stage, then do one thing on a real board: a meter log, a
gap you can measure, a top fastened on slots. The board is the
critic.

## Voice

No "comprehensive guide." No "whether you're a beginner or a pro."
If a sentence could sit on a big-box humidity pamphlet, it does
not belong here. A claim without a shop consequence is cut.

Uncertain coefficients, meter brands, and house-specific targets
get `[VERIFY]`. Do not invent a reading.
