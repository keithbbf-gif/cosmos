# Plywood until the veneer splits

A shop tired of oak will swing
to plywood and call the year
solved. For openings and for
boxes, it often is. For a
one-face veneer on a wet
installation, it is a
different crack with a
thinner voice.

## What shrugs

Crossed plies cancel most of
the Handbook width change.
A plywood cabinet opening
that was square on delivery
is usually square in August
if the box stayed a box —
not sitting on a wet slab,
not finished on one side
only, not a cheap core that
swelled at a cut edge like
a sponge.

MDF is even more of a shrug
until it sees water as
liquid. Then it is a
swelling problem, not a
coefficient problem. Keep
it out of wet floors and
unssealed edges in a steamy
room.

## What does not shrug

Veneer is thin wood. It
still has grain. A dry
room on a finished face
and a wet, unfinished back
will pull. A tabletop of
fancy-face plywood with a
naked underside is a cup.
A door with veneer on one
side and laminate on the
other is a factory problem
you should not invent in
a custom shop without
balancing.

Checks in a face veneer —
little splits along the
grain — show up when the
core and the room moved
enough to ask the glue
line to stretch. Winter
on a radiator, or a piece
left in a van, will do it.
The sheet "didn't move."
The face did.

## Edges

A raw plywood edge drinks.
In a wet week the edge
swells and the veneer
telegraphs a ridge. Edge
band, or finish, or both.
Solid-wood edge banding is
a quiet length on a
shrugging core — a small
cross-grain if you glue
it like a clamp around a
curved apron. On a
straight box it usually
behaves if the band is
narrow and the glue is
not a flood.

## When I want a sheet

Drawer boxes I want to
run in August. Case
openings that cannot
change. Painted doors
with a stable panel.
Dust bottoms. A built-in
that must meet a wall
and a fridge. I do not
want a sheet pretending
to be a 12-inch oak
cathedral unless we sell
it as a veneer and build
the underside like a
face.

## When I want solid

Show fields people will
touch and read as a
tree. Tabletops we can
fasten honestly. Frames
around a sheet panel.
Repair culture: a solid
rail can be remade; a
blown veneer on a cheap
core is often a
replacement.

## Honesty on the ticket

`Box: maple ply. Faces:
walnut veneer, balanced.
Edges banded.` or `Solid
oak field, floated.` If
the sales word is "solid"
and the construction is
a sheet, the year is not
the liar. You are.

Plywood until the veneer
splits is the real
sentence. Use the shrug.
Balance the faces. Seal
the edges. Do not file
sheet goods under
"doesn't move" and then
act betrayed by a
hairline in a winter
face. The core kept its
promise. The face needed
one too.

## A van and a fancy face

A walnut-veneer table,
balanced, left in a
closed van over a July
weekend for a show.
The face checked in
fine lines along the
grain. The core had
shrugged. The face had
been asked to follow a
climate the film could
not delay enough. We
now crack the van and
we do not treat veneer
as plywood's magic.
The ticket says
veneer. The care card
says no week in a
hot box.

## Cheap core, proud edge

A store sheet with a
thick face and a
hungry edge will swell
at a cut if you leave
it raw in a wet
kitchen. Band it. Seal
it. Do not use that
core as a painted
door field next to a
dishwasher without
the band. The shrug
stops at the first
open ply.

## When I will not use a sheet

A dining field someone
will read as a tree
and pay tree money
for. A repair culture
that wants to remake
a rail in twenty
years. A client who
said solid and meant
solid. I will use a
sheet for the box
under that field. I
will not hide the
sheet in the field
and call the year
the liar when a
veneer hairline
shows.
