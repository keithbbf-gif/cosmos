# A number you can defend

Comfort is 68 degrees and whatever humidity the
week brought. Joints do not take comfort as an
order. They take moisture change. So you pick a
range for the air the boards live in, and you
defend it the way you defend a square.

## Why 40 to 50 shows up so often

At shop temperature, that band of relative
humidity points at roughly 8 to 9 percent EMC.
That is close to what a lot of kiln-dried
furniture stock was aimed at, and close to what
a lot of closed, conditioned American houses sit
near when they are not being stoves or
greenhouses. `[VERIFY]` the Handbook cell if you
are going to print the band on a care card.

The band is not magic. A coastal shop delivering
to porches might honestly sit wetter. A shop
sending work into mountain winters might sit
drier and tell the truth about it. The sin is
not the number you pick. The sin is not picking
one, or picking April's number and ignoring
August.

## Defend means machines and doors

A target without a dehumidifier in this county
is a wish from June to October. A target without
some winter moisture — or at least a refusal to
bake the rack next to a unit heater — is a wish
from December to March. Doors that stay open on
a wet afternoon are a decision to abandon the
band for a few hours. Sometimes you need the
forklift. Write that afternoon down. Do not
glue the next morning as if the band held.

## Match the destination if you know it

A piece for a sealed condo with hard summer
air-conditioning is not a piece for a
farmhouse hall. If the client can tell you
the house sits at 35 percent all winter, you
can build drier or you can build more gap.
What you cannot do is hold 55 in the shop
because the building likes it and then act
surprised at the photograph.

When you do not know the house, the 40–50
band is a decent middle. Design the wide
fields for a few points of travel anyway.
Houses lie too.

## Comfort fights the number

People open bay doors because the shop is
hot. People crank heat because fingers are
clumsy. Both moves dump the band. A fan
that only blows on you does not dry a rack
evenly. A kerosene torpedo aimed down an
aisle is how you invent gradients and
headaches. If the number is real, the
building needs insulation, shade, a
dehumidifier sized for the wet months, and
a heat source that does not toast one
corner of the pile.

That is money. So is warranty work. Pick
which money you like.

## Write it where the argument happens

On the door. On the job ticket. In the
apprentice's first week. `We hold 40–50% RH
at the rack. We glue near 8–9% MC unless
the ticket says the house is different.`
When someone wants to glue a 12 percent
panel because the clamps are free, the
wall is the grown-up in the room.

## What you do not defend

You do not defend a single digit as if the
building were a lab. 43 percent on a Tuesday
is not a moral victory. A week inside the
band is. You do not defend a number you
never check. You do not defend a finishing
room that is ten points drier than the
assembly floor without treating that as a
second climate.

A number you can defend is a range, a
place (the rack), an instrument that can
be wrong, and a year of boring work. Boring
is what joints like. Comfort they will
survive if the boring held.

## When 40–50 is the wrong band

A shop that only delivers
to Gulf porches and wet
additions may honestly
sit wetter and design
the travel up. A shop
that only delivers into
mountain winters may sit
drier. The sin is
claiming 45 while the
logger writes 62, or
claiming 45 as a
promise on a care card
without a temperature.
Print the band as a
shop target. Print the
house as a maybe. The
product uses the
difference.
