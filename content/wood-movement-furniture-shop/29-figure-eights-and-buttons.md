# Figure-eights, Z-clips, buttons

Hardware catalogs make this look like
taste. It is placement. The part you
pick has to rotate, slide, or flex
enough for the product on the scrap.
The part you pick at the *end* of a
wide top cannot be the rigid cousin
of the part you picked in the middle.

## Figure-8s

A steel eight, one screw in the apron,
one in the top. The eight can twist as
the top moves. They are fast and they
fail when the recess is too tight, the
screws are too long and kiss a finish,
or you put them at the ends and then
add pocket screws that cannot twist.
Give the eight a clean mortise and
room to turn. Do not bury it in glue.

## Z-clips and table irons

A clip in a saw kerf or a manufactured
slot in the apron, screw in the top.
The clip can slide in the kerf. The
kerf has to be long enough for the
travel, not a snug parking space.
Cheap clips bend. That can be mercy
or it can be a rattle. I want the
slide to come from the slot, not from
a tired tongue of metal.

## Wooden buttons

A traditional shop move: a small wood
cleat, one end in a groove in the
apron, screw through a slot into the
top. You can make them from offcuts.
You can size the slot to the product.
You can also glue a button by
accident when finish or squeeze
reaches it. Dry-fit. Finish. Then
fasten. Buttons look like you meant
the underside. I like that more than
I like catalogs.

## Slotted screws

A pocket hole or a clearance hole,
elongated. The cheapest traveler and
the easiest to forget to elongate.
If the hole is round, it is a lock.
If you drive the screw down until
the head buries, you have added
friction that acts like a lock. Snug
is enough. The top should not rattle
on the apron. It should not be
clamped to it either.

## Where the tight ones live

Centerline, or the middle of the
long aprons. That keeps the top from
walking off the base as it shrinks.
Ends get the longest slots or the
freest eights. A pedestal plate gets
the same thinking at a smaller
scale: tight in the middle if you
can, travel at the corners.

## Mixing

A row of Z-clips plus two "temporary"
construction screws left in the ends
is a row of Z-clips that do not
matter. Temporary becomes character.
Circle the underside at delivery.
Remove the locks. If you need extra
hold for a truck, use extra travelers
or a removable cleat you actually
remove.

## Corrosion and kitchens

A wet kitchen and a raw steel clip
is a stain in a year. Finish, or
use a metal that can live there, or
use wood buttons. Do not invent a
humidity problem that is actually
a rust problem.

Pick a traveler. Place it like you
sized the gap in a door. Write the
choice on the ticket so a service
call does not add pocket screws
"to tighten the top." Tight is how
we got the last photograph.

## One system on a top

I have opened an
underside with Z-clips
on the longs, figure-8s
on the shorts, and two
pocket screws "for the
truck" still in the
ends. The clips were
doing nothing. We
pulled the pockets,
kept one system, and
the next winter was
quieter. Mixing is how
the stiffest fastener
becomes the only
fastener.

## Buttons you make

Offcut oak, a slot you
sized to the product,
a groove in the apron.
They look like you
meant the year. They
also glue themselves
if finish floods the
groove. Dry, finish,
then fasten. A glued
button is a wooden
lock with a
traditional name.

## How tight is snug

The top should not
drum on the apron.
It should not need a
driver to prove you
were here. Snug the
screw until the clip
or button seats, then
stop. Buried heads
are friction clamps.
Write "snug, not
buried" on the
apprentice's first
underside.
