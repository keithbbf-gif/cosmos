# Pin meters and the holes

The needles leave two dark points. On a show face those
points are a conversation. On a waste edge they are how
you know whether the core is in the same month as the
skin. A pin meter is a resistance toy with a scale. Used
like a toy, it flatters. Used like a tool, it argues
with your hope.

## What it is actually reading

Drier wood resists electricity more. Wetter wood less.
The meter translates that resistance into a moisture
content, after a species setting or a correction chart.
It is not weighing the board. It is not looking at
bound water with a tiny kiln. It is guessing MC from
how easily a trickle runs between two pins.

That guess goes bad when the species is wrong, the
wood is frozen, the pins kiss a glue line, or you are
in a species with extractives that confuse the scale.
It goes less bad when you do the same thing every
time and write it down.

## Where to poke

Waste. End grain is faster and wetter-looking than
the face; it is a rumor about the core, useful, not
sufficient. Face readings on 4/4 are often the whole
story. On 8/4 and up, a short pin is a skin reading.
Hammer-in pins or a deeper pair earn their annoyance
on slabs.

Along the grain, not across a ring line if you can
help it. Away from knots, pitch, and the KD stamp.
Several places: both ends, the middle, a sap streak
if you have one. A single heroic poke is how a wet
end becomes a dry door.

## Species and temperature

Oak is not pine. The manual has a chart or a species
button. Use it. A meter left on "Douglas fir" in an
oak shop will give you numbers you can still compare
to each other and cannot compare to a Handbook EMC.

Cold wood reads wrong. So does wood that just came
off a sunlit tailgate. Let the board be shop-warm.
If your meter has a temperature correction, do it.
If it does not, stop pretending a January slab and
a July slab share a scale without comment.

## The holes are a fee, not a shame

Clients do not need pin holes on a dining field. You
need them on offcuts from the same board, on the
underside, on a back rail. A shop that refuses pins
because it loves faces will believe pinless meters
in situations pinless cannot see — a wet core under
a dry skin, a gradient after a rain on the stack.

I poke. I also own a pinless. They argue. The
argument is the point.

## What a pin will not tell you

It will not tell you stress. A case-hardened board
can read "fine" and still smile when ripped. It will
not tell you EMC. That is the wall and a table. It
will not tell you the house. It will not make a
legal number without a protocol you can repeat.

If the two pins span a check, you are reading the
check. If they span a glue line in a panel, you are
reading glue. If you push until you feel the bench
under a thin board, you are reading the bench.

## Habit

Same meter. Same species setting. Waste edge. Three
places. Write the highest and the lowest, not an
average that hides a wet end. Date, RH, temperature
if the day is rude. Then you have a pin story, not
a poke.

The holes are ugly on a photograph and honest on a
ticket. I will take honest. The cathedral can stay
pretty. The waste can tell the truth.
