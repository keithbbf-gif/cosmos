# Finish the underside

You can tell who skipped
it from across the room
in a dry week. The top
has a shallow lens. A
bowl rocks. The
breadboard looks like it
is fighting a smile. The
show face is perfect.
The back was left raw
because nobody looks
there and the schedule
was short.

The air looks there.

## Same speed, both faces

Cup from a moisture
gradient is a speed
problem. The naked face
exchanges faster. It
shrinks or swells first.
The sealed face lags.
The board bends. An even
delay — finish on both
sides — does not stop
the year. It keeps the
year from turning into a
curve.

The underside coat can
be simpler: fewer
builds, a different
sheen, a cheaper cousin
of the show schedule if
they are compatible.
It cannot be a skip.

## Ends and edges

End grain is a highway.
A finished field with
raw ends is a proud
breadboard with a tan
line, or a checked end
on a bench top. Edges
on a plywood sheet are
the same sentence.
Flip the piece. Do the
unfashionable faces
while the film is still
in your hand.

## Inside cases

A chest with a finished
exterior and raw
interiors will take
house moisture from the
inside in a wet season
and look fine until a
door bind or a back
that bows. You do not
need a piano finish in
a drawer well. You
need a seal.

## Why shops skip it

Time. Overspray. A
belief that oil on the
face "feeds" the wood
and the back should
"breathe." Breathe is
how cups get a poetry
excuse. If you want
breath, you want even
breath. That is both
faces, or a construction
that does not care —
a box, a frame-and-panel
with both sides of the
field coated.

## Inspection

At delivery, flip the
top. If the back is
raw, you are not done,
or you are selling a
cup. Show the client
the travelers and the
coat in one gesture.
They will not ask for
it. They will photograph
the lens in January if
you skip it.

## Repair

A cupped top with a
naked back can sometimes
be helped by coating the
back and waiting in a
held room — not always,
not if the cup is a
ring story from the
log. Do not belt the
show face into a
thinner lens and leave
the back in charge.

## What "simpler coat" means

On a dining top I will
run the show schedule
on the face and a
thinner, faster cousin
on the back — same
family if I can, so I
am not stacking
incompatible films. Oil
face and oil back is
honest. Varnish face
and raw back is a cup.
Varnish face and a
single varnish back is
usually enough delay
to keep the lens out
of the photograph. A
waterborne face and a
naked back is a fast
cup in a wet week
because the back
drinks.

Do not put a plastic
film on the face and
a wiping oil on the
back and call them
even. They are two
speeds. Even is the
point.

## Leaves and the closet

Leaves stored in a
closet, stacked face
to face with raw
backs, pick up the
closet and then meet
a finished table that
lived in the hall.
The leaf cups. People
say the table
"rejected" the leaf.
The leaf lived in a
different EMC with
one face sealed.
Finish the leaf
backs. Store them on
sticks or at least
on edge, not in a
hug on a carpet.

## Inside a chest

The underside of a
lid is also an
interior. So is the
back of a door. A
raw interior in a
house that runs wet
in August will swell
the piece from the
inside while the
exterior film tries
to hold a winter
shape. You do not
need gloss in a
chest. You need a
seal that matches
the speed of the
outside well enough
that the box does
not become a
bellows.

## The five-minute flip

Before the truck:
flip every solid
top, every lid,
every wide door.
If you see raw
wood where air can
live, you are not
done. If you see
travelers and a
coat, you can
leave. That flip
is cheaper than a
January lens and
a theory about
oak being moody.

The underside is not
humble. It is half the
climate. Finish it.
Then the show face can
keep the compliment.
