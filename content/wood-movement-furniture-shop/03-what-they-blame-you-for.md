# What they blame you for

They do not call and say the equilibrium moisture content
drifted. They call and say the drawer is junk. Or the table
is cheap. Or the door that closed on delivery day now kisses
the strike like it wants a fight.

They are pointing at the only noun in the room that has your
name on it.

## Three costumes, one weather

A drawer that will not pass the divider in August is sides
that took on water. A door that rubs in February is a
stile-and-rail story or a slab that lost width and took the
hinge line with it. A crack in a solid top in January is
width leaving. Those are not three defects. They are one
climate wearing the piece that was least ready.

If you built the drawer with solid sides and a plywood
bottom, August goes after the sides. If you built a
frame-and-panel door and glued the field, February or August
will find the field. If you screwed a top down like a
machine lid, winter will find a glue line or a split near a
fastener.

The client sees the noun. You have to see the year.

## Language that keeps the job

"Humidity" on a first sentence sounds like a shrug. So does
"wood moves." Everyone has an uncle who said that while a
leafing table tore a breadboard off. You need a smaller
sentence.

Try the piece first. "The sides grew across the grain. The
opening did not." Then the room. "August air in this hall is
wetter than the shop was the week we fitted them." Then the
fix, if there is one that is not an insult: plane a whisper
off a runner, free a fastener, wait for October. If the fix
is "the joint was wrong," say that before you talk about the
weather. Weather is not a cloak for a tight screw.

Do not send a lecture. Do not send a Wood Handbook table.
A client who wanted a table did not order a class. One
measured gap, one season, one next step.

## What they are allowed to expect

They are allowed to expect the piece to still be a piece in
December. A drawer that needs a hip-check is not a seasonal
character trait. A table that walks its leaves out of flush
and still comes back in April is within the family. A door
that shows a winter stripe at the panel edge is a door that
worked, and you should have finished that edge so the stripe
is not raw.

They are not allowed, if you said it on the ticket, to
expect flush breadboards in every month, or a solid oak top
that never shows a line, or a piano finish on a porch. If
you did not say it on the ticket, they will expect all
three. That is not greed. That is what catalogs taught them.

Write the seasonal sentence before delivery. Short. On the
care card. "Solid wood changes width with the house. A small
winter gap is the joint working. Call us if a crack opens,
a door will not latch, or a drawer will not pass." You have
then named the three costumes. The call, when it comes, is
a continuation.

## Blame that is actually yours

You delivered from a wet shop into a dry house without a
meter note. You glued a field. You pinned both ends of a
top. You finished the show face and left the underside
thirsty. You fitted drawers on a Monday after the
dehumidifier had been running a week and the case had not
caught up. You used flatsawn oak four feet wide and called
the breadboard "just for looks."

Those are yours. Say so. Then fix the fastener or take the
piece back. Humidity talk after a pinned top is how shops
get a reputation for fog.

## Blame that is the house

A wood stove five feet from a sideboard. A vent that writes
a dry stripe on one door and leaves the other alone. A
sunroom that is a greenhouse in April and a cave in January.
A basement shop-to-be that is still a basement. You can
build for a house. You cannot build for a room that changes
its mind every time someone finds a new thermostat story.

Offer a hygrometer. Not as a gadget. As a witness. If the
hall sits at 25 percent all winter, the oak did not betray
anyone. If the hall sits at 50 and the top still split, you
are back to the joint.

## The photograph

They will send a phone picture, flash on, no sense of width.
Ask for one in raking light, and one of the underside or the
back of the door. The second picture is where the fasteners
and the groove live. The first picture is where the feeling
lives. You need both. You do not argue with a feeling. You
answer a groove.
