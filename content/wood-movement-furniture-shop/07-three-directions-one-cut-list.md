# Three directions, one cut list

Look at the end of the board. The rings are either smiling
across the width, standing up like lines in a book, or
doing something in between that the yard called rift and
charged you for. Those rings are the cut list. They tell
you which number you bought.

## Tangential is the wide, loud one

Tangential means along the ring — the direction a flatsawn
face runs. It is the larger coefficient for almost every
species you will glue into furniture. White oak about
0.00365. Red oak about 0.00369. Sugar maple about 0.00353.
Beech and hickory worse. Walnut and cherry kinder, still
not still. `[VERIFY]` the table cells for the species on
your bench against the Handbook edition you keep.

When a draft in this series says "flatsawn width," it means
this number. Cathedral grain on the face is the pretty
version of the same fact.

## Radial is the quieter twin

Radial means from pith to bark — the face of a quartersawn
board, rays showing on oak if you paid for them. The
coefficient is often near half the tangential. White oak
radial is about 0.00183. Red oak a little less. A
quartersawn panel of the same width and the same moisture
swing moves less, cups less, and costs more because the
log does not give you as many of those faces.

Rift is the compromise: rings around 45 degrees, straighter
grain on the face, movement between the two numbers. Chair
legs like it. So do stiles that should not look like a
topographic map.

## Long-grain is the one you stop talking about

Along the fibers, movement is small enough that furniture
treats it as zero unless you are in a trade that cannot.
You still watch bow and crook. Those are not the Handbook
coefficient. Those are how the board was dried and how you
finished two faces.

## Why the cut list needs all three

A case side that is flatsawn oak, 16 inches wide, is a
tangential problem. A face frame rail on that side is a
long-grain problem sitting on a tangential wall. If you
glue the rail's full length to the side as if both were
plywood, you have glued T to L. Next winter the side is
narrower. The rail is not. Something opens. People call it
a finish line. It is a direction line.

A breadboard is L on a T (or R) field. A breadboard that
works keeps the glue in the middle. A tabletop clip lets T
slide on L. A pinned end treats T as L. The words are
ugly. The failures are uglier.

## How to write it so an apprentice sees it

Species. Cut (flat, rift, quarter). Width that matters.
Which coefficient you are using. The ΔMC you are designing
for — shop to house, August to January, not green to
ovendry unless you are milling wet wood like a fool.

Example line:

`Panel, red oak, flatsawn, 11.25 in wide, T 0.00369, design ΔMC 4, allow 0.17 in in the groove.`

That line is a door that still closes in August. A line
that says `panel 11.25 oak` is a door that might.

## Mixed rings in one glue-up

A panel made of three flatsawn boards is one tangential
width if the rings all smile the same way. If you flip
every other board to fight cup, you still add the widths.
You do not get to average T and R because one board looked
a little quartered. Read the worst face. Design for that.

A glue-up that secretly includes a rift board next to a
wild flat will move differently across its own width. The
panel can go trapezoid by a little. You will see it in a
tight frame before you see it on a bench. Look at the ends
before you trust the face.

## The log already chose

You cannot talk a flatsawn board into radial numbers
because you need the width. You can choose not to buy that
board for a 14-inch door panel. You can choose quartered
for the panel and flat for a painted frame. You can choose
plywood and stop lying about solid. What you cannot do is
put T on the cut list and then fasten it like L.

Three letters. T, R, L. If they are not in the shop
vocabulary, the shop will keep meeting them in January
under other names.
