# The gap in the groove

Turn the door over. If you can see a
little dark at the bottom of the
groove in January, and the panel is
not glued, you are looking at a door
that still knows what year it is. If
you see glue squeeze on the panel
edge, you are looking at a frame that
was asked to be a slab.

Frame-and-panel is not a style. It is
a humidity machine that looks like
furniture.

## The treaty

The frame is long-grain around a
rectangle. Mortise and tenon, or a
cope-and-stick that still has a glue
surface you trust. The panel is width.
It lives in a groove. The groove is
deeper than the panel's tongue or
edge is long. The difference is the
travel you calculated, split, plus
mercy. Glue stays in the frame
joints. Glue does not get a vote on
the field.

A winter rattle on a cupboard door
is ugly if you sold silence. It is
still a passing grade if the panel
cannot migrate enough to show a
sliver of empty groove on one stile.
Space balls, a silicone bump that
is not a glue line, a pin at the
top center — those keep the field
from walking. They do not lock the
width.

## Size the gap like an adult

11.25-inch flatsawn red oak, 4
points of MC: about 0.17 inch of
travel. A 3/8 groove with a panel
seated to the bottom in August is
a split in January, or a bow, or
a frame that opens at a shoulder.
Do the product. Then look at the
cutter you actually have. If the
cutter cannot give you the depth,
change the panel width or the
construction. Do not change the
arithmetic.

Plywood or MDF in a frame still
wants a little room — not for
Handbook oak travel, for paint
and for the fact that "stable"
is not "dead." A painted kitchen
can use a sheet panel and tell
the truth about it.

## Finish the edges

A winter stripe of raw wood at
the panel edge photographs like
a defect. Finish the panel, edges
included, before it goes in. Both
faces. A varnished front and a
naked back is a cup in the groove.
The stripe, when it comes, should
be finished wood, a little paler
if the film aged, not a tan line.

## One pin, not a campaign

A spot of glue or a pin at the
*center* of one rail — usually
the top — keeps a raised field
from drifting so the reveals stay
even as the panel shrinks toward
that pin. One. Not four. Four is
glue in costume.

## What the back tells a buyer

Glue on the field: fail. A
plywood panel in a "solid oak
door" tag: a conversation. A
panel so loose it can show the
groove floor on one side:
sloppy, not seasonal. A silent
January oak panel in a tight
frame: do not praise it until
August. Silence can be a press
fit.

## This is not the joinery essay

Another pack in this shop's
orbit talks about shoulders and
copes. This draft is the humidity
inside that work. If the cope is
perfect and the groove is
starved of space, the door is
still a seasonal failure. If the
groove is generous and the cope
is a hairline, the door will
open at the corner and the panel
will be innocent.

Look at the back. Size the dark
line. Leave it. The rattle in
January is the treaty you can
still hear.

## Space balls are not glue

A rubber bump in the
groove keeps a winter
panel from sounding
cheap in a truck. If
the bump is so fat that
the panel cannot grow,
you have invented a
lock with a friendlier
name. Size the bump
after the product, not
instead of it. Silicone
that smears into a
bead around the field
is glue in costume.
Wipe it. The treaty is
space, not a gasket.

## Painted kitchens

A sheet panel in a
solid frame is how
most kitchens sleep.
Leave a little room
anyway — paint build,
a core that is not
dead, a day the
dishwasher runs. Do
not sell that door as
quartered oak. Sell
it as a treaty with
a stable field. The
back still tells the
truth if someone
looks.
