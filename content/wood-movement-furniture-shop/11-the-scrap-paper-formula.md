# The scrap-paper formula

You can remember "an eighth an inch a foot" until a
beech panel or a four-point swing makes a liar of you.
The formula is shorter than the proverb and it fits on
the back of a story stick.

`Change in width ≈ width × coefficient × change in MC`

Width in inches. Coefficient from the Wood Handbook
(tangential for flatsawn face, radial for quartersawn).
Change in MC in percentage points — 11 minus 7 is 4, not
"a lot."

## One oak panel, done in the open

Flatsawn red oak panel, 11.25 inches in the frame.
Coefficient 0.00369. You are designing for 4 points —
wet shop to dry house, or the other way.

`11.25 × 0.00369 × 4 ≈ 0.166 in`

Call it a strong 5/32. That is the travel you expect if
the coefficient is honest and the ΔMC is honest. In the
groove you do not leave 5/32 on one side and nothing on
the other unless you want the panel to bottom out in
August and rattle in January. You split the travel, and
you add a little because coefficients are not a warrant
and your ΔMC is a guess with a hygrometer in it.

A proverb eighth-per-foot on 11.25 inches is about
3/32. You would have been tight. Tight is how frames
split.

## Where the coefficient comes from

The Handbook numbers are for the middle of furniture
life, roughly 6 to 14 percent MC, and they are built
from green-to-ovendry shrinkage with an assumed fiber
saturation. They are linear on purpose. Real boards are
ruder. Still: this is the best scrap-paper physics the
shop has. Treat a reprint on a flooring site as a
pointer back to the Handbook, not as a second bible.

If you do not know the cut, use tangential. You will
oversize the gap a little on a quartered panel. Oversize
is a rattle you can quiet with a space ball. Undersize
is a split stile.

## ΔMC is the honest hard part

The coefficient is on a page. The moisture change is
your life. Shop at 11 percent MC into a house that will
sit at 7 is 4 points. Shop at 8 into a house at 8 is
zero — until January, when the house goes to 6 and you
had no winter in the gap. A piece that will live in two
houses (a gift, a move) needs the worse ΔMC.

Do not use green-to-ovendry as ΔMC for kiln-dried
furniture stock. That is 20-plus points and a different
trade. Do not use zero because the meter read 8 on a
dry Monday and you liked it.

## Mercy on top of math

I add a bit for the things the formula cannot see: a
meter that was kind, a finish that will slow one face,
a client who runs a stove. Mercy is not a second
proverb. Mercy is 1/32 to 1/16 on a dining-scale panel
after the product is written down. If your mercy is
bigger than the product, you do not trust your ΔMC.
Fix the ΔMC.

Space balls, rubber bumpers, a dab that is not glue —
those keep a winter panel from sounding cheap in a
truck. They are not a substitute for the number.

## Other places the same product lands

Breadboard pin slots: the field must be able to travel
that much past the last pin. Tabletop slots in an
apron: same product, often larger because the top is
wider. A solid chest lid on battens: the batten is
quiet length; the lid is live width; the screws go in
slots sized to the product.

A 36-inch flatsawn oak top, 4 points:

`36 × 0.00369 × 4 ≈ 0.53 in`

Half an inch is not a hairline. If you cannot stand
half an inch of hardware travel, you cannot stand that
top in that climate. Change the cut, the species, the
construction, or the climate you claim. Do not change
the arithmetic.

## Write it where the saw can see it

On the drawing, not in your head. The apprentice who
cuts the groove should see `allow 0.17 + mercy`. The
future you, on a service call, should see what ΔMC you
believed. If the piece split anyway, you learn whether
the coefficient failed you or the house did.

Proverbs are how we taught this before we kept books
on the wall. Keep the proverb if you want. Check it
with the product. The scrap is cheaper than a stile.
