# Buying the cut on purpose

A sticker that says QSWO is a
hope until you see the rings.
A face that looks straight for
three feet can still smile on
the end. You are not buying a
look. You are buying which
Handbook column you will live
in.

## What to pay for

**Flat** when you want cathedral
and you will float the width:
table fields, painted frames,
panels in a treaty. Cheap
width. Loud coefficient. Cup
habit.

**Rift** when you want straight
grain on a leg or a stile and
a coefficient between the
twins. Chair shops live here.
So should a door stile that
will show.

**Quarter** when the width is
live and you cannot or will
not frame it: a wide lid, a
slab door someone refused to
give up, a panel you want a
smaller winter stripe on.
Rays on the oaks that have
them. Fewer wide boards.
More money.

If the budget is flat and the
drawing is quarter, change
the drawing. Do not change
the end grain with a pencil.

## How to read a pile

Walk the ends. Smiles across
the width are flat. Lines
through the thickness are
quarter. Forty-five is rift
if it holds. A pile mixed of
all three with one sticker
is a mixed coefficient. Pull
the boards you will put in
one panel from one habit, or
design for the loudest face
in the glue-up.

Watch for pith, for a ring
pattern that will cup into a
trough when bookmatched, for
a "quartered" face that is
only rift for a foot and
then goes cathedral. That
foot is where a stile
becomes a surprise.

## Width after the rip

The coefficient applies to
the width you keep. A 9-inch
flat with 2 inches of sap
you will rip off is a
7-inch problem. Do the
product on the finished
width. Buy enough that the
rip does not land you in a
different cut — ripping a
flat toward the heart can
leave you a narrower flat,
not a quarter.

## Moisture at the yard

The cut is not the MC. A
beautiful quartered oak at
12 percent in a wet shed is
still traveling. Meter. Ask
when it left the kiln. A
yard that will not let you
meter is selling faces.

## Write the cut on the PO

`Red oak, quarter, 8/4, 7–9
in, KD 6–8, no pith.` If
they substitute flat, you
have a paper. If you did
not write the cut, you
have a shrug and a
cathedral.

## What I will not buy for a live width

Pith. A board that is
already cupped in the pile
with a dirty sticker line.
A mixed-cut glue-up sold as
"the same oak." A slab that
needs to be a door and
cannot be framed when the
client will not pay for
quarter. That last one is
a refuse, not a purchase.

Buy the coefficient on
purpose. The face can still
be pretty. Pretty is
allowed after the end grain
has voted. Before that,
pretty is how shops go
home with the wrong
column.

## A sticker that lied

A bundle marked QSWO
had three true quarter
faces and five smiles.
The smiles would have
become a "quartered"
panel with a flat
coefficient. We pulled
the three, paid for
what they were, and
sent the smiles to a
painted frame pile.
The sticker stayed on
the wrap. The ends
went on the PO as
what we actually
rolled out.

## Chair stock

Legs and stretchers
want rift or quarter
so the face is
straight and the
width is calmer. A
flat cathedral on a
front leg photographs
busy and moves more
across a mortise
cheek than you want.
Pay for the cut on
chair bills even when
you buy flat for
tops. Two POs. Two
columns. One shop.

If the yard will not
let you see ends, you
are buying faces.
Faces are a look.
Ends are the
coefficient. Walk
away or pay as if
every board were
flat and loud.
