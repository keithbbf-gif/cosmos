# Sources

This pack is shop writing. The physics is not folklore. When a
draft quotes a coefficient or an EMC cell, it is aiming at these
references. Cells that were not re-checked against a page number
in this session stay marked `[VERIFY]` in the draft.

## Primary

- USDA Forest Products Laboratory. *Wood Handbook: Wood as an
  Engineering Material* (FPL-GTR-282 and earlier GTR-190).
  Madison, WI. Chapters on moisture relations and dimensional
  stability. Dimensional change coefficients in the 6–14% MC
  range. EMC as a function of relative humidity and temperature.
- Glass, S.V., and S.L. Zelinka. Work derived from the Handbook
  on moisture relations; use the current Handbook table rather
  than a blog reprint.
- Forest Products Laboratory. *Measurement and practical
  application of tangential dimensional change coefficients to
  hardwood flooring.* FPL-RP-711. Useful caution: coefficients
  assume a linear relation below an assumed fiber saturation
  point, and they are based on dimension at about 10% MC.

## Shop books (practice, not a substitute table)

- R. Bruce Hoadley. *Understanding Wood: A Craftsman's Guide to
  Wood Technology.* Taunton. Movement, cut, and the reason a
  flatsawn board cups.
- Ernie Conover / standard furniture texts on breadboard ends,
  tabletop fasteners, and frame-and-panel clearance. Treat any
  "1/8 inch per foot" rule as a coarse check against the
  Handbook math, not as law.

## What we do not treat as authority

- Big-box "ideal indoor humidity" cards with no temperature and
  no species.
- Forum averages that mix radial and tangential.
- A moisture-meter box that never names the species setting.
- Marketing that says a finish "seals wood permanently."

## Coefficients used as working numbers in this pack

Tangential dimensional change coefficients (width change per
inch of width per 1% MC), Wood Handbook / commonly reprinted
shop tables. `[VERIFY]` against the edition on the shelf before
a public number is treated as legal:

| Species | Tangential (flatsawn width) | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| White oak | 0.00365 | Radial ~0.00183 |
| Northern red oak | 0.00369 | Radial ~0.00158 |
| Sugar maple | 0.00353 | High and pale |
| Black walnut | 0.00274 | Quieter than oak, not still |
| Black cherry | 0.00248 | Quieter still |
| American beech | 0.00431 | Shoves |
| True hickory (avg.) | 0.00411 | Shoves |
| Yellow-poplar | 0.00258 | |
| Eastern white pine | 0.00212 | Different animal |
| Teak | 0.00186 | Stable, still not zero |

Worked example used more than once: a **12-inch flatsawn red
oak** width, **4 percentage points** of MC change:

`12 × 0.00369 × 4 ≈ 0.177 in` — call it a strong 3/16 in.

## EMC sketch at ~70°F

From the Handbook EMC table (temperature matters; this is the
shop-temperature row people actually use). `[VERIFY]` the cell
against the edition you hold:

| RH | Approx. EMC |
| --- | --- |
| 30% | ~6.2% |
| 40% | ~7.7% |
| 50% | ~9.2% |
| 60% | ~11.0% |
| 70% | ~13.1% |
| 80% | ~16.0% |

A furniture shop held near **40–50% RH** is aiming at roughly
**8–9% EMC**. A closed-up heated house in January can sit in
the 20s and 30s of RH. A Gulf-coast August without
dehumidification sits the other way.

## Place

Climate examples assume a South Arkansas shop and a
conditioned American house unless a draft says otherwise.
Delivery to a mountain winter or a coastal summer is a
different EMC target. The formula does not change. The
ΔMC does.
