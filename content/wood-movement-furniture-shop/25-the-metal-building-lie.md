# The metal building lie

A lot of furniture in this county is
built under steel. The building is
honest. It gets hot when the sun hits
it. It sweats when the night air is
wetter than the walls. It dumps the
Gulf through a bay door in one
afternoon. The lie is thinking that
is "just a shop" in the same sentence
as a held EMC.

## Radiant weather

In August the west wall is a skillet.
Boards near that wall are not in the
same climate as boards in the core of
the rack. In January the same wall is
a cooler. A top stored against it
cups toward the story the wall told
overnight.

Pull the working pile off the skin of
the building. Shade if you can.
Insulation is not a luxury if you
sell solid wood. If you cannot
insulate, you can still stop using
the wall as a lumber rack.

## Condensation

Night air, cold steel, a warm wet
day behind you — water on the walls
and sometimes on the tools. That
water is RH you did not plan to
give the rack. Do not store porous
parts under a sweating eave. Do not
believe a morning disc that reads
pretty after a night of drip.

## Bay doors

The forklift needs them. So does
every weather front. An open door
is a climate change. Glue after it
closes and the logger sits down,
not while the oak is watching the
parking lot.

If you live with the door open
because heat is unbearable, you
have chosen August air as the shop
air. Design for it. Or fix the
heat. Do not claim 45 percent while
the door is a rectangle of Gulf.

## Slab

Big metal shops sit on concrete
that is a reservoir. Wash it and
you have humidified the first two
feet. Store sleepers under the
first layer of stock. A finishing
room on the same slab as a wet
bathroom or a leak is a finishing
room with a secret.

## What works anyway

Plenty of good work comes out of
steel. The shops that do it treat
the building as an opponent with
habits: logger at the rack, machines
sized for the wet months, pile in
the core, no heroic glue-up the
hour the door was open, winter heat
that does not toast one aisle.
They do not hang a flattering disc
by the man-door and call the
building conditioned.

## Wood buildings lie too

A pretty timber shop can be a wet
crawlspace and a tight roof and a
wood stove in the corner. I am not
selling brick. I am selling
suspicion. The building is part of
the formula. Steel just makes the
suspicion easier to justify because
you can feel the lie on your face
at 3 p.m. in July.

Write the building into the ticket
when the building won the week.
`Door open 2–4 p.m. Logger 58% at
rack.` That line explains a cup
better than "oak is moody." Oak is
not moody. Steel is hot. The pile
was too close to the wall.

## Night sweat

A July day that cooks
the roof and a night
that drops wet air on
cold steel will put
water on the walls and
sometimes on a pile
you left too close.
Morning disc can read
pretty after you open
the man-door and stir
the aisle. The boards
against the eave did
not get the stirring
first. Walk the ends
before you trust the
disc. Wipe the rust
off the tables later.
The EMC work is the
ends.

## What insulation buys

Not comfort only. It
buys a smaller swing
so the dehumidifier
is not emptying the
Gulf every hour and
the heater is not
toasting one aisle.
If you cannot
insulate this year,
you can still move
the rack to the
core, shade a west
wall, and stop
gluing the hour the
bay door was a
rectangle of weather.
Those three are free
compared to a dining
field.
