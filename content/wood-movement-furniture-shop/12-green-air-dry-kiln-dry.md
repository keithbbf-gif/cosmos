# Green, air-dry, kiln-dry

The end of the board has a stamp, or a story from the
yard, or a shrug. Green. Air-dried. Kiln-dried. Those
words tell you how water was asked to leave. They do
not tell you the number on a Tuesday in your rack.

## Green is not a furniture thickness

Green means the cell cavities still hold free water, or
recently did. MC can be 40, 80, more in some species
and sapwood. Width does not start changing in earnest
until you drop below fiber saturation. Weight does.
Bugs do. Checks do, if you rush the shell.

A furniture shop that mills green for chairs or
steam-bending is in a different draft. A furniture shop
that mills green for a dining top and hopes the house
will finish the drying is building a warranty.

## Air-dried is a climate, not a grade

Air-dried means sticks, roof, time, and the EMC of
whatever shed you used. In this county an air-dried
pile can sit for a year and still read high compared
to a winter house. It can also be surprisingly close
to a humid August shop. "AD" on a invoice is not 8
percent. It is "we did not pay for a kiln."

Air-dried stock that then sits in a held shop will
keep traveling. That travel is useful. It is not a
reason to skip the meter.

## Kiln-dried is a schedule someone else ran

KD means a kiln operator took the wood through a
schedule and, if they were any good, equalized and
conditioned so the shell and the core are not at war.
Furniture KD in much of the inland United States is
aimed at 6 to 8 percent. Coastal and wet-house markets
sometimes want 8 to 10. `[VERIFY]` what your supplier
actually targets. The stamp does not always say.

A kiln can also be a hurry. Case-hardened stock —
wet core, dry shell, stress locked in — machines
pretty and then moves when you rip it down the
middle. The two halves smile at you. That smile is
the schedule coming out.

## What the furniture shop actually buys

You buy a present moisture and a stress history.
The meter and a rip tell you more than the stamp.
If you rip a 6-inch KD board and the kerf closes or
opens like a mouth, the board has an opinion that
the stamp omitted. Sticker it in your air before
it becomes a rail.

KD stock stored in a wet shop will climb toward
that shop's EMC. In a few weeks your "6 to 8"
is 10, and you will glue it like it was still
what you paid for. The kiln does not follow the
truck.

## Equalizing in your room

Whatever the stamp, the last drying that matters
is your room. Sticks. Air. Time matched to
thickness. A finishing room that is drier than
the assembly shop will pull a skin you then
assemble into a wetter hall. Pick one climate
for the weeks before glue, and keep the board
there.

## Labels that lie a little

"Cabinet grade KD" can mean pretty faces and a
hope. "Furniture dry" can mean the salesperson
liked the sound. "Shipping dry" can mean dry
enough not to mold in a container, not dry
enough for a winter condo. Ask for a target MC
and a date it was pulled. Then meter it as if
they might be optimistic. They might be honest.
The meter still gets the last word.

## What you do not do

You do not put a wet slab in a household oven,
a closed truck with a heater, or a homemade box
with a dumpstered element and call it a kiln.
That is how shops buy a fire and a case-hardened
mess. Commercial drying is a trade. This series
stops at the rack, the sticks, and the held
room.

Green, AD, KD — useful history. Write it on the
tag. Write today's MC next to it. The second
number is the one the joint will meet.
