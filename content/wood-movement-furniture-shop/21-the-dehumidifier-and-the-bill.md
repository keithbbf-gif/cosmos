# The dehumidifier and the bill

In this county the wet months will fill a
building if you let them. You can live with
that as a person. The oak cannot. A
dehumidifier is how you buy a drier EMC
without pretending the Gulf left. It has a
power bill. So does a crack.

## What the machine is doing

It cools a coil, condenses water, and dumps
heat back into the room. The room gets drier
and a little warmer. The bucket or the hose
gets the Gulf. That is the whole trick. It
is not "drying wood" the way a kiln dries
wood. It is moving the air's RH so the
wood's destination drops. The wood still
needs time.

A unit pointed at one pile makes a gradient.
A unit in the office makes a comfortable
office. Put it where the rack is, with air
that can return, not in a closet of its own
exhaust.

## Drain it like a grown shop

If you are carrying a bucket twice a day,
you will skip a day. The skipped day is
August. Hose to a drain, or a pump that
you test. A full bucket that sits there
clicking is a monument to a target you
abandoned.

Clean the filter. A choked machine runs
and does nothing, which is the most
expensive personality a shop can keep.

## Winter is a different machine

Heat dries air. A closed shop with a unit
heater can sit below the band and pull
width out of stock you acclimated in
November. A small humidifier, or a less
savage heat, or simply not storing
finished work next to the burner, is the
winter half of the same job.

Do not run a humidifier into a mold
problem and call it furniture science.
Watch the slab, the walls, and the logger.
If you cannot hold the band without
growing a science fair on the north wall,
you have a building problem. Fix the
building.

## Sizing without a catalog sermon

Too small and it runs forever and loses.
Too large and it short-cycles and never
pulls the air down evenly. Wet load is
the building plus the bay door plus the
wood itself if you brought in a wet pile.
I will not print a pint number here. The
person who sold you the building, or a
supplier who has seen shops like yours,
can do that math. What I will print: one
appliance from a big box, sitting on a
pallet in the middle of a metal building,
is how people decide "dehumidifiers don't
work."

## The bill versus the photograph

Yes, it costs. So does a dining top that
splits in a condo. So does a kitchen of
drawers you refit in October. If you need
to choose where to spend, spend on the
months you already know you lose — here,
the wet ones — and on a logger that proves
the spend. A machine you never check is a
donation to the power company.

## Safety, short

Condensate on a slab is a slip. Electrics
on a wet floor are a different draft and
a code. Do not jury-rig a household unit
over a puddle and a daisy chain. This
series will not teach you to wire a
commercial system. It will tell you to
treat water and power as a pair that can
hurt you.

The bill is visible. The EMC is quiet.
Pay the visible one so the quiet one
stays in the band. That is the whole
argument for the machine. Personality
does not enter it.

## A bucket that taught the aisle

We ran a household unit
on a pallet for a month
and emptied a bucket
when we remembered.
The logger showed nights
at 60 percent after we
forgot. The oak on the
front of the rack
climbed. The oak in the
core lagged. A hose to
a drain and a unit that
could keep up ended the
personality phase. The
bill went up. The
August glue-ups stopped
looking like April
glue-ups that had lied.

## Winter without a second machine

If heat already holds
you in the band, do not
add a humidifier out of
symmetry. If heat pulls
you to 25 percent at
the rack, you are
building dry and
delivering into houses
that may be dry too —
or you are building
dry and delivering into
a wet hall in May.
Write which. A
humidifier is for the
gap you can measure,
not for a feeling that
the air is "crisp."
