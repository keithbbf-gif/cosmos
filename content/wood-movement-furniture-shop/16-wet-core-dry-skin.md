# Wet core, dry skin

You meter the face. Eight percent. You joint, you
plane, you glue before the dust settles because the
job is late. The next morning the panel has a
whisper of cup, or the rip you made for a rail has
closed at the end. The face was not lying. It was
not the whole board.

## How a gradient happens

Drying starts at the surface. A kiln that hurries
can leave a wet core inside a pretty shell. A wet
week on a stickered pile can wet the skin of a dry
board. A finishing room can pull a skin while the
core still thinks it lives in the assembly shop. A
slab with sealed ends and an open face can be three
climates in one piece.

Furniture thickness hides this better than a
turning blank, and worse than a veneer. 8/4 and
up is where shops get humbled. 5/4 dining stock
is not immune.

## What the instruments see

Pinless: shell. Short pins: shell. Deep pins or
a fresh rip: closer to the truth. End grain:
a rumor that is often wetter and always faster
to change.

If face and end disagree by a lot, do not
average them. You have a gradient. Wait, or rip
a witness and meter the new surface after it
stops flashing moisture into the room.

## Case-hardening is the mean cousin

A board can be dry enough on a meter and still
be stressed — the shell shrank while the core
was wet, then the core shrank later and the
shell was already set. You rip it and the two
halves bow away or pinch the kerf. That is not
a moisture number. That is a history. Sticker
the halves, give them your shop EMC, then
joint. If they keep moving, they are shorter
parts, not a door stile.

## Milling opens the core

Every pass of the planer is a new face. The
board can move after you "had it flat" because
you let a wetter plane out and it started
toward EMC from a new number. This is why
people who flatten a slab in one heroic
afternoon invent cup. Flatten, wait, flatten
again. The wait is not superstition. It is
the core arriving at the meeting.

A glue-up of freshly planed wet-core boards
is a glue-up of travelers. They will travel
after the clamps come off. Sometimes you get
away with it in April. August will send the
photograph.

## Rain on the rack

A storm on an open shed wets skins. Faces
read high. Cores may still be where they
were. If you wait a day and the face drops
and the end stays put, you had weather on
the paint, not a soaked pile. If the ends
climb too, you have a different week. Do
not kiln the panic with a heater aimed at
the stack. You will bake a new gradient.

## Shop rule

Thick stock, two readings: face and a
witness. Witness can be a deep pin in
waste, a cutoff, or a rip you were going
to make anyway. If they disagree, the
board is not late. You are early.

## A slab that flattered the pad

An 8/4 walnut from a yard
that stores under roof,
pinless on the face at
7.8, species set right.
We ripped a 3-inch
witness for a rail. The
new face read 10.1 after
an hour. The pad had
been honest about the
shell. The shell had
been to a kiln. The
core had not finished
the trip, or it had
climbed in the shed.
We stickered the halves
for a week. They moved.
Then they stopped. Then
we jointed. The lunch
number would have been
a dining rail that
walked after the
mortiser.

## Heaters make gradients

A torpedo heater aimed
at a pile dries skins
and leaves cores. So
does a sunny west wall
on a metal building.
The face will flatter
you at 3 p.m. The rip
at 7 a.m. will not.
If you must heat, heat
the room, not the pile.
If you cannot, treat
every afternoon reading
as a skin until a
witness says otherwise.

The lunch number is a skin. The joint
lives with the whole thickness. Wait until
those two are on speaking terms, then
glue. The afternoon is patient when the
photograph is not.
