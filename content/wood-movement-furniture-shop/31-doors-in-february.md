# Doors in February

The door that closed on delivery
kisses the strike, or misses it,
or shows a wedge of light that
was not there. February is dry
in a heated house. Width left.
The latch is where people point.
The hinge is where the geometry
started.

## Slab versus treaty

A solid slab is a width hanging
on two or three hinges. When it
shrinks, the latch side can open
a gap or, if the slab also
cups, bind at a corner. When it
swells, the latch side binds
even if you set the hinges in a
dry week. Wide flatsawn slabs
are a hard life. Wide quartered
slabs are a slightly easier
hard life. Wide painted plywood
slabs are a different product
and an honest one.

A frame-and-panel door keeps a
long-grain rectangle. The latch
and the hinge live on stiles
that do not change length. The
field changes. If you did not
glue the field, February should
not move the strike. If it
does, look at the frame joints,
the hinge screws in raw mortises
that took on moisture, or a
case that is no longer square.

## Hinge mortises

A mortise cut after finish, left
raw, will take water and swell
around the metal. A mortise cut
in a wet week and then dried
will loosen. Finish the mortise
or at least seal it. Use screws
that bite the long grain of the
stile, not a proud short-grain
lip you invented with a chisel.

A door that sagged is not always
humidity. Sometimes it is a
hinge that was always a little
soft. Check that before you
lecture the house.

## The latch gap as a diary

A gap that opened evenly on the
latch side of a *slab* is width
leaving. A gap that opened as a
wedge is a cup or a hinge. A
frame-and-panel that suddenly
misses the strike may be a case
side that moved — solid case
sides are widths — or a floor
that dropped a corner. Do not
plane a stile until you know
which diary you are reading.

## Pair doors

Two doors that met in April and
overlap or gap in February are
two slabs or two frames on a
case that also moved. A meeting
stile with a bead can hide a
little seasonal light. It cannot
hide a quarter inch you should
have built as plywood or as
frame-and-panel.

## Shop-fit versus house-fit

You fit in your air. The house
is drier in February in this
story. A proud fit in a wet
shop is a February bind. A
loose fit in a wet shop may
look right in February and
sloppy in August. For slabs,
I would rather a little winter
light than a slammed latch.
For frames, I would rather
the rectangle be right and
the panel be free.

## The plane

If you plane a binding edge in
February, you may have a gap
in August. Mark the month on
the back of the door. A door
that has been planed every
winter is a door that is
becoming a different width
than the drawing. At some
point you rebuild, or you
change the construction, or
you change the house air.

February is a dry editor. It
cuts width and then asks the
latch to comment. Build so
the comment is a sliver of
light, not a hip against the
handle.
