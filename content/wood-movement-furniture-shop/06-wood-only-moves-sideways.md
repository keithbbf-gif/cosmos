# Wood only moves sideways

Chalk a line along a six-foot oak apron. Chalk a line across
a twelve-inch oak top. Leave both in a house that goes from
a wet August to a dry January. The apron line is still a
six-foot line. The top line is shorter. Not by a hair you
need a feeler for. By a fraction you can see if you were
foolish enough to fasten the ends.

That is the job, reduced to two chalk lines.

## Along the grain is a rounding error

From green to ovendry, length change along the grain is on
the order of a tenth or two of a percent for most furniture
species. `[VERIFY]` the species on the bench against the
Handbook if someone is building a clock case that cannot
spare a thirty-second. On a six-foot rail, a tenth of a
percent is less than a sixteenth. From furniture-dry to
house-dry — a few points of moisture, not green to bone —
you will not find it without wanting to.

This is why a stile stays a stile. This is why a breadboard
does not grow in length to match a shrinking field. This is
why you can hang a door on a long-grain hinge stile and
trust the hinge spacing next year.

It is also why people get sloppy. They remember "wood moves"
and they treat every direction as a mood. Then they are
surprised when the thing that failed was the width they
pinned.

## Across the grain is the furniture

Width is radial or tangential depending on how the board was
sawn. Face of a flatsawn board is mostly tangential. Face of
a quartersawn board is mostly radial. Tangential is the
larger number, often near twice the radial. A twelve-inch
flatsawn red oak width through four moisture points is about
0.177 inch. The same width quartersawn is closer to half
that. Neither is zero. Both are the reason frame-and-panel
exists.

Thickness moves too. It is the same physics on the shorter
distance. A 5/4 top loses a little thickness in a dry house.
You notice it at a proud breadboard or a proud inlay before
you notice it on a caliper.

## The joint is a conversation between a dead direction and a live one

Mortise-and-tenon works because the tenon's length is
long-grain in the rail and the mortice's walls are
long-grain in the stile — the rectangle of the frame is
drawn in the quiet direction. The panel inside is the live
width. Breadboards work when the live width of the field
can slide on a quiet-length end. Tabletops fail when a
quiet apron is asked to hold a live width at both ends.

Draw a grain arrow on every wide part. If two arrows
disagree and you glued the disagreement, you have stored a
year.

## What "sideways" does not excuse

A long case can still go out of square if one side picks up
moisture and the other does not. A bow in a long stile is
not width; it is a board that was not straight and a finish
that treated two faces differently, or a pith that should
not have been in the door. Longitudinal movement will not
save you from a board that was never a door.

End grain drinks and leaves faster than face grain. A
tabletop with raw ends and a finished field will show its
mood at the ends first. That is not length change. That is
width and thickness changing where the air had a shorter
path.

## Shop habit

On the cut list, mark L and W. Length is the quiet number.
Width is the number you size a gap for. If a part's
important dimension is width — a panel, a solid door, a
chest lid — it gets a moisture note and a fastening note.
If a part's important dimension is length — a stile, an
apron, a breadboard — it gets grain orientation checked so
you did not accidentally make its width the thing that
shows.

People love to say wood is alive. Alive is a poster. Sideways
is a rule. Build as if only width could walk, and most of
the winter cracks stop asking for the glue bottle.

## The six-foot lie

I have heard, in more than one shop, that a long table
"must have shrunk in length" because the leaves no longer
meet a wall the way they did on delivery. Measure the
aprons. Measure the leaves across. The wall did not move.
The leaves lost width, the pins or the alignment cones
started to miss, and the story became length because length
is how people think tables get smaller. A tape on the apron
ends the story. The chalk line was right the first time.
