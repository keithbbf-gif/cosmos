# A top that can breathe

Turn the table over before you praise
the finish. If the apron is pinned to
the top with a row of tight screws at
the ends, you are looking at next
winter's crack, or last winter's,
filled. The show face can be perfect.
The underside is the joint.

## Two parts, two directions

Aprons and rails are long-grain. They
keep a rectangle. The top is a width,
often several boards glued, often
flatsawn because that is what a dining
field wants to look like. Across 36
inches of oak, a few points of moisture
is a serious fraction. The base cannot
grow and shrink to match. So the
connection has to slide.

Center fasteners can be tighter. End
fasteners cannot. That is the whole
underside.

## What "breathe" means here

Not a mystic pore. A path for the
width to change without taking the
apron with it. Slots in the apron,
elongated holes in a cleat, figure-8
fasteners that can twist, Z-clips in
a groove, wooden buttons in slots,
pocket screws in slots if you must
and if you actually slot them.

A tight screw into a solid apron at
the end of a wide top is a lock. Two
locks, one at each end, are a clamp
across the year. The wood will open
somewhere else. Glue lines like that
somewhere.

## Pedestals and four legs

A four-rail apron is a rectangle you
can clip to all around, slots on the
cross-grain sides. A pedestal with a
small plate is easier to get wrong:
a small plate pinned hard in four
corners is a small clamp. Slot the
plate. Or use a larger plate with
travel. A trestle with a wide cleat
is a breadboard problem wearing
different clothes — glue or pin the
center, slot the ends.

## Leaves and alignment

Leaves are their own widths. They
meet the table in the dry month and
miss in the wet, or the other way,
if you designed the alignment pins
for one EMC. A little forgiveness
in the cones is not sloppy. A leaf
that cannot come home in August is
how people store leaves in a closet
and then accuse the table of
shrinking in length. Measure the
aprons. The length is still there.

## Delivery habit

Do not tighten every screw "for the
truck" and forget to free them. If
you need extra screws for travel,
use extra slots, not extra locks.
Blankets, not a week of plastic.

## The look from above

A top that can breathe still looks
like a top. The clips do not show.
The winter glue line, if you locked
it, does. Clients do not ask to see
the underside until something fails.
Show them anyway at delivery. A
ten-second tour of the travelers
is cheaper than a January theory
about glue.

## If you already locked it

Free the ends. Fill the old holes if
you must, cut slots, refasten. Do it
before you fill a crack. A filled
crack in a still-locked top is a
wedge waiting for the next dry week.

Breathe is a joke word. Slide is the
real one. Build the underside so the
width can slide. The finish can be
as quiet as you like. The fasteners
should not be.

## Count the travelers

A 36-inch oak top on
a four-rail apron: I
want travelers every
six to eight inches
along the long rails,
tighter toward the
center, freest at the
ends. The short rails
are closer to quiet
length meeting quiet
length if the top
grain runs the long
way — still do not
pin the corners like
a crate. Corners are
where shops add
"just one more screw"
for the truck.

## Pedestal plates

A small steel plate
with four holes is a
clamp the size of a
hand. Slot the plate
or use a larger one
with travel at the
corners and a tight
middle. I have seen
a pedestal split a
glue line that a
four-rail apron would
have survived,
because the plate
was proud of its
job.

## Leaves in the year

Alignment pins fitted
in April will miss in
January if the leaves
lost width. A little
cone forgiveness is
not sloppy. A leaf
that cannot come home
gets stored in a
closet and then
someone says the
table shrank in
length. Tape the
aprons. The length is
still there. The
width left.
