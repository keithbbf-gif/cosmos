# Cross-grain, a year later

The joint looked like furniture in
the clamp. A year later there is a
hairline at the ends of an applied
molding, or a breadboard that split,
or a cleat that still holds the
middle of a lid and has let the
ends cup around it. Nobody
remembered the arrows. The year
did.

## What cross-grain means here

One part wants to change width.
The other part, along the same
meeting, does not — or wants to
change in a different direction.
A cleat screwed and glued across
a solid lid. A face-frame rail
glued its full length to a solid
side. A breadboard glued to the
ends of a field. A mitered
molding around a panel that is
still moving. A tabletop pinned
to an apron.

Hide glue, PVA, epoxy — they
all store the argument if you
do not leave slide. Some creep
a little. Creep is not a design
method. It is how a joint looks
all right until it does not.

## The year is the test

A lot of these joints survive
until the first real winter or
the first Gulf August, whichever
the shop did not build in. That
is why the failure feels like
betrayal. It is a delayed
receipt. The clamp was never
the test.

If you need the parts to meet
for looks, meet them in a way
that can slide: a slot, a
floating tenon with room, a
molding nailed in the middle
and free at the ends, a
finish line you accept.

## Cleats

A lid needs help staying flat.
A cleat can help if the screws
are in slots and the glue is
absent, or present only at a
centerline. A cleat glued
across the whole width is a
cup you scheduled. Battens on
the back of a trestle top are
the same sentence.

## Face frames on solid sides

A plywood box with a solid
face frame is a common peace.
A solid side with a rail glued
full-length is a peace that
expires. Use slots, or a
plywood side, or accept a
finish line at the rail in
winter and tell the client
before they find it.

## Miters on a moving field

A mitered breadboard look —
caps on three sides of a
field — is a pretty way to
ask three quiet lengths to
hold one live width. I have
seen it done with floating
corners and a lot of skill.
I have seen it open at every
miter and get called
"settling." Settling is what
houses do. This is grain.

## How to catch it on a drawing

Draw arrows. Where two arrows
disagree and a glue note
spans the full meeting, you
have a year-later joint.
Change the glue note or
change a material. Do it in
pencil. The photograph will
not accept a footnote.

A year later is still your
joint. The calendar is just
the inspector who works
weekends.
