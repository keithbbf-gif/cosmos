# Flatsawn is a movement choice

The yard pulls a board with a cathedral the size of a barn
door. Everyone at the counter makes the noise. You pay for
the look. You take it home and you build a panel as if you
had bought quartered stock, because the species is oak and
oak is what you always use.

You bought tangential movement and a cupping habit. The
cathedral was the receipt.

## What the sawyer did

A flatsawn board is taken off the log so the rings run
mostly across the width, smiling or frowning on the end.
The face shows the long V of the growth rings. That face
is the tangential plane. It moves more. It also cups as
the rings try to flatten when the board dries or, later,
when one face loses moisture faster than the other.

A quartersawn board is taken so the rings stand through
the thickness. The face is radial. Rays show on the oaks
that have them. Movement across the width is smaller. Cup
is less eager. You get fewer wide boards from the log,
and you pay for that.

Rift sits between. Straight grain, rings near 45 degrees,
a favorite for legs and stiles. Movement between the two.
The yard will sometimes call a lucky flat "rift" because
the face looks straight for a few feet. Read the end.

## Why shops still buy flat

Width. Price. The look people point at. A lot of good
furniture is flatsawn on purpose: tabletops with cathedral
bookmatched down the middle, painted casework where the
movement is handled by plywood and a face frame, panels
that are allowed to float. Flat is not a sin. Flat without
a gap is a sin.

Quartered oak in a frame-and-panel door is how you get a
smaller winter stripe and a panel that stays nearer to
flat. Quartered oak in a 42-inch dining top is money, and
it still wants fasteners that slide, because radial is
smaller, not zero.

## Cup is part of the purchase

Flatsawn cups. The rings flatten; the pith side goes
convex as the board dries. In a finished top the same
habit shows up if the underside is naked and the show
face is sealed, or if the top is stored leaned against a
wall. You can fight cup by flipping boards in a glue-up
so neighboring cups argue. You cannot vote it out of a
single wide flatsawn lid.

If you need a 14-inch solid door and you will not build
frame-and-panel, flatsawn is a harder life than quartered.
If you will not buy quartered, do not build the 14-inch
slab and then act betrayed in August.

## Buying notes that belong on the invoice

Ask for the cut you are paying for. Look at ends, not
only faces. A "quartered" pile with smiling rings is a
flat pile with good lighting. Measure width you actually
need after ripping off sap or a waney edge; the
coefficient applies to the width that remains.

For chair stock and stiles, rift or quarter pays you back
in straight grain and calmer width. For a painted
shaker door, a stable panel (plywood or MDF) in a solid
frame is a movement choice that lets you buy pretty flat
for the frame and stop asking a slab to behave.

## The bookmatch trap

Two flatsawn faces bookmatched are a look. They can also
be two cups that agree and make a panel into a shallow
trough. Alternate the cups unless the design needs the
mirror more than it needs the flat. Tell the client if
you chose the mirror. They should know you spent stability
on the photograph.

## What to write on the shop drawing

Not "oak panel." `Oak, flat, 12.5 wide, float 3/16`. Or
`Oak, quarter, 12.5 wide, float 3/32` — then check the
math. The drawing that only says oak will be built by
whoever is fastest, with whatever is on the rack, and
the rack is usually flat because flat is what the mill
wanted to sell.

Cathedral grain is a choice. Pay for it on purpose. Leave
room for it on purpose. Do not call it character when it
splits a stile. Character is what you say after you
refused to read the end grain.
