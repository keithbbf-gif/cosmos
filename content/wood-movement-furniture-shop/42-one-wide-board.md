# One wide board

There is a romance to a top
from a single board. One
cathedral. No glue line to
catch a thumbnail. Clients
pay for that romance. They
also pay, whether they know
it or not, for one cup and
one pith-to-bark argument
with nothing to vote against
it.

## The single field

The coefficient applies to
the whole width. A 16-inch
flatsawn oak, 4 points of
MC, is on the order of a
quarter inch of travel. The
rings decide the cup. If
pith is in the board, you
have a split scheduled
unless you are making a
rustic that is allowed to
speak. Fasteners must travel
that whole width. A
breadboard on a single
wide field is a serious
tenon, not a decoration.

I like a single board when
the cut is quartered or
rift, or when the width is
modest, or when the client
understands proud and shy.
I do not like it when the
board is a flatsawn trophy
and the drawing has four
tight screws.

## The committee

Three boards glued, cups
alternated, grain agreeable,
is how most dining fields
stay nearer to flat. You
still add the widths for
the product. You still
fasten for the total. You
get glue lines. Good glue
lines in raking light are
a craft. Bad ones are a
winter hairline people
blame on humidity.

Bookmatch two flats and
you may buy a trough if
the cups agree. Alternate
unless the mirror is the
job.

## Mixing widths in one top

A wide in the middle and
narrows on the edges can
look like a tree and
behave like a panel. It
can also move differently
across itself if the cuts
differ. Read the ends of
every member. Design for
the loudest.

## Thickness

A wide 8/4 that you will
plane to 13/16 after the
core speaks is a different
week than a 4/4 you glue
this afternoon. Wide and
thick is where wet cores
live. Witness rip. Wait.
The romance of one board
dies in a hurry if you
flatten it once and
deliver.

## What to tell them

"One board will show one
cup habit and a larger
seasonal step at a
breadboard. A glue-up will
show lines and stay
flatter." Let them pick.
Do not pick the romance
for them and then hide
the underside locks.

## Shop rule

If the finished width is
over a foot of flatsawn
oak, I want a treaty on
the ticket — float,
travelers, or a refuse —
whether it is one board
or five. The number of
glue lines does not
cancel the product. It
only changes the cup
politics.

## When the romance is worth it

A 9-inch quartersawn
walnut hall top from one
board is a quiet width
and a face that reads as
a tree. The product is
small. The cup habit is
mild. I will take that
romance. A 18-inch
flatsawn red oak from
one board with pith
near the edge is a
split wearing a price
tag. I will not take
that romance without a
refuse or a frame.

## Glue lines as honesty

Clients sometimes ask
for no glue lines as if
lines were a defect.
A good line in raking
light is two boards
that agreed. A winter
hairline in a glue line
is often a lock, not a
committee failure. Show
them a sample of a
clean glue-up and a
cupped single board.
Let the hand choose.
Hands are less romantic
than photographs.

## Flattening a trophy

One wide 8/4, cathedral
you could sell as a
poster: flatten, sticker
overnight, flatten
again, then decide if
it is still a top. If
the second flat spends
too much thickness, it
is shorter parts. The
trophy does not get to
override the caliper.
A thin trophy cups
easier and photographs
the same until January.

One wide board is a
choice. Buy it like a
coefficient. Fasten it
like a width. Photograph
it like a tree if you
want. The year will
photograph the cup if
you only did the last
part.
