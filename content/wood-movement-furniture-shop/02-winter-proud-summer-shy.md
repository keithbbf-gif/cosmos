# Winter proud, summer shy

In January the breadboard stands a little proud of the field.
You can catch a fingernail on the step. In July the field has
grown and the breadboard looks shy, a lip the other way, or
flush if you were lucky in the shop. The client photographs
the January step and sends it as a defect.

It is the table telling the truth.

## What a breadboard is for

A breadboard end is a strip whose grain runs across the end of
a solid top. It hides end grain. It tries to keep the end of a
wide top closer to flat. It is not a clamp. It cannot hold a
24-inch oak field at one width through a year unless you are
willing to split something.

The field changes width. The breadboard changes thickness a
little and length almost not at all. Those two parts do not
agree, and they were never supposed to. The joint that works
is a tenon, or a tongue, that is glued hard in the middle and
free toward the edges — pins in elongated holes, or pegs that
can walk, or screws in slots. The middle keeps the breadboard
on the centerline. The edges let the field come and go.

Glue in the last six inches of a wide top is how a breadboard
becomes a splitter. The field shrinks. The glue does not. The
tenon checks. Or the field splits behind the shoulder. Or the
breadboard bows into a smile because it is being asked to
compress a season in a weekend.

## Proud is a grade

If you delivered in a sticky September and the house went dry,
the field lost width. The breadboard, long-grain, barely
noticed. The ends of the field pulled in. The breadboard looks
proud. That is the winter report. If you finished the end of
the field before you closed the joint, the stripe that appears
is not raw wood. If you did not, the stripe is pale and the
photograph looks worse than the joint.

Summer shy is the other half. The field picks up moisture,
gains width, and the breadboard looks inset. A shy breadboard
that still has a clean shoulder is not a refund. A shy
breadboard that has lifted, or a pin that has started to
oval the hole into a tear, is a joint that was tight in the
wrong month.

People want flush in every season. Flush in every season is
plywood, or a lie.

## How much proud is honest

Back to the same 12-inch flatsawn red oak thought, except
now the field is 36 or 42 inches. Three feet of flatsawn oak
through four points of moisture is on the order of half an
inch of width change if you let the coefficient do all the
talking. `[VERIFY]` the exact field width and the actual ΔMC
before you quote a client a fraction as a promise. The
breadboard does not take that change in its length. The
field takes it in from both ends, more or less evenly if
the center is the only glue.

A sixteenth proud on a small breakfast top can be the whole
story. A quarter proud on a harvest table is still in the
family if the tenon is sound. A breadboard that has pulled
away from the field, showing a black line of dirt in the
shoulder, is a tenon that let go or a tongue that was never
seated. That is not humidity wearing a costume. That is a
joint.

## What you tell them in the kitchen

Bring a nickel. Stand it on the step if the step will hold
it. Talk about the year, not the glue. Show the underside if
you can: slots, not a row of tight screws into an apron that
also pinned the ends. If the underside is a row of tight
screws, you have two problems and the proud breadboard is
the polite one.

Do not sand the proud edge off in January to make the
photograph stop. You will be shy in July and you will have
cut the only witness mark the table had. A light arris so
nobody catches a cloth is manners. Flattening a seasonal
step with a belt sander is how shops create the next call.

## Shop habit that keeps the tenon

Glue the center. How much center depends on the width and
the species. A narrow cherry hall table can take more glue
than a wide red oak dining top. Pins toward the ends go in
slots. If you drawbore, the offset is a conversation with
the slot, not a chance to lock the edge.

Finish the field ends. Finish the underside. Do not deliver
a top that was in a dehumidified finishing room for a week
and a shop that swings twenty grains the rest of the year
without a meter note on the ticket.

## The photograph you hope for

The honest photograph is January and July of the same end,
same raking light, same nickel. Proud, then shy. The tenon
still a tenon. That pair of pictures is the job. A single
January picture, sent in anger, is only half a year. Half a
year is how breadboards get a bad name.
