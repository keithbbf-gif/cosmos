# Cups away from the pith

Set a flatsawn offcut on the bench, end toward you. The
rings smile or frown. Leave that offcut in a dry week
with one face open to air. It will not stay flat. The
rings try to become straighter lines. The board cups.

On a drying flatsawn board the pith side goes convex —
crowned toward the heart, hollow toward the bark. Shops
argue about the mnemonic. Look at the end instead of
the poster. The geometry is older than the argument.

## Why the rings flatten

Tangential shrinkage is larger than radial. A ring is
a longer arc on the bark side of a flatsawn piece than
it is toward the pith. As the board loses moisture,
that longer arc shortens more. The smile opens. The
board takes a set.

A moisture gradient makes it faster. The face that
dries first shrinks first. A top finished on the show
face and raw underneath will cup toward the finish in
a dry house — the underside loses water, shrinks,
pulls hollow. That is why both faces get finish, even
if the underside is a simpler coat.

## Quartersawn stays out of the fight

Rings standing through the thickness do not have a
wide smiling arc to flatten. Quartered stock still
moves in width. It does not cup with the same
enthusiasm. That is part of what you pay for. Rift
is in between.

A flatsawn glue-up can fight itself: alternate the
cups so neighboring boards disagree. The panel is
less of a trough. It is also a busier face. Choose
on purpose.

## Pith in the board

A board that still has pith in it is two radial
halves arguing along a rotten axis. It will move
in ways a clean flatsawn board will not. Keep pith
out of furniture widths unless you are making a
rustic that is allowed to speak. Even then, know
you invited a split.

## Show face politics

Some tablemakers put heart side up so seasonal cup
puts the edges down on the apron. Some put bark
side up for wear. Some ignore both and trust
fasteners and a fair glue-up. I care more about
even moisture and even finish than about winning
the mnemonic. If you pick a rule, write it on the
shop wall so two benches do not reverse it on the
same job.

What I will not do is pick a show face because the
cathedral is prettier and then leave the other face
naked. Pretty is how cups get commissioned.

## Storage cups

A board leaned against a wall dries on one face.
A stack without stickers dries on two edges and
stays wet in the middle. A top stored show-face
down on a cold floor picks up a climate you did
not meter. A lot of "the wood was bad" is a
storage cup. Flatten, sticker, wait, then decide
if the board is a top or a cutting list for
shorter parts.

## When cup is already a piece

A finished top that cups in the house may still
be fastened through the year if the fasteners
allow width change and the cup is shallow. A cup
that rocks a bowl is a refinish-both-faces and
a climate talk, or a rebuild. Do not belt-sand
the show face into a thinner lens and call it
flat. You have made a veneer of the cathedral
and left the underside in charge.

## Read the end every time

Before you joint a face, look at the rings. Before
you choose a lid, look at the rings. Before you
blame a season, look at the rings and the finish
history. Cup is not a character trait. It is
tangential shrinkage wearing a curve. The end
grain told you which curve you bought.

If you cannot see the
rings, you cannot name
the cup. Cut a witness
or look at the end you
already have. A cup
without readable rings
is still a cup. It is
just a cup you have not
bought honestly yet.
