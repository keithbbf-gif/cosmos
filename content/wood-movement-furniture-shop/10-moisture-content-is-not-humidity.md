# Moisture content is not humidity

Someone says the shop is at 8 percent humidity. Someone else
says the oak is at 45 percent moisture. Both sentences are
how tickets get stupid. The wall and the board do not share
a scale.

## Two instruments, two nouns

**Moisture content (MC)** is the water in the wood, as a
percent of the wood's oven-dry weight. A furniture board at
8 percent MC has eight pounds of water for every hundred
pounds of dry wood substance, in the lab sense. On the
bench it is the number a meter is trying to guess.

**Relative humidity (RH)** is how full the air is, compared
to how full it could be at that temperature. 45 percent RH
is not 45 percent water in the board. It is air that is
less than halfway to fog.

Those two numbers meet at **EMC**: the MC the wood is
headed for in that air. At shop temperature, 45 percent RH
points at roughly 8 to 9 percent MC. That meeting is why
people mash the words together. The mash still wrecks a
cut list.

## How the mash shows up

"I kept the humidity at 7 percent." No. You kept a room
that dry only if you are running a desert or a bad
hygrometer. You might have kept *boards* near 7 percent
MC. Say that.

"The wood was 50 percent, so I waited." If you mean RH,
you waited in a wet shop. If you mean MC, you had stock
that was still near fiber saturation and you should have
waited a lot longer than a lunch.

"The house is dry, so the dresser is 20 percent." The
house may be 20 percent RH. The dresser is not 20 percent
MC unless something has gone very wrong in a basement.

Write MC on the wood. Write RH on the wall. Write EMC
only when you mean the destination.

## Why ovendry weight is worth one sentence

The definition sounds like a lab. Keep one sentence of it
anyway. MC is not "how wet it feels." A dense oak and a
soft pine at the same MC do not weigh the same extra
ounces, and they do not move the same, but they share a
definition. The meter is a shortcut to that definition,
with species settings and lies of its own. If you do not
know what the number is a percent *of*, you will believe
a pin that is talking about a different story.

## Fiber saturation, in passing

Around 28 to 30 percent MC, for most species, the cell
walls are full and the cavities still hold free water.
Above that, the board can feel soaked and still not
change width much as free water leaves. Below that, every
point of MC you lose is width you lose. Furniture lives
below. Green lumber and a wet slab live above, then
cross. If someone says a kiln-dried board is at 25, you
are not in a finishing conversation yet.

## Talking to a client without the mash

"The air in the hall is dry. The wood will give off water
and the top will get a little narrower." That is RH
leading MC, leading width. You never need to say EMC at a
kitchen island. You do need to not say the dresser is at
30 percent humidity.

## Ticket grammar

A good line: `Shop 46% RH, 72°F. Red oak panel 8.4% MC
pinless, species set oak, 3/4 face. Target house ~40% RH.`

A bad line: `humidity 8`.

The bad line can mean anything. In a year it will mean
whatever the argument needs. The good line still knows
which instrument spoke.

Use the long words until the short ones behave. Then you
can say "the rack is at eight" and everyone in the shop
will hear MC. Until then, say the noun. EMC is
the third noun when wall and board meet — keep
all three on the ticket when someone argues from
slang.
