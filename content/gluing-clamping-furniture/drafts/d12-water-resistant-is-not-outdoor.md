The customer said the porch table could be “the same glue as the dining table, it’s under a roof.” The roof was a suggestion. Wind-driven rain in Bradley County does not read a label. I used epoxy on the joints that would stay out and I told her the top would still need finish like a boat needs paint. She wanted a yellow-bottle story. I would not sell her one.

Water-resistant PVA is a class. Sheets talk Type II, Type III, soak tests. `[VERIFY]` the class on the bottle you opened. A Type II line will take a wet rag and a spilled glass. It will not take a year of sun, standing water, and freeze. Type III goes further and still is not a dock.

I had already paid for a shorter version of her sentence. A garden bench, yellow bottle, “it will live under the tree.” The tree is not a roof. After one winter the stretchers were still together and the ends of the tenons were dark and furry where water had sat. The second winter I could rack a leg with my hand. I recut what I could recut. I bolted what I could not trust. I do not tell that as a customer story. I tell it as a bench I built and then repaired in the same shop.

## Kitchen is not climate

I will glue a kitchen table base in water-resistant aliphatic and sleep. I will not glue a sink-adjacent stool in interior-only and pretend the mop is theoretical. I will not glue a cutting board in hide and then talk about craft unless the owner already accepted maintenance.

The stool was mine. Interior yellow, a seat that lives by a mop bucket. The joints did not explode. They grayed. A year of wet and dry and the stretchers had a soft give that was not a loose pin. I took it apart as far as PVA will let you take a thing apart, which is not far, and I remade the rails in a grade that matches a mop. I sleep on that one. I do not sleep on a porch.

Outdoor furniture wants mechanical fasteners, a glue that is a thermoset or a construction cousin, finish that will be renewed, and joints that can drain. Hide is a joke outside. Interior PVA is a short story. Water-resistant PVA is a longer short story. Epoxy and good bolts are a paragraph I will sign.

Drain is a joint decision, not a bottle decision. I cut a little relief on the bottom of an outdoor stretcher so a puddle has a door. I do not bury an end grain tenon in a mortise that holds a cup of rain and then ask Type III to be a roof. The porch table got through-bolts at the legs and epoxy in the cheeks I could still call cheeks. The top got a film finish I said out loud would need to come back. That conversation is the job. The bottle is not the conversation.

## Labels are not roofs

“Waterproof” on a consumer bottle is a fight I do not have in the aisle. I read the technical line. If there is no technical line, I treat the bottle like interior. Marketing is not a soak test.

I also do not trust “exterior” on a bottle I have never used on a scrap left in the yard. A scrap on the shop roof for a season is a better priest than a cap color. I have run that scrap. I have also skipped the scrap and then paid for it. I run the scrap.

Two oak pairs, same morning, two bottles, both claiming more water than a kitchen. I screwed them to a scrap of plywood and I left the plywood on the shop roof from a late spring through a Bradley County summer and a freeze. One pair still took a mallet. One pair clapped apart in my hands after the first long wet week. I threw the polite bottle away for outdoor work. I kept it for kitchens. The roof does not care which cap was darker.

I do not invent the class numbers I did not read that day. `[VERIFY]` the sheet on the bottle in the shop. The roof scrap is not a laboratory. It is how I stop arguing with a label while a porch waits.

## Finish is half the water story

A perfect epoxy joint under a failed film finish is still a sponge at the end grain. Water-resistant glue does not excuse a porch table that never saw oil or varnish again. I say that out loud so the glue does not get blamed for a finish that died.

The tree bench taught that half too. The stretchers failed at the film and at the end grain long before the middle of the cheek gave up. I had oiled it once and called it done. Outdoor oil is a habit or it is a lie. I would rather refuse the job than glue a porch top for a person who wants a dining-table maintenance story. I have refused. The shop got quieter. The bench I already owed the weather still got epoxy and bolts.

I do not put hide outside and I do not put a kitchen Type II on a garden seat and then talk about craft when August thunderstorms come in off the river. Craft is the religion that matches the rain.

## The judgment

Water-resistant is a kitchen and a wet rag. Outdoor is rain, sun, and a joint that can drain. If the piece lives under a real roof and a door, pick the PVA grade for the mop. If the piece lives in weather, pick a different religion and a fastener. The porch table that wanted to be a dining table is why this page exists.
