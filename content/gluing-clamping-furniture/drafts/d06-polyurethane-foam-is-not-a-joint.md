The pin hole coughed brown bread. I had let someone talk me into “the glue that works on wet wood” for a knockdown test on an oak scrap. The joint held. The face looked like a science-fair volcano. The rag made it worse. That was the last time polyurethane ran a furniture mortise in this shop.

<!-- PHOTO: fig-01 D:\BBF\BBF Photos — PU foam squeeze-out on a test mortise, stained wood around it (filename pending shop pull) -->

Moisture-cure PU is a one-part glue that eats water and makes gas. The foam is the gas looking for a door. In a construction joint — a wet 2x, a gap you will never finish — the foam is a feature. In a dining rail the foam is a stain, a void filled with air, and a cleanup that laughs at your chisel.

## Wet wood is not a virtue here

The marketing is: it sticks when the board is damp. Furniture shops spend their lives getting boards *not* damp. We sticker. We meter. We wait. A glue that wants moisture is a glue that wants the opposite of a finish-ready panel. I will dampen a PU joint on a jobsite. I will not sprinkle a mortise “to help it foam” on a cherry apron.

The foam also means the glue line can be mostly bubbles. Bubbles are not cheeks. A well-fit film of PVA or hide has wood on both sides. A foamed PU line has wood, plastic, and air. Air shears.

## Stain is the rest of the review

PU darkens. It leaves a halo. On oak the halo looks like a water ring you cannot sand through without going to the next county of grain. On cherry it looks like a bruise. I have planed a test rail to get under it and still found the shadow. If the piece is paint-grade and ugly on purpose, maybe. If the piece is a table someone will oil, no.

Cleanup while it is wet is solvent and gloves and a decision not to smear. Cleanup when it has foamed is a carving project. I do not put PU on a show face and then hope the finish room is kind.

## Clamps still matter, and also do not save you

People think foam pressure closes a joint. Foam pressure also lifts a joint. I have watched a scrap pair creep apart while the bead rose. You still need clamps. The clamps then have to sit through a cure that is not PVA’s hour. The sheet will tell you. `[VERIFY]` the sheet. I still will not run it on a chair.

## Where it can sit without lying

A jig you will never finish. A shop fixture. An outdoor repair that will be painted and mechanically fastened anyway. A construction cousin in the same building. Not a mortise. Not a dovetail. Not a breadboard. Not a panel glue-up you intend to scrape and oil.

I do not keep the bottle next to the yellow caps. Someone will grab it because it says “wood.” The word “wood” on a PU bottle is a category, not a permission.

## Fumes are not folklore

Isocyanates are why the sheet talks about air and gloves. This essay is not a safety program. If we run PU at all, the window or the fan is already a decision, and the leftover bead is not something I wipe with a bare wrist. See *The window stays open* (essay 47) for the shop’s fume page. This page is only here so the foam does not get a second chance at a furniture cheek.

## The scrap I still keep

I kept the oak test mortise. The face is a brown halo and a torn crater where I carved the bread. The joint still holds if I pull. Holding is not the argument. A dining rail has to take oil, take a hand, take a look from a person who did not watch the foam. This scrap is what I show when someone swears by the brown bottle. They can swear on a sawhorse. They can hold the scrap. I do not run the bottle on a cheek I will finish.

## The judgment

Polyurethane foam is not a joint. It is a construction trick that sometimes holds wood. Furniture is a film, a fit, and a face you can finish. If the board is wet, dry the board. If the joint is gappy, recut or use epoxy on purpose. If someone swears by the brown bottle, let them swear on a sawhorse. The pin hole that coughed bread is still in my head when I reach past that shelf.
