The thermometer on the long wall said fifty-two. The oak said less. I know because the hide gelled on the cheek while I reached for a pad. The brush had been honest in the pot. The rail had been sitting on a steel table that had been sitting through the night. Steel is a thief.

Sheets print a lowest application temperature. `[VERIFY]` the one on the bottle you opened. A lot of PVA wants something in the mid-fifties or better. Hide wants parts that will not steal the heat out of a correct mix. Epoxy wants its own range and will punish you with a soft cure if you ignore it. The number on the wall is not the number in the wood.

I have stood under that same thermometer with a space heater roaring and a mug in my hand and still ruined a cheek. The air at face height was a shop I could work in. The oak on the steel was still January. I closed the joint because the brush had looked wet. The film was already rubber. Clamping rubber is clamping a gasket. The next day I could pick the rail off with a thumbnail at the shoulder. I warmed nothing and I called it a glue-up. It was a weather report I refused to read.

## Warm the parts, not only the air

A space heater aimed at the ceiling is a comfort. A rail on a cold table is still a cold rail. I bring parts into the warm end of the shop the night before a glue-up when I can. I keep them off concrete. I keep them off steel. I have used a sun lamp on a mortise like I was hatching something. It looks like folklore. It is just heat.

The night-before pile is a practice, not a mood. Stretchers on stickers, off the floor, away from the door that dumps Bradley County air every time I walk a board in from the truck. I used to leave the pile on the steel because the steel was clear. Clear is not warm. I have also left a case side on the concrete because I needed the bench. The concrete drank the night. In the morning the side felt like a tombstone. I glued it anyway once. The PVA looked closed. A humid week later the seam opened like it had been waiting.

The bottle itself can be cold. A frozen PVA that “thawed” is a bottle I throw away. The polymer does not forgive ice the way a person forgives a jacket. If it livered, if it coughs rope, it is trash. See `d09`.

I have set a yellow bottle on the heater “for a minute” so it would pour. The minute became a lunch. Heat ages PVA. A bottle that pours like cream after a bake is not a kinder bottle. It is an older bottle. I warm parts. I do not cook glue.

## Hide in January is a smaller play

I do not run a four-stretcher hide session in a fifty-degree shop. I run ends. I warm the cheeks. I have a pad and a clamp in my hand before the brush leaves the pot. Or I admit it is a PVA day and I do not pretend I am keeping a tradition warm.

The pot essay already taught the smell and the sheet. I am not writing that again. What January adds is a smaller map. One rail. Cheeks that have sat near the lamp. A person or a clamp already in the air. If I cannot have those, I do not open the pot for a dining base. I have tried the hero version. The first stretcher went home. The third gelled while I wiped the second. I pulled the third back out with a skin that looked like a rubber band. I scraped, I waited a day, I split the play. The table still exists. The hero hour does not.

Liquid hide in the cold is a slower story and still not a miracle. The urea keeps it pourable. The oak can still refuse to wet.

I rubbed a rail with my hands once like I was stealing heat from myself. It looks silly. It works better than a heater aimed at the ceiling while the rail sits on steel.

## What a closed-looking film hid

PVA in a cold shop does not always refuse you to your face. Sometimes it goes on, it squeezes, it looks like a seam. You congratulate the thermometer. A week of weather — a warm spell off the Saline, a wet week, a truck ride — and the seam is a hairline you will call figure if you are a coward. I jointed a panel like that and found the cutter smearing a gel I had named a board. That smear belongs to the four-clocks page. The cold is why the clocks were lying.

Epoxy in the same shop is a different punishment. The kit will kick eventually. The film can stay soft longer than your patience. I have unclamped an outdoor repair that still took a thumbnail. I clamped it back and waited in the warm end. I do not invent the kit’s number. `[VERIFY]` the range on the bottles you opened.

## Summer is the other temperature

This page is winter. August has its own page (`d29`). The shared rule is: the wood’s temperature is the glue’s temperature. A rail that sat in a truck cab at a hundred degrees will flash PVA while you are still spreading the second cheek. A rail that sat in a truck bed in January will gel hide. I do not glue off the tailgate. I let the parts become shop parts.

The tailgate glue-up is a story people tell like it is toughness. I have done it. The oak came off a truck on a Wilmar morning with frost in the grain. I spread hide because I wanted to be the kind of shop that glues when the wood arrives. The brush sheeted. The cheek rubbered. I drove home with a joint that had never been a joint. The parts become shop parts or they do not get a bottle.

## The judgment

If the shop is fifty, you do not have a glue problem yet. You have a heat problem. Warm the parts or wait. Recut nothing in a hurry because the bottle felt ready. The bottle is not the rail. The rail is the clock.
