The poplar stretcher had a rectangle in it, crisp as a stamp. I had padded one jaw and forgotten the other. Softwood is a guest book. Hardwood is ruder about moisture. The oak top I left on the tablesaw overnight cupped to the iron like a shy plant to a window.

Pads bigger than the jaw. Both jaws. Softwood especially. See *Pads, wax paper, and the black ring* (essay 15). I sand a print if I must and I still see it in raking light after stain. Better not to write it.

## Hardwood cup is air

A glued panel is wet on the seams and dry on the faces if you only give it one face of air. Sticker. Both sides. Off steel. Off a hollow bench. The craft pack said this. It stays said because I still want to leave a top on the saw.

## What the print actually is

A jaw print in poplar is crushed cells. Sanding the rectangle off is not the same as un-crushing it. Stain still finds the denser rim of the stamp. Paint will hide it if the piece is paint. Oil will introduce it. I treat a print like a glue smear: better not written.

Hardwood is not immune. A parallel corner on a cherry arris will shine after scrape. The shine is a burnish you will chase with 180 and still see at dinner.

## Stickers are a pattern too

Stickers too far apart and a thin top will take the sticker as a rib. Stickers that are not in line from face to face will twist a panel you just got flat. I use dry stickers, same thickness, stacked over each other, enough of them that the field cannot sag. I do not use a scrap of wet construction lumber as a sticker and then wonder why the oak stained.

## The jaw I loved

I had padded the show face because the show face is where I look. The other jaw was bare steel on poplar. I found the print the next morning when I turned the stretcher toward the long glass. I sanded the rectangle until my fingers said it was gone. Stain still found the denser rim. Oil introduced it like a photograph.

A pad that walks off the jaw after the first crank is a stamp on a new county of the rail. I have tightened, watched the pad slip, and kept cranking because the bead was closing. The print landed where I had not planned a pad at all. I check the pads after the first pull now, both sides, before I walk.

Pine in this county is cheap until you print it. Poplar is the furniture softwood we actually use, and it keeps a jaw the way pine does. I treat both like they will testify. Walnut still takes a parallel corner on an arris if I get lazy. I am not too proud to pad it.

## The tablesaw overnight

The oak top had come out of clamps honest. I needed the bench. The saw was empty and flat-looking. I left the top on the iron overnight “just until morning.” Morning was a shy plant. The underside had slept on cold steel. The top face had slept in shop air. It cupped toward the iron.

I have also left a panel on a hollow bench and blamed the jointer. The bench was the jointer. The saw is a convenient liar. Mystery cup is a week of weather. A banana is a clamp smile. See *The banana panel* (essay 36). This page is the overnight kind: steel you trusted, stickers you were too tired to pull.

If I must set a top down for ten minutes, I set it on three dry sticks, not on the saw, not on the pipes I just hung. Ten minutes in this shop in August is long enough for a thin panel to learn the steel.

## The 2×4 that lived in the weather

I used a scrap of wet construction lumber once because it was the stack nearest the door. The oak took a shadow where the wet sticker sat. Not a cup — a stain, a moisture brand. I jointed it off and I still knew where it had been.

I have also stacked two green tops to save space. The lower one stayed a gel in the field and skinned at the edges, a doughnut of cure, and it took the upper top’s stickers as ribs. One panel at a time. Dry sticks. Same thickness. Not the 2×4 that lived outside.

## The judgment

Pad the soft. Sticker the hard. The guest book and the shy plant are two ways a clamp job finishes after the iron comes off.
