I picked the base up. Four legs, two ends, the stretchers still dry. It racked a little and then it didn’t. The shoulders that had looked closed on the bench showed a hair when the thing became a thing I could carry. That hair is why the bottle was still closed.

The quiet-after-clamps essay already taught the tray, the pads, the person if you need a person. I am not writing the hunt. I am writing the *piece*. A dry-fit that only checks whether parts go together is a kit check. A dry-fit that is the glue-up is you living the next twelve minutes without glue in the way.

I had called that base done on the bench. Shoulders dark, pins home, a square I trusted because it sat still. Then I lifted. Weight found the long stretcher that was a spring. The hair opened at the far shoulder like a mouth. On the bench the spring had been a rumor I could press shut with a thumb. In the air it was a joint I would have asked a clamp to invent. I set the base down. I marked the spring. I recut. The second lift did not talk back.

## Pick it up if you can

A chair you cannot pick up dry is a chair you will not understand wet. Weight tells you which joint is taking the rack. A table base you can lift will tell you if a long stretcher is a spring. I mark the spring. I recut or I accept a two-session glue-up. I do not accept a spring I will “pull out in the clamps.”

I have pulled a spring out in the clamps. The bar made a good liar of the shoulder. Overnight the liar came back, and it brought a starved cheek where I had cranked. I sawed the stretcher out. I made another. The lift would have cost me twenty minutes. The recut cost me a day. I pick it up.

On a case, I dry-fit the box and I sight the corners. I put a lid on if the lid is the thing that will rack the rim. I do not glue a case whose lid I have not seen.

The lid I skipped was a flat panel I had already glued and stickered. I glued the box anyway because the lid was “just a lid.” The lid racked the rim a hair when I finally set it on. A hair in a rim is a door that never sits. I had to plane a lip I would have found in the dry-fit if I had treated the lid as part of the animal. I treat it now. The bottle waits for the lid.

## Mark the iron

Pencil on the bench where each clamp will sit. Pencil on the back of a rail where a pad must land so I do not hunt a show face. Triangle marks on a panel. A story on the inside of a chair — left front, right front — so I do not rotate a leg that only fits one way. The marks look like a shop that has been burned. It has.

I rotated a chair leg once because the dry-fit had no story. The front looked like the back until the seat went on. Glue was already in the mortises. I split a rail to get the leg back. I write L and R and I write which way the show grain faces.

I stage the square, the tape for diagonals, the rag, the brush, the clock. Then I take it apart. The take-apart is part of the rehearsal. If a tenon fights you coming out, it will fight you going in with glue, and you will panic. Pare now.

The fighting tenon is the one I remember with my wrist. Dry, it needed a wiggle and a curse. Wet, it would have been a pump — glue forced back out, a cheek that never wetted, a person hurrying. I pared the cheek. The second dry-fit came out with a tap I would use on a finished piece. I still take it apart after I mark the iron.

Diagonals: I write the two numbers on the bench in pencil. If they disagree, I find the long corner and I ask whether a clamp will square it or rack it. I refuse to discover the disagreement after the film has grabbed.

## Refuse

I have dry-fit a drawer stack and refused the glue because one runner was proud and would have locked the case out of square forever. I have dry-fit a frame-and-panel door and refused because the panel was tight in August. The refusal is the cheap disaster. The expensive disaster is the same information after the film has grabbed.

The runner sat proud at the back. On the bench I could press the case closed. With the stack in, the press became a twist. I would have glued a parallelogram and then blamed the drawers. I pulled the runner, I recut the housing, I dry-fit again. The bottle stayed closed through both tries.

The August door was pecan. The panel ticked in the groove when I dry-fit it at noon. By four, after the long glass had cooked the shop, it would not tick. It jammed. I planed the edges. I dry-fit at the hour I intended to glue. Weather is part of the piece. A rehearsal in the morning for an afternoon glue-up is a rehearsal of a different door.

If I need a second pair of hands, I ask before the bottle opens. Pride is a racked base. That sentence can live in two essays. It earns it.

I have started a dining base alone because I did not want to wait. The dry-fit had already told me I wanted a person at the far end. I opened the bottle anyway. The far shoulder went in late and hungry. Two sessions would have been the refusal that looked like a plan. I write “ask” on the scrap now, next to the clamp marks, while the bottle is still closed.

## The judgment

The dry-fit is not a mood. It is the glue-up without the clock. Pick it up. Rack it. Mark the iron. Refuse if the hair is still sky. Then, and only then, the bottle. Everything after is a shorter play you already rehearsed.
