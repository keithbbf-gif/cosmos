I peeled a sheet of hide off an oak cheek with my thumb. It came like fruit leather. The pot had sheeted off the brush like the pot essay promised. The rail had spent the night on the tablesaw top. The saw top is a heat sink. I had glued a fruit leather sandwich.

This is not the pot essay. This is the spread failure that looks like you did everything right. The mix was fresh. The bloom was the bag we opened. `[VERIFY]` the card. The part was the liar.

## Warm the wood

Hands, a lamp, a warm room overnight, a rag. I do not boil the pot to chase a cold rail. Boiling cooks strength and still does not heat the oak. Thinner glue on a cold cheek gels faster, not slower. You made tea and then you made leather.

## Size is not a fix for cold

A sizing coat on a frozen cheek is a thinner leather. Warm first, then size if you are on end grain, then the real coat, then close. The order matters.

## Liquid hide is not a heater

The bottle pours. The oak can still refuse. I warm liquid-hide jobs too. Convenience is not climate.

## The saw top keeps January

This shop has a warm end and a lean-to end. The tablesaw lives where the slab is convenient, not where the heater wins. Oak rails stacked on that iron overnight become the temperature of the iron. I picked one up in the morning and it felt like a wrench. I still brushed. The brush felt right in the pot. On the cheek the hide went from wet to rubber in the time it took me to reach for a pad I should have already had in my hand.

The peel was quiet. No drama. A sheet, the color of the mix, the shape of the cheek. Under it the oak was dry. Not stained even. Dry. I had never wetted the wood. I had dressed it.

I have done the same thing with a stile that sat on the concrete. Concrete is a slower sink than a saw top and just as sure. I keep hide parts off steel and off the floor the night before a pot day. They sleep on stickers in the warm end, or on the bench that is already in the heated room. It looks like fuss. It is cheaper than fruit leather.

## What gel feels like on the brush

You can feel a cold cheek steal the heat. The brush starts to drag. The film stops laying out and starts to rope. That rope is the beginning of the sheet. If I feel it, I stop. I do not "hurry and close" as if the clamp were a microwave. Closing leather is clamping a gasket. The gasket will look like a joint until August finds the dry counties.

Warming is not theater. I have rubbed a rail with my hands until the oak felt like a tool I had been using. I have put a lamp on a mortise cheek. I have walked a pair of stretchers to the heater and waited, which is the part of craft nobody photographs. When the wood is no longer a wrench, I brush again. A thin wash to see if it wets. If it wets, the real coat. If it still ropes, I am not ready.

I do not chase the rail by turning the pot up. The pot essay already told you what boil does to strength. I am telling you it also does nothing for the oak. You get a hotter tea on a colder board. The tea skins faster. You have added a second liar.

## The order I actually use

If the work is end grain — a miter I should have reinforced, a breadboard tongue I am only sizing — I still warm first. A size on a frozen end is a drink that never becomes a film. See *End grain drinks and lies* (essay 26). Warm, then the disappearing coat, then the coat I will close. If I skip the warm, I get two leathers and a mouth that opens when someone leans.

Long grain is the same order without the size unless the oak is thirsty in a way I can see. Thirsty and cold is a trap. The wood looks like it wants glue. It wants heat. I give it heat.

I stage the clamps and the pads before the brush leaves the pot. Open time on a correctly warm cheek is still a clock. See *Rehearse with a clock on the bench* (essay 16). A pad hunt is how a good film becomes a skin while I swear at a coffee can.

## Liquid hide on the same cold oak

I trusted the bottle because it poured in a shop that was not warm enough for the pot. The stile was still a wrench. The liquid hide laid out like it was doing me a favor. I closed. I even got a bead. The next week I could pick a skin at the shoulder with a fingernail. The urea kept the bottle honest. It did not keep the oak honest. Convenience is a pour. It is not a climate.

I still walk the parts to the warm end. See *Hide after the pot essay* (essay 3) for the bottle versus the pot. This page is only the cheek.

## A peel is a diagnosis

If you can lift a sheet, you never had wetting. Recut nothing in a panic. Warm the pair. Brush again. If the second coat wets and you are still inside a clock you trust, close. If the cheeks have been handled into a mess of half-skins, scrape to wood and start the day over. I have scraped. I have also pretended a half-skin would "mash in." It mashed. It did not wet.

Walnut and cherry will do this too. I wrote oak because oak is what was on the saw that night. The species is not the lesson. The heat sink is. I do not wait for a pretty frost. I touch the rail.

## The judgment

If you can peel a sheet, you never had a film on wood. Warm the part. Leave the pot alone. The fruit leather is why oak rails sleep in the warm end of the shop the night before a hide day.
