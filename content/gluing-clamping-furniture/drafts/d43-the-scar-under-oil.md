I thought I had sanded past it. Oil said I had not. A pale bolt across a cherry rail, bright as a mistake. See `d28`. This is the cure-room version: what you do when the scar is already there, and what you do so the next rail never gets one.

<!-- PHOTO: fig-01 D:\BBF\BBF Photos — pale glue scar on oiled cherry or maple (filename pending shop pull) -->

If the smear is shallow, a scraper and a local sand, then a test wipe of mineral spirits to see if the pale stays. If the pale stays, you are in the pore. You can try a stronger solvent on PVA if the finish has not started — carefully, on a scrap first. You can try to stain around it and make it worse. Sometimes the honest move is a deeper scrape or a new rail.

## Prevention is cheaper

Tape the show face. Lift the bead. Do not wipe dirty. Test the wood-and-glue-and-finish stack on a scrap when the species is mean. Cherry, maple, quartersawn oak.

## Film vs oil

A film can bury a small sin. Oil will not. If the piece is oil, treat glue like a finish contaminant from the first minute.

## Mineral spirits do not flatter

Spirits are not finish. They are a preview that does not flatter. If the pale disappears in the wet look and stays gone when it flashes off, I may have gotten past it. If the pale stays, I am in the pore. Plastic in the pore is a filled county that oil will keep introducing every time the table gets wiped.

I have stained around a bolt and made a halo. The halo was worse than the bolt. I have tried a stronger solvent on PVA before any finish was on, on a scrap first. Sometimes the scrap gives the smear back. Sometimes the scrap only gives you a fuzzed face and the pale still sitting. `[VERIFY]` any solvent against that bottle and that wood that day. I do not invent a magic wipe.

Water-based pigment will not bury a smear either. I have dyed maple over a pale patch and watched the patch stay a skin while the rest of the rail went color. If the piece is oil or dye, I treat glue like a finish contaminant from the first minute.

## Tape that stayed the night

Tape the show face of a dovetail. Tape the arris of a table seam if I am a sloppy roller. Peel the tape before the glue cures to it like a second skin. I have left tape overnight. The glue crawled under the edge and the peel took a little of the arris with it. Blue tape on a pin line looks like a shop that has been burned. It has.

I keep a scrap of the same cherry when I glue a rail I will oil. Same bottle. Same sanding. Same oil. The scrap is allowed to take the bolt so the rail does not have to. If the scrap shows pale, I change the cleanup before I change the story.

## The rail I should have recut

I have scraped a bolt down into a hollow and then tried to make the hollow look like wear. It looked like a hollow. I recut the rail. I do not invent that rail as a sold dining piece. I invent the refusal to keep a scar I can still recut.

If I already know the finish is oil, I do not wait for the oil to be the inspector. I inspect with spirits. I inspect with raking light from the long glass — the same light that will make a pale bolt into the only thing in the room.

## The rag that introduced it

I have oiled a cherry rail from one end and watched the bolt appear under the rag, a pale county arriving as the rest went amber. The wood had felt honest at 180. The rag did not care what my fingers thought. Maple does the same trick and is ruder about it — a shiny patch that will not take dye even after you think you have scraped. Quartersawn oak makes a map: the rays drink, the smear does not, and you get a lightning bolt that pretends to be figure until you stand where the long glass hits it.

I do not sand harder at that moment. Harder is a hollow. I stop. I decide scrape-deeper or recut. I have kept going with the rag to “see the whole thing.” The whole thing was a bolt I already knew was there, plus oil in a pore I then had to wait on before I could scrape again.

A dirty wipe at glue-up is how most of these bolts get scheduled. I have wiped a bead with a rag that had already painted three other seams. The fourth seam was the show face. See `d27`. The oil did not invent the scar. The rag did. Oil only introduced it.

## The judgment

Believe the oil. It is not dramatic. It is accurate. The bolt is why I keep a scrap of the same cherry when I glue a rail I will oil.
