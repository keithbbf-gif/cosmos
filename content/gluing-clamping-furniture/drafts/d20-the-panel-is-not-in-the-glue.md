I tipped the door and the panel ticked. A little travel, a little space. August in South Arkansas will take that space. A panel I had glued in a groove on a lesser afternoon did not tick. It split. The split was a straight line down the field, polite and ugly, the shape of a decision.

Frame-and-panel exists because boards get wider and narrower and frames do not want to become propellers. The panel lives in a groove or a rebate. The frame is the glue-up — mortise and tenon, bridle, whatever you cut. The panel gets space and a finish on the edges before you close the frame, because you will not see those edges again.

## Glue in the groove is a scheduled split

A smear that pins one edge of the panel is enough. The panel will pull against the pin and split or it will rack the frame. I dry-fit and I look for squeeze that would become a pin. I use space balls or rubber bumps if I want the field centered. I do not use glue.

Plywood and MDF panels move less. They still do not need to be glued in a solid frame unless you have a reason and you admit the reason. A solid raised panel always needs the float.

## How much space

Enough for the wettest month this shop has already lived. I will not tattoo an eighth of an inch on a walnut door of a certain width. `[VERIFY]` against the species, the width, and the meter. A winter-cut panel in a tight groove is a summer split. A summer-cut panel with a huge gap is a winter rattle. I would rather rattle a little than split.

## The frame still has to be a frame

I glue the frame like I glue any frame: dry-fit, square, pads, no hero daylight in a tenon. The panel is in during the glue-up so the grooves stay aligned, and it is not in the glue. I wipe the groove if I smeared. I have smeared. The tick test after is how I know I cleaned it.

## The door that did not tick

The lesser afternoon was a raised oak panel I wanted still. I ran a bead in the groove “so it wouldn’t rattle.” It did not rattle. In August it split from the top rail down the field, a line so straight I could have snapped a chalk line and been less honest. The frame was fine. The frame had done what frames do. The panel had been asked to be a board inside a rectangle that does not get wider when the air does.

I keep that split in the shop longer than a person should keep a failure. It is not a customer piece. It is a door I made to learn the groove. I tip the good doors now. If the field ticks, I have space. If it does not tick, I ask why before I finish the edges and close the play. A silent panel in July in this county is a panel I will hear from in a different way by September.

## Where the smear hides

Glue does not have to flood a groove to pin a field. A wipe from a fat tenon, a brush that touched the stile’s slot on the way to a cheek, a finger I thought I had cleaned — any of those will leave a tack. The tack is enough. The panel swells, the tack holds one long edge, the other long edge still wants to move, and you get a split or a racked frame depending on which religion is stronger that week.

I dry-fit with the panel in, then I take the panel out and I look down the grooves in the light. Shiny is a smear. I scrape the shiny. I dry-fit again. I have closed a door I had already “cleaned” and found, after the clamps, a corner of the field that would not slide. I opened it while I still could. The corner had a skin of PVA the size of a fingernail. The fingernail was the pin.

Space balls and rubber bumps are how I keep a field from sitting ugly in the opening. They are not glue. They do not pin. I use them when the door is wide enough that a floating panel looks drunk to one side. I do not use them as an excuse to skip the tick.

## The edges you will not see again

I finish the panel edges before I close the frame. The edges disappear. Raw edges in a closed groove drink and swell from the inside, which is a movement you cannot sand. A sealer on those edges is not fancy. It is how I stop the field from becoming a sponge in a slot I will never see again. How much space is still not a house eighth I can shout from Warren to Wilmar. Walnut moves unlike oak. A wide field moves unlike a narrow one. August against the glass wall is the wettest month this room has already lived.

## Plywood is not a license

I have glued a plywood panel in a solid frame because “it doesn’t move.” It moves less. It still does not need to be glued unless I have a reason I can say out loud — a painted door I want silent, a shop jig I am not selling as furniture. A veneered panel in a solid frame needs the float too if the core still takes a season. If the frame is only a rectangle because the field is jammed in, I have made a board again. I recut. I do not call the jam a tight craftsman fit.

## The judgment

If it is a field, it floats. If it is a frame, it glues. The door that ticked was a door. The door that split was a panel I had made into a board. I do not make panels into boards anymore.
