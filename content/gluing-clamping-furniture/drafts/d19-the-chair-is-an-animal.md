I set the chair on the floor dry. It did not rock. I racked the back and the front rail argued. One tenon was a little proud in the shoulder and the opposite one was a little shy. On the bench they had both looked like tenons. On its feet the chair had opinions.

A chair is not a box. A box can be square and done. A chair has to stand, take a hip, take a lean, take years of two-leg tilts. The rehearsal is on its feet. I do not glue a chair I have only known on its back.

## The clamp that teaches a limp

A bar across a side can pull a leg in and give you a chair that stands on three. I pad, I watch the feet, I use a band or a web when the animal is the shape. Handscrews at the rail, not a pipe that wants to be a diagonal. See the press chapter for the rack. Here I only refuse to glue a limp I can already see dry.

## Sessions are respect

Front assembly. Back assembly. Then the side rails. Hide in August will not give you a whole chair if you are alone. PVA will give you the minutes and still not give you four perfect corners if you rush. I have glued a whole chair in one hero session. I have also recut a rail. Two sessions is not cowardice. See *Two sessions is not cowardice* (essay 40).

Pins and drawbores are allowed. They are how a chair stays a chair when the film is thin. They are not how you skip the feet test.

## Hide is still the repair argument

If this chair should come apart in twenty years, the pot is on. If this chair is a painted kitchen cousin, PVA is honest. I do not mix them in one animal. Mixed religions are a future steam that only half-works.

## The floor, then the rack

I set the dry chair on the concrete and I look under the feet the way I look under a case for a coin of light. No rock. Then I put a hand on the crest, or on the back rail if there is no crest, and I lean like a person who will actually sit. The front rail argued. That argument is the review. A chair that stands is not yet a chair that can take a hip.

I have skipped the lean because the feet were pretty. The shoulders had both looked closed on the bench, one a little proud, one a little shy, each hiding the other when the animal was on its back. On its feet they became a parallelogram. I pared the proud shoulder. I left the shy one alone until I had a new rail. Glue would have made the shy one a forever limp.

The bench lies about chairs. A bench holds the back like a box. A floor asks the legs to agree. I do the feet test in the same room I will glue, not in the doorway where the floor is a different county. This shop’s concrete is not flat enough to trust a single glance. I try two places. If it rocks in both, the chair is the liar. If it rocks in one, I am standing in a dip.

## The afternoon I glued the whole animal

I buttered every tenon because I wanted to be the kind of person who can keep four corners alive. Hide, August, glass wall throwing heat. The front went home. The back went home. The first side rail went home with a tap I liked. The second side rail went home with a tap I did not, and I cranked a bar to close a shoulder that had already started to skin. That shoulder is the one I recut later. The clock was not the villain. I was the villain for asking one clock to be four assemblies.

Front, then back, then sides when both ends are things I can hold without a panic. I write the order on tape. I do not invent the order while the brush is wet. See *Two sessions is not cowardice* (essay 40) when the clock is the whole chapter. Here the clock is only why the animal gets to keep its opinions.

## What a bar does to a post

I have closed a side rail with one pipe because the pipe was already on the bench. The pipe pulled the back post in a hair. The hair was enough. Four feet became three and a tap dancer. I did not see it until I set the chair down to wipe the front. The film had grabbed the limp. Hide would have given me a steam. That one was PVA. I took it apart anyway and lost more cheek than I wanted.

If I have to choose between a closed shoulder and four feet, I choose the feet and I recut tomorrow. The press chapter can name the irons. This page refuses to invent a limp with the first clamp I touch.

## Pins after the feet, not instead

Drawbores and pins are how a chair stays a chair when the film is thin and a two-leg tilt arrives years later. I mark them in the dry-fit. I do not drive them to pull a shy shoulder home. A pin into a gap is a pin that split a rail I then got to make again. The feet test is still the test. A pinned limp is a limp you have notarized.

I dry-fit the pins. If a hole wants to wander, I wander it on purpose as a slot, or I recut. I do not discover a tight pin in the middle of a hide sprint. That minute is when I used to start hoping. Hope is not a drawbore.

## The judgment

Dry-fit on its feet. Rack it. Watch the shoulders. Split the session if the clock is mean. Do not let a bar teach a limp. The animal will tell you dry. Believe it.
