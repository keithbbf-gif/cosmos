I timed the dry-fit because I am tired of lying to myself. Four minutes to get the ends on. Three more to get the stretchers. The hide sheet I was about to use did not owe me seven minutes in August. I split the glue-up. Ends first. Stretchers after. The table still exists.

People rehearse the positions and forget the minutes. Positions without minutes are a play you have not run at speed. I start a timer when the first imaginary brush would hit the first cheek. I stop when the last clamp is on and the square has been read. If that number is longer than the sheet, I do not “go faster with glue.” Glue is slower than dry, not faster. Squeeze, wipe, panic — they eat time.

I used to time the dry-fit in my head. My head is a flatterer. It skips the wipe. It skips the pad that walks. It skips the diagonal I will actually measure. The first time I used a timer that sat on the bench I was insulted. Seven minutes. I had been calling it four. I had been about to open hide in a shop the long glass had already turned into a kiln. I wrote “ends only” on a scrap. The stretchers waited. The next day they went in on a clock the sheet could live with.

## Heat is a thief you can measure

August open time is not January open time. I do not use January’s memory. I use that day’s bottle and that day’s shop. `[VERIFY]` the sheet, then believe the timer in the room.

January’s memory is how I lost a pecan rail. I had timed a hide rehearsal in the cool of a morning and then I glued at two o’clock because a delivery delayed me. The shop had gone up. The first cheek went dull while I buttered the third. I closed a dotted line. I recut. I now time the rehearsal at the hour I intend to glue, or I time it again. A morning number is a morning play.

If I am alone, the timer is meaner. A second pair of hands is a shorter clock. I ask. I do not be proud on a dining base with hide and four stretchers.

I have been proud. The dry-fit with a person was five minutes. The dry-fit alone was nine. I told myself I would be crisp. Glue made me slower. I got three good corners and a fourth I rushed into a hungry shoulder. I recut that rail. I ask now, or I split, or I change the glue to a longer-open cousin and I accept what that cousin is.

## What the number tells you to change

Too long: fewer joints per session, or a longer-open glue, or pins that draw so you are not also the clamp. Too short: you got lucky in the rehearsal and you will not get lucky wet. Stage more. Mark more. Put the rag where your hand will be.

The lucky short rehearsal is a trap I still respect. I once dry-fit a case in three minutes because every part fell home and the clamps were already on the bench like a magazine photograph. I opened the bottle proud. Wet, a tenon hung, a pad walked, I hunted a square I had left on the other bench. Five minutes became a skin on the first corner. I now treat a suspiciously short number as a warning. I stage the square on the pile. I put the rag on the right side of the bench. I run the rehearsal a second time and I add a minute for the mess I know is coming.

I write the plan on a scrap: “ends, pull tomorrow, stretchers.” The scrap is uglier than a hero session. The hero session is how I get three good corners and one that was rushed. That sentence can live here and in the press chapter. It earns both.

The scrap sits under the timer. Bottle name, sheet open time if I read it, rehearsal minutes, the split if there is a split. `[VERIFY]` the sheet on the bottle I will actually open — not the bottle I remember from last winter, not the cousin on the next shelf. I have opened the wrong cousin because the scrap said “yellow” and two yellows live here. I write the grade. I write the hour I will start.

## The phone in the other room

I owned a timer that was my phone. The phone lives in another room when I am trying to have an afternoon. I have started a dry-fit, walked to the ring, and come back to a pile I had to start over because I could not swear where I was in the minutes. The timer on the bench does not take a call. It sits where the bottle will sit. If I leave the bench, it keeps lying to me in public.

I have also started the timer when I *meant* to start, which is to say after I had already spread a practice bead in my head and moved two clamps. That is a flatterer again. I start when the imaginary brush hits. I stop when the last iron is on and I have read the square.

## Glue is slower than dry

Dry wood slides. Wet wood grabs and then slides late. Hide grabs sooner than my PVA walk. A rehearsal of eight minutes against a sheet of ten is not a margin. It is a tie I will lose when I wipe. If I cannot get a margin a walked pad can eat, I split or I change religions for that session. I do not negotiate with optimism.

The seven-minute hide table was August, alone, a dining base, and a pot I had already decided was the point. The timer made the decision I did not want. Ends. Night. Stretchers. The table is in a room.

## The judgment

Time the dry-fit. Compare it to the sheet. Split the session or change the glue. Do not change physics with optimism. The seven-minute hide table is why I own a timer that is not my phone in another room.
