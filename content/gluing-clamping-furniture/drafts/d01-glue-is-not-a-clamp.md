The daylight in the shoulder was the width of a dime. I had the clamps already on the bench, pads cut, glue bottle standing like it was going to do something useful. The tenon went home with a shove that felt like work. That shove was the joint telling me it was not a joint yet.

I have watched people treat glue like a mash. A little more in the gap, a little more crank on the bar, and the wood will come to Jesus. What you get is a starved line at the places the clamp actually kissed, and a thick, shiny ridge where it did not. The ridge is not strength. The ridge is a gasket. Gaskets creep. Gaskets stain. Gaskets show under oil like a confession.

## What a glue line is allowed to be

A furniture glue line is a film. The two faces already had to meet. The glue wets both faces, you close them, and what stays is thin enough that the wood is still doing the carrying. If you need the clamp to *make* the meeting, you are using the screw as a jointer. The screw will not stay in the house.

I dry-fit until the shoulder seats with hand pressure or a tap I would use on a finished piece. If I need a dead-blow and a running start, I pare. Pride is cheaper than a table that opens in September.

Hide, PVA, urea, epoxy — they all want that film. Epoxy will fill a gap and still stick. That is its job, and it is also how people stop cutting. I use epoxy when the wood is gone or the joint is outdoor and ugly on purpose. I do not use it to hide a lazy cheek on a dining rail.

## The clamp is a hand, not a press

A clamp holds what you already have. It does not invent contact. Hungry clamps bow a panel and pump glue out of the middle. Lazy clamps leave a hairline you will call “figure” later, when you mean regret. Enough pressure is the pressure that closes the film and stops. Another quarter-turn after the glue has grabbed is a banana.

I learned this on a six-board walnut top that looked honest on the jointer and dishonest in the clamps. Two edges had a spring. I cranked until the spring disappeared. The next week the spring came back and brought friends. I sawed the top apart, recut the edges, and glued a joint that closed with two clamps per seam and no heroics. The second top is still a top.

## South Arkansas is not a laboratory

August in a 65-foot shop with glass on the long wall is a different glue than January. The bottle is the same. The clock is not. Heat thins PVA and shortens the minute you thought you had. Cold gels hide on the cheek before the bar is on. Humidity keeps a “dry” rail from being dry. None of that changes the first rule. A gappy joint in January is still a gappy joint. The weather only decides how fast you get to find out.

I do not keep a psi religion on the wall. Sheets talk in pounds. Shops talk in whether the seam disappeared and whether the panel stayed flat. If someone wants a number, they can read the sheet on the bottle we opened that morning and mark it `[VERIFY]` against that lot. I want the shoulder dark with contact, not dark with extra glue.

## What I will not do

I will not butter a 1/8-inch void and call it “good hide work.” Hide is honest in a fit and useless as a shim. I will not run a bead of polyurethane down a mortise and smile when it foams out the pin hole like bread. Foam is a mess, not a cheek. I will not reach for CA to “tack the shoulder” on a chair I expect to sit in. CA is a jig glue. Chairs are not jigs.

Pins, drawbores, wedges — those are wood doing clamp work after the glue has gone quiet. They are allowed. They are not an excuse to skip the fit. A drawbore on a fat tenon is a tenon you split on purpose.

## The test before the bottle opens

Pick the piece up. Rack it. Look at the shoulders in raking light. If you see sky, you are not in the glue chapter yet. You are still in the cutting chapter. The bottle can wait. The clamps can wait. The clock does not start until the joint is already a joint.

That is the whole religion. Everything else in this pack is a way of not forgetting it when the shop is hot and someone wants the table by Friday.
