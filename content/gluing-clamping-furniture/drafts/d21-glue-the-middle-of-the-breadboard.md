The pin at the end of the breadboard had a little oval of daylight. I had cut the hole that way on purpose. In February the oval was almost round. In August the pin sat at the other end of the slot. The top had moved. The breadboard had stayed a straight lip. That is the whole invention.

People glue a breadboard like a cap. All along the tongue, proud of the squeeze. Then the top wants to change width and the cap says no. The no is a split in the field or a bow in the breadboard or both. I have seen a cherry top open like a book at the end of a glued-on lip. The lip was still pretty.

![Plan view of table breadboard tongue with glue only at center mortise and elongated pin slots at wings while field width may change](images/svg/breadboard-center-glue-slots.svg)
*The middle may stick. The wings must walk. Glue the center; slot the pins.*

## What gets glue

The center of the tongue — a few inches, a short mortise, whatever will keep the breadboard from walking off as a unit. The wings get pins in elongated holes, or a sliding dovetail that is only glued in the middle, or a screw in a slot. The craft pack already has “room to move.” This is the glue version of that room.

I dry-fit the tongue. I mark the center. I mark the pin locations. I cut the slots longer than the pin. `[VERIFY]` how much longer against that top’s width and the wettest month we expect. I do not invent a house eighth for every species.

## Finish the tongue

The tongue will disappear. Finish it before you close, at least a sealer, so the end grain of the top does not stay raw in a dark slot. Raw end grain in a closed breadboard is a moisture argument you cannot get back to.

## Clamps without dishing

A caul across the breadboard, pads, enough pressure to close the shoulder, not enough to dish the end of a table someone will feel with a hand. I check the shoulder in raking light. A proud breadboard is a lip. A starved breadboard is a shadow.

## The cherry that opened like a book

The lip was still pretty. The field had a split that started at the end of the tongue and ran back like someone had opened a ledger. I had buttered the whole tongue because squeeze along a breadboard looks like virtue. Virtue was a cap. The cap said no when August said wider. Cherry in this shop will take that dare. I sawed the breadboard off, recut the tongue, and did the job the second time with glue in the middle and slots in the wings. The second lip is still a lip. The field is still a field.

I do not tell that story as a sold top. It was a top I made to have a top. The lesson was cheaper than a dining table and still expensive enough that I remember the sound of the split, which was not loud. It was a dry click in a quiet shop, the kind of click you hope is a stick falling over.

## How I mark the middle

I dry-fit the tongue until the shoulder seats with a tap I would use on a finished edge. I mark the center on both the tongue and the breadboard, a pencil line I can still see after the first wipe. Glue lives on that line and a little to each side — a few inches, a short mortise if I cut the breadboard that way — enough that the cap cannot walk off as a unit. The rest of the tongue stays a slide.

I have crept the glue out toward the wings because I was proud of the wet look. The wet look is how you glue a cap again. I stop when the center is wet and the wings are dry wood I can still see. If I smear a wing I wipe it now, not after the pin has locked the smear in place.

A narrow oak lid is not a wide walnut dining field. I do not invent a house eighth for every species and then act surprised when a wide top shears a pin or splits a wing.

## The slot I cut as a round hole

I have drilled a pretty round hole at the end of a breadboard and called the pin “close enough to walk.” The pin filled the hole. The top moved. The pretty hole became a split in the breadboard instead of an oval of daylight. The oval is ugly in February and honest in August. I would rather see daylight around a pin than a hairline in the lip.

I dry-fit the pins. I watch the slot, not the show. If a pin binds at the end of travel I have already failed the walk. I ease the slot. I do not ease the pin into an oval with hope and a hammer. A hammered pin in a tight wing is a wedge. Wedges split.

## The tongue I left raw

I closed a breadboard once with a raw tongue because I was already in the glue. The end grain of the top sat in a dark slot and drank whatever the season offered. The middle glue held. The wings walked. The slot still swelled around the tongue in a wet week and the shoulder that had been flush became a proud line I could feel with a hand. I cannot get back into that slot without sawing the lip off. A sealer on the tongue and on the end of the top, before close, is the last time I will see those surfaces. I take the time. If I need a hero clamp to close the shoulder, the tongue is fat or the shoulder is shy. I recut. I do not crush a lip to hide a cut.

## The judgment

Glue the middle. Pin the wings in slots. Let August walk. A breadboard that cannot walk is not a breadboard. It is a cleat you attached to the wrong axis. I will not glue a lip on a dining top and then talk about craft.
