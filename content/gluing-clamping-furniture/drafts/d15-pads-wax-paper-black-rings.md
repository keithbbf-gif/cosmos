The cherry rail came out of clamps with a bracelet. Black, crisp, the diameter of the pipe. I had wiped the squeeze and left the iron lying in it. Tannin, water, steel. The finish room does not have a stain that is “pipe.” The finish room has bleach and hope and a rail I should have padded.

Pads are not courtesy. They are how a jaw does not print a grid into pine, how a pipe does not drink glue, how a parallel clamp does not leave a shiny bruise on a leg you already scraped. I cut pads from scrap. I keep a coffee can of them. I do not hunt a pad in open time.

The bracelet sat on the show face of a rail I had already scraped for oil. I tried oxalic because that is what shops reach for when iron writes on cherry. `[VERIFY]` the chemistry if someone wants a lab. What I know is the ring went pale and then came back as a ghost when the oil went on. I sanded into the next county of grain. The ghost stayed a ghost. I recut the rail. The coffee can got filled that week. I tape pipes now. I do not leave a wet bead against bare steel and call the mark figure.

## Iron and wet

Pipe clamps are honest pressure and dishonest chemistry. I tape the pipes or I keep them off the work. I do not let a wet bead sit against bare steel on oak, walnut, cherry, pecan — anything with tannin enough to write. The writing is dark and it goes deeper than you want to sand.

Oak wrote next. A panel glue-up, pipes across the field because I was short on parallels, a bead I had not wiped because I was watching a seam. In the morning the oak had a row of commas, black, where the pipe had rested in the wet. I bleached. I sanded. I still oil that top and I still see one comma if I stand where the long glass rakes. I tape the pipes. I wipe the bead. I do not stack those two chores after the clock has already started.

Wax paper between a pad and a show face is extra kindness when the glue is hide or a smear-prone PVA. Newspaper prints. Shop towels leave texture. I have used all three. I use wax paper when I care.

Newspaper printed a want-ad into a walnut apron. I can still see the ghost of a letter if I am the kind of person who looks. I was that kind of person that morning. Shop towels left a waffle in hide on pecan that I had to scrape before it glassed. Wax paper lets the pad do its job and lets the glue stay off the pad. I tear sheets into the dry-fit pile. I do not walk to the kitchen roll while a cheek is wet.

## Softwood remembers a jaw

Pine and poplar crush. A parallel jaw without a pad will dish a stretcher you will see in raking light after the stain. Hardwood remembers less and still remembers a corner. I pad both. I am not too proud to pad walnut.

I crushed a poplar stretcher on a painted piece I thought could take a bruise. Paint does not hide a dish. It maps it. Raking light off the long wall found a shiny oval the width of the jaw. I had to add a pad after the fact and steam a little, which is a poor repair and a good memory. The coffee can now has pads bigger than the biggest jaw I own. Walnut still gets the same pad.

A corner of a parallel jaw will print a line into an arris even when the face looks fine. I have chased that line with a block plane and taken a reveal I did not want. The pad hangs past the jaw so the corner never meets the wood. A pad that was honest at snug can walk at crank.

## The pad that becomes the dent

A pad that is too small is a stamp. A pad that walks off the jaw is a stamp on a new county of the rail. I make pads bigger than the jaw. I check them after I tighten. The craft pack already said not to stack work on a setting panel. A fallen pad is the same sin in miniature.

I cut a pretty little square once because I was saving a nicer scrap for something else. The square stamped a clean dent in cherry, crisp as a hallmark. I had padded. I had still marked the work. I throw the small ones out of the can when I catch them.

The walking pad is the one I find on the floor after I think I am done tightening. It walked while I went to the next bar. The jaw sat down on the rail and printed a grid I then had to scrape. I check. I run a finger along the pad’s edge. If I can feel iron, I stop and reset. That finger is cheaper than bleach.

## The can and the dry-fit

The coffee can lives on the clamp rack, not in a drawer. A drawer is a hunt. I fill it when I rip scraps, a few minutes that do not belong to any glue-up. Pine for soft work, hardwood offcuts for the rest, a few with packing tape already on one face for hide days. I stage pads on the bench in the dry-fit, one pair per bar, already where the iron will sit. If I am short, I cut more before the bottle opens. I have glued short. Short is how the pipe found the cherry.

I also keep a roll of tape on that same rack for the pipes. Taping a pipe after the first squeeze is on the rail is a joke you tell with black ink. I tape a set of pipes on a slow morning and I hang them taped. The adhesive gets tired. I retape. I do not invent a chemistry beyond iron, tannin, and moisture. `[VERIFY]` anyone who wants a longer word.

## The judgment

Stage the pads in the dry-fit. Tape the pipes. Keep iron out of the bead. The black ring is not a story you want to tell a customer. I told it once. I tape now.
