# Marais Saline

Jann Woodard will not choose, and neither will this essay. The Saline, she writes, derived its name from a salty marsh near its mouth, called by the French the Marais Saline — though some historians claim a salt works near Benton, begun as early as 1827, gave the river its name. At one time those works supplied the bulk of the salt used in the territory and the surrounding states. The northern river was salt. The southern river, she adds, was known for lignite, the largest exposure at Goulett Island in Bradley County, land Governor Elias N. Conway owned in 1860.

Two christenings. One water. A heritage series that needs a single pretty etymology has failed the first test of the river.

Woodard’s own spelling in the encyclopedia lead is “Marias Saline” in one clause and the familiar *Marais* among the aliases. Merry Saline, Saline Bayou, Marie Saline, Marais Saline, Marais de Saline. The river has been called all of those. This draft will not pretend the French had a single orthography on a marsh. The pack’s house spelling is *Saline River* for the Arkansas stream. The marsh keeps the French. The seam in Woodard’s vowels is admitted, not smoothed.

## French families on the lower banks

In the late 1700s, Woodard writes, French families settled the lower Saline. She lists them: Fogle, LaBeouff, DuBose, Charron, Pevetoe, Ramsauer, De Ambleton, Carcuff, Bullet. Several place names along the river are theirs. This is not Wilmar’s founding population. It is the river’s. A mill town that later borrowed *Saline Valley* for a railroad charter was borrowing a French marsh and a set of households that predated Anderson’s dollar by two generations and more.

Anderson bought seven hundred acres in 1859. Govan was on the books in 1848. Hemingway since 1856. Teske’s white land-office sequence is the beginning of *Wilmar*, not the beginning of the water. The Fogle and LaBeouff names are older on the lower banks than the post office that honored Willie Elvira Anderson. A series that starts every river sentence at 1859 has chosen a town myth over a watershed.

David Dale Owen’s 1859–1860 geological reconnaissance is the scientific still. Woodard cites the *Second Report of a Geological Reconnaissance of the Middle and Southern Counties of Arkansas, Made during the Years 1859 and 1860* (Philadelphia: C. Sherman, 1860). Lignite, salt, the look of the country before the Iowa lumbermen. Owen is not a poet. That is why he is useful. This draft has not lifted Owen’s lithographs onto the desk. It will not invent a stratum description. The book is the next desk after Dunnahoo. The citation is Woodard’s.

1860 is also, in Heady, the year Drew County counted 5,581 white residents and 3,497 people held in bondage. Conway’s lignite holding at Goulett Island and Anderson’s dollar and Owen’s field season share a calendar. Salt and lignite and corn and enslaved labor are the extractive facts of that year. Pine as a syndicate crop is later.

## Salt as an industry before pine

Salt is heavy, necessary, and boring to later tourism. It is also the first extractive industry the river’s name admits. A works near Benton in 1827, if that dating holds, is older than Drew County (1846) and older than the state (1836) by a few years if the “as early as” holds through territorial time. Woodard says “as early as 1827” and “the territory as well as surrounding states.” Arkansas Territory existed. The works, in that telling, fed more than a county. The marsh at the mouth is older than the works. Either story makes the Saline a commodity before it was a postcard.

Some historians, Woodard says, prefer the Benton works as the name’s source. She does not name those historians in the paragraph. This draft will not invent them. Dunnahoo (1993) is the four-forks monograph on her shelf; whether he chooses marsh or works is a question for his pages. Unconsulted. Said so.

The lignite at Goulett Island is a separate essay’s job — essay 42 in this pack. Here it only has to unsettle the pine monopoly. The southern Saline was a fuel landscape in the state’s geological mind before it was Gates’s log landscape. Conway, governor, 1860, owner of the largest exposure Woodard names. A later writer who wants a deed should get the deed. The encyclopedia sentence is the card.

Steamboat days, in Woodard, put the head of navigation at Bridges Bluff in Cleveland County. Cotton, staves, timber, downriver to New Orleans and Monroe. Fifty-four boats documented. Captains: Bob Withers, Len Moore, John Wesley Smith, Frank Keeling. The Hampton brothers and John N. Wheeler as primary shippers until the early 1900s, then Slavonian stave families: Lipovac, Karlovic, Kusterin, Dorich, Piskulick. Rail after 1880 slackened the boats. The *Gate City* sank October 14, 1913, near Warren. Those sentences belong to later essays. They belong here only as proof that the named water was a road, not a mood, and that the French marsh sat on a commercial mouth.

## What a shop takes when it takes the name

Saline River Workshop, the public shop name already spoken in a 2025 interview, takes *Saline* in the English form. It does not have to settle the French argument. It should know the argument exists. A workshop named for a river named for salt is a better sentence than a workshop named for a river named for scenery. Salt is work. Scenery is a brochure.

No catalog. No SKU. The style guide allows one mid-body or late sentence on a river-name host essay. That sentence is the one above. The next sentence is the marsh, the works, the list of French names, and Owen’s report on the shelf.

Wilmar’s W&SVR used *Saline Valley* as a corporate landscape. Gates needed a valley more than he needed a marsh. The logging road and the workshop are a century apart and share a borrowed word. The word was French before it was a letterhead. The word was salt before it was furniture. Those two facts are the etymology this pack will carry.

Heady’s Drew County sits between Bayou Bartholomew on the east — longest bayou in the world, she writes — and the Saline on the southwest. The Monticello Ridge is the upland between. Anderson’s purchase sat in the conversation between Saline bottoms and upland. The French families Woodard lists sat on the lower banks, farther south and west, toward the mouth’s marsh. Wilmar is not Fogle’s town. Wilmar is the later mill stop that took the valley’s name for twenty-five miles of track. Keep the households apart.

## Two stills, no winner

The first still is the mouth: a salty marsh, French name, Ouachita waiting. The second is Benton: 1827, works, territorial salt, surrounding states. Woodard holds both. A Burns hour would print both cards and not dissolve one into the other. Comments on her encyclopedia page remember swimming at Prattsville, White Oak Bluff, Mahoney’s ferry, a hatchet-cut stair, a picnic; another comment remembers a lifeless river in the 1930s, chemicals from a factory upstream, children forbidden the water. Those memories are essay 27’s “relatively unspoiled.” They are not etymology. They are what the named water became after the works and after the boats.

The forks essay gave the lengths. This one gives the name’s argument. The dam essay will give the refusal. None of the three is a canoe brochure.
