# The Post Office and the Saw

Wilmar did not incorporate itself into existence and then go looking for a living. The living came first, in blades. James Thomas Dionysius Anderson built a sawmill in 1881. He opened the post office in 1884. Competitors built sawmills in 1887 and 1890. A stave mill — barrels, not boards — went up in 1898. On March 8, 1899, the city of Wilmar incorporated. Steven Teske’s chronology is a short film if you leave the music off. Each date is a still. The last still is a legal instrument, not a celebration.

George Balogh’s timber article for the Encyclopedia of Arkansas is the statewide explanation for why those dates cluster. After the Civil War, powered machinery let men put up small mills that served only the neighborhood. The thing that changed south Arkansas was not the saw. It was the rail. Samuel Fordyce’s Cotton Belt and the Iron Mountain’s branches gave northern buyers a way to take yellow pine out of the West Gulf Coastal Plain at a profit. Once the main lines existed, men from Illinois and Iowa bought timber by the tens of thousands of acres. Wilmar’s first mill, on Teske’s dates, is still the local kind: a settler’s saw, three years older than the town’s name. The 1890 mill, if it is Gates, is the other kind.

The order is the argument of this piece. Mail is a civic act. A charter is a civic act. Both arrived after someone had already decided that pine on this ridge was worth a blade. A heritage series that starts with a picnic and a mayor has chosen the wrong still. The first public fact here is cutting. The second is a cancellation stamp that compressed a daughter’s name until it would fit an envelope.

## The Warren Branch as a fact, not a metaphor

The 1907 *Advance* places Wilmar “about 8 miles nearly due west of Monticello on the Warren Branch of the Iron Mountain R. R.” That sentence is the geography lesson. Monticello sat on the high part of the dividing ridge and had the courthouse. Wilmar sat toward Warren and the Saline and had the timber. The branch traversed Drew County east to west. Cotton could ride it. So could boards. So could staves. A town that was store, post office, depot, and residence in one house did not need a philosopher to explain the railroad. The train was the explanation.

The same souvenir, writing while Anderson still walked the town, says that after the branch had been extended toward Warren he opened a small store and acted as postmaster and depot agent, “using the house which now serves as a depot for the combined purposes of store, postoffice, depot and residence.” Four functions, one roof. If a documentarian wanted a single still for the invention of Wilmar as a stop, that house would be it: sacks and letters and a timetable and a bed. The 1881 saw is older than that sentence. The 1884 post office is the year the house became a federal fact. The railroad did not invent the mill. It invented a reason for the mill’s boards to leave the county without waiting on a winter rise in the Saline.

Balogh notes the mutual dependence: railroads needed crossties and freight; mills needed transportation and the metal-working habits of shop men. Pine Bluff and Camden had the big shops. Wilmar had an interchange later, when Gates’s own logging road handed cars to the Iron Mountain at the sawmill. In the 1880s the picture is simpler. A local mill makes lumber for a local radius until a car can take a better price somewhere else.

Jann Woodard’s Saline River article is the other geography. River shipping slacked off considerably with the advent of the rail system in 1880. Fifty-four steamboats have been documented on the Saline; their cargo, in winter and spring, was cotton, staves, and timber, bound for New Orleans and Monroe. Anderson’s 1881 saw sits one year after that slackening. It is not a port mill. It is a ridge mill that arrived when the river had already begun to lose the argument. The stave mill of 1898 is a landlocked cousin of a river trade Woodard puts in the steamboat century. The Iron Mountain did not wait on a rise. The Saline did.

The 1907 paper, in a moment of unusual clarity, divided Drew County into three. The Bartholomew bottoms: about 100,000 acres, “immensely rich,” plantations, a bale and forty to fifty bushels of corn. The Saline bottoms: comparatively narrow, about 8,000 acres, poorer for the plow, heavy with timber. The rest: the uplands, the dividing ridge, sandy loam, ordinary farms. Monticello sat on the highest part of that ridge. Wilmar sat eight miles west, toward the timber and the Warren Branch, not yet in the 8,000 acres but in their conversation. Mary Heady’s modern county article says the same geography in a cleaner coat: Bayou Bartholomew on the eastern edge, the Saline on the southwestern border, the Monticello Ridge uplands running from north of Star City through the county into Louisiana. Anderson’s 1859 purchase — seven hundred acres at a dollar an acre — sat in the conversation between the second and the third of those parts. The 1881 saw is what he did with the timber side of the conversation once a car could take the boards.

## Rivals, and what a stave is

Teske’s “competitors” of 1887 and 1890 are not named in the encyclopedia paragraph. A later pass through DeArmond, Curry’s 1952 thesis, and the *Advance*’s advertising columns may name them. This draft will not invent firm names to look finished. What the dates do say is that Anderson’s 1881 mill did not remain a monopoly. Pine country in the 1880s was a rush. If one man had a saw, another man could hear it.

The 1890 date is the seam this series will keep visible. Mary Heady’s Drew County article says the first large-scale sawmill in the county was established by the Gates Lumber Company in 1889. Teske says Gates was established in Wilmar in 1890. The 1907 *Advance*, writing as if everyone still remembered, says the company’s advent was “17 years ago,” which, from December 1907, is 1890. A reader who wants a single year is asking the mill’s question. The historian’s question is the type of mill. 1881 is a settler’s saw. 1889 or 1890 is northern lumber capital of the kind Balogh describes. The unnamed 1887 competitor sits between those types. It may have been another neighborhood mill. It may have been an early Gates fact under another name. The record, in the sources this pack has on the desk, will not say.

The stave mill of 1898 is a different machine. Staves are the curved pieces of a barrel. South Arkansas shipped staves down the Saline in the steamboat years and kept shipping them after the rails came. The 1907 *Advance*, describing Wilmar at a thousand or twelve hundred people, still lists “a stave factory with a capacity of 10,000 per day.” Ten thousand staves a day is not a craft. It is a factory that smells of oak and steam. Barrel stock for oil, whiskey, produce, and the ordinary commerce of a country that still moved liquids and dry goods in wood.

The same souvenir, writing the county rather than the town, said stave timber had been “no small industry for many years” in Drew. Woodard names the later river labor: after the Hamptons and Wheelers, Slavonian families — Lipovac, Karlovic, Kusterin, Dorich, Piskulick — cut and shipped staves on the Saline. The encyclopedia does not assign those surnames a Wilmar address. This essay will not kidnap them for the 1898 mill’s pride. It will put them in the Saline economy that Wilmar’s factory belonged to. Hardwood bottoms, oak especially, made barrel stock. Pine made boards. A town that had both machines by 1898 was a town that had decided the ridge would leave as freight in more than one shape.

By 1907 the town also had a bank with $25,000 capital, five general stores, a dry-goods store, a drugstore, a livery, a blacksmith, and a Farmer’s Union warehouse rated at a thousand bales — the only one in the county that year. Those are mill-town furnishings. They are also farm-town furnishings. Wilmar, even at the Gates peak, marketed cotton. The *Advance* says the county’s crop moved through Monticello, Collins, Tillar, and Wilmar; the county yielded 15,000 to 20,000 bales in a normal year, 20,040 the previous year, 12,264 of them marketed at Monticello by compress receipt. The saw did not abolish the gin. The gin did not abolish the saw. The dividing ridge held both.

Kidd Bros. Company, in the same paper’s biographical back pages, acted as commissary for Gates and was among the largest mercantile concerns in the county. Dr. S. Harris owned the only drugstore. Those names belong to later stills. They are here only to keep the 1898–1899 town from looking like a single roof and a single blade. Incorporation arrived in a place that already had more than one way to make a dollar.

## Incorporation as a late act

March 8, 1899. Teske gives the day. A city that incorporates after eighteen years of milling is a city admitting that the camp has become a place people intend to tax, police, and pave — or at least to name as a legal person. The Methodist church had organized in 1893. John Jefferson Lee Spence’s Drew Normal Institute was already two years old. Gates had been in town for nine years if Teske’s 1890 is the year, ten if Heady’s 1889 is. Anderson’s post office was fifteen years old. The stave mill was a year old. Incorporation is not a beginning. It is a filing.

The petition is not in this folder. The names of the men who asked the state to recognize a mill stop as a municipal person are not in Teske’s paragraph. A later pass through the courthouse docket may produce them. This draft will not invent a mayor to stand on the steps. Small-town America, in the heritage register, likes to start with a charter and a picnic. Wilmar’s picnic, if it had one that March, is not in the encyclopedia. What is in the encyclopedia is a sequence of blades and a post office. That is a better origin. It keeps the town attached to work.

The census of 1900, the first that treats the new city as a row, counted 844 people. That is not a camp. It is also not Monticello, which the 1907 paper still put near three thousand. It is the size of a place that has a depot, a mill, a school about to call itself a college, and enough churches to argue about. Teske’s later table is the long film: 929 in 1910, 1,034 in 1920, 627 in 1930. The charter persisted. The population did not. A legal person can outlive the reason it was filed.

The 1907 paper, writing eight years after the filing, still treated “the real history of Wilmar” as the advent of Gates. The booster is not interested in charters. It is interested in payroll. A heritage series can afford to be interested in both, and in the stills the booster skips: the unnamed 1887 saw, the 1881 neighborhood mill, the four-function house, the daughter’s name on the stamp. Those are the years when Wilmar was already a place and not yet a city.

## What the record will not say

It will not name the 1887 competitor. It will not give Anderson’s 1881 mill a board-footage number, a brand of saw, or a payroll. It will not give Willie Elvira Anderson a date of birth or a married name in the sources this pack has on the desk. It will not produce a photograph of the four-function house that the *Advance* described in the present tense in 1907. Wanda Beaty Reaves’s *Scrapbook of Memories* and Rebecca DeArmond’s county book are on the bibliography as unconsulted leads. Until they are walked, the honest caption is: no likeness in hand, no firm list in hand, no output table in hand.

Absence is not emptiness. It is a seam. A series that irons the seam into a finished mill romance is doing the 1907 paper’s work. The 1907 paper had a job: invite capital, bless firstness, date the town from Gates. This essay’s job is shorter. Keep Teske’s dates in order. Keep the Warren Branch as a location, not a hymn. Keep the stave mill as a different machine from the pine mill. Keep the 1887 names blank until someone finds them.

A workshop that now takes its name from the Saline — the public shop name already spoken, not a catalog — can live with this chronology. Boards left this ridge before anyone filed a city. They still leave, in smaller numbers and for different rooms. The 1881 saw is not an ancestor to flatter. It is a reminder that the first civic act here was cutting, and the second was mail.
