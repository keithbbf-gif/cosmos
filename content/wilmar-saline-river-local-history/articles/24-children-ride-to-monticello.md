# Children Ride to Monticello

Steven Teske’s modern civic paragraph is almost bureaucratic. Children from Wilmar attend schools in Monticello, all of them part of the Drew County Central School District. The sentence is correct and cold. A town that once boarded 469 students on a college campus now sends its children east on a school run. The eight miles the 1907 paper called “nearly due west” have reversed. The gravity is Monticello’s.

Heady gives the district’s ancestor dates. Drew Central School was established in 1927 as a training school for teachers. Two years later the rural school districts of Drew County were consolidated with it. In 1935 the school separated from the college and kept the name Drew Central. That is a state-and-campus story. Wilmar High School, in 1907, had been a local boast: eleven grades, nine months, two hundred students, Massey, Mahling, expression, a boarding department. Consolidation is what happens when two hundred becomes a number a county will not fund in place.

1927 is three years after Heady’s Gates-to-Crossett date and three years after the mill-closure note this pack cites from Darling and Bragg. The training school opens as the mill town is already subtracting. 1929, rural consolidation, is the year before Wilmar’s census falls from 1,034 to 627. The district map and the population map move in the same weather. This draft will not draw a causal arrow the sources do not draw. It will print the years in one paragraph.

## What consolidation takes

It takes the weekday noise. It takes the ball field as a daily fact — Hudspeth’s heirs. It takes the chance that a child will see the foundation lines of Beauvoir as ordinary grass on the walk home. It leaves the June school ground as a weekend and a holiday, which is not nothing, and is not a school.

Rural Arkansas did this everywhere. The particular Wilmar wound is that the town had already lost a college to Monticello and then lost the high school to a district named for the college’s successor. Act 100 of 1909 put a state agricultural school in each quadrant. Monticello won the southeast. Teske: many of Beauvoir’s resources transferred there. Edwards: Spence himself worked in that lineage. By the 1930s the campus was Arkansas A&M. In 1971 it became the University of Arkansas at Monticello. Drew Central’s 1927 birth as a training school is the public campus reaching into the county’s rural districts with a weekday. A person is allowed to feel the pattern without writing a lawsuit.

The 1907 *Advance*, writing after Beauvoir’s removal, tried to call Massey’s high school a consolation: eleven grades, nine-month term, enrollment “in round numbers” 200, four literary teachers, one music, one expression, literary societies, a boarding department. “In round numbers” is a booster’s hedge. This draft will keep the hedge. Two hundred is the local high-school still. Four hundred and sixty-nine, in Edwards for 1906–07, was the college still. Teske’s present-tense sentence is the still after both numbers left town.

## Training school, which is a compliment and a raid

A training school for teachers, attached to the agricultural college, sounds like a gift to the county. It is also a magnet. Rural districts consolidated *into* it. Wilmar’s white high school of 1907 was already a finished thing. Twenty years later the county’s rural map was being redrawn around Monticello.

Heady’s earlier white map is loud. Rodgers Schoolhouse, 1836, near Union Ridge. Gaster Schoolhouse, 1842, near Gaster Hill. Brandon School, 1849, near Rock Springs. Phi Kappa Sigma Male College, 1859, burned by Federal troops in 1864. Rodger’s Female Academy, 1859, later Whaley Elementary. Hinemon University School, a white high school established in Monticello in 1890, acquired by the Drew County School Board in 1910 and converted into Monticello High School. The county seat collected secondary education the way it collected the agricultural school. Wilmar’s Massey campus was a mill-town answer that the 1929 consolidation did not have to preserve as a daily fact.

Black students’ map was redrawn later and harder. Monticello Academy, 1888–1933, was the county’s notable Black Presbyterian boarding school. Wilmar’s Colored School, undated in Teske, held the June Dinner and a dual-system weekday this pack cannot yet date. Drew High School integrated in 1970. Arkansas A&M had integrated in 1964. Teske’s present-tense “Drew County Central” is the end of those redrawings, not the start. Essay 17 stays with the yard the bus does not retire. This essay stays with the bus.

Teske does not print the year Wilmar’s last local high-school senior walked. Coleman’s “A School Tale” (*DCHJ* 1989) is an unconsulted door. Reaves’s scrapbook is another. This draft will not invent a last bell.

## A bus as a still

If this were a Burns hour, the still would be a bus door in early light, a town in the window getting smaller. No narration for a moment. Then Teske’s sentence. Then the 1907 enrollment of 200, hedge included. Then Heady’s 1927, 1929, 1935. Then 1970. Then stop.

The eight miles are not a metaphor until the essay has earned them. The 1907 paper placed Wilmar nearly due west of Monticello on the Warren branch. Teske’s coordinates put the city at 33°37’44″N, 91°55’53″W, elevation 154 feet, 1.53 square miles. The ridge Heady names — Monticello Ridge from north of Star City through Drew into Louisiana — is the dry boast the county seat sat on. Wilmar sat toward the Saline, lower, wetter in the bottoms, a mill stop. Children now ride toward the ridge on a weekday and, in June, some of their kin ride back toward the Colored School ground. Two directions. One county.

UAM’s School of Forest Resources is, in Heady, the only such institution in the state. The trees Gates took without replenishing became, two generations later, a curriculum in the county seat. Wilmar’s children can attend a district that shares a town with that school. They do not attend a college in Wilmar. The rehearsal hall emptied. The professional stage kept the name of a city that won the bid.

Charlie May Simon, born on a farm between Tillar and Monticello in 1897 — Beauvoir’s first year — became the children’s author the state named a book award for. William Shea has taught at UAM since 1974 and wrote, with Droessler, the 1915 baseball essay. The campus produced historians of the county that lost the campus’s rehearsal. Mild irony. Also how records survive.

In 2003, Heady writes, UAM added technology campuses at Crossett and at McGehee. The university’s reach now includes the town Cap Gates built after Wilmar and a Delta town on another rail. Wilmar’s children, Teske writes, attend Drew County Central. They do not attend a Crossett branch and they do not attend Beauvoir. The system that ate the rehearsal hall kept growing in other directions. The bus still points east, to the ridge, to a district named Central.

The shop does not belong on the bus. A furniture lane that wants a child as a heritage extra has failed the style guide. No price at the door. No “invest in our schools” close from a workshop. The weekday is the community’s. The June weekend is the community’s. This essay is only the map of who took the weekday.
