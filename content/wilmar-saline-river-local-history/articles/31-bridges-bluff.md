# Bridges Bluff

Head of navigation is a phrase that sounds like a title and is only a depth. Jann Woodard puts it at Bridges Bluff in Cleveland County. Winter and spring, a boat could make it that far. Above, the Saline was too thin, too shoaled, too much a mountain memory. Below, cotton and staves and timber could begin their ride to Monroe and New Orleans.

Cleveland County sits north and west of Drew. Rison is in Woodard’s community list. Staves — the town — is there too, a name that is already a cargo. Bridges Bluff is not a Wilmar suburb. It is the river’s business address in the steamboat century. A series about Wilmar and the Saline that never names the head of navigation has not learned how the water was used.

The river that arrives at that bluff has already come a long way. Woodard’s Saline rises north of Benton, four forks meeting near Riverside: South Fork thirty-eight miles, Middle Fork thirty-seven, Alum Fork sixty-one, North Fork twenty-nine. After the meeting it meanders through Saline, Grant, Dallas, Cleveland, Bradley, Drew, and Ashley counties before it empties into the Ouachita near Felsenthal. Two hundred and four miles. A watershed of about 3,350 square miles. Gravel bottom, pools and shoals. Bridges Bluff is not the mouth and not the origin. It is the point at which a commercial stack stopped being a wish.

Mountain stream at the origin, Delta-type bayou near the mouth: Woodard’s opening claim is the career that makes a head of navigation necessary. A river that changes its mind will have a place where a boat of a certain draft can no longer argue with the gravel. Cleveland County is that argument’s address in the steamboat century. Grant County, upstream, holds Jenkins’ Ferry. Bradley County, downstream, holds Warren. Drew County, east, holds a mill town that used the woods and not the wharf. The bluff is the hinge between a woods and a road.

## A bluff is a landing and a limit

Bluffs on southern rivers are where you put a warehouse if you are lucky and a rumor if you are not. This draft has no warehouse inventory for Bridges Bluff. It has Woodard’s function: the farthest a documented commercial boat would go. Limits are as historical as capitals. Monticello is a capital of a county. Bridges Bluff is a capital of a draft.

Fifty-four steamboats, Woodard writes, have been documented on the Saline. Bob Withers, Len Moore, John Wesley Smith, Frank Keeling among the captains; the Hampton brothers and John N. Wheeler among the shippers until the early 1900s. Those hulls, if they were doing the work she describes, were doing it below this bluff or at it. Above it, the river was still a river. It was not, in her sentence, a road.

Winter and spring are the seasons of that road. Hydrology as a schedule: you wait for a rise, you load, you hope the bars stay covered until Monroe. The Iron Mountain did not wait. When the rail system arrived in 1880, Woodard writes, shipping slacked. The bluff’s authority did not vanish in a day. The *Gate City* was still coming up in 1913. Authority thins. It does not always break.

The Iron Mountain’s Warren Branch, when it came, made a different head of navigation: a depot. Wilmar’s depot-house — Anderson as postmaster, storekeeper, depot agent, resident, the four-function building the 1907 *Advance* still called a house that “now serves as a depot” — is the rail equivalent of a bluff. Goods changed vehicles. The bluff’s authority ended, or became a memory a later fisherman could point at from a boat that no longer needed a stack.

## Cleveland’s war, Drew’s neighbor

Woodard’s Civil War list for the Saline includes Mount Elba and Marks’ Mills in Cleveland County. Those engagements are the next essay’s. They sit here only to say that the head of navigation was also a military geography. A landing is always a landing. Armies use the same shallow arithmetic as shippers: where can a body of men, or a wagon, or a pontoon, get across or along.

Cleveland County in 1864 was not a sidebar to Drew. It was the next drainage over, the same water, a different courthouse. Drew’s own calendar, in Mary Heady’s article, is a string of 1864 and 1865 dates that never become a named battle on the scale of Jenkins’ Ferry. The *Advance* of 1907, writing booster history, said no battles were fought in Drew, only a few skirmishes. Heady’s list is longer. The seam belongs in the war essay. Here the useful fact is simpler. The Saline’s working end in Cleveland County was a place people already knew how to find before they had a mill town named for a daughter.

Rison and Staves are Woodard’s Cleveland names. Warren, down in Bradley, is the mill neighbor this pack is not allowed to absorb. Longview, in Ashley, is a mouth-country landing and a fight. Bridges Bluff sits in that string as the commercial limit, not as a battlefield Woodard named. Do not promote it to a battle because the sentence needs drama. The limit is the drama. A river that will not float a boat past a certain sand is a harder fact than a reenactment.

## What Wilmar was not

Wilmar, Teske writes, is in western Drew County, elevation 154 feet, 1.53 square miles in the 2020 census table. Eight miles west of Monticello on the Warren Branch, the 1907 paper said. It is a timber town that named a railroad for a valley. It is not Bridges Bluff. The Wilmar and Saline Valley Railroad, chartered in 1904, did not make the town a port. It made the town a log dump with a mainline handshake. The Warren and Saline River Railroad, the Bradley County short line of 1905/1920, is a different confusion and a different county. Keep both off this bluff.

The 1907 *Advance* divided Drew County into Bartholomew bottoms, a dividing ridge, and Saline bottoms of about 8,000 acres, “not so good from an agricultural standpoint, but are heavily timbered.” The bulk of the pine, the paper said, lay in those bottoms and the contiguous territory and in the south of the county, most of it by then under Gates at Wilmar. Wilmar lived on the ridge’s western opinion of itself, in conversation with those 8,000 acres, not at the Cleveland landing where a steamboat turned around. Distance is the point. A mill town can live in a river’s timber and still be a morning’s ride from the river’s working end.

Staves, the Cleveland County town in Woodard’s list, is the cargo that became a place name. Rison is the courthouse town. Neither is Bridges Bluff. A writer looking for a warehouse photograph will want the bluff to have been a town. This draft has a function, not a municipal charter. If a later county history prints a store, a gin, or a shed at the landing, those facts should replace this paragraph’s empty lot. Empty lots are historical too. A head of navigation can be a bluff and a depth and still not be a city.

French families on the lower Saline, in Woodard’s late-1700s list — Fogle, LaBeouff, DuBose, Charron, Pevetoe, Ramsauer, De Ambleton, Carcuff, Bullet — are another kind of distance. They are the mouth’s people, the Marais Saline people, not the bluff’s and not Wilmar’s. The river had households before it had a head of steamboat navigation. The bluff is a nineteenth-century measurement. The French names are an eighteenth-century one. Both are true. Neither is a Wilmar founding myth.

## Film a USGS bend

The still is a map, not a reenacted packet boat. Label Bridges Bluff. Label Warren. Label the Drew line. Let a viewer see how far a mill town was from the water’s working end. A generated packet boat under a painted moon is a lie. A USGS historical topo of the Cleveland reach (the pack’s WC-USGS-01 wish) is the period object this essay can honestly want.

Do not put a shop on this landing. The bluff is a depth. A workshop named for the river, when that sentence is allowed, belongs to later essays about the name and the ridge. Head of navigation is not a hangtag. It is the sentence that keeps a landlocked mill town from pretending it was New Orleans.
