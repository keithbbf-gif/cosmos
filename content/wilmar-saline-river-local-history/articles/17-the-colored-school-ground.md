# The Colored School Ground

“From the original June Dinner to now the celebration has always been held at the same spot – the Wilmar Colored School.” Mayor Toni Perry, to Arkansas PBS. The sentence is architecture. It makes a schoolyard into a calendar.

This pack does not have the school’s opening date, builder, or Rosenwald status on the desk. Teske’s Wilmar article, which dates Anderson’s dollar, the post office, Gates, Beauvoir, baseball, a Klan chapter, and a turbine, does not date the Colored School. That silence is a fact about the encyclopedia’s town paragraph, not a proof that the school was slight. Selma Rosenwald School, elsewhere in Drew County, is the one Mary Heady names as a historical point of interest. Wilmar’s Colored School may be in Wanda Beaty Reaves’s scrapbook, in Rebecca DeArmond’s county history, in county board minutes. A later pass should go and get the file. Until then, the honest essay is about what a named ground does when the building’s legal life is over and the holiday is not.

PBS published two captions that this draft will treat as the available stills: “Wilmar, Arkansas Colored School in the mid 1950s,” and “Wilmar, Arkansas Colored School grounds and location of Wilmar June Dinner today.” The mid-century building is a photograph in their blog. The present ground is a photograph in their blog. This desk has not walked the lot. It will not invent a brick count, a bell, or a ruin. The captions are enough to say the name survived the dual system and the consolidation.

## Dual systems, one town

Wilmar’s white school story is loud in the 1907 paper. Beauvoir had already followed John Jefferson Lee Spence toward Monticello. In its place the *Advance* advertised Wilmar High School under W. B. Massey: eleven grades, a nine-month term, enrollment “in round numbers” 200, four literary teachers, one music, one expression, Otto Mahling from Berlin, a boarding department. The paper could inventory a white campus the way it inventoried stave capacity. The Black school story is quieter in that paper, which is a political fact about the paper. A booster souvenir is not required to notice the yard that would outlast the booster’s college.

Teske, writing later, says children from Wilmar now attend Drew County Central in the Monticello orbit. Heady gives the district’s ancestor dates: Drew Central School established in 1927 as a training school for teachers; rural districts consolidated into it in 1929; separation from the college in 1935, the name kept. Integration of Drew High School, Heady writes, came in 1970. Arkansas A&M, the college on the other side of those eight miles, had integrated in 1964. The Colored School as a dual-system institution belongs to the years before 1970, and to the memory after. Teske does not say in which year Wilmar’s Black children left that yard for a bus. This draft will not invent the year.

Monticello Academy (1888–1933) was the county’s notable Black Presbyterian school, a boarding and missionary project. Charles Mebane, pastor of Holmes Chapel Presbyterian Church, established it. Missionary Mary Emilie Holmes helped secure funding from the Presbyterian Board of Missions for Freedmen. Heady puts that campus in the county seat. Wilmar’s school was local. Local is not lesser. Local is the yard you can walk to, the yard you will not surrender to a park planner. A county can have a missionary academy and a neighborhood school and a Rosenwald building at Selma. They are not interchangeable plaques.

Hinemon University School, a white high school established in Monticello in 1890, was acquired by the Drew County School Board in 1910 and converted into Monticello High School. The white map of secondary education in this county is loud because the papers were loud. The Black map is louder in June, on a yard the papers of 1907 did not need.

## Why the ground cannot move

Perry’s “too close to everyone’s heart” is not a tourism slogan. It is a statement about the dead, the living, and the right to stay put. Black southern holidays often attach to a church or a school because those were the buildings a community could hold when the courthouse was not theirs. Moving the dinner to a fairground or a city park would be a kind of progress a grant writer understands and a grandmother does not.

The courthouse in this county has moved. Heady records a brick French-castle courthouse of 1871, torn down in 1933, the court shifted south on Main Street, the old site becoming “the square.” Institutions that belong to the county seat can be demolished and landscaped. The June ground, on Perry’s evidence, cannot. That contrast is not poetry. It is two kinds of public property.

PBS filmed the grounds. The building’s condition, whether a remnant or a memory, is a fact for a photographer, not for a sentence that pretends to have walked it from this desk. If a later editor obtains the mid-1950s still from PBS, the caption must say PBS and the decade. It must not say “period photograph, pack original.” IMAGE_SOURCES already marks the slot: ask PBS; do not scrape.

The dinner’s first shape, in the PBS narration, was dishes brought from home and eaten together. The later shape, in the 2006 Parade History list, was vendors and church fundraisers on the same dirt. Both shapes need a place that is not a rented hall. A schoolyard is already designed for a crowd that does not live there every night. That is why the name of the school can outlive the weekday of the school.

## School consolidation as a second removal

When Wilmar’s children were assigned to Drew County Central, the town lost a weekday. It did not, on Perry’s evidence, lose the weekend in June. That split is the modern civic structure: education elsewhere, reunion here. A suburb of Monticello, Teske’s phrase, is a weekday phrase. The school ground in June is a Wilmar phrase.

Consolidation in this county is older than 1970. Heady’s 1929 rural-district merger is the white and rural map being redrawn around a training school attached to the agricultural college. Black students’ map was redrawn later and harder. A series that treats “children ride to Monticello” as a single modern convenience has collapsed two removals into one bus. Essay 24 will stay with the bus. This essay stays with the yard that the bus does not retire.

Beauvoir’s campus, Edwards writes, left foundation lines in the grass; half the structures were dismantled and hauled to Monticello. The Colored School’s fate as lumber is not in Teske. Do not assume the same afterlife. Do not assume a different one. The June use is the afterlife we can document.

Teske’s 2010 race line — 364 African American of 511 — is the demographic still that prevents a reader from imagining the school ground as a minority shrine in a white mill remnant. The dinner’s thousands and the 2010 majority are the same town. The 2020 count, 395, is smaller. The ground, Perry says, is not smaller.

## What this yard is not

It is not Beauvoir. Beauvoir’s own advertising, in Edwards, called itself the pioneer boarding school in southeast Arkansas for whites. The Colored School was the institution that sentence excluded. A pack that writes ten essays about Spence and one clause about this yard has repeated the 1907 paper’s politics.

It is not Selma Rosenwald. Heady names Selma. This draft will not steal Selma’s file to furnish Wilmar.

It is not a brand site. No shop sentence belongs on this yard. If a documentary crew comes, they ask. If a heritage site wants a pin on a map, the pin is the community’s to approve. This essay exists so the pack’s other pieces cannot treat “school” as only Beauvoir.

Reaves’s *Wilmar, Arkansas: Scrapbook of Memories* (2005) and DeArmond’s *Old Times Not Forgotten* (1980) are the unconsulted leads for a founding date, a teacher’s name, a board minute. Coleman’s “A School Tale” in the *Drew County Historical Journal* (1989) is another door. This draft has not opened those doors. It says so. A later pass that finds a date should add the date, not a scene of children invented to decorate the date.

The still, if a crew can get permission, is the present ground in ordinary light, then the PBS mid-1950s frame, then Perry’s sentence on a card. No generated sepia. No child asked to smile for 1899. The yard is already doing the work.
