# Corn First, Then Lumber

The first crop Teske records on the Wilmar ground is corn. Five acres, 1859, five enslaved people in the rows: Simon and Lizza Taylor, Virginia and Wealthy Thurman, Gill Gordon. By the 1860s, Heady writes, timber was also a major industry in Drew County. By 1907 the *Advance* could still say agriculture was “the most important and stable of all industries,” and then spend its best pages on cotton, corn demonstration farms, peaches, and Bayou land at five dollars an acre rent. Pine had a large mill at Wilmar. The paper still thought the county was a farm.

This essay is the farm piece that the mill pieces owe.

Anderson’s five acres were a statement that a household intended to stay the winter. Corn is calories and fodder. It is not a picturesque crop. The rest of the seven hundred acres was woods and old field, the kind of ground a later booster would call heavily timbered without walking it. The sawmill waited until 1881. Gates waited until 1890, or 1889 if you take Heady’s first-large-mill date. That delay is the first fact a mill-town mythology likes to skip. The town that later printed itself as a payroll began as a field that other people’s hands made possible.

## Cotton, the staple the souvenir cannot quit

Drew County, the *Advance* says, yielded 15,000 to 20,000 bales in a normal year; 20,040 the previous year; 12,264 marketed at Monticello by compress receipt; the rest at Wilmar, Collins, Tillar, some at Dermott. Wilmar’s Farmer’s Union warehouse, a thousand bales, new in 1907, is a farm institution in a mill town. The saw did not abolish the gin. The gin did not abolish the saw. The dividing ridge held both.

Bayou Bartholomew bottoms — about 100,000 acres in the paper’s figure — were the rich cotton country, Major Tillar the named giant, headquarters at Tillar in the northeastern part of the county. Col. Charles T. Duke of Baxter and W. H. Lephiew of Dermott are the other plantation names the souvenir thought worth printing. Saline bottoms — about 8,000 acres — were “not so good from an agricultural standpoint, but are heavily timbered.” That sentence is the whole geography of this pack. East: alluvium. Southwest: trees. Middle: upland farms that the paper insists can make a half bale and twenty to thirty bushels of corn, or better if a man will learn. Creek bottoms, the paper added, would do three-quarters of a bale upward in a favorable season. Experienced farmers, it said, would tell you a bale and fifty bushels were possible with the right cultivation. Boosters always know a man who knows a man. The acreage figures are round. The boast is the document.

The Southern Compress Company in Monticello, the same edition says, handled 18,000 to 20,000 bales in a year, more than 17,000 the previous year, a payroll of $8,244.67, as many as forty men at times. Much of the cotton came in from the Valley branch of the Iron Mountain — the paper’s name for the same east-west road — and some from the M. H. & L. Most of Warren’s marketed cotton, the souvenir claimed, was shipped to Monticello for compressing. George H. McFadden & Bro., among the largest cotton factors in the United States, bought thousands of bales in the territory; C. B. Wood was their buyer on the square. That is a county-seat machine. Wilmar’s thousand-bale warehouse is a western annex. The mill town was never only a mill.

## Demonstration, Indiana, and the peach certificate

L. D. Owens, four miles north of Monticello, ordinary upland, government demonstration, a little over forty-eight bushels of corn, four bales on five acres. John Scharff, from Indiana five years earlier, Long Prairie, sixteen miles south, forty to sixty bushels, “never raised better corn.” A. J. Wilson, the “Peach King of South Arkansas,” about a mile and a half south of town on the east side of the dividing ridge, 20,000 trees on two places, Elberta and Alexander and Carmen for early fruit, a Wilson’s October of his own naming for fall, a certificate of highest award from the 1904 St. Louis world’s fair hanging in the circuit clerk’s office. He had begun after the war with apples, found peaches better, bought the second farm out of the first farm’s profits, expected 7,000 to 8,000 crates from the lower place in a reasonable next season, plus some 400 crates of Abundance and Red June plums, most of the peaches going to Little Rock, Pine Bluff, and the towns between, shipped direct to the retail dealer. These are booster stories with names. They are also a county teaching itself that it is not only Gates.

The paper’s garden list is longer than a mill brochure can use. Peas, sorghum, oats, hay. Pea vines and Bermuda, two cuttings a year, about a ton to the acre at each cutting if you believe the souvenir. Vegetables of all sorts. Melons on the hill land around Monticello. Strawberries in February at Z. J. Wood’s Terra Cotta hotel in a mild year, served through May or early June, the winter “exceptionally mild,” the season a month or six weeks early. Pears, plums, grapes, some apples if a man selected the varieties. Heady’s shorter list, in the modern encyclopedia: cotton, corn, wheat, hay, apples, peaches, pears, tomatoes. The overlap is the crop mix. The difference is tone. Heady does not need the Terra Cotta’s February berry. The *Advance* does, because a souvenir is a weather story as well as a yield story.

“It is often said that this is not a corn country,” the paper admits, and then spends a paragraph proving the saying wrong. Owens and Scharff are that proof. Anderson’s 1859 five acres are the older proof, unpaid, unboostered, worked by people who did not get their names in the 1907 list. This essay will not flatten that labor into a harvest festival. It will set the demonstration farm next to the first field and refuse to pretend they are the same kind of success.

## What the field owes the mill, and the reverse

A stave mill needs oak. A household needs corn. A bursar at Beauvoir took both. Tuition in crops is an earlier essay’s still. The five names in the first field made the first corn possible. Later field labor — Black, white, tenant, owner — made the 20,040 bales possible. Drew County in 1860, Heady writes, counted 5,581 white residents and 3,497 people held in bondage. The staple the souvenir cannot quit was built on that count and then on the tenancy that followed it. The 1907 paper wants a prospector to hurry less toward Texas. It does not want him to look too long at who is in the rows.

Gates, by 1907, controlled the greater part of the pine, the souvenir said, with the large mill at Wilmar. Hardwoods everywhere; small mills on them; stave timber for many years; tie timber by the thousands. Agriculture remained, in the paper’s ranking, the most important and stable industry. Lumbering was the other. The ranking is a political sentence. A county seat with a compress and a cotton mill needs agriculture to be first. A mill town eight miles west needs the ranking to be negotiable. Wilmar’s warehouse is the negotiation in lumber: a thousand bales in a town the paper called second importance.

Balogh’s statewide timber story is the later weather. Cut out and get out, then, for the firms that survived, pine as a crop. L. K. Pomeroy and Eugene P. Connor opened Ozark Badger in Drew County in 1925, Heady writes, and pioneered selective cutting as a model in the 1930s. The agricultural school that Monticello won in 1909 became, in time, a campus with the state’s only School of Forest Resources. The field and the woods taught each other late. In 1859 they had not. Corn was first. The lumber was already in the trees, waiting on a saw and a rail.

A shop that works wood is a cousin of the mill, not of the gin. The farm essays are in this pack so the cousin does not forget the household. Soft-link: none required. The corn is enough.
