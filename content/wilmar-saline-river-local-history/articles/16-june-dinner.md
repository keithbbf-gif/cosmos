# June Dinner

Steven Teske calls Wilmar’s June Dinner one of the oldest Juneteenth celebrations in Arkansas, begun in the nineteenth century, drawing thousands. The Encyclopedia of Arkansas puts that sentence in the town’s opening paragraph, before the mill, which is a kind of editorial justice. A reader who came for Gates Lumber Company has to pass the table first. Arkansas PBS, in a short film for *Celebrating Arkansas*, went to the ground and recorded what the books could not: oral history, Mayor Toni Perry, a grandfather’s grandfather, Galveston, four years enslaved, a table carried home.

This essay will not launder oral history into a footnote that looks like a deed. Mayor Perry’s account, as PBS reports it from 2023 interviews, is the account. A man from this country was held in Galveston. He came back. He brought the Texas emancipation holiday — June 19, 1865, the news arriving late, the table that followed — and his family kept it. The celebration has been held, the mayor said, at the same spot: the Wilmar Colored School. “We can’t move it from there because it’s too close to everyone’s heart.”

If the camera had a still for the beginning of the dinner, it would not be a printed charter. No nineteenth-century written constitution for the meal was located for this draft. The still would be a name said on a porch, then another name, then the schoolyard. Teske’s “nineteenth century” and PBS’s “late 1800s” are the same weather in two registers. Parade History, writing from a 2006 visit, said the town had kept the dinner more than a hundred years and that the start was thought to be a man who had lived in Wilmar, been taken to Texas, and come home with Galveston’s June. Thought to be. Oral. The pack will keep the hedge.

## What Juneteenth is doing in Drew County

Juneteenth is a Texas fact that became a Black Southern fact and, in 2021, a federal one. General Gordon Granger’s order in Galveston on June 19, 1865, is the national origin the holiday now carries in statute. Wilmar’s dinner is older than the federal holiday and local in the way that matters: a specific schoolyard, a specific set of families, barbecue and tamales and pound cake, people who left coming back. A city of a few hundred that can hold thousands is a city that exists in a larger geography of kin.

The 2010 census line Teske prints is the weekday photograph: 511 people, 134 white, 364 African American. The 2020 count is 395. Teske’s “thousands” of dinner attendees is not a contradiction of those cells. It is a different census, taken in June, of people who still answer to Wilmar when the calendar asks. Parade History, in 2006, called the town a community of “500 plus,” which sits between Teske’s 2000 figure (571) and his 2010 figure (511). The dinner does not care which decennial bar you circled. It cares that the ground is the same.

PBS’s short is eight minutes and forty-nine seconds in the PBS video catalog. That is not a history book. It is a visit. The producers talked to the mayor, to residents, and to people with personal ties to Wilmar. Beverly Railey, a Wilmar native, gave them a sentence a documentary can print on a card without ornament: “Chicago, I’m a visitor. Wilmar, I’m home.” The dinner is what makes that sentence possible for people who do not live on the 1.53 square miles Teske’s modern table assigns the city. Home, in this usage, is a weekend and a family and a date.

## Oral, which is a method

PBS is frank: the history has been passed down mostly through oral accounts. A heritage series that only trusts a land office will miss the oldest continuous civic event in the town. A heritage series that turns the mayor’s grandfather’s grandfather into a novelized hero will also miss. The method is: say who said it, say when it was recorded, do not invent the four years in Galveston as a scene.

Galveston is the national Juneteenth origin. That a Drew County man was there, in bondage, and came home, is a plausible historical path — the internal slave trade and wartime movements did such things — and it is, in this pack, an oral path. Both can be said in one paragraph. This draft will not name the ancestor beyond Perry’s kinship formula. PBS published a still of Arton Ragland Sr. holding a young family member. A photograph in a public-television blog is not a genealogical proof, and this essay will not make it one. The still belongs to the families. The sentence that can be used is Perry’s.

PBS also records the first shape of the meal. Initially, locals brought any dish they could make and sat down together. They celebrated togetherness and, the film’s narration says, freedom at last. Later Junes added vendors, a parade, the ordinary machinery of a reunion that outgrew a single household’s pots. The 2006 food-and-parade record is that later machinery, caught in a list: Divalicious Fixings, Margo’s Catfish, The Catfish Hut, A & W Concessions, a tamale stand, Simply the Best B & B, AAU boys’ basketball fundraisers, Revival Center Church of God in Christ youth-department plates. Goat, pork, beef. Ribs. Turkey legs. Catfish. Chicken. Tamales. Pound cake. Fried green tomatoes. Funnel cake. Families and social groups also came with their own grills, smokers, coolers, tables, and chairs. Joyce Steen spoke on camera that year about her life, her family, community, food, and work. Essay 40 will stay with the plate. This essay stays with the date and the ground.

A later year will have a different list. This draft will not update 2006 into a directory. The list is a still. The oral account is a still. Teske’s opening sentence is a still. Three stills, one holiday.

## A town that outlasted its mill

Anderson’s post office is 1884. Gates arrives 1889/1890. Incorporation is March 8, 1899. Beauvoir’s useful life is a decade. The mill’s useful life is a generation. Teske’s dinner sentence is older than most of those institutions if the nineteenth-century start holds, and it is younger than the five names in Anderson’s first field if the start is after emancipation. Either way, the dinner is the civic fact that did not require a company charter or a state agricultural school. It required people who remembered a late freedom and a yard they would not surrender.

Wilmar in Teske’s modern sentence is “mostly a suburb of Monticello.” That is a weekday. The June weekend is not a suburb. People come from Chicago and from the next county and from the houses that still sit on the 154-foot elevation Teske prints. They come because a grandfather’s grandfather came home. They come because the school ground is still the school ground. They come because Teske was right to put the dinner in the first paragraph.

The 2024 screenings PBS arranged are part of the dinner’s modern paper trail: Mosaic Templars Cultural Center in Little Rock, CALS branch libraries (Dee Brown, Rooker, McMath, Brooks in Wrightsville), and the June Dinner itself on Sunday, June 16, 2024. An expanded cut of the short traveled those rooms. The film did not invent the holiday. It borrowed the holiday’s people for an hour and then gave them back. A heritage series should do the same.

## What the dinner is not for

It is not for a furniture brand. It is not for a soft-link. It is not a closing image for an essay about Anderson’s dollar. Those essays may point here. This essay does not point back to a shop. The table is the point.

It is not Beauvoir’s cousin. Beauvoir advertised itself, Edwards records, as a boarding school for whites. The June table is a Black institution in a town that, by 2010, was majority African American. The two facts can live in one city. They are not the same picnic.

It is not Gates’s company outing. It is not a white mill town’s gift. It is not a tourism slogan that can be moved to a fairground because a grant writer prefers restrooms. Perry’s refusal to move it is the civic law this pack will honor.

If a later editor wants a photograph, the request goes to the families and to PBS, not to a generator. PBS already printed a mid-1950s view of the Colored School and a present-tense view of the grounds. Those frames are theirs. The school ground is the set. The work is the cooking. The theology, if any, is the date: news of freedom, late, then annually on time.

The next essay stays on that ground. Essay 40 stays with the food. This one is the origin as the record will bear it: Teske’s oldest-in-Arkansas clause, Perry’s Galveston kinship, a 2006 list of plates, a 2023 oral method, and a prohibition on selling any of it.
