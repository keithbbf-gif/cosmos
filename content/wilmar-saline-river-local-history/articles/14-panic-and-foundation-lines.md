# Panic and Foundation Lines

The Panic of 1907 was a national credit event with a New York plot: Knickerbocker, Morgan, a run, a rescue. Paul Edwards’s local sentence is shorter. The crisis devastated Wilmar’s economy and forced Beauvoir College to close. John Jefferson Lee Spence disassembled half of the college’s structures and moved them to Monticello. Only foundation lines remain in Wilmar today.

A mill town lives on credit even when it pays its bursar in hogs. Gates needed buyers. Cotton needed factors. A boarding school needed families who could still spare a child and a wagon. When the country’s money seized, the ridge’s money seized. Teske, pairing 1905 and 1907, treats the two blows as the end of the school. He is right. A plague and a panic are different species of ruin. Together they are enough.

The previous essay stopped on forty dead. This one starts on a credit freeze and ends on grass. Do not stack them into one hymn. The child is not the Panic. The Panic is not the mill’s 1924 death. Foundation lines are this year’s architecture.

## What “disassembled” means

It means lumber. It means a second use for the elegant wooden structures Edwards mentioned — the campus that had a parade ground and a five-hundred-seat auditorium, the hall Bryan filled in 1906. It means the county seat absorbing the mill town’s ambition, board by board. The 1907 *Advance*, dated December 17, already has Spence in Monticello as superintendent, Beauvoir “abandoned on the removal,” Wilmar High School under W. B. Massey, eleven grades, nine months, enrollment in round numbers 200, four literary teachers, one music, one expression, literary societies, a boarding department. The paper is writing after the move and trying, in the booster way, to call the high school a consolation.

Foundation lines are the opposite of a consolation. They are a plan without walls. A person can still walk them, Edwards implies, if the grass has not won. A Ken Burns last shot would be that rectangle at late afternoon, no narrator for a few seconds. This pack cannot offer the frame. It can insist that the frame is the fact.

Which half moved, which half stayed to become the lines, whether Bryan’s hall rode a wagon east — Edwards does not say. The catalogs at Taylor Library will not, by themselves, walk a later field. A Sanborn map, a courthouse deed, a photograph in Reaves’s scrapbook: those are later stills. Until they are in hand, “half” is the measure, and the hall is inside the uncertainty. This draft will not assign a particular rectangle to a particular night.

Otto Mahling of Berlin, concertmaster, teacher of violin and cornet, appears in the same souvenir as the high school’s music man. Edwards says Beauvoir’s music had been a crown jewel with internationally trained instructors. Mahling looks like what remained, or what Massey’s school hired to prove the jewel had not entirely rolled east. A concertmaster is not a college. He is the weekly version of a hunger the Panic did not kill: trained sound in a timber stop. The hunger survived. The buildings, half of them, did not.

## Monticello’s gain

Spence did not leave education. He ran Monticello’s schools, then Hinemon University, then the Fourth District State Agricultural School after Act 100 of 1909. The state’s four agricultural schools — Jonesboro, Russellville, Magnolia, Monticello — were the public answer to the need Beauvoir had named. Wilmar had named it first, in 1897, and could not fund it through a panic. The bidding process that put the Fourth District school in Monticello is a county-seat story. Wilmar, eight miles west, had already spent its college.

There is no villain in the haul if you do not need one. Buildings that can be moved will be moved to where the students and the state money are. The villain, if the word is allowed, is the idea that a mill town’s civic architecture is portable without loss. The loss is the foundation line.

Monticello sat on the highest part of the dividing ridge. The 1907 paper already knew the hierarchy: town of second importance, Wilmar; first, the seat, compact brick, waterworks, a cotton mill, three thousand people in the booster’s figure. Fountain and Polly Austin’s eighty-three acres, 1849. Incorporation, 1852. Hinemon University School, 1890. Dr. W. A. Brown, president of the Board of Trade in 1907 — Heady does not print the 1909 bid, but the souvenir already shows a seat that knows how to write a subscription list. The agricultural school is the next capture. The next essay will stay with Act 100. This one stays with the year the private answer died.

Spence had come to Monticello in 1892, left it for Wilmar’s offer of land and half the cost of construction, and came back when the money seized. Judge I. A. Bird and Major J. T. D. Anderson had donated the Wilmar property. A. H. Gates had given the 300,000-gallon well. Donors cannot endow a panic. A sliding scale that takes crops and livestock cannot slide below a household’s ruin. When Gates’s buyers tighten and cotton factors tighten, a shoat is not a note. Edwards says the panic devastated the local economy. The souvenir, dated in the same year, will not say the word.

## 1907, the souvenir year

The *Advance*’s industrial edition is a document of a county advertising itself in the year its second town lost its college. It still calls Wilmar thriving. It still lists the stave factory at 10,000 a day and the bank at $25,000 and the Farmer’s Union warehouse, the only one in the county, a thousand bales, established during the present year. It still needs Anderson alive as first settler, Capt. J. T. D. Anderson, liberal host whose policy “probably” drew the mill. It still dates “the real history of Wilmar” from Gates, “17 years ago.” It cannot bear to linger on a panic. That refusal is part of the record. Boosters write in the future tense while the foundations are still warm.

The same issue divides the county into Bartholomew bottoms, Saline bottoms, and the dividing ridge, and still insists agriculture is “the most important and stable of all industries.” Fifteen to twenty thousand bales in a normal year; 20,040 the previous year; 12,264 at the Monticello compress. Wilmar, Collins, Tillar, some at Dermott, took the rest. A mill town that files a farm warehouse in the year it loses a college is a town that has more than one ledger. The Panic hit both ledgers. The souvenir reprints the farm numbers and skips the freeze.

December 17, 1907. Knickerbocker had already failed. Morgan had already arranged a rescue in New York. The paper in Monticello is selling a county. Carolyn Haisty’s transcription, on ARGenWeb, is how this pack reached the text. The original cuts — courthouse tower, school buildings — are not in the transcription as images. A later editor should pull the microfilm. Until then, the words are the photographs. “Abandoned on the removal” is the caption the booster could not avoid. The inventory is the picture it preferred.

A later census will do the lingering the souvenir refused. The college’s death is not the mill’s death — that waits for 1924, Darling and Bragg’s closure note, Heady’s land sale to Crossett, Teske’s late-1920s equipment move — but it is the first major subtraction. A town that has been mill and college and becomes mill only is already smaller in the head, even if the 1910 count is 929 and the 1920 count is 1,034. Headcount is not the only population. 469 boarding students, a regional Bryan crowd, a Berliner on the faculty after the move: those are populations a census cell will not hold.

Teske’s long table still has to be read past 1920: 627 in 1930, then the mid-century lifts, then 395 in 2020. The Panic does not explain 1930. Cut out and get out explains 1930. The Panic explains why the rehearsal hall emptied before the payroll did. For thirteen years after 1907 the mill kept the town’s numbers climbing. The head had already lost a college. The weekday would later lose a high school to Drew Central. The pattern is Monticello’s gravity. 1907 is the year the gravity became lumber on a wagon.

## What the record will not say

It will not give a list of the buildings that moved. It will not give a wagon count, a carpenter’s name, or a date inside 1907 more precise than Edwards’s crisis and the souvenir’s December 17 present tense. It will not quote William Tyler Spence’s 1958 article. It will not invent a quarrel between Wilmar and the square. It will not say the Massey high school was a college. Two hundred in round numbers is a booster’s hedge, not a degree-granting enrollment.

The record will not print the 1909 bid in this essay. Heady does not print it. Board of Trade minutes are a later pass. The record will not say the Panic killed Gates. The mill ran until 1924. The W&SVR still had its inventory in the souvenir year: twenty-five miles, four locomotives, fifty-five cars, two loaders. Credit seized. The saw did not immediately seize. A college that lives on household surplus seizes sooner than a plant that lives on northern buyers — and even the plant would go when the woods were gone.

No shop CTA belongs on a foundation line. The grass is not a brand. The haul is not a delivery. A later workshop that takes the river’s name works in a county that once moved a campus eight miles and called the remainder a line. That sentence is geography, not a close.
