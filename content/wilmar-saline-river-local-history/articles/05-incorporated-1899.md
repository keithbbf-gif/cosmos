# Incorporated, March 8, 1899

March 8, 1899, is a Tuesday in the old calendars, if anyone cares to check. Steven Teske cares enough to give the day. The city of Wilmar incorporated. The sentence is short because the act was short. A group of men — the petition is not in this folder — asked the state to recognize a mill stop as a municipal person. After that, Wilmar could pass ordinances, or fail to. It could levy, or fail to. It could put its name on a letterhead that was not a lumber company’s.

The census of 1900, the first that treats the new city as a row, counted 844 people. That is not a camp. It is also not Monticello. It is the size of a place that has a depot, a mill, a school about to call itself a college, and enough churches to argue about.

The previous essay in this series stayed with the blades: 1881, 1884, 1887, 1890, 1898. This one stays with the filing. The two dates that sit on either side of March 8 are a stave mill a year old and a census a year young. Between them a legal person appeared. The person did not invent the work. The work invented a reason to file.

## What was already there

A Methodist church had organized in 1893. Teske records it the way he records the Presbyterian building of 1861: a date, a denomination, no sermon. John Jefferson Lee Spence had opened the Drew Normal Institute in 1897. Gates Lumber Company had been on the ground since 1890 if Teske’s year is the year, 1889 if Mary Heady’s county article is preferred. Anderson’s post office was fifteen years old. The stave mill was a year old. James Thomas Dionysius Anderson had bought the town site in 1859, seven hundred acres at a dollar an acre, and had cleared five of them for corn before anyone filed a city. Incorporation, in that company, looks like a man putting on a coat after the weather has already changed.

The 1907 *Advance*, writing eight years later, still treats “the real history of Wilmar” as the advent of Gates. The booster is not interested in charters. It is interested in payroll. A heritage series can afford to be interested in both. A city that incorporates is saying, at least on paper, that the payroll will not be the only government.

Whether that was true in practice is a different film. Company towns in south Arkansas — Crossett is the textbook — owned the houses. Wilmar, on the evidence of the 1907 inventory, was not Crossett. It had five general stores, not one commissary, though Kidd Bros. would function as Gates’s commissary and would be “one of the largest” mercantile concerns in the county. It had a bank with $25,000 capital. It had a Farmer’s Union warehouse, the only one in the county that year, rated at a thousand bales. Those are civic facts pretending to be commercial ones. Farmers who incorporate a warehouse are not waiting for the mill to define the town.

The same souvenir called Wilmar “the town of second importance in Drew County,” a thousand or twelve hundred people. The 1910 census would say 929. The booster was high. Second importance is a snub and a compliment in one. First is the county seat: compact brick, waterworks, a cotton mill, three thousand people in the paper’s figure. Second is the mill stop, eight miles west on the Warren Branch. The paper is writing from the square. It still needs Wilmar to look busy, because a county souvenir that only sells Monticello is a thinner souvenir. The 1899 charter is what made “second” a municipal fact instead of a booster’s ranking.

## A legal person in a county of older persons

Drew County was fifty-three years old in 1899. It had been cut from Bradley and Arkansas counties on November 26, 1846, and named for Governor Thomas Stevenson Drew. Monticello had been a town since 1852; Fountain and Polly Austin had given eighty-three acres for it in 1849. Warren, west on the same Iron Mountain branch, was already a lumber seat. Wilmar’s late charter is not an insult. It is a pattern. Railroad mill towns often live for a decade as places before they live as cities. Anderson’s 1881 saw and 1884 post office are the decade-plus. The interesting question is not why they incorporate. It is what they do with the charter when the mill leaves.

Wilmar’s later census is the answer, and it is not kind. Teske’s table, printed in the Encyclopedia of Arkansas, is the town’s longest film: 844 in 1900, 929 in 1910, 1,034 in 1920, 627 in 1930, then 695, 746, 718, 653, 747, 637, 571, 511, and 395 in 2020. The legal person persisted. The population did not. That is small-town America’s least romantic plot, and it is the plot this series will not talk around. Incorporation is not a guarantee. It is a filing cabinet.

The drop from 1,034 to 627 is the mill’s shadow, and it belongs to the cut-out essays. The 1899 filing belongs here because it is the instrument that survived the shadow. A city that can still elect a mayor in a town of 395 is a city whose charter outlasted the reason the charter was wanted. Teske’s modern sentence — Wilmar is now mostly a suburb of Monticello — is the twenty-first-century update of the 1907 paper’s “second importance.” *Mostly* is the word that saves the sentence. A charter is not suburban. June on the Colored School ground is not suburban. The weekday is.

## Churches, schools, and the other governments

Teske’s 2013 snapshot — Baptist, Missionary Baptist, AME, United Methodist, Israel of God; the Presbyterian church gone in 1974 with eight members — is a later still. In 1899 the Methodist organization was six years old and the Presbyterian building was thirty-eight. Black congregations, which the 1900 census would not have to invent, are under-named in the origin paragraph. The June Dinner’s ground at the Wilmar Colored School is the corrective, and it is an older institution than the charter if the oral history is right to put the dinner in the late nineteenth century. Oral history is oral history. Say so. Arkansas PBS recorded Mayor Toni Perry and others in 2023–2024. This draft will not promote a table into a municipal ordinance.

Spence’s school would be chartered as Beauvoir College in 1903, “with the privilege of conferring degrees.” For four years a city younger than a child could say it was a college town. Rex Nelson, writing in 2026, used that fact as a surprise. It is a surprise only if you think mill towns cannot also want Latin and elocution. They can. They often want them in the same decade they want a stave mill. The charter of the city and the charter of the college are cousins. Both are attempts to make a camp look inevitable.

The 1907 paper, after the college had already followed Spence back to Monticello, still treated the school and the mill as the two institutions that “have been responsible for the existence of Wilmar as a town.” The paper’s past tense is doing mourning and advertising at once. It then pivots to Wilmar High School under W. B. Massey: eleven grades, nine months, enrollment “in round numbers” 200. That high school is a consolation the booster needed. It is also evidence that the 1899 city intended to govern more than a whistle. A municipal person that can host a high school, a bank, and a Farmer’s Union warehouse is a person that has already outgrown the camp.

Kate Stewart’s work on Associate Reformed Presbyterians in Drew County belongs more to Monticello’s ARP story and to Spence than to the 1899 docket. The 1861 Presbyterian building Teske mentions may or may not be that stream. A later pass should not collapse Presbyterian into ARP without a minute book. The useful 1899 fact is simpler: churches were already governments of a kind, older than the city, and they did not need a charter to collect a congregation.

## Eight miles west, which is a political geography

The *Advance*’s location sentence — about eight miles nearly due west of Monticello on the Warren Branch — is the charter’s map. The dividing ridge is not a metaphor until the essay has earned it. Monticello sat on the highest, driest boast of that ridge and had the courthouse. Wilmar sat toward the Saline bottoms and the pine. Bayou Bartholomew’s fat alluvium lay east of the seat; the Saline’s narrower, heavier-timbered bottoms lay southwest. A city that incorporates on the west slope is filing itself into the timber side of a county that still thought, in 1907, that agriculture was “the most important and stable of all industries.”

County seats win certain arguments by sitting where they sit. They have hotels, a square, men who know how to write a subscription list. Wilmar, in 1899, had a mill, a school two years old, a Methodist organization, and a post office named for a daughter. The charter did not move the courthouse. It said the west slope was a place that intended to remain after the next car of boards left.

Heady’s county article will later record Act 100 of 1909 and the agricultural school that Monticello won. That is a later capture. The 1899 filing is the mill town’s attempt to have a government of its own before the captures begin. It worked, on paper. The legal person is still there. The weekday traffic is not.

## What the record will not say

The petition is not in this folder. The names of the incorporators are not in Teske. The first ordinances — if they survive — have not been walked into this pack. We do not have a mayor’s name for 1899. We do not have a picnic. We do not have a photograph of a filing stamped that Tuesday. DeArmond and Reaves may hold a paragraph or a clipping. Until they are read, the honest caption is Teske’s date and the 1900 count.

The record will also not say that incorporation made the town equal to the mill. Kidd Bros. as commissary, the brothers as stockholders in Gates and in the Bank of Wilmar, is the 1907 evidence that payroll still governed a good deal of the week. The Farmer’s Union warehouse is the evidence that it did not govern all of it. Both buildings postdate the charter. The charter made them possible as civic facts. It did not choose between them.

A Ken Burns hour would film a docket, if the courthouse kept it. The voice-over is Teske’s date, then the 1900 count, then the list of what already existed: church, school, mill, stave mill, post office, Warren Branch. No fife. The temptation is to treat March 8 as a birthday. Birthdays are for people. Cities have filing dates. Wilmar’s is late, honest, and smaller than the whistle. That is enough.
