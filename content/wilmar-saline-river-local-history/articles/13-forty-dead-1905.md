# Forty Dead

Paul Edwards’s sentence is almost unbearable in its balance. In 1905 a smallpox epidemic claimed forty lives in Wilmar, including those of one Beauvoir student and Spence’s five-year-old son. Then he moves on to 1907 and the Panic, because a college history has to. A local-history series that is trying to speak in a human voice has to stop.

Forty, in a city the 1900 census put at 844, is not a statistic you soften. It is one in twenty-one, if the population had not grown, and the town was growing. The 1910 count would be 929. The 1907 paper would boast a thousand or twelve hundred. Even on Teske’s cooler numbers, forty is a wound a town that size does not hide. A boarding school in the middle of an epidemic is a particular kind of fear. Students from across southeast Arkansas, paying in crops and promises, sharing halls and a well. One of them died. The president’s son died. The encyclopedia does not give the child a name. William Tyler Spence’s 1958 article may. This draft has not used that article as a quarry for a first name it cannot verify in hand. The boy is five. That is enough to ruin a year and a campus.

The student is unnamed too. The other thirty-eight are unnamed. Forty is a number that can become a chant if a writer is careless. This essay will say it, then sit with the machines that carry a pox, then sit with what the record will not give. It will not become a medical textbook. It will not become a shop’s lesson.

## What smallpox was, in a mill town

This essay will not become a medical encyclopedia. Smallpox, in 1905, was a known terror with a known vaccine that was not evenly had. A timber town with a boarding college, a commissary, and a railroad interchange is a machine for transmission. The Iron Mountain did not only carry boards. It carried people. The Warren Branch, about eight miles west of Monticello, was the town’s location sentence in the 1907 paper and its contact with every other stop on the way to Warren. The Wilmar and Saline Valley Railroad, chartered in 1904, carried crews into the pine: twenty-five miles, four locomotives, fifty-five log cars, two loaders, interchange at the Gates sawmill. Crews sleep. Crews share water. A company road is a public-health fact even when the charter is only about logs.

The June Dinner ground, if the dinner was already annual — oral history, Arkansas PBS, Mayor Toni Perry, late nineteenth century if the speakers are right — carried families. We do not have a public-health report in this folder that names the dinner as a vector. We do not have a report that clears it. We have Edwards’s forty and Teske’s “smallpox epidemic in 1905.” Oral history is oral history. Say so. Do not conscript a table into a plague narrative it does not claim.

Quarantine, pesthouses, vaccination orders — Arkansas towns used all of them, unevenly. To invent a pesthouse on Anderson’s land would be a lie. To invent a vaccination ordinance for March 8, 1899’s city would be a lie. The municipal person was six years old. Whether it had a board of health is a courthouse question this pack has not asked. To say the town was marked is not a lie. Forty dead is a mark.

The artesian well A. H. Gates gave the campus — 300,000 gallons of capacity, in Edwards — is the named water. Water for a boarding school is kitchens, laundry, livestock, and, in a pox year, a shared fact. The catalogs at Taylor Library might describe the well’s use. This draft has not opened them. The well is here because a campus that takes livestock and boarders is a campus that shares vessels. The encyclopedia does not say the well spread the disease. This essay will not say it either. It will say the well existed, and that shared water is how towns work.

Geography does not exempt anyone. The dividing ridge is not a quarantine. Bartholomew bottoms to the east, Saline bottoms to the southwest, upland farms between: people crossed those parts to market cotton at Wilmar, Collins, Tillar, and the Monticello compress. A Farmer’s Union warehouse would open in 1907, a thousand bales. In 1905 the cotton still moved. So did the stave factory’s oak, if the 1898 mill was already the 10,000-a-day machine the souvenir later listed. Freight is contact. A mill town is a contact machine that also happens to cut pine.

## A president who stayed, then left

Spence did not close the college in 1905. Enrollment, Edwards says, reached its apex in 1906–07: 469. The *Advance* says between 400 and 500. Bryan came in 1906 and filled a five-hundred-seat auditorium with a regional crowd. The school kept its doors open in the grief. That is either duty or numbness or the simple fact that a campus with livestock for tuition cannot pause without starving. The Panic closed what the pox did not.

When Spence went to Monticello, the 1907 paper treated it as a capture: the city had “prevailed upon” him to return and run its schools. Beauvoir was “abandoned on the removal.” Wilmar High School under W. B. Massey — eleven grades, nine months, two hundred students in round numbers — was the consolation. Otto Mahling taught violin and cornet. The paper does not mention the son. Boosters do not mention sons. A Ken Burns hour would mention the son first, then the forty, then the 469, and let the contradiction sit. A college can peak after a plague. People are stubborn. They are also tired.

Teske pairs 1905 and 1907 as the end of the school. He is right about the pairing. He is writing a town article, not a grief article. The two blows are different species of ruin. Together they are enough. This essay’s job is the first blow. The next essay’s job is the second. The pairing is not a reason to rush the child.

Judge I. A. Bird and Major J. T. D. Anderson had donated the campus land. Anderson had opened the post office in 1884 and named it for Willie Elvira. The Methodist church had organized in 1893. The city had incorporated on March 8, 1899. Gates had been cutting since 1889 or 1890. None of those dates is a consolation. They are the town the pox entered. A legal person can levy and fail to. It cannot vote a virus down.

Spence had come to Monticello in 1892 intending to teach a few years and read medicine. Edwards records the change of mind. The medical intention sits beside 1905 with a cruelty the encyclopedia does not underline. A man who once meant to be a physician buried a five-year-old in a mill town and then kept a college open. This draft will not invent his thoughts. It will not invent a grave. If a stone exists and a family allows it, that is a later still. If not, Edwards’s sentence is the stone.

## How not to film this

No reconstructed sickroom. No generated child. No invented first name lifted from a guess at William Tyler Spence’s pages. If a gravestone exists and a family allows it, that is the still. If not, the still is Edwards’s sentence on a black card, which is how Burns sometimes does the thing that cannot be shown.

IMAGE_SOURCES forbids a generated period photograph. The rule is stricter here. Do not draw a child. Do not draw a pesthouse. Do not caption a stock “old hospital” as Wilmar 1905. The pack’s timeline graphic can hold a tick. A tick is not a face.

This essay will not soft-link a shop. The style guide is plain: not on epidemic. Not on lynching, the Klan, or sacred community tables. Forty dead is not a reason a furniture lane exists. Boards left this ridge before 1905 and after. The dead are not a preface to hardwood.

It will not close on resilience. Forty dead is the close. The college’s later foundation lines are a different grief. The census of 1920, 1,034, is a different still. The 1930 drop to 627 is the mill’s method, not the pox. Do not stack every subtraction into one hymn. This subtraction has a number and a missing name.

## What the record will not say

It will not name the child. It will not name the student. It will not name the other thirty-eight. It will not give a month inside 1905. It will not give a public-health ordinance, a doctor’s ledger, a list of vaccinated households, or a map of houses marked. DeArmond, Reaves, and the ordinary issues of the *Advance* — not the December 17 souvenir — may hold obituaries. The souvenir is a booster dated two years later. It does not linger on the dead. That refusal is part of the record.

The record will not say the dinner spread the pox. It will not say the W&SVR spread it. It will not say the well spread it. Transmission is a machine we can describe without a verdict the folder does not contain. The record will not say Spence closed a door in 1905. The 469 and Bryan’s night say the opposite.

Coleman’s “A School Tale” (1989) is a bibliography lead. Stewart’s ARP pieces are neighbors. None of them has been used here as a quarry for a name. Until a first name can be pointed to on a page in hand, the boy is five. The student is a student. The town lost forty. That is the film.
