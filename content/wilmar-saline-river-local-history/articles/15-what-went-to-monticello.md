# What Went to Monticello

In 1909 the Arkansas General Assembly passed Act 100, creating a state agricultural school in each of the state’s four quadrants. The southeast campus went to Monticello after a bidding process. The school’s mission, Mary Heady writes, was to teach the latest in agricultural and domestic science to the young men and women of the region. By the 1930s it had become Arkansas A&M. In 1971 it merged into the University of Arkansas system and took the name University of Arkansas at Monticello. Teske says many of Beauvoir’s resources were transferred to that lineage. Edwards says Spence himself worked there after Hinemon.

The through-line is almost too clean for a careful series, which is why it must be said with the dirt still on it. Wilmar invented a private answer to a public problem. The panic killed the private answer. The state built a public one and put it eight miles east, on the ridge’s highest, driest boast. UAM’s School of Forest Resources is now the only such school in Arkansas. The trees that Gates took without replenishing became, two generations later, a curriculum in the county seat.

This essay is not a campus history. It is the Wilmar end of a transfer: buildings, a president, a need, and later the weekday the town’s children ride east. The seat’s own encyclopedia article is a growth story. Wilmar’s is a subtraction story that ends in Monticello’s orbit. Read together, they are one county.

## Bidding, which is a kind of politics

Heady does not print the bid. A later pass through legislative journals and Monticello’s Board of Trade minutes — Dr. W. A. Brown was president of that board in 1907 — would thicken this. What can be said now is that county seats win campuses. They have hotels, churches of five denominations, a square, a railroad, and men who know how to write a subscription list. Wilmar had a mill and foundation lines. The 1907 paper, promoting Monticello, had already called for more manufacturing and a north-south road — the Arkansas, Louisiana and Gulf, surveyed Pine Bluff to Monroe, grading toward Hamburg. It had already captured Spence. The agricultural school is the next capture.

Jonesboro, Russellville, and Magnolia got the other three. The pattern is statewide, not a slight invented for Drew. Still, for a person standing on Beauvoir’s grass, the slight is local.

The 1907 souvenir had already ranked the towns: Monticello first, Wilmar “the town of second importance,” a thousand or twelve hundred people in the booster’s figure, 929 in the 1910 census. First had a cotton mill, a compress that took 12,264 bales, waterworks, compact brick. Second had Gates, a stave factory at 10,000 a day, a bank at $25,000, a Farmer’s Union warehouse, and a college already “abandoned on the removal.” A bidding process in 1909 is a formal version of a ranking the paper printed two years earlier. The west slope had spent its private money. The square still had a list.

Geography is not innocent in a bid. Monticello sat on the highest part of the dividing ridge. Wilmar sat about eight miles nearly due west on the Warren Branch, toward the Saline bottoms — about 8,000 acres in the 1907 figure, heavily timbered, poorer for the plow. Bartholomew bottoms, about 100,000 acres, lay east of the seat, cotton country. A state agricultural school wants the county’s farm argument and the county’s hotel. It does not want a mill town’s foundation lines, even if those lines are where the need was first named. Drew Normal opened in 1897. The city of Wilmar incorporated in 1899. Beauvoir was chartered in 1903. Act 100 is 1909. The rehearsal is older than the statute.

Spence’s path is the human transfer. University of Mississippi, ARP, Monticello in 1892 with a plan to read medicine, Wilmar in 1897 with land from Judge I. A. Bird and Major J. T. D. Anderson and a well from A. H. Gates, Beauvoir in 1903, smallpox in 1905, Bryan in 1906, Panic and removal in 1907, city schools, Hinemon, then the Fourth District school. Teske’s “many of Beauvoir’s resources” is broader than a man. Edwards’s half the structures is lumber. The catalogs, if they moved, are paper. The foundation lines are what did not move. A later pass through Taylor Library may say which books sit on which shelf. This draft will not invent an accession number.

## Integration, branches, and the long campus

Arkansas A&M was integrated in 1964. Drew High School, Heady notes, waited until 1970. In 2003 UAM added technology campuses at Crossett and McGehee. The university’s reach now includes the town Cap Gates built and a Delta town on another rail. Wilmar’s children, Teske writes, attend Drew County Central. The college that was in their town is a system with a forest school and a name that does not mention Wilmar.

Heady gives the district’s ancestor dates. Drew Central School was established in 1927 as a training school for teachers. Two years later the rural school districts of Drew County were consolidated with it. In 1935 the school separated from the college and kept the name Drew Central. That is a state-and-campus story. Wilmar High School, in 1907, had been a local boast: eleven grades, nine months, two hundred students in round numbers, Massey, Mahling, expression, a boarding department. Consolidation is what happens when two hundred becomes a number a county will not fund in place. The particular Wilmar wound is that the town had already lost a college to Monticello and then lost the high school to a district named for the college’s successor.

Charlie May Simon, born on a farm between Tillar and Monticello in 1897 — Beauvoir’s first year — became the children’s author the state named a book award for. William Shea has taught Civil War history at UAM since 1974 and wrote, with William Droessler, the 1915 baseball essay this series will use. The campus produced historians of the county that lost the campus’s rehearsal. That is a mild irony. It is also how records survive.

Black Drew County’s other school fact sits beside the 1964 date. Monticello Academy, opened in 1888 by Charles Mebane of Holmes Chapel Presbyterian, with help from Mary Emilie Holmes and the Presbyterian Board of Missions for Freedmen, lasted until 1933. Beauvoir’s catalog had said “for Whites.” The public campus that inherited Beauvoir’s resources inherited a county that had run two higher-schooling stories on a color line. Integration in 1964 is late. Drew High in 1970 is later. Teske’s 2010 Wilmar line — 511 people, 364 African American — is the town that now sends its children into a district the seat named. The June Dinner’s ground at the Wilmar Colored School is the institution the weekday does not replace. Oral history; say so.

## Forest resources, the curriculum that came after the cut

A furniture lane that cares about woods has to say this once. The only forest-resources school in Arkansas sits in the county that clear-cut its first large mill’s woods and then learned, through Crossett and Pomeroy and a state campus, to talk about yield. Students now walk a curriculum Gates’s 1890 plant did not have — 1889, if Heady’s large-scale date is preferred. Wilmar’s contribution to that curriculum is negative space: a method that failed the town, a college that failed the panic, a ridge that still grows pine and oak.

Cap Gates walked south, built a company town, and in 1926 telephoned H. H. Chapman at Yale. W. K. Williams came. Crossett learned sustained yield. L. K. Pomeroy and Eugene P. Connor opened Ozark Badger in Drew in 1925 and pioneered selective cutting in the 1930s. The Wilmar mill had closed in 1924. Lands sold to Crossett Lumber Company the same year, in Heady. Equipment later, in Teske. The forest school is the public, later, county-seat version of a lesson the first mill town paid for in denuded woods and a census drop from 1,034 to 627. The lesson does not mention Wilmar in its name. The foundation lines are why the knowledge had to live somewhere.

Heady’s modern county geography is the campus’s map: West Gulf Coastal Plain, Bayou Bartholomew on the east, Saline on the southwest, Monticello Ridge from north of Star City into Louisiana. UAM sits on the ridge’s boast. Crossett’s 2003 technology campus sits in the town the brother built. McGehee sits on other water. Wilmar sits eight miles west of the main campus and sends a bus. Teske’s present-tense “mostly a suburb of Monticello” is the weekday version of Act 100. *Mostly*. The charter of March 8, 1899, is not suburban. The Colored School ground is not. The weekday is.

## What the record will not say

It will not print the 1909 bid. It will not give dollar figures, acre donations, or the names on the subscription list beyond Brown’s 1907 Board of Trade title. Legislative journals are a later pass. It will not list the Beauvoir resources Teske says were transferred — which books, which instruments, which chairs. It will not quote Spence 1958 on the haul. It will not invent a dedication speech for the Fourth District school.

The record will not say Wilmar was offered the 1909 campus and declined. The pattern of seats winning campuses is the evidence we have, not a rejected petition in this folder. The record will not say Drew Central’s 1927 training-school origin was a raid designed in 1909. It was a later magnet. A person is allowed to feel the pattern without writing a lawsuit.

No shop CTA belongs here. The campus is not a vendor. The sentence is only: the knowledge lives eight miles east of the foundation lines, and the foundation lines are why the knowledge had to live somewhere. Hardwood still leaves the ridge. The virgin pine does not. The school that talks about yield sits on the square’s side of the eight miles. The rehearsal sat on the mill’s. Both facts are the transfer. Neither is a catalog.
