# The Commissary

The 1907 *Advance*, in its biographical back pages, calls Kidd Bros. Company the largest mercantile concern in Wilmar and one of the largest in the county. It acted as commissary for the Gates Lumber Company and so took “the bulk of the trade of the small army of employees belonging to that concern.” The brothers were stockholders in the mill and in the Bank of Wilmar. B. P. Kidd was president of both those institutions. R. A. Kidd was vice-president and manager of the store, and a director of the mill and the bank. That is not a Main Street fable. That is how a mill town’s middle grew: one counter, two charters, a payroll walking in the door.

A commissary is not a general store in the civic sense. It is a company-adjacent counter. Credit, the temptation to own a worker’s grocery as well as a worker’s day, the word *scrip* that other southern mill towns have documented in ledgers this pack does not have. This draft will not invent a flour price or a brass token. It has the paper’s word *commissary* and the brothers’ stock in the mill. A reader who has read other southern mill towns can fill the risk without this essay fabricating a ticket.

Strange to say, the souvenir says, while the business was established when the Gates plant was built, for several years it did not have a very successful career, changing hands frequently, until it came into the present management. Under that regime it became, the paper claims, one of the most successful and best-paying institutions of the kind in the country. Eleven years in charge by December 1907. Floor space increased from 1,200 to near 5,000 feet, not including a large warehouse and an icehouse. Stock that would invoice $25,000 or more. Annual sales about $100,000. Treat the adjectives as advertising. Treat the sequence as the document: a company store that failed in other hands, then thickened in the Kidds’. Even a booster will admit a thin start if the thin start makes the present look like a rescue.

## Five general stores and one commissary

The same paper’s civic list gave Wilmar five general merchandise stores, one exclusive dry-goods shop, one drugstore, a livery, a blacksmith, a stave factory at 10,000 a day, a bank at $25,000 capital, and a Farmer’s Union warehouse of 1,000 bales, the only one in the county. Kidd Bros. is the named giant among the stores. The other four general merchants are not named in the Wilmar inventory. This essay will not invent their signs. Five plus a dry-goods specialist plus a drugstore is already a street. One of those counters held the mill’s grocery. That is the political economy.

Between the commissary and the warehouse sits 1907 in two buildings. Company provisioning and cooperative marketing, under the same water tower, in a town the paper would not choose between 1,000 and 1,200 people. Teske’s 1910 census is 929. Gates had been in Wilmar since 1890. The Wilmar and Saline Valley Railroad, chartered 1904, owned twenty-five miles of track, four locomotives, fifty-five log cars, and two log loaders by the year of the souvenir. A small army of employees is the paper’s phrase for the men those machines required. Their trade, the paper says, went in bulk to Kidd. The warehouse was for cotton. The commissary was for everything else a payroll could be asked to buy.

Dr. S. Harris, in the same biographical section, owned the only drugstore, having a few weeks earlier bought the stock of I. A. Bird & Son, consolidated it with his own, and moved to the Bird stand. Born in DeSoto County, Mississippi. Literary education at Lebanon, Louisiana. Medical degree, Louisville, class of 1892. First Arkansas stop, Wesson, Union County, seven years; then Wilmar, where he remained. He had entered the drug business in March by buying a store, then bought out his only competitor. Drugs, jewelry, novelties, sundries, and, in a damaged line of the transcription, a cold drink stand. Baptist. Knights of Pythias, past chancellor. Mason. Married Lou Risinger of El Dorado on January 3, 1894. One boy, one girl. The paper likes men who stay. It likes stockholders. It likes a physician who also sells jewelry. It does not like, or does not print, the mill hand’s ticket.

Harris is not a Kidd. He is the other named counter: the only drugstore, recently monopolized, a profession and a till in one room. The commissary sold the grocery and the dry goods a mill army needed. Harris sold what a doctor and a soda fountain could add. Together they are the souvenir’s idea of a complete street. The five unnamed general stores are the idea’s competition, or its scenery.

## Native sons, Bonner, and the bank

Both B. P. and R. A. Kidd, the paper says, were proud to be native sons of Drew County, born and reared a few miles from Monticello. Their parents were R. A. and Mary (Bonner) Kidd. The souvenir does not give birth years. It does not need to. Native is the credential that answers Mahling’s Berlin. The musician was imported. The merchants were local. Both kinds of man get a sketch. The mill hands do not.

Stockholders in the store: B. P. Kidd, R. A. Kidd, Thomas Stewart, E. L. Biggs. Officers: B. P. president; R. A. vice-president and manager. In addition to the mercantile business they held stock in Gates Lumber Company and the Bank of Wilmar. The senior brother was president of those two institutions; the junior was a director of both. The sentence is a wiring diagram. A man could draw wages at the mill, cash or credit them at the commissary, and put what remained, if anything remained, in a bank whose president sold him his flour. Other southern mill towns have written that loop as a closed system. This pack has the loop’s officers and not its ledgers. Do not invent the ledgers.

$100,000 in annual sales is a booster’s summit. $25,000 in stock on hand is another. 5,000 feet of floor is a third. The icehouse and the warehouse — not the Farmer’s Union warehouse, a company warehouse — are the physical claim that the store had outgrown a counter. Eleven years back from December 1907 is 1896, if the arithmetic is allowed. Gates’s plant, Teske says, was established in 1890. The paper’s “several years” of unsuccessful hands sit between those dates. Changing frequently: the souvenir will not name the previous owners. A later pass through deeds might. Until then, the Kidds arrive as the ending the paper wanted.

They have established their reputation as No. 1 business men and are leading citizens of the town and county. That is the last paste. Leave it. The wiring diagram does more work.

## What *commissary* holds

The word is older than Gates. Army posts used it. Plantations used it. Mining camps used it. A lumber commissary is a cousin of those counters: the company, or a firm licensed by the company, sells to men who have few other places to go, especially if the town is new and the rail is a company road. Wilmar by 1907 was not only a camp. It had five other general stores, a dry-goods shop, a drugstore, a livery, a blacksmith, a high school, churches, a bank, a stave factory, a union cotton shed. The “small army” still, the paper says, gave Kidd the bulk. Bulk is the claim that matters. Competition existed. The mill’s trade, in the souvenir’s telling, did not use it.

Scrip, tokens, forced trade, a dollar that was not a dollar off the grounds — those are documented elsewhere in the southern mill belt. They are not documented in the Haisty transcription of this souvenir. A writer who puts a brass coin in Kidd’s drawer has left the folder. A writer who says the risk is the reason the word *commissary* is not a cute name for a later shop has stayed in the folder. The style guide’s ethics are not a ledger. They are a refusal to invent one.

Credit is in the building even without a token. Floor space at 5,000 feet and sales at $100,000, if either figure is near the truth, are not cash-only numbers in a town of a thousand. The paper does not say so. It does not have to. A commissary that “gets the bulk of the trade” of a mill army is already a credit institution. The Bank of Wilmar, $25,000 capital in the civic list, had the same senior Kidd as president. Two kinds of paper, one family.

## After Gates, which this pack does not date

When the mill closed, a commissary lost its reason or became an ordinary store. Teske, following Darling and Bragg, puts the transfer of Gates equipment to Crossett in the late 1920s after the Wilmar woods had significantly declined. Heady puts the land sale to Crossett Lumber Company in 1924 and the exhaustion in the mid-1920s. The census drops from 1,034 in 1920 to 627 in 1930. This pack does not have the store’s death date, a dissolution, or a later Main Street photograph that would let a writer say “that brick was Kidd.” The type died with the type of town. A later editor may find a directory. Until then, 1907 is the year, and *commissary* is the word.

No shop today should call itself a commissary for fun. The word has a history. The household workshop this series explains in one other essay is not a commissary. It has no mill army, no stock in a saw, no icehouse attached to a payroll. The distinction is the ethics, not a catalog note.

Anderson, the 1907 paper said, had once used a single house as store, post office, depot, and residence. Four functions, one roof. Kidd is the later, thicker version of that house: not four civic jobs, but one commercial job wired to the mill and the bank. The founder’s combined building invented the town’s first counter. The brothers’ department store — the paper uses *apartment* and a transcriber offers *department* — invented the town’s largest. Both stills belong in a mill series. Neither is a folk tale.

## How to film a counter

If this were a Burns hour, the still would be a floor plan, not a smiling clerk. 1,200 feet, then near 5,000, plus warehouse and icehouse. Then the civic list’s five unnamed stores. Then Harris’s only drugstore. Then the union shed for cotton. Then stop. The mill hand’s ticket, if it ever existed on paper, is not in this folder. Absence is not emptiness. It is a seam. The souvenir named the brothers, their parents, their partners Stewart and Biggs, their offices, their sales. It did not name the army. Teske’s later table named the army as a subtraction: four hundred people gone in a decade. Some of them had stood at this counter.
