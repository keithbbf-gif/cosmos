# Churches After the Mill

Steven Teske, describing Wilmar in 2013, lists congregations the way a county history lists them when the mills are gone: Baptist, Missionary Baptist, African Methodist Episcopal, United Methodist, Israel of God. In parentheses he records a death. The Presbyterian church disbanded in 1974, having only eight active members. The first public building Teske mentioned in the origin story, the Presbyterian church of 1861 near Anderson’s land, outlasted Gates by half a century and then failed for want of eight people willing to keep the door.

That is a small-town still more accurate than a skyline. Steeples persist. Memberships do not. A denomination that planted itself in a war year can die in a Ford year.

1861 is not a decoration. Arkansas had left the Union that spring. Drew County in 1860 counted 5,581 white residents and 3,497 people held in bondage. A church went up near a farm whose first corn had been tended by Simon and Lizza Taylor, Virginia and Wealthy Thurman, and Gill Gordon. Teske does not say the 1861 building was built for those five. He says it was built near Anderson’s land. The nearness is the fact. The theology of that nearness is a sermon this pack will not write.

## What the list is saying

Five names in 2013 are not a religious census. They are a map of who still had a key. Teske does not give membership counts for the living congregations. He gives a count only for the dead one. Eight is the number that made a historian reach for parentheses.

Baptist and Missionary Baptist, in this part of Arkansas, often mark a historical color line as well as a doctrine line. This draft will not assign Teske’s two Baptist names to two races without a minute book. It will say the double listing is how southern towns often recorded a split that was more than Calvin and the ordinances. AME is a Black Methodist body with a long southern career. United Methodist is the 1968 merger name over the 1893 Methodist organization Teske dates. Israel of God is a later, particular community; this draft will not lecture its theology from a distance. The list, taken together, is a town that remained more church than storefront.

1893, Methodist organization, sits one year after Henry Beavers and three years after William Beavers. 1893 is also after Gates’s arrival and before incorporation. A series that wants the Methodist date to be only a steeple has not read the adjacent essays. A series that wants the Methodist date to be a verdict on 1892 has gone past the sources. The date is a date.

Kate Stewart’s work on Associate Reformed Presbyterians in Drew County (1990) and her Beauvoir piece (1992) belong to Monticello’s ARP story as much as Wilmar’s. Spence was ARP. Edwards records it. The 1861 Presbyterian building Teske mentions may or may not be that stream. A later pass should not collapse Presbyterian into ARP without a minute book. The 1974 closure is the fact we have. Stewart’s “Little Benjamin Goes West” is the unconsulted lead for the ARP map. It sits in the *Drew County Historical Journal* 5.1. This desk has the citation, not the pages.

## Eight members

Eight is a number a documentary should say twice. It is a choir that cannot fill a pew. It is also stubbornness: someone kept the church open until eight felt impossible. 1974 is after the 1970 school integration date Heady prints for Drew High, after the 1970 census’s 653, before the 1980 uptick to 747. The Presbyterian death is not the town’s death. It is one institution’s admission.

1974 is also one year before Governor David Pryor decided against the Saline dam. A county can refuse a reservoir and close a church in the same stretch of calendar. Those are not the same politics. They are the same decade’s willingness to end a thing.

Black churches, which Teske’s 2013 list includes and does not count, are the institutions the June Dinner’s people already know. Parade History’s 2006 vendor list named Revival Center Church of God in Christ as a youth-department fundraiser on the school ground. That congregation is not in Teske’s 2013 five. The seam is useful. Teske’s list is 2013 and is not a directory of every plate that ever sold in June. A series that mourns only the Presbyterian eight has chosen its grief badly. Mourn the eight. Then say the AME door is also the story. Then say a COGIC youth table in 2006 is also the story.

Monticello Academy, 1888–1933, was a Black Presbyterian missionary school in the county seat: Charles Mebane, Holmes Chapel, Mary Emilie Holmes, the Board of Missions for Freedmen. Heady’s paragraph is Monticello’s. Wilmar’s 1861 Presbyterian building is Teske’s. Do not merge them to make a prettier Presbyterian map. The academy closed in 1933. The Wilmar Presbyterian congregation lasted until 1974. Those are two closures.

## After the whistle, the calendar is a bulletin

Mills organize time by shifts. Churches organize it by weeks. When the shift ends, the week remains. That is why Teske can still list congregations in a city of a few hundred. 2013 is between the 2010 count (511) and the 2020 count (395). The churches outlasted the mill by two generations and were still there when Nelson, in 2026, reached for *dying*. A columnist can use that verb. A bulletin, if someone is still printing one, cannot.

The 1907 *Advance* treated churches the way it treated banks: as proof the town was civilized enough for more capital. This draft will not recover that paper’s church inventory unless a later pass lifts it. Teske’s 1861, 1893, 1974, and 2013 list are the dates this essay will carry. DeArmond and Reaves may have building photographs and pastor lists. Unconsulted. Said so.

Heady’s county religious firsts include the first Arkansas chapter of the Woman’s Christian Temperance Union, Monticello, 1876. That is the seat’s reform story. Wilmar’s story, in Teske, is smaller: a war-year Presbyterian plant, a Methodist organization in the mill decade, a 2013 list, an eight-member death. Temperance and timber are allowed to share a county. They are not required to share a paragraph’s moral.

If a photographer comes, photograph the bulletin, not the sunset through the glass. The bulletin is the census the government does not take. Do not photograph a child as a piety emblem. Do not ask anyone to smile for 1861.

## What a brand may not do

The style guide is blunt: congregations are not a brand halo. They are working rooms. A heritage lane that turns AME and Missionary Baptist into “community partners” for a furniture door has left the pack. Soft-link is not allowed to wear a choir robe. This essay has no shop sentence.

The June table is not a denomination. It is a holiday that has used a school ground and, in 2006, at least one church youth department’s cooker. Essay 16 and essay 40 hold that table. This essay holds the weekday keys Teske could still name in 2013, and the Presbyterian door that would not open after eight.

The still is Teske’s parenthesis. Eight active members. 1974. Then the 1861 date. Then the 2013 list read slowly. Then stop. No TED-talk about faith in hard times. The hard time is the membership. The faith, if any, is whoever still has a key.
