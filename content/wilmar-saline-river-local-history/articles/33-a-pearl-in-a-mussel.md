# A Pearl in a Mussel

Jann Woodard allows herself a little glitter, and she has earned it. In the 1890s pearl fishing became fashionable after Jim Davidson found a pearl in a mussel near Benton, a specimen considered the largest of its kind. Prices ranged from $30 to $250. The sport spread to all sections of the river. One of the most proficient hunters in the state, she writes, was Professor J. H. Hinemon, who taught at the Agricultural College in Monticello — the ancestor of UAM.

Hinemon is already in this pack’s Monticello school story. Here he is in waders, or whatever one wore to take mussels from the Saline. A college man on the river for a pearl is the opposite of a mill man on the river for a log, and the same river.

The 1890s are also Wilmar’s naming decade. Anderson opened the post office in 1884. Competitors built sawmills in 1887 and 1890. Gates, in Teske’s date, established in 1890; in Heady’s county date, the first large-scale mill is 1889. A stave mill arrived in 1898. The city incorporated on March 8, 1899. While a professor from the county seat opened mussels upstream, a timber town on the Warren Branch was learning to be a city. Fashion and extraction shared a calendar. They did not share a press agent. The *Advance* of 1907 would sell peaches and pine harder than pearls. Woodard kept the pearls anyway.

Shipping on the Saline, she writes in another paragraph, slacked after the rail system arrived in 1880. The Hampton brothers and John N. Wheeler were still the primary shippers into the early 1900s. Pearl fishing, in her telling, is a 1890s fashion on a river that had already lost the main argument to the Iron Mountain. A man could hunt a mussel and still watch a cotton boat, or a stave boat, wait on a rise. The two economies are not a conflict. They are a decade.

## Fashion, which is an economy

$30 to $250 is a farm month or a farm year, depending on the pearl and the year. Fashion is not the opposite of industry. It is a small industry with better press. All sections of the river means the sport did not stay in Saline County. Drew’s reach of the water, Bradley’s, Ashley’s — someone opened mussels there too. This draft has no Drew County pearl named. It has Woodard’s “all sections.”

The mussel beds paid a price. Freshwater pearl booms in American rivers are often a story of beds worked out. Woodard does not give a collapse date. She gives a fashion. A later environmental history should be written by someone with malacology. This essay will not fake one. It will not invent a tonnage of shells or a button factory on this water to look industrious. Buttons were a Mississippi Valley business in those years; they are not, in Woodard’s paragraph, a Saline fact. Leave them off the page.

Benton is the upper river’s town, the forks’ neighbor, the place that would later take the 1100s dugout on loan. Davidson’s mussel is a Benton-reach still. Hinemon’s hunting is a Monticello still that Woodard has walked downstream in a single sentence. The Saline is long enough for both. Two hundred and four miles, a watershed of about 3,350 square miles: a fashion can travel that without a railroad, though the railroad helped the news. The Iron Mountain’s Warren Branch already crossed Drew. A professor could get to a bend and back and still meet a class, if the class existed in the form Woodard names.

## Hinemon as a hinge — and a seam

The same surname sits on a white high school, a political ambition, and a pearl sack. That is small-town America’s overlap: the man who teaches also hunts, the man who hunts also runs for office. The 1907 *Advance* wanted him as an educator. Woodard wants him as a hunter. Both can be true. Neither makes the river a jewel box.

The seam should stay visible. Woodard says Professor J. H. Hinemon taught at the Agricultural College in Monticello, now the University of Arkansas at Monticello. Heady’s Drew County article dates the Fourth District State Agricultural School — the ancestor of Arkansas A&M and then UAM — to 1909, two years after Beauvoir closed and a generation after the pearl fashion Woodard puts in the 1890s. The school that does carry his name in the 1890s is Hinemon University School, a white preparatory boarding school established in Monticello in 1890 by the Monticello Educational Society. Heady says the Drew County school board acquired it in 1910 and converted it into Monticello High School. Teske’s Wilmar article, in a later clause Edwards also uses, has Spence working at the agricultural school after Hinemon. The encyclopedia house does not, in the pages this pack has open, reconcile the hunter with a 1909 campus that did not yet exist when Davidson opened the mussel.

John H. Hinemon appears in the 1907 *Advance* among the 1892 charter names of Monticello Lodge No. 1760, Knights and Ladies of Honor. That is a lodge roll, not a river roll. It puts the man in the county seat’s paper while he was still a name people printed. The souvenir also wanted him in the educational biography the earlier school essays in this series have already used as booster copy. A later pass through the Hinemon University School entry and through state-superintendent lists can thicken the résumé. This draft will not invent a year he stood in the Saline. Woodard’s “proficient” is the word we have. The campus ancestry is the word that needs a footnote.

Wilmar, in the 1890s, was naming itself, milling, incorporating. Its people may have gone to the Saline with a knife. Teske does not say. The honest link is only Hinemon and Monticello, eight miles from Wilmar, the educational town on the ridge’s highest boast, the town that would later take the agricultural school Wilmar had rehearsed as Beauvoir.

## What a pearl is not

A pearl is not a stave. It is not a bale. It is not a board-foot. It is a nacre accident that briefly paid like a crop. The Hampton brothers and John N. Wheeler were shipping cotton, staves, and timber down this river until the early 1900s. The Slavonian families were cutting the last barrel stock. Gates was building a mill town that would charter a logging railroad in 1904. Davidson’s mussel and Hinemon’s sack sit in that decade as a side economy, loud for a while, thin in the county’s later memory. The 1907 paper, which could not stop selling cotton and peaches, did not need a pearl paragraph to make Drew look busy. Woodard did, because a river article that is only mills and battles has not met the water’s fads.

Do not hang a pearl on a furniture tag. Do not write “hidden gem,” which this house has banned for more than one reason. The mussel is an animal. The price is a range. The professor is a seam. The river, Woodard says, remains relatively unspoiled in the twenty-first century and is used mostly for fishing and recreation. Pearl fishing is a closed fashion inside that longer sentence. Recreation is what replaced it. Commerce, she writes, had diminished by the 1930s. The beds’ later life — rest, ruin, or a biologist’s survey — is not in this file.

A shop named for the river is not a pearl shop. This essay does not owe that sentence a close. The names are Davidson, Hinemon, Benton, Monticello, $30 to $250. That is the still. A generated nacre close-up is a catalog. A card with the price range is a documentary.
