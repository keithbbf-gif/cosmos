# Gates Comes to Wilmar

There is a one-year seam in the record, and it should stay visible. Mary Heady’s Drew County article says the first large-scale sawmill in the county was established by the Gates Lumber Company in 1889. Steven Teske’s Wilmar article says Gates was established in Wilmar in 1890. The 1907 *Advance*, writing as if everyone still remembered, says the company’s advent was “17 years ago,” which, from December 1907, is 1890. A heritage series that irons the seam into a single year is doing the mill’s work, not the historian’s.

What is not in dispute is the type of arrival. This was not Anderson’s 1881 neighborhood saw. This was northern lumber capital of the kind Balogh describes: Iowa and Illinois men, yellow pine, a plant large enough to need its own railroad a few years later. Charles Warner Gates is the name that later sits on the Crossett Lumber Company board with Edward Savage Crossett and Dr. John Wenzel Watzek. Edgar Woodward “Cap” Gates, Charles’s brother, is the man the Crossett story sends south to build a town. Wilmar is the earlier Gates fact — a mill already running when Crossett was still a tract for sale at seven dollars an acre.

The 1887 competitor Teske names and does not name sits on the near side of this arrival. It may have been another neighborhood mill. It may have been an early Gates fact under another letterhead. Curry’s 1952 thesis and the *Advance*’s advertising columns are the unconsulted leads. This essay will not invent a firm to close the year. 1889 and 1890 are already enough work.

## What the 1907 paper thought it was looking at

“The real history of Wilmar, however, dates from the advent of the Gates Lumber Company.” The *Advance* is not shy. It has already conceded Anderson as first settler and landowner, Capt. J. T. D. Anderson, storekeeper, postmaster, depot agent, the four jobs in the house that “now serves as a depot.” Then it takes the story away from him. Boosters do this when a payroll has become the town’s vanity. In 1907 Wilmar had a thousand or twelve hundred people by the paper’s count, a college that had just moved its president to Monticello, a stave factory at 10,000 a day, and a mill that owned the conversation.

The paper also needed Anderson alive as a liberal host. “It was probably his liberal policy to a great extent that determined the Lumber company to locate its plant at this point.” Probably. The booster’s adverb is doing work. Gates came for pine and for a rail interchange on the Warren Branch, about eight miles west of Monticello, toward the Saline and the timber, not for a character reference. A man who owned practically all the land the town was built on could make the coming easy or expensive. Anderson, the *Advance* says, made it easy. That is a political fact as much as a commercial one. Towns on the Warren Branch did not all become mill seats. Wilmar did.

Teske, a century later, is cooler. Gates harvested. The Wilmar and Saline Valley Railroad, chartered in 1904, carried the harvest. By 1907 the company road had twenty-five miles of track, four locomotives, fifty-five log cars, and two log loaders. It interchanged with the Iron Mountain at the Gates sawmill. Those are inventory numbers, which is the only poetry a mill deserves.

Heady’s county paragraph is blunter still. The timber was clear-cut with no effort to replenish. It was exhausted by the mid-1920s. The lands were sold to Crossett Lumber Company in 1924. Darling and Bragg, in the *Arkansas Historical Quarterly* in 2008, note Gates’s use of the W&SVR to log virgin timber north of Crossett before the closure of the Wilmar mill in 1924, and the later transfer of logging and railroad equipment toward Crossett. Teske puts the equipment move in the late 1920s. Another seam. The direction is the same: out.

## Cut out and get out, with a town attached

Balogh’s phrase for the method is the one this series will keep using: cut out and get out. Buy virgin timber, build a mill, take the trees, leave a denuded woods, move the plant or the capital. The difference between a large operation and a small one was acreage and whether the mill stayed put while the rails walked into the woods. Gates in Wilmar was large enough to stay put and send track out. It was not, in the 1890s, in the sustained-yield religion that Cap Gates would later telephone Yale about from Crossett in 1926.

The 1907 paper is a tour in the present tense of a county that still believed it was inexhaustible. “The greater part of this timber is now controlled by the Gates Lumber Company, which has a large mill at Wilmar.” Pine in the Saline bottoms and the south. Hardwood everywhere, and small mills on it. Stave timber “for many years.” Tie timber by the thousands. The paper’s job was to invite capital. It did not invite a forester.

Yellow pine in south Arkansas grows faster than pine in the Ouachitas or the Ozarks, Balogh notes, because of sandy soil and heat. That speed is why the later conversion to sustained yield was possible at all. It is also why the first generation could believe the woods were a mine. A mine that grows back in twenty-five years is still a mine if you do not wait.

Wilmar’s population followed the method. 844 in 1900. 929 in 1910. 1,034 in 1920. 627 in 1930. The drop is the mill’s shadow on a census page. People did not become less real. The job did.

A reader who knows Crossett’s later fame — forestry capital, Yale camps, kraft paper — will want Wilmar to be a rehearsal. It is, in the family sense. It is also a different ending. Crossett became a company town that learned to grow trees as a crop. Wilmar became, in Teske’s modern sentence, “mostly a suburb of Monticello.” The Gates name is the hinge between those sentences.

## What a mill is, if you live beside it

Kidd Bros. Company, the 1907 paper says, acted as commissary for Gates and was among the largest mercantile concerns in the county. The brothers were stockholders in the lumber company and in the Bank of Wilmar. For several years after the Gates plant was built, the paper says, the store “did not have a very” — and the Haisty transcription breaks. Even the break is useful. A commissary’s first years are often thin. Then the mill’s payroll becomes a captive market. That is how a mill town’s middle class is made: not by opposing the mill, but by provisioning it.

Farmers still sold cotton through the new Farmer’s Union warehouse, the only one in the county in 1907, capacity a thousand bales. Schoolchildren still sat for Professor W. B. Massey after Spence left: eleven grades, nine months, enrollment in round numbers 200. A Berlin concertmaster, Otto Mahling, taught violin and cornet. The paper gave Mahling a full biography — Rachful, Russia, Count Einsiedel, Heavy Artillery, Italy, France, a flattering offer — and would not name the five people who had tended Anderson’s first corn. That imbalance is a later essay’s second subject. It belongs here only as a reminder that a mill makes a town that can hire a Berliner and still refuse a field a biography.

None of that abolishes the mill. It is what a mill makes possible for a while, and what a town tries to keep when the mill’s owners have a better woods in Ashley County. Darling and Bragg put Cap Gates “then of the Gates Lumber Company in Wilmar, Arkansas” when he learned of a 47,000-acre tract of virgin pine for sale in Ashley County and Morehouse Parish, the Michigan firm of Hovey and McCracken, seven dollars an acre, payable over seven years. The Crossett company article says no commercial timber sold until 1902; investors had spent a million dollars before the first real crop of boards. Wilmar, by then, had already been a Gates mill town for a decade.

It is easy, and wrong, to collapse Gates Lumber Company at Wilmar into Crossett Lumber Company at Crossett. The first is Teske’s 1890 Wilmar plant and Heady’s 1889 large-scale sawmill. The second is the 1899 Iowa corporation. Charles W. Gates is the name on both stories. Cap is the hands. A commenter on the Crossett Lumber Company page, Phillip Gossage, notes that his people always called the Crossett mill “the Gates Lumber Co.” anyway. Memory does what memory does. The historian’s job is to keep the charters apart and the brother on the road.

## Geography the mill actually used

Wilmar sat on the Warren Branch, eight miles west of the seat, on the ridge’s western opinion of itself. The Saline bottoms — about 8,000 acres in the 1907 figure — were “not so good from an agricultural standpoint, but are heavily timbered.” The Bartholomew bottoms, about 100,000 acres, were cotton country. Gates did not come for a bale. It came for the pine side of the county and for a mainline handshake at its own sawmill. The W&SVR’s twenty-five miles, when they existed, walked into that pine. They did not need the river except as a name on a charter.

Woodard’s fifty-four steamboats are the older extraction. The *Gate City* would sink on October 14, 1913, near Warren, a late wreck on a road the rail had already beaten. Gates’s 1890 plant is the newer extraction. Same woods, different vehicle. The 1904 company railroad is the vehicle made local: trees to the saw, boards to the Iron Mountain, Iron Mountain to a buyer who would never see Drew County.

We do not have, in Teske, a board-footage number for the Wilmar mill. Crossett’s early pine mill, the encyclopedia is willing to say, became an operation of two band mills, dry kilns, a planer, then a second pine mill in 1905, soon 84 million board feet a year. The absence at Wilmar is useful. It keeps us from pretending the two plants were the same size. Inventory we do have: twenty-five miles, four locomotives, fifty-five cars, two loaders, a stave factory at 10,000 a day, a bank at $25,000, five general stores, a commissary that was also a family firm. That is a mill with a town attached, not a town that was only a mill.

## What the record will not say

It will not choose 1889 or 1890. It will not name the 1887 competitor. It will not give the Wilmar mill a daily cut or a brand of band saw. It will not produce a photograph of the plant in this folder. It will not give the Kidd brothers first names in the transcription this pack used. It will not repair the broken commissary sentence. Darling and Bragg are cited as existing; this draft does not invent a quotation from their pages. Teske’s late-1920s equipment move and their 1924 closure note are left as a seam.

This pack will not flatten Gates into a villain or a founder-saint. It was a firm that did what south Arkansas firms did between 1880 and 1925, and it did it on a daughter’s postmark. The later shop that takes the river’s name works in the aftermath, not in the commissary. Hardwood still grows on the ridge. The virgin pine does not. That is the whole Gates lesson, and it does not need a moral tag at the end of the reel.
