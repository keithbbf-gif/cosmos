# Bryan in a Timber Town

William Jennings Bryan came to Wilmar in 1906. Paul Edwards records the visit without a transcript. The famed orator spoke in Beauvoir College’s five-hundred-seat auditorium to an audience that had traveled from across the region. That is the whole still: a national mouth, a wooden hall, a mill town at its peak population climb, one year after smallpox, one year before the Panic.

Bryan, in 1906, was already a three-time Democratic nominee (1896, 1900; 1908 still ahead) and the country’s most portable voice. He went where a hall and a crowd could be promised. That Wilmar could promise both is the local fact. Five hundred seats in a city the 1900 census put at 844, the 1910 census at 929, is not a modest room. It is a room that expected the county.

This essay will not reconstruct the night. It will sit with the adjacent dates and with what the record refuses to give. The refusal is the honest film.

## What a hall is for

Edwards says the campus had elegant wooden structures and a parade ground, and that the auditorium hosted musical productions and renowned guest speakers. Music and elocution were the school’s ornaments — the crown jewels, in Edwards’s phrase. Bryan is elocution’s prize hog. A college that trained teachers and took livestock for tuition also wanted a night when the ridge felt like a capital.

We do not have the speech. We do not know whether he talked silver, peace, or the Chautauqua set. A later writer who invents a text has left the encyclopedia. The honest film is faces, if we had them, and a date. 1906 is also the year enrollment would near 469. The *Advance*, a year later, would say between 400 and 500. The hall and the dormitories were full of the same bet: that Wilmar could hold more than a mill.

The catalogs of 1904–5 and 1906–7 survive at Taylor Library. They might list the auditorium’s dimensions, the speakers’ series, a fee at the door. This draft has not opened them. William Tyler Spence’s 1958 *AHQ* article might remember the night as family memory. This draft will not quarry it for a quotation it cannot verify in hand. Edwards’s sentence is the still: Bryan, 1906, five hundred seats, a regional crowd. Stop there, then widen to the town.

Wilmar sat about eight miles nearly due west of Monticello on the Warren Branch of the Iron Mountain. The branch could bring a man from a larger city. It could bring a wagon-load from Warren, from Collins, from Tillar, from the Bartholomew side of the dividing ridge. The Saline bottoms, about 8,000 acres in the 1907 figure, were timber. The Bartholomew bottoms, about 100,000, were cotton. The ridge held both. A regional crowd in 1906 is a crowd that crossed those three parts to hear a voice that had already failed twice for the presidency and would fail once more. Failure, at that scale, is still a draw. A mill town that can seat it has already decided it is not only a whistle.

Gates’s plant had been on the ground since 1889 or 1890. The Wilmar and Saline Valley Railroad, chartered in 1904, had by the next year its twenty-five miles, four locomotives, fifty-five cars, two loaders, interchange at the sawmill. Kidd Bros. provisioned the mill. The Farmer’s Union warehouse would open in 1907, a thousand bales. The stave factory ran at 10,000 a day. Bryan’s roof sat in that inventory even if the 1907 paper, writing after the college’s removal, did not linger on the night. Boosters prefer a payroll to a speech they cannot reprint.

## After the night

Smallpox had already come, in 1905, and taken forty lives in town, including a Beauvoir student and Spence’s five-year-old son. Bryan’s night sits in the grief’s second year. The Panic sits in the next. A documentary that treats 1906 as pure triumph has not read the adjacent sentences. A town can fill a hall and still be about to lose its college.

Spence did not close the school in 1905. Enrollment reached its apex in 1906–07. The doors stayed open in the grief. That is either duty or numbness or the simple fact that a campus which takes livestock for tuition cannot pause without starving. Bryan’s visit is evidence of the open door, not evidence that the grief had lifted. A five-hundred-seat hall can be full and a president’s house can still be missing a child. Both facts are 1906.

Spence would be in Monticello by the time the 1907 *Advance* went to press, running the city schools, the college “abandoned on the removal.” The auditorium’s lumber, or some of it, may have been among the structures dismantled and hauled east. Edwards says only that half the college’s structures moved and that foundation lines remain. If Bryan’s hall is now a rectangle in grass, that is the correct last shot. This draft will not assign a particular foundation line to the hall. The catalogs, a Sanborn map, a later walk with Edwards’s sentence in hand — those are later stills. Until then, “half the structures” is the measure, and the hall is inside the uncertainty.

Wilmar High School under W. B. Massey — eleven grades, nine months, two hundred students in round numbers, four literary teachers, one music, one expression — is the booster’s consolation in the same souvenir. Otto Mahling of Berlin taught violin and cornet. Edwards says Beauvoir’s music had been internationally trained. Mahling looks like what remained. A concertmaster is not Bryan. He is the weekly version of the same hunger: to be on a circuit, to have a trained voice in a timber stop. The hunger survived the night. The hall may not have.

## Why the visit still matters

Rex Nelson used Beauvoir to surprise readers who think they know which Arkansas towns were college towns. Bryan is the surprise inside the surprise. Presidential also-rans do not go to places that cannot deliver a crowd. For one evening, Wilmar delivered. The Iron Mountain could bring a man from a larger city. The college could seat him. The mill could pay, indirectly, for the roof.

This is small-town America’s hunger, not its charm: to be on the circuit, to be mentioned, to have the famous voice say the name of the stop. The name was a daughter’s — Willie Elvira Anderson, compressed into a cancellation in 1884. The voice was Nebraska’s. The roof was pine, if the campus was the elegant wood Edwards describes. The combination does not need a moral. It needs to be left on the screen long enough that a reader understands what was lost when the foundations became the architecture.

The 1907 paper called Wilmar the town of second importance in Drew County, a thousand or twelve hundred people. The 1910 census would say 929. Second importance is a snub from the square. Bryan’s night is the mill stop’s rebuttal for one evening. Monticello had Hinemon University School from 1890, a courthouse, a cotton mill, three thousand people in the paper’s figure. Wilmar had a 500-seat hall and a man who could fill it. The seat won the long argument: Act 100, 1909, the agricultural school, Spence himself. The night remains the short argument. For a few hours the west slope was the place to be.

Teske’s later table is the long film the night does not change: 844, 929, 1,034, 627. Bryan does not appear in a census cell. Neither does a hall. Headcount is not the only population. A town that has been heard is larger in the head, for a while, than a town that has only been counted. The head emptied when the buildings moved. The count kept climbing into 1920 on the mill’s last good decade, then fell. The night is not the mill. It is the other population — 469 students, a regional crowd, a Nebraska voice — that the mill’s town briefly held.

## What the record will not say

It will not give a transcript. It will not give a date inside 1906 more precise than the year. It will not say whether the crowd stood, whether the mill let a shift go early, whether Anderson sat in a reserved chair, whether the Kidd brothers took a box, whether Black residents of Wilmar were in the hall or on the ground outside or not present. The catalog said “for Whites.” A regional crowd in 1906 in this county has a color line even when the encyclopedia does not print the ushers.

The record will not give a photograph of Bryan on those steps. IMAGE_SOURCES will not allow a generated orator to stand in. It will not quote Spence 1958. It will not invent applause. It will not put words in Bryan’s mouth about silver, imperialism, or the pine. Chautauqua was a circuit. Wilmar was a stop. The speech, if it survives in a newspaper this pack has not seen, belongs to a later pass through the *Advance*’s ordinary issues, not the December 17 souvenir. The souvenir does not reprint it. That silence is part of the document. Boosters prefer a stave total to a visiting paragraph they cannot use as an advertisement the next year, when the hall is already a rumor of lumber on a wagon east.

No shop belongs on this night. The hunger to be mentioned is not a brand. The daughter’s name on the cancellation is not a logo. The roof was a college roof. The foundations are grass. Leave the reel there.
