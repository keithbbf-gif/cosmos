# A Concertmaster in Wilmar

The Monticello *Advance* of December 17, 1907, after its civic inventory of Wilmar, does a strange and useful thing. It prints a biography of the high school’s music teacher. Prof. Otto Mahling, native of Berlin. Study from age ten under Prof. Rachful, “a noted musician of that city.” A trip to Russia at fourteen with his instructor. Then Saxony, and “quite a while” in the orchestra of Count Einsidel. Heavy Artillery Regiment at seventeen, three years, concertmaster, “according to German custom.” Then a band of “high class musicians,” two years in Italy, six months in France, “considerable time in other countries.” Then Wilmar. The paper’s adjectives are the usual. The itinerary is not.

This is a Ken Burns still if there ever was one: a German concertmaster in a Drew County mill town the year the college has already left. Beauvoir’s music department had been, Paul Edwards writes, a crown jewel, with instructors of advanced and international training. Mahling looks like what remained, or what the high school hired to prove the jewel had not entirely rolled east. The souvenir wants both readings. This essay will not choose the one it cannot prove.

We do not have Mahling after December 17, 1907. No city directory after that date was consulted. He may have left with the next panic or the next better offer. He may have taught a generation of children who later sat at Hudspeth Park. This draft will not invent a grave, a marriage, or a wartime allegiance. The souvenir is the document. The itinerary is the film.

## How a booster writes a musician

“The present efficient music instructor at the Wilmar High School.” Efficient is the paper’s favorite praise for a man it wants to keep. Native of Berlin — the credential that does the most work in a county souvenir. Rachful, a noted musician of that city: the transcription gives no first name, and this pack will not supply one from a guess. Several years of tuition, then Russia at fourteen. On returning, Saxony, Count Einsidel’s orchestra. If the transcription’s *Einsidel* is the Saxon house usually spelled Einsiedel, a Monticello reader in 1907 could savor the particle without placing the manor. This essay will leave the spelling as Haisty has it and the identification as a seam.

Seventeen, Heavy Artillery, three years, concertmaster. Military service as German custom: the paper says so, which is how a booster translates conscription into character. Concertmaster is the peak word. At the expiration of his time, “still a mere youth,” he joined a band and traveled. Two years in Italy. Six months in France. Other countries, unspecified. The grand tour as a teacher’s curriculum vitae. Then a broken sentence in the transcription, which is itself a document:

“He then accepted a position as the people of Wilmar by a very flattering offer induced Prof. Mahling to remain there and he now has a large class and is meeting with much success.”

Accepted a position as the people of Wilmar. The typesetter, or the later typist, lost a clause. A position *in* something, perhaps, before the flattering offer induced him to remain. The seam is useful. We do not know what job he first accepted, or where, or whether Wilmar hired him from another Arkansas town or from the road. We know the paper wanted the offer to look flattering. The flattering offer is the town’s vanity. The travelogue is Mahling’s credential. Together they make a man who could have gone elsewhere and did not, as of December.

He is a musician of unusual ability, the paper says, master of the cornet and the violin, player of several other instruments. He takes great interest in his work. He is a very efficient and successful instructor. Those last sentences are chamber-of-commerce paste. The instruments are not. Cornet and violin in a stave town are a specific pair: one for the bandstand, one for the parlor. Edwards’s Beauvoir had musical productions in a 500-seat auditorium and, in 1906, William Jennings Bryan in that hall. Massey’s high school, after the college’s abandonment, kept a music teacher and an expression teacher on a faculty of six. The ratio is the fact.

## Massey’s school, two hundred in round numbers

The civic paragraph just above Mahling’s sketch is the stage. Beauvoir abandoned on Spence’s removal to Monticello. A flourishing high school under Prof. W. B. Massey. Eleven grades. Nine-month term. Enrollment “in round numbers” 200. Four literary teachers, one music, one expression. Literary societies. A boarding department. Best working condition. The *Advance* will not choose between 1,000 and 1,200 for the town; it will not choose an exact enrollment for the school. Round numbers are the booster’s honesty, which is a kind of hedge.

Teske’s 1910 census is 929. Edwards’s Beauvoir peak was 469 in 1906–07. The high school’s 200, if the round number is even roughly right, is a remainder and a boast at once. Four literary teachers and one musician for two hundred students is not a conservatory. It is a town that still wanted a Berliner on the payroll after the college’s crown jewel had left. Tuition at Beauvoir, Edwards writes, could be cash, crops, or livestock. The high school’s music fees are not in the souvenir. Mahling’s “large class” is the paper’s present tense. Do not turn it into a roster.

Expression — elocution — is the other jewel Edwards named. The souvenir keeps one teacher for it. Music and speech, after a college named for Jefferson Davis’s last house, are how a timber stop keeps performing class. Mahling is the performance after the departure. W. B. Massey is the principal the paper trusts. Spence is already in Monticello, on his way to Hinemon and then to the agricultural school the state will open in 1909. The musician stayed, as of December. That is all the souvenir will let us say.

## Berlin, and the names the paper would not print

Steven Teske, in the town’s origin paragraph, keeps five names from Anderson’s first corn: Simon and Lizza Taylor, Virginia and Wealthy Thurman, Gill Gordon. They tended the 1859 crop. They were enslaved. The encyclopedia, to its credit, refuses to lose them. The 1907 *Advance*, writing while Anderson still walked the town as Capt. J. T. D. Anderson, first settler, prominent merchant, honored resident, does not print those names. It will print Otto Mahling of Berlin, Prof. Rachful, Count Einsidel, Italy, France, concertmaster. The imbalance is the essay’s second subject.

Keep Mahling. Remember who did not get a biography. A stave factory at 10,000 a day and a violin teacher are the same town. Small-town America is not only a whistle. The paper, which would not name the five people in the first field, would name a Berliner. That is not a reason to throw the Berliner out. It is a reason to read the souvenir as a political document. Culture, in the booster’s mouth, is a European itinerary. Labor, in the same mouth, is a lumber company and a public-spirited citizenry brim full of local pride. The five names fall outside both sentences.

Drew County in 1860: 5,581 white residents, 3,497 held in bondage. Heady’s ratio. By 1907 the legal condition had been broken forty-two years. The souvenir’s silence had not. Black Wilmar, which would be 364 of 511 in Teske’s 2010 line, does not appear in Mahling’s paragraph. The June table does not appear. Hudspeth Park’s Black team of 1915 is eight years ahead. This essay will not invent Mahling in a segregated classroom beyond what a 1907 white high school already implies. Beauvoir had advertised itself as the pioneer boarding school in southeast Arkansas for whites. Massey’s school was the public successor in a dual system. The Colored School, which Mayor Perry would later refuse to move the dinner from, is not in the souvenir’s faculty list. Two campuses. One musician named.

A local history written for a later furniture mark can say this without a sermon. The wood that left this county as boards grew in a place whose printed culture preferred a Berlin concertmaster to five names in a field. The preference is the record. The camera, if it is honest, holds both stills long enough that a reader cannot have the violin without the field.

## What we will not invent

We will not invent a photograph. We will not invent a student who remembered him. We will not invent a 1914 enlistment or a 1917 register. We will not decide that Einsidel is certainly Einsiedel and then build a Saxon chapter. We will not decide that Rachful is a known Berlin master and then give him a first name from a lexikon. We will not place Mahling at Bryan’s 1906 Beauvoir speech; the college was still open then, and the souvenir does not put him on that platform. We will not give him a house on a named street. We will not bury him in Wilmar or in Berlin.

The transcription’s broken hire sentence is the warning. Even the document we have is damaged. A later editor who pulls the microfilm may recover the missing clause. Until then, the damage is part of the picture. Haisty’s ARGenWeb file is how this pack reached the text. The original cuts — if the souvenir ran a portrait — are not in the transcription as images. A later editor should pull the film. Until then, the words are the photograph. Concertmaster is the caption. Wilmar High School is the frame. 1907 is the year.

Edwards’s catalogs of 1904–5 and 1906–7 survive at Taylor Library, UAM. This draft has not had them in hand. They might or might not list a music faculty that would make Mahling a Beauvoir remainder rather than a high-school hire. Do not guess. The souvenir places him at Massey’s school in December 1907. That is the placement this essay will keep.

## Why he belongs in a mill series

Because a stave factory at 10,000 a day and a violin teacher are the same town. Because the paper understood that a county souvenir needed culture as well as board footage. Because internationally trained instructors were, Edwards says, how Beauvoir claimed prestige, and because the high school, after the panic and the departure, still wanted one. Because cornet and violin are work, not atmosphere. Because the five names Teske kept and the Berliner the *Advance* kept are the two kinds of naming this pack is responsible for. Lose either and the camera has already chosen.

W. B. Massey’s two hundred students, one music teacher. The ratio is the fact. Otto Mahling, as of December 17, 1907, was the one. After that date this folder is silent. Silence, after a full biography, is the correct last still. Do not fill it.
