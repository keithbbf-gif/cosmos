# Bottomland and High Ground

The Monticello *Advance* of December 17, 1907, in a moment of unusual clarity, divided Drew County into three. The Bartholomew bottoms: about 100,000 acres, “immensely rich,” the old rumor that the bayou was once the Arkansas River’s channel, plantations in the thousands of acres, a bale and forty to fifty bushels of corn, rent at five dollars. The Saline bottoms: comparatively narrow, about 8,000 acres, poorer for the plow, heavy with timber. The rest: the uplands, the dividing ridge, the “hills,” not all of them hilly, sandy loam, fruit, ordinary farms. Monticello sat on the highest part of that ridge. Wilmar sat eight miles west, toward the timber and the Warren Branch, not yet in the 8,000 acres but in their conversation.

Mary Heady’s modern encyclopedia article says the same geography in a cleaner coat. Drew County sits at the edge of the Mississippi Alluvial Plain in the West Gulf Coastal Plain. Bayou Bartholomew, the longest bayou in the world, runs the eastern edge. The Saline forms the southwestern border. The Monticello Ridge uplands run from north of Star City through the county into Louisiana.

The souvenir’s area figure is about 828 square miles, or 526,720 acres. Heady’s official skin is 828.66 square miles. Use both. Say which is which. The paper’s three-part acreage — 100,000, 8,000, and the remainder — is booster-round. The remainder is most of a county. “Hills,” the paper warns, should not be heard as broken ground. A great deal of the ridge is comparatively level. Slightly north of the center a chain of hills runs northwest to the county line. Long Prairie, in the south, is of considerable dimensions. Practically the whole county, the souvenir insists, is heavily timbered: pine, the oaks, hickory, gum, cypress mostly already cut. The bulk of the pine lies in the Saline bottoms and the contiguous territory and in the south, though pine is found everywhere except the Bartholomew bottoms. That last exception is the Delta speaking. Alluvium is not a pine woods. The ridge and the Saline side are.

## Two waters, two economies

East water is a bayou of cotton. West water is a river of trees and, once, boats. The ridge is the argument between them. A family on the ridge could see both weathers. A mill town on the west slope had chosen, or been chosen by, the timber side. The Farmer’s Union warehouse in Wilmar is the ridge refusing to let the east have all the cotton.

Bayou Bartholomew’s fame — longest bayou, plantation strip — is a Delta story this pack will not steal. It is here as the other wall of the room. Without the bayou, the Saline looks like the only water. With the bayou, Wilmar is a western decision.

Woodard’s Saline is 204 miles, four forks near Riverside, a mouth at Felsenthal, mountain stream at the origin and Delta-type bayou near the mouth. Drew County meets that career late, after Cleveland and Bradley, before Ashley. The southwestern border is not the forks and not the Marais Saline. It is the middle-lower river, the timbered narrow bottoms the *Advance* measured at 8,000 acres. Fifty-four steamboats used the water. Gates used the woods. The ridge used both as a horizon.

The 1907 paper’s county shape is almost a square: 27 miles north to south, greatest length 33, average a little over 30, second tier in from the Mississippi and from Louisiana. Formed November 26, 1846, mostly from Bradley; a piece from Chicot that December; a large cut to Ashley in 1848; a piece of Desha in January 1861 that “remains to this day a source of dissatisfaction to our eastern neighbors”; the Drew-Chicot line changed again in 1873. Heady’s parent counties are Bradley (1840) and Arkansas (1813); she adds the 1861 and 1873 Desha pieces and the 1873 Chicot settlement. The county is a made thing. The ridge is older than the making. The two waters were there when Rough and Ready was still a feud story.

## Long Prairie, Hungry Run, Ten Mile Creek

The 1907 historical section names older settlements: Clear Creek, Hungry Run, Bayou Bartholomew settlers from Louisiana in the 1830s, Ten Mile Creek. John S. Handly and his brother Jack, Clear Creek township, 1839. James Ferguson from Mississippi, same neighborhood, 1835. J. W. Ridgell, Bynum Nichols, and Fountain Brown, a Methodist preacher, on Hungry Run, 1835. Jesse Whittacre and Hugh Fannin from Kentucky, about 1836, northern part of the county. Stephen Gaster and Reece Bowden from Louisiana, 1832, on the Bayou. Ezekiel Owens from Tennessee, 1834, also the Bayou. John O’Neal, Jesse Hunt, Benjamin Nettles, and a man named Arnold on Ten Mile Creek. The old history the paper is quoting adds that these families were the county’s entire numerical strength. The paper will not swear to that. By 1850, it says, there were over 3,000 people; the decade table prints 3,276.

These are not Wilmar. They are the county’s first scatter, the reason a courthouse had to be invented at Rough and Ready and then moved. The first post office, Montongo, three miles west of the later Monticello, in Carney O’Neal’s store; the name surviving on a country office ten miles north of town. First election, while the land was still Bradley, at the Gaster schoolhouse on Gaster Hill. First county court, March 22, 1847, house of A. M. Rawles, Judge W. H. Wells, Isaac Holcomb’s $3 claim, $32.08 in delinquent taxes the first year. Seat of justice removed from Rough and Ready after the July 1849 term; commissioners Josiah Holcomb, John M. Carr, and J. Sanders; Fountain C. Austin’s 83 and 2-100 acres reported the following January. Heady’s version is Fountain and Polly Austin, eighty-three acres, Monticello incorporated 1852, first courthouse 1851. The souvenir and the encyclopedia agree on the gift and argue, slightly, about the furniture of the sentence. A ridge town that thinks it is the beginning is wrong. The bottoms were begun.

## Film a cross-section, not a postcard

The pack map (AS-MAP-01) is a cartoon of this paragraph: Bartholomew, ridge, Saline, Monticello, Wilmar, Warren. A cartoon is allowed if it is labeled as a cartoon. A generated “sunset on the bayou” is not. The 1907 acreage figures are booster-round. Heady’s 828.66 square miles is the county’s official skin. Use both. Say which is which.

A. J. Wilson’s peach farms sat “a little on the east side of the dividing ridge,” the souvenir said, a mile and a half south of Monticello, the second place another mile and a half on. The Peach King is an east-ridge fact. Gates’s pine is a west-ridge and Saline-bottom fact. Owens’s demonstration farm, four miles north of town, was “ordinary upland.” Scharff’s sixty-bushel corn was Long Prairie, sixteen miles south. The ridge is not a metaphor until the essay has earned it. It has earned it now: a sandy-loam argument between a bayou that made men famous for cotton and a river that made men famous, later, for a mill that left.

A shop on this ridge — glass front, 65 by 15, in the public interview — sits in the upland third. Figs in winter in the same building, the interview said, which is a farm-and-shop joke the furniture map already recorded. One sentence is enough. The ridge holds trees and pots and a bench. The bottoms hold the older money and the longer bayou. Wilmar holds the west.

Do not let the metaphor run. The dividing ridge is a landform the 1907 paper could walk. It is not a sermon about two Americas. East water and west water are drainage. The people who worked both sides — enslaved field hands in 1860, tenants after, stave cutters, mill hands, the households that still cook in June — were not symbols. They were on the ground. The map is for locating them, not for replacing them.
