# Willie Elvira

In 1884 James Thomas Dionysius Anderson opened a post office and named it Wilmar, to honor his oldest daughter, Willie Elvira Anderson. Steven Teske writes the sentence as if it were ordinary, which, in the nineteenth-century South, it almost was. Men named depots for daughters the way they named steamboats for wives: a public tenderness that also announced who held the paper.

We do not, in the sources this pack has on the desk, have Willie Elvira’s dates, her married name, or a photograph Teske felt safe to publish. The 1907 *Advance* still needed her father alive as founder and merchant. It did not need her. That imbalance is the essay.

## A house that was four buildings

The souvenir edition of December 17, 1907, is more useful than its adjectives. After the Warren Branch of the Iron Mountain had been extended toward Warren, Anderson “opened up a small store and also acted as postmaster and depot agent, using the house which now serves as a depot for the combined purposes of store, postoffice, depot and residence.” Four functions, one roof. If a documentarian wanted a single still for the invention of Wilmar, that house would be it: sacks and letters and a timetable and a bed, the railroad’s building doing the work of a town until the town caught up.

The *Advance* is a booster. It calls Anderson “Capt.” and “honored.” It also says he owned practically all the land the town was built on, and that his “liberal policy” probably drew the lumber company. Those claims can be held as 1907 self-portraiture. The four-function house is harder to invent. Railroad towns in south Arkansas did this often. The agent was the state for a while. The postmark was the town’s first published name.

Willie Elvira is in the postmark. She is not, in the 1907 copy, in the depot. Heritage writing likes to turn that into a soft-focus girl on a porch. The honest version is dryer. A father with a new federal office and a new rail stop chose a name that would fit a cancellation stamp and still point at his household. *Wilmar* is compact. It is not *Andersonville*. It is not *Gates*. It is a daughter’s two names compressed until they can travel on an envelope.

## Between the saw and the stamp

Anderson built a sawmill in 1881, three years before the post office. Teske’s order matters. The mill is first. The name is second. Competitors built sawmills in 1887 and 1890. A stave mill came in 1898. The city incorporated on March 8, 1899. The daughter’s name outran the father’s mill. That is the usual American joke, except it is not a joke. By the time Gates Lumber Company made Wilmar a word that meant lumber in Little Rock and Davenport, the word itself was already a child’s.

Elizabeth Eliza Bird had married Anderson in 1869. Ilrey A. Bird, her brother, would serve as Drew County judge from 1898 to 1902 and would donate land, with Anderson, for the school that became Beauvoir College. The Birds are the civic in-laws of the origin story. Willie Elvira is the name on the mail. A series that cares about small-town America has to care about who got to name the town and who only lived in it. Black residents, who would be the majority of Wilmar by 2010 (364 of 511, Teske), did not choose *Wilmar*. They made the June Dinner, which is a different kind of naming — a date and a table, not a post office.

## How a name settles

Town names in this county are a mixed lot. Monticello is a planter’s classicism, Jefferson’s house borrowed for a courthouse town. Rough and Ready was a brawl. Tillar is a man. Montongo survives as a country office after the first post office of the county had used the name west of the future square. Wilmar belongs with the affectionate compressions: a household word made official.

The risk, in a Ken Burns register, is to over-score it. A fiddle under a daughter’s name is how you get a commercial for a river workshop. This piece will not do that. The useful emotion is smaller. Somewhere in 1884 a clerk in Washington, or a route agent, accepted a name. Letters began to arrive addressed to a person who was also a place. Willie Elvira could, in theory, have received mail at her own name. That is a strange intimacy, and it is the whole point of the stamp.

By 1900 the census counted 844 people in Wilmar. By 1920, 1,034. The name had filled. The daughter, whoever she had become, is not in those tables. Tables do not work that way. The 1907 paper, writing the town at its boast, still dated “the real history of Wilmar” from Gates, seventeen years earlier, and treated Anderson as prelude. Even the founder’s own town was being retold as a mill’s town. The daughter’s name remained, which is a kind of victory the mill did not intend.

## Letters, which are the town’s first published literature

A post office is a literature machine. Once Wilmar existed as a cancellation, every invoice, every church notice, every mill order, every love letter that survived a fire became a document with a place-name at the top. We do not have a published packet of those letters. We have the condition for them. Teske’s 1884 date is the condition. The 1907 paper’s four-function house is the set. A later editor who finds an envelope in a Drew County attic will do more for this essay than another paragraph of atmosphere.

Railroad mail is a particular literature. The Warren Branch did not only carry boards. It carried the pouch. Anderson as depot agent and postmaster is the same man touching both. That overlap is how a mill town’s information system actually worked: one roof, two federal-ish jobs, a daughter’s name on the stamp.

## What we do not have

Wanda Beaty Reaves’s *Wilmar, Arkansas: Scrapbook of Memories* (2005) is on the Beauvoir bibliography. It may hold a photograph this pack has not seen. DeArmond may have a paragraph. A responsible later pass would go to Taylor Library in Monticello and to the Drew County Museum and ask. Until then, the honest caption is: no likeness in hand.

That gap is better than a generated girl in a white dress. The name is the document. The house that was a depot is the set. The rest is patience.

If a family ever offers a picture of Willie Elvira Anderson, it should be filmed the way Burns films a face we have only one of: long enough that the viewer understands the town was, for a while, a person. Then the camera should go back to the cancellation, which is what lasted.

The 1900 count of 844 is the first census that treats the daughter’s name as a city. Incorporation was the year before. In sixteen years the postmark had filled with enough bodies to satisfy a census enumerator. That is faster than a farm name fills, slower than a boom camp. It is the pace of a rail stop that also had a mill. Willie Elvira, if she lived to see 1900, could have walked a town that had taken her name and then taken Gates’s payroll as its biography. The *Advance* would do that retelling seven years later. The cancellation did not change. Names outlast the people who prefer a different origin story. That is the only romance this essay will allow, and it is not romantic. It is postal.
