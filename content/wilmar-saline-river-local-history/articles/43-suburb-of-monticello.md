# A Suburb of Monticello

Steven Teske, after the mill and the college and the dinner, says the quiet present tense. Wilmar flourished in the early twentieth century but is now mostly a suburb of Monticello, the county seat. *Suburb* is a metropolitan word doing rural work. There is no beltway. There is highway and habit. Children ride east. Workers ride east. The square, which Mary Heady says is the landscaped center where a French-castle courthouse stood until 1933, still collects the county’s errands.

Monticello’s own encyclopedia article — Rebecca DeArmond-Huskey’s — is a growth story: two roads, a railroad in 1880, commerce, agriculture, timber, a college that became UAM, SeaArk, a Walmart strip that did not kill the square. Wilmar’s article is a subtraction story that ends in Monticello’s orbit. The two pieces, read together, are a single county. The 1907 *Advance* already knew the hierarchy. “The town of second importance in Drew County is Wilmar, situated about 8 miles nearly due west of Monticello on the Warren Branch of the Iron Mountain R. R.” *Suburb* is the twenty-first-century update of that snub. It is also, for some households, a convenience. Eight miles is not exile. It is a commute. Teske’s “mostly” is the word that saves the sentence. Mostly. June is not suburban. The Colored School ground is not suburban. A city charter is not suburban. The weekday is.

## Two elevations, two areas, two tables

Wilmar sits at 154 feet, 1.53 square miles, 395 people in 2020. Monticello sits at 298 feet, 10.89 square miles, 8,442 people in 2020. The encyclopedia prints those cells without comment. The comment is geography. The county seat took the highest part of the Monticello Ridge, the uplands Heady runs from north of Star City through Drew into Louisiana. Wilmar took the western slope toward the Saline and the Warren Branch. The 1907 paper, in a moment of unusual clarity, had already divided the county into Bartholomew bottoms, Saline bottoms, and the dividing ridge. Monticello sat on the ridge’s high ground. Wilmar sat eight miles west, toward the timber.

The census tables, set side by side, are a double exposure. Monticello: 891 in 1880, 1,285 in 1890, 1,579 in 1900, 2,274 in 1910, 2,378 in 1920, 3,076 in 1930, then the long climb to 9,467 in 2010 and a drop to 8,442 in 2020. Wilmar: 844 in 1900, 929 in 1910, 1,034 in 1920, 627 in 1930, a mid-century lift to 746 in 1950, 747 in 1980, then 395. In 1900 the two towns were almost the same size. By 2020 the seat was twenty-one times the mill stop. That ratio is what *suburb* is trying to say without using a ratio.

Drew County as a whole, Heady’s 2020 line, was 17,350 people, 65.1 percent white, 27.9 percent Black. Wilmar in 2010 was 511, 134 white, 364 African American. The suburb, if that is the word, is not a white bedroom. It is a majority-Black city whose weekday institutions have moved east and whose weekend in June has not.

## How a seat eats a sister

Fountain C. and Polly Austin donated eighty-three acres in 1849. Incorporation came on December 20, 1852. The first courthouse went up on the square in 1851; a second building in 1857. Heady’s brick French castle of 1871 towered over the businesses until it was torn down in 1933 and the court moved farther south on Main. The landscaped square is what remains of that altitude. Lots for Baptist, Methodist, and Presbyterian churches in the early 1850s. A library in the courthouse in 1857. The *Sage of Monticello* the same year. G. W. Simms, I. A. Price, Henry Lephiew, Dr. H. F. Bailey — DeArmond-Huskey’s prewar counter list, names a mill town did not yet have.

After the war the seat accumulated magnets. The *Monticellonian* in 1870; the Drew County *Advance* in 1892. The Little Rock, Mississippi River, and Texas Railroad — later the Iron Mountain — in 1880. McCloy & Trotter in 1881, a mercantile that lasted, under Trotter sons, until 1970. First bank, 1887. Hannah Hyatt’s orphan home, 1887, later the Arkansas Baptist Home for Children. Telephone, 1898. Cottonseed oil mill, 1890. Warren Anderson’s Monticello Cotton Mill, 1900 — Heady’s Anderson Cotton Mill, later Burlington, closed 2001. Southern Compress, 1892–1940. Drew Cotton Seed Oil Mill, 1890s–1960s. WCTU’s first Arkansas chapter, 1876. Mack Wilson Hospital, 1930; Drew Memorial, 1950. Fourth District State Agricultural School, 1909, after a bidding process the county seat won. Hinemon University School, 1890, folded into Monticello High in 1910. A Coca-Cola plant, 1935. A municipal pool, 1936. SeaArk’s ancestor, MonArk, 1959. A Walmart Supercenter in the 1990s, and a two-mile commercial strip that, DeArmond-Huskey is careful to say, did not cause the old square to languish.

These are magnets. Wilmar had a mill and a college and lost both kinds of magnet. What it kept — a charter of March 8, 1899, churches Teske lists in 2013, a June table, a gas transmission station, 395 people — does not generate weekday traffic at seat scale. Teske’s children-from-Wilmar-attend-Drew-County-Central sentence is the weekday in one clause. Heady’s ancestor dates sit under it: Drew Central established in 1927 as a training school for teachers; rural districts consolidated into it two years later; the school separated from the college in 1935 and kept the name. Integration of Drew High waited until 1970. The agricultural school that took Beauvoir’s rehearsal is now UAM, with the only School of Forest Resources in Arkansas. The college that was in Wilmar is a system with a name that does not mention Wilmar.

The 1907 paper, writing from the square, needed Wilmar to look busy. A county souvenir that only sells Monticello is a thinner souvenir. It gave the second town a thousand or twelve hundred people, Gates, a stave factory at 10,000 a day, a bank at $25,000, five general stores, a Farmer’s Union warehouse. The 1910 census would say 929. The booster was high. The hierarchy was not. First is the seat. Second is the mill stop. *Suburb* is what you call the second town when the mill has been gone for a century and the seat still has the courthouse.

## What “mostly” saves

June is not suburban. Teske puts the dinner in Wilmar’s first paragraph: one of the oldest Juneteenth celebrations in Arkansas, nineteenth century, thousands. Mayor Toni Perry told PBS the celebration has always been held at the Wilmar Colored School and cannot move. A suburb does not keep a holiday the county seat cannot host. The Colored School ground is a Wilmar fact. The 2010 majority is a Wilmar fact. The city charter is a Wilmar fact. The Presbyterian church that went up near Anderson’s land in 1861 disbanded in 1974 with eight members; that death is local too.

The weekday is suburban. A child on a bus to Drew Central. A worker on the highway to a plant, a hospital, a campus, a strip. An errand to the square for a license the 1933 courthouse still issues farther down Main. Eight miles nearly due west, the 1907 paper said, which means the return trip is nearly due east. Gravity reversed. The Iron Mountain’s Warren Branch once made Wilmar a point on the way to Warren. The highway now makes Wilmar a point on the way to Monticello. Teske’s “mostly” holds both maps in one adverb.

Rex Nelson, in 2026, used *dying* for the 395. This series has already refused his verb. A suburb of 395 is not a death. It is a relationship. Relationships can be extractive. The seat took the college’s buildings — Edwards says Spence disassembled half and hauled them east. The seat took the agricultural school. The seat took the high school’s descendants. The seat did not take the June table. That remainder is why *mostly* matters.

## Allen House, Taylor House, the square

Heady’s attractions are Monticello-and-east geography. The Allen House, nationally recognized, renovated by Mark and Rebecca Spencer after 2007, opened to the public, a local legend about Ladell (Allen) Bonner and her son. The Taylor House, a two-story dogtrot of 1846 near Winchester, owned by UAM — Winchester, in the same county, the town where Robert Hill incorporated a different farmers’ union in 1918. The Drew County Museum in the former Garvin Cavaness house. Selma Rosenwald School, a point of interest Heady names; not Wilmar’s Colored School. Depression-era post-office art, now the Economic Development Commission. Lake Monticello.

Wilmar does not get the haunted-house tourism. It gets the dinner. A heritage lane that only points visitors to the Allen House has chosen the seat’s script. This pack points to Wilmar first. DeArmond-Huskey’s Monticello is allowed to be proud of a square that survived a strip. Teske’s Wilmar is allowed to be a suburb in the weekday and a city in June. Both sentences can be true. The work is not to pick a mood.

Monticello’s famous residents in the encyclopedia — Charlie May Simon, born on a farm between Tillar and Monticello in 1897; Hershel Wayne Gober; Major General John Shirley Wood — are seat-and-east names. William Shea has taught at UAM since 1974 and wrote, with William Droessler, the 1915 baseball essay this series uses. The campus produced historians of the county that lost the campus’s rehearsal. That is a mild irony. It is also how records survive. Wilmar’s names in Teske are Anderson, Willie Elvira, Gates, Spence, the Beavers, a Klan chapter in 1923, a turbine, a mayor who could tell PBS a Galveston story. The suburb did not erase the names. It relocated the weekday around them.

## Second importance, then orbit

The *Advance* of December 17, 1907, is a booster. Carolyn Haisty’s transcription is how this pack reached the text. “Town of second importance” is a caption written from the square. The inventory under it is evidence: mill, college just gone, high school under W. B. Massey, stave factory, bank, stores, warehouse. Public spirit, brim full of local pride — the sentence a parody would score with a piccolo. We will leave it unscored. Public spirit in 1907 meant subscriptions, a college half paid by the town, a well from Gates, a welcome to new enterprises. It also meant a color line, a recent smallpox, a panic already underway that the paper does not mention. December 17, 1907. Beauvoir abandoned that spring. The paper says abandoned, then pivots to the high school. That pivot is the document.

A century later the second town is mostly a suburb. The booster could not have written that sentence. It did not have Teske’s table. It had a thousand or twelve hundred and a need to look busy. Honesty is holding both pages. The souvenir wanted Wilmar to justify a county. The encyclopedia wants Wilmar to explain a commute. Neither page is the June table.

A shop in the county may sit closer to Wilmar’s silence than to the Walmart strip. That is a location fact, not a virtue. Do not advertise quiet. Quiet is other people’s lives. This is not the essay that explains a workshop name. It is the essay that explains why the weekday goes east.
