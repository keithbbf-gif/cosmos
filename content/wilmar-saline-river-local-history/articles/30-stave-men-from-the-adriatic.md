# Stave Men from the Adriatic

Jann Woodard’s migration sentence is one of the best in the river article, because it is specific. Shipping on the Saline was a Hampton and Wheeler business until the early 1900s, when Slavonians migrated to the area and became instrumental in cutting and shipping staves. She names families: Lipovac, Karlovic, Kusterin, Dorich, Piskulick.

Slavonian, in the American usage of that moment, usually meant South Slavic people from the Habsburg lands — Croatia, Slavonia, the Adriatic hinterland — coming into southern timber and barrel country. This draft will not invent a village of origin. The surnames are the document. A Ken Burns hour would print them the way it prints a ship’s manifest.

The encyclopedia does not give a year of arrival more precise than the early 1900s. It does not give a landing, a church, or a naturalization docket. Teske’s Wilmar article does not pick the names up. The 1907 *Advance*, which will inventory a stave factory at 10,000 a day and call stave timber “no small industry for many years,” does not print Lipovac. The absence is not a license. It is a limit. This series will keep the five names and refuse the invented dinner table, the same rule it used for Simon and Lizza Taylor, Virginia and Wealthy Thurman, and Gill Gordon in Anderson’s first field.

## Staves, again, because the word is work

A stave is a piece of a barrel. Split or sawn, dressed, set in a hoop, it holds whiskey, pickles, nails, oil, whatever a distant merchant needs a cylinder for. Wilmar’s 1898 stave mill and the 1907 factory at 10,000 a day are the industrial still. The river families Woodard names may have fed that mill, or Warren’s, or landings that never got a city charter. The encyclopedia does not assign them a town. This series will not kidnap them for Wilmar’s pride. It will put them in the Saline economy that Wilmar belonged to.

Hardwood bottoms, oak especially, made barrel stock. The 1907 *Advance* said stave timber had been “no small industry for many years” in Drew County. Hardwoods, the paper added, abounded in all parts of the county; a number of small mills were cutting them; tie timber moved by the thousands. Pine was the Gates story, concentrated in the Saline bottoms and the south, “the greater part of this timber” under the company that had the large mill at Wilmar. Staves are the other taking: not the yellow-pine cathedral, but the oak that could curve.

The Slavonian crews are the named labor in that sentence, at last. A lot of timber history is owners. Balogh’s statewide article is a parade of Illinois and Iowa surnames: Rittenhouse, Embree, Weyerhaeuser, Watzek, Gates, Crossett. Teske’s town article is Anderson, then Gates, then Spence. Woodard’s river paragraph, for once, is cutters. Lipovac is not a mill president. Karlovic is not a Yale forester. The work is in the verb she chose: cutting and shipping.

Ten thousand staves a day, if the 1907 figure is even roughly true, is a factory claim, not a woods claim. Boosters inflate. The souvenir edition is a booster. Use it for inventories and for how the town wanted to be seen. Do not treat its adjectives as measurement. Capacity is an adjective wearing a number. What can be said without the factory’s press agent is smaller: Drew County had been sending stave timber out for years, by river when the water was up, by rail when the Warren Branch would take a car. Someone had to cut that timber. Woodard named who, in the last chapter of the river trade, did.

## After the Hamptons

The Hampton brothers and John N. Wheeler, Woodard says, were the primary shippers until this migration. That is a succession: Anglo shippers, then immigrant cutters and shippers, then rail. The order is not a morality play. It is a labor market. People who knew stave woods took a boat or a train to a country that still had oak and a landing.

Primary shippers is an owner’s phrase. Instrumental in cutting and shipping is a labor phrase that has been allowed to share a sentence with capital. The Hamptons and Wheeler do not vanish from the valley because five Slavic surnames arrive. Woodard’s “until” is a change of emphasis, not a death certificate. The rail system, she writes elsewhere in the same article, had already slackened river shipping after 1880. The Slavonians walked into a trade that was already losing its water. They did not lose it. They worked what remained.

If descendants still live on the lower Saline, they have their own June and their own churches. This pack has not interviewed them. The rule is the same as for the five names in Anderson’s field: keep the names, do not invent the dinner table. A later pass through naturalization records, parish registers, and the Bradley and Cleveland censuses of 1910 and 1920 should be done by someone who will sit with the microfilm. This draft will not fake the household sizes.

## What Wilmar can claim, and what it cannot

Teske dates a stave mill in Wilmar to 1898. The city incorporated the next year. Gates had been on the ground since 1890. The Wilmar and Saline Valley Railroad, chartered in 1904, was a pine tool, not a barrel tool, though a logging road will haul what it is paid to haul. The 1907 paper, listing Wilmar’s second-importance inventory, puts the stave factory after Gates and after the high school that replaced Beauvoir, and before the bank and the five general stores. That is a town that still needed barrels as well as boards.

None of those sentences puts Lipovac on a Wilmar street. Woodard’s geography is the Saline. Her communities are Haskell, Traskwood, Prattsville, Farindale, Staves, Rison, Warren, Longview. Staves — the Cleveland County town — is already a cargo that learned to be a place name. A writer looking for a pretty coincidence will want the Slavonians to live in Staves. This draft has not seen that. The town name is older than this migration in the way cargo names are older than particular crews.

Do not flatten these men into extras in a pine romance. They are not a colorful paragraph for a mill brochure. They are the labor that makes Woodard’s “staves” a human sentence instead of a commodity line. The same rule applies in the other direction. They are not a brand story. They are not a reason to put a Croatian checkerboard on a furniture hangtag. The surnames are the document. The document is enough.

## A furniture sentence, once

Barrels are bent wood. Furniture is usually not. The kinship is only this: hardwood left this river as a curved piece for someone else’s liquid, and it still leaves, sometimes, as a flat piece for someone else’s room. The Adriatic surnames are not a shop story. They are a reminder that “local” labor in this valley was already international when the mill was young.

George Balogh notes that between 1880 and 1920 community development and the timber industry fed each other: schools, churches, stores, the institutions a payroll requires. He is thinking of Fordyce, Warren, Crossett, the company towns and the county seats. The Slavonian stave crews sit in a thinner version of that sentence. They needed a landing, a woods, a way to send a bundle out. They did not, in Woodard’s telling, need a city named for them. That is not romance. It is how a last extractive trade looks when the rail has already won.
