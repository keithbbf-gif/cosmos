# Lignite at Goulett Island

Jann Woodard’s southern Saline is not pretty. It is known, she writes, for large fields of lignite. Exposures showed in many places along the river. The largest was in Bradley County at Goulett Island. In 1860 Governor Elias N. Conway owned that ground. David Dale Owen’s geological reconnaissance of 1859 and 1860 is the scientific still behind her sentence. She lists Owen’s *Second Report* the way a careful river article lists a book it will not paraphrase without the page.

Lignite is brown coal, young, dirty, a fuel the later century mostly passed by when better coals and then gas and oil were easier. In 1860 it was a fact a governor could own. A river series that only loves canoes has not read the geology. The Saline was a mineral prospect as well as a road.

William B. Worthen, writing Conway’s life for the Encyclopedia of Arkansas, puts the two 1860 facts in the same administration without claiming they caused each other. Conway charged Owen with Arkansas’s first geological survey. Woodard puts Conway’s name on Goulett Island in the survey’s last year. This essay will not invent a purchase date, a deed, or a letter in which the governor tells the geologist to look at his island. It will leave the two sentences on the table. A man who hired the state’s first geologist also held the river’s largest lignite exposure in the year the geologist’s second report went to press. Patronage and science can share a calendar without sharing a motive we do not have.

## Conway, which is a statewide name

Elias Nelson Conway, fifth governor, November 15, 1852, to November 15, 1860. Born May 17, 1812, in Greeneville, Tennessee, youngest son in a planter household that moved to Missouri when he was six. He came to Arkansas in 1833, was appointed U.S. deputy surveyor in 1834, and served as territorial and then state auditor until 1849. Worthen’s Family is the word for it: Conways, Seviers, Johnsons, 188 years of cousin-service by John Hallum’s count. James Sevier Conway, the brother, had been the state’s first governor. Elias lasted longer in the chair than anyone until Orval Faubus.

He was the dirt-roads candidate in 1852, conservative about railroads, winner over Bryan H. Smithson by 15,442 to 12,414. He beat James Yell and the Know-Nothings in 1856. He created a Pulaski County chancery court to put the Real Estate Bank into receivership. He left $304,106.98 in the treasury and a pile of repudiated bank debt the postwar state would later refuse. He proposed that free Black people be prohibited from living in Arkansas after 1860. The legislature agreed. This draft will not wash Goulett Island in the surplus and forget the statute. Riverine mineral land in the last year of a Family administration was not a folk commons. It was property.

Conway never married. In 1860 he retired into a near-recluse’s life in Little Rock and died on February 28, 1892, after falling headfirst into his fireplace. Worthen records the death without decoration. The Family’s political domination had already ended in the 1860 election, when voters rejected Conway’s chosen successor. The island on the Saline does not appear in the gubernatorial essay. It appears in the river essay. That split is useful. A governor’s biography can ignore a brown-coal bank. A river article cannot.

This pack will not build a second Conway life. The name stays on the island as Woodard leaves it. The survey stays in the administration as Worthen leaves it. Bradley County is the sibling timber pack’s country — Warren, Bradley Lumber, the Warren and Saline River Railroad. This essay takes only the island and the fuel. The island is in the Saline. That is the passport.

## Owen, which is a book and not a poem

David Dale Owen (1807–1860) had been Kentucky’s state geologist when Arkansas hired him. The *Second Report of a Geological Reconnaissance of the Middle and Southern Counties of Arkansas, Made during the Years 1859 and 1860* was printed in Philadelphia by C. Sherman in 1860. Robert Peter did the chemistry. Leo Lesquereux the botany. Edward Cox assisted. Richard Owen saw the volume through after his brother’s death. Those names are on the title page this pack’s bibliography already carries. They are not local color.

Owen is not a poet. That is why he is useful. A reconnaissance is a first look: thicknesses where he measured them, tones of Victorian certainty where he did not. Cite the page when you have the page. Until then, Woodard-on-Owen is the chain, and the chain is enough to keep a writer from inventing a seam height or a British thermal unit for 1860. Later Arkansas Geological Survey work — twentieth-century drilling in the West Gulf Coastal Plain, Wilcox and Claiborne, estimates that belong to another office — is not this essay’s job. The 1860 still is an exposure on a river island a governor owned.

The northern Saline, Woodard writes, was popular for salt. A marsh near the mouth, the Marais Saline; or a works near Benton as early as 1827; at one time those works supplied the bulk of the salt used in the territory and the surrounding states. The southern river was lignite. Salt and brown coal are the first two extractive sentences the water admits. Pine is later. A mill town that thinks the valley was only trees has not been paying attention.

## Fuel before pine, gas after pine

The sequence in this pack’s economy, if a sequence is allowed, runs salt, lignite, corn, cotton, pine, gas. Anderson planted corn on five acres in 1859, the year Owen was in the southern counties. Five enslaved people tended it. Drew County in 1860 counted 5,581 white residents and 3,497 people held in bondage. Conway left office that November. Arkansas left the Union the next spring. Lignite did not fight the war. It sat in a bank a former governor owned while other men died in skirmishes Heady lists for Drew and Woodard lists for Jenkins’ Ferry, Mount Elba, Marks’ Mills, Longview.

After the war the county’s printed mind turned to cotton and then, by the 1860s already, to timber. Heady says timber was a major industry in Drew by that decade. The 1907 *Advance* still sold agriculture as the most important and stable of all industries, then spent its best pages on bales and peaches, and only then on Gates. Pine had the large mill at Wilmar. The paper still thought the county was a farm. Lignite does not appear in that souvenir. Brown coal had already lost the argument to wood that could float and, after 1880, ride a rail.

Wilmar’s later turbine pump is a different hydrocarbon. Teske: a 1928 Arkansas-Louisiana Gas pipeline aimed at St. Louis; a transmission station since 1948; the first turbine pump on an American natural-gas line, he writes; CenterPoint in the present tense. Lignite is the nineteenth-century version of “what else is in this valley.” Gas is the twentieth-century version. Neither is a canoe. A reader who comes to the Saline for scenery and stays for a mill has skipped the fuel chapters. This essay exists so the skip is harder.

## An island is a kind of still

Goulett Island, Bradley County. The largest exposure. The spelling is Woodard’s. This pack does not have a USGS sheet on the desk with the island labeled, and it will not pretend to. A later editor can pull the Warren and Hermitage topos and see whether the name still sits on a bar the river has not eaten. Rivers move islands. That is not a metaphor. It is why a mineral claim on a river island is a bet against water as well as a bet on coal.

Bradley’s later mills — the sibling pack’s job — cut pine and hauled it on a short line that must not be confused with Gates’s Wilmar and Saline Valley Railroad. The Warren and Saline River Railroad is 1905 and 1920, Johnsville, a different charter. This essay will not walk those tracks. The island is older than both roads. In 1860 there was no Gates plant at Wilmar. There was no Wilmar. Anderson had bought his seven hundred acres the year before. The post office that would take a daughter’s name was twenty-four years off. Conway’s lignite and Anderson’s corn share a pair of seasons and no town.

A Ken Burns hour would hold a map. Owen’s plates, if they show the southern counties, would be the picture. We do not have the plate in this folder. We have Woodard’s sentence, Worthen’s survey, a governor’s name, and a fuel that did not become a city. That is enough still. Music would be a mistake. Brown coal is not a theme. It is a deposit.

## What the later woods do not get to erase

Cut out and get out, when it came, erased towns more efficiently than it erased geology. Stumps and slash do not cancel a lignite bed. They only change the photograph. The Saline kept receiving silt from cutover and from farm in the same century. Farmers on Drew’s dividing ridge still planted cotton and corn. The Farmer’s Union warehouse of 1907 announced that Wilmar was never only a mill. After the mill, that announcement had to become a life. Lignite never had to become a life. It remained a sentence in a river article and a line in a governor’s property.

A shop that later took the river’s name — the one essay in this pack allowed to say so at length — takes water, not brown coal. That is correct. Lignite is a poor brand and an honest ancestor. The valley was fuel before it was furniture, and fuel before it was pine. Say it here. Do not say it on a product page.
