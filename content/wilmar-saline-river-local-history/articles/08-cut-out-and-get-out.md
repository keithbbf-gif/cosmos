# Cut Out and Get Out

George Balogh, in the Encyclopedia of Arkansas, does not decorate the phrase. Until the 1920s and later, timber-cutting operations in this state, even the large ones, practiced a technique known as cut out and get out. A company entered a country, bought virgin timber, built a mill, cut the marketable trees, shipped the lumber, and moved. The small mill bought less land and made less board footage. The large mill bought more, built a plant, and left the plant in place while its rails walked to the next parcel. The distinguishing marks, Balogh writes, were denuded land and the periodic relocation of production. For a town whose living was the mill, relocation meant decline and possible death.

Wilmar is a textbook still. 1,034 people in 1920. 627 in 1930. Rex Nelson, writing in the *Democrat-Gazette* in 2026, quotes Teske on the transfer of Gates equipment to Crossett and then says the quiet part: most companies used the method; the virgin forests around Wilmar had been denuded by the late 1920s. Heady’s county article puts the land sale to Crossett Lumber Company in 1924 and the exhaustion in the mid-1920s. Darling and Bragg, in 2008, note the closure of the Wilmar mill in 1924. Teske puts the equipment move in the late 1920s. The numbers do not need a violin. The seams do not need to be ironed. The method is visible in either year.

Nelson is a columnist. This series is not required to take his later verb about a dying town. 627 living people in 1930 were not a death. They were a count after a job had relocated. The count is the photograph. The method is the caption.

## What the woods looked like before the method

The 1907 *Advance* is a tour in the present tense of a county that still believed it was inexhaustible. “The greater part of this timber is now controlled by the Gates Lumber Company, which has a large mill at Wilmar.” Pine in the Saline bottoms and the south. Hardwood everywhere, and small mills on it. Stave timber “for many years.” Tie timber by the thousands. The paper’s job was to invite capital. It did not invite a forester.

The same souvenir divided the county into three parts and, for once, told the truth about which part the mill wanted. Bartholomew bottoms: about 100,000 acres, cotton, a bale and forty to fifty bushels of corn. Saline bottoms: about 8,000 acres, poorer for the plow, heavy with timber. Uplands: the dividing ridge, sandy loam, ordinary farms, Monticello on the highest ground, Wilmar eight miles west on the Warren Branch. Gates did not come for the bayou’s rent at five dollars an acre. It came for the 8,000 and for the pine the ridge still held on its western slope.

Yellow pine in south Arkansas grows faster than pine in the Ouachitas or the Ozarks, Balogh notes, because of sandy soil and heat. That speed is why the later conversion to sustained yield was possible at all. It is also why the first generation could believe the woods were a mine. A mine that grows back in twenty-five years is still a mine if you do not wait.

Anderson’s 1881 saw was already a taking. Teske’s unnamed competitors of 1887 and 1890 were takings. Gates’s 1889 or 1890 plant — Heady’s year and Teske’s year, the seam this series keeps — was a taking organized as a firm. The W&SVR, chartered in 1904, twenty-five miles of track by 1907, four locomotives, fifty-five log cars, two loaders, was a taking with locomotives. None of this is a revelation. The revelation, if the word is allowed, is how completely a town could attach its pride to a method that had already scheduled the town’s reduction.

The 1907 paper dated “the real history of Wilmar” from Gates and listed a stave factory at 10,000 a day, a bank at $25,000, five general stores, Kidd Bros. as commissary, a Farmer’s Union warehouse of a thousand bales. That inventory is what a method looks like when it is still paying. It is not a rebuttal of the method. It is the method’s furniture.

## 1926 is a different telephone call

In 1926 Cap Gates, from Crossett, called H. H. Chapman at the Yale forestry school. Chapman told him to hire a professional. He hired W. K. Williams. Crossett became a name in sustained yield. L. K. Pomeroy and Eugene P. Connor, Heady writes, opened Ozark Badger Lumber Company in Drew County in 1925 and pioneered selective cutting as a model in the 1930s. The county that had been clear-cut learned, in the next town over and the next decade, to treat pine as a crop.

Wilmar’s mill did not get to be the classroom. The classroom was Crossett, Urania, and a set of last names — Wackerman, Reynolds, Pomeroy — that belong to the forestry essay, not to a city of 627. Balogh’s point about towns is the one that belongs here. When production relocates, the institutions that grew up to serve the payroll — churches, stores, a college already gone, a high school — do not relocate on the same flatcar. They thin.

The college had already gone. Beauvoir closed after the Panic of 1907. Spence hauled half the buildings to Monticello. Foundation lines remained. The 1905 smallpox had taken forty, including a student and Spence’s five-year-old son. Those blows are other essays. They sit beside this one because the method did not invent every subtraction. It invented the subtraction that shows up as 1,034 to 627. Plague and panic took a school. Cut out and get out took a payroll.

Crossett, the encyclopedia is frank, was a company town: land, mills, residential real estate. Wilmar, on the 1907 evidence, was a mill town that still had other merchants and a cooperative warehouse. Ownership of the houses is a kind of planning. So is the later decision to telephone Yale. Cap Gates walked south from Wilmar to build the town that could make that call. He did not walk back with a forestry school under his arm. He sent for a forester after the first town’s woods were gone.

## Denuded is a landscape word

A documentarian who has walked cutover pine knows the look: stumps, slash, a sky that is suddenly too large, then, if the county is lucky, a second growth that is not the cathedral the first one was. Kenneth Smith’s *Sawmill*, about the last great virgin forest east of the Rockies, is the Ouachita book. Curry’s 1952 thesis is the Ashley-Bradley-Drew book. Clark’s *The Greening of the South* is the recovery book. This draft has not replaced them. It can still say that Wilmar’s surrounding woods, in the late 1920s, were a finished argument.

The Saline did not care. Rivers receive silt from a cutover as easily as from a farm. Woodard’s river article is the hydrology: winter and spring rises, a slackening after 1880, commerce diminished by the 1930s, fishing and recreation taking the channel. The *Gate City* sank in 1913. The logging road ignored the water except as a name. Cutover on the southwest border is a gift of mud the river did not request.

Farmers on the dividing ridge still planted cotton and corn. The *Advance* had already said agriculture was “the most important and stable of all industries,” and had given the county 15,000 to 20,000 bales in a normal year, 20,040 the previous year, 12,264 of them at the Monticello compress. Wilmar, Collins, and Tillar took the rest. The Farmer’s Union warehouse of 1907 had already announced that the town was never only a mill. After the mill, that announcement had to become a life.

Heady’s later industrial sentence — natural gas, a pipeline, a transmission station — sits under the 1940 and 1950 lifts, 695 and 746. Energy did not restore 1,034. It restored a few hundred and a reason not to vanish. That is a different method. It is not this essay’s job to bless it. It is this essay’s job to say the 1930 drop is the timber method, and the later lifts are someone else’s still.

## The method’s afterlife

Cut out and get out is finished, as official doctrine, in the firms that survived. It is not finished as a memory. People who left for Crossett, for Pine Bluff, for the plant in Monticello, for California, carried the method in their job histories. People who stayed carried it in a quieter way: fewer storefronts, a school consolidation, a census that kept stepping down after a brief mid-century lift.

Teske’s full table, if you read it aloud, is the long film: 844, 929, 1,034, 627, 695, 746, 718, 653, 747, 637, 571, 511, 395. The 1980 uptick to 747 is the number a careless narrative skips. Something — a plant, a road, a return, a count that caught people the 1970 count missed — lifted the town above 1970’s 653. This draft will not invent the something. Honesty is leaving the 1980 bar on the graph. The method explains 1920 to 1930. It does not explain every later tick.

Teske’s 2010 race line — 511 total, 134 white, 364 African American — prevents a reader from imagining the afterlife as a white mill remnant. The June Dinner’s thousands and the 2010 majority are the same town. Drew County as a whole, in 2020, was 65.1 percent white and 27.9 percent Black. Wilmar is not the county. It is a particular city on the west side, with a particular history of Black institutions and a particular out-migration. The table does not explain who left. It only records that leaving won the headcount.

A furniture shop in this county works in the second and third forests, not the first. If it takes a board from this ridge, the board is a crop or a remnant, not a virgin stick. That is the only honest soft-link this essay will allow. The method had a name. The town felt it. The river kept its own name. The shop borrowed the river’s. None of those sentences is a catalog.

## What the record will not say

It will not give a photograph of the cutover in this folder, and IMAGE_SOURCES will not allow a generated “old-timey mill” to stand in. It will not give a board-footage series for the Wilmar plant. It will not name the crews who cut the last virgin stands. It will not choose 1924 or the late 1920s as the single death year; both dates are in the sources, and both are true of different objects — mill, lands, equipment. It will not invent a quote from Balogh, Smith, Curry, or Darling and Bragg. Nelson’s 2026 adjectives are commentary. They are not 1924 fact.

The record will also not say the town died. 395 in 2020 is a count. The legal person of March 8, 1899, persisted. Churches Teske could still list in 2013 persisted longer than the whistle. The method relocated production. It did not erase a postmark. That distinction is the difference between Balogh’s “possible death” and a documentary that films the living.
