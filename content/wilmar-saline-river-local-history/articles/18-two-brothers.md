# Two Brothers

Steven Teske: two teen brothers, William and Henry Beavers, were lynched in the area two years apart, in 1890 and 1892. Each was accused of assaulting a white woman. Mary Heady’s county article adds a geographic seam: Henry Beavers was lynched in 1892, two years after his brother William was lynched in Bradley County.

The sentences are short. They should stay short. This essay will not reconstruct a night, a tree, a crowd, or a so-called crime. Lynching records in this country are often a white newspaper’s accusation plus a body. To novelize the accusation is to join the crowd. To omit the brothers from a Wilmar series is to join a different crowd, the one that wants the mill and the dinner and not the rope.

If the camera stops here, it stops on type. Two names. Two years. The word *teen*. A county line. An official pretext repeated. That is the film. There is no porch scene to invent, and no last word to ventriloquize.

## What the record, as cited here, will bear

- Names: William Beavers, Henry Beavers.
- Relation: brothers.
- Ages: “teen,” in Teske. No birth years, no household, no parents’ names in these two encyclopedia entries.
- Years: 1890, 1892.
- Counties: William in Bradley, in Heady; “in the area,” in Teske; Henry in the Wilmar/Drew orbit as Teske’s “two years apart” pair.
- Official pretext: alleged assault on a white woman, each. Heady does not name the accusers. Teske does not name the accusers. This draft will not name women the sources do not name, and will not treat the allegation as a finding.

That is the card. Everything else in this essay is adjacency: other dates, other names Heady lists, the method of not decorating a murder.

Heady’s wider list for Drew County is a calendar no civic club wanted. Buck Hunter, 1886, killed for allegedly threatening two white citizens. James Jones, 1895, killed after he had allegedly escaped from the Drew County Jail and injured a white man. James Redd and Alex Johnson, twice convicted of killing sawmill owner William Skipper, their convictions overturned both times, killed in their cell at the Monticello jail in 1898 while awaiting a third trial. Philip Slater, 1921, “the most recent lynching to occur in the county,” killed for allegedly assaulting an unnamed white woman and her children. The Beavers are not an isolated storm. They are two names in a weather system.

Heady calls the Redd–Johnson killing “bizarre.” The word is hers. This draft will not improve it with a corridor, a key, or a theory. Two African American men, two overturned convictions, a third trial pending, death in the jail. The official building is part of the calendar. Lynching is not only a woods.

## 1890, 1892 — and the town’s other dates

Anderson’s post office is 1884. Gates Lumber Company is established in Wilmar in 1890 — Teske’s year for the mill and Teske’s year for William Beavers. The Methodist church organizes 1893. Incorporation is March 8, 1899. Heady’s county sentence for the first large-scale sawmill is 1889, Gates, timber clear-cut, exhausted by the mid-1920s, lands sold to Crossett in 1924. William Beavers is killed in the same historical breath as the mill’s arrival. Henry is killed as the churches are organizing. A series that treats those as separate movies has chosen a lie of editing.

Bradley County, where Heady places William, is the next county west, Warren’s county, the Saline’s other mill shore. “The area” is Teske’s phrase and the right terrible phrase. The brothers’ deaths are a regional fact, not a town-limits fact. This pack’s job is Wilmar and the river. The river does not stop at the county line. Neither did the violence.

1890 is also, in the white civic story, a year of competitors’ sawmills and of a town not yet incorporated. 1892 sits between those mills and the Methodist organization. The encyclopedia does not say the mill owned the rope or that the church condemned it. This draft will not supply a causal arrow the sources do not draw. Juxtaposition is allowed. A screenplay is not.

Drew County’s 1890 census count, in Heady’s table, is 17,352. The 1900 count is 19,451. Wilmar’s first printed city count is 844 in 1900. The brothers die before the city has a municipal number. They die after the county has already been a county for forty-four years. The table does not have a cell for them. That is why this essay exists.

## The county’s longer weather

Heady’s Reconstruction paragraph is not a scene of 1890. It is the preface that makes 1890 less surprising than a booster history needs it to be. The Ku Klux Klan was active in Drew County during and after Reconstruction until around 1875. After violence and intimidation in the November 1868 election, Governor Powell Clayton declared martial law in fourteen counties, including Drew. Colonel Samuel W. Mallory led the state militia in southeastern Arkansas. Four prominent Drew County citizens met Clayton in Little Rock and offered a bipartisan home guard. Members of the militia and the home guard formed a military commission that tried, convicted, and executed one local Klansman for murdering a deputy sheriff and a Black man. Clayton revoked martial law in southeastern Arkansas in February 1869. A resurgence of the Klan, Heady writes, came in 1915 and dwindled by 1928. Teske puts a Wilmar chapter in 1923. Those later dates are the next essay. They are listed here so 1890 and 1892 are not orphaned from the county’s own article.

1860, in Heady: 5,581 white residents, 3,497 people held in bondage. The war’s last local shots came after the armies elsewhere had stacked arms, because word was slow. The Beavers are not Civil War dead. They are dead in a county that had already practiced the habit of deciding whose life counted.

Robert Hill of Drew County incorporated the Progressive Farmers and Household Union of America in 1918. A chapter of that union was at the center of the Elaine Massacre in Phillips County the following year. Heady records the Drew origin of the union without making Drew the massacre’s stage. This draft will follow her: adjacency, not appropriation.

## Method

If a later editor finds NAACP files, the *Monticello Advance* contemporary pages, or an Equal Justice Initiative marker text, those should be added as sources, not as color. This draft uses only the encyclopedia’s named sentences. That is a limitation. It is also a guardrail. DeArmond (1980) and DeBlack’s *With Fire and Sword* sit on Heady’s additional-information list. They have not been page-checked here for the Beavers. Do not pretend they have.

No photograph of a lynching will be sought for this pack’s figure list. PHOTO_NOTES is explicit: no reenactment lynching imagery. No generated image. No shop sentence. The style guide’s soft-link ban on this subject is not a courtesy. It is the difference between a history pack and a catalog that has lost its mind.

The close is the names and the years.

William Beavers, 1890. Henry Beavers, 1892. Teen brothers. William in Bradley County, in Heady; Henry two years later, in Teske’s pair. Each accused, in the record we have, of assaulting a white woman. The town went on to incorporate. Gates went on to cut. The June Dinner, if Teske’s nineteenth-century start holds, was already a fact in the same country. The record, such as it is, did not go on to give the brothers more than a clause. This series gives them an essay so the clause cannot be skipped.
