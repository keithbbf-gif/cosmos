# A Dollar an Acre

If the camera had a still for the beginning of Wilmar, it would not be a depot. It would be a price. In 1859 James Thomas Dionysius Anderson bought seven hundred acres in Drew County for a dollar an acre. Steven Teske, writing for the Encyclopedia of Arkansas, puts the figure down without romance, which is the correct way to put a frontier receipt on the table. A dollar then was not a metaphor. It was a man’s year measured against a county that had existed, as a county, for thirteen years.

Drew County had been cut from Bradley and Arkansas counties on November 26, 1846, and named for Governor Thomas Stevenson Drew. The seat of justice first sat at Rough and Ready, a village whose name the county histories do not bother to pretty. Fountain and Polly Austin gave eighty-three acres for Monticello in 1849. The town was incorporated in 1852. Anderson arrived on the western side of that new geometry, toward the Saline bottoms and the pine, not toward the brick square that would later call itself the center of everything.

He was not the first name on the ground. Andrew Govan had purchased land here in 1848. Collins Hemingway had held land since 1856. Teske lists them in that order, which is how a land-office story should be told: not firstness as destiny, but a sequence of entries. Anderson cleared five acres and planted corn. The crop was tended by five enslaved people whose names the encyclopedia, to its credit, refuses to lose: Simon and Lizza Taylor, Virginia and Wealthy Thurman, and Gill Gordon. The next essay in this series will stay with them. This one stays with the acre and the dollar, because the town that later printed itself as a mill town began as a farm that other people’s hands made possible.

Five acres is a small clearing in a seven-hundred-acre holding. The rest was woods, old field, and the kind of ground a later booster paper would call “heavily timbered” without walking it. Corn is not a picturesque crop. It is calories and fodder, a statement that a household intends to stay the winter. In the 1850s Drew County still thought of itself, when it thought of itself in print, as a cotton and corn county. Timber was already a major industry by the 1860s, Mary Heady writes, but it had not yet become the Iowa-and-Illinois syndicate story that would rename whole towns after mills. Anderson’s first act on the land was agricultural. The sawmill waited twenty-two years. That delay is the first fact a mill-town mythology likes to skip.

## A Presbyterian church in the year the war began

A Presbyterian church went up near Anderson’s land in 1861. The date is not a decoration. Arkansas had left the Union that spring. Drew County in 1860 counted 5,581 white residents and 3,497 people held in bondage. William Slemons and J. A. Rhodes sat for the county at the secession convention. The war that followed did not stage a set-piece battle on Anderson’s five acres. It did not have to. Skirmishes walked the county in 1864 and 1865; the last shots around Rough and Ready came after the armies elsewhere had already stacked their arms, because word was slow in the pine.

Anderson kept farming after the war. In 1869 he married Elizabeth Eliza Bird. The Birds would matter again when a college needed land. For the 1860s the useful fact is smaller: a man who had bought woods at a dollar an acre did not leave when the labor system that had planted his first corn was legally broken. He stayed. The county stayed. The pine, which the 1860s already treated as a second crop after cotton, stayed in the ground a little longer than the political order did.

Rebecca DeArmond’s *Old Times Not Forgotten* is the book the encyclopedia points to when a reader wants more than a paragraph. This pack does not pretend to have lifted her chapters onto the desk. What it can say, because Teske says it and the 1907 *Advance* later repeats a version of it, is that Anderson became the man on whose land the town was built. The souvenir edition of December 17, 1907, still calls him Capt. J. T. D. Anderson, first settler of the present town, owner of “practically all the land on which the town has been built,” storekeeper, postmaster, depot agent, the four jobs occupying one house after the Warren Branch of the Iron Mountain reached toward Warren.

That is a later photograph. In 1859 there was no Warren Branch to be an agent of. There was a price, a clearing, and a county that had not yet decided whether it would be remembered for cotton, for pine, or for a river that did not care what it was remembered for.

## What a dollar bought

A dollar an acre in 1859, in the West Gulf Coastal Plain, bought more than a surveyor’s joke. It bought timber that northern capital had not yet organized a railroad to steal at scale. It bought corn ground. It bought a position on the dividing ridge between Bayou Bartholomew’s fat alluvium to the east and the Saline’s narrower, heavier-timbered bottoms to the southwest. The 1907 *Advance*, in its booster voice, would later divide the county into those three parts: Bartholomew bottoms of about 100,000 acres, Saline bottoms of about 8,000, and the uplands between. Anderson’s purchase sat in the conversation between the second and the third.

It did not buy innocence. Five people in bondage made the first crop. A church went up in a war year. Two teen brothers, William and Henry Beavers, would be lynched in this country in 1890 and 1892, after Anderson’s post office had already named the place. The dollar does not wash those later years. It only starts the ledger.

## How a farm becomes a town site

The encyclopedia’s sentence is careful: the history of Wilmar is “generally said to begin” with Anderson. Generally said. The hedge is the historian’s honesty. Govan and Hemingway were already on the books. Native people had been forced out of the larger country by the Quapaw treaties of 1818 and 1824 and the removals of the 1830s; Caddoan pottery and the Hughes mound belong to the Saline’s northern story, not to a Drew County origin myth, but they belong to the river this pack is also about. Anderson is the beginning of *Wilmar* as a white land-and-mill story, which is not the same as the beginning of the ground.

He built a sawmill in 1881. He opened a post office in 1884 and named it for his oldest daughter. Competitors built mills in 1887 and 1890. A stave mill arrived in 1898. The city incorporated on March 8, 1899. Those dates are later chapters. They all sit on the 1859 receipt.

If we had the paper, we would film it the way Burns films a letter: close on the ink, then pull back to the county, then to the river that does not appear on a deed except as a boundary or a flood. We do not have the paper in this folder. We have Teske’s paragraph, DeArmond’s title, a booster newspaper that still needed Anderson alive in 1907 to bless the town’s firstness, and a census that would one day count 395 people where a mill once counted more than a thousand.

The 1907 paper, writing while Anderson still walked the town, needed him as founder and as living proof that the mill had chosen a liberal host. “It was probably his liberal policy to a great extent that determined the Lumber company to locate its plant at this point.” Probably. The booster’s adverb is doing work. Gates Lumber Company, when it came in 1890, came for pine and for a rail interchange, not for a character reference. But a man who owned the town site could make the coming easy or expensive. Anderson, the *Advance* says, made it easy. That is a political fact as much as a commercial one. Towns on the Warren Branch did not all become mill seats. Wilmar did.

A dollar an acre is a small sentence. It is also the only honest cold open this series has. Everything that follows — Gates, Beauvoir, a June table, a turbine on a gas line, a shop that took the river’s name — is what people did after the receipt was filed. The receipt, if it still exists, would be a poor photograph. The land is the better one. The names in the first field are the ones the photograph would have to include if it meant to tell the truth.
