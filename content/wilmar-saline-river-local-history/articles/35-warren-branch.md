# The Warren Branch

The 1907 *Advance* locates Wilmar with a rail sentence, not a river sentence: about eight miles west of Monticello on the Warren Branch of the Iron Mountain. The branch traversed the county from east to west. Monticello sat on it. Wilmar sat on it. Warren, in Bradley County, was the name of the line’s intention. A town that is “on the way to Warren” is a town that exists because a railroad needed stops.

Teske’s later sentence: the Wilmar and Saline Valley Railroad interchanged with the Iron Mountain, later the Missouri Pacific, at the Gates sawmill. The mainline name changed. The function did not. Boards went to a larger world. People went to Monticello and to Warren. Mail went to Willie Elvira’s cancellation.

Woodard’s river article puts the slackening of Saline shipping at the advent of the rail system in 1880. She does not name the Warren Branch in that clause. Locally the branch is the name that matters. The Saline had been a winter-and-spring road to Monroe and New Orleans. The Iron Mountain was a year-round road to whoever would buy a carload. Fifty-four documented steamboats are the older fleet. The branch is the newer one. Wilmar grew up on the newer.

## Iron Mountain, which was a company and a boast

The St. Louis, Iron Mountain and Southern is a long corporate story this essay will not retell. What matters locally is that south Arkansas’s timber boom, in Balogh’s telling, waited on rails. After the Civil War, small powered mills served only their neighborhoods. The means to reach northeastern and midwestern markets did not exist until railroad builders extended lines into Arkansas and Texas. Samuel Fordyce’s Cotton Belt — St. Louis to Pine Bluff, Camden, and on to Texas — is Balogh’s other spine. The Warren Branch is Drew’s. Without it, Anderson’s 1881 saw stays a neighborhood mill. With it, Gates can think in carloads.

Northern lumber men, Balogh writes, followed the mainlines: Rittenhouse, Embree, Weyerhaeuser, Watzek, Charles Warner Gates, Edward Savage Crossett. They bought timberlands, hired men, built feeder lines, raised large mills, and cut the virgin pine. The relationship was mutual. Railroads needed crossties and freight. Timber needed transportation and the metal-working skill of railroad shops. Wilmar is a small still of that mutual sentence. The Warren Branch gave the town a world. Gates’s later company road, the Wilmar and Saline Valley Railroad, chartered in 1904, gave the mill a woods. Twenty-five miles of track, four locomotives, fifty-five log cars, two loaders, interchange at the sawmill: Teske’s inventory. The branch did not have to go into the trees. The company road did.

The 1907 paper, promoting Monticello, already wanted a north-south road — the Arkansas, Louisiana and Gulf, surveyed Pine Bluff to Monroe — and reported grading toward Hamburg, trains hoped for between Monroe and Hamburg before winter, promoters announcing they would push on to Pine Bluff. Promoters always want a second line. Wilmar’s fate was tied to the first. When the mill left, the branch remained, thinner. A hoped-for north-south road is a county-seat vanity. An existing east-west branch is a mill town’s oxygen. The souvenir could afford to want both. Wilmar could not afford to lose the one it had.

## Two Salines, two railroads, one county line

The name work has to be done again, because readers lose counties. The Wilmar and Saline Valley Railroad is Gates’s logging road, Drew County, 1904, dead with the mill. The Warren and Saline River Railroad is Bradley County’s short line, incorporated August 7, 1905, as the Warren, Johnsville and Saline River, reorganized in 1920, a later remnant under other owners. The Warren Branch of the Iron Mountain is the third name and the oldest of the three as a town-maker: a mainline appendage that happens to be named for the same Warren. Three names. Three jobs. Confusing them is how a reader puts a steamboat on a logging road and a logging road on a passenger timetable.

Russell Tedder’s 2004 essay in the *Drew County Historical Journal* is about a fourth Drew County road — the Ashley, Drew and Northern, born as Crossett’s Crossett Railway, then Crossett, Monticello and Northern, then AD&N. That line is Crossett’s reach from the south. It is a neighbor, not a synonym. The W&SVR’s handshake was at Wilmar, with the Iron Mountain, at the mill. The AD&N’s story is Ashley County capital looking north. A map that paints all of these the same color is a map that has stopped being a map.

Missouri Pacific, in the later century, is the name on the waybills. The style guide is firm: say Iron Mountain becomes Missouri Pacific, and use the later name when the sentence is after the merger. Teske does that in one clause. This essay will not pretend to a current operating timetable. Shortline remnants and abandonments are a different map. The historical claim is Teske’s interchange and the *Advance*’s location sentence.

## A depot that was a house

Anderson as postmaster, storekeeper, depot agent, resident — the four-function building — is the human scale of the Warren Branch. The *Advance* says he opened the small store “soon after the extension of the Warren Branch to its present terminus of Warren,” and used the house which in 1907 still served as a depot for store, post office, depot, and residence. He owned practically all the land on which the town was built. It was “probably” his liberal policy that determined the lumber company to locate at this point. Probably: the booster’s adverb. Gates came for pine and for a rail interchange. A man who owned the town site could make the coming easy or expensive.

Willie Elvira Anderson is the daughter the post office was named for. The cancellation is a later essay’s still. Here it only has to say that the branch did not just carry boards. It carried mail, people, the 1907 drummer, the 1915 baseball clubs’ opponents from Monticello and Warren and Dermott, the children who would later ride to Drew Central. A railroad that is only a timber diagram has forgotten the depot-house.

Monticello, on the same branch, had the square, the waterworks, the cotton mill, the compress that took 12,264 bales the season before the souvenir. Wilmar had the mill and the siding. The branch made both of them possible in the form the souvenir could advertise. It did not make them the same town. Eight miles is a short ride and a long civic distance. The agricultural school of 1909 went to the county seat. The stave factory and the Farmer’s Union warehouse went to the mill stop. The branch served both arguments.

## Soft-link, one line

Hardwood that still leaves this ridge as a piece of furniture is more likely to leave on a truck than on the Warren Branch. The branch taught the county that wood travels. The shop lives in that lesson without needing a boxcar in the logo.

Balogh’s warning remains the harsher sentence. For towns whose livelihood relied on timber, relocation of production meant decline and possible death. Wilmar declined. The branch did not die on the same day the mill did. A siding can outlive a payroll. A city charter can outlive a siding’s importance. Teske’s modern Wilmar is “mostly a suburb of Monticello.” Suburbs are a kind of branch town that has learned to send people instead of boards. The Warren Branch, if a length of it still holds a rail, is a relic of the older sending. This pack will not invent a current train to make the relic feel useful.
