# The Last Free-Flowing River

Jann Woodard’s superlative is a legal and political fact, not a poem. The Saline is the last free-flowing river in the Ouachita basin. The Flood Control Act of 1937 proposed that every major stream in the basin be dammed. The Corps of Engineers studied an earthen dam with hydropower and recreation as justification. The plan died in Washington when someone noticed that Arkansas law prevented contact sports in lakes that generated electric power — a bureaucratic comedy Woodard records without smiling. Opposition peaked. Elected officials in several counties stood against. Support was thin, a few Benton residents. In 1973 Governor Dale Bumpers asked the Corps to restudy, citing central Arkansas’s long-range water needs. The Little Rock Water Department said it did not need the water. In February 1975 Governor David Pryor decided against the project. The fight, Woodard writes, was not easy, and the outcome was not assured until that decision.

A mill town in Drew County did not vote this river free. Counties along the whole length did the arguing. Wilmar’s interest, if it had one, is the interest of a place whose western horizon is this water: flood, silt, fish, a name on a shop. The dam that was not built is the reason the name still means a river and not a reservoir.

If the camera had a still for the refusal, it would not be a canoe. It would be a date. February 1975. Pryor. Against. Then Woodard’s clause: outcome not actually realized until then. Thirty-eight years after the Flood Control Act. Two years after Bumpers asked for a restudy. One sentence from a city water department that declined to be thirsty on cue.

## What a dam would have been

Hydropower, recreation, flood control — the mid-century trinity. An earthen dam, in the initial plan Woodard records. Contact sports as the legal snag is the sort of detail Burns would print on a card because it is too strange to invent. The later, more serious objection was water politics: Little Rock did not want to be the thirsty city in the justification. When the big city declined the water, the dam’s arithmetic failed.

Woodard does not name the reservoir. She does not print a crest elevation or a map of whose bottoms would have gone under. This draft will not invent a high-water line across Bradley or Drew. The plan is the plan as she states it: earthen, power, recreation, basin-wide ambition from 1937, local opposition, Benton as the thin yes, Bumpers 1973, Little Rock Water, Pryor February 1975.

1975 is after Wilmar’s mill, after the 1948 turbine station, during the Presbyterian church’s last years — Teske’s eight members, 1974. A free river is not a jobs program. It is a refusal. Refusals are part of small-town America’s record too, even when the refusal is made in Little Rock about a stream that begins above Benton.

Heady’s county table for 1970 is 15,157; for 1980, 17,910. Wilmar’s 1970 is 653; 1980, 747. The dam fight’s last year sits on the low side of those bars. A reservoir is sometimes sold as a recreation economy. Woodard’s support sentence is the opposite of a boom: only a select few Benton residents. Elected officials in several counties opposed. She does not name the officials. This draft will not invent a roster of judges and legislators. The opposition is a plural without a roll call.

## 1937 next to the boats’ ending

Woodard: commerce on the river diminished by the 1930s. Fifty-four documented steamboats; head of navigation at Bridges Bluff; the *Gate City* down on October 14, 1913, near Warren. Rail after 1880 had already slackened the shipping. The Flood Control Act arrives as the commercial river is already becoming a memory. A dam in that decade would have been a federal answer to a flood and a power need, not a revival of the Hampton brothers’ shipping. The trinity — flood, power, recreation — is what you build when cotton and staves have already left the water.

1937 is also nine years after Teske’s gas pipeline and nine years after Heady’s date for the county Klan dwindling. The basin’s mid-century future was concrete and turbines of a different kind. The Saline kept gradient instead. That is not because Drew County was virtuous. It is because the Corps’ plan failed a state law, then failed a city’s water department, then failed a governor.

Bumpers in 1973 is a restudy, not a blessing. Long-range need for water in central Arkansas is a serious sentence. Little Rock Water’s reply is the sentence that killed the seriousness as justification. Pryor in 1975 is the decision Woodard will not treat as predetermined. “Not easy.” “Not actually realized until.” Those adverbs are the historian’s honesty. A heritage series that writes the Saline as destined to be free has not read her.

Dunnahoo (1993) may have the fight in more pages. Unconsulted. Moseley (2015) may have folklore about the dam. Unused. The political film on this desk is Woodard’s paragraph.

## Unspoiled, which is a relative word

Woodard’s twenty-first-century close: commerce diminished by the 1930s; the river remains relatively unspoiled; the entire length is used mostly for fishing and recreation. Relatively. Mostly. The adverbs are doing work.

Comments on the encyclopedia page remember swimming at Prattsville in Grant County in the mid-1950s (David Young); hiking the Middle Fork toward a beginning near the Moonshine Shelter on the Ouachita Trail; swimming at White Oak Bluff and Mahoney’s ferry, steps cut with a hatchet, a tree swing, a picnic lunch (Tammy Davis, father from Grapevine). Another comment remembers a lifeless river in the 1930s, chemicals from a factory upstream, children forbidden the water (C. S. Stephens, mother and siblings). Relatively unspoiled includes both memories. This series will not sell a pristine stream. It will record, if it records anything, that the water still moves under its own gradient, and that some families were told not to touch it.

Encyclopedia page comments are comments, not archives. Attribute them as comments. Do not promote them to Corps files. Do not promote them to a water-quality study. They are oral color the way Perry’s Galveston account is oral history: say who said it, say where it was printed, do not novelize the factory or the hatchet.

Pearl fishing in the 1890s, a mussel near Benton, Jim Davidson, prices from $30 to $250, Professor J. H. Hinemon of the agricultural college as a proficient hunter — those are Woodard’s sentences and essay 33’s job. The dugout of 1999 is essay 34’s. Jenkins’ Ferry, Mount Elba, Marks’ Mills, Longview — Civil War along the banks, no major battle on the Saline itself, in Woodard — are essay 32’s. This essay will not steal them. It will say the free-flowing claim is the claim that lets those other essays still be about a river.

## What the name still means

A workshop named for the river is named for a survivor of the Corps. That is a better heritage sentence than a misty canoe. Use it once. Do not put a paddle on a product page. The style guide’s preferred host for a shop-name sentence is this kind of essay. The sentence is not a close. The close is Pryor’s month and year.

Wilmar, 395 people in 2020, sits inland of the bank. Teske never puts the city on Woodard’s community list. The dam that was not built did not spare a mill. The mill was already gone. The dam that was not built spared a gradient, a gravel bottom, a set of forks that still meet at Riverside, a marsh name, a French list, and a June town whose western weather is still a river and not a lake.

204 miles. About 3,350 square miles of watershed. Four forks. Seven counties. Last free-flowing in the basin. February 1975. Those are the cards. Film them in that order. No violin on the word *free*. The freedom is hydraulic and political. The dinner’s freedom is a different essay. Do not collapse them to make a theme.
