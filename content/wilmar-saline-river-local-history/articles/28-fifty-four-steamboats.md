# Fifty-Four Steamboats

Fifty-four is not a metaphor. Jann Woodard writes that fifty-four steamboats have been documented on the Saline. Among the captains: Bob Withers, Len Moore, John Wesley Smith, Frank Keeling. The Hampton brothers and John N. Wheeler were primary shippers until the early 1900s. The cargo, in winter and spring, was cotton, staves, and timber, bound for New Orleans and Monroe. The head of navigation was Bridges Bluff in Cleveland County. When the rail system arrived in 1880, the river’s argument weakened. One of the last boats up was the *Gate City*, which sank on October 14, 1913, near Warren.

A mill town eight miles from Monticello did not need to be a port to live on this fleet. Boards and staves that did not ride the Iron Mountain had ridden this water. The 1898 stave mill in Wilmar is a late cousin of a river trade Woodard puts in the steamboat century. Teske’s town history begins with a dollar an acre and a cornfield. The river history begins earlier and farther west: a 204-mile stream, a watershed of about 3,350 square miles, gravel bottom, pools and shoals, four forks meeting near Riverside, a mouth at Felsenthal where the Saline gives itself to the Ouachita. Wilmar sat inland of that career. The trees around it did not.

## Winter and spring, which is hydrology as a schedule

You ship when the water is up. That sentence is the whole difference between a river road and a rail road. The Iron Mountain did not wait on a rise. The Saline did. Fifty-four boats, over years, is a lot of waiting and a lot of nerve. A documentary would want a landing photograph: bales, barrels, a stack, mud. This pack has names.

Woodard’s season is not decoration. Cotton comes off in the fall. The river that can carry it is a winter and spring river on this reach. Staves and timber keep in the wet. A boat that tried the Saline in a dry August was a boat that learned the meaning of a bar. Bridges Bluff is the sentence that records the lesson: above that Cleveland County landing, the water was too thin, too shoaled, too much a mountain memory. Below it, export could begin.

Withers, Moore, Smith, Keeling — captains as a roll call, the Burns move. Hampton and Wheeler — shippers, which is to say capitalists of a local size. The Slavonian stave-cutters of the next essay arrive when this system is already losing to rail. They do not restore the fifty-four. They work the last of the stave woods.

The destinations matter as much as the captains. New Orleans is a Gulf. Monroe is a Louisiana town on the Ouachita. Neither is a Drew County square. The river’s economy was export. So was Gates’s. The difference is the vehicle and the century. A bale that left a Saline landing in March was already in another state’s conversation before a Wilmar commissary clerk had finished the week.

## What “documented” means

It means someone counted. It does not mean the list is closed. Woodard’s verb is a hedge and a boast at once. A later monograph may add a hull. Patrick Dunnahoo’s four-forks book and Ron Moseley’s *Ghosts of the Saline River* sit in her additional-information list; this series has Woodard’s paragraph, not their indexes. This series will not invent the fifty-fifth to look busy. Fifty-four is already enough to kill the idea that the Saline was only pretty.

Documented is also a word that excludes. It excludes the unnamed skiffs, the farm flats, the ferries that were not enrolled as steamboats. It excludes the twenty-four-foot dugout Charles Green found at Peeler Bend in 1999, tooled in the fashion of the Caddo and dated to the 1100s. That boat is an earlier essay’s job and a later one. Here it only has to say that “fifty-four” is a steamboat count, not a history of every hull the river has held.

Woodard’s community list along the working river is Haskell, Traskwood, Prattsville, Farindale, Staves, Rison, Warren, Longview. Wilmar is not on it. Wilmar is inland, on the Warren Branch of the Iron Mountain, toward the timber and not at the wharf. A series about Wilmar and the Saline that never says this will treat the town as a port it was not. The honest claim is smaller and heavier: the town lived in the Saline’s woods. The boats lived on the Saline’s water. The cargo was the same trees.

## 1880, which is a year and a verdict

Shipping on the river, Woodard writes, slacked off considerably with the advent of the rail system in 1880. She does not name the company in that sentence. Locally the name that matters is the St. Louis, Iron Mountain and Southern, whose Warren Branch would cross Drew County east to west and give Anderson a depot to live in. George Balogh’s timber article is the statewide version of the same year: northern lumber capital waited on rails; once the mainlines were in, the mills could think in carloads. The Saline had thought in rises.

1880 is not the death of the fleet. It is the beginning of the argument’s loss. Thirty-three years later the *Gate City* was still coming up. That is a long twilight. A boat still working in 1913 is stubbornness, a contract, or a rise too good to waste. The wreck is the next essay. The career is this one.

Two railroads will try to steal this paragraph if a writer is careless. The Wilmar and Saline Valley Railroad, chartered in 1904 for Gates Lumber Company, was a logging tool that used the valley as letterhead. The Warren and Saline River Railroad, incorporated in Bradley County in 1905 as the Warren, Johnsville and Saline River and reorganized in 1920, is a different road with a different owner and a later remnant. Neither is a steamboat. Neither should be allowed to blur the fifty-four. The company cars did not need the water. The boats had been the water.

## Cotton, staves, timber — three words that are three jobs

Cotton is a farm word. Drew County, the 1907 *Advance* said, yielded 15,000 to 20,000 bales in a normal year; 20,040 the year before the souvenir. Much of that crop went to Monticello’s compress. Some went to Wilmar, Collins, Tillar, Dermott. Before the Warren Branch, a portion of the western county’s cotton had a wetter option. Woodard puts cotton first in the winter-and-spring cargo. The river did not invent the staple. It carried it.

Staves are barrel stock. Hardwood bottoms, oak especially. The *Advance* called stave timber “no small industry for many years” in Drew County. Teske dates a stave mill in Wilmar to 1898. The 1907 factory claimed 10,000 a day. Those are land numbers. Woodard’s river sentence is the older cousin: staves shipped down to Monroe and New Orleans when the water allowed. The Slavonian families — Lipovac, Karlovic, Kusterin, Dorich, Piskulick — arrive in her paragraph as the later labor of that sentence. This essay names them so the next one does not have to invent a beginning.

Timber is the word that later ate the other two in memory. Pine in the Saline bottoms, the *Advance* said, and in the contiguous territory, and in the south of the county, most of it by 1907 under Gates at Wilmar. A log that rode a steamboat in 1875 and a log that rode a Gates flatcar in 1907 are the same species of taking. The vehicle changed. The export did not.

## After the last stack

Commerce on the river, Woodard says, diminished by the 1930s. Fishing and recreation took the channel. The *Gate City* is the wreck that stands for the end. The fifty-four are the career. Wilmar’s railroad charter of 1904 is, in this light, not a beginning of extraction but a change of vehicle. The trees were already leaving. They changed horses.

The 1930s are also Teske’s census drop: 1,034 people in Wilmar in 1920, 627 in 1930. The mill’s method and the river’s slack are not the same event. They rhyme. Cutover woods send less to a landing. A landing with less to send stops needing a stack. Recreation is what a documentary films when the commerce has gone and the water is still there. Woodard’s twenty-first-century close is that the river remains relatively unspoiled and that its entire length is used mostly for fishing and recreation. Relatively is the word that keeps the chemicals of a 1930s commenter in the file. This essay will not sell a pristine stream. It will keep the count.

A shop named for the river works in the recreation century with a memory of the export century. The name is older than the shop and heavier than a canoe. No SKU.
