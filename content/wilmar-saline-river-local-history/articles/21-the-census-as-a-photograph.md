# The Census as a Photograph

The Encyclopedia of Arkansas prints Wilmar’s decennial counts in a grid that looks like a hymn board. 1900: 844. 1910: 929. 1920: 1,034. 1930: 627. 1940: 695. 1950: 746. 1960: 718. 1970: 653. 1980: 747. 1990: 637. 2000: 571. 2010: 511. 2020: 395. If you read the numbers aloud, you can hear the mill, the Depression, a mid-century plateau, a 1980 ghost of recovery, and then the long stepping-down that Rex Nelson, in 2026, called dying.

Nelson is a columnist. This series is not required to take his verb. 395 living people are not a death. They are a count. The count is smaller than the 1920 count by 639. That subtraction is the story the table will tell even if every essay in this pack is burned.

Teske’s modern header cells are the other photograph: 33°37’44″N, 91°55’53″W; elevation 154 feet; area 1.53 square miles in 2020; incorporation March 8, 1899. A city that small in acreage can still hold a mill, a college, a park, a dinner of thousands, and a transmission station. The table does not measure those. It measures heads on a April morning every ten years.

## How to read a peak

1,034 in 1920 is the still of Gates’s last good decade, Beauvoir already gone, the W&SVR still theoretically useful, the Klan year still three years ahead. It is also just a thousand people. American memory inflates mill towns. Wilmar at its census peak was a large village with a factory and a park and a memory of a college. It was never Little Rock. It was never even a lasting Crossett.

The climb to that peak is modest. 844 to 929 is eighty-five people. 929 to 1,034 is 105. Incorporation in 1899 sits just before the first printed city count. The 1890 cell is a dash. Teske’s history does not invent an 1890 municipal number. This draft will not either. The town existed. The table begins when the Census Bureau began treating it as a place with a row.

627 in 1930 is the cutover. Four hundred and seven people gone in ten years. Balogh’s timber-industry warning, as this pack uses it elsewhere, is relocation of production, decline, possible death. Wilmar declined. The Arabic numerals are the method photographed. Heady’s county sentence for Gates is the same decade in other words: clear-cut, no replenishing, timber exhausted by the mid-1920s, lands sold to Crossett in 1924. Teske: equipment to Crossett in the late 1920s after the Wilmar woods had significantly declined. The table agrees with both paragraphs without needing their nouns.

The 1940 and 1950 lifts — 695, 746 — sit on Teske’s other industrial sentence: natural gas, a pipeline in 1928, a transmission station since 1948. Energy did not restore 1,034. It restored a few hundred and a reason not to vanish. 1960’s 718 and 1970’s 653 are the plateau leaning down. Essay 22 will stay with the turbine. This essay only needs the bars.

## The county table, so the town is not an orphan

Heady’s Drew County decennials are a different film, longer and flatter at the top.

1850: 3,276. 1860: 9,078. 1870: 9,960. 1880: 12,231. 1890: 17,352. 1900: 19,451. 1910: 21,960. 1920: 21,822. 1930: 19,928. 1940: 19,831. 1950: 17,959. 1960: 15,213. 1970: 15,157. 1980: 17,910. 1990: 17,369. 2000: 18,723. 2010: 18,509. 2020: 17,350.

The county’s peak is 1910, ten years before Wilmar’s. The county’s 1930 drop is real and smaller in percentage than Wilmar’s. The county’s 1980 uptick (17,910) and Wilmar’s 1980 uptick (747) share a decade and may not share a cause. This draft will not invent the cause. Honesty is printing both tables and leaving the rhyme unforced.

2020 county characteristics, Heady: 17,350 people; 11,291 white (65.1 percent); 4,846 African American (27.9 percent); American Indian 90; Asian 108; Native Hawaiian or other Pacific Islander 13; some other race 366; two or more races 636; Hispanic origin (any race) 656; density 20.9 per square mile; median household income (2019) $46,997; per capita (2015–2019) $25,543; poverty (2019) 18.8 percent. Wilmar is not the county. It is a particular city on the west side, 1.53 square miles of that 828.66.

## Race, which the 2010 line records

Teske gives a 2010 breakdown: 511 total, 134 white, 364 African American. The 2020 table in the encyclopedia does not repeat the race line in the same cell. The 2010 line is enough to prevent a reader from imagining Wilmar as a white mill remnant. The June Dinner’s thousands and the 2010 majority are the same town.

A reader who only has the county percentages will get Wilmar wrong. 65.1 percent white is Drew. 364 of 511 is Wilmar in 2010. The dinner, the Colored School ground, the Black nine at Hudspeth Park, and the 2010 cell are one argument. The table is the argument’s least sentimental form.

The table does not explain who left. It only records that leaving won. Out-migration after a mill is a textbook plot. Out-migration after a mill in a majority-Black city is also a plot, and the textbook is thinner. This draft will not fill the thinness with a novel of Chicago and Detroit. Beverly Railey’s PBS sentence — “Chicago, I’m a visitor. Wilmar, I’m home” — is the oral version of a cell the census does not print: people who left and still count themselves in June.

## 1980, the odd uptick

747 in 1980 is the number a careless narrative skips. Something — a plant, a road, a return, a count that caught people the 1970 count missed — lifted the town above 1970’s 653 by ninety-four souls. The county lifted too, 15,157 to 17,910. Burlington Industries, in Heady, was a major employer in the area until the plant closed in 2001; the Anderson Cotton Mill of 1900 had traded hands on the way to that name. Whether Wilmar’s 1980 bar is Burlington’s bar is a question for employment files, not for a guess in this paragraph.

1990: 637. 2000: 571. 2010: 511. 2020: 395. After 1980 the step-down is almost even, fifty to seventy people a decade, then 116 between 2010 and 2020. Nelson, in 2026, saw the last number and reached for *dying*. The table, read aloud, is quieter. It is a city getting smaller on a fixed acreage.

Teske’s 2020 header still calls Wilmar a city: incorporation date intact, coordinates intact, 1.53 square miles intact. A municipal charter does not dissolve when the hymn board loses a hundred names. The Encyclopedia of Arkansas also still leads the town paragraph with the June Dinner, not with the 395. That editorial order is a fact about the entry. A reader who only remembers Nelson’s verb has read a column. A reader who reads Teske’s grid and Teske’s first paragraph has read the town the encyclopedia was willing to print.

## What a furniture lane may say, once

A shop that works in this county in the 2020s works in a 395-person city’s hinterland, or in the county’s 17,350, or in the mail. It does not restore a census. Heritage copy that implies otherwise is a lie. The table is the photograph. The shop is a household. Those are different scales. One sentence. No SKU.

The still, if this were a Burns hour, would be the encyclopedia grid, then a finger on 1,034, then a finger on 395, then Teske’s 2010 race line, then stop. No violin swell. No civic sermon on the last bar. 395 people, a June table that can still draw thousands, a turbine on a line, churches Teske could still list in 2013. The table is not the whole town. It is the town’s longest film.
