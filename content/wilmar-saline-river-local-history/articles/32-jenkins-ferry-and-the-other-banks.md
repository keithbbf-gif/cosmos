# Jenkins’ Ferry and the Other Banks

Jann Woodard’s Civil War paragraph is a study in the word *minor*. There were no major battles along the Saline, she writes, and then: skirmishes and engagements at Jenkins’ Ferry in Grant County, Mount Elba and Marks’ Mills in Cleveland County, and Longview in Ashley County. Mary Heady’s Drew County calendar is longer and more local: multiple 1864 and 1865 clashes, the last at Rough and Ready after the war was over elsewhere. Thomas DeBlack’s *With Fire and Sword* is the book that holds the state. This essay will not restage the Camden Expedition. It will say that the river which later carried staves had already carried armies.

The 1907 *Advance*, writing booster history from Monticello, said no battles were fought in Drew County, though there were a few skirmishes, and that the country was “practically drained of able bodied men.” Companies, the paper named, were raised by Captains Jackson, Haslip, Whittington, Isom, Thomas, Ragland, Owens, Wolfe, Hellum, Reed, Miller, Taylor, Henry, and “Pink” Burks. That is a souvenir’s war: a roster and a denial. Heady’s encyclopedia article does not deny the dates. Woodard’s river article does not deny the crossings. DeBlack’s monograph is the place a reader goes when a paragraph is not enough. This pack has not quarried his pages for invented weather, invented dialogue, or a boy on a pontoon. The book is cited as the statewide narrative. The local sentences stay with the names and the calendar.

## Jenkins’ Ferry, April 1864

The Camden Expedition’s retreat, the muddy crossing, the Union and Confederate dead — Jenkins’ Ferry is a major fight by any ordinary measure, which is why Woodard’s “no major battles along the Saline” needs a reader’s raised eyebrow. She means, perhaps, that the Saline was not Vicksburg. Jenkins’ Ferry was still a killing on this water. Grant County is the Saline’s middle. A Wilmar series includes it because the river includes it.

The Encyclopedia of Arkansas’s engagement article — the same house as Woodard and Heady — puts the fight on April 29–30, 1864. Major General Frederick Steele’s Federals, falling back from Camden toward Little Rock after the losses at Poison Spring and Marks’ Mills, reached a swollen Saline and began a pontoon. Confederate commands under Edmund Kirby Smith, Sterling Price, and John Sappington Marmaduke caught the rear. The Union line held long enough to finish the crossing. The surviving column reached Little Rock on May 3. A small ground near Sheridan is now Jenkins Ferry Battleground State Park. Those are catalog facts. They are not a restaging. They are why *minor* will not do.

Mount Elba and Marks’ Mills are Cleveland County names that belong to the same disastrous spring. Longview, down toward the mouth, is an Ashley County landing and a fight. The Saline’s war is a string of crossings. Crossings are where you put a ferry and where you put a grave.

Marks’ Mills, on April 25, was a Confederate ambush of a Union supply train. The encyclopedia’s action article records a Confederate victory and the accusation that Confederate soldiers massacred African Americans there, a companion atrocity to Poison Spring. This essay will not reconstruct that crime beyond the record’s naming of it. It will not invent a victim list to thicken a sentence. It will not skip the sentence. A river series that loves steamboats and cannot say that Black soldiers and freedpeople were killed on the approaches to this water has chosen a prettier river than the one that exists.

Longview, in the northwest corner of Ashley County on the Saline, had a Confederate pontoon linking Camden’s camps to the Mississippi at Gaines’ Landing. On March 30, 1864, a Union cavalry raid out of Pine Bluff — the same spring’s work — destroyed that bridge. Mount Elba, the same day in Cleveland County, is the other half of that operation in the campaign timeline. Woodard’s four names are not four random skirmishes. They are the Saline’s week in the Camden Expedition, plus the Grant County crossing that ended it. The river was a military road before it was a stave road.

## Drew’s own calendar

Heady’s dates — January 13–14, March 18, June 17, September 9–11, 1864; January 26–31, March 21–23, May 16, May 23–27, 1865 — are the county’s. They are not Woodard’s four. They are smaller, closer, the kind of fighting that does not get a state park and does get a farm burned or a road closed. The last Rough and Ready firing, after Appomattox, is the southeast’s isolation as a military fact. Union troops searching for Confederate colonel William F. Slemons encountered Confederate soldiers at the old seat of justice; shots were fired. Word was slow. People died in the lag.

Slemons is the name the United Daughters of the Confederacy kept. Heady writes that he owned one enslaved person in the 1860 census; J. A. Rhodes, the county’s other secession-convention delegate, owned twenty. Slemons led the Second Arkansas Cavalry, men from Drew and Dallas counties, and in 1863 was assigned a brigade, the highest-ranking officer from the county. A river essay that only follows colonels is a Lost Cause cruise. This one names the engagements and then returns to the fact that Anderson’s church went up in 1861 on land five enslaved people worked: Simon and Lizza Taylor, Virginia and Wealthy Thurman, Gill Gordon. Teske kept those names. They did not get a UDC chapter.

Drew County in 1860, Heady writes, counted 5,581 white residents and 3,497 people held in bondage. The county had been formed in 1846 from Bradley and Arkansas counties, named for Governor Thomas Stevenson Drew. Heavily dependent on enslaved labor: that is Heady’s sentence, not a later mood. The war that followed did not have to stage a set-piece on Anderson’s five acres. It had the county already.

The *Advance*’s 1860 population figure is 9,087; Heady’s table is 9,078. The one-person-or-nine-person seam is a reminder that even the county’s size is a little unstable in print. The enslaved count is not a souvenir figure. It is the condition under which the first corn at Wilmar was possible and under which Slemons and Rhodes sat down to vote the state out.

## After the last shot

Reconstruction in Drew, in Heady’s paragraph, is not a fade. The Ku Klux Klan was active during and after Reconstruction until around 1875. After violence and intimidation in the November 1868 election, Governor Powell Clayton declared martial law in fourteen counties, including Drew. Colonel Samuel W. Mallory led the state militia in the southeast. Four prominent citizens offered Clayton a bipartisan home guard; a military commission tried, convicted, and executed one local Klansman for murdering a deputy sheriff and a Black man. Clayton revoked martial law in the southeast in February 1869. A later Klan resurgence, Heady writes, came in 1915 and dwindled by 1928. Teske puts a chapter in Wilmar in 1923. The river’s war and the county’s night-riding are not the same file. They are the same country.

A free-flowing river in 1975 is a political survivor. A county that shot into May 1865 is a communications failure. Both belong to the same water’s country. The Saline that Woodard calls the last free-flowing river in the Ouachita basin is the Saline that Steele’s engineers pontooned when it was up in April 1864. Hydrology does not care which army needs the rise. Shippers in the next century would wait on the same season.

No shop. No “brothers’ war” music. The still is a ferry name on a map and Heady’s last date. Do not use this essay to sell furniture. The style guide is not being delicate. Lynching, the Klan, secession, and the labor of bondage are history, not spice. A wreck essay refuses invented passengers. A war essay refuses invented last words. The names Woodard listed, the dates Heady listed, and the book DeBlack wrote are the file. A generated battle smoke is a lie. A USGS bend labeled Jenkins’ Ferry is enough.
