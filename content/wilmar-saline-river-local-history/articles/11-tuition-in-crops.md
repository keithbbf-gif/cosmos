# Tuition in Crops

Paul Edwards lists the payment methods as if he were copying a catalog, which he is. Students at Beauvoir College could pay in cash. They could pay in crops. They could pay in livestock. They could work on campus. If they could not pay, they could pay after graduation, once employed. The college offered job placement. In a mill town that also marketed cotton, this is not a charming eccentricity. It is how a school meets a household that has a bale and a shoat and not always a dollar.

The 1907 *Advance*, describing Drew County’s farms in the same issue that eulogizes Beauvoir’s departure, is a hymn to yield: a bale and forty to fifty bushels of corn on Bayou land; half a bale and twenty to thirty bushels on ordinary upland; L. D. Owens, under a new government demonstration, a little over forty-eight bushels of corn; John Scharff on Long Prairie, forty to sixty. Those numbers are booster numbers. They are also the units a bursar would have understood. A school that takes crops is a school that has decided the county’s real currency is not specie.

The previous essay stayed with the name and the color line. This one stays with the ledger. The two are not separable. “The Pioneer Boarding School in Southeast Arkansas for Whites” is the catalog’s door. Crops and livestock are the catalog’s price. Both sentences were printed for the same households.

## Who this was for, and who it was not

Edwards quotes the pioneer phrase so that no later writer can pretend the sliding scale was a Reconstruction project. Black Drew County had Monticello Academy, opened in 1888 by Charles Mebane of Holmes Chapel Presbyterian, with help from Mary Emilie Holmes and the Presbyterian Board of Missions for Freedmen. It lasted until 1933. It is not Beauvoir. It is the other half of the sentence about higher schooling in this county. A series that talks about tuition in pigs without mentioning the academy is doing the catalog’s work.

Beauvoir’s generosity, such as it was, was intra-racial and intra-class in a particular way: rural white youth who could not write a check. That is a real population. It is the population the 1909 agricultural schools were created to serve with public money. Spence, Edwards argues, had already seen the need. The state later saw it and put the campus in Monticello, which had won a bidding process. Wilmar had already spent its private college.

The June Dinner’s ground at the Wilmar Colored School is the local Black institution this pack will not flatten into a footnote. Oral history puts the dinner in the late nineteenth century; Arkansas PBS recorded Mayor Toni Perry and others in 2023–2024. Oral history is oral history. Say so. The dinner is not a bursar. It is a table. The academy is the named school on the other side of the color line. Beauvoir’s sliding scale did not slide across that line.

Teske’s 2010 race line — 511 people, 364 African American — is a later still of the town that once boarded 469 white students on a campus eight miles from the seat. The 1900 city count was 844. For a moment the college was a second population. It was not the town’s only population. The five names in Anderson’s first field — Simon and Lizza Taylor, Virginia and Wealthy Thurman, Gill Gordon — are the first workforce the origin story admits. They did not get a tuition plan. They got a sentence in Teske. Keep both facts on the table.

## A campus as a farm

If students paid in crops and livestock, someone had to receive, store, feed, and sell. The encyclopedia does not give us the barn. The catalogs at Taylor Library might. Until they are read, the honest statement is: a payment system implies an agricultural plant. The artesian well A. H. Gates provided — 300,000 gallons of capacity, in Edwards — is the one industrial-scale gift we can name. Water for a boarding school is not a metaphor. It is kitchens, laundry, and livestock.

Judge I. A. Bird and Major J. T. D. Anderson donated the property. Anderson had planted corn on five acres in 1859. The first crop Teske records on the Wilmar ground is the crop a bursar would later accept as tender. That is not a pretty symmetry. It is the same county using the same unit across fifty years. Corn first, then lumber, then tuition in corn again. The 1907 paper still thought agriculture was “the most important and stable of all industries,” even while it dated Wilmar’s “real history” from Gates.

Campus employment is the other ledger. A student who cannot pay can work. That sentence has a long American career, some of it honorable, some of it extraction. We do not have wages or hours. We have the offer. Job placement after graduation is the offer’s second clause. A school that promises a job is competing with the mill and the farm, not pretending to be above them. Gates’s commissary, Kidd Bros., was already teaching the town what a payroll looks like. Beauvoir’s placement office — if the catalogs use that word; this draft will not invent the letterhead — is the school’s version of a future.

The 1907 civic inventory is the town the bursar lived in: stave factory at 10,000 a day, bank at $25,000, five general stores, a drugstore, a livery, a blacksmith, a Farmer’s Union warehouse of a thousand bales, the only one in the county that year. Drew County yielded 15,000 to 20,000 bales in a normal year; 20,040 the previous year; 12,264 marketed at Monticello by compress receipt; the rest at Wilmar, Collins, Tillar, some at Dermott. A thousand-bale warehouse in a mill town is a farm building. A college that takes a bale is a farm institution that happens to teach elocution.

## Geography the bursar already knew

The *Advance* divided the county into three, and a bursar who took crops would have felt the division in the wagon. Bartholomew bottoms: about 100,000 acres, “immensely rich,” a bale and forty to fifty bushels of corn, rent at five dollars. Saline bottoms: about 8,000 acres, poorer for the plow, heavy with timber. Uplands: the dividing ridge, sandy loam, half a bale and twenty to thirty bushels of corn, or better if a man would learn. Monticello sat on the highest part of that ridge. Wilmar sat eight miles west, toward the timber. Long Prairie, where John Scharff from Indiana “never raised better corn,” lay sixteen miles south in the paper’s figure. L. D. Owens’s demonstration farm sat four miles north of Monticello, ordinary upland, a little over forty-eight bushels, four bales on five acres.

Those names are booster names. They are also the only named yields this pack has. A. J. Wilson, the paper’s “Peach King of South Arkansas,” 20,000 trees, a certificate from the 1904 St. Louis world’s fair hanging in the circuit clerk’s office, is fruit, not tuition. Heady’s shorter crop list — cotton, corn, wheat, hay, apples, peaches, pears, tomatoes — is the mix without the adjectives. The bursar’s units, if Edwards copied the catalog correctly, were crops and livestock, not Elberta peaches. The peach certificate is here to keep the county from looking like only pine and only cotton.

Mary Heady’s modern geography is the cleaner coat: Drew County at the edge of the Mississippi Alluvial Plain in the West Gulf Coastal Plain; Bayou Bartholomew on the east; the Saline on the southwest; the Monticello Ridge running from north of Star City into Louisiana. A student who paid in a shoat was paying from one of those three parts. The catalog does not say which. This draft will not assign a hog a township.

## What the Panic did to a crop bursar

The Panic of 1907 is the next essay’s storm. It belongs in a tuition essay because a school that lives on crops lives on prices. When a national credit crisis hits a timber county, the mill slows, cotton buyers tighten, and a shoat is not a reliable note. Edwards says the panic devastated the local economy and forced the college to close. Teske pairs it with the 1905 smallpox. A sliding scale cannot slide below a household’s ruin.

Smallpox had already taken forty lives in Wilmar, including a Beauvoir student and Spence’s five-year-old son. Enrollment nonetheless reached its apex in 1906–07: 469. Bryan came in 1906. The school kept its doors open in the grief. That is either duty or numbness or the simple fact that a campus with livestock for tuition cannot pause without starving. The Panic closed what the pox did not.

Spence dismantled half the buildings and moved them to Monticello. The 1907 paper, dated December 17, already has him in the seat as superintendent, Beauvoir “abandoned on the removal,” Wilmar High School under W. B. Massey, two hundred students in round numbers. The payment system did not move. The Fourth District State Agricultural School, after Act 100 of 1909, would charge in the state’s way. The private experiment — take the county’s actual produce — lasted as long as the county’s private boom.

William Tyler Spence’s 1958 *AHQ* article may describe the barn, the well, the work hours. This draft has not used that article as a quarry. The catalogs of 1904–5 and 1906–7 sit at Taylor Library. Until they are read, Edwards’s list is the ledger: cash, crops, livestock, campus work, pay later, placement. No invented fee. No invented hog weight. No reconstructed quarrel with a parent at the door.

## A farm sentence for a furniture lane

This pack’s farm essays are not seed catalogs. They are about the units people used to stay alive and to get a child out of the row for a winter of books. Tuition in crops is the cleanest still we have of that bargain. A later shop that works wood on this ridge is in the same county as that bargain. It is not the heir of Beauvoir. It is another household trying to turn what the ground and the shop will yield into a life. That is as close as this essay will come to a brand.

The first corn on this ground was made by people who could not leave. The later corn that paid a tuition was made by households the catalog would admit. Both facts are the farm history. A bursar’s kindness that stops at a color line is still a bursar’s kindness inside the line. Say the line. Then say the shoat. Then stop.
