# A Turbine on the Line

Steven Teske’s industrial second act is easy to miss if you are in love with pine. In 1928 the Arkansas-Louisiana Gas Company laid a pipeline meant to connect northern Louisiana supply with St. Louis customers. Since 1948 that line has been supplied by the Wilmar Transmission Station, which includes, Teske writes, the first turbine pump constructed on a natural gas pipeline in the United States. The pumping and distribution system based in Wilmar belongs, in his modern sentence, to CenterPoint Energy.

William J. Boyd and David T. Hyatt Jr. told the county’s energy story in the *Drew County Historical Journal* in 2010: “Drew County’s Role in Meeting Energy Needs,” pages 10–13. Teske cites them. This draft has Teske’s summary, not their diagrams. The journal is the place to go for the engineering. The local-history job is to put the station next to the census. 627 in 1930. 695 in 1940. 746 in 1950. The lift is modest and real. A town that had lost a mill kept a job that did not look like a mill.

The first-turbine clause is a superlative. Superlatives earn a second citation. Boyd and Hyatt have not been opened on this desk. A company history, a trade-journal page, or CenterPoint’s own archive may confirm or trim Teske’s wording. This pack flags the need and still reports what the encyclopedia reports. Do not upgrade “Teske writes” into “the historical record proves” until the second source is in hand.

## A pipeline is a different kind of railroad

The Iron Mountain took boards north and east. The gas line took fuel through. Wilmar, in both cases, is a point on someone else’s longer intention. St. Louis did not know the daughter’s name. It needed compression. Northern Louisiana did not incorporate the city. It needed a path. The first turbine pump is the sort of claim a company history loves and a town can still use. If Teske and the *Journal* are right, a national first sat in a city that the timber writers had already begun to treat as finished.

Energy work is quieter than a saw. Fewer men, more specialized, a fence, a hum. It does not fill a stave mill’s boardinghouses. It can keep a tax base and a reason for a road. Teske’s “fortunes improved” is the encyclopedia’s allowed optimism. It is not 1,034 people. It is not nothing.

1928 is four years after Heady’s date for the Gates lands going to Crossett, four years after Darling and Bragg’s mill-closure note as this pack cites it, and the same year Heady gives for the county Klan resurgence dwindling. A documentarian could cheat and call the pipe a replacement for the mill and a replacement for the klavern. It is neither. It is a second extractive geography: hydrocarbons instead of cellulose, Louisiana instead of the Saline bottoms, St. Louis instead of a lumberyard. Wilmar’s talent, if the word is not too much, was to be in the way of infrastructure. Rail. Then gas. The river, which had been infrastructure, was already losing boats. Woodard: commerce on the Saline diminished by the 1930s.

The Wilmar and Saline Valley Railroad had been chartered in 1904 to carry Gates lumber. By 1907 it owned twenty-five miles of track, four locomotives, fifty-five log cars, two loaders, and an interchange with the Iron Mountain at the mill. Teske: equipment transferred to Crossett in the late 1920s after the Wilmar woods had significantly declined. The gas line’s installation year sits on that transfer’s decade. One right-of-way fading. One right-of-way arriving. They are not the same company and not the same cargo.

## 1948, which is a different still

Twenty years between the pipe and the station Teske dates. 1928 is the line. 1948 is the Wilmar Transmission Station supplying that line. The first turbine pump, in Teske’s grammar, is part of the station. A later editor should keep those three facts from collapsing into one ribbon-cutting. A pipeline can exist without a named station. A station can exist without being first. The claim that wants confirmation is the firstness, not the existence of a Wilmar point on a north-south gas geography.

CenterPoint is a present-tense owner, and present-tense owners change names. Arkansas-Louisiana Gas Company is the 1928 name Teske prints. CenterPoint is the modern name Teske prints. The historical claim that matters is the 1928 line, the 1948 station, and the turbine sentence. PHOTO_NOTES wants a corporate-archive still of the station. IMAGE_SOURCES says ask; do not scrape. This draft will not caption a generated turbine as a 1948 object.

746 in 1950 is the highest bar after the cutover. 718 in 1960. 653 in 1970. The station did not make a city. It made a plateau that then leaned. 747 in 1980 is the odd later lift this pack will not assign to gas without an employment file. Teske does not assign it. Neither will this essay.

## What the station is not

It is not a college. Beauvoir’s 469 students in 1906–07 were a second population. A transmission station is a workforce measured in specialized jobs, not in a boarding department.

It is not a June table. The dinner draws thousands on a school ground. The station draws a fence and a hum. Teske can list both in one town article. They do not fund each other in any source on this desk.

It is not a reason to call the town a city of the future. Heady’s county industrial list — Brick Manufacturing (1903), Southern Compress (1892–1940), Drew Cotton Seed Oil Mill (1890s–1960s), Anderson Cotton Mill to Burlington until 2001, MonArk / SeaArk boats — is Monticello-orbit employment. Wilmar’s machine is the one Teske attached to Wilmar by name. Small-town America, in the honest register, is full of such machines: a prison, a plant, a substation, a prison’s cousin. Wilmar’s happens to be a turbine on a gas line, with a claim to a first. Film the fence. Do not film a triumph.

SeaArk and UAM’s School of Forest Resources are Heady’s modern county credits. They sit in Monticello’s gravity, which Teske already called Wilmar’s weekday fate. The station is the industrial fact that did not have to move eight miles east to stay on the map. That is enough pride for one paragraph.

## Boyd and Hyatt, as a door not yet opened

The *Drew County Historical Journal* volume 25 (2010) is twelve pages of county energy, if the page span Teske prints is the whole piece. This draft will not guess at diagrams, well names, or a turbine serial number. The citation is real. The consultation is not. INDEX.md already lists CenterPoint first-turbine confirmation among expansions later. That line is the assignment. Until it is done, Teske’s sentence stands with a flag.

A furniture lane has almost nothing honest to say here. Hardwood does not compress. Pine does not travel to St. Louis as therms. The allowed sentence, if any, is the same sentence the river essays may use: after the whistle, a household in this county still works wood. It does not belong on the fence. This essay leaves it off.

The still is a USGS sheet with a pipeline easement if the sheet shows one, then Teske’s 1948 date on a card, then the census bars 627–695–746, then the flag on *first*. Then stop.
