Herbert Evans and Katherine Bishop, in Berkeley in 1922, were not trying to invent an antioxidant aisle.

They were watching rats. On a diet that should have been enough by the lights of the time, the females conceived and then failed to carry. Something in lettuce, or in wheat germ oil, or in certain other fats, put the litter back. They called it a dietary factor necessary for reproduction. The later letter was E. The later chemistry was a tocopherol — a name that still has "birth" hiding in the Greek, another museum word we will not turn into a pitch.

This draft is a caution dressed as a story. Fertility is a commercial magnet. By the late twentieth century you could not walk through a drugstore without E being asked to do jobs Evans never assigned it. Skin oils. Heart stories. A capsule the color of amber pretending to be wheat. The 1922 paper is smaller: a missing fat-soluble passenger and a colony.

Bishop's name gets dropped in casual retellings. Keep it. The work is joint. The later Nobel-adjacent glow around Evans's wider endocrinology should not erase the person who stared at the same cages.

I will not say what E "does for you." I will say what the 1920s did with a letter. Once E existed as a tab, oil houses had a new reason to talk about germ and about cold pressing. Wheat germ is a milling leftover that became a health-food saint. That sainthood is culture. The Berkeley rats were not a church.

Tocopherol chemistry, when Karrer and others drew it, showed a family, not a single saint. Alpha, gamma, mixed tocopherols, tocotrienols — the grocery label usually shrugs and prints "E." Compression again. Compression is fine in a warehouse. It is dishonest in a sermon.

If you use wheat germ in a kitchen, you are cooking. If a carton tells you E is for your reproductive life, you have left this folder and entered labeling, which has its own rules and its own refusals. Our rule is simpler. We stop at the cage and the oil.

A failed litter, a leaf of lettuce, a letter that escaped the lab. The escape is the commercial history. The cage is the discovery. Do not let the escape write the cage's caption.


Wheat-germ oil as a 1920s reagent is already a mill leftover looking for a better name. Cold-press houses will later sell the leftover as a virtue. Evans and Bishop were not in that business. They needed a fat that carried the passenger. Leftovers are where passengers hide. The later virtue language is a commercial aftertaste, and it is not our aftertaste.
