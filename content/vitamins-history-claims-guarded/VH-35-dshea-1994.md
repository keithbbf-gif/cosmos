On 25 October 1994 Bill Clinton signed the Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act. Orrin Hatch of Utah and Tom Harkin of Iowa had shepherded the bargain. Bill Richardson had carried a House version. The mailbags, again, had been heavy. The early-1990s FDA, coming off an Advance Notice that sounded to the shops like a coming raid, had scared a constituency that votes and writes.

DSHEA does a few concrete things, and the ads that followed pretended it did a dozen more.

It defines *dietary supplement* — vitamin, mineral, herb, a concentrate, metabolite, constituent, extract, or combination — and parks the category in food, not in the new-drug lane, so long as the claims stay in the painted lines. It puts the burden on FDA to show a significant or unreasonable risk before yanking a product, rather than on the firm to pre-clear the old ingredients. It invents the "new dietary ingredient" file for the stuff that was not on the market before October 1994. It lets the Secretary write GMPs. It creates a Commission on Dietary Supplement Labels and, at NIH, an Office of Dietary Supplements. It withdraws a set of lingering proposals.

And it opens section 403(r)(6): the four kinds of nutritional-support statements, the substantiation duty, the 30-day letter to FDA, and the boldface disclaimer that the product is not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease.

That last clause is the spine of this folder. The industry got a category. The public got a warning sentence. Copywriters, from day one, tried to make the warning sentence small and the music around it large. 21 CFR 101.93 later told them how large the type had to be. Type size is a moral document.

Hatch's Utah had firms. Harkin's Iowa had voters who liked their shops. I do not need a cartoon of capture to say a statute has a geography. It also has a consumer movement that remembered the 1970s and did not want a prescription for fish oil.

What DSHEA is not: a finding that supplements work. A permission to talk like a drug. A substitute for a clinician. A vibe.

This series takes the painted lines and then paints a tighter box. We do not need 403(r)(6) to tell the Redoxon story. So we don't use it. The bargain of 1994 is a historical object. It is not a coupon.


The 75-day new-dietary-ingredient file is the sleeper in the bargain. Old letters — the alphabet this series spent its first half isolating — walk in without that file. New stuff has to knock. How hard FDA knocks back has varied by year and by courage. The alphabet got a grandfather. Grandfathers are how a 1912 word still owns a 1994 shelf.

## Claims desk

Legislative history. The statutory disclaimer is quoted as law, not as a product page. No 403(r)(6) claim is made.

**Staged historical education, not labeling.** This draft is not a dietary-supplement label, not a product, and not medical advice. Dietary supplements are not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease. Structure/function claims, deficiency-disease claims, and well-being claims are out of scope here.
