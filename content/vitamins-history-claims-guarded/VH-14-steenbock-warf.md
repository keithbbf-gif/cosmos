Harry Steenbock taught the University of Wisconsin a new trick: how to own a lamp.

In the early 1920s he showed that ultraviolet light — the kind a quartz mercury vapor lamp throws — could put the fat-soluble "antirachitic" activity into foods and into sterols that had been inert on the bench. His 1924 filing, later U.S. 1,680,818, is blunt in the period language. It talks of "antirachitic product and process." That is a patent talking the way 1924 patents talk. We quote it as a document. We do not pick up the adjective as a claim.

Wisconsin, to its credit and its profit, did not let the method wander into every margarine plant for free. The Wisconsin Alumni Research Foundation — WARF — was organized in 1925 partly so a public university could hold this kind of thing without the inventor having to become a company. Irradiated fats, irradiated yeast, later a cleaner route through ergosterol: licenses, lawsuits, a long argument about whether a university should collect on a public-health-adjacent process.

The lawsuits are the commercial history in ink. Vitamin Technologists, Inc. learned that WARF would sue. Other firms learned it too. In the 1940s the patents headed toward a consent decree and dedication to the public. That arc — enclosure, rent, then a forced opening — is as much the vitamin century as any crystal.

What the lamp actually did, chemically, would be drawn more cleanly by Windaus and the Göttingen group: certain sterols rearrange under UV into D activity. Steenbock had the agricultural hand. Windaus had the structure. Industry had the yeast tanks and the margarine lines.

I care about WARF here more than about the physics. It is an American invention: the campus as licensor. Later biotech will copy the move. In the 1920s it still looked a little indecent, a land-grant school collecting on breakfast food. Decency is a moving target. The foundation's later grants are real. So were the monopoly years.

No one on this page is being told to sit under a lamp or to buy irradiated milk. Those are old retail moves, some of them still around, none of them our offer. The offer is a story about a patent number and a foundation that learned to live with it.

A quartz tube, a filing date, a university that decided the lamp was an asset. That is enough.


WARF's later life — warfarin, other licenses, a professional campus technology office — can drown the lamp. I am leaving warfarin in the next building. The 1925 decision is enough: a public university would collect on a breakfast-adjacent process. Whether that was ugly or wise depends on which year you ask the alumni. The patent number does not care. It just sat on margarine plants until the decree opened it.
