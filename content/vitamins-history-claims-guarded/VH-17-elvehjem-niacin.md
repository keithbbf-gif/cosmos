Nicotinic acid was not a romantic find. It was already on the shelf.

Conrad Elvehjem's group at Wisconsin, in 1937, put that shelf chemical into dogs with the black-tongue picture that had been standing in for the human southern syndrome. The dogs improved. The paper is short in the way papers are short when the world has been waiting and the bottle was already inventoried.

Funk, you will remember, had probably had nicotinic acid in his 1911 rice fraction without knowing the right animal question. Chemistry is full of substances that sit in a jar waiting for someone to ask them the question they can answer. Elvehjem asked. The letter that stuck was niacin — a later, softer name, because "nicotinic" sounded like tobacco and frightened people who were already frightened enough.

Wisconsin again. Steenbock's lamp, Elvehjem's flask. A land-grant campus as a vitamin machine. That is not a conspiracy. It is what happens when you put agriculture, animals, and organic chemistry on the same muddy quad.

The commercial sequel is enrichment. Once a cheap white powder exists, you can put it back into the flour you made poor by milling. The 1941 identity standard for enriched flour names nicotinic acid (or the amide) among the required add-backs. That standard is a government document, not a store pitch from this series. It is how a New Deal-adjacent food state decided that the mill would return a short list of what the mill had taken.

I am keeping the dog in the room. Assay animals again. The dog with black tongue is not a mascot. It is the reason a jar of nicotinic acid became "the" factor instead of one more beige crystal.

Present tense, claim-free: nicotinic acid is a pyridine carboxylic acid. It can be made in a plant. It was added, as a matter of federal identity, to a defined flour. Those sentences do not tell you to swallow a flush. The flush, the later cardiology arguments, the "no-flush" marketing — out of scope. Those are other wars.

A flask that was already on the bench. A campus that knew dogs. A renaming to calm the public. The letter B3 is a nickname on top of a nickname.


Nicotinic acid had sat in dye chemistry before it sat in a dog study. Coal-tar cousins are always turning up in this decade, wearing new jobs. The rename to niacin is a public-relations molecule: same ring, less tobacco in the ear. Commerce loves a rename. So does a frightened shopper. The flask on the Wisconsin shelf did not change. The word on the flour sack did.
