At the 1939 Swiss national exhibition, Hoffmann-La Roche showed a scale model of an ascorbic-acid plant. The caption, in the spirit of the day, marveled that a rose hip does for free what a building full of tanks does with "an imposing outlay of intelligence and technique."

That caption is the whole commercial turn.

Tadeus Reichstein, at the ETH in Zürich, with Andreas Grüssner, published a workable synthesis in 1933–34. Roche bought the rights. In 1934 the firm put synthetic ascorbic acid on the market as Redoxon. The process that bears Reichstein's name is a hybrid: chemical steps around one microbial oxidation. Glucose goes in. A lot of engineering happens. The *l* acid comes out. You no longer need a mountain of paprika or a season of hips.

I like the exhibition caption because it is honest about the absurdity. Nature is not impressed by your columns. The market, though, is impressed by kilos that do not depend on a harvest. Once the kilo exists, the advertising department can go to work on fear, brightness, winter, "protection" — all the words this folder will not adopt. The factory is a fact. The copy is a choice.

Emil Barell, who ran Roche for a long stretch, later said the vitamin successes of the 1930s and 1940s had surprised him. That surprise is worth keeping. A pharmaceutical house did not glide into vitamins as if destiny had assigned the aisle. They acquired a process, spent money translating a bench route into "the proportions of technique," and discovered that people would buy an isolated food factor by the bottle.

Redoxon is not a hero. It is a brand. Brands die, get sold, get painted. Bayer later inherited the name. The process spread, got cheaper, moved east, became a commodity. A later draft in this folder watches the price-fixing. Another watches the Chinese plants. The 1934 object is simpler: the first time a shopper could hold a synthetic vitamin as a specialty chemical with a smile on the tin.

The rose hip in the caption is doing ideology. It says: we have built a cathedral to copy a berry. You can read that as pride or as unease. I read it as the moment food and chemical industry look at each other and realize they are in the same business.

No serving. No winter protocol. A model factory under glass, and a berry that did not need the glass.


Redoxon tins show up now in museum photographs, the type a little embarrassed, the red a little proud. I do not collect them. I note that a brand can outlive a process and a process can outlive a brand. DSM will buy tanks. Bayer will buy a name. The 1934 object is the first time those two futures are possible in the same aisle. Before 1934 the aisle did not yet know it was an aisle.
