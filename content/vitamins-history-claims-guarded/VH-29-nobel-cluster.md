Between 1928 and 1943 the Nobel rooms could not leave the vitamins alone.

Windaus, 1928, chemistry, sterols. Hopkins and Eijkman, 1929, medicine, accessory factors and the rice birds. Haworth and Karrer, 1937, chemistry, sugars and pigments. Szent-Györgyi, 1937, medicine, the hexuronic acid that became C. Dam and Doisy, 1943, medicine, K. Hodgkin's B12 chemistry prize comes later, 1964, a coda. Kuhn's 1938 chemistry prize sits in the flavin-and-carotenoid weather.

A cluster like that is not proof that "the universe wanted supplements." It is proof that a problem had become soluble with the tools of the time: combustion analysis, animal assays, a first generation of chromatography, X-ray crystallography waiting in the wings. Medals follow solubility.

Who is missing from the medal table is the other history. Grijns. Funk. Goldberger. Takaki. Steenbock. Williams. Wills. György. Folkers. The committee is not a census. If you treat the medal table as the story, you get a Europe-and-a-few-Americans cartoon, heavy on professors, light on cooks, sailors, orphans, and factory hands.

I read the lectures anyway. They are good prose when the laureate is tired enough to be plain. Hopkins defending his milk. Eijkman circling the birds. Szent-Györgyi enjoying himself. Dam explaining a German letter. The lectures are primary sources. The after-dinner speeches that cite them to sell a tin are not.

1937 in particular looks like a conspiracy of calendars. It is just a logjam. Structures had ripened together. The committees flushed the queue.

Commercially, a medal is a stamp. Roche did not need one, but copywriters in every country learned to put a silhouette of a medal near a bottle. That proximity is a claim-shaped gesture even when the sentence is only "as studied by Nobel laureates." We will not make the gesture.

The cluster ends as the war swallows the labs. After 1943 the vitamin story is less about first isolation and more about lots, cartels, statutes, and aisles. This series turns with it.

Medals in a row, then a quieter factory century. Keep the row for the dates. Do not kneel.


Read one lecture a night and the cluster becomes a neighborhood. You hear the same animals, the same insults about priority, the same relief when a structure finally sits still. The neighborhood is not the aisle. The aisle borrowed the neighborhood's nouns and left the arguments behind. Arguments are the science. Nouns are the inventory. A medal on a carton is a noun trying to skip the argument.
