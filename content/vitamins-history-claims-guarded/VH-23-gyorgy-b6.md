By the mid-1930s "vitamin B" was a boarding house with arguments in the hall.

Paul György, Hungarian-born, working through European and then American labs, helped evict one more tenant and give it a number. Rats on certain purified B fractions developed a skin picture and a fit picture that the known letters did not account for. The missing piece became B6. Pyridoxine, later, when the chemists caught the ring.

György also has a hand in the biotin story. The same person showing up in two letters tells you how small the human network was. A few dozen people were taking the B complex apart with adsorption columns and animal skin. It was not a mass movement. It was a craft.

B6's later retail life is a carnival: PMS aisles, dream-vivid rumors, mega-milligram bottles. None of that is 1934. None of that is this page. The carnival is what happens when a letter escapes into magazines. Our job is to put the letter back on the fractionation table.

I want the table to feel physical. Fuller's earth. Eluate. Filtrate. Autoclave. The 1937 Wills paper, sitting in this folder's bibliography, talks that language. People were naming factors by how they stuck to dirt. That is not mystical. That is chromatography before it had a pretty instrument.

Commercially, every new B number is a chance to reprint a label. "Now with B6." The phrase is a collector's lure. Completeness is a feeling shops sell. The boarding house was never complete. It was a temporary address for substances that would get proper names.

Pyridoxine, pyridoxal, pyridoxamine — the family again, hiding under one number. If you ever feel that a Supplement Facts line is too tidy, good. Tidy is a printing constraint.

György's rats, a skin finding, a number. I will not tell you what to do with a tablet that borrowed the number. The number was a cage code.


I keep saying boarding house because the 1930s papers sound like landlords. Eluate, filtrate, the tenant who will not pay rent to the known letters. György was a careful landlord. The later carnival that rented B6 to magazines was not. If a reader keeps one craft lesson from him, let it be the adsorption column, not the magazine. Columns are how you find out you were wrong. Magazines are how you stay wrong at scale. The boarding house, at least, kept a ledger of who had paid and who was still hiding in the filtrate. A ledger is a better ancestor than a carnival booth.
