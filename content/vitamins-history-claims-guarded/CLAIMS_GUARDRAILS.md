# CLAIMS_GUARDRAILS

**Series:** `vitamins-history-claims-guarded`
**Stage:** staged drafts (not labeling, not published product copy)
**House rule, stricter than the statute:** no disease claims. None. Not even the classical nutrient-deficiency kind that DSHEA would let a label carry if it also recited U.S. prevalence.

This file is the pen. Every draft in this folder is read against it. A sentence that sings and still trips a rule is cut.

## What this series is

Education about how vitamins were *found*, *named*, *made*, *sold*, and *regulated*. Discovery history and commercial history. Human voice. Named people, dated rooms, actual factories and statutes.

It is not:

- a supplement label
- a sales page
- a protocol
- medical advice
- a structure/function catalog
- a place to launder a disease claim through a story

A reader who finishes a draft should know more about a chemist, a patent, a cartel, or a statute — not walk away believing a bottle treats a condition.

## The statute, in plain English

DSHEA (Pub. L. 103-417, signed 25 October 1994) put dietary supplements in the food lane of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act and opened a narrow door for *statements of nutritional support* under section 403(r)(6) (21 U.S.C. § 343(r)(6)):

1. a benefit related to a **classical nutrient-deficiency disease**, *if* the labeling also says how widespread that disease is in the United States
2. the role of a nutrient or ingredient intended to affect **structure or function** in humans
3. a **documented mechanism** by which it maintains that structure or function
4. **general well-being** from consuming it

Those statements still need substantiation, a 30-day notification to FDA after first marketing, and the boldface disclaimer in 21 CFR 101.93:

> This statement has not been evaluated by the Food and Drug Administration. This product is not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease.

The disclaimer does not rescue a disease claim. Context is the whole page, not the footnote.

This series **does not use doors 1–4 as copy**. We are not labeling a product. We do not need a structure/function claim to tell the Reichstein story. If a draft can live without a claim, it must.

## Disease, as FDA draws the line

21 CFR 101.93(g)(1): a disease is damage to an organ, part, structure, or system so it does not function properly (example in the rule: cardiovascular disease), or a state of health leading to that (example: hypertension). Classical nutrient-deficiency syndromes (the rule names scurvy and pellagra) sit in a special statutory pocket for *labels*. **We do not use that pocket.**

A statement is a disease claim when, in context, it says a *product* — or, in our house rule, a *nutrient offered as if it were a product* — does any of the following (101.93(g)(2)):

| Code | Forbidden move | How it shows up in vitamin copy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| (i) | Effect on a specific disease or class | "Vitamin C for scurvy." "B12 for pernicious anemia." |
| (ii) | Effect on characteristic signs or symptoms | "Stops the bleeding gums." "Clears the pellagra rash." |
| (iii) | Effect on an uncommon or harmful abnormal condition tied to a natural state | "For the anemia of pregnancy." |
| (iv) | Disease implication by name, formula, citation, the word "disease," or pictures | A paper title about a syndrome used as if it were a use. A red-cross vibe. |
| (v) | Class of products meant to diagnose / mitigate / treat / cure / prevent | "This is your antirachitic." |
| (vi) | Substitute for a therapy | "Instead of the liver shots…" |
| (vii) | Augments a disease therapy | "Take with your heart medicine." |
| (viii) | Role in the body's response to a disease or vector | "Helps you fight the flu." |
| (ix) | Treats adverse events of a therapy, if those events are themselves diseases | |
| (x) | Otherwise suggests an effect on a disease | The catch-all. Tone counts. |

Implied claims count. A caption, a before/after, a "sailors used to die until they took C" that lands as a use instruction — all count.

## House rules (this folder)

1. **No diagnose / treat / cure / prevent / mitigate** in narrator voice, about a nutrient or a product, ever.
2. **No classical-deficiency sales line.** Do not write "vitamin C prevents scurvy" even with a prevalence clause. History may *name* the old syndrome. The nutrient must not be sold against it.
3. **No structure/function claims.** Not "supports immunity." Not "builds strong bones." Not "maintains cell integrity." Those are legal label moves. They are not this series.
4. **No general-well-being claims** used as a pitch ("for everyday vitality," "unlock your energy").
5. **No product.** No SKU, no "our formula," no dose, no "take with breakfast," no "ask for it at the counter" that functions as an offer.
6. **No implied drug.** Isolation of a factor in a hospital ward is history. It is not a reason to swallow a tablet.
7. **Past tense for the sickroom.** Birds recovered. Sailors were issued citrus. Orphans were fed meat and milk. Those are dated observations, not instructions.
8. **Period language stays in the museum case.** Funk wrote "scurvy vitamine." Steenbock's patent said "antirachitic." Quote it, date it, and step back. Do not adopt it.
9. **Present-tense science stays modest and claim-free.** "Ascorbic acid is an organic acid that plants make." That is chemistry. "Ascorbic acid keeps you from getting sick" is a claim.
10. **Names of old syndromes are allowed as history** when they are clearly the *puzzle of a year*, not the *use of a bottle*. Prefer "the syndrome then called beriberi," "the ward diagnosis of the period."
11. **B12, folate, K, D, A — extra quiet.** These discoveries ran through hospital wards. Tell the lab, the crystal, the patent, the factory. Do not tell the reader what to take for a blood, bone, skin, or bleeding problem.
12. **Advertising history may quote ads** if the quote is marked as an ad and the narrator refuses the pitch.
13. **Regulation history may quote the forbidden words** when the subject *is* the prohibition ("DSHEA says a supplement is not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease").
14. **No medical advice. No "talk to your doctor about this supplement."** If a reader needs care, they already know how to find it. We are not a label, so we do not perform the label's CYA paragraph as if we were selling.
15. **Human voice.** Named nouns. Uneven sentences. No brochure cadence. No "in today's wellness landscape."

## Allowed sentences (shape)

- "In 1912 Funk coined *vitamine* for a concentrate he pulled from rice bran."
- "Roche bought Reichstein's process and sold the first synthetic ascorbic acid under the Redoxon name in 1934."
- "Goldberger argued the southern orphanage outbreak tracked the pot, not a germ."
- "The 1976 Proxmire Amendment stopped FDA from calling a vitamin a drug just because the potency was high."
- "DSHEA, signed 25 October 1994, created the modern U.S. dietary-supplement category."

## Refused sentences (shape)

- "Vitamin C prevents scurvy."
- "Take B1 if you have nerve trouble."
- "Niacin is for pellagra."
- "This is why you should supplement in winter."
- "Supports a healthy immune system."
- "Clinically shown to reduce tiredness."
- "A natural alternative to the liver injection."
- "Fights free radicals that cause disease."

## Historical syndrome names — handle list

These words are **history, not uses**. They may appear as period names. They may not appear as reasons to ingest a nutrient.

beriberi, beri-beri, polyneuritis (in pigeons/chickens as lab history), scurvy, scorbutic, rickets, rachitic, antirachitic (patent-quote only), pellagra, xerophthalmia, night blindness (as period observation), pernicious anemia, macrocytic anemia, sprue (Wills's ward language), hemorrhagic (Dam's chicks), xerosis, keratomalacia.

If you can swap the sentence to "take X for [name]" and it still reads, rewrite.

## Voice

Write like a person who has sat with a paper, a patent, and a factory history — not like a brand. Contractions are fine. So is a short sentence after a long one. Specificity is the style: Elkhart, Indiana; a quartz mercury lamp in Madison; paprika from Szeged; a $500 million plea in 1999.

Refuse the usual machine furniture:

delve, tapestry, landscape (as metaphor), unlock, empower, nested "it's important to note," "in this article," "let's explore," "in today's world," "multifaceted," "a testament to," "plays a crucial role," "boost your," "supercharge," "hack," "wellness journey."

## Staging

Every draft in this folder ships as:

```yaml
status: staged
stage: claims-guarded
claims_class: history-education
dshea: no-disease-claim
product_claim: none
structure_function: none
voice: human
voice_check: edited
```

`staged` means: fit for human review, not for a label printer, not for a store page, not for an ad account. Promotion out of this folder is a later, human act.

## Required furniture on every draft

Frontmatter keys listed in `README.md`. A **Claims desk** section that states, in one breath, what the draft did *not* claim. The series disclaimer (fixed text — do not paraphrase into a softer claim):

> **Staged historical education, not labeling.** This draft is not a dietary-supplement label, not a product, and not medical advice. Dietary supplements are not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease. Structure/function claims, deficiency-disease claims, and well-being claims are out of scope here.

## Lint

`python content/vitamins-history-claims-guarded/tools/claims_lint.py`

Fails the folder if a draft is missing furniture, drops below the word floor, repeats a slug, uses brochure voice, or trips a disease-claim pattern in narrator voice.
