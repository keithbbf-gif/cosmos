In Tokyo, Umetaro Suzuki was in the rice bran before Funk's review had time to travel.

He pulled a concentrate he called oryzanin — from *Oryza*, the rice. Japanese journals, then German. The Western textbooks, for a long time, treated this as a footnote if they treated it at all. Hopkins, in the 1929 lecture, at least nods: Suzuki and others were describing their endeavours to isolate the substance. A nod is not a street name.

Priority fights across languages are where history gets ugly and useful. Ugly because nationalism loves a first. Useful because they show the problem was sitting in the mill, visible to more than one empire. Dutch Indies, British London, Imperial Japan — the same white rice, the same question.

Oryzanin was not a clean thiamine crystal. Neither was Funk's powder. Early isolates are boasts with ash in them. The boast still counts as a move: we believe there is a thing in the bran, and we have a fraction that moves the assay. Assays then were birds and, in some hands, people. I will not dress those people as volunteers in the modern sense.

Why does Suzuki matter to commercial history? Because Japan later built its own vitamin industry, its own enrichment arguments, its own brands. The rice-factor story is not a Western gift Japan received. It is a shared industrial problem Japan had already named.

I am careful not to turn oryzanin into a lost superfood. It was a lab fraction. The word sounds like a supplement SKU, which is why it must stay in 1911. If you see it on a modern carton, someone has gone grave-robbing in the letter cemetery.

The larger correction is geographic. Batavia, Tokyo, London, Madison, Szeged, Zürich, Bombay. The map is not a Boston-to-Basel corridor. People who write the corridor are writing a sales tour.

Suzuki's name in this folder is a pin in the map. Rice, a Japanese chemist, a concentrate, a word that did not become the world's word. Funk won the nickname. Suzuki did the same kind of afternoon, in another library, with the same bran.


Japanese industrial vitamin later — Takeda and others, a domestic letter business — does not need Suzuki as a mascot, and I will not make him one. I will say the bran was already a Tokyo problem in 1911. Mascots flatten that into a first. Problems do not care who is first. They care who is still milling white. Oryzanin stays a 1911 word. If it shows up on a 2020s carton, the graveyard draft in this folder already named the flower.
