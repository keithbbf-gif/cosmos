The health-food store of 1971 is a smell before it is a statute.

Brewer's yeast. A hopper of granola. Bottles with typeset that looks like a church bulletin. A clerk who will talk to you longer than a drugstore clerk is allowed to. The conversation is the product as much as the tablet. FDA, in that decade, looked at the conversation and saw labeling. The shops looked at FDA and saw a raid on a church.

I am not joining either choir. I am naming a retail form. The form trained a generation to think of vitamins as *theirs* — not a physician's, not a miller's, not Roche's. "Theirs" is a political feeling. Proxmire's mailbox was full of it. DSHEA's mailbox, twenty years later, still was.

The signs above the bins did the claims work. Handwritten, often. "For the blood." "For the nerves." "For women's days." Those phrases, in a present-tense aisle, are exactly the 101.93(g) list or the S/F list depending on the rest of the words. In 1971 the regulation was not yet that map. The impulse was already that impulse. This folder, looking backward, does not reprint the signs as advice. It files them as exhibits.

Adelle Davis, Linus Pauling (a later draft), prevention magazines — the print culture around the bins — taught a vernacular science. Some of it was leftover enrichment civic religion. Some of it was resistance to a medical profession that had been rude about food. Some of it was hustle. Vernacular science is mixed. That mix is the history.

The independent store loses, later, to the strip-mall chain and then to the website. The smell goes. The conversation becomes comments. The handwritten sign becomes a bullet list with a star to the disclaimer. I do not mourn this as a fall from grace. I note that the claim moved from chalk to CSS.

If you still have a shop like that in your town, it is a living archive. Archives are not protocols.

Yeast on the breath, a mailbox to a senator, a sign we will not repeat as ours. That is the 1970s in this series.


Prevention magazine and its cousins taught a monthly cadence the drugstore tin had already taught in a different accent. The store was a classroom with a cash register. Classrooms can be good. Cash registers edit the curriculum. I will not pretend the bins were only resistance. They were also a margin. A classroom that rings up yeast is still a classroom. It is also a shop. Hold both.
