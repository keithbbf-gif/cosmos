The purified diet is a kind of arrogance. You weigh the protein, the fat, the carbohydrate, the salts. You tell yourself you have the animal covered. Then the animal fails, and you have to decide whether your weighing was sloppy or your list was short.

Frederick Gowland Hopkins, at Cambridge, spent the years around 1906–1912 making that decision in public. He was not the first to notice that a "complete" ration of purified stuff was not complete. Pekelharing, among others, had already seen that a little milk changed the story. Hopkins's 1912 paper in the *Journal of Physiology* is the one the field still points at: feeding experiments that treat the missing piece as an experimental object, not a kitchen rumor.

He called the missing pieces accessory food factors. The phrase is deliberately humble. Accessory — riding along. Not the steak. The thing in the steak you had not named. He showed that young animals on the purified mix stalled, and that a small add-back of milk brought growth back. The add-back was not a meaningful load of extra calories. That is the whole joke, and the whole finding. Quantity was not the point. Presence was.

Hopkins and Funk later stepped on each other's footnotes. Priority fights are a tax on simultaneous work. Hopkins, in the 1929 Nobel lecture, is not generous about Funk's review. He says Funk had not isolated what he thought he had, and that the big physiological claim — that tiny unidentified factors sit in ordinary food — was Cambridge work. You can hear a man protecting a life's argument. You can also hear a truth: isolation and the *idea of isolation* are different trophies.

The Nobel committee, in 1929, split the medicine prize between Hopkins and Eijkman. That pairing is a map. Eijkman is the chicken yard in the Indies. Hopkins is the Cambridge balance. One man watched a syndrome on a colonial ration. The other watched growth curves on a diet that looked scientific and wasn't.

What I like in Hopkins is the refusal to rush a bottle. Accessory food factor is not a brand. It is a hole in a table. The later industry will fill the hole with letters and prices. That is a later story. In 1912 the honest sentence is still: we have been weighing the wrong columns.

If a reader keeps one habit from him, let it be this one — not a dose, not a product. Distrust a complete-looking list. Food is older than the columns we pour it into.
