A drum of ascorbic acid does not care which statute you love. A customs officer does.

The Codex Alimentarius — a FAO/WHO food-code house — writes guidelines that countries use when they argue about what a vitamin tablet is allowed to be at a border. Maximums. Minimums. Which substances count. How to label. The United States, after 1994, is an odd animal in that conversation: DSHEA built a wide domestic category (herbs riding along with vitamins) while many Codex-influenced rules treat vitamin-and-mineral supplements as a tighter food object.

I am not going to pick a side in the old "Codex will ban your vitamins" chain letter. That chain letter was a 1990s–2000s political object, full of fear that a guideline would become a raid. Guidelines are not raids. They become raids only if a government writes them into a regulation and then enforces. Some governments did tighten. Some did not. The chain letter flattened that into a thriller.

What is true, and dull, is that trade wants a common language. If Poland and Peru and a U.S. private-label house are going to share a drum, someone has to say what "vitamin D3 1000" means in a language a lab can test. Codex is one of those languages. USP is another. They do not always rhyme.

DSHEA's width — botanicals in the same legal family as ascorbic acid — is a U.S. choice with a geography (Hatch's state, the shops). Codex's narrower vitamin-mineral talk is a different choice. Exporters live in the gap. The gap is why a carton that is legal in Utah can be "too strong" or "the wrong kind of claim" in a Nordic pharmacy system.

Claims, again. Other countries do not owe us 403(r)(6). A structure/function sentence that is ordinary in a U.S. aisle can be a medicinal claim in a country that kept vitamins closer to the pharmacy. This folder's refusal is, accidentally, more portable than a U.S. label. History travels. "Supports immunity" does not.

If you export, you already have a regulatory person who is tired. This page is for everyone else: the letter is global as a molecule and local as a sentence.

A guideline, a border, a chain letter we will not forward. Keep the first two.


Exporters keep matrices — a spreadsheet where each country is a column of maximums and forbidden sentences. The spreadsheet is the real international vitamin text. Codex is one input. DSHEA is another. A lawyer's red cell is worth more than a slogan about harmonization. I have seen the matrices. They are not thrilling. They are how a drum gets a second passport.
