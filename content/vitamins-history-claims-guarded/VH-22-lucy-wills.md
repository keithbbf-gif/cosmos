Lucy Wills went to India with a hematologist's problem and came home with a yeast extract's reputation.

In Bombay she saw pregnant women with a macrocytic blood picture that did not sit neatly on the Boston liver story. Liver helped some. A cheap yeast spread — Marmite — helped in her hands in a way that made her suspect a second factor. She took the problem to monkeys, because you cannot build a letter on a ward anecdote alone, and then into fractionation at the Royal Free. The 1931 and 1934 papers, and the 1937 *Biochemical Journal* work with Clutterbuck and Evans, are the trail.

The factor would later be folded into folate. Folic acid as a cheap synthetic, folate as the family in food, the later fortification fights — those are decades after Wills. She had a brown jar and a monkey colony and a refusal to let one liver extract explain every large cell.

Marmite is funny, which is a danger. Funny things get turned into charming ads. The Unilever-adjacent spread already had a kitchen life in Britain. Wills used it as a reagent that happened to be edible. That is not an endorsement. It is a reminder that early vitamin work is full of groceries pressed into assay duty: milk, paprika, wheat germ, a salty yeast goo.

I am not going to walk into pregnancy advice. Pregnancy is exactly where a supplement page goes to get into trouble. Wills's patients were in a specific city, on specific diets, under a colonial medical system. Their blood pictures are not a coupon.

What she changed, for the commercial timeline, is the suspicion that "the B complex" was still hiding tenants. Each new tenant is a new isolation project, a new patent possibility, a new line on a label. Folate's later life in grain fortification is a public-policy sequel. Sequels need their own guardrails. This page stops at the jar.

Keep her name. The field is crowded with men who got letters. Wills got a factor that people called "Wills's factor" until the chemists caught up. Being a placeholder is a kind of honor, if the later chemists remember to say so.

A ship to Bombay, a brown spread, a monkey, a paper. No prenatal aisle.


The Royal Free fractionation notebooks, if you could sit with them, would look like every other B-complex bench of the decade: dirt, heat, a monkey, a hope that two factors are not one. Wills's difference is the city she started from and the grocery she trusted enough to mail. Colonial medicine is in the room. So is a British spread already on civilian toast. Neither is a protocol for anyone reading this now.
