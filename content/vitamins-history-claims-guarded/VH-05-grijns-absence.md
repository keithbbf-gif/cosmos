Absence is harder to think than poison.

If people are dying in a barracks, the mind reaches for a thing that *arrived*: a germ, a toxin in spoiled grain, a bad wind. Gerrit Grijns, working in the same Indies service that had employed Eijkman, had to talk his colleagues into the other direction. The white rice was not haunted. It was incomplete.

He published in the first years of the new century. The chickens still mattered. So did the human wards, which the colonial government could not ignore. Grijns's move sounds small when you reduce it to a sentence in a review article. It is not small. The whole later hunt — Funk's word, Williams's synthesis, the enrichment standards of 1941 — sits on the idea that a staple can fail by subtraction.

He did not get the 1929 prize. People who tidy history into medal tables lose him. People who work in labs know the type: the colleague who stays, who writes the less glamorous paper, who changes the verb from "intoxication" to "lack."

I like him for a reason that is not sentimental. Poison stories generate quarantines and blame. Absence stories generate questions about mills, wages, and what was thrown in the river as husk. That is a more uncomfortable politics, which may be why it took so long to become common sense.

There is a temptation, writing this series, to turn every discoverer into a saint of public health. Grijns was a government scientist in a colony. The chickens were cheap. The laborers were not the audience for the journals. Hold both facts. The chemistry that follows is real. So is the setting.

When later advertisers needed a morality play, they sometimes reached for "the gift of the husk" and a peasant who knew better than the miller. That play is too clean. Peasants did not "know about thiamine." They ate what they could store and afford. The husk stayed in some pots because it was food, not because it was a factor.

Grijns's paper is the moment the factor becomes thinkable. Thinkable is not bottled. Bottled is 1936 and after, and even then the bottle is a commercial object, not a moral.

If you remember one word from him, remember *missing.* Not antidote. Missing.


One more thing about the verb. Once a barracks problem is an absence, the quartermaster becomes a scientist whether he likes it or not. Menus get copied. Mills get blamed. A later tablet house will try to inherit that blame as a sales advantage — 'we put back what the mill took.' Grijns did not write that advertisement. He wrote that the bran was not an antidote. The difference is the whole later century: civic ration versus a tin that wants to be the civic ration's heir.
