James Lind is dragged into every vitamin story, usually wearing a halo he did not order.

In 1747, on *Salisbury*, he took twelve sailors with the mouth-and-skin misery the navy called scurvy and assigned them, two by two, to cider, elixir of vitriol, vinegar, seawater, a mash of garlic and mustard, and citrus. The citrus pair improved. He published the *Treatise* in 1753. The Admiralty did not instantly flood the fleet with lemons. Institutions are slow, citrus is expensive, and Lind himself kept other theories in his pocket — including a fondness for shipboard hygiene that diluted the finding.

He did not isolate ascorbic acid. He could not have. He did not use the word vitamin. He did not run a trial that would pass a modern IRB, and he did not even pick the twelve in a way a statistician would bless. What he did was refuse to treat the sick bay as a place for one favorite remedy. He put rival ideas on neighboring hammocks and watched.

That method is the inheritance, not the fruit.

The commercial history likes him because citrus is already a product. You can paint a lemon on a crate. You can sell "the navy's secret" in 1934 once Roche has a synthetic acid and a brand name. Lind becomes a mascot. Mascots are how companies borrow a century.

Stay with the actual book. The *Treatise* is long, fussy, full of other people's cases. The twelve-man comparison is a few pages inside a thicker argument. Later textbooks blow those pages up like a poster. Fine — posters teach. Just don't confuse the poster with a label claim. "Sailors ate oranges" is a sentence about a fleet. "Vitamin C is for scurvy" is a sentence this folder will not print.

Britain's later long fight over lemon juice, lime juice, and the copper kettle that wrecked the juice in storage — that is a logistics story. Copper and heat are unkind to ascorbic acid. The navy learned that the hard way, then unlearned it, then learned it again. A factor you cannot name is easy to lose in a barrel.

Lind belongs at the front of this series as a reminder of sequence. Observation of food and recovery is old. The letter C is new. The tablet is newer. Collapsing those three into one smiling citrus ad is how education turns into a pitch.

He was a naval surgeon with a notebook. Leave him there.


Edinburgh printed the book. The fleet read it late. That lag is not a gotcha on Lind; it is how navies buy. A method that splits twelve men is cheaper than a contract for citrus at scale. Contracts need a year, a grocer, a barrel that does not wreck the juice. The *Treatise* is the easy object to reprint. The barrel is the hard one. Commercial history starts when someone has to fill the barrel twice.
