Joseph Goldberger was sent south in 1914 because pellagra was filling orphanages, asylums, and cotton-county graveyards, and because the respectable theory was still a germ.

He walked through two orphanages in Jackson, Mississippi, and did the unfashionable thing. He looked at who ate what. The younger children, the ones getting the meat and milk, were not the ones in the rash. The middle children, on the thinner pot, were. He changed the pot — more animal food, more legumes — and watched the case lists fall. He did similar work in a prison farm, the part of the story that still makes people flinch: he induced the syndrome with a cheap diet in men who had a choice only in the legal sense.

Then, because the germ men would not shut up, he and his colleagues threw a party nobody wanted an invitation to. They swallowed pills made of scabs. They injected blood. They sprayed mucus. They did not catch pellagra. It is a grotesque paragraph and a clean argument. You cannot inoculate a missing dinner.

Goldberger died in 1929, before Elvehjem's 1937 nicotinic-acid paper. He never held the letter. He held a menu. The menu is why later enrichment of flour with niacin has a political ancestor in a Public Health Service officer arguing with Southern boards who preferred a weevil or a mosquito.

I will not turn this into a niacin advertisement. The orphanage meat and milk were food. The later tablet is a commercial object with its own decade. Collapsing them is how you get a disease claim wearing overalls.

What I will say, because it is history and not a pitch: Goldberger made diet thinkable in a region that wanted a bug. Thinkable diet is dangerous to landlords and to millers of a single grain. It is also dangerous to later marketers, who would like the story to end with a bottle instead of with wages and gardens.

The scabs are the detail students remember. Remember the ledger too. Who got the milk. Who got the molasses and meal. A vitamin century that starts in a chicken yard and runs through an orphanage is a century about rations, not about "lifestyle."

No dose. No "if you have a rash." A man in a federal job, a kitchen, and a refusal to catch what was not catching.


The Public Health Service was a federal job with a suitcase. Goldberger took the suitcase into counties that did not want a New York Jew telling them about dinner. The racism and the class of that reception are documented. A vitamin century that skips them is a prettier century than the one that happened. The pot was political. The later niacin powder did not make the pot less political. It made the mill able to obey a standard.
