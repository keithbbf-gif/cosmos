William Proxmire, who liked a good fight with a bureaucracy, gave the vitamin aisle a statutory shield in 1976.

The Vitamin-Mineral Amendment — section 411 of the Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act — said, in substance, that FDA could not treat a vitamin or mineral as a drug *just because* the potency was high or the combination looked odd to the agency. The 1970s had already been a courtroom decade. National Nutritional Foods cases had boxed the agency in. Consumers and shops were loud. Congress, as it does when mailbags are heavy, wrote a sentence.

I am not going to cheer or hiss. The amendment is a fact that later DSHEA will widen. If you want to understand 1994, you have to sit in 1976. Hatch and Harkin inherit a country that had already told FDA: potency is not a magic door into drug law.

What the amendment does *not* do is legalize disease claims. The four verbs in the later DSHEA disclaimer stay out of reach. The 1976 sentence is dryer: a vitamin or mineral does not become a drug merely by being a strong preparation or a strange stack. The carton language is a separate knife-edge. People still cut themselves on it.

The health-food stores of the 1970s — jars, handwritten signs, a smell of yeast — took Proxmire as a victory lap. Victory laps become origin myths. The myth says "we freed the vitamins." The statute says something dryer about classification. Dry is better. Myths turn into ads.

FDA still had tools: adulteration, misbranding, the old food powers. It used them unevenly. Industry learned to hear a permission that was only a limit on one theory of enforcement. That mishearing is part of the commercial history. Lawyers hear limits. Copywriters hear trumpets.

Sit with section 411 as a fence, not a parade. On one side, potency-as-drug is closed. On the other, the claim rules remain. This folder lives on the claim side, refusing even the statements the later statute would allow on a label.

1976, a senator from Wisconsin (the vitamin campus state, again), a fence. The aisle behind the fence grew. Growth is not the same as grace.


Wisconsin again. The senator who fenced potency sat in the same state that had licensed a lamp and fed a dog a shelf chemical. I do not think that is a plot. I think it is what happens when a campus makes a subject ordinary: the politics show up in the same zip codes as the rats. Ordinary subjects get statutes. Exotic subjects stay rumors.
