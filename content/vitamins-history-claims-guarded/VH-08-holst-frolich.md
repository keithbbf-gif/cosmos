Rats would have lied.

Most of the early vitamin work ran on rats and pigeons because they were cheap and because the rice story had already trained the pigeon. Rats, inconveniently, make their own ascorbic acid. Put them on a scorbutic mess and they shrug. If Holst and Frølich had trusted the usual laboratory mammal, the citrus problem would have stayed a sailor story.

Axel Holst, in Kristiania (Oslo), was thinking about shipboard outbreaks among Norwegian crews. Theodor Frølich was with him. In 1907 they reported that guinea pigs, on a diet of grain and water, developed the same mouth-and-joint wreckage the navy already knew, and that greens and citrus put the animals back together. The guinea pig, unlike the rat, is a sailor in fur. It has to eat the factor.

That single zoological fact is a fork in the road. Isolation chemistry needs an assay. An assay needs an animal that falls down when you omit the thing. Szent-Györgyi's later hexuronic acid, Haworth's name, Reichstein's process, Roche's Redoxon — all of them sit on a rodent that cannot do what a rat can do.

I have a soft spot for assay animals. They do not get streets named after them. They are the reason a powder can be called "active" instead of "beige." Every later factory certificate that says a lot of ascorbic acid is a lot of ascorbic acid is, in a crooked line, a descendant of those Oslo cages.

Holst was not hunting a tablet industry. Norway wanted fewer ruined crews. The path from a national shipping problem to a Basel production hall is the path this whole series keeps walking: a practical mess, a fussy animal, a crystal, a patent, a brand.

One more unromantic note. Guinea pigs were already in laboratories. They were not chosen because they were cute. They were chosen because they failed in a useful way. A lot of discovery is the search for a failure you can repeat.

When you see a modern bottle with a guinea-pig-free claim or a smiling orange, neither image is the Oslo work. The Oslo work is a grain bin, a small mammal, and two physicians writing in German-language journals that the sailor problem could be put on a bench.

No one needs a dose from this paragraph. What you might keep is the suspicion of the "obvious" lab animal. The obvious one sometimes writes its own vitamin and leaves you with a clean, false negative.


Oslo is a shipping town. That matters more than the cute animal. The assay was chosen because crews were coming home ruined and because a university sat next to a harbor. Later isolation chemistry looks cosmopolitan — Birmingham, Zürich, Szeged. The guinea pig is provincial on purpose. Provincial problems make portable methods. Portable methods make factories. The factory will forget Oslo. This page will not.
