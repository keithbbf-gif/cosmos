Kanehiro Takaki had a navy, not a vitamin.

In the 1880s the Japanese fleet was losing men to the syndrome then called kakke, the same family of trouble Western journals spelled beriberi. Takaki had trained in London. He thought in rations and in the fashionable ratio of nitrogen to carbon. He compared messes. He noticed the poorer tables — more white rice, less of everything else — lined up with the worse ships.

In 1884 he got permission for a test that still reads like a dare. The training ship *Ryujo* had already had a disastrous Pacific cruise on the old mess. A later ship, *Tsukuba*, sailed a comparable long route on a revised menu: more barley, more meat, more milk, more vegetables. The case counts were not similar. The barley ship came home with a story the Navy could not laugh off.

Takaki's theory was wrong in the way a good operational theory is often wrong. He thought he had a protein problem. Barley and meat do carry protein. They also carry the later B letters. He moved the pot for the reason he had, and the pot moved the outcome for a reason he did not yet own.

The Army, famously, was slower than the Navy. Institutional pride is a nutrient too. For years the two services could look like two different countries on the same islands, one eating as if Takaki had spoken and one eating as if he had not. That split is part of the history of the Russo-Japanese War and of later recriminations. It is also a reminder that a feeding change is a political act. Someone has to pay for barley.

I am not going to dress this up as "the discovery of B1." B1 arrives as a crystal decades later, in another country, under another name. Takaki belongs in the commercial history anyway, because he showed a modern state that a ration is a lever. Once a navy believes that, ministries of agriculture, millers, and eventually tablet firms have a customer.

The two-ship story gets told as if it were a clean controlled trial. It was cleaner than anecdote and dirtier than a protocol. Different hulls, different years, a crew that knew it was being watched. Still: the Navy changed its pots, and the sick-list changed with them.

Wrong theory, useful mess. Science is full of those. The danger is the ad that skips the "wrong theory" part and prints a modern letter next to a nineteenth-century sailor. That ad is a claim. This page is not.
