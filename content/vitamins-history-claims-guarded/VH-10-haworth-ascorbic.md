*Ascorbic* is a little Latin shrug. *a-* against, *scorbutus* the old name for the sailor misery. The word is a chemical title that still carries a hospital in it.

Walter Haworth, in Birmingham, with Edmund Hirst and a tired crew, pinned the structure in 1933 and helped lock the name. They were carbohydrate chemists. They thought in rings and optical rotations, not in advertising. The same year, Reichstein in Zürich published a synthesis. The letters between labs have the polite sting of people who know the calendar will decide the textbooks.

Haworth's 1937 chemistry Nobel is often bundled, in casual talk, with "he made vitamin C." He made a structure honest. Making a structure honest is how you stop calling a powder by a nickname and start calling it by a skeleton. Factories need that. So do later arguments about which isomer does anything in a guinea pig. Reichstein's notes already say the *l* form behaved like the natural acid in the Basel animal tests, and the *d* form did not. That is stereochemistry earning its keep.

The name is the problem for this folder. *Ascorbic* wants to be a claim. It was born as a claim. We use it anyway because it is the IUPAC-adjacent name the literature settled on. We do not use it as a verb. We do not say a bottle "ascorbs." We say: this is the acid Haworth described.

I have written and deleted three sentences that start "which is why you should." They do not belong here. The useful present-tense sentence is thinner: ascorbic acid is a six-carbon lactone that plants synthesize and that guinea pigs cannot. Chemistry. Zoology. No aisle.

Haworth's Birmingham was a sugar town of the mind — cellulose, starch, the stubbornness of rings. Putting the sailor's factor into that family was a way of saying: this is not a mystic amine. This is a cousin of glucose that a plant already knew how to make. Roche will later start from glucose for real, with a bacterial step in the Reichstein process. The name and the factory shake hands.

A refusal, then. We keep the word. We refuse the shrug it makes toward a disease claim. The Latin can sit on the bottle of history without sitting on a label we are writing.


Birmingham's sugar chemistry had industrial neighbors — starch, cellulose, the Midlands' idea that a ring can be a living. Putting the sailor factor into that family was also a way of inviting a factory. Factories already knew glucose. Reichstein would start there for real. Haworth's name is the handshake the factory needed so the invoice could say one word instead of a paragraph about oranges.
