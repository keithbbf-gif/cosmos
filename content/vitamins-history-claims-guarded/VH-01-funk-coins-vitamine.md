The word arrives with a flourish it cannot keep.

In 1912 Casimir Funk — Polish-born, trained in Switzerland and Paris, working then at the Lister Institute in London — published a review with a title that still startles a modern copy desk: "The etiology of the deficiency diseases." He gathered four old ward names (the rice sailors' syndrome, the citrus sailors' syndrome, the bone-softening of children, the southern rash then blamed on corn) and said they were not four infections. They were four absences. The missing pieces, he guessed, were organic bases. Vital amines. *Vitamines.*

He had already spent 1911 hunched over rice bran. Eijkman's chickens in Batavia had pointed at the polish, not at a germ in the grain. Funk chased a concentrate out of the bran with the chemistry he had. He got a nitrogenous fraction. It looked like an amine. The pigeons on polished rice that had gone floppy recovered when he put the fraction back. He wrote that up in the *Journal of Physiology*. The title of *that* paper uses "cures." That is his verb, in his year, about birds. It is not a label line, and it is not ours.

The guess about the amine was wrong in the way confident chemistry is often wrong. Later work showed the family was not a family of amines at all. Some of the letters we still use — A, C, D — are not nitrogen bases. Drummond will walk in eight years later and knock the *e* off the end so the word matches the naming customs for a neutral stuff of unknown shape. The nickname survives the correction. That is the usual fate of a good wrong name.

What Funk actually donated, more than a bottle, was a *category*. Before him you could still talk as if "good protein" or "a better carbon-to-nitrogen ratio" explained why one ship lived and the other limped home. After him the argument had a noun. Laboratories could hunt a thing. Journals could argue about whether the thing was one thing or four. Companies, later, could put a letter on a tin.

He did not isolate the anti-rice-bran factor in a form anyone would still defend. Later chemists, being unkind in the useful way, said his 1911 powder was mostly nicotinic acid with a smear of the real rice factor riding along. Nicotinic acid would have its own decade, in Wisconsin, with dogs. The rice factor would wait for Robert Williams and a long American industrial patience. Funk had the word. Other people got the crystals.

He knew he was late to the observation and early to the branding. Hopkins had already been muttering about "accessory food factors." Takaki had already changed a navy's pot. Lind had already split twelve sailors into six pairs. Funk's gift was to say the absences were *specific* — one missing piece per old syndrome — and to give the missing pieces a marketable, slightly wrong, unforgettable name.

Say the word out loud with the *e*. It sounds like a patent medicine from a trunk. That is not an accident. 1912 is still a world of tonics. Funk was trying to drag a tonic-shaped idea into a laboratory. The extra letter is the sound of that drag.

We keep the shortened word. We do not keep his titles as instructions. A review about "deficiency diseases" is a document in a bound volume. It is not a reason to print a claim on a carton.

## Claims desk
