If you stand in an aisle long enough, the cartons start to recite.

"Calcium builds strong bones." "Fiber maintains bowel regularity." "Antioxidants maintain cell integrity." Those three sentences are FDA's own examples of structure/function claims. They are legal *on a dietary-supplement label* if the firm can substantiate them, files the 30-day notice, stays out of 101.93(g), and prints the boldface disclaimer.

This series will not recite them as its own.

The map, for a reader who is not a lawyer:

- **Drug claim / disease claim:** an effect on a disease, its signs, its therapy, its vectors. Forbidden to a supplement. The regulation lists the disguises.
- **Classical deficiency claim:** "C and the old sailor syndrome," allowed on a label only with a prevalence sentence. We still refuse it.
- **Structure/function:** a role in normal structure or a documented mechanism. Allowed on a label. Refused here, because we are not labeling.
- **General well-being:** "helps you feel" — the soggy middle. Allowed on a label with the same chores. Refused here.
- **Authorized health claim:** a pre-cleared food/disease statement with its own petition path. Not our path.
- **Nutrient content claim:** "high in," "good source." A different toolbox.

The January 2000 final rule is the long version of the map. It is readable if you are stubborn. It is where FDA says context is the whole page, and that the disclaimer does not wash a disease claim clean.

Why refuse even the legal S/F sentences? Because this folder's job is history. "Calcium builds strong bones" is not how Steenbock's lamp happened. It is how a carton talks. If we start talking like a carton, we will slide, sentence by sentence, into the (g)(2) list. The slide is how good writers become bad labels.

I have written S/F sentences for practice and then thrown them out. The muscle wants to be helpful. Helpful is how implied disease arrives. "Helps you fight" is already (viii). "For your heart" is a class. "The anemia of…" is (iii).

Carry the map in your pocket when you read an aisle. Carry a tighter map when you write in this folder. The tighter map has one road: what happened, who paid, what the statute forbade.

No bones. No regularity. No integrity of cells. Not because those sentences are always false. Because they are not our genre.


I keep this map in the folder so a future editor cannot say nobody told them. The legal aisle dialect is available. We did not forget it. We walked past it. If a draft in this series ever grows a 'supports' in narrator voice, the map is how you catch the slide on the first draft, not in a warning letter.
