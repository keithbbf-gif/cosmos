The United States Pharmacopeia is a book of tests.

A vitamin monograph in the USP–NF says: this is the name, this is the structure or the source, this is how you identify it, these are the limits for the dirt you do not want, this is how it should dissolve if it is a tablet. The book is a craftsman's document. It is older than DSHEA and older than Funk. It has spent a century learning to talk about chemicals without preaching.

DSHEA's labeling section allows a firm to say it followed a quality standard — if the statement is true. "USP" on a label is supposed to be a test claim, not a halo. The halo arrives anyway. Shoppers read a monogram as a tiny medal. Medals, in this series, are a known hazard.

I like monographs because they are boring in a way ads cannot stand. Water content. Heavy metals. A chromatographic identity. No runner. No sunbeam. If your vitamin story cannot survive a page that only talks about assays, the story was never about the vitamin.

The book is not holy. Methods get outdated. Lots fail. A firm can print "USP" and still miss. FDA's GMP rule (Part 111) is the other boring document: the factory's behavior, not only the powder's. Between the book and the rule there is still a person who has to care.

This draft exists so "pharmaceutical grade" and "tested" do not sneak through as implied disease claims. They are manufacturing adjectives. They can be true or false. They do not say what a letter *does in a body*. When a carton stacks "USP" next to a body verb, the verb is the problem.

Internationally there are other books: BP, Ph. Eur., JP. Trade needs a common language of tests. Codex, later in this series if we get to it, is another language. Languages of tests are how drums cross borders. They are not how a sailor in 1747 got citrus.

If you work in a quality unit, you already know this page. If you do not, keep one sentence: a monograph is a recipe for skepticism, not a hymn to a cure. We will not sing.

Boring on purpose. Tests, limits, a monogram that should stay small.


If a firm boasts 'tested' and cannot tell you tested *for what*, you have met a halo. The monograph is the opposite sentence: tested for this, against these limits, by this method. I would rather a shopper meet the opposite sentence and walk away bored than meet the halo and walk away healed in the head. Boredom is a quality system. Healing-in-the-head is a caption.

## Claims desk

Quality-standard history. No "pharmaceutical grade means it works." No body verb.

**Staged historical education, not labeling.** This draft is not a dietary-supplement label, not a product, and not medical advice. Dietary supplements are not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease. Structure/function claims, deficiency-disease claims, and well-being claims are out of scope here.
