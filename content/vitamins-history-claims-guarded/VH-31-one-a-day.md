One-A-Day is a schedule pretending to be a product.

Miles Laboratories, in Elkhart, Indiana — the same town that gave America Alka-Seltzer — put the mark into commerce in September 1940. The Wyoming trademark file is almost comic in its plainness: "vitamin tablets." Class 18, medicines and pharmaceutical preparations. That classing is a period habit. The legal category later moves. The daily habit stays.

What Miles sold was not a new molecule. Roche and Merck and the rest were already making the letters. Miles sold a *cadence*. One, a day. The name is a compliance department in three words. It fits on a radio jingle. It fits on a kitchen table next to the salt.

I have seen later ads that promise pep, brightness, the look of a person who slept. Those ads are documents. They are not instructions we repeat. "Pep" is the 1940s cousin of "energy support," which this folder also will not say in its own voice. The history of the multivitamin is, in large part, the history of mood words looking for a legal home.

Elkhart is the right setting. A Midwestern tablet house, not a Basel cathedral and not a Cambridge bench. The American vitamin century is a drugstore century. The clerk, the glass jar, the newspaper coupon. Miles knew that room.

Bayer owns the brand now. Ownership is a relay. The cadence outlived the original corporation, which is the tell. People were not loyal to Miles. They were loyal to a sentence about tomorrow morning.

Is a daily multi a scientific object? Sometimes it is a hedge against a dull diet. Sometimes it is a ritual. Rituals are not banned. Selling a ritual as if it were a discovered letter is the move we refuse. The letters were discovered. The "one" and the "day" were marketing.

1940 is also a war-adjacent year. Enrichment is being argued. The Food and Nutrition Board is meeting. A tablet that says "we already did the loaf for you" is a private answer to a public conversation. That answer made a lot of money.

No serving suggestion from me. If you have a tin on the table, it is yours. This page is about a town, a date, and a hyphen.


Elkhart's other famous tablet taught America to drop two in water and listen. One-A-Day taught America not to listen — to swallow on a calendar. Those are opposite acoustics from the same town. I like the pairing because it ruins the idea that a vitamin brand is a scientist. It is a habit engineer. Habits are commercial objects. Calendars are their tools.
