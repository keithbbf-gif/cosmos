Not every letter lived.

Vitamin F was, for a while, a way some writers talked about essential fatty acids. Then the acids kept their own names and F was shown the door. G was a nickname that overlapped riboflavin. H was biotin before biotin won. M showed up in monkey work that later sat nearer folate. P was "permeability factor," the citrin / flavonoid episode, and it did not hold as a vitamin. T was a termite-related curiosity that never belonged in a human bottle. There were others. The alphabet is a graveyard with a gift shop at the front selling A through K, skipping a few, smiling as if the path had been straight.

I like the dead letters. They teach the only humility this industry reliably forgets: a vitamin is not a vitamin because a catalog number exists. It is a vitamin if an animal cannot make enough of it, if the absence makes a specific mess, and if the substance is organic and needed in small amounts. Even that school definition leaks. The leak is why committees exist.

Commerce hates graveyards. Dead letters are unsold inventory and embarrassed copy. You can still find "vitamin F" on a boutique label as if the funeral had been optional. That is not charming. That is a firm using a corpse as a spice.

Drummond's 1920 hope — A, B, C, and so on until chemistry retires the alphabet — assumed the "and so on" would stay small. It did not. B became a complex. K arrived from a German noun. E arrived from a litter. People tried to alphabetize flavonoids, carnitine, pangamic acid, B15, B17. Some of those were honest inquiries. Some were hustles. The hustles loved the word *vitamin* because Funk's extra *e* had already done the marketing in 1912.

This draft is a plea for the word *retired.* Science retires names. Shops should too.

If a carton leans on a letter your textbook does not list, you are not looking at a secret. You are looking at a graveyard flower. History can name the flower. A label that sells it as a missing essential has left education and entered a claim — often a poorly substantiated one.

We will keep the living letters for the rest of this series, and we will not pretend they were inevitable.


If you collect old textbooks you can watch the letters die in real time: a confident F in one edition, a footnote in the next, a silence after that. Silence is the correct last step. Shops that refuse the silence are not keeping a tradition. They are keeping a SKU. This folder's job includes funerals. Funerals are how a science stays smaller than a catalog.

## Claims desk

Nomenclature history. No essential-fatty-acid pitch, no flavonoid pitch, no "B17."
