# The 1983 Book

*Educational history for SLPWOW. Not a treatment plan and not a substitute for evaluation by a licensed clinician.*

College-Hill Press published Jeri A. Logemann’s *Evaluation and Treatment of Swallowing Disorders* in 1983. The author was a Northwestern speech pathologist — bachelor’s, master’s, and Ph.D. from the same university, 1963 to 1968 — who had been on the faculty since 1978 and held appointments that crossed into otolaryngology and neurology. The book did what a good textbook does when a job is forming: it gave teachers something to assign and gave the fluoro room a vocabulary that was not only the radiologist’s.

A second edition appeared from Pro-Ed in 1998. Students who met the later printing sometimes talk as if the field began there. It began, for this training line, on the 1983 spine.

## A cookie, not a legend

Clinic folklore calls her method the cookie swallow. The dignified name is the modified barium swallow, or a videofluoroscopic swallow study done with measured textures rather than a GI “drink this jug.” The folklore is not wrong about the object. A cookie is a solid you can see fail. It is also a joke a later patient can hear as a dismissal. Logemann’s papers and the book are more careful than the nickname. This pack will use the nickname once, as folklore, and then return to the method as a recorded, radio-opaque meal in motion.

What the 1983 text did, besides name a study, was teach SLPs to talk about swallow physiology in a sequence they could write down: a stage, a failure, a hypothesized biomechanical reason. Later readers will fight about whether the maneuvers that grew around that physiology were over-taught. That fight is a later chapter. The 1983 event is a *shared note*. Another clinician could read what you thought you saw.

She also already had another book-shaped object in the field: the Fisher–Logemann Test of Articulation Competence. The swallow work did not come from nowhere. It came from a mouth specialist who walked into a radiology room.

## What the decade thought it was solving

A profession that could describe a lisp in five lines and a swallow in a shrug. A radiology list that said “barium swallow” and meant esophagus. Patients after stroke and after head-and-neck treatment who were being fed or not fed on the basis of a bedside adjective.

Two or three institutions, not twelve: Northwestern’s Chicago campus and the swallowing center she directed there; College-Hill as a small press willing to bet on a new shelf; ASHA, which would later make her a fellow and, twice, a president — facts that belong to the life, not as a medal pile in this essay.

Wylie Dodds and Peter Kahrilas appear in the physiology papers around and after the book: Milwaukee and Chicago, manometry and pictures, GI names on SLP problems. The book is not a lone-genius myth if you read the reference lists.

## Who was left out

European cine radiology (Ekberg’s Lund, Donner's Hopkins) was already looking at the pharynx. The 1983 book is an American SLP event, not the first time a human swallow was filmed. Infants are thinner in this particular spine than in Bosma. Nursing-home ethics are thinner than they will be in Leslie’s later essays. A textbook cannot be the whole field. It can only become the one everyone is assigned.

## Residue

The residue is a generation of clinicians who can say “oral transit” without blushing and a generation of arguments about whether they were taught to see a swallow or to perform one. This pack will not reprint a maneuver list. It will say that the lists came from a book that was trying to make a job teachable. Logemann died on 19 June 2014, in her seventy-third year. The book is still on the shelf. That is not an endorsement of every later worksheet that quoted it.
