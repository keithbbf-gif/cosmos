# The Esophagoscope and the Safety Pin

*Educational history for SLPWOW. Not a treatment plan and not a substitute for evaluation by a licensed clinician.*

In 1897, in Freiburg, Gustav Killian used a rigid tube and a light to take a foreign body out of a living airway. The date is a national sport in the endoscopy histories; the published removal is the fact this pack will carry. Direct bronchoscopy — looking *down*, not guessing from a chest sound — turned the swallowed or inhaled object from a household catastrophe into a case you could teach.

On the other side of the Atlantic, Chevalier Jackson made that case into a life’s work. Pittsburgh, then Philadelphia. Jefferson, then Temple. A clinic that collected open safety pins the way another man collects stamps. The pins are what tourists remember. The children are what the clinic was for.

## A tube, not a swallow study

The rigid esophagoscope and bronchoscope are not videofluoroscopy. They are a visit to a dark tube with a lamp and a set of forceps. Jackson’s manuals — *Bronchoscopy and Esophagoscopy* in its Saunders editions from 1922 onward — teach posture, light, and the discipline of not tearing what you came to save. They do not teach a speech-language pathologist how to score residue. That job does not exist in this hallway yet.

What the tube did for later swallow work is still large. It made the hypopharynx and esophagus places you could *enter*. Killian’s triangle — the weak spot that later writers will attach to Zenker’s diverticulum — is an anatomist’s gift to people who would never meet him. Jackson’s foreign-body catalogue taught a century of residents that the history of the meal includes the history of the object.

## What the decade thought it was solving

Choking that did not resolve. Coins. Bones. The open safety pin, which Jackson used as a teaching demon because it is ordinary, American, and designed to spring. Caustic strictures in children who had found lye. Cancer later, when the same tube that saved a toddler could stage a tumor.

Two or three institutions, not twelve: Killian’s German university theater; Jackson’s Philadelphia clinic and the bronchoesophagology meetings that grew around him; the published plate — a pin, a coin, a denture — as proof you had been there.

Harris Peyton Mosher at Harvard is in the next rooms of this pack. He is the anatomy-and-esophagus man, the Semon lecturer of 1929, not the pin collector. The hallway is already splitting: endoscopy as extraction, endoscopy as physiology, radiology as a picture without a tube.

## Who was left out

Mothers. They brought the child. They are footnotes when they are lucky. Speech teachers are not in this clinic. Neither is a dietitian with a thickener. The “swallow problem” of 1910, in this literature, is an object or a stricture, not a delayed pharyngeal response after a hemisphere stroke. Stroke patients existed. They were not this specialty’s mascot.

Jackson’s autobiography (1938) is a primary source and a performance. He liked the legend of the poor boy from the Pennsylvania hills who taught the world to look down a tube. Use the clinic catalogues and the manuals when the legend gets too clean. The pins were real. So were the deaths when the tube came too late.

## Residue

A modern FEES is not a Jackson esophagoscopy. The fiberscope is flexible, the patient is often upright, the question is often residue and aspiration rather than a pin. Saying they are “the same” is a conference slide. Saying they are unrelated is a different lie. Both are arguments that the dark crossing of food and air should be looked at, not guessed.

Killian gets a life in this pack. Jackson gets a longer one. The residue of the safety-pin era is a glass case of objects and a permission: the swallow can hide a thing, and the thing can be retrieved if someone has practiced.
