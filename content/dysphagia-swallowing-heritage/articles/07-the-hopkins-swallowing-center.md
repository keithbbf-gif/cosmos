# The Hopkins Swallowing Center

*Educational history for SLPWOW. Not a treatment plan and not a substitute for evaluation by a licensed clinician.*

Martin W. Donner became director of radiology at Johns Hopkins in 1972. In 1981 he opened what colleagues would later call the country’s first swallowing center — some memorials say the first in the world. In 1986 he became founding editor of a new Springer journal, *Dysphagia*. In 1987 he left the department chair to give the center and the journal his days. He stepped down from the center in 1990, was named Distinguished Professor in 1991, and died at Hopkins Hospital on 13 April 1992, of complications after a heart transplant that March. He was seventy-one. The Baltimore *Sun* ran the obituary two days later. Birth day is not forced in this pack.

“First in the world” is a colleague’s sentence, not a census of every GI fluoroscopy service on earth. What Donner actually built is enough without the prize: a room where radiology was supposed to *ask* the other specialties, not only answer their requisitions.

## A journal as a building

Volume 1 of *Dysphagia* (1986) is an editorial act. Swallowing papers had been scattered through radiology, GI, ENT, and the occasional rehabilitation title. Donner’s journal announced a subject. Jeri Logemann is already in that early run — her 1986 piece on aspiration sits in the same volume a student might pull for the center’s origin myth. Susan Langmore’s 1988 FEESS paper will land in volume 2. The journal is not a Hopkins house organ. It is a bet that the subject can carry its own spine.

Bronwyn Jones, Donner’s Hopkins collaborator, would put the pictures into a book: *Normal and Abnormal Swallowing: Imaging in Diagnosis and Therapy* (Springer, 1991). Atlases are arguments. This one argued that the swallow had a radiology, and that the radiology was not a hobby for people who had finished the barium enema list.

## What the decade thought it was solving

Fragmentation. A patient who belonged to everyone and therefore to no one. Stroke here, head-and-neck there, GI downstairs, the X-ray list written as “rule out aspiration” by a house officer who would not stay for the fluoro. Donner’s answer was a *center*: a word American hospitals were learning to love in those years. The memorials insist he meant multidisciplinary as a working habit, not a letterhead.

Two or three institutions, not twelve: the Russell H. Morgan department he had run; the Swallowing Center; Springer’s new journal. The Society of Gastrointestinal Radiologists had already given him the Walter B. Cannon Medal in 1983. The medal is a GI-radiology object. The center is a swallow object. He lived in both.

## Who was left out

Jones and Donner’s 1991 atlas is the object a later fellow can still hold when the center’s original furniture is gone. Normal and abnormal, side by side, the pharynx not treated as a courtesy chapter before the barium column. An atlas is also a political document: it says the subject is large enough for plates. Hospitals that never bought the book still borrowed the idea that a swallow could be a *service line*.

Speech-language pathologists were entering this hallway in the same decade — Logemann’s book is 1983 — and the official Hopkins story is radiology-first. That is not an insult. It is a sequence. Nursing, dietetics, and the family in the waiting room are thinner in the memorials than they were in the actual corridor. Bosma, who wrote a Donner memorial in *Dysphagia* itself, is the pediatric physiologist crossing the adult threshold. Hold that crossing. Do not turn it into a merger.

## Residue

Every later hospital that prints “Swallowing Center” on a glass door is quoting this decade, whether or not the door opens onto Donner’s idea. Some doors open onto a single SLP and a shared fluoro slot. Some open onto a meeting. The residue of 1981 is the meeting: a radiologist who thought the question was larger than a film.

Donner gets a life in this pack. Jones gets one. The journal still arrives. That is a residue you can hold.
