# Infant Mouths at Bethesda

*Educational history for SLPWOW. Not a treatment plan and not a substitute for evaluation by a licensed clinician.*

A newborn who cannot coordinate suck, swallow, and breath is an old emergency. The science that treated that coordination as something you could film, measure, and atlas is younger. Its American address, for a generation, was Bethesda. James F. Bosma joined the National Institute of Dental Research in 1961 after two years at Karolinska Institutet in Stockholm, where he had already been looking at infant cry as a motion, not only as a sound. He retired from NIH in 1982 and kept working. Johns Hopkins University Press published *Anatomy of the Infant Head* in 1986. He died in June 2001, in Baltimore, of heart failure, at eighty-five.

The pediatric feeding team of a later decade will put his books on the shelf and then argue in a different language. This essay stays with the films and the atlas.

## Cry as a motion

The 1965 *Acta Paediatrica* supplement that carries Bosma, H. M. Truby, and John Lind is easy to misfile as “speech science.” It is airway science. Cry motions, swallow motions, the neonatal transition — cine and acoustic traces of a small head doing several jobs with one crossing. Stockholm had the collaborators. Bethesda had the long American appointment. The infant mouth, in this literature, is not cute. It is a mechanism that can distort when the upper pharynx is the wrong shape.

Bosma’s earlier and later papers on micrognathia, glossoptosis, and the Pierre Robin anatomy sit next to that supplement. A 1960 *Acta* note with Yasuaki Takagi on tongue displacement in an infant, a 1962 paper with Ralph L. Shelton on maintaining the pharyngeal airway, the 1962 “Upper Respiratory Mechanisms of Newborn Infants” essay with Lind — these are not a brand. They are a man staying with a small head. He is not inventing the pediatrician’s emergency. He is insisting that the emergency has an anatomy you can draw.

The dental-research address is easy to misread. NIDR paid the bills because the infant mouth is also teeth, palate, and sensation. Speech-language pathology would later claim the same mouth as a feeding caseload. Dentistry had already claimed it as a shape. The cine does not care who bills.

## What the decade thought it was solving

Why some infants cannot feed without turning blue. What “oral sensation and perception” could mean when the subject cannot give you a word. How to put the infant head on a page so a surgeon, a dentist, and a later therapist are looking at the same bone.

Two or three institutions, not twelve: Karolinska in the cry years; NIDR/NIH for the long atlas work; Hopkins and Maryland later, when Bosma’s retirement was only a payroll event. The 1986 book is the object a student can still hold. Four hundred-odd pages. Not a pamphlet. Not a blog.

## Who was left out

Mothers, again, except as historians of the night feed. Speech pathologists are scarce in the early cine credits. Occupational therapists who would later own “feeding” in some American hospitals are not yet in this sentence. The official story of pediatric dysphagia sometimes starts with a 1990s textbook. The films are older. Say so.

Bosma also sat, after NIH, with the Johns Hopkins Swallowing Center — a pediatric physiologist in a building that Martin Donner had opened for adults and anyone else who needed a meeting. The infant and the stroke patient share a crossing. They do not share a protocol. This pack will not invent one.

## Residue

Every later NICU swallow note that talks about coordination instead of “poor feeder” is speaking Bosma’s language, whether or not the writer has opened the 1986 atlas. The residue is the cine: a small hyoid, a small epiglottis, a breath that will not wait. Arvedson and Lefton-Greif will turn that cine into a training literature. They get their own rooms. This room stays dark, and flickering, and Bethesda.
