# Harris P. Mosher

*Educational history for SLPWOW. Not a treatment plan and not a substitute for evaluation by a licensed clinician.*

> **Portrait placeholder.** No public-domain or clearly licensed portrait located as of the pack date. See `PORTRAIT_SOURCES.md` for hunt status. Do not invent or generate a substitute likeness.


Maine, 1867. Death in 1954. Day dates are not forced in this pack; the *Annals of Otology, Rhinology & Laryngology* and the *Archives of Otolaryngology* both ran memorials that year and the next. Harris Peyton Mosher became the first person Harvard Medical School hired to teach laryngology and otology as a dedicated, full-time professorial job. The Mosher Course — a postgraduate grind through head-and-neck anatomy — is the object his department still mentions when it tells its own story.

This pack wants the esophagus man.

## The lower end

In 1929 he gave the Semon Lecture in London: “The Lower End of the Oesophagus at Birth and in the Adult.” Anatomy, microscopy, radiography. Barium as a way to find a fluid level. Seidlitz powder as a way to raise a bubble and make a wall visible. The lecture is a period piece and a research program. It treats the cardia and the lower tube as objects that change from birth to adulthood — Bosma’s infant head is still decades away, but the instinct is related: do not pretend the child’s crossing is a small adult.

Mosher’s American career is society presidencies and a generation of residents who learned anatomy from a man who thought you should put your hands on a cadaver before you put a tube in a living neck. The ABEA’s Mosher Award is a later memory habit. Awards are not biographies. They are a field saying: this name still sorts us.

## What later people kept

The anatomy course as a rite. The lower esophagus as a problem that is not only “cardiospasm.” The habit of mixing dissection with a plate.

What they discarded: some of the gas-and-powder tricks, and the idea that one professor could own both the ear and the meal. Specialties split. His chair was already a split from general surgery. The later splits would leave swallow people in three buildings.

He is not an SLP. He would not have understood the cookie. He would have understood a barium level, and he would have asked where the lower end sat on the plate.

## Residue

A Maine birth, a 1954 death, a course that still has his name on the brochure. The residue is a lecture about the lower end, and a reminder that Harvard’s swallow history does not begin in a speech department. Countway still owes this pack a cleared portrait file page.
