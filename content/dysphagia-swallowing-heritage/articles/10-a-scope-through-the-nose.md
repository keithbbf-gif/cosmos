# A Scope Through the Nose

*Educational history for SLPWOW. Not a treatment plan and not a substitute for evaluation by a licensed clinician.*

*Dysphagia* volume 2, number 4 (1988), pages 216–219. Susan E. Langmore, Kenneth Schatz, and Nels Olsen, United States Department of Veterans Affairs. Title: “Fiberoptic Endoscopic Examination of Swallowing Safety: A New Procedure.” Four pages. An acronym with an extra S — FEESS — that later branding would slim to FEES. The paper does not invent the flexible nasopharyngolaryngoscope. Silberman, Wilf, and Tucker had already put that instrument in the *Annals* in 1976. What the 1988 note did was name a *swallow* use: look at the pharynx before and after a swallow when videofluoroscopy is the wrong room.

Wrong room is the historical fact. ICU. Bed. A patient who cannot travel. A radiation budget you do not want to spend that day. The VA had those patients in bulk.

## White-out and the other picture

A fiberscope in the pharynx during the swallow itself goes briefly blind — the white-out every later student is warned about. The 1988 procedure, as described, is not a movie of the hyoid. It is a look at secretions, residue, the larynx as a place food should not be, the safety of trying a meal when the fluoro tower is downstairs. Later books (Langmore’s own Thieme text, 2001) will thicken the method. This essay stays with the four pages and the institutional fight they started.

Welch’s 1982 note in the *Journal of Laryngology & Otology* on the practical value of flexible laryngoscopy, and the 1970s ENT papers that treated a nose as a corridor, are the borrowed stairs. Langmore’s contribution is the swallow question at the top of the stairs: not “is the cord moving” but “is a meal a reasonable next sentence.” Those are different jobs that happen to share a nostril.

The fight was ownership. Who may pass a scope. Who bills. Who is qualified. ENT had been looking through noses for a long time. SLPs had just taken the swallow. A paper that put an SLP’s name first on an endoscopic swallow exam was never going to be only a methods note. ASHA’s later practice-portal language — SLP, physician, or both — is a truce document. Truces have histories. This is one of them.

Jonathan Aviv’s later FEESST work (sensory testing with an air pulse) is a branch, not the trunk. Mention it as a branch. Do not turn this essay into a menu of later acronyms.

## What the decade thought it was solving

The patient the cookie study could not reach. The question “is it safe to try a bite” asked at two in the morning. A picture that did not require a lead apron or a barium kitchen.

Two or three institutions, not twelve: the Boston VA hallway the authors were working in; Donner’s journal, two years old and hungry for a subject; the older ENT instrument papers that made the 1988 note possible.

## Who was left out

Children appear later in the FEES literature and remain a consent-and-anatomy argument. The 1988 patient is an adult, often a veteran. Community hospitals without a scope or without a political truce are not in the paper. Patients who hate a nose more than they hate a radiology suite are not a statistic here; they are a later clinic fact.

## Residue

Every later argument about “FEES versus VFSS” is a descendant of these four pages plus the 1983 book. The honest residue is not a winner. It is two pictures of two parts of one act, each blind to something the other can see. Langmore gets a life in this pack. The extra S in FEESS is the kind of small fact a magazine should keep. Acronyms get cleaned. Papers keep their titles.
