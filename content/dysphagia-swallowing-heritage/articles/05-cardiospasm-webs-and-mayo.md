# Cardiospasm, Webs, and the Mayo Corridor

*Educational history for SLPWOW. Not a treatment plan and not a substitute for evaluation by a licensed clinician.*

If you learned “dysphagia” in a speech-language pathology program, you may have met the esophagus as a courtesy chapter. History runs the other way. For the first half of the twentieth century the printed swallow that had a department, a bougie, and a barium list was the esophageal one. Guy’s Hospital in London. The Mayo Clinic corridor in Rochester. A British word, *cardiospasm*, that later writers would fold into *achalasia* without asking the older men for permission.

Arthur Hurst — Sir Arthur after the honors — is the name this pack uses for the British side. Henry S. Plummer and Porter P. Vinson are the names it uses for the Minnesota side. The point is not a founder fight. The point is that the meal had already been medicalized in the chest before anyone in Evanston asked a cookie to tell the truth about the hyoid.

## Barium as a habit, not a brand

By the 1910s and 1920s a radio-opaque meal was no longer Cannon’s student trick. It was a morning list. Hurst wrote about cardiospasm and about what the shadow was doing at the cardia. He was a physician of parts — later readers know him also for shell shock — and this pack will not flatten him into a single disease. The swallow papers are enough: a man at Guy’s who treated the unopening cardia as a thing you could argue from a plate and a history, not only from a dinner complaint.

At Mayo, Plummer and then Vinson attached their names to a syndrome that European writers often file under Paterson–Brown–Kelly: dysphagia, esophageal web, iron-deficiency anemia, mostly women in the published series. The eponyms are a mess and the mess is the history. National pride got into the throat. What the clinic actually had was a pattern — a web, a swallow that stuck, a blood picture — and a barium habit that made the web less of a rumor.

Mosher’s 1929 Semon Lecture, “The Lower End of the Oesophagus at Birth and in the Adult,” sits on this shelf from the Harvard side: anatomy, radiography, even a gas trick with Seidlitz powder to raise a bubble. The lower end was a research object. The pharynx was still a blur.

## What the decade thought it was solving

Strictures. Spasms that might not be spasms. Webs. The cancer that hides behind a benign story. Dilatation as a craft. Whether the cardia fails because a nerve fails or because a muscle will not relax — an argument that will outlive everyone in this essay.

Two or three institutions, not twelve: Guy’s; Mayo; the barium kitchen that every large hospital was installing whether or not it had a “swallowing center.”

## Who was left out

The pharyngeal stroke swallow. It existed. It did not yet have this corridor’s full attention. Infants appear as congenital atresias and caustic injuries, not as cine of suck–swallow–breathe. Speech correctionists in American normal schools are teaching /s/, not reading a cardia.

Women are over-represented in the web-and-anemia series and under-represented as authors. Say that once, then stay with the papers.

## Residue

A later SLP who treats “esophageal dysphagia” as someone else’s problem is repeating a training accident, not a law of nature. The Mayo corridor and Guy’s already proved that a meal can fail below the UES and still ruin a life. The residue of this era is a barium cup and a set of eponyms that do not agree. Hurst gets a life in this pack. Plummer and Vinson stay here, in the corridor, as a pair who do not need a forty-fifth profile.
