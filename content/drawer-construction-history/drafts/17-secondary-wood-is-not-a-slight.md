Poplar dust is a pale green you can taste. I was shooting a lining and the pile on the bench was already that color — not walnut, not the face the room would see. The front leaned against the wall, a pecan board with a knot I had decided to keep. The sides were tulip poplar from a stack that lives under the long glass in this shop. Two species, one box. Nobody in Bradley County needed a lecture about honesty. The lining was doing lining work.

Primary wood is the show. Fronts, sometimes the case, sometimes a cockbead, a pair of columns, a top you set a candlestick on. Secondary wood is the rest of the drawer: sides, back, bottom, the bits a hand finds only when the box is open. English shops said *lining* for that job. They said *deal* for a pine or fir board that was not trying to be wainscot oak. Hayward uses the word as a trade word, not as an insult. I will not flatten deal into a modern grade stamp. [VERIFY] any yard name before it goes on a tag.

The slight is in the modern mouth. Someone sees poplar sides and calls the drawer cheap. Someone sees oak linings in an English stack and calls it better. Species is a job. Oak wears and it is heavy and it can be rude under a leaf. Pine is light and it dents and it takes a groove without a fight. Poplar in this country is the quiet workhorse — stable enough, cheap enough, green enough in the dust that you know what you are breathing. Chestnut shows up in some Mid-Atlantic linings; it works like a cousin of oak until a ring-shake reminds you it is chestnut.

## The front is allowed to be a picture

A mahogany front on oak sides is 18th-century ordinary in more than one tradition. A walnut front on pine is ordinary. A cherry front on poplar is what I build when the case is cherry and I do not want the drawer to weigh like a sash weight. The pull lives on the front. The wear lives on the side. Those are different contracts. One board rarely wants both.

I have built all-walnut drawers because a client wanted the inside to match the outside in a photograph. The drawers were beautiful. They were also heavy, and the sides moved a little more than I like in a groove, and I spent a Saturday shooting them again after a wet week. I can do it. I do not recommend it as virtue. Matching species is a taste. Mixed species is a trade.

Veneer is the one place I will nod at a scar this pack already told. Oak is a rude ground for a leaf — rays telegraph, Hayward already drew it — so the ground of a veneered front often wants deal or a quieter hardwood. That is a ground problem, not a lining sermon. The sides can still be oak while the front’s ground is pine. Two jobs. Two boards.

## What each lining is for

<!-- PHOTO: fig-02 D:\BBF\BBF Photos — oak or chestnut lining next to a pine or deal bottom, or poplar dust on a bench (filename pending shop pull) -->

Oak sides in English work take a groove or a rebate and they last. They also add weight, and they can stain a little dark against a pale shirt. I like oak when the runner is oak and I want the wear pair to be even. I do not like oak when the drawer is a small jewelry box and the customer will feel the mass every time.

Pine — white pine in New England habits, yellow pine in the South when that is what the shed holds — makes a bottom that planes into a bevel and a back that is allowed to be humble. It dents under a tool. It splits if you nail a long-grain floor and lock it. I still use it. I just do not ask it to be a shoe in a kitchen that sees sand.

Poplar is what this shop reaches for when the front is the story. It takes a tail. It takes a groove. It paints if you need paint. The green in the dust is a reminder that you are not cutting furniture-show wood; you are cutting a lining. People who want “real wood” on every surface have not lifted a stack of poplar drawers and a stack of maple ones on the same afternoon.

Chestnut I treat as a regional guest. When it is sound it is a pleasure. When it is punky it crumbles in a pin. I do not hunt it. If it arrives in a repair, I keep it if I can and I do not stain it to impersonate oak.

Deal, again, is the English word I will not turn into a brand. Pine or fir board. Grounds, sometimes linings, sometimes a whole cheap chest. The word is useful because it keeps you from reading “pine” as “porch lumber.” Period deal can be a fine, even board. Modern construction pine can be a different animal with a different knot.

## The slight that is a real slight

Secondary wood becomes a slight when you use it where a show wood was promised, or when you use a junk board for a job that needs meat. A poplar front stained walnut is a costume. A drawer side so full of pith that the groove has no wall is not “traditional lining.” It is a board that should have been firewood.

I inherited a painted pine case whose drawer sides were a mix — one oak, one poplar, one something that might have been gum. The maker had used leftovers. The drawers ran. They also racked at different rates because the sides wore at different rates. Mixed linings *inside one box* are a different decision than mixed species *between* front and lining. I recut the worst side. I did not lecture the dead.

South Arkansas will grow you oak, pine, pecan, gum, and a poplar that wants to be a tree by the river. The mill-heritage story is not this essay. The shop fact is: I can put a pecan face on a poplar box and sleep. I cannot put a pecan face on a pith-and-bark box and call it Federal thrift.

## Failures

I once used walnut offcuts for sides “so nothing would be wasted.” The offcuts were too short. I joined them. The join landed in a tail. The tail let go at the join. Waste less on the front. Do not invent extra joints in a lining to feel frugal.

I also sold, early, a “all hardwood” drawer set as if the phrase were a grade. The customer got maple boxes under maple fronts and liked the weight. The next job wanted the same words and needed to be lighter. I stopped using the phrase. I say show wood and lining wood. If they want one species, they can have it on the traveler as a line, not as a moral.

A repair in Warren had “upgraded” pine sides to oak after a dog chewed a corner. The oak was thicker. The drawer no longer entered without a plane. Upgrade is the wrong verb. Match the job. The dog was not a dating clock.

I used chestnut for a pair of sides because a board showed up in a pile and I wanted the romance of a Mid-Atlantic lining. One side was sound. The other had a shake I did not see until the groove found it. The groove became a mouth. I recut in poplar and kept the sound chestnut for a sample. Regional is not a reason to ignore a crack.

A pine bottom under oak sides is ordinary. An oak bottom under pine sides is a heavy floor that will still split if you lock it. I have built that reversal because the oak was on top of the stack. The drawer ran. The owner felt the weight every time. Secondary is a job, not a leftover lottery.

## What this is not

This is not a veneer-telegraph essay. The leaf-and-deal scar already has a Saturday. This is not a claim that cheap wood is always the right wood. This is the trade fact that a drawer is a mixed object. The room is owed a face. The runner is owed a side that can be a side. The bottom is owed a board that can move. Those boards do not have to match.

I will not print a map of which colony used which lining as if I had counted attics. Poplar is common in a lot of American work. Pine is common. Oak is common in English linings and in some American ones. Chestnut happens. Deal is a word. [VERIFY] any regional stamp before it becomes a museum voice.

The judgment is small. Call the front what it is. Call the lining what it is. Do not apologize for poplar dust on a pecan job. Do not dress a junk board in the word *secondary* and expect the weather to honor it. The slight is a bad board, not a second species.
