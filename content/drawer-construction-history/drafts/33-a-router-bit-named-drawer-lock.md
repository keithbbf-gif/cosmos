The bit sang once in the router table — a short, ugly note, carbide on poplar — and two scraps locked like a handshake. I pulled them along the joint and they refused. I pulled them apart the other way and they came, because I had not glued them yet. On the shank, in small letters, a trade name and the words drawer lock. Not dovetail. Not 1780. A hook cut by a spinning profile, the same cutter for both parts if you flip the board. I set the corner on the glass wall’s sill and let the daylight show the step. It looked like what it is: a factory handshake with extra shoulder.

The catalogs and the shop magazines treat this as ordinary production now. Popular Woodworking has run the lock rabbet as a kitchen-and-jewelry-box joint more than once. Woodcraft’s own joinery note calls the drawer-lock bit a modern joint, unsuited to reproductions, useful for production and kitchens. I will stand on that published honesty. I will not invent a first Tuesday the bit was sold, or a patent number to hang on a tag. [VERIFY] any commercial birthday before anyone prints one. What I know from the bench is enough: this is 20th-century speed, cousin to a table-saw locking rabbet, grandchild of the nailed rebate, not a secret the London price book forgot to list.

## A hook, not a tail

A half-blind dovetail locks because the tail cannot come forward through the front. A through tail at the back locks because the pins and tails refuse the pull. A plain rabbet is a step. Glue and nails do the rest, and when they fail the step is a launch. A locking rabbet adds a tongue that seats in a groove — a modified tongue-and-groove at the corner — so the parts have to lift, not just slide, to separate. Glue surface goes up. Alignment comes for free if the bit is set. The finished drawer might not have the pizazz of tails, as one magazine put it. It goes together a lot faster.

That is the whole sell. I will not decorate it. Joyce, in the production chapters of *The Technique of Furniture Making*, would recognize the cousin without a blush: a joint a shop can repeat. The bit is fussy to set. Test scraps of the same thickness. Height, then fence, then another pair of scraps. Save the last good pair as a setup block. The forums are full of people who hated the first hour and loved the twelfth drawer. I am one of them, on kitchen runs. I am not one of them on a cherry stack for a sitting room.

Do not confuse this cutter with a lock-miter bit. A lock miter is a different religion — 45 degrees, a tongue in the miter, pretty on a post or a box that wants to look frameless. A drawer-lock is a square corner with a hook. If you sell the wrong name you will set the wrong fence and waste a front.

## Strong enough for a kitchen

I have put pots in these boxes. I have put silverware, junk, and a cast-iron skillet that should have lived on a shelf. The joint holds if the glue holds and the stock is flat. Baltic birch at half an inch is the magazine’s favorite, and for once the magazine and the kitchen agree: many plies, few voids, a sandwich that takes a cutter without crumbling if you back up the exit. Solid poplar takes it too. Solid oak will tear if you starve the fence and rush. Plywood blowout at the corner is the usual scar. A zero-clearance insert and a patient feed fix more of that than a lecture.

The pull lives on the front. The lock has to resist that pull. A drawer-lock bit, set so the hook has meat, resists in the same direction a tail does — front to back — and is polite about side-to-side racking if the box is square. It is not polite if the front is a slab of 5/8 plywood bowed from a wet garage and you force the hook. Flat stock. Same thickness. That is the law the bit actually enforces.

Undermount slides change the contract again — a later essay. The box still owes the corner a joint that does not open when a child hangs on the front. A locking rabbet will do that job. So will a dadoed back and a screwed front. So will half-blinds. Choose by the room and the hour, not by a ghost in a waistcoat.

## Do not fake the century

I will not run this bit on a mahogany front, cockbead the edges, and tell anyone in Warren the drawer is Hepplewhite. I will not stain the hook-ends to look like pins. I will not cut the joint in pine, sand the machine marks off the inside, and write “hand-cut lock” on an invoice. The bit has a name because the trade needed a name for a production corner. Use the name. Charge the kitchen. Leave 1788 on the shelf with the price book.

There is a table-saw version of the same idea — a locking rabbet you can cut with a flat-top blade and a few setups. Older than the named bit, still not an 18th-century lining. Country nailed rabbets are older still, and they have their own clock in this pack. A hook you cut with a saw is honest. A hook you cut with a saw and then describe as “the old way they locked drawers in Boston” is a story. Mussey’s Seymour pages are bottoms, grooves, and kerfed blocks. They are not this cutter.

If a customer wants the look of tails on a kitchen box, I cut tails, or I say no. If they want a box that works and a price that fits a run of twelve, I reach for the bit and I write *locking rabbet* on the traveler the way I write *slip* when the side is skinny. Operations want names. Names keep the century honest.

I still keep a table-saw locking-rabbet setup for days the router is in another job. Same hook, more fences, no shank with a trade name. It is older than the bit and younger than a nailed rebate. I do not call it period either. A student once cut the saw version, sanded the inside clean, and asked if we could say “hand-locked.” We could say *saw-cut lock*. We said that. The invoice stayed in this century.

## Failures

I set the bit from memory on a Monday and ran a stack of maple sides that were a hair thicker than last month’s poplar. The hooks bottomed. The corners stood proud. I planed the proud off and thinned the lock until it was a plain rabbet with a scar. Recut the fronts. Keep the setup block. Thickness is the joint.

I also used the bit on a show-wood cherry front for a piece that was trying to be “simple Shaker” in a catalog sentence I did not write. The corner looked like a kitchen. The knob looked like a prayer. I rebuilt the boxes with half-blinds and kept the cherry fronts. The bit went back in the drawer. Tools are allowed to be specialized. Specialized is not a slight.

A student ran the front face-down when the diagram said face-up, or the other way — I still mix the diagram — and cut the hook on the show face. We recut the front. The scrap went on the wall next to the through-tail-under-veneer warning. Diagrams are not decoration. The bit does not know which century you meant.

## What this is not

This is not a ban on locking rabbets. I like them in the shop. I like them in a painted run. I like them when the alternative is a butt joint and a finish nail, which is how too many kitchen boxes still leave town. This is not a claim that the joint is as strong as a good half-blind in every axis. It is not, and I will not run a load number I did not measure. [VERIFY] any pound-limit before it becomes a caption. What I will say: pots, yes. A tool drawer of chisels, yes, if the bottom is a floor. A wide sitting-room drawer with a heavy pull and a lock, I still want tails.

This is not 1780. It is not Sheraton. It is not a “traditional locking joint” you can put in a heritage paragraph without the word *production*. The judgment is small. If the room is a kitchen and the hour is a run, the bit is a good name. If the room is a parlor and the front is a picture, cut the religion the picture owes. Do not ask a router profile to impersonate a century. The shank already told you what it is.
