Hide glue on a slip is tacky on the thumb and smells like a kettle that has seen a horse. I press the stick to a thin oak side, rub it once to grab, and the lining is suddenly thicker where it needs to be thicker — at the bottom, where the drawer will ride and where a groove has to live. The side above the stick stays skinny. The inside of the box gains a little architecture, a step, sometimes a bead I scratch because I am in the mood. That strip is the slip. It is not a romance. It is extra wood on the wear.

English work likes this strip. Thin linings, a solid bottom, a runner under the box. If you plough the groove in the side itself, you spend meat you do not have. A 1/4-inch lining with a groove is a ribbon with a hope. The slip is how you keep the ribbon and still have a road. American shops, more often, just used a thicker side and ploughed it. That fork is the next essay. This one stays with the stick.

## What the stick is doing

Three jobs, one piece of wood. It widens the bearing on the runner, so the wear is a band instead of a knife-edge of side grain. It carries the groove for the bottom, so the plough does not cut the lining in half. It lets you replace the wear later: plane off a ruined slip, glue another, recut the groove. The side — the dovetailed wall — can survive a century of floors.

The profile is allowed to be humble. A square stick with a groove. A quadrant that makes the inside of the drawer look finished. A moulded slip with a bead the finger finds. I have cut all three. I charge for the moulded one. I do not tell anyone the quadrant is “more period.” Period is a pile of cousins.

The bottom slides in from the back, grain running side-to-side if you are in the late-18th habit this pack will get to, and the back is thin enough or cut away enough that you can get the floor in after the box is together. The slip’s groove is a tunnel. Glue in that tunnel is a future split. I dry-fit the bottom. I wax the runner. I do not glue the floor to the architecture.

## Hedge the century

Here is where shops get sloppy with captions. The witnesses are related. They are not the same object in every decade.

Popular Woodworking’s slip essay points at rebate-and-runner refinements in the 1720s, then at the *Cabinet-Makers’ London Book of Prices* (1788) and its line for slipping drawers, then at Sheraton in 1803 describing slips glued inside and planed to receive the bottom. That is a trade sequence: a rebate that starts to look like a stick, a billed operation, a dictionary that prefers the stick to a confined rabbet that splits a bottom. I will not quote them past a short phrase. [VERIFY] any sentence before it is set in marks.

Hayward, writing from the 20th century about four centuries of drawers, puts a slip *moulding* in the early 19th as an answer to sides weakened by a groove. That is a later shop voice looking at a later problem: people had been ploughing sides, the sides had gotten thin and grooved, the moulding arrives as a repair of method. It is a true story. It is not the same story as a 1720s rebate or a 1788 extra on a traveler.

I will not flatten those into “slips were invented in year X.” A deep rebate with a filler is a cousin. A glued square slip is a cousin. A moulded slip that looks like interior millwork is a cousin. Caption the cousin you are holding. If you do not know which, say *slip* and give the witnesses as a range. Green logs are dates that pretend the cousins were one birth.

The price-book essay in this pack already spent the bill. I will not spend it again. The book proves the operation was common enough to name. This essay is what you get for the money: a wider road and a groove that is not a wound.

## How I build one

I thickness the lining first. English-thin is thinner than I like in a kitchen; sitting-room drawers can go skinny if the slip is honest. [VERIFY] any thickness I am tempted to print as house law. What I will say: if I can see daylight through a groove test-cut in the side, I stop and glue a stick.

The slip is long-grain to long-grain on the inside face of the side. That joint holds. I plane a flat, I glue, I rub, I clamp with blocks that do not tip the stick. After the kettle cools I plane the slip to height so both sides match, then I plough the groove in the slip, not in the lining. The bearing surface under the slip is the runner’s road. I leave it a little fat and shoot it when I fit the drawer.

Front slip or no front slip: some boxes get a strip across the inside of the front so the bottom has a groove there too. I do that when the bottom is solid and I want four walls of tunnel and a free back. I do not glue the bottom to the front strip. Movement toward the back is the floor’s right. The later grain essay can have that sermon. Here I only refuse to lock the floor.

Wear shows as a polish, then a hollow, then a drawer that drops and racks. Repair is a new stick. I have steamed off slips that were hide-glued and kept the linings. I have also met PVA slips that took the side’s face off with them. Hide is how you make the architecture reversible. I will not invent a pot temperature. ## What the stick is not

It is not a dustboard. It is not a kicker. It is not a runner glued to the case. Those are case parts. The slip rides. The runner is the rail. If you glue a slip and then hang the drawer on metal slides, you have paid for a road you are not using. I have done that when a customer changed their mind after the boxes were made. I did not charge them twice for the slides without a conversation. I also did not call the leftover slips “traditional detail.” They were leftover roads.

It is not an American obligation. Plenty of good boxes never see a stick. Thicker sides and a groove are a complete language. Do not add slips to a Greene-thickness American drawer to make it “more English” unless the side is actually thin. A slip on a 1/2-inch poplar side is often just a smaller inside and a second glue line. I have built that vanity. The inside looked busy. The wear was already wide.

## Failures

I glued a moulded slip with a pretty bead and then ran the plough too deep. The bead became a hollow shell. The first heavy book in the drawer cracked the shell into a splinter that lived in someone’s palm. The groove wants to live in the meat of the stick, not in the decoration. If the stick is too small for both, cut a square stick and skip the bead.

I also let squeeze-out cure in the groove and called the bottom “tight.” The floor bound in a wet month — not the craft-pack feeler-stick sermon, just a panel trapped by glue — and the slip’s job as a free tunnel was lost. I recut the bottom and cleaned the tunnel with a slip of broken saw. Glue is for the stick to the side. The tunnel stays a tunnel.

A copied profile from a photograph, cut with a router bit that was close, made a kitchen moulding in an oak lining. I have already confessed that class of failure on a traveler essay. I confess it again because the architecture is where vanity lives. Match or invent. Do not halfway-copy a trade you are visiting.

## What I write on the pad

Slip or thicker side. Pick one on the estimate. If the maple comes in thin, the slip appears on the paper before it appears on the bench. If the poplar comes in at a full half inch, I plough and I do not perform. London would have understood the extra. Wilmar has to write it or the extra disappears.

I do not sell “18th-century English construction” as a package. I sell a thin wall, a replaceable road, and a bottom you can get out the back. The heritage page can sit in a footnote. The stick has to sit on the runner.

## What this is not

This is not a first-year stamp. 1720s rebate, 1788 bill, 1803 dictionary, Hayward’s early-19th moulding — say which witness you are standing on. This is not the American groove essay. This is not a claim that a slip makes a drawer old. I can glue a stick next Tuesday. This is not a wage table.

The judgment is small. Put extra wood where the floor meets the road. Keep the lining as a wall. Leave the tunnel free. When the road wears out, replace the road. That is the architecture. The century you hang on it is a caption, and captions owe the cousin they can actually see.
