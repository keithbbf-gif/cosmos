The peg came out like a toothpick and I rolled it between my fingers. Bamboo, still round in the middle, mashed a little at the business end where someone had driven it through a rebate and let the glue take what it could. The drawer was light. The front was a pale board I took for kiri — paulownia — and the corner had no tail. A visitor in the shop had already called it a cheap joint. He was standing in the wrong church.

I am not going to pretend a ko-dansu lives on the Wilmar bench every week. I am going to pretend I have to *see* the box I am holding, because Fine Woodworking’s 2024 tansu drawer (Len Cullum, part 2, issue 309) is the published starting point for a shop that wants to stop translating every foreign corner into a dovetail it failed to cut. Cullum’s drawers are pinned at the corners. The bottom is pinned directly to the box, not slipped into a groove. In use the whole floor sits on a dust shelf. That last fact is the one Western benches keep missing. We hang a bottom from a plough and ask the groove to be a joist. A tansu often asks the case to be the joist and the pins to keep the floor from walking off.

I will not invent a sentence from that article. The gist is enough, and it matches what I have opened on repairs: pegs, rabbets, fingers, a floor that is part of the frame.

## A rebate with a peg is a joint

The Japanese drawer I keep seeing in the wild — not every tansu, not every century, a habit with hedges — seats the side in a rebate cut in the front and drives pegs through the meat. Bamboo, or wood cut down to a skewer. At the back the sides and the back often meet in a finger, also pegged. The front may be rebated along the bottom edge so the floor can land and then be glued and pinned around the perimeter. No half-blind. No through tail. No slip. At first glance it looks like the 17th-century English nail-and-rebate box. It is not that box. The peg is chosen. The finger is chosen. The floor is chosen. The dust shelf is chosen. You can dislike the movement contract. You cannot call it an accident.

I built a teaching drawer that way after I got tired of looking at photographs. Poplar sides, a cherry front I had no business putting on a tansu-language box, bamboo skewers from a kitchen jar. The rebate held. The pegs held. The bottom, glued and pinned all around in a wet week, split by August. That split is not a scandal. It is the published scar. Antique tansu floors split. Antique tansu backs split when they are nailed or pegged on as a plank. The joints at the corners often outlive the floor. A trade that pins a floor to a frame is a trade that has decided rigidity is worth a board you can replace.

Cullum, working in a Western shop with Western humidity in mind, breaks wide floors into tongue-and-groove pieces so they can move while still being pinned. That is a translation with a conscience. I respect it. I also respect the old floor that split and kept working because the dust shelf never asked the pins to be joists.

## Kiri is a lining, not a romance

Kiri — paulownia — is the pale, light wood the catalogs put on the tag. It dents if you look at it. It takes a plane. It takes a peg. It does not weigh what oak weighs, which matters when a chest is a stack of boxes you pull every morning. I have seen kiri linings inside a darker case, and I have seen a whole chest that is kiri and iron hardware and nothing else. I will not print a moth-and-kimono paragraph as if I had a textile lab. [VERIFY] any insect claim before it becomes a caption. What I will say: the lining is a job. Soft, pale, light. You do not owe the drawer a walnut side to make it honest. Secondary wood is not a slight in English work either. The species changed. The job did not.

Bamboo pegs are not cute. They are a fastener that does not rust in the joint and can be cut from a culm or, in a hurry, from a skewer. Wood pegs do the same work. Cullum’s published shop pins are toothpick-small. A repair peg in an old drawer can be fatter. Do not date a chest by the diameter of a peg you have not measured against a documented shop. [VERIFY] any diameter you are tempted to treat as law.

South Arkansas humidity is not Kyoto and it is not the Pacific Northwest shop Cullum was writing from. Wood is still wood. A floor pinned across its grain will want to split when the year turns. If I build a tansu-language drawer for a house off the Saline, I say that out loud. I either break the floor into pieces that can move, or I groove it and admit I have left the religion, or I pin it and tell the owner the floor is a wearing part. What I do not do is cut half-blinds in the front and call the box corrected.

## Different religion

Tails on the sides, pins in the front, bottom in a groove or a slip — that is a European settlement. A tansu drawer is a different one. The pull still lives on the front. The box still has to leave the case and come home. The answer is a rebate and a peg, a finger and a peg, a floor that is a panel fastened down, a shelf underneath that takes the weight. If you stand in the dovetail church, the pegged rebate looks underbuilt. If you stand in the tansu church, the half-blind looks like a lot of sawing to hide a joint the iron hardware is already decorating.

I have heard the word *primitive* used on these corners. I have used it, once, before I had a peg in my hand. Primitive is a tourist word. The box in front of me had been pulled for a century. The pegs were still in shear. The split in the floor had been lived with. That is not a failed tail. That is a finished sentence in another grammar. The iron plates and corner braces are often the picture. The wood is allowed to be quiet.

## What I cut when I mean tansu

If a customer wants a chest in that language, I cut the rebate. I cut the finger at the back. I drill for pegs with the box taped dry, the way the published method does, because a racked box will take a crooked peg and keep it forever. I use kiri if I can get it and the job will pay for it; I use a quiet poplar or a cedar if I cannot, and I say so. I pin the floor or I float it. I do not print “dovetailed tansu” on a traveler. That phrase is a mash. It is the same mash as calling a mule chest a commode because both hold blankets.

I will not sell a “Meiji antique” I made in Bradley County. I will sell a pegged drawer and tell the year. The history is useful because it keeps you from apologizing for a corner that was never trying to be English.

## Failures

I once “helped” a split tansu floor by ripping it out and sliding a plywood panel into grooves I ploughed into the old sides. The grooves were skinny. The sides were skinny. The dust shelf still wanted a floor that sat on it, and the new panel sat *in* the box instead, a little high, and the drawer scraped the shelf. I had translated the floor into my own language without asking what the shelf was for. I put a thin solid back in, pinned, and left the next split to the next owner.

I also drove oak pegs into kiri because the oak was on the bench. Oak is a wedge in a soft hole. Two corners hairline-cracked. Bamboo, or a peg as soft as the wall, or a hole that is not a dare. I knew that. I was in a hurry. The drawer still shows the cracks if you know where to look.

If the box was built to ride a wooden shelf, let it ride the shelf. A metal kitchen slide on a tansu side is a different drawer. Say so.

## What this is not

This is not a claim that Japan never dovetailed. Japan dovetailed. This is not a claim that every tansu drawer is a pegged rebate and a pinned floor. Regions, shops, centuries, and repairs disagree. Cullum is one maker reading a tradition. Hill-country American copies are another. Museum pieces are another. [VERIFY] a corner against the piece in front of you before you write a label.

This is not a ban on putting a tail into a chest that looks like a tansu. It is a ban on calling the peg a mistake. The peg is the method. The split is the cost. The dust shelf is the partner. The kiri is the lining. None of that is a European dovetail that failed to show up.

The judgment is small. If the corner is a rebate and a peg, cut it as a rebate and a peg. Wax the shelf. Let the floor be a floor that can be replaced. And do not stand in one church and grade the other on a test it did not sit.
