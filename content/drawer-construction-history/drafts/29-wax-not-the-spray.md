The paraffin smelled like a pantry. I was rubbing a heel of it on an oak runner that had gone dry and noisy, and the drawer stopped complaining before I finished the second side. No drip. No shine you would call wet. Just a dull feed in the grain, the kind of polish a stair tread gets where a hand always lands. I put the heel back in the tin. The tin lives in the bench, not in the finishing room. It is a tool.

This is the lubricant Saturday. It is not the clearance Saturday. I will not tell you how to draw a June gap or how to sneak a stick through a bind. A drawer that is too fat will not be saved by wax. Wax will make a correct fit kind. Spray, and certain oils, will make a correct fit into a future problem that looks like weather.

People blame humidity when the box starts to grunt in year three. Sometimes it is humidity. Sometimes it is a film that turned into varnish and then into grit glue. Those are different patients.

## The old sticks

Paraffin — the canning wax, the cheap block, the heel you can buy without a lecture — is what I reach for on oak and maple runners. It is dry. It does not go rancid. It does not smell like a church. It burnishes. You rub, you run the drawer, you rub the high spots the running just showed you. The runner tells you where it is working.

Beeswax is softer and a little sticky in a warm shop. I like it on pine-on-pine, where paraffin can feel glassy and the pine wants something that will stay in the pores. I do not like a thick beeswax cake in August in Wilmar. It pills. A thin rub, then a dry cloth, then the drawer. If your beeswax still looks like frosting, you are lubricating the grit, not the wood.

A candle heel is the folk version. Most household candles now are paraffin anyway, sometimes with scent you will regret. An old tallow candle is a different chemistry — it can go stale, it can stain. I have used a white unscented heel in a house call because it was what the kitchen drawer already knew. I did not perfume a chest of drawers with vanilla. If I am in the shop, I use the tin.

I have heard soap recommended. Dry soap can work on a stubborn pine runner for a month. Then it collects. I do not start there. I start with wax.

Graphite — the locksmith’s powder, the pencil rubbed to a stain — has a place on a lock bolt and sometimes on a metal pin. On a wooden runner it is a grey smear that will migrate onto a linen cuff. I have done it on a tool-chest runner that already looked like a tool chest. I will not put graphite under a sitting-room drawer. The customer will tell me the shirts are dirty. They will be right.

## The spray that gums

Silicone spray is a house call I can often diagnose from the driveway. The drawer runs like glass the afternoon it is sprayed. A year later it runs like tape. Dust sticks to silicone as if it were paid to. The film is hard to wash. It is hard to finish over if you ever need to refinish a front that got overspray. I have had a stain crawl on a rail that someone oiled with a can while the drawer was out. I will not invent a journal article. I will say the rail is still a little blotchy and I know which Saturday did it.

The can is sold as kindness. It is kindness to the next ten minutes. It is unkind to the next ten years. If a customer has already sprayed, I scrape, I mineral-spirit what I dare, I let it dry, I wax. I do not promise the first waxing will feel like the first spray. I promise it will feel like wood again.

PTFE sprays and “dry lube” cans are cousins. Some are cleaner than silicone. Some are silicone with better copy. I do not keep them on the drawer bench. If a metal slide wants a manufacturer’s dry lube, that is the slide’s religion. Wooden travel wants a heel.

## Oil that becomes a finish

Linseed on a runner is a future bind. It oxidizes. That is what linseed is for when you are finishing a top. On a bearing, oxidation is varnish in a place you cannot sand without changing the fit. I have inherited runners that felt gummy and smelled sweet. Under the gum, oak. I scraped to wood and waxed. The drawer had been “maintained” every spring with a rag and a bottle. Maintenance had been the disease.

Tung, “Danish,” the supermarket blend that is half solvent and half hope — same family if they cure. A wiping oil is a finish. A finish on a runner is a bearing you did not mean to cast.

Motor oil, 3-in-1, the can in the junk drawer — they do not cure the same way. They stay wet enough to hold grit. Grit is grinding compound. Hayward’s grooves taught that; bottom-runners learn it without a groove. A wet runner is a mill. I wipe wet oils off and I do not start them.

I will wax over clean wood. I will not wax over wet oil and call it a system. The wax rides on the oil and then both ride on the dirt.

South Arkansas heat makes the oil story faster. A film that might stay slick in a cool parlor will tack in a dog-trot hallway in July. People then say the drawer “swelled.” The side may also have swelled. Check the film before you plane a historic side. I have planed a side that only needed a scrape. I still have the shaving. It is stained the color of old salad.

## Where I put it, and how often

Runners. The underside of the drawer sides if they bear. The kicker if the top of the side rubs. The bottom edges if the box still sits on a rail. A lock bolt if it grunts. I do not wax the inside of a drawer. I do not wax a show front. I do not wax a leather writing surface and then act surprised.

Once at delivery, again after the first season if the house is new to us, then when the drawer talks. A talking drawer is cheaper than a calendar. If a piece lives in a sandy place — a river house, a shop — the wax will take a grinding paste and you will need to scrape and renew. That is not a failure of paraffin. That is the river.

I do not soak end grain in wax and call it a moisture treatment. End grain of a drawer side at the back can take a rub so it does not drag. A bath is a different religion and a good way to confuse a later finish.

## Failures

I sprayed a shop cabinet because the can was in my hand and the paraffin tin was under a pile of clamps. The cabinet ran like a dream through a demonstration. By winter the runners were grey felt. I scraped for an hour and I moved the tin to a nail I can see from the vise. The can went to the metal-slide shelf, labeled, away from the wooden-travel work.

I also boiled a beeswax-and-mineral-oil mix on a hot plate and painted it on like finish. It was pretty. It collected every shaving in the shop. Mixes that stay soft stay dirty. I went back to a heel.

A third: I waxed a drawer that was too tight and told the owner the wax would “wear it in.” Wax is not a plane. The drawer still bound on the wet week. I had spent their patience. I took the drawer home, shot it, waxed it for real. The craft pack can keep the feeler stick. I keep the shame.

## What this is not

This is not a clearance table and not a June drawing. If the box is fat in the wrong month, plane it. This is not a finish schedule. Paraffin is not a topcoat. This is what you put on the road so the road stays a road.

It is also not a purity test. I will use a manufacturer’s packet on a metal slide. I will not use that packet on Hayward’s oak strip.

The judgment is small. Rub a dry wax on clean wood. Paraffin for hard runners, beeswax if the pine wants it, a candle heel in a pinch if it is unscented. Keep graphite for locks and tool chests. Keep oils for finishes and engines. Keep the spray for the hinge that has no other life. A drawer that starts to grunt is asking for a scrape and a heel, not a can and a story about the weather.
