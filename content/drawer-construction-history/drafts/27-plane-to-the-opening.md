The plane sang on the top edge of a drawer that was still too fat for its hole, and the shaving came off long enough to hang on the vise. I could hear the pitch change when the iron found the high corner — a little cry, then a hush, then the even whisper that means the edge is one thing again. I did not look at the tape. I looked at the opening. The opening had been the truth since the case came out of clamps. The tape had been a story I told myself on Monday so I could cut the sides on Tuesday.

Build the box fat. That is the whole method, and shops keep trying to replace it with a spreadsheet. Measure the hole, subtract a clearance, cut the sides to the subtraction, dovetail a perfect rectangle, and then act surprised when the hole is not the rectangle. Cases are not rectangles. They are wood. Rails sneak. Ends bow. A clamp that was a little greedy on Thursday is a tight corner on Friday. The old trade made the drawer to the opening because the opening was already there. The box is the guest. The guest dresses for the room.

I will not write the June-bind essay. Clearance as a seasonal drawing is another pack. This is the fitting order: fat, then the plane, then the hole that exists — not the hole you wished when you laid out the case on paper.

## Measurement is a rumor

A tape is useful for roughing. It will tell you the sides can be 18 and a bit instead of 24. It will not tell you that the left opening is a heavy thirty-second wider at the back than the right opening, or that the top rail dipped when you pulled the clamps. I have written those numbers down. By the time I had written them, the case had moved a hair in the night and the numbers were antiques.

A story stick is less of a liar because it is a piece of wood that has touched the hole. Even a story stick is a copy. The opening is the original. When I can, I take the drawer to the case, not the case to a notebook. The 65-foot shop has the glass on the long wall and the cases live under that glass. I walk. Walking is cheaper than a wrong side.

Digital calipers make the rumor more precise. Precision is not the same as truth. I have a pair. I use them to match a runner thickness to a groove. I do not use them to invent six identical drawer widths from one opening and then force the other five holes to agree. Factory work can do that because the case is a jig. Handmade cases are not a jig. They are a family.

South Arkansas humidity adds a second rumor. A hole you measured in the morning after the dehumidifier ran is not the hole at 3 p.m. when a thunderstorm has walked up the Saline. If I am shooting to a final fit, I do it at a boring hour, in a boring weather, and I leave the last whisper for the house the piece will live in if I know that house. If I do not, I leave the last whisper anyway. A piston in the shop is a bind in July. That sentence is as far as I will go toward the other essay.

## Each opening is its own

I number drawers. Chalk on the bottom, inside the back, a number that matches a number on the web. 1 is not 4. They look like twins from the sofa. They are not twins at the plane. Period cases that still have their original boxes often have this handwriting — a chisel nick, a pencil  III , a world that did not assume interchangeability. I have seen the nicks. Making six boxes the same and then “fitting the case to the drawers” is how you get a case that has been abused to satisfy a batch. The case is the expensive thing. The rails are already joined. You can plane a drawer. You cannot honestly plane a web without changing the next opening. Fat boxes, each to its hole, is the order that leaves the case alone.

Kitchen work on undermounts is a partial exception. The slides want a width. The carcase is often sheet goods. Sheet goods are a rumor of a different kind — they are flatter, and they still bow. I still sneak up. I just sneak from a number the slide manufacturer printed, and I treat that number as a starting hole, not as a religion. [VERIFY] the current spec for whatever slide we stock before printing it.

## What I leave fat

Sides get extra in height and a little in length if the back is going to be trimmed. The front gets extra in width if the end grain of the half-blinds will be planed after glue. The top edges of the sides are the first thing I shoot to the opening — they are the reveal you see, and they are the cross-grain of the side, the dimension that will move later. I shoot them as a pair so the drawer does not go trapezoid in plan.

I do not print a starting oversize. A number I say out loud becomes a law I did not intend. [VERIFY] if we ever put 1/16 or a heavy 32nd on a shop sheet. What I do: enough meat that two or three honest shavings can still be a conversation, not so much meat that I am flattening a door. Students leave a quarter inch and then live with the plane for a day. That is tuition. It is not how I price a job.

After the sides, the width. A shooting board, or a long plane on the side with the drawer in the vise, watching the opening. I want the drawer to enter, then kiss, then take a little wax and decide to be friends. If it slams in from the first try I have already gone too far. You cannot put the shaving back. You can put a slip of veneer in a shameful place. I have. I do not recommend the feeling.

The front is last among the planes that show. You can still take a whisper off a rail instead, but that is editing the case to save a box, and I do it only when the rail was the liar from the start.

## Period practice, without the costume

English and American shops that made drawers by hand did not wait for a CNC to promise identical openings. The case came first. The interiors were cleaned up. The boxes were made to those interiors. Hayward’s fitting notes, and the older trade habit underneath them, assume you will try the drawer and take wood off the drawer. They do not assume you will have a printout.

I will not dress this up as “the old ways.” It is just the order that matches the material. Machines can invert the order if the machine also makes the hole. A jig-built kitchen can invert it. A pecan chest with solid ends and web frames cannot invert it without lying.

When I copy a period interior for a repair, I make the new box to the old hole even if the old hole is ugly. Forcing the hole to a new rectangle is how you leave a bright rail in a dark case. The repair should disappear. The hole is part of the artifact.

## Failures

I batch-cut six maple sides to one measurement from the top opening of a chest, then dovetailed six boxes, then discovered the bottom opening was shy on the right. Five drawers fit. One lived in a clamp for a week while I argued with a rail. I should have numbered holes and cut sides per hole. The tape had been very confident. The rail had not read the tape.

I also shot a drawer to a piston fit on a dry January afternoon and delivered it in May. The house was kind. July was not. I will not retell the feeler-stick Saturday. I will say: fat is a starting condition, not a finished religion. Leave a whisper for the year.

A third: I planed the case opening because the drawer was already finished and I did not want to recut a tail. The next drawer down inherited a rail that was now thin. Two problems from one cowardice. Plane the guest. Leave the room.

## What this is not

This is not a taper essay. A slight narrowing toward the back is a courtesy and sometimes a lie; it has its own page. This is not a clearance table. I am not giving you June numbers. This is the order of fitting, and the refusal to let a tape outrank a hole.

It is also not a ban on measuring. Measure to start. Measure to mill. Then put the tape down and ask the opening.

The judgment is small. The case is first. The box is fat. The plane is the conversation between them. Number the drawers so you do not tell six rumors and hope they rhyme. Period shops did this because wood is like this. Wilmar wood is like this. A perfect rectangle is a drawing. A drawer is a guest in a room that already exists.
