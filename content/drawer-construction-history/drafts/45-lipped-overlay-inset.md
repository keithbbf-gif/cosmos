The lip was a quarter-inch of cherry standing proud of the rail, thumbnail-round, and the drawer stopped on wood I could feel with a fingernail. Not flush. Not a kitchen lid sitting on the frame. A rabbet on the ends and the top, the middle of the front still living in the opening like a flush drawer, the edges wearing a little roof over the gap. I ran the nail along that roof. Someone had chosen a meeting. The meeting is how I start dating the box, and how I decide what to cut next time.

Three meetings. Inset — flush — the front in the hole, the face of the front even with the face of the rail, a shadow line that is only the clearance you left. Lipped — a rabbeted overlay, the front bigger than the hole by the width of the lip, the lip covering the seasonal crack and the sloppy opening. Overlay — the 20th-century kitchen habit, a false front or a thick front that lives entirely on the frame and never enters the hole. They are not styles. They are geometries. A Chippendale chest can be inset. A pine blanket chest from this county can be lipped. A 1970s oak kitchen can be overlay and proud of it. Do not preach. Measure the meeting.

## Inset is a fit

Period drawers, once the lid has left the chest, mostly sit in the hole. You build the box fat and you plane it to the opening. The front is the end of the box, half-blind at the corners, maybe a cockbead around the edge as armor and a shadow. Cockbead is not a lip. A cockbead is a small applied or stuck molding that still lets the front live in the opening. A lip is extra width. If you cannot see the rail beside the front, you are not in inset anymore.

Inset is expensive in a shop that has humidity. The hole changes. The front changes. June in Wilmar will take a January piston and turn it into a bind, and I am not going to retell that sermon. I am going to say that inset is a drawing of clearances, not a vibe. Kickers, runners, a top gap that is not a hair. The reward is a face that reads as one plane — rail, stile, front — and a dating clock that says someone was willing to fit.

When I see inset on a later factory chest, I do not automatically call it 18th century. Factories cut flush fronts. They also cut lips. The tell is the rest of the box: pin size, machine symmetry, secondary wood, how the bottom sits. The meeting is one clock. It is not the only clock.

## A lip is a rabbet that covers a gap

American country work of the 18th and 19th centuries likes a lip. So does some English work of the second quarter of the 18th — a brief popularity the bench literature puts around 1730 to 1760, then the cockbead comes back in town shops. [VERIFY] those decades against the piece, not against a caption you liked. Country shops on this side of the water kept the lip longer because the lip is kind. It hides the gap when the pine case moves. It hides a hole that was never a piston. It gives the front a molded edge you can cut with a plane before you dovetail, which is the old sequence: rabbet and mold the front, then cut the half-blinds into the remaining meat.

The traditional lip is not on all four edges the same way. Ends and top get a rabbet so the extra wood can sit on the frame. The bottom often gets the profile without the rabbet — a molding that does not hang down as a hook. Christian Becksvoort, writing for Fine Woodworking, says the bottom lip is the one that breaks when someone takes the drawer out and sets it on a table. I have broken that lip. I will not invent his sentences. The geometry is enough. A hanging fillet at the bottom is a handle you did not mean to make.

The rabbeted part of a lipped front still has to fit the hole, the way a flush front does. The lip is a cover, not a substitute for a fit. If the rabbeted portion is sloppy, the drawer racks inside its own roof. I have seen that on pine dressers from sales in Warren: a fat lip, a skinny fit, a box that walks. The lip was doing the sales job. The hole was doing whatever it wanted.

Do not confuse a lip with a kitchen overlay. A lipped front is still a box front. The sides join it. The lip is the same board, rabbeted. A kitchen overlay is often a second board — a false front — screwed to a box that never entered the face frame. Different meat. Different dating. A lipped antique that has been given a later false front is a mash you can feel with a screwdriver.

## Overlay is a lid

Twentieth-century kitchens wanted a front that would cover the frame and the gap and the slide and a box that might have a stapler in its past. Overlay does that. Partial overlay leaves a strip of face frame. Full overlay leaves a reveal between fronts. The front is a lid. The box hides. This is a production meeting, not a fall. The meeting does not decide the quality. The box and the screws in the false front do.

I build overlay when the case is a kitchen and the customer already lives in overlay. I do not put a lipped thumbnail on a frameless European box to make it “country.” That is a costume. I do not put a full-overlay false front on a walnut hall table to make the fit easier unless I say I am making a lid. The sitting-room job in this shop still wants inset more often than not, because the case is a piece of furniture and the front is part of its face.

Offset / 3/8 lip / “lipped kitchen” of mid-century American cabinets is a cousin of the country lip, machine-cut, a rabbet on a door or a drawer that half-covers a 3/4 frame. It is a dating clock for a ranch house, not for a 1780 mule. If you call every rabbeted edge 18th century, you will date a 1958 sink base as Federal. I have watched that happen at a sale. I did not correct the room. I corrected myself later with a pencil.

## What I choose on the traveler

If the case is framed and the job is furniture, I write inset and I price the fit. If the case is pine and country and the owner wants the gap covered, I write a lip — ends and top rabbeted, profile on four edges, no hanging fillet at the bottom unless they ask for the fillet and I have warned them. If the case is a kitchen, I write overlay and a false front, and I write the slide. Three lines. Three prices. London would have understood the instinct. I will not pretend Bradley County is a price book.

A lip eats meat from the front before you cut the pins. The remaining thickness has to hold a half-blind. A 3/4 front that loses 1/4 to a rabbet is a 1/2 wall. Pins get shorter. Pull screws get choosier. Plan the pull and the lock before you rabbet, or you will mortise into air.

## Failures

I put a bottom lip on a cherry drawer because the four edges looked nicer in the sketch. The owner set the drawer on a granite counter to empty it. The fillet snapped out of the end grain, a clean three-inch stick. I glued it. I do not cut that fillet anymore unless the drawer is captive and the owner is not a granite person.

I also tried to date a walnut chest by the lips alone and called it 1750 in my head before I looked at the pins. The pins were machine-regular and the backs were dadoed. The lips were a later fashion on a later box, or a revival. I shut my mouth. A meeting can be copied on Tuesday.

A kitchen I fit with inset fronts in a case that had been built for overlay left reveals like teeth. The face frame was never meant to be a picture. I made overlay fronts and ate the inset hours. Choose the meeting the case was built for, or rebuild the case. Do not ask a lid-case to wear flush fronts without paying for the hole.

## What this is not

This is not a style sermon. Inset is not holier. Overlay is not a fall. A lip is not “rustic” as a brand word. This is not a complete clock. Lips get added. Overlay fronts get screwed onto older boxes. Inset fronts get planed down until they are proud, then someone nails a trim around the hole and calls it a bead. Look at the end grain of the front. If the lip is the same board, it is a lip. If the lid is a second board, it is an overlay. If there is no extra board and no rabbet, you are in the hole.

The judgment is small. Name the meeting before you name the century. Fit what lives in the hole. Cover what you mean to cover. Do not hang a fillet on the bottom unless you like repairing it. And do not tell a kitchen it is Federal because the designer liked a thumbnail. The rail knows which meeting you chose. So does the next person who has to plane it.
