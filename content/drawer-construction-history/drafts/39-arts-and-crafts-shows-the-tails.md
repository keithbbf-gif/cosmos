Raking light on quartersawn white oak, and the tails were the picture. Through, proud enough to read from the aisle, pins flashing ray flake like a second inlay. I had the drawer halfway out of a later oak case — not a Stickley I can name, a cousin that had learned the gesture — and I ran a thumb over the pins. They were even. They were meant to be seen. The front was inset, a board, no bead. The joint was the jewelry. That was the point, and the point was a sermon and a lock at the same time.

The 17th-century walnut-on-deal drawer hid through tails under a leaf and then watched them telegraph. This pack already felt that fever. Arts and Crafts turns the old scar into a virtue: show the joint. Ruskin and Morris had already made honesty a moral argument about work. Gustav Stickley’s Craftsman Workshops, Eastwood, New York, made it a factory product in quartersawn white oak, fumed or stained toward a brown that catalogs still love. I will not sneer at the sermon. People meant it. I will not worship the pin. A hidden half-blind can be as honest as a shown tail. Honesty is a practice, not a silhouette.

## Theater that still locks

A through tail at the front of a drawer is mechanically the same religion the back has always used. The side cannot pull out of the front without breaking wood. Glue helps. The lock is the shape. Showing it does not make it weaker. Hiding it does not make it a lie. Federal work hid the joint because the front was a picture of mahogany. Stickley-era work showed the joint because the front was a picture of work. Two pictures. Two Saturdays. I will cut either when the room asks. I will not cut a shown tail on a mahogany stringed front and call it a marriage. It is a costume.

The pins on the better work are large enough to read from a hallway. Tiny pins are a town religion from another century. Large pins on oak are a poster. They also have meat. I like that meat when the pull is a hammered plate and the load is a sweater drawer that will be yanked. I do not like large pins used as a personality. If the joint is sloppy and large, it is sloppy. Size is not virtue.

Machine or hand is a dating clock, not a halo. Craftsman Farms’ notes on a No. 621 chest put machine-cut drawer dovetails after Stickley’s shop bought a dovetail machine, sometime in 1902, and they use that to fence a piece toward 1902–1904. [VERIFY] those lines against the museum text before anyone stamps a year. Popular Woodworking’s close look at a Gus Stickley drawer guessed at a hand-cut tail in a factory setting and admitted the guess. I will admit mine: even spacing and a clean machine radius at the socket floor are clocks I trust more than a story about a lone benchman. Presence of shown tails does not prove a man stood there with a saw. Absence of a machine mark does not prove he did.

## Oak that is a picture too

<!-- PHOTO: fig-02 D:\BBF\BBF Photos — inset oak drawer, through tails at the front corner, center guide or worn underside (filename pending shop pull) -->

Quartersawn white oak is the other half of the theater. Medullary rays, flake, a surface that reads as stone and wood at once. Gustav cared enough about flake, the company histories say, to veneer it onto posts where the saw would not give four pretty faces. Leopold’s later quadralinear post is a different efficiency. I will not make posts the essay. I will say the drawer side, when it is QS, is doing the same job as the tail: showing a truth about how the board was sawn. That is theater. It is also a calmer board across its width than a flatsawn sister. The quartersawn-sides essay in this pack can own the movement sermon. Here the flake is a style choice that happens to behave.

Fuming with ammonia is the smell people like to name. I will not invent a shop schedule or a house tent. [VERIFY] any fuming practice before it becomes a caption. What I will say: a brown oak drawer that was dyed yesterday and a brown oak drawer that was fumed a century ago can look like cousins in a dim hall. Look at the joint, the hang, the underside. Color is a hat.

Underneath, the honesty gets more interesting. Early work sometimes shows chamfered, butt-joined boards on the floor — Craftsman Farms notes that habit on the 621 and says it was phased out. Later efficiencies include center guides and, in Leopold’s patents, side-hung center-guided drawers so the box does not scrape. I nod. I do not retell the hang. A shown tail on a box that rattles in a sloppy opening is theater without a play. The fit is still the daily honesty. Stickley’s own photographs of the Workshops, what little is published, show men fitting doors and drawers at the end of a line. That is a factory that still believed the last plane stroke was part of the product. I can respect that without kneeling.

## Do not sneer, do not worship

Sneer looks like this: calling shown tails a marketing gimmick and then cutting half-blinds because you are more serious. Worship looks like this: cutting shown tails on a junk box and believing you have joined a guild. I have done a little of the second and thought the first too loud. The joint is real. The catalog was also a catalog. Both things can sit on the bench.

I will show tails on an oak stack when the customer wants the picture and the wood is quartersawn enough to deserve the light. I will hide tails on the same oak when the room is a bedroom that wanted quiet fronts and a wooden knob. I will not tell either customer they are more honest. I will tell them which picture they bought.

Do not mash the drawer’s shown tail onto the case’s shown through-tenon and call them one joint. A pinned tenon on a post is the other poster of the style. It is a different lock and a different Saturday. I have built a case with proud tenons and then half-blinded the drawers because the fronts wanted quiet. That is allowed. The catalog liked to show both at once. The bench does not have to.

South Arkansas oak — what I can buy off a truck in Wilmar — is not always white oak, and it is not always quartersawn. I will not fume a red-oak kitchen and call it Craftsman. I will not pin a through tenon on a case and then dado the drawer backs and pretend the sermon was complete. If you are going to do the theater, do the joint. If you are going to do the joint, you may also hide it. The sermon does not own the lock.

## Failures

I cut large shown tails in flatsawn red oak because the pile was what I had. The pins looked busy. The sides moved enough to open a gap at the baseline in a dry spell. The theater read as a poster that had wrinkled. I recut the sides in QS white oak on the next job and kept the first drawer as a warning. Species and sawing are part of the picture. The tail cannot carry the flake.

I also stained a shown joint to even out a glue smear and lost the edge of a pin. The smear was honesty. The stain was makeup. I sand back a smear now, or I leave it, or I recut. I do not paint a pin into the dark so the room will not see I rushed the glue.

A student put through tails on a mahogany Federal-style front “for Craftsman honesty” and cockbeaded the field. Two sermons, one box, both muffled. We recut the front half-blind and left the bead. Pick a picture.

## What this is not

This is not a Stickley catalogue raisonné. I will not lift a shop photograph. Rights on those images are not mine to spend. This is not a claim that every Mission drawer shows tails — some fronts are half-blind, some later factory work hides what the catalog still talks about. This is not a ban on machine tails. A machine can show a joint. A hand can hide one. The moral is not in the motor.

The judgment is small. Through tails on a QS oak drawer are a lock you are invited to look at. Look. Then look at the hang, the floor, the baseline. If the joint is tight and the box runs, the theater paid for itself. If the joint is a silhouette on a rattling box, you have bought a poster. Do not sneer at the people who wanted the work visible. Do not worship the visibility. Cut the lock. Decide whether the room is allowed to see it.
