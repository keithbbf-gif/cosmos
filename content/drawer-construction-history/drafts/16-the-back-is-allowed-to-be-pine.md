I heard the back before I saw it. A pine board, thinner than the sides, ticking against the case when I set the drawer down on the bench. Through tails, a little proud, never stained. No one had ever asked that rectangle to be mahogany. I put a palm on it. It was cool and a little fuzzy where a century of dust had made a felt. The front was a different species and a different religion. The back was allowed to be a wall.

That is not a slight. It is a job description. The front takes a pull, a lock, raking light, a hand. The sides take the hang or the runner and the tails. The back closes the box, keeps shirts from falling into the dark, and gives the bottom a place to finish its travel. You can do that with pine. You can do it with poplar. English work often did it with oak when the linings were oak, or with deal when the trade had moved on. The species is local. The humility is structural.

I am not going to pretend every old back is pine. I have pulled oak backs out of English stacks and poplar backs out of Southern cases and a chestnut back that crumbled at a knot. The argument is that the back *may* be the cheaper, lighter, quieter board. If you make it as thick and as pretty as the front, you are spending meat where the room cannot see it and you may be fighting the one place the box has to stay free.

## Thinner on purpose

Greene, on American 18th-century furniture, puts ordinary sides and backs in a 3/8-to-1/2-inch neighborhood. The back is often the thinner of those two. [VERIFY] any tighter number before it becomes a house law. What I cut: a back that is a plane-shaving or two under the side, sometimes more, so the drawer can enter a case that is not a perfect rectangle. A fat back in a skinny opening is a drawer that slams its shoulders before the front sits down.

Thin is not weak if the corners are jointed. Through dovetails at the back are fast and strong. You saw them through. You do not hide them. Nobody is photographing the inside of the case unless you are, and then the tails are a dating clock and a pride you can afford. A dado — a housing — is the quieter cousin. The back slides into slots in the sides and you pin or glue what you must. That quieter joint has its own essay later. Here the point is that the back is allowed to use a joint that would be rude on a show front.

I have made the back as thick as the front because a pile of 5/8 poplar was already dressed. The drawer looked armored from the rear. It also refused to close square in a case that had a little belly. I planed the back down and the belly won. Build the back for the hole it has to pass, not for the scrap you have.

## Where the bottom finishes

The bottom has to go somewhere when it grows. If the grain runs side to side, that somewhere is front to back. The front groove can hold a ticket. The back is where you let the board come and go. Two common finishes: the bottom slides *under* the back, and the back sits on it like a lintel; or the bottom enters a groove in the back, and the back is a fourth wall with a slot.

Under is honest. You can see the floor’s extra at the rear. You can nail or screw in a slot so the board stays a floor and still moves. In a groove is tidier. It is also a place to trap the board if you glue the ticket. I have glued a bottom into a back groove because squeeze-out looked like a seal. The seal was a lock. The split started at the back and walked forward. Leave the back groove as a slipway, not a glue joint.

Nails through a pine back into the edge of a bottom are an old country finish. They work until the bottom shrinks and the nails tear a slot of their own. A slot you cut on purpose is kinder than a slot the nail invents. I prefer a screw in a slotted hole, or a nail in a slot I filed, or nothing but the side grooves and a back that does not pinch. The 65-foot shop is dry in January and wet in June. The back is where I admit that.

## Through tails, or a housing, or nails

Through tails at the back are the 18th-century settlement I still use when the box is a box. Pins in the back, tails on the sides — or the other church, which is a later argument. The joint can be a little proud. You plane it after. A pine back with walnut sides looks like what it is: a lining closed with a wall. I like that look. I do not stain the pine to impersonate the front. A stained pine back is a costume you notice every time you dump the drawer.

A dadoed back is faster in a production run and quieter in a painted kitchen. It is not a moral failure. It is a joint that depends on glue and a tight housing. If the housing is sloppy, the back rattles and the box lozenges when you pull a corner. I have chased a rattle with a shaving glued into a dado. Better to cut the housing to the back, not the back to a hopeful slot.

Nailed rabbets at the back show up in country work and in factory work and in my own shop tills when I am building a box that will live in sawdust. That clock has its own essay. The back can take a crude joint because the back is not the face. Crude is not the same as careless. A rabbet that misses the side’s thickness is a back that hangs in air.

## Failures

I once stained a pine back “walnut” so the inside of a case would look finished in a photograph. The stain blotched. The tails went dark and the flats went striped. I sanded what I could and left it. Now I oil pine or I leave it raw. The back is allowed to be the color of pine.

I also cut through tails in a back that was too thin for the pin board I had laid out. The pins had no meat. One blew out when I chopped. I glued the blowout and felt the repair every time I closed the drawer — a little click, a little proud. Thickness is a joint problem before it is a weight problem.

A student ran the bottom *over* the back, nailed down from above, because the groove had been cut too high. The nails were in the floor. The shirts found them. We recut a back and dropped the groove. The bottom passes under or in. It does not become a lid on the back.

I once made a mahogany back to “finish the inside” of a case that would be photographed with every drawer out. The backs matched the fronts. They added weight. They cupped more than pine in this climate. They cost a board I needed for a top. The photograph was pretty. The stack was heavier than the job. I went back to pine on the next one and left the insides the color of work.

A dado cut too close to the tail line left me a sliver of side between housing and joint. The sliver split when I drove the back. The housing and the tails have to leave each other meat. Humble wood still needs geometry.

## What this is not

This is not a ban on pretty backs. If you want oak all around, want it. If you want the inside of a tool chest to be one species, that is a shop drawer with a different audience. This is the observation that period boxes — and any box that has to move with the weather — treat the back as the free wall. Species can be humble. Thickness can be shy. The joint can show.

This is not the full religion of the dado. A housing that is enough is a later Saturday. This Saturday is the permission: pine, poplar, deal, a thinner board, through tails you do not hide, a floor that finishes its travel where no guest is looking.

The judgment is small. Spend the show wood on the show. Let the back be a wall. Put the movement there. If you hear a pine tick when you set the drawer down, that can be the sound of a box that was built in the right order — front for the hand, sides for the hang, back for the weather.
