The brass pull rang when I tugged it — a small bell of metal on a screw — and the front came with my hand a hair before the box did. Not off. A tick. Glue in a starved socket, a heavy drawer, a person who lifted with one finger through a bail. I set the drawer on the bench and looked at the corner. Tails on the sides. Pins on the front. The orientation was right. The glue was tired. If the orientation had been wrong, the front would have been in my hand and the box would have stayed in the case like a tray that had lost its lid.

That is the whole religion, and it is mechanical. A dovetail resists being pulled apart along the axis of the tail board. The tail is a wedge that cannot slide out of the pin board without breaking or shearing. Put the tails on the sides, and the side cannot be pulled off the front toward the room. The pull lives on the front. You pull the front. The front is the pin board. The pins are sockets in the thing you are pulling. The tails, locked in those sockets, bring the sides with you. The box follows the handle.

Reverse it. Tails on the front, pins on the sides. Now the front is the tail board. A pull on the front is a pull on the tails, trying to slide them out of the side sockets. Glue may hold. For a while. A heavy winter of blankets, a stuck runner, a yank — the front comes off as a lid. I have seen that lid. I have glued it back and I have recut the corner. I have also seen a student do it on purpose because the layout “looked nicer” with fans on the face. Nicer is not a load path.

## Orientation is the joint

People talk about dovetails as if the shape were the whole invention. The shape without the direction is a drawing. Direction is why the drawer became a better box than a nailed rabbet. Nails in a rabbet are in shear when you pull. They loosen. The tail, correctly handed, turns the pull into a compression of wedges.

I mark tails on the sides first, or I mark pins on the front first — that argument is a later essay and a church I will not join here. Either church still has to put the fans on the side boards. The saw can start wherever it likes. The wood of the tail must be the wood of the side.

Through tails at the back follow the same physics. You do not yank a drawer by the back. You do shove the back when you close a stuffed box. Tails on the sides still help: the side cannot slide off the back as you rack. Pins on the back, tails on the sides. Same sentence, quieter room.

Half-blinds do not change the handing. They only hide the pin board’s face. The side is still the tail. I have to say that because a half-blind *looks* like the front is doing all the work. The front is doing the hiding. The side is doing the locking.

## What the pull actually does

A knob in the middle of a wide front wants to rack the box. The joint at each end has to take a little twist as well as a pull. Tails on the sides still help; they keep the front from peeling. They do not make a wide drawer honest by themselves. Center guides and a bottom that is not a drum are other essays. Here I will only say: do not ask the orientation to fix a rack. Ask it to keep the face on the box while the rack is someone else’s problem.

Two knobs on a wide front split the load. A single cup pull at one end is a mean test. Campaign hardware and kitchen bars are later load-path essays. The handing does not change. If you put a bar across a false front, the false front’s screws become the joint. That is a different contract. On a real front, the tails still live on the sides.

I do not print a pound-count for a bail. [VERIFY] any load number before it becomes a spec. What I will say in this climate: hide glue in a dry August is not a reason to reverse the joint and hope. The orientation is the cheap insurance. The glue is the courtesy.

## How you see it on the bench

From the side, you should see fans. From the front — if it is through, which it should not be on a show face — you would see pins. From the inside corner you see the tail ends in the sockets. If you see fans on the front’s end grain, someone handed it backwards, or you are looking at a carcase joint that is a different sentence (tails on the case side, pins on the top, so the top cannot lift off). Carcases and drawers share the logic: the tail board is the one you do not want to slide out.

I keep a reversed sample on the wall. Pine, small, labeled in pencil, not a fake antique. Students pull both boxes by a screw-eye. The correct one complains at the glue. The reversed one opens at the front like a book. One demonstration saves a year of captions.

Box joints — fingers of a size — do not have this handing. They resist in both directions about the same, which is why they show up on shop drawers and campaign cousins. They also have no wedge. Glue does more of the work. I like fingers in a jig drawer. I do not pretend they are a tail handed for a pull.

## Failures

I reversed a back joint once because I was cutting four corners from a stack of sides and I flipped a back instead of a front. The drawer assembled. It even slid. The first time I shoved it closed on a tight runner, the back crept. Not off — crept. The tails were on the back, and the shove was a peel. I took it apart the same afternoon. The wall sample is not only for students.

I also repaired a kitchen drawer whose false front had been screwed into the end grain of through tails someone had put on the *front* of a plywood box. The screws had little to hold. The slab came off in a person’s hands, full of soup cans. I recut the box with tails on the sides and moved the screws into the face of a real front. The soup was not my job. The handing was.

A well-meaning repairer had glued blocks inside a reversed corner to “make it stronger.” The blocks were a confession. I left one box as a lesson and rebuilt the other. Blocks do not rewrite orientation. They hide it until the next yank.

## What I tell the bench

Sides get the tail layout. Front and back get the pins. If I am using a jig, I still check which member is against which bit. Jigs are happy to cut a pretty row in the wrong board. Pretty is how reversed joints survive until delivery.

If a customer wants fans on the face, I cut through tails on a show *side* of a chest, or I cut an Arts & Crafts case joint they can see. I do not flip a drawer’s handing to decorate it. Decoration that peels is not decoration. It is a warranty.

Wilmar humidity will loosen glue. It will not change which board is the wedge. I rely on the wedge more in August, not less. That is the opposite of a lot of modern thinking, which treats glue as the joint and the tail as a style. Glue fails on a schedule. The wedge does not know the month.

## What this is not

This is not a style sermon. You can hide the front and still hand it right. You can show the tails on a side and still hand it right. This is not the half-blind essay again; hiding is religion about the room. This is religion about the handle. This is not a claim that nails never held. Nails held until they did not. The tail is why we stopped asking them to.

The judgment is small. Before you saw, name the board that must not peel toward the pull. That board gets the pins. The boards that must come with it get the tails. If you get that right, the shape can wander, the pins can be country or town, the glue can be hide. If you get it wrong, the prettiest row in the shop is a lid waiting for a hand.
