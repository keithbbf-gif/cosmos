The birch flexed and came back. A half-sheet of Baltic, five-point-something millimeters or a full half-inch — I will not invent the stamp on that pallet — and it did not creak the way a pine floor creaks when you bow it. I set it on the bench next to a split solid bottom I had pulled from a wide drawer the day before. Same span, more or less. The pine had opened along the grain like a decision. The birch sat there being a sheet. I tapped both. The pine drummed. The birch clicked. That is not a virtue contest. It is two contracts.

Hoadley is the physics I will stand on without reprinting a table I did not copy this morning. A solid board moves across the grain. If you run that grain side to side — the late-18th settlement Hayward names, and a different essay — the growth lives front to back. The front groove can hold. The back must let go. Glue the board all around and you have bought the split. Plywood is crossbanded. The layers argue with each other. The argument is the stability. You may glue a ply floor on four edges and the season will not open a rib the way it opens pine. That is the contract. Say it. Do not pretend you have discovered a morally better wood. You have signed a different weather.

## What you give up, what you buy

A solid bottom, beveled into a skinny groove, can be replaced. You slide it out the back, you cut another board, you raise another ticket. The floor is a service part. English slips and American grooves were both, in part, about a floor you could service. A plywood panel glued into a rebate all around is not a service part. It is architecture. When it fails — delamination, a wet kitchen, a dog bowl spilled and left — you cut the box or you destroy the rebate getting it out. I have done both. I tell the customer that before the glue.

What you buy is a floor that does not split because July was wet and January was a furnace. You buy a sheet that stays closer to flat. You buy speed. You buy, in a kitchen, a surface that takes a liner and a can of beans without oil-canning if you chose a thickness that matches the span. Thin ply in a wide drawer still sags. Crossbanding is not a joist. A center muntin under a wide ply floor is still a joist. I use one when the span asks, and I do not call it jewelry.

The underside is the other half of the contract. In a sitting room people pull a drawer out and turn it over. They see pine or oak and a bevel and a little dirt in the groove, and they understand they are looking at a floor. They see birch plies and they understand they are looking at a kitchen, even if the front is walnut. That reading is allowed. It is also why I still put solid floors in parlor stacks unless the customer and I have said *ply* out loud. I will not hide a sandwich under a cockbeaded front and hope no one kneels.

## Kitchen weather, sitting-room weather

A kitchen drawer is a box that lives with steam, spills, and undermount hardware that wants a square, stable carcass. Ply sides and a ply floor, locking rabbets or dados, glue all around — that is a 20th-century language this pack will keep naming as production. It is honest in a kitchen. The pots do not read Hayward. The floor can be captured on four sides because the floor does not owe the season a walk.

A sitting-room drawer in a Warren parlor is a lining that lives with wool, paper, and raking light. The older contract still fits: solid floor, grain side to side, free at the back, sides that can be thin if you slip them or thick enough to take a groove. I build that when the piece is a piece. I do not build it because solid wood is cleaner for the soul. I have seen ugly solid floors and beautiful ply boxes. Morality is a poor thickness gauge.

The failure is mixing the contracts without saying so. A student in this shop glued a pine field to a plywood sub-bottom “for strength” and then beveled the sandwich — the bevel essay already kept that grin. I will not retell the mouth. The lesson is the same from the other side: a ply floor treated as if it must float, held by one nail at the back and left rattling in oversize grooves, is a waste of the stability you paid for. If you bought a panel, let it be a panel. If you bought a board, let it walk.

I have also used a thin ply floor in a solid-wood sitting-room box because the customer wanted a “quiet” underside and a cheap run. I wrote it on the estimate. I did not call it traditional. The front was cherry. The floor was birch. Anyone who emptied the drawer knew. That is the only honesty I owe that choice.

## Edges, sag, water

Ply has an edge. The edge is a sandwich. In a groove it can look like a stripe. Some people hate the stripe. I ease it or I hide it in a rebate with a solid lip if the room will see it, and I leave it if the room is a kitchen. Do not fill the edge with wood putty and paint and then tell me it is a board. It is a sheet with manners.

Water is the ply floor’s moth. A solid board swells and, if you left it free, goes home. A wet ply can let go between layers and never come back. I do not put an unsealed birch floor under a sink drawer without a finish on the top face. I do not invent a brand of finish here. [VERIFY] any product name before it becomes a caption. What I will say: the kitchen contract includes a film that can be wiped. The sitting-room solid contract includes a floor that can be replaced.

Species talk is mostly noise. Baltic birch is the shop’s usual sheet because the plies are many and the voids are few. Cabinet-grade oak ply is heavier and the face is a picture you may not want as a floor. Lauan has been the cheap floor for a generation and it sags sooner. I will not print a span table I did not measure. Wide and thin sags. Narrow and thick does not. That is as close to a law as I will sign.

South Arkansas humidity is not a climate-controlled gallery. A ply box still moves a little — more in the solid front than in the floor — and a solid front glued to a ply box is its own small argument. Leave the front as a skin that can be a different species. Do not glue a wide solid front onto a ply box as if they were one panel. The front will cup. The hooks will tell.

## Failures

I glued a quarter-inch birch floor into a wide walnut drawer with PVA on all four edges and no muntin, because the sheet felt stiff in my hands. In a year the middle had a belly. The belly rubbed the web frame. I added a muntin from below like a confession. Stiffness in the hand is not stiffness in the span.

I also put a solid cedar-looking pine floor into a kitchen box “to be nice” and glued it like ply. August split it. The owner thought wood was defective. The contract was defective. I recut a birch panel and wrote the word *ply* on the traveler so I would not be nice that way again.

A painted kitchen came back with the bottom edges swollen where a dishwasher had breathed on them for a summer. The ply had taken the breath and thickened. The dados were tighter than a joint should be. I eased what I could and told them a floor near steam wants a film. I did not tell them solid would have been holier. Solid would have cupped and sanded the rail.

## What this is not

This is not a hymn to plywood. Sheets can be ugly, thin, and wet. This is not a hymn to solid. Boards can be vain and locked and split. This is the observation that a floor is a weather machine, and you have to say which machine you built.

Hayward’s late-18th groove and side-to-side grain is still the right religion for a solid lining. Greene’s American groove-in-side is the same church in a thicker wall. Neither man was looking at Baltic birch. Joyce’s production pages were. I will not mash them so a caption can say “traditional plywood.” There is no traditional plywood in 1788. There is a 20th-century sheet that is allowed in a kitchen, and allowed in a sitting room if you say the word.

The judgment is small. If the floor is a board, let it walk at the back. If the floor is a sheet, you may glue it, and you should say so, and you should give it a span it can keep. Do not score the two against each other as if one were a better person. Score them against the room. The split pine on my bench had been asked to be a panel. The birch had been asked to be a sheet. Only one of those requests was fair.
