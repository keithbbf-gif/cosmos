The veneer had a fever. Not loose — not yet — but you could see the joint. Raking light on a walnut drawer front, and there they were: the ends of through dovetails, a little proud, a little pale, the ground moving and the leaf not moving with it. Someone had built a proper tail for a box and then asked a knife-thin skin to pretend the end grain was not there. The skin is loyal for a while. Then the front shrinks and the tails stand up like a lie you can feel with a fingernail.

Hayward’s second-half 17th-century drawer is the one I keep in my head. Walnut has started to replace oak as the show wood, mostly as veneer. The linings — sides, back, bottom — stay oak. The front’s ground is often *deal*, the English word for a pine or fir board, because oak is a rude ground. Medullary rays harder than the rest of the board, grain that telegraphs, shrinkage that is not polite. Deal is quieter. You can lay walnut on it and have a chance.

The tails run through. Coarse. A poor way, Hayward says, because the exposed end grain at the front offers a poor grip for the veneer, and because the joint eventually shows. He is not scolding a student. He is looking at a surviving box and telling you why it looks the way it looks.

## Why they did it anyway

Half-blind dovetails are not a mystery in the 17th century. People could lap a joint. They did not always. Through tails are faster to saw if you are thinking in oak-joinery habits. They are also a joint you can cut when the front is still a board you might change your mind about. Veneer arrives as a fashion and a savings — walnut is expensive, deal is not — and the joinery lags a generation.

I have done the same lag in this shop without meaning to. Built a through-tail sample because the saw was already in my hand, then remembered the front was going to wear a crotch walnut leaf. I recut the front. The sample went on the wall as a warning. Through tails under a show veneer are a future telegraph. Half-blind keeps the show face as a skin with no bones.

Secret-mitered dovetails are another religion. They hide everything and they cost a day. The 17th-century trade was not paying for a day on a drawer that would live in a bedchamber behind a bed-hanging. Coarse through tails and a leaf was the available honesty.

## Oak linings, deal ground

Secondary wood is not a slight. It is a job. Oak sides in English work of that period are hard, they take a groove or a rebate, they wear. The front is allowed to be a different species because the front is a picture. When the picture is a leaf, the ground should be the species that lets the leaf stay a picture.

I veneer on poplar and sometimes on mahogany when I want the ground to move with the leaf a little more politely. I do not veneer on quartersawn white oak unless I am trying to teach a class what telegraphing looks like. Hayward already taught it. The rays come through. People then blame the glue.

Hide glue is the right glue for a leaf you may have to lift in twenty years. PVA is a contract you cannot open. If I am laying walnut on a drawer front in Wilmar in August, I think about moisture in the ground and moisture in the leaf, and I do not invent a number here. [VERIFY] any shop moisture before it becomes a printed schedule. What I will say: a wet ground and a dry leaf is a future bubble. A through tail in that front is a future ridge.

## The joint that comes back

Wood moves across the grain. A drawer front’s grain is usually left to right. The tails are end grain of the *sides* coming through the *thickness* of the front. When the front shrinks in thickness or the ground cups a hair, the tails — denser, different grain, sometimes a different species — do not shrink on the same schedule. They stand. The veneer, glued to both, splits or shines or both.

Repair is a lift, a scrape, a new leaf, or a patch that will always be a patch. I have steamed a corner and laid a Dutchman of walnut so the flash was less ugly. I have also told an owner the honest thing: the joint is doing what Hayward said it would do in the 20th century, looking at the 17th. You can live with a faint telegraph as a dating clock. You can recase the front. You cannot scold the oak.

Machine sanding a telegraphed front to “flatten” the tails is how you get a leaf that is maple syrup in the middle and gone at the ends. Do not.

A telegraph is also a dating clock if you are honest about what it can prove. Through tails under a leaf do not make a piece 1680. They make a piece that used a method Hayward already scolded. The method lasted because it was fast. I have seen it on 19th-century factory work that never heard of William and Mary. Presence of the scar is a construction note. Absence of the scar is nothing. Hand-cut half-blinds can be cut next Tuesday on a pine ground and a walnut leaf, and then the front will be quiet for a while and you will still not have a century.

## What I cut now

If the front is solid show wood — cherry, walnut, a pecan that earned the face — through tails at the *back* and half-blind at the *front*. That is the 18th-century settlement, and it is still the settlement in this shop. If the front is veneered, half-blind or a locked rebate I am willing to sign, and the leaf wraps the edges or a cockbead covers the story. Through tails under a leaf are a demonstration, not a delivery.

I will veneer a door. I will veneer a fall front. I get stubborn about drawer fronts because a drawer front takes a pull, a lock, a thumb, and raking light from a window. It is the most photographed rectangle on the piece. Bones should not be in the photograph unless you meant them to be — Arts & Crafts through tails on a show side are a different essay. Those tails are the picture. These tails were supposed to be invisible. That is the failure class.

## Failures

A student once cut through tails on a pine ground and laid a walnut leaf the same afternoon. The glue was still singing. By October the tails were a Braille inscription. We recut the front. I kept the first one. It teaches faster than a paragraph.

I also bought, at a sale in Warren, a “William and Mary” lowboy whose drawer fronts had been sanded through the veneer at the corners by someone chasing a proud tail. The oak linings were honest. The fronts were a crime scene. I lined the worst corner with a new leaf and stopped. The piece was not going to become a museum object in my hands. It was going to hold napkins.

## What this is not

This is not a ban on through dovetails. Through tails at the back of a drawer are correct, fast, and strong. Through tails on a show front of *solid* wood are a style choice — Federal work usually hides them; Stickley often shows them. Through tails under a *leaf* are a dating scar and a repair list.

Hayward’s deal-and-walnut drawer is useful because it catches a trade in the act of changing clothes. Oak thinking, walnut fashion, a joint that had not yet learned to hide. The 18th century will lap the front and thin the pins until they almost vanish. That is the next Saturday. This Saturday is the telegraph you can still feel on a front that thought veneer was enough.
