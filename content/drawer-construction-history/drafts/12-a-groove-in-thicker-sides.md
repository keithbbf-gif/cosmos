The plough whispered in a half-inch poplar side and left a groove I could hide a pencil in. No stick glued on. No quadrant. Just a wall with a tunnel near the bottom edge, meat above and a little meat below, the kind of side I have stacked in this shop until the pile looked like a small town of pale boards. That is the American fork. The lining is thick enough to take the groove and still be a lining. The slip, which the last essay already spent, stays on the other ocean.

Jeffrey Greene, in *American Furniture of the 18th Century*, puts ordinary drawer sides and backs around 3/8 to 1/2 inch and illustrates the groove-in-side as the method. [VERIFY] the passage before anyone treats those fractions as a BBF spec. I use them as a window I can hold. A 3/8 side will take a modest groove if you leave a fillet under it. A 1/2 side is comfortable. Below 3/8 I start thinking like an Englishman, or I stop and admit I bought the wrong boards.

I am not going to pretend every colony read Greene. Kugelman’s Connecticut Valley work is a useful regional brake: nailed rabbets or grooved sides, slips not the local language. Mid-Atlantic and New England shops vary. Southern work I see in this parish — pine, poplar, a little walnut on the face — grooved the side when they grooved at all. The stick never became the habit. Timber was not as dear as London time. That is a guess I will mark as a guess. [VERIFY] any economic sermon before it becomes a caption. What I can stand on is the boxes: thicker walls, a plough, no architecture glued inside.

## Why the stick stayed English

English linings run thin. Thin is a city habit and a timber habit and a price-book habit. Once the wall is a ribbon, you cannot plough it and also walk away proud. You add wood at the wear. The slip is that add. American shops, looking at the same problem — a bottom that has to live in a groove and a runner that has to be ridden — often solved it by not making the ribbon. Start with a side that already has meat. Plough. Done.

That is not a virtue. It is a fork. A 1/2-inch poplar side wastes a little interior space and saves a glue-up. A thin oak lining with a slip saves space and costs a stick. Both boxes slide. I have built both. I do not add slips to a Greene-thickness side to make the photograph look London. I have already called that a vanity. I will not retell the bead and the moulding. The American side is allowed to be plain on the inside. Plain is the tell.

Secondary wood here likes poplar. It likes pine. It likes the occasional oak when the shop has oak. Poplar takes a plough cleanly and moves less nastily than a wild pine. I do not owe the room a primary-wood lining. The front can be pecan or walnut. The wall can be pale. Greene’s thicknesses are about the wall, not about the show.

## Where the groove lives

Too close to the bottom edge and the fillet below the groove is a splinter waiting for a nick. I have snapped that fillet off by setting a drawer down on a grit of gravel. The groove wants a floor under it — a little meat, long grain, enough to take a ding. Too high and the bottom sits like a shelf and the runner has to be built up, or the drawer looks empty at the rail. I aim low, not desperate.

Depth is enough to hold the bottom without turning the side into two boards. A groove that meets a half-blind socket or a tail waste is a conversation you have at layout, not at glue-up. I mark the groove before I cut tails so I am not surprised by a socket that opens into a tunnel.

Stopped or through: a through groove shows on the back end and sometimes on the front if you are careless. I often stop the groove behind the front and let the bottom into a shallow rebate or a short groove in the front’s thickness. The back I leave open so the floor can slide out. That is the late-18th floor habit arriving in an American wall. The next essay can have the grain. Here I only keep the tunnel from cutting the front’s face.

Width of groove matches the bottom’s thickness at the edge. Solid bottoms get a bevel or a rabbeted edge so a fat board can enter a skinny tunnel. Plywood bottoms — a later contract — can be a flat 1/4 in a 1/4 groove if you accept the different movement deal. I will not sell plywood as 18th-century American. I will plough for it in a kitchen.

## What I cut in Wilmar

Sitting-room drawers in this shop: 1/2 poplar if I have it, groove-in-side, half-blind front, through back, bottom side-to-side and free at the back. Kitchen boxes: the same wall if they are wood-on-wood; thicker if they are going on undermounts that specify a side. I will not invent a brand’s millimeter here. [VERIFY] whatever slide sheet we are actually using.

I keep a pair of offcuts on the shelf — 3/8 and 1/2 — with a test groove in each. Students hold them. The 3/8 feels like a decision. The 1/2 feels like a side. That is the whole lesson in the hand. Numbers on a page do not teach the fillet.

If the lumberyard sends 5/8 “because it is sturdier,” I thickness it. Overbuild is a modern comfort that eats interior and weight. A drawer side is not a joist. Greene’s window is already stout by English measure. I do not need a deck board.

Bradley County summers still move a poplar wall in height. The groove is a slot in that height. It gets a little tighter, a little looser. I do not cut a piston on the bottom in January. I leave the floor a sliding fit and I leave the back free. That is not the June-bind essay. That is a tunnel that has to stay a tunnel in two weathers.

## Failures

I ploughed a 3/8 pine side with a groove meant for 1/2 stock because the fence was still set from the last job. The fillet below was a postcard. It crumbled when I knocked the bottom in. I recut the sides. The fence is part of the fork. American groove-in-side assumes you still have a side after the plough.

I also glued a slip onto a 1/2 poplar “for looks” on a desk set and then had to plane the interior to get the specified width. The customer got a smaller box and a story I did not enjoy telling. Looks are not a method. If the wall is already American-thick, plough it and stop.

A nailed-rabbet country drawer came in with no groove at all, bottom boards running the long way, nails proud on the runner. I could have slipped it. I grooved new thicker sides instead, because the box was pine and American and the owner wanted it to remain the object they knew. I did not English it. I gave it a tunnel in meat.

## Why I do not convert the fork

Repair is allowed to match the language. An English lining gets a slip. An American side gets a groove, or a Dutchman in a worn groove, or a new side if the wall is shot. Converting a Greene-thickness drawer to slips so it will “last” is a conversion of identity. The wall already lasted. The groove wore. Recut the groove or glue a thin spline in a worn slot and plough again. Do not add a London extra to a Wilmar wall unless the wall has become a ribbon.

Factories later grooved thick sides by the thousand. The machine tail and the Knapp live on those walls. The fork is older than the machines. When I see a 1/2 pine side with a clean groove and machine pins, I am looking at an American habit that survived the line. When I see a thin oak lining with a moulded slip and town pins, I am looking across the water. I do not mash them for a caption.

## What this is not

This is not the slip essay again. The stick has its own file. This is the fork: thicker wall, plough, no architecture required. This is not a national law. Connecticut rabbets exist. Shop drawers exist. This is not a claim that 3/8 to 1/2 is what I must cut on every job. It is the published window I hold the board against. This is not mill-heritage theater. Bradley Brand Furniture is not a lumber-company heir in this paragraph.

The judgment is small. If the side is already meat, cut the tunnel in the meat. Leave a fillet you can ding. Leave the floor free at the back. Save the glued stick for the ribbon walls. The American drawer is not a failed English drawer. It is a wall that did not need a second wall.
