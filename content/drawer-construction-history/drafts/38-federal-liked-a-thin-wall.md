I could feel the lining through the front. Mahogany, a skin, and behind it sides so thin the drawer was a tray in my hands — light, a little flexible if I was rude, a cockbead catching my thumbnail the way the bead essay already taught. Stringing ran a pale line around the field and stopped at a wall that had no extra meat. I set the box on the bench and it made a smaller sound than a kitchen drawer. Not cheap. Light. The case that had held it was light too. That was the style. It was also the engineering.

Hepplewhite’s *Guide* (1788) and Sheraton’s *Drawing-Book*, then the *Cabinet Dictionary* (1803), are the English paper this American Federal habit read. I will not invent a plate or a sentence. [VERIFY] any passage before it is quoted. What the furniture shows, and what Greene shows when he draws American 18th-century boxes, is a lining in the 3/8-to-1/2-inch neighborhood, a show wood that is mahogany more often than not, a bead around an inset front, and a case that has given up being a chest. The sliding box is the furniture. The wall is allowed to be thin because the joint is no longer a nail in a plank and the floor is no longer a board asked to be a shoe.

## Thin is a picture

Federal work wants a surface. Veneer, crotch, satinwood banding, bellflower and eagle in the shops that could pay for them, stringing that reads from across a parlor. A thick wall fights that reading. A thick wall is a kitchen, or a tool chest, or a later revival that thought meat was honesty. The old picture needs a front that is a field and a side that disappears into a shadow under a bead.

The cockbead is the frame. I will not retell the rebate, the miter, the armor. One nod: the thread stands the front off the rail so the case reads as a stack of pictures. Shaker habit, last essay, usually refuses that frame. Federal likes it. The two words are not interchangeable hats. If you put a cockbead on a square cherry front and call it Hepplewhite because the wood is pretty, you have dressed the wrong meeting.

Inlay sits on thin construction the way a line sits on paper. There is not a second thickness to hide a mistake. I have let a stringing groove wander toward a half-blind socket and found the socket. The lining was never extra. That is the discipline. Greene’s numbers are not a dare to go thinner for a caption. They are a neighborhood in which the picture still has a wall.

English work can go thinner still when the slip carries the groove. This pack already billed the slip. American shops more often grooved a slightly thicker side. Do not mash those onto one “Federal thickness.” Say which bench you are standing on. I stand on Greene for the American number and on the piece in hand for the rest. [VERIFY] a lining before you write it on a tag.

## Thin is a weather machine

Less meat is less travel. A side’s height is cross-grain. A 3/8 lining that drinks a summer is a smaller problem than a 5/8 lining that drank the same summer in the same hole. The drawing-error essay already named the piston. Federal thinness is one historical refusal of that piston: keep the moving part small, keep the floor on a side-to-side grain, keep the back free. The style and the physics shook hands.

Boston’s Seymour shop, in Mussey’s account, is the published American witness I will use without inventing a job. Bottoms in grooves, grain side to side, fine dovetails, kerfed glue blocks under the floor as wear pads — that last has its own door, and I will not walk through it again. The lining is still thin. The show is still mahogany, often a leaf. The case is still light. I have not had a Seymour on this bench. I have had the book, and I have had later work that learned the lightness and then forgot it in a lumber pile.

The case is thin for the same reason the drawer is thin. French feet, a light plinth, dustboards that are boards and not armored frames — the whole object has given up coffer-weight. I will not retell web frames. I will say a parlor chest that weighs like a tool cabinet is wearing the wrong century, even if the fronts are mahogany and the beads are seated. Thin is a stack of decisions, not a lining trick.

A bow front is a different Saturday. The advanced pack already cut half-blinds on a curve. If a swell appears here, it appears as a reminder that thin linings on a curve are still linings, not an invitation to retell the radius. I will leave the radius on that bench.

Overbuild is the modern failure. I have drawn a “Federal” chest with 5/8 plywood boxes because the kitchen supplier had a stack and the traveler said *drawers*. The fronts were mahogany. The beads were real. The boxes were a cafeteria. You felt it when you pulled. Weight where there should have been air. I rebuilt the linings in poplar at a thickness closer to Greene and the piece sat down in its century. The plywood went to a pot drawer, where it belonged.

## Hardware on a thin front

Brass pulls — bails, rings, rosettes — are load paths. A later essay will own that sentence. Here the warning is local: a thin front still has to take screws or posts. Half-blind keeps the field as meat. Through tails under a leaf are the telegraph this pack already felt. Federal work, when it is being itself, hides the joint and leaves the middle of the front as wood for the hardware and the lock. A half-mortise lock in a skinny front is a tight piece of metalwork. I have split a thin front by boring as if it were a kitchen slab. Mark deep. Bore shy. The wall is the style. It is also the remaining meat.

Inlays around a keyhole want the same courtesy. Ivory or bone escutcheons on Boston work are published in catalogs I will not lift. I will say the inlay is a skin on a skin. Two skins on a 3/8 lining is enough. Three is how you meet a tail.

## Failures

I stuck a heavy reproduction bail on a lining I had cut to the old neighborhood. The posts were long. They kissed the inside of the front and dented the first folded cloth. I filed the posts. I should have chosen a lighter pull or a slightly fatter front and admitted the compromise. Thin has a load limit. I will not invent a pound. [VERIFY] any hardware load before it becomes a caption. What I will say: the pull should not be a door knocker on a tray.

I also ran stringing around a front after I had cut the half-blinds, and the groove broke into a socket at the end. The line went dark in the hole. I patched with a sliver and a blush. Sequence is part of thin work. Field first, joints second, or joints first and stringing that knows where the sockets will be. I now draw the sockets on the front before I cut a line I cannot move.

A student called a thick poplar box with a mahogany false front “Federal” because the feet were spade feet. The feet were a hat. The box was a kitchen. We kept the feet. We recut the boxes. Names of styles are not hats.

## What this is not

This is not a rule that every drawer from 1790 to 1820 is thin, beaded, and inlaid. Country work stayed thicker. Some city work is plainer than the books. This is not a Seymour commission in Wilmar. There is not one. This is not a bow-front lesson. This is not the cockbead lesson again.

Hepplewhite and Sheraton are paper. American Federal is what shops did with that paper and with mahogany and with linings they could still lift. The judgment is small. If you want the picture, thin the wall and spend the show wood on the show. If you want a kitchen, say kitchen and keep the meat. Do not armor a parlor tray and call the weight quality. The small sound that tray made on my bench was the sound of a style that had already done the engineering. Light was the point. The bead was the frame. The lining was the wall, and it was enough.
