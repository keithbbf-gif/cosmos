Two numbers on the traveler, same pencil, same height. Opening 5 1/4. Drawer 5 1/4. I had written them as if the box were steel and the hole were steel and the season were a rumor. The case was already glued. The drawer was still a stack of sides. I sat with the yellow pad in the 65-foot shop and heard, in my head, the sound that pair of numbers would make in a wet month: a wooden groan, then a stop, then a yank. The error was not waiting in the weather. It was already on the paper.

The craft pack already spent a Saturday on how a shop feels a running fit. I will not retell that Saturday. This pack owns the history of the sliding box, and the history is blunt: bind is a design error. Clearance is a dimension. Grain is a dimension. A glued floor is a dimension you did not mean to add. If those things are not in the drawing, they are not in the box. A humid week in South Arkansas is an audit. Audits do not invent the books.

## The gap is a line you failed to draw

A drawer side’s grain runs front to back. The height of the side is the cross-grain. Hoadley is the witness I will not paraphrase into a fake percent. Height changes. An opening that was drawn as the same number as the side, in the moisture of the day you dressed the stock, will not be the same number when the stock drinks. Old shops that built fat and shot the box to the hole were not being folksy. They were refusing to believe a drawing that treated wood as a printout.

I will not make this the plane-to-opening essay. That religion has its own door. The point here is earlier, on the paper: if you specify a piston, you have specified a bind. If you specify the side height from a story pole that has one number for both the hole and the box, you have specified a bind. If a kitchen elevation from a box-store plan lists the drawer as the opening — and I have used a plan like that once, to my cost — you have imported a metal-cabinet habit into a wooden hole.

Cabinet software is a new way to make the old error. The screen shows a box that fits. The screen does not owe Hoadley a cell. I do not invent a version number. I will say I have cut to a printout that was proud of its zeros, and I have then spent a day taking the zeros back off with a plane. The drawing was the problem. The plane was the apology.

Width is the other line. A wide drawer whose bottom grain runs front to back grows in width. The sides are shoved toward the stiles. Hayward, looking at late-18th work, puts the bottom grain side to side so the movement lives front to back, where the back can let go. That was not a fashion. It was a correction written into the architecture. A shop that still draws the long board the long way of a wide floor, because the rack had a long board, is making the 17th-century mistake on purpose. The price books do not bill “leave room.” They bill slipping and plowing. The room was assumed by people who had already paid for the split.

## A glued floor rewrites the width

<!-- PHOTO: fig-02 D:\BBF\BBF Photos — wide drawer, solid bottom glued all around, sides shoved tight to the rails (filename pending shop pull) -->

Here is a scar that is not a fitting ritual. A solid bottom glued to all four walls becomes a panel. The panel’s width is now the box’s width, and the panel moves. The sides, which should have been two cheeks free to stay the width you shot, are taken along for the ride. They kiss the rails. They polish themselves into a bind. I have opened a walnut drawer that was a piston in a wet spell and found the squeeze-out still shiny in the grooves. The traveler had said *glue up*. The traveler had not said *where not to*. That is a drawing error with a brush.

Plywood changes the contract — the other essay named it — and a ply floor glued all around does not rewrite the width the same way. If you draw a solid floor as if it were ply, you have drawn a split or a bind or both. If you draw a ply floor as if it were a board that must float, you have drawn a rattle. The paper has to know which machine you are building. Weather will not translate for you.

Overbuild is a drawing error too. Greene’s American linings in the 3/8-to-1/2 neighborhood are thin enough that a little movement is a little movement. A modern drawing that thickens every part to 5/8 “for strength” puts more cross-grain into the same hole. More meat, more travel. The extra thickness feels like honesty on the pad. It is often just a larger error. I have built that armored box. It fit in the dry week I assembled it. It did not fit when the sides took a drink. I planed the extra I had been so proud of. The drawing should have been shy.

## History is a set of refusals

The mid-17th-century hang, the nailed floor, the later groove, the slip, the free back, the side-to-side grain — this pack has been walking those refusals. Each one is a shop saying *do not draw it that way*. A nailed bottom with grain the long way of a wide drawer will split; do not draw that floor. A groove that eats a skinny side will weaken the cheek; draw a slip or a thicker wall. A back that pinches the floor will split the floor; draw the movement at the rear. None of those lines wait for a calendar page. They wait for a pencil.

I am not going to give you a first year when shops “discovered” clearance. People who built chests already knew lids swell. The sliding box made the knowledge expensive, because a lid can be left with a shadow and a drawer has to come home every day. Federal thin linings are, among other things, a way to keep the moving meat small. Kitchen ply boxes are a way to stop asking the floor to move. Two centuries, two drawings, same fear: a hole that was specified as the object.

When I inherit a binder, I look for the drawing first. Not the weather report. A side that was shot as a piston. A floor glued as a panel. Grain running the vain way because the board was pretty. A case opening that was cut to a number with no twin. Those are authorial. The season is a reader with a loud voice.

## Failures

I drew a wide cherry drawer at the same width as its hole because the customer wanted “no gaps.” I wrote *no gaps* on the pad as if it were a dimension. The front looked tight and expensive in the dry week we delivered. Later the sides took the rails and the pull taught someone to yank. I pulled the box, recut the floor’s grain the right way — it had been the pretty way — and shot the sides again. The gap they had forbidden was the gap the box required. I should have refused the note on the paper, not the season.

I also trusted a full-size rod that carried one tick for the opening and the drawer together, a habit I picked up on a rush job in Warren. The tick was honest the morning I made it. It was not a pair of numbers. I built both from the same mark. I will not do that again. A rod can hold two ticks. A traveler can hold two lines. The second line is the design.

A student brought a printout with a 3-D drawer seated in a 3-D case, green checkmark, no clearance cell. We built it. We unbuilt it. The software had not failed. It had done what it was asked: fit a solid into a solid. Wood was never in the file.

## What this is not

This is not a fitting manual. If you came for a ritual of seasonal checking, you are in the wrong pack. This is not a claim that weather is imaginary. South Arkansas will swell a tight box. That swell is the audit of a line you wrote or failed to write. This is not a ban on a close fit. Close is a dimension too. Close with a shadow is a drawing. Close with no shadow is a hope.

Hayward will not give you a fake year for the first shop that left a gap. Neither will I. The late-18th grain change is the published correction I will keep pointing at. Hoadley is the physics. The traveler on my bench, with the same number twice, is the scar I will keep. I crossed out one 5 1/4 and wrote a smaller number, and I wrote *why* in the margin so the next Saturday would not have to guess.

The judgment is small. Bind is designed. Put the gap on the paper. Put the grain on the paper. Put the floor’s freedom on the paper. If you specify a piston, a locked board, or a pretty grain that grows into the hole, you have not been surprised by a season. You have been read aloud.
