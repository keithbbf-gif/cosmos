The key turned a quarter and I heard the bolt hit the rail — a small iron sound, dry, the kind a half-mortise lock makes when the mortise is tight and the strike is just a notch in oak. No magnet. No plastic catch. A selvedge flush with the top edge of the drawer, a brass face on the inside, a bolt going up, and a hole in the rail that had been chopped to receive it the year the pins were chopped. I pulled the drawer a hair. It declined. The front was doing two jobs: it was a pin wall, and it was a lock wall.

I buy the lock before I saw the front. That is the whole essay if you want it short. The rest is why.

## Half-mortise is a neighbor of the pin

A half-mortise drawer lock is mortised into the inside of the front. The backplate sits flush on the inside face. The selvedge sits flush on the top edge. The body occupies a shallow pocket. The bolt, when you turn the key, shoots up into a notch in the rail or the web-frame above. A strike plate is optional on a drawer; a lot of old work is just a mortise in the wood. The show face keeps only a keyhole. That is why the lock and the half-blind belong to the same Saturday. Both of them treat the front as meat that must remain a picture on the outside and a machine on the inside.

Whitechapel and the hardware benches call this the lock you find on fine English and American work before the middle of the 19th century, more or less. Surface locks — screwed to the inside, no pocket — show up on country boxes and on later cheap work. Full-mortise locks, buried in the edge, ask for a deeper cut and a later toolkit. I am not going to make a morality of it. I am going to say that if you are cutting half-blinds, the half-mortise lock is the neighbor that was already in the house.

The neighbor needs room. Backset — the distance from the pin of the key to the selvedge — has to match the distance from the rail to the place you want the keyhole. If you lay out a pretty keyhole first and buy a lock second, you will chisel a pocket that either blows a pin or misses the rail. I lay out the lock, then the pins, then the pull. The pull screws and the lock body must not share a cubic inch. I have made them share a cubic inch. The screw kissed the bolt. The bolt hesitated. The owner thought the lock was failing. The lock was sharing.

## Escutcheons are edge armor

A keyhole without an escutcheon is a wound that grows. The key wobbles. The wood at the edges of the hole turns to felt. A thread escutcheon — the brass liner you tap into the hole — is the quiet fix. A plate escutcheon is the picture. Ivory, bone, brass, a little inlaid shield: those are dating and money, not virtue. I use a thread liner on a sitting-room drawer even when the customer wants a plain front. The liner is cheap. A chewed keyhole in walnut is not.

Do not date a piece by a pretty plate. Plates get replaced. Thread liners get punched out. An empty hole with a shadow of an oval is a clock that someone removed. I will not invent a century from an oval.

Wardrobes and linen presses accumulate escutcheons: one on the door, one on each drawer, sometimes a dummy on a drawer that never had a lock so the facade stays a rhythm. Dummy keyholes are a tell. Look inside. If there is no pocket and no blocked pocket, the hole is furniture, not a lock.

## One key, two religions

Dealers like to say a press “retains the original key, which works the doors and the drawers.” That sentence hides two mechanisms.

Keyed alike: each drawer has its own lock, each door has its own lock, the wards or the bits match, one key turns them all. That is a convenience. It is common. It is also how a later locksmith makes a house livable. I have re-keyed a bank of half-mortises so a customer could stop keeping a fist of iron. That is not original unless the locks say it is.

Shooting bolts: one lock throws rods or sliders that engage the other drawers, or the other door, or a pair of bolts that go up and down in a wardrobe stile. The key lives in one place. The iron lives in the case. Linen presses and wardrobes are where I look for this, because a stack of drawers under a pair of doors is a lot of pockets to cut, and a single lock with rods is a lot of security for one motion. Chests do it too — a top drawer whose lock drops a pin or shoots a bar into the drawers below. I have seen the bar. I have not seen it as often as the sales tag implied.

Look at the iron before you write the sentence. If there are pockets in every front, you are in keyed-alike country, or in “every drawer had a lock” country. If there is one pocket and a rod in a groove down the case, you are in shooting-bolt country. If there is one pocket and nothing else, you are in “they locked the top drawer and hoped.” Hope is a period option.

A wardrobe door lock is often handed. The bolt shoots left or right. The latch, if there is a latch, is a separate motion. Cupboard locks and drawer locks can share a body with two keyways so the same iron can sit horizontal or vertical. Read the body. Do not assume. I have mortised a right-hand lock into a left-hand door and then mortised a second pocket to hide the shame. Buy the lock. Hold it on the door. Turn the dummy key. Then chop.

## The rail is part of the joint

The bolt wants a home. A thin modern face-frame rail will not give it one. Period web-frames and drawer blades are thick enough that a notch in the underside still leaves a rail. A 3/4 kitchen rail with an undermount under it is a crowded neighborhood. I have had a bolt kiss a slide. I moved the lock, or I moved the slide, or I admitted the kitchen did not want a half-mortise and I used a different catch.

When I restore a press, I do not force a shooting-bolt system that is no longer there. I fit the locks that are there, or I fit new half-mortises that match the backsets of the old holes, and I tell the owner if the key is a choir or a solo. I will not invent a rod so a tag can say “original mechanism.”

South Arkansas rusts cheap steel. Brass bodies last. Steel bolts stain oak. I wax the bolt. I do not oil it into a gum that will catch dust and then catch the rail. The same wax religion as a wooden runner, different iron.

## Failures

I chopped a lock pocket after the half-blinds were closed and blew the end of a pin into the socket. The pin had been meat. The mortise made it a veneer. I sawed the front off and recut it. Buy the lock. Mark the pocket on the inside before you mark the tails.

I also ordered a lock by the picture and ignored the backset. The bolt rose into the middle of the opening, a brass tongue in space, and the rail sat behind it like a spectator. Backset is a measurement. The pretty catalog is not.

A third: I let a keyhole sit without a liner on a pecan front because the front was “simple.” Six months of a spare key and the hole was a teardrop. I installed a thread escutcheon into a wound. The liner looks like a repair because it is one.

## What this is not

This is not a locksmith’s manual. I will not publish a way around a bolt. This is not a claim that every linen press shoots rods from one lock. Many do not. This is not a dating clock you can take to a sale with one key in your pocket. Keys get cut. Locks get swapped. Escutcheons get polished into other centuries.

The judgment is small. The lock is joinery’s neighbor. Give it a pocket that misses the pins. Give the bolt a rail. Give the keyhole a liner. If one key works the stack, decide whether you are looking at matched wards or a rod, and write that down. If you are building the stack, buy the iron first. The front is a picture. The inside of the front is a machine. Chop them on the same day, or the machine will eat the picture.
