Four little pads came off the bench with the drawer. Not slips. Not runners. Glue blocks — short, kerfed, still wearing a shine on the face that had kissed the case rail. They had been planed flush with the underside of a pine bottom after the glue had set, so the drawer ran on wood that was extra, not on the floor itself. I rolled one in my fingers. The kerfs were saw cuts that stopped before they became two blocks. The hide glue had stained the end grain brown.

Robert D. Mussey, Jr., writing the Seymour furniture of Boston, puts this habit in the years the shop is famous for — roughly 1785 to 1815. John and Thomas Seymour. Bottoms in grooves, which is the American language. Then kerfed glue blocks under those bottoms, planed flush, doing the job of a wear pad. I am not going to invent a commission or a street. I am going to stand on the published description and on what a block does when you can still hold it.

The block is a cousin of the English slip. It is not a slip. A slip is extra wood on the *inside* of a thin side, grooved to take the bottom, often moulded, billed in London as an operation. These Boston pads live *under* the floor. The side can already have a groove. The block does not have to carry the bottom’s ticket. It carries the rub.

## What the pad is for

A grooved bottom wants to sit above the runner so the runner does not chew the field. In a lot of American work the lower edge of the side is the shoe. That edge wears. When it wears enough, the groove drops and the bottom starts to drag. A glue block under the bottom, planed flush with the side’s lower edge or a hair below the field, gives you a sacrificial pad you can renew. It also stiffens a thin floor at the place the rail will try to dish it.

I have glued pads under shop drawers for the same reason without calling them Federal. Maple scraps, hide glue, a few kerfs so the block would sit on a floor that was not perfectly flat. Planed after. The drawer ran quieter. That is the sell. The history is useful because it keeps you from calling every pad a slip and every slip a Seymour.

Kerfs are a shop kindness. A long glue block on a slightly cupped bottom will rock. Saw cuts across the block let it break into short seats. They also interrupt a glue line so you are not welding a six-inch bar to a moving floor. I will not print Mussey’s intent as if I had his notes. [VERIFY] any claim that the kerf was “for movement” before it becomes a caption. What I can stand on: a kerfed block sits better, and a sitting block planes into a pad instead of a ridge.

## How a cousin becomes a lock

The danger is the same sentence that makes the pad useful. If you glue the block to the *side* and to the *bottom*, you have tied the floor to the lining. If the bottom’s grain runs side to side, it wants to grow and shrink front to back. The side does not go along. A slip, done in the English way, can still let the bottom slide in a groove. A block glued hard to both parts can lock the floor at the very place the rail is loading it.

Mussey’s account is why I care about the pad: the block is a slip’s cousin, and a movement risk if the bottom is locked. I will not embroider a quotation. The split I have seen is not a Boston museum object. It is a pine floor in a later chest, blocks under both sides, PVA from a repair, a crack that started over the front block and ran back like a road. The blocks were still perfect. The floor had done the moving alone.

Hide glue is more forgiving here than a yellow bottle. You can steam a hide-glued pad and decide again. PVA under a loaded bottom is a different essay, and I will not steal it. What belongs here: if you copy a Seymour pad, copy the *release*. Glue the block to the side if you want a shoe that stays with the box. Let the bottom rest on it, or glue a short spot, or use a hide that will let go before the pine rips. Do not run a modern glue the full length of both faces and call it Boston.

I have done that hurry. Kitchen drawer, poplar sides, a pair of pads I was proud of, glue on every cheek. The traveler said “blocks.” It did not say “free.” February in Wilmar is a dry month. The bottom pulled and the pad did not. I cut the blocks out with a thin chisel and made smaller ones. The scar is still on the underside. I do not show it unless I am teaching.

## Not the English slip

I have to say this twice because the words wander. A slip widens the wear *and* holds a groove for a thin side. London priced it. Sheraton described slips glued inside and planed to receive the bottom. That is a lining architecture. This Boston pad assumes the groove is already in the side, or at least that the bottom is already a grooved American floor. The pad is extra meat under a floor that can already stand. It is not how you save a 3/8 English side.

If you put Seymour blocks *and* English slips on the same drawer, you have stacked two answers. I did that on a sample because I wanted to feel both. The drawer ran. It also looked like a committee. Pick the language the box is speaking. Thin English side: slip. Thicker American side with a groove: groove, and a pad if the rail is going to eat the shoe. Do not mash them so the caption can say “period detail.”

South Arkansas is not Federal Boston. The humidity swing off the Saline will punish a locked floor faster than a tight brick house in a different century. That is not a romance. It is why I am mean about glue on the underside.

## What I cut now

When I want a pad, I cut short blocks — two or three per side on a wide drawer, one near the front where the hand load lives, one farther back. Kerf them if they are longer than a couple of inches. Hide glue. Plane flush after the glue has set so the pad and the side’s lower edge are one shoe. I keep glue off the field except a tack if the block will not stay for the plane. The bottom stays a board that can come and go at the back.

I do not tell a customer they are buying Seymour construction. I tell them the drawer will run on a pad I can renew. If they brought a photograph of a Boston front, I look at the underside before I look at the veneer. The jewelry is the front. The religion is whether the floor is free.

## Failures

I planed the pads before the glue had set because I wanted to close the case that afternoon. The blocks smeared. I had a smear of maple on a pine floor and a pad that was no longer a rectangle. Wait. The plane is the last tool, not the one that proves you are busy.

I also used a single long block, no kerf, on a bottom that had a faint cup. It rocked. I clamped it until it looked seated. It was seated in the middle and hungry at the ends. The ends let go in a year and the drawer ticked. Short blocks. Or kerfs. Or both.

A well-meaning repair on a later country chest had replaced missing pads with construction adhesive. The adhesive had become a ridge. The ridge had worn a trench in the case rail. I staged the adhesive off as far as I could and put wood back. Do not ask a tube to be a Seymour block. The pad has to be a species you can plane.

## What this is not

This is not an English-slip essay. The slip has its own bill and its own groove. This is not a claim that every Boston drawer of the period wears kerfed pads — I have not counted, and I will not invent a percentage. Mussey is the witness for the habit in the Seymour shop. The habit is useful because it names a third thing between “groove in a thick side” and “slip on a thin one.”

The judgment is small. A block under a bottom is a wear pad. Plane it flush. Kerf it so it sits. Do not let it lock the floor to the side unless you are ready to own the split. The cousin of a slip can still be a trap. Boston knew the pad. The climate will teach you the lock, whether you asked for a lesson or not.
