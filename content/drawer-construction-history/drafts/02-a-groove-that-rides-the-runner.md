The drawer did not sit on anything. It hung. I had the case on its back in the shop — a dark oak thing someone had called Jacobean because the carving was busy — and the drawers came out with a groove in each side, long as the side, packed with a century of wax and grit. The runners were still on the case: two hardwood strips, nailed and maybe once glued, polished on the top edge where the groove had ridden. I ran a thumb along that edge. It was a rail. The drawer was a car.

Hayward, in the *Woodworker* years Lost Art Press later gathered, draws this as the ordinary mid-17th-century hang. He admits it looks like more bother than putting a runner *between* drawers. He is right. It also weakens the side — a groove is a missing strip of meat in a board that already has nails or a coarse tail at the front. They used thicker sides then. They had to.

I am not going to pretend I have a 1650 mule on the bench every week. I am going to pretend I have to *understand* the hang, because the same geometry shows up whenever someone side-hangs a drawer in a solid-end case and thinks they invented it.

## Why hang it at all

A bottom-running drawer needs a rail or a frame under it. Early joined chests are a box of stiles and panels. The interior is not yet a stack of web frames. The sides of the case are there. The sides of the drawer are there. A strip on the case and a groove in the drawer is a conversation you can have without inventing a dustboard.

It also keeps the bottom out of the wear path — or it should. Hayward’s early examples still nail the bottom on and let it drag when the groove wears down. That is the scar. The hang is clever. The bottom is still a floor asked to be a shoe. When the wood of the groove wears, the drawer drops, the nails in the bottom start to kiss the rail (if there is a rail) or the contents sag. You hear it before you date it.

Side-hung drawers in later country work — a pine blanket chest, a shop cabinet, a tool till — are not automatically 17th century. The method never died. It is cheap in a solid-end carcase. You plough a groove, you glue a strip, you hope. I have built kitchen drawer boxes that way when the customer wanted wood-on-wood and the case had no room for a frame. I do not call them period. I call them hung.

## The groove is a wear part

A groove collects dirt. Dirt is a grinding compound. The runner, if it is oak or maple and the side is pine, will win. The pine groove goes oval. The drawer develops slop in the hang — up and down, then rack. A side-hung drawer that racks is a miserable thing. There is no bottom edge to slap back onto a pair of runners. The whole box is a trolley with loose wheels.

Repair is honest. You can glue a slip into the worn groove and re-plough. You can replace the runner with a slightly fatter strip. You can do both and still have a drawer that looks like itself. What you cannot do is pour epoxy into a groove and call it a restoration. Epoxy in a hang is a bearing you cannot plane.

In this climate the side also *moves*. Grain on a drawer side runs front to back. The height of the side is the cross-grain. The groove is a slot in that height. In a wet month the slot gets a little tighter on the runner; in a dry month it gets sloppy. If you cut the groove to a piston fit in January in Wilmar, June will teach you. Leave the runner a plane shaving shy of proud in the groove, and wax it. The craft pack already argued June bind on bottom-runners. Here the bind is a pinch in a slot. Same weather. Different hole.

## Thickness, species, the runner’s job

Hayward says they used thicker sides. Greene, on American 18th-century work, puts ordinary sides at 3/8 to 1/2 inch — later, and usually grooved for a *bottom*, not hung. Do not mash those numbers onto a 17th-century oak groove. [VERIFY] any thickness you print as a house rule. I will say what I do when I hang a shop drawer: 5/8 side if the groove is going to eat the board, maple or poplar, groove stopped before the front if I do not want a dark line in the end grain. A through groove is a witness. Sometimes I want the witness. Sometimes the customer does not.

The runner wants long grain along the travel. End grain on a runner is a file. I stub-tenon the front of a runner into a case rail when I have a rail. When I do not — solid-end case — I seat the runner in a shallow housing so the downward load is wood on wood, not a nail in shear. Hayward’s later runner notes (the Lost Art Press “Drawer Runners” excerpt) are for framed cases: glue the tenon, slot-screw the back so the case end can shrink. That is the right religion for a web-frame stack. It is not how a 1640 joined chest was thinking. Use the later religion when you have a later case.

## The bottom still has to be a bottom

Side-hung does not forgive a stupid bottom. If the box hangs from the sides, the bottom can be thinner. It can also be nailed on and forgotten until it splits. I groove the bottom or I let it into a rebate and I do not ask the nails to be the floor of a tool drawer. Tools are patient about destroying a nailed bottom. They have all decade.

A center muntin on a wide hung drawer is not period jewelry. It is a board that keeps a wide bottom from becoming a drum. Hayward mentions muntins when he gets to grooved bottoms with grain side-to-side. The idea is older than his figure. A wide floor wants a joist.

## Failures

I cut a hang groove too close to the top of a shallow drawer so I could “hide” it. The remaining meat above the groove was a ribbon. The ribbon split the first time someone lifted the drawer by the side instead of the pull. The groove wants to live in the middle third of the side, or a little below, where the box has something to be. Look at the old ones. They were not shy.

I also made a runner from a scrap of red oak with the grain running *across* the travel because the scrap was the right thickness. It wore into washboard in a year. Long grain along the slide. I knew that. I was in a hurry. The drawer still remembers.

A metal kitchen slide in a side that already has a period groove is a translation I have already complained about. If you must modernize the hang, fill the groove first so the side is a side again. Do not leave a historic slot to collect crumbs behind a ball-bearing.

## What this is not

This is not an argument that bottom-runners are decadent. Bottom-runners won because they let you thin the side, raise the bottom, add a dustboard, and stop asking a groove to be a wheel. The 18th century spent a lot of Saturday on that problem. The 17th-century hang is the parent that still shows up in solid-end cases and in every cheap pine chest that never hired a framer.

The judgment is small. If the drawer hangs, the groove is the bearing. Cut it as a bearing. Wax it as a bearing. Repair it as a bearing. And do not date a piece by the hang alone — the method is too useful to stay in one century.
