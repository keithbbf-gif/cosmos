The pencil whispered on the end grain of the cherry front — four tails already sawn, already pared, already the only truth those pins were going to get. I held the side like a stamp. The front stood in the vise, inside face out, and the graphite made a little weather map of each tail. That is tails-first. It is how I was taught, how I still teach when a person is holding a saw and not a comb, and it is not a commandment I am prepared to die for.

A visitor once watched the transfer and asked why I did not cut the pins first, the way his people did it in a shop that was not this shop. I did not ask for a country. I did not write his uncle into a caption. I told him the honest thing: sequence is a church. You belong to the one that taught you to see. The joint does not care which door you used if the walls meet.

## Why tails first keeps winning the hand bench

A drawer front in this trade is usually half-blind. The tails live on the sides. The pins live in the front and stop short of the show face. If you saw the tails first, you have a physical object you can press onto the end grain of the front and mark. The marks are the pins. You chop what is waste. You leave what is wood. The transfer is the argument. That is why Anglo-American hand practice — Hayward’s world, the English manuals, the American bench that learned from them — leans tails-first on drawers. You are not proving a theory. You are copying a shape that already exists.

Through tails at the back are the same religion with more light. You can saw from either board. Most of us still cut the tails on the sides, stack, mark, and saw the pins. Habit is a jig you cannot put back in a box.

There is a mechanical courtesy in that order. The side is the long grain of the travel. It is the piece you already squared to length. The front is the piece you are afraid of — show wood, a pull, a lock mortise you have not cut yet. You want the scary board to receive a mark, not to invent a shape that the side must then chase. Tails-first lets the lining lead and the face follow.

I am not going to pretend every 18th-century journeyman swore this order. Piecework London did not publish a liturgy of sequence the way it published slipping. What survives is the logic of transfer and the habit of half-blinds. [VERIFY] any claim that a named shop “always” went tails-first before you print it on a class handout.

## The other church

Pins-first is not a heresy. It is a different transfer. You saw the pins — on the front, or on a practice board — and you stand the pin board on the tail board and knife the waste. Some Central European benches grew up that way. Some 20th-century teachers in this country taught it hard enough that students still arrive in Wilmar ready to fight. I will not hang a living name on that sentence. A name without a page is a green log.

Pins-first has a real virtue on through work. The pins are the little fellows. If you cut them first you can space them by eye, fat or skinny, and the tails become the leftover. People who like even pins and rhythmic tails sometimes see the pin board as the design. People who like even tails see the opposite. Neither is a moral. It is which silhouette you draw first.

On a half-blind drawer the pins-first church has more work to do. You are marking from a board that does not show a full tail outline on the inside face until you have chopped. You can still do it. You knife the end grain, you carry the lines down the inside, you saw what you can and chisel the rest. It is slower for my hands. It is not slower for a person who has only ever done it that way. Speed is a rumor that follows fluency.

I have cut pins-first on a through-tail sample because I wanted to feel the other transfer. The joint closed. The baseline was the joint, same as it is when I go the other way. What changed was the hour, not the drawer.

## A jig is a church

<!-- PHOTO: fig-02 D:\BBF\BBF Photos — router jig or comb on a drawer side, pins and tails in the same setup (filename pending shop pull) -->

A router jig does not care about your feelings. It has a comb, a stop, a left and a right, a sequence printed on a card that will outlive the student who ignores it. Some jigs cut both sides of the joint in one family of setups. Some want the pins on the first pass and the tails on the second. Some want half-blinds on a separate tower. I have a Leigh in the shop that I use when a kitchen wants twenty identical boxes and the customer is paying for identical, not for my handwriting. The jig assigns the order. I follow the assignment. That is not a conversion. That is a tool.

Keller-style through templates, Incra-style incremental fences, a cheap comb from a big-box aisle — each one is a small denomination. They agree on one thing: the human is not transferring with a pencil. The human is registering to a finger of metal or phenolic. Registration is the new transfer. If you fight the jig’s order because a magazine told you tails-first is “traditional,” you will cut a joint that looks like two churches sharing a ruin.

Machine dovetails from the factory era already settled this in another language. The machine cut what the machine could cut. Symmetry was a dating clock, not a virtue — that is another essay. The point here is smaller. Once a device owns the sequence, arguing about hand liturgy on that device is a category error.

I tell apprentices: pick a church for the hand saw. Pick a different church, without apology, for the jig. Do not baptize the jig in the hand church and then blame the bit.

## What the joint is actually asking

The joint wants faces that meet, a baseline you can plane to, and pins that do not blow out the show face. Order is a means. If tails-first gets you a tight transfer on a half-blind cherry front, use it. If pins-first gets you a spacing you can live with on a through-tail blanket-chest back, use it. If the jig wants pins on Tuesday and tails on Wednesday, Tuesday is pins.

South Arkansas humidity does not pick a church. The side still moves in height. The front still moves in width. A sloppy transfer in July is a sloppy transfer. I have seen tails-first joints that gap like a badly hung door and pins-first joints that look like they were grown. I have also seen the reverse. The order did not save them. The sawing did.

What I will not do is score a student’s character by the order. I have sat through enough internet wars. They read like temperance tracts. Tails-first people talk about “the right way” as if the 18th century had issued a bull. Pins-first people talk about “how the old country did it” as if a continent were one bench. Both are performing. The drawer is a box. The box has to leave the case and come home.

## Failures

I once switched churches in the middle of a chest because I had watched a demonstration the night before and wanted to be cosmopolitan. Top drawers tails-first, bottom drawers pins-first, same cherry, same saw. The pins did not match in rhythm. Nobody who opened the chest would have named the cause. I could see it every time I walked past the glass. Pick one church per job. The job is a family.

I also let a jig’s pin scale set the look on a piece that was supposed to be hand work, then tried to “match” the remaining drawers by eye. The hand drawers had fat tails and skinny pins. The jig drawers had even everything. It looked like two shops had shared a carcase. I recut the jig drawers by hand. The jig went back under the bench until the next kitchen.

A third failure was pride. A man told me pins-first was for people who could not saw a tail. I cut tails-first faster than I should have and blew a pin out the show face of a walnut front. The order did not cause the blowout. Hurry did. I still hear his sentence when I am tempted to speed. I keep the patched front in a box of warnings. It is not a trophy.

## What this is not

This is not a lesson on why tails live on the sides. That argument is mechanical and already has its own Saturday. This is not a lesson on the baseline, or on half-blind as the drawer-front religion. Those are neighboring parishes. This is the sequence.

It is also not a ban on jigs. A jig is honest when you admit it is a church with a manual. It becomes a lie when you use it to manufacture “hand-cut” and then lecture someone about tradition.

The judgment is small. Transfer something. Transfer it the same way for the whole job. Do not promote a sequence to a virtue. The 18th century left us the joint, not a catechism of which board you pick up first. A jig will give you a catechism. That is what you paid for. Follow it while the bit is spinning. When the saw is in your hand, belong to the church that lets you see the waste before you cut it — and let the other church alone.
