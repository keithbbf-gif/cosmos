The knob was cherry, worn oval on the crown where a thumb had lived, still tight on a tenon I could feel from inside the drawer. No brass. No cockbead. A square arris and a front that was one board. I drew the box out of a pine case I was repairing — not a museum object, a later copy that had at least learned the knob — and the half-blinds showed only when the side cleared the rail. The fit was the other hardware. No rattle. No yank. A wooden mushroom and a hole that had been taught.

That is what I will buy when someone in Warren says *Shaker* and means a drawer. Not a font. Not a slogan about simplicity. The knob and the fit. The rest is community habit, and the habits were not identical from Hancock to Pleasant Hill to Sabbathday Lake. I will not invent an Elder’s sentence to glue them together. Museum catalogs are the witness I am allowed. They caption what a photograph shows. I will do the same.

## What the catalogs actually show

Pleasant Hill’s collections database, on a four-drawer cherry chest (accession 1970.008.001A, dated in their record 1820–1855), describes graduated drawers, dovetailed, with nicely turned wooden pulls, poplar secondary wood, and — this matters — a scratch bead. So I will not write that Shakers never touched a bead. I will write that the Federal picture-frame cockbead, seated in a rebate as armor and shadow, is not the habit I am being asked for when a customer says the word. A scratch bead on a Pleasant Hill drawer is a light line. It is not a mahogany thread around a veneered front. [VERIFY] that accession against the record before anyone prints the number on a tag.

Another Pleasant Hill chest (C2003.001.001, 1857 in their record) is cherry with poplar boxes, dovetailed front and back, pulls wedged from behind, and a crowd of glue blocks under the floors. The catalog is proud of the construction. I am proud of the wedged tenon. A knob that is wedged from inside will still be there when a screw would have wandered. I copy that when I am copying the habit. I do not copy a number of glue blocks I did not count on a piece in my own hands.

Hancock’s published notes on the Massachusetts collection point at tapered drawer sides as a tell of that bishopric — Hancock, Tyringham, Enfield, Connecticut — on built-ins and on some case work. Why they tapered is, in their own language, unknown. I will leave it unknown. I will not invent a spiritual reason. I will say that a taper is a fitting trick and a community signature, and that “Shaker style” drawings which taper every side in Arkansas are performing a tell they did not earn. [VERIFY] a taper on the object before you call a piece Hancock.

The Shaker Museum’s Hancock five-drawer blanket chest (their object notes) records lipped, dovetailed drawers and holes for turned wood knobs behind later steel pulls. Hardware lies. The hole is the witness. If you are dating a “Shaker” drawer by a bin pull, look behind the pull. The knob was often there first.

Sabbathday Lake I will not fake a drawer from. It is a living community and a catalog I have not got on this bench. The name belongs in the list of places a responsible caption would check. It does not belong in a made-up interior.

## Half-blind, wood, no theater

The front hides the joint. That religion is older than the United Society, and this pack already gave it a door. Shaker drawers use it because the front is a useful rectangle, not a place to display a tail. Through tails belong at the back, or a housing if the shop was in a hurry — I have not flattened that choice across villages, and I will not. What I cut when I am asked for the habit: half-blind at the front, through tails at the back, pine or poplar linings, a show wood that can be cherry, maple, or painted pine.

Paint is allowed. A lot of the surviving work is wash or paint, not a furniture-store cherry. If you stain a pine case to look like a magazine “Shaker cherry,” you are in the slogan. If you paint a pine case and hang real knobs on boxes that fit, you are closer to the built-ins at Hancock than a glossy set with machine tails and a typeface.

Usually no cockbead. I said usually. The bead essay already warned me not to add a Federal thread to a square arris that wanted a knob. I will not retell the rebate. I will say the sitting-room picture-frame is a different tradition. If a catalog shows a scratch bead, copy the scratch, not the cockbead you have a cutter for.

The fit is the piety people actually lived with. Drawers in a dwelling get opened by the same hands every day. A binder is not a spiritual exercise. It is a broken tool. The superb fit that writers keep praising is a shop habit: build the box, shoot it to the opening, leave a shadow the climate can drink. I will not turn that into a numbered clearance. [VERIFY] any gap you print. What I will say: the drawer in a good museum photograph sits with a line you can see and a motion you can imagine as daily. That is the habit. A piston is a different religion, and this pack already called it a drawing error.

## Habit, not slogan

I am asked for Shaker kitchens, Shaker fonts, Shaker “clean lines” on a box that will wear a cup pull and a soft-close slide. I can build a clean box. I will not put the United Society’s name on a cup pull. If the hardware is a wooden knob and the front is inset and the joint is half-blind, we can talk about the habit. If the hardware is a bar pull and the front is a slab, we are talking about a kitchen, and the kitchen essays can have it.

I will not sell a “Hancock reproduction” I made in Wilmar. I will sell a chest with wooden knobs and boxes that fit, and I will say the year is this year. Community tells — a taper, a wedged tenon, a painted wash — I will copy when I have a photograph in hand and a catalog line that matches. I will not copy a feeling.

Country work in this part of Arkansas already knew a wooden knob and a pine box. That does not make it Shaker. It makes it a knob and a box. The Society’s habit is specific: communal shops, a limited ornament, a fit meant for daily use, villages that disagreed in small ways. Specific is the respect. Slogan is the slight.

## Failures

I once put a small cockbead on a cherry front because the customer said “make it a little more furniture” and I was too tired to defend the arris. The bead was pretty. The knob looked like it had dressed for the wrong meeting. I pulled the bead and plugged the rebate. The front went back to being a board. I should have said no on the pad.

I also ordered “Shaker knobs” from a catalog that meant a shape, not a tenon. The screws wandered in pine fronts. We recut knobs with integral tenons and wedged them. The catalog shape was a hat. The Pleasant Hill record of a wedge is a joint.

A painted pine case came in with machine tails too even to be a village Saturday and a story about an aunt’s “Shaker piece.” I did not argue with the aunt. I argued with the invoice I was writing. Repair the runners. Leave the story on the porch. Absence of a cockbead does not date a box to a meetinghouse.

## What this is not

This is not a monograph on the United Society. This is not a claim that every drawer from every village looks like the one on my bench. This is not an Elder speaking. I have no quote, and I will not mint one. Hancock, Pleasant Hill, Sabbathday Lake — check the photograph, then caption. If the catalog shows a scratch bead, say so. If it shows a later steel pull over a knob hole, say so.

The judgment is small. When the word *Shaker* is used about a drawer, ask whether it means the knob and the fit. Half-blind, wood, a square edge or a light scratch, secondary linings, a hole the hand can use every day. That is a habit you can still cut without pretending you live in a dwelling. A font on a website is not a habit. Do not charge for a font. Charge for the tenon and the shadow.
