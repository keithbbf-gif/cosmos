The sliding bevel sat at a number I had not set. Someone before me had locked it at 1:6 and written SOFTWOOD on the beam in pencil that had gone grey. I picked it up to mark a set of poplar tails and felt the little click of received wisdom — the kind of click that gets a student through a test and a drawer through a century of magazine reprints. I laid it down. I marked the tails by eye, the way the old fronts I keep on the wall were marked, and the bevel went back to the rack with its religion intact.

One-in-six for softwood. One-in-eight for hardwood. You can hear the shop-class cadence from here. Rise over run. Softwood needs more slope because it is weaker; hardwood can stand a shallower pin. It is neat. It fits a chalkboard. It is also a 20th-century convenience, or at least that is how it reads when you stop using it as a law and start using it as a rumor. [VERIFY] the first American manual that prints the pair as a rule before anyone hangs the rule on a named teacher.

I am not hunting a villain. A ratio is a kindness to a beginner. It keeps the first tails from looking like a comb and the first pins from looking like needles. Kindness is not statute.

## What a slope is doing

A dovetail is a wedge that refuses to withdraw the way it went in. The flare — the slope — is how much wedge you bought. Too little flare and the joint is a box joint that took longer. The fingers are honest. They just do not lock. Glue does the work, and glue is a contract that humidity will renegotiate. Too much flare and the pin becomes a skinny wedge with short grain at the tips. Under a yank, or under a rack, those tips can crush or split. The joint can slip. Not because wood is immoral. Because you asked a needle to be a hook.

The assignment is that simple, and the benches keep forgetting it. People argue degrees. People laminate the bevel. People buy gauges that only do 1:6 and 1:8 and then treat every other angle as a mistake. The failure modes do not care about the gauge. Steep slips or splits. Shallow looks like a box joint and acts like one when the glue lets go.

South Arkansas will teach you the glue part without a lecture. A shallow joint in poplar, glued in a wet week, will still look closed in October if the pins were fat. A shallow joint in the same poplar, pins like veneer, will open a hair on the baseline when the house goes on the furnace. The slope did not fail alone. The slope and the pin width failed together. Ratio talk that never mentions pin width is half a sentence.

## What the 18th century actually left

I have pulled drawers from English and American cases that were not born in a school shop. The slopes wander. Some are steeper than any 1:6 I would lock for a student. Some are lazy, almost upright, and the lock lives in skinny pins and a tight baseline instead of in drama. Some change from the top drawer to the bottom because a different hand took the saw after dinner. I will not give you a mean angle and call it Georgian. A mean without a measured set is a magazine number. [VERIFY] any table of “typical 18th-century slopes” against the pieces, not against a myth.

Hayward, looking at surviving work, cares more about the joint showing or not showing, about slips and grooves, about the hang, than he does about a sacred ratio. The trade priced operations. It did not, in the books I will stand on, price a 7-degree tail. Visual spacing — pins that almost vanish on a fine front, pins that stay fat on a country piece — is the published story of time and money. Slope is downstream of that story. If you are cutting pins the width of a saw kerf, you have already decided the slope has to behave or the pin disappears into dust.

American 18th-century work, in the Greene and Kugelman neighborhoods of the shelf, is not a second chalkboard. Country rabbets sit next to grooved sides. Slopes, when there are tails at all, are whatever the maker’s eye accepted that morning. I have a pine shop drawer on the wall — not a fake antique, a copy of a habit — whose tails are steeper on one end than the other because I turned the board around and forgot which way I had been seeing. It still holds. It looks like a person. That is more period than a matched pair of 1:8s.

## How I mark now

<!-- PHOTO: fig-02 D:\BBF\BBF Photos — shallow tails beside steep tails on sample sides, same baseline (filename pending shop pull) -->

On a drawer that will be seen when it opens — oak linings, a walnut front, a customer who likes the joint — I mark the baseline, I divide the width by eye, and I tip the pencil until the tail looks like a tail and not like a finger. I do not set the bevel unless I am matching an existing piece or cutting a run of kitchen boxes that have to look like they came from one jig. Matching is a ratio job. Inventing is a seeing job.

Softwood still likes a little more flare than hard maple. That part of the shop-class sentence is not stupid. Pine crushes. A shallow pine pin is a shallow pine crush waiting for a slam. I will steepen poplar a hair before I will steepen quartersawn white oak. I will not write 1:6 on the traveler and call it science. If a number goes on the traveler, it is because we are matching last week’s drawer, and last week’s drawer was measured. [VERIFY] any house angle before it becomes a printed standard. I do not have one I will swear in court.

A gauge is fine. A gauge that only has two settings is a church with two hymns. Use it. Then look at the board. If the tail looks like a wedge that will split the pin, flatten it. If the tail looks like a box-joint finger, tip it. The wood will tell you faster than a reprint.

I keep three sample sides on a nail: one almost square, one shop-class 1:6, one steep enough that students wince. We assemble them in scrap. The square one slides apart when you stop being gentle. The steep one leaves a crushed pin if you tap it home with a hammer. The middle one behaves. That is a demonstration, not a law. Change the species and the middle moves.

## The kitchen and the chest are different jobs

A kitchen box on undermounts can wear a jig slope for the rest of its life and no one will hold a protractor on it. The jig’s angle is the jig’s church — neighboring essay. What matters on that box is that every drawer matches. Consistency is the aesthetic. A chest of drawers in a sitting room, half-blind fronts, handmade pins, is a different contract. The eye reads the row. If the top drawer is 1:8 and the bottom is a steep country flare because you got bored, the chest looks like two shops. Pick a slope for the job and keep it, the way you pick a church for the sequence and keep it.

I have bored myself. I have also improved a piece by letting the bottom drawers go a little fatter and a little less steep, country under town. That only works when you mean it and the case can stand a change of dialect. Accidental dialect is a mistake.

## Failures

I once cut 1:8 tails in wet pine because the chalkboard said hardwood/softwood and I had decided pine was “soft” in the moral sense but I wanted the “fine” slope. The pins mashed at glue-up. I recut the sides. The chalkboard did not apologize.

I also copied a steep 18th-century look onto a hard-maple front and split a pin tip off the show face when I drove the joint. Maple will take a shallow slope. It will also take a steep one if the pins have meat. Mine did not. I had stolen the silhouette and left the meat in the century I was copying. The repair is a Dutchman I can still find in raking light.

A third one: I sold a “period slope” as if I had measured a parish. I had measured one drawer in a hallway and generalized. That is how fake laws get born. I do not do that on paper anymore. One drawer is a drawer. A law needs a pile.

## What this is not

This is not a claim that 1:6 and 1:8 are useless. They are training wheels that sometimes stay on because the bike works. This is not a box-joint essay. Fingers have their own Saturday, shop drawers and campaign cousins. This is the flare, and the habit of treating a 20th-century ratio as if Chippendale had signed it.

It is also not a dare to cut 1:4 in everything to prove you are free. Freedom with a failure mode is just a different school.

The judgment is small. Slope is a choice. The choice has two ditches — a finger that will not lock, a wedge that will slip or split. Shop-class numbers keep a beginner out of the ditches. They do not describe the 18th century, and they do not describe a pecan side in August in Wilmar unless you have looked at that side. Mark what you can see. Keep it for the whole job. Let the bevel rest if it is lying.
