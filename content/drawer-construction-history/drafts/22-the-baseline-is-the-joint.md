The gauge clicked when the pin hit the thickness of the front. That click is the whole religion. I had the drawer front in one hand and the gauge in the other, fence against the inside face, cutter finding the far cheek. No ruler. No “about 3/8.” The mate is the dimension. The line the cutter leaves on the side is the joint I am about to cut. If I ignore that line, I can still make a pretty tail. I cannot make a box that closes on the thickness it was given.

Every dovetail starts as a pair of thicknesses. The side is a thickness. The front is a thickness. The back is a thickness, often shy of the side, and it gets its own setting. The marking gauge is how those thicknesses become lines around the ends. You saw to the line or you chop to the line. You do not chop past it and hope the plane will invent a shoulder. The shoulder *is* the line. Hayward’s shop voice, and every decent bench after him, treats that scored line as the work. I will not invent a quotation. I will say what the wood does: a clean gauge line is a stop the chisel can hear.

## Set it from the mate, not from a wish

I have set a gauge from a ruler because the fronts were “all 3/4” on the traveler. They were not. One had been dressed again after a ding. The line sat a hair proud of the real front. The tails stood off. I planed the proudness and starved a pin. The traveler is a rumor. The board on the bench is the mate.

A cutting gauge — a knife, not a pin — leaves a cleaner stop for a chisel. A pin gauge leaves a furrow that can wander on end grain. I use both. I prefer the knife on the baseline I will chop. I will not start a brand war. Sharp is the requirement. A dull cutter rides the latewood and makes a line that looks like courage and is only tear-out.

The fence wants the *inside* face if that is the face you will keep true. Drawer sides are fitted on the outside; the inside is allowed to be the reference you marked from. If you flip the reference in the middle of a run, you will cut two religions into one box. I have done that. The drawer went together with a step at one corner. The step was my fence, not the weather.

Half-blind fronts get a baseline on the end grain and a depth line on the inside face. That depth line is the hide. It is still a mate: the length of the tail, the thickness you are willing to leave as a show wall. Through tails at the back get a line that goes around. Same tool. Same idea. Different Saturday only in whether the room will see the end grain.

## Chop to the line

The chisel wants to jump the last sixteenth if you let it. End grain is a cliff. I leave a whisper of wood and then I pare to the gauge cut, vertical, the bevel facing the waste, the line already a little wall because a cutting gauge has made a shoulder. Students blow past the line because they are in a hurry to see a socket. The socket is easy. The shoulder is the joint.

A line you can still see after the sockets are clean is a line you respected. A line that has vanished is a line you took with you. I have filled a vanished baseline with a smear of hide and a wish. The wish showed as a gap when the front shrank. Chop to the line. If you miss, recut the mate or accept a proudness you will plane as a confession, not as a style.

Saws are the same religion. The kerf wants to live on the waste side of the line. A kerf that eats the line eats the thickness of the mate. Your pins will sit low. Low pins are not charming. They are a drawer corner that will never quite flush without a shim.

## Proud pins you plane

The pins — or the tails, depending on which face you leave long — should stand a little proud of the mate after glue-up. That proudness is a witness. It means you did not sneak past the baseline to make the joint “easy.” It is also a clamp: the proud wood is what the plane will take, and until you take it the corner looks like work. I will not print a house proudness in sixteenths. [VERIFY] any number an editor wants. What I can feel: enough to plane, not enough to starve the glue by leaving a hollow you will later flatten through the joint.

Plane after the glue has set. Plane before, and you thin the pins, you thin the tails, you change the thickness the next gauge setting will want if you are running a stack. I planed a row of pins “so they would look done” on a dry fit. The dry fit looked done. The glue-up was hungry. I keep that side. It teaches.

A block plane across the end grain, a little camber, from the outside in so you do not blow the far cheek. The baseline, if you left it, is still there as a shadow. When the proudness is gone, the shadow is the flush. That is a good morning. A belt sander across a proud pin is how you dish the face and lose the arris. I have inherited that dish. I do not add to it.

## The line is a shop clock

You can date a maker’s mood by the baseline. A deep, even gauge cut all the way around is a person who believed the line. A pencil line is a person in a hurry or a person who was taught by a square and a hope. Pencil is not a stop. A chisel does not hear graphite. I allow pencil for layout of tails on a face. I do not allow it as the shoulder.

Machine dovetails have a baseline too, cut by the bit’s depth. That line is a setting on a turret, not a mate you held. The evenness is pretty. The religion is different. I will not settle pins-first versus tails-first here; that church has its own door. Both churches still owe the gauge. A jig that ignores the mate’s actual thickness and assumes a nominal 3/4 will do what my ruler did: lie politely.

In this shop the long glass throws raking light in the afternoon. I hold the end up and look for the line before I call the socket done. If I cannot see it, I have gone too far or I never cut it deep enough to see. Either way I stop. The 65 feet of bench will not save a shoulder I already ate.

## Failures

I set one gauge for a whole case of drawers because I wanted to be efficient. The backs were thinner than the fronts, which is correct, and I had used the front setting on the back ends. The through tails stood off like a porch. Two settings. Front mate, back mate. Efficiency is how you cut the wrong religion four times.

I also chopped past the line on a half-blind because the socket “needed to be deeper to hide the tail.” The show wall went thin. A lock mortise later found that thinness and made a window. The hide is a thickness. The baseline on the inside face is what keeps it.

A student used a combination square and a knife, ticking a line from a ruler mark, different each end because the square wandered. Four lines, four thicknesses. The drawer went together like a wind-broke crate. The gauge exists so both ends of one board agree with one mate. Use it.

## What this is not

This is not a tails-first sermon. This is not a 1:6 slope law. Those arguments spend other Saturdays. This is not a claim that every 18th-century pin stands proud by a pretty amount I measured. Proudness varies. Wear varies. Some old drawers were flushed with a rasp and a shrug.

This is the shop fact that the joint is a pair of thicknesses turned into a line. The marking gauge is how you tell the truth about the mate. The chisel is how you keep the truth. The plane is how you hide the witness after the glue has done its work.

The judgment is small. Set the gauge from the board that will sit against the line. Chop to that line. Leave the pins proud enough to plane. Do not let a ruler, a jig’s nominal, or a traveler’s 3/4 become the mate. The click when the cutter finds the thickness is the loudest quiet in the drawer. Listen to it. Then cut what it said.
