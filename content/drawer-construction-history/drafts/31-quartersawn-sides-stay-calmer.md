The oak side showed ray fleck the length of a hand, hard and glassy under the thumb. I had the drawer open on the bench, sunlight from the long wall, and the height of that side was the only dimension I was actually worried about. The rays are where the tree stored a year and then another year. The grain ran front to back, as it must. The height was the width of the board. That width is the movement. Quartersawn keeps that width calmer. It does not abolish July.

Hoadley is the published witness I will stand on: a drawer side’s *height* is the cross-grain. Bottoms that run side-to-side expand the other way, front to back. Shops mash those two sentences. I did, early. I quartersawed a bottom and left the sides flatsawn and then wondered why the reveal at the top of the drawer changed with the weather. The reveal is the side’s height. The side is the patient.

This is not a magic trick and it is not a clearance table. I will not give you a June gap. I will tell you why some linings stay ruder than others, and why the rude ones sometimes look like jewelry when the drawer is open.

## What quartersawn is doing

Look at the end of the side. If the rings run more or less perpendicular to the face — stripes standing up — the board is quartered or close. Its width, which is your drawer’s height, is mostly radial movement. Radial is the smaller number. If the rings run as smiles or as long shallow curves across the end, the board is flatsawn. Its width is mostly tangential movement. Tangential is the larger number. Hoadley will give you tables. I will not reprint a percent and pretend I measured it on a pecan log in Wilmar. [VERIFY] any number before it becomes a house spec. The direction is enough to steer a purchase.

Walnut quartersawn is quieter to look at than oak. The rays are there. They do not flash the same way. Oak — white oak especially — will show fleck that a certain kind of customer came to the shop to see. They open the drawer and they get a little Arts-and-Crafts theater without anyone cutting through tails on the front. The lining becomes a show. That is a bonus, not the reason. The reason is the height.

Flatsawn oak sides are not a sin. They are a taller mood swing. In a shallow pencil drawer the swing is a small number. In a deep file side the swing is a bigger number. Same species, same house, different height of board. People treat “oak” as a behavior. Oak is a family of behaviors. Cut and height do the deciding.

Pine and poplar are usually flatsawn because that is how the mill presents them and because nobody is paying extra to quarter a secondary wood. They move. We still use them. We leave the whisper at the top. We do not ask poplar to be white oak.

## Cost, waste, and the mill

Quartersawn costs more because the log is treated like a citizen instead of like a slab machine. More handling, more waste, a different pile at the yard. In Bradley County you can still find oak that was quartered on purpose for flooring or for a stair. Drawer sides from that pile are a kindness if the thickness is right. Drawer sides from a flatsawn 5/4 leftover are a kindness to the budget. I tell the customer when the lining will flash and when it will not. I do not tell them the lining is “museum quality” because I paid a premium at the rack.

I will not quarter a board on the bandsaw for a kitchen box that will live on undermounts behind a false front. Nobody will see the fleck. The slide does not care. The height movement still exists, but the slide’s contract hides a lot of sins and a lot of virtues. Save the quartered oak for a sitting-room stack whose sides are visible every time someone looks for a napkin.

Walnut QS is a quieter luxury. I use it when the front is walnut and the customer has already put their hand on a lining and smiled. I use flatsawn walnut when the lining is a servant. Both are walnut. One is calmer in height. One is cheaper. Say that out loud. Do not hide the cheaper choice inside “traditional secondary wood” as if tradition were a discount.

Pecan in this geography is a show wood I like on a front. As a side, pecan flatsawn will move. I have not built a measured study. [VERIFY] before anyone prints pecan radial numbers from this shop. I treat it as a hardwood with opinions and I leave room at the top.

## Not magic

<!-- PHOTO: fig-02 D:\BBF\BBF Photos — flatsawn side beside a quartersawn side, same species, end grain readable (filename pending shop pull) -->

A quartersawn side will still cup if you finish one face and starve the other. It will still split if you pin the height with a glued-in bottom that runs the wrong way, or with a runner screwed through the side as if the side were plywood. It will still bind if you shot it to a piston in January. QS is a smaller motion, not an exemption from physics.

I have seen people treat ray fleck as a talisman. They buy the oak, they ignore the opening, they skip the kicker, they spray the runners, and then they are disappointed in July. The fleck was doing its job. The rest of the drawer was unemployed.

Finish both faces of a side. The inside of a drawer is a climate. The outside is another. A side finished like a show board on the outside and raw as a crate on the inside will bow toward the wetter face. Quartersawn bows less than flatsawn in that drama. Less is not none. I oil or seal the inside as a lining, not as a piano.

Grain runout will still lift a sliver on a QS side if you plane against it. Fleck is hard. It can chip. A scraper is kinder than a plane that is a little dull. I learned that on a side I had already bragged about. The chip is in the fleck like a missing tooth. I did not fill it. It teaches.

## When I specify it

Sitting-room chests, writing desks, anything whose drawers will stand open in raking light — QS oak or QS walnut if the budget will stand it. Shop drawers, kitchen boxes, painted work — honest flatsawn poplar or maple, thick enough, sealed, waxed, shot to the hole. File drawers get the calmest sides I can afford because the height is large and the customer will blame the sky. If I cannot afford QS on a file, I leave more whisper and I do not pretend.

English oak linings in the Hayward neighborhood are often just oak — hard, wearable, not always a textbook quarter. Do not read a modern mill category backward onto a 1720 side. The old benches used what the yard had. The movement was still the height. They left room or they lived with a bind. We have the language now. Use the language. Do not costume a pile of 8/4 flatsawn as a period secret.

A demonstration pair lives on my wall: same oak, same height, one quartered, one flat, end grain labeled. Students touch them in August and in January if they are around for both. I will not invent the delta they would measure. The flat one is the ruder neighbor. That is the lesson.

## Failures

I specified QS white oak for a chest and then used a flatsawn offcut for the bottom drawer because I ran short and the offcut was “close.” The bottom drawer is the one that shows when a child leaves it open. It also moves the most — tallest side in the stack. I saved a board and spent a reputation. The next chest I buy the extra side first.

I also finished the outsides of a pair of QS walnut sides and left the insides raw because I was late for a delivery. They bowed in a wet week, fleck and all. I pulled them, sealed the insides, reshot. Magic had not applied.

A third: I sold the fleck as the point. The customer opened every drawer at the party and closed none of them, which is fine, but they had not heard the movement sentence. They called in July about a tight top drawer. The fleck was still pretty. I should have said both sentences on the same afternoon.

## What this is not

This is not a bottom-grain essay. Side-to-side bottoms have their own Saturday. This is not June bind as a drawing error, and it is not the wax essay. Calmer sides still want a heel on the road. This is the lining’s height, and the cut that makes that height less of an argument.

It is also not a rule that every secondary wood must be quartered. Cost is real. Paint hides fleck. A kitchen slide hides a side. Spend the quarter where a hand will see it and a season will feel it.

The judgment is small. The side’s height is the cross-grain. Quartersawn oak and walnut move less there. Buy that cut when the drawer is deep, when the lining shows, when the budget can stand the rack at the mill. Leave room anyway. Seal both faces. Do not hang a talisman on a ray and skip the hole. The fleck is a gift when the drawer is open. The calm is the reason you paid.
