The side slid into the front with a sound like a sash in a dry window. One long tail on the end of a 1/2-inch maple side, one long socket ploughed up the inside of a 3/4 cherry face, a little paraffin on the bevel, and then the click when the tail bottomed. No pins. No half-blind sockets. The kitchen catalog on the bench already had a name for it. French dovetail. I had not been to France. The bit had been to a factory in North Carolina and a hundred other factories that needed a drawer front to grab a side in one pass.

I am not going to pretend the sliding dovetail was invented by a kitchen wholesaler. Cabinetmakers have been sliding a flared tail into a flared groove for a long time — shelves into sides, partitions into a case, a breadboard that wants to move and still stay on the table. The *word*, in the American kitchen trade, is narrower. French dovetail means the drawer side runs into a dovetail-shaped groove in the drawer front. Machine. Often a stopped groove so the show face stays a skin. The back of the same box may be a dado, a staple, a rabbet, even a row of through tails if someone in the plant still had a Saturday. The name does not describe the whole box. It describes the front meeting.

Furniture-store English pairs it with “English dovetail,” by which they mean the pin-and-tail zipper you can see on the side. Popular Woodworking already grumbled that the rename did not come from benches. It came from showrooms that needed two flavors of the word *dovetail* once customers learned to ask for one. I will not invent a salesman’s speech. I have stood in those showrooms. The tags do the talking.

## What the joint actually is

A sliding dovetail is a mechanical grab. The tail cannot pull straight out the way a square tongue can. Glue helps. Geometry does the first job. In a drawer, that geometry is pointed at the pull. You yank the front. The front has to bring the sides. A half-blind pin wall does that with a row of wedges. A sliding socket does it with one long wedge. Less glue surface. Less interlocking. Faster to cut if you have a spindle or a router table and a fence you trust. Better than a staple through a butt. Not the same animal as a row of tails.

I cut them. I cut them for a shelf that has to come out later. I cut them for a case divider. I have cut them on a drawer when the front was a curve and a half-blind pin would have been a fight I was not being paid to have — and I will not retell that fight here. The joint is allowed to be a joint. What I will not do is print *French* on the traveler as if I had bought a nationality.

The machine likes a consistent side thickness and a front thick enough to hold a groove without blowing the show face. A 1/2-inch side into a 3/4-inch front is a common kitchen diet. A 3/8 lining into a thin lipped front is a dare. Stop the groove before the top edge if you do not want a dark line in the end grain. Through-cut the groove if you want the witness, or if the box will wear a false front and the groove can run wild. Production shops run wild more often than they admit. The false front is the other half of the secret.

## What it is not

It is not French cabinetmaking. French benches cut sliding dovetails. They also cut half-blinds, through tails, and the secret-mitered dovetail — the corner that hides everything behind a miter, a day of work, a joint I will not mash with a kitchen SKU. A secret miter is a hide. A sliding dovetail in a drawer front is a track. If a catalog or a conversation puts those two in the same sentence, stop the sentence. They do not share a religion. They share a bird’s tail in the name and nothing else.

It is not “the French way to hide a dovetail.” Half-blind is how a European drawer front hides the joint. The sliding socket hides the *row*. You see one channel on the inside, or you see nothing if the groove is stopped and you are standing in the room. Customers who have been taught that dovetails live on the side will look at a French-dovetail kitchen box and see a plain side. Some of them feel cheated. Some of them do not care. I tell them what they are looking at. I do not tell them they bought Paris.

European production kitchens are another pile. Frameless boxes, 32-millimeter holes, metal sides, dowels, cams, a Blum or Hettich runner that never asked for a tail of any nation. A sliding dovetail on a wood kitchen box is a mid-grade wood answer, common in American plants and in some imported wood drawers. Do not flatten “European kitchen” into “French dovetail.” A METABOX does not have a French dovetail. It has a steel side and a catalog. Different Saturday.

## What I write on the traveler

If the box is a sitting-room drawer with a solid front, I write half-blind or I write a locking rabbet I will sign. If the box is a kitchen drawer with a false front and a slide, I write the joint I am actually cutting. Sliding dovetail. Dado back. Plywood floor. I price the bit setup. I do not price “French.” The word has already been spent by people who are not in this shop.

A sliding tail in a front that *is* the show face wants a stopped groove and a clean entrance. I sneak the side in from the bottom or I cut the tail a hair short of the top and plug the start if I have been clumsy. [VERIFY] any depth or angle before it becomes a house number. What I will say: too tight and you split the front when you drive the side. Too loose and you have a tongue in a square groove that you are still calling a dovetail. Paraffin on the bevel. Glue on the flats if the flats exist. Do not glue a tail into a socket you may have to open in ten years unless you mean it. Hide glue is still the glue that lets you come back. PVA in a long sliding joint is a contract with no door.

Warren kitchens want boxes by the dozen. The sliding joint earns its keep there. A single walnut hall table does not need the word *French* to justify a groove I could have cut as pins.

## Failures

I once let a bid go out with “French dovetail drawers” because the kitchen designer had already put it on her sheet and I did not want a fight. The boxes I built were half-blind at the front and through at the back. The designer was pleased. The word on the sheet was a lie. I crossed it out on our copy and wrote the joints. I should have crossed it out on hers. A catalog name that has drifted is how a shop ends up defending a nationality it never claimed.

I also cut a through sliding socket in a show front because I was thinking like a shelf. The groove flashed at the top edge, a dark V in the end grain, and every raking light in the room found it. A stopped groove, or a false front, or a cockbead that was never in the budget. I filled it. The fill is still there. I know where.

A student glued a maple side into an oak front with no test fit and beat it home with a mallet. The front split along the groove, clean as a rail. Sliding dovetails do not forgive a socket that is narrower than the tail for the last two inches. Taper the groove or taper the tail so it goes easy and finishes tight. That is old advice. It is still the advice.

## What this is not

This is not a ban on sliding dovetails. They are a correct joint for a lot of jobs, including some drawers. This is not a sermon about English pins being morally taller. Pins are a different grab. They cost a different hour. They show a different face. This is not a history of French ébénisterie. I do not have a Paris price book on the stand, and I will not invent one.

The judgment is small. If the side slides into a flared groove in the front, say sliding dovetail. If a kitchen sheet says French, translate it on the traveler into the joint. Leave the secret miter in its own church. Leave the zipper tails in theirs. The word is a showroom. The wood is a track. Cut the track as a track, and do not buy a country for a bit.
