The extra was on the traveler in pencil. Not a London traveler — mine, yellow pad, a kitchen job in Warren — but the same idea: *slip sides, plough bottoms*. I had written it because the sides were going to be 3/8 maple and the tray was going to live in a house that swings from wet to furnace-dry. A groove in a 3/8 side is a dare. A slip is a billed strip. I wanted the strip on the paper before it disappeared into “drawer boxes” as if boxes were one price.

The London trade already knew that. The *Cabinet-Makers’ London Book of Prices* — 1788, then 1793, then 1803 — has a line the craft magazines keep pointing at: slipping drawers. Later editions talk about slipping sides and plowing in bottoms. I am not going to pretend I have the 1788 sheet on a stand in Wilmar. I am going to treat the published citations as a clock, not as a poem I memorized. [VERIFY] any phrase I am tempted to set in quotes before it goes on a public page.

A price book is piecework. It is how a city shop tells a journeyman what an operation is *worth* when the case is already sold. If slipping is in the book, slipping is a thing you can charge, which means it is a thing the bench was already doing often enough to name.

## Piecework is a memory

We like to talk about 18th-century drawers as if they were made by one man in a cottage who loved his dovetails. London was a trade. Specialists. Drawers as a sub-job. A book of prices is the opposite of romance: it is a list. The list is how you keep two shops from fighting about whether a slip is “included.”

Sheraton, in *The Cabinet Dictionary* (1803), describes slips glued on the inside of drawers and planed to receive the bottom. He calls it the better method for keeping bottoms from splitting when they are confined by a rabbet and the slip is glued down at the underside. That is a shop argument with a name on it. I will not embroider it. [VERIFY] the sentence against the page if we print more than the gist.

Hayward, looking back from the 20th century, puts a slip *moulding* in the early 19th as an answer to grooved sides that had been weakened by a plough. Popular Woodworking’s later essay pushes useful bearing and hidden runners earlier — rabbets in the 1720s, named slips by the time the price book can bill them. Those are not the same object in every decade. A deep rebate with a filler strip is a cousin of a moulded slip glued to a thin side. Do not flatten them so the story fits a caption.

In this pack the slip gets its own essay. This essay is the *bill*.

The book also reminds you that a drawer was not always made by the man who made the case. London could split the work: carcase in one pair of hands, drawers in another, veneer in a third. A billed slip is how those hands stay honest with each other. If the drawer man is not paid to slip, he will groove a thin side and hope. If he is paid, he will glue the stick. I have never run a London shop. I have run a traveler that forgot an operation and then watched the bench invent a cheaper one. The book is a fence against that invention.

## What I write on the traveler

If the sides are thin and the drawer is a sitting-room drawer, I write the slip. If the sides are 1/2 poplar and the drawer is a kitchen box on undermounts, I do not. The slip is not a virtue. It is an operation. London knew that. A modern shop that “always slips” is performing. A modern shop that never slips is leaving money and wear on the table when the side is skinny.

I price the slip as wood plus a plough plus a glue-up that has to stay straight. The bottom then slides in a groove that does not cut the side in half. The bearing on the runner is wider. Wear slows. The inside of the drawer gets a little architecture — a quadrant, a cove, a flush slip with a scratch bead if I am in the mood and the customer is paying for mood.

I do not tell the customer they are buying “18th-century English construction.” I tell them the side can stay thin and the bottom can be replaced. That is the sell. The price book is my private comfort that someone else already named the line.

Other extras that want their own pencil, if they are going to happen: cockbead, a lock, a cedar floor, a center guide on a wide box. Each one is an operation with wood and time. Bundling them under “drawers” is how a shop goes broke and then calls it craft. I have written *drawers incl.* on an estimate when I was tired. I have then cut slips I did not bill. Tired is not a price book.

## What a city trade forgets to tell Arkansas

London humidity is not a dog-trot house off the Saline. A slip glued along the grain of the side is a long-grain joint. It will hold. The bottom in the slip’s groove still has to run side-to-side and stay free at the back. If you glue the bottom to the slip the way a hurried kitchen glues a plywood panel into a rebate, you have bought the split Sheraton was trying to avoid.

I have done that hurry. PVA, a birch ply, a slip I was proud of, glue squeeze-out in the groove that I called “tight.” August lifted a rib in the ply and the drawer whispered when it ran. I steamed what I could, I recut a bottom, I stopped gluing floors to architecture.

Piecework also forgets the fitting. The book prices a construction. It does not price the hour you spend shooting a drawer into a crooked opening in a house that settled. That hour is still the hour. I keep it off the “box” line and on the “fit” line so I do not lie to myself.

A price book also cannot see a 65-foot shop with one man doing the case and the drawers and the install. The split of labor is not here. The *naming* of labor still is. When I write the slip, I am pretending, usefully, that a second pair of hands might ask to be paid. The pencil is the second pair of hands.

## Failures

I once billed a case of drawers as a dozen boxes and then slipped them because the maple came in thin. The extra ate the job. Now the slip is on the estimate or the side gets thicker. London would have understood. The book exists because someone got tired of extras disappearing.

I also copied a “London” slip profile from a photograph and cut it on a router table with a bit that was close enough. Close enough is a different moulding. The customer did not care. I did, later, when I put the drawer next to an English antique in a hallway and the slip looked like a kitchen. Match the profile or invent your own and sign it. Do not halfway-copy a trade you are not in.

## What this is not

This is not a claim that Bradley Brand Furniture descends from a London piecework shop. It does not. This is not a wage table. I will not invent what a 1788 journeyman took home. This is the observation that when a trade writes an operation down, the operation has already left the category of “trick I thought of.”

The judgment is small. If you are going to thin the side, bill the slip. If you are going to groove a thick side, bill the groove. Do not bury the architecture inside “drawers” as if drawers were one verb. London priced the slip because the slip was work. The work is still work. Write it on the pad before the glue hides it.
