The pins made a sound like a comb when I ran a fingernail across them. Even, bright, the same pitch all the way down the corner. No overcut clicking in a different key. No fat pin next to a skinny one. I had the drawer on its back under the long glass wall, late oak, a factory smell still in the secondary pine, and the joint was a dovetail that had never met a marking gauge a man walked around. It wanted to look hand. It had looked hand long enough to fool a porch sale. It did not fool a fingernail.

Popular Woodworking’s dating essay treats machine-cut dovetails as a terminus post quem around 1895. [VERIFY] that fence against the piece you are holding before you print it as law. The Knapp scallop is the louder factory clock; this one is quieter because it borrowed the old shape. Once a line could cut pins and tails that sat in a row like type, the catalog could say *dovetailed* and mean it. The customer saw a tail. The shop saw a fixture.

I am not going to moralize the fixture. A machine tail holds. Glue in a tight socket holds. The drawer opens. That is the job. The dating use is narrower: if the row is that even, that joint — on that drawer — is not an 1840 hand Saturday. It is after the machines that wanted to look hand. How long after is another question. Jigs in this century still want to look hand.

## What even looks like

Hand tails wander. Not always a lot. A good bench can cut a row that would pass a hurried glance. The tells pile up when you stop hurrying. Pins and tails of different widths in the same corner. Half-pins at the ends that are not twins. A baseline the chisel kissed past, a little overcut in the waste that the plane did not quite bury. Saw tracks that change angle because the sawyer’s elbow did. Layout lines that survive in a socket.

Machine work of the late factory kind likes symmetry. Pins approximately the size of tails. Spacing you could set a compass to. No overcuts, or overcuts so regular they are the cutter’s signature rather than a slip. The inside corner is clean in a way a chisel is not always clean. The slope of every tail is the same slope. If you stack two drawers from the same case, the rows register.

That last one matters. A person cutting eight drawers in a day will still have a mood. A machine has a setup. Setup is a dating clock. Mood is not.

I have cut hand tails that were even enough to worry me later. I had a story stick and a sharp saw and I was showing off. Showing off is allowed. It is also why “even” is a clue and not a verdict. Pair evenness with the rest of the box: secondary species, runner, bottom grain, nails, the smell of the glue. A perfect row on a mule chest with a till is a replacement or a lie. A perfect row on a golden-oak dresser with machine carving is the sentence you expected.

## Tuesday is still available

Hand-cut tails can be cut on a Tuesday. This year. In Wilmar. In a night class. In a factory that kept a bench for the odd job. Absence of a machine look proves nothing about a century. Presence of a machine look, on original drawers, is the useful half of the clock.

I have to say that twice because estate-sale dating is a sport that wants a single tell. People see irregular pins and say *old*. People see even pins and say *repro*. Both sentences are lazy. A country shop in 1790 could cut chunky irregular pins. A careful shop in 2020 can cut irregular pins on purpose because the customer watched a video about “hand-cut character.” Character as a product is a different essay. The dating clock still has to survive it.

If I am asked to date a piece for a neighbor, I say what I can stand on. Machine-even tails: not before the late machine period, unless the drawer is a later box in an earlier case. Hand-irregular tails: could be any year a person held a saw. Then I look at the carcase. Then I shut up.

## Do not make it a virtue contest

The machine wanted to look hand because the customer had been taught that a tail was quality. That is fashion, not theology. A Knapp scallop is more honest about being a machine and, for a while, less fashionable. A machine tail is a disguise that became the new ordinary. Neither joint is a soul.

I cut both. I cut half-blinds by hand when the front is show wood and the job is paying for time. I cut machine tails or a locking rabbet when the box is a kitchen and the pull is going to live on a screw through a false front. I do not tell the kitchen customer they have been cheated of romance. I tell them the box will take the load. Romance is a sitting-room price.

What I will not do is sand a machine row until it looks “softer,” or add fake overcuts with a chisel to age a factory drawer. That is the same class of lie as filling a Knapp cove. The next person deserves the clock.

There is a cousin problem in this shop: the dovetail jig. A jig row can be as even as 1900. It can also leave a rounded socket floor or a bit of tear-out that a 19th-century line did not leave. I do not treat a jig drawer I made last winter as a dating example. I treat it as a warning. If you use evenness as a clock, you have to know what year the evenness came from. 1895 is one machine. A plastic template is another.

## Pins that match tails

Old hand work often likes skinny pins and wider tails. Not as a law — the later essay on one-to-six will argue with the shop-class poster — but as a habit you see enough to use. Late machine work often likes pins and tails that share a width, because the cutter is simpler that way and the look is “full.” When I see pins and tails of a size, marching, I think factory or jig before I think London 1780.

Again: habit, not law. I have cut pins the size of tails by hand when I was teaching a student to saw straight. The student’s drawer is not a factory. It is a Tuesday.

The no-overcut tell is cleaner. A chisel in a hurry leaves a nick past the line. A well-set machine stops. If I see a whole case with no nicks and every slope identical, I stop calling it a one-man bench. If I see nicks, I do not automatically call it 18th century. I call it a chisel.

## Failures

I once dated a dresser for a hallway by the tails alone and missed that the carcase rails were pocket-screwed. The drawers were honest late machine. The case had been rebuilt. I had given a year-class to a hybrid. Now I pull a drawer and a backboard before I talk.

I also tried to “warm up” a machine row on a sample by taking a chisel to two sockets. The fake overcuts sat in a row that was still a ruler. They looked like acne on a mannequin. I kept the sample on the wall so I would not do it again.

A student asked me to cut tails “like the old ones” on a pine box and then was disappointed they did not look like the jig in the catalog. We had a useful hour. The catalog jig is the machine that wanted to look hand, still wanting. The old ones wander because people wander. I would not sand the wander off to please the catalog.

## What I look at after the row

Secondary wood. A factory pine lining with machine tails is a sentence. Oak linings with wild pins and a walnut front is another. Bottoms: nailed, grooved, slip, plywood. Runners: wood, metal, a plastic roller someone added. The joint is one clock. The box is a room of clocks.

If the tails are machine-even and the bottom is a beveled pine board running side-to-side in a groove, free at the back, you are in a shop that kept older floor habits on a newer corner. That is allowed. Trades change one part at a time. Do not demand that every tell agree to the same summer.

If the tails are machine-even and the sides are 1/2 poplar and the front is a screw-on slab, you are in a kitchen. Smile. Build the kitchen. Do not write *circa 1895* on the traveler.

## What this is not

This is not a sneer at the machine. The machine did the job the catalog asked for: a tail the customer recognized, faster than a bench. This is not a claim that hand work is older. Hand work is available every morning. This is not the Knapp essay. The scallop did not try to look hand. This joint did. That is why people still argue about it on porches.

The judgment is small. Evenness is useful when you admit what it measures. It measures a setup, not a soul. If the comb-sound is there and the rest of the box agrees, write *after the machines that copied the tail* and leave the halo off. If a person cut the row on a Tuesday, write that too. Tuesday is not a defect. It is a saw.
