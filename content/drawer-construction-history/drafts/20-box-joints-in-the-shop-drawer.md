The fingers were even as a comb. I pulled a shop drawer of bits and the ends flashed maple and walnut in a rhythm no 18th-century parlor ever paid for — rectangle, rectangle, rectangle, glue in every cheek, a corner that looked like a machine had been honest. I ran a thumbnail over a proud finger someone had forgotten to plane. It caught. The drawer had never stuck. The drawer had never been furniture. It had been a box for tools, which is a higher calling in this building and a lower one in a dining room.

A box joint is a finger joint. Interlocking rectangular pins, no slope, no half-blind hide, a lot of long-grain glue surface if you cut it so the cheeks kiss. It is strong in the way a lot of glue is strong. It is visible in the way a comb is visible. You can cut it by hand with a saw and a chisel. You can cut it on a table saw with a jig. You can cut it on a machine that exists because factories wanted drawers faster than a man with a dovetail saw. I will not invent the first year of that machine. [VERIFY] any patent before it becomes a clock. The evenness is the clock I will stand on: a parlor in 1780 did not owe you that comb.

## Where the joint belongs

Shop drawers. Tool chests. The tray that holds chisels and the drawer that holds screws. A box that will be grabbed by the sides, dropped, overfilled, run in sawdust. The finger joint likes that life. The glue area is huge. There is no thin pin to blow out. If a finger shears, you can see which one and you can repair a rectangle.

Some campaign furniture wears a related honesty — travel chests, military cousins, brass at the corners, a box that has to survive a ship. I will not stamp fingers on every campaign drawer. Plenty of campaign work is dovetailed and strapped in brass and proud of it. Look at the object. If the corner is a comb, it is a comb. If it is a tail, it is a tail. Do not mash them so a dealer tag can say “military.” A flush pull and a brass corner are not a joint. The joint is still whatever is holding the box at the end.

The machine era loved the joint because a machine loves a rectangle. Once you can index a row of fingers, you can make a kitchen’s worth of drawers with a sameness that reads as quality to a person who has never held a half-blind pin. Sameness is a dating tell and a shop convenience. I use it for shop interiors. I do not use it under a Federal cockbead unless I am making a joke I will have to explain.

## Strong is not jewelry

A half-blind dovetail on a drawer front is jewelry *and* a joint. The slope is a wedge. The hide is a picture. Arts & Crafts through tails are jewelry of a different church — they show on purpose. A box joint shows because it cannot hide. There is no slope to slip under a front. The end grain of every finger is in the room if the front is not a false face.

False fronts are how a later kitchen asks a box-jointed box to wear a parlor face. The box is a comb. The front is a slab. That is a contract I will sign for a shop cabinet or a painted run. I will not sign it on a mahogany stack and then tell a story about 1790. The story would be a lie with good glue.

Hand-cut fingers wander. That wander is attractive if you like wander. It is also slower than tails if you are already good at tails, which is why the old parlor trade did not switch. I have cut a row of fingers by hand to see. They were fine. They took a day I will not bill to a dining room. The machine jig takes an hour and looks like a machine. Pick the audience.

Joyce, in the production-minded *Technique of Furniture Making*, is the kind of witness who will treat a locking corner as a factory cousin of the handmade box. I will not lift a paragraph. I will say the obvious: production wanted speed and glue area. The finger joint is both. Period parlor wanted a front that did not show its bones. Different Saturday.

## What I cut in this shop

The 65-foot shop has drawers that are combs. Bit drawers. A plane till. A row under the long glass that holds sandpaper and pencils and the kind of hardware that rusts if you pretend it is pretty. Maple and poplar, fingers fat enough to be fingers, plywood bottoms with a different movement contract. I do not cockbead those. I do not half-blind those. I glue them and I plane the proud fingers and I wax the runners. They look like what they are.

A machinist’s chest with felted tills is the cousin I will name as a type, not as a commission I invented. Those drawers take yanking. They take weight. The comb is right for that life. I will not hang a maker’s label on a box I did not build.

When I build a sitting-room drawer, I cut pins and tails. The customer is buying a front. The front is buying a hide. A comb on that front would be a shop drawing that escaped.

I have been asked to put box joints on a “period” chest because a video made them look fast. I declined. Fast is not a tradition. If they want fast in a painted pine kitchen, I can do fingers and a false front and sleep. If they want the year on the tongue, they get the joint the year used.

## Failures

I cut fingers too skinny on a maple drawer because the dado stack was already set for a 1/8 groove. The fingers sheared when someone yanked the drawer off a runner and used it as a tray. Width is strength in this joint. A comb of needles is a comb of needles.

I also glued a box joint with a starved film — too little glue, too much confidence. The cheeks were dry in the middle. The drawer ticked. I could not steam it open the way I can steam hide on a tail; I had used PVA. I sawed the corner off and recut. Hide glue on a shop drawer is still legal. I forget that when I am in a production mood.

A student put machine-even fingers on a cherry jewelry chest and stained the end grain dark so it would “look like inlay.” It looked like a comb that was ashamed. End grain on a finger joint is the picture. Leave it or paint the whole box. Do not half-hide a joint that has no hide.

I also let a table-saw jig walk. The index pin was a hair loose. By the fourth corner the fingers no longer agreed with the first. Dry-fit was a staircase. I recut two sides and kept the staircase as a sample. A machine joint that is not indexed is just a messy rectangle. Check the first corner before you cut a kitchen’s worth.

A painted parlor chest came in with fingers showing at the ends of a false front — someone had “updated” the boxes and left the old mahogany slab screwed on. The comb flashed when you opened the drawer. The slab was older show wood. The boxes were a Saturday in a garage. I left the slab. I recut the boxes with half-blinds. Two dates do not belong in one corner unless you write the year of the repair on the back.

## What this is not

This is not a ban on fingers. I like them in the right room. This is not a claim that no 18th-century box ever wore a rectangular interlocking corner — I have not inventoried the world, and I will not invent a first parlor. The habit I will stand on is shop drawers, tool chests, some campaign cousins, and the machine era that could cut them even. Strong. Visible. Not the jewelry a bedchamber paid for.

This is not a machine-dovetail essay. Symmetry as a dating clock for *dovetails* is a different Saturday. Fingers have their own symmetry, and it is ruder. You will not mistake a comb for a pin unless you want to.

The judgment is small. Use the box joint where the drawer is a tool. Show the comb. Cut the fingers fat enough to be fingers. Do not ask a machine rhythm to impersonate a half-blind front. Parlor work hid a sloped pin for a reason. Shop work can afford to be a comb. Know which room you are standing in when you set the jig.
