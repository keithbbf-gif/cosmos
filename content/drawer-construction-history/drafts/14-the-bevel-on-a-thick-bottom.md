I turned the drawer over and the bottom was two thicknesses at once. The field was fat as a breadboard, pine, a little cupped toward the socks. The rim was a knife. It still carried a polish where it had lived in the groove — a bevel, dry, a line of old hide glue like a brown thread. I ran a thumbnail up that slope. The groove in the side was skinny. The floor was not.

That is the 18th-century habit once the bottom stops being a nailed board under the box and starts being a panel that enters the sides. Hayward, looking at late-18th work, puts the bottom in a groove with the grain running side to side. This essay is not that grain sermon. This essay is the thickness trick the groove forces: the plough wants a thin ticket, the shirts want a thick floor, and the bevel is how one board does both jobs.

I am not going to give you a first year. Grooved bottoms show up when the trade has already decided the nails under a drawer are a poor floor. The bevel shows up because nobody with sense ploughs a half-inch groove in a half-inch side. The field stays meat. The edge gets thinned until it will slide.

## The groove is skinny on purpose

A groove in a drawer side is a missing strip of the side. Make it deep and wide and you have a ribbon above the slot and a ribbon below. The old American habit — Greene’s illustrated groove-in-side — keeps the side thick enough to survive that slot. English work often moves the slot into a slip so the side can stay thinner. Either way, the *bottom’s* entering edge has to match a skinny architecture. You do not thicken the groove to flatter a fat board. You thin the board at the rim.

I plough a groove that will take a ticket, not a plank. [VERIFY] any width I am tempted to print as a house number. What I will say is the feel: the bottom should enter with a plane-shaving of air, not a piston, and the field should sit a little above the runner so the runner kisses wood that was meant to be kissed — the side’s lower edge, or a slip, or a block — and not the pretty face of the floor.

If you cut the groove to fit a quarter-inch bottom and then use a quarter-inch bottom throughout, you have a floor that oil-cans. Push in the middle. It drums. A wide drawer with a thin floor is a trampoline for tools. The 18th-century answer is not plywood. The answer is a thicker field and a bevel that pays the groove its due.

## This is not a door

A raised-panel door wants a picture. The raise is on the show face. The field stands up into the room; the bevel is a shadow line; the panel floats in a frame because a door is a weather machine. I have cut those. They are a different Saturday.

A drawer bottom wants a floor. The inside should be flat enough that a folded shirt does not catch a ridge. The bevel belongs on the *underside*. You turn the drawer over to see the architecture. From above you see a board. That is the whole visual argument, and I am done with doors.

People borrow a door-raising plane because it is on the wall and the iron is already sharp. I did that once on a pine bottom and left a field that stood up inside the drawer like a tiny tabletop. Socks hung on the step. I planed the field back down from the inside and felt stupid. Use a jack or a panel plane on the underside and stop when the rim matches the groove. You are making a ticket, not a cathedral.

## What the bevel owes the movement

Wood moves across the grain. If the bottom’s grain runs side to side — the late-18th settlement Hayward names — the growth and shrinkage live front to back. The front groove can hold. The back is where the board is allowed to come and go. A bevel that is glued all around is not a bevel. It is a locked panel. I have glued a bevel all around in June because squeeze-out looked like craftsmanship. August lifted a rib. The split started at a knot and ran to the back like a decision.

Leave the back free. A nail in a slot, a screw in a slot, or nothing but the groove and a back that does not pinch. The bevel at the back can withdraw a little in a dry month. That is not a failure. That is the board using the ticket you cut. If the withdrawal shows as a dark line at the back of the floor, you can live with it. If it shows as a gap you can drop a pencil through, the groove was too shallow or the bevel too short.

A bevel that feathers to nothing will crumble in the groove. Dirt is a grinding compound. The skinny edge becomes a ragged edge, then a missing edge, and the field starts to drop. I leave a blunt ticket — a little shoulder of thickness at the rim, not a knife you could shave with. The old ones I have turned over in Warren still have that bluntness where the polish is. The ones that failed go sharp and then go away.

## Thick is a stiffness, not a virtue

I have to watch myself in a 65-foot shop with a pile of 5/8 pine. Thick throughout feels like honesty. It is often just weight. A kitchen drawer on undermounts can take a plywood floor and a different contract; that is another essay. A solid floor in a sitting-room drawer wants enough field to stay flat under a winter of wool, and enough bevel to enter a groove that does not wreck the side.

Oak bottoms are stiff and they splinter at a greedy bevel. Pine and poplar take the plane and they cup if you leave one face soaked and the other dry. I raise the bevel after the board has been in the shop long enough to stop surprising me. I do not invent a meter reading for that wait. [VERIFY] any moisture number before it becomes a printed schedule. What I will say: a wet field and a dry rim is a bevel that moves after you cut it. Cut last.

A center muntin on a wide bottom is a joist, not jewelry. Hayward mentions muntins when he gets to grooved floors. I use one when the drawer is wide enough that a single field would become a drum no matter how thick. The muntin gets its own little bevels or it sits in a dado. It does not glue the two halves into one locked acre.

## Failures

I once beveled a bottom to fit a groove I had already cut too wide. The ticket rattled. I glued a veneer shim into the groove and called it a restoration. The shim wore through in a year and the rattle came back with a stripe of glue grit. Recut the slip or recut the side. Do not shim a bearing with a leaf.

I also ran a router on the underside with a panel-raise bit because I was in a hurry and the plane was in the other room. The bit left a curve that looked like a door and a square corner that would not enter the groove without a second pass I did not want to take. I finished with the plane anyway. The router mark is still on that underside, a little grin. The drawer works. I do not point at it.

A student glued the field to a plywood sub-bottom “for strength” and then beveled the sandwich. The plywood did not move. The pine did. The bevel opened like a mouth. We threw the sandwich away. Two thicknesses of different contracts are not a thick field. They are an argument.

## What this is not

This is not a claim that every 18th-century bottom is beveled. Some are thin and lucky. Some are nailed under and still surviving because the shirts were light. Some later factory floors are a skinny board in a skinny groove and they drum when you tap them. The bevel is a habit, not a law. Hayward will not give you a fake year for the first one. Neither will I.

This is not a raised-panel lesson. If you came here for sticking and a field you can see from the sofa, you are in the wrong box. The drawer’s picture is the front. The bottom’s job is to hold weight and enter a groove without asking the side to become a ribbon.

The judgment is small. Keep the field thick enough to be a floor. Thin the rim enough to be a ticket. Put the bevel underneath so the inside stays a board you can live with. Leave the back free. And do not glue a bevel as if it were a frame. The groove is skinny on purpose. The shirts are not.
