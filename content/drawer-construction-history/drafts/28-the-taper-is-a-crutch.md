The drawer whispered in and then tightened — a handshake that becomes a hold. I ran a thumb down the side and felt the long shy wedge, narrower toward the back, daylight at the rear of the opening and none at the front. Someone had planed that courtesy who knew how to make a crooked room feel welcoming. The case was in winding. The runners were a propeller. The taper was a crutch that had learned to dance.

A slight taper is also craft. I use one. English benches have used one for a long time: the drawer a hair easier at the rear so the box will start, so a swollen front will not lock the whole travel, so the hand is not asked to thread a piston from the first inch. Hayward wants more clearance at the back than at the front if you are going to err. He will even taper a *guide* wider at the rear so the drawer does not pinch as it goes home. That is hospitality. Hospitality is not the same as hiding a twist.

This essay is the difference. The plane-to-opening Saturday is the fat box and the hole. This Saturday is the long wedge you may add after the hole has been honored — and the wedge you must not use to avoid honoring it.

## When the taper is craft

A drawer that is a rectangle, shot true to a square opening, will still start more kindly if the last few inches of travel have a little extra air. Wood swells. Hands are impatient. A box that is tight for eighteen inches is a box people slam. A box that is easy for the first inch and honest for the rest is a box people will not hate in August.

I take that air off the *sides*, not off the runners, when I can. A couple of shavings, more at the back than at the front, long and even, both sides, so the drawer does not go trapezoid in plan. I do not print the thickness of the shaving. [VERIFY] if we ever put a number on a traveler. What I can say: if you can see a wedge from the sofa, you have left craft and entered theater.

The taper should be on a drawer that already fits. Fat, shot, numbered to its hole, running on roads that are not in winding. Then the courtesy. Then the wax. That order matters. If you taper first, you will not know what you are looking at. Every gap will have two parents.

Hayward’s tapered *guide* is easy to confuse with a tapered drawer. The guide gets a trifle more width at the back so two boxes sharing a center have air as they go home. The drawer taper is the opposite move in plan: the box itself is narrower behind. Both make starting easy. Only the drawer taper can also swallow a case that is already a rhombus. If I am tempted to taper a guide and a drawer on the same job, I stop and ask which liar I am dressing. Two tapers are usually two excuses.

On a deep file drawer the courtesy earns its keep. The box is a long tunnel. A tiny pinch at the back is a long pinch. I will give a file drawer more rear air than I will give a pencil drawer. I will not give it enough air that the back can wander and rack. Rear air is not a license to be sloppy in width.

South Arkansas will change the courtesy through the year. A taper that feels generous in January can still kiss in June at the front, which is what you want — the front is the show, the front is the piston you can live with if you left the whisper. The back should remain the easy one. If June binds at the *back*, you tapered the wrong end, or you never had a taper and the case narrowed behind. Those are different repairs.

## When the taper is a lie

A case in winding will take a rectangular drawer and make it a rocker. The honest fix is the runners and the webs. Sight them. Plane them. Slot-screw what must move. Do not plane a diagonal off the drawer sides until the box “works.” It will work the way a limp works. You can walk. You have not set the bone.

I inherited a walnut stack whose every drawer was a different wedge. Someone had fitted each box to a different lie in the same carcase — one end proud, one rail low, runners that had been nailed through and had crept. The drawers ran. They also advertised the case every time you looked down on them from above. Daylight, no daylight, daylight. I pulled the boxes, I fixed the roads, I recut two sides that had been ruined by the crutch. The other four I left a little tapered because the courtesy was still useful once the roads were true. That is the test: after the case is honest, does the taper still have a job? If it does not, it was makeup.

Twisted sides on the drawer itself get the same test. A cupped side will look like a taper from one angle. People then add more taper to “match.” You end up with a banana in a propeller. Flatten what must be flat. Then talk about wedges.

A taper can also hide a case that is narrower at the back because the ends were planed in a hurry, or because a solid end shrank more at the rear where a vent blows. That narrowing is real. A matching drawer taper will run. It will also teach the next person that the case is a rectangle. Leave a note, or leave the case honest. I pencil a line on the underside of a web when I have accepted a narrow back: *case shy rear, drawer tapered to match, 20—*. Year and a word. Not a novel. The next shop can read.

I will not invent a restorer who told me “they all did it.” They did not all do it. Some did. Some built square and let wax do the kindness. Both show up in the wild.

## How much, and where I will not taper

I do not taper height. The top edge of a drawer is a reveal. A wedge in height looks like a mistake from across the room. If the opening is tighter at the back in height, the case is the patient. Kickers and rails, not a sloped side.

I do not taper the front. The front is the picture. Its width is the opening’s width, shot to the rails. The wedge lives behind the front, in the lining.

I do not taper one side only unless the case is genuinely one-sided and I have written that down. One-sided taper is how a drawer learns to walk toward the fat side and wear a groove.

A jigged kitchen box on slides does not want this religion. The slide wants parallel. Taper a slide box and you are fighting steel. Different contract.

## Failures

I tapered a chest’s drawers to save a Saturday on runners I knew were in winding. I was tired and I could hear the plane, which I like more than I like winding sticks. The drawers ran for a year. Then a dry spell lifted a runner a hair and every box racked because the wedges no longer agreed with the propeller. I spent the Saturday I had saved, plus a second one, plus a conversation I did not enjoy. Winding sticks first. Courtesy second.

I also took too much off a pair of pecan sides and called it “period.” The backs wandered. The drawer racked on the way in and slammed one corner. I glued slips on the sides and recut the courtesy down to a shaving. Period is not a license to be loose.

A third: I copied a taper from a photograph of an English drawer and put it on a shallow pencil tray. The tray is four inches deep. It did not need a tunnel courtesy. It needed to not look drunk from above. I recut the tray. Deep boxes get the wedge. Trays get the plane and the wax.

## What this is not

This is not the plane-to-opening essay. Fat and shoot first. This is not the rack essay, though a bad taper will rack a wide box as surely as a missing center guide. This is not June clearance as a drawing error — no feeler stick, no seasonal table. This is the long wedge, and the moral difference between hospitality and hiding.

It is also not a ban. I will die with a little taper in my shop drawers. I will not die defending a propeller.

The judgment is small. After the hole is honest, you may ease the back. A shaving, both sides, still a drawer, not a slice of pie. If the case is the liar, fix the case. A crutch is for a bone that is already set. Used on a twist, it is a lie that runs well enough to keep you from doing the work.
