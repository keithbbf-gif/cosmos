The smell arrived before the drawer cleared the rails. Pencil shavings and a closed closet. Aromatic red cedar — *Juniperus virginiana*, a juniper that learned to sell itself as cedar — in a bottom someone had trusted to keep moths honest. I pulled the box the rest of the way and the smell filled the aisle between the benches. The floor was a red-and-cream board, knots like eyes, a faint cup. I turned it over. The cup had already started a split at the back where someone had glued the ticket all around. The moth story was intact. The board was not.

Eastern red cedar grows in this part of the South without anyone importing a romance. Fence posts, closet lining, a chest an aunt kept wool in. Bradley County knows the tree as a volunteer in old fields. I will not turn that into a brand story. I will say I have ripped the boards in Wilmar and they still smell when the saw hits heartwood. The smell is the product. The product is not a spell.

## The moth story, with a bulletin

People buy cedar because their grandmother bought cedar. The published science is older than the catalogs that still talk as if a chest were a fumigator. In 1922 the U.S. Department of Agriculture issued Bulletin 1051, *Red Cedar Chests as Protectors Against Moth Damage*, Back and Cotton. I am not going to fake a quotation from a bulletin I do not have open on a stand. I am going to treat the published conclusions the secondary literature keeps repeating: a cedar chest is not a reliable killer of adult moths or of larvae that have already grown; tightness and clean goods matter; young larvae are the stage the vapor is said to trouble more. [VERIFY] every tighter sentence against the bulletin before it goes on a public page.

Wood Magazine’s later shop note, standing on that kind of work, says the useful version out loud: vapors in a tightly closed chest can affect hatching larvae, and they do little to large larvae, adults, or eggs, and less still in an open closet. A sealed clean box without cedar does a lot of the same job. That is not a slight on the tree. It is a refusal to sell a floor as a pesticide.

Clothes moths eat wool that is dirty. They like sweat and food spots. A cedar drawer full of unwashed sweaters is a larder with a nice smell. I tell people to clean the wool first. I tell them a tight lid on a chest is part of the old design for a reason. I do not tell them the bottom will keep house.

## It is still a board

Cedar moves. The heartwood is pretty and the grain is often knotty and the board will cup if you finish one face and starve the other. Tangential movement on a juniper is not a parlor trick. I will not invent a percent. Hoadley’s kind of table is where those numbers live; [VERIFY] before anyone prints a house shrinkage. What I will say from the bench: a wide cedar floor glued on four edges splits like pine glued on four edges, and sometimes faster, because the board was never as calm as the smell suggested.

The bevel-and-groove religion still applies. Grain side to side. Ticket in a skinny groove. Back free. Do not glue the field to the sides as if the moth story had cancelled weather. I have watched a customer smell a split and decide the wood was “too dry,” as if a humidifier would close a glued-in rib. The rib was a locked panel. The smell had nothing to say.

A lining is a different architecture. Thin cedar slats or a thin sheet over a stable ply floor — that can be a contract that works, if the lining can move or is broken into widths that do not fight the box, and if you do not glue the lining into a frame that holds it like glass. I have lined a blanket drawer that way. The ply is the floor. The cedar is a smell and a color. When the lining wants to move, it is not also holding the socks.

Oils in the heartwood are why the tree is famous, and why some glues act shy. Hide glue has grabbed cedar for me when I warmed the room and the wood. PVA has grabbed too, and has also sat on an oily face like a rumor. [VERIFY] any “cedar hates PVA” rule before it becomes a caption. I scrape fresh, I do not finish the glue face, and I do not pretend a starved joint is a moth feature.

## The smell fades

Volatiles leave. Surfaces oxidize. Dust seals the grain. An old cedar chest may smell like a closet and not like the tree. That fading is ordinary. Sanding the inside opens the pores again and the smell comes back for a while. Catalogs love that Saturday. It is a refresh of scent, not a renewal of a pesticide license. I will not print a number of months as law. [VERIFY] any “every two years” schedule. What I will say: if you cannot smell it, do not trust it as a vapor, and even if you can smell it, clean the wool.

Finish kills the point if the point was vapor. A polyurethane inside a cedar drawer is a sealed box that happens to be red. If you want the smell, leave the inside raw and accept that raw cedar will pick up oil from hands and wool. If you want a wipeable kitchen floor, do not use cedar. Use a sheet and a film. Different room. Different contract. The plywood essay already argued that weather. This essay is the tree people keep asking to be a charm.

I will not sell “moth-proof drawers” as a line. I will sell a cedar lining or a cedar floor and tell the truth about fading, splitting, and laundry. I can smell the offcut from the glass wall. That is enough romance. The rest is a board.

## Failures

I glued a wide cedar bottom into a cherry sock drawer on all four edges because the customer had said “make it tight so the moths can’t get in.” Tight at the lid of a chest is a design. Tight at the floor of a drawer is a lock. The board split before the first summer was out, and the split was a highway. I recut a floor, left it free at the back, and put a thin cedar lining on the sides where a lining could be a lining. The moths were not consulted. The wool was cleaned.

I also finished the inside of a cedar till with oil because it looked thirsty. The smell flattened. The oil sat in the pores that had been the point. I sanded later and got a ghost of the tree back. Do not oil the inside if you sold the vapor.

A student used Western red cedar — a different tree, a different smell, a porch wood — because the rack was labeled cedar and the price was kind. The drawer smelled like a wet fence. We recut. Names on racks lie. *Juniperus virginiana* is the moth-story species in the American chest tradition. The other cedars are other Saturdays.

## What this is not

This is not a ban on cedar. I like the smell. I like the color in a blanket drawer. I like that the tree grows here and does not need a catalog to explain itself. This is not a claim that museum woolens should be stored in a shop-made cedar box and forgotten. Conservators have their own religion, and I will not invent it. This is not USDA fan fiction. The bulletin is a witness. The witness is cautious.

Hayward did not write a cedar chapter I am going to fake. Country American chests lined themselves with the tree because the tree was at the fence line and the story was already old. The story is allowed. The physics is still Hoadley. The floor is still a floor.

The judgment is small. If you want the smell, use the heartwood and leave it raw, and give the board the same freedom you give pine. If you want moths not to eat wool, clean the wool and close the lid. Do not glue a charm all around and call the split a mystery. The crack on that first bottom was older than the disappointment. It started at the glue. The smell, while it lasted, was just the smell.
