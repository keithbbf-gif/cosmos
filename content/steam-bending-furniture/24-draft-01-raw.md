# Draft 01 — raw assembly

First time the sections have to live in one mouth. Repetition left in on purpose. History still arrives a little late and then talks too long. Shop steps are in the right order. This is not the piece.

---

Wood will crumple. It will not stretch.

That is the whole craft, and it is older than any furniture brand, including this one. Wood is a bundle of tubes glued together. The tubes are cellulose. The glue is lignin and the hemicelluloses. Dry, that glue softens around 170 °C, which is a Forest Service number and a bad shop number if you stop there. You will not run a stick at 338 °F and like what is left. Water drops the temperature where the glue lets go. A kettle and a box and a wet blank get you to 200–212 °F. That is enough for the hardwoods that want to move.

Once the glue is soft, the tubes will take twenty-five to thirty percent compression along their length and only one or two percent stretch. So you are not stretching a bow. You are crumpling the inside and keeping the outside at zero. If you ask the outside to grow, you are breaking, slowly or all at once.

A curve sawn out of a wide plank is a different object. Pretty until it is in a chair. The grain on the edges is short. A steam-bent piece keeps the fiber running around the turn. That is the only reason to fool with a kettle. Not romance. Continuous grain.

There is a cheap way to fake the same silhouette: glue thin leaves over a form. That is a good way. Stronger than steam-bent solid. Tighter radius. Glue lines on the edge. We will not call it steam bending and we will not sneer at it. Different job.

We have mostly made rectangles. Moro islands. Caney Creek. Boards in gum and oak. That was an honest job. A kitchen island does not need a steamed stretcher to be real. It needs to be square in August. The next honest shape is a curve that still belongs on a kitchen floor, with the grain going around the corner instead of giving up at the edge.

Most breaks are decided before the kettle is hot.

Start with the woods the Forest Service already paid to list: white oak, red oak, elm, hickory, ash, beech, birch, maple, walnut, sweetgum, mahogany. Fortune, who has been doing this longer than we have been an LLC, tells a beginner to stay in ash, oak, hickory, elm, and walnut. Our building already knows some of those. White oak and gum and walnut and ash have been across these benches. Hickory we know from the planer. Sweetgum is on the lab list. That does not make it the first customer bow. Sycamore we used for carving boards. We will not advertise it as a bender. Southern yellow pine will take a mild boat curve. It will not take a chair hoop.

Ash: look at it. The borer is in the country. Sound ash still bends.

Grain slope no steeper than about one in fifteen. No pith, no shake, no rot, no knot in the bend. Rive if you can. If you saw, read the edge like a receipt you do not trust. Thickness is a design choice. Fortune says one person can pull about an inch and a half. After that, get help.

Meter the stick. The lab’s window for furniture is twelve to twenty percent moisture before steam. For a real curve over a form they want fifteen to twenty. Fortune lives at twelve to sixteen, air-dried. South Arkansas will hand you eighteen and call it dry. For this job, eighteen is not an insult. Green is tempting and it is how you crush the inside of a hard bend. Kiln-dried is the other ditch. If that is what we have, we soak it and we expect to break more of them.

Write species, thickness, and the meter reading on the end grain.

A shipwright did not need a catalog number to pull a rib. You steam the stock, you pull it over a mold, you fasten it before it argues. Windsor chairs were already doing the hoop in England and then here in the 1700s — oak, ash, or hickory around a plank seat. People say steam-bent chair and wave at the whole thing. Only the hoop did that work. The lesson is the blank: split out of the log, fiber end to end. Thonet industrialized a hoop. He did not invent the hoop.

Michael Thonet was a cabinetmaker from Boppard, born 1796. In the 1830s he got tired of carving a curve and wasting the block. First answer: stacks of veneer, boiled in glue. London, 1851, Crystal Palace. The jury said curious and medaled them anyway. Then bankruptcy, Vienna, Gebrüder Thonet in 1853. The useful invention: solid wood, steam or a boiling liquor, and a metal band on the face that would otherwise tear. Privilege in 1856. Factory in the Moravian beech at Koritschan in 1857. Ten thousand chairs that year. By 1913, a little over 1.8 million pieces a year. No. 14 in 1859, six sticks and cane, in a crate. By 1930, more than fifty million of that model.

What he changed: a factory that could do the same back on a Tuesday, and a strap that moved the stretch into the steel. They still bend beech in Frankenberg in a pressurized boiler for hours. That number does not travel to a plywood box on sawhorses.

Aalto’s birch and the Eames shells are veneers and glue. Do not steal their photographs for this.

Bradley County’s mill story is not Vienna. September 1901, Samuel Holmes Fullerton, Bradley Lumber Company, Warren. By 1907, on the order of a hundred thousand board feet a day and about three hundred fifty men. A postcard said they made furniture *stock*. Other people’s factories bought the rectangles. The courthouse is 1903. The LLC is 2008. Pennington Street, then Rock Springs Road in Wilmar. We will not flatten those dates.

A steam box is a wet oven that must not become a bomb. Long chamber, sloped to a drain, racks so the sticks are not in a puddle, steam in low, vent at the far end, a thermometer you can read. Plywood is fine. Pressure is not. Two hundred to two hundred ten in the box. If the thermometer says 180, you are warming lunch.

The rhyme is one hour per inch. The lab says about thirty-eight minutes per inch if the stick is already wet (20–25%), about seventy-five if you are putting water back. Fortune says one to two hours, and a third hour on a one-inch part wrinkles the inside. Do not load more sticks than you can bend in the window. One-inch stock: about ten minutes of useful heat after the door. Write the time-in on the end.

Stand to the side of the door. Gloves. The cloud is not always visible until it hits your glasses.

The pull: strap on the convex face, end stops, slight arc, then stop tightening. Form cut to the curve plus a little, because the piece will open. We do not have a universal springback percent. Write on the form what that blank did. If a kink starts, clamp behind it. Do not muscle through. One hour later the strap comes off or oak and the steel make ink.

The wait is the part that keeps it. Dry in the curve until the piece is as dry as the room it will live in. Fortune likes 7–8%. A kitchen in Warren in August may not be that. Meter. He guesses a week. Oak under a blanket the first day. More moisture later opens the curve. Less tightens it. Wet and dry, and it slowly remembers the log.

A bent piece is a little weaker than the same piece left straight. Seldom enough to matter, says the lab. Geometry still matters.

How it fails: outside break (no strap, runout, kiln-proud, wish radius). Inside wrinkles (oversteam, strap too tight, green crush). Checks (fan on wet oak). Blue stain (strap overnight). Opens (still wet). Tightens (kept drying). Straightens over months (cycling). Will not start (cold, or you talked for twelve minutes). Three in a row (stop changing three variables).

We have not bent twenty gum bows and kept the breaks. Until we do, gum is on the lab list and off the first customer part. We have the woods and the climate and the refusal to fake a number. The next twenty sticks are the rest of this article. Keep the breaks.
