# Section try — what the stick is doing

Wood is a bundle of tubes glued together. The tubes are cellulose. The glue is lignin and the hemicelluloses. You do not need a lab coat for that. You need it because the glue is the part that will change its mind.

Dry lignin softens around 170 °C. That is a number from the Forest Service handbook, and it is a bad shop number if you stop there, because you will not run a stick at 338 °F and have anything left you want. Water drops the temperature where the glue lets go. Atmospheric steam — a kettle, a box, a wet blank — gets you into the neighborhood of 200 to 212 °F. That is enough for the hardwoods that want to move.

Once the glue is soft, the tubes will do something uneven. They will take a lot of compression along their length. The handbook says twenty-five to thirty percent. They will take almost no stretch. One or two percent, then they let go. So a bend is not a stretch. A bend is a crumple on the inside and a refusal to grow on the outside. If you ask the outside to grow, you are not bending. You are breaking, slowly or all at once.

That is why a curve sawn out of a wide plank is a different object. The grain on the edges of that curve is short. It is pretty until it is in a chair. A steam-bent piece keeps the fiber running around the turn. That is the only reason to fool with a kettle. Not romance. Continuous grain.

There is a cheap way to fake the same silhouette: glue thin leaves over a form. That is a good way. It is stronger than steam-bent solid, and it will take a tighter radius. It also shows glue lines and end grain on the edges unless you hide them. We will not call it steam bending. We will not sneer at it either. It is another job.

If the inner face and the outer face of your part would have to differ by more than about three percent in length, the outside is already in trouble. That is the handbook’s threshold for a strap. The strap is not a nicety. It is how you move the stretch out of the wood and into the steel.

I keep wanting to write “the wood wants.” It doesn’t. The glue gets soft. The tubes crumple. You hold them there until the glue sets in the new shape. Then moisture will still try to argue with you for the rest of the piece’s life. That argument is later.
