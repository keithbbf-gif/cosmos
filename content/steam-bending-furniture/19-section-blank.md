# Section try — the blank

Most breaks are decided before the kettle is hot.

## Wood

Start with the list the Forest Service already paid for: white oak, red oak, elm, hickory, ash, beech, birch, maple, walnut, sweetgum, mahogany. Fortune, who has been doing this longer than we have been an LLC, tells a beginner to stay in ash, oak, hickory, elm, and walnut until the breaks teach you something.

Our building already knows some of those. White oak and gum and walnut and ash have been across these benches as boards and case parts. Hickory we know as a hard customer in the planer. Sweetgum is on the lab list. That does not make it the first customer bow. Sycamore we used for carving boards; we will not advertise it as a bender. Southern yellow pine will take a mild boat curve. It will not take a chair hoop and then a compliment.

Softwoods as a class are short in the fiber. Tropicals are often interlocked and tear. Exceptions exist — yew, yellow-cedar — and they are not what we have in the rack.

Ash: look at it. The borer is in the country. Sound ash still bends. Unsound ash is firewood that learned a new name.

## Grain

Slope no steeper than about one in fifteen. The handbook is not being precious. A stick that dives off the edge is a break waiting for a radius.

No pith. No shake. No rot. No knot in the working part of the bend. No light, brittle, punky feel. If you rive the blank from a bolt, the fiber is already telling the truth. If you saw it, stand it on edge in good light and read it like a receipt you do not trust. Throw away more than you want to. The scrap is cheaper before it is steamed.

Thickness is a design choice pretending to be a materials choice. Thicker takes longer, holds heat longer, and needs more body on the pull. Fortune says one person can handle about an inch and a half. After that, get help or get a block and tackle. We will not hero a two-inch oak bow.

## Water in the stick

Meter. Do not tap it on the bench and guess.

The lab’s window for furniture is twelve to twenty percent moisture before the steam. For a real curve over a form they want fifteen to twenty. Fortune lives at twelve to sixteen, air-dried. South Arkansas will hand you eighteen and call it dry. For this job, eighteen is not an insult.

Green is tempting. It is also how you crush the inside of a hard bend and then spend a month watching the curve walk while it dries. The green-wood school can do it because they chose the radius and the species and they live with the movement. We are not starting there.

Kiln-dried is the other ditch. Fortune says the lignin is set and the fibers tear. The lab says put the water back. If a kiln-dried stick is what we have, we soak it and we expect to break more of them. We do not start a paid part on the first one.

Write the species, the thickness, and the meter reading on the end grain. When it comes out of the box you will want to know what you put in.
