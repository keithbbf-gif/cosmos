# Wood will crumple. It will not stretch.

History and shop practice for Bradley Brand Furniture.

You can saw a curve out of a wide plank and call it done. The edges of that curve are short grain. Pretty until someone leans. Steam is how you keep the fiber running around the turn. The glue in the stick — lignin — will let go if you get it hot and wet. Dry, that happens near 170 °C, which is a lab number and not a shop setting. In a box with a kettle you live at 200–212 °F. Soft, the wood will crumple twenty-five or thirty percent along the grain. It will stretch one or two percent, then it lets go. So you crumple the inside and you do not let the outside grow. That last part is a strap.

Glued leaves over a form will make the same silhouette, stronger, tighter. They will also show glue. We will not call that steam bending.

We have mostly made rectangles — islands with creek names, boards in gum and oak and walnut. A Moro does not need a steamed stretcher to be real. It needs to be square in August. A curve still belongs here if the grain goes with it.

## The blank

Most breaks are decided before the kettle is hot.

White oak, red oak, hickory, ash, elm, walnut will do this work. So will beech, birch, maple, sweetgum, mahogany. We already handle several of those as case stock and boards. Sweetgum is on the Forest Service list; it is not the first customer bow. Sycamore we used for carving boards — we will not advertise it as a bender. Pine will take a mild boat curve. Sound ash still bends; look at it for the borer.

Grain no steeper than about one in fifteen. No pith, shake, rot, or a knot in the bend. Rive if you can. If you saw, read the edge. One person can pull about an inch and a half. After that, get a second body or a block and tackle.

Meter. For a real curve over a form we want the stick at about 15–18 percent. That sits inside the lab’s furniture window (12–20; they prefer 15–20 on a form) and next to the air-dry range a lot of chairmakers use (12–16). South Arkansas will hand you 18 and call it dry. For this job, 18 is fine. Green crushes the inside of a hard bend. Kiln-dried wants a soak and a higher scrap budget. Write species, thickness, and the reading on the end grain.

## The old work

Boat shops pulled ribs before anyone numbered a chair. Windsor makers in the 1700s were already steaming oak, ash, or hickory into a bow around a plank seat. The rest of that chair is a different technology. The lesson we need is the blank: fiber from end to end.

Michael Thonet, Boppard, born 1796, first tried stacked veneer boiled in glue. Then, in Vienna in the 1850s, solid wood, steam, and a metal band on the face that would otherwise tear. Gebrüder Thonet, 1853. Privilege, 1856. A factory in the Moravian beech at Koritschan, 1857 — 10,000 chairs that year, more than 1.8 million pieces a year by 1913. No. 14 in 1859, six sticks and cane in a crate. More than fifty million of that model by 1930 (V&A). The strap is the craft. The crate is the business. They still cook beech in a pressurized boiler in Frankenberg. Those hours do not travel to our box.

Aalto and Eames are veneer and glue. Different essay.

Warren is not Vienna. Fullerton’s Bradley Lumber Company, September 1901. By 1907, on the order of a hundred thousand board feet a day and about 350 men, selling oak and pine and **furniture stock** — rectangles for other people’s factories. Courthouse, 1903. This LLC, 2008, Pennington Street, then Rock Springs Road in Wilmar. If we take up a curve, we take it up as that shop.

## The box

A wet oven that must not become a bomb. Sloped to a drain. Racks, not a puddle. Steam in low. Vent at the far end, and do not cap it. A thermometer you can read. Plywood is fine. Pressure is not. Keep the burner out from under the lid. The pot will scald you as dead as the vapor.

200–210 °F. If it says 180, you are warming lunch. Gloves. Face out of the door.

The rhyme is one hour per inch. The lab’s finer mouth: about 38 minutes per inch if the stick is already at 20–25 percent moisture, about 75 if you are putting water back. A third hour on a one-inch part wrinkles the inside.

**House, 1-inch air-dried oak at 15–18 percent:** one hour. Pull. If it fights, twenty minutes more. Not three hours.

A one-inch part has about ten minutes of useful heat after the door. Do not load more than you can bend in that weather. Write the time-in on the end.

## The pull

Strap on the convex face, end stops, slight arc, stop tightening. If the inside and outside of the part have to become more than a little different in length — the lab says about three percent — you need that strap. It is not a nicety.

Form: the curve you want, plus a little. Write on the form what that thickness did. Anchor the strap. Pull. If a kink starts, clamp behind it.

If you are running more than one, move the piece to a rack that holds the same curve so the form is free. One hour after the pull, the strap comes off. Tannin and steel make ink.

## The wait

Leave it restrained until it is as dry as the room it will live in. Seven or eight percent is a northern furniture number. A kitchen here in August may not be that. Meter. A week is a guess. Oak under a blanket the first day. Later, more moisture opens the curve; less tightens it; wet and dry, and it slowly remembers the log.

A bent piece is a little weaker than the same piece left straight. Seldom enough to matter, if you designed the section for the lean.

## When it fails

Outside break: no strap, runout, kiln-proud, a radius you hoped for. Inside wrinkles: oversteamed, strap too tight, green crush. Checks: you fanned wet oak. Blue-black: the strap stayed. Opens: you took it off wet. Will not start: it is cold, or you talked too long in the lane. Three in a row: change one variable.

We have not bent twenty gum bows and kept the tally. Until we do, gum stays on the list and off the first customer part. Keep the breaks.
