# Wood will crumple. It will not stretch.

History and shop practice for Bradley Brand Furniture.

You can saw a curve out of a wide plank and call it done. The edges of that curve are short grain. Pretty until someone leans. Steam is how you keep the fiber running around the turn.

The glue in the stick will let go if you get it hot and wet. The Forest Service lab’s dry number for that glue — lignin — is about 170 °C. We do not live there. We live in a box with a kettle: two hundred to two hundred ten Fahrenheit in the chamber, water boiling near 212, and a blank that already has some water in it. Soft, the wood will crumple twenty-five to thirty percent along the grain. It will stretch one or two percent and then it lets go. So you crumple the inside. You do not let the outside grow. The second half of that sentence is a strap.

Glued leaves over a form will make the same silhouette, stronger and tighter, with glue on the edge. That is not this.

We have mostly made rectangles — islands named for the water around here, boards in gum and oak and walnut. A Moro does not need a steamed stretcher to be furniture. It needs to be square in August. A curve still belongs on that floor if the grain goes with it.

## The blank

Most breaks are decided before the kettle is hot.

White oak, red oak, hickory, ash, elm, walnut. Those will do this work. Beech, birch, maple, sweetgum, mahogany too. We already know several of them as case stock and boards. Sweetgum can wait. Sycamore we used for carving boards; we are not going to advertise it as a bender. Pine will take a mild boat curve. Sound ash still bends. Look at it.

Grain no steeper than about one in fifteen. No pith, no shake, no rot, no knot in the bend. Rive if you can. If you saw, read the edge. One person, about an inch and a half. After that, another body or a block and tackle.

Meter. For a curve over a form we want 15–18 percent. The lab’s wider furniture window is 12–20; they like 15–20 when the form is serious. A lot of chairmakers air-dry to 12–16. Down here, 18 is not an insult. Green crushes the inside of a hard bend. Kiln-dried wants a soak and a scrap budget. Write species, thickness, and the reading on the end grain.

A bench stretcher, white oak, one inch thick, grain running, meter at 16. That stick is allowed in the box. A pretty stick with a dive at the edge is not.

## The old work

Boat shops pulled ribs before anyone numbered a chair. Windsor makers were steaming bows in the 1700s. The lesson is the blank: fiber from end to end.

Thonet started with veneer boiled in glue, then — Vienna, 1850s — solid wood, steam, and a metal band. Factory in the Moravian beech, 1857: 10,000 chairs that year, more than 1.8 million pieces a year by 1913. No. 14, 1859, six sticks and cane in a crate. More than fifty million of that model by 1930, says the V&A. The strap is the craft. The crate is the business. They still cook beech under pressure in Frankenberg. Those hours stay there.

Aalto and Eames are veneer and glue. Different job.

Warren is not Vienna. Fullerton’s Bradley Lumber Company, 1901. On the order of a hundred thousand board feet a day and about 350 men by 1907, oak and pine and furniture stock — rectangles for other people’s factories. Courthouse 1903. This LLC 2008, Pennington, then Rock Springs. If we take up a curve, we take it up as that shop.

## The box

A wet oven that must not become a bomb. Slope it to a drain. Racks, not a puddle. Steam in low. Vent at the far end — do not cap it. Thermometer you can read. Plywood is fine. Pressure is not. Burner out from under the lid. The pot will scald you as dead as the vapor. Gloves. Face out of the door. The box is not a conversation pit.

Two hundred to two hundred ten. If it says 180, you are warming lunch.

One hour per inch is the rhyme. House, for that 1-inch oak at 16 percent: one hour. Pull. If it fights, twenty minutes more. Not three. The lab’s finer mouth, if you want it: about 38 minutes per inch at 20–25 percent moisture, about 75 if you are putting water back. A third hour on a one-inch part wrinkles the inside.

About ten minutes of useful heat after the door. Write the time-in on the end. Do not load more than you can bend.

## The pull

Strap on the convex face. End stops. Slight arc, then stop tightening. If the inside and the outside have to become more than a little different in length — about three percent, says the lab — you need the strap.

The form is the curve you want, plus a little. Write on it what that thickness did. Anchor. Pull. Kink starts, clamp behind it. Duplicates go to a rack that holds the same curve. One hour after the pull, the strap comes off. Tannin and steel make ink. Oak under a blanket the first day.

## The wait

Leave it restrained until it is as dry as the room it will live in. Seven or eight percent is a northern furniture number. August here may not be that. Meter. A week is a guess. Later moisture opens the curve. Drying past the house tightens it. Wet and dry, and it slowly remembers the log.

A bent piece is a little weaker than the same piece left straight. Seldom enough to matter if you designed the section for the lean. Cut joinery after it has sat in the finish room.

## The day, and the breaks

In at 9:10. Out at 10:10. Strap, slight arc, onto the form. Kink at the far horn — clamp, then finish. Stays on. Strap off at 11:10. Blanket. Do not cut the tenon this week.

Outside break: no strap, runout, kiln-proud, a radius you hoped for. Inside wrinkles: oversteamed, strap too tight, green crush. Checks: you fanned wet oak. Blue-black: the strap stayed. Opens: you took it off wet. Will not start: it is cold, or you talked too long in the lane. Three in a row: change one variable.

We have not bent twenty gum bows and kept the tally. Until we do, gum stays on the list and off the first customer part. Keep the breaks.
