# Wood will crumple. It will not stretch.

History and shop practice for Bradley Brand Furniture.

You can saw a curve out of a wide plank and call it done. The edges of that curve are short grain. Pretty until someone leans. Steam is how you keep the fiber running around the turn.

The glue in the stick will let go if you get it hot and wet. The Forest Service lab’s dry number for that glue — lignin — is about 170 °C. We do not live there. We live in a box with a kettle: two hundred to two hundred ten Fahrenheit in the chamber, water boiling near 212, and a blank that already has some water in it. Soft, the wood will crumple twenty-five to thirty percent along the grain. It will stretch one or two percent and then it lets go. So you crumple the inside. You do not let the outside grow. The second half of that sentence is a strap.

Glued leaves over a form will make the same silhouette, stronger and tighter, with glue on the edge. That is not this.

We have mostly made rectangles — islands named for the water around here, boards in gum and oak and walnut. A Moro does not need a steamed stretcher to be furniture. It needs to be square in August. A curve still belongs on that floor if the grain goes with it.

## The blank

Most breaks are decided before the kettle is hot.

White oak, red oak, hickory, ash, elm, walnut. Those will do this work. Beech, birch, maple, sweetgum, mahogany too; the lab paid for the longer list. We already know several of them as case stock and boards. Sweetgum can wait. We have sold it as a bread board, which is not the same as trusting it on a customer radius. Sycamore we used for carving boards. We are not going to advertise it as a bender. Southern yellow pine will take a mild boat curve. Sound ash still bends. Look at it. The borer is in the country.

Grain no steeper than about one in fifteen. No pith, no shake, no rot, no knot in the bend. Rive if you can. The fiber is already telling the truth. If you saw, stand the stick on edge in good light and read it like a receipt you do not trust. Throw away more than you want to. The scrap is cheaper before it is steamed.

One person can pull about an inch and a half. After that, another body or a block and tackle. We will not hero a two-inch oak bow.

Meter. For a curve over a form we want 15–18 percent. The lab’s wider furniture window is 12–20; they like 15–20 when the form is serious. A lot of chairmakers air-dry to 12–16. Down here, 18 is not an insult. Green is tempting. It is also how you crush the inside of a hard bend — water in the cells with nowhere to go — and then watch the curve walk for a month. Kiln-dried is the other ditch. Soak it if that is what you have, and budget scrap. Do not start a paid part on the first kiln stick.

Write species, thickness, and the reading on the end grain. When it comes out of the box you will want to know what you put in.

Say the part is a bench stretcher, white oak, one inch thick, a fair curve a person would see on the long side. The blank is longer than the finished piece. The grain runs. The meter says 16. That stick is allowed in the box. A pretty stick with a dive at the edge is not.

## The old work

Boat shops pulled ribs before anyone numbered a chair. You steam or boil, you pull over a mold, you fasten it before it argues. The lab still lists the softwoods that will take a moderate curve that way. Moderate is the word they used. A boat plank is not a café back. The physics is the same.

Windsor makers in the 1700s were already steaming oak, ash, or hickory into a bow around a plank seat. The legs are turned. The seat is saddled. Only the bow did this work. People say steam-bent chair and wave at the whole thing. The lesson we need is the blank: fiber from end to end.

Michael Thonet was a cabinetmaker from Boppard, born 1796, tired of carving a curve out of a block and wasting the rest. His first answer was not steam on a solid rod. It was stacks of veneer, boiled in glue — laminated furniture with extra heat. It got him a medal at the Crystal Palace in 1851. The jury also called the chairs curious. Then bankruptcy, Vienna, Gebrüder Thonet in 1853. Then the useful invention: solid wood, steam or a boiling liquor, and a metal band on the face that would otherwise tear. The V&A dates that breakthrough to about 1855. The Austrian privilege is 1856. In 1857 they put a factory in the Moravian beech at Koritschan — Koryčany now — ten thousand chairs that year, more than 1.8 million pieces a year by 1913. No. 14, 1859, six sticks and cane, packed in a crate and screwed together at the other end. More than fifty million of that model by 1930.

He did not discover that wet hot wood moves. He made a factory that could do the same back on a Tuesday, and a strap that moved the stretch into the steel. The strap is the craft. The crate is the business. A curve that cannot ship is a hobby.

They still bend solid beech in Frankenberg, in a pressurized boiler, for hours. That number does not travel to a plywood box on sawhorses. If you copy their cycle into our chamber you will cook the inside of the stick and call it tradition.

Aalto’s birch stools and the Eames shells are veneers and glue and a mold. Different job. Both honest if you name them. A caption that says steam-bent on a plywood seat is wrong.

Warren is not Vienna. Samuel Holmes Fullerton stood up the Bradley Lumber Company here in September 1901. By 1907 it was on the order of a hundred thousand board feet a day and about three hundred fifty men, and a postcard from the plant said they sawed Arkansas soft pine, mild-textured oak, and other native hardwoods, and that they made flooring, furniture stock, pine millwork, and construction lumber. Stock. Other people’s factories bought the rectangles. The courthouse on the square is 1903, which is probably why a company page likes that year. This LLC is 2008. Pennington Street in Warren, then Rock Springs Road in Wilmar. Islands named Moro, Caney Creek, Canyon Creek, Little Red River, Shiloh, Honeysuckle. Boards in gum, walnut and ash, white oak, sycamore. That was the job, and it was honest.

If we take up a curve, we take it up as a mill-town shop that already knows oak and gum, not as a design museum, and not as a rebrand of 1901.

## The box

A steam box is a wet oven that must not become a bomb.

Long enough for the longest blank. Sloped so condensate runs to a drain and not onto the work. Racks or dowels so the sticks are in the weather, not sitting in a puddle. Steam in low. A vent at the far end, and do not cap it. A hole for a thermometer you can actually read. Plywood is fine. A liner is nice. Pressure is not. Atmospheric steam. If someone wants to weld a vessel and hang a gauge on it, they can go work for a factory that already has the insurance.

The kettle can be a wallpaper steamer or a pot on a burner you do not forget. Keep the hose where you will not trip and the burner out from under the lid. The water in that pot will scald you as dead as the vapor.

Two hundred to two hundred ten in the chamber. If the thermometer says 180, you are warming lunch.

Gloves. Face to the side of the door. The cloud is not always visible until it hits cooler air, which may be your glasses. Open, take the piece, close. The box is not a conversation pit.

The rhyme is one hour per inch. Learn it, then do not worship it.

House, for that one-inch oak at sixteen percent: one hour. Pull. If it fights, back in for twenty minutes. Not three hours.

The lab’s finer mouth, if you want it: about thirty-eight minutes per inch if the stick is already at twenty to twenty-five percent moisture; about seventy-five minutes per inch if it is drier and you are putting water back. A third hour on a one-inch part is how the inside wrinkles instead of slips.

A one-inch part keeps about ten minutes of useful heat after the door. A two-and-a-half-inch part, about twenty. Do not load more sticks than you can bend inside that weather. The last stick in a crowded box is how you oversteam without meaning to. Write the time-in on the end grain.

If a part cools and fights you, put it back. That is allowed. Macho is not a process.

## The pull

Have the form, the strap, the clamps, and the extra body in the room before you open the door.

Steel on the convex face. End stops so the blank cannot get longer. The stretch lives in the steel. Snug the adjustable stop until the strap and the stick take a slight arc, then stop. Too loose, the outside tears. Too tight, the inside wrinkles. If the inner face and the outer face would have to differ by more than about three percent in length, you need this. The lab’s threshold. Not a nicety.

The form can be stacked particleboard or plywood. Fair. A lump in the form is a lump in the work. Cut past the finished curve. The piece will try to open. We do not have a universal springback percent in this shop yet. Overbend a little. Write on the form what that species and thickness did. The next blank is cheaper if the form tells the truth.

Anchor one end of the strap. Pull. If a kink starts, stop the lever. Clamp the strap and the work to the form just behind the kink and let the clamp do that inch. Then go on. Muscling through a kink is how you write a failure in a wood that would have forgiven you.

Clamp it as if you mean it. The whole face, not three heroic points. The stick is still plastic. A clamp pad can leave a dent you will sand for an hour.

If you are running duplicates, move the piece to a drying rack that holds the same curve so the form is free. One hour after the pull, the strap comes off. Tannin and steel make ink. A card scraper will get a blush. It will not get a night’s sleep.

Oak and the other ring-porous woods do not get a fan in the face on day one. Cover them. Tomorrow they can breathe. Walnut and the rest can take air sooner.

## The wait

The pull is the loud part. The wait is the part that keeps it.

The glue has to set in the new shape, and the water has to leave until the piece is as dry as the room it will live in. Seven or eight percent is a northern furniture number. A kitchen in Warren in August may not be that. Meter the room. Meter the piece. A week is a guess. The meter is the boss.

While it is wet and restrained, the curve is a rumor. Take the stays off early and you will watch it open. Leave it until service moisture and it will hold — not forever, not against a stew pot, but hold.

Moisture after the fact still has an opinion. More water later and the piece loses some curve; the wrinkled inside face swells along its length. Less water and the curve can tighten. Wet and dry, wet and dry, and the piece slowly remembers the log. That is why a bent rail over a simmering pot is a bad design, and why you do not finish in a bone-dry room and ship to a house that is never cooled in July without thinking about the move.

A bent piece is a little weaker than the same piece left straight. The lab says so, and says it is seldom enough to matter. Geometry still matters. Design the section for the lean, then bend it. A long unsupported 5/8-inch bar is not “seldom.”

Cut the joinery after the stick has sat in the finish room. A piece that is still arguing will open a shoulder you already cut square.

## The day

Meter the oak. Sixteen percent. Write it on the end. Fire the kettle. Wait until the box is two hundred. In at 9:10. Out at 10:10. Strap, slight arc, onto the form. Kink at the far horn — clamp, then finish. Stays on. Strap off at 11:10. Blanket on the oak. Tomorrow the blanket comes off. Do not cut the tenon this week.

## When it fails

Write the failure on the form. The next blank is a cheaper teacher if the last one left a note.

Broke on the outside, long splinters: the outside was asked to stretch. No strap, or a strap that was theater. Or the grain ran out. Or the stick was kiln-dried and proud. Or the radius was a wish.

Broke on the outside, short and brash: bad wood. Pith, brittle, a knot you talked yourself into.

Wrinkles on the inside, like a wet rope: oversteamed, or the strap was cranked down like a vise, or the stick was too green and the water had nowhere to go.

Checks, especially on oak: you dried the skin too fast. Fan on a wet ring-porous face.

Blue-black: steel and tannin. The strap stayed the night.

It came off the form and opened: it was still wet, or you did not overbend, or the room is wetter than the room you dried it in.

It came off the form and tightened: it kept drying. You targeted a number the house will never see.

It slowly straightened over months: wet, dry, wet, dry. The wrinkled face keeps arguing. A finish helps. A steam cycle over a dishwasher vent does not.

It would not start around the form: cold, understeamed, or you stood in the lane talking with a three-quarter-inch stick in your hand. Put it back in the box.

It started, then a kink, then a break: you kept pulling. Clamp behind the kink next time.

Three in a row: stop changing three variables. Change the blank or the time or the radius. Not all three.

We have not bent twenty gum bows in this shop and kept the tally. Until we do, gum stays on the lab list and off the first customer part. Keep the breaks.
