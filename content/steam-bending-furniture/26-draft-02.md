# Wood will crumple. It will not stretch.

Steam-bent furniture, the old way, for a hardwood shop in Bradley County.

Wood will crumple. It will not stretch. That is the whole craft, and it is older than any furniture brand, including this one.

Wood is a bundle of tubes glued together. Dry, the glue — lignin — softens around 170 °C. That is a Forest Service number, and it is not a shop setting. Water drops the temperature where the glue lets go. A kettle, a box, and a wet blank get you to 200–212 °F. Enough for the hardwoods that want to move.

Once the glue is soft, those tubes will take twenty-five to thirty percent compression along their length and only one or two percent stretch. You are not stretching a bow. You are crumpling the inside and keeping the outside at zero. If you ask the outside to grow, you are breaking.

A curve sawn out of a wide plank is a different object. The grain on the edges is short. A steam-bent piece keeps the fiber running around the turn. That is the reason for the kettle. Continuous grain.

You can get the same silhouette by gluing thin leaves over a form. Stronger. Tighter radius. Glue lines on the edge. Different job. We will not call it steam bending.

We have mostly made rectangles. Kitchen islands with creek names. Boards in gum and oak and walnut. That was an honest job. A Moro island does not need a steamed stretcher to be real. It needs to be square in August. The next honest shape is a curve that still belongs on a kitchen floor.

## The blank

Most breaks are decided before the kettle is hot.

The Forest Service list of woods that will do this work includes white oak, red oak, elm, hickory, ash, beech, birch, maple, walnut, sweetgum, and mahogany. Michael Fortune, writing in *Fine Woodworking*, tells a person still learning to stay in ash, oak, hickory, elm, and walnut. Our building already knows several of those as case stock and boards. Hickory we know from the planer. Sweetgum is on the lab list; it is not the first customer bow. Sycamore we used for carving boards. We will not advertise it as a bender. Southern yellow pine will take a mild boat curve. Not a hoop. Sound ash still bends. The borer is in the country — look at the stick.

Grain slope no steeper than about one in fifteen. No pith, no shake, no rot, no knot in the bend. Rive if you can. If you saw, read the edge like a receipt you do not trust. One person can pull about an inch and a half. After that, get help.

Meter. The lab’s window for furniture is 12–20 percent moisture before steam; for a real curve over a form, 15–20. Fortune works at 12–16, air-dried. South Arkansas will hand you 18 and call it dry. For this job, 18 is not an insult. Green crushes the inside of a hard bend and then walks for a month. Kiln-dried is the other ditch — soak it if that is what you have, and expect more scrap.

Write species, thickness, and the meter reading on the end grain.

## The old work

A shipwright did not need a catalog number to pull a rib. Steam or boil, pull over a mold, fasten it before it argues. Windsor shops in the 1700s were already steaming oak, ash, or hickory into a bow around a plank seat. People say steam-bent chair and wave at the whole thing. Only the hoop did that work. The lesson is the blank: fiber from end to end. Thonet industrialized a hoop. He did not invent the hoop.

Michael Thonet was a cabinetmaker from Boppard, born 1796. In the 1830s he tried stacked veneer boiled in glue — laminated furniture with extra heat. Crystal Palace, 1851: the jury said curious and medaled him anyway. Gebrüder Thonet, Vienna, 1853. Then the useful invention: solid wood, steam, and a metal band on the face that would otherwise tear. Austrian privilege, 1856. A factory in the Moravian beech at Koritschan in 1857. Ten thousand chairs that year; a little over 1.8 million pieces a year by 1913. No. 14, 1859, six sticks and cane, packed in a crate. More than fifty million of that model by 1930. Those are V&A numbers.

The strap is the craft. The crate is the business. They still bend beech in Frankenberg in a pressurized boiler for hours. That number does not travel to a plywood box.

Aalto’s birch and the Eames shells are veneers and glue. Leave their photographs in their essay.

Warren is not Vienna. Samuel Holmes Fullerton stood up the Bradley Lumber Company here in September 1901. By 1907 it was on the order of a hundred thousand board feet a day and about three hundred fifty men, and the postcard said the quiet part: they made furniture *stock*. Other people’s factories bought the rectangles. The courthouse on the square is 1903. This LLC is 2008. Pennington Street, then Rock Springs Road in Wilmar. If we take up a curve, we take it up as a mill-town shop that already knows oak and gum, not as a design museum.

## The box

A steam box is a wet oven that must not become a bomb. Long chamber, sloped to a drain, racks so the sticks are not in a puddle, steam in low, vent at the far end, a thermometer you can read. Plywood is fine. Pressure is not. 200–210 °F. If the thermometer says 180, you are warming lunch.

The pot will scald you as dead as the vapor. Gloves. Stand to the side of the door. The cloud is not always visible until it hits cooler air.

The rhyme is one hour per inch. The lab says about 38 minutes per inch if the stick is already at 20–25 percent moisture, about 75 if you are putting water back. Fortune says one to two hours at 200–210 °F, and a third hour on a one-inch part wrinkles the inside.

**House rule, 1-inch air-dried oak at 15–18 percent:** one hour. Try the pull. If it fights, back in for twenty minutes. Not three hours.

Do not load more sticks than you can bend while they are still hot. A one-inch part has about ten minutes of useful heat after the door. Write the time-in on the end.

## The pull

Strap on the convex face. End stops. Snug until the strap and the stick take a slight arc, then stop. Too loose, the outside tears. Too tight, the inside wrinkles.

The form is the curve you want plus a little, because the piece will open. We do not have a universal springback percent. Write on the form what that species and thickness did.

If a kink starts, clamp behind it. Do not muscle through. One hour after the pull, the strap comes off. Tannin and steel make ink.

## The wait

Dry in the curve until the piece is as dry as the room it will live in. Fortune likes 7–8 percent. A kitchen here in August may not be that. Meter. A week is a guess. Oak under a blanket the first day, then air. More moisture later opens the curve. Less tightens it. Wet and dry, wet and dry, and it slowly remembers the log. Do not hang it over a stew pot and act surprised.

A bent piece is a little weaker than the same piece left straight. The lab says the loss is seldom enough to matter. Geometry still matters. Design the section for the lean, then bend it.

## When it fails

Outside break: no strap, runout, kiln-proud, a radius you hoped for. Inside wrinkles: oversteamed, strap too tight, green crush. Checks: you fanned wet oak. Blue-black: the strap stayed. Opens: you took it off wet. Tightens: it kept drying past the house. Will not start: it is cold, or you talked too long in the lane. Three in a row: change one variable.

## Close

We have not bent twenty gum bows and kept the tally. Until we do, gum stays on the lab list and off the first customer part. We have the woods, the climate, and the refusal to fake a number. The next twenty sticks are the rest of this article. Keep the breaks.
