# Research — history

Notes, not the essay. Dates we will stand on. Arguments we will not paper over.

## Before furniture had a brand

Heat and water make lignin move. People who needed a rib, a rim, or a bow figured that out without a patent office.

- **Boat shops.** Frames and ribs steamed or boiled and pulled over a mold. That is the oldest industrial cousin of what we want. The Forest Service still lists Douglas-fir, southern yellow pine, white-cedar, and redwood as woods that take a *moderate* curve after a steam or a soak — boat planking, not a café back.
- **Native work in this continent.** Snowshoe frames, canoe ribs, bows. Split along the grain, then heat. The lesson that matters in a furniture shop: riven stock follows the fiber. Sawn stock lies about the fiber.
- **Windsor chairs, England then America, 1700s–1800s.** The bow back and the arm bow are steam-bent, usually oak, ash, or hickory. The seat is a plank. The legs are turned and wedged. A Windsor is a mixed technology. Do not call the whole chair “steam-bent.”
- **Cooperage and agricultural stuff.** Barrel hoops, yoke pieces, sleigh runners. Same physics, less catalog.

We do not need a museum tour of every culture that ever boiled a stick. We need the reader to feel that Thonet walked into a river that was already moving.

## Michael Thonet, the part that is true

- Born 1796, Boppard am Rhein. Trained cabinetmaker.
- 1830s: tired of carving curves. First experiments are **stacked veneers boiled in glue**, not solid steam-bent beech. V&A is clear on this. Thonet’s own later telling sometimes compresses it. We will not.
- Bankruptcy. Moves to Vienna with wife and five sons. Near fifty.
- 1851: Great Exhibition, Crystal Palace. Jury calls the chairs “curious” and still medals them.
- 1853: Gebrüder Thonet.
- Mid-1850s: solid wood, steam or boiling liquor, **metal band on the tension face**. Austrian privilege to the brothers in 1856 for furniture of bent wood. V&A dates the solid-wood-plus-strap breakthrough to 1855. Thonet’s modern site still describes the same sequence: turn the blank, boil it, strap it, two men into the mold.
- 1857: factory at Koritschan / Koryčany in the Moravian beech. Rail. Labor. A forest that matches the process. Production 10,000 chairs that year.
- 1859: Konsumstuhl Nr. 14 — six pieces of wood, screws, cane. Ships knocked down. The IKEA joke is tired; the logistics are the point.
- By 1912–1913: on the order of 1.8 million pieces a year. By 1930: more than 50 million No. 14 chairs. Those numbers are V&A’s. Use them. Do not round them into “millions and millions” like a brochure.

What he actually changed:

1. Solid section instead of glued veneer packets (after he had already done the packets).
2. A strap that takes the stretch.
3. A factory that divides the work — men on steam and bend, women on sand and cane — which we will mention as history, not as a staffing plan.
4. A numbered catalog and a crate of parts.

What he did not change: the fact that wood compresses better than it stretches.

## After Thonet, the fork in the road

- **Bentwood factories** keep doing atmospheric or low-pressure steam on solid beech, oak, etc. Thonet still does it in Frankenberg. Their public numbers: steam over 100 °C, on the order of six hours in a *pressurized* boiler for their beech rods. That is a factory with a vessel. It is not our box. Note the difference so nobody copies a six-hour pressurized cycle into a plywood coffin.
- **Laminated modernism.** Aalto, Breuer’s plywood, Eames. Glue lines. Crossed grain. Compound curves. Do not steal their photographs for a steam essay.
- **Green-wood revival, late 20th century.** John Alexander, Drew Langsner, then the Windsor people still working: Curtis Buchanan, Peter Galbert, Elia Bizzarri. Their religion is riven grain and a kettle. Useful. Not the only church.
- **Studio furniture.** Michael Fortune’s Fine Woodworking piece (issue 238, 2014) is the clearest shop write-up in English that is not a government bulletin. We will use it. We will not sound like we are him.

## Arkansas, the part that is ours

Warren, 1901–1902: three big mills. Bradley Lumber (Fullerton), Southern Lumber (Weyerhaeuser/Denkman money in the background), Arkansas Lumber (Rittenhouse and Embree). By 1907 Bradley is 100,000 board feet a day, about 350 men, and one of the larger hardwood dealers going. Potlatch buys Bradley and Southern in the 1950s. The plant on that land has had other names since. Rand Wood tells the pine side of the story now.

BBF sits in that weather. We did not steam café chairs for the Ringstrasse. We cut and assembled hardwood for kitchens and for people who wanted a board that would not move on them in a week. If we take up a curve, we take it up as a mill-town shop that already knows oak and gum, not as a design museum.
