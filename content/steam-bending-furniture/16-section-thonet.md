# Section try — Thonet, without the gift shop

Michael Thonet was a cabinetmaker from Boppard, born 1796. In the 1830s he got tired of carving a curve out of a block and wasting the rest. His first answer was not steam on a solid rod. It was stacks of veneer, boiled in glue, pressed to a shape. That is laminated furniture with extra heat. It worked well enough to show in London in 1851. The Crystal Palace jury called the chairs curious and gave them a medal anyway.

Then he went broke, moved the family to Vienna, and took almost another decade to get Gebrüder Thonet on its feet (1853). The useful invention comes in the middle of that decade: solid wood, steam or a boiling liquor, and a metal band along the face that would otherwise tear. The Austrian state gave the brothers a privilege in 1856. The V&A dates the solid-and-strap breakthrough to 1855. Close enough. The point is the band.

In 1857 they put a factory in the Moravian beech at Koritschan — Koryčany now — with rail and a forest that matched the process. Ten thousand chairs that year. By 1913, a little over one million eight hundred thousand pieces a year. The famous one is No. 14, 1859, six sticks and some cane, packed in a crate and screwed together at the other end. By 1930 the company had sold more than fifty million of that model. Those are museum numbers, not ours.

What he changed is easy to overstate and easy to undersell. He did not discover that wet hot wood moves. Boat shops and Windsor shops were already in that river. He made a factory that could do the same back on a Tuesday, ship it in a box, and sell it from a numbered catalog in more than one language. The strap is the craft. The crate is the business.

They still bend solid beech in Frankenberg. Their own pages talk about a pressurized boiler, wood over 100 °C, on the order of six hours, then two or four people on a back. That is a vessel and a crew. It is not a plywood box on a pair of sawhorses. If you copy their hours into our box you will cook the inside of the stick and call it tradition.

One more thing, because captions lie. Aalto’s birch stools and the Eames shells are not this. Those are veneers and glue and a mold. Different religion. Both honest if you name them.
