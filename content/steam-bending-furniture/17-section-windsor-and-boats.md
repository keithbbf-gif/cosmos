# Section try — the shops that did not wait for Vienna

A shipwright did not need a catalog number to pull a rib. You steam or boil the stock, you pull it over a mold, you fasten it before it argues. The Forest Service still lists the softwoods that will take a *moderate* curve that way: Douglas-fir, southern yellow pine, the white-cedars, redwood. Moderate is the word they used. A boat plank is not a café back. The physics is the same. The radius is kinder.

Windsor chairs are the furniture version that was already common in England and then here in the 1700s and 1800s. The seat is a plank, often elm or pine or poplar, saddled. The legs are turned and wedged. The part that belongs in this folder is the bow — oak, ash, or hickory, steamed and pulled into a hoop or an arm. People say “steam-bent chair” and wave at the whole thing. Only the hoop did that work.

The Windsor lesson that matters more than the silhouette is how the blank is born. The good ones are split out of the log, not sawn across a growing twist. The fiber runs from end to end. A saw can fake that if you read the edge and throw away the liars. A saw can also give you a stick that looks straight and dies at the first hard pull.

Green-wood people still work in that church. They will tell you a wet stick bends tighter and that the box is a dryer as much as a cooker. They are not wrong in their shop. They are not the only shop.

We are not going to write a Windsor manual. We are going to steal the only two things we need: rive or read grain like it matters, and remember that a hoop is an old idea. Thonet industrialized a hoop and a back and a crate. He did not invent the hoop.
