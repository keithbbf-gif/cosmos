# Cuttings that actually take

Figs want to be cuttings. That is
the kindness of the plant. People
still rot them by the dozen
because they treat a stick like a
seed.

I take wood in the dormant season
— pencil-thick, last year's
growth, 8 to 10 inches, a couple
of nodes. I avoid the very tip
if it is skinny and the very
base if it is old and reluctant.
A straight piece with two or
three nodes is plenty.

The cut goes into something
draining and barely moist.
Perlite, a sharp mix, coarse
bark — not garden soil, not a
soggy peat puck in a closed
box you never open. I bury at
least one node. I do not need
a heat mat in this band in
April. I need patience and no
hero watering.

Outdoors in spring, a pot of
cuttings on the east side of
the house is a good life.
Indirect light, no noon slab.
Indoors, a plastic bag can
help and can also grow mold
if you sealed a wet stick.
If you bag, vent.

Roots before leaves is the
dream. Leaves before roots is
the usual. A cutting that
leafs is not a plant yet. It
is a stick spending its
savings. Do not pot it up,
do not fertilize, do not put
it in full sun because you
are excited. Wait until you
feel resistance when you tug,
or until roots show at a
drain.

Hormone is optional. Figs
root without it. I use it
when the wood is not ideal.
I do not use it as a
personality.

Summer cuttings of green
wood work if you treat them
like softer things: shade,
humidity, faster rot if you
err wet. I prefer dormant
wood because I am lazy and
it matches the prune.

Label. You will not remember
which brown stick was the
good Celeste. Masking tape
fails in the rain. A plastic
tag fails in the sun. Write
twice.

Legal and neighborly: take
cuttings from a plant you
have permission to touch.
Do not strip a botanical
garden. Do not assume a
named variety in a yard is
yours to distribute as that
name. The plant may be
mixed. Grow it, fruit it,
then decide what it is.

I start more cuttings than
I need and I give the extras
away after they have roots,
not after they have a name
I am still guessing. A
cutting that dies is a
stick. A cutting I hoarded
until it circled a cup is
a delay.

A rooted cutting is a
first-year plant. Read that
draft. Do not skip to the
25-gallon because the
cutting "looks strong." It
looks strong. It is not
strong. The second pot is
earned by roots you can
see, not by leaves you can
photograph.
