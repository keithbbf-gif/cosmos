# How big the pot actually needs to be

A fig will live in a 5-gallon bucket. It will also sulk, dry out before
lunch in July, and give you three figs you photograph more than you eat.
Survival is not the same as culture. The question is not "will it stay
green." The question is "what volume lets this plant carry a real crop
without becoming a daily emergency."

I treat 15 gallons as the floor for a fruiting plant you intend to keep.
That is a nursery can or a stout fabric bag, not a decorative urn with a
three-inch soil pocket. Fifteen gallons holds enough mix to buffer a missed
watering on a 85°F day. It does not buffer a missed watering on a 98°F
patio in Zone 9. There the floor is 20, and 25 is more honest.

Twenty to twenty-five gallons is the working size for most varieties in
this series. Brown Turkey, Celeste, an LSU Purple, Chicago Hardy — they
will fruit well in that range if the mix drains and the sun is real. You
can step a young plant through 3, then 7, then 15. Skipping straight from
a quart cutting pot into a 25-gallon vat is how you get a wet core and a
plant that sits there looking offended for a year. Roots do not sprint
into a cold lake of unused mix. They rot at the edge of it.

Forty-five gallons and half barrels are for people who have decided the
plant is furniture. They work. They also weigh what a small appliance
weighs once wet, and in Zone 7 you still have to move that weight before
the first hard freeze. If you cannot move it, you are not in pot culture
anymore. You are in above-ground planting with extra drought. Buy the
dolly first, or do not buy the barrel.

Dwarfs change the math, not the physics. Little Miss Figgy and Fignomenal
are bred to stay short. They still have leaves the size of a hand, and
those leaves still transpire. A true dwarf is happy long-term in 10 to 15
gallons. Petite Negra is similar. Do not punish them with a 3-gallon
because the tag said "patio." The tag was written by someone who does not
water your deck in August.

Depth matters as much as gallons. Figs are not carrots, but they want a
root run that is not a pancake. A wide shallow bowl looks pretty and cooks
on top. Aim for at least 14 inches of usable depth, 16 to 18 if you can.
Fabric bags slump; overbuy the gallon rating so the slump still leaves
you depth.

One plant per pot. I have seen two cuttings dropped into a 20-gallon "to
see which one wins." They both win at making a knot you will hate in three
years. If you want a hedge of figs, that is a row of pots, not a roommate
situation.

How do you know the pot is too small? The plant wilts before 11 a.m. on a
day that is not brutal. Water runs straight through and the mix is more
root than soil. Fruit sets and then yellows in a week. New growth is
short, pale, and nervous. How do you know it is too big? The mix stays
cold and sour in spring. The plant grows leaves and no fruit. You smell
the pot when you water. Step down, or wait a season and stop feeding.

Zone 7 people: remember that every extra gallon is extra mass you must
shelter. A 15-gallon you can lift with a grunt is better than a 45-gallon
you will leave out "just this once." Zone 9 people: extra gallons are
cheap insurance against heat. Buy the volume. The water bill is already
lost.

I keep a cheap kitchen scale off the books for this. Weigh a pot after a
full water and again when the plant first droops. That delta is your
reservoir. Once you have felt it, you stop asking the internet how often
to water a 20-gallon fig. The pot told you.
