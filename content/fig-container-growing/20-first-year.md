# Year one without the grocery-store fantasy

A first-year fig in a pot is a root project
that happens to have leaves. If it gives you
fruit, that is a courtesy. If you force it
to give you fruit, you get a cute photo and
a smaller plant next May.

Start with the plant you actually have. A
cutting that rooted this spring is not a
three-year nursery specimen. A nursery
specimen in a 3-gallon is often a cutting
that rooted last year and got a lot of
food. Both can become a good pot fig. Both
will stall if you jump them into a vat of
wet mix or stand them in a windy full-sun
corner the week they come home.

I pot up one size at a time. Quart to 2–3
gallon, then 7, then 15. The plant should
root the container before it gets the next
one. You can check by the way it dries:
when a 3-gallon starts behaving like a
thirsty 3-gallon, it is ready to move, not
the day you feel ambitious.

Sun: build it. A plant from a greenhouse
will bleach and spot if you drop it onto
a Zone 9 slab at noon. A week of morning
sun, then more. This is boring and it
prevents the "my new fig is dying" week.

Fruit: pick off the first-year figs on a
cutting or a small plant if the plant is
still trying to make a frame. I know. You
waited. The fruit is the size of a marble
and you have named it. Take it off. The
plant will make more later, on wood that
can carry it. A larger 3-gallon nursery
plant can ripen a few. Let it, then stop
bragging and start watering.

Food: light. The new mix has a charge.
Water: even, and do not drown a plant
that is not drinking much yet. A first-year
fig in a too-big pot is the classic drown.

Winter of year one is when small plants
die because they are easy to forget and
easy to freeze. A 3-gallon is not a
20-gallon. It goes cold fast. Bring it
in with the others. Write it on the same
note.

What success looks like in November: a
plant with a few sturdy canes, leaves
that were dark most of the summer, roots
that hold the mix when you slide it from
the can, and you still like the variety.
What success does not look like: a
five-gallon harvest. That is year two or
three, if you kept the wood.

If the plant sulked all summer, do not
replace it in October in a rage. Look at
the roots. If they are white and there
is a lot of unused mix, it was the pot
size or the wet. If they are brown and
few, it was drain, heat, or a bad
start. Fix that. The variety may be
fine.

Year one is how you decide whether you
will still be rolling this pot in year
five. Keep the work small and the plant
alive. The grocery store will still be
there. Your fig, if you do this part,
will not be.
