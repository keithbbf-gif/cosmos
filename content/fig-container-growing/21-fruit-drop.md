# Why they throw the crop

A fig sets a lot of fruit and then decides
which ones it can afford. In a pot, that
decision is louder. You watch a dozen
small figs yellow, get soft at the neck,
and hit the patio. It feels like a
betrayal. It is usually arithmetic.

Water is the first number. Dry, then wet,
then dry — the plant aborts. A single
hot afternoon in a 10-gallon can do it.
So can a week of standing water and
suffocated roots. The drop looks the
same. The pot weight does not. Check
that before you invent a pest.

Sun is the second. A fig that looked
fine on an east porch in May may not
have the energy to finish a crop there
in the short light of a Zone 7
September. Fruit drops, or sits green.
Move the pot or accept a smaller load.

Nitrogen is the third. A leaf factory
will drop fruit because it is busy
being a leaf factory. If the plant is
huge and soft and the figs leave, stop
feeding and restore the sun.

Transplant drop is ordinary. You
repotted in May, it sulked, the
embryonic figs left. That is the plant
paying for new roots. Do your
upsizing when there is no crop, or
accept the tax.

Young plants drop because they are
young. I said this in the first-year
draft and I am saying it again because
it is the email I would get. A
one-year plant throwing pea-sized
figs is not sick. It is ambitious.

Variety and pollination: common figs
do not need a wasp. If you somehow
bought a Smyrna type, the main crop
will set and fail without caprification.
This is rare at a big-box, not
impossible from a fancy list. If
everything else is right and every
fig drops at marble size every year,
look up what you actually own.

Pests are lower on my list than
people want. A heavy rust year can
weaken a plant into a drop. A scale
outbreak can. Birds take ripe fruit;
they do not yellow the small ones.
Look at the leaves and the wood
before you spray.

What I do when the drop starts: weigh
the pot, look at the newest leaves,
think about the last ten days of
weather, and then change one thing.
One. Flooding, shading, feeding, and
moving in the same afternoon is how
you get a new problem and the same
drop.

A worked example: 15-gallon fabric bag,
Zone 8, first heat week. Plant was
watered every other day in May. June
arrives at 95°F. Fruit yellows on a
Thursday. The pot is light. That is
not rust and it is not a wasp. It is
a tank that shrank while the leaves
got larger. Step up the water or the
gallons. Do not spray.

A little drop is thinning. A total
drop is a message. Learn to tell
them apart or you will treat a
healthy plant like a patient all
summer.
