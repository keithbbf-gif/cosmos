# A soil mix that drinks and lets go

Garden soil in a pot is how you make a brick. It looks responsible —
"real dirt" — and then it slumps, seals, and stays wet in the middle while
the top cakes. Figs forgive a lot. They do not forgive a pot that cannot
breathe in January.

I want a mix that takes a drink and then lets the extra leave. The plant
should not sit in a saucer of leftover rain, and it should not go
hydrophobic two days after you watered. Those two failures are different
and people treat them as one "watering problem."

A working recipe, by volume, for Zones 7–9:

- Half of a good soilless potting mix. Not "moisture control." Not the
  bag that boasts it stays wet for a week. Read the front. If it
  promises you can forget the plant, put it back.
- A quarter to a third pine bark fines or small fir bark. This is the
  structure. It keeps the mix from collapsing into peat pudding.
- The rest perlite or pumice. Pumice does not float away after the
  third storm. Perlite is cheaper and fine if you accept the white
  specks on the porch.

If you are in a wet summer — Piedmont, Gulf, Mid-South — lean bark and
pumice. If you are in a dry Zone 9 interior, you can keep a little more
of the base mix so the pot does not become a sieve you stand over with a
hose. I still would not go heavier than about 60 percent peat-or-coir
base. Coir is less sour than old peat and wets again more kindly after
it dries. Either works if the rest of the mix is air.

pH: slightly acid to neutral. Figs are not blueberries. They are also
not cement plants. If your tap water is liquid limestone, you will see
it in a year as a white crust and yellowing new leaves. That is a later
essay. For the mix itself, do not add lime "to sweeten" unless you have
a reason you can say out loud.

What I do not add: fresh manure, straight compost as the whole pot, sand
from the creek, and those water-holding crystals. Compost is food and
fines. A scoop in the mix is a kindness. A pot of compost is a swamp
that then becomes a brick. Sand sounds like drainage and behaves like
grout once it packs with peat.

Fill the pot the boring way. Screen over the drain holes — mesh or a
shard, not a fist of gravel. The gravel layer does not make drainage. It
raises a perched water table and steals depth. Wet the mix in a tub
before it goes in, or you will water the pot for twenty minutes and still
have dry pockets. Set the plant at the same depth it was. Figs will
throw roots from buried wood, but a plant buried to the first fork in a
pot is a plant you will have trouble watering evenly.

The mix settles. Leave an inch or so of headspace, then check in a
month. Top with more mix, not with a pretty mulch that sheds water. A
thin layer of bark fines on top is fine. Landscape pebbles look tidy and
bake the crown in Zone 9.

Reuse last year's mix only if it still has structure and does not smell
like a ditch. I dump the old stuff into the vegetable beds and start
clean when I repot. Nematodes, salt, and collapsed peat are not a
loyalty program.

You will know the mix is right the first hot week. Water, wait ten
minutes, lift or tip the pot. It should be heavy but not sloshing. Stick
a finger to the second knuckle. Cool, not muddy. If you pull out a
chocolate paste, you built a swamp. If you pull out dust, you built a
sieve or you waited too long. Adjust the mix next time. Do not invent a
fertilizer to fix a texture problem. Fertilizer is not drainage.
