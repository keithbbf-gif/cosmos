**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 2005 *Professional Psychology: Research and Practice* printed Sue C. Bratton, Dee Ray, Tammy Rhine, and Leslie Jones, "The Efficacy of Play Therapy with Children: A Meta-Analytic Review of Treatment Outcomes." The placement mattered. This was not only *IJPT* talking to *IJPT*. This was a general professional-psychology journal, volume 36, number 4, pages 376–390.

The paper treated play therapy as a countable family. It reported an overall effect and split humanistic-leaning from nonhumanistic-leaning approaches in ways later slides flattened. This page will not reprint a number as a promise. It will say: a 2005 review made a graph the field could carry into rooms that wanted graphs.

Sue Bratton and Dee Ray are living people. Public paper only. No gossip, no scraped UNT photographs, no health.

## What a meta-analysis is

It is a method for combining studies that already exist. It inherits their weaknesses. If the pile includes small samples, wait-list controls, and mixed methods under one umbrella word, the combined number is a combined number. It is not a child in Arkansas.

Inclusion rules are the honest part. A reader who wants to argue with 2005 should argue with what was in and what was out, not with a conference slogan that says "research proves play therapy works." The slogan is a theft. The paper is a paper.

Later reviews and critiques exist. This series will not pretend 2005 ended the debate. The evidence-fights essay returns to the temptation to stop reading after the abstract.

## Humanistic vs. not, and the cost of a split

The 2005 authors distinguished approaches. Slides later turned the distinction into a championship. Child-centered trainings liked one bar. Prescriptive trainings liked another sentence. Both bars are historical objects inside one article. They are not a reason to pick a weekend.

Filial studies sat in the pile in ways later filial marketers liked. Cite the paper, not the marketer.

## What not to do with 2005

Do not put the effect size on a service page next to a booking button. Do not write "gold-standard." Do not skip limits. Do not treat four living or recently working authors as a brand you own.

Do not ignore the paper because you prefer 1947 stories. The field asked for a graph. Someone drew one. Drawing is a historical event.

## Why a small-city clinic inherited a 2005 sentence

Grant applications learned to say "meta-analysis." Parents learned to say "research-based." Insurers learned to ask. A practice that never read pages 376–390 still lives in the weather of those pages. Weather is not a protocol. It is why this pack gives the paper its own essay instead of a footnote.
