**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

I am going to waste the first page on what we will not do. If that sounds like a lawyer got to the manuscript first, good. The words *play therapy* have spent a generation sliding off training institutes and onto parenting blogs, and the slide is almost always greased with a child's symptom name and a photograph of a dollhouse.

This folder is a stack of historical essays for WOW Therapies. It does not diagnose, treat, or prevent any condition. It is not medical advice. It is not a silent conversion of a Southeast Arkansas therapy site into a child-mental-health mill.

That is not a humility ritual. It is the only way to talk about these rooms without lying in a soft voice.

Here is the usual lie, in its polite form: a writer tells you that a Viennese analyst, a London pediatrician, a Columbus graduate student, and a Denton professor were all, without knowing it, doing the same thing. Then the writer names a modern distress — a tantrum that sounds like a chart, a silence that sounds like a diagnosis — and lets a sand tray sit next to the condition until your eye does the rest. No one typed the forbidden verb. The sentence still did the work.

I refuse that sentence, including the version that swaps "treats" for "can help with" and hopes a history page will hold.

## What I will keep in its own decade

I will keep **child analysis** in the first half of the twentieth century, where the quarrel was about whether play could stand in for free association. Hermine Hug-Hellmuth published "On the Technique of Child-Analysis" in the *International Journal of Psycho-Analysis* in 1921. Anna Freud's *Einführung in die Technik der Kinderanalyse* is 1927. Melanie Klein's *The Psycho-Analysis of Children* is 1932. Those are dated objects. They are not a license to reprint a play interpretation.

I will keep **Virginia Axline** in 1947. *Play Therapy: The Inner Dynamics of Childhood* applied Carl Rogers's then-new non-directive adult work to a playroom. Houghton Mifflin put a book in the world. That is a publishing fact. It is not eight principles for your Tuesday.

I will keep **filial therapy** in 1964, when Bernard Guerney Jr. described a rationale in the *Journal of Consulting Psychology*. Parents as therapeutic agents is a historical claim about a research clinic. It is not a home-program handout.

I will keep **the Association for Play Therapy** in 1982, when Charles Schaefer enlisted Kevin O'Connor and a first board that included Louise Guerney, Ann Jernberg, and Garry Landreth. A society is a building with dues. It is not a state license.

I will keep **uncertainty** visible. Secondary literature does not agree on who "started" play therapy, because the question is bad. Play is older than a clinic. The clinic is what this series is about.

## What I will not hand you

I will not give you a script. The minute a history essay hands over a tracking phrase, a limit-setting sequence, or a "notice the feeling" exercise, it has changed jobs. Form matters — individual hour, parent-child session, hospital playroom, school counselor's closet — and I will talk about form as history. I will not talk about form as a recipe.

I will not republish a toy list. Landreth's 1991 book *named* a room. Naming that a book did that is history. Reprinting the list so a reader can build the room is a protocol. The difference is the verb.

I will not invent a notebook I have not held. I have not sat in Hug-Hellmuth's Vienna hour. I have not graded the 2005 Bratton meta-analysis case by case. When I mention a workshop economy, I am describing a public workforce — APT conferences, UNT summer institutes — not a count I performed in a hotel ballroom.

I will not do the "balanced" trick where the last paragraph says "see a professional" after the first twelve paragraphs have already sold the method. If a draft cannot live without a symptom payoff, the draft should die.

I will not generate a child's face. There will be no AI playroom, no stock "smiling child in sand," no scraped workshop photograph of a living trainer. Type is enough. See the essay on portraits we will not fake.

## Why bother, then?

Because the documents are real, the rooms are real, and the borrowed word *play* is real, and they are not the same object. A person who wants to understand why a credential, a catalog, and a county clinic now share a vocabulary is entitled to a history that does not recruit their child's chart. A person who loves psychoanalytic history is entitled to hear Klein as Klein, not as a preface to a 1991 Texas textbook. A person who is tired of both wellness copy and sneering debunking is entitled to a third tone: specific, a little dry, unwilling to convert a dollhouse into a press release.

There is a fourth reader I am writing against: the one who thinks a claims fence is cowardice. I do not think it is cowardice to refuse a sentence that would be reckless on a service page and still tempting in an essay. Essays have more room than service pages. That is exactly why they leak.

If you want a clinician, see a clinician who knows you. If you want a named play-therapy service, that belongs on a clinical page with training, supervision, and consent — and WOW Therapies does not, by hosting this history, claim to be that page. If you want the story, stay here. The story has a 1921 paper, a 1947 Houghton Mifflin book, a 1964 journal article, a 1982 society, a 1988 center in Denton, and a 2005 review that did not end the argument.

None of that is a treatment plan. I am repeating myself on purpose. Repetition is how a fence stays up when the next sentence wants a worksheet.
