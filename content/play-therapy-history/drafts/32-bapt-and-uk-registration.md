**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

The British Association of Play Therapists dates its founding to 1992 — the same calendar year APT's journal began, and a decade after APT itself. Coincidence is not affiliation. BAPT is a UK professional body with its own training routes, titles, and later entanglement with health-and-care registration politics. APT is an American membership society with a credential that is not a state license. A website that treats them as chapters of one church is doing a merger.

This essay will stay high. UK title protection, HCPC-adjacent arguments, and the later existence of more than one British play-therapy organization are facts a reader can confirm on the societies' own dated pages. This series will not referee a live registration fight. It will say the fight exists, and that Lowenfeld's 1928 institute is one reason Britain did not need to import 1982 in order to have a play-clinic memory.

## Two 1992s

American 1992: a journal, a binding machine, a society already ten years old.

British 1992: an association for people who wanted *play therapist* to be a job noun in a country that already had child psychotherapists, hospital play staff, and a National Health Service.

The NHS is a patron the United States does not have in the same way. A method that wants to live in a clinic in Manchester has to talk to a different buyer than a method that wants to live in a private office in Fresno. Registration quarrels follow the buyer.

Play Therapy UK (PTUK) and Play Therapy International appear in later public arguments about standards and about who may certify whom. This draft will not adjudicate those arguments. ## Lowenfeld, the Tavistock, and a longer memory

Britain already had the Institute of Child Psychology (1928), analytic child trainings, and hospital play staff. BAPT did not invent British play. It organized a late-century *profession* noun the way APT organized an American one. The nouns are easier to export than the health systems under them.

A student who takes an American RPT path and a student who takes a BAPT-recognized UK training are not in the same file cabinet. Employers sometimes pretend they are. Historians should not.

## What not to do with a British acronym

Do not print "licensed play therapist" as if the UK and Arkansas shared a statute. Do not import a UK title onto a US service page. Do not flatten hospital play staff into BAPT. Do not flatten child and adolescent psychotherapists (a different UK training world) into play therapists.
