**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Clark E. Moustakas (1923–2012) spent a long American career at the Merrill-Palmer Institute and later at the Center for Humanistic Studies in Detroit. In 1953 McGraw-Hill published *Children in Play Therapy*. In 1959 Harper published *Psychotherapy with Children: The Living Relationship*. Play-therapy syllabi that want a name between Axline (1947) and Landreth (1991) often put him in the gap.

He wrote as a humanistic psychologist, not as a British analyst and not as a later APT exam author. The word he leaned on was relationship. The word is easy to steal. The 1953 book is harder.

## A relationship that is not a protocol

Moustakas treated the adult's presence as the method. That sentence sounds like Rogers and like Axline, because it is in that weather. It also sounds like a thousand workshop titles. The historical job is to keep the 1953 and 1959 books as books: mid-century American, clinical-philosophical, written before the Association for Play Therapy (1982) turned "play therapist" into a membership category.

He later became better known, in some psychology departments, for phenomenological research methods than for play. That second fame is why some counseling students have never heard him and some qualitative researchers have never heard Axline. The split is a departmental accident. This series keeps the play books.

## What he added to the American shelf

If Axline gave the field a 1947 stance and a 1964 story, Moustakas gave it a language for the adult's inner life in the room without becoming a Kleinian interpreter of the child's toys. "Living relationship" is a 1959 title, not a 2026 brand. Later writers who say "the relationship is the therapy" are often closer to this shelf than they know.

He is not a substitute for Landreth's later systematizing. He did not build a Denton center. He did not co-found APT. He wrote books a library could buy in the Eisenhower and Kennedy years. That is enough to keep him from being a ghost between two more famous covers.

## What not to do with him

## Why a humanist still sits on a play-therapy reading list

Humanistic psychology and play therapy shared people, campuses, and a suspicion of interpretation-as-power. Moustakas is a document of that share. A later evidence culture (2005 and after) would ask for numbers. His books do not pretend to be those numbers.
