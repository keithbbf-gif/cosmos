People tell me they have “loam.” They have never squeezed it. They have a
feeling about their yard, and the feeling is hopeful. I would rather have a
ribbon and a jar than a feeling.

Wet a handful of the dirt you will actually plant in — not the pretty topsoil
you bought, the dirt at eight inches in the hole. Squeeze. If it makes a
cast and you can press a ribbon between thumb and finger that hangs an inch
or more before it breaks, you are in clay country. If it will not make a
ribbon and it feels gritty and falls apart, you are in sand. Silt is the
smooth flour in the middle that makes a short ribbon and a slick sheen. Most
South Arkansas holes I have stuck a hand in are some argument among those
three. Name the argument. Do not name a catalog.

The jar is the overnight version. Half a jar of dirt, water, a drop of
dish soap, shake, set it down. Sand drops first. Silt next. Clay stays
cloudy the longest. In the morning you can see layers. I will not turn those
layers into a percentage I will guarantee, because I did not weigh them. I
will say the jar keeps you from lying to yourself. If the top third is a
gel that will not clear, stop talking about sandy loam.

Texture is not drainage. A sandy clay can still pond if it sits in a dish.
A silt loam can still bake into a crust. The ribbon tells you how the dirt
will hold a drink. The puddle test in the next stage tells you whether the
drink can leave. Do both. I have watched people skip the ribbon, buy a
“clay-buster,” and never notice they were standing on silt that sealed
because they walked it wet.

Zone 7 growers sometimes assume their dirt is “heavy” because winter is
heavy. Zone 9 growers sometimes assume their dirt is “light” because the
Gulf is light in their heads. 8a does not care. Bradley County timber dirt
and a Saline County back yard are not the same sentence. The ribbon does not
need a zone. It needs a wet handful.

I do not send every new fig to a lab before I plant a tool tree. I do send
my hands. If the ribbon is long and the jar stays muddy, I am planning a
berm before I shop a name. If the jar is a sand stack, I am planning
nematodes and a hose. That is enough to stop the most expensive mistakes.
The county soil test can come later for pH and salt. The ribbon is free, and
it does not wait on a form.

Do the ribbon in the hole, not in the bag you just bought.
I have watched a person ribbon a truck of topsoil and then
plant into the slick clay under it and call the site loam.
The jar from eight inches is the one I believe. If you want
a second jar, take one from the surface after you have
mulched a year. That is a progress jar, not a planting jar.
Kids like the overnight layers. Let them shake it. The
lesson sticks better than a lecture about percentages you
will not weigh. Name the argument. Then build the berm or
buy the bag. The handful was always free.
