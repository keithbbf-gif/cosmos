Somebody read that figs like 6.0 to 6.5 and now they are sulfur-bombing a
limestone county. I have watched this. The meter said 7.4. The person looked
offended. The fig was already fruiting on the other side of the driveway in
the same dirt.

Figs grow on soils high in lime. UC IPM says so, then adds the part people
skip: they are sensitive to *excessive* sodium and boron. Tipburn. Yield
off. That is not a pH sermon. That is a salt sermon. A cheap meter that
reads 7.1 in Arkansas clay is not an emergency. A well that tastes like a
battery and leaves a white crust on the pot rim might be.

The working window I will stand on is roughly **6 to 7.5**. Below that, in
true acid sand, you can see micronutrient oddities and a tree that never
looks settled. Above that, in caliche or heavy lime, iron can lock up and
the new leaves come out pale. Both ends are real. The middle is wide. Figs
are not blueberries. Do not treat them like blueberries.

I want a county soil test if I am planting a keeper in-ground and I have
never tested that yard. I want it for phosphorus, potassium, salt, and a
second opinion on pH — not so I can chase 6.2 with a bag of elemental sulfur
I will apply wrong. Sulfur takes time. Lime takes time. A fig you plant this
March will live with the pH you have this March. Drainage will kill it
faster than a 0.4 pH miss.

Pots are a different meter. Promix and peat start acid and drift as you
fertigate. Coir is closer to neutral and can carry salt from the mill.
Castings and compost pull their own way. I do not pH every #3. I watch the
leaves. Interveinal yellow on new growth in a limey mix is the iron
conversation. Leaf-edge burn after a summer of Miracle-Gro tea is the salt
conversation. Those two get confused in every forum. One wants a different
mix or a chelate. One wants a clear-water leach. **[VERIFY]** on your own
can before you buy a third product.

Zone 7 acid woods and Zone 9 alkaline irrigation districts will pull you
toward opposite religions. 8a in South Arkansas is often slightly acid clay
or a limestone influence depending on the ridge. Test once. Do not join a
church. If the tree is growing a foot of wood and the fruit tastes like
itself, I would leave the pH alone and go look at the hole after a storm.
That hole has been trying to talk to you longer than the meter has.

A cheap probe you jab once in dry dust will lie. Wet the
spot, wait, then read, or send the bag. I have chased a
probe around a berm and got three numbers in three feet.
That is why the lab sheet still earns a stamp on a keeper.
Pots drift as you fertigate. If the new leaves come out
pale on a limey can, I look at iron and at water before I
look at a sulfur product I will apply wrong. If the edges
burn, I look at salt. Two different yellows. Two different
hoses. The religion wants one knob. The fig has two.
