I do not need a meter for the first conversation. I need the morning
after a rain, a trowel, and a willingness to smell dirt.

Good news looks like this: the surface is dark, it crumbles, there are
worms, it smells like a woods floor, and my finger goes in without a
fight. Bad news looks like this: a gray crust, a sour or ditch smell, a
slick face that shines, no worms, and a trowel that skates. Worse news
is a crust over a soup. That is the bowl with a lid.

Color lies if you only use color. Red clay can be a pantry. Gray
mottling in the top few inches is the water table writing a note. Black
dirt can be compost you dumped in a dish. I want structure more than I
want a paint chip. If I can tease the top two inches apart and see pores,
the fig has a chance. If I can slice a ribbon that looks like liver, I
am planning a berm before I shop.

Smell is rude and useful. A swamp smell in a fig hole is not “rich.” It
is anaerobic. Roots do not throw fruit from a ditch. I have stuck a
hand in a pretty raised bed that smelled like a bait bucket because
somebody lined it with plastic “to hold moisture.” They held moisture.
They also held a funeral.

Worms are not a fertility score. They are a sign the top is livable. I
will not count them and publish a number. I will say a ring with no
worms and a pottery crust is a ring I mulch and stop walking. A ring
with worms and a sour smell is a ring I drain. Those are different
jobs.

After a dry week the top two inches tell a second story. Clay that
cracked wide enough to drop a pencil is clay that will take a storm
straight to the root and then seal. Sand that is dust to the second
knuckle is sand that needed water yesterday. Pots tell the same story
faster. Lift. If the top is pale and the can is light, drink. If the
top is pale and the can is heavy, you have a hydrophobic cake or a
swamp under a crust. Poke deeper than your manners want.

Zone 7’s top two inches in March are a mud season. Do not judge a hole
only then. Zone 9’s top two inches in August are a crust season. 8a
gets the mud and the crust in the same calendar. Look twice. The cheap
lab is already open. It does not mail a sheet. It waits for you to
kneel.

I also look at what is growing there without me. Sedge and
a shine after rain is a wet story. Spurge on a crust is a
dry-surface story. Neither is a fig. A ring that grows
nothing but algae is a bowl. A ring that grows healthy
weeds I can pull is at least alive. I would rather pull
weeds in crumbs than stare at a slick face and buy a
conditioner. The top two inches are the cheap lab. Kneel
after the next rain. Take a picture if you forget what
you smelled. Next year the picture is the test you
already ran.
