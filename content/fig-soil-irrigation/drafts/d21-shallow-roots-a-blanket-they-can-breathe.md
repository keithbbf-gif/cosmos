Fig roots are a mat, not a taproot drama. Texas A&M says mulch
because of that. UAEX says the same. NC State says mulch
instead of a hoe. I say mulch because I have cooked a ring of
bare clay into pottery in July and then wondered why the tree
looked thirsty after a rain.

The blanket does three jobs. It keeps the top inches from
swinging like a screen door. It keeps the hose honest — you
water less often because the surface does not bake off by noon.
It feeds the top as it rots, which is where the mat already
lives. A fourth job people want — winter coat — is a different
sentence. Mulch is not a wrap. The cold-hardiness set can argue
that. This set says: summer mulch is climate control for a
shallow plant.

How thick: enough that I cannot see dirt in July. On our clay
that is often two to three inches after it settles, sometimes
four if the chips are coarse. A foot of piled chips against
the trunk is a wet collar and a vole hotel. Wide beats tall.
Out to the dripline if you can stand the look. The pretty
clean circle you edge every Saturday is a compaction machine.

I want a blanket the rain can enter and the air can move
through. Fine dust from a chipper that sat in a pile and went
anaerobic is a crust, not a mulch. Fresh arborist chips from
a clean tree are my favorite ugly. They look unfinished. They
work. Hay and straw and leaves all work if they are not a
soaked mat against bark. The next draft is the church
argument. This one is the physics.

Pull it off the trunk. A wet collar is how you grow slime at
the crown. I want to see the flare. I want a hand of bare dirt
or a light scatter, then the blanket. Every year the chips
creep in. Every year I rake them back. That is the whole
maintenance.

Zone 7 mulch is also a little insurance at the crown if you
keep it from becoming a soaked heap. Zone 9 mulch in a wet
summer can hold rust litter and slugs if you never lift it.
8a needs the July blanket more than it needs a theory. Bare
clay here is a griddle. I would rather look at chips than
look at a pottery ring and a dropped crop.

Do not cultivate the mat to “work the mulch in.” Lay it. Let
it sit. The fig will find it. Your hoe will find the roots
first.

I refresh before I can see dirt, not after the pottery
has already written a week of stress into the fruit.
May is the look. August is the bill. A chip delivery in
April is a gift to July. A chip delivery in a heat wave
is a thatch over dust unless you water it through. Wet
the ring once after you spread, then let the clock slow.
The blanket is climate. Climate has a season. Start it
before the griddle.
