Sand looks like the answer if you just came from a clay bathtub. Water
leaves. The shovel is easy. The hole does not smell like a ditch in the
morning. Then July arrives, the sieve runs empty in two days, and the tree
drops fruit because the shallow roots never found a drink. That is one sand
problem. The other one is quieter. Root-knot likes sand.

Texas A&M is blunt: figs grow from coarse sands to heavy clay, and they can
take extensive damage from root-knot nematodes in sandy soils. NC State
treats nematodes as one of the two limits on fig culture there, with cold as
the other. [Pick The Right Fig](https://figroots.com/2025/07/02/pick-the-right-fig-for-you/)
already said the collector’s sentence: if you have nematodes, grow in pots.
I am not rewriting that control program. I am saying sand is not “good
drainage” as a personality. Sand is a sieve that also rents rooms.

You knock a tired fig out of a pot, or you lift a sad in-ground tree, and the
roots have beads and knots. Not the even, fibrous mat you want. The bad ones
look like the root swallowed BBs. Fertilizer will not talk those galls into a
working system. The plant is short, yellow-leaning, wilted when the pot is
still heavy. A lot of “I fertilized and nothing happened” is this.

Clay is not innocent. I have seen galls in heavier ground. Sand is still the
usual story. LSU AgCenter Pub. 1802 adds a crop-history warning: avoid old
tomato, okra, or tobacco ground. Those crops share the pest. Louisiana label
law is not Arkansas law. The warning about the hole still travels.
**[VERIFY]** with a sample if you are planting a keeper in sand that grew
tomatoes.

What I do on sand, in order:

1. Rare names stay in mix I trust. A $40 cutting into sand is a donation.
2. Tool trees I am willing to share with the worms can go in the ground if
   the hole drains and I will water the first two summers like I mean it.
3. Organic matter at the surface — not a buried Promix shaft — so the sieve
   holds a little longer between drinks.
4. Mulch, because sand plus July sun is a griddle under a shallow mat.

Zone 7 sand is often a pot climate anyway because of winter. Zone 9 sand on
the Gulf is nematode country with extra humidity. 8a sand in the Coastal
Plain is the conversation I would have before I spent money on a dessert
name. Bradley and Saline dirt is not all sand. Know which county you are
standing in. Do not copy a Florida pot rule onto a clay ridge and call it
science. Do not copy a clay berm onto a sand sheet and call it done.

Drainage was never the whole sand story. The sieve and the hotel come in the
same load of dirt. Name both before you shop.
