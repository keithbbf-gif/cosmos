Some dirt was born heavy. Some dirt you stepped on until it was. I can
tell the second kind because there is a path to the hose bib, a mower
wheel that always takes the same turn, and a pottery ring under last
year’s mulch that you never fluffed. The ribbon still says silt loam.
The roots say pavement.

Figs keep a shallow, fibrous mat. Texas A&M and UAEX both talk that way.
That mat lives where your boots live. Every wet-day walk to pick a fig
is a vote for a sealed surface. Water hits the crust, runs off, and the
tree looks droughted after a rain. You water more. The runoff writes a
gully. Nobody sends that to the lab. The lab would still say silt loam.

I do not rototill a fig ring. A tiller in established roots is a hoe
with a motor. NC State will not cultivate a shallow fig. I mulch, I keep
the grass back, and I stop parking the truck on the dripline because the
dripline looked like a convenient pad. If I have to redo a hole before
planting, I will loosen the sides with a fork and I will not powder the
bottom into a slick pan. A slick pan is a bowl you ironed in.

The pottery ring is a July story. Bare clay in South Arkansas sun turns
to a crust you can thump. Mulch stops the thump. A half-inch of tired
chips that blew off in March does not. I would rather see three inches
of ugly chips than a pretty clean circle you edge every Saturday. The
pretty circle is a compaction machine.

Pots compact too. A peat-heavy mix that sat wet all winter collapses
into a cake. The pot is heavy. The top looks dry. You water. The water
runs down the inside wall and out the hole and the cake in the middle
stays hydrophobic. That is compaction you bought in a bag. Perlite you
can see is how I sleep. A collapsed #3 gets dumped, not “fluffed” with a
stick you jabbed twice.

Zone 7 compaction plus a freeze is a wet pan that holds ice against the
crown. Zone 9 compaction plus a Gulf rain is a week of standing water
you will blame on clay. 8a gets both seasons in one year. Walk the ring
when it is dry. Pick when you can without making a path. Lay a board if
you have to harvest in the mud. That sounds fussy until you watch a
ten-year fig decline because the family took the same shortcut for ten
years.

You cannot always change the county’s clay. You can stop manufacturing a
second soil on top of it. The path is optional. The mat is not.

New berms compact if you build them wet and then walk them
to admire the work. I have ironed a mound I was proud of.
Build it, water it to settle, then stay off it except to
plant. A board as a temporary path is uglier than a story
and kinder than a slick pan. If the ring already rings
under a trowel, I loosen the *surface* with a fork, far
enough from the flare that I am not cutting the mat, and
I cover it the same day. Bare loosened clay in our sun
is a crust by Thursday. Mulch is the lid that is allowed.
