# Two Brothers, Two Price Lists

Lay a Craftsman Workshops catalog of about 1907 next to an L. & J.G. Handcraft catalog of the same season. The slats agree. The leather agrees. The oak species agrees. The prices do not, not line for line, and the shop numbers refuse to be translated. A buyer in a Midwestern retail store — or a dealer in a city that carried both franchises — could open the two books on one desk and feel, for a minute, that the American movement had become a double-entry problem. This chapter is that desk. It is not a family argument. It is a pair of lists.

Gustav’s book teaches. Even the later catalogs, after *The Craftsman* had done ten years of sentences, still sound like a monthly. Leopold and John George’s book sells. The plates are cleaner of sermon. Albert’s Quaint list (chapter 17) is a third book on a larger desk, dated 1 January if you have the 1912 issue, mahogany and oak in the same column. Three surnames, three prices for a settle that a customer might call, incorrectly, the same chair. The trade knew they were not the same chair. The trade also knew that a customer might not stay for the lecture.

## What a price list is for

A catalog plate without a price is an illustration. A price list keyed to plates — Eastwood’s shop numbers, Fayetteville’s numbers, Grand Rapids’ 1912 sheet — is a contract waiting for a signature. Freight, finish, leather grade, and whether the settle includes cushions will move the number. The number is still the fact that survives when the moral language has been forgotten. Cathers reprints and the Turn of the Century Editions facsimiles are how a modern reader gets the plates. The originals sat in retail drawers next to *Furniture World* and *The Furniture Record*.

Competition, here, is not a soap opera about who spoke to whom at Thanksgiving. It is two factories in the same county and a third in Michigan, all spending the same surname on the same fashion, in years when Grand Rapids as a city could add a Mission page without anyone’s permission (chapter 39). Gustav could be severe in the magazine about Mission as a trade word. He could not stop Leopold from printing Handcraft in red. He could not stop Albert from printing Quaint on brass. He could not stop Sears from printing a suite that looked enough like both. The lists are what a serious buyer used to keep the three factory products from becoming one mood.

Look at a Morris chair in each book. The Eastwood chair has a shop number a collector can still recite. The Fayetteville chair has a different number and, after 1906, a clamp. The Grand Rapids chair has a Quaint tag and a line in Catalogue No. 38. Reclining mechanisms, slat counts, and bow-arm versus straight-arm variants do not line up. A retailer who stocked two of them was not stocking a backup SKU. He was stocking two answers to the same English chair (chapter 36 will take the Morris form as an American problem). The price difference, when you can recover both numbers for the same year, is the competition made arithmetic. `[CITE NEEDED: a paired year in which both an Eastwood and an L. & J.G. Morris chair price can be quoted on the page.]`

A retailer wrote the order in the shop’s language. Eastwood wanted a shop number and a finish. Fayetteville wanted a Handcraft number, then, after about 1912, a “work of L. & J.G.” number. Grand Rapids wanted a Quaint plate from Catalogue No. 38 and a line on the January list. Cushions, leather color, and whether the piece shipped set up or in a crate moved the freight. None of that is in *The Craftsman*. All of it is in the letters and ledgers COL 60 kept when the companies’ papers landed in the same archive. The facing catalogs are the public half. The invoices are the private half. Together they are the American oak trade, which did not wait for a brother to finish a sentence.

## Franchises and the same window

Winterthur says Craftsman furniture was sold by more than fifty franchises while the Homebuilders’ Club grew. L. & J.G. used traveling sales and city accounts John George already knew how to work. Albert used the Grand Rapids markets. In a town large enough for two oak lines, the window could hold a Craftsman settle and a Handcraft settle and a Quaint rocker without the retailer feeling dishonest. He was not dishonest. He was reading three lists.

The 1905 Grand Rapids show, if the pairing holds (chapter 16), is the public version of the desk: two Stickley booths, one hall. Buyers walked the aisle and wrote numbers. No one needed a biography. They needed terms, delivery dates, and whether the finish would match a suite already on the floor. *Furniture World* existed for those sentences. *The Craftsman* existed for other sentences. A history that only reads the second paper will miss the competition. A history that only reads the first will miss why anyone cared.

There is a limit to what facing catalogs can prove. They do not prove affection or its absence. They do not prove that Leopold designed by looking over Gustav’s fence, or the reverse. Shops in a fashion copy rooms, not brothers. Baillie Scott and Mackintosh were in the magazines for anyone to see. McHugh had already named Mission. The lists prove only that the surname was valuable enough to spend twice, and that the market was large enough, for a while, to pay both bills.

## 1918: a notice, not a reconciliation

On 24 March 1915 Gustav’s corporation was declared bankrupt (chapter 15). The last *Craftsman* is December 1916. Craftsman Farms sold in 1917. A 1918 issue of *Furniture World* carried a notice for a new Stickley company: Leopold president, Gustav vice president, J. George treasurer. Winterthur’s finding aid repeats the notice and adds the next fact: a year later, Gustav was no longer associated with the firm. That is the whole reunion this chapter will allow. It is a trade-paper paragraph and a disappearance.

Later writers expand the paragraph into Stickley Associated Cabinetmakers, a 1916–18 combination of L. & J.G., sometimes Albert’s Grand Rapids plant, and the wreckage of The Craftsman, Inc. A circular shopmark that tries to marry the clamp to the compass, and Handcraft to Craftsman, is described in the collector literature. `[VERIFY the mark against a photographed example and a dated catalog; do not treat a rare decal as a merger statute.]` Common stock divided among Gustav, Leopold, and a financier named G. Tracy Rogers, from whom Gustav had borrowed on the order of $100,000, appears in the same secondary accounts. COL 60 is where those papers, if they survive, should be read. Until they are read, this chapter will stay with *Furniture World* and the Winterthur sentence.

What the notice meant on the floor is easier. Leopold and John George were in a position to use Eastwood’s plant and Gustav’s remaining name. Production of Craftsman-like oak continued at Eastwood into the 1920s; Winterthur says until 1927, when a Colonial line took over. Gustav’s title in 1918 is vice president. A vice president who is gone in 1919 is a vice president whose name was useful on a letterhead and then was not. That is not a tragedy in a magazine sense. It is how solvent brothers treat an insolvent brother’s trademark when the trademark still opens a buyer’s door.

John George died in 1921. The treasurer’s death is a firm date in a chapter that has too many soft ones. Leopold continued. The lists after 1922 that say Cherry Valley and Colonial revival are someone else’s problem (chapter 41). The lists that matter here are the oak lists of 1904–16, when a buyer could still choose Craftsman, Handcraft, or Quaint and be, in each case, buying a Stickley.

## How to read the two books without a favorite

Prefer the shop mark to the silhouette. Prefer a priced plate to a memoir. Prefer a year to a “period.” If a Fayetteville chair is better made than an Eastwood chair of the same season, say so; it happens. If an Eastwood chair is a better drawing, say so; that happens more often. Albert’s Quaint mahogany page will offend a reader who thinks Mission is a moral about oak. The page is still a list. Offense is not evidence.

Avoid the story in which Leopold is the practical brother and Gustav the dreamer. The practical brother printed Handcraft, which is a dream word. The dreamer signed a midtown lease, which is a practical document that went wrong. Avoid the story in which 1918 heals 1904. The 1904 incorporation of L. & J.G. was already a competition. Avoid the story in which Albert is the forgotten middle brother unless you have just come from his 1912 catalog, in which he is not forgotten at all.

What remains, if the stories are left out, is a market that could support multiple oak factories under one surname until it could not. The crash of 1915 is Gustav’s crash. The 1920s taste-change is everyone’s. Between those dates the two (and three) lists are the American Arts and Crafts furniture trade at its most ordinary and therefore at its most useful. Ordinary is where most households bought their settles.

## A vice president for a year

The residual image is not a handshake. It is a masthead. Leopold president, Gustav vice president, J. George treasurer — then a year, then two names. *Furniture World* did not print the feeling in the room. It printed officers. COL 60 will give a researcher the checks and the minutes if the researcher goes to Winterthur. This essay is a magazine chapter. It can only keep the notice from becoming a healing.

The leftover fact is the facing catalogs themselves. Two books, one desk, two numbers for a chair a customer wanted to call by a brother’s first name. The next essays leave the surname and take a different American campus: Elbert Hubbard’s Roycroft at East Aurora, which printed first and made furniture because the buildings needed chairs — and because visitors bought the chairs. That campus used a mark of its own. It did not need Stickley on the rail.

A buyer in 1910 who asked for “a Stickley chair” was already speaking a blurred sentence. The salesman who answered with one catalog was doing the buyer a kindness or a cheat, depending on the mark under the rail. Associated Cabinetmakers and the later Rogers-loan stories in the collector literature are later attempts to legalize the blur `[VERIFY against COL 60]`. This chapter does not need those later firms to make the 1904–16 point. Two lists were enough. Three, if Albert’s mahogany page is on the same desk. The surname was a search term before there were search boxes. The shops knew it. They printed it. They also printed different numbers, which is the only reason the surname did not become a single factory.


A salesman with two books on one desk is the American movement at retail scale. The books disagreed about price and sometimes about a slat count. They agreed about a surname. That agreement sold chairs. The disagreement is why a mark still matters more than a silhouette in a later sale room.


The 1918 masthead lasted a year because a name can be an officer and then not be. Lists last longer than officers. A Handcraft clamp and a Craftsman decal still disagree in a drawer.
