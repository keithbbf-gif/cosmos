# Philadelphia, 1876

The Japanese Dwelling stood in Fairmount Park with its joinery still explaining itself. Japanese carpenters had come with the timber. Illustrated papers ran the construction as news before the Centennial Exhibition opened to the public in May 1876. Beside the dwelling was a bazaar that sold wares. In the Main Exhibition Building, Japan took a floor that later souvenir books put at about 17,000 square feet: porcelain, bronzes, lacquer, screens, textiles, bamboo. More than 7,000 packages had been shipped. Only Great Britain sent more. American cabinetmakers who came to Philadelphia that summer did not have to invent Japan. They could walk through a house, buy a cup, and then walk into a British furniture court and a New York Modern Gothic stand. What they did with those walks is the problem. The fair did not convert a nation. It put objects in a park and let a trade look.

Robert Judson Clark’s 1972 Princeton catalog, *The Arts and Crafts Movement in America 1876–1916*, starts the American clock here. The start is a curator’s decision, not a thunderclap. Eastlake’s Boston *Hints* was already four years old (chapter 3). Boston’s society was twenty-one years away. Stickley’s magazine was twenty-five years away. Clark needed a public date. 1876 is the public date. This essay stays on the ground: what a cabinetmaker could actually see.

## Two Japanese buildings, and a hall of goods

Japan built on the grounds. That fact mattered as much as the lacquer. Most foreign courts were installations inside American sheds. Japan was one of the few governments to raise its own buildings. The Dwelling was a house. The Bazaar was a shop and a tea house. Materials and workmen crossed the Pacific. Philadelphia newspapers treated the raising as a spectacle: joints without the nails a Pennsylvania framer expected, a roof that sat on a logic you could watch. The later Shofuso house in West Fairmount Park is a different building, a twentieth-century gift on related ground. Do not confuse the two. The 1876 structures were temporary exhibition architecture. They were also the first time a large American crowd stood inside Japanese carpentry instead of inside a print.

Inside the Main Building the Meiji government spent to be seen. Saigō Jūdō headed the official effort `[VERIFY romanization as used in the 1876 reports]`. The point, from Tokyo’s side, was export and currency. The point, from a Philadelphia joiner’s side, was finish. Lacquer that did not pretend to be carving. A screen that was a plane. Metal that was inlaid, not applied as a grape. Bamboo that was a material, not a novelty chair from a seaside catalog — though the novelty chair would come, and Stickley himself would later machine imitation bamboo for the 1890s trade (chapter 8). The “Japan Craze” is a real retail weather. It is not a spirit that entered American oak and stayed. Most of what factories took from the Japanese courts was a linear ornament and a darker stain. They did not take the house.

A cabinetmaker who was honest with himself already knew some of this. Japanese goods had been in Atlantic shops since the 1860s. The Iwakura embassy had passed through American cities in 1872. What 1876 added was scale and a ticket. You could spend a day. You could take a pencil. You could go back to a shop on Chestnut Street or a plant in Cincinnati with a sketch of a bracket.

## The British rooms: Collinson and Lock, not a guild

Great Britain sent furniture, not a manifesto. The London firm of Collinson and Lock, already a name in “art furniture,” showed a drawing room and related settings. The BIFMO entry is specific enough to use: two parlor cabinets; a large ebonized satinwood and lacquer cabinet designed by E. W. Godwin; an oak cabinet designed by Bruce Talbert; a buffet, a walnut corner cabinet, and a rosewood chair designed by H. W. Batley. The firm took a medal. *The Furniture Gazette* noticed (14 October 1876). G. T. Ferris’s *Gems of the Centennial Exhibition* (Appleton, 1877) put them in the souvenir literature. Gillow, the other English name a later reader expects, did not exhibit in Philadelphia. The British court was not the complete London trade. It was the slice that paid for the crossing.

What an American saw in those rooms was ebonized wood, Japanese-facing lacquer, Talbert’s Gothic oak, and a quality of cabinetwork that New York’s better shops could already match and Grand Rapids could not yet advertise as its own. Godwin’s Anglo-Japanese line — the ebonized geometry later collectors file under Aesthetic — sat in the same park as the Japanese Bazaar. That adjacency is not a theory. It is a map. A man could walk from one to the other in a afternoon and decide that black furniture with a linear gilt incision was modern. Kimbel and Cabus had already decided. Collinson and Lock confirmed the decision at international scale.

Morris & Co. is not the story of this court. Morris as an American sentence belongs to later reprints and to Irene Sargent’s first *Craftsman* (chapters 2 and 10). In 1876 the British furniture that reached Fairmount Park was art-furniture commerce: Godwin, Talbert, Batley, a drawing-room installation. A Philadelphia journeyman did not meet a guild. He met a stand.

## What American shops put on the floor

Kimbel and Cabus took the Modern Gothic to Philadelphia and were praised for it. The ebonized cabinets, the strap hinges, the incised gilt, the tiles — the stock already in the Twentieth Street photographic catalog — now had a world’s-fair caption. Brooklyn, the Met, LACMA, and the Hudson River Museum still hold the furniture that explains the stand. The Centennial did not invent Kimbel and Cabus. It advertised them to people who would never walk East Twentieth Street.

Other American furniture at the fair was not reform. Machinery Hall and the main furniture displays were a census of the national trade: walnut Renaissance Revival sideboards with a bounty of carved game, patent folding beds, Grand Rapids suites, Cincinnati production from houses like Mitchell & Rammelsberg. A man from those plants could admire a Japanese bracket and go home to cut a more expensive grape. Many did. The fair was large enough to contain both the lesson and the refusal.

Philadelphia’s own reform cabinetmaking — Daniel Pabst, the Furness orbit, Modern Gothic in a city that had read Eastlake — had a home advantage and did not need Japan to begin. The Centennial put that local work into a crowd that included buyers from the West. It also put a New England Kitchen on the grounds, a colonial-room display that later historians correctly treat as an origin of the Colonial Revival. Cabinetmakers saw that too. If 1876 “changed American furniture,” it changed it in several directions at once: Japan, Britain, Colonial, and more of the same walnut. A sentence that names only Japan is a later convenience.

## What a cabinetmaker could carry home

Three portable things left the park in a notebook.

First, joinery as a spectacle. The Japanese Dwelling made a joint worth watching. American balloon-frame houses did not. A furniture man who already believed, with Eastlake, that construction should show, now had a popular picture of showing. He did not have to become a temple carpenter. He had to stop being ashamed of a peg.

Second, a darker, flatter surface. Lacquer, ebonized art furniture, and the stain that made cheap walnut look older all fed a taste for plane over bulge. The 1880s parlor could still be crowded. It was less likely to be carved in high relief. Incised lines (chapter 3) and ebonized cherry are the American middle of that taste. They are not Mission slats.

Third, the idea that a foreign court could be a shop. The Japanese Bazaar sold objects. Collinson and Lock sold a reputation. Kimbel and Cabus sold a line. The Centennial taught American retailers that a “look” could be installed as a room and ticketed. McHugh’s Popular Shop would later sell rooms (chapter 6). Stickley’s *Craftsman* would later print rooms (chapter 10). The fair is an ancestor of that retail form. It is not the ancestor of a through-tenon.

What did *not* leave the park: a national style called Arts and Crafts. The English phrase was not yet a society. No one in Machinery Hall was discussing Ruskin’s wage. Quartersawn white oak as a Mission material was a generation away. The Forbes chairs for a San Francisco church were eighteen years away. Anyone who stands in Fairmount Park in 1876 and sees a Stickley armchair is writing backward.

## Clark’s 1876, and the survey’s need for a door

When Clark and his colleagues opened the 1972 Princeton exhibition at the Art Museum, they needed a beginning that was not Red House and a close that was not a living factory. 1876–1916 does that work. The Centennial is the first year an American public, in American weather, stood in front of Japanese carpentry and British art furniture at once. 1916 is the last *Craftsman*. The bracket is clean. Kaplan’s 1987 MFA show shifted the American start earlier, to 1875, and kept Boston in the frame. Both dates are arguments. Neither date is a conversion experience.

This series uses Clark’s door without kneeling to it. The Centennial is a shop floor with flags. Cabinetmakers saw a Japanese house, a British drawing room, a New York Gothic stand, a Colonial kitchen, and a mile of walnut they already knew how to make. Some of them changed a template. Most of them added a page. A few, in New York and later in Syracuse, would spend the 1890s trying to turn the looking into a line that could be branded.

The cosmic sentence — Japan changed everything — is the sentence this chapter is not allowed. Japan changed some catalogs. Britain confirmed a Gothic already in print. American factories kept their saws. The oak that later got called Mission would need a different theft, a different coast, and a New York decorator willing to misread a church.

## Named shops, and the walk that did not leave a diary

Daniel Pabst’s Philadelphia Modern Gothic — the Furness orbit, the carved walnut that a local man could already make without a Japanese house — had a home-city advantage. A Cincinnati man from Mitchell & Rammelsberg could walk the British court and go home to a plant that already filled catalogs. A Grand Rapids jobber could do the same and add a page of incised suites for 1877. Named shops left traces. Most furniture men did not. Attendance figures in the souvenir books like a round million-count; treat them as publicity `[VERIFY official paid attendance]`. The useful number is smaller: how many cabinetmakers from named cities left a record of the walk. The ones who did left sketches and, later, catalog pages. That thin paper trail is why 1876 can be a door without being a miracle.

Herter Brothers, if they showed or only visited `[VERIFY a Herter stand versus a Herter visit]`, were already past the price of a factory parlor table. The fair’s lesson for them was prestige. The lesson for a journeyman on Chestnut Street was a bracket he could copy on Monday. Both lessons are American. Neither is a conversion.
