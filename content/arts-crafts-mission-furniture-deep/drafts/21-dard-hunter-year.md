# Dard Hunter’s Year

The window in the Roycroft Inn is dated, in the shop talk that still follows it, to 1906: a leaded flower — later tiles and auction captions call it a poppy; older campus language likes a rose — set in a field of green and amber squares. The lead lines are a grid, not a Gothic tracery. The glass does not pretend to be a medieval church. It pretends, if it pretends at all, to be modern in East Aurora, which in 1906 still meant a campus that printed books, hammered copper, and sold oak chairs under an orb-and-cross. The man who drew the window was twenty-three. His name on the payroll was already Dard. The furniture shop had been making chairs before he arrived. It would keep making them after he left.

This chapter is not a life of Elbert Hubbard and not a census of branded oak. Chapter 19 walks the campus. Chapter 20 walks the mark. The problem here is narrower and easier to get wrong. William Joseph Hunter (1883–1966), who took the peculiar first name and kept it, spent six years at Roycroft — 1904 to 1910 — and those years are the ones a furniture reader meets first. Dealers will point at a copper lamp with a lavender-and-green shade and say Hunter. They will sometimes point at a chair the same way. The second pointing is the habit this essay refuses. Hunter designed glass, books, metal, and, on a few documented occasions, furniture. He is not the furniture designer of record for the Roycroft shop. The distinction is not a slight. It is how a campus actually worked.

## Steubenville, a California hotel, and a July arrival

Hunter was born in Steubenville, Ohio, where his father ran a newspaper and a printery. He spent time at Ohio State (1900–03) without treating the university as a vocation. Cathleen Baker’s biography, *By His Own Labor* (2000), is the modern life; Hunter’s own *My Life with Paper* (New York: Knopf, 1958) is the late, terse autobiography a papermaker wrote after the furniture decade had become a preface. The 1958 book is useful precisely because it is not a Roycroft memoir. He looks back from Chillicothe and from a career spent reconstructing vats, moulds, and type. East Aurora is a chapter. Paper is the plot.

The Metropolitan Museum’s object note on a later window design records a conversion story Hunter and his later interpreters liked: in 1903 he saw the interior of the New Glenwood Hotel in Riverside — the building that became the Mission Inn — and decided the Arts and Crafts look was the look he wanted. The next year he applied to Hubbard. The campus biography that circulates from the Dard Hunter house in Chillicothe is blunter about the hiring. In June 1904 he asked for a summer place and was refused. In July he showed up anyway and was put to work. Within months he was drawing title pages and thinking about glass.

Hubbard sent him to New York, to the studio of J. & R. Lamb, for a short course in leaded glass. The length of the stay is given as a month in one museum note and as two months in another `[VERIFY: Lamb studio duration, 1904]`. The assignment when he came back is not in dispute. By 1906 Hunter was in charge of stained-glass production at Roycroft. The Inn windows, the peristyle panels, and the hanging fixtures that later catalogs called electroliers are the objects that still carry his name without a fight.

## What the library already knew about Vienna

He did not need a steamer ticket to meet Vienna. The Roycroft library held German periodicals — *Deutsche Kunst und Dekoration* is the title later writers keep — and those pages already showed Josef Hoffmann, the Wiener Werkstätte, and a Glasgow geometry that American shops were slower to steal than they were to steal a slat. Hunter’s early glass, the Museum of the American Arts and Crafts Movement has argued, still remembers English Arts and Crafts and a California Mission interior. His later glass is a grid: green and purple rectangles, vertical and horizontal leads, square cut-outs in the copper fittings. The change is visible without a manifesto.

In 1908 he married Edith Cornell, a pianist at the campus, and they honeymooned in Vienna because he was already reading Hoffmann. That sentence is the one a furniture series has to keep. The honeymoon is not a finishing school that invented his style. It is a confirmation. He had been drawing Vienna from a magazine. Then he walked the streets. He came back to East Aurora for two more years and put the geometry into glass and metal that the shop could sell. In 1910 he left again, this time to study at Vienna’s K. K. Graphische Lehr- und Versuchsanstalt — the Royal-Imperial Graphic Teaching and Experimental Institute — and then to London. The furniture shop did not follow him.

A three-part watercolor in the Metropolitan Museum (1982.1136 A–C), dated about 1909–11, is the paper version of the argument. The museum calls it a design for a window “in the style of Mackintosh.” The sheets are narrow, 11⅝ by 4 inches each. They are not a chair. They are what Hunter actually was at Roycroft: a graphic mind asked to think in lead.

## A rose, a poppy, and a peristyle

The Inn window of 1906 is the opening object because it is the one later reproductions taught a wider public. A tilemaker’s poppy stone, sold in museum shops a century later, names Hunter and 1906 and the Inn in one caption. Auction houses have sold a 1909 leaded window with a Roycroft Inn peristyle provenance — unsigned, as much of the glass is unsigned — and the catalog language still says Hunter. The flower’s botany is less stable than the lead line. Rose and poppy trade places in the secondary literature `[VERIFY: Inn window motif as rose vs. poppy in period documents]`. What does not trade places is the job. This is architectural glass for a campus hotel, not a cartoon for a furniture catalog.

The electrolier of 1909–10, made to hang from a beam in the Inn’s dining room, is the other object a reader can hold in mind. Three leaded shades in green and lavender drop from a copper cap. Square cut-outs run in bands. Wire spirals sit where a lesser shop would have put a cast leaf. The Museum of the American Arts and Crafts Movement has published the fixture as Hunter’s design. Later Roycroft lamps often split the credit the way a campus should: a shade geometry that still looks like Hunter, a copper base that later hands — Victor Toothaker among them, after Hunter had gone — knew how to repeat. Chapter 20 can take the copper. This chapter only needs the glass.

He designed furniture. The sentence has to be said once, without being allowed to become the chapter. Auction records include a unique hall chair, dated 1904–06, carved with “Sit Down & Rest Thy Weary Bones” and a coat of arms, offered as Hunter. Catalogs and later monographs allow that he “experimented” with furniture, pottery, and jewelry. Experiment is the right verb. Roycroft oak as a product line — the settles, the pedestal tables, the chairs with the orb-and-cross cut into the front rail — is a shop output with many hands and a campus brand. Hubbard’s copy sold the brotherhood. The furniture shop cut the tenons. Hunter’s year is the year the glass and the books changed their geometry. It is not the year the chairs acquired a single author.

## What *My Life with Paper* does with East Aurora

The 1958 autobiography is a papermaker’s book. Knopf put it out when Hunter was seventy-five and already the man museums called when they wanted the history of the vat. The Roycroft pages are brief beside the later travel, the Marlborough mill, the Mountain House Press in Chillicothe, and the museum of paper that began at M.I.T. and moved to Appleton. He is not writing a furniture history. A reader who goes to 1958 for a shop number will be disappointed. A reader who goes there for tone will not. He is already done with East Aurora as a home. A letter published in later Hunter literature is ruder than the memoir: he had been “vaccinated,” he said, by something nearer the ideal; the village had a surface of good cheer and a gizzard of strife. The furniture series does not need the gossip. It needs the fact that the man who designed the Inn’s best glass did not stay to become the campus’s Chippendale.

That leaving is the American Arts and Crafts story in a smaller key. Hubbard’s campus wanted a resident genius who would also fill a catalog. Hunter wanted a craft that could not be batched as easily as a tabouret. Handmade paper, once he saw it in London in 1911, gave him a problem that lasted the rest of his life. Leaded glass at Roycroft had given him a problem that lasted six years. Both problems are real. Only one of them produced a national furniture look, and that look was not his.

## How a later market merged the jobs

The collector market of the 1970s and 1980s, which chapter 42 will treat as a market, liked a name on every surface. Roycroft already had a name. Hunter, because he had a biography and a later fame in paper, became a convenient second name for objects the shop had issued without one. A lamp shade in a Hunter geometry is a fair attribution when the comparison set is tight. A chair with an orb-and-cross and a dark finish is a Roycroft chair. Calling it a Hunter chair is a dealer’s compression. Compression is how Mission, Craftsman, and Arts and Crafts became one search term in chapter 1. It is not how a shop floor assigned work.

The same compression runs the other way. Because Hunter is famous, the glass is sometimes asked to apologize for the oak, as if the campus were a furniture factory that happened to hire a stained-glass man. The sequence is the reverse. Roycroft began as a press. Furniture and copper and leather were additions. Hunter was hired as a book designer and illustrator. The glass was an expansion of his graphic job, not a sideline from the chair shop. A series about Mission furniture can forget that and turn him into a cabinetmaker who also liked roses. The window is the correction.

## What the year actually changed

If there is a “Hunter year” inside the six, it is 1908–09: marriage, Vienna, the Inn dining-room fixtures, the peristyle glass, the drawings that already look like the Met’s Mackintosh sheets. 1904 is the arrival and the Lamb course. 1906 is the Inn window and the moment he is named as head of glass. 1910 is the exit. Those dates are enough. They do not require a claim that he redesigned the furniture line, or that Hubbard’s oak became Viennese because a young man from Steubenville had seen Hoffmann.

What he changed, and what later Roycroft work kept after he left, was a modern surface the campus could attach to older shop habits: a lantern that is a grid, a title page that is a rectangle of black and gold, a copper band with square voids. The chairs could sit under that lantern and still be the chairs chapter 20 has to judge on their own terms — some of them serious, some of them campus product, none of them automatically masterpieces. Hunter’s glass makes the room look later than the chair. That tension is the point of putting his chapter after the oak-and-mark chapter and not before it.

He died in Chillicothe in 1966. The autobiography had been in print for eight years. The Inn still had the windows. The furniture shop’s descendants, and the collectors who bought the descendants, still wanted a single designer for a campus that had been a small town of jobs. The rose in the lead — or the poppy, if that is what the 1906 cartoon actually was — does not settle the furniture question. It settles a smaller one. For a few years an Ohio printer’s son made East Aurora look as if it had a window on the Ringstrasse. Then he went back to Vienna, and then to paper, and the chairs stayed where they were.
