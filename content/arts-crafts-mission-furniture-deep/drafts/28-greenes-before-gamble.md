# The Greenes Before the Gambles

The Bandini house is gone. What remains of the furniture argument is a set of photographs, a Huntington pergola reconstruction, and the memory of walls that were vertical redwood boards with three-inch battens — a barn language, not the teak hall the later houses taught the world. Arturo Bandini and Helen Elliott Bandini commissioned the one-story U-shaped house at 1149 San Pasqual Street, Pasadena, in 1903. Almost every room opened onto a covered veranda around a courtyard. A fountain stood in the middle. Posts, in a late change Charles Greene made to the drawings, sat on natural stones instead of a cement porch edge. Edward R. Bosley has pointed to the book behind the change: Edward S. Morse, *Japanese Homes and Their Surroundings* (1886), which Charles bought in 1903. The house was board-and-batten and split-shake, not adobe, a hacienda plan in American lumber. It burned in part in 1923, was patched by other hands, and was demolished in 1961. Randell L. Makinson treated it as a turning point in the brothers’ idea of a California house. The furniture inside was built-in benches, custom woodwork, bedroom pieces later described as bird’s-eye maple, an entry bench, a plant stand, a light — objects that dispersed into private rooms when the house died. They are not the Gamble dining chairs. They are the sentence before those chairs.

Charles Sumner Greene (1868–1957) and Henry Mather Greene (1870–1954) had been in practice in Pasadena since 1894. This chapter stays on that practice and on the furniture that practice had already learned to want before David and Mary Gamble signed in 1907. Chapter 29 gets the dining room. Chapter 30 gets the Hall brothers’ pegs as a shop problem. Starting at Gamble is how a style becomes a single house. The Greenes had been building for more than a decade.

## Cincinnati, St. Louis, MIT, a stop in Chicago

They were born in Cincinnati, raised in part in St. Louis, and sent as teenagers to Calvin Woodward’s Manual Training School at Washington University — hand and mind in the same week, a fact every later wood essay repeats because it is true and because the furniture looks like men who had already met a tool. They took the two-year architecture course at M.I.T., apprenticed in Boston offices that still remembered H. H. Richardson, and in 1893 went west because their parents had already moved to Pasadena. The train stopped at the World’s Columbian Exposition in Chicago. Henry, late in life, remembered Japanese architecture there. The Ho-o-den, a half-scale of a temple at Uji, was the official Japanese building. A year later they saw Japanese hill-and-water gardens at a San Francisco exposition. Clay Lancaster’s *The Japanese Influence in America* and Bosley’s later Huntington talk are the places that keep this sequence from becoming a single conversion.

Japanese design, in the Greene & Greene Virtual Archives’ own summary, does not take over the drawings until after Charles visited the Louisiana Purchase Exposition in St. Louis in 1904. The 1903 Morse purchase and the Bandini stone bases are the first domestic signs. John C. Bentz’s Pasadena shop — Bentz dealt in Oriental antiques; the Greenes designed a building for him in 1900, later demolished — gave Charles manuscripts and photographs he could sit with. Makinson (1977/1979) is the older furniture monograph; Bosley’s Phaidon *Greene & Greene* (2000) is the later life. Neither book needs a fairy tale in which Japan arrives all at once in a single cabin trunk.

A 1906 trip to Japan appears in some secondary lists and in the outline this series was handed. It does not appear, under that date, in the usual Bosley or Makinson itinerary, nor in the Charles Sumner Greene papers’ travel folders, which flag a 1909 European stay and a miscellaneous 1890–1909 travel file `[VERIFY: a 1906 Japan voyage against Bosley, Makinson, and the Greene papers; do not treat the year as a documented crossing unless a primary ticket or letter says so]`. What 1906 does document is the start of the furniture years the Gamble House’s own later essay calls the productive span: about 1906 through 1912, hundreds of individual designs, some hundred and fifty residential commissions in a short crush. Charles’s 1901 honeymoon with Alice Gordon White — England, Scotland, the Continent — is the documented Arts and Crafts crossing. The 1904 St. Louis visit is the documented Japanese intensification. 1906 is the year the shop, and the Hall brothers, begin to make the furniture a later public will mistake for a single masterpiece house.

## Long Beach, 1904: oak that still remembers Stickley

Before the Halls’ mahogany years, the Greenes designed furniture a Mission reader will recognize and then misfile. Edward S. Cooke, Jr., in *American Furniture* 1993, puts two Long Beach objects on the table: a bureau for Jennie A. Reeve, oak and cedar, about 1904, and a fall-front desk for Adelaide M. Tichenor, ash, the same year. The Tichenor desk has slab sides, a writing surface over battened doors, butterfly splines — Stickley’s early desk grammar. The Reeve bureau shows projecting through-tenons. Cooke is blunt. The stock is thick. The woods are ring-porous natives, oak and ash. The joinery is meant to be seen. A lift in a drawer pull and a setback along a side are the Asian notes. The rest is still an Anglo craftsman language: rectilinear, overbuilt, proud of the tenon. It is not yet the modeled mahogany of 1908. It is not known who built these pieces. Pasadena had millwork firms. Carl Enos Nash advertised fine furniture and mantels. Ye Arts and Crafts Company, later Crown City Manufacturing, would advertise “mission furniture” in 1906. Pasadena Manufacturing Company milled doors and sash. Cooke thinks a mill is more likely than a lone bench. The Hall shop is not yet the answer.

Tichenor herself is the client who sent Charles to St. Louis. “I do not want you to go on with my home until you see the Fair,” she wrote. “You will be able to get so many ideas of woods and other things for finishing what you now have on.” The house at 852 East Ocean Boulevard, Long Beach, 1904–05, was a two-story block with single-story wings, U-shaped around a courtyard garden, a tile roof that later writers call irimoya without needing the word to do all the work. She wanted the whole interior: furniture, hardware, lanterns. Makinson published the lanterns and a bench. A hanging lantern of brass and glass, later in Makinson’s own collection, is the lighting version of the argument. The Greenes wanted the room before they had the shop that could make the later chairs.

Jennie Reeve had already asked for a complete environment on a smaller Long Beach lot. Hanging dining-room cabinets from that house, 1903–04 in Makinson’s plates, are the built-in habit Bandini also shows: a cabinet that is a wall, not a suite that can be ordered from Syracuse. Reeve introduced Tichenor to the office. The two Long Beach commissions are how a Pasadena practice learned, in public, to design furniture as architecture. A reader who starts at Westmoreland Place will call the Tichenor desk a rehearsal. A reader who starts on Ocean Boulevard will call the Gamble chairs a decision about wood.

## What the early rooms actually held

Before the Halls, the Greenes had trouble getting the work built at the level the drawings asked for. Built-ins were the practical answer: a bench that is a wall, a sideboard that is a chimney breast, woodwork that does not have to survive a separate contractor’s idea of a chair. Bandini’s interiors, in the surviving photographs, are simpler than the later teak. Redwood boards. Beams. A stone fireplace. Furnishings that look, in a colorized period view, like a California hacienda parlor rather than like a cloud-lifted mahogany set. A Western Museums Association memoir by a later resident of the surviving wing is useful because it is disappointed: the inside “looked nothing like the Craftsman woodwork so celebrated at the Gamble House.” That disappointment is evidence. 1903 is not 1908.

James Culbertson’s house (1902 and later work) picked up clinker brick, carved wood, Japanese-leaning ceiling panels, and a 1906 *Good Housekeeping* notice as an inexpensive California house. Charles is sometimes said to have seen Stickley and Tiffany at Buffalo in 1901 `[VERIFY: Charles at the Pan-American Exposition]`. The point is not another fairground conversion. The point is that Pasadena’s young architects were looking at the same American objects the rest of this series looks at, and then going home to timber and a dry season. Cora Hollister’s Hollywood house took the Morse stone-foot again. The detail travels. The Gamble dining table has not yet been asked for.

Robert R. Blacker’s house (1907–09) is already the mature integration — grounds, architecture, furniture, lighting — and it overlaps the Gamble commission. This chapter will not steal the Blacker dining table from the Detroit Institute of Arts except as a warning: the “early” Blacker furniture is early only relative to the house tourists know. The table, under a lighting tray hung by leather straps from a mahogany canopy (the canopy now at LACMA), with silver and mother-of-pearl flower inlay, is not a Bandini bench and not a Tichenor ash desk. It is the style at full spend. Sixteen chairs for dining and breakfast rooms, designed to stand against the walls when not in use, are a room-as-architecture idea, not a Mission suite idea. James W. Tottis’s DIA note is the public text. Makinson, Thomas A. Heinz, and Brian Pitt’s *The Blacker House* (2000) is the house book. Use them in chapter 29’s neighborhood, not as this chapter’s opening.

A better hinge object for 1906 is the dining armchair John Hall made for the Laurabelle A. Robinson house: a Chinese Ming humpback stretcher — the cloud lift — on a chair that is already Greene and Greene in the later sense. Exhibition notes put that chair where it belongs, at the moment the Halls’ joinery and the brothers’ drawings lock. Peter Hall (1867–1939) and John Hall (1864–1940), Swedish-born, in Pasadena from the mid-1880s and mid-1900s depending on the brother, are the shop. The Greenes’ most productive furniture years do not exist without them. They do not, however, exist only at 4 Westmoreland Place. The Robinson drawings are 1906. Cooke dates some of the furniture 1907, against a Huntington label that still prints 1905–06. Chapter 30 will keep that fight. This chapter only needs the sequence: Bandini’s stone bases, Long Beach oak and ash, then a Hall chair with a lift in the stretcher, then the winter house the next essay sits in.

## Japan without a boarding pass

Bosley’s Huntington lecture “Two Sides of the Pacific” listed the doors he trusts: Boston years and apprenticeships (1888–93), expositions and publications, and Charles’s introspective turn toward Buddhism and other esoteric reading. Morse’s book. The Ho-o-den. St. Louis 1904. Bentz’s photographs. Posts on stones. Timber pergolas. Roof ridges that lift like a temple eave. None of that requires a 1906 landing at Yokohama. If a letter or a passenger list later proves the landing, the furniture will not change. The furniture already had the books.

What the Japan talk must not do is turn Bandini into a Japanese house. It was a courtyard house for a Californio client and an American wife, with a Japanese detail added to the porch posts and a Morse book on the desk. What it also must not do is turn the later mahogany chairs into “Mission oak with Oriental trim.” Chapter 29 will refuse that in the dining room. This chapter refuses it in the courtyard and on the Tichenor fall-front. Spanish-Mexican plan, English Arts and Crafts reading (the 1901 honeymoon), Japanese construction details, Pasadena lumber, a Long Beach ash desk that still remembers Syracuse: the mixture is the American fact. A single-source story is a slide.

## Practice, not pilgrimage

Henry married Emeline Augusta Dart in 1899 and was, in the firm’s own mythology, the brother who got the building built. Charles married Alice in 1901 and was the brother who drew as if each house were a first house. The mythology is too clean — they signed as a firm — but it explains why the furniture, once the Halls are in the shop, looks drawn rather than ordered from a booklet. Ashbee, visiting in 1909, would praise walnut and lignum vitae, pegging, a feeling for material “quite up to the best of our English craftsmanship.” That sentence is a 1909 sentence. It is earned in the years this chapter covers: Bandini’s stone bases, Morse on the table, St. Louis, the Reeve and Tichenor oak-and-ash, the Robinson cloud lift, the first Blacker drawings, the Hall shop running. It is not earned by a winter house that had not yet been asked for.

Makinson’s *Furniture and Related Designs* (1979) remains the book that treats the chairs as a sequence rather than as illustrations of a bungalow. Bosley 2000 remains the book that keeps the sequence attached to a practice. Cooke 1993 remains the essay that will not let the early Long Beach pieces hide behind the later teak. The Gamble House’s later “Design Without Compromise” note, which this series will use again next, is careful about dates: Halls from 1904/05, productive furniture 1906–12, Charles in England in 1909 for months of rest. Those dates leave 1894–1907 looking like what it was — a Pasadena office learning, house by house, how much of a room it could control.

The Bandini pergola at the Huntington is a fragment and a teaching aid. It is also a reminder that the first California courtyard is not in the visitor’s path at Westmoreland Place. A reader who begins there and walks backward will get the dining room as a miracle. A reader who begins on San Pasqual Street, even if the street now holds a different building, and then stands in front of an ash desk from Long Beach, will get the dining room as a decision. The next essay sits at that table.
