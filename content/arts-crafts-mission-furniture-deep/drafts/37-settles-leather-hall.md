# Settles, Leather, and the Hall

The drop-arm settle in a mid-decade Craftsman catalog is a bench that has not decided to be a bed. The arms hinge or drop so a cushion can run the length. The back is a plane of slats or a solid paneled oak. The seat is leather, tacked, a brown that wants to be mistaken for the fumed posts and usually is not. Under the front rail, if the shop is Eastwood, a decal or a brand. This is not a sofa. A sofa is a stuffed argument about the parlor. A settle is a hall argument that you can sleep on if the house is small and the afternoon is long.

American dealers still say sofa when they mean this object. Auction houses say even-arm or drop-arm and then, in the next sentence, sofa anyway. The word matters because the room matters. Chapter 13’s Craftsman house plans put the settle in an inglenook or along a living-hall wall, facing a fire, with a table that could take a meal. That is a medieval English piece of furniture rewritten for a balloon-frame house. It is not a Chesterfield.

## Hall, not parlor

The Victorian parlor (chapter 3) wanted a suite: sofa, two chairs, a table, a language of receiving. The living hall wanted a bench that could hold two people without looking like a reception. Stickley’s magazine sold that substitution as a moral. The settle is the furniture of the substitution. You sit next to someone. You do not perch at an angle for conversation with a caller. You face the fire or the table. The back is high enough to make a room inside the room, not as high as Wright’s dining wall (chapter 31), not as open as a spindle cube (chapter 38).

Even-arm settles — arms that stay up — are the stricter hall version. Drop-arm settles are the version that admits a household might need a spare bed. The mechanism is not the Morris chair’s peg (chapter 36). It is a hinge and a pin, or a sliding arm, depending on the shop and the year `[VERIFY a specific shop number’s hardware from a dated catalog]`. People confuse the two because both change their minds. The chair changes for a spine. The settle changes for a length.

L. & J.G. Stickley made settles that collectors now prefer to some Eastwood examples: tighter slats, a different corbel, a Handcraft clamp. Albert’s Quaint line made settles that look English and oak at once. Grand Rapids made settles by the carload and called them Mission davenports when the word davenport sold better. Lifetime’s Cloister pages include long seats that want to be monastic and are not. The type is public. The mark is the only honest name.

## Leather, tacks, and the other trade

The seat is a second shop. Oak is Eastwood or Fayetteville or Hastings. Leather is a tannery and an upholsterer. Tacks are a hardware count. A 1904 catalog that shows a hard leather seat is showing a surface that will crack along the tack line in fifty years and be replaced in eighty. Original leather on a documented settle is rarer than original finish. Collectors who demand “original leather” are often demanding a later replacement that has learned to look tired.

Tacks in a straight line, close, no welt, no bunched pleat — that is the Craftsman picture. Buttoned leather is a parlor leftover. Nailhead patterns that draw a diamond are a later decorative habit. The 1902–08 shops wanted the leather to read as a hide on a bench, not as upholstery. Hide still is upholstery. The picture is the point, as it was with the through-tenon (chapter 34).

Canvas and rush appear on cheaper or earlier seats. Stickley’s first years used more variety than the later brown-leather myth allows. Cathers keeps the variety visible. A settle with a replaced cushion in a fabric that the catalog never listed is a household object, not a crime. The crime is a dealer who calls the fabric original because it is old.

## Length, slats, and the wall

A settle is architecture at bench scale. Too short and it is a chair for two that no one uses. Too long and it is a hotel piece. Craftsman even-arm settles in the middle lengths — the ones that fit a 1909 house-plan nook — are the ones that still look like they know a fire. Slats in the back can be broad and few, or thin and many. Broad slats are the Mission/Craftsman plane. Thin slats drift toward Prairie and toward a later factory look. Do not sort by slat count alone. Sort by whether the back is trying to be a wall or a screen.

Some settles have boxed bases, some have through-stretchers, some have a drawer under the seat like a chest that grew a back. The chest-settle is an older European type. American Arts and Crafts used it less than the open-base bench. When a boxed base appears, look at the construction before you look at the caption. A boxed base can hide a later conversion. It can also be a shop’s way to store blankets in a house without closets.

Greene and Greene benches in Pasadena, when they exist as hall pieces, are not this object. They are modeled rails and pegs, a different mill, a different client (chapters 29–30). Do not hang a Gamble hall bench next to an Eastwood drop-arm and say settle as if the word were enough. The word is a type. The shops are the history.

## What the parlor did anyway

Most American houses kept a parlor. They bought a Mission settle and put it where the sofa had been, under a picture rail, facing the wrong way for a fire that was in another room. Mail-order pages (chapter 40) encouraged that. A knockdown settle that arrives in a crate is already a parlor object: it has to fit a stair and a door. The living-hall ideology did not travel as well as the bench. That is why so many surviving settles look slightly lost in later photographs. They were designed for a plan the buyer did not build.

The 1920s Colonial Revival sofa — maple, rolled arm, a different cushion — replaced the settle in those same rooms without a fight (chapter 41). The settle went to the porch, the camp, the attic. The 1970s market brought it back as a trophy. A drop-arm with a red decal became a living-room centerpiece in a loft that had no hall. The object survived. The job did not.

## A bench that remembers it is a bench

Sit on a documented even-arm. The seat is hard. The back is a plane. Two people can talk without performing. That is the leftover fact. A sofa asks for a performance. A settle asks for a fire or a table. The leather will be replaced. The tacks will be replaced. The arms, if they drop, will have been repaired. The oak, if the finish is intact, will still be the hall’s color. Call it a settle. If you must say sofa, say so as a later use, not as a translation.

The next essay takes the back apart: spindles against slats, a cube that lets light through versus a plane that stops it, Prairie and Mission in the same room refusing to share a name.

## Shop numbers and the afternoon

A catalog plate is not a sofa advertisement. It is a length, an arm type, and a price. Even-arm settles in the middle lengths — the ones that fit a 1909 nook in *Craftsman Homes* — are the ones that still look as if they remember a chimney. Longer “hotel” settles exist in Grand Rapids cuts and in a few Eastwood specials. Shorter “love-seat” settles are later collector language. Period paper says settle, seat, or davenport. It does not say sofa unless a retailer is translating for a parlor customer.

Drop-arm hardware fails in predictable ways. Pins walk. Hinges loosen. A later restorer welds a plate where a pin should slide. The conversion to a bed, which the catalog treated as a virtue, becomes a conservation problem. Photograph the arms up and down before you buy a story about originality. The oak does not care. The hinge does.

Cushions are a third shop. Some settles were sold with a thin pad; some with a thicker mattress that made the drop-arm version look like a camp bed. Households added quilts. Dealers added foam. Foam is not 1907. A magazine chapter can say that without becoming a buying guide. The leftover object is still a bench. If you cannot feel the front rail through the cushion, someone has already won an argument the hall did not ask for.

Fayetteville’s tighter slats and Eastwood’s broader ones are a connoisseur’s fight. It is a real fight. It is not this chapter’s center. The center is the room: fire, table, two people who do not have to perform. A loft that uses a drop-arm as a statement piece has borrowed the silhouette and left the hall behind. That borrowing is how the type survived. It is also how the word sofa got glued to a bench.

Roycroft long seats, when they appear, are campus furniture first: Inn corridors, a print shop’s rest, a visitor who bought the mark. They belong in chapter 20’s quality range, not in Eastwood’s house-plan nook. Greene benches belong in Pasadena. A series that keeps those long seats in their buildings will not need to call everything a settle. The American Arts and Crafts hall seat is specific enough. It has tacks, a slat back, and a job the parlor refused.

Keyed through-tenons on a settle arm (chapter 34) are the loudest version of the hall ethic: you can check the work while you sit. If the key is missing and a plug has been stained to match, you are sitting on a caption. The leather will still be warm in July. The oak will still be the hall’s color if anyone left the film alone. That is enough residual fact for a bench. Two people, a fire or a table, a back that is a plane, leather that will be replaced, a mark under the rail if you are lucky. The parlor can keep the word sofa. The hall can keep the bench.


A mail-order settle in a crate (chapter 40) is already a parlor object because it has to turn a stair. The living-hall ideology did not travel as well as the bench, which is why so many survivors look slightly lost in later rooms. They were designed for a plan the buyer did not build. The 1920s Colonial sofa replaced them without a fight. The 1970s market brought the bench back as a trophy in lofts that had no hall. The object survived. The job did not. Call the trophy a settle anyway. The word keeps the fire in the sentence even when the chimney is gone.
