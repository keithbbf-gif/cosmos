# Settles, Leather, and the Hall

The arms of an L. & J.G. Stickley settle, Handcraft shop number 263, drop. They do not stay at sitting height because the piece was asked to do a hall’s work: seat three people in afternoon light, then lie flat enough that a body can sleep. Dealers call it a drop-arm or slant-arm settle. Measured examples run about 37 inches high, 72 wide, 25 deep, oak, a spring cushion in leather. That is not a sofa. A sofa is a parlor claim. A settle is a bench that remembers a hall.

Gustav’s Eastwood catalogs are full of settles that do not drop: even-arm 208, the giant knock-down 210, hall settle 205, lift-seat 224, the later 226 at $50 in the July 1910 catalog, $60 with a spring cushion in soft leather in the April 1912 western edition. Chapter 13 already used the built-in inglenook as architecture. This chapter stays with the free-standing bench, the leather, the tacks, and the word people keep getting wrong.

## Hall, not parlor

A settle, in English domestic language, is a high-backed bench, often in a hall or at a fire, a medieval and vernacular form that never needed a tufted back. The Victorian parlor wanted a sofa: sprung, padded, carved, a place to display a dress. Eastlake had already scolded that parlor (chapter 3). *The Craftsman* replaced it with a living hall — partitions out, a table that could take a meal, a fire, a bench. Samuel Howe’s December 1902 visit to Stickley’s Columbus Avenue house walked a reader into an alcove with a bench facing brick. The magazine’s later house plates make the settle a piece of the plan even when it is not built in.

If you can sink into the piece, you have left the hall. A sofa swallows. A settle presents. The back is slats or a broad board. The seat is a plane. Cushions are allowed; they are not the structure. Drop arms are the settle’s concession to a nap without becoming a davenport. Grand Rapids sold davenports as Mission (chapter 39). The word davenport is a parlor word wearing brown oak. The Handcraft 263 is still a bench when the arms are down. You sleep on a bench. You do not, if the language is being careful, lounge on a sofa that has forgotten it is furniture for a hall.

The Victorian parlor wanted a suite: sofa, two chairs, a table, a language of receiving. The living hall wanted a bench that could hold two people without looking like a reception. You sit next to someone. You do not perch at an angle for conversation with a caller. You face the fire or the table. The back is high enough to make a room inside the room, not as high as Wright’s dining wall (chapter 31), not as open as a spindle cube (chapter 38).

Craftsman house plans put the settle where a visitor entered or where the fire was. That is medieval hall memory filtered through an American magazine, not a castle revival. The parlor, in those plans, has been demoted or deleted. A family that kept a front parlor and put a Mission suite in it was using Grand Rapids’s social map, not Eastwood’s. Both maps sold. They are not one room.

## Leather and the tack line

The seat is a second shop. Oak is Eastwood or Fayetteville or a Grand Rapids plant. Leather is a tannery and an upholsterer. Tacks are a hardware count. Catalog copy for the 210 is almost comic about hide: the seat cushion is so large that only one in a hundred hides will cover it. Leather was a Craftsman luxury and a Craftsman advertisement. Canvas was the cheaper cover. Spring cushions cost more than a plain pad. Those dollars are the hall’s comfort budget.

Tacks — brass, copper, or iron, sometimes “armor bright” — run the perimeter of the leather where it turns the frame. They are fasteners and a dotted line. A settle without a tack line, upholstered like a 1920s club sofa, has left the grammar. A settle whose leather has been replaced but whose tack rhythm was kept is closer. Original tacks over original leather are a conservation problem; the hide cracks, the tacks stain. Replacement leather over original tacks is the common honest repair. Replacement leather in a tufted pattern with hidden staples is a sofa pretending.

Tacks in a straight line, close, no welt, no bunched pleat — that is the Craftsman picture. Buttoned leather is a parlor leftover. Nailhead patterns that draw a diamond are a later decorative habit. The 1902–08 shops wanted the leather to read as a hide on a bench, not as upholstery. Hide still is upholstery. The picture is the point, as it was with the through-tenon (chapter 34).

A 1904 catalog that shows a hard leather seat is showing a surface that will crack along the tack line in fifty years and be replaced in eighty. Original leather on a documented settle is rarer than original finish. Collectors who demand “original leather” are often demanding a later replacement that has learned to look tired. Canvas and rush appear on cheaper or earlier seats. Stickley’s first years used more variety than the later brown-leather myth allows. Cathers keeps the variety visible. A settle with a replaced cushion in a fabric the catalog never listed is a household object, not a crime. The crime is a dealer who calls the fabric original because it is old.

The leather is not Spanish colonial. Mission as a trade name stole a church and then stole a color of hide (chapters 1 and 7). Craftsman leather is upholstery leather, sometimes “Craftsman leather” as a shop term, brown or russet, a surface that takes a handprint. It matches the fumed oak only in the sense that both are dark. They age apart. A chair or settle with original film and replaced leather is the usual survivor (chapter 35). Believe the wood first.

## Drop-arm as a mechanism, not a mood

The 263’s arms pivot or slant so the piece can read as a settee and then as a couch-length bench. Gustav’s shop no. 369 Morris chair drops its arms too (chapter 36); that is a chair changing posture. The settle changes function. Catalog language for the 210 does not need drop arms to claim sleep: it is “long enough to allow the body to recline at full length.” Eighty-four inches is a bed that still looks like a bench. The 263 gets the same result with a shorter length and movable arms.

Even-arm settles — arms that stay up — are the stricter hall version. Drop-arm settles admit a household might need a spare bed. The mechanism is not the Morris chair’s peg. It is a hinge and a pin, or a sliding arm, depending on the shop and the year `[VERIFY a specific shop number’s hardware from a dated catalog]`. People confuse the two because both change their minds. The chair changes for a spine. The settle changes for a length.

L. & J.G. Stickley’s Onondaga / Handcraft line, begun 1904–05 in Fayetteville (chapter 16), is not Gustav’s factory B. The 263 is their settle. Gustav’s even-arms are his. A collector who says “Stickley drop-arm settle” without a shop and a number is using a family as a style. The family was a competition. Two price lists faced each other (chapter 18). The hall could be furnished from either list. It could not be furnished from a synonym.

Albert’s Quaint line in Grand Rapids made settles that look English and oak at once. Lifetime’s Cloister pages include long seats that want to be monastic and are not. The type is public. The mark is the only honest name.

Lift-seat hall settle 224 is another function: 48 inches wide in one catalog listing, the seat lifting on a box 45 by 8 inches. A hall wants storage. A parlor sofa does not want you to lift the seat. The 224 is almost rude in its usefulness. That rudeness is the point of the living hall. Housekeeping, the 1909 *Craftsman Homes* book said, should be light; some cottages are planned for a family that keeps no maid. A bench that hides blankets is in that sentence.

## Length, slats, and the wall

A settle is architecture at bench scale. Too short and it is a chair for two that no one uses. Too long and it is a hotel piece. Craftsman even-arm settles in the middle lengths — the ones that fit a 1909 house-plan nook — are the ones that still look like they know a fire. Slats in the back can be broad and few, or thin and many. Broad slats are the Mission and Craftsman plane. Thin slats drift toward a later factory look. Do not sort by slat count alone. Sort by whether the back is trying to be a wall or a screen.

Some settles have boxed bases, some have through-stretchers, some have a drawer under the seat like a chest that grew a back. The chest-settle is an older European type. American Arts and Crafts used it less than the open-base bench. When a boxed base appears, look at the construction before you look at the caption. A boxed base can hide a later conversion. It can also be a shop’s way to store blankets in a house without closets.

Greene and Greene benches in Pasadena, when they exist as hall pieces, are not this object. The Blacker bench at the Museum of Fine Arts, Boston (chapter 30) is modeled mahogany and ebony plugs, no Mission slat wall. The word settle will stretch to it in casual speech. This chapter’s leather-and-tacks object will not stretch to Pasadena mahogany without tearing.

## What a settle is not, and what the parlor did anyway

It is not a sofa. Repeat it because auction titles will not. “Mission sofa,” “Arts and Crafts sofa,” “Craftsman sofa” are dealer compressions for a sprung, fully upholstered back that happens to have square arms. Some of those objects are period factory pieces. They are still sofas. A settle’s back is wood first.

It is not a pew. Church seating (the Forbes chairs of chapter 7) can look related. A hall settle is domestic. You can put your feet toward the fire. A pew does not want your feet.

It is not an inglenook. The built-in bench that grows a chimney is architecture you cannot ship. The 208 and the 263 ship. They can be placed wrong: against a parlor wall, under a window that makes the slats a radiator cover, in a front room that is still for company. The object does not enforce the hall. The plan did.

Most American houses kept a parlor. They bought a Mission settle and put it where the sofa had been, under a picture rail, facing the wrong way for a fire that was in another room. Mail-order pages (chapter 40) encouraged that. A knockdown settle that arrives in a crate is already a parlor object: it has to fit a stair and a door. The living-hall ideology did not travel as well as the bench. That is why so many surviving settles look slightly lost in later photographs. They were designed for a plan the buyer did not build.

The 1920s Colonial Revival sofa — maple, rolled arm, a different cushion — replaced the settle in those same rooms without a fight (chapter 41). The settle went to the porch, the camp, the attic. The 1970s market brought it back as a trophy (chapter 42). A drop-arm with a red decal became a living-room centerpiece in a loft that had no hall. The object survived. The job did not. In that later life the settle was often called a sofa because American rooms had forgotten hall as a living room. Hall had become a corridor. A bench in a corridor is a wait. A bench in a Craftsman hall was the room.

Drop arms, in that later life, made the piece more sofa-like and therefore more saleable to households that wanted a nap and did not want a lecture. The mechanism is innocent. The caption is not. If a museum label says sofa under a slatted oak bench with exposed arms, the label has taken the parlor’s side.

Sit on a documented even-arm. The seat is hard. The back is a plane. Two people can talk without performing. That is the leftover fact. A sofa asks for a performance. A settle asks for a fire or a table. The leather will be replaced. The tacks will be replaced. The arms, if they drop, will have been repaired. The oak, if the finish is intact, will still be the hall’s color.

The residual fact is the tack line on a 263 or a 208. Count the tacks. They follow the leather around a wooden seat frame. Under the leather, if the shop was spending money, there are springs. Under the springs, oak. The arms, if they drop, are still arms of a bench. You can sleep. You are not on a sofa unless you have decided that every place a body lies is a sofa. The Craftsman hall refused that decision. Call it a settle. If you must say sofa, say so as a later use, not as a translation. The next essay refuses another collapse: that every vertical in a chair back is the same vertical. Spindles and slats share a catalog and split the light.
