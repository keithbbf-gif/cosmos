# Roycroft Oak and the Orb-and-Cross

The mark is cut into the wood, often on a front stile where you cannot miss it: a circle, a cross rising from the circle, an R inside the orb. It is not a paper decal you can soak off. It is not Gustav Stickley’s joiner’s compass. It is not L. & J.G.’s clamp. It is a campus brand borrowed from a medieval colophon — monks, Hubbard said, ending a manuscript to show they had done their best — and from whatever other orbs he had seen in paper mills and biscuit companies. The explanations he gave for the symbol changed with the audience. The furniture does not care. The furniture carries the cut. A bookcase that has it is Roycroft in the trade’s sense. A bookcase that has it is not, thereby, a masterpiece.

This chapter starts on the brand because the brand is how East Aurora’s oak entered shops that had never seen the Inn. Chapter 19 walked the campus and the *Lusitania*. Chapter 21 will walk Dard Hunter’s glass. The problem here is narrower and ruder: what the orb-and-cross sits on, what the copper is doing beside it, and why a single mark has been asked to guarantee a quality the shop did not always deliver.

## What the mark is, and what it is not

Hubbard used a medieval colophon shape and put an R in it. Later talk about the hand of God over the world is tour talk. A similar orb without the R appears in older European paper. Nabisco’s later N-in-an-orb is a cousin that produced a joke about biscuits and books. None of that identifies a chair. What identifies a chair is a carved or branded orb-and-cross of the right generation, in the right place, on construction that matches the catalogs of 1906 and 1912.

The R changes. Collectors use those changes to date: early single-R forms, a double-cross orb with an R used about 1906–10 in some metal and print contexts, later variants. `[VERIFY a dated mark chart against objects, not against a single dealer card.]` Furniture marks and copper-shop marks are not one chronology. A tray and a settle can wear related orbs and still come from different benches in different years. The campus was several shops. The brand pretended they were one hand.

It is also not a designer’s signature. James Cadzow and Albert Danner, if the names hold, did not sign the stile. Dard Hunter did not design the sideboard. Hubbard did not draw the tenon. The orb says Roycroft, East Aurora, a product of the community as the community wished to be seen. Attribution past that point is a catalog number, a photograph in *Roycroft Handmade Furniture* (1912), or a guess. Guesses have furnished many living rooms.

Fake orbs exist. So do honest repairs that recut a mark after a refinish. So do pieces from the later, thinner years that still carry a crisp brand. The mark is necessary. It is not sufficient. Construction, wood, and a catalog mate have to agree. This series will not become an authentication guide. It will say the thing collectors already know and museums sometimes forget to print: the brand is a claim.

## Oak, and the other woods

Most Roycroft furniture that a Mission search returns is quartersawn oak, often red oak as well as white, with exposed tenons, a dark finish, and hardware that may be iron or copper. The 1906 furniture catalog — drawings, dimensions, prices — is the early public list: china cabinets, dressers, sideboards, davenports, chairs with heavy leather. The 1912 *Handmade Furniture* catalog shows more than eighty forms. Rare maple and mahogany examples exist; they are not the line. Ash appears. The trade, then and now, means oak unless a tag says otherwise.

A distinguishing foot, when the shop is on its better behavior, is a taper that swells at the bottom — the Mackmurdo foot, named for the English designer Arthur Mackmurdo and for the Voysey furniture that used a related thickening. Gothic-looking glass in the best china cabinets and bookcases is another high-end sign. Model numbers travel: a Thirty-Third Degree bookcase, model 86 in a Christie’s note, circa 1905–06, orb carved on the case, is the kind of object that makes the masterpiece story tempting. Fifty-five inches high, forty wide, fifteen deep, in that listing. Tempting is not a method. For every model 86 there is a table whose feet are only feet, whose tenons are only adequate, whose orb is the most carefully made thing on the piece.

Early iron from the blacksmith shop — andirons, latches, lighting, 1899–1902 in the usual dating — can be primitive in the useful sense: thick, a little rude, made to work in a stone building. The copper shop that took over the 1902 smithy in 1904 is a different ambition: lamps, bowls, trays, candlesticks, bookends, desk sets, the “anvil chorus” Hubbard liked to hear from the porch. Copper is the second Roycroft product after print, if you believe the campus histories. Furniture is third or fourth depending on whether you count leather. The objects in a room do not rank themselves. A copper lamp on an oak table is a campus sentence. A copper lamp on a Grand Rapids table is a later mix. The orb on both can be genuine.

Hardware on the furniture is a third copper problem. Some cases wear iron latches from the early smithy; some wear copper pulls and hinges that match the shop across the path; some were later fitted with whatever a restorer had in a drawer. A matching lamp and table from the same year is a campus intention. A lamp added in 1980 is a living-room intention. The 1906 catalog will sometimes specify coppered glass or copper hardware. Read the specification before praising the ensemble. The ensemble may be two dates.

## Coarse to fine, on purpose and by accident

Hubbard’s origin story (chapter 19) already contains the quality problem. Three carpenters, leftover from building wings, making furniture “as good as they could.” As good as they could is not a design studio. It is a crew. Some of those early pieces are heavy, simple, and right. Some are heavy, simple, and dull. Visitors bought both. The shop that grew after 1904 had German-trained cabinetmakers and a catalog. The range did not collapse to the top. A campus that believed anyone could become an artisan — the friendly literature’s sentence — will produce artisan work and apprentice work and Friday work. The orb does not say which.

Fine: a well-chosen quartersawn case, glass that understands a pointed arch without becoming church furniture, a foot that has been drawn, a finish that still shows ray fleck, an orb cut clean. Coarse: a table whose top has moved because nobody allowed for a New York winter, a chair whose slats are thick because thick was faster, an orb mashed into a rail that was not worth the chisel. Middle: most of what survives, which is how survival works. Hotels, inns, and bungalows ordered the middle. Upper-middle households ordered the fine when they could find it. The Inn itself held all three, which is why a night there was always a mixed lesson.

Stickley’s Eastwood line has a range too, but the magazine worked to hide it. Roycroft’s personality worked, sometimes, to advertise it: handmade means irregular, irregular means alive. Ruskin’s leftover sentence (chapter 2) is dangerous in a factory that has a catalog with measurements. A customer who paid the 1912 price for a sideboard did not want alive in the door gap. He wanted the door to shut. The best Roycroft work shuts. The lesser work asks you to love the gap as philosophy. This chapter will not.

## What the catalogs will and will not settle

The 1906 book gives dimensions and prices. Use them. A number on a page will puncture a masterpiece story faster than a memoir. The 1912 book is thicker and more sure of itself. It will give you a bed, a desk, a cellarette, a chiffonier, a china cabinet with coppered glass, a chair whose leather is specified as heavy. Those nouns are the shop’s range in English. Neither book is a census. Forms left the shop that never sat for a photograph. Forms in the photograph were made in more than one quality. A collector who matches a piece to a plate has begun the work. A collector who stops at the plate has bought a picture.

Model numbers help when they are original. They do not help when they are a dealer’s retrospective. Thirty-Third Degree furniture — made, the story goes, with Masonic clients in mind — is a named group that has attracted money. Money attracts stories. Keep the orb, the construction, and the 1905–06 date range; treat the degree as a sales name unless a commission paper appears. `[CITE NEEDED: a contemporary Roycroft notice that uses the Thirty-Third Degree name.]`

Distribution is the other catalog problem. Campus rooms and traveling lecturers sold a lot. Retail outlets, if the high counts are real, sold more. Later Sears pages, under Bert, sold something that still said Roycroft and was not the 1906 shop. This chapter’s working years stop with the father’s death. The orb after 1915 is a different, longer problem: a brand continuing without its preacher, then without its market. Chapter 41 can have the end. Chapter 43 can have the reproductions. A later shop still cutting an orb is someone else’s catalog.

## Not Syracuse, not a masterpiece machine

Roycroft oak is Arts and Crafts furniture. It is only Mission when a dealer needs the word. It is never Craftsman unless someone is being sloppy. The through-tenon, the dark oak, the leather seat will sit in a room with a Stickley settle and not start a fight. The orb will start the identification. The foot, the glass, the copper hardware will start the argument about whether the piece is one of the good ones.

A good one is worth the argument. The campus produced bookcases and tables that belong in any American survey: cleaner than McHugh, more personal than Grand Rapids factory Mission, less programmatic than Eastwood, less exquisite than Greene and Greene. A bad one is also Roycroft. The refusal that this chapter owes the outline is simple. Not every piece a masterpiece. The orb-and-cross is a brand. Brands exist to sit on a range.

Stand, if you can, in front of a marked bookcase whose doors still close and a marked table whose top has gone wavy. Same cut on the stile. Same village. Same decade, perhaps. The difference is the day’s work, the wood that came in, the man at the bench, the price someone was willing to pay. Hubbard’s colophon said, in his mouth, that the monk had done his best. A factory cannot swear that oath on every Friday. It can only cut the mark and ship.

The leftover fact is the cut itself, deep enough to survive a later scrape, shallow enough that a refinisher can still ruin it. The next essay leaves the stile and takes a window: Dard Hunter’s year, Vienna in his head, rose glass in East Aurora, a designer who should not be made the furniture designer of record just because the glass is better than the coarse table in the same room.
