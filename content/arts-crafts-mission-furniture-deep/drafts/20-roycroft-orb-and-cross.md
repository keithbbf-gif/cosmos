# Roycroft Oak and the Orb-and-Cross

The orb-and-cross is cut into the front rail of a magazine pedestal, model 080, quartersawn white oak, about 1910: a circle, a double-barred cross rising from it, an *R* in the orb. The same device is carved on a drop-front writing desk, model 091, and on a thirty-third-degree bookcase, model 86, that a later Christie's sale dated about 1905–06. The mark is how a buyer in 1910 knew the oak was East Aurora’s and not Eastwood’s. It is also how a later market learned to stop looking at the joint. A brand that sits that far forward on a rail is doing sales work. This chapter is about that work, and about the range of objects it was asked to bless.

Chapter 19 walked the campus: press first, Inn, shops, the *Lusitania*. Chapter 21 will take Dard Hunter’s glass and refuse to hang his name on every chair. The problem here is narrower. The orb-and-cross is a real shopmark, borrowed from a printer’s colophon and applied to furniture and copper as if both were books. It sat on work that ran from a carefully made bookcase to a tray a visitor could carry home in a satchel. Not every piece a masterpiece. That is the sentence a dealer’s caption still tries not to print.

## A printer’s mark on a chair rail

Roycroft’s device is not a compass. It is not Stickley’s joiner’s mark. It is a medieval printer’s sign: the earth as a circle, a cross for the faith above it, an *R* for the shop. Hubbard liked the story that fifteenth-century printers had used it, and that Samuel and Thomas Roycroft had put a version on London books. Whether the genealogy would survive a type historian is less important than the use. On a title page the mark says edition. On a chair it says campus. On a copper tray it says the same campus in a metal a middle-class household could afford.

Furniture usually received the device as a carved relief, often centered on a front rail or a desk fall, large enough to read from a standing start. Copper received it as a punch under the base or on the rim: an *R* inside the orb, the tail of the letter curling in the earlier punches and running straighter after about 1920, sometimes with the word *Roycroft* stamped below. Dating by punch is a collector’s sport, useful when the object is otherwise mute, useless as a quality grade. An early experimental punch on a crude rectangular tray (the copper-shop literature confines that punch to trays and letter openers, about 1906–08) does not make the tray better than a later bowl. It makes it earlier, or it makes it a warning. If the experimental punch appears on a form it was never seen on, the mark is the problem, not the virtue.

The carved furniture mark is harder to fake in a hurry and easier to add on a later rail. The 1970s–80s market, which chapter 42 will treat as a market, liked a visible *R*. Added marks exist. A missing mark does not by itself exile a piece; unsigned Inn furniture and unsigned glass are part of the campus record. A mark that looks cut yesterday on a rail that looks like Grand Rapids is a different sentence.

## Oak the shop actually used

The 1912 catalog *Roycroft Handmade Furniture* shows more than eighty designs: chairs, tables, beds, desks, cabinets, magazine pedestals, the oak cases built to hold *Little Journeys*. The wood is primarily quartersawn white oak. Maple and mahogany exist and are scarce. The Christie's bookcase note is still the cleanest short list of what the better pieces share: rectilinear simplicity, exposed tenons, a tapered leg that swells at the foot — the so-called Mackmurdo foot, an English citation the shop did not need to explain — and, on the best cabinets, Gothic-inspired glass in the doors. The finish is dark, sometimes a red-brown the later shop talk attributed to a barrel of scrap-metal water kept outside the copper shop and wiped onto the oak before wax. Treat that barrel as shop lore until a finishing notebook says otherwise `[VERIFY the scrap-metal wash against a period shop note, not a dealer’s card]`.

Model numbers are how the furniture entered the trade. 080 is the tall magazine pedestal, orb-and-cross carved on each side, a form the campus needed because it sold books. 091 is a drop-front writing desk with ten compartments behind the fall. 86 is the thirty-third-degree bookcase. Those numbers are not Stickley shop numbers, and they do not behave like them. Eastwood’s numbers sit inside a magazine argument and a factory system (chapter 11). Roycroft’s numbers sit inside a campus catalog that also sold mottos. The chair does not become a Craftsman chair by sharing a decade and a slat.

Who designed the line is a thinner file than the mark suggests. Hubbard hired German-trained cabinetmakers; James Cadzow and Albert Danner are the names that travel `[VERIFY both against a payroll or a signed piece]`. Little has been recorded about a furniture designer of record. That absence is not a license to hand the chairs to Hunter. He designed books and glass. He experimented with furniture; chapter 21 already refused to let the experiment become the shop. Karl Kipp came from the bindery (1906) into the copper shop (about 1910) and left a metal grammar the trays still show. He is not the oak. Victor Toothaker, after Hunter’s departure, is not the oak either. The furniture shop had hands. The mark had a publicist.

## Copper the same mark had to cover

The metal shop is why the mark cannot be told as a furniture story only. A blacksmith operation of about 1899 made latches, hinges, andirons, lighting. A copper shop followed in the first years of the new century — 1902 in one chronology, a 1904 conversion in another (chapter 19). The objects are lamps, bowls, trays, candlesticks, bookends, desk sets, smoking goods: hammered surfaces, simple outlines, a finish the catalogs called Aurora Brown, later brass washes, and, after Hubbard’s death, a run of colored and plated experiments that already smell of the 1920s.

Hunter’s geometry — square cut-outs, a grid a Viennese magazine had already taught him — belongs to chapter 21. What this chapter needs is the split credit a campus actually practiced. A shade may still look like Hunter. A base may be a later hand. A tray may be unsigned. The Grove Park Inn commission of 1913, for Edwin Wiley Grove’s hotel in Asheville, is the volume test: furniture and decorative objects for a mountain hotel, and, on the copper side, hardware and lighting in numbers the punch-dating literature puts above two thousand pieces. A hotel order is not a masterpiece problem. It is a production problem that still wanted the orb-and-cross on the goods. The Inn in East Aurora had already taught the shop how to furnish rooms in quantity. Asheville paid for the lesson at another scale.

Copper is also where the souvenir lives without apology. A muffin dish or a pair of short candlesticks could be mailed. They are honest about being small. The furniture equivalent is the *Little Journeys* bookcase as a product: as late as 1925 the *East Aurora Advertiser* said the furniture shop was producing 1,200 oak cases for fourteen-volume sets. Twelve hundred is a factory number wearing a medieval *R*. The 1906 bookcase and the 1925 case share a family of marks. They do not share a claim.

## What “quality” means on this campus

The market that revived Roycroft in the 1970s wanted the Inn’s best oak and the copper shop’s best bowls, and it wanted them to stand for the brand. That is how brands work after the fact. On the ground the range was the point. Hubbard’s own origin story is already a quality sentence: the carpenters made furniture as well as they could. As well as they could is not a masterpiece guarantee. Early tables can be coarse — thick, dark, a little farm-shop in the foot. The later catalog pieces, once Cadzow and Danner (if those are the right names) had a shop and a list, can be as carefully proportioned as anything Eastwood shipped in the same years. Between them sit the Inn’s own rooms, which had to be furnished fast and often, and the mail-order objects that kept the press’s readers buying after they had finished the booklet.

A reader who has stood in front of the model-86 bookcase and then in front of a later production pedestal will not need a sermon. The first has glass, a foot that remembers England, a rail that was worth carving. The second has a mark and a dark finish and a job: hold magazines. Both are Roycroft. Only one should be asked to carry the American Arts and Crafts argument into a museum gallery. The other is campus product. Campus product is not a slur. It is how a village industry stayed open. The slur is the caption that calls both of them masterworks because both of them say *R*.

Finish complicates the range. Original dark surfaces were later stripped, then stained to a 1980s idea of fumed oak, then praised as original. Chapter 35 will take ammonia as a Syracuse problem. East Aurora’s brown is not automatically a fumed brown. A refinished pedestal with a crisp carved mark can look more “period” than a dirty original. The mark does not disclose the stripper.

Reproductions are someone else’s decade. The Roycroft Renaissance and later campus revivals used versions of the mark; they are not 1906. This chapter only needs the warning. Chapter 43 can take the reissue problem as a national habit. The habit started early here because the mark was always a little too easy to read.

## Mission, Craftsman, and a village *R*

Joseph P. McHugh had already put Mission on a New York chair (chapter 6). Stickley had already put Craftsman on a magazine and a shop (chapters 10–11). Roycroft put a printer’s colophon on oak that a trade catalog could have called Mission without asking anyone in East Aurora. The later glossary entries still do: solid oak, exposed joinery, hammered copper hardware, rectilinear forms, orb-and-cross. Those are indicators. They are not a Spanish colonial survival, and they are not a Stickley license. A Roycroft armchair can sit next to a McHugh rail and a Craftsman settle in a 1910 parlor and still be a third thing: campus furniture, sold with a motto, made in a shop that had begun as leftover carpentry.

The hardware is part of the third thing. Hammered copper pulls and strap hinges are not an Eastwood signature. They are the copper shop visiting the furniture shop, the way the bindery had once visited the carpenter. A desk whose fall is oak and whose hardware is Aurora Brown is a single campus speaking twice. A desk whose hardware is later brass from a supply house is a campus that had a shipping date to meet. Look at both. The mark will be on the oak either way.

What the mark will not do is settle authorship, date, or grade. It will not tell you whether Cadzow stood at that bench. It will not tell you whether the tray is 1909 or 1924 unless you already know the punch. It will not tell you whether the chair was made for the Inn, for Grove Park, or for a reader who had just finished *Garcia* and wanted a place to put the rest of the set. Those are documents, provenances, catalogs, and, sometimes, a refusal to pretend.

## The leftover *R*

The leftover fact is small enough to hold in a photograph. The orb-and-cross on the 080 pedestal is the same family of signs as the punch under a copper tray and the colophon on a 1896 title page. One campus, three materials, one publicist. The press invented the need for the furniture. The furniture borrowed the press’s mark. The copper shop borrowed it again. Quality did not travel with the device. It traveled with the day’s hands, the day’s wood, and whether the object was for a hotel corridor or a cabinet a Mason might actually fill.

A later collector who pays masterpiece money for a souvenir tray has been reading the mark as a guarantee. Hubbard would have sold him the tray anyway. He sold the guarantee as a caption. This series can keep the caption in *The Philistine*, where it belongs, and keep the oak on the bench, where the tenon either fits or does not. The next essay leaves the carved *R* for a leaded flower in the Inn — Hunter’s year, and a different job.
