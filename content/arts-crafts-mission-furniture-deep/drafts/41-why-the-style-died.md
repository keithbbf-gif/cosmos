# Why the Style Died in the 1920s

A *House and Garden* advertisement for L. & J.G. Stickley of Fayetteville, printed in the 1920s, offers Early American in cherry, maple, or pine: butterfly tables, slat-back chairs with cushion seats, wing chairs, the “exact mode of the early 1700s.” The copy says that sooner or later you will live in Early American surroundings because most of the new houses are already of that period `[VERIFY the issue; the 85 percent claim is the ad’s, not a census]`. On a dealer’s floor that still had to move last year’s stock, or in a warehouse that still smelled of ammonia and stain, the leftover Mission oak sat in fumed brown — marked down, or waiting for a buyer who had decided the dining room should be mahogany.

The usual sentence is that people got tired of brown. Brown is in the picture. It is not the picture. The style died because a war rearranged labor and lumber, because Gustav Stickley’s company was already in the courts, because the brothers who stayed in business changed woods, and because Colonial Revival had a better story for a country that wanted ancestors more than it wanted a manifesto.

## The bankruptcy that arrived before the armistice

Gustav Stickley was declared bankrupt on 24 March 1915. The Winterthur papers (COL 60) still hold the creditors’ letters and the corporate sequence: United Crafts, Craftsman Workshops, The Craftsman, Inc. Chapter 15 walks the 29th Street lease. This chapter only needs the date and the timing. The twelve-story Craftsman Building, opened in 1913 on a long lease later writers have called fatal, sat on the books while *The Craftsman* lost the easy confidence of 1904. Stickley Museum narratives put the debt in the neighborhood of a quarter of a million dollars `[VERIFY the petition figure from the 1915 filing, not from a museum label]`. The last number of the magazine is December 1916. Craftsman Farms sold in 1917. A later remainder story says leftover stock went to John Wanamaker at a deep discount in the early 1920s `[VERIFY 1922 and the 50 percent figure before repeating them as fact]`.

Bankruptcy is not a taste. It is a court. But a court can finish a taste. Once Eastwood stopped being a national argument, factory Mission (chapter 39) and mail-order Mission (chapter 40) had to live on their own silhouettes. They lived a few more seasons. They did not live as a movement. Elbert Hubbard died on the *Lusitania* in May 1915. Roycroft continued; the campus voice did not (chapter 19). The year is crowded with endings that are not coincidences. They are the same weather.

In 1916 *The Craftsman* announced Chromewald, a finish and a line that reached back toward historic revival rather than toward the slat. L. & J.G. used related revival language. In 1918 *Furniture World* printed a notice of a brief brothers’ reunion: Leopold as president, Gustav as vice president, J. George as treasurer (chapter 18). A year later Gustav is generally gone from that letterhead. The new company, in the accounts that follow the Eastwood plant, made Craftsman-like furniture into the mid-1920s and then a Colonial line. L. & J.G.’s last Arts and Crafts catalog is usually dated 1922; after that the Fayetteville energy goes into Cherry Valley and other Early American work `[VERIFY 1922 as the last A&C catalog year]`. John George died in 1927. Leopold kept making furniture. The furniture was no longer the brown manifesto.

## War, mills, and a different order book

The First World War did not invent Colonial Revival. It accelerated a change already visible in the order books. Oak that had been cheap and plentiful in the upper Midwest was not an infinite stack. Grand Rapids had been cutting hard for a generation. Labor that had walked out in 1911 walked into wartime production after 1917. A later local history says a group of Michigan furniture manufacturers tried airplanes and did not succeed `[VERIFY the “fifteen manufacturers” airplane story before using it as more than a rumor of diversion]`. Even without that episode, a plant that had tooled for slat-and-leather suites faced a market that wanted something that photographed as “American” in a different way: turned legs, a maple stain, a mahogany dining table with a pedigree story.

Mahogany and maple are not morally lighter than oak. They are a different class signal. Mahogany had always been the wood of the formal dining room. Maple and cherry could be sold as Pilgrim, as cottage, as the furniture of a imagined New England farm. Mission oak, especially factory oak that had been stained almost black, photographed as last decade. A 1920s shelter magazine is full of that substitution. Butterfly tables. Windsor chairs. Duncan Phyfe talk for the households that could afford the name. The leftover Mission settle looks suddenly institutional — a lodge, a library, a men’s club that has not been redone.

World War I also changed the bungalow conversation. Robert Winter’s California books and the Oakland survey (chapter 1 pointed at them) keep the house-style and the furniture-style from pretending to be one inheritance. A 1920s bungalow could take Colonial trim as easily as it had taken a slatted settle. The house did not die. The oak suite inside it was what a realtor began to call dark.

## Leftover oak beside a new advertisement

The opening pair — a Colonial Revival ad next to leftover oak — is the image this chapter has to hold. Dealers did not throw the Mission out on 1 January 1920. They discounted it. They moved it to the basement. They sold it to a furnished-room house, a YMCA, a school. Some of it stayed in the bungalows it had been bought for, because a household does not refurnish on a critic’s schedule. A lot of it stayed because it was hard to break. Quartersawn white oak (chapter 33) outlasts a fashion. That durability is why the 1970s had objects to collect (chapter 42). It is also why a 1925 parlor could look confused: a maple drop-leaf already in the window, a fumed rocker still by the stove.

Grand Rapids, which had added Mission pages in a season, added Colonial pages in a season. The *Record* and *Furniture World* did not mourn. They printed the new cuts. Factory Mission died the way it had lived: as a line a plant could discontinue. There was no funeral for a through-tenon. There was a market calendar. Plants that had never believed the moral had nothing to recant. Plants that had borrowed the moral — Lifetime’s “will neither go out of style” — were left with copy that 1925 could laugh at.

A reader who wants a single cause will pick one: Stickley’s overreach, or the war, or Hollywood’s later Colonial rooms, or the simple fatigue of dark oak in a small room. The series will not pick one. The bankruptcy is dated. The last magazine is dated. The Fayetteville ad is dated. The wood shift is visible in the ads themselves — cherry, maple, pine, mahogany named where oak had been named. Fatigue is real and is the least sufficient explanation. People get tired of colors every decade. They do not usually abandon a national furniture grammar unless the shops have already moved.

## What “died” did not mean

Greene and Greene’s houses did not throw out the ebony pegs. Prairie interiors that had been built as architecture (chapters 31–32) did not become Chippendale overnight. Rohlfs was already not Mission (chapter 22). Byrdcliffe and Rose Valley had never been factories. The death this chapter means is narrower and ruder: the trade death of factory Mission and of Craftsman-as-a-product-line. The reform language went into schools and into a few architects. The suites went into the remainder pile.

J. M. Young of Camden, New York, is the footnote that keeps “died” from being too clean. Later company histories say Young continued some Gustav and L. & J.G. models, mainly chairs, for government and business orders into the 1940s `[VERIFY the span and the clients]`. A shop can keep a grammar alive for a purchasing agent after the shelter magazines have moved on. That is not a revival. It is inertia of a useful form. Office oak and church oak and library oak kept looking “Mission” because the slat still made a strong back. The parlor stopped asking for it.

Stickley himself lived until 1942. The man outlived the line. That fact is easy to sentimentalize. It is more useful as a reminder that “the style died” is a market sentence. A market can kill a line and leave a person, a plant, and a pile of very good oak. Leopold’s Fayetteville factory is the plant that proves it. The Audis will buy what is left of that plant in 1974 (chapter 43). In the 1920s it survives by becoming the thing the ads already wanted: Early American, solid wood, a different century’s story printed on the same workroom floor.

## Maple, mahogany, and the ancestor the oak never had

Colonial Revival needed an ancestor. Mission had tried to invent one — the California mission, the Gothic workman, the Franciscan bench — and the invention had always been a little embarrassed (chapter 7). Early American could point at a surviving chair, or at a museum, or at a photograph of a kitchen in a restoration village. Maple takes a bright finish. Mahogany takes a polish. Oak takes a fleck that 1904 had loved and 1925 read as heavy. The taste shift is a wood shift. It is also a story shift. The country after the war wanted a usable past that looked older than 1901.

That is why “people got tired of brown” is a lazy sentence. They got tired of a particular brown that had been sold as honesty and then sold as a suite and then sold as a premium with soap. Honesty is a hard product to repurchase. A butterfly table can be purchased without a lecture. The lecture had been the American movement’s extra cargo. When the cargo began to feel like last year’s magazine, the table that required the lecture was easy to leave in the warehouse.

There is a last, smaller death: the word Craftsman leaks, and the word Mission stays as a flea-market adjective, and Arts and Crafts goes back to being a society name that a few people remember from Boston. For forty years the oak is furniture you inherited or furniture you put on the porch. It is not a category. Categories come back in the 1970s, when a Princeton exhibition and a SoHo gallery need a name large enough to hang a show. The leftover brown, the stuff the 1920s could not sell, becomes the stock. The style dies as fashion. It waits as wood.

The next essay begins with a dated headline, not with a dawn. A monthly that preferred Newport block-and-shell is suddenly willing to treat Eastwood oak as antiques. That willingness is a market. It is not 1904 come back. It is 1977 deciding that leftover brown has a price again.
