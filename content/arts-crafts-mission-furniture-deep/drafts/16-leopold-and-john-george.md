# Leopold and John George at Fayetteville

The first mark is not a red compass. It is a small oval brown paper label with an S, or a rectangular decal that says The Onondaga Shops — the county, not the older brother. Leopold Stickley and John George Stickley were making furniture in Fayetteville, New York, a few miles from Gustav’s Eastwood plant, by the first years of the new century. The purchase that gave them a floor is usually told as Collins, Sisson & Pratt, about 1900–02. Incorporation as L. & J.G. Stickley comes in 1904. The Handcraft clamp, red, with the brothers’ names, arrives about 1906. None of those dates is a branch office. A collector who calls a Fayetteville settle “Gustav’s other shop” has the county right and the firm wrong.

Winterthur’s COL 60 note is blunt in the way finding aids are blunt: as Gustav’s business grew, competition increased, and “his brothers, who founded L. & J.G. Stickley, Inc. around 1900, were among his competitors.” Around 1900 is softer than 1904. Soft is honest. The paper trail has a purchase, a county name, an incorporation, and a clamp. It does not have a memo in which Gustav assigns them a factory B.

## Two jobs in one firm

Leopold — Lee to people who knew him — was the shop man: production, cost, the decision to grow a line rather than a magazine. John George had already learned the road. He had gone to Grand Rapids with Albert in 1891, left that plant about 1900, and brought a salesman’s map to Fayetteville. The split of labor is family lore that happens to match the catalogs. One brother kept the floor quiet. The other kept the buyers talking. Neither brother printed a monthly on the order of *The Craftsman*. That refusal is part of the product. Fayetteville sold furniture. Eastwood sold furniture and a program.

They had rotated, as younger brothers do, through older shops. Gustav’s Eastwood, Albert’s Grand Rapids, Charles’s Binghamton world: the surnames are a maze if you let them be. Chapter 8 walked the Binghamton and Auburn years. Chapter 17 will walk Albert. This chapter stays in Onondaga County and refuses to treat L. & J.G. as a leftover of those earlier partnerships. By 1904 they had a name that used their initials and a town that was not Syracuse. The oak they put into a settle could look, across a room, like Eastwood oak. The mark under the rail was how a buyer told the room the truth.

Cathers’s furniture books are still the place a reader goes to keep the silhouettes from collapsing. L. & J.G. made Morris chairs, settles, bookcases, and tables in the same American grammar: quartersawn white oak, visible joinery, leather, a dark finish. They also made pieces that are slighter, or more openly “Mission” in the trade-paper sense, than Gustav’s severe line. A spindle, a slat count, a corbel that Eastwood would have omitted — those are not failures of imitation. They are a second shop answering a first shop’s market. Sometimes the answer is cheaper. Sometimes it is simply other.

## Onondaga Shops, then Handcraft

Until the brothers had finished whatever contract kept them on the Collins, Sisson & Pratt work, the story goes, they were not free to put out a full Arts and Crafts line of their own. 1904 is the year most accounts give for that freedom. The oval S and the Onondaga Shops decal are the first signatures. Onondaga is a useful word. It locates the factory in a county a New York buyer already knew, and it avoids the word Craftsman, which Gustav was spending money to attach to a magazine, a house plan, and a shop mark.

In 1906 the clamp appears: a red woodworker’s clamp and the line L. & J.G. STICKLEY — HANDCRAFT. Family memory, which is not the same as a letter, says Gustav disliked the rhyme of Handcraft with Craftsman and the rhyme of a red clamp with his red joiner’s compass. `[CITE NEEDED: a contemporary complaint, if one exists; otherwise leave it as lore.]` Whether or not he wrote an angry note, the two red marks did sit in the same marketplace. A retailer who already carried Craftsman could add Handcraft without changing his window color. That is competition. It is not theft unless a court said so. No chapter in this series will pretend a court did.

By 1912 the clamp gives way, on many pieces, to a red-and-yellow rectangle: THE WORK OF L. & J.G. STICKLEY. The same words appear as a brand. The usual explanations are pressure from Gustav or confusion among buyers, or both. Either explanation grants the thing this chapter needs: by 1912 the Fayetteville firm was a name people could mix up with Eastwood, and the firm chose a sentence that named the two brothers and no one else. “The work of” is a craftsman claim without the trademark Craftsman. It is also, if you are unkind, a way of keeping the surname and dropping the echo.

A 1905 Grand Rapids trade show is often named as the public debut of an L. & J.G. Mission oak line alongside Gustav’s. `[VERIFY the 1905 pairing in Furniture Record or the show program.]` If the pairing holds, it is a pretty fact: two Stickley booths, two price lists, one convention hall. Chapter 18 will put those lists on a table. This chapter only needs the booth to be Fayetteville’s.

## What they did not do

They did not publish Irene Sargent. They did not run a twelve-story headquarters on 39th Street. They did not buy six hundred acres in Morris Plains and promise a school for boys. They did not, in the years that matter here, go bankrupt on a midtown lease. Those refusals can be told as caution or as a smaller appetite. The objects do not require the psychology. The objects require a factory in Fayetteville that kept shipping while *The Craftsman* was still an argument and after it was a file.

They also did not become, in 1915, the rightful heirs in a morality play. Gustav’s bankruptcy (chapter 15) put Eastwood’s name and some of its assets into a market Leopold understood. A 1918 *Furniture World* notice lists a Stickley company with Leopold as president, Gustav as vice president, and J. George as treasurer. Winterthur repeats the notice. A year later Gustav is gone from the association. The Eastwood plant, under arrangements that later writers summarize as a purchase of Craftsman shops and a spell of Craftsman-like production, went on making oak that looked like the old line until the later 1920s, when Colonial revival forms took the floor. `[VERIFY the 1918 corporate papers; do not treat Furniture World as a merger statute.]` Chapter 18 will stay with that year. The point for Fayetteville is simpler. The younger firm outlasted the older brother’s corporation because it had been a furniture firm all along.

John George died in 1921. Leopold died in 1957 or 1958 — the year slips in the secondary literature `[VERIFY]`. Those deaths close a shop history this series does not need to carry into a living catalog. Chapters 1–43 will not. The Fayetteville oak of 1904–16 is the subject: Onondaga labels, Handcraft clamps, “the work of” rectangles, chairs that are not Eastwood chairs even when a new collector wants them to be.

## How to see a Fayetteville piece

Start with the mark. An Onondaga Shops decal is earlier and scarcer than the clamp. A clamp is 1906-and-after, not a lifetime brand. A “work of” rectangle is a later correction. A piece with no mark and a dealer’s word is a piece with a dealer’s word. Construction helps but will not save you: the brothers used the same American oak economy as everyone else in the trade. A keyed tenon on an L. & J.G. arm is not a secret handshake. It is a joint the market had already learned to like.

Then look at the ambition of the piece. Gustav’s best work wants to be a sentence in *The Craftsman*. Leopold’s best work wants to be sold to a household that may never have subscribed. That difference can produce a settle of real weight and a settle that is only a brown sofa with slats. Quality in Fayetteville is a range, as quality at Roycroft is a range (chapter 20). The Handcraft name does not guarantee a hand on every surface. It guarantees a shop that wanted the word.

There are forms L. & J.G. made that Eastwood barely touched, and the reverse. A collector’s identification guide will list them; this magazine essay will not become one. The historical claim is smaller. Two brothers in Fayetteville built a factory that competed with a third brother in Eastwood, using his surname, his oak species, and a mark that almost said his magazine’s name. They were not his factory B. They were the nearest proof that Craftsman was a trademark, not a weather.

## The county, not the myth

Onondaga County already had Syracuse, Eastwood, and a furniture trade that did not wait for a manifesto. Fayetteville is a short ride. The closeness is why the myth of factory B is so easy. Why would two younger Stickleys open a shop next door unless they were finishing Gustav’s overflow? Because a surname is an asset, a county is a labor market, and a booming oak style will support more than one payroll. Albert had already proved the third term in Grand Rapids. Leopold and John George proved it again, nearer home.

The 1918 notice will tempt a sentimental reader: the brothers together at last. Winterthur’s next sentence is the colder one. A year later Gustav was no longer associated with the firm. Sentiment does not keep a vice president on a letterhead. Chapter 18 will look at the two lists that made the 1918 experiment necessary. This chapter leaves the clamp on the table. Red. HANDCRAFT. Two initials that are not G.

The leftover fact is the county name. Before Handcraft, before the clamp, before the 1918 notice, the shop called itself Onondaga. That is a place. It is not a younger Craftsman. The next essay leaves the county and rides the other brother’s older move: Albert Stickley, Grand Rapids, and a line he was willing to call Quaint.

Fayetteville’s distance from Eastwood is a few miles and a whole invoice. Buyers who treated the two shops as one factory learned it in the finish, in the hardware, and in the mark under the rail. A red clamp is not a red decal. The mistake is expensive now. It was already a mistake in 1906, when both plants were still shipping oak to the same class of store on the same railroad line.


County filings will settle the incorporation year when someone opens them. Until then the marks are the chronology a buyer can still read under a rail.
