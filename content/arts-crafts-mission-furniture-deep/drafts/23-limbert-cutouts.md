# Limbert’s Cutouts and the Holland Plant

The oval top is a single board-sense of oak, about forty-five inches on the long axis, riding a pair of plank sides that have been pierced until they are more void than post. Trapezoids, a lower shelf, compound angles: Charles P. Limbert Co. sold the form as model 146, a “single oval library table,” and put it on page 17 of a catalog the firm was willing to call *Limbert’s Arts and Crafts Holland Dutch Furniture*. Examples still carry the brand on the underside: *Limberts Arts and Crafts Furniture Trademark Made in Grand Rapids and Holland*. The corners of the cutouts are slightly round. That rounding is not a soft heart. It is a router’s convenience. A Glasgow tea-room table that this silhouette remembers could afford a sharper inside corner. A plant in Holland, Michigan, that had to make the silhouette many times, could not.

The table is the object because it is the one later writers treat as a Japanese print in oak, or as a Mackintosh souvenir, or as a Dutch folk leftover, depending on which slide they showed last. The plant’s own paper said Dutch. The brand said Grand Rapids and Holland, in that order, even after the machinery had moved west to Ottawa County. This chapter keeps all three claims in play and refuses the one-source explanation. Cutouts are not a Japanese monopoly. They are a factory decision with a marketing department.

## Grand Rapids first

Charles P. Limbert was born in Linesville, Pennsylvania, in 1854 — some later notices say 1856 `[VERIFY: 1854 vs. 1856]` — the son of Levi H. Limbert, a cabinetmaker and dealer. The family moved to Akron in 1866. The son learned the trade in a store, tried carriages, then sold furniture for other men: Munk & Roberts in Connersville, Indiana; John A. Colby in Chicago. In 1894 he was already making chairs he could put beside other people’s goods. In 1899 he and Philip J. Klingman opened a Grand Rapids showroom that exhibited several makers. Klingman & Limbert Chair Co. is one of the late-nineteenth-century collaborations the city histories list. The firm that matters for the cutouts is later.

In 1902 he organized Charles P. Limbert Co. in Grand Rapids, with a payroll later writers put near two hundred. Grand Rapids was already a system: kilns, carving rooms, catalogs, a trade press (*The Furniture Record*), a reputation for being able to add a “Mission” page when a “Mission” page would sell. Albert Stickley’s Stickley Brothers plant was in the same city, selling Quaint Furniture (chapter 17). Lifetime’s parent would sell Cloister oak from Hastings and still put Grand Rapids in the company name (chapter 24). Limbert’s first years are those years. The early Arts and Crafts line of 1902, in Christian Carron’s *Grand Rapids Furniture* (1998), came in oak and ash, fumed, with leather or woven hickory-bark seats, and was already being talked about as Dutch peasant furniture rather than as a California mission leftover.

He was a salesman who manufactured. The logo the catalogs used — a man bent over a bench — is a picture of hand work printed by a firm that also ran machines. Later copy said assembly and finishing were done by hand. The cutouts’ rounded inside corners are the machine speaking. Both sentences can be true. Chapter 11 will have said the same of Eastwood. Holland is not a rebuke of Syracuse. It is the western Michigan version of the same split.

## The move to Holland, 1906

In 1906 the manufacturing plant moved to Holland, a lakeshore city whose civic story was already Dutch. The Grand Rapids name stayed on the brand. The new town went into the catalog title. *Limbert’s Holland Dutch Arts and Crafts Furniture* is not a historian’s phrase. It is a trade name. The Henry Ford’s West Michigan note, written from objects in its collection and from a circa-1910 catalog, is blunt about the strategy: the firm linked itself to the Dutch origins of many western Michigan households. Indoor and outdoor sets, tabourets, library tables with a pull-out writing leaf that hinged into a well — the line is a factory’s idea of a complete house, not a theorist’s idea of a guild.

Limbert traveled to the Netherlands. Secondary lives say the trips were frequent and that the later plank forms with naive curves remember Dutch folk furniture as much as they remember Glasgow `[VERIFY: dated itineraries for the Netherlands trips]`. What does not require a steamer ticket is the local audience. A buyer in Grand Rapids or Chicago in 1908 could read “Holland Dutch” as a place-name twice over: the Michigan plant and an old-world cousin. Mission, as a competing adjective, named a California alibi chapter 7 has already dismantled. Dutch named a Michigan fact. The adjective was not innocent. It was better grounded than Mission.

By 1912 the payroll is given as about 250. Until about 1915 the company repeated the same models with omissions and additions rather than inventing a new style each season. Limbert sold his share in 1922, in poor health. He died in 1923. The firm, in the usual short notice, lasted until 1944. Those later dates are a corporate afterlife. The cutout tables that matter are the Holland years.

## What the voids are looking at

A Fine Woodworking analysis that has been widely borrowed — and that should be treated as shop talk, not as an archive — is still useful for the comparison set. Several Limbert pieces sit close to Charles Rennie Mackintosh. The oval table’s cutout pattern is a cousin of a Willow Tea Rooms table. Limbert’s version rounds the inside corners so a machine can cut them without a dead stop. He also built in plank rather than in the heavy post-and-rail that Stickley’s early catalogs preferred. Negative space becomes the ornament. That is a Glasgow lesson. It is also a Vienna lesson, and a Dutch-folk lesson, and, if a writer insists, a lesson that Japanese interiors had already taught American architects about void. Insisting on Japan alone is the move this chapter will not make.

American Arts and Crafts had been looking at Japanese objects since Philadelphia in 1876 (chapter 4). Greene and Greene, in Pasadena, would make a different American use of that looking (chapters 28–29). Limbert’s catalogs do not sell Japan. They sell Holland Dutch. A tabouret in the Henry Ford collection, with curves that refuse the usual Arts and Crafts angle, is the firm at its least Mackintosh and its most willing to be odd. A dresser with a mirror, same collection, is the firm at its most ordinary: oak, stretchers, a chest a hotel could buy. The cutout table sits between them. It is the object that made the brand visible in a later auction room. It was never the whole line.

Japanese print geometry can be made to “explain” any rectangle with a hole in it. So can a cheese. The responsible comparison for model 146 is the Mackintosh oval and the catalog page that called the family Dutch. A third comparison, which Grand Rapids already understood, is production. Plank sides with repeating voids are a good way to decorate a table without carving grapes. They photograph. They ship. They let a 250-person plant look like a studio. Limbert was not a copyist in the sense that he issued one stolen drawing. He was a manufacturer who took a few foreign themes and ran them into a cohesive line the way Grand Rapids knew how to run a theme.

## Brand, catalog, and the man at the bench

The underside brand is the document a collector actually has. *Made in Grand Rapids and Holland* is a double address. It is also a boast. The city of Grand Rapids was a quality claim in the furniture trade; Holland was a story. Winterthur’s library holds copies of the Holland Dutch booklets — one of them possibly Booklet no. 100 — and later numbered booklets (no. 112 turns up under auction tables). Stephen Gray’s reprints taught the 1980s market the same pages. Don Marek and the Grand Rapids Public Museum literature are the local spine. The Henry Ford object records (a library table with a hinged writing surface, THF159607; a dresser, THF159601; a tabouret, THF185486) are the public pieces a reader can check without a dealer’s lights.

What the catalogs do not do is name a single designer the way a Rohlfs monograph can name Rohlfs. Limbert’s name is the firm. “His designers,” the Henry Ford text says, which is the right plural. A later market that wants an auteur will push the cutouts toward Mackintosh or toward a Japanese ghost. The plant’s paper pushed them toward Dutch peasants and a benchman in a logo. Both pushes are advertisements. The table is a third thing: oak, glue, a rounded void, a price.

He was not Gustav Stickley, and he was not trying to be. Stickley’s magazine needed a moral. Limbert’s booklet needed a freight rate and a story a Midwestern buyer already half knew. The oak can look related. The sentences are not. A Limbert settle with cutouts is still Mission in the trade-name sense chapter 1 allowed — slatted or planked oak sold as Arts and Crafts to a house that would never join a Boston society. It is not Craftsman unless someone has stolen Stickley’s word. It is not a Japanese shrine table. It is a Holland, Michigan, product with a Grand Rapids education.

## The middle of the country, looking at holes

The cutout table’s afterlife is a collector’s afterlife. The 1970s–80s shows that hung Rohlfs next to Stickley also hung Limbert, because the voids photographed and because a Grand Rapids factory with a good mark was easier to explain than a mail-order suite. Price-guide culture treated model 146 as a trophy and the plain dressers as also-rans. That ranking is not how the plant lived. The plant lived by the whole booklet.

A reader standing under a 146 can do a better job than the guide. Look at the rounded corners. Look at the brand that still says Grand Rapids after the move. Look at the catalog adjective *Dutch* before reaching for *Japonisme*. Japan is not banned from the paragraph. It is demoted to one of several looks a western Michigan designer could have seen in a magazine, a world’s fair, or a neighbor’s parlor. The fair that taught Limbert’s generation the most, for this purpose, was not Kyoto. It was the American trade press, which could turn a foreign word into a season.

The table remains odd among American Mission oak because it is genuinely odd. The voids are not applied. They are the sides. That oddness is why the Japanese explanation was tempting: American writers have long used Japan as a solvent for any geometry they cannot credit to a Michigan salesman. Limbert was a Michigan salesman. He built a plant in a Dutch town, printed the town on the booklet, and cut holes a machine could repeat. The rose window in East Aurora (chapter 21) is a different use of the grid. The desk in Buffalo (chapter 22) is a different use of the pierce. Holland’s version is the one that shipped.
