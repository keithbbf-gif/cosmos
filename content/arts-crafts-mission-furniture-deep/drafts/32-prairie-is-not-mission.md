# Prairie Is Not Mission

Two chairs can share a room and still refuse the same name. One is a spindle cube: four posts, a screen of thin verticals, light coming through the cage. The Art Institute of Chicago’s example is Frank Lloyd Wright’s, 1902–06, from the Oak Park home and studio, accession 2007.79 — poplar and leather, twenty-nine inches on a side. The other is a slat armchair from a Craftsman Workshops catalog: five wide blades of oak, a leather seat, a shop number burned or papered under the arm. A dealer who learned the language in the 1980s will call both Mission. A search box will agree. The chairs do not.

This chapter is a naming problem, not a taste war. Chapter 31 kept Wright’s Robie dining chair on its own table. Chapter 38 will ask what spindles do that slats do not. Here the question is smaller and ruder: why a furniture that was designed as architecture and a furniture that was sold as a line were later hung on one wall under one adjective.

## Architecture in a chair

Wright built a house on Forest Avenue in Oak Park in 1889 and opened the attached studio a decade later. The early seating experiments were heavy solid cubes. By the first years of the new century the cube had been opened: spindles, a crest that tapers a little, feet that ease. Brandon K. Ruud’s note for the Art Institute (2008) is the museum sentence. The chair is an interior of a specific building. It sat in Wright’s own rooms, went to Taliesin after 1911, passed through Edgar Kaufmann, Jr., a Sotheby’s sale in 1989, and a private collection before Chicago bought it in 2007. That provenance is the opposite of a freight shipment to a store.

Prairie is an architectural argument. Wright, Walter Burley Griffin, Marion Mahony Griffin, William Gray Purcell, George Grant Elmslie, and a cluster of Chicago and Minneapolis offices designed houses whose furniture, when they could control it, belonged to the elevations. Hanks’s 1979 book is again the furniture spine. A Purcell and Elmslie clock or a built-in settle in a Prairie living room is not a SKU. It is a wall that happens to hold a clock. The client paid an architect. The mill took a drawing.

Mission, by 1904, was a word a retailer could order. McHugh had already branded a rail (chapter 1). Grand Rapids could add a page between markets (chapter 39). Sears could print a suite. The customer did not hire Wright. The customer hired a price. The oak can look related. The jobs cannot.

L. & J.G. Stickley later printed a “Prairie” cube chair and settle in a 1910 line — wide arms, large mass, a catalog adjective borrowed from the architects. That borrowing is evidence, not a merger. A Fayetteville plant can name a chair after a school. It cannot make the chair into the school. Gustav’s own spindle cube, shop number 391, about 1905–09, is Craftsman product that learned a screen. Chapter 38 will sit with that number. This chapter only needs the fact that a shop number is already a different kind of authorship from an Oak Park interior.

## What the two chairs are doing in the room

Put the Art Institute cube and a documented Eastwood slat armchair in the same bungalow living room. The cube leaks light. You see the window through it. The slat chair is a small fence: five planes of quartersawn oak, the fleck running, the leather seat a pad you are meant to use. One object is a screen that continues the studio’s vertical moldings. The other is a seat that continues a catalog page of seats. Both can be sat in. Only one was designed to finish a particular set of walls.

The fog that joins them is a later sentence that treats Prairie as a child of Mission, or the reverse, without a date or an invoice. This chapter will not write that sentence. A causal arrow used that way is a handshake that erases the shop. Wright knew Arts and Crafts furniture; he used Morris-type armchairs in the Oak Park living room in the 1890s; Hanks and the Wright Trust both say so. Stickley knew that architects were making cubic chairs; the 1905 spindle line is not an accident. Knowing is not a merger. A man can read a neighbor’s chair and still be in a different business.

What they shared, when they shared anything, was a refusal of the Victorian parlor suite as a carved set. That refusal was already American by 1901. It does not make Wright a Mission designer. It does not make Stickley a Prairie architect. Chapter 1 spent its length keeping Mission, Craftsman, and Arts and Crafts from collapsing. Prairie is a fourth word. It belongs to a house plan and a Chicago conversation, not to a branded New York rail.

## Catalog time versus house time

Eastwood’s time is a catalog year. Model 324 this season, a spindle variant the next, a finish name, a price in canvas or leather. Cathers’s *Gustav Stickley* (2003) and the Dallas 2010 catalog are the modern spine for those years. The plant could change a slat count and still ship. The customer in Ohio received a chair that had never seen Ohio until the crate opened.

Wright’s time is a job number. Robie is 1908–10 on Woodlawn. Dana is Springfield, 1902–04. Willits is Highland Park, 1902. The chair is late if the house is late. You cannot reorder last year’s Robie side chair for a different lot without making a different object. Reproductions exist; they are not this chapter’s business, and living manufacturers are not this series’ business before chapter 44. The original cube at the Art Institute is twenty-nine inches of poplar. Poplar. Not even the species the Mission story prefers. A factory Mission suite in *The Furniture Record* is white oak or a cheaper oak stained to look like the idea of oak. Already the materials disagree.

Elmslie’s furniture for Purcell, Feick and Elmslie houses — the sawed and spindled chairs, the clocks — lives in the same architectural time. Milwaukee Art Museum, Minneapolis Institute of Art, and a handful of house museums hold pieces that were never offered to a Grand Rapids buyer. To call them Mission is to use the 1980s sale title as if it were a 1908 shop word. Auction houses still do this. The sale title is a convenience. It is not a history.

## How a bungalow taught the blur

The California bungalow and the Midwestern Craftsman cottage mixed the chairs for a practical reason. A household could buy a Stickley settle from a catalog and later inherit a high-back that some uncle said was “like Wright.” Robert Winter’s bungalow books keep the house-style and the furniture-style from pretending to be one inheritance. A Pasadena bungalow can wear Mission Revival stucco and hold an Eastwood armchair that never saw a mission. A Chicago apartment can hold a spindle cube that never saw Pasadena. The room is not a proof.

The 1970s collector market (chapter 42) needed a wall large enough for a show. “American Arts and Crafts” was that wall. Jordan-Volpe and the early Cathers books were right to hang Wright, Stickley, Rohlfs, and Greene in one exhibition if the lesson was oak, reform, and a date range. They were wrong if a visitor left thinking the words were synonyms. Kaplan’s *The Art that is Life* (1987) is the survey that still keeps Prairie architecture in the movement without handing Wright a Mission label. Use it that way.

A teacher’s shortcut — “Prairie is the Midwestern Mission” — is the sentence this chapter exists to retire. Midwest is a region. Mission is a trade name. Prairie is a school of houses. A slat is a board. None of these words will do the others’ work.

## What to call the chairs, then

Call the Art Institute cube a Wright studio chair, Prairie furniture, architectural furniture. Call the Eastwood slat armchair Craftsman if the mark says so, Mission only if you are speaking the trade language of 1906 and know you are doing it. Call a Grand Rapids matching suite Mission without apology; that is what the *Record* called it. Call a McHugh rail McHugh Mission; that is what the brand said. Do not call Wright’s cube Mission. Do not call a Sears settle Prairie because it has a few thin verticals.

The living hall of a Craftsman house plan (chapter 13) and the living-dining continuum of a Prairie house are cousins in plan: partitions come out, a fire is a mass, furniture has to make rooms inside rooms. Cousins in plan are not one style of chair. Stickley’s answer was often a slat settle and a table that could take a meal. Wright’s answer was often a high-back or a spindle screen. The household that owns both is not confused. The caption that names them both Mission is.

A catalog that derives Wright from Mission, or Mission from Wright, has declined to do the work of a date. The Art Institute cube is 2007.79, poplar, twenty-nine inches, a documented Oak Park object. A Grand Rapids Mission suite in the *Record* is a cut with a price. Those two facts can sit in one paragraph without a causal arrow. If a designer in Holland, Michigan, saw a photograph of a Prairie interior, say so when the photograph is found. Until then, two chairs in one room are two jobs, not a lineage.

Purcell and Elmslie, Griffin, Mahony — the Prairie furniture that is not Wright — deserve their own sentences in a longer architectural series. Here they are a warning: even “Prairie furniture” is already a bundle. Do not make the bundle bigger by stuffing McHugh into it. The spindle cube is enough object for one argument. The slat armchair is enough object for the other. The room that holds both is a later collector’s room, or a museum’s. It is not 1906 in a single shop, and it is not a proof that the names were ever the same.

If a museum must hang both chairs on one wall, the label should say two jobs, two woods if it comes to that, and two ways of charging for a room. A Limbert cutout in Holland, Michigan, is a third job again: Grand Rapids system, a void in a board, a “Dutch” caption (chapter 23). It does not make the cube Mission. It does not make the slat armchair Prairie. The wall can hold three captions. It cannot hold one adjective.

Stand in the Art Institute gallery and look through the cube. You can see the next gallery. That is the Prairie sentence: furniture as a continuation of space. Stand in front of a slat armchair and you see oak. That is the Mission and Craftsman sentence: furniture as a plane you can price. The next essay starts on the plane itself — the end grain of a white-oak arm, the medullary ray that factories loved — because once the names are pulled apart, the wood still has to be cut.
