# Craftsman Farms, Morris Plains

The log house at Craftsman Farms is chestnut, not advertising copy. The trunks were cut on the property, left in the round, and laid up with stone taken from the same ground. The building went up between 1908 and 1911, in three published minds before it had one roof. *The Craftsman* for December 1908 called it a club house: meals indoors or out, lectures, guests, work to start “early in spring.” The Stickley Museum’s later reading of that notice is dry. There were no visitors. There was no school. There was a clearing, a family on the way from Syracuse, and a manufacturer who had begun buying a tract near Morris Plains, New Jersey — six hundred acres in the Winterthur finding aid, about six hundred fifty in the museum’s own history. `[VERIFY acreage against the deeds in COL 60.]` Mark Alan Hewitt’s *Gustav Stickley’s Craftsman Farms* (Syracuse, 2001) is the monograph that keeps the chestnut and the accounts in the same book.

The title of Hewitt’s study uses the word utopia. This chapter will not. A utopia without a ledger is a caption. The farm had a ledger. The ledger lost.

## A school that stayed on the page

Stickley began the purchases in 1908. He wanted a farm school for boys: work, craft, food from the same fields that would later send cream and vegetables to a restaurant on the twelfth floor of a Manhattan loft (chapter 15). The Winterthur biographical note still says he “opened a school for boys on the property.” The museum on the site is plainer. The school was conceived. It was never fully realized. The log house, first drawn as the clubhouse for those boys, became the family house. Eleven buildings went up between 1909 and 1912; nine remain. Of the original tract, about thirty acres stay with the buildings. The rest is a later subtraction, some of it Farny ground, some of it township ground, some of it gone to ordinary New Jersey uses.

The White Cottage, finished in 1910, is now thought to be the house the Stickleys occupied while the log house was still a hole and a pile of trunks. It may also have been the residence he first intended for himself. The museum is willing to say “now thought” and “may have been.” That caution is the right tone. Plans changed as the property taught him what it would accept. The Stickley Museum’s essay on the log house follows three concepts in the magazine from 1908 to 1911: Craftsman’s House, Club House, Log House. The siting moved. The spiritual claim — Hewitt’s word more than Stickley’s — stayed. The building was to be the center from which a campus radiated. Without students, the radiation had nowhere to go except into a household.

Eda Simmons Stickley and the six children — Barbara, Mildred, Hazel, Marion, Gustav Jr., and Ruth — came in the spring of 1910. By the autumn of 1911 they were in the log house. For a short stretch the magazine’s circulation, the furniture’s reputation, and the family under one chestnut roof lined up. The museum’s narrative of that stretch is almost happy. Happiness is not an account. The mortgages were already on the land. The Homebuilders’ Club (chapter 13) was sending drawings across the country while the man who signed them was learning how much a New Jersey well and a chestnut wall actually cost.

## What the log house is made of

Round logs are a choice. Stickley’s factory furniture is quartersawn oak, fumed, jointed so a customer can see the tenon. The house is the opposite mill practice: trees used as trees, bark off, the cylinder kept, chinking and stone doing the work a rail would do in a settle. Local stone meets the logs at the chimneys and the foundation. The interior is not a cabin souvenir. It is a Craftsman hall with the volume turned up: a living room that wants a settle, a dining room that wants a table long enough for the family and a guest, metalwork and oak that came from the same shops that filled the catalogs. Hewitt walks the fittings as if they were a monograph chapter, because they are. The farm was supposed to prove that the magazine’s house and the factory’s chair could be one life.

It proved something narrower. A manufacturer can build himself a handsome house from his own woods and still fail to open a school. The boys’ program needed staff, a legal shape, a curriculum, and a reason for parents to send a son to Morris Plains instead of to a manual-training high school that already existed. Stickley had the woods and the rhetoric. He did not, on the evidence of the finished campus, have the school. Products from the farm did reach the Craftsman Restaurant after 1913; dairy and vegetables are easier to truck than a pedagogy. The restaurant is a later chapter’s bill. The farm’s first job was supposed to be teaching. Its lasting job was housing a family and, after 1917, housing someone else’s family.

Outbuildings matter because they are where a farm either works or becomes a backdrop. Cottages, a garage, farm buildings: the New Jersey Historic Trust summary lists them without romance. A garage is not a guild hall. A dairy that later sold into the Morristown market under the Farnys is not a Craftsman parable. Hewitt is useful here because he will follow a drainage problem as far as a manifesto. The manifesto is in *The Craftsman*. The drainage is in the ground.

## The accounts, not the sermon

Stickley united his enterprises under The Craftsman, Inc., in 1912–13. He opened the twelve-story Craftsman Building in May 1913 on a twenty-year lease. He was declared bankrupt on 24 March 1915. He owed, the museum’s short history says, nearly a quarter of a million dollars. *The Craftsman* printed its last number in December 1916. The farm went in 1917. Those are the dates this chapter is willing to moralize with, which is to say it is not willing to moralize. A man can overreach. A man can also be holding a magazine, a factory, a franchise network, a house-plan club, Adirondack sawmills, a restaurant, and a New Jersey tract at the moment the oak market softened and a midtown lease came due. Chapter 15 will put numbers on the lease. This chapter puts numbers on the land.

When the property had to be sold, the asking conversation — as reconstructed from period notices the museum quotes — estimated that Stickley had spent about $250,000 developing the place. The ask was $100,000, with a note that a reasonable bid would be considered and that acreage could be left out. George and Sylvia Weinberg, later Farny, bought it for $60,000. `[VERIFY the $250,000 / $100,000 / $60,000 sequence against the 1917 sale papers; the museum’s Circa 1917 project is the public source.]` Winterthur’s COL 60 holds the deed, the mortgage, and the legal file. A sermon about pride would be cheaper than those folders and less accurate.

The Farnys kept the core and sold some of the rest. They enlarged the White Cottage. They farmed, then rented ground to other farmers, then converted buildings to residential use. Descendants owned or lived on the property until 1989. When a plan for fifty-two townhouses threatened the remainder, Parsippany-Troy Hills took the core by eminent domain and partnered with the Craftsman Farms Foundation, now the Stickley Museum at Craftsman Farms. That afterlife is not Stickley’s success. It is a township refusing a subdivision. The chestnut logs were still there. The school still had not opened.

## Family house, not campus

The temptation, standing in the living hall, is to say the farm succeeded as a house even if it failed as a school. The temptation is half true. Eda and the children did live there. Guests did come. Photographs exist. The hall does what the 1904–09 plates wanted a hall to do. But a family house on a mortgaged tract that must be sold to “stave off foreclosure” — the museum’s phrase — is not the campus the December 1908 page promised. The boys were never the audience. The audience was a reader of *The Craftsman* who liked the idea of boys. That is a harder sentence than utopia. It is also the sentence the buildings will bear.

Stickley himself did not stay to become a New Jersey squire in reduced circumstances. After the sale he went back toward Syracuse. The 1918 *Furniture World* notice that puts him under Leopold’s presidency (chapter 18) is a factory sentence, not a farm sentence. The farm had already changed surnames. Cyril Farny, years later, told National Register interviewers that the place was never a winter resort; there was a short rope tow for the boys to ski. A rope tow is an account of a kind. It is not in the 1908 prospectus.

Hewitt’s subtitle, *The Quest for an Arts and Crafts Utopia*, is fair as a description of what Stickley thought he was doing. It is a poor description of what a visitor now walks through. The visitor walks through a National Historic Landmark whose log house was a clubhouse, then a family house, then a Farny house, then a museum. The quest is in the magazine. The chestnut is in Morris Plains. This series will not collapse the two. A wish that fails is still a wish. A house that stands is still a house. The failure that matters for furniture history is narrower: the farm did not become a production campus, and it did not save the shop that paid for it.

## What the furniture took from the farm

Very little oak in an Eastwood catalog grew on this tract. The sawmills Stickley owned were in the Adirondacks. The farm’s gift to the furniture was rhetorical and, after 1913, edible. Readers were invited to believe that a chair and a field could belong to one life. The restaurant made the belief briefly literal. When the restaurant closed, the belief went back to being a caption. Collectors in the 1970s (chapter 42) would treat the log house as a shrine to the chairs. The chairs did not need the shrine. They needed a solvent factory.

What a visitor can still learn, without the shrine voice, is the difference between a plate and a wall. Chapter 13’s inglenook is a drawing. The log house’s hall is a volume you can hear. The joints in the furniture inside the hall — original pieces, later pieces, museum pieces — are the same joints the catalogs taught. The logs are a different honesty: not quartersawn, not fumed in a shed, just trees asked to be a house. That honesty is expensive. The sale price in 1917 said how expensive.

The leftover fact is the $60,000. Not the dream, not the boys, not the word utopia. Sixty thousand dollars for a property a later writer would say had eaten a quarter million. The next essay opens a midtown lease that ate the rest, at 6 East 39th Street, in a twelve-story building the magazine called Headquarters for Home Builders.
