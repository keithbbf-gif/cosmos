# Oscar Onken’s Shop of the Crafters

The cabinet is oak, sixty-four inches tall, with glass, copper, and strips of colored wood the museum is willing to call imported Austrian. LACMA’s label gives it a number the shop used: China Cabinet No. 326. The designer line says Pál Horti, Hungary, active in the United States, 1865–1907. The manufacturer line says Shop of the Crafters, Cincinnati, 1904–1920. The gift line says Ellen and Max Palevsky. Designed about 1905. That is as much particular as a furniture essay gets on a first look. The cabinet is not a slat settle. It is a Cincinnati argument that Mission oak could take inlay and still keep the adjective.

Oscar Onken (1858–1948) is the American name on the gold paper label: *Shop of the Crafters* above a lantern, then *At Cincinnati / Oscar Onken Co. Sole Owners*. He was the son of German immigrants and a Cincinnati businessman, not a Buffalo carver and not a Syracuse editor. The shop’s history, as M. J. and W. Michael McCracken reconstructed it in 2017, is a purchase story. Onken bought The Shop of the Crafters from a business associate in 1904 and grew the line over the next years. Other short notices say he founded a shop after seeing the St. Louis fair. Purchase and foundation can both be true if an older small shop changed owners and purpose `[VERIFY: corporate sequence immediately before the 1904 Onken purchase]`. What the 1906 catalog does not leave in doubt is the European pitch.

## St. Louis, 1904, and a name the catalog printed

The Louisiana Purchase Exposition is the hinge the Cincinnati literature agrees on. Onken saw furniture and rooms he wanted to hire. The 1906 Shop of the Crafters catalog — later reprinted as a whole by Turn of the Century Editions — names “Professor Paul Horti” in language a furniture series can quote as advertising, not as a sworn affidavit. Horti’s dining room at the St. Louis fair, and his designs for Hungarian sections in the Palace of Fine Arts, the Manufacturers’ Building, and Mines and Metallurgy, had been “so wholly delightful in their originality as to have exerted a far reaching influence on the general, crafts’ movement.” His work with the Cincinnati shop, the copy said, contributed to productions “pure in style and of artistic beauty.” Then the catalog directed the reader to the special pieces on the following pages.

Pál Horti (the Hungarian form; Paul in the American booklet) is not an invented name. LACMA’s cabinet is the museum proof. The McCrackens’ 2017 book repeats the meeting at St. Louis and Horti’s role as the designer of the inlaid “Crafters” line. He died in 1907. The collaboration, if the dates are right, was short. A short collaboration can still set a look. The inlay — contrasting woods, sometimes metal — is what later collectors mean when they say Shop of the Crafters instead of saying another Grand Rapids oak.

German and Hungarian are the two adjectives the secondary literature leans on. Cincinnati had German-language newspapers; Onken’s own family story is German-American. Horti is Hungarian. Designers “sometimes given as Hungarian/German” in dealer talk beyond Horti are not, in the sources this chapter has in hand, a second and third named person with a catalog credit `[VERIFY: any other designer named on a period Shop of the Crafters drawing or payroll; do not supply a name to fill the gap]`. The honest sentence is the one that stops. Influence is not a staff list.

## What the inlay is doing on Mission

American Mission, by 1905, was already a repeatable slat. Stickley’s better work had inlay in the Ellis months (chapter 12) and then mostly stepped away from it. Limbert cut voids (chapter 23). Lifetime thickened the post (chapter 24). Onken’s shop put colored wood into the surfaces of cabinets, desks, and tables that still used the straight post and the dark stain. The 1906 catalog’s own explanation, as later quoted, is that Horti introduced “a touch of … work of colored woods or metal, that enlivens the strong simple lines of Mission.” Enlivens is a sales verb. The objects are more specific. Marquetry panels, copper stringing, leaded glass in doors, an occasional carved or painted passage that a purist would call a relapse into the 1890s. Lost Art Press, writing from Cincinnati, has said some of it looks overwrought. The catalog had an answer for that reader: he must be a philistine. The argument is period. It is also the reason the shop is easy to tell from Eastwood in a photograph.

Viennese room settings in the catalog — the reprint’s introduction notes them — place the oak in a Central European interior rather than in a Craftsman living hall. That placement is a claim about class as much as about style. Cincinnati, a river city with a German-speaking bourgeoisie, could take a Secession cabinet seriously. A mail-order customer in a smaller town wanted the Lifetime arch. Shop of the Crafters advertised hard enough, the reprint editors say, that the furniture was widely distributed. Wide distribution and inlay are not contradictions. They are a factory that hired a fairground designer.

*The Lantern*, a short-lived promotional magazine, is the shop’s *Craftsman* in miniature — house organ, philosophy, product. The McCrackens reprint volume 1, number 1, in full. Use it as what it is: a company talking to itself and to the trade, not as minutes of a guild. Onken remained sole owner. The lantern on the gold label is a picture of a light, not a cooperative.

## A city that already made things

Cincinnati is easy to skip in a series that runs Syracuse, East Aurora, Grand Rapids, Pasadena. It had Rookwood. It had a German furniture trade older than Mission. It had, in Onken, a businessman who had seen Gustav Stickley’s stand and the Austrian stands at St. Louis and decided there was room for a third thing. Encyclopedia notices that collapse those impressions into a single conversion scene are too neat; the McCracken purchase story is neater in a different way. Either way, 1904 is the year the label that collectors know begins to mean the inlaid line.

The shop’s dates on the LACMA record, 1904–1920, are the working bracket. After Horti’s death the inlay did not vanish; shops do not bury a successful cartoon because a designer has died. How much later work is Horti, how much is shop adaptation, how much is other hands — that is the `[VERIFY]` that should stay on any cabinet without a number and a drawing. Number 326 has both a number and a museum designer line. Start there. Do not promote every gold-label bookcase to Horti.

Mahogany appears in some notices beside oak. If a piece is mahogany and inlaid, it is still Shop of the Crafters when the label says so. It is not therefore “not Mission.” It is Cincinnati’s version of the adjective: European surface, American case. The same city would later winter-home the Gambles, who hired Pasadena architects and did not buy this oak (chapter 29). That coincidence is geography, not a school.

## How not to invent a workshop

The temptation, in a chapter about a less-monographed shop, is to complete the staff. A Hungarian, a German, a forgotten Cincinnati carver, a woman in the finishing room — the symmetrical three-item sentence the style sheet bans. The McCrackens had catalogs the rest of us can now see: a 32-page 1902 booklet, a 16-page Fall 1904 booklet, pages omitted from earlier 1906 reprints, a 1907 sales pamphlet. Those pages are the place to look for names. Until a name appears on one of them, or in a signed drawing, this series will not supply one. Horti is enough. Onken is enough. The lantern label is enough.

Attribution by “looks Hungarian” is the cousin of attribution by “looks Japanese” on a Limbert void. It is a slide, not a document. Central European influence is visible: the inlay colors, the catalog’s Viennese rooms, Horti’s fair biography. Visible influence is not a second biography. A reader who wants more names can read the 2017 book and the 1906 catalog and report what they find. A reader who wants a story will be offered, here, a cabinet and a label.

## Cincinnati as a German furniture city

Onken’s shop makes more sense if Cincinnati is allowed to be what it was: a river city with a German-speaking furniture trade, a world’s-fair memory of 1888’s local industrial pride, and a 1904 connection to St. Louis that a Hungarian designer could ride. Lost Art Press’s 2020 note is local reading, not a monograph. It is enough to stop the chapter from treating Ohio as a blank between Grand Rapids and Eastwood. A gold lantern label from Cincinnati is a Midwestern fact. The inlay woods being Austrian is another. The two facts together are an Atlantic trade, not a cottage.

The shop’s date range in museum lines — 1904 to about 1920 — is shorter than Onken’s life. That is ordinary. A businessman can outlive a line. What is less ordinary is how completely the shop dropped out of the 1920s Colonial Revival conversation (chapter 41) and had to be retrieved by Palevsky gifts and a 2017 book. Retrieval is not the same as a continuous reputation. Stickley never needed retrieving in the same way; he needed correcting. Onken needed a label photographed.

## The leftover gold square

Shop of the Crafters furniture in a later auction is often cheaper than a Stickley decal and stranger than a Lifetime arch. That strangeness is the shop’s afterlife. The 1980s market that needed a wide Arts and Crafts wall could hang No. 326 next to an Ellis inlay chair and teach a true lesson about colored wood and a false lesson about a single American taste. There was not a single American taste. There was a Cincinnati businessman, a Hungarian designer who had done a world’s fair, a gold label with a lantern, and a catalog willing to say Mission and then decorate it.

Onken lived until 1948. The shop’s furniture life, as museums date it, did not. A 1920 end-date on a museum line is not a bankruptcy headline; it is a factory that stopped issuing the gold label. The cabinet at LACMA is still the object that prevents the Ohio chapter from becoming a footnote to Grand Rapids. Inlay is a choice. So is refusing to name the nameless. The next essay leaves the factory label altogether for a colony on a Catskill shelf, where the benches were few and the factory never quite happened.

No. 326’s Austrian woods are a customs fact as much as a design fact. Colored veneers crossed an ocean to sit on Cincinnati oak. That is not a Craftsman workshop story. It is a Midwestern importer’s story with a lantern on the gold label.


Cincinnati’s German furniture trade is the floor under the lantern. Without that floor, Horti is a visiting name and the cabinet is a curiosity.
