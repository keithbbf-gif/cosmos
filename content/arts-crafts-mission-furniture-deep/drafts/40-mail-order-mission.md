# Mail-Order Mission

The February 1916 Larkin premium catalog, mailed from Buffalo, offers a Three-Piece Mission Suite — chair, settee, rocker — in fumed-oak flat finish, slatted seats, shipped knocked down. The chair is given with a $5 purchase of Larkin products, or $5 in coupons. The settee wants $6. The rocker wants $5. Shipping weights sit on the page like a second price: twenty-five pounds, twenty pounds, twenty-four pounds. The suite is not a Craftsman shop number. It is soap furniture. John D. Larkin’s company sold laundry soap, toilet preparations, and a club system that taught a household to pool orders until the oak arrived.

A Sears page from the same decade does the work without the soap. Catalog No. 124, Spring 1912 — a book of more than twelve hundred pages, the “great price maker” in a later season’s language — carries furniture among the stoves and shoes. Mission oak is in that furniture section `[VERIFY the exact page cluster in No. 124; the line is also in the 1908 No. 117 and the 1909 book]`. The customer is not in a Grand Rapids sample room (chapter 39). She is at a kitchen table, a pencil in the margin, a freight station in mind.

## What a premium was

Larkin, founded in Buffalo in 1875, is the American mail-order story that still surprises people who think Sears invented the crate. The company sold soap direct, cut out the jobber, and paid in goods. The Larkin Club and the pantry party — later writers reach for a Tupperware comparison — were a way to gather $10 and $22 purchases until a library table qualified. Library Table No. 280110 appears on the 1916 catalog’s early leaves as a handsome table given with $22 in products or coupons; the copy promises staunch construction and points the reader to a later description `[VERIFY the finished description on the page the catalog itself cites as 104]`.

Elsewhere in the same book a Mission-style library table in quarter-sawed oak, waxed fumed or waxed golden, has a 44-by-28-inch top, a drawer with square wood knobs, two-inch legs, invisible sliding casters, and a note: shipped knocked down. Shipping weight, 98 pounds. A heavier cousin — “massive,” the copy says — has a plank top about 48 by 28, three-inch legs, and ships at about 140 pounds from a factory in Jamestown, New York, for points east of the Mississippi. A china cabinet on “square Mission lines” matches buffet 150110, offered on page 69 of that catalog, in golden-oak or fumed waxed finish. “Mission dining-room furniture is popular because always in good taste.” That sentence is 1916 talking, late in the style’s first life. Popular. Good taste. Not Ruskin.

Some Larkin furniture came from Jamestown. Some copy in the same catalog ships other pieces from Syracuse `[VERIFY which Larkin numbers left which city]`. A 1912 Larkin catalog, as later company histories quote it, said the factories were “wisely located where supremacy in the particular line of work of each has been attained.” That is a polite way of saying Buffalo did not have to be Grand Rapids. It had to know where Grand Rapids, Jamestown, and the chair towns were. Buffalo Pottery and Buffalo Leather were Larkin subsidiaries for other premiums. The oak was a premium among premiums. The Chautauqua desk — a drop-front Mission-style desk that still turns up in small-town parlors — is the piece later columnists recognize on sight. It is mostly a prewar form `[VERIFY the years the Chautauqua desk was in the premium list]`.

## What Sears was willing to print

Sears, Roebuck and Co., Chicago, is the catalog a reader already knows. The Mission pages are not a boutique. They sit in a book that also sold harnesses. That is the point. Between about 1906 and 1918 — the span the bibliography allows, and the span a reader can still check in Winterthur’s copy of No. 124 and in the abridged 1908 reprint — a farm household could order a slatted oak rocker without visiting Furniture City. The copy used the same adjectives as the *Record*: solid, fumed or golden, quartered where it showed. The prices undercut a branded shop. They did not always undercut the construction.

Sears was not a factory in the Eastwood sense. It was a buyer and a printer. The chairs came from the same American system chapter 39 described: plants that could cut a slat and stain it dark, then crate it for a railroad. A collector who calls every mail-order rocker “Sears Mission” is using a store name as a wood name. Some of it was Sears. Some was Montgomery Ward. Some was a regional house that advertised in the same newspapers. The grammar is the same: a number, a cut, a finish choice, a shipping weight.

Freight is the hidden design constraint. A fully assembled settle is empty air in a boxcar. Knockdown — rails and posts packed flat, hardware in a bag — paid less to the railroad and shifted the last hour of labor onto the household. Larkin’s 1916 suite says the quiet part: shipped knocked down. Come-Packt said it in the company name.

## Come packed, with a screwdriver

The Come-Packt Furniture Company of Ann Arbor, Michigan, and Toledo, Ohio, issued Catalogue H in 1913. The Metropolitan Museum holds a copy (1993.1077.14), a photomechanical book about twelve inches tall, gift of Jane D. Kaufmann. The 1912–13 illustrated Mission catalog, later reprinted, runs to some four hundred items: beds, highboys, armchairs, kitchen cabinets, settles, taborets, even an “Ann Arbor” Mission piano. Many pieces were sold to be assembled at home with a screwdriver. The pitch, as later reprints and dealers still quote it, was a 25 to 50 percent saving over furniture that traveled standing up. A customer could order stained or “in the white,” and had to say so.

Brooks Manufacturing Company of Saginaw ran a parallel knockdown line. A company pamphlet told the buyer there were no holes to bore and no pieces to glue; a hammer and a screwdriver would do; the work was “a pleasant form of recreation” `[VERIFY the pamphlet text in Clark and Thomas-Clark, 2001, against a surviving Brooks leaf]`. Come-Packt is generally gone by 1918. Brooks lasts a little longer and fails in the early 1920s `[VERIFY 1922 bankruptcy]`. Dealers near those towns disliked the direct sale. That dislike is part of the history. Mail-order Mission was not only a service to a distant farm. It was a way around the man who kept a store on Main Street and went to Grand Rapids twice a year.

Knockdown is not a moral category. Eastwood shipped some goods that needed setting up. Grand Rapids did too. The difference is whether the catalog admits that the chair is a kit, and whether the joints were cut to be a kit. A pinned through-tenon that the buyer never sees because the post arrived in two pieces is a different object from a keyed tenon driven in a shop. A reader who loves the joint (chapter 34) has to keep that distinction on the table. A reader who only loves the silhouette can ignore it. Most households ignored it. They wanted a room.

## The freight bill as a document

Shipping weights in the Larkin book are better evidence than a memoir. Ninety-eight pounds for a library table. A hundred and forty for the massive one. A hundred and ten for a china cabinet from Jamestown. Twenty-five for a slat chair. Those numbers are the furniture talking about railroads, not about Venice. Points east of the Mississippi from one factory; Wisconsin excepted from another; a Syracuse crate for a third. The map is a cost map. Ruskin would not have recognized it. Chapter 2 already said so.

The other document is the coupon. Larkin’s customer did not always send a money order for a table. She sent proof that she had already bought soap. The table is a rebate that you can sit at. That fact offends a certain kind of Arts and Crafts piety, the kind that wants every oak board to be a chosen life. It should not offend a furniture history. American Mission furniture reached more rooms as a premium and a catalog number than it did as a Craftsman Farms weekend. The piety, when it is honest, still has to count the crates.

Sears asked for cash, or for the credit system the company was building in those years `[VERIFY how Mission suites were sold — cash, time payments — in a named catalog]`. Either way the chair was a line item. Either way the finish choice — fumed or golden — was a checkbox. Golden oak is the older American taste, the 1890s parlor. Fumed is the 1904 brown (chapter 35), or a stain that impersonates it. Mail-order Mission offered both because a catalog cannot afford a single theology.

## What nostalgia does to a page

A later reader looks at a Sears Mission cut and feels a warmth the original customer did not need. The warmth is an antique-show feeling: look how cheap, look how complete, look at the typeface. This essay is not a nostalgia catalog. The 1916 Larkin suite is late. The style is already losing the stores that set the pace (chapter 41). A household that ordered the suite in 1916 was not founding a movement. It was furnishing a veranda or a small parlor with slatted seats and a flat finish, because the catalog still had the cuts and the soap still needed to move.

Quality is a range here too. Some mail-order oak is honest quartersawn on the faces that show. Some is red oak where the copy implied white `[Come-Packt’s own pages warn against “red oak” furniture — treat that warning as trade talk, not as a species lecture]`. Some hardware is thin. Some leather is not leather. A Chautauqua desk that has survived in Equinunk, Pennsylvania, or any other town that had a Larkin club, can be a decent piece of factory joinery. It can also have been repaired by every owner since the Coolidge administration. The catalog does not follow it home.

Stickley was severe about this market when he noticed it. He was not wrong that a knockdown suite is not a Craftsman house. He lived, even so, in a country whose interiors were being decided by freight rates. Grand Rapids had already decided that Mission was a line (chapter 39). Sears and Larkin decided it was a line that could travel without a salesman. Come-Packt decided the customer could be the last workman. Those are three businesses. They share a silhouette and a decade. They do not share a shop mark.

## A crate on a depot platform

Imagine the crate, because the object began as a crate. Stenciled destination. A weight. A warning not to store it in the wet. Inside: posts, rails, a bag of screws, a seat frame, a leaflet that assumed you had a hammer. The smell is oak and machine oil and, if the box rode with Larkin goods, soap. The woman who opens it is not thinking about McHugh’s Popular Shop or about the Swedenborgian chairs in San Francisco. She is thinking about a wall that needs a settee. The settee she gets is Mission because that is the word the page used, and because the slats were what the plant had jigs for.

Mail-order Mission is why the collector market of the 1970s had so much oak to argue about (chapter 42). There was a lot of it. It was not all Eastwood. Some of it still has a catalog number penciled on a rear rail. Some of it has nothing. A price guide that folds all of it into “Mission oak” and then into a dollar column is describing 1988, not 1912. The 1912 fact is simpler. You could get the look by freight. You could get it with soap. You could get it in pieces. The next essay is what happened when the stores that had ordered those crates decided the dining room should be maple, or mahogany, and the leftover brown oak had to stand next to a Colonial Revival advertisement that had already won.
