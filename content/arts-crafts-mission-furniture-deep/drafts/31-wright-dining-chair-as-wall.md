# Wright’s Dining Chair as a Wall

The side chairs designed for the dining room of the Frederick C. Robie House, 5757 South Woodlawn Avenue, Chicago, stand about fifty-two inches high. David A. Hanks, in *The Decorative Designs of Frank Lloyd Wright* (1979), prints them in situ on page 107 and gives the Smart Gallery’s numbers: 52⅜ by 17 by 19¾ inches. The University of Chicago later accessioned the dining table and six side chairs as Smart Museum of Art 1967.73–79. When the chairs are pulled up to Wright’s table, the slatted backs become a wall. The room is already a room. The chairs make another one inside it.

That is the object this chapter will stay with. The outline allowed Dana or a Hill House cousin. Dana’s dining chairs in Springfield are taller in places, and they alternate high and low for a formal service Susan Lawrence Dana actually used. Charles Rennie Mackintosh’s Hill House chairs in Helensburgh are a European high-back that dealers file next to Wright because both men thought a chair could be a screen. Neither is this set. The Robie chairs are oak, severe, and documented. They are not Mission furniture.

## A table that wanted a fence

Frederick C. Robie asked Wright for a house on a corner lot in Hyde Park in 1906; the building that stands was finished in 1910. The dining room and living room share a long horizontal, divided by a chimney mass, opened by art-glass doors to a porch. There is no Victorian parlor wall to hide a meal behind. Wright’s answer, already rehearsed in his own Oak Park dining room in 1895, was to let the furniture supply the missing enclosure. The Frank Lloyd Wright Trust still uses the sentence: the tall-backed chairs, brought up to the table, create a room within a room.

The table is part of the wall. Piers rise at the corners and carry electrified leaded-glass fixtures. Hanks treats the suite as one decorative design, not as a chair that happened to match a house. The lamps pull the diners’ eyes inward. The chair backs stop the room’s long draft. A person sitting down is inside a wooden enclosure that can be broken by pushing a chair away. That is architecture you can move. It is the opposite of a Craftsman inglenook, where the settle is built into the chimney (chapter 13). Wright’s wall packs up.

George Mann Niedecken, the Milwaukee interior architect, is the shop name that belongs beside Wright’s on much of the Robie movable furniture. Wright designed. Niedecken’s studio executed oak pieces the house required. Robie himself left the house in 1911; later occupants and the University of Chicago’s near-demolition years scattered the suite. The Smart accession is a recovery, not a continuous household. The Hall brothers are the Pasadena version of that sentence (chapter 30). The American Arts and Crafts house that wanted custom furniture needed a mill that would take orders from a drawing. Eastwood took orders from a catalog. The jobs are not interchangeable.

## What Hanks was looking at

Hanks’s 1979 book — Dutton first, later Dover — is still the monograph a furniture historian opens before a house monograph. He is not writing a life of Wright. He is writing the decorative designs: chairs, tables, lamps, windows, the “certain simple form” Wright wanted to characterize one building. The Robie photograph he used came from the David and Alfred Smart Gallery. The caption’s inches are why a later copyist can be caught. The chairs in the 1911 *Ausgeführte Bauten* plates are the same set in the same room, photographed when the house was new.

Hanks will not let the dining chair float as a “Prairie style” SKU. He places it after the Oak Park home-and-studio experiments and beside the Dana and Martin work of 1903–04. The Oak Park dining chairs of 1895 already had the high slatted back and the wooden spheres that later disappear. By Robie the stiles run clean from crest to stretcher. The house has gone horizontal; the chairs have gone more vertical, as if to compensate. That compensation is the wall.

A reader who knows only the later reproductions knows a thinner chair. This chapter will not name the living firms that still sell a “Robie 1.” The original set has a museum number and a loan history. In 2019 the Smart Museum lent the table and chairs back to the Frank Lloyd Wright Trust for the restored dining room. A wall that can be inventoried is still a wall.

## Dana, Willits, and the temptation to pluralize

The Susan Lawrence Dana House in Springfield, finished 1902–04, is the other American dining room people mean when they say Wright high-back. Hanks gives it a long look: sumac glass, butterfly lamps, a painted frieze, three tables that can be joined for a party of forty. The chairs there are not one height. Formal dinner service at Dana’s scale wanted a visible distinction between places. That is a different social problem from Robie’s family table. Do not collapse them into “the Wright dining chair,” as if he issued a shop number.

The Ward W. Willits House chairs of 1902 are the documented cousin a European museum actually owns. The Victoria and Albert Museum’s dining chair W.4-1992, stained oak, leather later replaced, is one of a set of six. The V&A note is careful: Wright had already designed high-backs for his own house in 1895; the Willits backs, pulled up, form a second space in the room. Height there is even more extreme — a Wright chat that compared published numbers put the Willits side chair at 55¾ inches. Robie is a little lower and a little later. The idea is the same. The object is not.

Mackintosh’s Hill House chairs (1903–04) are the trap for a chapter that was allowed to say Hill. They are ebonized, laddered, barely sittable, made for a White Bedroom in Scotland. Comparison is honest if it stays at the level of a high back used as a spatial tool. It becomes fog when a dealer says both men were “doing Mission.” Mackintosh was not. Wright was not. Mission is a New York and Grand Rapids trade name (chapters 1, 6, 39). Wright sold houses, not suites.

## Why the oak fools people

The Robie chairs are oak. They have vertical slats. They are dark. A collector who learned the language in a 1980s gallery will feel the word Mission rising in the throat. The word is wrong. Wright’s early oak has the Arts and Crafts preference for straight lines and visible structure. Hanks says as much. The preference is not a catalog. Wright did not offer a matching davenport, rocker, and library table to a retailer who had never seen the lot. He designed furniture because he did not trust the client, or the store, to finish the room.

He said so. The 1910 German portfolio preface — the V&A quotes the English of it — refuses to consider the building, the furnishings, and the setting as three problems. “The very chairs and tables… are of the building itself.” That sentence is the opposite of McHugh’s branded rail and the opposite of a Sears Mission page. It is also the opposite of Stickley’s achievement, which was to print a monthly and a price list so a household that would never hire an architect could still buy a related chair. Wright’s dining chair is a custom wall. Stickley’s slat armchair is a product. Chapter 32 will put those two facts in one room and refuse to let them share a name.

Comfort is not the Robie chair’s job. The seat is a pad on a hard frame. The back is a screen at the spine. People who have sat in original or accurate examples report a posture the table requires. The Dana chairs, built for spectacle, are no kinder. Wright’s later office chairs would fail in other ways — Hanks is dry about the Johnson and Price seating that proved too precarious or too heavy. The 1908 dining chair is not a Morris chair (chapter 36). It does not recline. It encloses.

## What the chair is not

It is not a Craftsman shop number. It is not a Greene and Greene cloud-lift cousin; the Greenes were solving a different house with a different mill. It is not Spanish, not Franciscan, not a California mission survival. It is not “the Prairie chair,” as if one cube or one barrel could stand for a school. Purcell and Elmslie designed other furniture for other houses. Griffin did too. Prairie is an architectural argument that sometimes produced chairs. Mission is a look a factory could add between markets.

The slats on the Robie back are closer, visually, to a Stickley slat than a Wright spindle cube is. That is an accident of this particular dining problem. A wall wants a plane. Spindles, which chapter 38 will treat as a way to keep light moving, would have made a thinner enclosure. Wright used both grammars in his career. The dining chair as wall is the slat version of his spatial habit. Do not let that slat drag him into Eastwood’s catalog.

The house itself has been emptied and refilled. Furniture was sold; windows were removed; demolition was discussed; the University of Chicago and later the National Trust and the Frank Lloyd Wright Trust kept the shell. Original pieces now live as a split identity: Smart numbers, house loans, a sofa that has been in New York since 1982. Hanks’s page 107 is a photograph of a completeness the house had to borrow back. The wall around the table is therefore partly a museum wall. That does not make the design Mission. It makes the design mortal.

A bungalow in a later suburb can hold a dark slat armchair and a poster of Robie and still be a bungalow. The poster is not a chair. The chair in the dining room on Woodlawn was a wall for one family, at one table, under one set of lamps. The next essay puts that chair’s cousin — a spindle cube — next to a catalog slat armchair and asks the only question that matters once the photographs are put away: whose room is this?

Dimensions help when adjectives fail. Hanks and the Smart Museum give the side chair about 52⅜ inches of height on a seat roughly 17 by 19¾. That is a wall a seated person disappears into. A Stickley dining chair of the same years is shorter in the back and willing to be pulled away from the table. Wright’s chair does not want to be pulled away. It wants the table to remain a room. The difference is not taste. It is a job, and the job is architectural rather than retail. A store cannot stock a wall.


Woodlawn Avenue is a Chicago street. The dining chair is a wall on that street. A poster of the house is neither.
