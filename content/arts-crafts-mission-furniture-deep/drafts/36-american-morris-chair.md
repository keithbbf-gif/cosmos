# The American Morris Chair

The chair is shop number 2342, an early Gustav Stickley reclining armchair, red joiner’s-compass decal under the left arm of the kind used about 1902–04. Five horizontal slats make the back. Through-tenons show in the front posts. A rope foundation waits under a loose cushion. Catalogs and collectors call it a Morris chair. William Morris did not draw it. The name is a type, not a signature.

This chapter is the American type: oak, fumed or stained, adjustable, sold with a shop number from Eastwood and then from every plant that could copy a hinge. The English chair it borrowed is a Morris, Marshall, Faulkner & Co. product, later Morris & Co., adapted by Philip Webb from a Sussex carpenter’s armchair Warrington Taylor saw in 1866. Comfort is the whole point of both chairs. The manifesto is what American copy had to decide whether to keep.

## What Taylor sent Webb

Warrington Taylor, business manager of the young firm, found a vernacular late-Georgian armchair in the workshop of Ephraim Colman, a carpenter in Herstmonceux, Sussex, in 1866. He sent Webb a sketch and a description. The National Art Library at the V&A keeps the letter (86.SS.57). Webb designed much of the firm’s early furniture. He turned the sketch into an adjustable-back armchair the company produced from about 1869. The first version is often dated in the 1860s; marketing around 1866 travels in later summaries. The V&A’s own notes prefer the 1869 production date. Mark the slack `[VERIFY first advertised year]`.

The English chair uses a wooden rail and notches, or a rod, to set the back. Cushions are separate. Legs may be bobbin-turned. The finish may be ebonized — a Japanism of the 1870s — or left as mahogany or oak. Covers came in Utrecht velvet, chintz, or, on the Kenrick chair at the V&A, Morris’s “Violet and Columbine” wool. William and Jane Morris used the type at Kelmscott House. Burne-Jones had one. The 1912 Morris & Co. catalog still listed it. Liberty copied it in London. The chair was a commercial success before it was an American moral.

It was not a through-tenon sermon. It was a comfortable chair the firm could sell to industrialists and painters. John Pollard Seddon’s name sometimes attaches to related adjustable chairs; the Victorian Web and the Fine Art Society’s 1979 Morris and Company catalog keep Webb and the Colman prototype at the center. This chapter will not become a patent history. The American problem does not need every English cousin.

## What Eastwood did with the type

Stickley’s 1898 English trip (chapter 9) is often told as a pilgrimage. It was also a shopping trip. He saw shops that had already priced a moral. The Morris adjustable chair was one of the types an American factory could translate into quartersawn white oak without importing bobbin turning or Utrecht velvet. The translation is heavier. The arms become flat boards of radial oak (chapter 33). The posts take through-tenons (chapter 34). The back slats become five horizontals, or, in later spindle versions, a cage. The cushion becomes leather or canvas over rope or springs.

Shop 2342 is the early thick one. Dalton’s and other documented examples give the arm at about 36 inches, shorter than the later 332’s 38. The tenons stand proud. The seat is a rope grid. The red box decal with *Als ik kan* sits under the left arm. Christie’s has catalogued a 2342 at 38 inches high, 31 wide, 36 deep. Those inches are a sitting machine, not a dining wall (chapter 31).

Shop 332 is the later flat-arm slat Morris, about 1904 onward, still Eastwood, still adjustable, a little longer in the arm, a little more catalog-regular. Shop 369, about 1905–12, is the drop-arm spindle Morris: sides of spindles instead of slats, arms that slant, a back that still reclines. Shop 367 is a related spindle Morris. Shop 346 appears in later lists. The numbers matter because they are how Eastwood thought. Morris & Co. thought in a firm style and a fabric name. Stickley thought in a shop number you could telegraph.

L. & J.G. Stickley and Stickley Brothers made their own reclining chairs. Grand Rapids made “Morris chairs” by the carload, some with the rod-and-notch grammar, some with a cheap ratchet, some with a back that barely moves. Sears printed the name. Once the type was a type, the English letter in the V&A was nobody’s problem but a curator’s.

## Comfort against manifesto

*The Craftsman* sold a life that could be photographed: a settle, a table, a fire, a chair you could tip back with a book. Readers who turn Ruskin into a ban on comfort have not read the American magazine (chapter 2 already said this). Stickley sold cushions. He sold reclining chairs. He sold the idea that a joint you can see and a back you can adjust were the same honesty. They are not the same honesty. One is a load path. The other is an afternoon.

The manifesto wanted the worker to be visible in the chair. The cushion hides the worker. Rope seats and spring seats are shop labor; leather is another trade; the loose back pillow is a soft object that can be replaced without touching a tenon. A 2342 with replaced leather and original film on the arms is typical. The manifesto survives in the posts. The comfort survives in the parts that wear out.

American advertising sometimes treated the Morris chair as if Morris himself had designed a Craftsman oak recliner for Syracuse. He had not. He had been a principal in a firm that sold an English vernacular adaptation. The American chair is closer, in spirit, to Colman’s Sussex shop than to Red House as a myth. A carpenter’s comfortable armchair, thickened and darkened, shipped by rail: that is the honest genealogy. The dishonest genealogy is a poster of Morris with a long beard leaning on an Eastwood 332.

Wright put Morris-type armchairs in the Oak Park living room in the 1890s. The Wright Trust says so. Those chairs are English-minded comfort in a house that would soon invent the dining wall. Wright did not become a Mission designer by owning a reclining armchair. Stickley did not become Morris by numbering one.

## What the American chair refuses

It refuses bobbin turning, usually. It refuses the ebonized Japanism of the V&A’s black example, usually. It refuses a floral textile as the main event; the oak is the main event, the leather a second. It refuses, in the best Eastwood versions, a hidden ratchet in favor of a peg or a rod you can see — the through-tenon ethic applied to leisure. Cheap copies refuse nothing except expense: they will use a metal mechanism that fails, a stain that outlines the rays (chapter 35), a dowel where a tenon would have shown.

It also refuses, in Stickley’s own copy, the word Mission more often than the market did. Customers called the recliner a Mission Morris. The plant called it a reclining armchair and a shop number. Chapter 1’s vocabulary fight is audible here. A search box in 2026 will still return “Mission Morris chair” and mean a brown oak recliner with slats. The search is honest about the look. It is careless about the invoice.

Drop arms on the 369 confuse people who have just learned the drop-arm settle (chapter 37). The chair’s arms slant or drop as a posture; the settle’s arms drop as a conversion toward a bed. Different mechanisms, same appetite: the living hall wanted furniture that could change its mind in the afternoon.

## A type that outlived the magazine

Stickley’s bankruptcy in 1915 and the last *Craftsman* in December 1916 did not kill the Morris chair. The type was already public. Colonial Revival living rooms absorbed a darker, sometimes maple, recliner. Later American “porch Morris” chairs in painted wood are cousins by mechanism only. The collector market of the 1970s (chapter 42) put the Eastwood numbers back at the top of a hierarchy the 1908 buyer did not use. A 2342 with a red decal became a prize. A Grand Rapids Morris with a paper label became a remainder. Both recline. Both were answering Colman, whether they knew the name or not.

L. & J.G. Stickley sold reclining chairs of their own, Handcraft-marked, with a different corbel and sometimes a slat count that is not Gustav’s. They are not shop 2342. A Fayetteville recliner is a brotherly competitor, not a factory B. Grand Rapids understood the lean without needing the manifesto: a paper label, a price, a cushion. Most Americans who leaned back in 1910 leaned in those chairs, not in a 2342. The 1970s market ranked the decal. The body still ranked the angle of the back.

Shop 2342 remains the teaching chair: early, thick, pinned tenons, a back that moves on a visible rod or peg. Shop 332 is the later flat-arm most people mean. Shop 369 is the spindle drop-arm that will confuse the settle chapter. Three numbers, one English type, no Morris drawing. Patent histories of ratchets belong elsewhere. This chapter needs the peg you can see, the cushion that hides labor, and the American habit of putting Morris’s beard on an Eastwood invoice.

The residual tension is the cushion. The manifesto can live in a rail. The reason anyone bought shop 2342 is that the back goes back and the body follows. Morris & Co. knew that in 1869. Eastwood knew it in 1902. The American contribution is the oak, the shop number, and a catalog that dared to price leisure in the same moral language as a settle. A history that treats the recliner as a footnote to honest posts has the footnote and the text reversed. The next essay is the other hall piece that people misname as a sofa: the drop-arm settle, leather, tacks, a bench that remembers it is a bench. A chair that cannot lean is a chair that borrowed the name and forgot the type. Colman would have recognized the lean before he recognized the oak.
