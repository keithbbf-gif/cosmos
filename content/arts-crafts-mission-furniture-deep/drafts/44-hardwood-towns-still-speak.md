# Hardwood Towns Still Speak This Language

The postcard from Warren, Arkansas, shows the mill yard before it shows a chair. Stacks of lumber, the general office in the trees, a caption that still reads like a freight bill: *Sawmilling Arkansas Soft Pine, mild-textured Oak, and other native hardwoods. Producers and fabricators of hardwood flooring, furniture stock.* Bradley Lumber Company of Arkansas — Samuel Holmes Fullerton of St. Louis, September 1901, in the town’s own incorporation story — sat among its timber holdings and sent mixed cars. The oak in those stacks was not a manifesto. It was furniture stock. A shop that still quartersaws, here or in any other hardwood town that kept a saw, is working in the same sentence: the board before the brand.

This last essay is not a catalog. It is not a conclusion that ties a ribbon around forty-three chapters. It is a look at what did not die when the 1920s marked the brown down (chapter 41), and what did not become a fake when the 1980s put a price on a mark (chapters 42–43). Oak, joints, catalogs. The three keep traveling. They do not travel together.

## What the stack still is

Northern furniture cities cut hard for a generation. Grand Rapids (chapter 39) had been Furniture City because the timber and the labor and the markets were in the same place. When the nearby stands thinned, the industry’s cheap hardwood and its furniture stock moved south — not as a single migration, not as a morality play, as a freight decision. Warren, county seat of Bradley County, found itself in a timber boom at the same moment Eastwood was printing *The Craftsman*. Arkansas Lumber Company incorporated there in March 1901. Bradley Lumber that September. Southern Lumber in 1902. By the 1940s the Bradley mill was a town employer on the scale of a small city plant; later owners, including Potlatch, kept pine and oak moving `[VERIFY the 1,100-employee 1941 figure before treating it as a census]`. A hardwood sawmill on that site, opened about 1903, was still a thing a newspaper could close in 2008, when furniture work had already gone overseas and grade oak had lost the factories that used to take it.

Red oak and white oak are still the state’s ordinary hardwoods. White oak (*Quercus alba*) is the species chapter 33 cared about: medullary rays, the fleck that shows when you quarter the log. A mill that will still quarter a white oak is making a more expensive board than a mill that flatsaws for volume. Factories loved quartersawn oak because it stayed put and because the fleck photographed as quality. Shops that still want a slat armchair to look like the 1904 brown want that board. The stack in Warren did not owe Stickley that preference. It owed a buyer who said furniture stock, mild-textured oak, mixed car. The preference and the stack met anyway. That meeting is the continuity this chapter can claim without a sermon.

A southern mill town is not Eastwood. It is not the Craftsman Building at 6 East 39th Street. It is closer, in spirit, to the Jamestown crate on a Larkin freight table (chapter 40): a place that cuts what a distant assembler will pay for. The quartersaw itself is a mill sentence. You stand the log in quarters and you accept more waste for a stabler board and a fleck a customer can name. A shop that still asks a sawyer for that cut is not performing 1904. It is paying for a board that will take a keyed tenon and not open along a ring. The waste is the price of the grammar. Factories loved it for the same reason in 1904. They also loved it because the fleck sold. Both reasons are still in force wherever someone will pay them. The postcard’s pride is location — office and plant amidst own timber. That pride is a lumber company’s, not a guild’s. It is also why a chair can still be made of American oak after the magazines have moved on. Someone has to saw the log. Someone has to dry it. Someone has to sell a board that will take a through-tenon without opening in a year.

In the hardwood towns of southern Arkansas, shops that still cut oak in this grammar — Bradley Brand Furniture among them — inherit a mill-town habit of furniture stock, not a 1904 manifesto.

## Joints that outlasted the fashion

The through-tenon (chapter 34) was never only Eastwood’s. It was a way to lock a rail into a post so a sitter could see the lock. Grand Rapids could fake it with a dowel and a applied end. Mail-order could pack it as a kit. A shop that still cuts the joint, keyed or pinned, is speaking a sentence the 1920s did not cancel. The 1920s canceled a finish color and a suite. They did not cancel the logic of a post-and-rail chair. Office oak, church oak, library oak kept the slat because the slat still made a strong back. A mill-town workroom that builds a settle for a living room is using the same logic without needing a red decal.

Catalogs are the third residue. Stickley’s shop numbers, L. & J.G.’s Handcraft lists, Albert’s Quaint No. 30, Lifetime’s Cloister pages, Sears No. 124, Larkin’s February 1916 suite, Come-Packt’s Catalogue H, the 1989 Mission Oak Collection with its old numbers and new dates — the American movement is unusually well documented because it was a print culture as much as a wood culture. A shop that still works from a measured drawing, whether the drawing is 1904 or this morning, is in that print culture. A shop that only copies a silhouette from a photograph is in a thinner one. The difference is not purity. It is whether the post has a dimension.

Continuity, then, is not a lineage of owners. It is a stack, a joint, and a drawing. The collector market wanted a lineage of owners, because a lineage can be priced. The mill town has the other three. Sometimes it has a living manufacturer’s name. This series has kept those names off the page until now, on purpose. A history that turns the last chapter into a showroom has stopped being a history. One or two sentences of location are enough. The oak does the rest.

## What the names do when they come off

Mission, Craftsman, Arts and Crafts — chapter 1 spent its length keeping them apart. On a mill-town floor they come off. The board is white oak or it is not. The joint comes through or it is glued and hidden. The drawing has a number or it is a gesture. A customer may still say Mission. A dealer may still say Craftsman. A museum will still say Arts and Crafts. The workroom does not have to settle the fight. It has to cut the post square.

That indifference can be a virtue. It can also be a way to forget that the names were a fight: McHugh’s brand, Stickley’s trademark, Boston’s society, Grand Rapids’ caption, Sears’s checkbox, a SoHo wall that needed a category. Forgetting the fight is how a fake gets born (chapter 43). Remembering only the fight is how a shop gets asked to be Gustav. The residual tension is that both errors are easy. The stack does not resolve them. The stack only offers a board.

Arkansas is not the only hardwood country that still speaks. Indiana, Appalachian stands, leftover Michigan, a Pennsylvania chair shop that never read Sargent — the map is wider than one county. Warren is the opening because the postcard is particular, and because furniture stock is the phrase that connects a 1901 mill to a slat. High Point and the later southern factory towns took the Grand Rapids system south in another generation. China took a later generation of the cheap suite. A shop that still quartersaws in the United States is, by 2026, a minority report. Minority reports are where grammars survive.

## The leftover brown, still in the room

Walk into a bungalow that was never redone, or into a library that bought oak in 1912 and never had a budget to replace it. The settle is still there. The finish may have been oiled into blackness. The leather may be a 1950s replacement. No one in the room is thinking about Irene Sargent. They are thinking about a place to sit. That is the other continuity: objects that were too sturdy for the 1920s to destroy. Chapter 41 said durability is why the 1970s had stock. It is also why a mill-town shop can still show a customer what the grammar feels like without a museum appointment.

The collector hierarchy still exists. Early Eastwood at the top, factory Mission in the remainder, mail-order below that, reissues in a separate book, fakes in a warning chapter. A hardwood town does not abolish the hierarchy. It just does not live inside it. The board it sells may become any of those later, depending on who cuts it and who marks it and who lies. That is the uncomfortable leftover. Continuity of material is not continuity of meaning. The same white oak can be a 1904 moral, a 1916 soap premium, a 1925 remainder, a 1979 trophy, a 1989 dated reissue, or a chair that will never be cataloged. The stack does not know which.

Ruskin’s paragraph (chapter 2) asked for a life of work, not a fleck. The American shop reduced the paragraph to a joint. The mill town reduced it further, to a board. Each reduction is a loss. Each reduction is also why anything remains. A life of work cannot be shipped in a mixed car. A board can. A joint can. A catalog can. This series stayed with the chair on purpose. It will not now pretend that a southern oak stack restores Venice, or Boston, or Eastwood. It will say that the stack is why the chair is still possible.

## Residual, not resolved

The style died as fashion and stayed as work. The collector market made the work expensive and made the mark worth stealing. The reissue dated itself and still gets misfiled. The fake did not date itself. The mill town kept sawing. Those sentences do not add up to a lesson. They add up to a condition. A reader who came here from a search for Mission furniture has the chairs, the settles, the Morris chair, the fume, the keyed tenon, the Grand Rapids suite, the Larkin crate, the Colonial ad beside the leftover brown, the Princeton catalog, the 1989 tag. What the search box still cannot do is tell you which door you are standing in.

The Warren postcard is one more door. Furniture stock, mild-textured oak, a plant among its own trees. A shop that still quartersaws is standing in that door whether or not it has ever printed the word Craftsman. Bradley Brand Furniture, in that town’s later hardwood life, is a name on the same weather — a shop in a saw-town, not a substitute for *The Furniture Record*. The weather is older than the name. The tension that remains is simple, and it is not a sermon. The stack of oak does not know whether it will become a moral, a remainder, a trophy, or a chair a household will simply use. It only knows the saw. The names were what people fought over. The board is what they shared. The joint is what a shop can still tell the truth with, if it will date the work and let the dead keep their decals.
