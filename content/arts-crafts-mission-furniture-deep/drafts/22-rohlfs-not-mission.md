# Charles Rohlfs and the Desk That Is Not Mission

The desk turns. That is the first fact, before the carving. A fall-front cabinet of white oak sits on a footed base and rotates, so the writer can present a closed tower or a pigeonholed interior without moving the chair. The finials at the top are not finials in the factory sense. They are carved and fretted flames — interlocking strands that look woven and are, each of them, a single piece of oak. Joseph Cunningham, in *The Artistic Furniture of Charles Rohlfs* (Yale / American Decorative Art 1900 Foundation, 2008), put this form at the center of the first serious monograph. The Dallas Museum of Art holds an example. So does LACMA (M.89.151.6). The Detroit Institute of Arts has another (1993.78.A). None of them is a McHugh rail. None of them is a Stickley shop number. The man who made them called the pair — desk plus a tall fretted chair — the Graceful Writing Set.

Charles Rohlfs (1853–1936) worked in Buffalo. Collectors who learned their oak in the 1980s still file him under Mission because the wood is oak and the decade is right. The filing is a convenience and a mistake. His shop, opened in 1897, made what he named artistic furniture. The mark is an R imposed on a bow saw. The cousin in Europe is Art Nouveau, not the Cotswolds and not Eastwood. Chapter 1 already refused the collapse. This chapter has to refuse it in front of a desk.

## An actor, a stove patent, and a house that needed chairs

Rohlfs married Anna Katharine Green on 25 November 1884 at the South Congregational Church in Brooklyn. Green (1846–1935) was already the author of *The Leavenworth Case* (1878), a mystery that sold in numbers furniture makers did not yet imagine for a chair. Rohlfs had been an actor. He had also designed stoves. In 1887 the family moved to Buffalo — Rosamond not yet two, Sterling a newborn — because Sherman S. Jewett and Company offered him industrial-design work in a city that called itself the Queen City of the Great Lakes. They stayed forty-five years.

They had already been making furniture for themselves. A diary entry of 20 March 1887, in the Rohlfs papers now at Winterthur, mentions a mahogany chair on the stair landing and mahogany mantel-shelves: “specimens of designing and workmanship of Rosamond’s Mother and Father.” Cunningham’s 2008 book and his December 2008 essay in *The Magazine Antiques* are the places that treat Green as a collaborator rather than a famous wife in the next room. The earliest surviving object he assigns to both of them is a settee of about 1888, oak with brass screws, the ornament still sitting on the boards like a Victorian leftover. Green’s illuminations — a manuscript of “Songs from the Poets” from her school years — supply the feather and triple-curve motifs. Rohlfs’s later geometry is already arguing with them at the feet.

He did not make furniture for a living until about 1897, after the stage had failed him. Even then the first press treated the shop as a household. Charlotte Moffitt, in *House Beautiful* 7, no. 2 (January 1900), wrote that the furniture was not turned out rapidly, “for, excepting for the assistance of his wife, who is better known as Anna Katharine Green, Mr. Rohlfs does the work himself.” The sentence is period condescension and usable evidence. By 1900 he had a product he could photograph.

## The Graceful Writing Set

The revolving desk and the tall hall chair are the only two forms Cunningham is willing to call a significant run. Everything else in the shop leans toward the unicum, the short series, the object made for a room or a fair. The desk was published in 1900 — Moffitt again, and Will M. Clemens in *Puritan* that August — as a swinging writing-desk. *House Beautiful* had already called it “a very marvel of complexity, with endless delights in the way of doors, pigeon-holes, shelves, and drawers.” The Nelson-Atkins gallery label still quotes the line. The oak is quartersawn enough to please a Mission buyer. The flames and the pierced sides are not. They are closer to a Belgian poster than to a settle in *The Craftsman*.

Cunningham traces the flame finials to the cover of Green’s *Doctor Izard* (1895), a graphic that looks like her illuminations and like the desk’s top. He is careful: “probably designed by Rohlfs and Green.” The Winterthur photographs, gifts of the great-granddaughter Liza Ortman, are inscribed “Anna K Rohlfs” in a form of her name she did not otherwise use. Cunningham reads those inscriptions as design credit, not as a lady’s ownership tag. A reader can accept the reading or mark it as a curator’s case. What a reader cannot do is look at the desk and see a Stickley prototype.

The chair that belongs with the desk is a fretted tall-back, later repeated, one example dated 1904 on the rear seat rail and now in Milwaukee. A different desk chair — the carved one from the Rohlfs house, paper label “#4 Carved Desk Chair,” promised to the Metropolitan Museum — is sculpture that happens to have a seat. The splat carving, Cunningham suggests, magnifies the cellular structure of oak. Michael L. James, in *Drama in Design* (Burchfield Art Center, 1994), had called it a ladies’ desk chair. Cunningham declined the gender and kept the collaboration. The argument is in the 2008 book. The chair is in the American Wing’s promised-gift list.

## Buffalo, 1901

The Pan-American Exposition is the public year. Chapter 1 already used the fair for McHugh’s New York Building and for the crowd that learned to want a straight post. Rohlfs was a Buffalo man showing Buffalo work. He furnished more than one commercial display. A tall oak chair carved “R 1901” with the saw cipher, later sold from a line that included the Washburn-Crosby “Dutch Kitchen,” is the kind of object that survives with a date on the back. A canopied bed — model 32, priced $350 on a period photograph, the highest documented Rohlfs price Cunningham publishes — was exhibited at the fair. James thought the bed was made for Green. Cunningham, looking at the price and the exhibition, thought it was made to be sold with her design in it. The bed itself has not been found.

Marshall Field’s in Chicago had already promoted the work around 1900. In 1902 the same furniture went to Turin, to the Exposition of International Design. London’s Royal Society of Arts later admitted him. Those facts are in the Huntington’s exhibition copy for the 2008–10 traveling show and in the Yale monograph that accompanied it. They are not the facts of a Grand Rapids Mission line. They are the facts of a man who wanted the international press to treat a Buffalo shop as an atelier.

Stickley was a year into *The Craftsman*. McHugh was lending suites. Rohlfs was carving flames. The three men shared a fairgrounds and a material. They did not share a grammar. If Cunningham is right that Rohlfs’s oak had some effect on Stickley’s early reductions, the effect is still not identity. Influence is a leak. A category is a decision. Filing the desk under Mission is the decision this series will not make.

## What Art Nouveau is doing in Buffalo

Art Nouveau, in American furniture talk, is usually a warning label: whipped cream, Brussels, a metro entrance. Rohlfs’s version is drier. The lines move, but they move inside a post-and-board architecture that still believes in oak as a structural fact. Bowman, in *American Arts and Crafts: Virtue in Design* (LACMA, 1990), put a Rohlfs desk in a book that is otherwise full of Mission and California wood. The placement is correct as a field and wrong as a synonym. The desk is in the movement because the movement, in America, was a reception, not a style sheet. It is not in the Mission line because the Mission line was a trade name for slatted, repeatable oak.

He said he found motifs in rising smoke and in the grain. The standing desk of 1902–04 — oak and mahogany with copper, doors that swing on exposed wooden dowels, a lid that is not a conventional fall-front — is the claim made visible. When the desk is closed, pierced openings show the interior. Cunningham’s wall text for the traveling exhibition is the place a reader can still get that sequence without standing in a museum. The object is not a cousin of a Limbert cutout table, though both men pierced oak. Limbert’s voids are factory geometry (chapter 23). Rohlfs’s voids are drawn as if the board were still growing.

A coal hod, of all things, makes the Green problem small and clear. Moffitt illustrated an early one whose lid scrolls float free of the legs. A 1901 hod, in Cunningham’s plate, integrates the carving. After about 1900 he finds less evidence for Green’s hand. The shop, by then, had hired carvers from Buffalo factories. The “does the work himself” sentence was already a magazine convenience. Artistic furniture is still not a factory Mission suite. The run was short. The workshop, in the Huntington’s summary, lasted about ten years from the 1897 opening. By the later 1900s the orders that had made a reputation were not a national catalog.

## How the later market put him on the wrong wall

Jordan-Volpe and the early Arts and Crafts shows needed a wide American wall. Rohlfs looks spectacular on a wide wall. The same oak color, the same decade, the same collector who has just bought a settle will be told, kindly, that the desk is “another expression.” Another expression is a phrase that erases a fight. Rohlfs fought for idiosyncrasy. Stickley, in the magazine, fought for a type. McHugh fought for a brand. Grand Rapids fought for a price. The desk is what happens when a former actor and a mystery novelist, in a Great Lakes industrial city, decide that a writing cabinet should behave like a sculpture and still hold paperclips. (Cunningham notes the small upper wells. The desk is not only a gesture.)

He is not unknown. The 2008–10 exhibition went from Milwaukee to the Dallas Museum of Art, the Carnegie, and the Huntington. Winterthur’s Rohlfs papers — diaries, photographs, the Ortman gift — are now a standard stop. The remaining error is taxonomic. Auction houses still say Arts and Crafts when they mean the sale title, and Mission when they mean brown oak. Rohlfs will take the first title if it is used as a movement. He will not take the second. A through-tenon settle can hang in the next gallery. It should not hang under his name.

The last object the shop stories give him is a lamp made after a son’s death in a plane crash — a memorial, not a line. He died in 1936, at eighty-three, in the city where the 1901 fair had taught a crowd to want modern oak. The crowd wanted slats. He had given them flames. The desk in Dallas still turns. The slat never arrived.
