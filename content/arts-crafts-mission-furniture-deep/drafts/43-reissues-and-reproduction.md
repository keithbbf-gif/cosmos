# Reissues and the Reproduction Problem

The tag on a 1989 Mission Oak Collection chair is not a red decal from Eastwood. It is a burned current shopmark beside the older L. & J.G. device — the co-joined mark the firm had used since the late 1910s — and a cabinetmaker’s date. The factory, still speaking from Fayetteville while work moved toward a new plant in Manlius, was telling a buyer, in hardware, that this was a reissue and not a 1904 chair that had been waiting in a barn. Alfred Audi, interviewed that spring, said the line used construction the present company already stood on, and that the market for original pieces had become visible enough to answer `[Chicago Tribune, 30 April 1989]`.

This essay is not a brand advertisement. It is not a brand attack. L. & J.G. Stickley is a continuing historic firm: Leopold and John George at Fayetteville (chapter 16), Early American when Mission died (chapter 41), a thin payroll by the early 1970s, then a second life under new owners. A reissue from that firm is one kind of new oak. A fake with a distressed finish and a lying mark is another. A shop that still cuts through-tenons in quartersawn white oak because that is how this chair is made, without pretending the chair left Eastwood in 1904, is a third. The collector market of the 1970s and 1980s (chapter 42) created the incentive for all three. The objects get stupid when the three are merged.

## What the 1989 line was

Alfred and Aminy Audi bought L. & J.G. Stickley in 1974 from the family that still held it after Leopold’s death in 1957. Company histories put the shop at a couple of dozen people and a small year’s sales; the exact figures wander by teller `[VERIFY 1974 headcount and the $235,000 sales figure that travels with the purchase story]`. For fifteen years the surviving business was the Colonial and mahogany work that had kept Fayetteville alive: Cherry Valley, then an 18th-century mahogany and cherry line in 1984, a new plant at Manlius in the mid-1980s `[VERIFY 1985 as the move year]`. Mission was not the payroll. Mission was the thing the auction rooms had started to treat as a specialty.

The 1988 Christie’s sideboard that became a Streisand-era headline (chapter 42) is the date later firm histories name as a prompt. Whether a single lot decided a factory or only confirmed what Cathers and Jordan-Volpe had already made obvious, the factory acted. Studying old catalogs, shop records, and borrowed antiques, Alfred Audi and the designer William DeBlaay put more than thirty forms back into production. The Mission Oak Collection is dated April 1989. The *Chicago Tribune* of 30 April described about thirty dining, bedroom, and occasional pieces, many wearing original catalog numbers but dated by the present cabinetmakers. Quarter-sawn white oak. A current logo burned beside a 1916 solid brass shopmark, in the Tribune’s wording — the metal and the burn are worth checking against a surviving 1989 chair `[VERIFY brass versus burned 1917 device; firm later descriptions mention both]`.

A reissue, in the sense this chapter needs, is a new object made from an old drawing by a shop that has a historic claim on the drawing, marked so that a careful buyer can tell the date. It is not a 1904 chair. It is not a lie if the mark is dated. It becomes a problem when a later seller forgets the date, or scrapes it, or talks as if ammonia and a Saturday had produced a Craftsman original. The 1989 catalog, which this series will not quote as a sales pitch, is useful as evidence that the firm wanted the distinction printed. Side-hung drawers and other later construction details were, by the company’s own later account, part of that distinction. A collector who knows 1904 hardware can see the difference. A collector who only knows the silhouette cannot.

## What a fake is

A fake is a new or altered object that borrows a name the shop did not give it. The 1980s market made fakes rational. If a red decal and a fumed surface could move a chair out of “used furniture” and into “important,” someone would print a decal. Someone would distress a rail. Someone would burn a joiner’s compass into a Grand Rapids post. Bruce Johnson’s later price-guide editions and the Rago–Johnson book made detection a chapter because detection had become a need. This essay will not write a field manual. It will say what the category is.

There are grades. A honest reproduction by a shop that never claimed to be Stickley — a 1990s “Mission” suite from a factory that used the trade name the way *The Furniture Record* had used it — is not a fake. It is factory Mission’s grandchild (chapter 39). A chair that carries a counterfeit Eastwood mark, or a paper label photocopied from a catalog, is a fake. A genuine old chair with a later mark added to “improve” it is a fake of a meaner kind: the object was already true as oak and false as authorship. Refinish problems (chapter 35) sit next to this. A stripped and restained 1910 settle is not a fake. It is a damaged document. A stripped settle with a new decal is a damaged document that has been made to lie.

J. M. Young of Camden, New York, complicates the middle. Later histories say Young kept cutting some Gustav and L. & J.G. models, mainly chairs, for institutional orders after the style had died in the parlor `[VERIFY]`. Those chairs, if they exist as described, are neither 1904 Eastwood nor 1989 Manlius. They are a shop still making in a borrowed grammar for a purchasing agent. Marks, if any, have to be read as Young’s, not as a miracle survival. The collector market was not always careful. A hierarchy that only knows “Gus” and “not Gus” will misfile a Camden chair and a Grand Rapids rocker in the same remainder bin.

## What a shop still making in the grammar is

The third category is the one a living workroom actually occupies. A shop that quartersaws oak, cuts a through-tenon, and builds a slatted armchair without a 1904 shop number is not a reissue and not a fake. It is a continuation of factory skill and of the portable moral chapter 2 left in the trade: a joint you can see. It may be better or worse than Eastwood. It is not Eastwood. The collector language that asks such a shop to be “authentic Stickley” is the 1980s category leaking into a workbench. Chapter 1 already refused that leak. This chapter refuses it again, from the other end of the century.

Reproduction, as a word, is too large. It covers the 1989 dated chair, the lying decal, the honest new suite, and the Camden institutional seat. English furniture history has had this problem since the first Chippendale revival. American Mission has it in a tighter window, because the first life of the style was short and the leftover oak was plentiful, and because the 1970s assigned sudden money to a mark. A short first life plus a rich afterlife is a recipe for confusion. The cure is not a sermon about honesty. The cure is a dated tag, a catalog, a refusal to scrape the date, and a sentence that names the shop that actually cut the post.

L. & J.G. as a continuing firm sits in two categories at once. The 1920s Colonial work is neither reissue nor fake; it is the plant surviving (chapter 41). The 1989 Mission Oak Collection is a reissue. The shop after 1989 that keeps cutting those forms, dated, is a historic manufacturer making new furniture in an old grammar. None of that is a crime. None of it is 1904. A buyer who wants 1904 needs a different kind of looking — Cathers, a mark that has not been improved, a provenance that is not a celebrity headline. A buyer who wants the grammar needs to know whether the shop will say so.

## Why the 1980s needed the tag

Without the collector market, Fayetteville might have stayed in cherry. The Audis’ fifteen years of Colonial work were not a failure of nerve. They were the order book. Princeton, Jordan-Volpe, *Antiques*, Cathers, the 1988 rooms at Christie’s — those made Mission oak a product a factory could price again for households that could not buy the leftover brown at the new leftovers’ prices. The *Tribune* said the quiet part: there is a market; original pieces are getting money; a new line will bring the feeling to people who cannot pay the auction feeling. That sentence can be read as democratic or as opportunistic. It is both. A factory is allowed to be both. A historian is not allowed to pretend the 1989 chair is a farm-sale discovery.

The tag is the ethical piece of the opportunistic sentence. Dated, doubled marks, a catalog that shows the old number and the new construction — those are how a historic firm stays out of the fake bin. When a later dealer removes the date, the firm’s ethics leave the object. The object then travels as a problem. Reproductions “bring new life to the movement,” the *Tribune* headline said. Life is the wrong metaphor if it means the dead have sat up. What came back was a product line and a set of drawings. The movement’s arguments — wage, guild, house as teacher — did not come back with the drawings. Chapter 2’s leftover sentence, the honest joint, sometimes did. That is not nothing. It is not 1901.

Other shops, in the same years, issued “Mission” lines without a Stickley letterhead. Some were careful about names. Some printed Craftsman as a synonym, spending a word Gustav had spent money to own (chapter 1). This chapter will not hunt them. It will say that the word problem returned with the furniture. Arts and Crafts, Mission, Craftsman — the three names blurred on the 1970s wall and blurred again on the 1990s showroom card. A reissue that says L. & J.G. and 1989 is, at least, a name and a year. A card that says “authentic Craftsman oak” with no shop and no date is the blur for sale.

## A chair, two dates

Stand a 1904 Eastwood slat armchair, a 1989 Mission Oak Collection armchair, and an unmarked new chair from a shop that still works in slats. The silhouettes agree, as they agreed in chapter 39. The marks do not. The finishes do not, if you know what fume and later stain do in a raking light. The through-tenon, if all three have one, is the only sentence they share without lying. That sentence is a method. It is not an authorship. The reproduction problem is what happens when a market pays for authorship and a shop is paid for method. The two payments look the same from across a room.

L. & J.G. Stickley, as a continuing historic firm, is allowed to be in that room. So is any workroom that will date its work and not borrow a dead man’s decal. The fake is the chair that will not date itself. The leftover 1910 oak that the 1920s could not sell and the 1970s learned to price is a fourth object, and still the one a museum wants when the question is 1904. These essays have tried to keep the four apart. The last essay leaves the auction room. It goes to a mill-town stack of oak — southern, still sawn, still furniture stock — and asks what, if anything, of this language is left when the names come off.
