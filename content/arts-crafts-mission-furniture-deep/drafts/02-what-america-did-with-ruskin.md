# What America Did with Ruskin

The sentence Americans liked was not a chair. It was a paragraph from the second volume of *The Stones of Venice* (1853), the chapter Ruskin called “The Nature of Gothic.” He had been looking at workmen and at ornaments that still showed a hand. He wanted a reader in industrial England to feel the difference between a man who was allowed to invent a leaf and a man who was asked to repeat a leaf forever. American printers put that chapter into cheap editions. American professors assigned it. American furniture men quoted it when they needed a moral for a catalog that also listed prices.

This essay is not a life of Ruskin. The life is English, long, and already written. The American problem is smaller and ruder: which sentences crossed the Atlantic, who printed them, and what a factory in Eastwood or a society in Boston was willing to do with them once the oak was in the kiln.

The paragraph also traveled as a book of its own. Morris’s Kelmscott Press printed *The Nature of Gothic* in 1892, a slim object American reformers could hold as a relic. Cheaper American houses had already been reprinting *The Stones of Venice* for students who would never see St. Mark’s `[VERIFY a specific New York or Boston reprint house and year before naming one on a caption]`. Furniture men did not need the relic. They needed a sentence that would fit a catalog. The relic stayed on the Boston side of the split. The sentence went to Syracuse.

## Norton, Boston, and a society with minutes

Charles Eliot Norton, at Harvard, is the usual American door. He knew Ruskin, edited him for students, and helped a generation of New Englanders treat Gothic labor as an ethical subject and not only as an architectural style. The Society of Arts and Crafts, Boston, founded in 1897, is the institutional door. Kaplan’s *The Art that is Life* puts the society where it belongs: before Stickley’s magazine, after the Centennial shock, beside a group of people who thought an exhibition and a jury could reform the rooms of a city. Minutes and catalogs from those early years are full of metal, pottery, and printing. Furniture is there. It is not yet a slatted national product.

Boston’s Arts and Crafts was closer to London’s exhibition society than to Grand Rapids. It wanted standards. It wanted names. It wanted to keep the phrase Arts and Crafts attached to a maker you could write to. That is a different ambition from McHugh’s branded rail. It is also a different ambition from Stickley’s monthly, which wanted to reach a household that would never join a society. Eileen Boris is the historian who keeps this split visible. *Art and Labor* will not let a through-tenon stand in for a wage.

When later writers say the American movement “comes from Ruskin and Morris,” they are repeating a Boston sentence. The sentence is not false. It is incomplete. The movement also comes from a planing mill, a leather supplier, a freight rate, and a reading public that had already bought Eastlake by the tens of thousands (chapter 3). Ruskin supplied the caption. He did not supply the quartersaw.

## What *The Craftsman* took, and what it left

Irene Sargent, a Syracuse University professor, wrote the early *Craftsman* essays that taught Stickley’s readers how to talk. The first volume (October 1901 onward) is full of European names: Morris, Ruskin, Ashbee, the guild idea. Sargent could move from a Venetian paragraph to an American oak settle without pretending the settle was medieval. That tact is the American reception at its best. The magazine needed a genealogy. It also needed to sell furniture and house plans to people who lived in a balloon-frame house, not in a palazzo.

Stickley organized United Crafts in 1901 with a profit-sharing story and a guild vocabulary. The Winterthur finding aid and the Craftsman Farms histories are careful: he remained the owner. The name implied a cooperative. The payroll implied a factory. Ruskin’s Gothic workman, in this setting, is a rhetorical employee. He is not on the time sheet. Chapter 11 will walk the Eastwood plant, where machines and hands shared the same chair. This chapter only needs the language the plant borrowed.

*The Craftsman* quoted “The Nature of Gothic” the way a church quotes a psalm: often, selectively, as if the rest of the book were understood. What dropped out, in most American shop talk, was Ruskin’s hatred of the machine as such, his specific Venetian examples, and his later, stranger politics. What stayed was a portable idea: a joint you can see is a moral; a veneer that pretends to be a board is a lie. That portable idea is useful. It is also thinner than the book. A Grand Rapids factory could adopt the look of the moral — a through-tenon, a flat stretcher, a dark stain — and ignore the wage. Many did. Chapter 39 is that story.

Sargent’s early numbers also did a quieter job: they gave American readers a short list of English names they could drop in a parlor and a longer list of American rooms they could actually build. A reader in Ohio did not need to have stood in the Ducal Palace. He needed to know why a slat was better than an applied garland. The magazine made that translation over and over, which is why later dealers treat the first volume as scripture. It is scripture with advertising.

## Morris as a second American sentence

William Morris arrived in American rooms as wallpaper, as a cheap Sussex chair in a few imported interiors, and as a political name. Fiona MacCarthy and E. P. Thompson are the English lives. American furniture history does not need those lives again. It needs the fact that Morris & Co. was a firm with a catalog, and that American readers met him as both socialist and shopkeeper. Stickley’s 1898 crossing (chapter 9) is often told as a pilgrimage. It was also a shopping trip. He saw shops that had already solved the problem of selling a moral at a price.

Ashbee’s Guild of Handicraft, which left London for Chipping Campden in 1902, was easier to admire than to copy. An American factory town did not relocate to a Cotswold village. Eastwood was a suburb of Syracuse with rail and labor. Roycroft, in East Aurora, came closer to the campus fantasy (chapter 19), and even Roycroft printed and manufactured at a scale Ruskin would not have blessed. The American reception of English guild socialism is a story of partial uptake. The furniture shows the part that could be priced.

## What a factory could not use

Ruskin wanted irregularity as a sign of life. A factory that has just bought a bandsaw wants regularity as a sign of control. American Arts and Crafts furniture lives in that argument without resolving it. Stickley’s best chairs are regular. The posts match. The slats are spaced on purpose. The “handmade” quality is in the chamfer, the fume, the hardware, and the refusal of applied gingerbread — not in a medieval wonkiness that would have failed a retailer. Greene and Greene, in Pasadena, took the opposite American path: more time per joint, more ebony, more cloud lift, a house client who could pay for irregularity that was in fact extreme control (chapters 29–30). Neither path is Ruskin’s Venice. Both paths used his name.

There is a second thing a factory could not use: Ruskin’s suspicion of the Renaissance, of polish, of the very idea of a drawing-room. American clients still wanted a drawing-room. They wanted it simpler. *The Craftsman* offered them a living hall with a settle and a table that could take a meal. That is a reform of the Victorian parlor (the Eastlake parlor of chapter 3, the horsehair parlor of the older survey series). It is not the abolition of the parlor. Readers who turn Ruskin into a ban on comfort have not read the American magazine. Stickley sold cushions. He sold reclining chairs. He sold a life that could be photographed.

## Teachers, settlement houses, and the other Ruskin

The sentence also went to places that did not sell oak. Settlement houses, manual-training schools, and women’s societies used Ruskin to justify clay, metal, and a workbench in a city. Boris again: the craftsman ideal in America is larger than the furniture trade. A series about Mission furniture can forget that and become a catalog of men. This chapter keeps the wider reception in view so the later shop chapters do not pretend to own the word craft.

Adelaide Alsop Robineau’s porcelain, the Boston society’s jewelry, the Chicago workshops, the California potters — none of them is a Stickley settle. They are the field in which the settle made sense. A moral about honest work that only attaches to a $30 oak chair (or whatever the 1902 price list said `[VERIFY shop number and price]`) is a moral that has already been captured by a product. The societies were trying, with mixed success, to keep the moral attached to a person.

Chicago’s Industrial Art League and the later crafts societies around Hull House used the same English books for a city argument. A settlement class that taught a boy to cut a joint was not trying to produce a collectible. It was trying to produce a person who had made one complete thing. Boris and the Chicago settlement literature are the paper trail; this series will not pretend those classrooms were Eastwood’s research department. They were a parallel American use of Ruskin that the furniture trade cited when it was convenient and ignored when an order was due Friday.

There was also a church use. Episcopal and Swedenborgian interiors, university halls, and a few libraries ordered oak that looked “honest” because the building committee had read the same paragraph. The Swedenborgian chairs in San Francisco (chapter 7) sit in that weather: a reform interior, a local joiner, a New York retailer who stole the wrong noun. Ruskin would have recognized the beamed room more quickly than he would have recognized a Sears suite. He would not have recognized the freight bill.

## The leftover sentence in a shop today

A contemporary shop that still cuts a through-tenon will sometimes say, without quoting, that the joint should be honest. That leftover sentence is Ruskin after a century of American use: stripped of Venice, stripped of the wage argument, attached to a detail a customer can photograph. It is not nothing. A joint you can see is still a better teacher than a wrap of veneer that hides particle board. It is also not enough. The English critic was talking about a whole life of work. The American shop is talking about a chair.

This series will stay with the chair, on purpose. It will not pretend a furniture history is a labor history. It will not, either, pretend the caption was invented in Syracuse. Ruskin wrote the paragraph. Boston taught it. Sargent translated it for a monthly. Eastwood and Grand Rapids decided how much of it would survive contact with a price list. The next essay is the other English book Americans actually bought in bulk: Eastlake’s *Hints on Household Taste*, in the Boston edition of 1872, and the factory furniture that stole his name faster than Mission ever did.
