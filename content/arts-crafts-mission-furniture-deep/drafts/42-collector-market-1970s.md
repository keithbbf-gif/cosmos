# The Collector Market, 1970s–80s

The May 1977 American furniture issue of *The Magazine Antiques* printed “The furniture of Gustav Stickley” under the names Carol Lorraine Bohdan and Todd Mitchell Volpe. The cover of that issue was not a slat chair. It was a Hook chamber organ in Salem, because that is the magazine *Antiques* still was: Newport, Salem, a sunflower chest as a frontispiece. The Stickley article is the intrusion. SoHo had a gallery. Princeton had already hung the movement. A monthly that preferred block-and-shell was willing, in its furniture issue, to treat Eastwood oak as antiques.

This essay is a market history. It is not a history of 1904. The objects are old. The category is new. A price guide, a gallery card, a Streisand-era headline — those are evidence of what people would pay after leftover brown (chapter 41) became a specialty. They are not evidence of what a shop number meant in Syracuse.

## Princeton, and a modest auction first

Robert Judson Clark’s exhibition *The Arts and Crafts Movement in America 1876–1916* opened at the Art Museum, Princeton, in October 1972. The catalog, Princeton University Press, is still the book a later footnote leans on. Clark hung Stickley with Tiffany, whose fashion had already returned, and with the wider field Kaplan and others would enlarge. George W. S. Trow, writing in *The New Yorker* of 3 December 1979, called Princeton the most important moment in the restoration of Stickley’s reputation. He listed what the show had included: an inlaid Harvey Ellis side chair and writing table, a folding screen of oak and burlap and linen, a settle, a bookcase, a copper candlestick. The catalog did the other work. It made a movement a thing a collector could study without pretending to have been alive in 1901.

Trow also kept an earlier, smaller date. In April 1972, PB 84 — Sotheby Parke Bernet’s more modest rooms — included Stickley in a Young Collectors sale. A drop-front desk estimated at $80 to $120 brought $480. A library table estimated at $120 to $150 brought $320. Those figures are Trow’s report of that sale. They are useful because they are small. They describe a market that has just noticed the oak, not a market that has already decided a celebrity sideboard is news. After Princeton, Trow said, interest and prices rose sharply. There were more auctions, including two at the Plaza Gallery. Then, in 1976, a SoHo gallery opened that was devoted largely to the American movement, and especially to Stickley and to L. & J.G.

## Jordan-Volpe, West Broadway

The Jordan-Volpe Gallery, at 457 West Broadway in the years that matter here, is the dealer interior of this chapter. Vance Jordan and Tod Volpe; Beth Cathers buying for the rooms. Trow walked a 1979 desk show: fall-fronts in quartersawn white oak with hand-hammered copper pulls, trestle tables, and a spindle library table and side chair in mahogany that Cathers priced at $10,000. “People are coming to realize that a price like this is not high when you are talking about an important piece of signed furniture.” That sentence is 1979. It is not a Craftsman catalog. It is a gallery teaching a loft how to want a moral as a trophy. Trow said so, with less kindness than a dealer would use: the socialism in the history, he wrote, had become a way to make the furniture more interesting to people who did not want the stigma of other kinds of luxury.

Cathers, at the same visit, looked at a PB 84 sale Trow had just watched and declined most of it. The table was an early version Stickley did better later. The china closet’s top and bottom did not work together. Some lots were “used furniture.” That distinction — work of art versus used furniture — is the collector market inventing itself in a sentence. Condition, early date, spindle versus slat, a mark that can be read: these become the hierarchy. Factory Mission (chapter 39) and mail-order Mission (chapter 40) fall to the bottom without anyone having to say Grand Rapids is a crime. The gallery wall says it.

The gallery did not last forever under that name. Addresses move; the partnership later splits into other letterheads. For this series the 1976–87 West Broadway rooms are enough. They are where a reader of *Antiques* could go after the May 1977 article and see the oak in air-conditioning. They are also where the three names in chapter 1 began to blur on purpose. American Arts and Crafts was a category large enough to hang McHugh, Eastwood, Roycroft, and a Limbert cutout on one wall. The lesson about oak was true. The lesson about authorship was leaky. Dealers still live on that leak.

## Cathers in hard covers

David Cathers’s *Furniture of the American Arts and Crafts Movement: Stickley and Roycroft Mission Oak* appeared from New American Library in 1981 (Getty’s AAT short cite; a Plume paperback follows in 1982). Photography by Peter W. Curran. The book did what a gallery cannot: it sat on a dealer’s desk as a way to identify a mark, a form, a probable date. Later editions, including a Philmont revision, kept it in the trade. Cathers’s 2003 Phaidon *Gustav Stickley* is the monograph this series has already used as spine. The 1981 book is the collector-market artifact: identification, dating, a language of “better” and “important” that auction rooms could quote.

Tod Volpe and Beth Cathers’s *Treasures of the American Arts and Crafts Movement, 1890–1920* (Abrams, 1988), text by Alastair Duncan, introduction by Leslie Bowman, is the coffee-table version of the same decade. Color plates, a wide field, a title that says treasures. 1988 is also the year the newspapers learned they could put a singer’s name next to a sideboard. The book and the headlines are cousins. Both need a movement large enough to be a treasure. Neither is a 1904 price list.

Joseph Bavaro and William Mossman’s *The Furniture of Gustav Stickley* (Van Nostrand Reinhold, 1982) belongs on the same shelf: techniques, projects, a how-the-joint-works book for a reader who had started to care. Stephen Gray’s Turn of the Century Editions reprints — Craftsman catalogs, Lifetime Cloister, Come-Packt, Stickley Brothers Quaint — are the other 1980s infrastructure. A market that cannot see the original catalog page will invent the object. Gray made the pages cheap again. Dover’s reprint of *Craftsman Homes*, which Trow already noticed in 1979, did the same for the house-plan side.

## *Antiques*, and the monthly as a market

*The Magazine Antiques* is in this chapter because a collector who kept it — the reader this series was written for — met Stickley there as a peer of Quervelle and a Newport Townsend, not as a flea-market brown chair. The May 1977 Bohdan–Volpe article is the dated flag. David A. Hanks’s essay on the American movement, 1876–1916, had already appeared in the August 1973 issue, in the weather of Clark’s catalog. Later numbers, into the 1980s, take the subject as established. Architectural Digest and the shelter press follow, as they always follow a market that has become photogenic.

A monthly of this kind does two jobs. It publishes a footnote a curator can use. It also publishes advertisements in which a gallery names what it has. The 1973 and 1977 ads around those articles are as useful as the articles. They show what was being offered, in what language, to whom. They do not show what a 1904 retailer charged. A reader who collapses those two prices has left history for fantasy.

## Streisand-era headlines, used as headlines

In 1988 Christie’s sold a Gustav Stickley sideboard into Barbra Streisand’s collection. The newspapers treated the number as news. Later writers repeat mid-six-figure hammers as if they were as stable as a shop number `[VERIFY the 1988 Christie’s lot and hammer from the sale catalog; do not borrow a magazine’s memory]`. The useful fact for this essay is not the dollar. It is that a sideboard could be a headline. Trow had already explained the mechanism: a moral history attached to a plain object makes the object more expensive for people who want plainness as a luxury. A celebrity completes the mechanism. The leftover brown of chapter 41 is now a social fact.

The 1999 Christie’s sale of Streisand’s Arts and Crafts — Craftsman Farms corner cabinets among the lots, the same sideboard returning to the rooms — is just after this chapter’s window. Mention it only as the afterlife of the 1980s headline. Craftsman Farms’ later effort to recover house furniture is a museum story, not a 1908 shop story. This series will not turn a singer into a curator. It will use the dated type as evidence that the market had left the Young Collectors estimates of 1972.

Bruce E. Johnson’s identification and price guides — the House of Collectibles *Official Identification and Price Guide to American Arts and Crafts*, a second edition in 1992, later work with David Rago — belong here as market paper. The bibliography already warned: popular price guides are evidence of the 1980s and 1990s trade, not of 1901. A dollar column printed in 1992 describes what a dealer hoped to get in 1992. It does not describe Eastwood. It does not authenticate a finish. It can, if used badly, teach a reader to see 1904 through a 1992 window. This chapter exists to keep that window labeled.

## What the market merged, on purpose

Chapter 1 said the 1970s and 1980s did the merging on purpose. This chapter is that sentence with rooms attached. Princeton needed a movement. Jordan-Volpe needed a wall. *Antiques* needed an article that could sit next to Federal card tables. Cathers needed a book that could treat Stickley and Roycroft under one subtitle. Abrams needed treasures. Christie’s needed a lot description a reporter could spell. “American Arts and Crafts” did all of that work. Mission, in the same years, became the look-word again. Craftsman became the money-word. A McHugh rail and a red-decal shop number could hang together and teach oak and lie about authorship.

The hierarchy the market built is not worthless. Early Eastwood is not a 1916 Larkin suite. An Ellis-period inlay chair is not a Grand Rapids dowelled rocker. Condition matters. A refinish that stripped the fume (chapter 35) is a loss you can see. What the hierarchy cannot do is stand in for the trade history. A price guide that lists “Mission oak rocker, $800–1,200” has told you about a weekend in 1992. It has told you nothing about Jamestown shipping weights or about the 1911 strike.

Trow watched a 1979 PB 84 sale in which the important lots — a circular dining table with an X stretcher, six chairs, a sideboard, a china cabinet — beat or met estimates, and lesser Stickley lots failed. Cathers’s explanation: they were not in great condition. The market was already sorting. It has been sorting since. Reissues (chapter 43) will enter that sort as a new problem: a chair that looks right, dated 1989, with a mark that is honest about being new. Fakes will enter as a worse problem: a mark that is not honest. The 1970s–80s market created the incentive for both. Leftover brown had become worth stealing.

The leftover brown was also, for a few years, still cheap enough that a person who was not a loft could buy a settle at a farm sale and only later learn it had a shop number. That window closed. The next essay is what a historic firm did when it saw the window close from the other side: not a fake, not a farm sale, a reissue tag that tried to say, in burned letters, that this oak is new and this name is old.
