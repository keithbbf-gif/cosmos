# The Craftsman House as Furniture

The February 1904 number of *The Craftsman* ends a house article with a club. Page 524, volume 5, number 5, announces the Homebuilders’ Club: a subscriber may have working drawings for the residences the magazine has begun to publish. The plate above the offer is not a chair. It is a plan — rooms drawn as if a settle, a sideboard, and a pair of bookcases were walls that happened to be movable. That is the American product this chapter is about. Stickley had been selling oak since the New Furniture of 1900. From 1904 he sold a house in which the oak was already sitting down.

Harvey Ellis, dead the month before, had been hired into the architecture department, not the chair shop (chapter 12). The club outlived him by a decade. Avery Library’s *Guide to The Craftsman Architects Drawings, 1904–1915* is the paper spine: with few exceptions, each monthly issue printed plans, perspectives, and descriptions of one to four houses; the same designs were prepared as working drawings and offered through a membership that cost nothing beyond the subscription. *Craftsman Homes* (1909), *More Craftsman Homes* (1912), *24 Craftsman Houses* (1912), and *Craftsman Houses: A Book for Home-makers* (1913) gathered the plates into books a builder could leave on a table. The furniture catalogs of the same years are a different pile. They share a typeface and a moral. They are not the same order form.

## A settle that grew a chimney

The piece that makes the argument visible is the inglenook settle. In the Syracuse house Stickley occupied on Columbus Avenue, Samuel Howe’s “A Visit to the House of Mr. Stickley” (*The Craftsman*, December 1902) already walked a reader into an alcove off the dining room: a brick fireplace on the end wall, a window seat, a bench that faced the fire. The 1902 article is earlier than the club. It is the domestic rehearsal. By the time House No. 79 appears in November 1909 — a cement house whose eight-room interior the magazine called “typically Craftsman” — the inglenook and the built-in fittings are listed as if they were elevations, not afterthoughts. *24 Craftsman Houses* repeats the doctrine in a paragraph a draftsman could memorize: one or more open fireplaces; an inglenook or built-in seats and bookshelves that “carry out the structural design of the rooms”; the absence of unnecessary partitions in the main living rooms; porch room enough to live outdoors without leaving the house.

A free-standing drop-arm settle in an Eastwood catalog is furniture. The same slats, built into a nook whose back is a chimney breast, are architecture. The customer who ordered only the catalog settle could put it against any wall. The customer who ordered the house got the settle whether he liked the price of the chair or not. That is why this chapter refuses the real-estate voice. The plans are not a bungalow brochure. They are a method for making a piece of furniture too large to ship.

Built-in sideboards, china closets, linen cases, window seats, and bookcases do the same work in smaller rooms. The 1909 book is blunt about housekeeping: arrange the fittings so the work is light; in some cottages, plan for a family that keeps no maid. A sideboard that is a wall cannot be sold twice to the same client, which is a problem for a furniture factory and a solution for a magazine that wants the factory and the house to be one pitch (chapter 10 already named that pitch). The architectural department and the chair shop were arguing, on paper, about who owned the living hall.

## Who drew the plates

The renderings in *The Craftsman* do not all look like one hand. Ellis’s signed houses are a short list. Charles D. Wilsey was hired in March 1904 to head the new department and lasted a few months `[VERIFY duration from payroll]`. George E. Fowler was in charge by the bankruptcy years, about 1914. Furniture designers and draftsmen sat in the architectural room; a few outside architects were paid as consultants. Stickley’s own name is on the published houses more often than any draftsman’s. David Cathers’s periodization of the furniture — experimental, then a detailed home period, then a mature line — can be laid over the plans without much violence: 1900–03 as experiment (the Syracuse house, the first interiors); 1904–07 as the club’s first flood of detailed cottages; 1909–15 as the thicker books and the cement-and-shingle mature house. The mapping is a historian’s convenience. The plates themselves are a monthly product.

What the plates refuse is the English house as a transplant. Voysey’s white roughcast and Baillie Scott’s inlaid interiors are in the magazine’s memory. They are not on the American lot. A Craftsman house for a subscriber in Ohio or New Jersey is balloon frame or cement, a porch, a living hall, oak trim that can be ordered from Eastwood or faked by a local mill. The through-tenon ethics of chapter 34 have a domestic cousin here: a joint you can see becomes a bench you cannot move. Prairie houses (chapters 31–32) solve the same problem with a different chair. The Craftsman solution is the inglenook and the built-in, printed in a monthly that also sold a Morris chair.

The Craftsman Home Building Company, Avery notes, appears to have supervised construction in the New York and New Jersey region. That is a different business from a plan in the mail. A subscriber in Oregon received drawings and a local contractor. A client near Morris Plains might have received a crew. Chapter 14 will walk the farm where Stickley tried to live inside his own plate. This chapter stays with the plate.

## The club as a sales floor

Membership was free to subscribers. Free is a price. The magazine gained a reason not to lapse. The furniture shop gained a room already drawn to its widths. Hardware, textiles, and, later, a Craftsman furnace appear in the supporting literature as installations a homebuilder could specify `[VERIFY furnace as a catalog item versus a contractor’s option]`. More than fifty franchised furniture outlets, the Winterthur finding aid says, were selling Craftsman goods while the club grew. The house plan is the largest advertisement those outlets never had to pay for. A reader who built House No. 79 still needed chairs. A reader who only wanted chairs had been taught, month after month, what room the chairs were for.

The living hall is the room. Victorian parlors (chapter 3) had been for display. The Craftsman hall is for a fire and a settle and a table that can take a meal. Partitions come out so the hall can see the dining room. Privacy is pushed upstairs or into a den. The plan is a social argument dressed as convenience. It is also a furniture argument: if the hall is one space, you need fewer suites and more of the big pieces — settle, table, sideboard — that Eastwood already knew how to make. Grand Rapids could print a “Mission” parlor suite (chapter 39) and ship five matching chairs. The Craftsman house plan is a way of making that suite look overdressed.

There is a limit. A built-in bookcase is only as good as the carpenter on the lot. A subscriber who cheapened the oak or omitted the nook still lived in “a Craftsman house” because the magazine had sold him the name. Stickley could not police every lintel. He could only keep printing the plate. The later Craftsman Building in Manhattan (chapter 15) tried to police the plate by putting model rooms and a drafting studio under one roof. The club of 1904 is the cheaper, paper version of that ambition.

## What the 1909 book thinks a home is

*Craftsman Homes* is not a pattern book in the old mail-order sense, though it was used that way. It is a bound argument that a middle-class American household could substitute “the luxury of taste for the luxury of costliness” — Avery quotes the claim, and the books repeat it. Taste, here, means a fireplace you walk toward, woodwork that is the decoration, and furniture that does not apologize for looking like the house. Costliness means the applied ornament the Eastlake factory had already worn out (chapter 3) and the parlor suite the trade papers still pushed.

Look at a typical first-floor plate from the 1904–09 run. The living room and dining room share a sight line. A chimney mass sits on the inner wall, not only on the gable. Benches are drawn in. A sideboard is a run of cupboards. The kitchen is smaller than a Victorian kitchen and more tightly tied to the dining room, because the book is already imagining a house without a servant’s full day. Sleeping porches appear as the decade wears on. Cement, shingle, fieldstone, and clapboard take turns on the elevations; the interior grammar stays. That grammar is furniture grammar: post, rail, panel, a horizontal that stops the eye where a chair rail would.

The inglenook is the most stubborn of these fittings because it wastes floor space on purpose. A builder trying to wring bedrooms out of a small lot will cut it. Stickley’s plates keep drawing it. The nook is where the settle becomes a room. Without it, the house is a bungalow with good trim. With it, the house is a piece of Craftsman furniture you can walk into. Robert Winter’s bungalow studies and the later California literature will have their own nooks. This chapter’s nook is the one on a Syracuse editor’s page, offered to a nation of subscribers who might never see Eastwood.

## A plate is not a house

Avery warns that published designs sometimes differ from the working drawings. A magazine plate can lie a little: a porch deepened, a closet invented, a stair made prettier than a carpenter will build it. The club’s job was to turn the pretty lie into dimensions. That translation is the unglamorous half of “house as furniture.” A settle in a catalog has a shop number and a price. A settle in a wall has a rough opening and a mason. The Craftsman program wanted both to feel like the same honesty. Only one of them could be returned.

By 1909 the furniture line and the house line were close enough that a reader could confuse them, which was the point. By 1913 the confusion had a twelve-story address. The farm in Morris Plains was already trying to be the life the plates described. None of that makes the February 1904 announcement a footnote. The club is the moment the magazine stopped being only a caption for chairs and became a caption for a floor. Ellis had drawn a few of those floors. Wilsey, Fowler, and unnamed draftsmen drew the rest. Stickley signed the book.

The leftover fact is small and physical. In a well-built surviving house from these plates, the settle in the nook is still there when the movable chairs have been sold off. The architecture kept the furniture. The next essay is the house Stickley built for a school that never opened, then lived in with Eda and the children, then sold in 1917 because the numbers under the plates had come due.
