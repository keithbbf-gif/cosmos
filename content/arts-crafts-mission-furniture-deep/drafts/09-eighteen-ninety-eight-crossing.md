# The 1898 Crossing

In July 1900, on the floor of the Grand Rapids Furniture Exposition, Gustave Stickley showed a line he was willing to call New Furniture. The English crossing that later accounts attach to 1898 sits behind that floor, with Henry Wilkinson’s drawings and, possibly, LaMont A. Warner’s. Morris did not put him on a payroll. The shops he walked — if 1898 is the year Winterthur’s finding aid means, and if 1895 and 1896 are the earlier lookings chapter 8 already dated — were already selling a moral at a price. The American problem is not whether a Wisconsin-born manufacturer admired William Morris. The problem is what he shipped to Grand Rapids in 1900, who drew it, and how a trade city in Michigan received a Syracuse plant’s first Arts and Crafts line.

This essay will not make Red House an American workplace. It will not make Morris & Co. his employer. It will stay with a crossing, a partnership of drawings, and a catalog name: New Furniture.

## Which crossing, and what a man could see

The later preface likes a single pilgrimage. Stickley, newly in possession of his firm after 5 May 1898, crosses to England, sees the truth, and returns to design honest oak. Winterthur’s COL 60 note supports a European visit in 1898. Cathers and Fish support earlier crossings in April 1895 and in 1896. All three can be true. A salesman-manufacturer who had already put imitation bamboo on a machine (1896) and mahogany in a hotel foyer was not a virgin traveler in 1898. What 1898 adds is ownership. He walked London as the principal of the Gustave Stickley Company, not as Simonds’s partner. That change of letterhead is the change that matters. A partner looks. An owner buys a direction.

What he could have seen, without inventing a diary, is the public English stock. Morris & Co. still sold Sussex chairs and a Morris chair with an adjustable back. Liberty’s sold a version of the same taste at a different price. C. F. A. Voysey’s furniture and houses were in print. M. H. Baillie Scott’s drawings were circulating; they would matter later, when Harvey Ellis was in the Eastwood orbit (chapter 12). C. R. Ashbee’s Guild of Handicraft was still in London; the move to Chipping Campden is 1902, after Stickley’s line had already been shown. He did not need to sit in Kelmscott’s garden. He needed to see that an English firm could attach a sentence about labor to a chair that had a price.

He did not meet Morris. Morris died in 1896. He did not meet Ruskin in any sense that produced a collaboration. The American reception of those two names is chapter 2. The 1898 crossing, whatever shops it included, is a furniture man in England with a plant at home. The danger in the older biographies is the word *disciple*. A disciple does not fire his partner and then shop the competition. Stickley shopped.

Voysey and Baillie Scott offered something Morris’s older firm was less able to: a modern house grammar that could be printed. Stickley’s later magazine would print houses. The 1900 furniture already wants a room. That want is English in its sources and American in its freight. No one in Hammersmith invoiced Eastwood.

## Wilkinson, Warner, and the drawings that are not a signature

Henry Wilkinson was an architect. The museum and foundation chronologies put him at Stickley’s side for the first Arts and Crafts works, the experimental New Furniture of 1900. LaMont A. Warner is the more cautious name: “possibly,” “later,” “soon his first staff designer.” Wikipedia’s hedge — “possibly, LaMont A. Warner” — is the hedge this chapter keeps. Cathers and the Dallas 2010 catalog are the places to check a specific drawing before a chair is given to one man `[VERIFY individual attributions in Cathers and Tucker]`. What can be said without a false precision: the 1900 line was not a solitary midnight vision in a Syracuse suburb. An architect and, perhaps, a staff designer were in the room. Stickley’s later habit of absorbing collaborators into a shop mark starts here, before Ellis, before the magazine’s house-plan architects.

Wilkinson’s training shows in the early pieces that still carry a Gothic arch or a plant form that an architect would draw before a factory would simplify. Warner, if he is already there, is the man who would become the in-house designer the plant needed once the line had to repeat. The “possibly” is not a weakness. It is the record. American Arts and Crafts furniture is full of rooms in which the owner’s name survived and the draughtsman’s did not. McHugh and Dudley are the New York version (chapter 6). Stickley and Wilkinson are the Syracuse version. Pretending otherwise is how a brand gets written as a life.

## New Furniture, July 1900: what was on the floor

Stickley’s own later recollection, in *Chips from the Workshop of Gustave Stickley*, is the primary text for the turn. In 1900 he stopped using the standard patterns and finishes and began to make furniture after his own designs, independently of what other factories required. For about a year he experimented with “more or less fantastic forms.” His European journeys had interested him in the decorative use of plant forms. The Arts and Crafts movement was closer to what he had in mind than those plant forms were, but even that movement, he said, did not mean a return to the sturdy and primitive shapes meant for usefulness alone. He began to apply structural principles directly.

The sequence inside 1900 is therefore not a single style. There are floriform tables — the Celandine and the Poppy — that found a press. *House Beautiful* (the Chicago magazine, not Wilde’s talk) promoted the Poppy table in October 1900 and gave the new work a national caption. Those tables are closer to Art Nouveau and to a designer’s plant study than to a slatted settle. They sold. They are easy to leave out of a Mission genealogy because they do not look like the 1904 catalog. Leaving them out makes 1900 look like 1904. It was not.

Beside the plant tables, and then instead of them, came the rectilinear oak: plain surfaces, exposed mortise-and-tenon, hardware in iron or copper that had been given a hammered face, finishes that tried not to hide the grain. White oak, already a factory wood, became the moral wood. Ammonia fuming as a standard Eastwood process is slightly later — the January 1902 inventory, in the Craftsman Farms plant study, lists only eighteen fumed samples (chapter 11). In 1900 the color argument is already there; the sealed fuming room is not yet the plant’s habit.

Grand Rapids in July was the right floor. It was the trade’s town. A Syracuse maker who wanted more than a local hotel job had to be seen there. The reception was good enough that the Tobey Furniture Company of Chicago took the line for distribution and promoted it as “modern.” Tobey is a department-store and wholesale name, not a guild. The first national life of Stickley’s Arts and Crafts furniture is a Chicago dealer’s adjective. Craftsman, as a trademark, is still ahead. Mission, as McHugh’s word, is already on Fifth Avenue. Stickley was late to the popular noun and early to the magazine.

Some of the tea tables and plant stands that would appear at Buffalo in 1901 carried Grueby tiles, the Boston matte green. Grueby had its own stand at the Pan-American. The combination is a period friendship between two reform products. It is also a price. A tile inset is not a medieval workshop. It is a purchased square of ceramic in a wooden frame. Morris’s sentence about usefulness and beauty — the sentence American catalogs never tired of — fits a Grueby-topped stand if you want it to. It also fits a tobacconist’s tray. The sentence cannot choose.

## What he did not become in England

He did not become a member of a guild. Ashbee’s model, soon to leave London for a Cotswold town, was admirable on paper and unworkable as a plan for Eastwood. Eastwood had a rail siding and a payroll. Syracuse was a city with a university, a salt history, and a furniture labor pool. Relocating the plant to a village would have been a different career. Roycroft, in East Aurora, came closer to the campus fantasy and still printed and manufactured at scale (chapter 19). Stickley’s United Crafts of 1901 would borrow the guild word and keep the ownership. The 1898 crossing taught him, if it taught him anything structural, that the English moral could be attached to a firm. It did not teach him to stop being the firm.

He did not become Morris’s continuation. Morris & Co. was a London decorating business with a catalog, a history of church work, and a political name. Stickley took the chair type (the adjustable-back armchair that American shops already knew as a Morris chair) and the motto *Als ik kan* — “if I can,” Flemish, which Morris had used at Red House — and put both on American oak. Taking a motto is not employment. Taking a chair type is not a license. The American Morris chair is its own later chapter (36). The 1900 line is broader: tables, seats, stands, the first settles, the first insistence that a joint should be visible on purpose.

He did not become an English designer who happened to live in Syracuse. The New Furniture is American in its wood, its machines, its Grand Rapids debut, and its Tobey contract. The English trip is a source, not a passport. Writers who need Stickley to be “the American Morris” are trying to make a disciple out of a manufacturer who had just fired his salesman. The better sentence is the one Cathers’s kind of monograph allows: he saw shops that had solved the problem of selling a moral at a price, and he went home to solve it with a plant he already owned.

## After Grand Rapids, before the monthly

The rest of 1900 is expansion of a familiar kind. He moved the family to a larger Syracuse house. He leased space in the Crouse stables for offices and a showroom — the local building he would call the Craftsman Building, a name chapter 11 will keep separate from the later twelve-story Manhattan lease. He was still Gustave, with the e. He had no magazine. He had a line that a Chicago house would sell as modern and that a later collector would file as early Craftsman or, lazily, as Mission.

Buffalo in 1901, the Pan-American, put some of this furniture in public beside McHugh’s New York Building rooms and Rohlfs’s Buffalo work. The fair did not choose. It displayed. Stickley’s choice, that same year, was to rename the firm United Crafts, to have Irene Sargent write a catalog, and to found *The Craftsman*. Those are the next essay’s objects. They are not inevitable. A man could have stayed with Tobey, stayed with New Furniture as a trade name, and never printed a monthly. The 1898 crossing does not contain the magazine. It contains a look at English shops and a return to a plant that then spent a year on fantastic forms and structural ones at once.

The residual tension is the plant form. The Poppy table and the slatted settle share a year and a maker. Only one of them became the American memory. The other is what a crossing actually looks like before a brand edits it: a manufacturer trying plant ornament and Gothic structure and English talk, in a Michigan hall, with an architect’s drawings on the table and a Chicago dealer waiting for a crate.

The next essay opens a monthly that edited the year: *The Craftsman*, volume 1, number 1, October 1901, Irene Sargent on every article, a house and a chair argued as one pitch.
