# Eastwood: Plant, Address, Catalog

Shop number 155, the Double Manor Hall Seat, is in the early paper at twenty-three dollars. The Craftsman Farms plant study that walks the 1901 inventory calls it the costliest of the benches in the New Furniture catalog: wrapped in Spanish leather, over two feet long, against a single hall seat (no. 151, wooden top) that retailed at thirteen. A related wooden-topped hall seat is no. 154 in the same study. It was built at Eastwood, a Syracuse suburb with a rail siding and a payroll, not in a Cotswold shed. A shop number is an address in that plant. It is a finish option, a photograph, and a line in a catalog that had to be printed after a man named Lester Standen took the picture in the rubbing room. This chapter starts on that number and that suburb. The romantic sentence — handmade only, a craftsman at a bench, no machine in the story — is the sentence the plant will not support.

Stickley & Simonds put a factory in Eastwood in 1893 (chapter 8). The Gustave Stickley Company took the plant in 1898. United Crafts named it in 1901. The Craftsman Workshops named it again in 1904. The buildings outlasted the names. What happened inside them is machines and hands on the same chair, ammonia on quartersawn white oak, and a paper trail of shop numbers that collectors still use because the wood does not speak a date by itself.

## The suburb, the stables, and two Craftsman Buildings

Eastwood is now part of Syracuse. In the 1890s it was the suburb that had land for a plant and a way to ship. Gustav’s family life in these years still ran through Auburn and then through Syracuse houses; the work ran through Eastwood. At the very end of 1900 he rented the Crouse stables in Syracuse for executive offices and a showroom and called the place the Craftsman Building. The name is local. It is a reused stable. It is not the twelve-story midtown Manhattan Craftsman Building he would lease in 1913 — the department-store fantasy that chapter 15 will have to price. Mixing the two buildings is how a caption gets written by someone who has seen a postcard of 29th Street and not a Syracuse city directory.

The Crouse showroom is where a middle-class customer in Onondaga County could stand in front of ammonia-fumed quartersawn oak without going to Grand Rapids or to McHugh’s Fifth Avenue. The plant remained in Eastwood. The magazine, for its first years, said Eastwood on the title page. The paper label that collectors call the Eastwood label — the word prominent at the bottom, often with a pencil note of model, finish, and upholstery — is a short-lived shop mark, about 1905–07 in the usual dating. It is also a confession of address. The factory wanted the suburb on the goods.

Syracuse University is the other local fact. Irene Sargent taught there. The early *Craftsman* is a campus-and-plant collaboration in one city (chapter 10). Eastwood did not need Manhattan to exist. Manhattan, when it came, needed Eastwood to keep sending crates.

## What the floor actually did

The Stickley Museum’s factory study, using the 1901 inventory, is the unromantic source. After a seat was assembled it was stained and finished. W. Michael McCracken’s note, quoted there, puts serious fuming late in 1901: the January 1902 inventory lists only eighteen pieces treated that way, all samples. Fuming was not the origin myth of 1898. It was a process the plant adopted as the line hardened. Ten finishers are documented at the time of New Furniture. One of them, Upfold, English-born, March 1871, is on the books as early as 1911 in one reading of the records `[VERIFY Upfold’s first year; the study’s “as early as” is the hedge]`. He was good enough that Stickley kept him.

Photography was in-house. The inventory lists a dark room and a photo stand among the rubbing-room fixtures. The finished piece did not have to be carried to a downtown studio. Lester Standen worked as a photographer for Stickley by 1899. Frank J. Russell was the artist on the catalog team. Frederick Meeker, English-born, is the probable printer. Publishing a catalog at all was a departure; no catalogs have been located from the Binghamton and Auburn years. The plant became a print shop for its own goods. That is why a shop number exists as a public fact and not only as a chalk mark on a bench.

Machines did the converting, the ripping, the sanding that a plant of this size cannot do with a plane if it intends to fill Tobey’s orders and, later, a hundred retailers’ orders. Hands did the fitting, the chamfer, the hammering of hardware, the leather, the inspection. Stickley wrote about the use and abuse of machinery in *The Craftsman* 11, no. 2 (November 1906). Frank Lloyd Wright’s 1901 Hull House lecture, “The Art and Craft of the Machine,” is the Chicago cousin of the same American sentence: the machine, rightly used, could make a clean form available. English Ruskinism had less use for that sentence. Eastwood had no choice. A handmade-only Eastwood is a dealer’s lullaby. The inventory is a factory.

Hidden screws and concealed iron brackets appear even on early “honest” pieces. The visible through-tenon is a chosen show joint. It is not a ban on metal. Chapter 34 will take the ethics of that choice. This chapter only needs the shop truth. Honesty was a design, executed with whatever fasteners the bench required.

## Quartersawn white oak, and the brown that fuming made

*Quercus alba*, quartersawn, shows medullary-ray fleck on the face. American furniture factories already knew the cut. It is stable. It is decorative without carving. It wastes more log than a plainsawn board, which is why it reads as quality in a catalog. Stickley preferred it as a native wood — “as an American by birth, I chose to work with native growths,” he wrote in October 1904 — and he liked the grain to remain visible under color. Chestnut, mahogany, maple, and other woods are in the early lists. Oak is the memory.

Ammonia fuming is a chemical conversation with the tannin in the oak. Concentrated vapor darkens the wood without a pigment stain sitting on top like paint. Peter Betjemann, writing much later, described the caustic reality: a 26 percent solution, not household strength, dangerous to skin and lung. This series will not give a method. It will say that Eastwood fumed, that the brown of 1902–10 is often that brown, and that later refinishers stripped it and then stained, which is why a “fumed” chair in a shop today may be a guess (chapter 35). The January 1902 sample count is the beginning, not a medieval inheritance. Early color notes also mention gray-brown, green, and a darker gray, color-effects “largely from wood.”

A catalog that says “fumed quartersawn white oak” is making a materials argument and a sales argument at once. The fleck photographs well. The brown photographs as serious. Grand Rapids could stain a similar cut and call it Mission. The Eastwood difference, when there is one, is in the hardware, the proportion, the shop mark, and the monthly that told you why the brown was a virtue. The wood is not a trademark.

## Catalogs, marks, and the number on the seat

*Chips from the Workshop of Gustave Stickley* (1901) is the pamphlet that is already a catalog in prose. United Crafts issued a catalog with Sargent’s writing the same year. Retail plates of 1902, later Craftsman Workshops books, the Dover and Turn of the Century reprints a collector now uses — those are the public numbers. Shop no. 155 is one. A Morris chair will have another (chapter 36). A spindle cube and a slat armchair will have others (chapter 38). The number is how a plant talks to a retailer. It is not a poem, and it is not a fake when it is real. It becomes a fake when a later reproduction borrows the silhouette and not the paper (chapter 43).

Marks changed with the letterhead. The earliest Arts and Crafts paper sticker, Gustave Stickley Company, is thought to run about 1900–01; whether it reaches back into Stickley-Simonds is unclear. The compass with *Als ik kan*, illustrated in the October 1901 *Craftsman*, is the guild claim. Red decals, branded marks, the Eastwood paper label, a transitional label after New York showrooms opened, a late branded mark of about 1912–16 — Cathers’s *Furniture of the American Arts and Crafts Movement* and the Dallas 2010 catalog (pp. 244–45) are the places a reader checks before trusting a dealer’s dating. This chapter will not reprint a complete mark chart. It will say that Eastwood put its name on paper because the plant knew the difference between a Syracuse chair and a Grand Rapids cousin.

The 1901 inventory’s photo stand is why the catalog look is consistent. Russell drew. Standen shot. The rubbing room was both a finish room and a studio. A handmade-only myth has no need for a dark room. A plant that sells by mail has every need.

## What the plant was not

It was not a medieval guild. United Crafts’ profit-sharing story and compass do not rewrite the deed. Stickley remained the owner. The workmen were employees. Some of them were skilled enough to be named in an inventory. Most of them are not named in a collector’s caption.

It was not the Manhattan Craftsman Building. The Crouse stables, late 1900, are the Syracuse showroom that borrowed the name first. The twelve-story lease of 1913 is a different building, a different city, and a different chapter of the accounts.

It was not McHugh’s Popular Shop. No willow-and-Mission hybrid needed to leave Eastwood. No Fifth Avenue decorator’s seasonality ruled the floor. Eastwood made a line and then made a magazine to keep the line attached to a life. When the line had to be cheaper and more standard, from about 1904, the plant invested in more equipment, not less. The “standard Stickley” of 1904–12 is a factory success. Collectors who prefer the 1901–03 experimental pieces are preferring the year the plant was still deciding. Both years were Eastwood.

It was not handmade only. The sentence is worth a paragraph of its own because the afterlife depends on it. A through-tenon can be cut by a machine and fitted by a hand. A slat can be ripped on a saw and scraped at a bench. Fuming is a chemical process in a sealed space, not a monk’s stain. Leather is tanned elsewhere and tacked here. Grueby tiles were bought. Iron and copper hardware were made in a metal shop Stickley added in 1902, which is still a shop, not a forge in a fairy tale. The catalog’s hammered face is a finish. It is a beautiful finish. It is not a proof that no bandsaw stood twenty feet away.

Harvey Ellis walked into this plant, and into the magazine, in 1903. He was dead in January 1904. The inlay chairs that carry his name were made here, by more than one pair of hands, on the same floors that made shop number 155. The next essay takes a documented inlay chair — the Met’s 2014.633 is the object the outline allows — and keeps Ellis to six months and a myth.
