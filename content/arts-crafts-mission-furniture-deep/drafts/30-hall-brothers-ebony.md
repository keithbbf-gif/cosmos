# Hall Brothers, Ebony, Cloud Lift

The arm of a living-room armchair made for the Robert R. Blacker house, Pasadena, 1908–09, does not stay level. Two short arcs rise in the mahogany, meet, and flatten. A square of ebony is let into the front post. Los Angeles County Museum of Art holds the chair; Edward S. Cooke, Jr., published X-rays of its joints in *American Furniture* 1993. The lift is cut in the rail. The peg sits in a hole that was drilled, then squared with a chisel. The joint behind the peg is a tight-cheeked tenon in a deep mortise. The peg does not pin that tenon. It is a dark period in a sentence the shop already knew how to finish.

That is the object this chapter starts on. Chapter 29 has the Gamble dining room. Chapter 28 has the Greenes before the Gambles. This chapter has the shop that could make the rail: Peter Hall (1867–1939) and John Hall (1864–1940), Swedish-born, Pasadena-trained, the mill and the contract behind almost every mature Greene and Greene piece a museum will now show you.

## Who was on South Raymond

Cooke is still the essay that treats the Halls as makers rather than as a caption under the Greenes’ names. John and Peter were born in Stockholm and were in Illinois by 1872. Peter reached Pasadena in 1886 and made a name as a stairbuilder. He worked in Seattle and in Port Townsend from 1889 through 1891 — Cooke writes Oregon; the town is in Washington `[VERIFY Cooke’s state]`. He came back to the millwork firm called Pasadena Manufacturing Company. John worked there in the 1890s, was a foreman by 1899, and was still listed that way in Thurston’s Pasadena directory for 1906, compiled in the summer and issued in September.

Peter left the company in 1900 and opened his own contracting firm in 1902. The Greenes first used him on renovations to the Todd Ford house in 1905. On 15 June 1906 he took a permit for a one-story carpentry shop on South Raymond Avenue, just as work began on the Henry M. Robinson house. The William T. Bolton house started the next month. House and furniture are not the same calendar. Drawings for the Robinson hall table are dated October 1906. Oral history in the Greene and Greene Library says Peter did not enlarge the shop, the machines, or the crew until 1907. Cooke therefore dates the Robinson furniture 1907 against a tradition that still prints 1906. The Huntington’s dining host chair for the Laurabelle A. Robinson house (object 000.121.1) is labeled 1905–06 and names John Hall as maker. The disagreement is the point. A rail this careful does not appear the week the foundation is poured.

The firm’s letterhead kept changing. Thurston’s 1908–09 directory says Peter Hall. A piano plate for their brother, the Rev. Albert Hall, says Hall and Hall, probably 1908 or 1909. In 1911 they incorporated as Hall Manufacturing Company; a granddaughter kept a seal with that year. Peter was the contractor. John ran the mill and was the head cabinetmaker. Makinson’s *Greene & Greene: Furniture and Related Designs* (1979) and Bosley’s *Greene & Greene* (2000) keep the brothers in the story. Cooke is the one who puts them on the bench.

## What a cloud lift is doing

A cloud lift is a rise in a horizontal, two arcs and a plateau, the American shop name for a form Chinese furniture historians call a humpback stretcher when it sits under a Ming chair. The Greenes had already been looking at Japanese and Chinese work; Charles’s 1906 trip is chapter 28’s problem. The lift is not a decal. It is not a stencil. It is not a brass tag. On the Blacker armchair the lift is a change in the rail’s top edge that a spokeshave and a scraper had to follow. On a Gamble House living-room rocker the same idea is bolder. On a dining-room built-in it can be almost shy. The Gamble House Conservancy’s own furniture notes — and the photographs that circulate with them — show the peg on a master-bedroom chair following the upward arch of the crest instead of sitting flat. Even the peg was asked to belong to that particular rail.

A catalog Mission chair cannot afford that sentence. Eastwood, at its best, repeats a slat. The Hall shop, at its best, repeats a method and varies the lift. That is why “Greene and Greene style” as a later furniture-store phrase is a theft. The style was a conversation between a drawing and a bench, one piece at a time. Tradition says Charles stopped at the shop on his daily rounds and talked to John about whatever was in the clamps. The tradition is late and oral. Treat it as weather, not as a signed minute. The furniture is the minute.

## What the X-rays show

Cooke’s X-rays of the Blacker armchair are the least romantic evidence we have. The mortises that take the splat at the rear seat rail and at the crest were chopped roughly before the splat was finished. The finished splat then became the pattern for housed mortises scribed tight. The mortise in the crest, where it meets the rear post, is overcut in depth; the cheeks are tight enough that no pin or screw shows. The groove for an ebony bead set the mortise’s thickness: the maker chopped from the groove in. The bead fits, adds a little strength, and gives the eye a dark line so the joint line can disappear. At the front arm, the tenon is glued; the decorative ebony plugs sit in holes piloted and then squared; a bead is pieced and bent in a groove that runs past the joint.

None of that is a through-tenon ethics sermon (chapter 34). It is millwork thinking: machine the stock, house the joint, hide the screw, spend the hand time on the surface the client will touch. The Gamble master-bedroom chiffonier uses the same economy. Rails, stiles, and panels were cut, shaped, scraped, and sanded first; then the joints. Intersections are offset, not flushed. Drawer fronts are finger-jointed — table saw or multiple dado — then screwed, the screws under modeled ebony plugs. Drawer backs are butted and screwed. An ebony cover strip with a machine-cut dovetail key hides the top edge. Bottoms are plywood in grooves. An oak center strip runs in a rabbet on the dust panel so a slightly out-of-square drawer still slides. The guest-room dressing table uses a different drawer: blind tongue-and-groove, again a machine joint. David Swanson, who worked those bedroom suites with Bror Krohn, called that joint a “Swedish joint.” Cooke records the phrase from Swanson’s family.

The shop did not dovetail drawers. Gottlob Karl Lapple, German-trained, worked for the Halls from 1908 until about 1913 and dovetailed his own family’s cabinet. He never dovetailed a Hall drawer. The method was a house method. Erik Peterson and George Nelson were the chairmakers who made prototypes; the rest of the crew copied the prototype. That is not a medieval workshop. It is a mill that could also carve a pull.

## Mahogany, ebony, and the bill

The early Greene furniture — Reeve, Tichenor, the oak-and-ash years — still speaks Stickley: thick stock, through-tenons, butterfly splines, ring-porous native hardwoods. Makinson publishes those pieces. The Robinson dining room at the Huntington is a different wood system: Honduras mahogany, thinner verticals, modeled edges, no tool marks left as a moral. Cooke’s point is blunt. The change is inside out. A new shop made a new furniture, not only a new drawing.

Ebony is the punctuation. Some plugs cover screws, especially on breadboard ends and battens. Many plugs are set in their own little mortises and imply a pin that is not there. Popular woodworking later called the decorative majority a disappointment, as if a peg that does not pin were a fraud. The Hall shop was not pretending the plug was a pin. It was writing a rhythm the eye could count. The Blacker bench at the Museum of Fine Arts, Boston, shows the other honesty: a wide top tongued into battens, the middle screws fixed, the outer screws in slotted washers so the board can move, metal buttons with slotted holes underneath, ebony over every countersunk head. The wood is allowed to live. The plug is allowed to lie a little, if lying means covering a screw.

A bill dated 8 January 1913, in the Gamble House Craftsman file, prices a plain sideboard for Charles Sumner Greene himself: 186¾ hours at fifty cents, $93.37; 128 feet of mahogany; a little oak; plywood; hardware; plating; express. Total $141.60. Labor is two thirds. That is the shop’s own arithmetic for a piece without the inlay the Blacker armchair wears. Makinson estimated about four hundred pieces from the Halls while they worked almost exclusively for the Greenes, 1907 to 1913. Peter’s letter of 9 August 1909, written while Charles was in London, says the Gamble furniture is “almost completed,” the Blacker furniture “well underway,” the Thorsen pianos “underway.” The letter is better evidence than a later adjective.

## What the shop was not

It was not Eastwood. It was not a Mission factory. Crown City Manufacturing, in the same town, advertised “mission furniture” in 1906. The Halls did not. They made houses and, inside the houses, furniture that belonged to the trim. A reader who files a Blacker armchair under Mission oak has not looked at the mahogany. A reader who files it under Craftsman has not looked at the shop mark — there is no red compass. The names in chapter 1 do not stretch this far without tearing.

It was also not a lone-genius bench. The Greenes had taken Woodward’s manual-training courses in St. Louis; Charles could make a table as a wedding present in 1900. He could not have produced the Robinson, Gamble, Blacker, Thorsen, Pratt, and Culbertson furniture alone, on the calendar those houses required. Alan Marks, writing in *Fine Woodworking* in 1978, was among the first to look inside the joints. Cooke revised Marks with the X-rays, the directories, and the payroll of Scandinavian names. The furniture is a collaboration that has a letterhead and a permit date.

By late 1913 the Greene furniture commissions thinned. Key men left: Swanson and Anton Erikson to Binderheim Studios; Sven Carlson to Crown City; Lapple after them. The Halls turned the same saws and shapers to redwood boxes for dried fruit — finger joints, molded edges, a smooth finish. A fire in 1921 took the two-hundred-foot frame shop, the boxes, the seasoned redwood, the machinery, and, the families later said, eight upright pianos from the Greene years. Peter went back to contracting: St. Paul’s Cathedral in Los Angeles in 1923, houses, a chapel. John kept finishing and cabinetmaking, sometimes with his brother. The Fleishhacker game table and chairs of 1923–25, often given to Charles alone in Carmel, share Hall conventions; Cooke thinks John was still in the work. After that the cloud-lift rail is a museum problem.

The next essay leaves Pasadena for a Chicago dining table whose chairs are a wall. Wright is not a Mission designer either. He is the other American who thought a chair could be architecture. He did not need ebony pegs to say it.
