# Wilde on the Lecture Platform

Chickering Hall, Fifth Avenue and Eighteenth Street, 9 January 1882. Oscar Wilde, twenty-seven, lectured on “The English Renaissance.” The New York papers had been practicing on his fur coat and his knee breeches since the harbor. The hall was a concert room that took paying talks. The audience was mixed: people who had read *Patience*, people who had read Ruskin, people who wanted to see the costume. What they heard, once the costume jokes thinned, was a decorative-art argument that American rooms would later file under House Beautiful. The argument was not a chair. It would be sold, by the end of the year, as a second lecture with that title.

This chapter is a tour, not a biography. The Irish life, the trials, the later fame — other books. The American problem is dated and small: what a talk did to rooms that still held Eastlake parlor tables (chapter 3), ten years after Osgood and four years before McHugh’s Mission brand. Wilde did not design Mission furniture. He did not found a shop. He moved a sentence through a country that already knew how to turn sentences into invoices.

## Two talks, a hundred halls

The 1882 itinerary is long enough to be its own document. Lloyd Lewis and Henry Justin Smith’s *Oscar Wilde Discovers America* (1936) is the old narrative census. Later reprints of the lecture texts — “The Decorative Arts” and “The House Beautiful” as reconstructed from notes and newspaper transcripts — are the words a furniture historian can use. The first talk leaned on a Renaissance of English making. The later talks got more practical: what to put on a wall, what not to varnish, why a young man in a factory town might still care about a pot. Newspapers paraphrased. They also invented. A quote without a paper and a date is a parlor game. Mark those `[VERIFY]`.

He went west. He went south. The titles moved with the box office. By early February the long English-Renaissance talk had been recut as “The Decorative Arts”; Wilde wrote his manager, Colonel W. F. Morse, to stop advertising the old name. The most conscious switch is usually put at Chicago on 13 February 1882. He needed a second lecture for cities that booked him twice. On Saturday, 11 March, at Central Music Hall, State and Randolph, Chicago, he gave the domestic version that began as “Interior and Exterior House Decoration” and became known as “The House Beautiful.” Platt’s Hall, 216 Montgomery Street, San Francisco, took four nights: 27 March (a revived Renaissance, because four dates had been sold), 29 March (art decoration applied to the home), 1 April (a ladies’ matinee billed as House Beautiful and Decorative Arts), and 5 April (Irish poets, invented for the extra night). Charles E. Locke managed the local dates; the Palace Hotel held the rooms. The *San Francisco Chronicle* of 2 April noticed the Saturday hybrid. He went to Leadville, Colorado, in April, and the story that followed him out of the Matchless mine — reciting Cellini to miners, drinking, being liked because he could take a joke — is the American Wilde the later furniture world prefers, because it makes taste look democratic. The Leadville papers are checkable. The Cellini flourish is the kind of flourish a lecturer uses when the room is not Chickering Hall. None of it designs a settle. A modern count puts the year at 141 lectures. Chickering Hall, demolished in 1901, is only the first dated room.

What the tour did design, if the word is allowed, was a public who had heard an Englishman say that a house could be an art problem without being a palace. American women’s clubs and lecture bureaus already sold that idea in thinner form. Wilde made it theatrical. *House Beautiful* as a magazine title (later, 1896, in Chicago, under a different enterprise) is not his trademark. The phrase was in the air. His tour is why furniture people still put his name next to it. The magazine will furnish bungalows. The 1882 talk furnished an evening.

## What he praised, and what shops could use

Wilde praised Morris’s work, Japanese things, and a general refusal of sham. He was not precise about American factories. He did not need to be. A listener in Cincinnati or St. Louis could walk home and look at an incised Eastlake table with a new suspicion, or with a new permission to call it artistic. Both outcomes happened. The tour is not a cause of Mission oak. It is a weather system. McHugh’s customers in the 1890s had grown up in that weather, or their decorators had.

He also praised the handmade in a country that was already the world’s machine shop. The contradiction is the same one chapter 4 left on the Centennial lawn: a Japanese dwelling without iron next to Machinery Hall. Wilde’s platform version is prettier and less specific. A miner in Leadville who liked him was not about to throw out a machine-made chair. A decorator on Fifth Avenue could use the talk as a caption for goods already on the floor. The Popular Shop (chapter 6) is that kind of business. It sells rooms. A lecture is advertising that the shop did not have to buy.

Do not make Wilde a secret furniture designer. The chairs associated with his London rooms are a different literature — Godwin, the aesthetic interior, ebonized Anglo-Japanese. American Mission oak is not that interior with a darker stain. The posture is different. The wood is different. The moral caption, by 1901, will be Stickley’s, not Wilde’s. What survives from 1882 in the later oak is only this: a middle-class American had already been told, in a rented hall, that beauty in a house was a serious subject. *The Craftsman* will tell them again, monthly, with a price list.

## Newspapers, sunflowers, and the wrong object

The sunflower and the lily were the tour’s logos, assigned by *Punch* and by Gilbert and Sullivan before the ship docked. American cartoonists kept them. A furniture history that starts with the sunflower has started with the costume. The object in this chapter is the platform: a rented hall, a pitcher of water, a man paid by a bureau to talk for an hour. That object is closer to *The Craftsman*’s later lecture culture — Irene Sargent, visiting speakers, the Craftsman Building’s later programs (chapter 15) — than it is to a slat armchair.

When later house magazines use House Beautiful as a title, they are spending a phrase that Wilde spent for a season and that American editors spent for a century. The 1896 Chicago *House Beautiful* is a periodical with its own editors and its own bungalow years. It is not a Wilde product. It is evidence that the phrase had become American property. A Mission series mentions it so a reader does not think Stickley invented the idea of a beautiful house. He invented a magazine that tried to sell the house and the chair together (chapter 10). Wilde sold only the evening.

## Associated Artists, and the rooms that were already “aesthetic”

Wilde did not arrive in an undecorated country. Associated Artists — Louis Comfort Tiffany, Lockwood de Forest, Samuel Colman, Candace Wheeler — had been selling artistic interiors in New York since 1879. Herter Brothers could still deliver a Japanese-knowing room to a Vanderbilt. The aesthetic interior, ebonized and peacock-blue, is a 1870s–80s American fact. The lecture tour rode that fact. It did not create it. A furniture history that treats 1882 as year zero of American taste has not walked those shops.

What Wilde added was a portable lecture that smaller cities could buy. Tiffany did not go to every town. A bureau speaker did. That is why the tour matters more than the sunflower for this series. Mission oak will be a portable product. The House Beautiful talk was a portable argument. Both depend on a rail system and a middle class that pays for culture by the evening or by the suite. The content differs. The distribution rhyme is real.

Wheeler’s later writing on decoration, and the women’s decorative-art societies that predate and outlast the tour, are the other half of the portable argument. Wilde was a man on a platform. Much of the actual reforming of American rooms was done by women who ran classes, juries, and shops. Kaplan’s survey keeps them in the frame. A Mission series that only follows male factory names will repeat the 1980s dealer habit. This chapter is a place to say the habit is wrong, even though the opening object is still a man in a hall.

## What the talks said about making

The reconstructed “Decorative Arts” lecture praises the craftsman who is allowed to invent, which is Ruskin in a lighter coat (chapter 2). It praises children’s art education. It attacks the American habit — as Wilde saw it — of stuffing a room with things that have no job. Newspaper transcripts vary. One night’s version leans on Greece; another on Morris; another on Japan. The variance is the archive. Do not quote a tidy paragraph as if it were a published essay. Do quote the job: an hour of paid speech that made making sound like a public subject.

American manual-training schools were already making making a public subject. Calvin Woodward in St. Louis, the later Sloyd imports, the city school shops — Boris again, if the reader wants labor rather than lectures. Wilde’s tour did not found those schools. Teachers used the fashion of the tour when it helped a levy. That is reception. It is not authorship.

Leadville remains useful because it refuses the idea that the tour was only Fifth Avenue. A mining town paid for a talk. The talk changed nothing about the mine. It added a night to the town’s record of visiting curiosities. American Arts and Crafts will always have that double audience: the decorator who can order a room, and the crowd that comes to look. Stickley’s magazine tried to merge them. Wilde’s hall kept them in their seats, then sent them out the same doors they came in.

The costume, once more, is a trap. Knee breeches sold tickets. They also let later writers treat the whole year as comedy. The comedy is real. The tickets are real. The leftover sentence — a house can be thought about — is the part that still sits under a Mission search. A reader who came for oak should know the sentence was spoken in a concert hall in January, in a mining town in April, and in a dozen forgotten opera houses in between, years before anyone branded a rail Mission.

## What a dated hall still teaches

Chickering Hall is gone in the way New York concert rooms go. The date is not. 9 January 1882 is a better opening than “Wilde influenced American taste.” Influence is fog. A hall, a title, a newspaper the next morning — those are facts. Leadville in April is another fact. The reconstructed lecture texts, with their uneven authority, are a third. Together they say: before Mission had a brand, American paying audiences sat still for an English talk about pots, walls, and the refusal of veneer.

The talk did not clear the parlor of Eastlake tables. It did not stop Grand Rapids. It did not found Eastwood. It added a voice to a stack that already held Ruskin (chapter 2), Eastlake (chapter 3), and the memory of a Japanese dwelling (chapter 4). The next essay opens a New York shop that knew how to turn such voices into a line called Mission, designed in-house by Walter J. H. Dudley, advertised as if the California missions had been a lumberyard.

A last dated scrap: the D’Oyly Carte office booked the tour because *Patience* had already sold the costume. The furniture series can thank the opera for the tickets and still refuse the opera as a source of oak. The oak comes later, from other shops, with other lies.
