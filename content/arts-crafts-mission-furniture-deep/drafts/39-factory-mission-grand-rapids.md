# Factory Mission in Grand Rapids

The cut in *The Furniture Record* is a matching suite: a davenport with square arms, a rocker, an armchair, and a library table with a lower shelf. The caption says Mission. The maker’s name, when the plate bothers to print one, is a Grand Rapids plant that already knew how to cut a slat before Gustav Stickley printed a manifesto. The posts are straight. The leather is tacked. The oak is dark enough to read as “honest” from a retailer’s chair. The page is not a sermon. It is a price.

This essay is not a sneer at factory oak. Snobbery without shops is a cheap history. Grand Rapids — Furniture City by its own billboards, and by the twice-yearly markets that brought buyers to the river — is the American reason Mission furniture furnished the middle of the country. Eastwood could argue. Fifth Avenue could decorate. A plant on the Grand River could add a Mission page between the January market and the July one, and ship a suite to a store that had never heard Irene Sargent’s name.

## What the trade paper was for

*The Furniture Record* (often *The Grand Rapids Furniture Record*) was the house organ of a manufacturing town. *Furniture World* was the other weekly a buyer actually read. Neither paper was *The Craftsman*. They printed cuts, prices, finish names, freight talk, and the gossip of a trade that measured success in carloads. A 1912 volume of the *Record* still treats Mission as a current line `[VERIFY a specific plate and page in vol. 25]`. W. L. Kimerly’s *How to Know Period Styles in Furniture*, issued by the Grand Rapids Furniture Record Company in 1912, is the same weather in a bound book: Mission is a period a retailer can learn, not a religion a factory must join.

The papers also printed labor. In August 1911 the *Record* ran “The backbone of the strike broken” (index: GRF 08-01 1911, p. 518). The strike had begun on 19 April. Furniture workers wanted a nine-hour day, a pay raise, an end to piecework, and the right to bargain as a body. The manufacturers’ association refused collective bargaining. The walkout lasted seventeen weeks. Dutch Reformed pulpits and a city that lived on furniture payrolls helped end it. A history that treats Grand Rapids Mission as a moral failure of “copying Stickley” has not read the paper the factories read. The oak left those plants because men stood at machines, and because those men could stop.

A November 1906 clipping still circulating in the Grand Rapids Public Library’s furniture files jokes that Mission chairs look like Franciscan penance, and that hair shirts were found in the California missions at the same time. The joke is dated. It is also evidence. By 1906 the look was common enough to be mocked in the same breath as a holiday advertising season. Mockery is what a trade does when a line has already sold.

## How fast a suite could appear

Knockoff speed is the Grand Rapids skill this chapter has to name. A New York decorator’s Mission chair (chapter 6), an Eastwood shop number (chapter 11), a Limbert cutout from Holland (chapter 23) — any of them could be seen at a market, sketched, and translated into a suite a midwestern retailer could advertise by fall. The translation was not always theft of a shop mark. It was often theft of a silhouette: slat back, square post, leather seat, a stain that read fumed even when the ammonia never touched the board (chapter 35). Glue and dowel could imitate a through-tenon that never came through (chapter 34). A factory that already cut Eastlake incising in the 1870s (chapter 3) did not need a Ruskin seminar to cut a flat stretcher.

Albert Stickley’s Stickley Brothers plant is the Grand Rapids case that still confuses collectors. Chapter 17 walks that factory. Here it is only a fact of the system: the 1908 *Quaint Furniture* catalog, No. 30, offered to furnish a hall, a den, a dining room, a bedroom, a club, and a taproom in “this justly popular school of design.” That sentence is Grand Rapids talking. The school is popular. The assortment is complete. The plant is prepared. Quaint was Albert’s word. Mission was the word the *Record* used when it wanted a buyer to recognize the look without learning a brother’s trademark.

Charles P. Limbert’s Grand Rapids years, before the Holland plant took the cutout tables, belong to the same market calendar (chapter 23). So does the middle-market oak that Grand Rapids Bookcase and Chair, over in Hastings, sold as Lifetime — Cloister Styles in a catalog of about 1910–11 (chapter 24). Hastings is not Grand Rapids. It sold into the Grand Rapids system: buyers, finish names, a matching suite, a paper label. Michigan Chair Company, Grand Rapids Chair Company, Phoenix, and a dozen lesser marks added pages. The point is not a census. The point is that “factory Mission” is a trade category, not a single guilty plant.

## What a suite was selling

A suite is the factory’s unit. Stickley sold chairs and settles that could live with a house plan. A Grand Rapids Mission suite sold a room: davenport, rocker, armchair, table, sometimes a tabouret and a plant stand, all in one finish, all numbered so a retailer could telegraph an order. The leather could be real or a substitute. The oak could be quartersawn where a customer would see the fleck and flatsawn where he would not. The “Mission” in the caption did the work McHugh’s brand had done in New York: it told a clerk what to say.

That is why the look spread faster than the argument. *The Craftsman* needed a monthly reader. A suite needed a furniture man who had two days in Grand Rapids and a notebook. Don Marek and the Grand Rapids Public Museum’s furniture literature are the modern place that keeps the plants attached to streets and to the Exposition buildings. The Exposition was not a world’s fair. It was a twice-yearly indoor market. A line that missed one season could be on the floor for the next. That is knockoff speed as infrastructure, not as a personality defect.

The construction is mixed, and it should stay mixed in the reader’s eye. Some factory Mission is dowelled and proud of a clean machine surface. Some shows a peg or a false through-tenon applied as hardware. Some, especially from plants that had already learned English “quaint” oak, is better joined than a collector who only handles Eastwood wants to admit. Quality is a range. Chapter 20 said the same of Roycroft. It is true of Furniture City. A Lifetime Cloister settle and a cheap mail-order rocker (chapter 40) are not the same object. Both were possible because Grand Rapids had already standardized the slat.

## The moral that factories could use, and the one they could not

Chapter 2 left a portable idea in the shop: a joint you can see is a moral; a veneer that pretends to be a board is a lie. Grand Rapids could adopt the look of that moral and ignore the wage. Many plants did. The 1911 strike is the proof that the wage was not a caption. A factory could stain a chair dark, print “Mission” under the cut, and still run piecework. Ruskin’s Gothic workman is not on that time sheet. Neither is Stickley’s profit-sharing story. The *Record* is.

There is a second thing a factory could use: regularity. A bandsaw wants matching posts. A retailer wants a suite that looks like the cut. American factory Mission is regular on purpose. The “handmade” quality, when it is there, is in the chamfer, the hardware, and the refusal of applied gingerbread — the same refusals Eastwood made, at a different price, with a magazine attached. Greene and Greene took the opposite American path (chapters 29–30). Neither path is a California mission. Both paths used a name the *Record* could set in type overnight.

Collectors later learned to say “Grand Rapids” the way an earlier generation said “Eastlake”: as an insult that meant machine, glue, and a finish that hid the grain. The insult is sometimes fair. A dowelled rail that pretends to be a keyed tenon is a lie of a kind. It is not the only fact. The other fact is that the United States furnished itself from plants that could change a line in a season. Without those plants, Mission furniture is a few shops and a magazine. With them, it is a national interior. Chapter 1 promised that Mission, in this series, means the trade name and the slatted oak look that McHugh, Grand Rapids, Sears, and lesser marks sold. This chapter is the Grand Rapids half of that sentence.

## What the Exposition taught a buyer

A buyer who came to Grand Rapids for the market walked buildings full of samples. He did not walk a Craftsman house. He walked aisles. Mission stood next to golden oak, next to mahogany parlor suites, next to the first Colonial reproductions that would eat the line after the war (chapter 41). The buyer’s notebook is the missing primary source of American Arts and Crafts. We have the catalogs. We have the *Record*. We rarely have the note that says: take the slat armchair, skip the settle, match the table to last year’s stain.

Stickley could be severe about that notebook. He was not wrong that a factory suite was not his program. He was wrong if he thought the notebook was a misunderstanding. It was the American furniture business doing what it had done with Eastlake: take a name that had moral heat and cool it into a line. The heat did not vanish. It became advertising copy — “simple,” “sturdy,” “no useless ornament” — the same words Lifetime’s Cloister catalog would print in Hastings. Words travel faster than shop marks. Grand Rapids knew that before Syracuse did.

The city also knew how to protect a price. Furniture manufacturers had organized; later writers have accused them of price-fixing in the years around 1898 `[VERIFY the 30 percent market-share claim that travels with that accusation]`. Whether the number holds or not, the habit holds: Grand Rapids thought of itself as a system. The Furniture Manufacturers Association of 1911 is that habit given a letterhead. A Mission suite is a product of the system. So is the strike the system refused to bargain with.

## A chair that is neither Craftsman nor a crime

Stand a Grand Rapids Mission armchair next to a documented Eastwood slat chair and a McHugh branded rail. The silhouettes agree more than the sentences do. The Grand Rapids chair may have a paper label, or a burned mark that is not Stickley’s, or nothing. The fleck may be real quartersawn white oak on the crest and a cheaper cut on the seat frame. The leather may have been replaced in 1960. A dealer in 1982 will still say Mission, and he will be using the *Record*’s word, whether he knows it or not.

The collector market of the 1970s (chapter 42) needed a hierarchy. Gustav at the top, the brothers next, Limbert and Roycroft in the next bin, factory Mission in the remainder. Hierarchies are useful for a gallery wall. They are a poor account of 1908. In 1908 the *Record* was not ranking souls. It was moving suites. A shop that still builds in this grammar (chapter 44) needs the 1908 account more than it needs the 1982 one. The joint is the work. The name on the cut is the market.

Factory Mission in Grand Rapids is the reason the style could die in the 1920s and still leave oak in a thousand houses that never subscribed to a magazine. It is also the reason a later fake can look “right” from across a room. The factories taught the country the silhouette. They taught it too well. The next essay is the silhouette at a still lower price: a Sears page, a Larkin premium, a freight rate, and a chair that arrived in a crate, knocked down, smelling of soap and oak.
